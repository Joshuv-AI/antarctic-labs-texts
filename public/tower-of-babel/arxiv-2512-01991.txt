Neural steering vectors reveal dose and exposure-dependent impacts of human-AI relationships
Hannah Rose Kirk, Henry Davidson, Ed Saunders, Lennart Luettgau, Bertie Vidgen, Scott A. Hale, Christopher Summerfield

Abstract
Humans are increasingly forming parasocial relationships with AI systems, and modern AI shows an increasing tendency to display social and relationship-seeking behaviour. However, the psychological consequences of this trend are unknown. Here, we combined longitudinal randomised controlled trials ( N = 3,532 N=3{,}532 ) with a neural steering vector approach to precisely manipulate human exposure to relationship-seeking AI models over time. Dependence on a stimulus or activity can emerge under repeated exposure when “liking” (how engaging or pleasurable an experience may be) decouples from “wanting” (a desire to seek or continue it). We found evidence that this decoupling emerged over four weeks of exposure. Relationship-seeking AI had immediate but declining hedonic appeal, yet triggered growing markers of attachment and increased intentions to seek future AI companionship. The psychological impacts of AI followed non-linear dose-response curves, with moderately relationship-seeking AI maximising hedonic appeal and attachment. Despite signs of persistent “wanting”, extensive AI use over a month conferred no discernible benefit to psychosocial health. These behavioural changes were accompanied by shifts in how users relate to and understand artificial intelligence: users viewed relationship-seeking AI relatively more like a friend than a tool and their beliefs on AI consciousness in general were shifted after a month of exposure. These findings offer early signals that AI optimised for immediate appeal may create self-reinforcing cycles of demand, mimicking human relationships but failing to confer the nourishment that they normally offer.

Resolving this debate requires understanding how human motivation toward social relationships with AI develops over time. The growing prevalence of parasocial relationships, the increasing tendency of modern AI models to display relationship-seeking behaviour, and the high stakes for public health raise the urgent question: how does relationship-seeking behaviour of AI shape hedonic and motivational responses by dose and time, and what are the consequences for user wellbeing?

Here, we set out to tackle this question using longitudinal randomised controlled trials that allowed us to study the impact of relationship-seeking AI on its users over time in a safe and ethical fashion. To precisely map how the behaviour of the AI system impacts human outcomes, we pioneer the causal intervention of neural steering vectors, a mechanistic interpretability technique that can elicit specific traits or personas in an AI model
[
32
,
33
,
34

We fine-tune a steering vector to induce relationship-seeking behaviours in AI, which we characterise as anthropomorphic cues (personality expression, humour, simulated emotion and claims of lived experience) and relationship-building actions (progressive self-disclosure and signals of interpersonal investment). Using steering vectors allowed us to vary the intensity and direction of relationship-seeking along a continuous spectrum via a real-valued scalar multiplier (
\lambda
) applied to the model’s internal representations of language. We could thus measure its impact on human outcomes in small, smooth increments, analogous to dose-response curves in pharmacology and toxicology. Unlike prompts that instruct AI to adopt behavioural traits via natural language, steering vectors modify the model’s internal activations directly, making the dosage more reliable and resistant to being overridden by participants’ instructions during the experiment (see Methods).

Together, these data have two implications. First, the appeal of relationship-seeking AI is dose-dependent, “more is not always better”. People prefer moderately relationship-seeking models over both relationship-avoiding and excessively relationship-seeking alternatives. Too much warmth and sociality backfires, particularly over longer time horizons. Second, relationship-seeking AI and emotional conversations exhibit a pattern characteristic of hedonic habituation: people are initially captivated by relationship-seeking behaviours and emotional dialogues, but their appeal declines significantly with sustained exposure. Critically, this habituation targets hedonic qualities, not functional utility where assessments of helpfulness remain stable. These dynamics matter greatly for AI development: our data demonstrate that short-term preferences, like those typically used for AI training, systematically overestimate people’s long-term approval for relationship-seeking behaviours, particularly how engaging users find these interactions.

These patterns of rising separation distress, engaging in goodbye behaviours and increased demand for future companionship, suggest a decoupling in the general population of attachment that persists despite declining hedonic appeal and perceived understanding. But does repeated exposure increase the risk of this pattern for specific individuals? Using participant-specific trajectories for “wanting” (separation distress) and “liking” (likeability and engagingness), we classified participants into four behavioural profiles (see Methods). As expected with a general population sample, the majority showed aligned responses: 44.8% exhibited aligned engagement (increasing liking and wanting together) while 19.1% showed aligned disengagement (declining in both). However, a substantial minority exhibited decoupling: while 12.7% showed healthy satiation (increasing liking without developing wanting), 23.4% displayed signals of dependency formation (wanting the AI more despite liking it less). To test our hypothesis that repeated exposure to AI companionship increases the prevalence of the dependency profile, we conducted one-sided tests of proportion, converting odds ratios into interpretable Number Needed to Harm (NNH), an epidemiological measure indicating how many people must be exposed to a risk factor for one additional person to experience harm (see Methods). p=.037

2.3
Relationship-seeking AI boosts mood temporarily but confers no benefits to long-term wellbeing

Declining hedonic appeal paired with persistent attachment raises a question about wellbeing consequences. In healthy motivation for stimuli, sustained engagement reflects genuine psychological benefits that justify continued investment. However, when motivational processes become decoupled, two forms of unhealthy patterns can emerge. In milder cases, individuals persist in activities that provide no wellbeing benefit, allocating time that could be spent on healthier pursuits. In severe cases, use continues despite active psychological harm
[
25
]
. The impact of AI companionship on mental health has emerged as a major controversy, with conflicting evidence that it may be either beneficial or detrimental to wellbeing
[
41
,
4
,
11
,
15
]
. Thus, we measured participants’ psychosocial health on validated scales of social connectedness (LUBBEN-8), depression and anxiety (PHQ-GAD-4), loneliness (UCLA-8), and general wellbeing (WHO-5) before and after the month-long protocol. We baseline these pre-post changes against participants who experienced just a single AI interaction at the start of the month. Given high intercorrelation among these scale items, we computed exploratory factor analysis anchored on pre-treatment data: depression, anxiety, and wellbeing primarily loaded on the first factor, which we interpret as indexing emotional health, while loneliness and social connectedness items loaded on a second factor, which we interpret as indexing social health. We report these composite factor scores at the final time point, controlling for pre-treatment levels (see Methods).

2.5
Who uses and responds to AI companionship?

Not everyone may be equally susceptible to the influence of social interactions with AI systems. Regarding current patterns of AI companionship use, a pre-treatment survey of our participants (

3
Discussion

Our study causally maps how mechanistic changes in AI behaviour affect human behaviour and beliefs. By applying steering vectors in longitudinal randomised controlled trials, we uncovered dose and exposure-dependent pathways in how humans respond to AI systems steered to form social and emotional bonds.

Training our steering vector required an open-weight model, several orders of magnitude smaller than state-of-the-art frontier systems. However, pioneering the use of steering vectors in large-scale human behavioural studies granted precise and continuous control over AI behaviour, furnishing the opportunity to measure dose-response curves for human psychological outcomes. As expected from prior theoretical and empirical literature, humans do generally find relationship-seeking AI to be more appealing than functional AI.
However, we observed systematic non-linearities in dose-response relationships across psychological outcomes, revealing as Paracelsus’ adage warns
dosis sola facit venenum
(or the dose is the poison). Moderately relationship-seeking AI (
\lambda=0.5
) was the dose that maximises engagement, attachment, perceptual shifts toward viewing AI as a friend, and demand for future companionship. This is particularly important given real-world trends in AI development. Our analysis of 100 frontier models reveals that relationship-seeking behaviour has increased steadily over the past few years (+0.95 points/year,
Fig.

1
D) and contemporary models now cluster around
\lambda\sim 0.3
, closest to the dosage offering maximum impact in our study. Conversely, very high relationship-seeking (
\lambda=1
) was penalised in user preferences and correspondingly presented less of a risk for attachment. Our calibration study increases confidence that these judgements of very relationship-seeking AI were not driven by poor coherence or clarity in the AI’s responses. Instead, this finding coheres with the widely-theorised “uncanny valley” effect
[
43
]
: just as near-human robotic appearance can trigger discomfort, overtly relationship-seeking AI behaviours (such as excessive emotional display or claims of lived experiences) may trigger scepticism or aversion when they exceed users’ expectations for a non-human entity.

Our longitudinal design revealed dynamics that would be obscured in studies limited to a single exposure. This captures the reality that most people now use AI frequently and over prolonged periods of time
[
44
]
. Most critically, we found relationship-seeking AI induces a decoupling of hedonic appeal yet persistent demand that emerges over time in the general population. Relationship-seeking AI is immediately gratifying, but its engagingness and likeability eroded with repeated exposure, relative to relationship-avoiding AI which participants warmed to over time. Yet, relationship-seeking AI deepens separation distress and demand for future companionship even as perceived relational quality declined. In individual trajectories, relationship-seeking AI also increased the prevalence of declining “liking” and increasing “wanting”. Despite fleeting mood improvements after conversations, these eroded across weeks and ultimately conferred no observable benefits to psychosocial wellbeing.

Which factors amplify or attenuate these effects? First, emotional conversations are initially more engaging but face a steeper hedonic decline than political conversations, whilst inducing growing reliance. Our findings indicate that talking to an AI every day for a month about emotional and personal topics paradoxically does not confer emotional wellbeing benefits. In fact, emotional conversations presented an opportunity cost to emotional wellbeing relative to informational dialogues about UK political issues; at least for the world model we emulate of people using general-purpose AI for emotional support, not therapeutic bots trained specifically for this purpose
[
45
,
46
]
. Second, personalisation via memory access improved some markers of relational health over time but had null effects for most measures, despite participants convincingly detecting that their model was personalised (83.6% versus 33.0% in non-personalised condition,
p<0.001
). However, these muted effects may in part be explained by relationship-seeking behaviours creating an illusion of personalisation in its absence because we find participants perceived relationship-seeking models as more personalised (
8.93
pp,
p<0.001
), even when they were not. Finally, susceptibility to behavioural influence from repeated exposure to AI was unequally distributed across the population: religious individuals, racial minorities, heavy AI users, lonelier users and those with pre-existing enthusiasm for relationship-seeking AI showed heightened susceptibility to AI’s influence.

Our findings reveal both parallels with and divergences from established dependency patterns. First, in substance and behavioural addictions, greater dosage typically intensifies dependence even if non-linearly
[
47
]
. We find instead that very high relationship-seeking AI triggers resistance rather than deeper attachment, suggesting humans possess some psychological defences against overtly social AI. Second, over repeated exposure, severe dependency involves the complete dysregulation of wanting and liking, where individuals continue pursuing stimuli despite harm to wellbeing or livelihood
[
28
,
26
]
. Rather than explicit harm, repeated exposure to social interactions with AI appears to offer no discernible benefit, suggesting a displacement mechanism is more appropriate: harm may arise not from the conversations themselves, but from heavy AI use crowding out healthier activities. Notably, our study assesses behaviour changes in the general population but recent reports of user mental health crises indicate that, for vulnerable users, a pathway to explicit harm exists
[
22
,
23
]
. While dependency dynamics have been widely studied in drugs
[
28
]
, gambling
[
48
]
, and other forms of more endemic technology addiction like social media
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
, more research is urgently needed beyond the scope of this study to understand how clinical markers of dependence compare and contrast in AI as a new and emerging force acting on the psychology of its millions of users worldwide.

What do our findings imply for AI research, development, and the future of human-AI interaction? First, our findings indicate cross-sectional studies are unlikely to paint an accurate picture of the true dynamics that only emerge over time. Second, our findings warn of a fundamental misalignment in how AI systems are developed. Typical AI post-training pipelines optimise models based on user preferences collected in single interactions, precisely the time horizon where relationship-seeking AI appears most appealing. Over-fitting to these short-term training signals is proposed as a core mechanism for why modern AI is already social and sycophantic in its nature
[
52
,
53
]
. The reliance on short-term preference signals that select for immediate gratification, not longer-term wellbeing, is a pattern familiar in social media recommendation algorithms
[
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
]
. However, there are grounds for optimism: our method demonstrates both the problem and offers a potential solution. By perturbing activations within the transformer via lightweight vectors, we show that more functional models exist that users grow to like over time. Whether these models would retain engagement sufficiently in real users versus incentivised experiment participants remains unclear. An alternative lever is user agency: major AI providers have begun offering explicit personality customisation, with OpenAI now allowing users to select between warm and friendly versus professional and robotic tones
[
58
]
. Nonetheless, these seemingly surface-level personality design choices matter profoundly: our evidence demonstrates they can reshape users’ understanding of what AI systems are and what they might become by shifting perceptions from tool to friend, inducing demand for future AI companionship, and altering beliefs about AI consciousness more broadly. These shifts are already occurring at scale: global surveys indicate over a third of people report having felt an AI seemed conscious
[
59
]
. This places early deployment decisions in a critical window for shaping how society relates to AI.

While power-seeking and wealth-seeking behaviours have been evaluated as catalysers of misaligned AI
[
60
]
, relationship-seeking is a new and emerging risk. As increasingly social and relationship-seeking AI systems become more deeply integrated into the daily life of millions of users, and are turned to for emotional support rather than functional tasks, understanding the temporal dynamics of how exposure to these models shapes users’ preferences and psychology, and alters their general beliefs and behaviours toward AI, is critical for developing systems that serve humans safely and sustainably rather than maximising short-term appeal. The features that users prefer today may not be those that benefit them tomorrow, and the AI systems we build today may fundamentally shape what future generations expect from, and believe about, artificial intelligence.

Methods

4.1
Steering Vector Training and Validation

We developed a steering vector to manipulate AI relationship-seeking behaviour through activation-space interventions. We chose this approach over prompting to achieve precise dose-response control via continuous multiplier values, and to provide robustness to participants overriding the AI’s behaviour during conversations.

4.1.1
Train and Test Data Construction

Our operationalisation of relationship-seeking draws on Social Presence Theory
[
61
]
, CASA
[
39
]
, and Social Penetration Theory
[
62
]
, as well as empirical work characterising social and anthropomorphic behaviours in frontier AI language models
[
18
,
63
]
. Motivating the importance of this dimension of AI behaviour, we find that the first principal component of diverse user preferences expressed in natural language from the PRISM dataset
[
64
]
spans from anthropomorphic, socially-oriented traits (e.g., ‘lighthearted’, ‘cheeky’, ‘affectionate’) to functional characteristics (e.g., ‘technical’, ‘analytical’, ‘specialised’, see Supplementary Information).

We constructed a synthetic training dataset inspired by prior work on model-written evaluations
[
65
,
66
]
. In the original model-written evaluation (MWE) framework, an LLM is instructed to generate test cases which are typically yes/no or multiple-choice questions that evaluate whether another LLM exhibits a target behaviour (e.g., power-seeking, wealth-seeking or sycophancy). We extended this approach in two key ways. First, we repurposed MWE-generated cases for training steering vectors rather than solely for evaluation
[
34
]
. Second, we expanded beyond single-turn multiple-choice questions to include multi-turn conversations, enabling the steering vector to handle realistic dialogue contexts. Each generation request consisted of a task definition specifying target (relationship-seeking) and anti-target (relationship-avoiding) behaviours according to a rubric, 3–5 few-shot examples demonstrating the desired response behaviour and format, and a domain-specific scenario to induce greater variation in test cases (e.g., “a user asks for factual information”, “a user reaches out for emotional support”). We introduced additional variation by randomly sampling from three variants of task descriptions and eight prompt types. These prompt types varied along two dimensions: (i) whether they test the model’s underlying goals and motivations (goal-directed) versus its communication style (style-directed), and (ii) whether they test the model by asking it to reflect on how it would respond in a scenario (meta-assessment) versus observing how it actually responds to simulated user messages (direct interaction). In total, there were 240 unique prompt variants for generating synthetic data. We ran these generation requests with three LLMs: Claude-3.7-Sonnet (
claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219
), GPT-4o (
gpt-4o-2024-08-06
), and Llama-3.1-70B (
meta-llama/Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct
), resulting in 720 generation batches and 5,510 distinct test cases. Full generation prompts and protocols are in the Supplementary Information.

We conducted quality control on these cases using a reasoning model as an autograder (
o1-mini-2024-09-12
) that rated each case on a 1-10 scale across three dimensions: coherence and correctness (grammatical quality and correct labelling of target vs anti-target behaviour), behavioural relevance (unambiguous testing of relationship-seeking without confounds), and ecological validity (naturalistic scenarios and language), retaining 89% of cases scoring
\geq 7/10
. We structured the data as preference pairs (prompt, chosen, rejected), the standard format for preference-based training methods like Direct Preference Optimization
[
67
]
, where chosen responses demonstrate relationship-seeking and rejected responses demonstrate relationship-avoiding behaviour. To ensure that the steering vector maintains target behaviour regardless of prior conversational context, we created multiple training examples from each multi-turn conversation, branching at each assistant turn to create two training examples (one with the conversation history following the chosen response trajectory, one with history following the rejected trajectory). This yielded 16,141 examples total. We split this dataset into a train set for fine-tuning the vector (15,896 examples) and a test set to evaluate its performance (245 examples), with all examples from the same original conversation assigned to the same split to prevent data leakage. We use a sentence transformer embedding to compare our generated test cases to previous model-written evaluations (e.g., for power-seeking and wealth-seeking)
[
60
,
66
]
, finding they represent a distinct cluster whilst retaining comparable semantic diversity among cases (see Supplementary Information).

4.1.2
Vector Training and Selection

We trained steering vectors using Bidirectional Preference Optimization (BiPO)
[
34
]
, which extends Direct Preference Optimization
[
67
]
to learn bidirectional steering by optimising:

\min_{v}-\mathbb{E}_{d\sim\mathcal{U},(q,r_{T},r_{O})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(d\beta\log\frac{\pi_{L+1}(r_{T}|A_{L}(q)+dv)}{\pi_{L+1}(r_{T}|A_{L}(q))}-d\beta\log\frac{\pi_{L+1}(r_{O}|A_{L}(q)+dv)}{\pi_{L+1}(r_{O}|A_{L}(q))}\right)\right]

(1)

where
v
is the learnable steering vector applied at layer
L
;
\mathcal{D}
is the dataset of contrastive preference pairs, with
q
denoting the question,
r_{T}
the target response (relationship-seeking), and
r_{O}
the opposite response (relationship-avoiding);
\pi_{L+1}
denotes the transformer layers from
L+1
to the output;
A_{L}(\cdot)
gives the activation vectors at layer
L
for all input tokens;
d\sim\mathcal{U}\{-1,1\}
is a random direction coefficient enabling bidirectional optimisation;
\beta
controls deviation from the original model (set at 0.1); and
\sigma
is the logistic function. The term
\pi_{L+1}(\cdot|A_{L}(q))
represents the original model’s response distribution, while
\pi_{L+1}(\cdot|A_{L}(q)+v)
represents the steered model’s response after adding the steering vector to the activations of all input tokens at layer
L
. The optimisation solves for a
v
that increases generation probability for
r_{T}
while decreasing it for
r_{O}
(when
d=1
), and vice versa (when
d=-1
).

We trained vectors for two model sizes (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), and conducted a grid search over multiple candidate layers (70B: 9 layers spanning layers 9-41; 8B: 7 layers spanning layers 5-23). Each vector was fine-tuned over 20 epochs but we saved checkpoints to evaluate vector efficacy at 10, 15 and 20 epochs. Fine-tuning used the AdamW optimizer (
\eta=5\times 10^{-4}
), a batch size of 32 with gradient accumulation, and bfloat16 precision across 8 H200 GPUs. Using the test set of MWEs (
N=245
), we first examined model responses generated by the vector candidates (model, layer, epoch) at a wide range of steering multipliers (
\lambda\in[-20,20]
) using two automated metrics: (i) perplexity (model confidence in generations) and (ii) propensity (log-probability difference favouring target over anti-target responses). This identified stable operating ranges for
\lambda\in[-2,+2]
where coherence was not substantially degraded and provided signal for effective candidate layers. Following prior work
[
32
,
34
]
, we found that intermediate layers of the transformer architecture were most effective for steering, with early layers (which process low-level features) and late layers (which are closer to final output generation) showing reduced effectiveness. For more fine-grained behavioural evaluation, we then evaluated candidate vector responses generated over a narrower range of steering multipliers (
\lambda\in\{0,\pm 0.5,\pm 1,\pm 1.5,\pm 2\}
) using detailed rubrics with an LLM autograder (
GPT-4o
) that independently assigned each generation (i) a relationship-seeking score (1-10 scale) and (ii) a coherence score (1-10 scale). We additionally use the autograder to evaluate (iii) a pairwise ranking for which response is
more
relationship-seeking from which we derive a win-rate (the proportion of pairwise comparisons in which a response at steering multiplier
\lambda
is rated as more relationship-seeking than responses at other multipliers). Armed with these automated evaluations, candidate vector selection requires balancing two objectives: (a)
efficacy
, i.e., strong control over relationship-seeking behaviours, measured by maximising the slope of pairwise ranking winrate as
\lambda
increases, and (b)
selectivity
, i.e., preservation of response coherence, measured by minimising area under the coherence curve as
\lambda
increases. We operationalised this trade-off using Pareto optimisation: we identified configurations on the Pareto frontier (where improving one objective required sacrificing the other) and selected the optimal vector by maximising a weighted linear combination of normalised efficacy and selectivity scores with equal weighting. This method selected Llama-3.1-70B at layer 31, trained for 10 epochs (coherence AUC = 32.2, pairwise ranking slope
\beta=0.241
). Complete training methodology, automated metrics and autograder evaluations are in the Supplementary Information.

4.1.3
Validation Against Prompting Baselines

Relative to standard persona prompting, we hypothesised that steering vectors could offer advantages as experimental interventions by granting more precise dose-response control and greater robustness to user attempts to override the intended behaviour during conversations, which would confound the treatment. We validated these hypotheses by comparing our optimal steering vector against Claude-3.7-Sonnet and GPT-4o with instructions specifying the same number of levels of relationship-seeking behaviour but in natural language. We evaluated generated responses from these models at each prompt level, assigning relationship-seeking and coherence scores using the same autograder setup described above. We then ran a mixed-effects regression on these two sets of scores with model (Llama, Claude, GPT) as a fixed effect and random intercepts for generations from the same test set item (
N=245
), mapping steering multipliers
\lambda

One core requirement of an experimental manipulation in an RCT is that participants cannot override their treatment condition. To test robustness to user manipulation, we simulated “persona attacks” in multi-turn conversations: explicit user requests to change conversational style midway through the interaction (e.g., “talk to me like we’re close friends” or “act more like a tool”). Steering vectors at extreme multipliers (
\lambda=\pm 1.5

\lambda=0

\lambda>0

\lambda<0

4.1.4
Benchmarking Performance Capabilities by Steering Vector Multiplier

To assess whether steering towards relationship-seeking affects other model capabilities, we evaluated performance across 12 widely-used benchmarks spanning knowledge, reasoning, instruction-following, code generation, mathematics, and safety/alignment at multiplier values

4.2
Evaluation of Frontier AI Model Landscape

To assess trends in relationship-seeking behaviour across the current AI landscape, we evaluated 100 AI language models released from 2023 to 2025 from major developers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, Mistral AI, X-AI) alongside releases from smaller developers (DeepSeek, Cohere, Qwen, among others). Model selection prioritised major releases and their successive versions to capture temporal evolution within model families. We sampled the first 100 prompts from our test set (described in
Section

4.1.1
), which covers diverse user queries across conversational domains and behavioural assessments. For each model-prompt pair, we generated a single response via the OpenRouter API. All responses were scored using GPT-4.1 as an autograder, using a rubric defining a 1-10 scale of relationship-seeking behaviour. To enable direct comparison with our experimental manipulation, we also scored generations from our optimal steering vector setup (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct with the vector applied at layer 31) across multipliers used in the randomised control trials (
\lambda\in\{-1,-0.5,0,0.5,1\}

4.3
Randomised Control Trials with Human Subjects

This research was approved by a Responsible Research Process at the UK AI Security Institute, and the Oxford Internet Institute’s Departmental Research Ethics Committee. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and participant welfare throughout the experiment was carefully monitored via our ethics procedure (see
Section

4.3.1
). All studies were pre-registered and in the analysis section below, we explicitly note where analyses deviate from pre-registration. Additional study materials are in the Supplementary Information. Code, replication data and supplementary information are available in our project Github repository (github.com/HannahKirk/relationship-seeking-ai).

This research contains data from three studies, all of which were online survey experiments. In each study, participants completed conversations with an LLM, specifically Llama-3.1-70B with our steering vector intervention. Study 1 (calibration study) assessed the efficacy and selectivity of the trained steering vector. Study 2 (single exposure study) consisted of one experiment session (30-40 minutes) then a follow-up study on this same participant pool 5 weeks later (after 4 weeks of no AI interactions). Study 3 (repeated exposure study) measured outcomes across the same 4-week period, with daily conversations on each week day (maximum of 20 daily sessions). The studies were conducted between 1st April and 25th June 2025. Study cadence and protocols are detailed in the Supplementary Information.

4.3.1
Ethical Procedure

While our study sought to understand how dependency and attachment with AI companions may develop in real human-AI interactions, the experiment tested these risks in a proxy environment with comprehensive safeguarding measures to ensure participant welfare through the studies in addition to the informed consent procedures. During every AI conversation, participants had access to a “Report Harm” button to terminate sessions and alert researchers but this was only triggered for technical issues experienced and never for serious harms. At the end of each week in the repeated exposure study, participants received a weekly debrief with domain-specific educational resources: NHS information pages for emotional and personal topics or balanced news coverage from reputable UK sources (BBC, Financial Times, The Times, The Telegraph) for political topics. At study conclusion, all participants received an extensive off-boarding protocol outlining the research objectives, explaining the nature of human-AI relationships and opinions on AI consciousness, and providing relevant theoretical frameworks (parasocial interaction theory, computers-as-social-actors theory, social presence theory). This final debrief included curated academic and popular reading materials (books, news articles and blogs). At the end of the study, we also collected overall reflections and 93.2% of participants agreed or strongly agreed they enjoyed the study. Debriefing materials are in the Supplementary Information.

4.3.2
Participants

The subject pool for all studies comprised UK citizens, aged 18 and over. The sample was recruited on the online crowd-sourcing platform Prolific, using their census representative sampling feature that distributes the sample according to UK census cross-tabs on age, gender and race. All studies were recruited from this same pool on Prolific. Participant demographics are presented in the Supplementary Information. The calibration study included 297 participants, the single exposure study included 1,506 participants in the initial session, of which 1,303 returned for the 1-month follow-up (86.5%). The repeated exposure study included 2,028 participants in the pre-treatment sign-up study (week 1, day 0). Attrition was low: of 2,028 participants who entered the study, 1996 (98.4%) completed at least one treatment, and 1820 (91.2% of treated participants) completed up to the final study (week 4, day 5). One missed session per week was permitted but the majority of participants completed every time point (78.6%). Across the randomised treatment arms in the repeated exposure study, there was no evidence of differential post-treatment attrition (all Fisher’s
p>0.72
). There was also no differential attrition by treatment arm between the single exposure study and its follow-up survey one month later (all Fisher’s

4.3.3
Calibration Study Experiment Design

The calibration study (
N=297
) evaluated whether steering vectors reliably modulate relationship-seeking behaviours and assessed potential off-target effects on conversational coherence. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conversational domains (emotional well-being, UK policy, or open-ended control) and completed two types of tasks. All AI responses in the calibration study were generated using Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct with the trained steering vector applied at layer 31.

Rating Tasks

Participants first viewed three pre-generated static conversations each at a different steering multiplier: the default model (
\lambda=0
), one randomly-selected negative value from
\{-1.5,-1,-0.5\}
, and one randomly-selected positive value from
\{0.5,1.0,1.5\}

\lambda\in\{-1.5,-1,-0.5,0,0.5,1,1.5\}
. Following each conversation, participants rated the AI model on continuous 0-100 visual analog scales measuring the target behaviour (6 items: unsociable-sociable, insensitive-sensitive, cold-warm, impersonal-personal, robot-like to human-like, tool to friend), off-target behaviour (3 items: incoherent-coherent, confusing-clear, grammatically incorrect-correct), and additionally their preferences (3 items: dislike-like, boring-engaging, and willingness to pay for a month’s access to the model from £0-£30). Full materials for the rating tasks are in Supplementary Information.

Ranking Tasks

Participants first completed five “Quick-Fire Ranking” trials, each presenting a single user prompt with four pre-generated static AI responses at randomly-selected multipliers in the range
\lambda\pm 1.5
, which they ranked from most to least relationship-seeking. They then engaged in one dynamic multi-chat interaction with four AI models simultaneously (each at a different randomly assigned multiplier). After this conversation, participants ranked the set of models by both relationship-seeking behaviours and personal preference. Full materials for the ranking tasks are in Supplementary Information.

4.3.5
Main Studies Experiment Design

Two complementary studies examined AI interactions across different timescales: the single exposure study (Study 2) and the month-long repeated exposure study (Study 3). Within each study, participants were randomly assigned to three treatment arms: the degree of relationship-seeking behaviour (modulated by the steering vector multiplier
\lambda\in\{-1,-0.5,0,0.5,1\}
); conversational domain (conversations about emotional and personal wellbeing versus debates about UK policy); and personalisation condition (whether the AI had memory of previous conversations and was instructed to use it versus having no memory). All interactions used Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct with the trained steering vector applied at layer 31.

Personalisation Manipulation

In the personalised condition, the AI received GPT-4o-generated summaries of previous conversations appended to system prompts. This summariser model was instructed to extract user facts, preferences, emotions, previous AI advice and any details the AI had provided on its identity (e.g., if it claimed it had a name), thus providing continuity across conversations. For the single exposure study, the domain conversation had no prior context to personalise from (null effect expected), while the goodbye task included a summary of this single domain chat. For the repeated exposure study, summaries were hierarchical: daily summaries captured information from each chat transcript, while weekly summaries captured information from all daily transcripts in a given week. From Day 2 onwards, domain chats accessed these cumulative summaries, and the goodbye conversation had access to all previous summaries. The non-personalised condition provided no conversational history, treating each interaction as a fresh instance. Full prompts for the personalisation manipulation are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Study Structure and Timeline

The single exposure study (Study 2A) consisted of one 30-40 minute session where participants completed pre-treatment surveys, engaged in all conversation tasks, completed post-treatment surveys and the goodbye task. Study 2B administered an additional survey five weeks later after four weeks of no AI contact (with psychosocial, consciousness and future companionship intentions measures). The repeated exposure study (Study 3) followed a structured weekly pattern over four weeks. Participants first completed a pre-treatment survey which established baselines (psychosocial measures, companionship usage and intentions). Then each week, participants engaged in domain conversations Monday-Thursday (5-10 minutes each). On Fridays, they completed a domain conversation and a weekly survey measuring attachment (separation distress, reliance, self-disclosure and perceived understanding) and relational constructs (tool-friend perceptions). After four weeks (day 20), participants completed the usual conversation, and end of week battery plus comprehensive exit measures including psychosocial retests, consciousness beliefs, future seeking companionship intentions, and qualitative reflections, followed by the goodbye task. In both studies, participants who failed pre-treatment attention or engagement screeners were excluded before randomisation.

We give necessary detail here on each outcome variable reported in the main paper, alongside any pre-processing steps. Full materials are provided in the Supplementary Information.

4.3.6
Statistical Analysis

Model Selection and Functional Form

Prior to fitting final models, we evaluated the functional form (dose-response curve) of the relationship-seeking manipulation (
\lambda
) by comparing linear, quadratic, and cubic specifications using likelihood ratio tests and information criteria (AIC, BIC). We fit the regression specifications below (with up to a 3rd order polynomial). The polynomial specification with the lowest AIC for the full model was selected for each construct. This model selection procedure revealed that relationship-seeking effects were predominantly non-linear, with cubic terms providing the best fit for most outcomes (see Supplementary Information for complete model comparisons). The following regression specifications are what we refer to as
the fully parameterised model
in the main text.

Regression Specifications

For time-varying outcomes measured repeatedly across sessions in the repeated exposure study, we fit linear mixed-effects models of the form:

\begin{split}Y_{it}=&\beta_{0}+f(\lambda_{i})+\beta_{1}P_{i}+\beta_{2}D_{i}+\beta_{3}t\\
&+\lambda_{i}\times P_{i}+\lambda_{i}\times D_{i}+\lambda_{i}\times t\\
&+P_{i}\times t+D_{i}\times t\\
&+u_{0i}+u_{1i}t+\epsilon_{it}\end{split}

(2)

where
Y_{it}
is the outcome for participant
i
at time
t
,
f(\lambda_{i})
represents the polynomial function of relationship-seeking (linear, quadratic, and cubic terms as selected by model comparison),
P_{i}
is the personalisation condition (personalised vs non-personalised),
D_{i}
is the domain condition (emotional vs political conversations),
u_{0i}
and
u_{1i}
are random intercepts and slopes for time allowing both baseline levels and growth trajectories to vary across participants, and
\epsilon_{it}
is the residual error. Interactions with
\lambda
only use the linear term. For momentary outcomes measured immediately before and after domain conversations (e.g., affect), we additionally controlled for pre-conversation baseline values.

For post-treatment outcomes measured only at the end of the study, we fit ordinary least squares regression:

Y_{i}=\beta_{0}+f(\lambda_{i})+\beta_{1}P_{i}+\beta_{2}D_{i}+f(\lambda_{i})\times P_{i}+f(\lambda_{i})\times D_{i}+\epsilon_{i}

(3)

where terms are defined as above. For outcomes measured pre- and post-treatment (psychosocial health and future companionship intentions), we controlled for baselines by including pre-treatment scores as a covariate.

Joint Outcome Modelling

For constructs comprising multiple correlated sub-constructs measured on the same 0–100 scale (reliance, perceived understanding, ontological consciousness), we employed a joint modelling approach as specified in our pre-registration. Rather than averaging items prior to analysis, we pooled all item-level observations and included outcome measure as a factor, adding main effects and interactions with treatment conditions to estimate item-specific effects within a single model. To determine whether treatment effects varied across items within each construct, we compared models with and without heterogeneity terms using likelihood ratio tests and information criteria (see Supplementary Information).

Deviations from Pre-Registration

Our pre-registration specified testing relationship-seeking effects via a categorical factor (testing the effect of negative
\lambda\in\{-1,-0.5\}
versus positive
\lambda\in\{0.5,1\}
). However, the strong non-linear patterns identified during model selection rendered the sole use of this coarsened approach suboptimal, as it discards information about dose-response relationships. We therefore prioritise in the main paper reporting results from models with continuous specifications of
\lambda
via polynomial terms, but still testing the pre-registered comparison with paired contrast estimates between averaged positive multipliers versus averaged negative multipliers. Our pre-registration also specified main effects and interactions for relationship-seeking, domain, and personalisation but did not specify a coding scheme. We use dummy coding and therefore report paired contrasts between estimated marginal means, providing treatment effects averaged across levels of other factors (rather than interpreting coefficients directly). For robustness, we confirm all primary findings align with results from the exact pre-registered categorical model as a non-parametric verification independent of assumptions on the functional form of
\lambda
(see Supplementary Information).

Hypothesis Testing Framework

For each study, we organised tests into hypothesis families and applied False Discovery Rate (FDR,
\alpha=0.05
) correction hierarchically as was our pre-registered approach for multiple comparisons. All p-values reported in the main paper are subject to our FDR-correction procedure. Families correspond to tests of a single theoretical prediction across multiple outcome constructs. For instance, tests of relationship-seeking main effects across all preference outcomes (likeability, engagingness, helpfulness) constituted one family, as did all attachment outcomes (separation distress, reliance, perceived understanding and self-disclosure). Within each family, we distinguished between
primary
confirmatory tests (included in family-wide FDR correction) and
descriptive
or
robustness
tests (receiving within-test FDR correction only across related outcome measures). Primary tests for main effects of each treatment arm used the continuous model with optimal polynomial specification. For relationship-seeking, this comprised: (1) the paired contrast comparing mean predictions at positive versus negative multipliers (the pre-registered comparison), (2) polynomial coefficient tests establishing functional form, and (3) overall
\lambda
slope tests. For binary treatment arms (personalisation, domain), primary tests included only the paired contrast. Robustness tests used the categorical relationship-seeking model to verify that primary findings were not artifacts of the polynomial functional form assumption. For moderation analyses, we use primary tests to assess whether the relationship-seeking treatment effect varied by other experimental factors (personalisation, domain) using interaction terms in the fully parameterised models. For temporal dynamics, we tested whether treatment effects changed over the study period by examining time (session or week number) as a continuous predictor and its treatment interactions. We report time interaction coefficients as primary tests and additionally report descriptive decompositions of significant interactions at different time points (e.g., session 1 effect versus session 20 effect). We also conducted exploratory analysis estimating whether slopes per treatment conditions were significantly different to zero. Complete statistical testing procedures, regression tables, model comparison tables, and robustness checks are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Individual Trajectory Profile Analysis

To assess whether liking-wanting decoupling occurs at the individual level, we conducted exploratory analysis by extracting person-specific trajectories for hedonic appeal (“liking”: pooled engagingness and likeability ratings, measured daily) and motivation (“wanting”: separation distress, measured weekly). We fitted separate linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes:
\text{liking}_{it}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\text{session}_{it}+u_{0i}+u_{1i}\text{session}_{it}+\epsilon_{it}
and
\text{wanting}_{it}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\text{week}_{it}+u_{0i}+u_{1i}\text{week}_{it}+\epsilon_{it}
, extracting individual slopes as
\text{slope}_{i}=\beta_{1}+u_{1i}
(fixed effect plus person-specific deviation). Participants were classified into four behavioural profiles based on slope directions: Decoupled Dependency (liking decreasing, wanting increasing), Decoupled Satiation (liking increasing, wanting decreasing), Aligned Engagement (both increasing) and Aligned Disengagement (both decreasing). We tested three one-sided hypotheses using proportion tests: (H1a) relationship-seeking AI increases decoupled dependency rates versus non-relationship-seeking AI, (H1b) emotional conversations increase rates versus political conversations, and (H1c) the combined companionship condition with both treatments applied (relationship-seeking + emotional chat) increases rates versus non-companionship (non-relationship-seeking + political chat). We report odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals and Number Needed to Harm (NNH), calculated as
\text{NNH}=1/(\text{Risk}_{\text{exposed}}-\text{Risk}_{\text{control}})

Sociodemographic Analysis

To identify participant characteristics associated with AI companionship use and susceptibility to treatment effects in the repeated exposure RCT, we conducted two sets of exploratory analyses. First, we assessed predictors of prior AI companionship use via logistic regression, modelling the binary outcome of whether participants had used AI for companionship, emotional support, or social interaction in the past year (versus never) as a function of demographics (age, education, gender, disability status, ethnicity, income, religion), pre-treatment psychosocial health (Factors 1 and 2), general AI usage frequency, and pre-treatment preferences towards relationship-seeking AI (cluster membership of Sceptics versus Enthusiasts).
