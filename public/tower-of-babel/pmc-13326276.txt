Discordant Cardiomyopathy Phenotypes in Siblings With Homozygous FKRP c.1100T>C
Authors: Noelle Mahon, Sandra Coad, Omid Kiamanesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPathogenic FKRP variants cause limb-girdle muscular dystrophy R9, with variable cardiac involvement. How genotype influences the cardiac phenotype remains poorly defined for non-European founder variants.Case SummaryWe report 2 siblings of South African Afrikaner descent, both homozygous for FKRP c.1100T>C (p.Ile367Thr), with discordant cardiac phenotypes. The brother (age 25 years) presented at age 17 with nondilated cardiomyopathy (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF]: 41%) that progressed to dilated cardiomyopathy, with fibrosis involving 12 to 14 of 17 AHA (American Heart Association) segments late gadolinium enhancement segments over 7 years despite partial reverse remodeling. His sister (age 30 years) had nondilated cardiomyopathy with preserved LVEF (59%) and stable fibrosis in 4 lateral segments over 2 years.DiscussionSerial cardiac magnetic resonance revealed dissociation between functional recovery and fibrosis progression: LVEF improved on guideline-directed therapy, while fibrosis burden remained extensive.Take-Home MessagesEjection fraction alone underestimates disease progression in FKRP-related cardiomyopathy. Serial cardiac magnetic resonance with late gadolinium enhancement should guide surveillance, especially in non-c.826C>A genotypes where cardiac onset occurs decades earlier.

History of Presentation

Two siblings of South African Afrikaner descent, a brother (patient 1) and sister (patient 2) were evaluated at the Broderick Cardiomyopathy Program (Calgary, Canada) for cardiac surveillance in the setting of genetically confirmed limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD) R9. Both were homozygous for the FKRP c.1100T>C (p.Ile367Thr) pathogenic variant, an autosomal recessive founder mutation in the Afrikaner population distinct from the common European c.826C>A variant. The diagnosis was established through muscle biopsy of patient 2, which demonstrated reduced α-dystroglycan immunostaining. Subsequent genetic testing confirmed the homozygous FKRP c.1100T>C variant in both siblings.Take-Home Messages•In FKRP-related cardiomyopathy, serial cardiac magnetic resonance with late gadolinium enhancement quantification can detect progressive myocardial fibrosis even when ejection fraction is stable or improving on guideline-directed therapy.•Because limb-girdle muscular dystrophy R9 is autosomal recessive, siblings of an affected proband should undergo cascade genetic testing, with cardiac surveillance initiated only for those confirmed to carry biallelic pathogenic variants.

Patient 1

A 25-year-old man was first referred at age 17 years after echocardiography demonstrated reduced left ventricular (LV) systolic function. Elevated creatine kinase (>6,000 U/L) had been detected at age 6 months, and he received a presumptive diagnosis of LGMD R9 based on his sister's muscle biopsy findings, later confirmed by genetic testing. Proximal weakness was documented by age 14, and he lost ambulation at age 23. He denied dyspnea, orthopnea, chest pain, palpitations, or syncope. Examination revealed blood pressure 124/79 mm Hg, heart rate 65 beats/min, oxygen saturation 97%, and normal heart sounds without murmurs or gallops.

Patient 2

A 30-year-old woman was referred for cardiac screening after her brother's diagnosis. Proximal muscle weakness was first identified at age approximately 1 year, with creatine kinase >10,000 U/L. She walked at 15 months and met early motor milestones but developed progressive weakness with bilateral calf hypertrophy and heel cord contractures. She lost ambulation at age 27. She denied cardiac symptoms but reported nocturnal episodes of chest discomfort with diaphoresis and tremor, attributed to autonomic dysfunction. Examination revealed blood pressure 118/87 mm Hg, heart rate 104 beats/min (resting sinus tachycardia, attributed to autonomic dysfunction), and oxygen saturation 97%.

Investigations

Laboratory findings

Creatine kinase was chronically elevated in both siblings (3,612 U/L in patient 1; 519 U/L in patient 2), consistent with ongoing myopathic disease. Serum creatinine was markedly low in both patients (26 and 10 μmol/L, respectively), reflecting reduced muscle mass from the underlying dystrophy. Cystatin C, a renal biomarker independent of muscle mass, was within normal limits for both (0.84 and 0.55 mg/L, respectively), confirming preserved renal function and illustrating why creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate is unreliable in this population. High-sensitivity troponin T was elevated in both (43 and 19 ng/L, respectively). Because the high-sensitivity troponin T assay has known cross-reactivity with skeletal muscle troponin isoforms, and as both patients had active myopathic disease with chronically elevated creatine kinase, these values were likely to at least in part be attributable to skeletal muscle injury. Concurrent myocardial injury was also probable given documented myocardial fibrosis on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) in both patients. Echocardiography

Echocardiography was the initial screening modality for both patients. In patient 1, reduced LV systolic function was first detected by echocardiography during routine neuromuscular follow-up, prompting referral to our program. Serial echocardiograms demonstrated LV ejection fraction (LVEF) fluctuations between 35% and 50%, though precise volumetric assessment was limited by suboptimal acoustic windows. In patient 2, the most recent echocardiogram estimated LVEF at 45% to 50%, with basal inferior to lateral wall motion abnormalities. This was lower than the concurrent CMR-derived LVEF of 59%, a discrepancy attributable to chest wall deformity (Haller index: 3.4) and reduced intercostal muscle mass. This systematic underestimation illustrates why CMR is the preferred modality for cardiac phenotyping in the muscular dystrophy population.

Serial CMR

Patient 1 underwent 4 CMR examinations over 7 years (ages 17 to 25 years). Initial CMR showed normal ventricular dimensions (end-diastolic volume index [EDVi]: 99 mL/m2) but reduced LVEF (41%), with mid-wall fibrosis in the basal-to-mid anteroseptum and mid-apical inferoseptum (12 segments with late gadolinium enhancement [LGE]). At that time, the phenotype met criteria for nondilated LV cardiomyopathy. Over the subsequent 3 years, progressive ventricular dilation was documented: EDVi increased to 109 mL/m2 and then 129 mL/m2, marking the transition to dilated cardiomyopathy, with LVEF declining to 38% and LGE involving 12 segments. The most recent CMR showed partial reverse remodeling on guideline-directed medical therapy (EDVi: 104 mL/m2, LVEF: 43%) with fibrosis involving 14 of 17 segments, now including mid-wall striae and subepicardial LGE in a nonischemic distribution (Figures 1 and 2, Table 2).Figure 1Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction Over TimeLeft ventricular ejection fraction plotted by patient age from echocardiography (Echo; open circles and dashed lines) and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR; filled circles and solid lines). Management

Patient 1 was treated with sequential uptitration of guideline-directed medical therapy to target doses: bisoprolol 10 mg daily, sacubitril-valsartan 97/103 mg twice daily, dapagliflozin 10 mg daily, and ivabradine 5 mg twice daily. Spironolactone was attempted but was discontinued for persistent hyperkalemia (potassium up to 6.5 mmol/L); finerenone was deferred for the same reason. Despite severe systolic dysfunction with extensive fibrosis, he has remained free of heart failure symptoms or hospitalization. He was counseled regarding elevated sudden cardiac death risk and declined an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, consistent with his Goals of Care Designation (GCD-R2, no chest compressions or defibrillation).

Patient 2 was started on perindopril 2 mg daily. She declined spironolactone, beta-blockade, and ivabradine (the latter 2 offered for resting tachycardia attributed to autonomic dysfunction rather than cardiac decompensation).

Discussion

These siblings share an identical homozygous FKRP genotype, yet they developed divergent cardiac phenotypes: dilated cardiomyopathy with progressive fibrosis in the brother, and nondilated cardiomyopathy with stable, limited fibrosis in the sister. This intrafamilial discordance parallels the variable cardiac expression described among FKRP-affected siblings by Kefi et al1 and raises the question of whether sex, modifier genes, or other factors shape cardiac involvement independently of the primary FKRP defect. Cardiomyopathy in LGMD R9 correlates with male sex in c.826C>A cohorts;2 the worse phenotype in our male patient is consistent with this pattern, though the contribution of sex versus other modifiers cannot be disentangled in a single family.

Phenotypic fluidity and the fibrotic substrate

The FKRP c.1100T>C variant in this family is a South African Afrikaner founder mutation, distinct from the European c.826C>A (p.Leu276Ile) variant that accounts for 67% to 88% of LGMD R9 cases in Western cohorts.2,3 Libell et al4 showed that patients with non-c.826C>A genotypes develop cardiomyopathy at a median age of 18.1 years, compared with 54.2 years for c.826C>A homozygotes (P < 0.0001). Both patients in our series had detectable cardiac involvement by their late teens to late 20s, consistent with this earlier-onset pattern.

Patient 1 illustrates the phenotypic fluidity between nondilated and dilated cardiomyopathy in FKRP-related disease. Initial presentation met criteria for nondilated LV cardiomyopathy, which progressed to overt dilation, then partially reversed on medical therapy. Throughout this oscillation between phenotypic categories, fibrosis remained extensive. What remains constant across both phenotypic states and both patients is the fibrotic substrate itself. Extensive fibrosis was present before and independently of LV dilation, suggesting that the FKRP c.1100T>C genotype may drive a primary fibrotic cardiomyopathy in which adverse ventricular remodeling is a secondary, and potentially modifiable, consequence.

CMR reveals hidden fibrosis progression

The central finding in this series is the dissociation between functional recovery and fibrosis progression in patient 1. Over 7 years, LVEF declined from 41% to a nadir of 38% before partially recovering to 43%, while EDVi rose from 99 to 129 mL/m2 before decreasing to 104 mL/m2 on guideline-directed therapy—changes that might suggest disease stabilization. Yet LGE burden was extensive throughout, ranging from 12 to 14 of 17 segments with mid-wall and subepicardial involvement. To our knowledge, this dissociation between functional recovery and fibrosis progression has not been previously documented in FKRP-related cardiomyopathy, and it argues against using ejection fraction or volumes alone to track disease activity.

In patient 2, focal subepicardial LGE was present, with entirely normal parametric mapping: native T1, extracellular volume, and T2 values were all within the normal range on 2 serial studies. This indicates focal replacement fibrosis without diffuse interstitial involvement or active inflammation, and it raises the possibility that fibrosis in early stage FKRP-related cardiomyopathy is a discrete, localized process rather than a diffuse myocardial disease. Rosales et al5 identified epicardial or mid-wall LGE in 47% of FKRP patients with preserved ejection fraction, and Hollingsworth et al6 demonstrated selective subepicardial dysfunction with impaired cardiac energetics as the mechanistic basis. The mid-wall and subepicardial distribution in both of our patients is characteristic of dystrophic cardiomyopathy and distinguishes it from ischemic or infiltrative patterns.

Surveillance implications

Current guidelines recommend cardiac evaluation at LGMD diagnosis, with follow-up every 1 to 2 years for asymptomatic patients.7 Sadasivan et al8 recently advocated for earlier initiation of heart failure therapies in the LGMD population. Our experience supports both recommendations but with an additional consideration: Ejection fraction–based surveillance alone may miss ongoing myocardial destruction. Serial CMR with LGE quantification is better suited to detect fibrosis progression, which has direct implications for device therapy discussions, particularly in patients such as patient 1, who declined implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement despite extensive fibrosis.

Whether earlier cardiac onset in non-c.826C>A genotypes should prompt shorter surveillance intervals remains an open question. In the absence of targeted antifibrotic therapies for dystroglycanopathy-related cardiomyopathy, the clinical utility of detecting fibrosis progression earlier is uncertain, though it may inform discussions about device therapy timing and prognosis.

Limitations

This report describes 2 patients from a single family, and the observations may not generalize to all FKRP genotypes or populations. The apparent phenotypic discordance may partly reflect differences in disease stage, as patient 2 had a shorter cardiac follow-up and may yet develop progressive fibrosis. A comprehensive cardiomyopathy gene panel was not performed; however, the characteristic subepicardial and mid-wall LGE pattern in both siblings, combined with concordant genotype and clinical context, strongly support FKRP as the causative gene. Fibrosis was assessed semiquantitatively using AHA segment-level LGE reporting rather than formal LGE mass quantification; the latter would have provided a more precise measure of disease burden. Longer follow-up and larger genotype-specific registries will be needed to confirm these observations.

The resting sinus tachycardia in patient 2 is consistent with autonomic dysfunction, which has been described in neuromuscular disease populations. As discussed earlier, the chronic high-sensitivity troponin T elevation in both siblings likely reflects a combination of skeletal muscle and myocardial injury; coronary evaluation should be considered if levels change acutely.

Follow-Up

At the most recent follow-up, both patients remained free of heart failure symptoms or hospitalization. Patient 1 continues on quadruple neurohormonal therapy, with stable functional status. Patient 2 continues on perindopril monotherapy, with CMR planned at 2-year intervals.

Conclusions

This sibling pair demonstrates that identical FKRP genotype does not predict identical cardiac phenotype, and that ejection fraction can improve while fibrosis progresses silently. For clinicians managing FKRP-related cardiomyopathy, 3 principles emerge: 1) serial CMR with LGE quantification, and not ejection fraction alone, should anchor disease monitoring and inform device therapy decisions; 2) non-c.826C>A genotypes carry earlier cardiac onset, supporting initiation of cardiac surveillance from the time of diagnosis; and 3) because FKRP-related LGMD R9 is autosomal recessive, siblings of an affected proband should undergo cascade genetic testing, with cardiac surveillance initiated for those confirmed to carry biallelic pathogenic variants—the phenotypic discordance in this family demonstrates that a sibling's cardiac phenotype cannot be predicted from the proband's disease course (Figure 4).Figure 4Genotype-Phenotype Discordance in FKRP-Related CardiomyopathyConceptual diagram illustrating how a single homozygous FKRP c.1100T>C genotype produces divergent cardiac phenotypes through the interplay of sex, modifier genes, and environmental factors.
