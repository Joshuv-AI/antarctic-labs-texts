A Smoluchowski equation for a sheared suspension of frictionally interacting rods
Chris Quiñones, Peter D. Olmsted

Abstract
In this work we develop constitutive equations for a dense, sheared suspension of frictionally interacting rods by applying Onsager’s variational method as formulated by Doi. We treat both solid friction, of the Amontons-Coulomb form; and lubricated friction, which scales with relative tangential velocity at the contact point. Dissipation functions in terms of the rod angular velocity are derived via a mean field approach for each form of friction, and from these, a Rayleighian for dense suspensions of rigid rods under shear constructed. Derivatives of this Rayleighian with respect to rod angular velocity and velocity gradient give a Smoluchowski equation and stress tensor, respectively. We show that these are representable as perturbations to Doi’s model for a sheared liquid crystal. We also suggest a form for the average number of contacts between rods as a function of volume fraction, aspect ratio, and nematic order parameter, generalizing Philipse’s random contact equation for disordered packings.



I
Introduction

The effects of flow on suspensions of rigid rod-like particles has been studied for more than a century
Björkman 2008
. Rods are typically considered to interact through a combination of hydrodynamic forces and potential energies such as electrostatics van der Waals interactions
Odijk 1977
;

The presence of friction in rods undergoing shear flow prompts several questions analogous to those studied for spheres: how does friction affect the orientational state and the stress? Can these interactions lead to discontinuous shear thickening and the onset of jamming, in the fashion of the Wyart and Cates
Wyart and Cates 2014
model? How many contacts are there per rod in a flowing system, compared to that found in a disordered packing
Philipse 1996

Recent experimental and computational work has suggested that such unexplained rheological behaviors are indeed due to frictional contacts (

Frictional contacts have been included for rods by
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993
and
Toll and Månson 1994

Djalili-Moghaddam and Toll 2005
explored the effect on suspension stress from rod contacts that interacted with a boundary lubricated frictional force, though they did not address the effects on equations of motion.
Bounoua et al. 2016
studied both sliding Coulomb friction and boundary lubricated frictional interactions between rods in three dimensions. They started from Jeffery’s equation and constructed an additional torque from frictional contacts between rods. They assumed the orientational dynamics that determined relative rod-rod velocity (and thus frictional forces) was exactly Jeffery’s equation,
i.e.
simply the motion in the dilute case. As in
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993

A

Despite the null result of
Bounoua et al. 2016
, an important point stands: frictional contacts are a source of dissipation in dense suspensions, and should lead to an accompanying torque. Doi
Doi 2011
showed how to implement Onsager’s dissipation principle to re-derive his model for flowing liquid crystals
Doi 2011
, by minimizing the total dissipation in a suspension of rods under shear, which leads
to an equation of motion for
\dot{\bm{u}}
from the corresponding balance of torques (each arising from a separate source of dissipation). This is in contrast to previous methodologies

;
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993
that relied on assuming an equation of motion
\dot{\bm{u}}
appropriate for dilute rods. We show here that by following the framework of Doi and including the contribution to total dissipation due to frictional sliding between rods in contact, the proper frictional torque (that belongs in
\dot{\bm{u}}
) can be derived. This will result in a self-consitent equation of motion, as the included torque is naturally a function of
\dot{\bm{u}}
.

II

III

IV

IV.3

\mathbf{Q}

V.2.1

II
Rayleighian Derivation of the Smoluchowski Equation

II.1
General Approach

We follow
Doi 2013
to derive a Smoluchowski equation for a suspension of rods. First, a Rayleighian
{\cal R}(\bm{\omega})
is written as the sum of the rate of change of free energy of the rods
\dot{A}
and the dissipation due to rod angular velocity
\bm{\omega}=\hat{\bm{u}}\times\dot{\bm{u}}
, expressed via a dissipation function
\Phi
:

\mathcal{R}=\Phi(\bm{\omega})+\dot{A}.

(II.1)

For example, Doi
Doi 2013
considered a simple drag constant
\zeta_{r}
, which leads to a drag torque on the
i
th rod of

\bm{\Gamma}^{i}_{\text{drag}}=-\zeta_{r}\bm{\omega}^{i}.

(II.2)

By integrating to get power
P^{i}=-\int\bm{\Gamma}^{i}_{\textrm{drag}}\cdot d\bm{\omega}^{i}
we find the dissipative contribution to the Rayleighian:

\displaystyle\Phi^{i}_{\text{drag}}

\displaystyle=-\int\bm{\Gamma}^{i}_{\textrm{drag}}\cdot d\bm{\omega}^{i}

(II.3a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{r}(\omega^{i})^{2}.

(II.3b)

We can compute the average dissipation per rod due to drag by integrating
II.3
over all possible rod orientations
\hat{\bm{u}}
,

\Phi_{\text{drag}}=\frac{1}{2}\int\zeta_{r}\omega^{2}\psi(\hat{\bm{u}})\,\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}},

(II.4)

where we have introduced the distribution function
\psi(\hat{\bm{u}})
of rod orientations.

The Rayleighian is differentiated with respect to
\bm{\omega}
to obtain a net torque

\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\omega})=\frac{\delta R}{\delta\bm{\omega}}.

(II.5)

[For a Rayleighian function (as in Eq.
II.3b
),
\bm{\Gamma}^{i}=\partial{\cal R}/\partial\omega_{i}
, while for a Rayleighian functional (Eq.
II.4

\bm{\Gamma}=\delta{\cal R}/\delta\omega

\bm{\omega}_{\text{min}}
is easily translated to the equation of motion for the rod orientation,

\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{u}}(t)}{\partial t}\equiv\bm{\omega}_{\text{min}}\times\hat{\bm{u}}.

(II.6)

The Smoluchowski Equation for the orientational distribution function
\psi
can be calculated by using
\bm{\omega}_{\text{min}}
to calculate the continuity equation for probability density:

\dot{\psi}=-\hat{\bm{R}}\cdot(\bm{\omega}_{\text{min}}\psi),

(II.7)

where
\hat{\bm{R}}
is the Doi-Edwards rotational differentiation operator,

\hat{\bm{R}}=\hat{\bm{u}}\times\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\bm{u}}}.

(II.8)

The average free energy per particle is given by

A=\int\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}\left[k_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{B}}T\ln{\psi}+U(\hat{\bm{u}})\right]\,\psi,

(II.9)

where
U
is the inter-rod potential and
k_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{B}}T\ln{\psi}
the so-called “Brownian potential”. The corresponding rate of change of the energy in the presence of rod rotation is

\displaystyle\dot{A}=\int\bm{\omega}\cdot\psi\hat{\bm{R}}\left[k_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{B}}T\ln{\psi}+U(\hat{\bm{u}})\right]\,\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}},

(II.10a)

so the Rayleighian becomes

\mathcal{R}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}\int{\zeta_{r}\omega^{2}\psi\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}}+\int\bm{\omega}\cdot\psi\hat{\bm{R}}\left[k_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{B}}T\ln{\psi}+U(\hat{\bm{u}})\right]\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}.

(II.11)

Upon taking the derivative

, we find the torque

\bm{\Gamma}=\zeta_{r}\bm{\omega}+\hat{\bm{R}}\left[k_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{B}}T\ln{\psi}+U(\hat{\bm{u}})\right].

(II.12)

Note that this is the torque on the fluid due to the particles. By demanding zero net torque, we find

\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{min}}=-\hat{\bm{R}}\left[k_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{B}}T\ln{\psi}+U(\hat{\bm{u}})\right]/\zeta_{r},

(II.13)

which together with Eq.
II.7

\dot{\psi}=\frac{1}{\zeta_{r}}\hat{\bm{R}}\cdot\left[\psi\hat{\bm{R}}\left(k_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{B}}T\ln\psi+U\left(\hat{\bm{u}}\right)\right)\right].

(II.14)

If the suspension is under flow with velocity gradient

(\nabla\bm{v})_{ij}\equiv\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}},

(II.15)

then the dissipation function due to drag becomes

II.2
Frictional Forms

To incorporate inter-rod friction into the Rayleighian we include an additional contribution
\Phi_{\text{fric}}
to the dissipation function, yielding

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}&=\Phi_{\text{drag}}+\Phi_{\text{fric}}(\bm{\omega})+\dot{A}\\
&=\mathcal{R}_{0}+\Phi_{\text{fric}}(\bm{\omega}).\end{split}

(II.20)

Contact area for two rods. Each of the dotted lines has length

D^{2}/|\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\times\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}|

To this end, we assume that all rods’ surfaces in contact are in relative motion with a sliding frictional force that dissipates energy. By nature of the Rayleighian method, contacts interacting through static friction cannot be properly accounted for in the resulting dynamical equation. While these contacts do impart torques on rods, they do not contribute directly to the Rayleighian as they do not dissipate energy.

Here, superscript

and

is a viscosity proportional to that of the suspending fluid. The factor

(III.1)

Here,

is the point along the centerline of a rod where the rods touch, which satisfies

-\frac{L}{2}\leq\epsilon\leq\frac{L}{2}.

(III.2)

We define a “contact” by the condition

\bm{r^{i}}_{\text{com}}+\epsilon^{i}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}=\bm{r^{j}}_{\text{com}}+\epsilon^{j}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}.

We have assumed that the rods have high aspect ratio, so the rod diameter is ignored in the contact condition (Eq.

\Delta\bm{v}^{ij}=\bm{\omega^{i}}\times\epsilon^{i}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}-\bm{\omega^{j}}\times\epsilon^{j}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}+\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\left(\epsilon^{j}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}-\epsilon^{i}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right).

Friction, and its associated dissipation, is determined by the contact velocity

A given rod in a dense suspension has some number of “contact neighbors”

\displaystyle\Phi^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in c}\Phi^{ij}

\displaystyle=\int\psi^{j}d\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\,\,{\Phi}^{ij}\,\,{\cal P}_{\textrm{con}}\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i},\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\right)

In the above, the sum is over all rods

The average number of contacts

{\langle c\rangle}=\int\int\psi^{j}d\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\psi^{i}d\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\,\,{\cal P}_{\textrm{con}}\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i},\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\right),

IV
Dissipation Functions and Smoluchowski Equations

IV.1
Solid Contact Dissipation Function

Following Eq.
III.19

II.24
and also averaging over
\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}

\begin{split}\Phi_{\text{sol}}^{\text{mf}}&=\int\psi^{i}\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\left\langle\mu_{k}|\bm{F}_{\text{N}}||\bm{v}_{c}|\right\rangle_{j}\\

(IV.1)

where the second pair of angle brackets denotes the additional

\mu_{k}

The torque associated with Eq.

\begin{split}\bm{\Gamma}_{\text{sol}}^{\text{mf},i}&=\frac{\delta}{\delta\bm{\omega}_{i}}\Phi_{\text{sol}}^{\text{mf}}\\

(IV.2)

III.4
and

III.19

\epsilon_{i}
and
\epsilon_{j}

The form of Eq.

Bounoua et al. 2016

Sandstrom and Tucker 1993

;
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993
obtained an average frictional torque that depended on odd powers of
\epsilon^{i}
and
\epsilon^{j}
(specifically in the large aspect ratio limit, see Appendix
A
) inside the same average as Eq.
III.19
, which vanishes when integrating over the contact position
\epsilon^{i}
. By contrast, we did not assume that the equation of motion (analogous to
\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{min}}
) is a Jeffery-like equation with an additional torque. Rather, we
derived
the dynamics based on a dissipation function that incorporates (solid or lubricated) frictional drag. This yields an
\bm{\omega}_{\text{min}}
for the rods, with an associated torque, after taking the variation. Our approach necessarily results in even powers of
\epsilon^{i}
(Eq.
IV.2
), whose average over all contacts is non-zero. Hence, as
\left\langle\frac{(\epsilon^{i})^{2}}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\right\rangle_{j}\neq 0
, the solid frictional torque is non-zero and so is its contribution in the Smoluchowski equation.

IV.3
Smoluchowski Equation

The Rayleighian (per rod) for a suspension of rods under shear (Eqs.
II.10
,
II.16
,
II.1
) is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{0}=\int\psi^{i}\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}&\left[\frac{\zeta_{r}}{2}(\bm{\omega}^{i}-\bm{\omega}_{0})^{2}+\frac{\zeta_{r}}{4}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})^{2}\right.\\
&\quad\left.+\bm{\omega}^{i}\cdot\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}\right]+\frac{\eta_{s}}{\bar{\rho}}\mathbf{D}:\mathbf{D},\end{split}

(IV.5)

where
\tilde{U}=k_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{B}}T\ln{\psi}+U\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right)
is the combination of Brownian and mean field excluded volume potentials,
\mathbf{D}=1/2\left(\nabla\bm{v}+\nabla\bm{v}^{\text{T}}\right)
is the symmetric velocity gradient tensor,
\bar{\rho}
is the mean particle density, and
\bm{\omega}_{0}
is defined by Eq.
II.17
. The total Rayleighian to minimize is given by Eq.
II.20
, which is the sum of
{\cal R}_{0}
and the dissipation from lubricated and solid frictional contacts:

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{0}+\Phi_{\text{sol}}^{\text{mf}}+\Phi_{\text{BL}}^{\text{mf}}.

(IV.6)

Finally, we follow Wyart and Cates
Wyart and Cates 2014
and presume that some fraction
\Theta
of the
\langle c\rangle
contacts are solid and the remaining fraction
1-\Theta
are lubricated.
\Theta
is in principle a function of
p\equiv P/P^{*}
, where
P
is the pressure and a characteristic pressure
P^{*}\sim F_{N}
is set by the rod-rod interactions (such as the pressure at which lubrication films break down), which is related to the average contact force
|F_{\text{N}}|
between rods. We assume that
\Theta
has the following limiting behavior:

\Theta(p)=\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{lr}1,&\text{for }p\gg 1\\
0,&\text{if }p\ll 1,\end{array}

(IV.7)

and varies sharply from 0 to 1 at
P=P^{*}
. Hence,

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{0}+\left[1-\Theta\left(p\right)\right]\Phi_{\text{BL}}^{\text{mf}}+\Theta(p)\Phi_{\text{sol}}^{\text{mf}}.

(IV.8)

Upon minimization with respect to
\bm{\omega}^{i}
(see Eqs.
IV.2
,
IV.4
), we find

\bm{\omega}^{i}_{\text{min}}=\bm{\omega}_{0}-\frac{\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}+\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v})},

(IV.9)

where

\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v})=(1-\Theta(p))\frac{\eta_{s}\phi L^{3}}{3\pi}+\Theta(p)\mu_{k}|F_{N}|\left\langle\frac{(\epsilon^{i})^{2}}{|\bm{v}_{c}(\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{min}})|}\right\rangle_{j}

(IV.10)

is the rotational drag function due to friction, which depends on the stress through
F_{N}
and the local rotational dynamics through
|\bm{v}_{c}|
. Eq.
IV.9
is a self-consistent equation of motion for
\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{min}}
, since the drag force itself depends on the rotation rate
\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{min}}
.

The corresponding Smoluchowski equation is

\dot{\psi}=-\hat{\bm{R}}\cdot\left(\bm{\omega}_{0}\psi\right)+\hat{\bm{R}}\cdot\left[\frac{\psi\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}+\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v}))}\right].

(IV.11)

This too is a self-consistent equation (analogous to the Doi model near the isotropic-nematic transition
Doi and Edwards 1989
, which depends on the average value of the order parameter in the excluded volume potential
\tilde{U}
), since it relies on an average over the rod distribution to calculate the contact torque.

V
Predictions from Smoluchowski Equation

V.1
Lubricated Contact Limit

For only lubricated contacts (
\Theta(p)=0
), Eq.
IV.10

is of the same form as Eq.

\tilde{\zeta}_{r}=\zeta_{r}+\frac{\eta_{s}\phi L^{3}}{3\pi}.

(V.1)

As this quantity is independent of
\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}

II.19
), as is
\dot{\mathbf{Q}}
. The effect of lubricated contacts is to increase
\zeta_{r}
;
i.e.
, the rate of rotation of rods is decreased by the presence of contacts, as expected.

V.2
Solid Contact Limit

For all solid-like contacts (
\Theta(p)=1
), we obtain

\bm{\omega}^{i}_{\text{min}}=\bm{\omega}_{0}-\frac{\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}+\mu_{k}|F_{N}|\left\langle\frac{(\epsilon^{i})^{2}}{|\bm{v}_{c}(\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{min}})|}\right\rangle_{j}},

(V.2)

where now the denominator on the RHS is a function of
\bm{\omega}^{i}
, so that the equation is self-consistent (The angular velocity dependence of
\bm{v}_{c}
is shown in Eq.
III.4
)
. This is not an unexpected result; the dependence of solid friction on the
direction
of
\bm{v}_{c}
, but not its magnitude, leads to an associated torque (Eq.
IV.2
) that is a non-linear function of
\bm{\omega}
. Similarly, the Smoluchowski equation (Eq.
IV.11
) will be self-consistent, as the denominator on the RHS depends on
\psi
.

We choose to scale the rod length
L
out of
\epsilon^{i}
, and a characteristic relative speed
L\dot{\gamma}
between rod contacts out of the contact velocity (since the dominant motion leading to frictional torque is possibly inter-rod rotation), defining

\begin{split}\hat{\epsilon}^{i}&=\frac{1}{L}\epsilon^{i},\\

(V.3)

Then we can write Eq.

\bm{\omega}^{i}_{\text{min}}=\bm{\omega}_{0}-\frac{\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}\left[1+\Delta\left\langle\frac{(\hat{\epsilon}^{i})^{2}}{|\hat{\bm{v}}_{c}|}\right\rangle_{j}\right]},

(V.4)

where the ratio

\Delta=\frac{\mu_{k}|F_{N}|L}{\zeta_{r}\dot{\gamma}}.

(V.5)

compares the characteristic torque from solid friction to that of hydrodynamic drag.
Hence, solid frictional contacts increases the effective rotational drag constant and brings
\bm{\omega}_{\text{min}}
closer to Jeffery’s equation
\bm{\omega}^{i}_{\text{min}}=\bm{\omega}_{0}

The ratio
\Delta

F_{N}
between rods is induced by flow. This force

should contain a component of the shear stress, so we expect that

), where

In the case where the frictional torque is much smaller than the rotational drag torque,
\Delta\ll 1
, we can expand in powers of

to obtain the following Smoluchowski equation

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}=

\displaystyle-\hat{\bm{R}}\cdot\left\{\psi\bm{\omega}_{0}-\frac{\psi\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}}\left(1-\Delta\left\langle\frac{(\hat{\epsilon}^{i})^{2}}{|\hat{\bm{v}}_{c}|}\right\rangle_{j}\right)\right\}.

(V.6)

The first two terms above follow Doi

IV.2

VII
Conclusion

We have shown how dissipation
cdue to contact friction can be incorporated into the Rayleighian of rods in suspension of rigid rods under flow, in terms of the angular velocity
\bm{\omega}
for rigid rod rotation.
We apply Onsager’s variational method
Doi 2011
and minimize the Rayleighian with respect to
\bm{\omega}
, and thus derive a new Smoluchowski equation and stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
for flowing rigid rod suspensions. If the contribution to the torque on a given rod from frictional contacts is weak (compared to that from viscous forces), this Smoluchowski equation leads to a dynamical equation for the order parameter tensor
\mathbf{Q}
analogous to the Doi model
Doi and Edwards 1989
, with an additional term proportional to the magnitude of the average torque due to frictional contacts. Correspondingly, the stress tensor is that of the Doi model with additional terms,
due to frictional contacts, which scale with the magnitude of the average frictional torque. We identify distinct contributions from lubricated or solid contacts.

The expressions for the
\mathbf{Q}
dynamics and stress involve an average over the reciprocal of the contact velocity between rods, which impedes further analytic progress. The expressions for
\dot{\mathbf{Q}}
(Eq.
V.11
) and
\bm{\sigma}
(Eq.
VI.5
) can, in principle, be simplified by approximating the distributions of contact velocities (Appendix
C
). A numerical approach could also be also adopted to address a given flow geometry (such as planar shear) as done in
Yamane et al. 1994
.

Critical to our approach is treating frictional dissipation (due to two-particle interactions) via a mean field,
i.e.
by averaging over the distribution of the relevant positional and orientational variables of the interacting pairs. In deriving this mean field we obtained an equation (Eq.
III.18
) for the mean number of contacts per rod
\langle c\rangle
, as a function of the scalar nematic order parameter
S
and nematic strength
\phi L/D
. This form extends the known isotropic result
Philipse 1996
to the ordered regime, such as that found for dense suspensions of rods under shear. Verification of this function is challenging experimentally
Demirkurt et al. 2024
, but would be straightforward in simulations.

In our formulation, if there are solid frictional contacts we find a coupled, self-consistent set of equations for the dyanmics of the order tensor
\mathbf{Q}
, the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
, and the microscopic dynamics
\bm{\omega}^{i}_{\text{min}}
of the rods.
The frictional contribution to the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
depends on the normal force
|F_{N}(\bm{\sigma})|
between particles, which is controlled by the fluid stress tensor itself, and hence the shear rate
\dot{\gamma}
, leading to a self consistent relation to determine the stress. The rod rotation rate
\bm{\omega}^{i}_{\text{min}}
and hence the order tensor
\bm{\sigma}
are controlled by the contact velocity
\bm{v}_{c}
(see Eq.
IV.9
), which is, by
III.4
, a function of
\bm{\omega}^{i}_{\text{min}}
and the force
|F_{N}(\bm{\sigma})|
. The self-consistent nature of our equation set can be traced back to the form of Coulomb friction, which is a non-polynomial function of
\bm{\omega}
. This leads to a balance of torques that is non-linear in
\bm{\omega}
(
i.e.
a necessarily self-consistent
\bm{\omega}^{i}_{\text{min}}
).

In the limit of only lubricated contacts,
i.e.

\Theta(p)=0
in Eq.
IV.9
, we find an increase to the rotational drag constant in the
\mathbf{Q}
equation.
Importantly, this perturbation to the drag constant (
\tilde{\zeta}_{r}-\zeta_{r}
in Eq.
V.1
) is not simply proportional to the average number of contacts in the isotropic case,
\phi L/D
. This is because Eq.
II.21
, which defines the form of the boundary lubricated between rods, includes a coefficient of
1/|\bm{\tau}|=1/|\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\times\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}|
,
which cancels the excluded volume term (proportional to
\bm{\tau}
) eventually applied in Eqs.
IV.3
,
IV.4
to count the expected number of contacts.

In previous work, inclusion of frictional contacts in rod dynamical models was either limited to boundary lubricated friction (Eq.
II.21
), as in
Toll and Månson 1994
;
Ferec et al. 2009
, or was found to have no effect on
\dot{\mathbf{Q}}

Bounoua et al. 2016
;
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993
. The latter finding was due to the assumption that the frictional torque between rods could be calculated directly by averaging over the expected distribution of interacting rods, treating each as undergoing the motion prescribed by Jeffery’s equation,
\dot{\bm{u}}_{\text{Jeffery}}
. This contact torque would then be included as a term in the equation of motion for the rod motion,
\dot{\bm{u}}
. In
Bounoua et al. 2016
;
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993
the average of the torque over contact positions results in a zero mean contact torque. By contrast, the Rayleighian that we construct is positive semidefinite in the relative rod-rod contact velocity
|\bm{v}_{c}|
, and we determine
\dot{\bm{u}}
from the balance of torques produced from minimizing the Rayleighian, which leads to a non-zero assocated contact torque.

A fundamental assumption in our approach is that solid frictional contacts are undergoing sliding friction and have exceeded the static limit. This allows us to include the power dissipated during friction in the Rayleighian as a function of
\bm{\omega}
. However, in reality the rods will stick and interact through solid friction without relative motion; this effect cannot be treated by a variational approach, as static friction does no work. Large clusters of stuck rods can contribute to shear thickening
Heussinger 2013
, so our model misses this contribution. A potential solution might be to consider the dissipation of larger structures (such as clusters of rods), but that is beyond the scope of this project. We have also assumed that suspensions are spatially homogenous, which may break down during DST. It has recently been shown that in the regime of DST there are significant stress fluctations in tandem with nonaffine flow
Moghimi et al. 2025
. The observation that frictional forces can induce branched frictional contact networks
D’Amico et al. 2025
also suggests a more careful treatment that includes spatial inhomogenity.

Appendix A
Average “Fiber” Torque

An alternative method to determine the effect of contact torque in suspensions of rods was explored by
Bounoua et al. 2016
,
Djalili-Moghaddam and Toll 2005
,
Toll and Månson 1994
, and
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993
. In this approach the angular equation of motion
\dot{\bm{u}}
is a sum of the advective dynamics given by Jeffery’s Equation
Jeffery 1923
, and the torque
\bm{\Gamma}
due to frictional contacts:

\dot{\bm{u}}=\dot{\bm{u}}_{\text{Jeffery}}+\alpha\langle\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{v}_{c})\rangle_{j}.

(A.1)

Here,
\alpha\sim 1/\zeta_{r}
is a rotational drag coefficient proportional to the magnitude of the torque part of the resistance tensor
Kim and Karrila 1991
, the angle brackets are the same as in Eq.
III.19
, and

\dot{\bm{u}}_{\text{Jeffery}}=\mathbf{\Omega}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}+\lambda\left[\mathbf{D}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}-\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{D}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right)\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right],

(A.2)

where
\lambda
depends only on rod aspect ratio
Larson 1999
,

\lambda=\frac{\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)^{2}-1}{\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)^{2}+1}.

(A.3)

As
\bm{\Gamma}
depends on
\bm{v}_{c}
, it implicitly depends on
\dot{\bm{u}}
, according to

\bm{v}_{c}^{ij}=\epsilon^{i}\dot{\bm{u}}^{i}-\epsilon^{j}\dot{\bm{u}}^{j}+\nabla\bm{v}\cdot(\bm{r}_{\text{com}}^{i}-\bm{r}_{\text{com}}^{j}),

(A.4)

which is equivalent to Eq.
III.1
(recall that
\bm{\omega}\times\hat{\bm{u}}=\dot{\bm{u}}
). To resolve this
Bounoua et al. 2016
;
Djalili-Moghaddam and Toll 2005
;
Toll and Månson 1994
;
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993
,
one can
replace
\dot{\bm{u}}
in the contact velocity by the dilute rod form due to Jeffery (Eq.
A.2
), to write
\bm{v}_{c}
explicitly in
\hat{\bm{u}}^{i},\hat{\bm{u}}^{j},\epsilon^{i},\epsilon^{j}
, and
\nabla\bm{v}
. If it is assumed that rods are in close proximity the term linear in
\nabla\bm{v}
can be dropped and in the limit
\lambda\simeq 1
, (
i.e.

\frac{L}{D}\gg 1
), the contact velocity becomes

\bm{v}_{c}\simeq\epsilon^{j}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\cdot\mathbf{D}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{j})\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}-\epsilon^{i}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{D}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}.

(A.5)

This form was used by
Bounoua et al. 2016
,
Sandstrom and Tucker 1993
, and
Djalili-Moghaddam and Toll 2005
to obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\alpha\mu_{k}|F_{\text{N}}|\left\langle\frac{\hat{u}_{i}\times(\epsilon_{i}\hat{u}_{i}\times\hat{v}_{c})}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\right\rangle_{j}

(A.6a)

\displaystyle=\alpha\mu_{k}|F_{\text{N}}|\left\langle\frac{\epsilon^{i}\epsilon^{j}}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\cdot\mathbf{D}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\right)\left[\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{j})-\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\right]\right\rangle_{j}.

(A.6b)

This torque vanishes upon averaging over the contact positions, and
\dot{\bm{u}}
is unchanged. However, if one uses
\dot{\bm{u}}_{\text{Jeffery}}
in Eq.
A.4
and makes no further assumptions about
\bm{v}_{c}
, the contribution is instead

\bm{\Gamma}=\mu_{k}|F_{\text{N}}|(1-\lambda)\left\langle\frac{(\epsilon^{i})^{2}}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\left[\mathbf{D}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}-(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{D}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right]\right.\\
\left.\phantom{\dfrac{10^{2}}{10}}+\left.\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{i}\epsilon^{j}\right)\right]\right\rangle_{j}.

(A.7)

The terms proportional to
\epsilon^{i}\epsilon^{j}
will average to zero as before, but the first term is non-zero for
\lambda\neq 1
.

Appendix B
Stress Tensor

The stress tensor for our model (Eq.
VI.3
) has two parts: that due to dissipation from non-frictional sources, and a contribution from frictional dissipation (both lubricated and solid). The former is modified by the minimum angular velocity of the rods, and thus in the following we insert Eq.
IV.9
and collect terms to show the relation to the Doi model. The latter frictional born terms we also calculate here.
From Eq.
VI.3
, the non-frictional dissipation term is
\bar{\rho}\frac{\delta\mathcal{R}_{0}}{\delta(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}
; so we have

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}^{0}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}_{0}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}

(B.1)

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\int\left[\zeta_{r}(\bm{\omega}-\bm{\omega}_{0})\cdot\frac{\partial(\bm{\omega}-\bm{\omega}_{0})}{\partial\nabla\bm{v}_{\alpha\beta}}+\frac{\zeta_{r}}{2}(\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\frac{\partial\bm{\omega}}{\partial\nabla\bm{v}_{\alpha\beta}}\cdot\hat{R}\tilde{U}\right]\psi\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}+2\eta_{s}\mathbf{D}.

(B.2)

The first two terms in brackets constitute the form of the stress tensor for non-interacting frictional suspensions derived by
Doi 2013
using the Rayleighian approach, while the last term due to
U(\hat{\bm{u}})
appears in the presence of excluded volume interactions, as derived in
Doi and Edwards 1989
. The angular velocity
\bm{\omega}
is given by Eq.
IV.9
, and can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}

\displaystyle=\bm{\omega_{\textrm{DE}}}+\frac{\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v})}{\zeta_{r}+\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v})}\frac{\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}},

(B.3)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\omega_{\textrm{DE}}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\omega}_{0}-\frac{\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}}

(B.4)

is the angular velocity used by Doi and Edwards
Doi and Edwards 1989
.

In the Doi-Edwards case, for
\bm{\omega}=\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{DE}}
, the stress tensor

\bm{\sigma}^{0,\textrm{DE}}_{\alpha\beta}
is

\bm{\sigma}^{0,\textrm{DE}}_{\alpha\beta}=\bar{\rho}\int\left[\zeta_{r}(\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{DE}}-\bm{\omega}_{0})\cdot\frac{\partial(\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{DE}}-\bm{\omega}_{0})}{\partial\nabla\bm{v}_{\alpha\beta}}+\frac{\zeta_{r}}{2}(\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\frac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{DE}}}{\partial\nabla\bm{v}_{\alpha\beta}}\cdot\hat{R}\tilde{U}\right]\psi\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}+2\eta_{s}\mathbf{D}.

(B.5)

Using
\frac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{DE}}}{\partial\nabla v_{\alpha\beta}}=\frac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{0}}{\partial\nabla v_{\alpha\beta}}
, which follows from Eq.
B.4
,
this simplifies to

\bm{\sigma}^{0,\textrm{DE}}_{\alpha\beta}=\bar{\rho}\int\left[\frac{\zeta_{r}}{2}(\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\frac{\partial\bm{\omega}_{\textrm{DE}}}{\partial\nabla\bm{v}_{\alpha\beta}}\cdot\hat{R}\tilde{U}\right]\psi\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}+2\eta_{s}\mathbf{D}.

(B.6)

Using Eq.
B.3
in Eq.
B.2
and comparing to Eq.
B.6
, we find the frictional contribution to the angular rotation,

\bm{\sigma}^{0}_{\alpha\beta}=\bm{\sigma}^{0,\textrm{DE}}_{\alpha\beta}+\bar{\rho}\int\left[\bm{\Omega}_{F}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{\Omega}_{F}}{\partial\nabla\bm{v}_{\alpha\beta}}\right]\psi\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}},

(B.7)

where

\bm{\Omega}_{F}=\frac{\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v})}{\zeta_{r}+\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v})}\frac{\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}}.

(B.8)

The second two terms in the stress tensor (Eq.
VI.3
) account for contact dissipation.
If all frictional contacts are solid-like (
\Theta(p)=1
), then by using Eq.
IV.1
we find

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}^{\text{solid}}}{\bar{\rho}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Phi_{\text{sol}}^{\text{mf}}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}

(B.9a)

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}|F_{N}|\left\langle\left\langle\frac{1}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\bm{v}_{c,\mu}\frac{\partial\bm{v}_{c,\mu}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}\right\rangle\right\rangle

(B.9b)

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}|F_{N}|\left\langle\left\langle\frac{\bm{v}_{c,\alpha}}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\left(\epsilon^{j}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}-\epsilon^{i}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right)_{\beta}+\frac{2(\epsilon^{i}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\times\bm{v}_{c})}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{\omega}^{i}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}\right\rangle\right\rangle

(B.9c)

\displaystyle=2\mu_{k}|F_{N}|\left\langle\left\langle\frac{(\epsilon^{i})^{2}}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\left[-(\bm{\omega}^{i}\times\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})_{\alpha}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\beta}+(\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})_{\alpha}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\beta}+(\bm{\omega}^{i}-\bm{\omega}^{i}_{0})\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{\omega}^{i}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}\right]\right\rangle\right\rangle

(B.9d)

\displaystyle=2\mu_{k}|F_{N}|\left\langle\left\langle\frac{(\epsilon^{i})^{2}}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\,\left[\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\alpha}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\beta}\,\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right)+\bm{f}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}\right]\right\rangle\right\rangle.

(B.9e)

where we have used Eq.
III.4
in the third and fourth lines. We have used Eq.
IV.9
to write
\bm{\omega}^{i}\equiv\bm{\omega}_{0}+\bm{f}
in the final line, where
the rotational velocity
\bm{f}
due to interactions is

\bm{f}=-\frac{\hat{\bm{R}}\tilde{U}}{\zeta_{r}+\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v})}.

(B.10)

Note that
\zeta_{r}\bm{\Omega}^{F}=-\zeta_{F}(F_{N},\bm{v})\bm{f}
. Similarly, if all contacts are lubricated (
\Theta(p)=1
), we use Eq.
IV.3
to find

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}^{\text{BL}}}{\bar{\rho}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Phi_{\text{BL}}^{\text{mf}}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}

(B.11a)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left\langle\frac{2\eta_{s}D}{|\bm{\tau}|}\bm{v}_{c,\mu}\frac{\partial\bm{v}_{c,\mu}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}\right\rangle\right\rangle

(B.11b)

\displaystyle=4\eta_{s}D\left\langle\left\langle\frac{(\epsilon^{i})^{2}}{|\bm{\tau}|}\,\left[\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\alpha}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\beta}\,\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right)+\bm{f}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}}\right]\right\rangle\right\rangle

(B.11c)

\displaystyle=4\eta_{s}D\left\langle\left\langle\frac{(\epsilon^{i})^{2}}{|\bm{\tau}|}\,\left[\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\alpha}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\beta}\,\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right)\right]\right\rangle\right\rangle

(B.11d)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi\phi\eta_{s}L^{3}}{3}\int\hat{\bm{u}}_{\alpha}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\beta}\,\left(\hat{\bm{u}}\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{u}}\right)\psi(\hat{\bm{u}})\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}},

(B.11e)

where in the fourth line we have used the independence of
f
on
(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}
, and in the fifth line applied the definition of the angle brackets, Eq.
III.19
. This form is that of the Doi-Edwards model (Eq.
B.6
), with a modified leading coefficient and without the free energy driven term.
Both forms of friction contribute to the anisotropic Newtonian stress tensor (first term) and have a higher order contribution that is second order in the rod-rod interactions.

Appendix C
Approximate form of
\left\langle\dfrac{\left(\epsilon^{i}\right)^{2}}{|v_{c}|}\right\rangle_{j}

In order to approximate
\left\langle\frac{\left(\epsilon^{i}\right)^{2}}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\right\rangle_{j}
, which appears in Eqs.
IV.9
,
V.8
, we must first consider
1/|{\bm{v}}_{c}|
. We seek a form that removes dependence on
\hat{\bm{u}}_{j}
and
\bm{\omega}_{j}
. So, we take

\begin{split}\bm{\omega}^{i}&=\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\times(\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})\\
\bm{\omega}^{j}&=\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\times(\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}),\\
\end{split}

(C.1)

as well as an approximation from
Bounoua et al. 2016
,

\frac{1}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\approx\frac{|\mathbf{D}\epsilon^{i}|}{\left[v_{c}^{2}\right]},

(C.2)

where the square brackets denote the average

[x]\equiv\frac{1}{L^{2}}\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{\frac{L}{2}}{\text{d}\epsilon^{i}}\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{\frac{L}{2}}\text{d}\epsilon^{j}\int{x\psi^{j}\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}}.

(C.3)

Ref.
Bounoua et al. 2016
argues that the approximation above underestimates
1/|\bm{v}_{c}|
by a factor of
\frac{1}{\ln{L/D}}
.
Inserting Eq.
C.1
into the contact velocity, Eq.
III.4
, we find

\bm{v}_{c}^{2}=(\epsilon^{i})^{2}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})^{2}+(\epsilon^{j})^{2}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{j})^{2}.

(C.4)

Using this form in Eq.
C.2
gives

\begin{split}\frac{1}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}&=\frac{|\mathbf{D}\epsilon^{i}|}{[(\epsilon^{i})^{2}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})^{2}+(\epsilon^{j})^{2}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{j})^{2}]}\\[5.0pt]
&=\frac{12|\mathbf{D}\epsilon^{i}|}{L^{2}\left[\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\right)^{2}+\left(\nabla v\right)_{\alpha\beta}\left(\nabla v\right)_{\mu\nu}\int u^{j}_{\alpha}u^{j}_{\beta}u^{j}_{\mu}u^{j}_{\nu}\psi^{j}\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\right]},\\
\end{split}

(C.5)

where in the second line we have used the definition of the square brackets (Eq.
C.3
). With an approximation for
\frac{1}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}
we can now address the term that appears in
\dot{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\bm{\sigma}
:

\begin{split}\left\langle\frac{\left(\epsilon^{i}\right)^{2}}{|\bm{v}_{c}|}\right\rangle_{j}&\approx\frac{12|\mathbf{D}|}{L^{2}}\left\langle\frac{|\epsilon^{i}|\left(\epsilon^{i}\right)^{2}}{\left((\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}^{i})^{2}+(\nabla v)_{\alpha\beta}(\nabla v)_{\mu\nu}\int u^{k}_{\alpha}u^{k}_{\beta}u^{k}_{\mu}u^{k}_{\nu}\psi^{k}\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}^{k}\right)}\right\rangle_{j}\\
\end{split}

(C.6)

The second term in the denominator is an orientational average over four powers of
\hat{\bm{u}}
, which we cast in terms of
\mathbf{Q}
by invoking Doi’s quadratic closure
Doi 1981
,

X_{\mu\eta}\langle u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}u_{\mu}u_{\eta}\rangle\approx X_{\mu\eta}\langle u_{\mu}u_{\eta}\rangle\langle u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}\rangle.

(C.7)

By inserting this closure and assuming that the velocity gradient tensor is traceless (
i.e.
, an incompressible fluid), we obtain

(C.8)

The angle brackets in
C.6
include an integral over

(see Eq.
III.19
). We use an approximation for
\bm{\tau}
from

and

,

\begin{split}\int|\bm{\tau}|\psi^{j}\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}&\approx\int\frac{\pi}{4}\left[1-\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\alpha}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}_{\beta}-\frac{\delta_{\alpha\beta}}{3}\right)\left(\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}_{\alpha}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}_{\beta}-\frac{\delta_{\alpha\beta}}{3}\right)\right]\psi^{j}\text{d}\hat{\bm{u}}^{j}\\
&=\frac{\pi}{4}[1-\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}\hat{\bm{u}}^{i}:\mathbf{Q}],\end{split}

(C.9)

where only terms up to second order have been kept. Finally, the full
\epsilon

is

\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{\frac{L}{2}}{\text{d}\epsilon^{i}}\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{\frac{L}{2}}\text{d}\epsilon^{j}|\epsilon^{i}|(\epsilon^{i})^{2}=\frac{L^{5}}{32}.

(C.10)

Inserting

C.9
and
C.8
