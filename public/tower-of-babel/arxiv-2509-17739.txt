Existence and Synthesis of Multi-Resolution Approximate Bisimulations for Continuous-State Dynamical Systems
Rudi Coppola, Yannik Schnitzer, Mirco Giacobbe, Alessandro Abate, Manuel Mazo Jr

Abstract
We present a fully automatic framework for synthesising compact, finite-state deterministic abstractions of deterministic, continuous-state autonomous systems under locally specified resolution requirements. Our approach builds on multi-resolution approximate bisimulations, a generalisation of classical ϵ \epsilon -approximate bisimulations, that support state-dependent error bounds and subsumes both variable- and uniform-resolution relations. We show that some systems admit multi-resolution bisimulations but no ϵ \epsilon -approximate bisimulation. We prove the existence of multi-resolution approximately bisimilar abstractions for all incrementally uniformly bounded ( δ \delta -UB) systems, thereby broadening the applicability of symbolic verification to a larger class of dynamics; as a trivial special case, this result also covers incrementally globally asymptotically stable ( δ \delta -GAS) systems. The Multi-resolution Abstraction Synthesis Problem (MRASP) is solved via a scalable Counterexample-Guided Inductive Synthesis (CEGIS) loop, combining mesh refinement with counterexample-driven refinement. This ensures soundness for all δ \delta -UB systems, and ensures termination in certain special cases. Experiments on linear and nonlinear benchmarks, including non- δ \delta -GAS and non-differentiable cases, demonstrate that our algorithm yields abstractions up to 50% smaller than Lyapunov-based grids while enforcing tighter, location-dependent error guarantees.



I
Introduction

Formal verification and synthesis of control systems have become essential in ensuring safety and reliability in safety-critical domains such as robotics, autonomous driving, and cyber-physical systems. The key challenge is that continuous-state dynamical systems are often too complex for direct analysis, requiring the construction of finite-state abstractions that preserve essential properties of the original system. Symbolic methods based on bisimulation relations have been highly successful in bridging this gap between continuous dynamics and discrete verification frameworks. Essential to these developments is the notion of approximate relations that relax the strict requirements of exact bisimulation
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Early work introduced
\epsilon

[

,
5

6
]
.

Classical approaches rely on uniform-resolution abstractions, typically based on gridding the state space. While such methods provide rigorous guarantees, they often suffer from scalability issues due to the curse of dimensionality. Moreover, many of these techniques require the existence of certificates, such as Lyapunov-like functions
[
7
,
8
]
, which can be difficult to obtain in practice. This motivates the search for abstraction techniques that are both more flexible and applicable to a broader class of systems. To improve scalability,
[

proposed abstractions using a set of grids of different coarseness, leading to more compact abstractions. In parallel, the notion of multi-resolution approximate bisimulation has been proposed to exploit heterogeneous error bounds across the state space, allowing for finer approximations in regions of interest while maintaining coarser approximations elsewhere
[
13
,

To alleviate the computational complexity of abstracting systems when no certificates are known, data-driven and learning-based abstraction methods such as

In the following, we provide a detailed comparison with the relevant existing literature.

Related work
: Mitigating the curse of dimensionality is a central challenge in abstractions for dynamical systems and has received significant attention over the past decade. Instead of a uniform grid,

introduce multi-scale abstractions by combining grids of different coarseness, extending

In contrast, we employ multi-resolution approximate bisimulations, first proposed in

The authors use local linearisation (under differentiability), a candidate abstraction, and a norm-based template to cast the existence of such an abstraction as a linear program, refined by a greedy heuristic if infeasible. Their method is efficient for linear systems, though local linearisations can be costly

In data-driven settings,

Main Contributions
: This work provides the first existential results for multi-resolution approximate bisimulation relations. Since this notion strictly generalises
\epsilon
-approximate bisimulations, any
\delta
-GAS system admits a multi-resolution bisimilar abstraction. More significantly, we show that
\delta
-UB systems, a strictly larger class, also admit such abstractions.

We then formalise the Multi-resolution Abstraction Synthesis Problem (MRASP), cast it as a satisfiability problem in first-order logic, and solve it efficiently through a fully automatic Counterexample-Guided Inductive Synthesis (CEGIS) loop, which iteratively refines both the abstraction and its relation until validity is achieved. Unlike existing methods, our approach requires neither
\delta
-GAS Lyapunov functions nor local linearisation, making it applicable to non-
\delta
-GAS and even non-differentiable systems.

The synthesis algorithm is parallelisable, leveraging the abstraction’s graph structure to distribute refinement and verification. Empirical results show that the produced abstractions are substantially more compact than grid- or Lyapunov-based ones, while location-dependent error bounds yield tighter guarantees than uniform resolutions. Benchmarks on linear, nonlinear, and non-differentiable systems confirm scalability and soundness, providing a fully automated tool for constructing abstractions under local resolution requirements.

(8)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{con}}(x)\doteq\bigwedge_{\mathclap{i=1}}^{k}\!\left[d(x,\hat{x}_{i})\leq\hat{\epsilon}_{i}\!\!\implies\!\!d(f(x),\hat{x}_{g(i)})\leq\hat{\epsilon}_{g(i)}\right],

(9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{res}}(x)\doteq\bigwedge_{\mathclap{i=1}}^{k}\!\left[\hat{\epsilon}_{i}\leq\epsilon(x,\hat{x}_{i})\right],

(10)

where
g\colon[1,\dots,k]\to[1,\dots,k]
maps the index of an abstract state to that of its successor, i.e.
\hat{x}_{g(i)}=\hat{f}(\hat{x}_{i})
.
\hfill\lhd

The relaxed Problem
1
represents an MRASP.
In the absence of Assumption
2
, Relaxed Problem
1
would give rise to a second-order logic formula of the form
\exists\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{k},R\subseteq X\times\hat{X}

\forall x\dots

23

Even by parametrising the relation

over a set of conjunctions and disjunctions of (in the best case) linear inequalities, which can be in general computationally expensive to check even for mature first-order solvers
[
24

V
Counterexample-Guided Multi-resolution Abstraction Synthesis

In this section, we describe how we construct a solution for Relaxed Problem
1
.
We adopt a CEGIS scheme to address the presence of a quantifier alternation.
Typically, a CEGIS loop involves a
learner
and a
verifier
component. The learner proposes a candidate parameter assignment
\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{k},\hat{\epsilon}_{1},\dots,\hat{\epsilon}_{k}
satisfying (
8
), (
9
), and (
10
) over a finite number of sample states
D\doteq\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subseteq X
; this allows to transform the quantifier alternation into a single existential query over a finite set of conjunctions as
\exists\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{k}\in X,\hat{\epsilon}_{1},\dots,\hat{\epsilon}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\text{ s.t. }

\bigwedge_{x_{j}\in D}\Phi_{\text{cov}}(x_{j})\wedge\Phi_{\text{con}}(x_{j})\wedge\Phi_{\text{res}}(x_{j}).

(11)

Given the candidate parameter assignment, the verifier checks whether there exists a counterexample in the domain
X
as
\exists x\in X
s.t.
\neg(\Phi_{\text{cov}}(x)\wedge\Phi_{\text{con}}(x)\wedge\Phi_{\text{res}}(x))
. Observe that, while the learner may use an arbitrary methodology to craft a candidate, potentially based on heuristics, the verifier provides a formal guarantee of correctness in case the check returns no counterexamples.
The learner, solving (
11
), must assign a total of
k\cdot n
real variables for the abstraction’s state set and
k
real variables for the scalars
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}
. While gradient-based methods could be used to craft candidate solutions, encoding the constraints in (
11

f(x)
and
d(x,\hat{x})
are polynomials,
the learner and the verifier may consist of an SMT solver, which natively handles logical constraints. Unfortunately, obtaining abstractions of practical use typically requires a large
k
, making an SMT-based learner excessively slow. Hence, we further split (
11
) into two simpler problems, where the learner comprises a Clustering and a Relation Learner stage. The verifier consists of a single SMT stage.

Relaxed Problem 2

.

1.

(Clustering) Select
\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{k}\in X
.

2.

(Relation Learner) Given
\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{k}\in X
, find
\hat{\epsilon}_{1},\dots,\hat{\epsilon}_{k}
s.t.
\forall x\in X
it holds
\Phi_{\text{cov}}(x)\wedge\Phi_{\text{con}}(x)\wedge\Phi_{\text{res}}(x)
, defined as in (
8
), (
9
), (
10
).
\hfill\lhd

The second problem relaxation separates the choice of the abstract states
\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{k}
from the query yielding the appropriate scalars
\hat{\epsilon}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\epsilon}_{k}
; the former are instead computed beforehand by a fast heuristic, specifically by a clustering algorithm. Problem Relaxation

transforms the MRASP in Relaxed Problem
1
into 1) finding a deterministic abstraction of
S
with
k

This reduces the solution space but, in practice, dramatically speeds up the computation of a solution and enhances the scalability of the approach. Any solution to Relaxed Problem

also constitutes a solution to Relaxed Problem
1

V-B

Counterexample Feedback

If the verifier does not provide a counterexample, the CEGIS loop terminates, having successfully synthesised an abstraction

and relation

If the verifier provides a counterexample

V-C

Proposed Algorithm - In Detail

Referring to Figure

Remark 1

V-D

Relation Templates

From this point onward, we relax Assumption

Assumption 3

Let
L_{\theta_{i}}(\hat{x}_{i})
denote a compact semialgebraic set in

\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
and associated with

R_{\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}}\doteq\{(x,\hat{x}_{i}):x\in L_{\theta_{i}}(\hat{x}_{i})\wedge d(x,\hat{x}_{i})\leq\hat{\epsilon}_{i}\},

(15)

where
\bm{\theta}\doteq\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
and
\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}\doteq\hat{\epsilon}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\epsilon}_{k}.

\hfill\lhd

We next discuss different choices for the templates.

V-D
1
Purely Metric Relation Template

In the simplest situation, the sets
L_{\theta_{i}}(\hat{x}_{i})
can themselves be defined by the distance; for instance if
d(x,\hat{x}_{i})=||x-\hat{x}_{i}||_{2}
and
L_{\theta_{i}}(\hat{x}_{i})=||x-\hat{x}_{i}||_{2}^{2}-\theta_{i}^{2}\leq 0
(with
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
), then the space of relations reduces to

R_{\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}}\doteq\{(x,\hat{x}_{i})\in X\times\hat{X}:||x-\hat{x}_{i}||_{2}\leq\min(\hat{\epsilon}_{i},\theta_{i})\},

(16)

thus we recover the template in Assumption
2
.

V-D
2
Polytopic Relation Template

Alternatively, the clustering stage produces a mesh given by the Voronoi diagram induced by the abstract states. Every Voronoi cell admits a half-space representation of the form

C_{i}=\{x\in X:A_{i}x\leq b_{i},A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}.

The rows of
A_{i}
correspond to the normal vectors defining the cell. The scaling of a cell
C_{i}
around a point
\hat{x}_{i}
by a factor
\theta_{i}>0
is defined as

L_{\theta_{i}}(\hat{x}_{i})\doteq\{x\in X:A_{i}(x-\hat{x}_{i})\leq\theta_{i}(b_{i}-A_{i}\hat{x}_{i})\}.

(17)

Note that for
\theta_{i}
equal to 1,
L_{\theta_{i}}(\hat{x}_{i})=C_{i}
; to enforce the coverage condition (
8
) in the Relation Learner we impose
\theta_{i}\geq 1
, as anticipated in Section
V-A1
.

V-E

Low Level Loop - One Shot

The Low Level Loop, comprising the Relation Learner and the Verifier, encodes and solves by CEGIS the following first-order satisfiability problem

\exists\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\geq 1,\forall x\in X\ .\ \Phi_{\text{con}}(x)\wedge\Phi_{\text{res}}(x).

(18)

The inputs are the initial dataset
D
, the candidate abstraction
\hat{S}
, the mesh
\mathcal{M}
, and a relation template
R_{\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}}
. Each
R_{\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}}(\hat{x}_{i})
describes a scaled mesh cell as introduced in Section
V-D2
.

Recall that every
\theta_{i}
influences the scaling of the mesh cell
C_{i}
containing
\hat{x}_{i}
.
By lower bounding each
\theta_{i}
by 1, we ensure that the coverage condition always holds. Additionally, let us fix
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}=\max_{x\in L_{\theta_{i}}(\hat{x}_{i})}d(x,\hat{x}_{i})
; we can rewrite the radius as

\hat{\epsilon}_{i}=\theta_{i}\max_{x\in L_{1}(\hat{x}_{i})}d(x,\hat{x}_{i})=\theta_{i}\gamma_{i}.

(19)

We have reduced the number of parameters to be determined in the Low Level Loop to one per cluster. This parallels Relaxed Problem
2
: instead of directly choosing the radii
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}
, we equivalently determine the appropriate scaling factors
\theta_{i}
.

Relation Learner.
The Relation Learner aims to solve:

\exists\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\geq 1\text{ s.t. }\bigwedge_{x_{j}\in D}\Phi_{\text{con}}(x_{j})\wedge\Phi_{\text{res}}(x_{j}),

(20)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{con}}(x_{j})\doteq\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\left[x_{j}\in R_{\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}}(\hat{x}_{i})\implies f(x_{j})\in R_{\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}}(\hat{x}_{g(i)})\right],

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{res}}(x_{j})\doteq\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\left[\theta_{i}\leq\frac{\epsilon(\hat{x}_{i})}{\gamma_{i}}\right].

For the chosen template, for any
i
, the antecedent
x_{j}\in R_{\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}}(\hat{x}_{i})
is equivalent to the condition
A_{i}(x_{j}-\hat{x}_{i})\leq\theta_{i}(b_{i}-A_{i}\hat{x}_{i})
and the consequent
f(x_{j})\in R_{\bm{\theta},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}}(\hat{x}_{g(i)})
is equivalent to
A_{g(i)}(f(x_{j})-\hat{x}_{g(i)})\leq\theta_{g(i)}(b_{g(i)}-A_{g(i)}\hat{x}_{g(i)})
.

Observe that, independently of the transition function
f
, the predicates in the constraints listed above are linear in
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
. The same applies to any template
L_{\theta_{i}}
that is linear in
\theta_{i}
. As such, we have encoded the Relation Learner stage in a single existential sentence in the theory of Linear Real Arithmetic (
\exists
-LRA).
This can be efficiently solved with SMT solvers like Z3, see
[
26
]
.

Verifier.

The verifier follows verbatim Section
V-A2
. It checks
\exists x\in X\text{ s.t. }\neg\Phi_{\text{con}}(x)
. Where
\Phi_{\text{con}}(x)
is given by the implication (

\exists

For this fragment, Z3 relies on heuristics, and the solution time depends on the number of constraints, number of variables, and degree of polynomials; usually it can handle quickly small (usually less than
6

8

4

Leveraging Proposition

Incrementally Uniformly Bounded Systems

In this section, we present a class of systems that admit a deterministic abstraction and a multi-resolution bisimulation relation for any resolution relation
\overline{R}
. We rely on the notion of Incrementally Uniformly Bounded systems
(\delta
-UB).

Definition 12

A TS
S=(X,X_{0},f)
is
\delta
-UB if

\forall x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime}\in X_{0},k\in\mathbb{N}\quad d(x_{k},x_{k}^{\prime})\leq\alpha(d(x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime})),

(27)

where
\alpha
is a class
\mathcal{K}
function.
\hfill\lhd

The condition above is an adaptation of
[
22
, Definition 17]
for autonomous systems. Note that
\delta
-UB implies continuity of
f
.

Theorem 2

Let
S=(X,X_{0},f)
be a
\delta
-UB TS with an asymptotically stable equilibrium at
x^{*}
, where
X
is a compact subset of its region of attraction.
Then, for any resolution function
\epsilon(x,\hat{x})
, there exists a finite-state deterministic abstraction
\hat{S}
and a multi-resolution approximate bisimulation relation between
S
and
\hat{S}
satisfying
\overline{R}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{W}_{S}(\overline{R})\neq\emptyset
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
2
relies on the existence of an abstraction
\hat{S}
whose graph has a single cycle at the equilibrium. Then, by providing a refinement scheme for the cells of the underlying mesh that monotonically shrinks the size of the cells, hindering the solution of any Subproblem
23
, we show that by exploiting
\delta
-UB, any desired resolution can be achieved anywhere in the domain, see Appendix
-C
.

Corollary 4

Let
S=(X,X_{0},f)
be a
\delta
-UB TS, where
X
is a compact transient set. Then
\mathcal{W}_{S}(\overline{R})\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Corollary

drops the requirement for the presence of an asymptotically stable equilibrium, requiring instead that the domain of abstraction

VIII
Conclusions and Discussion

In this work, we’ve presented a novel framework for the synthesis of multi-resolution approximate bisimulation relations, designed to construct provably correct abstractions for a broad class of continuous systems. We introduced the first existential proofs for multi-resolution approximate bisimulation relations. By showing that the class of
\delta
-UB systems, which strictly contains
\delta
-GAS systems, admits such relations (Theorem
2
), we extend the class of systems for which approximate bisimulations can be found.

Theorem
2
is constructive, forming the basis of Algorithm
1
, which synthesizes an abstraction
\hat{S}
and a multi-resolution relation
R
for any prescribed resolution. For systems with asymptotically stable equilibria, termination may require forward invariant sets around the equilibrium; if the chosen template cannot capture them, the algorithm remains sound but may not terminate. This limitation could be addressed by more flexible polytopic templates (cf. Remark
3
) or by allowing nondeterminism in the equilibrium cell, which would guarantee termination—directions we leave to future work.

The resulting abstraction size depends on the heuristics in the clustering and mesh refinement stages, yet our experiments show that they are often significantly smaller than those obtained with Lyapunov-based methods
[
27
]
, while neither requiring a Lyapunov function nor explicit growth bounds. Our algorithm instead adapts relation parameters directly from sampled system behaviours in the form of transitions. When abstraction size is not critical and a
\delta
-GAS Lyapunov function is available, Theorem
1
provides a lightweight alternative.

In contrast with
[
13
,
14
]
, our approach does not rely on a local linearisation of the system’s dynamics, making our algorithm applicable to a larger set of systems. As mentioned
[
14
]
, computing such linearisations can be computationally expensive for abstractions with a large state set. In fact, differentiability is not strictly required by Theorem
2
, as shown in Example
VII-D
. Moreover, Algorithm
1
exploits the abstraction’s graph structure and allows for a parallelised solution. The algorithm presented in
[
13
,
14
]
is not parallelisable, as the refinement procedure relies on the solution of an optimisation program involving the entire state set of the abstraction. We compared our approach with
[
13
]
on a linear system, for which
[
13
]
does not require any linearisation. We observe that for large abstractions, our algorithm can converge to a solution faster. However, the heuristics-based refinement technique presented in
[
13
]
performs well in practice, yielding smaller abstract state sets.

Currently, verification dominates runtime, as it relies on SMT solving and scales poorly with system dimension, while learning remains fast. Computation time is also sensitive to clustering quality, since High Level Loop iterations are expensive. Future work will focus on more efficient refinement schemes and tighter coordination between the High and Low Level Loops, aiming to combine the efficiency of
[
13
]
with our graph-based parallelism. Finally, we plan to extend the approach to nondeterministic multi-resolution abstractions, broadening applicability to systems with unstable equilibria or topological attractors.

\hat{x}_{0}\doteq x^{*}
; clearly
\hat{x}_{0}
is its own predecessor, that is
0\in g^{-1}(0)
. As we have argued in (
37
), the lower bound elicited by the abstract states
\{\hat{x}_{j}\in\hat{X}:j\neq 0\in g^{-1}(0)\}
on
\hat{\epsilon}_{0}
can be made arbitrarily small following the same reasoning. Finally, by asymptotic stability,
f
admits a Lyapunov function
V:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Let
\beta=\min_{d(x,x^{*})=\epsilon(\hat{x}_{0})}V(x)
.Then the set
\Omega_{\beta}=\{x\in X:V(x)\leq\beta\}
is positively invariant. Setting
R(\hat{x}_{0})=\Omega_{\beta}
satisfies the condition. Note that, in contrast with the abstract states in
\mathcal{M}
, the concrete states related to the equilibrium
x^{*}
,
R(\hat{x}_{0})
, are not necessarily in the form

\{x\in X:d(x,x^{*})\leq\hat{\epsilon}_{0}\}
, since
\Omega_{\beta}
is a sub-level set of a Lyapunov function.
