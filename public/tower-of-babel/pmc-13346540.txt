A study on question answering in the ceramics domain using large language models with retrieved triples and generated textual knowledge prompts
Authors: Qixian Zhang, Fubao He, Kaihua Hu, Juan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The propensity of Large Language Models (LLMs) to hallucinate poses a significant challenge in specialized domains with fragmented knowledge, such as ceramic question answering. A critical yet underexplored factor is how the format of retrieved knowledge—structured versus unstructured—affects model accuracy. In this study, we develop a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework for the Chinese ceramic domain. A domain-specific knowledge graph was constructed from heterogeneous sources, and a BERT-based module was employed for hop prediction and path reasoning. The retrieved subgraphs were provided to LLMs either as structured triples or converted textual descriptions. We evaluate four LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, ChatGLM2-6B, LLaMA-2-7B) on a Chinese ceramic QA benchmark. Across all models, structured triples yield higher accuracy, with ChatGLM2-6B achieving the best performance (97.24%). Smaller models benefit more from structured representations, while larger models process textual descriptions more effectively. These results highlight the importance of knowledge representation for reducing hallucination in domain-specific QA. However, as all experiments are conducted on Chinese datasets within a single domain, the generalizability of our findings across languages and other vertical domains remains an open question. We discuss this limitation and outline directions for future multilingual and cross-domain validation.

Introduction

Knowledge-intensive question answering (QA) in specialized domains poses substantial challenges for large language models (LLMs). In ceramic research, domain knowledge is highly fragmented and heterogeneous, spanning academic literature, archaeological reports, experimental records, museum archives, and industrial documentation. This dispersion not only complicates systematic knowledge integration but also increases the likelihood of factual inconsistency when models generate answers without reliable grounding. As a result, improving the accuracy and reliability of QA systems in the ceramic domain remains a critical and unresolved problem.

Large language models pretrained on massive open-domain corpora have demonstrated impressive performance across a wide range of natural language understanding and generation tasks1,2. However, their effectiveness in specialized, low-resource domains such as ceramic engineering and cultural ceramics is limited. A key obstacle is hallucination, whereby models produce fluent yet factually incorrect or unsupported statements3. In domains where factual precision is essential, such hallucinations severely undermine the trustworthiness of LLM-based systems.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has been proposed as an effective strategy to mitigate hallucination by grounding model responses in external knowledge sources4. By retrieving relevant documents or knowledge snippets at inference time, RAG-based frameworks have shown promising results in domains such as biomedical question answering5, intelligence analysis6, and enterprise knowledge management7. In parallel, knowledge graphs (KGs) provide a structured semantic representation of entities and relations, enabling the integration of heterogeneous data sources and supporting multi-hop reasoning through explicit relational modeling8. Recent studies have explored the synergy between LLMs and knowledge graphs, demonstrating improvements in knowledge grounding and reasoning robustness9,10.

Despite these advances, the application of RAG and knowledge graph–enhanced QA in the ceramic domain remains limited. Existing ceramic-oriented systems primarily focus on knowledge extraction and organization rather than systematically addressing reasoning reliability or hallucination control. For example, Nie et al.11 developed a ceramic QA system based on named entity recognition and relation extraction to construct a domain knowledge graph. While effective for knowledge aggregation, such approaches do not explicitly examine how retrieved knowledge is represented to the language model, nor how representation choices influence model behavior.

More broadly, recent work suggests that the format in which external knowledge is provided to language models can significantly affect downstream QA performance12–14. Knowledge can be represented either as unstructured natural language text or as structured forms such as subject–relation–object triples. Structured representations offer explicit relational semantics and reduced linguistic ambiguity, whereas textual descriptions provide richer contextual information but require additional parsing and interpretation. However, systematic investigations into how different knowledge representation formats impact hallucination rates and reasoning reliability—particularly in specialized and fragmented domains—are still scarce.

This gap raises a fundamental research question: how does the representation format of retrieved knowledge—structured triples versus unstructured text—affect the effectiveness and reliability of LLMs in ceramic-domain question answering? Addressing this question is essential not only for ceramic research, but also for other domains characterized by fragmented knowledge and limited training resources.

To answer this question, we propose a retrieval-augmented QA framework that integrates a ceramic knowledge graph with multiple large language models. Under controlled experimental settings, we systematically compare structured triple-based knowledge prompts and textual knowledge representations across LLMs of different scales, including GPT-3.5, GPT-4, ChatGLM2-6B, and LLaMA-2-7B. Our evaluation focuses on both answer accuracy and hallucination behavior, providing a comprehensive assessment of representation effects.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

Domain-specific resource: We construct a ceramic knowledge graph that integrates fragmented cultural and technical knowledge into a unified structured semantic framework.

Representation-focused evaluation: We conduct a systematic comparison between structured and textual knowledge representations within a RAG framework across multiple LLMs.

Reliability insights: We provide empirical evidence that structured knowledge representations significantly reduce hallucination and are particularly effective for smaller models, offering insights applicable to other fragmented domains such as archaeology, cultural heritage, and materials science.

Related work

Although LLMs have been trained on massive corpora, they still face challenges in practice, including rapidly evolving knowledge, limited data in vertical domains, and the high cost of full-parameter updates. RAG15 was introduced in 2020 as an effective solution to these problems by grounding model outputs in external factual knowledge. Compared with traditional fine-tuning, RAG offers better interpretability, flexibility, and cost-effectiveness. Since then, three paradigms have been developed: plain RAG16, advanced RAG, and modular RAG17. While plain RAG defines the basic retrieval–generation pipeline, advanced RAG enhances retrieval quality through pre-retrieval optimization (e.g., ranking, filtering, or complex retrieval strategies). Modular RAG further decomposes the process into independent components to improve extensibility and adaptability.

Prompt engineering has evolved from simple heuristic instructions into a systematic methodology for optimizing the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs). Recent comprehensive reviews highlight its potential as a core mechanism to unlock the latent reasoning capabilities of pre-trained models18,19. Unlike early static templates, contemporary prompt design is treated as a critical variable influencing the consistency and reliability of model outputs20.

Methodological rigor in prompt design is essential for ensuring effective interaction with AI models, particularly in complex reasoning tasks21. Studies have shown that prompt variability significantly impacts performance across diverse domains, such as energy cost optimization and social bias identification. In domain-specific contexts, evidence-based guidelines suggest that structured prompts can mitigate the inherent instability of LLMs, providing a more reliable framework for knowledge-intensive tasks like ceramic question answering22.

In parallel, prompt learning23 has gained attention as a lightweight alternative to fine-tuning. By designing task-specific prompts, LLMs can adapt to new tasks without retraining. Approaches such as cloze-style prompting24 and prefix-tuning25 extend this idea and have been widely applied in classification and generation tasks. In the context of QA, prompt learning and retrieval augmentation are often combined, with researchers exploring different strategies for injecting knowledge into prompts26,27. Some studies28 linearize knowledge graph triples into paragraphs for retrieval via Best Match 25(BM25)29 or Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR)30, while others employ predefined templates to convert triples into natural language text31. Lü et al.32 further evaluated RAG-based methods across multiple tasks, including summarization, text continuation, hallucination correction, and question answering.

Recent advancements continue to refine these approaches. In their 2024 work, Sairaj and Balasundaram33 addressed tail entity hallucination issues in T5-based question generation through dependency edge prediction. Building on this foundation, they further proposed an integrated learning framework combining ensemble methods with RAG to reduce redundant question topics in automatically generated exam questions34. These studies demonstrate the ongoing optimization of RAG-prompt learning pipelines for specific application scenarios.

With the rapid development of open-source models such as GPT, BERT, T5, LLaMA, and ChatGLM, LLMs now provide strong language generation capabilities and flexible integration with retrieval-based methods. In this study, we leveraged these advancements to build a ceramic-domain QA framework, combining retrieval-augmented knowledge with prompt-based learning. Our work differs from prior studies by systematically comparing structured and unstructured knowledge representations, thereby providing new insights into how knowledge format influences QA performance in a highly specialized domain.

Building upon these foundations, our study leverages open-source LLMs (e.g., LLaMA, ChatGLM) to construct a ceramic-domain QA framework. As illustrated in Table 1, our work is distinguished by its systematic, empirical comparison of structured (knowledge triples) versus unstructured (textual paragraphs) knowledge representations within a RAG-prompt learning pipeline. This focus provides novel insights into how knowledge format impacts QA performance in a highly specialized, vertical domain.

Methods

The framework of this paper mainly consists of information retrieval and responses generated by integrating text conversion. This study draws on the integrated learning approach proposed by Sairaj et al. (Ref.34), but introduces innovations at the knowledge representation level, particularly focusing on systematic comparison between structured and unstructured knowledge. Stepwise path relationship prediction with hop-based guidance

For a query q and its predicted hop count h, the model sequentially predicts h single-hop relations. Each step is a multi-class classification task. The prediction at step t is conditioned on the entire history of previous relations, which are appended to the original query as a prompt. Given the reasoning paths M, we will sort the reasoning path scores in descending order and reasonably sample the top-M highest-scoring paths, which will be used as edge knowledge to enhance the question answering capability of the LLMs.

Knowledge-graph-to-text generation task

For locally deployed models, this study devised a prompt-based training approach. The predicted relational paths are first used to construct a task-relevant knowledge subgraph closely aligned with the input question. Then the training is guided using prompts by providing the model with a structured template: “Please convert the given knowledge graph {} into natural language statements” during the training process, where placeholder {} will dynamically populate the constructed subgraphs. Eventually the model learns to convert structured KG triples (e.g., Celadon vase, representative color, blue flowers on white ceramic body) into fluent textual descriptions (e.g., “The Celadon vase features blue floral motifs on a white ceramic body, with blue being its representative color.”). Training optimizes semantic accuracy and linguistic coherence.

Knowledge-prompted enhanced QA task

To ensure methodological rigor, we designed a unified structured prompt template based on evidence-based guidelines for domain-specific reliability. The prompt follows a “Context-Task-Constraint” framework to minimize ambiguity. Specifically, the template “Please answer the question based on the relevant triples or textual knowledge: {knowledge} Question: {Q}, Answers:” was selected to isolate the knowledge representation as the primary variable.

This design choice is motivated by the need to provide clear semantic boundaries for the model, especially in the fragmented ceramic domain where linguistic variability often leads to reasoning failures. By providing knowledge in a standardized format, we reduce the “dual burden” of simultaneous textual parsing and knowledge extraction, allowing the model to focus its computational resources on factual accuracy.

Quality assessment of generated textual knowledge

To address the potential concern that the performance difference between structured and textual prompts may be influenced by the quality of triple-to-text generation, we conducted a lightweight quality assessment of the generated textual knowledge. We randomly sampled 100 triple–text pairs and performed human evaluation along three dimensions: faithfulness, completeness, and linguistic fluency, using a 5-point Likert scale.

The evaluation results indicate that the generated texts are highly faithful to the source triples and linguistically fluent, suggesting that the quality of textual knowledge is not a major limiting factor in downstream QA performance.

Experiment

This section presents the datasets used in our study. We describe the data sources, collection process, as well as the key characteristics of the ceramic-domain knowledge involved. These details provide the necessary context for understanding how the models are trained and evaluated in the subsequent sections.

Dataset

The primary data source for this study is the Ceramic Culture section of the Encyclopedia of China, which provides authoritative domain-specific entries and descriptions. To broaden coverage, we further collected supplementary materials from WeChat public accounts and mainstream search engines. The construction process consisted of four steps: (1) Entry–description extraction: each ceramic-related entry and its corresponding descriptive text were collected. (2) Triple generation and filtering: a large language model was employed to extract candidate knowledge triples (subject, relation, object) from the descriptive texts, which were then manually reviewed to ensure factual correctness and remove noise. (3) QA pair generation: based on the filtered triples and descriptive texts, diverse QA pairs were generated using a large language model, with additional pairs gathered from web resources. (4) Final dataset composition: the resulting dataset contains 11,980 valid instances, each consisting of a question, its corresponding answer, and the associated knowledge triples. Specifically, 8,411 instances were used for training, 2,032 for testing, and 1,537 for validation.

The Zhejiang Question Answering (ZJQA) dataset, released by Zhejiang Lab (Ref. 9), contains 20,491 Chinese Knowledge Graph QA questions. It is divided into 14,999 training, 2,147 development, and 3,345 test questions. The queries are centered on the sports domain and involve either single-hop or two-hop reasoning.

Large language model

The information retrieval module of this paper has two main tasks: hop relation prediction and relation path prediction. Both of these tasks are fine-tuned with BERT for classification tasks. In the Generate Answer module, it mainly includes two tasks: triple-to-text conversion and QA based on big model prompts. For the triple to text conversion task this paper uses LLaMA-2-7B model, and for the QA task this paper uses LLaMA-2-7B, GPT, and ChatGLM2-6B for QA. In addition, the Chinese versions of the models are used in all this paper.

BERT is a PLM based on the Transformer architecture proposed by Google in 2018. Because BERT can efficiently capture the semantic information of text through bidirectional context modeling and pre-training-fine-tuning mode, it is widely used in tasks such as text classification prediction.

Chinese LLaMA-2-7B performs large-scale pre-training and instruction fine-tuning for Chinese language characteristics, which maintains high performance while requiring less hardware resources and better supports Chinese language comprehension and generation tasks. In this paper, we adopt LLaMA-2-7B as a ceramic triple-to-text generation model and apply the model to the prompt-based answering task as well. Since LLaMA-2-7B has been open-sourced, in this study the model is fine-tuned to make it satisfy the QA needs of the ceramic domain.

ChatGLM2-6B is an efficient and open-source bilingual dialog LLMs, which is used as a QA model in this paper because of its advantages of low resource requirements and support for long context comprehension capabilities especially in Chinese scenarios.

GPT, as one of the current state-of-the-art generative models, has strong context understanding and generative capabilities because it has been pre-trained on large-scale corpora and human annotated data. We access and use it as a QA model through an API interface.

The experimental results in Table 3 demonstrate the impact of structured and textual knowledge prompts on four LLMs in the ceramic QA task. Across all models, structured triples lead to substantial performance improvements compared with knowledge-free baselines. For example, GPT-4 achieves 95.08% accuracy with structured prompts, compared to only 22.1% without knowledge. The best overall performance is obtained by ChatGLM2-6B, which reaches 97.24% accuracy when guided by structured triples.

In addition, human evaluation results showed that the generated textual knowledge achieved average score of 4.6 in faithfulness, 4.4 in completeness, and 4.7 in fluency, indicating overall high-quality text generation.

However, when structured knowledge is converted into natural language text, performance consistently declines. The drop is most pronounced for LLaMA-2-7B (from 90.94% to 79.28%), suggesting that mid-sized models are more sensitive to the additional semantic burden introduced by textual prompts. In contrast, larger models such as GPT-4 show relatively smaller performance degradation, indicating stronger generalization to free-text input.

Figure 4 visually illustrates this performance degradation when converting triples to text. The bar chart clearly shows the accuracy gap between structured and textual prompts across all models, with the largest drop observed in LLaMA-2-7B (11.66% points) and the smallest in ChatGLM2-6B (2.51% points). This visualization enhances the comprehension of how knowledge representation format affects model performance, particularly for resource-constrained models.

This performance degradation can be attributed to several factors. First, textual descriptions often introduce linguistic variability and peripheral details that can distract the model from core factual relationships. Second, smaller models like LLaMA-2-7B struggle with the increased semantic complexity of free-text input, as they must simultaneously parse linguistic structures and extract relevant knowledge. The structured triple format, by contrast, provides a direct and unambiguous representation of relationships, thereby reducing cognitive load and enabling more reliable reasoning, particularly for domain-specific tasks requiring precision.

Cross-dataset validation

Specifically, the original question was used as a query to retrieve the most relevant text fragments from the knowledge base. The retrieved results were directly filled into the prompt template without further reasoning or integration.

Error analysis

Despite the overall high accuracy, we conducted an error analysis on the test set to better understand the limitations associated with different knowledge representation formats. Rather than probing internal model mechanisms, our analysis focuses on behavior-level error patterns. The results reveal systematic differences between structured and textual knowledge prompts, which help explain the observed performance gap, particularly for smaller language models.

Observations on the dual burden of textual parsing

A recurring challenge observed in our experiments with free-text inputs is what we refer to as the “dual burden” hypothesis for textual parsing. This hypothesis posits that when processing textual prompts, smaller models (e.g., LLaMA-2-7B) must first parse heterogeneous linguistic expressions—such as long sentences, descriptive clauses, and varied syntactic patterns—and then identify and extract task-relevant knowledge for reasoning. While our analysis does not probe internal model mechanisms, the following observable patterns support this behavioral-level interpretation:

Typical error categories

Our analysis identifies three recurring error categories:

Category 1: peripheral detail distraction

Example Case: For the question “What is the characteristic color of Jun porcelain? “Textual Prompt: “Jun porcelain, a traditional Chinese ceramic, is known for its unique glaze colors. Its firing process is complex, and the glaze often shows opalescent effects, with purple splashes and lavender-blue as common hues.”

Model Error: LLaMA-2-7B incorrectly identifies “opalescent” or “purple splashes” as the answer.

Interpretation: The model becomes distracted by peripheral details (firing process, opalescent effects) and fails to identify the core characteristic among multiple color descriptions.

Category 2: linguistic variability confusion

Example Case: Queries involving nuanced temporal or quantitative relationships.

Textual Prompt: Descriptions containing vague temporal phrases like “during the early period,” “commonly used in,” or qualitative modifiers like “primarily,” “mostly,” “often”.

Model Error: Inconsistent interpretation of temporal sequences or quantitative relationships.

Interpretation: These errors are consistent with difficulties in handling semantic ambiguity and contextual variability in natural language descriptions, particularly for smaller models.

Category 3: multi-hop reasoning failure

Example Case: Questions requiring inference across multiple facts.

Textual Prompt: Extended paragraphs containing related but distributed information.

Model Error: Inability to connect disparate facts within long textual contexts.

Interpretation: These failures are consistent with challenges in maintaining and integrating distributed information across long textual contexts.

Comparative success with structured knowledge

In direct contrast to the error patterns observed with textual prompts, structured triple representations consistently demonstrate superior performance:

Immediate Precision: When presented with the triple “(Jun porcelain, characteristic color, lavender-blue)”, models directly output the correct answer without parsing overhead.

Reduced Parsing Complexity: The standardized format eliminates linguistic variability, reducing the need for linguistic interpretation and enabling models to focus more directly on answer selection.

Explicit Relationship Modeling: Triples make relational structures explicit, avoiding the inference challenges posed by implicit relationships in natural language.

Hallucination rate analysis

To evaluate the effect of knowledge triples in constraining the generation process of large language models, we used ChatGLM2-6B as the base model and compared the impact of different knowledge representation methods on hallucination rates based on manual evaluation of 200 test samples. The experimental results show:

Text-based knowledge representation: When using raw text as context, the model exhibited a hallucination rate of 5.22% observed in the 200 test samples, with errors primarily manifesting as fact distortion and information addition.

Triple-based knowledge representation: When structured triples were used as the knowledge source, the hallucination rate significantly decreased to 0.74%, representing an 85.8% relative reduction.

This significant improvement provides quantitative evidence that structured triples impose clearer knowledge boundaries for ChatGLM2-6B, helping to constrain generation and reduce factual errors associated with free interpretation.

Discussion on Hallucination Mitigation:

While our approach focuses on retrieval-augmented grounding to constrain the model, this finding complements existing research on hallucination detection through specialized prompt engineering35. Unlike purely prompt-based detection methods that rely on the model’s internal self-consistency—often achieved through multi-step reasoning prompts—our framework reduces “fact distortion” and “information addition” by providing a direct, unambiguous representation of relationships. This suggests that structured knowledge prompting, as a source-side constraint, is an effective mitigation strategy that enhances the reliability of AI-generated content in the ceramic domain, beyond mere performance gains.

This substantial reduction in hallucination rate provides strong quantitative evidence that structured triples impose clearer informational boundaries than free-text descriptions. Crucially, these results are not attributable to artifacts in text generation quality. As noted in our controlled experiment with a template-based textual baseline, even high-fidelity, deterministic textual representations underperform structured triples. Therefore, the observed performance gap—encompassing both higher overall error rates and specifically higher hallucination rates for textual prompts—likely stems from the inherent properties of the representation formats. Combined with the systematic error patterns observed in Sect. 4.4.4 (e.g., peripheral distraction, linguistic confusion), these findings collectively support the interpretation that unstructured textual inputs introduce additional interpretive variability and increased processing demands, a challenge that disproportionately affects smaller models with limited capacity for simultaneous parsing and reasoning.

Conclusion

This study presents a retrieval-augmented QA framework for the ceramic domain that integrates a domain-specific knowledge graph with large language models. Through hop prediction and path reasoning for knowledge retrieval, and a systematic comparison of structured triples versus textual representations, we provide new insights into how knowledge representation shapes LLM performance in domain-specific QA tasks.

Our results show that structured triples substantially enhance answer accuracy across multiple models, with ChatGLM2-6B achieving the highest accuracy of 97.24%. Converting triples into natural language text results in a noticeable performance decline, particularly for smaller models. These findings underscore two key contributions:

structured knowledge is highly effective in mitigating hallucination in low-resource settings, and.knowledge representation significantly influences reasoning quality in specialized domains.

Practically, the proposed framework offers a reliable solution for building intelligent QA systems for ceramic culture, supporting applications in digital museums, cultural heritage preservation, and domain-specific knowledge services. Theoretically, our findings highlight the importance of structured representations in improving LLM robustness in knowledge-intensive but fragmented domains.

Nonetheless, limitations remain. The dataset is relatively small and focused primarily on ceramic culture, and the current evaluation is limited to text-based QA. Future work will involve expanding dataset coverage, integrating multimodal information (e.g., ceramic images), and exploring cross-domain transferability to related fields such as archaeology and materials science.

Limitations and future work

Although this study provides meaningful insights into the impact of knowledge representations on domain-specific QA, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the dataset is limited in both scale and scope, focusing mainly on ceramic culture extracted from encyclopedias, online articles, and cultural resources. Consequently, the knowledge coverage may not fully represent the diversity of ceramic studies or industrial applications.

Second, the experiments focus solely on text-based QA tasks and do not incorporate multimodal elements such as images or videos, which are highly relevant to ceramic artifact analysis.

Third, the framework was only evaluated within the ceramic domain. The generalizability of our findings to other fields with fragmented knowledge structures remains to be validated.
