Salvage STA–MMA anastomosis for intraoperative middle meningeal artery in moyamoya disease: a technical note
Authors: Jinghui Lin, Jiancong Zheng, Ben Chat Fong Ng, Lin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn numerous cases of Moyamoya disease (MMD), the middle meningeal artery (MMA) develops as a spontaneous collateral prior to surgery, providing an extracranial–intracranial compensatory pathway. However, the MMA is vulnerable to iatrogenic injury during surgery. There is a lack of literature regarding managing iatrogenic injuries to the MMA which has collateral supply to the cerebral hemisphere.Case descriptionWe report a case of a 61-year-old woman with MMD with collateral flows from the MMA. During a right-sided direct revascularization surgery, the MMA was inadvertently ruptured. After the bleeding is controlled, a salvage bypass was created by anastomosing the frontal branch of the superficial temporal artery (STA) end-to-end to the MMA, while the parietal branch of STA branch was anastomosed to a cortical middle cerebral artery (MCA) branch in an end-to-side fashion. Intraoperative indocyanine green video angiography (ICG-VA) confirmed good patency of the bypasses. Follow-up cerebral computed tomography angiography (CTA) confirmed the patency of the STA-MMA and STA-MCA anastomosis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed no cerebral infarction.ConclusionA remedial STA–MMA anastomosis can be a technically feasible salvage strategy when the MMA with collateral flow to the brain is injured intraoperatively. This approach preserves cortical collateral flow and expands the surgical options for complex revascularization cases.Keywords: Moyamoya disease, Bypass surgery, Middle meningeal artery, Vascular injury, Salvage technique

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a chronic occlusive cerebrovascular disorder involving progressive narrowing of the terminal internal carotid arteries and formation of abnormal basal collaterals [9]. The surgical management of MMD has evolved significantly, with revascularization surgery now established as the mainstay of treatment [1, 7, 11]. Since Houkin et al. [6] described the combination of STA–MCA bypass and encephalo-duro-arterio synangiosis (EDAS), now it has been widely adopted as a hybrid revascularization strategy.

The middle meningeal artery (MMA) of patients with Moyamoya disease can develop collateral blood supplies to the brain, especially the anterior frontal branch supplying the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) territory [4]. Because the MMA is fragile and vulnerable to iatrogenic injury, its preservation is increasingly regarded as essential during revascularization procedures [8, 10]. Once the MMA is injured, ischemia in the frontal lobe can readily occur. However, at present, there are no case reports concerning repairing the injured MMA during surgery.

In this case report, we present the first reported case of a remedial STA-MMA anastomosis performed to salvage an intraoperatively injured MMA during revascularization surgery for MMD.

Illustrative case

A 61-year-old woman presented with intermittent dizziness for three years. She then developed several episodes of worsening left limb weakness for over one month. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed at a local hospital revealed multiple small ischemic lesions in the deep white matter of the bilateral frontal and parietal lobes. The patient was subsequently transferred to our hospital for further evaluation and management. To confirm the diagnosis of the patient, a cerebral computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed, which showed a stenosis at the terminal right internal carotid artery while the M1 segment of right middle cerebral artery was occluded. On the other hand, the left ICA and the left MCA M1 segment were also stenotic. A digital subtraction angiogram (DSA) was then performed confirming the CTA findings. It also showed compensatory collateral supplies from the MMA to the cerebral cortex through the falx cerebri (Fig. 1). Based on these imaging results, the patient was diagnosed to have MMD. As such, a STA-MCA anastomosis combined with EDAS was performed.

The patient was positioned supine with the head rotated approximately 60° to the left.A 15-cm question mark incision was made over the right frontotemporal region. The temporalis muscle was dissected and reflected anteriorly, with careful preservation of the STA. During craniotomy, the anterior branch of MMA was injured by milling cutter at the dural-bony interface, where the vessel was tightly embedded in a bony groove. Temporary hemostasis was immediately achieved using an aneurysm clip. After inspection of the injured segment, direct primary repair was considered but was not deemed optimal because the vessel was short due to trimming and it could not be sufficiently mobilized for a simple tension-free repair after hemostatic control. Given the preoperative DSA evidence that the MMA contributed transdural collateral flow to the cerebral hemisphere, we chose to preserve this collateral pathway by performing a salvage end-to-end anastomosis between the frontal branch of the STA and the MMA (Fig. 2). After that, intraoperative indocyanine green video angiography (ICG-VA) and infrared confirmed good patency of the bypass.Then, an end-to-side anastomosis was performed between the parietal branch of the STA and a M4 branch (Fig. 3), resulting in a “Y”-shaped configuration. The patency of the STA-MCA bypass anastomosis was also confirmed using intraoperative ICG. After the hemostasis, and the dura was closed while avoiding the compression of the bypasses. The bone flap was replaced, and the donor artery was laid without tension through a subcutaneous tunnel. Finally, the skin was closed in layers. No complications such as hyperperfusion or ischemia occurred afterwards. On postoperative day three, CTA showed that both bypasses remained patent, and MRI revealed no new cerebral infarction. At the six-month follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of her symptoms. Follow-up DSA demonstrated patency of both bypasses and improved cortical perfusion in the right hemisphere (Fig. 4). The patient had an uneventful recovery.

Discussion

Recent research has increasingly recognized the MMA as a critical collateral channel in the anterior cerebral circulation of patients with MMD existed. Studies have demonstrated that the anterior branch of the MMA is fragile [8, 10, 12]. Therefore, iatrogenic injury to the MMA during craniotomy is not uncommon, especially for junior residents, and numerous strategies have been proposed to preserve it during surgeries. For example, Tanabe et al. reported that intraoperative ICG-VA is a safe and valuable technique for preserving the anterior branch of the MMA during craniotomy for MMD [10]. However, there is a lack of literature concerning salvage techniques for MMA injury. In the present case, the injury occurred during the craniotomy stage, underscoring the importance of careful handling around the pterional-sphenoidal region, where the frontal branch of the MMA may course within a bony canal or deep groove [4]. Preoperative or intraoperative localization of the MMA may help reduce this risk. In addition, although primary direct repair may theoretically be possible in selected cases, it may not be feasible when the injured segment is short, or difficult to anastomose for a tension-free reconstruction after hemostatic control. The frontal STA branch was readily available, size-matched, and allowed preservation of the planned parietal STA–MCA bypass, making it the most practical rescue option in our case.

If the frontal branch of the STA had not been available or suitable, several alternative strategies could have been considered. These include primary MMA repair when technically feasible, the use of another extracranial donor artery such as the occipital artery or posterior auricular artery, or the use of an interposition graft in selected cases. Indirect revascularization techniques, including dural inversion or other indirect bypass procedures, might also serve as salvage options [2, 3, 5]. However, compared with using of the frontal STA branch, these approaches would be the meaning of more surgical procedures, additional donor-site dissection and less likely to provide immediate restoration of flow to the collateralized vessel.

We propose the technique of STA-MMA anastomosis as a salvage technique for iatrogenic MMA injury for MMA with collateral flow to the anterior circulation. The rationale behind this technique lies in the fact that the MMA often develops an important collateral channel from the external carotid system to the intracranial circulation in MMD. Previous studies have demonstrated that the MMA contributes significantly to dural and leptomeningeal collateral formation in MMD, and its enlargement correlates with favorable postoperative collateral development [4].

Compared to the traditional STA–MCA anastomosis, the STA–MMA bypass offers several technical advantages in salvage scenarios. First, the STA–MMA bypass allowed preservation of the cerebral perfusion supplied by the damaged MMA after an iatrogenic injuryallowing it to continue to supply the ischemic cerebral hemisphere during and after the surgery. We believe this remedial approach has considerable clinical significance. Furthermore, the MMA’s pressure gradient from the external carotid system to the intracranial circulation can facilitate collateral recruitment through existing anastomotic networks between the dura mater and the cortical surface.

Nevertheless, several technical challenges and limitations should be considered. The MMA wall is thinner and more fragile than that of the cortical arteries, which requires meticulous microsurgical handling to prevent intraoperative vessel wall injury. To our knowledge, only a few reports have described the anastomosis the MMA in MMD, but as a donor vessel [12]. Also, Intraoperative ICG demonstrated that STA–MMA bypass does not affect the combination of STA-MCA bypass and EDAS, because another branch of STA was available.

Our case reports the feasibility of STA–MMA bypass as a salvage option in iatrogenic MMA injury.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that a salvage STA–MMA anastomosis can effectively restore the cortical perfusion when the MMA, a key collateral channel in MMD, is injured intraoperatively. By incorporating the MMA as a recipient artery, the original collateral network can be preserved. Although technically demanding, this salvage technique is feasible and further clinical experience is required to establish its long-term efficacy and indications.
