The treatment intensity paradox in stable coronary artery disease: When upstream escalation depletes surgical durability
Authors: Jeffrey H Shuhaiber
Published: 2026

Escalation-first care depletes anatomic capital, raises risk, and constrains later CABG.

Central Message

In stable coronary disease, therapeutic sequencing determines durability. Premature escalation can spend anatomic capital, shift risk to late referral, and convert CABG to salvage revascularization.

Stable ischemic heart disease is one of the most evidence-rich areas of cardiovascular medicine, yet the translation of evidence into practice remains uneven. Trials such as the Clinical Outcomes Utilizing Revascularization and Aggressive Drug Evaluation (COURAGE) , ISCHEMIA, Objective Randomised Blinded Investigation with optimal medical Therapy of Angioplasty in stable angina (ORBITA), and ORBITA-2 collectively show that an initial invasive strategy does not uniformly reduce death or myocardial infarction compared with guideline-directed medical therapy in broad stable populations, although selected patients may experience symptom relief.1, 2, 3, 4 These data, however, largely reflect percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)-dominant invasive pathways and should not be misread as direct evidence against early coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in patients who are anatomically suitable. That distinction matters because surgeons increasingly encounter the downstream consequence of iterative escalation: patients referred later, after multiple procedures, with more compromised coronary anatomy and fewer durable reconstructive options.

The treatment intensity paradox is our term for this sequence-dependent mismatch between short-term procedural success and long-term durability. In stable coronary disease, upstream treatment choices can spend what may be called anatomic capital: graftable distal targets, intact branch points, preserved runoff, and the possibility of complete arterial reconstruction. A patient may feel better after a technically successful PCI while simultaneously moving onto a less-durable trajectory. That possibility is often invisible to standard trial end points but highly visible to the surgeon who meets the patient later in the course of their disease.

PCI and CABG are Different Therapies

PCI and CABG are not equivalent therapies delivered through different access routes. PCI is usually a focal lesion treatment. CABG is a territorial and reconstructive operation that depends on the quality of distal targets, the integrity of side branches, the feasibility of complete revascularization, and the physiologic reserve of the patient. PCI may solve an immediate problem without preserving future graftability; CABG derives much of its value from bypassing current lesions while also protecting against future proximal disease progression. Conflating the 2 runs the risk of confusing immediate technical success with long-horizon durability.

Defining Stable Disease for Surgeons

Defining “stable” for surgeons requires more than the absence of infarction. Stability is a clinical state integrated with anatomy, ischemic burden, ventricular function, and revascularization reserve. Angiography still matters, but physiologic assessment using fractional flow reserve or instantaneous wave-free ratio helps distinguish lesions that look severe from those that are truly flow-limiting.5,6 For the surgeon, stability also includes substrate: the number of diseased vessels, target quality, diffuseness of disease, and whether complete durable revascularization remains possible. In ischemic cardiomyopathy, CABG can provide durable survival benefit, underscoring that “stable” does not mean “low stakes” when ventricular reserve is already limited.7

What ISCHEMIA Did—and Did Not—Answer for Surgery

Interpretation of the results from the ISCHEMIA deserves precision. As several surgical commentaries and multisocietal critiques have emphasized, in the ISCHEMIA trial investigators compared 2 initial strategies rather than CABG versus medical therapy, and only a minority of patients in the invasive arm underwent bypass surgery.8,9 The trial therefore informs the timing of an invasive strategy in stable disease, but it does not eliminate the question of when surgical reconstruction should enter the pathway for patients with complex multivessel anatomy, diabetes, ventricular dysfunction, or anatomy in which durability matters more than momentary convenience.

Anatomic Capital Depletion

The coronary tree is not an infinite resource. It contains anatomic capital. Long stented segments can eliminate landing zones, side branches may be jailed or compromised, disease may be pushed distally, and prolonged dual-antiplatelet therapy may delay operative timing. Observational studies and meta-analyses examining previous PCI before CABG have shown heterogeneous but clinically plausible signals of increased early risk and reduced revascularization flexibility, particularly as PCI burden increases.10, 11, 12, 13 The surgeon's most meaningful downstream outcomes are often not coded as formal outcomes at all: poor or absent distal targets, incomplete revascularization, loss of conduit strategy, and operations performed under less-favorable biologic conditions. The Late Operative Phenotype and Risk Migration

Delayed or iterative escalation may also change the operative phenotype itself. The patient who is referred later for CABG is more likely to have lower ejection fraction, greater symptom burden, more diffuse disease, and a greater predicted operative risk. Some patients arrive with coronary anatomy so advanced that adjunctive reconstructive maneuvers such as coronary endarterectomy are required to obtain any meaningful runoff. National database analyses suggest that coronary endarterectomy identifies a rare but greater-risk surgical subset with increased perioperative morbidity and mortality, serving as a marker of end-stage coronary substrate rather than an attractive destination of routine sequencing.14 Late referral may also narrow the practical opportunity for multiarterial grafting and complete arterial planning, which further erodes long-term durability.

As PCI expands into progressively complex anatomy, the residual CABG population becomes enriched with patients least likely to do well: diffuse multivessel disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, frailty, previous intervention, ventricular dysfunction, and nonelective presentation. This is risk migration. CABG is then judged as though it were being offered to the same earlier-stage denominator it once served. Public reporting and institutional comparison become misleading when the case mix has already been reshaped upstream. The challenge of maintaining approximately 1% mortality for isolated CABG becomes more unrealistic if systems continue to push increasingly depleted and sicker patients toward surgery only after less-durable pathways have failed.15

Toward Lifetime Management of Coronary Artery Disease (CAD)

The more useful frame may be lifetime management of CAD. Guideline-directed medical therapy, PCI, and CABG should not be treated as competing tribes but as tools deployed over time with different biological consequences. Early choices affect future options. A durability-centered strategy asks not only, “Can this lesion be treated now?” but “What course preserves the best options for this patient over the next 5 to 10 years?” That is analogous to the emerging lifetime-management paradigm in structural heart disease and better reflects the realities of recurrent CAD.

Why, then, do escalation-first pathways persist? Part of the answer is cultural and structural. Diagnostic angiography and intervention often occur in the same setting, under the same operator, inside a workflow optimized for action. When deliberation is not financed, the system finances action. PCI and CABG are frequently normalized as equivalent early options, even though their anatomic consequences differ fundamentally. What looks efficient from the catheterization laboratory may be costly from the standpoint of coronary stewardship.

The growing role of noninvasive diagnostics may offer an important inflection point. Computed tomography coronary angiography, calcium scoring, and multimodality risk assessment move decision-making upstream, before invasive commitment and before ad hoc intervention gathers procedural momentum.16,17 That shift creates a more realistic opportunity for unbiased triage and earlier heart-team review in patients with complex or potentially surgical anatomy.

A durability-first argument that ignores the socioeconomic pull toward PCI will fail to persuade. PCI usually offers shorter hospital stay and faster return to work, and that matters to patients with employment, caregiving, or insurance pressures. However, the early convenience of PCI can coexist with a downstream pattern of recurrent testing, repeat procedures, prolonged dual-antiplatelet exposure, and fragmented longitudinal care. Lower income has also been associated with reduced adherence to antiplatelet therapy after PCI, a problem that can further complicate later conversion to surgery.18,19

Operational Guardrails

This problem will not be solved by another abstract debate over trial interpretation. It will be solved by operational guardrails. First, stable patients being considered for elective escalation should have documented initiation, optimization, or contraindication of guideline-directed medical therapy. Second, institutions should create early heart-team triggers not only for left main disease but also for diabetes, diffuse multivessel disease, high anatomic complexity, impaired ventricular function, and previous PCI burden.17,20,21 Third, systems should measure the phenomenon they currently normalize: previous PCI burden before CABG, time from diagnostic definition to surgical referral, rates of poor or no target documentation, delay to surgery because of antiplatelet exposure, and the operative risk profile of patients who are referred late to CABG (Table 1).

Beyond Stable Disease: Relevance to Acute Coronary Syndrome

Although this expert opinion focuses on stable disease, the same principle of anatomic stewardship has relevance in acute coronary syndromes. Contemporary guidelines support culprit-lesion treatment with staged management of nonculprit disease when appropriate, rather than reflexive ad hoc multivessel intervention in every patient.20,22 In anatomically complex acute coronary syndrome, preserving future options may still require early surgical input before an irreversible PCI-first pathway is set.

Conclusions

The treatment intensity paradox does not argue that PCI fails or that CABG should dominate all decisions. It argues that therapeutic sequence matters. In stable coronary disease, procedures performed too early or too repeatedly may relieve symptoms while silently spending anatomic capital, raising future operative risk, and converting CABG from strategic reconstruction into constrained salvage. The central question is therefore no longer simply which therapy is superior in isolation but how the sequence of therapies shapes long-term durability. Coronary anatomy is a finite resource, and stewardship of that resource should be part of modern revascularization strategy.
