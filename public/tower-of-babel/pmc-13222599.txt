Evolution of the Healthcare System in Saudi Arabia Over the Last Century: A Historical and Policy Analysis
Authors: Haytham A Sheerah, Shouq M Alzaaqi, Ahmed Arafa, Weam M Banjar, Keyang Liu, Mellissa Withers, Fadi El-Jardali, Pasi Penttinen, Ahmed H Al-Jedai, Duncan Selbie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents a narrative historical review of the development of the Saudi healthcare system from the establishment of the Public Health Directorate in 1925 to the ongoing reforms under Vision 2030. Saudi Arabia’s healthcare journey has included major milestones, including the founding of the Ministry of Health in 1951, national immunization programs, the expansion of hospital infrastructure, and the introduction of primary healthcare services. Each era of national development contributed to shaping a system that today serves a rapidly growing and diverse population. Major reforms under the reigns of King Khalid, King Fahd, and King Abdullah significantly improved healthcare access, quality, and health outcomes. These reforms were supported by evolving governance mechanisms, including centralized administration through the Ministry of Health, national development planning, expanded public financing, and regulatory frameworks guiding healthcare delivery. In the contemporary period, under Vision 2030, healthcare transformation has accelerated through initiatives emphasizing public–private partnerships and sustainable financing. Digital health has expanded through national electronic health records, telemedicine platforms, integrated health information systems, and emerging artificial intelligence applications. The healthcare system has also demonstrated resilience in responding to challenges such as the MERS-CoV and COVID-19 pandemics, as well as the health demands of the Hajj pilgrimage. Despite substantial progress, challenges remain, including regional disparities, rising non-communicable diseases, and workforce sustainability. The Saudi experience offers important policy and leadership lessons for countries pursuing large-scale health system reform.Keywords: healthcare services, public health, ministry of health, vision 2030, health system reform

Introduction

Over the past century, healthcare services in Saudi Arabia have undergone one of the most remarkable transformations in the Middle East. The Kingdom’s healthcare journey reflects a rapid evolution from fragmented, traditional care practices to a modern, institutionalized healthcare system grounded in principles of equity, universal access, and technological innovation.1,2 Understanding how this transformation occurred is important not only for documenting institutional development but also for interpreting the leadership decisions and governance reforms that shaped the trajectory of the Saudi health system. Before the establishment of formal health institutions, healthcare in the Arabian Peninsula was primarily informal and community-based. It relied on traditional healers, herbal medicine, and spiritual practices.3 The absence of organized public health institutions contributed to a high burden of infectious diseases, poor maternal and child health outcomes, and limited access to care.2 The creation of the Public Health Directorate in Mecca in 1925 marked a turning point. It represented the earliest efforts to institutionalize public health services, particularly in response to the needs of the Hajj pilgrimage and urbanizing populations.4 The establishment of the Ministry of Health (MOH) in 1951 provided a centralized mechanism for coordinating healthcare delivery and laid the foundation for a national health system.2

Since then, successive eras of leadership and national development planning have shaped the trajectory of Saudi Arabia’s healthcare sector. From the system-building phase of the 1960s, through waves of expansion in the 1970s and 1980s, to the specialization and modernization of the 1990s and early 2000s, the Kingdom’s health sector has consistently expanded its scope, infrastructure, and human resources. Today, the Saudi healthcare system is a multifaceted structure comprising an extensive network of primary healthcare centers, secondary hospitals, and tertiary referral institutions.5–8 The MOH remains the dominant provider and regulator of healthcare services, but the system also includes significant contributions from military institutions, university hospitals, and the private sector.7,8 In line with the Kingdom’s broader national development strategy, known as Vision 2030, ongoing reforms aim to enhance healthcare quality, efficiency, equity, and sustainability. Key priorities include strengthening service integration, promoting preventive care, developing human resources, advancing digital health, and increasing private sector participation.7,9,10 These developments were not solely the result of economic expansion but also reflected strategic leadership decisions that progressively reshaped governance structures, institutional arrangements, and national priorities in the Saudi health sector.

Despite these achievements, the Saudi healthcare system continues to face notable challenges. These include rising demand for services driven by population growth and demographic aging, the growing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), disparities in access and quality across regions, and the need for sustainable healthcare financing.11,12 Additionally, the shift from an oil-based to a diversified economy requires new approaches to health governance and resource allocation.13

Although several studies have described aspects of healthcare development in Saudi Arabia, fewer analyses have examined the evolution of the health system through an integrated leadership and governance perspective. We, therefore, provide, in this narrative review, a historical analysis of the evolution of healthcare services in Saudi Arabia, tracing major milestones, reforms, and institutional shifts across different historical phases (Table 1). In addition to documenting system expansion, this report highlights how leadership decisions and governance reforms during different political eras influenced the strategic direction and pace of health system development (Table 2). To guide this historical analysis, the evolution of the Saudi healthcare system was interpreted through a simplified health system leadership framework that highlighted five interrelated domains: governance and institutional structures, health workforce development, financing and resource allocation, digital and technological innovation, and strategic system management (Table 3).

Methods

This study is a narrative historical review of the evolution of healthcare services in Saudi Arabia over the past century. Major bibliographic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE and Scopus, were searched using combinations of keywords such as “Saudi Arabia”, “healthcare system”, “health system reform”, and “Vision 2030”. Historical sources were identified from official government publications, Ministry of Health archives, institutional reports, and publicly available historical documents related to healthcare development in Saudi Arabia. Sources were included if they described major milestones in healthcare development, governance structures, workforce expansion, financing mechanisms, infrastructure growth, digital health initiatives, or national reform programs relevant to the evolution of the Saudi health system. Preference was given to peer-reviewed articles, official policy documents, and authoritative institutional reports with clear historical or policy relevance. Sources focusing primarily on clinical outcomes without health system relevance, non-Saudi settings without comparative policy context, duplicate reports, or materials lacking verifiable institutional origin were excluded. Information obtained from these sources was cross-checked with peer-reviewed literature to ensure consistency and accuracy whenever feasible. Relevant data were manually extracted from the identified materials and organized according to key themes, including governance reforms, infrastructure expansion, workforce development, financing mechanisms, and digital health initiatives. Additional indicators were incorporated when relevant to illustrate the scale of health system expansion across different phases. The selection and synthesis of sources were conducted collaboratively by the first and second authors.

The historical phases were defined based on major shifts in national leadership, health policy priorities, institutional reforms, and healthcare system expansion. These include the early public health era (1925 to 1952), the establishment and system-building period (1953 to 1975), the expansion and modernization era (1976 to 2005), the early transformation era (2006 to 2014), and Vision 2030 transformation (2015 to present).

Phases

Early Healthcare Services and the Founding of the Ministry of Health (1925 to 1952)

The formal establishment of healthcare services in Saudi Arabia dates to the early 20th century, following the unification of the Kingdom under King Abdulaziz in 1932. Before this period, healthcare in the Arabian Peninsula was primarily informal. Treatment generally occurred in homes or outdoor settings, utilizing herbal remedies, cauterization, spiritual healing, and basic techniques passed down through generations. The early public health landscape was characterized by a heavy burden of infectious diseases, such as malaria, smallpox, tuberculosis, and trachoma, alongside limited infrastructure for sanitation or disease control.2

The increasing influx of international pilgrims during the annual Hajj season created additional public health challenges. The arrival of large numbers of pilgrims often led to outbreaks of infectious diseases, posing a significant risk to both visitors and the local population. Recognizing these risks, King Abdulaziz established the Public Health Directorate in Mecca in 1925. This was the first governmental health body in the Kingdom. One year later, the Directorate was renamed the Directorate of Public Health and Ambulance Services, with an expanded mandate to provide basic health services, control infectious diseases, and implement sanitation measures, especially during the Hajj season. One of the Directorate’s early successes was the establishment of quarantine stations at major ports of entry in Jeddah to prevent the spread of infectious diseases among pilgrims and the local community.14–17 Additionally, the existing healthcare infrastructure began to be strengthened. The Ajyad Hospital in Mecca, originally built during the Ottoman period, was expanded to serve both residents and pilgrims and became one of the first institutionalized hospitals under the Public Health Directorate.16 Regional healthcare administration also began to take shape, with the Kingdom organized into six health regions: Mecca, Riyadh, Medina, Al-Ahsa, Asir, and Taif.17

A major institutional development in emergency care occurred with the founding of the Ambulance Charity Association in 1934, during the conflict between Saudi Arabia and Yemen. The mountainous regions of Asir and the Tihamah plain lacked formal ambulance services. The association mobilized physicians, medical supplies, and ambulances to deliver emergency care to injured soldiers and civilians. The association also provided essential health services to Hajj pilgrims, addressing cases of heatstroke, dehydration, and infectious diseases. In 1935, the association was formalized by a royal decree, with strong public support and financial backing from the Ministry of Finance. By the end of the decade, the association had expanded its services to cover outpatient care and public health campaigns, reaching approximately 85,000 patients annually.16

During this period, the legal foundations of modern public health in Saudi Arabia began to emerge. In 1929, the first royal decree regulating the licensing of medical and pharmaceutical practice was issued, followed by legislation prohibiting non-medical practitioners such as barbers and cuppers from performing medical procedures. Further decrees established smallpox vaccination requirements in 1930, codified the role of midwives in 1933, and introduced the first infectious disease control regulations in 1934. The Decree on Hospital Administration of 1935 formalized operational standards for hospitals, while the 1938 Decree on Medicinal Drug Trade introduced controls on the manufacture and sale of pharmaceutical products.17

The discovery of oil in the late 1930s provided the Kingdom with new financial resources to expand state services, including healthcare. Public investment in disease control campaigns and healthcare infrastructure increased, and by the late 1940s, further developments began to take shape. The Arabian American Oil Company (ARAMCO) played a pivotal role in public health during this era. In 1948, ARAMCO launched a malaria control program in the Eastern Province. The initiative combined water management techniques with community health education and was an early example of targeted, evidence-based public health intervention. The success of this program demonstrated the potential for private-public collaboration in improving population health.18,19

Following the early public health successes, a royal decree in 1951 elevated the Directorate of Public Health and Ambulance Services to a full MOH, responsible for the centralized planning, funding, and management of healthcare services throughout the Kingdom. The new ministry had specific departments for preventive medicine and hospital administration. The initial budget was 6.5 million riyals, a significant increase compared to previous years.1,17 By 1952, the MOH had opened 11 general hospitals and 55 PHCs and clinics across the country. It also transformed the Eastern Lebanese Hospital in Jeddah into a public women’s and maternity hospital and established clinics for managing neuropsychiatric diseases and tuberculosis.17 Notable hospital expansions included Ajyad Hospital in Mecca and the new Al-Zahir Hospital, both of which enhanced capacity for managing the healthcare needs of residents and the growing Hajj pilgrim population.17

Despite these advances, the healthcare system faced significant limitations. Services remained highly centralized, with rural areas underserved and reliant on traditional healers. Geographical challenges, including deserts and mountains, made service delivery difficult. Early healthcare infrastructure lacked many of the architectural and technological features of modern hospitals, and the healthcare workforce remained small and largely composed of expatriates.2 Nonetheless, this period laid the essential institutional and regulatory foundations for the development of the modern Saudi healthcare system.

These early initiatives reflected the leadership vision to institutionalize public health governance and create centralized mechanisms capable of addressing infectious disease threats and the health needs of rapidly increasing Hajj pilgrim populations.

Institutionalization and System Building (1953 to 1975)

The reign of King Saud (1953 to 1964) marked a period of accelerated investment in healthcare, institutional development, and system building. During this era, the MOH began to adopt a more structured and diversified organizational model. The MOH budget rose significantly, from 36.9 million riyals in 1953 to 103 million riyals by 1964, representing a nearly threefold increase in state commitment to health sector financing. New directorates were established within the MOH, focusing on preventive medicine, curative services, immunization and vaccination programs, pharmaceutical regulation, and social studies. A policy of geographical decentralization was introduced to enhance service delivery, with the creation of six regional branches in the Eastern Region, Riyadh, Mecca, the Western Region, Medina, and Asir.17

One of the major achievements of this period was the expansion of the healthcare infrastructure. Several landmark facilities were built, including King Saud Hospital in Riyadh (with 400 beds), as well as regional hospitals in Jeddah, Taif, and Abha. The MOH also established hospitals specializing in maternal and child health and the treatment of infectious diseases such as tuberculosis, malaria, schistosomiasis, and sexually transmitted infections. By the mid-1960s, the number of hospitals had grown to 72, providing a total of 7588 hospital beds. The number of dispensaries rose to 157, and the network of PHCs expanded to 219 facilities. These developments were accompanied by a significant growth in human resources. By 1964, the MOH employed 510 physicians, 798 nurses, and 199 midwives.17

During this decade, the MOH prioritized rural outreach through the deployment of mobile healthcare units and emergency services to underserved areas. By 1963, the Kingdom operated more than 80 health points, 30 outpatient clinics, and 10 quarantine stations. A milestone in emergency care was the opening of the first dedicated emergency center in Riyadh in 1963, and a hospital affiliated with the Saudi Red Crescent Society was also established.17

ARAMCO kept doing its role in enhancing public health by supporting government vaccination campaigns against diseases such as smallpox, typhoid fever, tetanus, diphtheria, and polio. It also contributed to health infrastructure by constructing advanced hospitals in the Eastern Province, including Dhahran Central Hospital, Ras Tanura Hospital, Buqayq Hospital, and Abqaiq Hospital.17

The period of King Faisal’s reign (1964 to 1975) brought a more strategic and planned approach to health sector development. The Kingdom’s First Five-Year Development Plan (1970 to 1975) identified healthcare as a national priority. It emphasized the expansion of healthcare infrastructure, the development of preventive services, and the investment in human capital. The plan called for the construction of 100-bed regional hospitals in Zulfi, Tabuk, Sakaka, and Jouf and encouraged private sector involvement. The MOH budget reflected this ambitious agenda, increasing to 421 million riyals by the mid-1970s.17

Public health programs intensified during this period. The MOH conducted widespread immunization campaigns, implemented hygiene improvement initiatives in marketplaces, expanded tuberculosis control programs, and launched a major malaria eradication effort.17 By 1975, the national healthcare network included 150 hospitals with 8132 beds, 533 polyclinics, 12 preventive health offices, 22 quarantine centers, and 5 centers dedicated to maternal and child health services. These facilities employed 1900 physicians, 134 pharmacists, 1695 male nurses, and 2539 female nurses and midwives.17

However, the Kingdom faced a severe shortage of trained medical personnel, with Saudi nationals representing only a small fraction of the healthcare workforce.1,2 Endemic diseases such as tuberculosis and malaria continued to pose significant public health threats. In response, the government and its partners strengthened disease surveillance and control programs.20,21 Nonetheless, the structural and institutional foundations laid during this period proved critical for enabling the more expansive modernization that would occur in subsequent decades. Leadership during the reigns of Kings Saud and Faisal emphasized state-led investment, administrative decentralization, and long-term development planning, shaping governance structures that enabled the rapid scaling of healthcare services nationwide.

Expansion, Modernization, and the Rise of Primary Healthcare (1976 to 2005)

The reign of King Khalid (1975 to 1982) marked a period of strategic healthcare expansion and modernization. Guided by the Kingdom’s Second and Third Five-Year Development Plans, this era saw major investments in hospital infrastructure, the rapid scaling up of primary healthcare services, and the institutionalization of preventive medicine.

One of the landmark initiatives of this period was the Five Hospitals Project, which added 2275 new hospital beds across key regions of the country. Hospitals in Jeddah, Madinah, Jazan, Al-Hofuf, and Al-Khobar were completed between 1976 and 1979, improving geographic access to inpatient services. In parallel, the Kingdom made significant progress in expanding its primary healthcare system. By the late 1970s, more than 219 PHCs, 157 dispensaries, and 893 health points had been established nationwide. PHCs became the foundation of the healthcare delivery system, providing first-contact care, preventive services, maternal and child health, immunization, and treatment of common illnesses. Besides, preventive medicine gained greater prominence during this period. National immunization programs were expanded, malaria control efforts intensified, and maternal-child healthcare services were systematically strengthened. Free medical services and medications were guaranteed to all citizens, and government sponsorship of treatment abroad was provided for patients requiring specialized care unavailable domestically.17

In line with the growing complexity of healthcare services, human resources in the sector increased. The workforce expanded to add 1081 physicians and 4742 pharmacists. Efforts to improve the Kingdom’s self-sufficiency in healthcare also took shape. The establishment of medical colleges, such as the College of Medicine at King Saud University in 1976, marked the beginning of a strategic investment in domestic medical education and training.17

During the reign of King Fahd (1982 to 2005), Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system entered a phase of extensive growth, modernization, and specialization. Four successive national Five-Year Development Plans further advanced healthcare as a central pillar of the country’s broader development strategy. Government investment in health infrastructure surged. By the early 2000s, the MOH operated 270 hospitals with a total capacity exceeding 40,300 beds. This expansion ensured more equitable access to inpatient care across the Kingdom’s diverse geographic regions. PHCs remained the cornerstone of healthcare delivery. More than 824 new PHCs were opened during this period. These centers provided the majority of preventive and outpatient services, including management of chronic diseases, maternal and child health, health education, and immunization. A significant hallmark of this era was the development of specialized tertiary care centers. Advanced hospitals and specialist centers for cardiology, oncology, organ transplantation, diabetes, and dental care were established. Among these, the King Faisal Specialist Hospital & Research Centre in Riyadh emerged as a national leader in advanced clinical care and medical research. The private healthcare sector expanded during this period. By 2006, the Kingdom had 127 private hospitals with a total of 12,590 beds, alongside more than 1057 private dispensaries and 416 polyclinics. This expansion necessitated an increase in the manpower in the health sector. In 2001, the number of physicians and nursing staff reached 16,111 and 3766, respectively.17

Medical education continued to expand. The number of health institutes grew considerably during the 1990s. Six health institutes for girls and one for boys were in operation by the early 1990s. By the year 2000, the number of co-educational health institutes had increased to 24. Another flagship achievement was the construction of King Fahd Medical City (KFMC) in Riyadh. Designed as an integrated medical complex, KFMC combined hospitals, specialty clinics, research centers, and training institutions. The facility housed 510 inpatient beds across multiple specialties, including internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, orthopedics, psychiatry, and rehabilitation medicine. The complex also featured a 280-bed children’s hospital, a 245-bed maternity hospital, a 300-bed psychiatric facility, and a 140-bed rehabilitation center.

Alongside infrastructure development, improvements in governance and quality assurance began to take shape. The MOH introduced new policies, regulatory frameworks, and quality standards aimed at improving patient experience and clinical outcomes. These reforms and investments translated into significant gains in population health. Life expectancy rose from approximately 53 years in 1970 to over 70 years by 2000. Infant mortality decreased from 120 per 1000 live births in the early 1970s to fewer than 20 per 1000 by the end of the century. The incidence of many infectious diseases declined markedly due to expanded immunization and improved disease control efforts.17

Despite these achievements, challenges persisted. The healthcare workforce remained dependent on expatriate professionals, and regional disparities in healthcare access were still evident. The growing burden of NCDs and the need for sustainable health financing became more apparent as the Kingdom’s health profile evolved.12,13 Nevertheless, the institutional foundations laid during this period set the stage for the transformative reforms of the twenty-first century. Strategic leadership during the reigns of Kings Khalid and Fahd prioritized infrastructure expansion, specialization of medical services, and the development of domestic medical education, reflecting a deliberate shift toward building a comprehensive national healthcare system.

Health Sector Transformation (2006 to 2015)

Since 2006, the Saudi health sector has undergone a rapid transformation. The reign of King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz (2005 to 2015) laid the groundwork for modernization through ambitious investments in infrastructure, expansion of medical education, the initial adoption of digital health technologies, and a renewed focus on primary healthcare.

During this period, healthcare spending rose sharply, exceeding 12% of the total government budget by 2013. More than 100 new hospitals were constructed across the Kingdom, increasing national bed capacity from approximately 50,000 in 2005 to more than 68,000 by 2014.10,22 The establishment of King Abdullah Medical Cities in Riyadh, Jeddah, and the Eastern Province helped address regional disparities in access to specialist tertiary care.22

Significant progress was also made in medical education. The number of medical colleges grew from 5 in 2000 to 34 by 2014, complemented by expanded postgraduate residency programs and international scholarship opportunities for Saudi healthcare professionals.22 Digitization efforts also began during this time. The 2010 launch of the National e-Health Strategy marked Saudi Arabia’s first endeavor to modernize health information systems and develop an integrated national electronic health record, although progress was inconsistent due to fragmented data systems and limited technical capacity.23 Primary healthcare networks continued to evolve during this era. New family medicine training programs and basic information technology systems were introduced at selected PHCs.10

The emergence of Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV) in 2012 provided an early test of the healthcare system’s resilience. Saudi Arabia, as the epicenter of the outbreak, responded by strengthening infection prevention and control programs, implementing strict isolation measures, enhancing diagnostic laboratory capacity, and adopting digital surveillance tools.24,25

These developments reflected leadership recognition that strengthening governance capacity, expanding medical education, and introducing digital technologies were essential for improving system resilience and preparing the health sector for emerging health threats.

Vision 2030 Transformation (2015 to Present)

Since the accession of King Salman in 2015, healthcare transformation has advanced at an accelerated pace, under the Vision 2030 framework. This framework positions healthcare as one of the central pillars of national development and economic diversification, in alignment with international targets for Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). At the heart of these reforms is the value-based Model of Care (MoC), which organizes services along six priority pathways: keeping well, planned procedures, women and children’s health, urgent care, management of chronic conditions, and end-of-life care. Initially, the MoC often remained specialist-oriented. Many patients continued to access tertiary services directly, which contributed to higher costs and inefficient use of resources. Recognizing this imbalance, the MOH has emphasized a shift toward population health, family medicine, and preventive care, with primary care placed at the center of the new MOC This new MoC is supported by 42 targeted interventions designed to enhance prevention, disease management, and population health outcomes.7,26

A unified National Guidelines Center has been established within the MOH to promote the adoption of standardized, evidence-based clinical protocols across the Kingdom’s healthcare system. This initiative addresses historical variability in clinical practice and aims to reduce unwarranted variations in care quality, improve patient safety, and align clinical services with international best practices. The Center has developed and disseminated national clinical guidelines for a broad range of priority conditions, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, oncology, maternal and child health, and infectious disease management. As of 2024, over 70 national clinical guidelines had been issued, with implementation supported through clinical education programs and integration into electronic health record systems. In parallel, the Ada’a Health Performance Program was launched to drive continuous quality improvement and transparency across the healthcare system. Ada’a monitors more than 40 digital performance indicators, covering key dimensions such as patient safety, clinical outcomes, operational efficiency, patient experience, and public health preparedness. For example, the program tracks hospital readmission rates, infection control metrics, waiting times for elective procedures, adherence to clinical pathways, and patient satisfaction scores.27

Digital transformation has progressed rapidly since 2016. The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 catalyzed further digital innovation. National telemedicine platforms were expanded to cover the entire country, facilitating virtual consultations for remote and underserved populations. The SEHA Virtual Hospital, launched during the pandemic, now connects 224 hospitals across 44 specialties, providing a nationwide platform for teleconsultation, remote monitoring of patients, and specialist referral.28,29 The Mawid electronic appointment system was launched nationwide, enabling citizens to schedule services at PHCs and hospitals online. The 937 Health Contact Center significantly expanded its teleconsultation and triage capabilities, reaching over 80,000 calls per week by 2019.28 The Tawakkalna app became the Kingdom’s national tool for vaccination certification, contact tracing, and movement permits. Integrated testing platforms and digital surveillance systems enabled rapid epidemic response.29 The Wasfaty e-prescription platform was expanded to connect physicians, pharmacies, and insurance systems, promoting safe and efficient medication management.30 Efforts to create a fully interoperable national health information system are ongoing. The MOH is implementing the Unified Health Information System (UHIS), which will link public and private healthcare data and enable seamless exchange of patient information across providers.9,31

Privatization of healthcare services is another strategic priority, aiming to increase the private sector’s share of total healthcare spending to 35% by 2030. This is supported by legislative frameworks such as the Private Sector Participation Law and the National Centre for Privatization.32 Health financing reforms seek to shift the system toward value-based reimbursement models and expand insurance coverage to reduce reliance on oil revenues.33 The MOH is implementing a National Health Insurance Program to foster competition, improve efficiency, and enhance equity in healthcare access.34

Workforce development remains a critical focus of the Kingdom’s healthcare reform agenda. The government has launched multiple initiatives aimed at building a more self-sufficient and sustainable health workforce. These efforts include the expansion of postgraduate medical training programs, the establishment of new nursing and allied health colleges, and the provision of academic scholarships for international study. The MOH is also investing in new clinical training pathways in family medicine, emergency medicine, and public health to address shortages in key specialties. In addition, workforce localization targets have been introduced to progressively increase the percentage of Saudi nationals in clinical and administrative healthcare roles.35

One of the distinctive aspects of Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system is its ability to manage the extraordinary health demands of the annual Hajj pilgrimage, the world’s largest recurring mass gathering. Each year, between two and three million pilgrims from more than 180 countries converge on Mecca and Medina during Hajj season, creating significant public health challenges related to infectious disease control, heat-related illnesses, crowd injuries, and the management of chronic conditions among pilgrims. To address these challenges, the MOH implements an extensive seasonal healthcare operation. In recent years, more than 25 hospitals and 156 health centers have been designated specifically for Hajj services in the holy cities and surrounding areas, providing a total of more than 5000 inpatient beds, including intensive care and isolation units. Over 25,000 healthcare professionals, including doctors, nurses, pharmacists, paramedics, and public health personnel, are deployed annually to provide care during the pilgrimage. Advanced surveillance systems are activated during Hajj, including mobile applications for health monitoring, electronic immunization records linked to entry requirements, and real-time epidemic intelligence platforms for early detection of infectious disease outbreaks. For example, during the 2023 Hajj season, more than 300,000 electronic health records (EHRs) were created for pilgrims, and nearly 70,000 pilgrims accessed services through the 937 Health Contact Center and SEHA virtual care platforms. The Saudi Red Crescent Authority also contributes to emergency preparedness by deploying hundreds of mobile ambulance units and field teams, while mobile laboratories and portable radiology units are positioned at key sites to ensure rapid diagnostic capacity.36

The healthcare transformation of the Kingdom under King Salman’s leadership has demonstrated resilience and adaptability in the face of both domestic and global health challenges. These reforms illustrate a strategic leadership shift toward value-based healthcare, stronger system governance, and greater integration between public and private health sectors under the Vision 2030 reform agenda. While significant progress has been made, ongoing challenges include addressing regional disparities in healthcare access, sustaining growth in the Saudi healthcare workforce, managing the rising burden of NCDs, and ensuring long-term financial sustainability. These issues remain central to the success of Vision 2030 healthcare reform efforts and will shape the future trajectory of Saudi Arabia’s health system.

Lessons Learned

The century-long evolution of Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system offers several lessons for both national stakeholders and the wider international community. These lessons also provide actionable guidance for healthcare leaders seeking to strengthen health system governance, primary healthcare delivery, digital transformation, and preparedness for large-scale public health events.

Public Health Investment Remains Central

As described in the early public health phase (1925–1952), the Kingdom’s first investment in institutionalized health was the creation of the Public Health Directorate in 1925, with a focus on infectious disease control and Hajj preparedness. Nearly a century later, under Vision 2030, the first newly established entity approved by royal decree was again in public health; the Public Health Authority. This continuity underscores that public health is not only a historical foundation but also the strategic priority for the future, echoing a “CDC-like” model for prevention, health security, and population health.

For contemporary healthcare leaders, this lesson underscores the importance of sustained investment in preventive and population health infrastructure. Leadership strategies should prioritize strong public health institutions, integrated disease surveillance systems, and preparedness planning for emerging threats. Governments should ensure stable financing for public health functions, strengthen coordination between national and regional health authorities, and embed prevention within primary healthcare systems.

State-Led Investment Drives Rapid Gains

During the system-building phase (1953–1975), which was characterized by rapid expansion of health infrastructure and workforce development, the MOH budget increased from 6.5 million riyals in 1951 to more than 421 million riyals by the mid-1970s. These investments translated into concrete health gains: life expectancy rose from ~53 years in 1970 to > 70 years by 2000, while infant mortality fell from 120 per 1000 live births in the early 1970s to fewer than 20 by 2000.

Healthcare leaders should recognize that strategic public investment remains a key driver of health system development, particularly in rapidly expanding health systems. Policymakers should align infrastructure expansion with workforce development, health financing reform, and service integration to ensure sustainability. Long-term national development plans can serve as effective tools for aligning political commitment, financing priorities, and system-wide performance goals. Evidence-based leadership approaches emphasize the systematic use of health system data, monitoring frameworks, and research evidence to guide policy design and investment priorities, ensuring that infrastructure expansion and workforce development translate into measurable improvements in system performance and long-term sustainability.37

Primary Care Expansion Requires Cultural and System Redesign

Although PHCs expanded dramatically, especially during the expansion and modernization phase (1976–2005), patient preference for direct specialist access and system-level incentives meant that tertiary care remained dominant. The lesson is that physical expansion alone is insufficient; a shift in both care culture and financing is needed. Vision 2030’s focus on family medicine pathways and preventive services reflects recognition of this gap.

Comparable primary healthcare reforms have been implemented in several other health systems. For example, the United Kingdom’s National Health Service has long relied on general practitioner–led care coordination as the backbone of service delivery,38 while Brazil’s Family Health Strategy expanded community-based primary care teams to improve access and continuity of care.39 Within the Gulf region, the United Arab Emirates and Qatar have also implemented health system reforms emphasizing integrated care models, digital health infrastructure, and stronger primary care networks to improve service coordination and population health outcomes.40

Strengthening primary healthcare requires leadership strategies that go beyond expanding physical infrastructure. Health system leaders should prioritize the development of family medicine training programs, introduce financing models that incentivize primary care utilization, and implement referral systems that reduce unnecessary tertiary care use. Public education campaigns and digital appointment systems can further encourage patient engagement with primary healthcare services as the first point of contact within the health system.

Preparedness for Mass Gatherings and Epidemics Is Globally Relevant

The ability to deliver health services to 2–3 million pilgrims annually during Hajj, including the deployment of > 25 hospitals and > 25,000 health professionals, represents a unique lesson in large-scale surge capacity. More recently, responses to MERS-CoV (2012) and COVID-19 (2020) demonstrated how early investments in surveillance, infection control, and digital platforms can be leveraged for both domestic resilience and global health security.

Healthcare leaders responsible for mass gathering health operations should prioritize integrated planning across public health, emergency medicine, and digital surveillance systems. Effective strategies include establishing dedicated mass gathering health units, deploying mobile healthcare infrastructure, integrating real-time disease surveillance platforms, and strengthening international collaboration for infectious disease monitoring. Lessons from the Hajj experience demonstrate the value of combining public health preparedness, emergency response capacity, and digital health tools to manage large-scale population health risks.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, the analysis focuses on the historical development of the healthcare system in Saudi Arabia; therefore, the findings may have limited generalizability to other countries with different political, economic, and institutional contexts. Second, as a narrative historical review, the study inherently involves some subjectivity in selecting, interpreting, and synthesizing historical sources, which may introduce selection or interpretive bias. In addition, the study did not include primary stakeholder or expert interviews to validate historical interpretations, which may have provided additional contextual insights into policy decisions and institutional developments. Third, the availability of early epidemiological, demographic, and health system indicators was limited, particularly for the earlier phases of healthcare development, which constrained the ability to provide detailed quantitative comparisons across historical periods. Fourth, although the study highlights associations between policy reforms, leadership decisions, and health system development, it does not establish causal relationships between specific reforms and observed health outcomes. Future research using quantitative or comparative health system analyses may help further clarify these relationships.

Conclusion

The historical evolution of Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system demonstrates that health sector expansion has been closely linked to shifts in governance structures, national development priorities, and state investment strategies across successive political periods. Rather than representing a continuous linear process, system development occurred through distinct phases characterized by institutional consolidation, infrastructure expansion, specialization of services, and, more recently, efforts toward integration, digital transformation, and financing reform under Vision 2030. This analysis suggests that earlier reform phases were primarily infrastructure-driven, with measurable gains in service availability and population health indicators, whereas current reforms place greater emphasis on system efficiency, coordination of care, and sustainability. However, persistent challenges, including regional variation in access, dependence on expatriate health professionals, the rising burden of non-communicable diseases, and limited utilization of primary healthcare services, indicate that structural transformation remains incomplete. The expansion of digital health platforms and public–private partnerships represents an important shift in policy direction, but their long-term contribution to equity, quality of care, and cost containment requires further evaluation. These findings suggest that future reform efforts will depend not only on continued investment but also on strengthening institutional coordination, regulatory capacity, workforce sustainability, and the performance of primary healthcare systems. Viewing these developments through a leadership and governance lens highlights the central role of state planning in shaping reform trajectories while also underscoring the importance of institutional capacity for implementation and regulation. Future research should examine how these reforms influence service integration, financing sustainability, and population health outcomes, particularly in the context of demographic change and the increasing burden of NCDs.
