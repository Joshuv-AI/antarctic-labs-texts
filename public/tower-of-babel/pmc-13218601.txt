Near-death experiences
Authors: Jorge Andrés Delgado-Ron
Published: 2026

Near-death experiences are common, clinically meaningful events

These profound, subjective experiences occur in people who are physiologically close to death, including 15% to 17% of critically ill, hospitalized patients.1 Near-death experiences can include panoramic memories, feelings of profound peace, encounters with deceased relatives, and out-of-body perceptions (with details of patient narratives often corroborated by medical personnel).2–5 Unlike with hallucinations or delirium, narratives of these experiences are characterized by their clarity, narrative organization, and durable recall.4,6

Such experiences can occur during periods of severely impaired or absent observable brain activity

Vivid perceptions and lucid cognition during cardiac arrest, deep anesthesia, or profound hypoxia have been documented in both retrospective and prospective studies.2,3 These experiences have occurred at times when activity on electroencephalography would be expected to be flat or severely disorganized, with null to minimal cortical activity.3 An analysis of physiologic aspects of near-death experiences suggested that current biomedical models cannot fully account for all observed phenomena.2,3

They may result in positive changes to mental health

Such experiences often lead to enhanced psychological functioning rather than impairment, placing them outside the expected clinical profile of neuropsychiatric disturbances.1,2 Near-death experiences often result in reduced fear of death, increased empathy and prosocial behaviour, a change in life priorities, and a heightened sense of meaning.1,2

Near-death experiences should not be pathologized

Existential confusion and distress may stem from near-death experiences but also from fear of disclosure to clinicians or family members.1,6 A nontrivial proportion of patients have reported dismissing or pathologizing responses from health care providers, which can exacerbate patient distress and undermine trust in care.1,7

Clinicians should validate and explore these experiences using patient-centred, evidence-informed communication

Studies of patients in recovery after intensive care emphasize the importance of providing supportive, nonjudgmental space for discussion and helping patients articulate the emotional and existential impact of the event.1,7 Medical ethicists suggest that clinicians ask open questions, normalize the experience, and avoid dismissive attitudes or imposing beliefs that contradict the patient’s account.1
