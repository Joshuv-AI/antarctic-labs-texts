Tighter confidence intervals for quantiles of heterogeneous data
John H.J. Einmahl, Yi He

Abstract
It is well known that the asymptotic variance of sample quantiles can be reduced under heterogeneity relative to the i.i.d. setting. However, asymptotically correct confidence intervals for quantiles are not yet available. We propose a novel, consistent estimator of the reduced asymptotic variance arising when quantiles are computed from groups of observations, leading to asymptotically correct confidence intervals. Simulation studies show that our confidence intervals are substantially shorter than those in the i.i.d. case and attain nearly correct coverage across a wide range of heterogeneous settings.



Key words: Heterogeneous data; quantile; confidence interval.

1
Introduction

Quantiles are fundamental characteristics of a probability distribution; see, e.g., the monographs
Reiss 1989
,
David and Nagaraja 2004
, and
Arnold, Balakrishnan, and Nagaraja 2008
. The standard framework for nonparametric quantile inference assumes independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data, yet in many modern applications the observations are not identically distributed.
In this article we consider the much more general framework in which the data are independent but each observation arises from its own distribution, potentially very different from the others.
Specifically, we observe independent univariate data from a triangular array

X_{i}\sim F_{n,i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

where
F_{n,i}
denotes the distribution of
X_{i}=X_{i}^{(n)}
, that is,
P\bigl(X_{i}^{(n)}\leq x\bigr)=F_{n,i}(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
i=1,\ldots,n
. Throughout, we define the quantile function corresponding to any distribution function
F
as its left-continuous inverse.

Motivated by the i.i.d. case, a statistician typically pools the observations, forms the order statistics
X_{1:n}\leq\cdots\leq X_{n:n}
, and computes the empirical quantile
\widehat{Q}(\tau)
at a desired level
\tau\in(0,1)
. The inferential target of this empirical quantile now turns out to be
\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)
, the quantile corresponding to the average distribution

\bar{F}_{n}=n^{-1}(F_{n,1}+\cdots+F_{n,n})
. A statistically appealing yet well established phenomenon is that the asymptotic variance of the empirical distribution function of independent data is maximized under i.i.d. sampling from
\bar{F}_{n}
, meaning that the asymptotic variance can be (much) smaller for heterogeneous data;
see, for example,
Shorack 1973
,
van Zuijlen 1976
, and Chapter 25 of
Shorack and Wellner 1986
.

Despite these classical results, their practical relevance for statistical inference has remained limited.
To our knowledge, no asymptotically correct nonparametric method exists in general that quantifies the variance reduction attributable to heterogeneity. Statisticians are therefore compelled either to use i.i.d.-based (very) conservative confidence intervals, or to restrict to the, often unrealistic, setup where the heterogeneity is so close to homogeneity that the limiting distribution coincides with the i.i.d. limit.

In the next section, we develop a new method that yields asymptotically
correct
confidence intervals for
\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)
at any fixed
\tau\in(0,1)
under general heterogeneity. Our procedure provides valid inference without requiring the existence of the quantile density function, by exploiting the Galois connection between the distribution and quantile functions. As a result, the resulting intervals can be substantially tighter than those obtained under i.i.d. assumptions. Our approach partitions the data into
g
groups of sizes
m_{j}
(with
n=m_{1}+\cdots+m_{g}
), such that observations within each group share a common distribution, while distributions may differ arbitrarily across groups.

Assumption
1
.

For all
n\geq 2
, there exists a partition
\mathcal{I}_{n,j},j=1,\ldots g,
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
F_{i}=F_{i^{\prime}}
for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}
, and each
\mathcal{I}_{n,j}
contain at least 2 indices.

The i.i.d. setting is a special case with only
g=1
group containing all observations. A canonical example of a heterogeneous setting is the balanced case in which
m_{j}\equiv m
, so that
n=mg
; in particular,
m
can be
2
, which yields the maximal number of groups in our setup.

We also provide a simulation study which shows that our confidence intervals are substantially shorter than those in the i.i.d. case and their nearly correct coverages across various heterogeneous settings. The proofs are given in Section
4
at the end.

2
Main Results

Consider the empirical distribution function

\widehat{F}(x)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\bigl\{X_{i}\leq x\bigr\},

which estimates the average distribution
\bar{F}_{n}
, which we assume to be continuous, so that its (left-continuous) quantile function
\bar{Q}_{n}
is strictly increasing (for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
). Let
\tau\in(0,1)
denote a given quantile level. The estimation variance of
\widehat{F}
at the target quantile
\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)
, after appropriate scaling and writing
F_{i}=F_{n,i}
, is

V_{n}(\tau)=\operatorname{var}\left(\sqrt{n}\,\widehat{F}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\right)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\Bigl[1-F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\Bigr].

In particular, for i.i.d. data this variance is constant, since

V_{n}(\tau)\equiv\tau(1-\tau),\qquad n\geq 1.

This corresponds to the worst case because, in general, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle V_{n}(\tau)=\tau-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}^{2}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\leq\tau-\left(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\right)^{2}=\tau(1-\tau).

The Lindeberg condition then holds provided that

Assumption
2
.

nV_{n}(\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\bigl[1-F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\bigr]\rightarrow\infty

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

This condition is trivial for i.i.d. data, for which

nV_{n}(\tau)=n\tau(1-\tau)\rightarrow\infty
. It also holds whenever
V_{n}(\tau)
is bounded away from zero, for example when
V_{n}(\tau)
stays constant for large
n
. Under this condition, the Lindeberg central limit theorem yields

\left(n/V_{n}(\tau)\right)^{1/2}\bigl(\widehat{F}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)-\tau\bigr)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(1)

where
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
denotes a standard normal random variable.

For statistical inference, the heterogeneous variance
V_{n}(\tau)
must be replaced by a ratio-consistent estimator, which is not available in the existing literature for heterogeneous data. We now provide such an estimator, thereby enabling the construction of the confidence intervals described above. Under Assumption
1
,

V_{n}(\tau)=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\bigl\{1-F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\bigr\}.

For all
i,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}
with
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}
, independence implies

\displaystyle F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\bigl\{1-F_{i}\bigl(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\bigr)\bigr\}

\displaystyle=P\bigl(X_{\ell_{1}}\leq Q_{n}(\tau),\,X_{\ell_{2}}>Q_{n}(\tau)\bigr).

Averaging over all possible pairs within each group yields

\displaystyle V_{n}(\tau)=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{1}{m_{j}-1}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}P\bigl(X_{\ell_{1}}\leq Q_{n}(\tau),\,X_{\ell_{2}}>Q_{n}(\tau)\bigr),

where
P\bigl(X_{\ell}\leq Q_{n}(\tau),\,X_{\ell}>Q_{n}(\tau)\bigr)=0
by definition and recall that
m_{j}
denotes the cardinality of
\mathcal{I}_{n,j}
. This representation motivates the following estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(\tau)=

\displaystyle\,n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{1}{m_{j}-1}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\bigl\{X_{\ell_{1}}\leq\widehat{Q}(\tau),\,X_{\ell_{2}}>\widehat{Q}(\tau)\bigr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{1}{m_{j}-1}\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\{X_{\ell}\leq\widehat{Q}(\tau)\}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\{X_{\ell}>\widehat{Q}(\tau)\}\right).

Both the population quantity
V_{n}(\tau)
and its estimate
\widehat{V}(\tau)
are rank-based and hence invariant under strictly increasing transformations of the data. The following proposition gives its ratio consistency.

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for every
\tau\in(0,1)
,

\widehat{V}(\tau)/V_{n}(\tau)\xrightarrow{p}1,

where
\xrightarrow{p}
denotes convergence in probability.

Combining Proposition
1
with the CLT (
1
) yields the main result.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for every
\tau\in(0,1)
,

\left(\frac{n}{\widehat{V}(\tau)}\right)^{1/2}\bigl(\widehat{F}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))-\tau\bigr)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
\Phi^{-1}
denote the standard normal quantile function and define

\widehat{c}(\alpha)=\sqrt{\widehat{V}(\tau)/n}\,\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2).

By inverting via the Galois inequalities and using the continuity of
\bar{F}_{n}
, we obtain the asymptotic
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence interval for
\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)
,

\bigl(\widehat{Q}(\tau-\widehat{c}(\alpha)),\,\widehat{Q}(\tau+\widehat{c}(\alpha))\bigr),

where
\widehat{Q}
denotes the empirical quantile function. This interval is asymptotically correct since, as
n\to\infty
,

P\bigl(\widehat{Q}(\tau-\widehat{c}(\alpha))<\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)<\widehat{Q}(\tau+\widehat{c}(\alpha))\bigr)\to 1-\alpha.

Simulations

We consider three times four heterogeneous data generating mechanisms. For all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}
,
j=1,\ldots,g
, the distributions are specified as follows:

I

X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j},1)
with mean
\mu_{j}=(\log j)^{\gamma/2}
;

II

X_{i}\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda_{j})
with rate
\lambda_{j}=1/j^{\gamma}
;

III

X_{i}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(x_{j}^{*}/2,\,x_{j}^{*})
with endpoint
x_{j}^{*}=1+e^{\gamma}j/g
;

We vary the parameter
\gamma\in\{1,2,3,4\}
to control the degree of heterogeneity.

4
Proofs

We need some more lemmas. The first one gives the well-known uniform converge rate of the empirical distribution function; see Chapter 25 of
Shorack and Wellner 1986
.

Lemma 1
.

\sup_{\tau\in(0,1)}|\tau-\bar{F}_{n}(\widehat{Q}(\tau))|=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.

Proof.

From
Bretagnolle 1981
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{F}(x)-\bar{F}_{n}(x)|=O_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

Substituting
x=\widehat{Q}(\tau)
yields that

\sup_{\tau\in(0,1)}|\widehat{F}(\widehat{Q}(\tau))-\bar{F}_{n}(\widehat{Q}(\tau))|=O_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

But
\widehat{F}(\widehat{Q}(\tau))=\tau+O_{P}(1/n)
uniformly by construction, then the lemma follows.
∎

The second lemma is needed to establish the stochastic continuity of our estimator around the target quantile level.

Lemma 2
.

Define the oracle estimator

\widetilde{V}(\tau)=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{1}{m_{j}-1}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{1}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau),\,X_{\ell_{2}}>\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right],\quad\tau\in(0,1).

Then, for every
\tau\in(0,1)
and all small
\delta>0
,

E\left(\sup_{|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|<\delta}\left|\widetilde{V}(\tau^{\prime})-\widetilde{V}(\tau)\right|\right)<2\delta.

Proof.

Observe that

\widetilde{V}(\tau)=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{1}{2(m_{j}-1)}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j},\,\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}}\left|\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{1}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]-\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{2}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]\right|.

Then, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{V}(\tau^{\prime})-V(\tau)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\,n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{1}{2(m_{j}-1)}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j},\,\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}}\left|\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{1}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau^{\prime})\right]-\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{1}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]\right|

\displaystyle+n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{1}{2(m_{j}-1)}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j},\,\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}}\left|\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{2}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau^{\prime})\right]-\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{2}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\mathds{1}\left[X_{i}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau^{\prime})\right]-\mathds{1}\left[X_{i}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]\right|.

Hence, for all small
\delta>0

\displaystyle\sup_{|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|<\delta}\left|\widetilde{V}(\tau^{\prime})-V(\tau)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\,n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\mathds{1}\left[X_{i}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau+\delta)\right]-\mathds{1}\left[X_{i}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau-\delta)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\widehat{F}\left(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau+\delta)\right)-\widehat{F}\left(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau-\delta)\right).

Taking expectation yields that

\displaystyle E\left(\sup_{|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|<\delta}\left|\widetilde{V}(\tau^{\prime})-V(\tau)\right|\right)\leq\bar{F}_{n}\left(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau+\delta)\right)-\bar{F}_{n}\left(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau-\delta)\right)=2\delta,

where the last step is due to the continuity of
\bar{F}_{n}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Consider any fixed
\tau\in(0,1)
. Let
\epsilon>0
be small. By Lemma
1
, there exists some large constant
M
and
\delta_{n}=M/\sqrt{n}
such that

P\left(|\tau-\bar{F}_{n}(\widehat{Q}(\tau))|<\delta_{n}\right)>1-\epsilon/2.

When the event in the last line occurs,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{V}(\tau)-\widetilde{V}(\tau)\right|\leq\sup_{|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|<\delta_{n}}\left|\widetilde{V}(\tau^{\prime})-\widetilde{V}(\tau)\right|,

where
\widetilde{V}
is the oracle estimator defined in Lemma
2
. Therefore, for all large
n
such that
\sqrt{n}V_{n}(\tau)>4M/\epsilon^{2}
,

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\widehat{V}(\tau)-\widetilde{V}(\tau)\right|/V_{n}(\tau)>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle P\left(|\tau-\bar{F}_{n}(\widehat{Q}(\tau))|\geq\delta_{n}\right)+P\left(\sup_{|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|<\delta_{n}}\left|\widetilde{V}(\tau^{\prime})-\widetilde{V}(\tau)\right|>\epsilon V_{n}(\tau)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon/2+2\delta_{n}/(\epsilon V_{n}(\tau))=\epsilon/2+2M/(\epsilon\sqrt{n}V_{n}(\tau))<\epsilon,

where we used Lemma
2
and the Markov inequality in the last line. Since
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small,

\left|\widehat{V}(\tau)-\widetilde{V}(\tau)\right|/V_{n}(\tau)\xrightarrow{p}0.

It remains to show that

\widetilde{V}(\tau)/V_{n}(\tau)\xrightarrow{p}1.

(2)

It is straightforward to verify that, using Assumption
1

\displaystyle E\left(\frac{\widetilde{V}(\tau)}{V_{n}(\tau)}\right)=\frac{E\widetilde{V}(\tau)}{V_{n}(\tau)}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}P\left[X_{i}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]P\left[X_{i}>\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))[1-F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))]}=1,

Moreover,

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\left(\frac{\widetilde{V}(\tau)}{V_{n}(\tau)}\right)=

\displaystyle(nV_{n}(\tau))^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{1}{(m_{j}-1)^{2}}\operatorname{var}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{1}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau),\,X_{\ell_{2}}>\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]\right).

But we can rewrite

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{1}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau),\,X_{\ell_{2}}>\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{1}}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]\right)\left(\sum_{\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell_{2}}>\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{j}(m_{j}-M_{j})

for the binomial variable

M_{j}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}\mathds{1}\left[X_{\ell}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)\right]\sim\mathcal{B}(m_{j},p_{j}),\quad p_{j}=F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)),\,i\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}.

Using the well-known formulas for the mean and variance of the binomial random variable
M_{j}
,

E(M_{j})\;=\;m_{j}p_{j},\qquad E(M_{j}^{2})\;=\;\operatorname{var}(M_{j})+\bigl(E(M_{j})\bigr)^{2}=m_{j}p_{j}(1-p_{j})+m_{j}^{2}p_{j}^{2},

together with the factorial moment formulas

E\bigl((M_{j})_{r}\bigr)=E\bigl(M_{j}(M_{j}-1)\cdots(M_{j}-r+1)\bigr)=m_{j}(m_{j}-1)\cdots(m_{j}-r+1)p_{j}^{r},\quad r=1,2,\ldots,

for the binomial distribution (see
Iyer 1958
),

\displaystyle E(M_{j}^{3})\;=

\displaystyle\;E\bigl((M_{j})_{3}\bigr)+3E\bigl((M_{j})_{2}\bigr)+E(M_{j})\;=\;m_{j}(m_{j}-1)(m_{j}-2)p_{j}^{3}+3m_{j}(m_{j}-1)p_{j}^{2}+m_{j}p_{j},

\displaystyle E(M_{j}^{4})\;=

\displaystyle\;E\bigl((M_{j})_{4}\bigr)+6E\bigl((M_{j})_{3}\bigr)+7E\bigl((M_{j})_{2}\bigr)+E(M_{j})

\displaystyle\;=

\displaystyle\;m_{j}(m_{j}-1)(m_{j}-2)(m_{j}-3)p_{j}^{4}+6m_{j}(m_{j}-1)(m_{j}-2)p_{j}^{3}+7m_{j}(m_{j}-1)p_{j}^{2}+m_{j}p_{j}.

Using these first four moments of
M_{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\bigl(M_{j}(m_{j}-M_{j})\bigr)=

\displaystyle\;m_{j}(m_{j}-1)p_{j}\bigl(1-p_{j}\bigr)\bigl(m_{j}-1-2(2m_{j}-3)p_{j}(1-p_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;m_{j}(m_{j}-1)p_{j}\bigl(1-p_{j}\bigr)(m_{j}-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;(m_{j}-1)^{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))\bigl(1-F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))\bigr),

where the inequality is immediate since
2m_{j}-3\geq 1>0
. It follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\left(\frac{\widetilde{V}(\tau)}{V_{n}(\tau)}\right)\leq

\displaystyle(nV_{n}(\tau))^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n,j}}F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))\bigl(1-F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))\bigr)=(nV_{n}(\tau))^{-1}\rightarrow 0.

This yields (
2
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

By Proposition
1
, it remains to verify the central limit theorem stated in (
1
), namely,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{n,i}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1),\qquad\mbox{with }\,Z_{n,i}=(nV_{n}(\tau))^{-1/2}\left(\mathds{1}[X_{i}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)]-\tau\right).

By the definition of
V_{n}(\tau)
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{var}(Z_{n,i})=(nV_{n}(\tau))^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))\left[1-F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))\right]=1.

Thus it remains to verify Lindeberg’s condition. Note that

\left|Z_{n,i}-EZ_{n,i}\right|=(nV_{n}(\tau))^{-1/2}\left|\mathds{1}[X_{i}\leq\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau)]-F_{n,i}(\bar{Q}_{n}(\tau))\right|\leq(nV_{n}(\tau))^{-1/2}.

For any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}E\left[\left(Z_{n,i}-EZ_{n,i}\right)^{2}\mathds{1}\left(\left|Z_{n,i}-EZ_{n,i}\right|>\epsilon\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}E\left[\left(Z_{n,i}-EZ_{n,i}\right)^{2}\mathds{1}\left((nV_{n}(\tau))^{-1/2}>\epsilon\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{var}(Z_{n,i})\cdot\mathds{1}\left((nV_{n}(\tau))^{-1/2}>\epsilon\right)=\mathds{1}\left((nV_{n}(\tau))^{-1/2}>\epsilon\right)\rightarrow 0,

because
nV_{n}(\tau)\to\infty
. ∎
