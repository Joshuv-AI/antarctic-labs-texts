Resveratrol protects against triclosan–induced impairment of colonic barrier function in chickens
Authors: Yixin Zhang, Huayu Zhang, Qiushi Li, Fucheng Wang, Tianning Luo, Qiaochu Sun, Wen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Triclosan impairs colonic barrier integrity and induces inflammation in chickens.•Resveratrol restores mucus secretion and tight junction proteins in the colon.•Resveratrol suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation in the chicken colon.•Triclosan disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis via SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling.•Resveratrol alleviates triclosan-induced colonic injury in chickens.

Introduction

Triclosan (TCS) has been extensively incorporated into hand sanitizers, cosmetics, and various personal care products, as well as impregnated into materials such as sportswear and food packaging since it was first approved for use by Ciba–Geigy in Switzerland in 1964 (Yueh and Tukey, 2016; Wang and Liang, 2021). Global TCS production is estimated to reach approximately 1,500 tons annually, and consumption of TCS–containing products in the United States alone exceeds 132 million liters per year (Alfhili and Lee, 2019). Following the COVID–19 outbreak in 2019, heightened demand for disinfection further accelerated TCS usage (Iacopetta et al., 2022). TCS can enter systemic circulation through dermal absorption or gastrointestinal ingestion (Queckenberg et al., 2010), and has been widely detected in human urine, blood, and breast milk worldwide (Bever et al., 2018). Moreover, TCS exposure can induce bacterial resistance by altering target sites, thereby diminishing its antibacterial efficacy (Drury et al., 2013). Environmental bacteria may also acquire resistance at environmentally relevant exposure levels, disrupting microbial community structure and potentially promoting cross–resistance to other antimicrobial agents (Li et al., 2019). Clinically, increasing TCS resistance has been associated with the emergence of additional antibiotic resistances (Zeng et al., 2020). The improper use of disinfectants, including TCS, may thus contribute to the spread of antimicrobial resistance, which is projected to become one of the most significant global health challenges in the post–COVID–19 era (Kumar et al., 2021). These concerns highlight the urgent need to identify safe and effective natural therapeutic alternatives.

Accumulating evidence indicates that TCS exposure can cause a range of adverse health effects, including hepatic and renal dysfunction, intestinal injury, and thyroid disruption (Chen et al., 2023a). Local exposure to TCS has also been shown to activate the NLR Family Pyrin Domain Containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and impair mitochondrial function (Weatherly et al., 2020). Among its target organs, the intestine is particularly vulnerable to TCS–induced toxicity, characterized by reduced tight junction protein expression, increased intestinal permeability, and aggravated chronic inflammation (Liu et al., 2022). In addition, intestinal barrier function depends not only on the structural integrity of epithelial cells, but is also critically regulated by the composition of the gut microbiota and its metabolites. The gut microbiota maintains intestinal barrier homeostasis by modulating the production of short-chain fatty acids, immune signaling pathways, and the expression of tight junction proteins. In contrast, dysbiosis induced by exogenous chemicals can further exacerbate barrier dysfunction and inflammatory responses (Dmytriv et al., 2024; Hays et al., 2024). Recent studies further demonstrate that human–relevant TCS exposure disrupts gut microbiota composition, promotes colonic inflammation, exacerbates colitis severity, and accelerates colon tumorigenesis in mouse models (Sanidad et al., 2022). Previous studies have demonstrated that bioactive compounds derived from natural plants (phytochemicals) can improve intestinal barrier function and alleviate inflammatory responses by reshaping the gut microbiota structure, promoting the proliferation of beneficial bacteria, and enhancing epithelial tight junctions (Su et al., 2023). This “microbiota–barrier–immune” regulatory axis provides a novel theoretical basis for intervening in triclosan (TCS)-induced intestinal injury. Although these findings suggest that TCS aggravates inflammation and tumor formation in colitis models, the underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently defined. In particular, the contribution of mitochondrial dysfunction–inflammasome interactions to TCS–induced intestinal toxicity has yet to be fully elucidated, limiting the development of effective prevention and intervention strategies.

In poultry production, TCS can enter the farm environment through the use of reclaimed water for irrigation or the application of biosolids containing antimicrobial residues to nearby farmland (Agarwal et al., 2025) . Given the strong environmental persistence and hydrophobicity of TCS, it can accumulate in soil and water bodies (Chen et al., 2020) and enter poultry through the "soil‒plant–feed–poultry" pathway (Pannu et al., 2012). Once absorbed into the body, its uptake primarily depends on passive diffusion across the intestinal epithelium, and it can subsequently distribute and accumulate in the liver and egg (Guo et al., 2018b). Previous studies have confirmed that avian species are highly sensitive to TCS; for example, chicken embryo exposure experiments have shown that TCS markedly alters the liver transcriptome, interfering with lipid metabolism and stress response pathways (Guo et al., 2018a) . Furthermore, TCS has recently been detected in eggs of wild birds, and its lipophilic nature and high bioaccumulation potential imply that poultry raised in contaminated environments may accumulate substantial tissue concentrations. This accumulation not only disrupts physiological homeostasis in birds, potentially affecting production performance, but also poses a serious threat to human food safety through the "soil–plant–poultry–human" transfer chain. However, most toxicological research has focused on rodents and aquatic species, with comparatively few studies addressing avian organisms. Considering the persistence and bioaccumulation of TCS in birds, evaluating its potential hazards and clarifying its toxicological mechanisms in avian species are of considerable importance.

Resveratrol (RSV) is a natural stilbenoid compound abundant in various plant–derived foods, including grape skins, berries, and peanuts (Bryl et al., 2022). As a phytoalexin, RSV is synthesized by plants in response to biotic and abiotic stressors (Pannu and Bhatnagar, 2019). In recent years, RSV has attracted considerable attention due to its diverse biological activities. Both experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that RSV exhibits antioxidant, anti–inflammatory, anti–apoptotic, and immunomodulatory properties and is closely associated with the regulation of mitochondrial function, suggesting its potential roles in chronic disease prevention and metabolic regulation (Bejenaru et al., 2024; Salehi et al., 2018; Yadav et al., 2022). As one of the plant-derived bioactive compounds, RSV shares certain commonalities in its biological functions with other plant extracts applied in animal production. Previous studies have shown that various plant-derived additives can also promote animal health and production performance by enhancing antioxidant capacity, regulating immune function, and improving intestinal microecology. For instance, Chen et al. (Chen et al., 2023b) reported that Litsea cubeba essential oil significantly increased antioxidant levels, enhanced immune function, and improved intestinal microbiota structure as well as nutrient digestibility in pigs, suggesting that plant-derived bioactive ingredients hold broad potential for modulating oxidative stress and intestinal health.

Oxidative stress is a key contributor to intestinal injury, and the Nrf2/HO–1 signaling pathway plays an essential role in cellular antioxidant defense. Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2–Related Factor 2 (Nrf2) regulates the transcription of multiple antioxidant and cytoprotective genes, including Heme Oxygenase–1 (HO–1), thereby maintaining redox homeostasis under stress conditions(O’Rourke et al., 2024). Extensive in vitro and in vivo studies, as well as comprehensive reviews, have demonstrated that RSV reduces intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, activates the Nrf2/HO–1 signaling pathway, and enhances the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including Superoxide dismutase (SOD), Catalase (CAT), and Glutathione (GSH), thereby mitigating oxidative stress–induced cellular and organ damage (Gu et al., 2021). In models of intestinal inflammation (Qiu et al., 2022) and liver injury (Wu et al., 2022), RSV has consistently exhibited potent antioxidant effects. Beyond its antioxidant activity, RSV also exerts robust anti–inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. It attenuates tissue inflammation by inhibiting the Nuclear Factor kappa B (NF–κB) signaling pathway and downregulating the expression of pro–inflammatory cytokines such as Tumor Necrosis Factor alpha (TNF–α), interleukin–1β (IL–1β), and interleukin–6 (IL–6)(Ren et al., 2025). Moreover, RSV regulates mitochondrial homeostasis through activation of sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) and peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor gamma coactivator 1–alpha (PGC–1α), while suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and inflammasome–dependent pyroptosis under oxidative stress conditions (Bissacotti et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2024). Systematic reviews suggest that RSV can synergistically modulate key inflammatory pathways, including NF–κB, Mitogen–Activated Protein Kinase, and NLRP3, thereby reducing the levels of pro–inflammatory cytokines and ameliorating tissue pathological damage in diverse models, such as intestinal inflammation, arthritis, neuroinflammation, and liver injury (Meng et al., 2021). Regarding intestinal protection, studies have demonstrated that RSV improves the mucosal architecture of the small and large intestine, attenuates inflammatory cell infiltration, and restores barrier integrity (Gu et al., 2024), providing a theoretical basis for its potential application as an intestinal protective agent or feed additive. In light of research progress on other plant-derived functional additives in livestock and poultry production, it is further suggested that RSV may exert similar, or even more targeted, biological effects in regulating intestinal oxidative stress and barrier homeostasis. Nevertheless, whether RSV can repair intestinal barrier damage specifically induced by TCS has not yet been investigated.

Based on these considerations, the present study aimed to investigate the protective effects of RSV against TCS–induced colonic injury in chickens. Specifically, we examined whether RSV mitigates intestinal barrier dysfunction, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial homeostasis imbalance, with a particular focus on the NLRP3 inflammasome, Nrf2/HO–1 pathway, and SIRT1/PGC–1α signaling axis. This study provides mechanistic insights into TCS–induced intestinal toxicity and explores the potential application of RSV as a protective nutritional strategy in poultry.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and grouping

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northeast Forestry University (approval No. 2022–23) and were conducted in accordance with its guidelines. All procedures involving animals complied with the Guidelines on Ethical Treatment of Experimental Animals (2006, No. 398) issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of China. One hundred healthy 10–week–old chickens (Hy–Line Pink) with similar body weights were acclimated for one week and then randomly assigned to five treatment groups, with 20 chickens per group: blank control (CON), solvent control (VCON), TCS treatment (TCS), RSV treatment (RSV), and combined TCS+RSV treatment (TCS+RSV). TCS was dissolved in corn oil and administered daily via oral gavage at a dose of 150 mg/kg body weight (BW), based on reported avian LD₅₀ data (Reiss et al., 2009) and considering the differences in toxicokinetics between avian and rodent species. Chickens in the VCON group received an equivalent amount of corn oil. RSV was thoroughly mixed into the feed at a concentration of 400 mg/kg feed and fed continuously (Zhang et al., 2017). This dose was applied to evaluate preliminary toxicity and the protective effects of RSV, and the health status of the animals was closely monitored throughout the experiment.

To evaluate the effects of TCS and RSV on the growth performance of chickens, eight chickens were randomly selected from each group as representative samples for performance analysis. Initial BW, final BW, and average daily gain (ADG) were recorded. ADG was calculated using the formula: ADG = (final BW – initial BW) / 28 days. All measurements were taken at the same time each day to minimize the influence of diurnal fluctuations in body weight. Growth performance data (initial BW, final BW, and ADG) are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

All chickens had ad libitum access to feed and water, and the housing environment was maintained with consistent temperature, lighting, and ventilation. After four weeks of treatment, chickens were euthanized by decapitation, and blood and colon tissue samples were collected for subsequent analyses.

Histopathological examination

For Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, colon tissues were immediately fixed in 4% neutral–buffered formaldehyde after collection, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin and sliced at a thickness of 6 μm before staining

For Alcian blue (AB) staining, paraffin–embedded sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with Alcian blue solution (Servicebio, G1049) for 8 minutes, followed by rinsing with distilled water. The nuclei were then counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. All stained sections were examined under a light microscope. For each tissue sample, three sections were randomly selected, and five fields per section were evaluated to ensure comprehensive and representative analysis.

ELISA detection

A 10% colon tissue homogenate was prepared in physiological saline. The levels of CAT, GSH, and total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), as well as Malondialdehyde (MDA), were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) to assess tissue antioxidant capacity. In addition, serum concentrations of D–lactic acid and diamine oxidase (DAO) were measured to evaluate intestinal barrier permeability.

Quantitative real–time PCR (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from colon tissues using the TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Japan) and reverse–transcribed into cDNA with a commercial kit. qRT–PCR was conducted on a LightCycler® 480 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and β–actin served as the reference gene. Immunofluorescence detection

For immunofluorescence staining, colon sections were first dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. After washing with PBS, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies against ZO–1, Claudin–1, and MUC2 were then applied, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at 37°C for 1 h and counterstained with 4′,6–diamidino–2–phenylindole (DAPI). For each sample, three random fields were imaged, and fluorescence intensity was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad, USA). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In each independent experiment, 6 samples per group were randomly selected for molecular analyses, and at least three independent experiments were conducted. After confirming that the data met the assumption of normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (Tukey’s multiple comparison test) was applied. Different letters indicate significant differences among groups (P < 0.05), whereas identical letters denote no significant difference.

Results

RSV improves TCS–induced colonic histomorphological injury

Histopathological evaluation of colonic tissues from each treatment group was conducted using H&E staining (Fig. 1). In the control groups (CON and VCON), the colonic architecture appeared normal, characterized by well–organized villi, intact crypt structures, and the absence of observable tissue damage. In contrast, the TCS–treated group exhibited marked pathological alterations, including villous destruction, disorganized crypts, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration (red arrows), indicating significant TCS–induced colonic injury. Tissues from the RSV group displayed largely preserved morphology, with more intact villi and crypt structures and reduced inflammatory infiltration compared with the TCS group, suggesting a protective effect of RSV. Notably, the TCS+RSV co–treatment group showed further improvement, with restored villous and crypt architecture and a pronounced reduction in inflammation, approaching the structural integrity observed in the control groups. These findings demonstrate that RSV effectively mitigates TCS–induced colonic tissue damage.Fig. 1Histomorphological observation of colonic tissue. Colonic H&E staining. RSV improves TCS–induced damage to colonic tissue barrier function

To assess the protective effects of RSV against TCS–induced colonic injury, a comprehensive evaluation of colonic morphology and barrier–related indicators was performed. AB staining (Fig. 2A) showed that the control groups (CON and VCON) exhibited intact mucosal architecture with uniformly distributed mucin, whereas the TCS group displayed a marked reduction in mucin content, indicating disruption of the mucus barrier. Both RSV and TCS+RSV treatments substantially improved mucin distribution. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed that the integrated optical density in the TCS group was significantly lower than in the control groups (P < 0.05), while RSV and TCS+RSV groups demonstrated significant increases, approaching control values, suggesting that RSV effectively ameliorates TCS–induced mucus barrier impairment. RSV inhibits TCS–induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation and inflammatory response

To investigate the regulatory effects of RSV on TCS–induced intestinal inflammation, the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome–related proteins and inflammatory cytokines was assessed. Western blot analysis (Fig. 4A, B) revealed that TCS treatment significantly upregulated the protein levels of NLRP3, ASC, and Caspase–1 compared with the control groups (CON and VCON, P < 0.05), indicating activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Both RSV alone and TCS+RSV co–treatment markedly reduced the expression of these proteins, approaching control levels. Consistently, qRT–PCR results (Fig. 4C) showed that mRNA levels of NLRP3, Caspase–1, and GSDME were significantly elevated in the TCS group (P < 0.05), whereas RSV and TCS+RSV treatments significantly suppressed their transcription, demonstrating that RSV inhibits inflammasome–related gene expression. RSV alleviates TCS–induced oxidative stress by restoring the Nrf2/HO–1 signaling pathway

To assess the regulatory effects of RSV on TCS–induced oxidative stress, the expression of antioxidant–related proteins and genes was evaluated. Western blot analysis (Fig. 5A, B) showed that TCS treatment significantly upregulated the protein levels of HO–1, NQO1, and Nrf2 compared with the control groups (CON and VCON, P < 0.05), indicating activation of an oxidative stress response. Both RSV alone and TCS+RSV co–treatment markedly reduced the expression of these proteins, approaching control levels. Consistently, qRT–PCR analysis (Fig. 5C) demonstrated that mRNA levels of Nrf2, Keap1, NQO1, and HO–1 were significantly elevated in the TCS group (P < 0.05), whereas RSV and TCS+RSV treatments significantly suppressed their transcription, suggesting that RSV effectively inhibits TCS–induced upregulation of antioxidant stress–related genes. Analysis of oxidative stress indicators (Fig. 5D) revealed that TCS significantly increased MDA content while decreasing GSH levels, total SOD activity, and CAT activity (P < 0.05). RSV alleviates TCS–induced mitochondrial homeostasis imbalance by regulating the SIRT1/PGC–1α signaling pathway

To further investigate the regulatory effects of RSV on mitochondrial function under TCS exposure, the expression of the SIRT1/PGC–1α signaling pathway and genes related to mitochondrial dynamics was systematically evaluated. Western blot analysis (Fig. 6A, B) revealed that TCS treatment significantly upregulated protein levels of SIRT1, PGC–1α, and their downstream transcription factor NRF1 compared with the control groups (P < 0.05), suggesting activation of compensatory pathways for mitochondrial biogenesis. These findings were corroborated at the mRNA level, with SIRT1, PGC–1α, NRF1, and TFAM all significantly elevated (Fig. 6C, P < 0.05). Discussion

TCS is a widely used antibacterial agent, incorporated into over 2,000 consumer and industrial products, and is frequently detected in the human body. Recent studies have demonstrated that TCS exposure can alter the gut microbiota in both healthy animals and ulcerative colitis models, affecting intestinal immune function, inducing colonic inflammation, and exacerbating disease severity (Liu et al., 2022). The intestinal barrier, as the first line of defense against exogenous pathogens and toxins, plays a critical role in maintaining gut homeostasis and systemic immune balance. Damage to this barrier increases intestinal permeability, allowing harmful molecules and bacterial products to penetrate the epithelial layer more readily, thereby triggering mucosal immune responses and systemic inflammation (Horowitz et al., 2023; Kuo et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). RSV, a plant–derived polyphenol, exhibits potent antioxidant and anti–inflammatory properties. Previous studies have shown that RSV can preserve intestinal barrier integrity and counteract damage caused by exogenous toxins by regulating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, activating the Nrf2/HO–1 signaling pathway, upregulating tight junction proteins (ZO–1, Occludin, Claudin–1), and enhancing mucus barrier function (Wang et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2024). In this context, the present study systematically investigates TCS–induced colonic injury and the protective effects of RSV, with particular emphasis on elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Histological analysis revealed that TCS exposure caused marked atrophy of colonic villi, crypt shallowing, epithelial cell shedding, and infiltration of inflammatory cells, indicating a direct disruption of intestinal epithelial architecture and a potential compromise of barrier integrity. At the molecular level, TCS significantly downregulated the expression of tight junction proteins (ZO–1, Occludin, Claudin–1) and mucin MUC2, thereby leading to increased intestinal epithelial permeability. These findings are consistent with previous reports that TCS interferes with gut microbiota composition and tight junction protein expression (Sanidad et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). In the present study, TCS exposure led to a reduction in ADG, a decline that can be primarily attributed to the compromised intestinal physical integrity. Specifically, the observed villous atrophy and epithelial damage likely impaired the gut’s nutrient absorption capacity. Although RSV supplementation effectively alleviated colonic molecular and histological injuries, its impact on overall weight gain remained limited within the 4-week trial. This discrepancy suggests that the recovery of growth performance may lag behind the restoration of molecular barrier function, or alternatively, may require a longer intervention period to manifest.

Intestinal barrier dysfunction not only results from disruption of epithelial structure but is also closely associated with alterations in gut microbiota and their metabolites. Previous studies have indicated that specific beneficial bacteria and their metabolites play important roles in maintaining intestinal metabolic homeostasis and barrier integrity, and their reduction may promote the development of metabolic disorders (Li et al., 2025) . Moreover, avian gut metagenomic studies have confirmed that the composition of gut microbiota and its spatiotemporal dynamics are closely linked to regional intestinal development and barrier function, thereby further influencing host metabolic status (Ma et al., 2024). Therefore, the TCS-induced downregulation of tight junction proteins and disruption of barrier integrity may be partially mediated by alterations in gut microbiota composition and their associated metabolic networks, thereby exacerbating intestinal functional imbalance. This hypothesis warrants further investigation. Importantly, TCS-induced intestinal barrier injury extends beyond local structural damage and is accompanied by systemic effects, including elevated serum D-lactate and DAO levels, which are indicative of increased intestinal permeability and potential endotoxin translocation, ultimately contributing to hepatic and systemic inflammation.

Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death mediated by inflammasomes, characterized by pronounced inflammatory features. It is primarily initiated by the activation of inflammatory caspases, including Caspase–1 and Caspase–4/5/11, which cleave Gasdermin family proteins—particularly Gasdermin D (GSDMD) and GSDME—to release their active N–terminal fragments. These fragments insert into the plasma membrane to form pores, resulting in the leakage of cellular contents and ultimately leading to cell lysis and death (Robinson et al., 2019). Pyroptosis generally occurs more rapidly than apoptosis and is accompanied by the release of large amounts of pro–inflammatory cytokines, such as IL–1β and IL–18. Caspase–1, besides cleaving GSDMD, also mediates the maturation of pro–IL–1β and pro–IL–18, which are secreted through GSDMD–formed pores, thereby amplifying local inflammatory responses (Liu et al., 2024; Stoess et al., 2023). Among inflammasomes, the NLRP3 inflammasome is one of the most extensively studied and consists of NOD–like receptor protein 3, the apoptosis–associated speck–like protein containing a CARD domain, and pro–Caspase–1 (Swanson et al., 2019). In the intestine, moderate pyroptosis contributes to the clearance of infected or damaged epithelial cells, thereby maintaining the dynamic homeostasis of the mucosal barrier. However, excessive activation can lead to sustained release of pro–inflammatory cytokines, activation of intraepithelial immune cells, disruption of intestinal epithelial structure and barrier integrity, and the initiation of chronic inflammatory responses (Zhao et al., 2024b). The present study demonstrates that TCS exposure markedly activates the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, as evidenced by Caspase–1 activation and elevated levels of downstream pro–inflammatory cytokines, including IL–1β and IL–18, suggesting that TCS may exacerbate intestinal inflammation via induction of pyroptosis. RSV intervention significantly suppressed the expression of NLRP3, ASC, and Caspase–1, thereby reducing the secretion of pro–inflammatory cytokines such as IL–1β, IL–18, TNF–α, and IL–6, indicating that RSV can effectively inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and the associated pyroptotic process. This mechanism of inhibiting inflammation through the regulation of key signaling pathways by natural compounds is also reflected in studies of other phytochemicals. For example, He et al. demonstrated that graveoline effectively alleviates acute liver injury by inhibiting the JAK1/STAT3 signaling pathway. Such precise modulation of specific inflammatory signal transduction shares a similar anti-inflammatory pharmacological logic with the intervention of RSV on the NLRP3 pathway in the present study (He et al., 2024). These findings are consistent with previous reports by Bissacotti et al. (Bissacotti et al., 2025), which showed that RSV attenuates immune cell overactivation and tissue inflammation by modulating the P2 × 7/NLRP3 signaling pathway, further confirming the pivotal regulatory role of RSV in environmentally induced intestinal inflammation.

Nrf2 is a critical regulator of ROS and is regarded as a potential therapeutic target for various gastrointestinal disorders. Under basal conditions, Nrf2 remains inactive in the cytoplasm through binding to Keap1. Upon oxidative stress stimulation, Nrf2 dissociates from Keap1 and translocates into the nucleus, where it binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) to upregulate the expression of antioxidant enzymes, including HO–1, NQO1, and SOD, while concurrently inhibiting the production of pro–inflammatory mediators such as TNF–α, IL–6, IL–1β, and iNOS, thereby maintaining redox homeostasis and protecting intestinal integrity (Bourgonje et al., 2023). In the present study, TCS exposure induced pronounced oxidative stress in colonic tissues, as evidenced by increased MDA levels and decreased antioxidant enzyme activities. Concurrently, the Nrf2/HO–1 signaling pathway was significantly upregulated, which likely represents a compensatory cellular response to excessive oxidative damage rather than effective antioxidant protection. RSV supplementation not only restored antioxidant enzyme activities but also attenuated the excessive activation of the Nrf2/HO–1 pathway, suggesting that RSV contributes to reestablishing redox homeostasis rather than merely enhancing antioxidant signaling.

Mitochondria are central regulators of cellular energy metabolism and oxidative stress responses, and their homeostasis relies on a precise balance between mitochondrial biogenesis and dynamics, including fusion and fission processes (Chen et al., 2023c). Cellular and organismal health depends on the tight regulation of this balance, whereas large–scale remodeling of the mitochondrial network is often associated with cellular damage and disease. Dysfunction of key dynamic proteins, such as DRP1, MFN1, MFN2 and OPA1, can result in abnormal mitochondrial morphology, impaired energy metabolism, and is closely linked to neurodegenerative disorders, metabolic syndrome, and inflammatory diseases (Yapa et al., 2021). Consequently, mitochondrial dynamics dysfunction is recognized as a central pathogenic mechanism in multiple pathological conditions.

In the present study, TCS exposure significantly upregulated the expression of SIRT1, PGC–1α, and its downstream transcription factor NRF1, accompanied by increased expression of mitochondrial dynamics–related genes, including MFN1, MFN2, OPA1, DRP1, FIS1, and MFF. These findings suggest that TCS induces excessive activation of the SIRT1/PGC–1α signaling axis, triggering compensatory mitochondrial biogenesis responses. Although moderate activation of the SIRT1/PGC–1α axis supports mitochondrial biogenesis, enhances antioxidant capacity, and maintains cellular energy homeostasis (Zhao et al., 2024a), previous studies have indicated that persistent or excessive activation may disrupt the equilibrium between biogenesis, dynamics (fusion/fission), and clearance, leading to mitochondrial network dysfunction, reduced membrane potential, excessive ROS accumulation, impaired tissue energy homeostasis and function, and induction of systemic oxidative stress and inflammatory responses (Alcendor et al., 2007; Chen et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2025). Besides SIRT1, other members of the silent information regulator family also play critical roles in the regulation of inflammation. For example, SIRT6 has been shown to alleviate lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory responses by inhibiting the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway (Wang et al., 2024), highlighting the multi-target, synergistic regulatory features of the sirtuin family in anti-inflammatory processes. This finding further supports the potential link between aberrant activation of the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis and enhanced inflammatory responses in our study, and suggests that TCS-induced injury may involve a broader sirtuin-mediated regulatory network rather than being limited to a single signaling pathway.

Notably, RSV intervention significantly restored the excessive activation of the SIRT1/PGC–1α axis induced by TCS and concurrently reduced the expression of mitochondrial dynamics–related genes, including MFN2, DRP1, and FIS1, to levels comparable to the control group. These findings suggest that RSV exerts a bidirectional regulatory effect on mitochondrial homeostasis. Previous studies have demonstrated that moderate activation of the SIRT1/PGC–1α pathway by RSV can promote mitochondrial biogenesis and enhance antioxidant capacity. Simultaneously, RSV can optimize mitochondrial network structure, improve energy metabolism, and attenuate oxidative stress by facilitating mitophagy to remove damaged mitochondria (Wu et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2022). In this study, TCS exposure induced abnormal upregulation of SIRT1, PGC–1α, and mitochondrial dynamics–related genes, indicating excessive mitochondrial remodeling. RSV intervention attenuated this overactivation and restored the expression of mitochondrial fusion and fission regulators toward physiological levels, suggesting a normalizing rather than inhibitory role of RSV in mitochondrial homeostasis. Furthermore, based on the overall regulatory features of the sirtuin family, it is plausible that the protective effects of RSV may not only depend on modulation of the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis, but also involve the coordinated regulation of anti-inflammatory molecules such as SIRT6, thereby jointly suppressing excessive activation of inflammatory signaling pathways. In this way, RSV may exert comprehensive protective effects along the “mitochondrial homeostasis–inflammation” axis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that RSV markedly alleviates TCS–induced intestinal barrier damage through multiple mechanisms. It restores intestinal epithelial architecture and tight junction protein expression, thereby reinforcing the mucus barrier; suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation and downstream pro–inflammatory cytokines; attenuates oxidative stress by modulating the Nrf2 signaling pathway; and maintains mitochondrial homeostasis and energy metabolism via the SIRT1/PGC–1α axis. By simultaneously targeting these interconnected pathways, RSV demonstrates a “multi–target, systemic protective” effect, highlighting its potential as an effective, feasible, and safe natural agent for preventing and mitigating intestinal injury and barrier dysfunction induced by environmental toxins. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the application of RSV in promoting intestinal health in poultry.
