Potential neuroprotective and antidepressant effects of Ajwa date extract in an animal model of chronic mild stress
Authors: Syed Mohammed Basheeruddin Asdaq, Mohd. Imran, Walaa F. Alsanie, Abdulhakeem S. Alamri, Majid Alhomrani, Amal F. Alshammary, Rokeya Sultana, Basheerahmed Abdulaziz Mannasaheb, Syed Imam Rabbani, Mansour Almuqbil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Depression, often induced by chronic stress, results in significant behavioral and biochemical changes in the brain. Due to the limited effectiveness and potential side effects of traditional antidepressants, there is growing interest in exploring alternative therapies. Natural substances, particularly those found in foods with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory benefits, have emerged as promising candidates. Phoenix dactylifera (Ajwa date), rich in bioactive compounds like polyphenols and flavonoids, may help manage stress-induced depression. This study assessed the antidepressant and neuroprotective effects of Ajwa date seed powder extract (ADSP) and fruit pulp extract (AFP) in a chronic mild stress (CMS) mouse model. Male mice were treated with two doses of ADSP (400 and 800 mg/kg) and AFP (500 and 1000 mg/kg) daily for three weeks in their respective groups. To assess depressive-like behaviors, the study employed behavioral assessments such as the tail suspension test (TST) and the sucrose preference test (SPT). Biochemical analyses measured plasma corticosterone, nitrite levels, antioxidant enzyme activities (catalase, GSH, SOD, GPx), and cytokine levels (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, CRP, IL-10, TGF-β1). Administration of both ADSP and AFP led to a significant decrease in immobility time during the TST (p < 0.001) and a marked increase in sucrose preference in the SPT (p < 0.001), reflecting antidepressant-like activity. Additionally, both extracts significantly reduced plasma corticosterone and nitrite concentrations (p < 0.001), implying a modulatory effect on the HPA axis and oxidative stress. Antioxidant enzyme activities were elevated (p < 0.001), and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, CRP) were reduced, while anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β1) were increased (p < 0.001). ADSP and AFP demonstrate notable antidepressant and neuroprotective properties, primarily attributed to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms. A comparison analysis revealed that AFP was more effective in reducing inflammation and enhancing behavioral outcomes. These results highlight the potential of food-based compounds, such as those found in Ajwa dates, as natural alternatives for addressing stress-related depression and neuroinflammation.

Background

Depression is a widespread psychiatric disorder characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia, feelings of worthlessness, and cognitive impairments. It is closely linked to chronic stress, which disrupts the balance of neurotransmitters, exacerbates oxidative stress, and induces neuroinflammation in the brain1. Chronic stress triggers excessive activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in elevated levels of glucocorticoids, especially cortisol, that can lead to hippocampal impairment and neurodegenerative changes2. Conventional antidepressant treatments, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), aim to correct these neurotransmitter imbalances. However, approximately one-third of patients do not respond effectively to these medications, and others experience significant side effects3,4. This has spurred interest in exploring alternative therapeutic strategies, including the use of plant-based compounds with neuroprotective and antidepressant potential. Previous studies have highlighted the potential of various natural products as effective treatment options for depressive disorders. These include Allium cepa5, Ganoderma mediosinense6, Ocimum basilicum7, and Citrus reticulata var8.

Phoenix dactylifera (date palm) is one of the many plants investigated for its potential antidepressant properties, with particular attention given to the Ajwa variety, native to Saudi Arabia. Traditionally, dried fruits have been consumed worldwide to enhance blood iron levels and aid in the treatment of anemia. In folk medicine, they are also used to support brain health, muscle development, and overall vitality9. The fruits are known for their various nutritional benefits, although allergic reactions may occur in rare cases. Ajwa dates are notable for their dense nutritional content, containing abundant polyphenols, flavonoids, carotenoids, and dietary fiber10. These bioactive compounds contribute to their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties, making them promising candidates for managing neurodegenerative diseases and depression. While much of the focus has been on the fruit pulp of Ajwa dates, recent studies have also examined the potential therapeutic effects of date seeds, which are often discarded as waste but contain an equally impressive array of bioactive compounds, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, and sterols (Chao et al., 2007; Hamad et al.11).

Previous studies have demonstrated that both the fruit pulp and seeds of Phoenix dactylifera possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties. The Ajwa fruit pulp has been shown to enhance antioxidant defenses, reduce lipid peroxidation, and protect against neuronal damage in animal models of neurodegenerative diseases12,13. For example, extract from Ajwa fruit pulp has been shown to alleviate oxidative stress and lower inflammatory cytokine levels in rodent models of Alzheimer’s disease14. Similarly, Ajwa date seed powder (ADSP) has exhibited strong antioxidant properties, leading to reduced oxidative stress markers and enhanced cognitive performance in several preclinical studies15 . However, despite the extensive research on the health benefits of both Ajwa fruit pulp and seed extracts, no study has yet directly compared the neuroprotective and antidepressant effects of these two forms of Phoenix dactylifera.

Current research has extensively characterized the individual effects of Ajwa date fruit pulp and seed extracts, yet their comparative efficacy against depression and neuroinflammation remains unexplored. While both extracts demonstrate antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, their distinct phytochemical compositions suggest potentially divergent therapeutic mechanisms. The fruit pulp, enriched with sugars, fiber, and carotenoids11, appears particularly effective in modulating inflammatory pathways. In contrast, the seed extract, with its higher concentration of polyphenols and flavonoids13, likely exerts stronger antioxidant effects. These pharmacological differences underscore the need for a systematic comparison of their neuroprotective potential in chronic stress-induced depression, a multifactorial condition involving oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and HPA axis dysfunction.

This study employs a chronic mild stress (CMS) rodent model to conduct a head-to-head evaluation of Ajwa date seed powder extract (ADSP) and fruit pulp extract (AFP). Through integrated behavioral, biochemical, and inflammatory profiling, we aim to: (1) establish their relative antidepressant efficacy, (2) characterize their distinct neuroprotective mechanisms, and (3) identify the most promising candidate for further development. Our findings could advance the formulation of evidence-based, natural alternatives for managing stress-related mood disorders and associated neurodegeneration, potentially addressing limitations of current pharmacotherapies.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures in this study were performed in strict compliance with the ethical guidelines approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of AlMaarefa University (IRB24-007) and conducted according to the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for animal research reporting. We adhered to international standards for humane animal experimentation by implementing veterinary-supervised protocols, environmental enrichment, pain management measures, and humane endpoint criteria. Every effort was made to minimize animal discomfort while ensuring scientific validity through appropriate sample size calculations and rigorous experimental design that fully complied with institutional animal welfare and biosafety regulations.

Study samples

Albino mice (male) weighing between 20 and 25 g and aged 10–14 weeks were housed in an air-conditioned environment under standard animal care conditions. All variables were carefully controlled throughout the experiment to prevent any impact on the animals’ behavior. The mice were provided with standard animal pellets obtained from a local supplier.

Preparation of Ajwa seed and fruit extracts

The methanol extracts of Ajwa seed and fruit pulp were prepared using a standardized method. The seeds and pulp were carefully separated, washed with distilled water, and fully dried. After drying, the materials were finely powdered, with the seeds first ground manually using a mortar and pestle, followed by additional processing with an electronic grinder. Approximately 100 g of each powdered material was separately soaked in 1 L of 97% methanol to obtain the methanol extracts. This mixture was subjected to magnetic stirring for 3 h at 37 °C. The extracts were then filtered for purification and concentrated using a rotary evaporator at reduced pressure and 40 °C to isolate the raw active components. Both extracts were stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis16. The percentage yield of the extracts was calculated using the following equation:

Phytochemical screening

Phytochemical screening of Ajwa seed and fruit pulp extracts was performed based on a previously published protocol17. Carbohydrates were detected by adding 1000 µL of iodine solution to 3000 µL of methanol extracts from the seeds and pulp. Carbohydrates were identified by the development of a purple color. To detect saponins, 5 mL of distilled water was added to 5 mL of each extract in a test tube, which was then shaken vigorously and heated. The formation of stable froth indicated the presence of saponins. For the detection of condensed tannins, 2000 µL of extract was mixed with 2000 µL of distilled water in a vial, and a few drops of FeCl3 were added. The appearance of a green precipitate signified the presence of tannins. Flavonoids were identified by adding 2 mL of extract to a 20% sodium hydroxide solution, which resulted in a strong yellow color. After adding dilute HCl, the yellow color disappeared, confirming the presence of flavonoids. Finally, phenolic compounds were detected by adding a few drops of FeCl3 to the extracts, resulting in a blue-black color, indicating the presence of phenolic compounds.

Quantitative determination of Ajwa date seed powder extract

The total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), and total tannin content (TTC) of the ajwa seed (ADSP) and fruit (AFP) extracts were measured using quantitative spectrophotometric techniques. The results were represented as equivalents of gallic acid (GAE), quercetin (QE), and catechin (CE), respectively. These were quantified using standard calibration curves for gallic acid, quercetin, and catechin. To prevent quantification artifacts from potential plateau effects, sample absorbances were maintained between 0.2 and 1.0 AU through appropriate dilution, with calibration curves verified every 10 samples using mid-range standards. Any measurements approaching the upper limit of linearity (≥ 80% of maximum standard) were automatically repeated at higher dilution.

Determination of phenolic contents of methanolic extract of Ajwa date seed powder

The Folin-Ciocalteu method, described by Isika et al.18, was used to determine the phenolic acid content in the methanolic extract of both extracts. Gallic acid (25 mg) was dissolved in 25 ml of methanol to prepare a standard solution (1 mg/ml). Test extracts (10 mg) were dissolved in 10 ml of methanol, and various concentrations of gallic acid (100 to 600 µg/ml) were used for the estimation. Each concentration was mixed with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, sodium carbonate, and distilled water to a final volume of 25 ml. A UV spectrometer was used to measure the absorbance at 750 nm following a 90-minute incubation. The gallic acid equivalent of the phenolic content was calculated using a standard calibration curve.

Determination of flavonoids of the methanolic extract of Ajwa date seed powder

The aluminium chloride colorimetric method, as reported by Isika et al.18, was used to determine the flavonoid content of the methanolic extract of both extracts. Quercetin (25 mg) was dissolved in 25 ml of methanol to make a standard solution (1 mg/ml). After being diluted, the extract had a concentration of 100 µg/ml. The extract was then combined with potassium acetate, methanol, aluminum chloride, and water. Absorbance was measured at 415 nm, and a standard calibration curve for quercetin was generated. The flavonoid content was expressed as quercetin equivalents.

Determination of total tannins contents of methanolic extract of Ajwa date seed powder

The method described by Mani et al.19. was used to determine the total tannin concentration in both extracts. In this technique, 200 µL of the Ajwa extract (0.1 mg/mL) was combined with 1.5 mL of a 4% vanillin solution in methanol and 750 µL of concentrated HCl in a test tube. The mixture was then allowed to stand for fifteen minutes at room temperature in the dark to ensure full reaction. At 500 nm, absorbance was measured. Three separate measurements were used to calculate the overall tannin concentration, which was then expressed as catechin equivalents per gram of dried ADSP and AFP.

Chemicals and reagents

Imipramine hydrochloride [≥ 99% (TLC)] was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, while Ajwa dates were sourced from the local market in Al-Madinah City, Saudi Arabia. All chemicals and consumables utilized in the study were sourced from trusted suppliers, and their labels were kept for future reference. The dosage of ADSP20 and AFP21 in rats was available from literature that was further converted to a mouse dose as per the FDA guidelines (https://www.dcchemicals.com/dose-conversion.html), and the dosage of imipramine was selected as per our earlier research findings22.

Experimental grouping

The experimental animals were assigned to six groups, each consisting of eight. Group I received a vehicle treatment of 0.2% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) in 0.9% sodium chloride. Groups II and III administered Ajwa date seed powder (ADSP) at low (400 mg/kg) and high (800 mg/kg) doses, respectively. Groups IV and V received low (500 mg/kg) and high (1000 mg/kg) doses of Ajwa fruit pulp extract (AFP). Group VI was treated with imipramine at a 15 mg/kg dose. The doses of the extracts were selected based on research reported in the literature15,19,20 . All treatments were given daily for three weeks, with chronic mild stress (CMS) applied 30 min after oral administration of the substances. On day 21, 60 min after CMS exposure, a sucrose preference test was conducted22. Additionally, on day 21, the locomotor activity of the mice was measured 60 min after stress exposure, followed by the tail suspension test (TST) on day 22. The details of the experimental grouping and the steps involved in conducting the study are outlined in Fig. 1.

Chronic mild stress (CMS)

Neuronal toxicity induced by prolonged stress is a known contributor to depression. In this study, mice were exposed to chronic mild stress (CMS) using a previously established method23. The mice were subjected to a daily stress protocol for three weeks, with the designated treatments given 30 min before each stress exposure. During the first week of CMS, the stressors included immobilization for 2 h on day 1, deprivation of water by providing empty bottles for 1 h on day 2, enforced darkness at night on day 3, exposure to foreign objects for 24 h on day 4, tilting of cages at a 45-degree angle for 7 h on day 6, and tail pinching for 30 s on day 7. The sequence of these stressors varied throughout the experiment to prevent habituation.

Measurement of locomotor activity

Locomotor activity was systematically assessed to control for potential confounding effects of treatment-induced hyperactivity or sedation on antidepressant-like behaviors observed in the tail suspension test (TST). Using an established open-field paradigm24, we quantified horizontal movement by measuring total distance traveled (cm) and grid crossings during a 10-minute session. This evaluation was conducted on day 21 of the chronic mild stress protocol, immediately before final treatment administration, to obtain baseline activity measurements unaffected by acute pharmacological effects. The standardized testing conditions (illumination: 50 lx; apparatus dimensions: 40 × 40 × 35 cm; acclimation period: 5 min) ensured reliable detection of true locomotor changes while minimizing environmental stress.

Tail suspension test (TST)

The Tail Suspension Test (TST) was conducted to evaluate depressive-like behavior following established protocols25. Each mouse was individually suspended by its tail (approximately 1 cm from the tip) using non-toxic adhesive tape in a sound-attenuated chamber (50 × 50 × 50 cm), allowing free-hanging movement while preventing escape or climbing behaviors. Immobility time (defined as the complete absence of limb or body movements) was automatically recorded for 6 min using a video tracking system (ANY-maze, Stoelting Co.). This test was performed on treatment day 22, 60 min after the final administration of Ajwa date seed powder (ADSP) or imipramine (15 mg/kg, i.p.), to assess their antidepressant efficacy against chronic mild stress (CMS)-induced behavioral despair. A significant reduction in immobility time compared to CMS controls was interpreted as an antidepressant-like effect, while concurrent locomotor testing (open field) controlled for potential psychomotor influences.

Sucrose preference test

The sucrose preference test was performed following an established protocol (Dhingra et al., 2015) with modifications for chronic stress assessment. After a 2-day acclimation period with ad libitum access to 1% sucrose solution, mice underwent a 23-hour fasting period followed by a baseline preference test. In this test, animals were simultaneously presented with two bottles - one containing 1% sucrose solution and the other with plain water - for 1 h. Baseline sucrose preference was calculated as: (sucrose intake/total fluid intake) × 100. Following 21 days of chronic mild stress (CMS) and concurrent treatment administration, the test was repeated under identical conditions to evaluate treatment efficacy. A significant decrease in sucrose preference (> 30%) in CMS controls compared to baseline was interpreted as anhedonia, a core symptom of depression, while restoration of sucrose preference in treated groups indicated antidepressant-like activity. Bottle positions were alternated between tests to control for side preference artifacts.

Nitrite and corticosterone measurements

On day 22 of the experimental protocol, blood samples were collected following an established procedure26 with modifications for stress minimization. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine-xylazine cocktail (75 mg/kg and 16 mg/kg, respectively) delivered in a single syringe. Prior to collection, the tail was warmed for 2 min in a 37 °C water bath to achieve optimal vasodilation. Approximately 100 µL of blood was then aseptically collected from the lateral tail vein using a 26-gauge needle attached to a heparinized 0.5 mL insulin syringe under direct supervision by experienced researchers. Immediate hemostasis was achieved by applying gentle pressure with sterile gauze for 30 s post-collection. Blood samples were immediately transferred to pre-chilled EDTA tubes and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain plasma, which was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until analysis of corticosterone and nitrite concentrations. This protocol ensured minimal stress and maximal sample integrity while complying with all ethical guidelines for rodent blood collection.

Biochemical measurements

On the 23rd day, mice were anesthetized to facilitate blood collection. Following blood collection, a trained researcher performed humane euthanasia by decapitation, using sharp, sterilized surgical scissors to ensure a quick and minimally stressful procedure. Once decapitated, the brain tissues were carefully and aseptically extracted from the skull. The procedure was executed with meticulous care to prevent any damage to brain structures, ensuring the preservation of tissue integrity necessary for downstream biochemical and histological analysis. Rigorous handling and extraction protocols were employed to obtain high-quality samples suitable for investigating biochemical markers aligned with the study’s aims. After that, the brain samples were cleaned in a cold buffer (pH 7.4) that contained 0.02 Methylenediamine tetraacetic acid, 0.1 M Tris, and 0.25 M sucrose. The washed brain tissue was homogenized and subsequently centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. This supernatant was used for biochemical assessments, including the measurement of catalase activity, superoxide dismutase, reduced glutathione, Glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and Malondialdehyde (MDA)22. The concentrations of tumor necrotic factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) were measured using specific ELISA kits according to the method described by Mani et al.19.

Data analysis

Data from eight animals per group (n = 8) were analyzed using GraphPad Prism and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. After verifying normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test), one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons, with significance thresholds set at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***) when compared to control group in all figures (2–10). Results are reported with F-statistics (including degrees of freedom, e.g., F (5,42) and exact p-values (unless p < 0.001), supplemented by effect sizes (η²) to quantify treatment magnitude. For example, “F (5,42) = 1085.4, p < 0.001, η² = 0.992” indicates near-complete separation between groups.

Sucrose preference model

Figure 4 shows the sucrose preference percentage before and after 21 days of Chronic Mild Stress (CMS) in experimental animals, a measure of anhedonia often used to assess depressive-like behavior. Before CMS, all treatment groups, including the control, exhibited similar sucrose preferences (around 50%) [(F (5,42) = 0.742, p = 0.596)]. Following the 21-day CMS protocol, the control group exhibited a notable decrease in sucrose preference, falling to 17%, reflecting a significant increase in anhedonia [F (5,42) = 178.3, p < 0.001].

In contrast, treatment with low-dose ADSP (ADSP-LD) and high-dose ADSP (ADSP-HD) led to significantly higher sucrose preference after CMS, with p < 0.01 for ADSP-LD and p < 0.001 for ADSP-HD compared to the control. Similarly, both low-dose AFP (AFP-LD) and high-dose AFP (AFP-HD) treatment groups showed significant improvements in sucrose preference post-CMS, with p < 0.001 for both doses. The reference drug imipramine also significantly increased sucrose preference (p < 0.001). These results suggest that treatment with ADSP, AFP, and imipramine mitigates the effects of chronic stress, effectively reducing anhedonia and indicating potential antidepressant-like effects.

Plasma nitrite and corticosterone

Figure 5 presents the levels of plasma nitrite and corticosterone in experimental animals following various treatments. Plasma nitrite, a marker of nitric oxide production and oxidative stress, was significantly reduced in all treatment groups compared to the control [F (5,42) = 215.7, p < 0.001)]. The control group had a plasma nitrite level of 37 µg/ml. Treatment with low-dose ADSP (ADSP-LD), high-dose ADSP (ADSP-HD), low-dose AFP (AFP-LD), and high-dose AFP (AFP-HD) significantly lowered plasma nitrite levels, with p < 0.001 for all treatment groups compared to the control. The reference drug, imipramine, also significantly decreased plasma nitrite levels (p < 0.001).

Similarly, plasma corticosterone, a hormone commonly elevated during stress, was significantly decreased in treatment groups [F(5,42) = 86.4, p < 0.001]. The control group exhibited a corticosterone level of 29 µg/ml. After treatment, both ADSP-LD and AFP-LD reduced corticosterone levels to 23 µg/ml and 20 µg/ml, respectively, with p < 0.01 for ADSP-LD and p < 0.001 for ADSP-HD, AFP-HD, and imipramine compared to the control. High-dose ADSP (ADSP-HD) and AFP (AFP-HD) treatments, as well as imipramine, led to the lowest levels of corticosterone (20 µg/ml and 15 µg/ml, respectively), with highly significant reductions (p < 0.001).

These results suggest that treatments with ADSP, AFP, and imipramine effectively lower both plasma nitrite and corticosterone levels, indicating their potential in reducing oxidative stress and stress-induced hormonal responses.

Discussion

The therapeutic use of medicinal plants, especially those exhibiting potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, has garnered increasing scientific interest for their potential role in supporting mental health and alleviating symptoms associated with mood disorders. One such plant, Phoenix dactylifera, commonly known as Ajwa date, is of particular significance due to its rich phytochemical profile and recognized nutraceutical value. Traditionally consumed in various cultures for their health-promoting effects, Ajwa dates have recently been explored for their neuroprotective potential, particularly regarding oxidative stress and neuroinflammation—two key pathological mechanisms implicated in the development of depression and other neuropsychiatric disorders. In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the antidepressant-like and neuroprotective effects of extracts derived from Ajwa date seed powder (ADSP) and Ajwa fruit pulp (AFP) using well-established animal models of chronic mild stress (CMS), a paradigm known to induce behavioral and biochemical changes analogous to human depression. Our investigation encompassed a detailed assessment of behavioral parameters, oxidative stress markers, and inflammatory mediators. The results revealed that both ADSP and AFP extracts exhibited significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, which were associated with marked improvements in depressive-like behaviors in stressed animals. Notably, the AFP extract demonstrated superior efficacy in mitigating the elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative biomarkers, as well as in restoring behavioral deficits, compared to the seed extract. These findings suggest that the fruit pulp of Phoenix dactylifera may offer greater therapeutic promise than its seed counterpart in modulating neuroinflammation and oxidative stress-related pathologies involved in depression. This study adds to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of Ajwa dates as a functional food with potential psychotropic benefits20.

Chronic stress, particularly CMS, leads to an imbalance in oxidative stress and inflammation in the brain, which is closely associated with the pathophysiology of depression27. Emerging evidence suggests this imbalance creates a vicious cycle where oxidative damage exacerbates neuroinflammation, which in turn generates more reactive oxygen species, ultimately leading to neuronal dysfunction and depressive behaviors28. This study shows that both ADSP and AFP extracts effectively reduced plasma nitrite levels, MDA (malondialdehyde), and other markers of oxidative stress. These results corroborate previous studies that have shown that date products possess significant antioxidant properties29. The reduction in nitrite levels is particularly significant as excessive nitric oxide can form peroxynitrite, a potent neurotoxic compound30. The reduction in nitrite levels following ADSP and AFP treatment suggests a decrease in nitrosative stress, a key contributor to neurodegenerative diseases31. The antioxidant effects were further confirmed by the significant increases in catalase, reduced glutathione, and superoxide dismutase levels following treatment with both extracts. These enzymatic antioxidants work synergistically to neutralize different reactive oxygen species, with catalase breaking down hydrogen peroxide while SOD converts superoxide radicals32. However, higher doses of AFP exhibited more potent antioxidant effects compared to ADSP, highlighting the superior efficacy of Ajwa fruit pulp in scavenging free radicals and protecting against oxidative damage20.

The ability of both ADSP and AFP to reduce lipid peroxidation, as indicated by decreased MDA levels, is a noteworthy finding, given the critical role of lipid peroxidation in neurodegenerative processes and depression33. MDA is particularly damaging as it can form cross-links with proteins and DNA, leading to cellular dysfunction34. The antioxidant action of these extracts is attributed to their high polyphenol and flavonoid content, which has been shown to enhance the body’s natural antioxidant defenses35. Recent studies have demonstrated that polyphenols can upregulate endogenous antioxidant systems through activation of the Nrf2-ARE pathway, providing long-lasting protection against oxidative stress36.

In addition to their antioxidant properties, both Ajwa seed and fruit extracts demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory effects by modulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, and CRP. This is in line with previous reports indicating that date palm products possess anti-inflammatory effects, particularly in conditions like diabetes and inflammatory bowel disease37,38. The cytokine network in depression is particularly complex, with pro-inflammatory cytokines activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis while simultaneously being stimulated by cortisol, creating a self-perpetuating cycle39. In our study, CMS significantly increased the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, while treatment with both ADSP and AFP extracts reduced these levels. Notably, the fruit pulp extract (AFP) was more effective in reducing TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6 levels, suggesting that AFP may have a stronger anti-inflammatory action than ADSP. This finding is consistent with earlier studies that reported a greater anti-inflammatory effect of Ajwa fruit pulp compared to the seed extract29. The superior anti-inflammatory activity of AFP may be attributed to its higher content of specific flavonoids like apigenin and luteolin, which have been shown to potently inhibit NF-κB signaling40.

Behavioral assays are essential in assessing the potential antidepressant effects of plant extracts41. In this study, we used the tail suspension test (TST) and sucrose preference test to assess depressive-like behavior and anhedonia, respectively. These tests evaluate different but complementary aspects of depression: behavioral despair in TST and loss of pleasure in SPT, mirroring the core symptoms of human depression42. In the TST, CMS-induced immobility was significantly reduced following treatment with both ADSP and AFP, particularly at higher doses. These results align with those reported by Abdullah et al.43, who showed that Ajwa date extract exhibited mood-stabilizing properties. Additionally, both extracts significantly enhanced locomotor activity, further supporting their potential as central nervous system stimulants. The improvement in locomotor activity is particularly important as it rules out sedation or motor impairment as confounding factors in the antidepressant-like effects44.

The sucrose preference test, which is a measure of anhedonia, revealed that CMS reduced the preference for sucrose, a common symptom of depression. Both ADSP and AFP treatment reversed this decrease in sucrose preference in a dose-dependent manner. High doses of both extracts, along with imipramine (a common tricyclic antidepressant), resulted in a recovery of sucrose preference, emphasizing their potential to alleviate depression-induced anhedonia. Anhedonia is particularly resistant to many current antidepressants, making these findings clinically relevant45. However, it was observed that the AFP extract, particularly at higher doses, had a more pronounced effect on behavioral outcomes, including both immobility time and sucrose preference, suggesting that the fruit pulp extract might be more effective than the seed powder in relieving depressive symptoms.

The role of neuroinflammation in depression is well-established, with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reduced levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines being associated with depressive symptoms20. In this study, both ADSP and AFP were found to reduce levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which are elevated during chronic stress and have been implicated in the development of depression. These cytokines can directly affect neurotransmitter metabolism, reducing serotonin synthesis while increasing glutamate excitotoxicity, thereby contributing to depressive symptoms3. Additionally, both treatments led to a significant increase in the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 and TGF-β1, which are known to play protective roles in the brain by modulating immune responses and promoting neuronal repair46. Notably, the AFP extract exhibited more potent effects on increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines, particularly TGF-β1 and IL-10, which may contribute to its enhanced neuroprotective and antidepressant effects. IL-10 is particularly important as it can inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokine production while promoting neurotrophic factor expression47.

While both ADSP and AFP extracts showed significant therapeutic effects in reducing oxidative stress and inflammation and improving behavioral symptoms, our data suggest that Ajwa fruit pulp extract (AFP) is generally more effective than the seed extract (ADSP), particularly in reducing pro-inflammatory mediators and enhancing behavioral outcomes. This is consistent with the findings of Mani et al.20, who reported that Ajwa date seed exhibited potent antidepressant-like effects, though our results indicate that the fruit pulp extract may have a broader spectrum of action. The higher flavonoid and polyphenol content in the fruit pulp48,49 could account for the enhanced effectiveness of AFP, particularly in modulating neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, which are central to the pathophysiology of depression. Recent metabolomic studies have identified several unique phenolic compounds in AFP that are either absent or present in lower concentrations in ADSP, which may explain its superior efficacy50.

Limitations

While this study provides compelling evidence for the neuroprotective and antidepressant effects of Ajwa date extracts, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although rodent models are essential for preclinical screening, translational differences in stress response systems and drug metabolism may affect direct extrapolation to human depression. However, our multimodal approach - combining validated behavioral tests (TST, sucrose preference) with comprehensive biochemical analyses (reduced oxidative stress [↓MDA, ↑SOD/GSH] and modulated neuroinflammation [↓TNF-α/IL-6, ↑IL-10]) - aligns with established depression biomarkers and collectively supports neuroprotection. Second, while the 3-week treatment period demonstrated efficacy using the well-validated CMS model, longer-term studies are needed to evaluate sustained effects and chronic toxicity, as dedicated safety assessments (e.g., histopathology, clinical chemistry) were not conducted in this investigation. Third, while doses were selected based on published safety data20, systematic toxicity profiling remains an essential future step. Fourth, the absence of histopathological analyses (e.g., Nissl or Hematoxylin & Eosin staining) should be noted; nonetheless, the functional and biochemical outcomes employed here are well-established proxies of neuroprotection, and histological validation will be a valuable addition in future studies. Fifth, while LC-MS/MS quantification was not performed, standardized phytochemical methods confirmed key bioactive compounds (phenolics, flavonoids). Finally, although mechanistic pathways were inferred from biochemical changes and aligned with established actions of date polyphenols (e.g., NF-κB/Nrf2 modulation), detailed molecular investigations remain necessary. These limitations reflect the study’s focus on foundational proof-of-concept data, consistent with early-phase translational research paradigms.

Recommendations

Future studies should: (1) incorporate histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses to directly evaluate neuroprotection; (2) employ LC-MS/MS to quantify specific bioactive constituents; (3) investigate molecular mechanisms through targeted omics approaches; (4) examine long-term safety and efficacy in chronic models; and (5) control for additional variables like microbiota composition. These steps will enhance clinical translatability while building on this study’s mechanistic framework.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both Ajwa date seed powder (ADSP) and fruit pulp extract (AFP) exhibit significant neuroprotective and antidepressant-like effects in a chronic mild stress model, as evidenced by their ability to reduce oxidative stress (decreased MDA and nitrites), modulate neuroinflammation (reduced TNF-α and IL-6; increased IL-10), and improve depressive-like behaviors in validated tests (TST and sucrose preference). Notably, the fruit pulp extract (AFP) showed superior efficacy, likely due to its higher content of bioactive phenolics and flavonoids. These findings support the potential of Ajwa dates, particularly AFP, as a natural therapeutic option for stress-related mental health conditions. Future studies should focus on elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms, establishing optimal dosing and long-term safety profiles, and validating these effects in human clinical populations through advanced analytical techniques and controlled trials. This research provides a strong foundation for developing standardized, evidence-based phytomedicines from this traditional food source.
