Abstract.

We study nonlocal convolution-type operators with singular, possibly anisotropic kernels. Our main objective is to establish and quantify their nonlocal-to-local convergence to a local differential operator with natural boundary conditions, as the kernels concentrate at the origin in a suitable way. Such convergence results provide a useful tool for the physical justification of mathematical models, particularly in situations where the desired local differential operator cannot be directly derived from microscopic laws. The present work substantially extends previous results by allowing kernels with stronger singularities (comparable to those of fractional Laplacians), anisotropic and non-localized kernels, and by proving strong convergence in general
L^{p}
spaces together with explicit convergence rates.

Keywords:
Nonlocal-to-local convergence, singular integral operators, nonlocal operators, anisotropic kernels, convergence rates, singular limits.

1  Introduction

We consider nonlocal convolution type operators of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}_{\ast}u(x)&=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\Omega}J(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\underset{r\searrow 0}{\lim}\,\int_{\Omega\cap\{\left|x-y\right|\geq r\}}J(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\end{split}

(1.1)

for all
x\in\Omega
and any suitable function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a sufficiently smooth domain and

J:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptJ(x)=\frac{\rho(x)}{\left|x\right|^{2}},

(1.2)

is a prescribed interaction kernel.
Throughout this paper, the function
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is supposed to be even and has to fulfill certain integrability conditions that will be specified in Section
2
. In particular, the convolution kernel
J
is also even. Because of this symmetry, the nonlocal energy associated with
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}_{\ast}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\ast}^{\Omega}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}_{\ast}u(x)\;u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J(x-y)\big|u(x)-u(y)\big|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

for any suitable function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Main objective of the paper.

The main goal of this paper is to study the
nonlocal-to-local convergence
of nonlocal operators as introduced in (
1.1
).
This means that we intend to show that such a nonlocal operator converges to a local differential operator in some suitable sense as the function
\rho
approaches a multiple of the
\delta
-distribution. To formulate this in a mathematically precise way, we define the functions

\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)\coloneqq\varepsilon^{-n}\,\rho\big(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\big),

(1.3)

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002ptJ_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{\rho(x)}{\left|x\right|^{2}}

(1.4)

for any
\varepsilon>0
. This means that
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is a
Dirac sequence
, i.e., it approaches a multiple of the
\delta
-distribution in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
(cf. Lemma
2.4
). For any
\varepsilon>0
, we write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\end{split}

(1.5)

for all
x\in\Omega
, to denote the nonlocal operator corresponding to
\rho_{\varepsilon}
.
We want to show that in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, the nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
converges to a local differential operator
\mathcal{L}
of the type

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u(x)\coloneqq-\operatorname{div}(M\nabla u)=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}M_{ij}\,\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}u(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $x\in\Omega$},

(1.6)

where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a symmetric matrix with entries

\displaystyle M_{ij}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(z)\,z_{i}\,z_{j}\;\mathrm{d}z\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$}.

(1.7)

M
is usually referred to as the
momentum matrix
. In case
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which means that
\partial\Omega\neq\emptyset
, we interpret the second-order differential operator
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}
to be equipped with the natural boundary condition

M\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\partial\Omega$}.

(1.8)

Here,
\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}
denotes a unit normal vector field on
\partial\Omega
.
Under suitable assumptions on the domain
\Omega
and on
\rho
,
J
and
p
, we intend to show that

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\to\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $L^{p}(\Omega)$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$}

(1.9)

for all functions
u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
with (
1.8
) if
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In addition, we are planning to establish the convergence rate

\big\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Omega}\,\sqrt[p]{\varepsilon}\,\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\Omega)}.

(1.10)

for all functions
u\in W^{3,p}(\Omega)
with (
1.8
) if
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Here,
C
is a positive constant that depends only on
\Omega
,
\rho
and
p
.

State of the art and related results.

The nonlocal-to-local convergence of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
to
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}
was first established in the weak sense. In this case, it can be derived from the convergence of the corresponding energies. The energy functional associated with the nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}u(x)\;u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big|u(x)-u(y)\big|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\left(\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|}\right)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

and the energy functional associated with the local differential operator
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}
reads as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\Omega}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}M\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Given these energies, one may intuitively expect that the weighted squared difference quotient
\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(\tfrac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|}\big)^{2}
approaches an expression of the form
M\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)
as the kernel
\rho_{\varepsilon}
concentrates at the origin, since in this regime only points
y
close to
x
contribute significantly to the integral. Here, the matrix
M
accounts for anisotropic effects when
\rho
is even but not radially symmetric. In case
\rho
is radially symmetric, we expect
M
to be a scalar multiple of the identity matrix and thus,
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}
is a scalar multiple of the negative Laplacian.

A rigorous proof of the nonlocal-to-local convergence of such energies in a more general setting was provided by
Ponce

[
29
]
(see also
[
28
,
27
]
).
These investigations of nonlocal energies provide an extension of earlier works by
Bourgain
,
Brezis
and
Mironescu

[
6
]
. Some improvements were obtained later, for example, by
Brezis
and
Nguyen

[
7
]
.
In particular, it was shown in
[
29
]
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}(u)\to\mathcal{E}^{\Omega}(u)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as $\varepsilon\to 0$ for all $u\in H^{1}(\Omega)$.}

(1.11)

Moreover, also the
\Gamma
convergence of such energies was discussed in
[
29
]
. Further results on nonlocal-to-local
\Gamma
-convergence were found by
Brezis
and
Nguyen

[
8
,
9
]
.

From the pointwise convergence (
1.11
), the weak convergence

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\rightharpoonup\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{weakly in $H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$ for all $u\in H^{1}(\Omega)$}

(1.12)

was derived by
Melchoinna
,
Ranetbauer
,
Scarpa
and
Trussardi

[
26
]
for radially symmetric, sufficiently regular kernels and
\Omega
being a flat torus. An analogous result for bounded domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and radially symmetric kernels
J_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
was established by
Davoli
,
Scarpa
and
Trussardi

[
14
]
. The main goal of these works was to relate the
nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard equation
to the classical
(local) Cahn–Hilliard equation
by a nonlocal-to-local limit.
This is an important advance because, as shown by
Giacomin
and
Lebowitz

[
20
]
(see also
[
21
,
22
]
), the local Cahn–Hilliard equation, in contrast to its nonlocal counterpart, cannot be derived directly from microscopic physical principles.
In this sense, the nonlocal-to-local convergence offers a physical justification for the local Cahn–Hilliard equation.
Therefore, besides
[
26
,
14
]
, the weak convergence (
1.12
) for radially symmetric
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
-kernels has been applied to variants of the Cahn–Hilliard equation and related models in various settings. We refer, for example, to
[
4
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
16
,
17
,
25
]
.

However, the aforementioned results are merely qualitative and do not yield any quantitative information on the nonlocal-to-local convergence, such as convergence rates. Also, at least for more regular functions
u
, it may be expected that the convergence
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\to\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u
actually holds in a strong topology. A first step in this direction was achieved in
[
1
]
by the first two authors of the present paper. For radially symmetric, localized (i.e., compactly supported)
W^{1,1}
-kernels and a sufficiently regular bounded domain
\Omega
, it was shown in
[
1
]
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\to\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $L^{2}(\Omega)$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$}

(1.13)

for all
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
with
\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, and

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\varepsilon^{\gamma}\|u\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}

(1.14)

for all
u\in H^{3}(\Omega)
with
\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
Here,
C
denotes a positive constant that may depend on
\Omega
but is independent of
\varepsilon
and
u
.
The exponent
\gamma
is given by
\gamma=1
if
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
(a flat torus), and
\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}
if
\Omega
is a sufficiently regular bounded domain.
In
[
1
]
, these convergence results were also applied to prove nonlocal-to-local convergence of the Cahn–Hilliard equation and the Allen–Cahn equation. In subsequent works, this technique was applied in
[
2
,
23
,
24
,
15
]
to analyze further models related to the Cahn–Hilliard equation.

Furthermore, we remark that the kernels in the nonlocal-to-local convergence results mentioned so far were always assumed to be radially symmetric. However, in concrete applications, this might not always be the case. For example, models related to crystal growth usually involve anisotropic interactions. We point out that in the contributions
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
by
Giacomin
and
Lebowitz
, the interaction kernel was merely assumed to be even, but not radially symmetric. Also in the work
[
29
]
by
Ponce
on the convergence of nonlocal energies, radial symmetry of the kernel is not required. This leads to the conjecture that it should be possible to generalize the aforementioned nonlocal-to-local convergence results to anisotropic kernels.
A first step in this direction was achieved very recently by the first author of the present paper and
Terasawa

[
5
]
by establishing the weak nonlocal-to-local convergence for an anisotropic variant of the Cahn–Hilliard equation. It is also worth mentioning that the strong
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
-regularity assumption on the convolution kernel was also relieved there. Instead, weaker assumptions similar to those by
Gounoue
,
Kassmann
and
Voigt

[
18
]
were imposed.

In the context of our work, we also want to mention the recent contribution
[
10
]
by
Bungert
and
del Teso
, where nonlocal-to-local convergence rates in the
W^{2,s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-norm were established for the the Dirichlet problem to the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
as
s\rightarrow 1
. For a discussion of nonlocal Neumann problems in a broader sense with general measurable, nonnegative convolution kernels, we refer to the work
[
19
]
by
Frerick
,
Vollmann
and
Vu
.

Novelties and main results of our paper.

In the present paper, we intend to provide a generalization of the aforementioned results on nonlocal-to-local convergence.
Under suitable assumptions, we will show the convergences

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\to\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $L^{p}(\Omega)$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$}

(1.15)

for all
u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
with
M\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, and

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\varepsilon^{\gamma(p)}\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\Omega)}

(1.16)

for all
u\in W^{3,p}(\Omega)
with
M\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
Here,
p\in[1,\infty)
and

\displaystyle\gamma(p)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}$,}\\
\frac{1}{p}&\text{if $\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a sufficiently regular domain.}\end{cases}

Compared with the previous results in
[
1
]
, our new results provide the following improvements:

(i)

The domain
\Omega
does not need to be bounded, but we merely require
\Omega
to have a sufficiently regular, compact boundary. This means that exterior domains (e.g.,
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)
) can also be considered.

(ii)

The strong nonlocal-to-local convergence is established in a general
L^{p}
framework. This enhances the applicability of the results, as in concrete applications one may need convergence in an
L^{p}
space other than
L^{2}
, or the relevant function
u
may lie only in
W^{k,p}
instead of
H^{k}
for
k\in\{2,3\}
.
Note that the rate obtained in (
1.16
) is consistent with the one from
[
1
]
(cf. (
1.14
)) for
p=2
.

(iii)

We demand significantly weaker assumptions on
\rho
and
J
, see
(A1)
and
(A2)
.
In particular,
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
-regularity of
J_{\varepsilon}
is not required,
and we allow for a much stronger singularity at the origin, comparable to that of a fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
of order
s\in(0,1)
(cf. Remark
2.2
).

(iv)

As in
[
5
]
, we also do not require
\rho
to be radially symmetric. This allows for anisotropic effects, since different directions may be weighted differently, resulting in a non-diagonal momentum matrix
M
.
Compared to
[
5
]
, our advance is that we establish the nonlocal-to-local convergence in the strong sense as well as associated convergence rates.

Especially
(iii)
and
(iv)
give rise to considerable technical difficulties in our proofs.

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2
, we first introduce our fundamental assumptions on the convolution kernel. We also give a concrete example of class of kernels, which satisfy these assumptions. Furthermore, we explain in Remark
2.2
, that our assumptions allow the kernels to exhibit singularities at the origin that are comparable to those of fractional Laplacians.

Our main results are successively stated and proved in Section
3
. In Subsection
3.1
, we first show that the nonlocal operator (
1.5
) is actually well defined under our assumptions on the convolution kernel. Then in Subsection
3.2
, we establish our main result in the case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Afterwards, we prove the convergences (
1.15
) and (
1.16
) in the special case that
\Omega
is a sufficiently regular curved half-space. Finally, in Subsection
3.4
, this allows us to establish our main result in the general case that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a domain with sufficiently regular, compact boundary.

2  Assumptions and preliminaries

In the remainder of this paper, let the dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary. We make the following general assumptions on the convolution kernel.

(A1)

We assume that
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
,
\rho\not\equiv 0
is measurable and even (i.e.,
\rho(x)=\rho(-x)
for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
) with

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(x)(1+\left|x\right|)\;\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

(2.1)

The associated kernel is given by

J:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptJ(x)=\frac{\rho(x)}{\left|x\right|^{2}}.

(2.2)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we introduce the functions

\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)\coloneqq\varepsilon^{-n}\,\rho\big(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\big),

(2.3)

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002ptJ_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)}{\left|x\right|^{2}}.

(2.4)

Since
\rho\geq 0
and
\rho\not\equiv 0
, the momentum matrix

\displaystyle M\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(z)\,z\otimes z\;\mathrm{d}z

(2.5)

is positive definite.

(A2)

In addition to Assumption
(A1)
, we assume that
\rho\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
and that there exist
c_{0},c_{1}>0
,
\alpha\in(0,2)
with
N>3-\alpha
and

\begin{split}|\rho(x)|&\leq c_{0}|x|^{2-\alpha-n}\,(1+|x|)^{-N}\\
|\nabla\rho(x)|&\leq c_{1}|x|^{1-\alpha-n}\,(1+|x|)^{-N}\end{split}

(2.6)

for all
x\in B_{1}(0)\setminus\{0\}
.
Moreover, we assume that the associated kernel
J
fulfills the conditions

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}J\left(AQ\begin{pmatrix}z^{\prime}\\
z_{n}\end{pmatrix}\right)z^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}$ and all $Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)$,}

(2.7)

where

A\coloneqq\sqrt{M}.

(2.8)

Remark 2.1
.

We make the following observations.

(a)

If
\rho\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it suffices to demand

\begin{split}|\rho(x)|&\leq c_{0}|x|^{2-\alpha-n}\\
|\nabla\rho(x)|&\leq c_{1}|x|^{1-\alpha-n}\end{split}

(2.9)

instead of (
2.6
). In this situation, (
2.9
) implies that (
2.6
) holds for every
N>0
, provided that
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are chosen sufficiently large.

(b)

If
\rho
is radially symmetric, so is
J
. In this case, condition (
2.7
) is automatically fulfilled. This is because
A=a\mathrm{I}
for some
a>0
, where
\mathrm{I}
denotes the identity matrix, which implies that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}J(AQz)z^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}J(az)z^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=0.

Here, the second equality can be shown by applying the change of variables
z^{\prime}\mapsto-z^{\prime}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Recall that, in case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
fractional Laplace operator
of order
s\in(0,1)
can be represented as

\displaystyle(-\Delta)_{\Omega}^{s}u(x)=\frac{4^{s}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{n}{2}+s\right)}{\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\left|\Gamma(-s)\right|}\;\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho(x-y)}{|x-y|^{2}}\big(u(x)-u(y))\;\mathrm{d}y,

with

\displaystyle\rho(x)=|x|^{2-n-2s}

(2.10)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function. If
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the above operator is referred to as a
regional fractional Laplace operator
of order
s
.
We notice that the singularity of
\rho
at the origin is as demanded in (
2.6
) with
\alpha=2s
.
However,
\rho
needs to be modified for large values of
|x|
in a suitable way in order to satisfy (
2.1
) and (
2.6
).
In case
\Omega
is a bounded domain, such a modification for large
|x|
does not constitute a true restriction. If
\Omega
is bounded, we can find a radius
R>0
such that
\Omega\subset B_{R}(0)
. This means that for any
x\in\Omega
,
(-\Delta)_{\Omega}^{s}u(x)
does not depend on the values of
\rho
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}(0)
. Therefore, we can simply replace
\rho
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(x)=\rho(x)\chi(x)=|x|^{2-n-2s}\chi(x),

where
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\chi=1
on
B_{2R}(0)
, without changing the values of
(-\Delta)_{\Omega}^{s}u
in
\Omega
. It is straightforward to check that
\tilde{\rho}
satisfies
(A1)
and
(A2)
with
\alpha=2s
and any
N>3-\alpha=3-2s
.

Remark 2.3
.

In order to construct a nontrivial,
anisotropic
density function
\rho
with associated kernel
J
, such that
(A1)
and
(A2)
are fulfilled, we proceed as follows.
Let
\mathring{\rho}\in C^{1}\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\};[0,\infty)\big)
be a radially symmetric function, which satisfies
(
2.1
) and (
2.6
).
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\|\mathring{\rho}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=2n
. This implies that

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\mathring{\rho}(z)}{\left|z\right|^{2}}z\otimes z\;\mathrm{d}z=I.

Moreover, let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric, positive definite matrix.
To simplify the computations, we further assume, without loss of generality, that
\det B=1
. We now define
\rho\in C^{1}\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\};[0,\infty)\big)
with

\displaystyle\rho(x)\coloneqq\mathring{\rho}(Bx)\frac{\left|x\right|^{2}}{\left|Bx\right|^{2}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}$.}

In this way,
\rho
is clearly even but, in general, not any more radially symmetric.
Its associated kernel can be expressed as

\displaystyle J(x)=\frac{\mathring{\rho}(Bx)}{\left|Bx\right|^{2}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}$.}

It is easy to check that
\rho
and
J
satisfy
(A1)
and Condition (
2.6
). Moreover, a straightforward computation yields
M=B^{-2}
and
A=B^{-1}
. To verify (
2.7
), let
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
be arbitrary.
Recalling that
\mathring{\rho}
is radially symmetric, we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}J\left(AQ\begin{pmatrix}z^{\prime}\\
z_{n}\end{pmatrix}\right)z^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\frac{\mathring{\rho}(BAQz)}{|BAQz|^{2}}z^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\frac{\mathring{\rho}(Qz)}{|Qz|^{2}}z^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\frac{\mathring{\rho}(z)}{|z|^{2}}z^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=0

for all
z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.
This shows that
J
satisfies Condition (
2.7
) and consequently,
(A2)
is also fulfilled.

The following lemma provides two important properties of the family
\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
(A1)
holds.

(a)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, it holds
\rho_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with

\|\rho_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty.

(2.11)

(b)

For any
\delta>0
, it holds that

\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\lim}\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\delta}(0)}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

(2.12)

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.
Since
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the identity in (
2.11
) follows from the change of variables
x\mapsto\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}
. Thus, assertion (a) is verified.

Let now
\delta>0
be arbitrary.
The change of variables
x\mapsto\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}
yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\delta}(0)}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\delta/\varepsilon}(0)}\rho(x)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Since
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the left-hand side converges to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
due to Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem. This verifies (b).
∎

3  Nonlocal-to-local convergence of the nonlocal operator

In general, we assume that
(A1)
holds and
we consider a sufficiently smooth domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The main goal of this paper is to investigate the convergence of the nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
, which is (at first formally) given by

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y

(3.1)

for all
x\in\Omega
and any sufficiently regular function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
to the local differential operator
\mathcal{L}
,
which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u(x)=-\operatorname{div}\big(M\nabla u(x)\big)=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}M_{ij}\,\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}u(x)

(3.2)

for all
x\in\Omega
and any sufficiently regular function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
.
Here,
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the symmetric matrix introduced in
(A1)
, which is positive definite.
If the boundary of
\Omega
is not empty, the differential operator
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}
is equipped with the natural boundary condition

M\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\partial\Omega$},

(3.3)

where
\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}
denotes the a unit normal vector field on
\partial\Omega
. For any
k\geq 2
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, we define the corresponding Sobolev spaces as

W^{k,p}_{M}(\Omega)=\big\{u\in W^{k,p}(\Omega)\,:\,M\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0\;\text{on $\partial\Omega$}\big\}.

(3.4)

3.1  Well-definedness of the nonlocal operator

In this subsection, we first show that the expression

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y

is actually well defined if Assumption
(A1)
is fulfilled. In case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the following proposition shows that
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
actually defines a bounded linear operator.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, suppose that Assumption
(A1)
holds and let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Then, the following statements hold.

(a)

The operator

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}:C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\\
&\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)-\nabla u(y)\cdot(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\end{split}

(3.5)

is well-defined and linear. Moreover, for any
u\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u
can be expressed as

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y

(3.6)

for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(b)

The operator introduced in
(a)
can be extended to a bounded linear operator

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}:W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{*}\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

for all
u\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where the constant
C_{*}>0
depends only on
\rho
and
p
.

By means of Proposition
3.1
, we can draw conclusions on the well-definedness of the nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
if
\Omega
is a domain of class
C^{2}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a (not necessarily bounded) domain with
C^{2}
-boundary, suppose that
(A1)
holds, and let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.
Then, the operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}:C_{c}^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\to L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega),\\
&\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\end{split}

(3.7)

is well-defined and linear.

Provided that
(A1)
and
(A2)
and certain assumptions on the domain are fulfilled, we will see later that the operator introduced in (
3.7
) can actually be extended to a linear and bounded operator mapping from
W^{2,p}(\Omega)
to
L^{p}(\Omega)
.

In the remainder of this subsection, we present the proofs of Proposition
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.
In the following, the letter
C
will denote generic positive constants depending only on
\rho
and
p
.
The concrete value of
C
may vary throughout the proof.

Proof of
(a)
. Let
u\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary.
By means of Taylor’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle u(x)-u(y)-\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)=-R_{2}(x,y),

(3.8)

where the error term is given by

\displaystyle R_{2}(x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{|\beta|=2}\frac{2}{\beta!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}t\>(x-y)^{\beta}.

(3.9)

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)-\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\big)\big|\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\sum_{|\beta|=2}\frac{2}{\beta!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\big|J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,(1-t)\,D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)\>(x-y)^{\beta}\big|\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\,\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|\;\mathrm{d}y=C\,\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

This proves that for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

J_{\varepsilon}(x-\cdot)\big(u(x)-u(\cdot)-\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-\cdot)\big)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(3.10)

and therefore, the integral in the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
actually exists.
Recalling (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we use Hölder’s inequality and Fubini’s theorem to deduce

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigg|\sum_{|\beta|=2}\frac{2}{\beta!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{0}^{1}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,(1-t)\,D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)\>(x-y)^{\beta}\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}y\,\Bigg|^{p}\!\!\!\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{|\beta|=2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{0}^{1}|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)||D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)|\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}y\right)^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\sum_{|\beta|=2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{0}^{1}|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)||D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\sum_{|\beta|=2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)||D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}t.

Applying first the change of variables
y\mapsto z=x-y
and afterwards the change of variables
x\mapsto w=x-z+tz
, we infer

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{|\beta|=2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)|\;\mathrm{d}z\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\beta}u(w)|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}w\right)\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\sum_{|\beta|=2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\beta}u(w)|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}w\right)\leq C_{*}^{p}\,\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}

(3.11)

for some constant
C_{*}>0
depending only on
\rho
and
p
.
This proves that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
is well-defined and bounded in the sense of (
3.1
). Moreover, it is easy to check that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
is linear.

It remains to verify the representation (
3.6
). To this end, let
r>0
be arbitrary. Recalling (
3.10
), we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{\left|x-y\right|\geq r}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\left|x-y\right|\geq r}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)-\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\int_{\left|x-y\right|\geq r}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y

(3.12)

for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We point out that

\displaystyle\int_{\left|x-y\right|\geq r}\big|J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\big|\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\int_{\left|x-y\right|\geq r}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|\left|\nabla u(x)\right|\frac{1}{\left|x-y\right|}\;\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{1}{r}\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

This means that the second integral on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) actually exists and therefore, the identity (
3.1
) is justified.
As
\rho
is an even function so is the kernel
J_{\varepsilon}
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\int_{\left|x-y\right|\geq r}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y=0.

(3.13)

Moreover, invoking (
3.10
), we obtain

\int_{\left|x-y\right|\geq r}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)-\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\to\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)

(3.14)

as
r\to 0
for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by means of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem.
Eventually, combining (
3.1
), (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), we conclude that

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)=\underset{r\searrow 0}{\lim}\int_{\left|x-y\right|\geq r}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y.

By the definition of the principal value, this is exactly (
3.6
).

Proof of
(b)
.
We already know from
(a)
that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}:C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is well-defined, linear and bounded in the sense of (
3.1
).
As
C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
we conclude via estimate (
3.1
) that
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
can be extended to a bounded linear operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}:W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{*}\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Hence, the proof is complete.
∎

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
was already handled in Proposition
3.1
.
Let
u\in C^{2}_{c}(\overline{\Omega})
be arbitrary. Since
\Omega
is of class
C^{2}
, we can find an extension

\tilde{u}\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\tilde{u}|_{\Omega}=u
.
We now fix an arbitrary
x\in\Omega
. Since
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, we can find a radius
r(x)>0
such that
B_{r(x)}(x)\subset\Omega
.
Then, for any
r\in(0,r(x)]
, we have
B_{r}(x)\subset\Omega
and thus,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{r}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\end{split}

(3.15)

We already know from Proposition
3.1
(a)
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\to\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\tilde{u}(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as $r\to 0$.}

Therefore, it remains to show that the second integral on the right-hand side of (
3.15
) actually exists. Since
B_{r(x)}(x)\subset\Omega
, we obviously have

|x-y|\geq r(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}.

Recalling the definition of
J_{\varepsilon}
, this implies that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big|\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big|\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{2}{r(x)^{2}}\,\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\frac{2}{r(x)^{2}}\,\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty.

Hence, sending
r\to 0
in (
3.15
), we conclude that the expression

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\\
&\quad=\underset{r\searrow 0}{\lim}\,\int_{\Omega\cap\{\left|x-y\right|\geq r\}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\\
&\quad=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\tilde{u}(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\end{split}

(3.16)

is well-defined for every
x\in\Omega
provided that
u\in C^{2}_{c}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Let now
K
be an arbitrary compact subset of
\Omega
. Since
\Omega
is open, we know that
r(K)\coloneqq\textnormal{dist\,}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,K)>0
. As the pointwise limit of a sequence of measurable functions is measurable, we further know that the mapping

\Omega\ni x\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u(x)\in\mathbb{R}

is measurable. Using (
3.16
) along with Proposition
3.1
, and proceeding similarly as above, we infer that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(K)}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}+\int_{K}\Bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\Bigg|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}+\frac{2^{p}|K|}{r(K)^{2p}}\,\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}\,\|\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}<\infty.

As the compact subset
K
was arbitrary, this proves that
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\in L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
. This means that the operator

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}:C_{c}^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\to L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002ptu\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u

is well-defined. Moreover, the operator is obviously linear and thus, the proof is complete.
∎

3.2  Convergence on the full space

In this subsection, we consider the case where
\Omega
is the full space, i.e.,
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Based on the results established in Proposition
3.1
, we obtain the following nonlocal-to-local convergence properties.

Theorem 3.3
.

We consider
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Suppose that Assumption
(A1)
holds and let
p\in[1,\infty)
be arbitrary.
Then, the following statements hold.

(a)

For all
u\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\to\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$.}

(b)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
u\in W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it holds

\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\varepsilon\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.
In the following, the letter
C
will denote generic positive constants depending only on
\rho
and
p
. The concrete value of
C
may vary throughout the proof.

Proof of
(b)
.
Since
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we first verify the assertion for functions
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Therefore, let
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary.
By the definitions of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}
and
\mathcal{L}
(see (
3.5
) and (
3.2
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)+\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y+M:D^{2}u(x)\Bigg|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\big(I_{1,\varepsilon}+I_{2,\varepsilon}\big),\phantom{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}

(3.17)

where

\displaystyle I_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{B_{1}(x)}\!\!J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)+\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\,+\,M:D^{2}u(x)\right|^{p}\!\!\!\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle I_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)+\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x.

We now estimate the terms
I_{1,\varepsilon}
and
I_{2,\varepsilon}
separately.

Ad
I_{1,\varepsilon}
: Applying Taylor’s theorem, we obtain the expansion

\displaystyle u(x)-u(y)+\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)=-\frac{1}{2}(x-y)^{T}D^{2}u(x)(x-y)-R_{3}(x,y),

(3.18)

where the error term is given by

\displaystyle R_{3}(x,y)=\sum_{|\beta|=3}\frac{3}{\beta!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\,D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}t\;(x-y)^{\beta}.

(3.19)

Therefore,
I_{1,\varepsilon}
can be estimated as

\displaystyle I_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigg|-\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{2}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)(x-y)^{T}D^{2}u(x)(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y\,+\,M:D^{2}u(x)\Bigg|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigg|\int_{B_{1}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)R_{3}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}y\Bigg|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x.

(3.20)

Employing the change of variables
x\mapsto z=x-y
and recalling the definition of
M
(see (
2.5
)), we observe that

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{2}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)(x-y)^{T}D^{2}u(x)(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y\,+\,M:D^{2}u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{1}(0)}J_{\varepsilon}(z)z^{T}D^{2}u(x)z\;\mathrm{d}z\,-\,\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(z)z^{T}D^{2}u(x)z\;\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)}J_{\varepsilon}(z)z^{T}D^{2}u(x)z\;\mathrm{d}z.

(3.21)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Plugging this identity into (
3.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)}J_{\varepsilon}(z)z^{T}D^{2}u(x)z\;\mathrm{d}z\Bigg|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigg|\int_{B_{1}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)R_{3}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}y\Bigg|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x.

(3.22)

Since
|z|\geq 1
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)
, the first summand on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) can be estimated as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)}J_{\varepsilon}(z)z^{T}D^{2}u(x)z\;\mathrm{d}z\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\,|z|\,\left|D^{2}u(x)\right|\;\mathrm{d}z\right)^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\,|z|\;\mathrm{d}z\right)^{p}\left|D^{2}u(x)\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq C\varepsilon^{p}\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}.

Recalling the definition of the error term, we obtain the following estimate for the the second summand on the right-hand side of (
3.2
):

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{B_{1}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)R_{3}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|=3}\frac{3}{\beta!}\int_{B_{1}(x)}\int_{0}^{1}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)(1-t)D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)\;(x-y)^{\beta}\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{|\beta|=3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{B_{1}(x)}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|\,|x-y|\,|D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)|\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}y\right)^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

Next, we use the splitting

\displaystyle\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|\,|x-y|=\big(\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|\,|x-y|\big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\big(\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|\,|x-y|\big)^{\frac{1}{p}},

(3.23)

where
q
denotes the conjugate exponent to
p
, i.e.,
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{p}=1
. Then, using Hölder’s inequality as well as the changes of variables
y\mapsto z=x-y
and
x\mapsto w=x-z+tz
, we infer

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{B_{1}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)R_{3}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\sum_{|\beta|=3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\,|z|\;\mathrm{d}z\right)^{p-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002pt\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\,|z|\,\left|D^{\beta}u(x-z+tz)\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}z\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\varepsilon^{p-1}\sum_{|\beta|=3}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\,|z|\,\left|D^{\beta}u(w)\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}z\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\varepsilon^{p}\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}.

Altogether, this shows that

\displaystyle I_{1,\varepsilon}\leq C\varepsilon^{p}\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}.

(3.24)

Ad
I_{2,\varepsilon}
: Applying Taylor’s theorem, we obtain the expansion

\displaystyle u(x)-u(y)-\nabla u(x)\cdot(x-y)=-R_{2}(x,y),

(3.25)

where the error term is given by

\displaystyle R_{2}(x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{|\beta|=2}\frac{2}{\beta!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}t\>(x-y)^{\beta}.

(3.26)

Hence,
I_{2,\varepsilon}
can be estimated as

\displaystyle I_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(x)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)R_{2}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{|\beta|=2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|\left|D^{\beta}u\big(y+t(x-y)\big)\right|\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}t\right)^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

Using the splitting

\displaystyle\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|=\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|^{\frac{1}{q}}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right|^{\frac{1}{p}},

(3.27)

where
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{p}=1
,
Hölder’s inequality, and the changes of variables
y\mapsto z=x-y
and
x\mapsto w=x-z+tz
, we infer

\displaystyle I_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{|\beta|=2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\;\mathrm{d}z\right)^{p-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\,\left|D^{\beta}u(x-z+tz)\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}z\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{p-1}\sum_{|\beta|=2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)}\left|\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\,|z|\,\left|D^{\beta}u(w)\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}z\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{p}\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p},

(3.28)

Combining (
3.2
) with (
3.24
) and (
3.2
), we eventually conclude that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\varepsilon\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

(3.29)

for all
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Let now
u\in W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary. Then, because of density, there exists a sequence
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
u_{k}\rightarrow u
in
W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
In particular, this entails that
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Thus, it follows from (
3.29
) that

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u_{k}-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u_{k}\Big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is bounded. Hence, according to the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, there exists a function
w\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that, up to subsequence extraction,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u_{k}-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u_{k}\rightharpoonup w\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ as $k\rightarrow\infty$.}

Because of Proposition
3.1
, we know that

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u_{k}\to\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ as $k\rightarrow\infty$.}

Moreover, recalling the definition of
\mathcal{L}
, it is further clear that

\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u_{k}\to\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ as $k\rightarrow\infty$.}

Consequently, due to the uniqueness of the weak limit, we have
w=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u
.
Thus, by means of weak lower semi-continuity, we conclude

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u_{k}-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}C\varepsilon\|u_{k}\|_{W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=C\varepsilon\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Since
u\in W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
was arbitrary, this proves (b).

Proof of
(a)
.
We already know from Proposition
3.1
(b)
that the operator norm of the nonlocal operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}:W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is bounded by the constant
C_{*}
, which is independent of
\varepsilon
. Together with the definition of
\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u
(see (
3.2
)), this implies that

\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

for all
u\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
We further know from part
(b)
that for any
u\in W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it holds that

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\Omega}u-\mathcal{L}^{\Omega}u\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$.}

As the inclusion
W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense, the assertion of
(a)
directly follows from the Banach–Steinhaus theorem.

Hence, the proof of Theorem
3.3
is complete.
∎

3.3  Convergence on a curved half space

In this section, we next consider the case where our domain is a curved half space. More precisely, we consider

\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,x_{n}>\gamma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})\big\}

(3.30)

for a prescribed function
\gamma\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\gamma\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
,
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, and suppose that
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold with
M=I
.
Then, if
\|\gamma\|_{C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}
is sufficently small,
the operator introduced in (
3.7
)
\big(
restricted to
C^{2}_{c}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})\cap W^{2,p}_{I}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})\big)
can be extended to a bounded linear operator

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}:W^{2,p}_{I}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})

(3.31)

with

\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})}\leq c_{\gamma}\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})}

(3.32)

for all
u\in W^{2,p}_{I}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})
, where
c_{\gamma}
is a positive constant depending only on
\gamma
,
\rho
and
p
.
Moreover, there exists a positive constant
C_{\gamma}
depending only on
\gamma
,
\rho
and
p
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
u\in W^{3,p}_{I}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})
, it holds

\big\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}u+\Delta u\big\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})}\leq C_{\gamma}\,\sqrt[p]{\varepsilon}\,\|u\|_{W^{3,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})}.

(3.33)

Furthermore, for all
u\in W^{2,p}_{I}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})
, it holds

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}u\to-\Delta u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$.}

(3.34)

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\gamma\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
,
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, and suppose that
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold with
M=I
.
Then, the results of Theorem
3.4
hold true for
Q\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}
instead of
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}
and
W^{k,p}_{Q}(Q\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})
instead of
W^{k,p}_{I}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})
for
k=2,3
.

To provide a cleaner presentation, we will usually refrain from indicating the principal value by the symbol
\mathrm{P.V.}
whenever the meaning is clear. In the following, the letter
C
will denote generic positive constants depending only on
\gamma
,
\rho
and
p
. The concrete value of
C
may vary throughout the proof. The proof is split into three steps.

Step 1: Proof of Estimate (
3.32
) for
u\in C^{2}_{c}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})\cap W^{2,p}_{I}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})
.

Let
u\in C^{2}_{c}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})\cap W^{2,p}_{I}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})
be arbitrary. Hence, according to Corollary
3.2
, the expression

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}u(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y

is well-defined.
Our first goal is to show that
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})
and that

\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})}.

(3.35)

By construction, it is clear that
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}u
is measurable. Since
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}
is of class
C^{3}
, we can find an extension
\tilde{u}\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
u|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}=u
. In particular,
\tilde{u}
can be chosen in such a way that

\|\tilde{u}\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})}.

(3.36)

Using Proposition
3.1
, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}u\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}+C\|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{W^{2,p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p}+C\|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p},

(3.37)

where the error term is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{u}(x)\coloneqq\int_{({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})^{c}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}.

Therefore, it remains to show that

\|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{2,p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p}

(3.38)

since (
3.35
) then follows by combining (
3.3
) and (
3.38
).

Since
\gamma\in C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
, we infer from
[
30
, Lemma 2.1]
that there exists a
C^{2,1}
-diffeomorphism
F_{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
F_{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}
, which satisfies

F_{\gamma}(x^{\prime},0)=\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
\gamma(x^{\prime})\end{pmatrix}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{x_{n}}F_{\gamma}(x)|_{x_{n}=0}=-\mathbf{n}_{\partial{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}}(x^{\prime},\gamma(x^{\prime}))

(3.39)

for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
Since
F_{\gamma}\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we further have
DF_{\gamma}\in W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
with

\|DF_{\gamma}\|_{W^{2,\infty}}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\leq C\|\gamma\|_{C^{3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}\leq C.

(3.40)

In the following, we write

d_{F_{\gamma}}\coloneqq|\det DF_{\gamma}|

as an abbreviation.
In particular, due to (
3.40
), we have

\|d_{F_{\gamma}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C.

(3.41)

Moreover, assuming
\|\gamma\|_{C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}
to be sufficiently small, we can ensure that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|DF_{\gamma}(x)-I|\leq\frac{1}{6}.

(3.42)

Now, by the change of variables by
x\mapsto F_{\gamma}(x)
, we infer that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}}\left|\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma})^{c}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(u(x)-\tilde{u}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.43)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(F_{\gamma}(x)-F_{\gamma}(y)\big)\Big(u(F_{\gamma}(x))-\tilde{u}(F_{\gamma}(y))\Big)\,d_{F_{\gamma}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}d_{F_{\gamma}}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x,

To simplify the notation, we introduce the functions

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002ptw(x)=\tilde{u}\big(F_{\gamma}(x)\big),

(3.44)

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002ptG_{\gamma}(x,y)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}DF_{\gamma}(y+t(x-y))\;\mathrm{d}t.

(3.45)

In particular, this means that
G_{\gamma}(x,x)=DF_{\gamma}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Using the chain rule along with (
3.36
), (
3.40
) and (
3.41
), we deduce

\|w\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|\tilde{u}\|_{W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{2,p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}.

(3.46)

Moreover, by means of the fundamental theorem of calculus, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}(x)-F_{\gamma}(y)=G_{\gamma}(x,y)\,(x-y).

Let now
x,\tilde{x},y,\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be arbitrary.
Recalling that
G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{x})=DF_{\gamma}(\tilde{x})
, we infer from (
3.42
) that

\big|\big[G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{x})-I\big](x-y)\big|\leq\frac{1}{6}|x-y|.

(3.47)

This implies that

\big|G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{x})(x-y)\big|\geq\Big||x-y|-\big|\big[G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{x})-I\big](x-y)\big|\Big|\geq\frac{5}{6}|x-y|.

(3.48)

Moreover, recalling the definition of
G_{\gamma}
in (
3.45
) and invoking once more (
3.42
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\big|\big[G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})-G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{x})\big](x-y)\big|\\
&\quad\leq\Big[\big|G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})-I\big|+\big|G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{x})-I\big|\Big]\,|x-y|\leq\frac{1}{3}|x-y|.\end{split}

(3.49)

Combining (
3.48
) and (
3.49
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\big|G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})(x-y)\big|\\
&\quad\geq\Big|\big|G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{x})(x-y)\big|-\big|\big[G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})-G_{\gamma}(\tilde{x},\tilde{x})\big](x-y)\big|\Big|\geq\frac{1}{2}|x-y|\end{split}

(3.50)

for all
x,\tilde{x},y,\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Next, using (
3.45
), we can reformulate (
3.43
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p}\\
&\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)\big(w(x)-w(y)\big)\,d_{F_{\gamma}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}d_{F_{\gamma}}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(3.51)

Now, we introduce the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,2)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(-2,0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}\,.

(3.52)

Hence, from (
3.51
) we infer that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma}})}^{p}\leq C\big(I_{\varepsilon}^{1}+I_{\varepsilon}^{2}+I_{\varepsilon}^{3}\big),

(3.53)

where

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{B}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)(w(x)-w(y))\,d_{F_{\gamma}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}d_{F_{\gamma}}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\,,

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}\setminus\mathcal{B}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)(w(x)-w(y))\,d_{F_{\gamma}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}d_{F_{\gamma}}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\,,

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{B}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)(w(x)-w(y))\,d_{F_{\gamma}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}d_{F_{\gamma}}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\,.

These integral terms will now be handled separately.

Ad
I_{\varepsilon}^{1}
:

We first observe that

I_{\varepsilon}^{1}\leq C(I_{\varepsilon}^{1,1}+I_{\varepsilon}^{1,2}),

where

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{1,1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)|w(x)|\,d_{F_{\gamma}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{1,2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)|w(y)|\,d_{F_{\gamma}}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x.

We further observe that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}
and all
y\in\mathcal{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|x-y\right|\geq\left|x_{n}-y_{n}\right|=x_{n}-y_{n}\geq 2.

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}
and all
y\in\mathcal{B}
.
Hence, due to (
3.50
), we have

\big|G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big|\geq\frac{1}{2}|x-y|\geq 1.

(3.54)

Hence, using Assumption
(A2)
and applying the change of variables
y\mapsto z=\frac{x-y}{\varepsilon}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathcal{B}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y&\leq C\varepsilon^{-n}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big|\frac{x-y}{\varepsilon}\Big|^{2-\alpha-n}\Big(1+\Big|\frac{x-y}{\varepsilon}\Big|\Big)^{-N}\;\mathrm{d}y\\
&=C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|z|^{2-\alpha-n}(1+|z|)^{-N}\;\mathrm{d}z\leq C.\end{aligned}

(3.55)

By (
3.41
), (
3.55
) and Hölder’s inequality, we find that

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{1,1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}}|w(x)|^{p}\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq C\|w\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})}^{p}.

Proceding similarly, we use (
3.41
), (
3.55
) and Hölder’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{1,2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)^{p-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\cdot\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)|w(y)|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}y\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\;\rho_{\varepsilon}\big(G_{\gamma}(x,y)(x-y)\big)|w(y)|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}x.

Finally, recalling Assumption
(A2)
, using Fubini’s theorem, and preoceeding similarly to (
3.55
), we conclude

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{1,2}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\;\Big|\frac{x-y}{\varepsilon}\Big|^{2-\alpha-n}\Big(1+\Big|\frac{x-y}{\varepsilon}\Big|\Big)^{-N}|w(y)|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\|w\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}.

In summary, this shows

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{1}\leq C\|w\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}.

Ad
I^{3,1}_{\varepsilon}
:

Due to the construction of
F_{\gamma}
, there exist continuously differentiable functions
U:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\text{SO}(n)
,
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
H^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle H(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}&&&0\\
&H^{\prime}(x)&&\vdots\\
&&&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\ldots&0&1\end{array}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

such that

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}(x,x)=\text{D}F_{\gamma}(x)=U(x)H(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}

(3.61)

For more details on this decomposition, we refer to
[

. In particular, we have

\displaystyle H(x)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}&&&0\\
&H^{\prime}(x)^{-1}&&\vdots\\
&&&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\ldots&0&1\end{array}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

and since
\det U(x)=1

\det H(x)=d_{F_{\gamma}(x)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\det\big(H(x)^{-1}\big)=\big(d_{F_{\gamma}(x)}\big)^{-1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}

\displaystyle I^{3,1}_{\varepsilon}\leq C\big(I_{\varepsilon}^{3,3,1}+I_{\varepsilon}^{3,3,2}\big),

where

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{3,1,1}

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{3,1,2}

Ad
I^{3,1,1}_{\varepsilon}

, and finally

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{3,1,1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(x_{n},x_{n}+2)}J_{\varepsilon}\big(U(x)H(x)z\big)\nabla w(x)\cdot z\;\mathrm{d}z\;d_{F_{\gamma}}(x)\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(x_{n},x_{n}+2)}J_{\varepsilon}\big(U(x)\tilde{z}\big)\,H(x)^{-T}\nabla w(x)\cdot\tilde{z}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{B}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(U(x)(x-y)\big)\,H(x)^{-T}\nabla w(x)\cdot(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x

H(x)^{-T}

2.7

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{3,1,1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left|\partial_{x_{n}}w(x)\int_{\mathcal{B}}J_{\varepsilon}\big(U(x)(x-y)\big)(x_{n}-y_{n})\;\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x.

(a)

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}^{3,1,1}\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x_{n}}\int_{0}^{x_{n}}|\partial_{x_{n}}^{2}w(x^{\prime},s)|\;\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

Ad

F_{\gamma_{z}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}}
,
F_{z}(x^{\prime},0)=\big(x^{\prime},\gamma(x^{\prime})\big)
and

\big(DF_{z}(x^{\prime},0)\big)^{-1}\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}}}\big(F_{z}(x^{\prime},0)\big)\in\mathrm{span}\{e_{n}\}

(3.139)

for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. For any
\delta>0
, we now introduce the functions
\phi_{z}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1};[0,1])
and
\psi_{z}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R};[0,1])
with

\displaystyle\phi_{z}=1\;\;\text{on $B_{\delta/2}(z^{\prime})$},\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\textnormal{supp\,}\phi_{z}\subset B_{\delta}(z^{\prime})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1},

\displaystyle\psi_{z}=1\;\;\text{on $\big(-\tfrac{\delta}{2},\tfrac{\delta}{2}\big)$},\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\textnormal{supp\,}\psi_{z}\subset(-\delta,\delta)\,.

Next, we define

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}_{z}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{\varphi}_{z}(y)=\phi_{z}(y^{\prime})\psi_{z}(y_{n})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\varphi}_{z}\coloneqq\widehat{\varphi}_{z}\circ\big(Q_{z}F_{z}\big)^{-1}\,.

Since
(Q_{z}F_{z})^{-1}(U)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, we can ensure that

\displaystyle\textnormal{supp\,}\widehat{\varphi}_{z}\subset(Q_{z}F_{z})^{-1}(U_{z})\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{supp\,}\tilde{\varphi}_{z}\subset U_{z}

\delta_{z}
sufficiently small. Moreover, as
F_{z}^{-1}\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we infer that

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{z}\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};[0,1])\cap W^{3,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Let now
y\in\partial\widehat{\Omega}\cap U_{z}=\partial\big(Q_{z}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}}\big)\cap U_{z}
be arbitrary. Hence, there exists
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
such that
Q_{z}F_{z}(x^{\prime},0)=y
.
By the construction of
F_{z}
, we have

\displaystyle D\big((Q_{z}F_{z})^{-1}\big)(y)=\big(D(Q_{z}F_{z})(x^{\prime},0)\big)^{-1}=\big(DF_{z}(x^{\prime},0)\big)^{-1}Q_{z}^{T}

(3.140)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle Q_{z}^{T}\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial(Q_{z}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}})}(y)=\mathbf{n}_{\partial(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}})}\big(Q_{z}^{T}y\big)=\mathbf{n}_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}}}\big(F_{z}(x^{\prime},0)\big)

(3.141)

Now, using the chain rule as well as (
3.140
) and (
3.141
), we derive the identity

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\nabla\tilde{\varphi}_{z}(y)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\widehat{\Omega}}(y)=D\tilde{\varphi}_{z}(y)\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial\widehat{\Omega}}(y)=D\tilde{\varphi}_{z}(y)\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial(Q_{z}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}})}(y)\\
&\quad=D\widehat{\varphi}_{z}\big((Q_{z}F_{z})^{-1}(y)\big)\,\big(DF_{z}(x^{\prime},0)\big)^{-1}Q_{z}^{T}\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial(Q_{z}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}})}(y)\\
&\quad=D\widehat{\varphi}_{z}\big((Q_{z}F_{z})^{-1}(y)\big)\,\big(DF_{z}(x^{\prime},0)\big)^{-1}\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{z}}}\big(F_{z}(x^{\prime},0)\big)\end{split}

(3.142)

By the construction of
\widehat{\varphi}_{z}
, we have

\displaystyle D\widehat{\varphi}_{z}(y)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}&&&0\\
&D\phi_{z}(y^{\prime})&&\vdots\\
&&&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\ldots&0&\psi_{z}^{\prime}(y_{n})\end{array}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

Consequently, since
\psi_{z}^{\prime}(0)=0
, it holds that

\displaystyle D\widehat{\varphi}_{z}\big((Q_{z}F_{z})^{-1}(y)\big)e_{n}=D\widehat{\varphi}_{z}(x^{\prime},0)e_{n}=0

Thus, in view of (
3.139
), we conclude from (
3.142
) that

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\varphi}_{z}(y)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\widehat{\Omega}}(y)=0

for all
y\in\partial\widehat{\Omega}\cap U_{z}
.

By the above construction, it is clear that the sets
\textnormal{supp\,}\tilde{\varphi}_{z}
,
z\in\partial\widehat{\Omega}
, are an open cover of
\partial\widehat{\Omega}
. Hence, since
\partial\widehat{\Omega}
is compact, we can select
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}
such that

\displaystyle\partial\widehat{\Omega}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\{\tilde{\varphi}_{z_{j}}>0\}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}U_{z_{j}}.

Therefore, in the following, we simply write

\displaystyle U_{j}\coloneqq U_{z_{j}},\hskip 10.00002ptQ_{j}\coloneqq Q_{z_{j}},\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{j}\coloneqq\gamma_{z_{j}},\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\varphi}_{j}\coloneqq\tilde{\varphi}_{z_{j}}

for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. We further find an open set
U_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\overline{U_{0}}\subset\widehat{\Omega}
and a function
\tilde{\varphi}_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};[0,1])
with
\textnormal{supp\,}\tilde{\varphi}_{0}\subset U_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\overline{\widehat{\Omega}}\subset\bigcup_{j=0}^{N}\{\tilde{\varphi}_{j}>0\}\subset\bigcup_{j=0}^{N}U_{j}.

To simplify the notation, we further set
Q_{0}\coloneqq I
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{0}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Finally, we define

\displaystyle\varphi_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi_{j}(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\tilde{\varphi}_{j}(x)}{S(x)}&\text{if $S(x)>0$}\,,\\
0&\text{if $S(x)=0$}\end{cases}

for all
j\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
, where

\displaystyle S(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tilde{\varphi}_{j}(x)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}

This ensures that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N}\varphi_{j}=1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\overline{\widehat{\Omega}}$.}

By the above construction, the family

\displaystyle\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=0,...,N}\subset C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};[0,1])\cap W^{3,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is a partition of unity of
\widehat{\Omega}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}\cap U_{j}=Q_{j}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{j}}\cap U_{j},\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{supp\,}\varphi_{j}\subset U_{j},\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\varphi_{j}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\widehat{\Omega}}=0\;\;\text{on $\partial\widehat{\Omega}$}

(3.147)

for all
j\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
.

Step 3. Proof of the key estimates (
3.135
) and (
3.136
).

Let
v\in C^{2}_{c}\big(\,\overline{\widehat{\Omega}}\,\big)\cap W^{2,p}_{I}(\widehat{\Omega})
be arbitrary.
For every
j\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
, we further choose a function

\psi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n};[0,1])\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{supp }\psi_{j}\subseteq U_{j}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\psi_{j}=1\;\;\text{on $\text{supp }\varphi_{j}$}.

Now, we set
v_{j}\coloneqq v\varphi_{j}
for

.
Using the product rule along with (
3.147
), we find that

\displaystyle v_{j}

\displaystyle\in C^{2}_{c}\big(\overline{Q_{j}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{j}}}\big)\cap W^{2,p}_{I}(Q_{j}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{j}})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $j\in\{0,\ldots,N\}$.}

Recalling that
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\widehat{\Omega}}
is linear, we
use the decomposition
v=\sum_{j=0}^{N}v_{j}
, to obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\widehat{\Omega}}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\widehat{\Omega}}v_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\Big(J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1}+J^{\varepsilon}_{j,2}\Big).

(3.148)

with

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1}\coloneqq\psi_{j}\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\widehat{\Omega}}v_{j},\hskip 20.00003ptJ^{\varepsilon}_{j,2}\coloneqq(1-\psi_{j})\,\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}^{\widehat{\Omega}}v_{j}

(3.149)

for
j=0,\ldots,N
. We will now estimate these summands separately.

Ad
J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1}
:

Let
j\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
be arbitrary.
We obtain

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi_{j}(x)\int_{{\widehat{\Omega}}}\widehat{J}_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big(v_{j}(x)-v_{j}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,1}+J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,2}-J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,3}

with

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,1}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\psi_{j}(x)\int_{Q_{j}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{j}}}\widehat{J}_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,\big(v_{j}(x)-v_{j}(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,2}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\psi_{j}(x)\int_{{\widehat{\Omega}}\setminus U_{j}}\widehat{J}_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,v_{j}(x)\;\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,3}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\psi_{j}(x)\int_{Q_{j}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\gamma_{j}}\setminus U_{j}}\widehat{J}_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,v_{j}(x)\;\mathrm{d}y

for all
x\in\widehat{\Omega}
.
Since
\widehat{\rho}
and
\widehat{J}

(A1)
and
(A2)

Theorem
3.3
(for
j=0

\|J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,1}\|_{L^{p}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\|v_{j}\|_{W^{2,p}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\|v\|_{W^{2,p}(\widehat{\Omega})}.

(3.150)

We further recall that
\textnormal{supp\,}v_{j}\subseteq\textnormal{supp\,}\varphi_{j}\subset U_{j}
. Thus, for
x\in\textnormal{supp\,}\varphi_{j}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus U_{j}

Moreover, invoking the definition of
\widehat{\rho}

3.130

(b)

\displaystyle|J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,2}(x)|

\displaystyle\|J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,2}\|_{L^{p}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\varepsilon\|v_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\varepsilon\|v\|_{L^{p}(\widehat{\Omega})}.

The term

\displaystyle\|J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1,3}\|_{L^{p}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\varepsilon\|v\|_{L^{p}(\widehat{\Omega})}.

(3.152)

Combining (
3.153
) and (
3.4

\displaystyle\|J^{\varepsilon}_{j,1}\|_{L^{p}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\|v\|_{W^{2,p}(\widehat{\Omega})}.

(3.153)

for all

Ad

Hence, for all

\displaystyle|J^{\varepsilon}_{j,2}(x)|\leq\big(1-\psi_{j}(x)\big)\tilde{\delta}_{j}^{-3}\,\,\int_{\widehat{\Omega}}\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,|x-y||v_{j}(y)|\;\mathrm{d}y\,.

This directly implies

\displaystyle\|J^{\varepsilon}_{j,2}\|_{L^{p}(\widehat{\Omega})}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\tilde{\delta}_{j}^{-3p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigg(\int_{\widehat{\Omega}}\widehat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,|x-y|\;\mathrm{d}y\Bigg)^{p-1}
