Abstract
. Let S S be a concrete operator system represented on some Hilbert space H H . A C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -support of S S is the C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebra generated (via the Choi–Effros product) by S S inside an injective operator system acting on H H . By leveraging Hamana’s theory, we show that such a C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -support is unique precisely when C ∗ ​ ( S ) \mathrm{C}^{*}(S) is contained in every copy of the injective envelope of S S that acts on H H . Further, we demonstrate how the uniqueness of certain C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -supports can be used to give new characterizations of the unique extension property for ∗ * -representations, as well as the hyperrigidity of S S . In another direction, we utilize the collection of all C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -supports of S S to describe the subspace generated by the so-called abnormalities of S S , thereby complementing a result of Kakariadis.



1.
Introduction

In the theory of operator systems, a fundamental problem is to identify certain special extreme points among completely positive maps; these are the so-called absolute extreme points
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
or the nc extreme points
[

]

To be more precise, fix a Hilbert space

be an operator system. Put
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Let
\pi:A\to B(H_{\pi})
be a unital
*
-representation. Let
E
denote the set of unital completely positive maps
\varphi:A\to B(H_{\pi})
agreeing with
\pi
on
S
. When
E
reduces to the singleton
\{\pi\}
, we say that
\pi
has
unique extension property
with respect to
S
. As is now well understood, the irreducible
*
-representations of this type can be meaningfully interpreted as points in the noncommutative Choquet boundary of
S

[
31
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
12
]
. Furthermore, the question of
E
being a singleton relates to computations of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S

[
20
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
17
]
, as well as the hyperrigidity conjecture (see
[
16
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
6
]
and the references therein).

Since the unique extension property is well behaved with respect to direct sums, in determining whether
\pi
has the unique extension property, we may assume without loss of generality that
\pi
is in fact injective. To simplify notation in the foregoing discussion, we thus choose to focus on the case where
\pi
is simply the identity representation of
A
. When
A
contains the ideal of compact operators on
H
, Arveson’s famous boundary theorem
[
2
, Theorem 2.1.1]
gives a simple mechanism to detect the unique extension property. Our main results do not require this assumption, and are altogether quite different in spirit, as we explain next.

In order for
E
to be a singleton, it must be that
\varphi(a)=a
for every
a\in A
and
\varphi\in E
. Following the terminology introduced by Kakariadis in
[
26
]
, an element of the form
\varphi(a)-a
, for
\varphi\in E
and

, will be called an

of
S
. Throughout, we denote by
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\subset B(H)
the collection of all abnormalities.
Our new contributions in this paper are inspired by a result from
[
26
]
about the “restricted” abnormalities
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap A
, which utilized the theory of injective envelopes. To properly frame our work, we recount some of the details below; a more thorough account is given in Section
2
.

While the original impetus to study the unique extension property was Arveson’s proposed program for constructing the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope
[
1
]
, this took several decades to fully crystallise. In the meantime, Hamana
[
24
]
had given the first proof of existence of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope, building on a rather different set of tools and perspective. An
S
-projection
is a unital completely positive idempotent
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
\theta|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
. There is a natural partial order defined on the set of all
S
-projections, and it can be shown that minimal elements exist. These minimal
S
-projections are pivotal in Hamana’s construction. Indeed, if
\theta
is a minimal
S
-projection, then its range
R
is a so-called
injective envelope
for
S
that can, in addition, be turned into a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra using the Choi–Effros product induced by
\theta
. Furthermore, the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra generated by
S
inside of
R
can be verified to be the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
.

Naturally, then, minimal
S
-projections carry significant information about the operator system
S
. Kakariadis further reinforced this by showing in
[
26
]
that

(1)

\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap A=\ker\theta\cap A

for any minimal
S
-projection
\theta
. As well, he showed that this set coincides with the so-called Shilov ideal of
S
inside of
A
, as introduced by Arveson in
[
1
]
. Notably, not only does this statement describe a portion of
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
, but it also shows that
\ker\theta\cap A
is
independent
of
\theta
. (While the results of
[
26
]
are stated within the framework of unital completely contractive maps on unital operator spaces, there is a well-known equivalence between this category and our category of interest consisting of unital completely positive maps on operator systems; see
[
33
, Proposition 2.12]
).

From the point of view of the foregoing discussion on abnormalities and their connection with the unique extension property for the identity representation of
A
, it appears as though (
1
) paints an incomplete picture. Indeed, the natural problem that arises is to describe the full set
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
, and not just its intersection with
A
. In Section
3
, we aim to understand the configuration of all injective envelopes of
S
inside of
B(H)
, relative to
A
. While our motivation is to use the resulting information to describe the full set of abnormalities in order to improve (
1

[
32
]
. In order to summarize our results, we introduce some terminology.

A
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension
of
S
is a pair
(B,j)
consisting of a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
along with a unital completely isometric map

such that

containing

\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of

if there is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension

of

along with a unital surjective completely isometric map
\Gamma:B\to X
such
\Gamma\circ j=\operatorname{id}
. In other words, a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support is, roughly speaking, a linear copy of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension. We note here that operator systems that are completely isometric to
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras have been of interest in various settings; see for instance
[
30
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
and the references therein.

We show in Theorem

that all
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports arise in a rather concrete way, namely from the Choi–Effros product induced by an
S
-projection. Furthermore, this projection may be chosen to be minimal precisely when
(B,j)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
(Corollary
3.2
).
The following is a summary of Theorem
3.3
along with Corollary
3.4
, and is our first main result.

Theorem A
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and let
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Consider the following statements.

(i)

A
is the unique
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
.

(ii)

A
is contained in every copy of the injective envelope of
S
inside
B(H)
.

(iii)

A
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
.

(iv)

A
is contained in some copy of the injective envelope of
S
inside
B(H)
.

Then, we have

{\rm(i)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(ii)}\Rightarrow{\rm(iii)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(iv)}.

The proof of this result requires a fairly delicate analysis of Choi–Effros products, which we perform in Section
2
. As an application, we combine Theorem
A
with results of Paulsen
[
32
]
to give in Corollary
3.5
the following new characterization of the unique extension property for injective
*
-representations.

Theorem B
.

Let
\pi:A\to B(H)
be an injective unital
*
-representation. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\pi
has the unique extension property with respect to
S
.

(ii)

\pi(A)
is the unique
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
\pi(S)
.

Similarly, Corollary
3.7
characterizes hyperrigid operator systems using our notion of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support.

In Section
4
, we turn to the question of describing the full set of abnormalities.
We define the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-expanse
of
S
as

\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)=\operatorname{span}\{\theta(A)\}

where the span is taken over all minimal
S
-projections
\theta
. We show in Theorem
4.4
that, when the Shilov ideal of
S
in
A
is trivial, then the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-expanse of
S
coincides with the subspace generated by all
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports of
S
.
Our second main result reads as follows; see Theorem
4.3
.

Theorem C
.

Assume that
A
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
. Then,
we have

\operatorname{span}\operatorname{Ab}(S)=\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)\cap\ker\theta

for every minimal
S
-projection
\theta
.

Much like in Kakariadis’ result (
1
), we find that the intersection
\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)\cap\ker\theta
does not depend on the choice of minimal projection
\theta
.

It is a consequence of Hamana’s theory that, for a minimal
S
-projection
\theta
, the quotient map
B(H)\to B(H)/\ker\theta
enjoys what we call the
unique
(B(H)/\ker\theta)
-extension property
with respect to
S
(see Proposition
4.6
). Here,
B(H)/\ker\theta
is given the operator system quotient structure.
This naturally leads us to the question of whether there is a quotient of
B(H)
which, similarly, “forces” some sort of unique extension property on the identity representation of

.
More precisely, we are looking for a closed subspace
D\subset B(H)
, with corresponding quotient map
\delta:B(H)\to B(H)/D
, for which
\delta\circ\psi=\delta
whenever
\psi:A\to B(H)
is a unital completely positive map acting as the identity on

. To avoid trivialities, we also require that

be completely isometric on

, where
B(H)/D
is given the operator space quotient structure. As an application of Theorem
C
, we show in Corollary

that the norm-closed subspace generated by
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
has this property.

Finally, in Section
5
, we close the paper by analysing the set of abnormalities in some concrete examples, and by contrasting it with Kakariadis’ space
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap A
.

2.
Injective envelopes and Choi–Effros products

In this preliminary section, we gather some background material and prove some technical facts that are required later.

2.1.
Hamana’s theory of injective envelopes

We will use standard terminology and facts for operator spaces and operator systems, as can be found in many references such as
[
21
]
and
[
33
]
.

Let
R
be an operator system. Recall that
R
is said to be
injective
if, given an inclusion
S\subset T
of operator systems and a unital completely positive map
\psi:S\to R
, there exists a unital completely positive map
\Psi:T\to R
extending
\psi
. In the case where
R
is concretely represented on some Hilbert space
H
(so that
R\subset B(H)
), it easily follows from Arveson’s extension theorem that
R
is injective precisely when there is a unital completely positive idempotent map
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
with range equal to
R
.

Next, let
S
be an arbitrary operator system. An
extension
of
S
is a pair
(R,\iota)
consisting of an operator system
R
and a unital complete isometry
\iota:S\to R
. When
R
happens to be injective, then we say that
(R,\iota)
is an injective extension of
S
.
An injective extension
(R,\iota)
will be said to be an
injective envelope
of
S
if it has the property that given any other injective operator system
T
with
\iota(S)\subset T\subset R
, we must have
T=R
.

There are two other important properties that are relevant for our purposes. Let
(R,\iota)
be an extension of
S
. We say that
(R,\iota)
is
rigid
if the only unital completely positive map
\varphi:R\to R
satisfying
\varphi\circ\iota=\iota
is the identity map. Also, we say that
(R,\iota)
is
essential
if, whenever
X
is an operator space and
\varphi:R\to X
is a completely contractive map such that
\varphi\circ\iota
is completely isometric, it follows that in fact
\varphi
is completely isometric.

The following summarizes the well-known relationships between these notions, which we record here for ease of reference below.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
S
be an arbitrary operator system and let
(R,\iota)
be an extension of
S
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

(R,\iota)
is an injective envelope of
S
.

(ii)

R
is injective and
(R,\iota)
is a rigid extension of
S
.

(iii)

R
is injective and
(R,\iota)
is an essential extension of
S
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): See
[
33
, Corollary 15.7]
.

(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii): See
[
24
, Lemma 3.7]
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
T
be an injective operator system with
\iota(S)\subset T\subset R
. Let
\varphi:T\to R
denote the inclusion map, which is unital, completely isometric and satisfies
\varphi\circ\iota=\iota
. Let
\psi:R\to T
be a unital completely positive extension of the identity
T\to T
. Then,
\varphi\circ\psi:R\to R
satisfies
\varphi\circ\psi\circ\iota=\iota
. Since
(R,\iota)
is rigid, we infer that
\varphi\circ\psi
is the identity on
R
. Thus,
\varphi
is surjective and
R=T
.
∎

A straightforward consequence of this result is the uniqueness of the injective envelope. More precisely, if
(R,\iota)
and
(E,j)
are two injective envelopes of
S
, then there is a unital surjective completely isometric map
\theta:R\to E
such that
\theta\circ\iota=j
. We now turn to the question of existence of the injective envelope.

Choose a Hilbert space
H
on which
S
is concretely represented, so that we may view
S
as an operator system in
B(H)
. A unital completely positive map
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
is called an
S
-map
if
\theta|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
. If, in addition,
\theta
is idempotent (i.e.
\theta\circ\theta=\theta
), then
\theta
is called an
S
-projection
. We shall make use of the standard partial order on the set of all
S
-projections, defined as follows:
\psi\prec\theta
precisely when
\psi\circ\theta=\theta\circ\psi=\psi
. The following is one of Hamana’s fundamental ideas
[
24
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and let
i:S\to B(H)
denote the inclusion. Let
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
be an
S
-projection. Then,
\theta
is minimal if and only if
(\operatorname{ran}\theta,i)
is an injective envelope of
S
.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
2.1
along with
[
32
, Theorem 2.9]
.
∎

In light of this, we see that the existence of injective envelopes reduces to the existence of minimal projections. The following result insures that these always exist.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system. Let
\psi:B(H)\to B(H)
be an
S
-projection. Then, there is a minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
with
\operatorname{ran}\theta\subset\operatorname{ran}\psi
.

Proof.

By
[
24
, Theorem 3.5]
, there is a minimal
S
-projection
\theta_{0}:B(H)\to B(H)
. If we put
\theta=\psi\circ\theta_{0}
, then by
[
32
, Proposition 2.11]
it follows that
\theta
is a minimal
S
-projection as well, and by construction its range is contained in the range of
\psi
.
∎

2.2.
Choi–Effros products and the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope

Fix a Hilbert space
H
and let
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
be a unital completely positive idempotent map. Let
R\subset B(H)
denote the range of
\theta
, which is then an injective operator system. The
Choi–Effros product

*_{\theta}:R\times R\to R
is defined as

s*_{\theta}t=\theta(st),\quad s,t\in R.

Equipped with this product,
R
becomes a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra that we denote by
\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)
. The inclusion map
\alpha_{\theta}:R\to\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)
is unital, surjective and completely isometric
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
. Throughout our work, it will be important to distinguish between
R
and
\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)
, which will be accomplished by keeping track of the map
\alpha_{\theta}
. Given an operator system
X\subset R
, we denote by
\operatorname{CE}(X,\theta)
the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra generated by
\alpha_{\theta}(X)
inside of
\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)
.

Since
\theta
is idempotent and hence acts as the identity on its range
R
, we see that

\theta(st)=\theta(\theta(s)\theta(t))=\theta(s)*_{\theta}\theta(t),\quad s,t\in R.

Thus,
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta:\mathrm{C}^{*}(R)\to\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)
is a surjective
*
-homomorphism. In fact, much more is true as we shall see below in Proposition
2.6
.

Next, let
S\subset B(H)
be an arbitrary operator system. Recall from the introduction that a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension
of
S
is a pair
(A,j)
consisting of a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
along with a unital completely isometric map
j:S\to A
such that
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(j(S))
. A
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension
(A,j)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope
of
S
if it satisfies the following universal property: given another
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension
(B,\iota)
, there must exist a surjective unital
*
-homomorphism
\pi:B\to A
such that
\pi\circ\iota=j
. We then say that
\ker\pi
is the
Shilov ideal
of
\iota(S)
in
B
. It is easily seen that a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope is essentially unique, and that the Shilov ideal is unique.
On the other hand, the existence question for these objects is highly non-trivial, and it was first resolved by Hamana
[
24
, Theorem 4.1]
, as follows.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system. Let
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
be a minimal
S
-projection. Then,
(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta),\alpha_{\theta}|_{S})
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
.

In the remainder of this section we collect some further technical facts about Choi–Effros products and their corresponding
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras that will be of use later.
We start with a basic observation, found in
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
A
and
B
be unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras and let
\gamma:A\to B
be a unital completely isometric map. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

There is a unital
*
-homomorphism
\eta:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\gamma(A))\to A
such that
\eta\circ\gamma=\operatorname{id}
.

(ii)

If
\psi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\gamma(A))\to A
is a unital completely positive map satisfing
\psi\circ\gamma=\operatorname{id}
on
A
, then
\psi=\eta
.

We record an important uniqueness property for minimal
S
-projections.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and let
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Let
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
be a minimal
S
-projection with range
R
. Let
\pi:B(H)\to R
be an
S
-map. Then,
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\pi|_{A}:A\to\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)
is a
*
-homomorphism. Moreover,
\pi
agrees with
\theta
on
R+A
.

Proof.

Let
i:S\to B(H)
denote the inclusion. By Proposition
2.1
and Theorem
2.2
,
(R,i)
is a rigid extension of
S
. Since
\pi|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
, we infer by rigidity that
\pi|_{R}=\operatorname{id}
. In particular,
\pi
and
\theta
agree on
R
.

Next, let
\psi:R\to B(H)
be a unital completely positive extension of the identity on
S
, so that
\psi|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
. We find
\pi\circ\psi|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
, so once again by rigidity we infer
\pi\circ\psi=\operatorname{id}
on
R
. In particular,
\psi
is completely isometric and
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\pi\circ\psi\circ\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}=\operatorname{id}
on
\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)
. By applying Proposition
2.5
with
\gamma=\psi\circ\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}:\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)\to B(H)
, we find that
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\pi
restricts to a
*
-homomorphism on the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra of
B(H)
generated by
\psi(R)
, which in particular contains
A
.

Applying the argument of the previous paragraph with
\theta
instead of
\pi
, we see that
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta|_{A}
is a
*
-homomorphism. Finally, since
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta|_{A}
and
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\pi|_{A}
are
*
-homomorphisms agreeing on
S
, they must agree everywhere. Using that
\alpha_{\theta}
is completely isometric on
R
, it follows that
\theta
and
\pi
agree on
A
as well.
∎

Next, we establish a crucial technical tool, which allows for the comparison of various Choi–Effros products.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system. Let
\psi:B(H)\to B(H)
be an
S
-projection and let
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
be a minimal
S
-projection. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

The map

\pi=\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta\circ\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}|_{\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)}:\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)\to\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)

is a unital surjective
*
-homomorphism whose kernel is the Shilov ideal of
\alpha_{\psi}(S)
in
\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)
.

(ii)

If
\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))
is contained in the range of
\theta
, then

\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)).

(iii)

Assume that there is a unital
*
-homomorphism
\sigma:\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)\to\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)
such that
\sigma\circ\alpha_{\theta}|_{S}=\alpha_{\psi}|_{S}
. Then, there is a minimal
S
-projection
\theta^{\prime}:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
\alpha_{\theta^{\prime}}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta^{\prime}))=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we let
i:S\to B(H)
denote the inclusion. We let
R_{\psi}
and
R_{\theta}
denote the ranges of
\psi
and
\theta
respectively, which are both injective operator systems. Also, we consider the unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras
A=\operatorname{CE}(R_{\theta},\theta)
and
B=\operatorname{CE}(R_{\psi},\psi)
. Recall that the inclusion maps
\alpha_{\theta}:R_{\theta}\to A
and
\alpha_{\psi}:R_{\psi}\to B
are unital, surjective complete isometries.

(i) Since
R_{\psi}
is injective, there is a unital completely positive map
\gamma:R_{\theta}\to R_{\psi}
extending the identity map on
S
. Then,
\theta\circ\gamma:R_{\theta}\to R_{\theta}
is an
S
-map. On the other hand,
(R_{\theta},i)
is a rigid extension of
S
by Proposition
2.1
and Theorem
2.2
, so we find
\theta\circ\gamma=\operatorname{id}
on
R_{\theta}
. In particular,
\gamma
must be completely isometric on
R_{\theta}
, and thus so is
\alpha_{\psi}\circ\gamma\circ\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}:A\to B
. Applying Proposition
2.5
(ii), we infer that the map
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta\circ\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}:B\to A
restricts to a
*
-homomorphism on the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra of
B
generated by
(\alpha_{\psi}\circ\gamma)(R_{\theta})
, which certainly contains
\alpha_{\psi}(S)
and hence
\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)
.
In particular, this means that
(\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta\circ\alpha_{\psi}^{-1})(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))
is the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra of
A
generated by
S
, which in turn is the very definition of
\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)
. Therefore

(2)

(\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta\circ\alpha_{\psi}^{-1})(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))=\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta).

We have thus shown that the map
\pi=\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta\circ\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}|_{\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)}:\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)\to A
is a
*
-homomorphism with range
\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)
.

Next, observe that
(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta),\alpha_{\theta}|_{S})
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
by virtue of Theorem
2.4
. Since
\pi\circ\alpha_{\psi}|_{S}=\alpha_{\theta}|_{S}
by construction, it follows that
\ker\pi
is the Shilov ideal of
\alpha_{\psi}(S)
in
\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)
.

(ii) Using (
2
), we find
\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))=(\theta\circ\alpha_{\psi}^{-1})(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))
. If
\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))
is contained in the range of
\theta
, then the fact that
\theta
is idempotent yields

\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))

as desired.

(iii) Let
T=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))
, which is an operator system in
R_{\theta}
. By assumption, we see that
\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}\circ\sigma\circ\alpha_{\theta}:T\to R_{\psi}
is an
S
-map. By injectivity of
R_{\psi}
, we can extend this map to an
S
-map
\Phi:B(H)\to R_{\psi}
. Then,
\theta^{\prime}=\Phi\circ\theta
is another minimal
S
-projection by
[
32
, Proposition 2.11]
.

Recall now that for an
S
-projection
\omega:B(H)\to B(H)
with range
R
,
\alpha_{\omega}\circ\omega:\mathrm{C}^{*}(R)\to\operatorname{CE}(R,\omega)
is a
*
-homomorphism, so we infer that

\alpha_{\omega}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\omega))=\omega(\mathrm{C}^{*}(S))

is the norm-closed subspace of
B(H)
consisting of elements of the form
\omega(t_{1}t_{2}\ldots t_{n})
for any choice of finitely many
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in S
.

Therefore, to establish our claim that
\alpha_{\theta^{\prime}}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta^{\prime}))=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))
, it suffices to establish that, given
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in S
we must have
\theta^{\prime}(t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{n})=\psi(t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{n})
.
To see this, note that

(\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta)(t_{1}t_{2}\ldots t_{n})=t_{1}*_{\theta}t_{2}*_{\theta}\cdots*_{\theta}t_{n}\in\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)

so
\theta(t_{1}t_{2}\ldots t_{n})\in T
and

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}(t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{n})

\displaystyle=\Phi(\theta(t_{1}t_{2}\ldots t_{n}))=(\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}\circ\sigma\circ\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta)(t_{1}t_{2}\ldots t_{n})

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}\circ\sigma)(t_{1}*_{\theta}t_{2}*_{\theta}\cdots*_{\theta}t_{n})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\sigma(t_{1})*_{\psi}\sigma(t_{2})*_{\psi}\cdots*_{\psi}\sigma(t_{n}))

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(t_{1}*_{\psi}t_{2}*_{\psi}\cdots*_{\psi}t_{n})=\psi(t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{n})

since
\sigma
is a
*
-homomorphism.
∎

We remark here that the special case of statement (i) where
\psi
is the identity map is found in
[
26
, Corollary 3.1]
.

3.
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports and the unique extension property

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system. In this section, we study
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports as a means to examine the configuration of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
relative to all the copies of the injective envelope of
S
contained in
B(H)
. We then utilize the resulting information to characterize the unique extension property for injective
*
-representations.

3.1.
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports and containment in an injective envelope

An operator system
X\subset B(H)
containing
S
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
if there is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension
(B,j)
of
S
along with a unital, surjective, completely isometric map
\Gamma:B\to X
such
\Gamma\circ j=\operatorname{id}
. We note that if
X
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
, then it is a so-called
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-system
in the sense of
[
30
]
.

The collection of all
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports of
S
is actually exhausted by Choi–Effros products, as we show next.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system. Let
X\subset B(H)
be an operator system containing
S
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

X
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
.

(ii)

There is an
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
X=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))
.

Proof.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
B=\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)
. Then,
(B,\alpha_{\theta}|_{S})
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension of
S
. If we let
\Gamma=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}:B\to X
, then
\Gamma
is unital, surjective, completely isometric map such that
\Gamma\circ\alpha_{\theta}=\operatorname{id}
, as needed.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): By assumption, there is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension
(B,j)
of
S
along with a unital, surjective, completely isometric map
\Gamma:B\to X
such
\Gamma\circ j=\operatorname{id}
. Choose an isometric unital
*
-representation
\sigma:B\to B(K)
. Apply Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
to the operator system
X
to find a minimal
X
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
whose range
R
is an injective envelope of
X
. Then,
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\Gamma:B\to\operatorname{CE}(R,\theta)
is unital and completely isometric, and
\mathrm{C}^{*}((\alpha_{\theta}\circ\Gamma)(B))=\operatorname{CE}(X,\theta)
. By Proposition
2.5
, there is a unital
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\operatorname{CE}(X,\theta)\to B
such that
\pi\circ\alpha_{\theta}\circ\Gamma=\operatorname{id}
on
B
. In particular, this implies that
\sigma\circ\pi\circ\alpha_{\theta}|_{X}=\sigma\circ\Gamma^{-1}
is completely isometric.

Let
\psi:R\to B(K)
be a unital completely positive extension of
\sigma\circ\pi\circ\alpha_{\theta}:\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(X,\theta))\to B(K)
. Then,
\psi
is completely isometric on
X
by the previous paragraph. Letting
i:X\to B(H)
denote the inclusion,
(R,i)
is an essential extension of
X
by Proposition
2.1
. Consequently, we infer that
\psi
is completely isometric on
R
and hence on
\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(X,\theta))
. In other words,
\sigma\circ\pi\circ\alpha_{\theta}
is completely isometric on
\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(X,\theta))
. In turn, because
\sigma
is isometric, this implies that
\pi
must also be isometric, so it is a
*
-isomorphism.

Finally, observe that, on one hand,

\pi(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))=\mathrm{C}^{*}((\pi\circ\alpha_{\theta})(S))=\mathrm{C}^{*}((\pi\circ\alpha_{\theta}\circ\Gamma\circ j)(S))=\mathrm{C}^{*}(j(S))=B

and, on the other,

\pi(\alpha_{\theta}(X))=(\pi\circ\alpha_{\theta}\circ\Gamma)(B)=B.

Since
\pi
is a
*
-isomorphism, we must have
X=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))
.
∎

In light of the previous result, it is natural to wonder what kind of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
arises via the Choi–Effros product associated to a
minimal

S
-projection. Theorem
2.4
indicates that an answer should involve the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope in some way. More precisely, we have the following.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system. Let
X\subset B(H)
be an operator system containing
S
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

If
(A,j)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
, then there is a unital, surjective, completely isometric map
\Gamma:A\to X
such
\Gamma\circ j=\operatorname{id}
.

(ii)

There is a minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
such that

X=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)).

Proof.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): By the uniqueness property of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope, it suffices to establish the desired statement for a specific choice of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
. Thus, by virtue of Theorem
2.4
, we may choose
(A,j)=(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta),\alpha_{\theta}|_{S})
, in which case it suffices to take
\Gamma=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): By Theorems
2.3
and
2.4
, there is a minimal
S
-projection
\omega:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
(\operatorname{CE}(S,\omega),\alpha_{\omega}|_{S})
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
. By assumption, we obtain a unital, surjective, completely isometric map
\Gamma:\operatorname{CE}(S,\omega)\to X
such that
\Gamma\circ\alpha_{\omega}|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
.

Next, note that our assumption implies in particular that
X
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
. By applying Theorem
3.1
we can find an
S
-projection
\psi:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
X=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))
. Then, the map
\pi=\alpha_{\psi}\circ\Gamma:\operatorname{CE}(S,\omega)\to\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)
is a unital, surjective, completely isometric map between two
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, so by Proposition
2.5
it must be a
*
-isomorphism.
Moreover, we have

\pi\circ\alpha_{\omega}|_{S}=\alpha_{\psi}\circ\Gamma\circ\alpha_{\omega}|_{S}=\alpha_{\psi}|_{S}.

Applying Proposition
2.7
(iii), we may find another minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
such that

\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))=X.

∎

We now combine our findings to elucidate the relative configuration of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
with respect to the various injective envelopes of
S
inside of
B(H)
. The following is the first main result of the paper.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

If
X\subset B(H)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
and
(A,j)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
, then there is a unital, surjective, completely isometric map
\Gamma:A\to X
such that
\Gamma\circ j=\operatorname{id}
.

(ii)

(\mathrm{C}^{*}(S),i)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
, where
i:S\to B(H)
is the inclusion map.

(iii)

\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
is contained in some injective operator system
R\subset B(H)
such that
(R,\kappa)
is an injective envelope of
S
, where
\kappa:R\to B(H)
is the inclusion map.

(iv)

For each
a\in\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
, there is an injective operator system
R\subset B(H)
such that
a\in R
and
(R,\kappa)
is an injective envelope of
S
, where
\kappa:R\to B(H)
is the inclusion map.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Let
(A,j)
be a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
. Plainly,
(\mathrm{C}^{*}(S),i)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-extension of
S
, and hence a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support. By assumption, there is a unital, surjective, completely isometric map
\Gamma:A\to\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
such that
\Gamma\circ j=\operatorname{id}
. By Proposition
2.5
, we infer that
\Gamma
is a
*
-isomorphism. Thus,
(\mathrm{C}^{*}(S),i)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Fix a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support
X\subset B(H)
of
S
. By Theorem
3.1
, there is an
S
-projection
\psi:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
X=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi))
. Furthermore, denoting the range of
\psi
by
R_{\psi}\subset B(H)
, we know that
\alpha_{\psi}\circ\psi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(R_{\psi})\to\operatorname{CE}(R_{\psi},\psi)
is a unital
*
-homomorphism by construction of the Choi–Effros product. Let
\pi=(\alpha_{\psi}\circ\psi)|_{\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)}
, which is a
*
-homomorphism satisfying
\pi|_{S}=\alpha_{\psi}|_{S}
. In particular, the image of
\pi
is
\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)
.

Next, our assumption is that
(\mathrm{C}^{*}(S),i)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
, so there must exist a unital surjective
*
-homomorphism
\sigma:\operatorname{CE}(S,\psi)\to\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
such that
\sigma\circ\alpha_{\psi}|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
. We have

\sigma\circ\pi|_{S}=\sigma\circ\alpha_{\psi}|_{S}=\operatorname{id}

and

\pi\circ\sigma\circ\alpha_{\psi}|_{S}=\pi|_{S}=\alpha_{\psi}|_{S}.

Consequently, because
\pi
and
\sigma
are
*
-homomorphisms, we find
\pi=\sigma^{-1}
so
\pi
is completely isometric.
Hence, we may define
\Gamma=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}\circ\pi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)\to X
, which is a unital, surjective, completely isometric map satisfying
\Gamma|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
. This establishes the desired statement for the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope
(\mathrm{C}^{*}(S),i)
. The corresponding statement for any
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
follows readily by uniqueness.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): We know that
(\mathrm{C}^{*}(S),i)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
. Thus, applying Corollary
3.2
, we find a minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
such that

\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)).

In particular, this shows that
\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
is contained in the range of
\theta
. Furthermore, if we let
\kappa:\operatorname{ran}\theta\to B(H)
denote the inclusion, then
(\operatorname{ran}\theta,\kappa)
is an injective envelope of
S
by Theorem
2.2
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv): This is trivial.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Let
a\in\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
be an element in the Shilov ideal of
S
in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. By assumption, there is an injective operator system
R\subset B(H)
containing
a
such that
(R,\kappa)
is an injective envelope of
S
, where
\kappa:R\to B(H)
denote the inclusion map. Since
R
is injective, we may choose an
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
with range
R
. In turn, Theorem
2.2
implies that
\theta
must be a minimal
S
-projection. By virtue of Proposition
2.7
(i) applied with
\psi=\operatorname{id}
, we see that
a
must lie in the kernel of
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta
, and hence
\theta(a)=0
. On other hand,
\theta(a)=a
since
a
lies in the range of the idempotent
\theta
, so we conclude
a=0
. Therefore, the Shilov ideal of
S
in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
is trivial, so that
(\mathrm{C}^{*}(S),i)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
.
∎

The previous result characterizes when the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

inside of

.

Let

be an operator system. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
is the unique
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
.

(ii)

\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)

containing

.

\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Let

\theta:B(H)\to B(H)

(ii)
\Rightarrow

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let

\mathrm{C}^{*}

. By assumption, we see that
\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
is contained in every operator system

. Invoking Theorem

, we infer that if

\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of

, then there is a unital, surjective, completely isometric map

.
∎

3.2.
A new characterization of the unique extension property

Let
A
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
S\subset A
be an operator system such that
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Let
\pi:A\to B(H)
be a unital
*
-representation. We say that
\pi
has the
unique extension property
with respect to
S
if, given a unital completely positive map
\psi:A\to B(H)
such that
\pi|_{S}=\psi|_{S}
, we must necessarily have that
\pi=\psi
.

As an application of the main results of this section, we now give a new characterization of this property for injective representations.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
A
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
S\subset A
be an operator system such that
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Let
\pi:A\to B(H)
be an injective unital
*
-representation. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\pi
has the unique extension property with respect to
S
.

(ii)

\pi(A)
is the unique
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
\pi(S)
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\subset B(H)
denote the set of elements
t
for which
\varphi(t)=t
for every
\pi(S)
-map
\varphi:B(H)\to B(H)
. It is readily seen that the identity representation of
\pi(A)
has the unique extension property with respect to
\pi(S)
if and only if
\pi(A)\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. By virtue of Arveson’s well-known invariance principle for the unique extension property (see for instance
[
1
, Theorem 2.1.2]
), we see that (i) is then equivalent to
\pi(A)\subset{\mathcal{F}}
.

Corollary
3.4
shows that statement (ii) is equivalent to the fact that
\pi(A)\subset\bigcap R
, where the intersection is taken over all operators systems
R\subset B(H)
such that
(R,i)
is an injective envelope of
\pi(S)
(here
i:R\to B(H)
denotes the inclusion, as usual). Thus, the desired equivalence of (i) and (ii) will follow once we show that
{\mathcal{F}}=\bigcap R
, but this is precisely the content of
[
32
, Proposition 4.3]
.
∎

We remark that condition (ii) above can further be understood in terms of containment of
\pi(A)
inside every injective operator system
R\subset B(H)
that contains
\pi(S)
; see Corollary
3.4
.

The operator system
S
is said to be
hyperrigid
in
A
if every unital
*
-representation of
A
has the unique extension property with respect to
S
. The following is well known; it appears implicitly for instance the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
. Since we lack an exact reference, we give the short argument here.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
A
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
S\subset A
be an operator system such that
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

S
is hyperrigid in
A
.

(ii)

Every injective unital
*
-representation of
A
has the unique extension property with respect to
S
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): This is trivial.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
\pi:A\to B(H)
be an arbitrary unital
*
-representation. Choose an injective unital
*
-representation
\sigma:A\to B(K)
. Then,
\pi\oplus\sigma
is injective on
A
, so that
\pi\oplus\sigma
has the unique extension property with respect to
S
by assumption. In turn, by
[
11
, Lemma 2.8]
, we conclude that
\pi
has the unique extension property with respect to
S
. Hence
S
is hyperrigid in
A
.
∎

We can also give a new characterization of hyperrigidity for operator systems in terms of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
A
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
S\subset A
be an operator system such that
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

S
is hyperrigid in
A
.

(ii)

Let
\pi:A\to B(H)
be an injective unital
*
-representation. Then,
\pi(A)
is the unique
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
\pi(S)
.

Proof.

This follows at once upon combining Corollary
3.5
with Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Hyperrigid operator systems are plentiful amongst naturally occurring concrete classes of operator systems; see for instance
[
9
]
,
[
29
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
36
]
and the references therein. For such operator systems, the previous corollary shows that
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports are severely constrained.

4.
The space of abnormalities

As discussed in the introduction, one of our motivations for performing a detailed study of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports is to improve our understanding of abnormalities and to sharpen Kakariadis’ result (
1
). We tackle this question in this section.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and put
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Let
E
denote the set of unital completely positive maps
\varphi:A\to B(H)
agreeing with the identity on
S
. The set of
abnormalities
of
S
is defined to be

\operatorname{Ab}({\mathcal{S}})=\{\varphi(a)-a:a\in A,\varphi\in E\}.

Our first aim is to show that the set of abnormalities is always contained in the kernel of certain maps. To streamline the exposition, we introduce the following terminology.
Let
R
be another operator system and let
\pi:A\to R
be a unital completely positive map. We say that
\pi
has the
unique
R
-extension property
with respect to
S
if, given any unital completely positive map
\psi:A\to R
such that
\pi|_{S}=\psi|_{S}
, it follows that
\pi=\psi
. The key point here is that we restrict the range of the extension
\psi
to lie in
R
, thus making this notion a priori weaker than the previously introduced unique extension property (see Subsection
3.2
). Such relaxed versions of the unique extension property have appeared in recent works
[
13
]
,
[
19
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and put
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

Let
R
be an operator system and let
\pi:B(H)\to R
be a unital completely positive map with the property that
\pi|_{A}
has the unique
R
-extension property with respect to
S
. Then,
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\subset\ker\pi
.

(ii)

Let
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
be a minimal
S
-projection. Then,
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\subset\ker\theta
.

Proof.

(i) Let
d\in\operatorname{Ab}(S)
, so that there is a unital completely positive map
\varphi:A\to B(H)
with
\varphi|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
and
a\in A
such that
d=\varphi(a)-a
. We see that
\pi\circ\varphi:A\to R
is a unital completely positive extension of
\pi|_{S}
. Thus, the assumption on
\pi
yields
\pi|_{A}=\pi\circ\varphi
, so
\pi(a)=(\pi\circ\varphi)(a)
. Equivalently,
\pi(d)=0
, as desired.

(ii) Let
R\subset B(H)
denote the range of
\theta
. Then,
\theta|_{A}
has the unique
R
-extension property with respect to
S
by virtue of Proposition
2.6
, so the conclusion follows from (i).
∎

In what follows, we let
\Sigma\subset A
denote the Shilov ideal of
S
in
A
. Recall that if
(A,j)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
,
\Sigma
is the kernel of the unique unital surjective
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)\to A
such that
\pi|_{S}=j
. Apply Proposition
2.7
(i) with
\psi=\operatorname{id}
to see that

(3)

\Sigma=\ker(\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta)\cap A

for any minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
. Combining this with Lemma
4.1
, we see that

(4)

\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap A\subset\Sigma.

The converse inclusion also holds, as shown in
[
26
, Proposition 3.4]
. We record the statement for ease of reference below.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and let
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Then,
\Sigma=\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap A
.

Our next task is to give a description of the full set
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
. We define the
expanse
of
S
to be the operator system

\operatorname{Ex}(S)=\operatorname{span}\{\varphi(A)\}

where the span is taken over all
S
-maps
\varphi:B(H)\to B(H)
. We now arrive at our announced description of the abnormalities, which is the second main result of the paper.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system. Then,

\operatorname{span}\operatorname{Ab}(S)=\operatorname{Ex}(S)\cap\ker\theta

for every minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof we write
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. It follows immediately from the definition that
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\subset\operatorname{Ex}(S)
. Fix a minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
.
Then,

\operatorname{span}\operatorname{Ab}(S)\subset\operatorname{Ex}(S)\cap\ker\theta

by Lemma
4.1
. Conversely, let
t\in\operatorname{Ex}(S)\cap\ker\theta
. Then, there are elements
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
and
S
-maps
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
such that
t=a_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varphi_{j}(a_{j})
. Let
R\subset B(H)
denote the range of
\theta
. For each
1\leq j\leq n
, we see that
\theta\circ\varphi_{j}:B(H)\to R
is a completely positive map with
\theta
on
S
. By Proposition
2.6
, we infer
\theta\circ\varphi_{j}=\theta
on
A
. On the other hand, we know that
t\in\ker\theta
, whence

0=\theta(t)=\theta(a_{0})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\theta\circ\varphi_{j})(a_{j})=\theta(a_{0}+a_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}).

Thus, the element
b=a_{0}+\ldots+a_{n}
lies in
\ker\theta\cap A
, so in fact
b\in\Sigma
by (
3
). In turn, Proposition
4.2
implies that
b\in\operatorname{Ab}(S)
. Finally, we find

t=a_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varphi_{j}(a_{j})=b+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\varphi_{j}(a_{j})-a_{j})\in\operatorname{span}\operatorname{Ab}(S).\qed

In light of the previous result, one may wonder if
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
is a subspace of
B(H)
. It follows from Proposition
4.2
that
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap A
is indeed a subspace, but we do not know if the same is true of
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
.

Next, Theorem
4.3
makes it desirable to gain a good understanding of the set
\operatorname{Ex}(S)
. Towards this goal, we examine a more structured subset of it. We define the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-expanse
of
S
as

\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)=\operatorname{span}\{\theta(A)\}

where the span is taken over all minimal
S
-projections
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
.

This last definition can be reformulated slightly. Indeed, let
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
be an
S
-projection. Because
\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta
is a
*
-homomorphism on
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\operatorname{ran}\theta)
and
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
, it follows that
\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta)=(\alpha_{\theta}\circ\theta)(A)
, so that
\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))=\theta(A)
. We conclude that

(5)

\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)=\operatorname{span}\{\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))\}

where the span is taken over all minimal
S
-projections
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
.
As a consequence, we obtain the following interpretation of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-expanse.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and let
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Assume that the Shilov ideal of
S
in
A
is trivial. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

If
\varphi:B(H)\to B(H)
is an
S
-map, then there is a minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
\varphi=\theta
on
A
.

(ii)

\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)=\operatorname{Ex}(S)

(iii)

\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)
is the smallest subspace generated by all
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports of
S
.

Proof.

(i) Since the Shilov ideal of
S
in
A
is trivial, we see that
(A,i)
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
S
, where
i:S\to B(H)
is the inclusion map. We may apply Theorem
3.3
to find an injective operator system
R\subset B(H)
containing
A
such that
(R,j)
is an injective envelope of
S
, where
j:R\to B(H)
is the inclusion. By Theorem
2.2
, there is thus a minimal
S
-projection
\omega:B(H)\to B(H)
with range
R
, and in particular
\omega=\operatorname{id}
on
A
. Finally, by
[
32
, Proposition 2.11]
, we see that the map
\theta=\varphi\circ\omega
is another minimal
S
-projection which satisfies
\varphi=\theta
on
A
.

(ii) Trivially, the inclusion
\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S\subset\operatorname{Ex}(S)
always holds. Conversely, we may apply (i) to see that
\operatorname{Ex}(S)\subset\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)
.

(iii) Let
Y\subset B(H)
denote the smallest subspace generated by all
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-supports of
S
. By virtue of (
5
), we see that
\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)\subset Y
. Conversely, let
X\subset B(H)
be a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-support of
S
. Combining Corollary
3.2
with Theorem
3.3
, we see that there is a minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
X=\alpha_{\theta}^{-1}(\operatorname{CE}(S,\theta))\subset\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)
. This shows that
Y\subset\mathrm{C}^{*}\operatorname{Ex}(S)
.
∎

As an application, we show next that in the definition of
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
, it suffices to consider only those maps
\varphi\in E
that are minimal
S
-projections, provided that the Shilov ideal is assumed to be trivial.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and let
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Assume that the Shilov ideal of
S
in
A
is trivial. Let
t\in B(H)
. Then,
t\in\operatorname{Ab}(S)
if and only if there is a minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
and an element
a\in A
such that
t=\theta(a)-a
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorem
4.4
(i).
∎

We close this section with an application of the previous results, motivated by the following complement to Proposition
2.6
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and let
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Let
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
be a minimal
S
-projection, and let
q_{\theta}:B(H)\to B(H)/\ker\theta
denote the quotient map. Then,
q_{\theta}
is completely isometric on the range of
\theta
, and
q_{\theta}|_{A}
has the unique
(B(H)/\ker\theta)
-extension property with respect to
S
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we let
R\subset B(H)
denote the range of
\theta
.
As explained in
[
28
, Section 3]
,
B(H)/\ker\theta
admits a natural operator system structure with the property that
q_{\theta}
is unital and completely positive.
Since
\theta
is idempotent, we have that
\theta(t)-t\in\ker\theta
for each
t\in B(H)
, so
q_{\theta}\circ\theta=q_{\theta}
. This implies that the linear isomorphism

\lambda_{\theta}:B(H)/\ker\theta\to R
defined as

\lambda_{\theta}(q_{\theta}(t))=\theta(t),\quad t\in B(H)

satisfies
q_{\theta}\circ\lambda_{\theta}=\operatorname{id}
on
B(H)/\ker\theta
. In particular, we see that
\lambda_{\theta}
is completely isometric, and
q_{\theta}
is completely isometric on
R
.

Let
\varphi:A\to B(H)/\ker\theta
be a unital completely positive map agreeing with
q_{\theta}
on
S
. Then,
\lambda_{\theta}\circ\varphi:A\to R
is a unital completely positive map agreeing with
\lambda_{\theta}\circ q_{\theta}=\theta
on
S
.
Hence, Proposition
2.6
implies that
\lambda_{\theta}\circ\varphi=\theta|_{A}
, so that

q_{\theta}|_{A}=(q_{\theta}\circ\theta)|_{A}=q_{\theta}\circ\lambda_{\theta}\circ\varphi=\varphi.

∎

We now give an application of Theorem
4.3
that has a flavour similar to that of the previous proposition. Indeed, we show that, when the Shilov ideal is trivial, there is a single quotient of
B(H)
that is completely isometric on
S
and for which a conclusion reminiscent of Proposition
4.6
can be obtained
simultaneously
for every
S
-map on
A
.

For this purpose, we recall that given a closed subspace
D\subset B(H)
, the quotient
B(H)/D
can be given a natural operator space structure
[
33
, Exercise 13.3]
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
S\subset B(H)
be an operator system and let
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. Assume that the Shilov ideal of
S
in
A
is trivial. Let
D\subset B(H)
denote the norm-closed subspace generated by
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
, and let
\delta:B(H)\to B(H)/D
denote the quotient map. Then, the following statements hold for each unital completely positive map
\varphi:A\to B(H)
satisfying
\varphi|_{S}=\operatorname{id}
.

(i)

The map
\delta
is completely isometric on
\varphi(A)
.

(ii)

We have
\delta\circ\varphi=\delta
on
A
.

Proof.

(i) Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
[a_{ij}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(A)
. Put
b=[\varphi(a_{ij})]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(B(H))
. By Theorem
4.4
(i), we may find a minimal
S
-projection
\theta:B(H)\to B(H)
such that
\varphi=\theta
on
A
. In particular,
\theta(\varphi(a_{ij}))=\varphi(a_{ij})
for each
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Hence,
\theta^{(n)}(b)=b
. By Proposition
4.6
, we see that
q_{\theta}
is completely isometric on the range of
\theta
, so in particular

\|q_{\theta}^{(n)}(b)\|=\|b\|.

In turn, it follows from Theorem
4.3
that
D\subset\ker\theta
, whence
\|\delta^{(n)}(b)\|\geq\|q_{\theta}^{(n)}(b)\|
and
\|\delta^{(n)}(b)\|=\|b\|
as desired.

(ii) For each
a\in A
, we see that
\varphi(a)-a\in\operatorname{Ab}(S)\subset D
, whence
\delta(\varphi(a)-a)=0
.
∎

5.
Some calculations of abnormalities

In this final section, we examine some examples from the perspective of our results on abnormalities.

Example 1
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 2
. Let
{\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d}
denote the full Fock space over
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
and let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}\in B({\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d})
denote the isometric left creation operators. Let
V_{d}\subset B({\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d})
denote the operator system generated by
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
. Then,
V_{d}
generates the Cuntz–Toeplitz
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}\subset B({\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d})
, which contains the ideal
K
of compact operators on
{\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d}

[
35
, Theorem 1.3]
. The quotient
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}/K
is
*
-isomorphic to the Cuntz algebra
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
, and we let
q:{\mathcal{E}}_{d}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
denote the corresponding quotient map. For each
1\leq j\leq d
, we let
s_{j}=q(\lambda_{j})
. Then, each
s_{j}
is an isometry and
\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}s_{j}^{*}=1
. By virtue of
[
34
, Theorem 3.1]
, we see that
q
is completely isometric on
V_{d}
. We claim that
\operatorname{Ab}(V_{d})=K
.

To see this, first note that since
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
is simple and
q
is completely isometric on
V_{d}
,
({\mathcal{O}}_{d},q|_{V_{d}})
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-envelope of
V_{d}
. Hence, the Shilov ideal of
V_{d}
inside of
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}
is simply
\ker q=K
. We conclude that
K=\operatorname{Ab}(V_{d})\cap{\mathcal{E}}_{d}\subset\operatorname{Ab}(V_{d})
by Proposition
4.2
. For the converse, consider the quotient map
\pi:B({\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d})\to B({\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d})/K
. Choose a Hilbert space
H
such that
B({\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d})/K\subset B(H)
, so that
\pi
may be viewed as a unital completely positive map from
B({\mathbb{F}}^{2}_{d})
to
B(H)
satisfying
\pi(\lambda_{j})=s_{j}
for each
1\leq j\leq d
. A well known multiplicative domain argument shows that
\pi
has the unique extension property with respect to
V_{d}
. Applying Lemma
4.1
we infer that
\operatorname{Ab}(V_{d})\subset\ker\pi=K
.
∎

Finally, we illustrate that the set
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
may be much smaller than
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
, thereby showing that our Theorem
4.3
gives information that was previously unattainable via Kakariadis’ original result (
1
).

Example 2
.

Let
A_{0}
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra generated by some operator system
S_{0}
. Assume that all irreducible
*
-representations of
A_{0}
have the unique extension property with respect to
S_{0}
, while
S_{0}
is
not
hyperrigid in
A_{0}
. In other words, we are assuming that the inclusion
S_{0}\subset A_{0}
fails to satisfy Arveson’s hyperrigidity conjecture
[
4
]
; such operator systems do exist as recently shown in
[
5
]
.

By Lemma
3.6
, there is an injective unital
*
-representation
\pi:A_{0}\to B(H)
without the unique extension property with respect to
S_{0}
. Let
S=\pi(S_{0})
and
A=\pi(A_{0})
, so that
A=\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)
. By
[
1
, Theorem 2.1.2]
it then follows that the identity representation of
A
does not have the unique extension property with respect to
S
, so
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
is non-zero.

On the other hand, by our assumption on
S_{0}
and
A_{0}
, it is still true that every irreducible
*
-representation of
A
has the unique extension property with respect to
S
. In particular,
the Shilov ideal of
S
in
A
is necessarily trivial
[
1
, Theorem 2.2.3]
, so that
\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap A=\{0\}
by Proposition
4.2
. Thus, in this case we see that the inclusion

\{0\}=\operatorname{Ab}(S)\cap A\subset\operatorname{Ab}(S)

is strict.

By definition of
\operatorname{Ab}(S)
, this is equivalent to the existence of an
S
-map
\varphi:B(H)\to B(H)
that does not send
A
back into itself. We note in passing that this conclusion is consistent with the findings of
[
37
, Corollary 4.5]
, since in this case
\pi
has the so-called approximate unique extension property with respect to
S_{0}
. ∎
