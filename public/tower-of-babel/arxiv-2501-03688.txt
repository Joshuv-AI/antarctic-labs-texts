On Beating 2 n 2^{n} for the Closest Vector Problem
Amir Abboud, Rajendra Kumar

Abstract




The Closest Vector Problem (CVP) is a computational problem in lattices that is central to modern cryptography.
The study of its fine-grained complexity has gained momentum in the last few years, partly due to the upcoming deployment of lattice-based cryptosystems in practice.
A main motivating question has been if there is a
(2-\varepsilon)^{n}
time algorithm on lattices of rank
n
, or whether it can be ruled out by SETH.

Previous work came tantalizingly close to a negative answer by showing a
2^{(1-o(1))n}
lower bound under SETH if the underlying distance metric is changed from the standard
\ell_{2}
norm to other
\ell_{p}
norms (specifically, any norm where
p
is not an even integer).
Moreover, barriers toward proving such results for
\ell_{2}
(and any even
p
) were established.

In this paper we show
positive results
for a natural special case of the problem that has hitherto seemed just as hard, namely
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}
where the lattice vectors are restricted to be sums of subsets of basis vectors (meaning that all coefficients are
0
or
1
).
All previous hardness results applied to this problem, and none of the previous algorithmic techniques could benefit from it.
We prove the following results, which follow from new reductions from
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}
to weighted Max-SAT and minimum-weight
k
-Clique.

•

An
O(1.7299^{n})
time algorithm for exact
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
in Euclidean norm, breaking the natural
2^{n}
barrier, as long as the absolute value of all coordinates in the input vectors is
2^{o(n)}
.

•

A computational equivalence between
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
and Max-
p
-SAT for all even
p
(a reduction from Max-
p
-SAT to
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
was previously known).

•

The minimum-weight-
k
-Clique conjecture from fine-grained complexity and its numerous consequences (which include the APSP conjecture) can now be supported by the hardness of a lattice problem, namely
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
.

Similar results also hold for the Shortest Vector Problem.

Introduction

A lattice
\mathcal{L}
of rank
n
is the set of all integer linear combinations of a set of
n
linearly independent

\mathbf{B}=(\bm{b}_{1},\bm{b}_{2},\ldots,\bm{b}_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}
,
i.e.

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}):=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}z[i]\bm{b}_{i}\;|\;z[i]\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

The two most important computational problems on lattices are the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (
\mathrm{CVP}
).
Given a basis
\mathbf{B}

\mathrm{SVP}
asks to find a shortest non-zero vector in the lattice

\mathrm{CVP}

\bm{t}

\gamma\geq 1

\gamma

\mathrm{SVP}
asks to find a non zero lattice vector whose length is atmost
\gamma
times the length of shortest non-zero lattice vector. Similarly in
\gamma
-
\mathrm{CVP}
we need to find a lattice vector whose distance from the target is at most
\gamma
times the minimum distance between the lattice and the target vector.

Starting from the celebrated
LLL
algorithm by Lenstra, Lenstra, and Lovász in 1982
[
LLL82
]

[

]
, convex optimization
[

,
FT87
]
, cryptanalytic tools
[

,
Bri84
,

]

[

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
is based on their hardness.

The first reason cryptographers are excited about these problems is that they may be the key to the holy grail of basing security on NP-hard problems.
The motivation for this was in Ajtai’s works that (1) proved the NP-hardness of
\mathrm{SVP}

[
Ajt98
]
(it was already known for
\mathrm{CVP}

[
vEB81
,
ABSS97
]
),
and (2) designed a cryptographic hash function that is secure assuming
\mathrm{SVP}
is hard to approximate up to a

[
Ajt96
]
.
Many follow-up works have tried to reduce the gap between the approximation factors that are provably NP-hard
[

,
Mic01
,
Kho06
,
Kho05
,
HR12
,
Mic12
]
and those on which crypto can be based
[

,

,

,

]

However, there is still a gap with certain formal barriers against closing it

The second reason for excitement is that these problems are believed to be hard for

Indeed, such a scheme

]
will soon replace the currently used number-theoretic schemes that are known to be breakable if a large quantum computer is built

]
.
In practice, where there is a trade-off between security and efficiency, system designers assume the hardness of approximate

Thus, even a “mild” improvement from

Many papers across the last 25 years aim to improve the base of the exponent for
\mathrm{CVP}
and

The first exact algorithm was designed by Kannan

Ajtai, Kumar and Sivakumar introduced a randomized sieving technique and proposed a

1.1
Our Results

In this paper we present new algorithms suggesting that, in the Euclidean norm,
\mathrm{CVP}
and
\mathrm{SVP}
are easier than previously thought, or rather that they are genuinely easier under assumptions that were hitherto considered mild.

Specifically, our results concern the

-

\mathrm{CVP}

1.2
Technical Overview

The first idea in our reduction from
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}
to minimum-weight
k
-Clique (
Theorem

3.2
) is to use a
split-and-list
approach; a standard technique in exponential time algorithms similar to the famous meet-in-the-middle algorithm for
Subset Sum
.
We partition the
n
basis vectors
\mathbf{B}
arbitrarily into
k
sets
\mathbf{B}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{B}^{(k)}
of size
n/k
each, and then for each set we enumerate all
N:=2^{n/k}
vectors attainable by taking the sum of a subset of vectors from the set.
This produces
k
lists
\mathbf{C}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{C}^{(k)}
of
N=2^{n/k}
vectors each.
Observe that the closest vector we are looking for
\bm{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z[i]\bm{b}_{i}
can be represented as the sum of
k
vectors
z=\bm{c}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{c}_{k}
, one from each list
\bm{c}_{i}\in\mathbf{C}^{(i)}
, and moreover, any sum of
k
vectors from
\mathbf{C}^{(1)}\times\cdots\times\mathbf{C}^{(k)}
is a valid candidate for being the closest vector.
Thus, our task becomes to find the optimal way of picking one vector from each list.
A similar idea of applying split-and-list to the
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}
problem (over any norm) was used by Gupte and Vaikuntanathan
[
GV21
]
to prove conditional lower bounds for the
Sparse Linear Regression
problem.

Superficially, it may seem that we are done: simply represent each vector
\bm{c}_{i}
with a node and let a
k
-clique represent the sum of
k
vectors. All we have to do is ensure that the total weight of the
k
-clique corresponds to the distance to the target
\bm{t}
. Then, the minimum-weight
k
-clique will give us the closest vector.

However, the total weight of a
k
-clique can only be influenced by
pairwise
contributions from its
k
nodes.
In the
\mathrm{CVP}
interpretation, we require that the distance of the vector
z=\bm{c}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{c}_{k}
from the target
\bm{t}
can be measured by only considering the sum of
{k\choose 2}
values that depend only on
\bm{c}_{i},\bm{c}_{j}
for all
i,j\in[k]
, i.e. the sum of some pairwise contributions
\sum_{i,j\in[k]}f(\bm{c}_{i},\bm{c}_{j})
.
Unfortunately, this is impossible (and would refute SETH)
unless we restrict the metric space
.

Our second (and main) idea is that
under the Euclidean norm
the expression
\|\bm{c}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{c}_{k}-t\|^{2}
can be broken into a sum that depends only on pairs
\bm{c}_{i},\bm{c}_{j}
and can therefore be implemented as the weight of a
k
-clique under a careful choice of weights.
Interestingly, this ability to separate the distance in
\ell_{p}
norm into
p
-wise contributions only holds when
p
is even, and it is also the underlying technical reason that enables the barrier results of Aggarwal and Kumar
[
AK23
]
.

At a high level, the reduction to Max-SAT (
Theorem

1.4
) can be viewed as setting
k
to be
n
in the above reduction, and then adjusting several implementation details that have to do with SAT vs. Clique.
When
k=n
we are essentially partitioning the set of basis vectors into singletons and thinking of all possible
2^{1}=2
choices of either choosing the vector or not. Naturally, each such singleton can be represented with a Boolean variable that determines if the corresponding vector is chosen in the solution.
Then it remains to encode the distance of the solution from the target using pairwise contributions (that can be encoded as the weight of a width-
2
clause). Extra challenges (compared to the
k
-clique reduction) arise because we are forced to consider contributions from variables set to
0
(because they may also satisfy clauses).

\mathbb{R}

2.3

The following notion of multi-vector products (

) defined in

]

Notice that it is an extension of inner-product to

.

Simple calculations prove the following helpful lemma stated in
[
AK23
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
AK23
, Lemma 3.1]

For any
p\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and vectors

, for any

,
\|a_{1}\bm{v}_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}\bm{v}_{n}\|_{p}^{p}
can be computed in polynomial time given only

and
\mathsf{mvp}(\bm{v}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bm{v}_{i_{p}})
for all
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{p})\in[k]^{p}
. Moreover,

\|a_{1}\bm{v}_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{p})\in[k]^{p}}(a_{i_{1}}\cdots a_{i_{p}})\mathsf{mvp}(\bm{v}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bm{v}_{i_{p}}).

We will be using the following corollary of

.

For any

, and vectors

, we have

Here, for any tuple of

denote the number of occurrences of

in

.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any vectors

\|\bm{v}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{v}_{k}-\bm{t}\|^{2}=\|\bm{t}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\|\bm{v}_{i}\|^{2}-2\cdot\langle\bm{v}_{i},\bm{t}\rangle\right)+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,j)\in[k]^{2}\\

Theorem 3.2
.

For positive integers

, and

, there exists a Karp-reduction from

-
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
on lattices of rank

Furthermore, if the absolute value of the coordinates in all basis and target vectors is at most

Proof.

Given a
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}
instance with

\mathbf{B}=(\bm{b}_{1},\ldots\bm{b}_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
, target
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and a number
d>0
, where each coordinate is bounded by

, we construct a minimum-weight-
k
-Clique instance on a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with
k\cdot 2^{n/k}
vertices. For simplicity, let’s assume that
n
is an integer multiple of
k
.

First, partition the basis vectors into
k

\mathbf{B}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{B}^{(k)}
each consisting of
n/k
vectors such that
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}=\left\{\bm{b}_{\frac{n}{k}(i-1)+1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{i\frac{n}{k}}\right\}
. We construct
\mathbf{C}^{(i)}=[\bm{c}_{1}^{(i)}\ldots\bm{c}_{2^{n/k}}^{(i)}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times 2^{n/k}}
for
i\in[k]
, where the columns of
\mathbf{C}^{(i)}
represents
\{0,1\}
-combinations of vectors from
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}
and each vector in
\mathbf{C}^{(i)}
will correspond to a vertex in the reduced graph.
Let

\mathcal{V}:=\{v_{j}^{(i)}|i\in[k],j\in[2^{n/k}]\}

be the set of vertices and

\mathcal{E}:=\{e_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}}=(v_{j_{1}}^{(i_{1})},v_{j_{2}}^{(i_{2})})|i_{1},i_{2}\in[k],i_{1}<i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}\in[2^{n/k}]\}

be the edge set. Notice that it is a
k
-partite graph. For all edge
e_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}}\in\mathcal{E}
, we define the edge weight as follows:

w(e_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}}):=2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{1}}^{(i_{1})},\bm{c}_{j_{2}}^{(i_{2})}\rangle+\frac{1}{k-1}\left(\|\bm{c}_{j_{1}}^{(i_{1})}\|^{2}+\|\bm{c}_{j_{2}}^{(i_{2})}\|^{2}-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{1}}^{(i_{1})},\bm{t}\rangle-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{2}}^{(i_{2})},\bm{t}\rangle\right)+\left(\binom{k}{2}\right)^{-1}\|\bm{t}\|^{2}.

It is trivial that the reduction takes
O(m\cdot\eta^{2}\cdot(k\cdot 2^{n/k})^{2})
time and space. Notice that the edge weights are also bounded by
O(m\cdot 2^{2\eta})
. In the rest of the proof, we will show that there exists a
k
-clique of weight at most
d^{2}
if the given
(0,1)
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
instance is a
YES

k
-cliques have weight greater than
d^{2}
.

We claim that the weight of a
k
-clique in the reduced graph is at least
\min_{\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\|\mathbf{B}\bm{z}-\bm{t}\|^{2}
. Notice that, any
k
-clique in a
k
-partite graph must have exactly one vertex from each partition. Let the vertices
{v}_{j_{1}}^{(1)},\ldots,{v}_{j_{k}}^{(k)}
form a
k
-Clique, then the weight of the the clique is

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{1},x_{2})\in[k]^{2}\\
\text{ and }x_{1}<x_{2}\end{subarray}}w(e_{{x_{1}},j_{x_{1}}},e_{{x_{2}},j_{x_{2}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{1},x_{2})\in[k]^{2}\\
\text{ and }x_{1}<x_{2}\end{subarray}}2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{x_{1}}}^{({x_{1}})},\bm{c}_{j_{x_{2}}}^{({x_{2}})}\rangle+\frac{1}{k-1}\left(\|\bm{c}_{j_{x_{1}}}^{({x_{1}})}\|^{2}+\|\bm{c}_{j_{x_{2}}}^{({x_{2}})}\|^{2}-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{x_{1}}}^{({x_{1}})},\bm{t}\rangle-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{x_{2}}}^{({x_{2}})},\bm{t}\rangle\right)+\left(\binom{k}{2}\right)^{-1}\|\bm{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{t}\|^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{1},x_{2})\in[k]^{2}\\
\text{ and }x_{1}<x_{2}\end{subarray}}2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{x_{1}}}^{({x_{1}})},\bm{c}_{j_{x_{2}}}^{({x_{2}})}\rangle+\frac{1}{k-1}\left(\|\bm{c}_{j_{x_{1}}}^{({x_{1}})}\|^{2}+\|\bm{c}_{j_{x_{2}}}^{{x_{2}}}\|^{2}-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{x_{1}}}^{{x_{1}}},\bm{t}\rangle-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{x_{2}}}^{({x_{2}})},\bm{t}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{t}\|^{2}+\sum_{x\in[k]}\left(\|\bm{c}_{j_{x}}^{({x})}\|^{2}-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{x}}^{({x})},\bm{t}\rangle\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{1},x_{2})\in[k]^{2}\\
\text{ and }x_{1}<x_{2}\end{subarray}}2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{x_{1}}}^{({x_{1}})},\bm{c}_{j_{x_{2}}}^{({x_{2}})}\rangle

\displaystyle\|\bm{c}_{j_{1}}^{(1)}+\cdots+\bm{c}_{j_{k}}^{(k)}-\bm{t}\|^{2}\geq\min_{\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\|\mathbf{B}\bm{z}-\bm{t}\|^{2}.

\mathrm{CVP}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2}\in[k]^{2}\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{t}\|^{2}+\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})\xleftarrow{2}[k]}2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{i_{1}}}^{(i_{1})},\bm{c}_{j_{i_{2}}}^{(i_{2})}\rangle+\frac{1}{k-1}\left(\|\bm{c}_{j_{i_{1}}}^{(i_{1})}\|^{2}+\|\bm{c}_{j_{i_{2}}}^{(i_{2})}\|^{2}-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{i_{1}}}^{(i_{1})},\bm{t}\rangle-2\langle\bm{c}_{j_{i_{2}}}^{(i_{2})},\bm{t}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=
