Research progress on the metabolic fate of citrus flavonoids and strategies to improve bioavailability
Authors: Jingjing Geng, Jing Deng, Wenhu Zhou, Wen Li, Jintao He, Qinlu Lin, Jie Bai, Qingming Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Citrus flavonoids have various health-promoting effects, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory activities, whereas their low oral bioavailability greatly impairs their in vivo efficacy. Substitution patterns of hydroxyl, methoxyl, and glycosyl groups determine their metabolic fate. As a narrative review, this paper follows the thread of “structure-metabolism-efficacy” and reviews literature covering metabolism, gut microbiota, delivery systems, structural modifications, in vivo bioactivity, and clinical studies. Molecular-structure-based comparisons of bioavailability-enhancement strategies are made. Most bioactivity findings are derived from cellular and animal studies, while human pharmacokinetic and clinical data remain scarce. Prospects, including AI-assisted formulation, personalized nutrition, and high-value utilization of citrus by-products, are discussed, aiming to provide an in-depth theoretical reference for the development of citrus-flavonoid-based functional products.

Introduction

Citrus fruits belong to the genus Citrus in the family Rutaceae; those commonly consumed include mandarins, pomelos, lemons, and limes. Citrus flavonoids are primarily classified into flavanones, flavones (including polymethoxyflavonoids (PMFs)), flavanols, flavonols, and anthocyanins. Different flavonoid components are unevenly distributed among the various tissues of citrus fruits, such as the pericarp, juice sacs, vascular bundles, and seeds. For example, the flavedo is rich in PMFs such as nobiletin and tangeretin, while the albedo and vascular bundles contain large amounts of hesperidin and narirutin (Mahato et al., 2019). A large amount of citrus flavonoids, up to 90%, are retained in industrial by-products of the citrus juicing and canning industries (Mahato et al., 2019). Numerous in vitro experiments and animal studies have confirmed that citrus flavonoids possess a variety of bioactivities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, gut microbiota-regulating, lipid metabolism-improving, and cardiovascular protective effects, rendering them of great interest in the fields of functional foods, dietary supplements, and drug development (Assini et al., 2013; Deng et al., 2022; Wang, Cao, et al., 2021; Y. Wang et al., 2022).

Despite their promising development prospects, these bioactive compounds generally suffer from drawbacks, including poor water solubility, limited intestinal epithelial permeability, susceptibility to hepatic first-pass metabolism, and reliance on gut microbiota for their biotransformation. These effects result in a low amount of active ingredient circulating in the system upon oral administration, and it is difficult to achieve the in vivo activity of these substances that has been observed in vitro (M. Zhang, Zhu, Yang, et al., 2021). It is a bottleneck in the clinical translation and industrialization of citrus flavonoids.

The work is a narrative critical review. Literature was searched in Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and CNKI with an end date of May 2026 on citrus flavonoid chemistry, metabolism, gut microbiota, bioavailability, delivery, and structural modification. The only articles and reviews (2000–2026) that were retained were peer-reviewed in English and Chinese; conference abstracts and patents were not included. Two authors independently performed literature collection and screening following deduplication; any disagreement during the screening process was resolved by the corresponding author. Meta-analysis could not be done because of the lack of consistency in pharmacokinetic indicators, and all bioactivity data were stratified into in silico, in vitro, animal, and human evidence levels. Supplementary Table S1 presented seven modular Boolean search strategies, which covered chemistry, metabolism, bioactivity, processing, delivery, structural modification, and clinical evidence in order to achieve full reproducibility.

The majority of the current reviews on citrus flavonoids are likely to focus on a specific sub-discipline and discuss chemistry, metabolism, delivery systems, and bioactivity separately. Bioavailability enhancement strategies are often listed without any comparative evaluation within one structural framework. The intrinsic relationships between the molecular characteristics of flavonoids, full in vivo metabolic pathways, and interventions that enhance bioavailability are rarely determined by few reviews. The review is concerned with the most popular flavanones and typical PMFs of citrus, in order to establish a logical connection between the molecular structures of citrus flavonoids, their metabolism processes in vivo, and biological activities, as well as intervention strategies for improving bioavailability. This paper will discuss four groups of improvement of the bioavailability in a structure-based approach. To begin with, it explains the way in which the structural parameters determine the metabolic fate and absorption bottlenecks of citrus flavonoids, and also the corresponding structure-activity relationship. Secondly, this study gives an overall evaluation of the targeted approaches to increase bioavailability according to these features of molecules. Lastly, the research prospects are proposed, such as AI-based structure-activity relationship analysis, microbiota-in-gut personalized nutrition, and green recycling of resources. From a structural viewpoint, this review seeks to lay a theoretical foundation for the high-value utilization of citrus resources and the development of precision-oriented health-related products.

Classification and structure of citrus flavonoids

Flavonoids are a family of secondary metabolites that occur in large numbers in the plant kingdom. They have the chemical structure with two benzene rings (A ring and B ring) linked by an oxygen heterocycle (C ring), which is the fundamental skeleton C6-C3-C6 (Schnarr et al., 2024). The diversity of their structures mainly arises from the oxidation state of the C ring, the attachment position of the B-ring, and the type, number, and linkage of functional groups, including hydroxyl, methoxy, and glycosyl groups on the parent nucleus (Alseekh et al., 2020). Based on these structural characteristics, flavonoids can be broadly classified into flavanones, flavones, flavanols, isoflavones, flavanols, and anthocyanins, among others. Among citrus flavonoids, flavanones and polymethoxyflavones are considered characteristic components and are the primary focus of our discussion. These structural modifications not only determine the physicochemical properties of flavonoid molecules, such as water solubility and lipid solubility, but also directly influence their metabolic stability in the human body, affinity for specific biological targets, and eventual biological activity (Table 2). It is noteworthy that flavonoids in citrus predominantly exist in glycoside form, in which the sugar moiety is linked to the hydroxyl group of the aglycone via a glycosidic bond; the non-glycosylated form is referred to as the aglycone. Metabolism and bioavailability limitations of citrus flavonoids in the human body

The physicochemical properties of the citrus flavonoids are determined by the structural diversity of the compounds and their fate in human metabolism (Fig. 2). The intestinal microbiota and enzyme system of the host interact with the citrus flavonoids to form a barrier that prevents them from being taken orally. The structural variation between the flavonoids as well as the personal differences among people also make the situation more confusing when it comes to the pathways and products of metabolism.

The complete metabolic process of citrus flavonoids in the human body

The citrus flavonoids are absorbed and digested in the mouth; however, the mouth, esophagus, and stomach do not have a significant impact on the concentration and composition of these compounds. In the small intestine, there is only one type of flavonoid glycoside that can be hydrolyzed by lactase-phlorizin hydrolase (LPH) in the brush border or cytosolic beta-glucosidase (CBG) in the intestinal cells, which is monoglucoside flavonoid; so, disaccharide glycosides like narirutin and neohesperidin cannot be absorbed in the small intestine (Murota et al., 2018). The hydrolysis products may pass through the intestinal wall into the body as free aglycone, but the P-gp, MRP2, and BCRP efflux pumps of the intestinal wall would pump back most of the flavonoids and primary metabolites into the intestinal lumen, and the rest of them will enter the intestinal cells (Crozier et al., 2010). These PMFs are not broken down before being taken up by the intestinal cells, but their absorption is limited by their poor solubility in the digestive tract. Inside the cell, the flavonoids that are absorbed are initially transformed by the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) system of enzymes in phase I, and then conjugated in phase II reactions such as glucuronidation and sulfation, which are catalyzed by uridine-diphosphate glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs) and sulfotransferases (SULTs). The metabolites of water-soluble conjugates are conveyed to the liver through the portal vein (M. Zhang, Zhu, Yang, et al., 2021).

The liver undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism, whereby the biotransformation of the absorbed flavanones and PMFs are carried out (Nagayoshi et al., 2020). Some metabolites are released via MRP1/MRP3 into the systemic circulation and distributed to various tissues; a large number of metabolites are secreted back into the intestine via bile, forming the enterohepatic circulation. The conjugated metabolites released through the biliary pathway may be again deconjugated by the enzymes of the intestines releasing free aglycones which are capable of being reabsorbed thus repeating the intestinal absorption process as well as hepatic processing.

Unabsorbed flavonoids by the small intestine and substrates that are returned through the enterohepatic circulation eventually end up in the colon where anaerobic gut bacteria perform extensive transformation. The aglycone is first released by deglycosylation of flavanone glycosides, which is then absorbed; the aglycone is cleaved to phenolic acids on the C-ring, which are subsequently broken down into short-chain fatty acids. PMFs undergo only gradual demethylation without ring-opening of their parent flavonoid skeleton. Small-molecule products generated by the microbiota can be reabsorbed into the bloodstream via the colon and undergo secondary conjugation and modification in the liver (Kawabata et al., 2019).

After metabolism, the flavanones and the ring-opened phenolic acids of the microbiota are mostly eliminated by the kidneys as urine. PMFs and their de-methylated derivatives are not water soluble and they are excreted through the feces. Moreover, gut-derived metabolites often exhibit altered antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities relative to the parent citrus flavonoids, with either enhanced or diminished potency.

Flavonoid structure determines metabolic fate

Since the time of metabolism, the chemical composition of citrus flavonoids determines their metabolism and even the end products of these metabolites. Differences in the structural arrangements of glycosylation, methoxylation, hydroxylation, types of glycosidic linkage, and C-2 chiralities (2S−/2R-) result in significant variation in solubility, permeability of membrane, recognition by enzymes, and binding to efflux pumps. In this regard, the structure of flavonoids will be directed to different ways in every step of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. Structure determines physical and chemical properties

During digestion, citrus flavonoids enter the gastrointestinal tract mixed with digestive fluids. High aqueous solubility facilitates full dissolution and enlarges contact with digestive enzymes, whereas favorable lipophilicity enables molecules to cross the intestinal epithelial barrier and promotes bloodstream absorption. The solubility and absorption characteristics of citrus flavonoids are regulated by their molecular structure. Flavanone aglycones contain only a small number of hydroxyl groups, resulting in low water solubility and moderate lipophilicity. The introduction of sugar moieties in glycosides increases the number of hydroxyl groups, resulting in water solubility superior to that of the aglycone; however, the improvement is limited. This is because the planar aromatic skeleton of citrus flavonoids readily forms strong π–π stacking interactions, resulting in dense crystals with high lattice energy. Consequently, both flavanone aglycones and PMFs are difficult to dissolve due to their strong planar structures and dense crystalline forms; glycosides can only mitigate this effect to a certain extent, and natural disaccharide glycosides also contain the hydrophobic rhamnose (Berga et al., 2023). Monosaccharides are more water-soluble than disaccharides. In addition, the solubility of aglycones is highly pH-dependent; their solubility increases only under strongly alkaline conditions, when the phenolic hydroxyl group is deprotonated, whereas they exhibit poor solubility in neutral and acidic environments. In contrast, naringin, which is substituted with a disaccharide, is less susceptible to pH fluctuations and maintains higher solubility in neutral to weakly acidic digestive environments (Chen et al., 2021). PMFs are extremely fat-soluble, stable in gastric acid and digestive enzymes, and easily absorbed through epithelial membranes; however, they are almost insoluble in gastric juice and are mostly found in crystal form. Moreover, the temperature and composition of food matrix further modify the apparent dissolution pattern of citrus flavonoids. Within the gastrointestinal tract, flavonoids must undergo crystal dissolution and disperse in digestive fluids before they come into contact with enzymes or be absorbed by the intestinal epithelium; insufficient crystal dissolution directly reduces their bioavailability.

Structure determines the absorption pathway

The glycosidic modifications, the planarity of the C-ring skeleton, and the substitution patterns of hydroxyl and methoxy groups on the aromatic rings all contribute to the intestinal absorption sites, transmembrane transport mechanisms, and magnitude of efflux-mediated loss for citrus flavonoids during the absorption process.

In the case of flavanone glycosides, which are substituted by disaccharides, LPH in small intestine is unable to identify rhamnose residue and they cannot be absorbed through the small intestine but can be transferred directly to the colon (Felgines et al., 2000). The colonic bacteria, Bacteroides, Clostridia and Lactobacilli release two types of essential rate-limiting enzymes: on one hand, α-L-rhamnosidase breaks the terminal rhamnose to form the monoglucoside intermediate (prunin), and then β-D-glucosidase breaks the glucose, which leads to passive diffusion across the colonic epithelium into absorption (Kobayashi et al., 2008). Flavanones have shown a quick peak at the small intestine as well as a slow peak at the colon in human experiments showing that gut microbiota has an important role in the metabolism of citrus flavonoids (Muralidharan et al., 2023). α-L-rhamnosidase exhibits differential hydrolytic preferences towards rutinoside (α-1,6) and neohesperidoside (α-1,2) glycosidic linkages, which may lead to divergent hydrolysis rates (B.-C. Li et al., 2025). PMFs are polymethoxylated aglycones and polymethoxy-substituted compounds with high lipid solubility. They can easily pass through the intestinal mucosa by passive diffusion without hydrolysis; a small fraction can also be transported by lymphatic system, a secondary pathway, which avoids partial first-pass metabolism in the liver.

Efflux mediated by transporters is one of the significant limiting factors in absorption. Naringin is extruded by MRP2 and P-gp, whereas phase-II conjugated metabolites derived from hesperidin are mainly effluxed by BCRP (ABCG2). This causes the aglycones and metabolites that enter intestinal cells to be returned to the intestinal lumen, thereby reducing bioavailability (Visvanathan & Williamson, 2022).

Structure determines metabolic pathways

After citrus flavonoids are transported across the intestinal epithelial membrane into the portal vein, they undergo processes such as modification by intestinal cells, phase I and II metabolism in the liver, bile-mediated enterohepatic circulation, and aglycone cleavage by colonic microbiota; their metabolic pathways and efficiency in the body are regulated by molecular structure.

Monomeric flavonoids that enter the intestinal epithelium undergo phase II conjugation catalyzed by UGT and SULT; the reaction rate depends on the number of free phenolic hydroxyl groups. Flavanone aglycones, which are rich in hydroxyl groups, rapidly form mono- or diglucuronide and sulfate conjugates; a large number of polar conjugates are effluxed by the MRP2 pump back into the ileal lumen, thereby reducing oral bioavailability (Matsumoto et al., 2004). PMFs contain almost no free phenolic hydroxyl groups and are poor substrates for intestinal UGT-mediated conjugation. Consequently, a significant proportion of the parent PMF molecules cross the basolateral membrane and enter the portal vein.

After transport to the liver, the substrate selectivity of Phase I CYP450 enzymes is determined by the aromatic ring substituents: PMFs primarily undergo CYP450-mediated sequential demethylation (preferentially at the 4′-position), which is the rate-limiting step for their in vivo clearance (Nagayoshi et al., 2020). Flavanones are not extensively substituted with methoxy groups and undergo only limited hydroxyl-related oxidation. Phase II metabolism in the liver further modifies the aglycone and Phase I oxidation products, determining the major forms present in plasma: flavanones are virtually undetectable in their free form in plasma, with more than 95% present as mono- or diglucuronide conjugates (Kanaze et al., 2007). In the case of PMFs, the parent compound coexists with the monodemethylated conjugate, and the parent-compound accounts are higher compared with flavanone aglycones (S. Li et al., 2006). Intact disaccharide glycosides cannot cross the intestinal barrier, and intact glycoside prototypes are virtually absent in plasma.

The intensity of the enterohepatic circulation is determined by conjugate polarity, molecular weight, and recognition by biliary transporters. Highly polar diglucuronide products are readily recognized by hepatocellular MRP2 and extensively excreted into the intestine via bile, establishing pronounced enterohepatic recycling and prolonging systemic residence time. Monomethylated PMF conjugates have low polarity, result in minimal bile secretion, and exhibit a weak enterohepatic circulation effect; only a small amount of small-molecule phenolic acids participate in bile re-absorption. Structure-induced differences in polarity are a key factor behind the variations in AUC and half-life among different classes of citrus flavonoids. Naringenin exists in plasma 98% as a glucuronide conjugate, with low concentrations of the parent compound. Due to its short half-life (approximately 2.31–4 h), the enterohepatic circulation is crucial for maintaining effective exposure levels in the body (Erlund et al., 2001).

Following deglycosylation, free aglycones reaching the colon are subjected to C-ring cleavage catalyzed by gut microbial enzymes, including chalcone isomerase (CHI), flavanone-flavanol cleavage reductase (FCR), and phyllodrin hydrolase (PHY), generating short-chain phenolic-acid products. This process occurs only in aglycones following desugaring, and the cleavage efficiency is influenced by substituents. Flavanone aglycones have more free hydroxyl groups, resulting in faster C-ring cleavage; PMFs are shielded by methoxy groups, and aglycone cleavage rarely occurs. The A-ring of naringenin is metabolized to phloroglucinol, while the B-ring is metabolized to 3-(4′-hydroxyphenyl) propionic acid, and sageol is produced; phloroglucinol can be further metabolized into acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid, while 3-(4′-hydroxyphenyl) propionic acid can be metabolized into small molecules such as p-coumaric acid, 3-phenylpropionic acid, and 4′-hydroxybenzoic acid (Zhou et al., 2024). In addition, molecular chirality also influences metabolic outcomes: in preparations of hesperidin with a high 2S-isomer content, total urinary metabolite excretion can reach 70%, primarily due to a significant increase in the levels of open-ring phenolic acid catabolites produced by the colonic microbiota, which are significantly higher than those in standard mixtures of isomers (Crescenti et al., 2022).

Individual variations in flavonoid structure and metabolism

The basic metabolic patterns of citrus flavonoids are determined by their glycosidic, methoxy, and hydroxyl structures, while individual differences among host organisms further increase this uncertainty. Some studies have classified metabolic variations in humans into continuous types (where the yield of the same metabolite varies significantly among individuals) and clustered types (where differences among individuals lie in the presence or absence of a particular metabolite or in the proportions of its components), reflecting the diverse nature of metabolic variations (Favari et al., 2024). For the same dose and the same flavonoid, pharmacokinetic parameters can vary by a factor of several to more than ten among different populations (Ávila-Gálvez et al., 2021; Coutinho et al., 2024; Gardana et al., 2007). Differences in gut microbiota composition are the most significant factor. There is considerable interindividual variation in the abundance of microbial metabolic enzyme genes, and differences in microbial abundance directly affect the desugaring efficiency of flavanone glycosides. While many β-glucosidase-expressing bacteria dominate the human gut microbiota, α-rhamnosidase-producing species are relatively scarce. Accordingly, plasma AUC values for glycosides can be markedly decreased in individuals with low α-rhamnosidase-producing capacity (Braune & Blaut, 2016). Differences in microbial composition also regulate the rate of ring-opening degradation of flavonoid aglycones; since only a limited number of bacterial species carry cleavage enzymes, the types and concentrations of phenolic acid metabolites are highly susceptible to population-specific variations (Pateriya et al., 2025). At the same time, the gut microbiota is also the factor with the greatest regulatory potential. The concept of enterotypes classifies human gut microbiota into three major categories, aiming to summarize the role of microorganisms in human health and disease and thereby enable targeted interventions (Arumugam et al., 2011). Probiotics, prebiotics, and dietary interventions may be potential strategies. Some studies have also established a probiotic-naringenin feedback loop and validated it in mouse models. Although these approaches are still a long way from practical application, they demonstrate that the gut microbiota is the most promising target for personalized nutrition (Wendler Miranda et al., 2025; Zayed et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).

Next is the host's genetic polymorphism. In terms of tissue distribution, individuals with high expression of jejunal CYP3A4, UGT2B17, and CES2 enzyme genes primarily metabolize flavonoids in the intestine rather than in the liver (Wenzel et al., 2024). In the liver, individuals with high UGT2B7 gene expression display rapid metabolic rates, yielding low plasma aglycone concentrations and elevated conjugate concentrations. Upregulated CYP2C9 gene expression promotes naringenin to undergo preferential methylation. Pronounced genetic polymorphism of UGT2B17 contributes to remarkable interindividual disparities in the in vivo residence time of metabolites (Wenzel et al., 2024). Moreover, single nucleotide polymorphisms in hepatic SULT1A1 (rs4788068), SULT1C4 (rs1402467), and efflux transporter ABCC2 (rs8187710) demonstrate a significant association with increased urinary excretion of phase II flavanone conjugates. Those carrying genotypes associated with high excretion are more capable of catalyzing sulfation of flavanone aglycones (Fraga et al., 2022). It is important to note that there is no direct correlation between the genetic changes of the liver and intestinal metabolic enzymes, and this leads to a significant difference in the metabolism of various populations.

Finally, there are the effects of environmental regulatory factors such as diet, age, and gender. The high-fat and high-sugar diets over an extended time suppress the positive gut commensals and reduce the activity of flavonoid hydrolysis. In contrast to this, sufficient dietary fiber helps in regulating the intestinal microbiota composition and also contributes to the formation and assimilation of citrus-flavonoid-derived metabolites (Zhou et al., 2024). It is still not clear how much factors like sex, age, and BMI have an impact on the metabolism of flavonoids; they are usually overshadowed by genetic and microbiome variability (Favari et al., 2024). There is evidence of sex-specific metabolic tendencies based on some studies, with females showing a tendency towards eriodictyol-derived metabolites and males having a higher capacity for naringin biotransformation (Hernández-Prieto et al., 2023).

In conclusion, the metabolism of citrus flavonoids is a multi-stage and multi-organ process with the compulsory participation of gut microbiota, extensive first-pass conjugation, and high enterohepatic circulation. The structure is the main driving force; the site-specificity and efficiency of intestinal hydrolysis depend directly on the type and number of glycosylation sites; methylation resists first-pass metabolism and prolongs retention time in the body; the rate and site of phase II conjugation are determined by the number and position of phenolic hydroxyl groups; saturation of the C-ring and molecular planarity determine the action of transporters and efflux pumps and thus absorption efficiency. Furthermore, individual variability further amplifies metabolic heterogeneity. Jointly, structural properties and host-specific variation underpin the low oral bioavailability of citrus flavonoids. This indicates that bioavailability-improving interventions should be tailored to these rate-limiting steps.

Structure-bioactivity relationships of citrus flavonoids

The molecular structure of citrus flavonoids (Fig. 1) determines their physicochemical properties and metabolic pathways in vivo, and further regulates their local intestinal and systemic biological activities. Substituent groups not only govern the metabolic fate of flavonoids—including solubility, hydrolysis, absorption, and clearance—but also influence their binding to protein targets, thereby mediating signaling pathways such as Nrf2/HO-1, NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, AMPK, MAPK, PPAR, SIRT1, and those associated with the gut microbiota. Importantly, evidence for the activation of most of these pathways stems from in vitro interventions at supraphysiological concentrations. Under physiological oral doses, the in vivo metabolites consist primarily of phase-II conjugated metabolites and phenolic acids derived from microbial degradation, with extremely low exposure levels of the parent compound. Consequently, the pathway effects observed in vitro cannot be reliably extrapolated to in vivo scenarios (Felgines et al., 2000).

All bioactivity data in this chapter are classified into five evidence tiers (IS = in silico, IV = in vitro, Ex-vivo = ex-vivo, A = animal, H = human), with corresponding labels embedded in text and tables. Data from non-citrus flavonoids used for mechanistic comparison are explicitly labeled as indirect references. There is currently limited evidence from clinical trials in humans; a summary of the relevant studies is provided at the end of this chapter (Table 5).

Antioxidant

The antioxidant effects mediated by citrus flavonoids can be divided into direct free radical scavenging and Nrf2-mediated indirect antioxidant protection of cells. Nrf2 is a key transcription factor in the body's oxidative stress response; upon nuclear translocation, it initiates the transcription of a series of antioxidant target genes, such as HO-1, NQO1, and GCL, to counteract oxidative damage (Y. Wang et al., 2022).

Under in vitro prototype molecular conditions, the phenolic hydroxyl group of flavanone aglycones exerts potent antioxidant activity by directly scavenging free radicals, while also moderately activating the Nrf2-HO-1 pathway; this activation is independent of Keap1 protein modification and provides indirect antioxidant protection (Tsuji et al., 2013). In contrast, methoxy modification significantly enhances the ability of citrus flavonoids to induce the Nrf2 cascade (Tsuji et al., 2013). For example, tangeretin inhibits CUL3-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2, thereby enhancing the cell's antioxidant defense capacity (IV + A) (Wang, Jin, et al., 2021). Naringenin can also moderately activate the Nrf2–HO-1 pathway, promote Nrf2 nuclear translocation, alleviate oxidative stress, and inhibit programmed cell death rather than damage (IV + A) (Cao et al., 2024). in vitro studies have shown that the free radical-scavenging and anti-inflammatory activities of fat-soluble hesperetin are superior to those of hesperidin and its water-soluble glucoside derivatives, suggesting that water solubility is not directly equivalent to biological activity and that glycosylation exerts context-dependent effects (IV) (Choi et al., 2022).

Anti-inflammatory

NF-κB and MAPK are central signaling pathways that regulate inflammatory responses. The phosphorylation and activation of MAPK (ERK, JNK, p38) further upregulate the transcriptional activity of NF-κB; together, they synergistically induce the massive release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, thereby exacerbating inflammatory tissue damage (Wang, Cao, et al., 2021).

The parent molecule of naringenin can dose-dependently inhibit NF-κB transcriptional activity in vitro, downregulate the expression of inflammatory mediators, and alleviate LPS-induced cellular inflammatory damage (IV) (Choy et al., 2024). In an LPS-induced acute pulmonary inflammation model, sinensetin was able to inhibit the phosphorylation and activation of NF-κB p65 and STAT3 signaling networks, downregulate the expression levels of numerous pro-inflammatory factors, chemokines, and NLRP3 inflammasome-related genes, and thereby alleviate acute inflammatory damage (IV + A) (Xu et al., 2025). As an agonist of ROR nuclear receptors, nobiletin can also reverse the surgery-induced downregulation of key clock genes—Bmal1, RORs, and Rev-erbα—and suppress NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammation via the ROR-Bmal1 pathway (A) (Sun et al., 2022).

Interventions for metabolic diseases

AMPK, PI3K/Akt, PPAR, and SIRT1 collectively form the body's energy metabolism regulatory network. AMPK perceives the cell's energy status; PI3K/Akt is involved in insulin signaling; PPAR and SIRT1 regulate lipid metabolism, glucose homeostasis, and mitochondrial function. Dysregulation in these pathways is closely associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome (Assini et al., 2013).

Studies using a recombinant protein system have shown that PMFs act as STAC-type agonists to allosterically activate SIRT1, with the activation effect being highly dependent on the methoxy substitution sites; in contrast, flavanones do not possess direct SIRT1-activating capacity in this system (IS+IV) (Ko et al., 2026). In an in vitro 3 T3-L1 adipogenesis model, both flavanone glycosides and PMFs regulate the AMPK-ACC cascade, inhibit lipogenesis, and promote glucose uptake; molecular docking suggests that flavanone glycosides exhibit higher binding affinity (IV) (Natraj et al., 2023). Animal models have confirmed that naringenin improves fatty acid oxidation in the liver via the PGC1α–PPARα pathway and enhances glucose tolerance in mice (A) (Mulvihill et al., 2009). Nobiletin, due to its planar structure and multiple methoxy substituents, effectively regulates circadian rhythms and improves metabolic syndrome by enhancing the amplitude of the circadian clock (Ambe et al., 2022). In a mouse model of chronic liver injury, nobiletin and tangeretin mitigated hepatocyte damage: on the one hand, they activated the Nrf2 cascade by inhibiting DNMT-dependent epigenetic modifications; on the other hand, they restored the gut microbiota disrupted by APAP, suggesting that DNA methylation-mediated epigenetic regulation and the gut-liver axis jointly contribute to liver protection (A) (Tsai et al., 2026). Citrus flavonoids regulate metabolic homeostasis by reshaping the gut microbiota and promoting the production of short-chain fatty acids, thereby indirectly modulating the AMPK, Nrf2, and NF-κB pathways in the liver via the gut-liver and gut-adipose tissue axes. This microbiota-mediated indirect effect is also a significant mechanism through which citrus flavonoids exert their metabolic protective effects in vivo (Zeng et al., 2020).

Protection of the cardiovascular system

Vascular endothelial homeostasis, lipid metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress collectively determine the function of the cardiovascular system. The Nrf2–HO–1, NF–κB/MAPK, AMPK, PPAR family, and SIRT1 pathways are involved in vascular endothelial damage repair, lipid metabolism regulation, and vascular inflammatory responses; the PI3K/Akt cascade mediates endothelial cell survival and NO signaling. Preclinical data suggest that citrus flavonoids may exert potential cardiovascular protective effects through the aforementioned networks (Deng et al., 2022).

Molecular docking model predictions indicate that two PMFs, tangeretin and scutellarin tetramethyl ether (indirect reference), bind to the nifedipine-binding pocket of the CaV 1.2 channel in vascular smooth muscle, exerting a calcium channel-blocking effect; the methoxy substitution pattern directly affects binding affinity, and excessive B-ring methoxylation introduces steric hindrance and weakens molecular binding (IS) (Ramunno et al., 2024). An in vitro model of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) stimulated with TNF-α showed that naringin can inhibit the activation of the IKK/IκB-α/NF-κB signaling pathway and reduce the expression of endothelial adhesion molecules and chemokines (IV) (Hsueh et al., 2016). By upregulating specific PIWI-interacting RNAs, naringin also promotes angiogenesis in HUVECs (IV + A) (Pi et al., 2023).

Neuroprotection and cognitive improvement

Oxidative stress and excessive inflammation are major drivers of neural tissue damage; the Nrf2 and NF-κB/MAPK pathways are also involved in the regulation of neuronal and microglial function. In addition, the AMPK-SIRT1 cascade mediates the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis in neurons (Cirmi et al., 2016).

Animal studies have shown that nobiletin can improve cognition in mice in a 5XFAD Alzheimer's disease model, reduce Aβ plaque burden, and simultaneously regulate multiple signaling pathways—including TLR4/NF-κB, AMPK-SIRT1-PGC-1α, and PI3K/Akt-CREB-BDNF—thereby exerting antioxidant and anti-neuroinflammatory effects and improving synaptic plasticity (A) (Baek et al., 2026). Hesperidin can alleviate damage to retinal ganglion cells caused by high sugar levels and block mitochondria-dependent neuronal apoptosis (IV) (Liu et al., 2017). Hesperetin promotes morphological differentiation and myelination of oligodendrocyte-like cells via the Rnd2/Prag1/Fyn signaling cascade (IV) (Fukatsu et al., 2023).

In addition, by regulating the homeostasis of the gut-brain axis, citrus flavonoids can indirectly inhibit inflammation in the central nervous system. Most orally administered citrus flavonoid glycosides are hydrolyzed by the gut microbiota into aglycones and phenolic acid metabolites, which not only enhance molecular bioavailability and the potential to cross the blood-brain barrier but also promote the proliferation of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and stimulate the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). SCFAs can alleviate neuroinflammatory damage through immune, neural, and endocrine pathways, in part by activating intestinal GPR41/43 receptors (Kim et al., 2013; Silla et al., 2025).

Antitumor

The proliferation, apoptosis, and migration of tumor cells are jointly regulated by multiple signaling networks, including Nrf2, NF-κB, MAPK, and AMPK. Dysregulation of these pathways promotes tumor cell survival and the formation of an inflammatory microenvironment (Qiu et al., 2023a). Citrus flavonoids can interfere with the aforementioned pathways in preclinical models, thereby exerting a potential antiproliferative effect.

Hesperetin reduces DOT1L protein abundance and histone H3K79 methylation, thereby inhibiting the expression of genes associated with gastric cancer cell metastasis (IV) (Wang, Sheng, et al., 2021). In an animal model involving a murine intraperitoneal-injection model, PMFs exerted an anti-ER+ breast cancer effect in synergy with tamoxifen by directly promoting the degradation of ERα protein to block the estrogen signaling cascade; methoxy substitution at the C-3 position enhanced this activity, suggesting that the site of methoxy substitution can significantly alter the antitumor effect (A) (Wang et al., 2024). Assays performed with parent compounds have shown that flavanones bearing fewer hydroxyl groups can induce cell cycle arrest in tumor cells in vitro; this effect is accompanied by downregulation of cyclin-CDK complexes but is independent of CDKI networks such as p53, p21, p27, and p16. In contrast, polyhydroxylated naringenin and naringin exhibit relatively weak in vitro antiproliferative activity (IV + A) (Hsiao et al., 2007).

In conclusion, natural transformations of citrus flavonoids (hydroxylation, glycosylation, and mono- or poly-methoxylation) have a significant impact on their physicochemical properties, affinity to targets, and in vivo metabolic behavior. These structural changes further govern core biological activities such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and glucose-lipid homeostasis, and mediate protective effects against multiple pathological conditions and various diseases. Human clinical trials on the biological activity of citrus flavonoids.

Current evaluations of the biological activity of citrus flavonoids primarily focus on classic antioxidant and inflammatory signaling pathways. However, several emerging pathological axes have not yet been fully explored in the context of citrus flavonoids. For example, in recent natural-product research, the BCAA metabolic disorder–inflammation–aging axis has been shown to be involved in the progression of chronic inflammatory diseases, yet the potential of citrus flavonoids to regulate this metabolic axis remains to be further validated in in vitro and animal models (Wang et al., 2025). At the same time, this chapter clarifies the core limitations in translating pre-clinical evidence to human applications. Most existing findings on pathway regulation are based on in vitro interventions using supraphysiologicall concentrations of parent flavonoids. After intestinal hydrolysis, UGT/SULT-catalyzed phase-II conjugation, and C-ring cleavage driven by gut-microbiota, circulating entities in vivo are dominated by phase-II conjugates and microbiota-derived phenolic acids, whereas systemic exposure to parent compounds remains extremely low. Because of the variation in dosage, species-specific metabolism, and heterogeneity of individual gut microbiota, positive findings in vitro and in animals are hard to extrapolate directly to humans. At present, there is a low level of clinical evidence regarding citrus flavonoids overall, and studies are highly heterogeneous; additional validation by standardized human trials according to real levels of exposure in vivo is still needed (Bas, 2026).

Cutting-edge strategies for improving the bioavailability of citrus flavonoids

Citrus flavonoids exert diverse potential biological effects, yet their practical translation is hindered not only by scarce human clinical evidence but also by intrinsic physicochemical properties and metabolic fates dictated by their molecular structures. Structure-related limiting factors in the oral absorption of citrus flavonoids

The substitution patterns of citrus flavonoid molecules (glycosyl, hydroxyl, and methoxy moieties) are inherent and, as discussed in Chapter 3, these patterns combine to form three interrelated rate-limiting bottlenecks to oral absorption. Hence, all the optimization strategies can be seen to focus on these molecular-level failures.

Solubility bottleneck

Citrus flavonoids are inclined to crystallize and exhibit low solubility in water; PMFs are very hydrophobic, leading to poor dissolution in gastrointestinal fluids and less contact with intestinal epithelial surfaces.

Transmembrane permeability bottleneck

Intact disaccharide glycosides are not hydrolyzed in the small intestine but have to use colonic microbiota. Polyhydroxy aglycones are quickly UGT/SULT-catalyzed phase II conjugated after absorption, and then expelled out of the intestinal lumen by MRP2 transporters.

Stability bottleneck

Some of the flavonoids are sensitive to changes in pH, oxidation, and intestinal enzymes, which cause them to be degraded in the gastrointestinal tract. Simultaneously, there is also the enterohepatic recirculation and C-ring cleavage by gut microbiota that further alters the composition of bioactive compounds circulating in the body.

Solubility, ionization, membrane permeability, transporter interactions, first-pass metabolism, gut microbial transformation, and food-matrix effects represent interrelated and highly interdependent factors. Accordingly, these interplays should not be neglected when developing structure-based intervention strategies for citrus flavonoids.

Targeted optimization technology framework

Physical and non-thermal processing strategies

Physical and non-thermal processing strategies primarily improve crystal dissolution and address dissolution bottlenecks; they have virtually no effect on the chemical structure of the molecules themselves. The micronization process, eutectic processing, as well as spray drying breaks the crystal structures and also raises the specific surface area and the dissolution rates of the crystals into gastrointestinal fluids. There are some non-thermal processing procedures (high-pressure treatment, pulsed electric fields, ultrasound, and microwave treatment) that can be applied to plant matrices so as to liberate flavonoids and increase the extraction rate (Fu et al., 2021).

A randomized, double-blind, crossover human trial indicated that micronization may enhance the systemic exposure to the bound metabolite of phase-II-conjugated metabolites of hesperidin in humans as compared with a standard mixture of hesperidin diastereoisomers; but the C-2 stereoisomer (2S−/2R-) of hesperidin has a major effect on overall pharmacokinetic behavior it is important to note that the study population was pre-screened and only volunteersrs were included who had an intermediate metabolic phenotype, and because the two variables micronization and stereoconfiguration are not independent of each other, their respective contributions cannot be completely separated (Crescenti et al., 2022). The relative bioavailability (AUC) of hesperetin-piperine co-crystal increased by 2.18-fold and Cmax by 2.33-fold in a rat model; however, since piperine itself is a P-gp inhibitor, the observed gains are due to a dual effect (Liu et al., 2022).

The micronization increases the solubility of poorly soluble flavonoids only by decreasing the size of the particles; however, the molecular chemical structure does not change, and there is a danger of recrystallization when storing. Conversely, drug co-crystallization involves creating a completely new crystal lattice with a co-former, which can be used to control solubility, dissolution rate, and powder processing characteristics at the same time. Nevertheless, this method is strongly influenced by the choice of the co-former and in certain cases even decreases the dissolution and in vivo exposure of the target material; hence, there is no general solution (Almeida et al., 2026). Despite the fact that most of these strategies are based on food-approved processes and possess comparatively low regulatory barriers, they are mainly aimed at enhancing solubility and have little effect on correcting metabolic handicaps caused by intact disaccharide glycosides; thus, they usually require integration with other methods.

Biotransformation strategies

Biotransformation techniques are also the pre-treatment of rhamnose with hydrolysis by enzyme or fermentation by microorganisms, whereby disaccharide glycosides become monoglucosides and aglycones. It changes the location of absorption to the small intestine as opposed to the colon so that the gut microbiota is not heavily dependent on inter-individual variation. Food-grade enzyme preparations are also consistent with the product's positioning as a dietary ingredient.

A randomized, double-blind, crossover human trial demonstrated that enzymatic processing of hesperidin to selectively remove rhamnose to give hesperidin-7-O-glucoside shifted the absorption site from the colon to the small intestine; at equivalent aglycone intake, the plasma AUC of total hesperetin was twofold higher, Cmax increased fourfold, and Tmax was reduced from 7 h to 0.6 h (Nielsen et al., 2006). This monoglucoside scaffold has been shown to dramatically increase systemic exposure in humans. However, hesperidin-7-O-glucoside is not a significant natural constituent of citrus fruits. There are technological hurdles to targeted large-scale enzymatic manufacture. In addition, its absorption relies on LPH enzyme activity, so efficacy in individuals deficient in LPH is unknown.

In engineering terms, the lack of thermal stability in natural α-L-rhamnosidase is a major drawback to its use in production. The thermostability of the enzyme can be enhanced by semi-rational modification and immobilization on silica zeolite and magnetic chitosan microspheres, which will allow the continuous biocatalysis process with satisfactory results (Carceller et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2025). Even so, the technology has not yet been able to overcome the issue of high cost of enzymes and the complicated fermentation products and separation processes that need to be carried out. It is also the case that the desaccharified aglycones are still being conjugated in the intestinal phase-II and excreted. Additionally, the water solubility of the aglycones decreases, and without supplementary solubilization interventions, they will recrystallize in gastrointestinal fluids, offsetting the benefits gained from biotransformation.

Delivery and encapsulation systems

Delivery and encapsulation systems can simultaneously improve solubility and gastrointestinal stability, and some carriers can modulate transmembrane transport; however, they cannot directly eliminate the inherent phase I and phase II metabolism in the liver.

Lipid-based delivery system

Lipid-based nanocarriers, including liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), possess good biocompatibility and can encapsulate lipophilic and amphiphilic active components, making them suitable for the hydrophobic nature of PMFs and aglycones. Taking tangeretin as an example, in a whey protein-based delivery system administered orally to rats, HPMC-modified emulsions/emulgel significantly increased the plasma AUC and tissue exposure of tangeretin, with levels 4–20 times higher than those in the oil suspension control group, and prolonged the in vivo release time (Hu et al., 2021). The study also suggests that improved bioavailability observed in in vitro digestion simulations does not necessarily translate to improved oral exposure in vivo; cinnamaldehyde and gum arabic can significantly increase in vitro bioavailability but do not result in a corresponding improvement in in vivo pharmacokinetics. However, conventional nanoemulsions are not suitable for PMF delivery because emulsifiers, the oil phase, preparation methods, and cosolvents can only regulate crystal morphology and sedimentation behavior; they cannot completely suppress the precipitation of supersaturated PMF crystals (Li et al., 2012a). Moreover, these systems tend to have physicochemical stability problems like aggregation on storage, lipid oxidation, and active ingredients leakage; certain methods of preparation involve organic solvents, freeze-drying enhances the stability of storage but increases the cost of processing, whereas the rehydration process can also compromise the structure of the carrier (Kumar et al., 2022). They are also generally susceptible to degradation by gastrointestinal lipases and bile salts.

Supramolecular/host-guest assembly systems

Cyclodextrins (CD) and their derivatives can improve the water solubility and dissolution behavior of flavonoids through host–guest inclusion complexation. Among these, HP-β-CD is the most widely used subtype; it is virtually not absorbed in the intestine under oral administration and is relatively safe. The inclusion efficiency was found to be greatly influenced by preparation techniques like freeze-drying, co-milling, and anti-solvent precipitation (Dos Santos Lima et al., 2019a). Micelle systems produced the most significant increase in plasma peak concentration (Cmax); although phospholipid complexes have limited solubilization capacity, they can considerably increase the elimination half-life and therefore can be used in the creation of long-acting preparations (Taldaev et al., 2025). Hesperetin encapsulated with HP-2-CD showed a relative oral bioavailability of about two times greater in rats than in other studies; nevertheless, it cannot alter the metabolism of hesperetin, since metabolites are mostly conjugated into glucuronide and sulfate forms (Shulman et al., 2011). Although many oral pharmacokinetic studies in rats have shown these effects, this methodology has severe limitations. The inclusion efficiency strongly depends on the molecular structureure of the flavonoids and the inclusion constant. In the complex environment of the gastrointestinal tract, the inclusion complexes are susceptible to dissociation because of pH fluctuation and competitive guest molecules from food matrices. Currently, there is little human oral-pharmacokinetic evidence for citrus-flavonoid CD formulations; most conclusions are based solely on animal experiments (Dos Santos Lima et al., 2019b).

Polysaccharides and macromolecular carrier systems

These systems primarily create synergistic effects through gut-targeted sustained release and local probiotic-modulating effects, including high-molecular-weight nanoparticulate assemblies and polysaccharide-based hydrogels. The human body is almost unable to absorb citrus pectin-based carriers; mouse experiments with modified citrus pectin (MCP) show that its systemic bioavailability is less than or equal to 0.01%, most of which stays in the gastrointestinal tract. Hence, the pharmacological activity of pectin-flavonoid complexes should be considered as a result of local protection and synergistic actions in the gut and should not be mistaken for an increase in flavonoid transepithelial transport or plasma exposure (da Silva et al., 2026). Highly developed structural domains of pectin RG-I/AG-II control the fermentation of gut microbiota, hence indirectly changing the intestinal metabolic environment of flavonoids (Li et al., 2026). The polysaccharide extraction process significantly alters the material's physicochemical properties; indicators such as in vitro bile acid binding cannot be directly used to infer its in vivo absorption-promoting effects (Li, Dou, et al., 2024). A food-grade Pickering emulsion based on β-cyclodextrin and cationic cellulose can anchor naringenin and increase drug loading capacity. Combined with low-methoxy pectin to form an acid-resistant three-dimensional network, the formulation achieves a 1.98-fold AUC increase after oral administration in SD rats and exhibits sustained-release behavior (Liu et al., 2025). However, cationic cellulose nanocrystals have not yet been included as common food ingredients.

Hydrodynamic cavitation enables green extraction of IntegroPectin from citrus by-products. This material is not an artificial microencapsulation delivery system; instead, it is an in-situ-formed natural pectin-flavonoid complex generated during extraction, characterised by an RG-I-rich hairy scaffold. It remains stable without degradation when stored at room temperature for 28 months, demonstrating far greater stability than traditional flavonoid extracts (Meneguzzo et al., 2019). Grapefruit-derived IntegroPectin contains up to 98% naringin. In vitro ischemia-reperfusion models have demonstrated that its cardioprotective effects are superior to those of pure naringin, confirming synergistic bioactivity (Flori et al., 2022). However, there is currently a lack of pharmacokinetic datasets (AUC, Cmax) for oral administration. Hence, it cannot be confirmed that it increases systemic plasma exposure to flavonoids following oral administration.

Bio-based nanocarriers

Exosomes, as naturally occurring nanovesicular carriers, encapsulate hydrophobic bioactive molecules within their phospholipid bilayer structure. They can be engineered through various methods—including genetic engineering, chemical conjugation, physical encapsulation, and microfluidics—to enhance the potential for oral delivery of flavonoids (Mondal et al., 2023). It was developed by loading hesperidin into exosomes by sonication to form an exo-HES system. The pharmacokinetics of the orally administered drug in mice indicated that the oral absorption and half-life of the drug were approximately 2.5 times higher than those of free hesperidin. Moreover, after being given orally to tumor-bearing animals, the antitumor effect of the system is greater and there is no evidence of toxicity (Kumar et al., 2024). The high cost of producing exosomes poses challenges to their industrial application in the food sector.

Covalent modification of molecules

Covalent molecular modifications can alter the solubility and lipophilicity of flavonoids at the molecular level and modify enzyme-binding sites, with the goal of evading phase-II enzyme conjugation and transporter-mediated efflux. The hydrophilic glycosylation, phosphorylation, and ionizable-group addition can add highly polar groups to increase the solubility. The one that has a high potential of being applied is phosphorylation. The water-soluble derivatives of hesperetin formed in the process of biotransformation using Bacillus natto are more stable in the simulated gastrointestinal fluids. Caco-2 tests indicate better permeability of intestinal epithelium. These derivatives, when administered orally in rats, also enhance the systemic absorption of hesperetin much higher than the oral dose of hesperidin, and they can be dephosphorylated in the intestine to transform into the parent compound (Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Adding hydrophobic molecules like acyl and methoxy groups would make it more lipophilic and passable through membranes. The whole-cell two-enzyme cascade can be used to catalyze the esterification of fatty acids, which leads to the production of fat-soluble fatty acid esters in naringin, and this product has a higher log P value and antioxidant effect when compared to the parent compound (naringin) in vitro, and the Caco-2 results also show that it is more permeable to intestinal epithelium (Li et al., 2020). Apart from their solubility and absorption characteristics, enzymatic changes like glycosylation and methylation have been found to enhance the chemical stability of flavonoids during processing and storage (Fu et al., 2021).

Such alterations of the lipophilicity and activity of flavonoids in vitro can be achieved through targeted changes like glycosylation, deglycosylation, acylation, methylation, and isopentenylation; however, the effects of these modifications are bidirectional. For example, deglycosylation can enhance membrane permeability but reduces water solubility. Even for the same type of modification, the substitution site and the length of the substituent can significantly affect the gastrointestinal stability and in vivo metabolic behavior of the derivatives, which can easily lead to a discrepancy between in vitro activity and in vivo pharmacological efficacy (Cunha et al., 2026).

Prodrug design represents a vital branch of rational molecular optimisation (Cheng et al., 2023). Reversible covalent modification can improve the physicochemical properties of molecules; once inside the body, these molecules are hydrolyzed by phosphatases and esterases to revert to regenerate bioactive parent compounds. Hesperetin phosphorylated derivatives are a typical example. Prodrug performance is highly dependent on the efficiency of in vivo hydrolysis, and incomplete hydrolysis makes it difficult for them to exert their therapeutic effects. Total chemical synthesis (synthetic analog development) enables the construction of citrus-flavonoid synthetic analogues by altering ring-substitution patterns to circumvent phase-II conjugation and efflux recognition. However, as these are entirely novel chemical entities, they face substantial toxicological and food-regulatory barriers, and most candidates remain confined to laboratory-scale research (Wang et al., 2013).

Whether it involves enzyme-mediated modification, prodrug design, or synthetic analogue development, both pharmacokinetic and bioefficacy endpoints must be assessed in parallel; these two dimensions cannot be equated. Physicochemical and bioactivity advantages observed in vitro do not guarantee enhanced in vivo efficacy.

Unlike conventional trial-and-error synthesis, computer-aided molecular design and AI can offer predictive instructions for structural modifications. AI-QSAR models train deep learning models on available flavonoid data sets to high-throughput predict solubility properties, enzymatic metabolic stability, plasma protein binding, and oral bioavailability of derivatives, facilitating virtual screening before synthesis and reducing R&D cycles. ADMET prediction platforms can evaluate drug-likelihood risks such as intestinal absorption and organ toxicity at early developmental stages, permitting pre-emptive elimination of high-risk modification schemes (Yang et al., 2022).

Safety, process reproducibility, and regulatory considerations for translation across different application scenarios

Molecular interactions

The in vivo fate of flavonoids and their modified derivatives depends on molecular recognition by metabolic enzymes and transporters in the intestine and liver. Natural citrus flavonoid aglycones are substrates for phase-II conjugating enzymes (UGT and SULT), as well as the MRP2 efflux transporter; this is the primary molecular reason for the low oral bioavailability of the parent molecules.

Covalent modifications are designed to alter the molecular structure to circumvent phase-II UGT/SULT conjugation and transporter-mediated efflux. Nevertheless, most modified derivatives are hydrolyzed in the gastrointestinal tract and revert to the parent flavonoid, once again becoming substrates for enzymes and transporters, causing the in vitro advantages to be lost in vivo. Some delivery carrier excipients can also directly modulate intestinal enzymes and transporters, which may produce synergistic benefits but may also trigger undesired metabolic disturbances.

Safety risk assessment

Long-term oral administration of physically modified flavonoid formulations poses potential physiological risks. One 60-day human trial reported reduced heart rates among participants receiving long-term supplementation with a micronized diosmin-hesperidin mixture, suggesting that modified products require further human safety data to justify practical application (Rodrigues et al., 2025).

Despite the fact that enzymatic structural modification has been demonstrated to be promising in the laboratory, most of the synthetically modified flavonoid derivatives have not yet been subjected to systematic human pharmacokinetic and toxicological studies. Non-natural derivatives covalently altered by biocatalysis are new chemical entities and do not equate to natural citrus flavonoids. When implemented into food or dietary supplements, full-scale safety evaluation on novel-food-ingredient approval is required. Moreover, practical industrial bottlenecks, including controlling the regional selectivity of biocatalysts, suppressing byproducts, and scaling up production costs, need to be resolved (Cunha et al., 2026). Artificially modified products like acetylated or phosphorylated compounds are not natural ingredients of citrus and cannot be used directly as ordinary food ingredients; even when they demonstrate excellent in vitro activity, they should be subjected to a comprehensive toxicological evaluation before proceeding, and the safety of the parent flavonoid cannot be extrapolated. Moreover, the safety of delivery vehicle excipients in the long run should also be assessed. Risks related to endogenous metabolic disturbance or food-drug interactions can occur when modified derivatives react with CYP enzymes or when excipients interfere non-specifically with intestinal transport machineries.

Barriers to industrial commercialization

Bottlenecks in preparation and scale-up processes

Although nanocomposite delivery systems have shown good results in vitro and in animal models, scaling them up often leads to issues such as poor batch-to-batch reproducibility, difficulty in removing organic solvent residues, and high costs. Most studies have not yet progressed to human trials (Yadav et al., 2026). Natural exosomes suffer from the drawbacks of extremely low yield from raw materials, difficulties in large-scale production, and high costs. Accordingly, mixed-vesicle and exosome-mimetic vesicles have been developed as an alternative technology for mass production (Mondal et al., 2023).

Regulatory issues and intellectual property barriers

Cyclodextrin complexes are subject to restrictions on their use as food additives, lack sufficient evidence of human oral safety, are susceptible to competitive dissociation by dietary components, and their in vivo benefits are uncertain (Dos Santos Lima et al., 2019b). Most commercially available lipid-nanoparticle formulations are injectable drugs, and there are few established examples of oral lipid nanoparticle carriers for use in foods and dietary supplements; established processes for injectable drug delivery cannot be directly applied to food applications, as compliance with raw material regulations and cost considerations must also be taken into account (Kumar et al., 2022). Covalently modified flavonoid derivatives represent novel chemical entities. Their deployment within food matrices requires comprehensive toxicological evaluation and safety dossiers for novel-food-ingredient authorization. In addition, patents related to biocatalysts, nanomanufacturing processes, carrier crystal forms, and formulations may also impose intellectual property constraints on industrialization.

Gaps in the evidence chain

The amount of dosage used in animal research is usually very high compared to the real intake of food, and AUC at a high level can not be extrapolated to the human population. Flavonoid aglycones are taken as test substrates by most studies, which does not allow one to generalize the findings on disaccharide glycosides that exist in nature. The majority of modification and delivery methods have only focused on acute oral pharmacokinetics; when consumed over a long period of time, the metabolic pathways of humans may adapt to this, and the levels of bioavailability found with acute administration will not remain consistent. The effectiveness should be tested in human trials where it would be necessary to measure the stability of efficacy with regard to the long-term consumption of the drug (Muralidharan et al., 2023).

Real-world interferences caused by food matrices

It is also necessary to consider the interactions of food matrix and modified/delivery formulation. During processing and storage, matrix components can promote or inhibit the oxidative degradation of flavonoids, thereby altering the actual content of active compounds; once in the digestive tract, they can also affect the free concentration of flavonoids in the intestinal lumen, as well as their hydrolysis by gut microbiota and absorption (Fu et al., 2021; Kamiloglu et al., 2021). Hence, even if a formulation exhibits excellent in vitro performance, interference can occur throughout the entire process from production to in vivo digestion. The increased total flavonoid content and enhanced in vitro antioxidant activity observed during processing do not necessarily equate to improved in vivo bioavailability. Food matrices can also indirectly regulate microbial hydrolytic enzymes, intestinal epithelial transporters, and phase II metabolic enzymes. Consequently, promising results of in vitro and animal experiments can be quite different under the conditions of real diet. This is one of the key causes of high inter-individual variability and low reproducibility of some human trials.

This review summarises delivery platforms, including liposomes, nanoemulsions, solid lipid nanoparticles, nanostructured lipid carriers, polymeric nanoparticles, plant phospholipid complexes, micelles, cyclodextrin inclusion complexes, hydrogels, and exosomes. Adaptation boundaries for different application scenarios

General foods

Natural citrus ingredients only and physical processing are allowed. It is not permitted to chemically alter products and use synthetic nanocarriers in non-food grade.

Dietary supplements

The food-based enzyme-converted products and the cyclodextrin complexes can be consumed, but the synthetic non-natural derivatives cannot be taken in. They are controlled by the laws of ingredient listing, dosages, and labeling statements.

Pharmaceutical formulations

Nanocarriers and artificial prodrugs are worth developing, but they need complete packages of pharmacokinetic, toxicological, and clinical evaluation.

Cosmetics

The new food substances are not to be filed as cosmetics. The physical modification, enzymatic alteration, artificial modification, and a number of nanodelivery systems can all be created. The criteria of evaluation in this case are skin irritation, transdermal effect, and skin effectiveness. It is not necessary to perform a risk analysis of oral gastrointestinal absorption and systemic exposure; however, it should be noted that ingredients which are prohibited in cosmetic use cannot be used.

To conclude, the problem of the bioavailability bottleneck of citrus flavonoids is in its molecular composition; it has to be solved by various methods as a result of various metabolic problems due to various structures. The physical alteration is mostly focused on solubility; the microbiota-dependent bottleneck of disaccharide glycosides is mainly overcome by biotransformation; the delivery encapsulation also enhances solubility and gastrointestinal stability; the covalent modification enables restructuring of the molecules at the molecular level but requires high regulation standards; food matrices may provide synergistic effects and can have an inhibitory effect. It is a multi-factor trade-off strategy selection that does not allow for a one-size-fits-all solution.

Summary and outlook

The bibliometric data indicate that the amount of studies published in the field of citrus flavonoids has been on a growing trend since 1991, and it has been focused on the early stages of research on the antioxidant and cellular activity of the flavonoid compounds, but the attention to the latter has shifted to the study of molecular signaling mechanisms, the interaction between the gut microbiota, and human clinical trials (Qiu et al., 2023b). Human pharmacokinetic studies conducted in the early 21st century have updated key understandings in the field. Citrus flavanone glycosides are difficult to absorb directly in the small intestine and are highly dependent on hydrolysis and conversion by the colonic microbiota. in vivo circulating forms are mainly UGT/SULT phase-II conjugates, where the parent compound constitutes only a negligible fraction. Thus, the results of the in vitro activities of the parent cannot be generalized to human situations (Kanaze et al., 2007; Manach et al., 2003). As the information about the pharmacokinetics of humans have increased, the metabolism of different individuals has also been found to vary greatly; this led to the development of the concept of the enterotypes which was introduced in 2011 as a way of explaining differences in the gut microbiota among individuals (Arumugam et al., 2011). The contradiction between the bioactive potential of citrus flavonoids and their low bioavailability has gradually garnered attention. Since 2012, food-grade delivery systems such as nanoemulsions have been increasingly applied to polymethoxyflavone research, while practical formulation challenges, including crystallization and precipitation instability, have also been systematically identified (Li et al., 2012b). Overall, although various delivery strategies have been extensively explored, most of the evidence at this stage still comes from in vitro digestion models and animal studies. It is clear that the quality of human pharmacokinetic and intervention validation still needs improvement.

This review takes the “molecular structure–metabolic fate–bioactivity–synergistic optimization strategies” for citrus flavonoids as its central theme, analyzing how substituent groups regulate metabolism, distinguishing between different levels of research evidence, identifying key oral absorption bottlenecks, comparing the advantages and disadvantages of four major technical categories (physical modification, biotransformation, delivery encapsulation, and covalent molecular modification), and the formation of a clear logic-based model of the strategy. But all technologies in existence are not yet fully validated in vivo, with their bottleneck issues including matrix interference, process scale-up, and regulatory issues. Looking ahead, rather than simply increasing the plasma concentration of citrus flavonoids, greater attention should be paid to precisely regulating the spatiotemporal distribution of active molecules. Specifically, the following three areas warrant further breakthroughs.

AI and QSAR modelling can be integrated for rational preliminary screening of modified flavonoid candidates. Research should establish a dedicated dataset for citrus flavonoids, focusing on targets related to oral processing such as UGT/SULT metabolic enzymes, MRP2/BCRP efflux transporters, and intestinal hydrolases. Molecular docking and ADMET should serve only as theoretical screening tools. The selected candidates must sequentially undergo in vitro evaluation and animal pharmacokinetic studies, and ultimately be validated through human trials to avoid the limitations of relying solely on simulation to infer in vivo effects (Yang et al., 2022).

Given individual differences, the potential of microbiome stratification to guide personalized nutrition can be further explored. If engineered probiotic interventions are to be advanced, priority must be given to addressing the stability of strain colonization in the gut, thoroughly assessing the safety risks of horizontal gene transfer, and completing population cohort validation. Only after solidifying the evidence should consideration be given to translating these findings into general dietary settings. Moreover, network pharmacology combined with untargeted metabolomics is also an effective means of clarifying the signaling pathways of natural products in vivo. It is capable of detecting alterations in host endogenous metabolites, revealing inflammation-related regulatory axes, and offering a research paradigm in explaining the mechanisms of bioactive compounds (Lang et al., 2023).

The food industry will benefit more from the green, complete use of citrus processing by-products. In future studies, the focus should be on how the release and absorption of citrus flavonoids in the gastrointestinal tract is being observed as a result of their behavior, instead of the mere analysis of the dissolution in vitro to predict the effects in vivo. Generally speaking, the bioavailability enhancement does not have to be restricted to enhancing the plasma concentrations only, but it has to be based on the solution of the structural issues with the help of specific strategies of intervention, follow the human pharmacokinetics as the gold standard of evaluation, and ensure safety, processability, and regulatory requirements to ensure the high-value exploitation of citrus flavonoid resources.

In summary, through in-depth interdisciplinary collaboration and synergistic innovation across chemistry, biology, materials science, pharmacy, and food engineering, it is expected that we will achieve a fundamental breakthrough in overcoming the bioavailability barriers to the application of citrus flavonoids, unlock their full health-promoting potential, and promote the high-value utilization of citrus resources.
