Ice-phase optothermal tweezers
Authors: Jianxing Zhou, Yuhang Peng, Peng Du, Zhen Chen, Jinming Zhang, Zhengtian Jin, Xiaoqi Dai, Yinyue Ji, Meiting Wang, Yuye Wang, Yuebing Zheng, Jiajie Chen, Junle Qu, Yonghong Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The deterministic and reconfigurable assembly of heterogeneous micro- and nanostructures remains challenging, where Brownian motion, nonspecific interfacial adsorption, and method-specific material constraints undermine precision and cross-material compatibility. Here, we present ice-phase optothermal tweezers (IOT), a platform that utilizes the ice–water interface for fast (10–100 μm/s), programmable manipulation of diverse targets, including dielectric/metal nanoparticles, proteins, DNA, and even microbubbles within ice, which have long been regarded as passive byproducts of freezing. In this scheme, the optothermally induced mobile melting zone within the ice matrix enables directed transport and nanostructure assembly with nanometer-scale resolution. We further fabricate multi-material heterostructures with tailored anisotropic and chiral optical responses, enabling applications in chiral photonics and polarization-controlled nano-optical devices under cryogenic environments. Accordingly, by unifying precise control of micro- and nano-entities across fluidic and solid-state regimes, IOT provides a general cryogenic platform for meta-structure fabrication, in-situ optical spectroscopy, and nanoparticle-interaction studies.

Introduction

The programmable assembly of heterogeneous nanoscale components into complex, functional architectures or patterns would unlock transformative technologies, from optical metamaterials to sophisticated bio-interfaces. Yet this goal remains fundamentally constrained by a persistent paradox: Liquid-phase manipulation enables nanoscale mobility but guarantees instability, while solid-state fixation ensures stability at the cost of dynamic programmability. Current high-precision techniques (e.g., optical/acoustic tweezers) inevitably suffer from disruptive Brownian motion and irreversible surface adsorption, precluding robust reconfiguration of nanoscale assemblies1,2. Conversely, directed self-assembly strategies, despite achieving thermodynamic stability, lack the arbitrary programmability or broad material compatibility required for constructing truly heterogeneous architectures with multi-component complexity3,4. This impasse has stifled progress toward applications demanding both structural integrity and biocompatibility, such as optical metamaterials, bio-interfaces engineering, and biosensing.

Currently, various advanced optical manipulation techniques have been developed, including plasmonic tweezers5–9, hybrid electro-optical tweezers10–15, optothermal tweezers16–23, and low-temperature optothermal tweezers24–26, which enable low-power trapping of diverse colloidal particles across expanded size and material ranges. However, these delicate approaches remain fundamentally constrained by two persistent challenges. First, their operation necessitates stringent environmental conditions, such as specific particle surface charges, solvent conductivity, or the introduction of auxiliary solutes to generate thermophoretic or electrokinetic forces. Second, akin to conventional optical tweezers27, their manipulation fidelity is inevitably compromised by Brownian motion28,29, limiting nanoscale positional stability. Crucially, reconfigurable manipulation of particles immobilized within solid media is inherently difficult, as van der Waals, electric double-layer, and friction forces render adhesion effectively irreversible under gentle actuation. Current state-of-the-art strategies, including surfactant-modified substrates30–32 and pulsed optothermal modulation33, impose material-specific requirements (e.g., light-absorbing particles), provide only a limited manipulation size range, and compromise biocompatibility, thereby restricting their broader applicability.

Here, we overcome this fundamental limitation by introducing a paradigm that harnesses the physics of ice-water phase change. We developed ice-phase optothermal tweezers (IOT), a technique that uses a focused laser to create and precisely steer a localized melting zone within a solid ice matrix. This mobile melting zone acts as a fluidic workspace where particles can be manipulated, while the surrounding ice matrix provides a cryogenic reference frame. The manipulation itself is driven by the forces exerted by the advancing solidification front, a process governed by a delicate interplay of intermolecular, thermal, and viscous forces. Because the manipulated particles are propelled by the slippery ice–water interface with a relatively large curvature on the micrometer scale, we achieve transport at exceptionally high speeds (10–100 µm/s), far surpassing previous surface-manipulation strategies based on optical forces or sol–gel phase transitions, which typically remain below 10 µm/s3,30.

This approach enables the user-defined assembly of a broad spectrum of materials, including dielectric and metallic nanoparticles, proteins, DNA, and microbubbles. We demonstrate reversible merging, splitting, deformation, and programmable sculpting of microbubbles within ice, whereas previous studies achieved only passive, one-directional deformation under freezing dynamics34. This capability transforms bubbles from passive markers of freezing into programmable elements for probing cryogenic interfacial mechanics and engineering reconfigurable ice-templated materials35–37. Notably, we also achieve on-demand fabrication of gold–SiO₂ heterostructures that exhibit engineered anisotropic and chiral optical responses, establishing a route toward polarization-programmable nano-optical devices and chiral photonics38,39.

Thus, IOT extends optical manipulation into frozen environments. By integrating dynamic, reconfigurable control with stable fixation, it bridges the fundamental gap between the flexibility of liquid-phase manipulation and the stability of solid-state fabrication. This platform enables functional microdevice fabrication and nanoparticle-interaction studies, while offering a biocompatible route for cryogenic biomolecular research.

Results

Working principle

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, b, target particles were initially dispersed in a microfluidic cell (~30 μm height; Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1) filled with water and mounted on a cooling stage. Lowering the temperature below the freezing point induced ice formation, immobilizing particles near the substrate through a downward-propagating ice–water interface. After complete freezing, a focused laser beam was directed onto the gold-coated substrate, where optothermal heating established a stable temperature gradient. Once the local temperature exceeded 0 °C, a hemispherical, dome-shaped melting zone formed, releasing particles into a confined liquid micro-environment. This melting zone exhibits a tilted arc interface in the ZX plane and a circular footprint in the XY plane. The melting zone confines a femtoliter-scale liquid volume at the microscale (Fig. 1c). Benefiting from the high thermal conductivity of the gold film, melting and re-solidification occur within ~10 ms, enabling a rapid steady state as confirmed by simulations (Fig. 1d). This fast response allows real-time particle manipulation. Figure 1e and f show that the melting zone radius depends on laser power and ambient temperature; tuning these parameters provides precise, high-throughput particle control with adjustable zone sizes. Notably, the melting zone can be repositioned by steering the laser focus. Inside this microscale volume, particles are directed by thermophoresis, local flow fields, or the receding ice–water interface. Subsequently, rapid refreezing upon laser shutoff enables precise printing onto the substrate. Particle manipulation operates in two distinct modes when the XY stage is translated with the laser fixed: suspension mode (Fig. 1a) and surface mode (Fig. 1b).

For the suspension mode, the particle disengages from the substrate and traces a vortex-like trajectory in the XZ plane at an average height of 3.7 μm above the surface (See Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2), realizing a non-contact, three-dimensional motion within the melting zone. When the laser focus is displaced, melting occurs at the leading edge of the melting zone while solidification takes place at the trailing edge. The density variations associated with these phase transitions generate a flow from the rear toward the front of the melting zone40, thereby transporting the particle along with the advancing molten region. The use of flow fields within this femtoliter-scale, ice-encapsulated liquid region is particularly significant, as it enables a synergistic trapping mechanism that addresses key limitations of conventional optothermal approaches. Within this confined micro-environment, a low-power laser generates a steep thermal gradient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla T$$\end{document}∇T), giving rise to both a thermophoretic force and thermo-osmotic flow. These flows act in concert with the rigid boundary of the surrounding ice–water interface, which completely suppresses particle escape. This dual-confinement system, combining a soft potential well with a hard physical trap, ensures robust particle capture irrespective of their intrinsic thermophoretic properties (i.e., thermophobic or thermophilic), thereby offering broad material versatility without the need for specific solutes16,17,45. In addition, the bulk ice functions as an efficient heat sink, confining thermal effects to the microscale and substantially reducing the risk of damage to sensitive biological specimens.

For the surface mode, as shown in Fig. 1b, by translating the laser focus, the ice–water interface is displaced, pushing particles located at the ice–water–substrate boundary. The particle is pinned at the narrow junction where the tilted ice–water interface (in ZX plane) meets the substrate. Consequently, particle manipulation in this mode is governed by two coupled factors, i.e., the particle–substrate interaction and the influence of the tilted ice–water interface.

In this mode, particles are propelled by the advancing ice–water interface. As shown in Fig. 2b (and Supplementary Movie 2), translating the laser spot displaces a single PS particle within the melting zone, akin to repositioning cargo, with the particle consistently residing at its trailing edge. A well-known limitation of optical tweezers is that particles adhered to the substrate cannot be confined within the optical potential, rendering conventional manipulation ineffective. Our method readily overcomes this barrier.

As demonstrated in Fig. 2c (Supplementary Movie 3), a population of 1 μm PS particles can be precisely guided toward an existing aggregate, enabling intentional merging and subsequent collective manipulation. This functionality resembles a microscale sweeping microrobot operating within ice, capable of selectively gathering or removing target particles such as microplastics46–48, within a defined micrometer-scale region. Such capability provides a powerful tool for studying environmental microcontaminants in frozen systems and opens a pathway to explore swarm-like nanorobotic behaviors under cryogenic conditions.

Therefore, by harnessing the interfacial dynamics of the ice–water boundary, our method enables remote transport of a broad spectrum of particle types. For PSs, particles with diverse surface chemistries, including carboxylated, aminated, and unmodified surfaces, can all be manipulated. We further demonstrated precise control of particles of varying compositions, including PS, silica, and gold, across a size range from 100 nm to 5 μm in diameter. This unique ability to manipulate particles irrespective of size, surface charge, or material composition greatly expands the operational scope of optical manipulation, paving the way for applications in heterogeneous colloidal assembly, surface chemistry studies, and bio-interface engineering, as further illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 8–10.

Manipulation ability analysis

We have demonstrated manipulation of both individual and ensembles of polystyrene particles (PSs) using IOT. In suspension mode (Fig. 2a), particles are entrained by local flow fields to form a vortex ring, rolling inward and outward along the loop (Fig. 1g). The entire group can be transported together with the melting zone, and although particles cluster near its trailing edge due to inertial effects, they remain fully suspended without attaching to the ice–water interface. This mode provides a non-contact strategy for collective transport in confined femtoliter-scale aqueous environments, enabling dynamic particle transportation without substrate adhesion. In surface mode (Fig. 2b), by contrast, particles are immobilized at the substrate by the advancing ice–water interface and propelled by its motion, enabling stable printing into defined structures. Both suspended and surface-bound particles can thus be manipulated within IOT. Suspension mode is suited for dynamic, floating transport, whereas surface mode provides reconfigurable anchoring and substrate-level control. Together, these complementary modes expand the operational scope of IOT, allowing flexible transitions between reconfigurable manipulation and stable nanostructure fabrication. Notably, IOT is not limited to manipulating individual colloids. As shown in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 8, entire particle ensembles can be arranged into symmetric architectures with sub-micrometer precision. While the laser remains on, the melting zone allows dynamic reconfiguration; upon laser deactivation, the rapid refreezing step locks the particles into the desired configuration without distortion.

Particle confinement in IOT is dynamically stabilized by interface-driven transport and localized flow fields rather than a static conservative trapping potential (Details are shown in Supplementary Note 3). To assess manipulation capability, we quantified the effective trapping stiffness (ETS) of 1-µm PS particles with different surface functional groups under varying laser powers and ambient temperatures. For particles with stronger attraction to the gold film (typically PS–NR³⁺ and plain PS at temperatures below −10 °C), which are normally confined on the substrate, the ETS reaches ~10 pN/µm (Fig. 2g, h), nearly an order of magnitude greater than that of conventional optical tweezers. In contrast, particles with weaker attraction to the gold film (e.g., plain PS or PS–COOH) typically remain suspended and exhibit circular, radially oscillatory trajectories in the ZX plane (Fig. 1g) together with tangential circular motion in the XY plane (Fig. 1h); under these conditions, the ETS is weak(~10 fN µm⁻¹; Fig. 2g at −5 °C and Fig. 2i), resulting in kx and ky values smaller than kr and approximately two orders of magnitude lower than those of conventional optical tweezers. This indicates a low-force, non-contact transport regime that causes minimal perturbation, making it suitable for fragile specimens. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 2g–i, tuning the laser power and ambient temperature also determines whether particles remain suspended or attach to the substrate. At low laser power, the melting zone contracts and favors substrate printing, corresponding to the high-ETS regime. Notably, control measurements on firmly deposited particles (subject to stage and atmospheric vibrations) produced ETS values identical to those in surface mode (shaded regions in Fig. 2g–i), confirming that our method applies forces sufficient to manipulate particles rigidly attached to the substrate.

Furthermore, it is worth noting that the IOT can trap and manipulate particles at high speeds (10–100 μm/s) regardless of whether they are printed onto the substrate or floating in the melting zone. These results demonstrate that IOT provides a highly programmable force landscape, from non-invasive transport to robust substrate-level manipulation, enabling both gentle handling of delicate assemblies and active control of surface-bound particles for applications in micro-assembly, surface chemistry, and biological interface studies.

Remarkably, IOT is not limited to solid particles but can also manipulate microbubbles within ice. Previous studies have shown that air bubbles trapped in ice undergo only passive deformation, with their final shape dictated by freezing rate, capillarity, and mass diffusion, processes entirely governed by the natural dynamics of solidification34,56. In contrast, our IOT provides versatile, active manipulation of bubble dynamics within ice.

Reconfigurable particle assembly and spectral detection

Remarkably, in the surface mode, IOT enables reconfigurable and high-precision assembly of colloidal particles directly on solid substrates. By simply toggling the laser on or off, we can rapidly (~10 ms, Fig. 1d) switch particles between a fixed state, i.e., printed onto the substrate, and a controllable state where they can be repositioned at will. Furthermore, fine modulation of the laser power allows precise control of the melting zone size on the heat-absorbing substrate, thereby dictating the minimum achievable inter-particle spacing and enabling tunable printing accuracy. By combining high-NA objective lenses with reduced laser power, we achieve nanometer-scale printing resolution.

Firstly, we demonstrated the highly reconfigurable patterning capability of IOT. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, we first assembled 1 μm PS particles into a square configuration. By selectively moving the particles at the upper-left and lower-right corners to the left and right edges, respectively, the square was transformed into a straight line. Subsequent repositioning of the left-edge particles to the bottom-right corner and the right-edge particles to the top-left corner restored the square geometry, but with the diagonal particles interchanged. Besides, to assess the post-printing stability of IOT-fabricated structures, we examined 400 nm Au particles arranged into a square pattern on the substrate. As shown in Fig. 5b, the printed structures retain their geometry both in dark-field optical images after ice melting and in SEM images following drying, confirming that the assemblies remain firmly anchored to the substrate. We also quantified the patterning accuracy of IOT. As shown in Fig. 5c(i), a 3 × 3 array of 400 nm Au particles was assembled with the target positions defined at the array nodes. Using grayscale centroid analysis (Fig. 5c(ii)), we determined that the mean positional deviation from the targets was ~330 nm. For one-dimensional patterns, we assembled 400 nm Au particles into a straight line (SEM image in Fig. 5d) and measured the vertical displacement of each particle relative to the predefined line, yielding an average deviation of ~80 nm. These results demonstrate that IOT achieves sub-wavelength positioning precision in both 2D and 1D assemblies, enabling the real-time fabrication of highly controllable micro- and nanostructures. Discussion

In this work, we introduce ice-phase optothermal tweezers (IOT), a paradigm for nanoscale manipulation that harnesses a controllable solid–liquid phase transition to extend optical manipulation into frozen environments. IOT resolves the trade-off between manipulation flexibility and structural stability by seamlessly integrating dynamic, reconfigurable control within a localized melting zone with the ability to statically print structures upon refreezing. Freezing fixes the assembly in place, effectively quenching Brownian motion and allowing structures to be written, erased, and rewritten. Compared with conventional optical tweezers, IOT enables direct manipulation of particles on solid substrates, where optical trapping typically fails, while simultaneously achieving precise particle-level control without relying on high-NA objectives. This distinction arises because particle motion in IOT is governed by interface-driven actuation and displacement-stage precision rather than optical confinement, thereby overcoming these fundamental constraints of optical tweezers.

The versatility of IOT, which can assemble building blocks ranging from nanoparticles and proteins to microbubbles, arises from a manipulation mechanism governed by universal physical principles rather than material-specific properties, achieving a fabrication resolution of 80 nm. This broad applicability is crucial for constructing heterogeneous structures that underpin advanced functional materials and bio-interfaces. In particular, achieving site-selective and asymmetric assembly between biomacromolecules and inorganic nanoparticles remains challenging due to the difficulty of deterministic co-localization and controlled interfacial binding while suppressing nonspecific adsorption. Leveraging the cross-material manipulation capability of IOT, a promising future direction is to combine IOT with the freezing-induced concentration effect to co-confine DNA and gold nanoparticles within a localized melting zone60–62, followed by controlled refreezing to promote proximity-driven, site-selective binding at nanoparticle interfaces. While we demonstrate controlled transport of DNA ensembles, deterministic manipulation of free single biomolecules remains challenging due to biomolecule–substrate interactions and limitations in optical imaging for tracking individual biomolecules. Nevertheless, experiments (Supplementary Fig. 10) with 40 nm and 100 nm fluorescent nanoparticles indicate that IOT remains effective well below the 100 nm scale.

IOT also offers a versatile platform for assembling directionally anisotropic nanostructures from diverse material combinations with tunable polarization responses, enabling applications in chiral photonics, metasurfaces, and polarization-controlled nano-optical devices. Beyond nanoparticle assembly, IOT transforms microbubbles from passive byproducts of freezing into actively programmable elements34,56. We have achieved reversible and precise merging, splitting, elongation, and even sculpting microbubbles within ice, resolving a fundamental challenge and unlocking opportunities for bio-inspired cryopreservation, reconfigurable ice-templated materials, and bubble-based photonic architectures.

While powerful, we acknowledge the current limitations of IOT, which also define clear avenues for future development. The reliance on a cryogenic ice matrix, essential for its unique stability, presently restricts applications requiring physiological temperatures. Furthermore, our demonstrations are primarily confined to quasi-2D assembly on a substrate. A significant future challenge is to extend this method to true layer-by-layer 3D fabrication, creating complex volumetric architectures within the ice. Looking forward, we envision several key advancements: exploring alternative phase-change, biocompatible materials to enable operation at or near room temperature; integrating IOT with nanofluidic systems for high-precision biomaterial delivery and sensing63; and implementing super-resolved photonic force microscopy under cryogenic conditions64. Such developments could transform IOT from a specialized tool into a general-purpose platform for nanoscale manufacturing and analysis.

Methods

Optical setup

Our system integrates a Nikon inverted microscope (Ti2-E) with a 785 nm solid-state laser. The laser beam undergoes 5× expansion before passing through a non-polarizing beam splitter (reflection ratio 90:10; transmission ratio in the 700–1100 nm wavelength range, from Thorlabs). Three objectives were employed: a 100× oil dark-field objective (Nikon, NA = 1.3), a 40× objective (Nikon, NA = 0.6), and a 20× objective (Nikon, NA = 0.45). Optical images were recorded using a color sCMOS camera (Dhyana 400DC, Tucsen Ltd.) with a short-pass filter (bandwidth 4, cutoff wavelength 675 nm, Edmund) to mitigate the impact of reflected 785 nm laser light. FITC fluorescence is excited by a 475 nm LED light source (Nikon).

Microfluidic cell setup

The microfluidic cell consisted of a glass slide and a gold-coated (10 nm) coverslip joined by parafilm, with a thickness of approximately 30 μm. The cooling stage features a glass aperture at both the top and bottom to ensure the objective lens receives images and allows illumination light to enter normally. The detailed structure of the microfluidic cell and the cooling stage is shown in Supplementary Note 1. When using the 100× oil dark-field objective, the cover beneath the cooling stage must be opened to maintain an appropriate working distance for the objective. Under the configuration of the experiment in this article, the cooling water temperature was set at 3 °C. The cooling stage temperature is set to -5, -10, or −20°C, with the cooling start temperature set to 25 °C.

Substrate and material preparation

The fabrication process for the optothermal substrate is as follows: First, a 5nm-thick chrome adhesion layer is deposited onto the glass cover slip (20 × 20 mm), followed by a 10 nm-thick gold layer. These deposition procedures were performed by Suzhou Hengxin Microelectronics CO., Ltd. The cooling stage (TES120E-IM) was procured from Wuhan Congtical Technology Co., Ltd. Reagents such as anti-human IgG, and ssDNA were procured from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Carboxylated PS particles (spheres, 1 μm, 10.09 wt %) were sourced from Bangs Laboratories, Inc. Plain and trialkylammonium-modified PS particles (spheres, 50 mg/ml) were sourced from micromod particle technology, GmbH. Carboxylated Polystyrene particles (spheres, 2, 3, 4 μm, 2.5 wt %) were purchased from Jiangsu Zhichuan Technology CO., Ltd. Green-fluorescent polystyrene particles (spheres, 0.04, 0.10 μm, 1 wt %) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.
