Unveiling the unseen: generalized myoclonus status and MODS in scrub typhus, a case report and literature review
Authors: Xiaojin Gan, Xianbin Cai, Tangxin Xin, Feng-E Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrientia tsutsugamushi, the causative agent of scrub typhus, poses a significant public health threat across the Asia-Pacific region. Clinical manifestations of scrub typhus vary widely, delays in treatment are associated with high mortality, whereas prompt and appropriate antimicrobial therapy, typically with doxycycline or suitable alternatives, can significantly reduce fatality rates. Early diagnosis and effective treatment are thus critical for patient outcomes. Here, we present a rare case of scrub typhus featuring generalized myoclonus status and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).Case presentationA 72-year-old Asian male with a history of schizophrenia presented to our hospital with a one-week history of persistent, involuntary, and synchronous jerkings involving the extremities with preserved consciousness for one week. Physical examination revealed an eschar on the left scrotal region. Definitive diagnosis was confirmed via targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) of alveolar lavage fluid, which detected Orientia tsutsugamushi. The patient was diagnosed with scrub typhus, accompanied by generalized myoclonus status and MODS.ConclusionClinicians in endemic regions should consider scrub typhus in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with acute febrile illness of unknown origin and neurological abnormalities. A thorough and detailed physical examination, timely and effective treatment are essential for curing the disease.

Introduction

Orientia tsutsugamushi is the causative agent of scrub typhus—an acute febrile illness transmitted through the bite of infected mite larvae (chiggers) [1]. This disease remains a major neglected tropical disease that is easily overlooked, predominantly endemic to the “tsutsugamushi triangle” spanning the Asia-Pacific region, with an estimated one billion people at risk and over one million annual cases. The convergence of high vector density in rural agricultural areas, limited diagnostic infrastructure, and non-specific symptomatology contributes to persistent outbreaks. This necessitates that clinicians improve their familiarity with the disease and their ability to identify and diagnose it [2, 3]. Scrub typhus typically manifests with fever, headache, and myalgia, but its protean clinical presentation often delays diagnosis. A pathognomonic feature manifests as the formation of an eschar at the bite site, typically presenting as a necrotic ulcer surrounded by an erythematous halo [4]. In severe cases, scrub typhus can lead to serious complications affecting multiple organ systems, including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), meningoencephalitis, pericarditis, pneumonitis, acute renal failure, myocarditis, and disseminated intravascular coagulation [5], with untreated mortality rates reaching 30% [6]. Notably, while meningitis, cerebellar dysfunction, and parkinsonism have been reported, other neurological complications, such as generalized myoclonus, are scarcely documented [7]. This study describes the first documented case of scrub typhus presenting with generalized myoclonus status and progressing to multiorgan dysfunction syndrome (MODS), offering novel insights into neurotropic manifestations of Orientia tsutsugamushi infection while highlighting therapeutic complexities in critical care management.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old Asian male with schizophrenia (maintained on olanzapine 10 mg daily) presented to our hospital with a 7-day history of generalized myoclonic jerks and progressive functional decline. Notably, he had self-discontinued olanzapine 7 days prior due to exacerbation of hyperkinetic movements. The patient reported no substance use (tobacco/alcohol) or recent travel.

Seven days before admission, the patient began experiencing persistent involuntary jerking, characterized by regularity, brevity, rapidity, and synchrony across all four limbs (predominantly the upper extremities). The synchronous limb jerking was repetitive, with each short-lived jerk clearly distinguishable. The patient maintained a bedbound posture with flexed upper limbs and extended lower limbs. These attacks were continuous and unremitting, lasting for a week, and the symptoms gradually worsened, severely disrupting the patient’s sleep and activities of daily living. Throughout the episodes, he retained full consciousness with symptom exacerbation during emotional tension and excitement. Additionally, the patient presented with hallucinations and delusions. Assistance was required for basic activities such as walking, eating, urinating, and defecating. The patient denied coughing, dysphagia with oral intake, or urinary/fecal incontinence. Three days prior to admission, the patient developed a fever (peak 39 °C), cough, gradually decreased urine output, and anorexia. The patient’s symptoms worsened one day prior admission, so he was taken to the local hospital. The following test results were obtained: blood glucose 19.54 mmol/L, carbon dioxide 14.9 mmol/L, urea 24.82 mmol/L, creatinine 461.3 µmol/L, glutamic aminotransferase 269.0 U/L, and D-dimer 2.69 mg/L. Neuroimaging (brain CT) demonstrated chronic small vessel disease (periventricular lacunar infarcts) without acute ischemia. Chest CT revealed bilateral lower lobe infection. Despite treatment with cefotaxime tazobactam sodium, the patient’s symptoms persisted.

Upon admission, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: respiratory rate of 28 bpm, temperature of 37.3℃, heart rate of 96 bpm, and blood pressure of 107/66 mmHg. Oxygen saturation instability (92–100%) with limb movements. Pulmonary auscultation revealed bibasilar crackles. On examination, the patient was lying supine in bed, with bilateral upper limbs flexed, lower limbs extended, and persistent synchronous rhythmic jerking of all four extremities at a frequency of 2–3 Hz. The jerking of the upper limbs was more pronounced than the lower limbs, and there was also an involuntary facial tremor. Additionally, the patient had conjunctival injection, slightly sluggish pupillary light reflexes bilaterally, and nuchal rigidity. The patient was unable to speak or cooperate with the physical examination.

Laboratory examinations revealed metabolic acidosis accompanied by rapidly escalating blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels, consistent with severe acute kidney injury. A rapid decline in platelet count was indicative of severe thrombocytopenia, while critically low serum albumin levels suggested substantial endothelial damage. Hepatic dysfunction was evidenced through moderate elevations in gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase, and alanine aminotransferase. Biomarker analysis revealed acute myocardial injury evidenced by elevated levels of cardiac troponin, myoglobin, and creatine kinase-MB levels. Additional findings included moderately increased lactate dehydrogenase, marginally prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and mild hypocalcemia, with sodium and potassium concentrations remaining within physiologic parameters (Table 1). Microbiological workup yielded negative results in sputum/blood cultures, 1,3-β-D-glucan assay, and serological tests for syphilis, HIV, as well as hepatitis B/C viruses. Despite a negative Weil-Felix test, the identification of an eschar prompted clinical suspicion for scrub typhus. Confirmatory targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid detected Orientia tsutsugamushi genetic material alongside suspected colonization by the Gram-positive bacterium Enterococcus faecium. On hospital day 9, the patient’s gastric fluid appeared slightly dark red, and a positive occult blood test suggested stress-related gastropathy.

Multidisciplinary supportive care was concurrently administered, encompassing maintenance of homeostasis, platelet transfusion, proton pump inhibitor-based gastric protection, and hepatoprotective therapy. Following the rescue treatment, clinical improvement was observed manifested by improved breathing and oxygenation and gradual healing of the eschar in the left scrotum on hospital day 7 (see Fig. 1B).

The patient with schizophrenia infected scrub typhus and developed multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. They required long-term comprehensive antibiotic treatment and intensive care. Even with continuous treatment, the coexistence of neurological and psychiatric complications and the unstable multiple organ function made a full functional recovery unlikely. Considering the poor prognosis of the patient and the unbearable burden of long-term treatment, rehabilitation and care, the family eventually chose to discontinue the treatment.

Discussion

This report delineates a diagnostically challenging case of scrub typhus in a 72-year-old male presenting with Orientia tsutsugamushi-induced generalized myoclonus status (persistent limb myoclonus with preserved consciousness) and MODS, ultimately confirmed through tNGS of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. The patient experienced multiple complications, including renal failure, septic shock, respiratory failure, thrombocytopenia, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, impaired liver function, hypoalbuminemia, and myocardial injury.

Eschar is highly specific for scrub typhus (98.9%), though its reported prevalence varies significantly, ranging from 7.0% to 97% [4, 8]. Patients with fever should be thought of this disease and carry out targeted comprehensive physical examination as soon as possible. This diagnostic variability necessitates a multimodal testing strategy, combining initial serological assay—such as the Weil-Felix test, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), immunofluorescence assay, and rapid immunochromatographic tests. Weil-Felix accuracy is not ideal, although it has been widely used in resource-limited settings due to its short turnaround time and low testing cost [9, 10]. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) tests extensive DNA/RNA from a sample to detect unexpected pathogens, and tNGS enriches specific genetic targets, including particular pathogens, offer improved diagnostic precision [11, 12]. To date, few scrub typhus cases have been confirmed by tNGS from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid.

Following transmission via mite bite, Orientia tsutsugamushi exhibits a 7–10 day incubation period prior to symptom onset [13]. The common symptoms of scrub typhus are diverse and can mimic other febrile illnesses. Fever is often one of the earliest and most prominent symptoms, and it can be accompanied by lymphadenopathy, headache, nausea, conjunctival injection, vomiting, myalgias, diarrhea or constipation [14]. Scrub typhus can affect multiple organ systems, commonly presenting with respiratory complications. Pulmonary involvement was documented in 61.5% of cases in a Thai case series (n = 130) [15]. Radiographic abnormalities were detected in up to 64.6% of patients upon initial evaluation, with bilateral reticular opacities being most prevalent (48.5%), followed by air space nodules (13.1%) and pleural effusion (10.8%) [15]. While acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) remains an uncommon complication (11.1% incidence in a retrospective cohort of 72 patients), it carries significant mortality risk, with a case fatality rate of 25% reported in affected individuals [16]. Concurrent cardiac manifestations in scrub typhus including cardiomegaly (28.5%) and congestive heart failure (18.5%), were also observed [15]. Electrocardiographic abnormalities, including ST-segment deviation, QT interval prolongation, and T wave inversion correlate with disease severity and myocardial inflammation [17, 18]. Gastrointestinal manifestations typically emerge 3–7 days post-fever onset in scrub typhus infections. The disease may mimic acute abdominal syndrome without identifiable surgical cause, frequently presenting with diarrhea, acalculous cholecystitis, gastrointestinal bleeding, pancreatitis [19]. Hepatic dysfunction is particularly prevalent, evidenced by elevated alanine aminotransferase (64–92%), aspartate aminotransferase (78–89%), bilirubin (16–38%), and alkaline phosphatase levels (27–84%) [20, 21]. Abdominal pain, indigestion, abdominal tenderness, nausea/vomiting, and splenomegaly are routinely documented in clinical observations [5, 19]. Acute kidney injury represents a frequently overlooked yet prognostically significant complication of scrub typhus, serving as an independent mortality predictor [22]. A retrospective Indian cohort study (n = 49) revealed renal involvement in 82% of scrub typhus cases, with acute kidney injury incidence reaching 53% [23]. Hematological complications of scrub typhus commonly encompass disseminated intravascular coagulation [24], haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis [25], monoclonal gammopathy [26], and persistent thrombocytopenia [27]. Endocrine manifestations may involve adrenal insufficiency [28] and subacute thyroiditis [29]. Notably, rare clinical presentations, such as digital gangrene, are being increasingly reported in recent literature [30].

Neurological complications occur in approximately 20% of scrub typhus cases, with rare manifestations demonstrating substantially lower incidence [31]. The most common clinical features reported in neurological involvement of scrub typhus were fevers (100.0%), altered sensorium (67.4%), headache (65.0%) and neck stiffness (55.6%). seizures(43.8%) [32]. Adult patients predominantly exhibit headache and neck stiffness, whereas altered sensorium and seizures represent hallmark pediatric presentations [32]. Orientia tsutsugamushi accounts for approximately 25% of acute encephalitic syndromes in India and 18% of all bacterial central nervous infections in Laos [33, 34]. Meningitis cases typically manifest with headache, neck stiffness, vomiting, and altered sensorium. Encephalitic presentations primarily feature altered sensorium and seizures [35, 36]. Seizure prevalence ranges from 6.3 to 21.6%, with status epilepticus was observed in 15% of cases in a prospective hospital-based observational study [14, 37]. Case reports document opsoclonus with/without myoclonus in scrub typhus [38, 39]. Misra et al. described a case of scrub typhus with stimulus-sensitive myoclonus [40]. Additionally, generalized convulsive status epilepticus with cortical multifocal myoclonus has been documented in a 23-year-old female [41]. In scrub typhus-associated cranial neuropathies, the abducens nerve (CN VI) is most frequently involved, with reported cases of optic (CN II), oculomotor (CN III), and vestibulocochlear (CN VIII) nerve involvement [14, 42]. Bilateral facial nerve (VII) palsy may occur [43]. Approximately one-third of scrub typhus patients develop acute reversible hearing loss [44]. Less common but well-documented neurological sequelae include: cerebral infarction [45], subdural hemorrhage, intracerebral hemorrhage [46], cerebral venous thrombosis [47], post-infectious transverse myelitis and longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis [48, 49], acute disseminated encephalomyelitis [50], cerebellitis [51], Guillain-Barré syndrome [52], brachial plexopathy [53], Parkinson’s syndrome [7, 54, 55], and psychiatric symptoms are less well described but well established clinical entities [56].

During evolution, Orientia tsutsugamushi has developed a refined obligate intracellular lifestyle, exhibiting tropism for both endothelial cells and professional phagocytes. Histopathological analysis of murine cortex and cerebellum revealed marked immune cell infiltration (predominantly T cells and macrophages) and concurrent activation of brain-resident microglia and astrocytes during the infection peak [57].The study reveals significant enrichment of immune signalling and inflammatory pathways in brain during Orientia tsutsugamushi infection. Genes exhibiting the most pronounced upregulation are associated with interferon responses, immunoglobulin-mediated immunity, defence responses to bacteria, IL-6/JAK-STAT signalling, and TNF signalling via NF-κB. In severe Orientia tsutsugamushi infections, core genes linked to blood-brain barrier disruption and dysregulation demonstrate markedly elevated expression, with microglial cells acting as both replication sites for Orientia tsutsugamushi and key contributors to pro-inflammatory gene expression [58]. In a series of 25 patients who underwent lumbar puncture in the absence of overt central nervous system (CNS) signs, 48% exhibited cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities with mild mononuclear pleocytosis, while Orientia tsutsugamushi DNA was detected via PCR in 24%, demonstrating subclinical neuroinvasion is much more common [59].

Myoclonus manifests as sudden, brief, involuntary muscular contractions (positive myoclonus) or inhibitions (negative myoclonus), classifiable into four anatomical patterns: focal, multifocal, segmental, and generalized [60]. The etiological spectrum of myoclonus is vast and diverse. Pathological myoclonus is observed in the context of cerebrovascular disorders, neurodegenerative disorders, acute and chronic post-hypoxic states, epileptic disorders, para-infectious or postinfectious states, autoimmune disorders, mitochondrial disorders, metabolic disorders, and congenital disorders [60].

Upon admission, the patient exhibited generalized myoclonic jerks affecting all four limbs and facial muscles upon awakening, persisting for seven days. This condition was considered to be generalized myoclonic status. Myoclonic status generally involves prolonged or frequent myoclonic seizures, which are typically associated with comatose states in other conditions, making this presentation in the patient unusual. The occurrence of generalized myoclonic status as a complication of scrub typhus has never been reported in the medical literature. While scrub typhus is known for various systemic impacts, its direct association with such severe neurological involvement, especially in the form of generalized myoclonic status without coma, is not yet documented [5, 14].

The temporal association strongly suggests Orientia tsutsugamushi infection as the precipitating factor for the myoclonic status. While the precise cause of the myoclonus remains uncertain, We hypothesise that the myoclonus could be due to direct cortical hyperexcitability triggered by Orientia tsutsugamushi-induced neuroinflammatory cascades, resulting in status epilepticus. Concurrently, we postulate that generalized myoclonus status might originate from meningitis or meningoencephalitis caused by scrub typhus. This hypothesis aligns with literature documenting central nervous system infection is the main cause of scrub typhus-induced status epilepticus [37, 61]. Unfortunately, the patient’s family refused to allow the patient to undergo a lumbar puncture, the absence of cerebrospinal fluid analysis limits confirmation of meningitis or meningoencephalitis.

Another potential cause of generalised myoclonus is hypoxaemia. Upon admission, the patient’s oxygen saturation varied between 92% and 100%. Transient desaturation may have been related to motion artefact due to limb shaking, which disrupted sensor adherence. Although oxygen saturation decreased on the second day, necessitating emergency endotracheal intubation due to clinical deterioration, wihle the myoclonus had already persisted for one week prior to admission, during which oxygen saturation remained stable. Consequently, prolonged hypoxia was deemed an unlikely cause.

Uremia represents another potential cause of myoclonus. One day prior to admission, laboratory tests at a local hospital revealed elevated creatinine (461.3 μmol/L) and urea (24.82 mmol/L) levels. Although urine output was maintained at approximately 80 mL/h on the first day after admission, it declined markedly on the second day. We surmised that the patient's renal insufficiency underwent an acute exacerbation three days prior to admission, as the patient manifested a gradual decrease in urine output. However, as the myoclonus had already been present for one week during which urine output remained normal, uremia-induced myoclonus was not considered at that time.

Electrolyte abnormalities, such as hypocalcaemia or hypomagnesaemia, may also increase neuronal excitability and lead to involuntary muscle contractions. On the second day, the patient’s serum calcium was slightly reduced (1.95 mmol/L), although magnesium levels were within normal range. For this reason, ion imbalance-related myoclonus was initially considered less likely.

Patients with myoclonus status caused by scrub typhus may have better outcomes than those with myoclonus status from other CNS infections, potentially due to more effective antibiotic response. Doxycycline, tetracycline, azithromycin, and chloramphenicol are the most common antibiotics for treating scrub typhus [62]. Initially, delayed recognition of disease severity by the family members led to clinical deterioration progressing to MODS. However, after the patient was admitted to the hospital, he received accurate treatment, and his condition gradually improved. Regrettably, due to financial burden and limited rehabilitative care capacity constraints, his family made the difficult decision to give up treatment.

Conclusion

In endemic regions, clinicians should maintain heightened clinical suspicion for scrub typhus when evaluating patients with acute febrile illness accompanied by neurological manifestations. We report the case of a patient with scrub typhus who presented with generalized myoclonic status and MODS to offer guidance for the diagnosis and management of similar cases. Managing scrub typhus with MODS requires a comprehensive approach encompassing pathogenic treatment of Orientia tsutsugamushi and multidisciplinary critical care support for systemic complications.
