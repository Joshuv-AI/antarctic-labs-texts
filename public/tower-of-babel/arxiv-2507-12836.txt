Quantum geometrical bound relations for observables Preprint: APS/123-QED
Koki Shinada, Naoto Nagaosa

Abstract
The quantum geometric tensor (QGT) provides nontrivial bound relations among physical quantities, as exemplified by the metric-curvature inequality. In this paper, we investigate various bound relations for different observables through certain generalizations of the QGT. First, by generalizing the parameter space, we demonstrate that bound relations hold for all linear responses. As an application, we show the thermodynamic inequality originating from the convexity of free energy can be further tightened. Second, by extending the projection operator, we establish a bound relation between the Drude weight and the orbital magnetization. The equality is exactly satisfied in the Landau level system, and systems with nearly flat bands tend to approach equality as well. We apply the resulting inequality to two orbital ferromagnets and support that the twisted bilayer graphene system is close to the Landau level system. Moreover, we show that an analogous inequality also holds for a higher-order multipole, magnetic quadrupole. Finally, we discuss the analogy between the QGT and the uncertainty principle, emphasizing that the existence of nontrivial bound relations necessarily reflects quantum effects.



I
Introduction

Bound relations expressed as inequalities provide fundamental insights in physics.
The second law of thermodynamics, which expresses the principle of entropy increase, and the uncertainty principle in quantum mechanics are representative examples, both constituting fundamental inequalities underpinning their respective theoretical frameworks.

The significance of bound relations is increasingly being recognized in condensed matter physics as well.
In particular, these are closely related to the quantum geometry, describing the geometric structure of the Bloch wavefunctions in periodic crystals on a parameter space, such as the momentum space.
The bound relation between the quantum metric and the Berry curvature serves as a representative example
Roy 2014
;
Peotta and Törmä 2015
;
Ozawa and Mera 2021
: the former is a Riemannian metric capturing local geometric structure, while the latter is a topological quantity characterizing global geometric features, and the bound highlights that the two geometric quantities mutually constrain each other.
From a physical perspective, the former represents the spatial spread of the wavefunction in real space
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
;
Resta and Sorella 1999
, while the latter reflects the non-commutativity of position operators in a crystal. This inequality can thus be regarded as an uncertainty principle in crystalline systems.
Moreover, in systems with strong orbital hybridization, such as topological insulators, the wavefunction is expected to spread more widely in real space
Thonhauser and Vanderbilt 2006
;
Brouder et al. 2007
. The inequality is thus consistent with this physical expectation.
This localization length is also related to the band gap of insulators, and related inequalities have been actively studied
Souza et al. 2000
;
Onishi and Fu 2024a
;
Onishi and Fu 2024b
;
Onishi and Fu 2025
;
Souza et al. 2025
.
Furthermore, bound relations involving other topological quantities have also been investigated
Xie et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2023
;
Kwon and Yang 2024
;
Jankowski et al. 2025
;
Yu et al. 2024
.

Inequalities themselves are essential relations by providing limitations to the magnitude of physical quantities; however, the conditions for equality are equally critical.
The aforementioned metric–curvature inequality attains equality in the Landau level system
Ozawa and Mera 2021
. Accordingly, the condition for equality, together with the uniformity of the Berry curvature in momentum space, provides a criterion for assessing the similarity to Landau levels
Roy 2014
;
Jackson et al. 2015
;
Claassen et al. 2015
;
Lee et al. 2017
;
Ozawa and Mera 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
.
Furthermore, the condition for equality, referred to as vortexability
Ledwith et al. 2020
;
Ledwith et al. 2023
;
Dong et al. 2023
;
Fujimoto et al. 2025
, constitutes a sufficient condition for realizing fractional quantum Hall states (FQHS) in flat-band systems. Notably, it does not require uniform Berry curvature, indicating that FQHS can be formed even in systems that are not exact analogs of Landau levels.

In this way, useful discussions on inequalities are being explored from the perspective of quantum geometry.
In particular, the quantum geometric tensor (QGT) plays a crucial role in the metric-curvature bound relation, which originates from the semipositive definiteness of the QGT.
In recent years, generalizations of QGT have been explored.
For instance, the time-dependent QGT is connected to certain sum rules associated with the electrical conductivity tensor
Komissarov et al. 2024
;
Verma and Queiroz 2025a
;
Verma and Queiroz 2025b
.
In addition, a QGT that connects the Drude weight and the orbital magnetization has been proposed
Resta 2017
.
Furthermore, the extension to higher-order geometric quantities such as connection and its relation with nonlinear responses have recently been discussed
Mitscherling et al. 2025
;
Avdoshkin et al. 2025
.
An important question is how far the QGT can be generalized, what physical quantities it can encompass, and what kinds of constraints it imposes on the physical quantities it describes.

In this paper, we discuss several aspects of the generalization of the QGT. Furthermore, we identify the physical quantities described by the QGT and examine the bound relations among them.
In Sec.
II
, we generalize the parameter space beyond the Bloch wavenumber space and demonstrate that bound relations can be obtained for all linear responses.
We present some applications and discuss an inequality that is tighter than the thermodynamic bound as an example.
In Sec.
III
, we generalize the projection operator, provide a detailed analysis of the bound relation between the Drude weight and orbital magnetization, and we analyze the condition of equality using some models. We further show that an analogous bound relation holds for a higher-order multipole, spin magnetic quadrupole. Moreover, we elucidate the connection of this bound relation to the uncertainty principle, highlighting the essential role of quantum effects.

II
bound relations in linear response theory

II.1
Proof of inequalities

We consider a system described by a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
with a (discrete) translation symmetry and a time translation symmetry.
Here, we consider a general Hamiltonian without assuming whether the system consists of fermions or bosons, or whether it is interacting or non-interacting.
Then, a linear response function is written, using the Lehmann representation with a wavenumber
\bm{q}
, as

\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}=-\frac{1}{V}\sum_{nm}\frac{\rho_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}+E_{nm}}O^{i}_{nm}(\bm{q})O^{j}_{mn}(-\bm{q}).

(1)

Here,
\rho_{n}=e^{-\beta(E_{n}-\mu N_{n})}/\Xi
is the grand canonical distribution with the energy
E_{n}
and the particle number
N_{n}
for a microstate
\ket{n}
, and
\Xi=\sum_{n}e^{-\beta(E_{n}-\mu N_{n})}
is the grand partition function.

\mu
and
\beta
are a chemical potential and an inverse temperature, respectively.

O^{i}_{nm}(\bm{q})=\braket{n|\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{q})|m}
is the transition matrix of a second-quantized operator
\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{q})=\int_{V}d\bm{r}\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{r})e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}
. Because a local operator
\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{r})
is Hermitian, the identity
\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{q})^{\dagger}=\hat{O}^{i}(-\bm{q})
is satisfied.
In addition, we impose the commutativity with the particle number operator on the operator
\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{r})
.
In this case, the particle number is not changed by the operator
\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{r})
, and thus, we get
N_{n}=N_{m}
.

V
is the volume of the system.
We use some abbreviations:
\rho_{nm}=\rho_{n}-\rho_{m}
,
E_{nm}=E_{n}-E_{m}
, and
\tilde{\omega}=\omega+i\delta
, where
\delta=+0
is the adiabatic factor.

The absorptive part of this tensor is given by

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathrm{abs.}}_{O^{i}O^{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}[\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}-(\Phi_{O^{j}O^{i}})^{*}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Im}\Phi^{\mathrm{(S)}}_{O^{i}O^{j}}-i\mathrm{Re}\Phi^{(\mathrm{AS})}_{O^{i}O^{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{V}\sum_{nm}\rho_{nm}O^{i}_{nm}(\bm{q})O^{j}_{mn}(-\bm{q})\delta(\omega+E_{nm}).

(2)

This tensor is Hermitian by definition.
(S) and (AS) are the symmetric part and antisymmetric part for the interchange of indices
i
,
j
.
In the regime of non-negative frequency
\omega\geq 0
, this part has the semipositive definiteness, i.e., its eigenvalues are semipositive, as shown in the following.
There,
E_{nm}\leq 0
leads to
\rho_{nm}\geq 0
, and for any complex vector
\bm{v}
, this tensor satisfies

v_{i}^{*}\Phi^{\mathrm{abs.}}_{O^{i}O^{j}}v_{j}=\frac{\pi}{V}\sum_{nm}\rho_{nm}|\tilde{v}|^{2}\delta(\omega+E_{nm})\geq 0.

(3)

Here, we define a complex value
\tilde{v}=O^{j}_{mn}(-\bm{q})v_{j}
.
The integral of this tensor divided by
\omega^{\alpha}
over the range
\omega=+0
to
\infty

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathscr{P}\frac{\Phi^{\mathrm{abs.}}_{O^{i}O^{j}}}{\omega^{\alpha}}d\omega=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{V}\sum_{E_{n}<E_{m}}\frac{\rho_{nm}}{(E_{mn})^{\alpha}}O^{i}_{nm}(\bm{q})O^{j}_{mn}(-\bm{q})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle M^{\alpha}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q})

(4)

also satisfies semipositive definiteness.

\alpha
is taken to be an integer in the following discussion.

\mathscr{P}
denotes the principal value of the integral, meaning that
\omega=0
is excluded from the integration range.
This integral yields one of the sum rules of linear responses.
We note that the semipositive definiteness is maintained even if the frequency integral is restricted to a certain range of non-negative frequencies.

This semipositive definiteness leads to some nontrivial inequalities between the real and imaginary parts of this tensor
M^{\alpha}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q})
.
Because eigenvalues of the semipositive matrix are not negative, resulting in
\mathrm{det}M^{\alpha}(\bm{q})\geq 0
,
if we choose two operators
\hat{O}^{1}
and
\hat{O}^{2}
and construct a
2\times 2
matrix composed of these operators,
the semipositive definiteness gives an inequality

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathrm{det}[\mathrm{Re}M^{\alpha}(\bm{q})]}\geq|\mathrm{Im}M^{\alpha}_{O^{1}O^{2}}(\bm{q})|.

(5)

Furthermore, the real part itself is semipositive, thus the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means gives

\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}[\mathrm{Re}M^{\alpha}(\bm{q})]\geq\sqrt{\mathrm{det}[\mathrm{Re}M^{\alpha}(\bm{q})]}.

(6)

These inequalities provide bound relations between various linear response functions.

Finally, we comment on the constraints imposed by time-reversal symmetry.
Onsager reciprocal theorem states

\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\omega,\bm{q},\bm{B})=\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}\Phi_{O^{j}O^{i}}(\omega,-\bm{q},-\bm{B}),

(7)

where
\varepsilon_{i}(=\pm 1)
is the sign determined by the time-reversal operation
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
and is defined by
\hat{\mathcal{T}}\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{r})\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=\varepsilon_{i}\hat{O}^{i}(\bm{r})
.

\bm{B}
is a time-reversal symmetry (
\mathcal{T}

Therefore, either the real or the imaginary part of the off-diagonal parts at the limit
\bm{q}\to 0
,
M^{\alpha}_{O^{1}O^{2}}(0)
, becomes zero when
\mathcal{T}
-symmetry is preserved.

II.2
Relation with quantum geometric tensor

Considering the uniform limit
\bm{q}\to 0
at zero temperature
, the tensor
M^{\alpha}_{O^{i}O^{j}}
is related to a quantum geometric tensor (QGT) for a ground state on a general parameter space.
Here, the ground state at zero temperature
\ket{\mathrm{GS}}\equiv\ket{n=0}
is defined as a microstate satisfying the condition
E_{0}-\mu N_{0}<0
, i.e.,
\rho_{0}=1
, and it is assumed to be nondegenerate. Excited states
\ket{n\geq 1}
are defined as microstates satisfying the condition
E_{n}-\mu N_{n}>0
, i.e.,
\rho_{n}=0
.
We introduce a parameter
X_{i}
, which is conjugate to the operator
\hat{O}^{i}
.
It is included in a Hamiltonian by
X_{i}\hat{O}^{i}
.
Then, using the Hellmann–Feynman theorem, where
\braket{n|\partial\hat{H}/\partial X|m}=-E_{nm}\braket{n|\partial_{X}m}
for
n\neq m
,
the tensor can be rewritten by a QGT as

M^{\alpha}_{O^{i}O^{j}}=\frac{\pi}{V}\braket{\partial_{X_{i}}0|\hat{Q}(\hat{H}-E_{0})^{2-\alpha}\hat{Q}|\partial_{X_{j}}0}.

(8)

Here,
\hat{Q}=1-\hat{P}
, where
\hat{P}=\ket{0}\bra{0}
is a projection operator to the ground state.
Here, we assume
\braket{0|\hat{Q}|0}
= 0.

II.3
Thermodynamic inequality and tighter relation

As an application of inequalities derived above, we consider the case of
\alpha=1
and the uniform limit
\bm{q}\to 0
.
In this case, the real part of the tensor
M^{1}_{O^{i}Oj}(0)
corresponds to a static susceptibility
\chi_{O^{i}O^{j}}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}M^{1}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{V}\sum_{E_{n}<E_{m}}\frac{\rho_{nm}}{E_{nm}}\mathrm{Re}[O^{i}_{nm}O^{j}_{mn}]

\displaystyle\equiv\pi\chi_{O^{i}O^{j}}/2.

(9)

For instance, when the operator
\hat{O}^{i}
is a spin operator or an electric polarization operator, the above equation is a static spin susceptibility
\chi^{\mathrm{m}}_{ij}=-\partial^{2}\Omega/\partial h_{i}\partial h_{j}
or an electric polarizability
\chi^{\mathrm{e}}_{ij}=-\partial^{2}\Omega/\partial E_{i}\partial E_{j}
, respectively.
Here,
\Omega[\beta,\mu,X]=-(\beta V)^{-1}\ln\Xi[\beta,\mu,X]
is a grand potential density.
Note that the tensor
M^{\alpha}_{O^{i}O^{j}}
is defined for the absorptive part of linear response functions, while
\chi_{O^{i}O^{j}}
is a static susceptibility. They are related by the Kramers-Kronig relation (see Appendix
A
for details).

For general
\alpha
, the real part
\mathrm{Re}M^{\alpha}
itself is semipositive, thus
\det\mathrm{Re}M^{\alpha}\geq 0
.
In the present case, this inequality becomes a thermodynamic inequality originating from the convexity of the grand potential,

\chi_{O^{1}O^{1}}\chi_{O^{2}O^{2}}-(\chi_{O^{1}O^{2}})^{2}\geq 0.

(10)

However, we note that, due to the presence of the imaginary part, a tighter inequality can be obtained compared to this thermodynamic inequality,

\chi_{O^{1}O^{1}}\chi_{O^{2}O^{2}}-(\chi_{O^{1}O^{2}})^{2}\geq(2\mathrm{Im}M^{1}_{O^{1}O^{2}}(0)/\pi)^{2}.

(11)

In the following, we will discuss two examples.
When we set spin operators
\hat{S}^{1,2}
(here, the indices 1 and 2 denote one of the real-space coordinates,
x
,
y
, or
z
. The same applies to the following) as the operators
\hat{O}^{1,2}
,
we obtain an inequality

\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\chi^{\mathrm{m}}_{11}\chi^{\mathrm{m}}_{22}-(\chi^{\mathrm{m}}_{12})^{2}}\geq\Bigl|[\mathrm{Re}\chi^{\mathrm{m}(\mathrm{AS})}_{12}]^{1}\Bigr|.

(12)

Here, we use an abbreviation about a sum rule:
[A]^{\beta}=\int^{\infty}_{0}A(\omega)/\omega^{\beta}d\omega
.
The real part is a spin susceptibility, as introduced above. The imaginary part is the spectral summation of the antisymmetric part of the dynamical spin susceptibility
\chi^{\mathrm{m}}(\omega)
.
As discussed above, the imaginary part is finite only when
\mathcal{T}
-symmetry is broken, and it leads to the tighter inequality in Eq. (
11
).
If
\mathcal{T}
-symmetry is not broken, the tighter inequality is reduced to the thermodynamic inequality.
If
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}
, the same discussion is valid for other linear response functions.

For the other example, we set a polarization operator
\hat{P}
and a spin operator
\hat{S}
as the operators
\hat{O}^{1,2}
, respectively.
Here, although subscripts for
\hat{P}
and
\hat{S}
are omitted, one of the components along
x
,
y
, or
z
is implicitly selected. The same applies below.
In this case,
\varepsilon_{1}=-\varepsilon_{2}
,
and the resulting inequality is

\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\chi^{\mathrm{e}}\chi^{\mathrm{m}}-(\chi^{\mathrm{me(S)}})^{2}}\geq\Bigl|[\mathrm{Re}\chi^{\mathrm{me}(\mathrm{AS})}]^{1}\Bigr|.

(13)

Here,
\chi^{\mathrm{me(S)}}
is a static
\mathcal{T}
-odd magnetoelectric polarizability, and
\chi^{\mathrm{me(AS)}}(\omega)
is a
\mathcal{T}
-even optical magnetoelectric response function, which can be observed by optical activity.
While the thermodynamic bound on the magnetoelectric susceptibility derived from the inequality for the real part is well known
Brown et al. 1968
, here a tighter inequality is obtained due to the contribution of the imaginary part.

II.4
Some examples of inequality

We will discuss some examples of inequalities.
However, inequalities for all other linear responses and cross correlations are established and can be produced in the same manner as discussed below.

II.4.1
Case:
\hat{O}^{1}=\hat{J}^{1}
and
\hat{O}^{2}=\hat{J}^{2}

We choose the
i

\hat{J}^{i}
as the operator
\hat{O}^{i}
.
In the case of
\alpha=1
, the tensor corresponds to the spectral summation of optical conductivity
\sigma_{ij}(\omega)
.
The resulting inequality is

\frac{1}{8\pi}\sqrt{\omega^{2}_{\mathrm{p},11}\omega^{2}_{\mathrm{p},22}-\omega^{4}_{\mathrm{p},12}}\geq\Bigl|[\mathrm{Im}\sigma^{(\mathrm{AS})}_{12}]^{0}\Bigr|.

(14)

Here,
\omega_{\mathrm{p},ij}
is the plasma frequency matrix, and
\omega_{\mathrm{p},ij}=\sqrt{4\pi e^{2}n_{e}/m_{e}}\delta_{ij}
for a non-relativistic Hamiltonian, where
n_{e}
is a number density of electron and
m_{e}
is an electron mass.
The imaginary part is the sum rule of the magnetic circular dichroism.

In the case of
\alpha=3
, the real part is a static electric polarizability
\chi^{\mathrm{e}}_{ij}
and the imaginary part is the spectral summation of the optical Hall conductivity divided by
\omega^{2}

The resulting inequality is

\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\chi^{\mathrm{e}}_{11}\chi^{\mathrm{e}}_{22}-(\chi^{\mathrm{e}}_{12})^{2}}\geq\Bigl|[\mathrm{Im}\sigma^{(\mathrm{AS})}_{12}]^{2}\Bigr|.

(15)

These inequalities can be derived by using the time-dependent QGT for noninteracting systems
Komissarov et al. 2024
;
Verma and Queiroz 2025a
;
Verma and Queiroz 2025b
.

II.5
Noninteracting periodic system

In the following, we focus on noninteracting periodic and fermionic systems described by the Bloch states.
Throughout the following, all operators are represented in the first-quantization.
In this case, the tensor
M^{\alpha}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q})
reads

M^{\alpha}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q})=\pi\int_{\bm{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\\
<\epsilon_{m\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}\end{subarray}}\frac{f_{nm\bm{k},\bm{q}}}{(\epsilon_{mn\bm{k},\bm{q}})^{\alpha}}O^{i}_{nm}(\bm{q})O^{j}_{mn}(-\bm{q}),

(17)

where
O^{i}_{nm}(\bm{q})=\braket{u_{n\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}|\hat{O}^{i}|u_{m\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}}
,
f_{nm\bm{k},\bm{q}}=f_{n\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}-f_{m\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}
,
\epsilon_{mn\bm{k},\bm{q}}=\epsilon_{m\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}
, and
\int_{\bm{k}}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}d^{d}\bm{k}/(2\pi)^{d}
.
The notations introduced above are summarized below.
The Bloch Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}
with a Bloch wavenumber
\bm{k}
is diagonalized by the periodic part of a Bloch function
\ket{u_{n\bm{k}}}
with a band index
n
, and the eigenenergy is
\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}
.

f_{n\bm{k}}=1/(e^{\beta(\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\mu)}+1)
is the Fermi distribution function.

In the following discussion, we take the limit
\bm{q}\to 0
.
When taking the limit, we need to be careful about the intraband contribution (
n=m
), because the energy difference
\epsilon_{nn}(=0)
in the denominator can potentially cause a divergence.
In the case of
\alpha\geq 2
, this contribution causes a divergence.
Accordingly, to exclude this divergence, we consider only the interband contribution.
In the case of
\alpha\leq 1
, no singularities are caused.
In particular, the intraband contribution makes the Fermi surface term
\partial f_{n\bm{k}}/\partial\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}
appear with the factor
1/2
in the case of
\alpha=1
, and it becomes zero for other cases.
This factor of
1/2
arises because the contribution from the Fermi surface appears as an absorption spectrum at
\omega\to 0
, and only half of it contributes due to the integration being restricted to the positive frequency domain.
These remarks are summarized in the TABLE
3
.
We note that, for insulating systems with a finite excitation gap, the divergence problem is absent, and the tensor
M^{\alpha}_{O^{i}O^{j}}
can be fully expressed by the interband contribution.

In particular, considering an insulator, it has a finite gap, thus the eigenvalue of

III
Generalized quantum geometric tensor

In this section, we will study bound relations given by a generalized quantum geometric tensor (g-QGT).
The original QGT is defined by
Shapere and Wilczek 1989

\displaystyle Q^{ij}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{P}_{\bm{k}}(\partial_{k_{i}}\hat{P}_{\bm{k}})(\partial_{k_{j}}\hat{P}_{\bm{k}})]

\displaystyle=g^{ij}_{\bm{k}}-\frac{i}{2}\varepsilon_{kij}\Omega_{\bm{k}}^{k}.

(23)

The real part and the imaginary part of this QGT correspond to the quantum metric tensor
g_{\bm{k}}^{ij}
and the Berry curvature
\Omega_{\bm{k}}^{k}
, respectively. The semipositive definiteness of this QGT leads to the well-known bound relation between them
Roy 2014
;
Peotta and Törmä 2015
.

III.1
A generalized QGT with semipositive definiteness and inequalities

We introduce a g-QGT as

R^{ij}_{\bm{k}}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}(\partial_{\lambda_{i}}\hat{P}_{{\bm{k}}})(\partial_{\lambda_{j}}\hat{P}_{{\bm{k}}})].

(24)

Here,
\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}
is a Hermitian operator with semipositive definiteness, thus
\braket{v|\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}|v}\geq 0
for any state
\ket{v}
.
No assumptions other than these two properties of
\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}
are required in the following discussion.

\lambda
is a general parameter such as the Bloch wavenumber
\lambda=k
.
Then, we can prove that this g-QGT is also semipositive as

\displaystyle v^{*}_{i}R^{ij}_{\bm{k}}v_{j}=\sum_{v}\braket{v|\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}|v}\geq 0

(25)

for any complex vector
\bm{v}
.
After taking a sum over
\bm{k}
in the B.Z., the integrated tensor
R^{ij}(\equiv\int_{\bm{k}}R^{ij}_{\bm{k}})
is also semipositive in the same manner.
Then, if we consider the
2\times 2
matrix
R
, we obtain an inequality between real and imaginary parts as

\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}[\mathrm{Re}R]\geq\sqrt{\det[\mathrm{Re}R]}\geq|\mathrm{Im}R^{12}|.

(26)

If we choose the projection operator
\hat{P}_{\bm{k}}
as
\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}

Q^{ij}_{\bm{k}}

In the following, we will discuss another operator as
\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}

III.2
Interband Drude weight and orbital magnetization

We set
\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}=|\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\mu|\equiv(\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\mu)(1-2\hat{P}_{\bm{k}})
and
\lambda=k

Resta 2017
.
This operator is semipositive by definition.
The real part and imaginary part of the g-QGT given by this operator are

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}R^{ij}_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\mathrm{Re}\braket{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{j}}u_{n\bm{k}}},

(27a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}R^{ij}_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\mathrm{Im}\braket{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n\bm{k}}|2\mu-\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{j}}u_{n\bm{k}}}.

(27b)

The real part corresponds to the Eq. (

\alpha=1

A physical interpretation can be assigned to these parts

The real part is related to the Drude weight

The Drude weight can be decomposed into two terms as

D_{ij}=e^{2}\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\int_{\bm{k}}\frac{\partial^{2}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}=D_{ij}^{\mathrm{intra}}-D^{\mathrm{inter}}_{ij},

(28)

where
D^{\mathrm{intra}}_{ij}(=e^{2}\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\int_{\bm{k}}\braket{u_{n\bm{k}}|\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}|u_{n\bm{k}}}

For the first-principle Hamiltonian (
\hat{H}=\hat{\bm{p}}^{2}/2m_{e}+V(\hat{\bm{x}})

V(\hat{\bm{x}})
is a periodic potential),

On the other hand,

M^{i}_{\mathrm{orb}}=-\frac{e}{2}\varepsilon_{ijk}\mathrm{Im}R^{jk}.

(29)

Then, we obtain inequalities between the Drude weight and the orbital magnetization as

\frac{1}{2e}\sqrt{\det[D^{\mathrm{inter}}]}\geq|M_{\mathrm{orb}}|.

(30)

In ordinary metals, the Drude weight

is positive, then, the inequality in Eq. (

If focusing on particular bands of interest, e.g., using a tight binding model near a Fermi surface, the inequality in Eq. (

III.4
Relation with uncertainty principle

The inequality derived from the semipositive definiteness of the tensor
R^{ij}_{\bm{k}}
is mathematically equivalent to the uncertainty principle.
This tensor is regarded as an expectation value with respect to the probability-like function
\hat{O}_{\bm{k}}
, and is expressed as follows:

R^{ij}\equiv\braket{\hat{P}_{i}\hat{P}_{j}}_{O}.

(37)

For clarity, we omit
\bm{k}
and we use
\hat{P}_{i}=\partial_{\lambda_{i}}\hat{P}
. Regarding the real part, the diagonal part is a standard deviation of
\partial_{\lambda_{i}}\hat{P}
, and the off-diagonal part is the expectation value of the symmetric part as

\mathrm{Re}R^{ii}=\braket{(\hat{P}_{i})^{2}}_{O},~\mathrm{Re}R^{12}=\frac{1}{2}\braket{\{\hat{P}_{1},\hat{P}_{2}\}}_{O}.

(38)

The imaginary part represents the non-commutativity,

\mathrm{Im}R^{12}=\frac{1}{2i}\braket{[\hat{P}_{1},\hat{P}_{2}]}_{O}.

(39)

Here, we define the commutator
[\hat{A},\hat{B}]=\hat{A}\hat{B}-\hat{B}\hat{A}
and the anti-commutator
\{\hat{A},\hat{B}\}=\hat{A}\hat{B}+\hat{B}\hat{A}
.
Thus, the inequality in Eq. (
26
) leads to the Schrödinger-type uncertainty principle,

\braket{(\hat{P}_{1})^{2}}_{O}\braket{(\hat{P}_{2})^{2}}_{O}-\frac{1}{4}\braket{\{\hat{P}_{1},\hat{P}_{2}\}}^{2}_{O}\geq\frac{1}{4}|\braket{[\hat{P}_{1},\hat{P}_{2}]}_{O}|^{2}.

(40)

This demonstrates that the imaginary part of the g-QGT in Eq. (
39
) stems from the non-commutativity of the operators and cannot be finite in classical systems, becoming finite solely as a result of quantum effects.
For example, the fact that orbital magnetization vanishes in thermal equilibrium in classical systems is known as the Bohr-van Leeuwen theorem, and this is consistent with the necessity of non-commutativity for orbital magnetization because it is the imaginary part of the g-QGT in Eq. (
29
).
Moreover, it is precisely the presence of this non-commutativity that ensures the left-hand side in Eq. (
40
) is bounded to a finite value, making this inequality a manifestation of quantum effects.

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we have discussed various bound relations between physical quantities by generalizing the quantum geometric tensor (QGT).
First, we have extended the parameter space from the Bloch wavenumber to more general spaces including external parameters such as a magnetic field, and have demonstrated that bound relations exist for all linear response functions.
We have presented several examples, and have shown that one of the resulting inequalities can be tighter than the conventional thermodynamic bounds.

Second, we have generalized the notion of the projection operator and have performed model calculations to examine in detail the bound relation between the Drude weight and orbital magnetization.
In the Landau level system, the equality holds, similarly to the metric-curvature inequality.
However, it should be noted that due to the contribution from edge states, the orbital magnetization can exhibit dependence on the chemical potential even within the bulk gap.
The equality holds when the edge states are either fully occupied or completely empty.
Furthermore, by analyzing models in which the flatness of the band can be tuned, we found that as the band becomes flatter, the inequality approaches equality.
Importantly, the obtained inequality can be evaluated using only simple and static physical quantities — the electron
n_{e}
and orbital magnetization
M_{\mathrm{orb}}
— and they are more accessible to experimental estimation than the quantum metric obtained by a sum rule.
While the inequality is not exact when focusing on low-energy bands near the Fermi surface, in such cases Eq. (
30
) should be used.
Nonetheless, it is expected to capture the characteristic features of the electronic states in orbital ferromagnets.
Indeed, we evaluated the inequality for a TBG and a kagome metal, and found that in the TBG case, it is very close to the equality.
This supports the view that the electronic states in TBG are close to those of the Landau level system.
It would be interesting to apply this analysis to other materials with orbital magnetism, such as rhombohedral graphene or twisted
\mathrm{MoTe_{2}}
.
In addition, we have shown that a similar inequality also holds for a spin magnetic quadrupole.
Finally, we have discussed the analogy between the QGT and the uncertainty principle.
The imaginary part of the QGT becomes finite due to operator non-commutativity, while it vanishes in the classical limit.
Moreover, the fact that the nontrivial inequalities are bounded to finite values can also be traced to this mechanism, thereby explicitly demonstrating that they are manifestations of quantum effects.

Appendix A
Kramers-Kronig relation and sum rule

The linear response function

\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega)=-\frac{1}{V}\sum_{nm}\frac{\rho_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}+E_{nm}}O^{i}_{nm}(\bm{q})O^{j}_{mn}(-\bm{q})

(41)

is analytic in the upper half of the complex
\omega
-plane (
\mathrm{Im}\omega\geq 0
),
and the Kramers-Kronig relation holds.
The Kramers-Kronig relation connects the real and imaginary parts of the linear response function,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\mathscr{P}\frac{\mathrm{Im}\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}-\omega}d\omega^{\prime},

(42a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{-1}{\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\mathscr{P}\frac{\mathrm{Re}\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}-\omega}d\omega^{\prime}.

(42b)

In the following, we focus only on the even part of
\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega)
for the wavenumber
\bm{q}
, since our primary interest lies in the
\bm{q}\to 0
limit.
We denote this part with a superscript
(\mathrm{even})
.
The linear response function has a symmetry

\Phi^{*}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega)=\Phi_{O^{i}O^{j}}(-\bm{q},-\omega).

(43)

Thus, for the even part
\Phi^{\mathrm{(even)}}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega)
, the real part and the imaginary part are even and odd functions of
\omega
, respectively.
Therefore, using Eq. (
42a
) at the
\omega\to 0
limit, we obtain

\mathrm{Re}\Phi^{\mathrm{(even)}}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},0)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathscr{P}\frac{\mathrm{Im}\Phi^{\mathrm{(even)}}_{O^{i}O^{j}}(\bm{q},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}}d\omega^{\prime}.

(44)

Here, we use the fact that the integrand is an even function of
\omega
.
Taking the limit
\bm{q}\to 0
of this expression yields exactly Eq. (
9
), and the sum rule yields the static susceptibility.
On the other hand, the sum rule for the real part of the response function does not give the static susceptibility, due to the opposite parity in
\omega
.

B
is a uniform magnetic field.
In this system, the ordinary translation symmetry is absent; however, it has a magnetic translation symmetry.
The magnetic unit cell is a rectangle with the length of
x
-direction
a_{x}
and the one of
y
-direction
a_{y}
, satisfying
|B|a_{x}a_{y}=2\pi

The magnetic symmetry guarantees the application of the Bloch theorem, and we can define the Bloch wavefunction
\psi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{k})=e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
, where
\bm{k}
lives in the magnetic Brillouin Zone:
[-a_{x}/\pi,a_{x}/\pi)\otimes[-a_{y}/\pi,a_{y}/\pi)
.
We leave the detailed calculations to Ref.
Ozawa and Mera 2021
and list the necessary formulas here:

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{u}_{n\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{n\bm{k}}}=a_{y}l_{B}\sqrt{\pi}2^{n}n!,

(46a)

\displaystyle\frac{\braket{\tilde{u}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}|\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{n\bm{k}}}}{\braket{\tilde{u}_{n\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{n\bm{k}}}}=-il_{B}[(n+1)\delta_{n+1,n^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n-1,n^{\prime}}]+\frac{ik_{y}}{B}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}},

(46b)

\displaystyle\frac{\braket{\tilde{u}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}|\partial_{y}\tilde{u}_{n\bm{k}}}}{\braket{\tilde{u}_{n\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{n\bm{k}}}}=-\frac{1}{l_{B}B}[-(n+1)\delta_{n+1,n^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n-1,n^{\prime}}].

(46c)

Here,
l_{B}=1/\sqrt{|B|}
is the magnetic length and
\epsilon_{n}=|B|/m_{e}(n+1/2)~(n\geq 0)
is the eigenenergy.
First, the real part of the g-QGT can be rewritten by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}R^{ij}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\mathrm{Re}\braket{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{j}}u_{n\bm{k}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\sum_{m:\mathrm{unocc}}\epsilon_{mn\bm{k}}\mathrm{Re}\braket{\partial_{i}u_{n\bm{k}}|u_{m\bm{k}}}\braket{u_{m\bm{k}}|\partial_{j}u_{n\bm{k}}}.

(47)

Using the above formulas, the real part is

\mathrm{Re}Q^{ij}_{\bm{k}}=(\epsilon_{r\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{r-1\bm{k}})\mathrm{Re}\biggl(\frac{\braket{\partial_{i}\tilde{u}_{r-1\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{r\bm{k}}}\braket{\tilde{u}_{r\bm{k}}|\partial_{j}\tilde{u}_{r-1\bm{k}}}}{\braket{\tilde{u}_{r-1\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{r-1\bm{k}}}\braket{\tilde{u}_{r\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{r\bm{k}}}}\biggr).

(48)

Each component is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}Q^{xx}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{|B|}{m_{e}}(l_{B}r)^{2}\frac{2^{r-1}(r-1)!}{2^{r}r!}=\frac{r}{2m_{e}},

(49a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}Q^{yy}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{|B|}{m_{e}}\Bigl(\frac{r}{l_{B}B}\Bigr)^{2}\frac{2^{r-1}(r-1)!}{2^{r}r!}=\frac{r}{2m_{e}},

(49b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}Q^{xy}_{\bm{k}}=\mathrm{Re}Q^{yx}_{\bm{k}}=0.

(49c)

Second, the imaginary part of the g-QGT can be rewritten by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}R^{ij}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\mathrm{Im}\braket{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n\bm{k}}|2\mu-\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{j}}u_{n\bm{k}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\sum_{m:\mathrm{unocc}}(2\mu-\epsilon_{m\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}})\mathrm{Im}\braket{\partial_{i}u_{n\bm{k}}|u_{m\bm{k}}}\braket{u_{m\bm{k}}|\partial_{j}u_{n\bm{k}}}.

(50)

Using the above formulas, it can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}Q^{xy}_{\bm{k}}=

\displaystyle(2\mu-\epsilon_{r\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{r-1\bm{k}})\mathrm{Im}\biggl(\frac{\braket{\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{r-1\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{r\bm{k}}}\braket{\tilde{u}_{r\bm{k}}|\partial_{y}\tilde{u}_{r-1\bm{k}}}}{\braket{\tilde{u}_{r-1\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{r-1\bm{k}}}\braket{\tilde{u}_{r\bm{k}}|\tilde{u}_{r\bm{k}}}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Bigl(2\mu-\frac{2|B|}{m_{e}}r\Bigr)\frac{r^{2}l_{B}}{l_{B}B}\frac{2^{r-1}(r-1)!}{2^{r}r!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{r\mu}{B}+\frac{r^{2}}{m_{e}}\mathrm{sign}(B).

(51)

Similar to the quantum metric and the Berry curvature, these quantities are uniform across the Brillouin zone in the Landau level system.
Then, integrating these quantities over the magnetic Brillouin zone, we obtain the interband Drude weight and the orbital magnetization as

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{inter}}_{xx}=D^{\mathrm{inter}}_{yy}=\int_{\mathrm{B.Z.}}\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}2\mathrm{Re}R^{xx(yy)}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{r|B|}{2\pi m_{e}},~~~D_{xy}^{\mathrm{inter}}=D_{yx}^{\mathrm{inter}}=0,

(52a)

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{orb}}=-\int_{\mathrm{B.Z.}}\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mathrm{Im}R^{xy}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{|B|}{2\pi}\Bigl(\frac{r\mu}{B}-\frac{r^{2}}{m_{e}}\mathrm{sign}(B)\Bigr).

(52b)

Here, we use the equation
|B|a_{x}a_{y}=2\pi
.
In insulators, the interband Drude weight is identical to the intra Drude weight.
We can explicitly check this identity as

D^{\mathrm{intra}}_{ij}=\sum_{n:\mathrm{occ}}\int_{\mathrm{B.Z.}}\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\braket{u_{n\bm{k}}|\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}|u_{n\bm{k}}}=\frac{n_{e}}{m_{e}}\delta_{ij}.

(53)

Here,
n_{e}=r|B|/2\pi
is the number density of electrons occupying the
r
Landau levels.

Appendix C
Bound relaton between interband Drude weight and orbital magnetization at finite temperaure

C.1
Mathematical preliminary: Inequalty satsfied by convex and concave functions

Considering a convex function
F(x)
satisfying
F^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0
, Taylor’s theorem yields

F(x)=F(y)+F^{\prime}(y)(x-y)+\int^{x}_{y}(x-t)F^{\prime\prime}(t)dt.

(54)

Using this equation, we obtain

(F(x)-F(y))-(x-y)\frac{F^{\prime}(x)+F^{\prime}(y)}{2}=\int^{x}_{y}(x-t)F^{\prime\prime}(t)dt-(x-y)\frac{F^{\prime}(x)-F^{\prime}(y)}{2}.

(55)

Integrating by parts the integral term, we obtain

\displaystyle\int^{x}_{y}(x-t)F^{\prime\prime}(t)dt=-(x-y)F^{\prime}(y)+\int^{x}_{y}F^{\prime}(t)dt.

(56)

The convexity
F^{\prime\prime}(x)
means that the function
F^{\prime}(x)
is non-decreasing,
F^{\prime}(a)\geq F^{\prime}(b)
for
a\geq b
. Thus, we obtain an inequality

\int^{x}_{y}F^{\prime}(t)\leq(x-y)F^{\prime}(x).

(57)

Using this inequality and Eq. (
55
) yields

(F(x)-F(y))-(x-y)\frac{F^{\prime}(x)+F^{\prime}(y)}{2}\leq(x-y)\frac{F^{\prime}(x)-F^{\prime}(y)}{2}.

(58)

For a concave function
G(x)~(G^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0)
, the corresponding inequality holds with the opposite sign,

(G(x)-G(y))-(x-y)\frac{G^{\prime}(x)+G^{\prime}(y)}{2}\geq(x-y)\frac{G^{\prime}(x)-G^{\prime}(y)}{2},

(59)

that can be proven by replacing
F(x)
with
-G(x)
.

C.2
Interband Drude weight and orbital magnetization at finite temperature

Here, we consider a Hermitian tensor

\tilde{R}_{ij}=e\int_{\bm{k}}\sum_{n\neq m}\Bigl(g_{nm\bm{k}}-\frac{\tilde{f}_{nm\bm{k}}\epsilon_{nm\bm{k}}}{2}-\frac{f_{nm\bm{k}}\epsilon_{nm\bm{k}}}{2}\Bigr)\braket{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n\bm{k}}|u_{m\bm{k}}}\braket{u_{m\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{j}}u_{n\bm{k}}}.

(60)

f_{n\bm{k}}=1/(1+e^{\beta(\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\mu)})
is the Fermi distribution function and
g_{n\bm{k}}=-\beta^{-1}\ln(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\mu)})
is the grand potential density.
We use some abbreviations:
a_{nm\bm{k}}=a_{n\bm{k}}-a_{m\bm{k}}
and
\tilde{a}_{nm\bm{k}}=a_{n\bm{k}}+a_{m\bm{k}}
.
The grand potential density
g_{n\bm{k}}
is the concave function for
\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}
and
\partial g_{n\bm{k}}/\partial\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}=f_{n\bm{k}}
is satisfied.
Therefore, Eq. (
59
) provides an inequality

g_{nm\bm{k}}-\frac{\tilde{f}_{nm\bm{k}}\epsilon_{nm\bm{k}}}{2}-\frac{f_{nm\bm{k}}\epsilon_{nm\bm{k}}}{2}\geq 0.

(61)

This inequality leads to the semipositive definiteness, as, for any complex vectors
\bm{v}
,

v_{i}^{*}\tilde{R}_{ij}v_{j}=e\int_{\bm{k}}\sum_{n\neq m}\Bigl(g_{nm\bm{k}}-\frac{\tilde{f}_{nm\bm{k}}\epsilon_{nm\bm{k}}}{2}-\frac{f_{nm\bm{k}}\epsilon_{nm\bm{k}}}{2}\Bigr)|v_{i}^{*}\braket{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n\bm{k}}|u_{m\bm{k}}}|^{2}\geq 0.

(62)

Then we obtain the inequality between the real part and the imaginary part of the
2\times 2
matrix
\tilde{R}
,

\sqrt{\det[\mathrm{Re}\tilde{R}]}\geq|\mathrm{Im}\tilde{R}^{12}|.

(63)

The real part is the interband Drude weight

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\tilde{R}_{ij}

\displaystyle=-e\int_{\bm{k}}\sum_{n\neq m}\frac{f_{nm\bm{k}}\epsilon_{nm\bm{k}}}{2}\mathrm{Re}[\braket{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n\bm{k}}|u_{m\bm{k}}}\braket{u_{m\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{j}}u_{n\bm{k}}}]

\displaystyle=e\int_{\bm{k}}\sum_{n}f_{n\bm{k}}\mathrm{Re}[\braket{\partial_{k_{i}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{j}}u_{n\bm{k}}}]=D^{\mathrm{inter}}/2e,

(64)

and the imaginary part is the orbital magnetization
Shi et al. 2007

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\tilde{R}_{12}

\displaystyle=e\int_{\bm{k}}\sum_{n\neq m}\Bigl(g_{nm\bm{k}}-\frac{\tilde{f}_{nm\bm{k}}\epsilon_{nm\bm{k}}}{2}\Bigr)\mathrm{Im}[\braket{\partial_{k_{1}}u_{n\bm{k}}|u_{m\bm{k}}}\braket{u_{m\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{2}}u_{n\bm{k}}}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{k}}\sum_{n}(f_{n\bm{k}}m_{n\bm{k}}-eg_{n\bm{k}}\Omega_{n\bm{k}})=M_{\mathrm{orb}}.

(65)

Here,
m_{n\bm{k}}=e\mathrm{Im}[\braket{\partial_{k_{1}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{2}}u_{n\bm{k}}}]
is the orbital magnetic moment and
\Omega_{n\bm{k}}=-2\mathrm{Im}[\braket{\partial_{k_{1}}u_{n\bm{k}}|\partial_{k_{2}}u_{n\bm{k}}}]
is the Berry curvature.
Using the above relations, we finally obtain the inequality

\frac{1}{2e}\sqrt{\det[D^{\mathrm{inter}}]}\geq|M_{\mathrm{orb}}|,

(66)

that is the extension of Eq. (
30
) to the finite temperature case.
