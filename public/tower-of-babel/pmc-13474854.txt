Meta-ERS: an exposome-based risk score using non-genetic factors to guide osteoporosis prevention
Authors: Yi Zheng, Yucan Li, Yawen Gao, Jincheng Li, Chen Suo, Yanfeng Jiang, Li Jin, Kelin Xu, Xingdong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoporosis is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, yet the relative contribution of the exposome remains unclear. This study aimed to systematically identify non-genetic exposures related to osteoporosis and develop an exposome risk score (ERS) to evaluate individual osteoporosis susceptibility.MethodsWe conducted an exposome-wide analysis of 477,792 UK Biobank participants to identify key exposures associated with osteoporosis. The selected exposures were combined into a weighted Meta-ERS and validated in the Scotland/Wales cohort. The Meta-ERS was further compared with polygenic risk scores (PRS) and linked to plasma proteomics to explore underlying biological pathways.ResultsWe identified 41 independent non-genetic exposures spanning socioeconomic status, mental health, sleep, diet, smoking, physical activity, environment, and marital status, with socioeconomic status and mental health emerging as the most significant drivers. Based on the identified exposures, we constructed eight domain-specific exposure risk scores and integrated them into a weighted Meta-ERS. The Meta-ERS (R2 = 5.1%; Proportion of Chi−Square = 14.3%) demonstrated an ability to explain osteoporosis variation that was on par with polygenic risk scores (R2 = 4.8%; Proportion of Chi−Square = 12.0%). Importantly, modifying unfavorable exposures mitigated the negative effect of PRS on osteoporosis, particularly among high PRS individuals (1.5- to 1.8-fold greater absolute risk reduction than in those with low PRS). Proteomic analyses further revealed potential mechanisms through which the exposome influences osteoporosis, including hormonal regulation, inflammation, ossification, muscle development, lipid metabolism, and accelerated bone aging. Among these, growth/differentiation factor 15 was identified as a key mediator protein, with a mediation proportion of 13.13%-36.52%.ConclusionsThe Meta-ERS facilitates the quantification of individual osteoporosis risk and identifies modifiable exposures for targeted prevention. Its application can enable personalized risk stratification and guide lifestyle or environmental interventions.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08379-5.Keywords: Exposome, Osteoporosis, Cohort study, Proteomics, Genetic risk

Introduction

Osteoporosis is an age-related skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration, leading to increased risk of fractures and related mortality [1]. It is estimated that more than 200 million people are affected globally, with the burden expected to rise as populations age [2]. Despite substantial advances in understanding genetic determinants of osteoporosis, genetic factors explains only a proportion of inter-individual variation in osteoporosis risk [3]. From a public health perspective, the human genome remains relatively stable across the life course, modifiable environmental and lifestyle factors may play a more pivotal role in the early prevention of osteoporosis and may even compensate for genetically conferred susceptibility.

The concept of the exposome, defined as the totality of environmental exposures across the life course, provides a comprehensive framework for investigating non-genetic contributors to complex diseases [4, 5]. Traditional epidemiological studies have identified individual exposures such as smoking, physical inactivity, and air pollution as risk factors for osteoporosis [6–8]. However, these studies have typically been hypothesis-driven and limited in scope, overlooking the broader interplay and correlation structure of exposures [9]. Similar to the aggregate effects of multiple genetic variants in genomics, exposome-wide approaches highlight the importance of the combined influence of multidimensional environmental factors on disease susceptibility, thereby offering a more comprehensive understanding of disease risk factors.

Moreover, large-scale, deeply phenotyped population cohorts have made it feasible to conduct systematic investigations of the exposome [10]. To date, only a few large studies have adopted exposome-wide designs to explore the impact of environmental exposures on mental health, aging, and mortality, whereas similar efforts focusing on skeletal health remain scarce [5, 9]. Meanwhile, advances in proteomics have provided powerful tools to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying osteoporosis, as well as to serve as effective biomarkers for quantifying organ-specific aging processes [11–13]. The integration of exposome and proteomics offers a unique opportunity to elucidate the biological mechanisms through which environmental exposures influence osteoporosis, while also providing novel insights into the potential modulatory role of bone aging in this process.

In this study, we leveraged a large prospective cohort and an exposome-wide approach to systematically evaluate the associations of hundreds of environmental, lifestyle, and social factors with osteoporosis. Our investigation focuses on four principal objectives. First, we aimed to identify environmental exposures independently associated with osteoporosis risk and to compare the relative contributions of the exposome and genetic factors. Second, we assessed whether environmental factors could modify the risk of osteoporosis among individuals with high genetic susceptibility. Third, we sought to explore the biological mechanisms associated with the influence of environmental exposures on osteoporosis from a proteomic perspective. Finally, we examined the mediating role of bone aging in the relationship between environmental exposures and osteoporosis risk.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

Participants in this study were drawn from the UK Biobank (UKB), a large prospective cohort comprising approximately 500,000 community-dwelling adults recruited between 2006 and 2010. After applying exclusion criteria, 477,792 individuals were included in the analysis, with exclusions for participants who had withdrawn consent or were lost to follow-up, had a diagnosis of osteoporosis or fracture at baseline, or had more than 30% missing data on exposure variables (Fig. 1). We partitioned the dataset by recruitment region into England and Scotland/Wales. Among participants recruited in England, we applied stratified random sampling based on osteoporosis incidence (maintaining the same proportion of osteoporosis incidence across subsets) to divide individuals into a discovery set (n = 212,508) and a replication set (n = 212,507) for the exposome-wide association analyses and the construction of the exposure risk score (ERS). Participants recruited from Scotland and Wales (n = 52,777) served as an independent validation cohort to verify the significance of the Meta-ERS and its compensatory effect on genetic susceptibility. Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations, generating five imputed datasets with five iterations. The study was approved by the NHS North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/0382) under application number 92,718.

Study overview

Exposures

We included non-genetic variables collected or derived at baseline in the UKB, restricting the analysis to external exposome. Variables derived from physical examinations, laboratory assays (e.g., blood or urine biomarkers), health status and any variables influenced by these health conditions, or short-term exposures capable of eliciting rapid biochemical responses (such as medication use) were excluded. Meanwhile, to ensure data quality and generalizability, we retained only variables with less than 30% missing rates and those available across all assessment centers. Ultimately, 136 variables were included in the analysis.

Assessment of osteoporosis

In the UKB, disease outcomes were determined through linkage with hospital admission databases, primary care records, and mortality registers. Osteoporosis cases were identified according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes (M80, M81, and M82) [14]. Follow-up time was defined as the interval between baseline recruitment and the earliest of osteoporosis diagnosis, death, or the censoring date (November 30, 2022).

Exposome-wide association analyses (XWAS) and variable selection

The XWAS was conducted separately in the discovery and replication datasets to enhance the robustness of the findings [5]. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to examine the associations between exposures and osteoporosis risk, using age as the timescale, stratified by sex and 5-year birth cohorts, and adjusted for ethnicity, assessment center, and body mass index (BMI). 5-year birth cohorts were categorized into 5-year intervals based on participants’ year of birth (e.g., 1935–1940, 1940–1945, 1945–1950) and treated as a categorical variable in the analyses. p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Bonferroni method. All ordered categorical variables were recoded to test only for linear associations, without evaluating other polynomial contrasts (e.g., quadratic or cubic relationships). This approach preserves the ordinal structure while providing a parsimonious and consistent framework for high-dimensional XWAS analyses. It also facilitates comparability across variables and supports the subsequent construction of composite exposure scores. Similar approaches have also been adopted in other exposome-based studies [5]. After completion of the XWAS analyses, the two datasets were merged for subsequent analyses.

To further identify variables independently associated with osteoporosis risk, we first assessed pairwise correlations among exposure variables that showed significant associations in the XWAS. Hierarchical clustering was then applied to determine the clustering structure of exposures, with the number of clusters determined based on the within-cluster sum of squares and considering conceptual factors related to the exposures. All variables within each cluster were entered into a multivariable Cox regression model, where multicollinearity was assessed using the generalized variance inflation factor (generalized variance inflation factor(1/(2×d.f.)) > 1.6 were excluded) [5, 15, 16]. GVIF assesses multicollinearity among exposure variables, particularly for multi-category predictors represented by dummy variables, by accounting for the joint confidence region of their associated coefficients. After removing collinear variables, non-significant exposures in the models were further eliminated.

Mendelian randomization

To further strengthen the evidence of the association between exposures and osteoporosis and to explore the genetic causal relationships, we performed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses. Summary statistics for exposure-related Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The GWAS summary statistics for osteoporosis, lumbar spine bone mineral density (BMD), and femoral neck BMD were obtained from the FinnGen Biobank (Release 12; 10,461 cases and 473,264 controls) and the GEFOS consortium (lumbar spine BMD: ieu-a-982, n = 28,498; femoral neck BMD: ieu-a-980, n = 32,735) [17, 18]. In the two-sample MR framework, genome-wide significant variants (p < 5 × 10− 8) were selected as instruments, followed by clumping and harmonization of datasets. MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) was applied to detect and remove pleiotropic outliers (p < 0.05). To reduce the risk of false positives and enhance the robustness of the findings, we applied six methods, including inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted model, Contamination mixture method (ConMix), and constrained maximum likelihood and model averaging (cML-MA), each relying on different assumptions. All genetic instruments had F-statistics > 10, suggesting no weak instrument bias [19]. Directionality was further assessed using the MR Steiger test [20]. Causal associations were considered significant if more than half of the applied methods reached significance, whereas associations were regarded as suggestive significance when only two methods showed significance.

Construction of ERS

We constructed domain-specific ERSs and a composite Meta-ERS in three steps: (1) Conceptual grouping. Exposure variables associated with osteoporosis risk were grouped a priori into eight domains: socioeconomic status (SES), mental health, sleep, diet, physical activity, smoking, environment, and marital status. (2) Unweighted scoring. Following prior work, each variable was scaled to 0–1 range [21–23]. Continuous variables were min–max scaled to 0–1; categorical variables were assigned proportional scores from 0 to 1 (e.g., never = 0, rarely = 0.25, sometimes = 0.50, often = 0.75, daily = 1). (3) Weighted calculation. Within each domain, Cox models were fit to obtain coefficient estimates (βj) for each exposure. In addition, we conducted sensitivity analyses on the association between Meta-ERS and osteoporosis. First, we performed sensitivity analyses by considering alternative scaling strategies (using Z-score standardization) and weighting schemes (using HRs instead of original Cox coefficients) to assess the robustness of the Meta-ERS construction. Second, given that individual exposures may change over time and that early, undiagnosed symptoms may lead to behavioral modifications, potentially affecting the accuracy of the Meta-ERS, we conducted additional analyses. We excluded incident osteoporosis cases within the first 2 years of follow-up to reduce reverse causality. In addition, we used repeated exposome measurements from the UK Biobank follow-up survey (2012–2013; n = 19,317) to reconstruct the Meta-ERS and assess its robustness. Due to missing data at follow-up, several variables (Townsend deprivation index, natural environment percentage (buffer 300 m), nitrogen oxides, PM2.5 absorbance, and population density) were not included in this analysis.

Moreover, to enhance the translational applicability of the Meta-ERS, we further constructed a simplified Meta-ERS by selecting the exposure with the largest coefficient from each of the eight domain-specific ERSs, thereby improving the practicality of the score for real-world use.

Integration of genetic and exposome risks

We utilized the osteoporosis polygenic risk scores (PRS) provided by UKB (Field ID 26258) to compare exposome and genome. The derivation of the PRS has been described in detail elsewhere [24]. Specifically, we examined the associations of both PRS and Meta-ERS with osteoporosis risk in Cox proportional hazards models, considering them as categorical variables (three-level) as well as continuous variables. To evaluate their relative contributions, we fitted four models: Model 1 included age and sex; Models 2 and 3 additionally incorporated PRS and Meta-ERS, respectively; and Model 4 included all covariates (age, sex, PRS, and Meta-ERS). Model performance was compared using the C-index and R2. Furthermore, to assess the combined effects of exposures, we constructed two additional Cox models: one including age, sex, PRS, and Meta-ERS, and the other including age, sex, PRS, and the eight domain-specific ERSs. The relative importance of variables in the model was assessed using the Wald Χ2 statistic derived from analysis of variance.

To investigate the potential compensatory effect of favorable exposures on genetic susceptibility to osteoporosis, participants in both the England and Scotland/Wales cohorts were stratified into nine subgroups according to combinations of ERS (low, medium, high) and PRS (low, medium, high). Cox regression models (adjusted for the same covariates as in the XWAS) were used to assess associations. In addition, standardized incidence rates (per 100,000 person-years) and absolute risk reduction (ARR) were calculated to quantify risk differences across subgroups [22].

Proteomic analyses and construction of bone biological age

We utilized baseline Olink proteomic data from 37,621 participants, covering a total of 2,923 proteins. Details of the proteomic measurement procedures have been reported elsewhere [25]. Four proteins with more than 20% of values below the lower limit of detection were excluded. Full protein names and abbreviations are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to identify proteins associated with the risk of osteoporosis, and multivariable linear regression models were used to examine associations between the ERS and these proteins. Linear regression models were adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, assessment center, and BMI. The 5-year birth cohort covariate was excluded from the models because of its collinearity with age. Subsequently, proteins associated with both the ERS and osteoporosis were integrated, and protein–protein interaction networks were constructed using the “STRINGdb” R package [26]. Functional categorization was assessed through enrichment analysis based on Gene Ontology biological processes (GO: BP). In addition, mediation analysis (R package ‘‘regmedint”) was conducted to explore key protein biomarkers mediating the relationship between the ERS and osteoporosis [27]. Moreover, we identified Growth/differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) as an important mediator protein in the mediation analysis. Considering that GDF15 is closely related to aging and inflammation, and that its observed mediation effects may be influenced by these factors, we further adjusted for bone accelerated aging and IL-6 in the mediation models to assess the robustness of the results.

Given the important role of age in osteoporosis risk, it is critical to evaluate the interrelationships among exposures, aging, and osteoporosis. However, chronological age does not adequately capture bone-specific aging processes. In contrast, biological age estimated from plasma proteomic signatures has shown considerable potential for reflecting aging across multiple organ systems. Therefore, we constructed a bone biological age using elastic net regression based on 258 plasma proteins associated with osteoporosis risk. Bone age acceleration was further quantified as the residuals of bone biological age regressed on chronological age, representing deviations from expected age-related changes. Mediation analysis was then performed to evaluate the mediating role of bone age acceleration in the associations between the ERS and osteoporosis risk.

Results

Sample characteristics

The sample included 477,792 participants from the UKB. Baseline characteristics of participants in England (n = 425,015) and Scotland/Wales (n = 52,777) are summarized in Table 1. During a median follow-up of 13.6 years, 12,949 cases of osteoporosis were recorded among participants recruited in England, with a proportion similar to that observed in Scotland/Wales. Individuals with osteoporosis were generally older, more likely to be female, had lower BMI, educational attainment, and experienced poorer economic conditions compared with those without osteoporosis in both regions. Smoking status demonstrated a statistically significant difference only between the normal and osteoporotic groups in England. Additionally, a higher proportion of white individuals was observed in Scotland/Wales.

Identification of exposures associated with osteoporosis

We conducted XWAS analyses of 136 exposures in both the discovery and replication datasets (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 3). In the discovery dataset, 65 exposures were significantly associated with osteoporosis risk, of which 60 (92.31%) were robustly replicated (Figs. 2B, 2C, and Supplementary Table 4). Among them, the number of family cars, ease of getting up in the morning, and being employed were the strongest protective factors for osteoporosis, whereas tiredness/lethargy, the Townsend deprivation index, and renting from local authority, local council, housing association were the exposures most significantly associated with increased osteoporosis risk. Notably, the top 10 most significant exposures were predominantly related to SES, mental health, sleep, and smoking.

Considering the high correlations and potential multicollinearity among exposures, we further identified variables independently associated with osteoporosis risk based on the XWAS results. Hierarchical clustering grouped the 60 exposures into eight clusters (Fig. 2D). All exposures within each cluster were then included in a single Cox model, and those failing collinearity tests or lacking statistical significance were removed. Ultimately, 41 exposures were identified as independently associated with osteoporosis risk (Fig. 2E).

Two-sample MR further supported that a subset of exposures exhibits a genetic causal relationship with osteoporosis (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Table 5–8). We identified significant or suggestive associations between exposures related to smoking (cigarettes smoked per day), early-life factors (comparative height size at age 10), mental health (worrier/anxious feelings, loneliness, irritability), SES (Townsend deprivation index, financial difficulties), physical activity (accelerometer-based physical activity measurement, leisure/social activities: Sports club or gym, vigorous physical activity), and environment (Nitrogen oxides air pollution) with bone loss (osteoporosis, femoral neck BMD, and lumbar spine BMD).

Comparative importance of exposome and genetic risk to osteoporosis

We constructed eight domain-specific ERSs and a Meta-ERS based on the 41 exposures independently associated with osteoporosis. The detailed variables and their corresponding weighting coefficients for these scores are provided in Supplementary Table 9. Marital status, dietary, and physical activity scores were negatively associated with osteoporosis risk, whereas SES, mental health, sleep, environment, and smoke scores showed significant positive associations (Fig. 3A). In addition, higher Meta-ERS was associated with increased risk of osteoporosis (HR high Meta-ERS vs. low Meta-ERS = 2.16; 95% CI: 2.05–2.26; HR Meta-ERS SD = 1.51; 95% CI: 1.49–1.54; Fig. 3B), with performance comparable to the PRS (HR high PRS vs. low PRS = 2.08; 95% CI: 1.98–2.17; HR PRS SD = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.36–1.41; Fig. 3B). In the independent Scotland/Wales validation cohort, the Meta-ERS remained significantly positively associated with osteoporosis risk (Scotland/Wales), supporting the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Table 10). In sensitivity analyses, the Meta-ERS constructed using Z-score standardization (England: HR Meta-ERS SD = 1.49; 95% CI: 1.47–1.52; Scotland/Wales: HR Meta-ERS SD = 1.33; 95% CI: 1.27–1.40) or HR–based weighting (England: HR Meta-ERS SD = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.18–1.22; Scotland/Wales: HR Meta-ERS SD = 1.07; 95% CI: 1.02–1.13) remained significantly associated with osteoporosis, although its performance was slightly attenuated (Supplementary Table 11). These findings support the robustness of the Meta-ERS construction approach. After excluding incident osteoporosis cases within the first 2 years of follow-up, the association between the Meta-ERS and osteoporosis remained robust (England: HR Meta-ERS SD = 1.51; 95% CI: 1.49–1.54; Scotland/Wales: HR Meta-ERS SD = 1.33; 95% CI: 1.26–1.41) (Supplementary Table 12). In addition, using repeated exposome measurements from the follow-up survey, the reconstructed Meta-ERS remained significantly associated with osteoporosis (HR Meta-ERS SD = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.11–1.35; HR high vs. low = 1.60; 95% CI: 1.24–2.07), supporting the robustness of the findings (Supplementary Table 13).

The simplified Meta-ERS, constructed using the most important exposure from each of the eight domains, remained significantly associated with osteoporosis (England: HR SD = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.37–1.42; HR high vs. low = 1.94; 95% CI: 1.86–2.03; Scotland/Wales: HR SD = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.19–1.31; HR high vs. low = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.19–1.31) (Supplementary Table 14). Although its performance was attenuated compared with the original Meta-ERS, it demonstrated improved feasibility for practical application.

We further evaluated the relative contributions of the Meta-ERS and PRS in explaining osteoporosis variation. Compared with the base model including only age and sex (Model 1; C-index = 0.746), the addition of either PRS (Model 2) or Meta-ERS (Model 3) achieved slightly higher C-indices of 0.760 and 0.762 (Fig. 3C), respectively, while including both PRS and Meta-ERS (Model 4) yielded the best performance (C-index = 0.774). Meanwhile, compared with Model 1, Model 2 and 3 increased the R2 by approximately 5% points. When both PRS and Meta-ERS were included (Model 4), the R2 increased by approximately 9% points (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, individual domain-specific ERSs accounted for only a small proportion of osteoporosis variation, whereas the combined effect of exposome explained a large proportion (Proportion of Chi−Square = 14.3%), comparable to that explained by the PRS (Proportion of Chi−Square = 12.0%) (Fig. 3E).

Compensatory role of exposome in genetic risk for osteoporosis

The risk of osteoporosis varied significantly across categories defined by the combination of PRS and ERS (Figs. 4A and 4B). Compared with participants with low PRS and low ERS, those with high PRS and high ERS had the highest risk of osteoporosis (England: HRhigh PRS with high ERS vs. reference = 4.60; 95% CI: 4.16–5.07; Scotland/Wales: HRhigh PRS with high ERS vs. reference = 4.26; 95% CI: 3.26–5.57; Fig. 4A). Within the same genetic risk category, favorable exposome characteristics can mitigated the negative impact of PRS on osteoporosis risk (e.g., England: HRhigh PRS with high ERS vs. reference = 4.60; 95% CI: 4.16–5.07; HRhigh PRS with low ERS vs. reference = 2.40; 95% CI: 2.16–2.67; Fig. 4A). Similar patterns were observed in the Scotland/Wales population, confirming the reproducibility of these findings (Fig. 4B). Moreover, change from high to low Meta-ERS conferred a 1.5- to 1.8-fold greater ARR in standardized osteoporosis incidence (per 100,000 PYs) among participants with high PRS (England: 430.6 to 226.8 events; Scotland/Wales: 458.7 to 292.9 events; Figs. 4C and D) than those with low PRS (England: 228.2 to 94.6 events; Scotland/Wales: 201.8 to 111.8 events).

Biological mechanisms of osteoporosis-related exposures

Among the 2,919 proteins, 258 were significantly associated with osteoporosis risk, with GDF15 and sclerostin (SOST) showing the strongest associations (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 15). These proteins also demonstrated significant associations with eight domain-specific ERSs, with the number of significant proteins ranging from 105 to 243 (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table 16), covering nearly all osteoporosis-related proteins (257/258). We hypothesized that some of these proteins might interact as members of different biological mechanisms Therefore, we integrated the 257 proteins jointly associated with both exposome and osteoporosis and performed clustering analysis using the STRING network, which identified six major functional clusters (Supplementary Table 17). Enrichment analysis revealed that the first cluster was involved in regulation of protease activity and glucocorticoid response; clusters 2 and 4 were immune-related, including immune cell activation and migration, as well as antigen processing and presentation; cluster 3 was related to ossification processes, including parathyroid hormone (PTH) response and regulation of BMP signaling; cluster 5 was primarily associated with muscle development; and cluster 6 was enriched in lipid metabolism–related functions (Fig. 5C). Detailed functional annotations are provided in Supplementary Table 18. In addition, mediation analysis identified 219–254 proteins that mediated the association between ERSs (excluding marital status related ERS) and osteoporosis risk. Notably, whether in the associations between domain-specific ERSs (SES, mental Health, sleep, diet, physical Activity, smoking, environment) or the Meta-ERS and osteoporosis, GDF15 was consistently identified as the most significant mediating protein, with mediation proportions ranging from 13.13% to 36.52% (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Table 19). In sensitivity analyses of the GDF15 mediation models, additional adjustment for bone accelerated aging and IL-6 led to a modest attenuation in the mediation proportion (10.59%–28.75%) (Supplementary Table 20). However, GDF15 remained a significant mediator of the associations between ERSs and osteoporosis, indicating the robustness of the findings.

Mediating role of aging in the exposure–osteoporosis relationship

Given that age contributed more strongly to the model than either genetic or exposome risk, we further investigated the role of aging in the association between exposome and osteoporosis. We constructed a bone biological age based on 258 proteins associated with osteoporosis risk, and the model showed good performance in predicting chronological age (Male: R = 0.88, MAE = 3.14; Female: R = 0.89, MAE = 2.90; Fig. 6A). Linear regression analyses revealed that ERS related to physical activity and marital status were significantly negatively associated with bone age, whereas ERS related to mental health, smoking, sleep, SES, as well as the Meta-ERS, were significantly positively associated with bone age (Fig. 6B).

All models were adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, assessment center, and BMI. Abbreviations: BMI, body mass index; ERS, Exposome Risk Score; HR, hazard ratio; MAE, mean absolute error; SES, Socioeconomic status; 95% CI, 95% confidence interval

Moreover, we assessed the mediating role of bone accelerated aging (defined as the residuals from regressing bone age on chronological age) in the associations between exposome risk and osteoporosis (Fig. 6C). Bone accelerated aging significantly mediated the associations of ERS related to SES, mental health, sleep, smoking, and physical activity, as well as the Meta-ERS, with osteoporosis, with mediation proportions ranging from 3.45% to 7.40%. The mediation proportion was highest for the association between smoking-related ERS and osteoporosis risk.

Discussion

Our study evaluated a wide range of exposures from an exposome-wide perspective and developed the Meta-ERS to quantify individual exposome-related risk for osteoporosis. We identified 41 exposures independently associated with osteoporosis risk, spanning domains such as SES, mental health, sleep, smoking, diet, physical activity, environment, marital status, and early-life exposures. The Meta-ERS based on modifiable exposures explained osteoporosis variation comparable to the PRS. Importantly, our results further revealed that improving unfavorable exposures could mitigate the negative effect of genetic risk factors on osteoporosis, particularly among individuals with high PRS. Furthermore, proteomic analyses supported the potential impact of the exposome on osteoporosis risk, both through biological mechanisms (such as hormonal regulation, inflammation, ossification, muscle development, and lipid metabolism) and through the process of bone aging.

Osteoporosis-related exposures span multiple domains, with SES and mental health emerging as the two most significant drivers in the present study. Previous evidence has consistently supported the strong associations of SES and mental health with osteoporosis risk [28–30]. Disadvantaged SES is associated with restricted access to healthcare resources, inadequate nutritional intake, and poorer health outcomes [31, 32]. Adverse mental health is frequently accompanied by hormonal dysregulation (e.g., altered glucocorticoid levels) and elevated systemic inflammation, mechanisms further supported by our proteomic evidence [33]. Beyond medication, public health strategies for osteoporosis prevention and management commonly emphasize lifestyle modifications such as improving diet, increasing physical activity, and avoiding smoking, relatively little attention has been given to SES and mental health [34]. From a policy perspective, interventions aimed at improving social security for economically disadvantaged populations and expanding access to mental health services may represent crucial strategies to reduce the burden of osteoporosis at the population level.

By comparing the explanatory power of exposome and genome for osteoporosis variation, we revealed the unique contribution of the exposome to osteoporosis risk. GWAS have greatly advanced our understanding of how genetic variation influences osteoporosis and have facilitated the construction of PRS as powerful tools for risk stratification [3, 35, 36]. However, as with other complex traits, GWAS results cannot fully account for variation in osteoporosis [3]. Beyond factors such as missing heritability, polygenicity, and negative selection, our evidence suggests that the exposome may play a similar important role in influencing osteoporosis risk [37, 38]. The ability of the Meta-ERS to capture variation in osteoporosis comparable to that of PRS highlights the importance of focusing on environmental and lifestyle exposures. Nevertheless, given that Meta-ERS and PRS serve only as proxies for the exposome and genetic component of osteoporosis, they may underestimate the true exposure and polygenic risk, and caution is warranted in interpreting these results.

Notably, our study further revealed the potential compensatory role of improving unfavorable exposures in mitigating osteoporosis risk among individuals with high genetic susceptibility. With the increasing availability of genetic testing and the gradual refinement of PRS construction, large-scale precision risk stratification in populations will become feasible. However, given that genetic risk is non-modifiable, the development of strategies to mitigate its adverse impact is of particular importance [21]. Targeting modifiable exposure factors may represent a more cost-effective and feasible approach to alleviating genetic burden, particularly among high PRS individuals. In this context, we developed the Meta-ERS as a tool to assess individual exposure risk. Importantly, the Meta-ERS encompasses not only lifestyle-related factors but also broader domains such as socioeconomic status and mental health, which cannot be fully improved through individual effort alone. This highlights that effective precision prevention of osteoporosis requires not only individual-level interventions but also coordinated efforts at the societal and policy levels to achieve risk compensation and osteoporosis burden reduction. This is the vision of precision prevention.

From a proteomic perspective, we identified a series of biomarkers that were significantly associated with both osteoporosis risk and the exposome. Among these, GDF15 was identified as the most important cross-exposome mediator, highlighting its important role in mediating the influence of exposome factors on bone loss. GDF15 mRNA is highly expressed in skeletal muscle, suggesting that it may influence bone health through muscle–bone crosstalk [39]. As skeletal muscle functions as an endocrine organ that secretes myokines regulating bone remodeling and mineral metabolism, GDF15 may act as a stress-responsive myokine participating in bone metabolism via paracrine and endocrine mechanisms [40, 41]. In addition, previous studies have reported that GDF15 mRNA levels are associated with inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, indicating that its role in bone metabolism and muscle homeostasis may be partly mediated through inflammation-related pathways [42, 43]. However, given that GDF15 is a pleiotropic cytokine, the mediation results should be interpreted with caution [44, 45]. Its repeated identification in our analyses may reflect broader systemic stress biology rather than a bone-specific mechanistic axis, and this possibility should be carefully considered. GDF15 is expressed in multiple cell types and can be upregulated in response to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation, highlighting its diverse biological functions [39]. Notably, a large-scale proteomic study has demonstrated that GDF15 exhibits strong predictive ability across approximately 200 diseases, underscoring its role as a broad biomarker of systemic physiological stress [46]. In addition, adverse exposome factors (e.g., smoking, mental health, and diet) are known to influence a wide range of disease processes, rather than affecting bone health alone [47–49]. In this context, the observed mediation effect of GDF15 may reflect its role as a downstream integrator of multiple adverse exposures, capturing the cumulative biological impact of the exposome on systemic physiological dysregulation. Such dysregulation, including chronic inflammation and metabolic imbalance, has been consistently linked to bone loss and impaired bone remodeling [50, 51]. GDF15 may represent an intermediate signal through which diverse exposome factors converge to influence osteoporosis risk.

Furthermore, proteomic analyses deepened our understanding of potential biological mechanisms through which the exposome influences osteoporosis risk. The identified protein clusters pointed to several interconnected pathways, including inflammatory responses, lipid metabolism, hormonal regulation, ossification, and muscle development. Inflammation is a well-established driver of bone loss. The immune and skeletal systems share multiple molecular mediators such as cytokines, chemokines, and signaling molecules, and chronic or dysregulated immune activation can disrupt bone metabolic homeostasis, ultimately accelerating bone loss [52]. Secondly, abnormal lipid metabolism may disrupt bone homeostasis through dual mechanisms [53]. On the one hand, it alters the bone microenvironment via cross-organ communication, promoting the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into adipocytes rather than osteoblasts. On the other hand, it disrupts bone metabolic balance by promoting cytokine secretion from osteoblasts and stimulating osteoclast differentiation. Moreover, there is a close relationship between lipid metabolism and Inflammation. Proteins involved in lipid metabolism pathways include Group 10 secretory phospholipase A2 (PLA2G10) and Phospholipase A2 (membrane associated) (PLA2G2A). Recent studies have shown that PLA2G10 hydrolyzes phospholipids into small lipid metabolites and inhibits chemokine-mediated T-cell migration, underscoring its dual role in lipid metabolism and immune regulation [54]. Meanwhile, hormonal regulation also plays a direct role in ossification processes. Pharmacologically, PTH binds to PTHR1 to initiate signaling that promotes either osteoclast differentiation under conditions of excessive dosing or continuous infusion, or osteoblast activation with intermittent injection [55]. Glucocorticoids are implicated in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis by suppressing the production of insulin-like growth factor-1 or growth hormone, thereby affecting bone metabolism [56]. Furthermore, our findings revealed a mediating role of bone aging in the relationship between exposome and osteoporosis risk. Aging is a multidimensional process encompassing elevated inflammation, dysregulated lipid metabolism, hormonal imbalance, and decline in muscle mass [57, 58]. It integrates external exposures with the molecular mechanisms of osteoporosis risk, forming a network that jointly influences osteoporosis development.

The novelty of this study lies in several aspects: (1) the use of a large-scale population-based cohort with long-term follow-up and the assessment of an extensive range of exposome variables; (2) the investigation of the importance of the exposome in the context of genetic risk; (3) the integration of high-throughput proteomics to elucidate underlying biological mechanisms; and (4) the development of Meta-ERS to evaluate osteoporosis-related exposure risk. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results: (1) participants in the UK Biobank tend to be healthier and live in less deprived areas compared with the general UK population, which may introduce a healthy volunteer bias [59]; (2) the diagnosis of osteoporosis was based on primary care, hospital records, self-report, and mortality data, but baseline femoral neck and lumbar spine BMD measurements across the entire cohort were lacking, which may have resulted in partial under-ascertainment of cases. This limitation is commonly observed in prospective osteoporosis research within UKB [14, 60, 61]; (3) although 2,923 proteins were available for analysis, they do not fully represent the human proteome and may not capture all biological mechanisms linking exposures to osteoporosis; (4) as the Meta-ERS was developed and validated in a predominantly European population, its generalizability to other populations remains to be established. Our future studies will prioritize validating the Meta-ERS in more diverse populations, including non-European and lower-resource settings; and (5) exposures were assessed at a single baseline time point, which may not fully capture temporal changes in individual behaviors over the long follow-up period. Future studies incorporating more repeated and longitudinal exposure measurements are warranted to better characterize dynamic exposome profiles and their impact on osteoporosis risk.

Taken together, the present study comprehensively highlights the significant and multidimensional role of the exposome in osteoporosis risk. Our findings emphasize the considerable potential of the Meta-ERS for osteoporosis prevention, particularly among individuals with high genetic susceptibility. Future research is warranted to validate and refine the Meta-ERS across diverse populations and to develop efficient exposome-related biomarkers, which could enable more precise exposure assessment and facilitate the translation of exposome risk stratification into clinical and public health practice.
