Association of dietary phytochemical index (DPI) and risk of overweight and obesity in school-aged girls in Southeast Iran
Authors: Nooshin Jannati, Mohammad Reza Mahmoodi, Leila Azadbakht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Childhood overweight and obesity are growing public health concerns worldwide. Among dietary elements, dietary phytochemicals have been suggested to play a role in weight management. We aim to investigate the association between dietary phytochemical index (DPI) and the risk of overweight and obesity in primary school girls in Kerman City, Iran. Three hundred and thirty primary school girls between the ages of 6 and 12 entered this study. We used standard protocols for measuring anthropometric indices. Obesity and overweight were defined according to BMI-for-age Z-Score (BAZ) based on World Health Organization Z-Scores charts for girls aged 5–19 years old. The dietary phytochemical index was calculated using McCarty method. We assessed sociodemographic status and physical activity using valid questionnaires. Participants in higher tertile of DPI have significantly higher intakes of energy (p = 0.003), energy from phytochemical food groups (p < 0.001), fiber (p < 0.001), whole grains (p < 0.001), fruits (p < 0.001), vegetables (p < 0.001), and nuts and seeds (p < 0.001). A higher intake of supplements was associated with a higher DPI (8.5% vs. 3.9%; p = 0.034). Participants in the higher tertile compare with a lower tertile of DPI have significantly higher BAZ (0.47 ± 0.12 vs. 0.01 ± 0.12; p = 0.023). However, a significant direct association was revealed between DPI and overweight (OR 2.05; CI 1.02–4.15, p = 0.037). In conclusion, our findings suggest that a diet high in phytochemicals might be associated with a higher prevalence of overweight in children, but not obesity.

Introduction

There has been a global increase in the prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity 1,2. In Kerman, the prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity was reported as 10% and 9%, respectively, in 2015 3. Childhood obesity represents a major public health concern due to its association with adverse health outcomes later in life, including metabolic syndrome, coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, fatty liver disease, and certain cancers 4–6. Obesity is a complex condition shaped by multiple factors, including lifestyle, genetic predisposition, and environmental influences 7. Dietary habits play a critical role in the development of obesity 8.

Epidemiological studies indicate that adequate consumption of vegetables, fruits, and whole grains is associated with improved weight control 9,10. Phytochemicals are bioactive compounds naturally present in plant based foods and may play a role in reducing the risk of chronic conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and certain cancers 11,12. These compounds include alkaloids, terpenes, flavonoids, lignans, plant sterols, curcuminoids, saponins, phenols, and glycosides 13.

The dietary phytochemical index (DPI) is calculated as the proportion of total dietary energy derived from phytochemical rich foods 14. Previous studies revealed controversial results. Some studies found that a higher DPI is associated with a significantly lower risk of overweight and obesity in children 8,15. In contrast, a meta-analysis found no statistically significant association between the highest phytochemical index and overweight or obesity among children and adolescents 16.

Assessing and monitoring the nutritional status of girls is particularly important, as they represent future mothers and play a critical role in the health of the broader community. To our knowledge, no previous studies have examined the association between phytochemical rich food intake and anthropometric indices among school aged children in Kerman, southeast Iran. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association between the dietary phytochemical index (DPI) and overweight and obesity among primary school girls in Kerman, southeast Iran.

Methods

Participants eligibility and study design

Participant recruitment began in January 2022. The study included 330 primary school girls aged 6–12 years recruited from primary schools across five regions of Kerman, Iran. Participants were selected using a cluster systematic random sampling method. The city of Kerman was divided into five geographic zones (north, south, east, west, and central), representing different socioeconomic levels within the city. Two schools were randomly selected from each region using a computer-generated random number list. Within each selected school, students were randomly selected from class lists, and a random sample of 30–35 eligible students was enrolled.

The sample size was calculated based on the mean and standard deviation of body mass index (BMI) reported in a previous cross-sectional study of Iranian children aged 7–11 years (mean ± SD = 16.0 ± 2.9 kg/m2) 17. Inclusion criteria included willingness to participate, as indicated by written informed consent from parents or guardians; age between 6 and 12 years; absence of chronic diseases such as diabetes, thyroid disorders, epilepsy, asthma, or metabolic disorders including maple syrup urine disease and phenylketonuria; and no use of medications such as corticosteroids, thyroid medications, antiepileptic drugs, or allergy medications. Exclusion criteria included refusal to participate or withdrawal of consent, age outside the specified range, presence of any diagnosed chronic or metabolic disease, use of medications known to affect growth, metabolism, or appetite, incomplete questionnaire data, or implausible energy intake reports.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all procedures involving human participants were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.603). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ guardians.

Determination of socioeconomic status

Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of participants were assessed using a reliable and validated socioeconomic status (SES) questionnaire 18, completed by parents or guardians. Collected information included age, supplement use, parental smoking status, parental education and occupation, household size, home ownership status, number of vehicles, number of bedrooms, and ownership of household appliances such as an LCD television, side by side refrigerator, washing machine, dishwasher, vacuum cleaner, computer, laptop, air conditioner, and advanced heating system 18. To calculate the SES score, each item was assigned a numerical value, and total scores were summed and categorized into three levels: low 1–5, medium 6–10, and high (11–15 scores).

Physical activity assessment

Physical activity was assessed using a daily activity schedule that recorded the frequency and duration of low, moderate, high intensity, and sedentary activities during the previous seven days. The proportion of daily minutes spent in moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was calculated using the method proposed by Saint Maurice et al. 19. Participants were categorized as either meeting physical activity guidelines (≥ 60 min per day) or not meeting guidelines (< 60 min per day), based on the global recommendation of an average of at least 60 min of MVPA per day 20.

Determination of anthropometric indices

Weight, height, and mid upper arm circumference (MUAC) were measured by a trained dietitian. Body weight was measured to the nearest 100 g using a calibrated digital scale, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a non stretchable measuring tape fixed to a wall. MUAC was measured using a flexible measuring tape at the midpoint between the acromion (shoulder) and olecranon (elbow). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

Z scores for BMI for age (BAZ), height for age (HAZ), and weight for age (WAZ) were calculated using World Health Organization reference standards. Weight status was classified based on BAZ as obese (BAZ ≥ 2 SD), overweight (1 SD ≤ BAZ < 2 SD), thin (− 3 SD < BAZ ≤ − 2 SD), and severely thin (BAZ ≤ − 3 SD) 21.

Determination of dietary phytochemical score

Dietary intake data were collected using a reliable and validated dish based food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) designed for the Kerman population 22. A trained nutritionist interviewed the children’s guardians to report the frequency and portion size of food consumption per day, week, or month over the previous year for each food item. Reported portion sizes were converted to grams using standard household measures. Energy and nutrient intakes were calculated using Nutritionist IV software.

The dietary phytochemical index (DPI) was calculated using the method proposed by McCarty as follows: DPI = (daily energy intake from phytochemical rich foods [kcal]/total daily energy intake [kcal]) × 100 14. Phytochemical rich foods included whole grains, fruits, vegetables, soy products, legumes, nuts, seeds, olive oil, tomato sauce, and pure fruit and vegetable juices. Potatoes were excluded due to their low phytochemical content, whereas tea and coffee were included because of their high phytochemical content.

After calculating DPI scores, participants were categorized into tertiles, with the highest tertile representing those with the greatest intake of phytochemical rich foods.

Statistical analysis

The normality of variables was assessed using histogram plots and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Chi square tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare categorical and continuous variables across tertiles of the Dietary Phytochemical Index (DPI), respectively. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was applied to evaluate the association between dietary intake and anthropometric measures across DPI tertiles, with post hoc comparisons performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test.

Three statistical models were examined: Model 1 (crude); Model 2 (adjusted for age and energy intake); and Model 3 (adjusted for age, energy intake, supplement use, parental smoking, physical activity, and socioeconomic status). Multiple logistic regression analyses were conducted to investigate the association between DPI and anthropometric outcomes, including overweight and obesity, which were analyzed as separate categories. Binary logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DPI and overweight and obesity

Higher DPI was associated with an increased likelihood of being overweight but not obesity. Discussion

In the present study, we examined the association between the dietary phytochemical index (DPI) and the risk of overweight and obesity among girls aged 6–12 years. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate this association in Kerman Province, and evidence in this area remains particularly limited in recent years, especially among pediatric populations. Our findings demonstrated a significant positive association between DPI and overweight, indicating that girls with higher DPI scores were more likely to be overweight than those with lower DPI scores. However, no significant association was observed between DPI and obesity in this study. These findings are particularly relevant for school-aged girls in this region, as they suggest that dietary quality alone, without attention to total energy intake, may not be sufficient to prevent excess weight gain during a critical period of growth and development.

Most previous research on DPI has focused on adult populations, with limited evidence available for children and adolescents. In a cross-sectional study conducted among children aged 7–10 years in Tehran, Iran, an inverse association was reported between DPI and the odds of overweight and obesity 8. One key difference between that study and the present study is that overweight and obesity were analyzed as a single outcome in the previous research, whereas we examined these conditions separately. Additionally, we included tea and coffee in the calculation of DPI despite their low energy content, which was not considered in the Tehran study. Another distinction is the broader age range of participants in the current study (6–12 years versus 7–10 years), which may partly explain differences in the observed associations. These methodological differences highlight the need for context-specific evidence when developing nutrition guidance for children in different regions and age groups.

Fruits, vegetables, and grains constitute a major proportion of phytochemical rich foods, and higher consumption of these foods contributes to an increased dietary phytochemical index (DPI). However, fruit consumption may exert a dual effect on body weight. Increased intake of fruits, vegetables, and grains can also be accompanied by higher total energy intake, which may contribute to weight gain. In the present study, this dual effect may partly explain the J shaped association observed between DPI and overweight. For children, this suggests that encouraging unrestricted consumption of phytochemical-rich foods without guidance on portion size and energy balance may unintentionally increase the risk of overweight.

Experimental evidence suggests beneficial effects of phytochemical rich fruits on body weight regulation. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that polyphenol rich fruit extracts reduced total energy intake, weight gain, and visceral adipose tissue in animal models, indicating potential protective effects against overweight and obesity 23. Conversely, fruits contain varying amounts of naturally occurring sugars, including fructose, glucose, and sucrose, which may contribute to increased energy intake and weight gain 24. Nevertheless, epidemiological studies have generally shown that most whole fruits are associated with anti-obesity effects, highlighting the complex and sometimes paradoxical role of fruit intake in weight management. These findings suggest that different types of fruits may exert diverse effects on body weight 24.

Evidence from 17 prospective cohort studies and 14 cross sectional studies indicated no consistent association between 100% fruit juice consumption and obesity outcomes in most children, although a modest positive association was observed in some overweight or very young children 25. However, important methodological differences and limitations across studies should be considered when interpreting these findings. This distinction is important for the current population, where fruit juice consumption may represent a meaningful source of excess energy without the satiety benefits of whole fruit.

Legumes are an important source of plant based protein, bioactive peptides, and phytochemicals, and their consumption has been associated with a reduced risk of overweight and obesity 26. Evidence also suggests that whole grain consumption may be modestly associated with lower body weight among individuals with overweight or obesity 27. However, findings across studies remain inconsistent. In a cross sectional study conducted among Iranian children and adolescents, Azizi Soleiman et al. reported no significant association between DPI and the risk of overweight, obesity, or abdominal obesity 28. Similarly, some studies have found no significant effects of whole grain consumption on body weight, body mass index, or fat free mass, and therefore did not support whole grain intake as an effective strategy for obesity control 29. Notably, this latter study was conducted in a larger sample of 4,296 participants aged 6–18 years.

Age and methodological differences may partly explain these discrepancies. In a study of Korean preschool children, Han et al. observed that higher DPI was associated with a lower prevalence of obesity in boys based on weight percentiles but not BMI percentiles, while no significant association was found among girls 15. This study included children aged 3–5 years and assessed dietary intake using a 24-h dietary recall, which differs from the food frequency questionnaire used in the present study.

Potential biological mechanisms underlying weight regulation may also involve gut microbiota. Greater consumption of plant-based proteins has been shown to promote weight loss and beneficial modulation of gut microbiota compared with animal protein intake. Diets rich in dietary fiber and plant proteins, such as the Mediterranean diet, may increase anti-inflammatory, butyrate producing bacteria that contribute to improved gut microbial composition and energy regulation. In contrast, diets high in animal protein, characteristic of Western dietary patterns, may reduce beneficial gut bacteria and contribute to metabolic disorders including obesity 30. Gut microbiota dysbiosis may disrupt energy homeostasis and play a key role in obesity development.

Nuts and edible seeds, which are rich in dietary fiber and polyphenols, may also beneficially interact with gut microbiota. Three mechanisms have been proposed to explain their potential role in intestinal balance and weight management: preservation of intestinal barrier integrity, enhancement of anti inflammatory responses, and increased butyrate production 31.

Evidence from adult populations generally supports a protective role of higher dietary phytochemical intake against obesity. In a cross sectional study, Asgari et al. reported that higher dietary phytochemical index (DPI) was associated with a lower risk of central obesity among adults 32. Similarly, two studies conducted among Iranian adults aged 19–70 years demonstrated an inverse association between DPI and obesity 33,34. In a Korean population based cross sectional study, a significant inverse relationship was observed between DPI and abdominal obesity among women 35. In addition, a retrospective study by Kapolou et al. identified an inverse association between polyphenol intake and waist to hip ratio 36.

Further support comes from a meta-analysis of nine studies including 100,753 participants, which showed that a higher phytochemical index was associated with a reduced risk of overweight and obesity (pooled OR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.74–0.90). Dose response analyses indicated a nonlinear relationship between phytochemical index and overweight and obesity risk. Meta regression analyses further identified sex and geographic location as significant sources of heterogeneity across studies 16. These findings highlight the importance of considering key covariates when interpreting associations between dietary phytochemical intake and obesity related outcomes.

In our previous study examining plant based dietary patterns and anthropometric indices, we found that a higher overall plant based diet index (PDI) was associated with lower odds of overweight, whereas a higher unhealthy plant based diet index (uPDI) was associated with increased odds of overweight and obesity 37. In the present study, the observed positive association between DPI and overweight contrasts with much of the existing literature suggesting a protective role of phytochemical rich diets against excess weight gain. This unexpected finding may be influenced by several factors. One possible explanation relates to the overall dietary patterns of participants, whereby girls with higher DPI scores may also have consumed greater amounts of energy dense foods such as nuts and seeds, which are high in fat and protein and may contribute to increased total energy intake. Although most studies report an inverse association between moderate nut consumption (approximately one to two servings per week) and weight gain or obesity risk, higher intake levels or different dietary contexts may yield different effects. Future research should further explore whether these findings are consistent across diverse ethnic groups, lower socioeconomic populations, and settings in developing countries 38.

The observed J shaped association illustrates that while moderate DPI levels were associated with a lower risk of overweight, the highest DPI tertile was associated with an increased risk. In contrast, obesity risk across DPI tertiles followed a symmetric J shaped pattern across all models. This pattern suggests that a balanced intake of phytochemical rich foods may be beneficial, whereas excessive intake accompanied by higher energy consumption could attenuate or reverse these benefits. The lowest point of the J shaped curve may reflect an optimal balance between dietary quality and energy intake, supporting the role of moderation in dietary recommendations.

From a public health perspective, these findings highlight the importance of promoting balanced, nutrient dense dietary patterns rather than focusing solely on increasing phytochemical rich food intake. Policies that limit the marketing of high calorie, nutrient poor foods to children while increasing the availability and promotion of healthier options such as fruits and vegetables may contribute to improved dietary behaviors and reduced risk of childhood overweight and obesity.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first investigation to assess the association between DPI and the risk of overweight and obesity among primary school girls in Kerman. The use of a dish-based food frequency questionnaire specifically developed for local dietary patterns likely improved dietary recall compared with standard food-based questionnaires. Additionally, we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, including socioeconomic status, physical activity, and parental factors, and conducted separate analyses for overweight and obesity, allowing for more nuanced interpretation of the findings.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Dietary assessment using a food frequency questionnaire may have resulted in underestimation or overestimation of absolute intake. Further longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm these findings and to better elucidate the relationship between dietary phytochemical intake and weight related outcomes in children.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that higher dietary phytochemical index (DPI) levels were associated with a greater prevalence of overweight, but not obesity, among school aged girls. The consumption of phytochemical rich foods appears to have a dual effect: while these foods contribute to higher dietary quality and increased DPI, they may also increase total energy intake, potentially influencing weight status. This dual effect was reflected in the observed J shaped association between DPI and overweight. In contrast, the J shaped pattern observed for obesity risk suggests that a balanced intake of phytochemical rich foods may be beneficial for obesity prevention.

Further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms driving these associations and to determine optimal levels and patterns of phytochemical rich food consumption. Addressing these gaps will be essential for informing effective dietary strategies to prevent and manage overweight and obesity in children.
