Treatment outcomes and factors associated with recovery among children aged 6–59 months with severe acute malnutrition admitted to therapeutic feeding units in Central Tanzania: a longitudinal descriptive study
Authors: Lembo Joseph, Dedius E Peter, Rehema Bakari, Julius Ntwenya, Golden Mwakibo Masika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study was to investigate treatment outcomes and identify factors associated with recovery among children aged 6–59 months with severe acute malnutrition who were admitted to therapeutic feeding units in Tanzania’s central zone.DesignHospital-based longitudinal descriptive study.SettingThe study was conducted in the therapeutic feeding units of Dodoma Regional Referral Hospital and Singida Regional Referral Hospital, Tanzania, between 1 April and 30 June 2024.ParticipantsA total of 166 children aged 6–59 months admitted with severe acute malnutrition were consecutively enrolled and followed from admission until recovery, death, default, non-response or the end of the study period.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome was recovery from severe acute malnutrition according to the Tanzanian Integrated Management of Acute Malnutrition guidelines. Time to recovery was assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression. Secondary outcomes included mortality, defaulting, non-response, average weight gain, length of hospital stay and factors associated with recovery.ResultsOf the 166 enrolled children, 123 recovered, yielding a recovery rate of 74.1% (95% CI 67.5% to 80.7%). The mortality rate was 17.5%, exceeding the Sphere standard threshold of <10%. The default and non-response rates were 5.4% and 1.8%, respectively. The median time to recovery was 10 days (95% CI 9.0 to 11.0). In multivariable Cox regression, urban residence (Adjusted Hazard Ratio(AHR)=1.615, 95% CI 1.231 to 2.031), breastfeeding (AHR=1.332, 95% CI 1.101 to 1.970), caregiver occupation as a housewife (AHR=1.921, 95% CI 1.408 to 2.078) or food server (AHR=6.727, 95% CI 1.324 to 34.179), mebendazole supplementation (AHR=1.927, 95% CI 1.136 to 3.267) and folic acid supplementation (AHR=1.459, 95% CI 1.222 to 1.950) were independently associated with a higher likelihood of recovery.ConclusionsRecovery rate among children with severe acute malnutrition was influenced by place of residence, breastfeeding status, caregiver occupation and the use of mebendazole and folic acid supplementation. However, mortality remained a concern, highlighting the need for improved management of severe acute malnutrition in therapeutic feeding units. Further research is needed to explore additional factors affecting recovery and treatment outcomes.Keywords: Caregivers, Child, Infant, Nutrition, Observational Study

Introduction

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among children under 5 years of age worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Globally, approximately 42.8 million children under 5 years of age were wasted, representing about 6.6% of all children under 5, while an estimated 12.2 million children suffered from severe wasting, the most life-threatening form of undernutrition.1 Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate share of this burden, with approximately 14 million children under 5 years of age affected by wasting, accounting for nearly one-third of the global burden despite ongoing interventions.1

The high burden of acute malnutrition in Africa is driven by multiple interconnected factors, including recurrent food insecurity, poverty, infectious diseases, climate-related shocks and weak health systems, all of which contribute to persistently high rates of child morbidity and mortality.2 Acute malnutrition continues to be a major contributor to childhood morbidity and mortality, with undernutrition underlying nearly half of all deaths among children younger than 5 years globally, most of which occur in low- and middle-income countries.3

SAM is defined as a weight-for-height/length z-score below −3 SDs of the WHO Child Growth Standards, a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) <11.5 cm among children aged 6–59 months, and/or the presence of bilateral pitting oedema.4 Children with SAM are particularly vulnerable to illness because malnutrition impairs immune function and disrupts normal metabolic processes. Common comorbidities include diarrhoeal diseases, pneumonia, malaria, tuberculosis (TB), HIV infection and anaemia, all of which increase the risk of treatment failure, prolonged hospitalisation, poor recovery and death.5 6 As a result, children with SAM frequently experience poor treatment outcomes, including delayed recovery, prolonged hospital stays, defaulting from treatment and increased mortality.5 7 Moreover, mortality among children admitted with SAM remains unacceptably high in many settings, particularly where severe infections and delayed health-seeking behaviour coexist.6

Despite substantial investments in nutrition programmes, treatment outcomes among children with SAM remain suboptimal in many African countries. The expansion of Community-Based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) and Integrated Management of Acute Malnutrition (IMAM) programmes has improved access to treatment; however, outcomes continue to vary across settings. A previous study reported that the pooled mortality rate among under-5 children admitted with SAM was 11%, exceeding the Sphere recommendation of less than 10% in several settings.6 Recent evidence further highlights variations in recovery and mortality rates across African countries. In Ethiopia, data show a pooled recovery rate of approximately 71.4%, which falls below the Sphere target of 75%, while mortality remained a significant concern among children with medical complications.8 In Somalia, the pooled recovery rate was approximately 95.39%, whereas the death rate was 0.18%, with substantial regional variation attributable to differences in healthcare access and food security.9 Similarly, a multicountry evaluation of CMAM programmes across 16 Eastern and Southern African countries demonstrated that although treatment coverage has expanded considerably, programme performance remains heterogeneous, with recovery and survival outcomes differing markedly across countries and health systems.10 Furthermore, a meta-analysis of inpatient treatment outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa estimated an average inpatient mortality rate ranging from 3% to 18.2%, indicating that mortality among hospitalised children with SAM remains unacceptably high despite advances in therapeutic management.11

To monitor and evaluate the effectiveness of therapeutic feeding programmes, international standards have been established to guide programme performance. The Sphere Humanitarian Standards recommend a recovery rate greater than 75%, a mortality rate below 10%, a default rate below 15% and an average length of stay below 28 days for children treated for SAM.12 Despite the widespread adoption of WHO and UNICEF treatment guidelines, many African countries continue to report outcomes below these standards. Variations in treatment outcomes are often attributed to differences in health system capacity, availability of therapeutic foods and medications, disease burden and socioeconomic circumstances.6

In Tanzania, child malnutrition remains a major public health concern despite improvements in nutritional indicators over recent years. According to the Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey 2022, approximately 30% of children under 5 years were stunted, 3% were wasted and 1% were severely wasted.13 Although the prevalence of wasting has declined over time and remains below the WHO emergency threshold of 5%, acute malnutrition continues to contribute substantially to childhood illness and mortality. In addition, important regional disparities persist, with central regions such as Dodoma and Singida continuing to report considerable numbers of children requiring treatment for acute malnutrition.13

To address this challenge, Tanzania implements the IMAM strategy, which includes promotion of optimal infant and young child feeding practices, micronutrient supplementation, immunisation, nutrition education and management of SAM. According to national IMAM guidelines, children admitted with complicated SAM receive F-75 therapeutic milk during the stabilisation phase, followed by F-100 therapeutic milk or ready-to-use therapeutic foods (RUTFs) during rehabilitation. In addition, routine antibiotics such as ampicillin and gentamicin are administered during the stabilisation phase because bacterial infections are common among children with SAM. Other medications, including ceftriaxone, metronidazole, folic acid, zinc supplementation and anthelmintic drugs, may be prescribed according to clinical indications.14

Although these interventions have improved access to treatment, outcomes among children with SAM continue to vary across healthcare facilities and regions. Recent evidence from Tanzania indicates that factors such as age, HIV status, oedema, anaemia and coexisting infections significantly influence recovery rates and length of hospital stay.7 However, there is limited evidence regarding treatment outcomes and associated factors among children admitted to therapeutic feeding units in the Central Zone of Tanzania, particularly in Dodoma and Singida regions, where the burden of acute malnutrition remains substantial. Generating local evidence on treatment outcomes and their determinants is essential for improving the quality of care and informing future nutrition interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to assess treatment outcome rates and associated factors among infants and children aged 6–59 months with SAM admitted to therapeutic feeding units in the Central Zone of Tanzania.

Material and methods

Study design

This was a hospital-based longitudinal descriptive study involving a single cohort of infants and children aged 6–59 months admitted with SAM to therapeutic feeding units at Dodoma Regional Referral Hospital (DRRH) and Singida Regional Referral Hospital (SRRH) in the Central Zone of Tanzania. Participants were enrolled consecutively on admission and followed prospectively from admission until the first occurrence of one of the predefined treatment outcomes: recovery and discharge, death, default or non-response. Data collection was conducted from 1 April to 30 June 2024, and all enrolled participants were followed until their outcome was recorded within this study period.

Study setting

The study was conducted at DRRH and SRRH, which are regional referral hospitals equipped with inpatient therapeutic food units for managing SAM cases. These facilities serve as key centres for paediatric care in the Dodoma and Singida regions, handling referrals from lower-level health facilities.

Study population

The target population included all infants and children aged 6–59 months admitted to the therapeutic food units at DRRH and SRRH with a diagnosis of SAM during the study period.

Inclusion criteria

Infants and children aged 6–59 months who were newly admitted to the therapeutic food units with a confirmed diagnosis of SAM and whose caregivers provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria were children with SAM who were readmitted during the study period, those transferred from other facilities with incomplete baseline data or those whose caregiver declined participation.

Treatment programme

All children received standard treatment according to the Tanzania IMAM guidelines. On admission, each child was assessed for medical complications and nutritional status. Treatment was provided in two phases. The stabilisation phase, which usually lasted 1–7 days, focused on treating medical complications and stabilising the child’s condition. During this phase, children received treatment to prevent low blood sugar, rehydration with ReSoMal when needed, antibiotics such as ampicillin and gentamicin and therapeutic feeding with F-75 formula (75 kcal/100 mL) at a recommended volume of 100–130 mL/kg/day.

The rehabilitation phase continued until the child recovered. During this phase, children were given F-100 formula (100 kcal/100 mL) or RUTF to support weight gain. The amount of food was gradually increased to achieve a target weight gain of 8–10 g/kg/day. Children also received micronutrient supplements, including vitamin A, folic acid and zinc sulfate. Mebendazole was given for deworming when appropriate for the child’s age. Additional treatments were provided to manage any coexisting illnesses.

Children were monitored daily by trained healthcare workers. Weight, MUAC, oedema and vital signs were assessed regularly to track progress. Caregivers also received nutrition education, including information on breastfeeding and appropriate complementary feeding practices.

Patient and public involvement

None.

After adding a 10% attrition rate, the sample size was 164, whereas the proportionate sampling resulted in the sample size for DRRH being 95 and SRRH had 69 participants. The participants were selected consecutively on admission and followed prospectively until discharge, death, default or non-response. A design effect was not applied during sample size determination because the study used consecutive enrolment of eligible participants from the selected hospitals rather than using a probability sampling approach.

Data collection instruments and procedure

The current study used a pretested questionnaire adapted from Teshome et al15 to collect data (online supplemental file 1). The principal investigator and one registered nurse from each hospital served as research assistants during data collection. Before data collection began, the research assistants received training from the principal investigator on the study objectives and data collection procedures. The study period was 90 days, from 1 April to 30 June 2024. The collected data were used to assess treatment outcomes among children with SAM, including recovery, death, default and non-response. The data were also used to determine the time to recovery and identify factors associated with treatment outcomes.

Variable measurement

SAM was defined according to the WHO and IMAM guidelines as a weight-for-height/length z-score (WHZ) below −3 SDs of the WHO Child Growth Standards, a MUAC less than 11.5 cm and/or the presence of bilateral pitting oedema. Recovery was defined as meeting the discharge criteria outlined in the IMAM guidelines, including improvement in nutritional status and clinical condition. Specifically, a child was considered recovered if they achieved a WHZ of at least −2 SD or a MUAC of at least 12.5 cm, had no bilateral pitting oedema on two consecutive assessments, showed satisfactory weight gain and had no medical complications requiring inpatient care. In addition, the child had to be assessed as clinically well and ready for discharge by the attending physician.

Other treatment outcomes were defined as follows: death referred to the death of a child during hospitalisation; default referred to a child leaving the hospital against medical advice or missing treatment for more than three consecutive days; non-response referred to failure to meet the recovery criteria after 6 weeks of treatment despite receiving the recommended care and censored referred to children who were transferred to another facility, lost to follow-up or whose final treatment outcome could not be determined during the study period.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered, cleaned and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. Time-to-event data were analysed using the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate recovery time, while Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with recovery from SAM.

Initially, univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted for each independent variable to assess its association with time to recovery. Variables with a p value less than 0.25 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. HRs with 95% CIs were calculated to measure the strength of the associations. Variables with a p value less than 0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant.16

Results

Characteristics of the infant and children aged 6-59 months and health-related services

A total of 166 infants and children with SAM were enrolled in the study from the therapeutic feeding units of DRRH and SRRH. More than half of the participants were enrolled from DRRH (n=96, 57.83%), while the remaining participants were enrolled from SRRH (n=70, 42.2%). More than half of the children were male (n=84, 50.60%), and (n=100, 60.24%) were aged 0–23 months. In addition, (n=71, 42.77%) of the children were being breastfed at the time of admission. Nearly one-third of the children were firstborn (n=49, 29.52%). Most participants lived in households with one to three children under 5 years of age (n=142, 85.54%) (online supplemental table 1).

Sociodemographic characteristics of the caregiver of infants and children aged 6-59 months

The age of the caregivers ranged from 16 to 81 years, with a mean age of 30.54 years (SD=11.60). About two-thirds of caregivers were married or living with their husbands (n=110, 66.27%). More than half had completed primary education (n=94, 56.63%). The majority of caregivers were the children’s biological mothers (n=130, 78.31%), and more than half were engaged in farming as their main occupation (n=84, 50.60%). Most caregivers had a monthly income of less than TZS 200 000 (n=137, 82.5%). More than half of the household heads were fathers (n=94, 56.63%), and more than half of the caregivers were living in their own houses (n=90, 54.22%) (online supplemental table 2).

Treatment outcome of SAM for the category of regional hospitals in the central zone

At the end of the follow-up period, the treatment outcomes at DRRH were as follows: out of (n=96) children admitted with SAM, (n=74, 77%) recovered, meeting the minimum recovery rate recommended by the Sphere standards. The death rate was (n=11, 11.4%), which exceeded the acceptable Sphere standard. The default rate was (n=7, 7.2%), which was within the acceptable range, while non-response was observed in (n=3, 1.81%) of the children. The average weight gain was 10 g/kg/day, and the median length of hospital stay was 10 days. At SRRH, out of (n=70) children admitted with SAM, (n=49, 70%) recovered. The death rate was (n=18, 25.7%), the default rate was (n=2, 2.8%), and no cases of non-response were reported. The average weight gain was 9 g/kg/day, and the median length of hospital stay was 8 days (online supplemental table 3).

Survival time to recovery from SAM

The overall observed time to recovery from SAM in the Central Zone hospitals was 10 days (95% CI 9.0 to 11.0) (figure 1). The median length of hospital stay was 10 days (95% CI 8.592 to 11.408) at DRRH and 8 days (95% CI 6.957 to 9.043) at SRRH (figure 2). The median recovery time was similar to the median length of hospital stay because children were discharged immediately after meeting the recovery criteria specified in the IMAM guidelines. Several factors were significantly associated with time to recovery. These included breastfeeding status, TB, HIV infection, pneumonia, urinary tract infection (UTI) and the type of SAM (p<0.05).

Children who were being breastfed had a shorter median recovery time of 9 days (95% CI 8.08 to 9.91; p=0.012). In contrast, children diagnosed with TB had a longer median recovery time of 15 days (95% CI 7.07 to 18.92; p=0.002). Children with pneumonia had a shorter median recovery time of 9 days (95% CI 8.18 to 9.81; p=0.010), while those with HIV infection had the longest median recovery time of 17 days (95% CI 9.43 to 24.56; p=0.005). Children with UTI recovered within a median of 7 days (95% CI 4.85 to 9.14; p=0.004). Recovery time also varied by the type of malnutrition. Children with kwashiorkor recovered within a median of 9 days (95% CI 8.05 to 9.94; p=0.033), compared with 10 days (95% CI 8.89 to 11.10) among children with marasmus and 12 days (95% CI 9.49 to 14.50) among those with marasmic-kwashiorkor.

The use of routine micronutrient and antihelminthic supplements was also significantly associated with recovery time (p<0.05). Children who received zinc sulfate had a shorter median recovery time of 9 days (95% CI 7.84 to 10.15; p=0.006). Similarly, children who received vitamin A recovered within a median of 9 days (95% CI 7.84 to 10.15; p=0.003). Children who received mebendazole also recovered more quickly, with a median recovery time of 9 days (95% CI 8.31 to 9.68; p<0.001) (online supplemental table 4).

Multivariable regression analysis for factors associated with treatment outcome

After adjusting for potential confounding factors, children from urban areas had a higher likelihood of recovery from SAM than those from rural areas (AHR=1.615; 95% CI 1.231 to 2.031; p=0.031). Similarly, children who were being breastfed during the study period were more likely to recover from SAM than those who were not breastfed (AHR=1.332; 95% CI 1.101 to 1.970; p=0.015). Caregiver occupation was also significantly associated with recovery. Children cared for by caregivers enrolled in the Inpatient Therapeutic Care (ITC) management programme had a higher likelihood of recovery compared with those whose caregivers were students (AHR=5.186; 95% CI 1.199 to 22.425; p=0.028). Likewise, children cared for by housewives were more likely to recover than those cared for by student caregivers (AHR=1.921; 95% CI 1.408 to 2.078; p=0.034).

The use of certain medications during hospitalisation was also associated with recovery. Children who received mebendazole were more likely to recover from SAM than those who did not receive the drug (AHR=1.927; 95% CI 1.136 to 3.267; p=0.015). Similarly, children who received folic acid had a higher likelihood of recovery compared with their counterparts (AHR=1.459; 95% CI 1.222 to 1.950; p=0.036). Children who received ceftriaxone had a higher likelihood of recovery than those who did not receive the antibiotic (AHR=5.186; 95% CI 1.199 to 22.425). However, this association was not statistically significant (p>0.05).

After controlling for confounders, children who were coming from the address of urban areas had over one time the odds to recover from SAM compared with those who were coming from rural areas (AHR=1.615; 95% CI 1.231 to 2.031; p=0.031). Children who were breastfeeding during the study period had over one time higher the odds of recovering from SAM compared with their counterparts (AHR=1.332; 95% CI 1.101 to 1.970; p=0.015). Children’s ITC management programme had over five times the odds (AHR=5.186; 95% CI 1.199 to 22.425; p=0.028) compared with caregivers who were students.

Children who were cared for by caregivers who were housewives had twice the chance of recovering from SAM compared with children who were cared for by caregivers who were students (AHR=1.921; 95% CI 1.408 to 2.078; p=0.034). Children who received mebendazole drugs during ITC were almost twice as likely to recover from SAM compared with their counterparts (AHR=1.927; 95% CI 1.136 to 3.267; p=0.015). Children who were given routine medication of folic acid during hospitalisation in ITC had one time higher odds of recovering from SAM compared with their counterparts (AHR=1.459; 95% CI 1.222 to 1.950; p=0.036). Children who were given antibiotics (ceftriaxone) were five times more likely to recover from SAM compared with their counterparts (AHR=5976; 95% CI 4.625 to 6.522; p=0.951); however, it was not statistically significant (online supplemental table 5) (figure 3).

Discussion

This study assessed treatment outcomes and factors associated with recovery among children aged 6–59 months with SAM admitted to therapeutic feeding units in the Central Zone of Tanzania. The recovery rate observed in this study was 74.1%, which was only 0.9 percentage points below the Sphere minimum standard of ≥75% recovery.12 Although slightly below the recommended target, the recovery rate was close to the acceptable international benchmark, suggesting generally satisfactory programme performance. This finding may reflect ongoing national efforts to improve SAM management in Tanzania, including the implementation of treatment guidelines, capacity-building for healthcare workers and caregiver education on the appropriate use of therapeutic foods.14

The recovery rate was comparable to findings reported in South Africa, Northern Ethiopia and Tigray, where recovery rates of 74.1%, 74.49% and 75.9%, respectively, were documented.17–19 The similarity may be attributed to the fact that these studies were conducted in inpatient therapeutic feeding units, where treatment protocols and clinical management approaches are relatively similar.

In contrast, the mortality rate observed in this study exceeded the Sphere standard threshold of <10% and was greater than 15%, indicating a substantial burden of mortality among hospitalised children with SAM.12 This finding is consistent with reports from other studies that documented similarly high mortality rates among children admitted for SAM treatment.17 20 A possible explanation is that the study hospitals were referral facilities that typically receive critically ill children with severe complications, thereby increasing the risk of mortality. However, the mortality rate was higher than that reported in studies from Ethiopia, where death rates remained within acceptable limits.18 21 These differences may be due to variations in hospital level, referral patterns, case severity at admission and patient management practices.

The default rate in the present study was below the Sphere standard threshold of <15%, indicating good treatment retention and follow-up.12 This result is consistent with previous studies that reported low default rates ranging from 4.37% to 7.1%.22 23 The similarity may reflect comparable health-system characteristics and programme implementation across East African settings. In addition, supportive hospital environments and positive caregiver engagement may have contributed to improved adherence to treatment. Conversely, the default rate observed in this study was lower than rates reported in the Somali Region of Ethiopia (12.6%), Northwest Ethiopia (16.52%) and Finote Selam Hospital (13.7%).16 22 24 Differences in hospital capacity, accessibility of services and referral mechanisms may explain these variations.

The non-response rate was low, suggesting effective clinical management of SAM within the selected hospitals. One possible explanation is the availability of specialised healthcare professionals and close monitoring of admitted children. Although the non-response rate was higher than that reported in the Somali Region of Ethiopia (0.9%) and Eastern Ethiopia (0.2%),22 25 it was comparable to rates reported in Northern Ethiopia (4%) and the Amhara Region (7.4%).26 27 The observed differences may reflect variations in patient characteristics, disease severity and treatment practices across settings.

The average weight gain among recovered children was 10 g/kg/day, exceeding the Sphere minimum standard of 8 g/kg/day and indicating satisfactory nutritional rehabilitation.12 This favourable outcome may be linked to strong caregiver adherence to feeding recommendations provided by dietitians and nutritionists. In particular, many caregivers were housewives or food vendors who may have been more available to follow feeding instructions and provide consistent care. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Aksum, Mekelle and Northern Ethiopia.28–30

The median time to recovery was 10 days, which falls well within the recommended treatment duration and below the Sphere standard maximum length of stay of 28 days.12 This finding indicates efficient clinical recovery among children receiving inpatient care. The relatively short recovery period may be attributed to caregiver compliance with treatment and feeding instructions, facilitated by the fact that many caregivers had at least a primary-level education. Similar median recovery times have been reported in Finote Selam Hospital and South Wollo Zone, Ethiopia, where children recovered after approximately 11 days.16 27 However, the recovery time in the current study was shorter than that reported in other Ethiopian studies, where median hospital stays ranged from 12 to 15 days.31 Differences in treatment protocols, patient characteristics and disease severity may explain these variations.

Several factors were significantly associated with recovery, including place of residence, breastfeeding status, caregiver occupation and administration of routine medications such as mebendazole and folic acid. Children from urban areas were more likely to recover than those from rural areas, possibly because urban residents have better access to healthcare services and follow-up care. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Southern, Northern and Eastern Ethiopia.25 32 33 However, it contrasts with findings from Northwest Ethiopia, where children from rural areas had better recovery outcomes.16 Differences in healthcare accessibility, socioeconomic conditions and community support systems may account for these inconsistencies.

Breastfeeding was also positively associated with recovery. Children who continued breastfeeding had higher recovery rates than those who were not breastfed. This may be because breast milk provides essential nutrients, bioactive compounds and immune protection that support nutritional rehabilitation and reduce infection risk.34 This finding is consistent with studies conducted in South Wollo, Addis Ababa and Nigeria,27 31 35 as well as recommendations in international SAM management guidelines.

Children who received mebendazole were more likely to recover than those who did not receive the medication. This finding may be explained by the effectiveness of deworming in reducing intestinal parasite burden, improving nutrient absorption and correcting nutrition-related anaemia. A similar association was reported in a study conducted at Ayder Referral Hospital, Ethiopia.36

Similarly, folic acid supplementation was associated with improved recovery outcomes. Folic acid plays a critical role in red blood cell production, DNA synthesis and the prevention of folate-deficiency anaemia, all of which may support nutritional recovery.37 This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Southwest Ethiopia, Northwest Ethiopia and Dessie Comprehensive Specialised Hospital.16 21 38 The consistency across studies may reflect the routine provision of folic acid in inpatient therapeutic feeding units and the close monitoring of treatment adherence.

Although a higher proportion of children who received intravenous ceftriaxone recovered compared with those who did not receive the drug, the association was not statistically significant. Ceftriaxone is a broad-spectrum antibiotic effective against a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and may contribute to improved outcomes among children with concurrent infections. This observation is similar to findings reported in Northeast Ethiopia.38 However, other studies conducted in Southern Ethiopia, Eastern Ethiopia and the Nagele Arsi District identified amoxicillin as the antibiotic more strongly associated with recovery among children with SAM.25 39 40 These discrepancies may be related to differences in local patterns of infection, antimicrobial prescribing practices, bacterial resistance profiles and patient characteristics.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several methodological strengths. The longitudinal design enabled follow-up of children with SAM from admission to treatment outcome, reducing recall bias and allowing assessment of recovery over time. The use of standardised IMAM guidelines ensured consistent diagnosis, treatment and outcome assessment across study sites. In addition, survival analysis methods, including Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression models, provided a robust approach for evaluating time to recovery and associated factors. The inclusion of comprehensive clinical, nutritional, treatment-related and caregiver characteristics further strengthened the analysis by allowing adjustment for potential confounders.

However, several limitations should be considered. The study was conducted in two regional referral hospitals, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to lower-level health facilities and community settings. Some clinical and anthropometric measurements may have been subject to measurement error despite standardised procedures and training. The relatively small sample size and short study period may have reduced the ability to detect associations for less common factors. Furthermore, important variables such as household food security, maternal nutritional status, caregiving practices and postdischarge outcomes were not assessed, leaving the possibility of residual confounding.

Also, the study was conducted among children admitted with SAM, and the available data did not contain sufficient anthropometric information to classify children according to the Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure. Consequently, the analysis was limited to the classification of SAM into marasmus, kwashiorkor and marasmic-kwashiorkor. Finally, the observational nature of the study precludes definitive conclusions about causality between identified predictors and recovery outcomes.

Conclusions

The recovery rate was close to the Sphere standard, while weight gain and length of stay met recommended international benchmarks. However, the mortality rate remained higher than the acceptable standard, indicating a need for improved management of critically ill children with SAM. Recovery was significantly associated with urban residence, breastfeeding, caregiver occupation and the use of mebendazole and folic acid supplementation. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening adherence to standard treatment protocols, promoting breastfeeding and ensuring appropriate routine medication. Further efforts are needed to reduce mortality through early detection, timely referral and enhanced inpatient care for children with SAM.

Completed data collection forms were stored securely in a locked location, while electronic data were protected using password-secured devices and storage systems accessible only to the principal investigator.
