The effect of a robot’s presence on human task performance: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Gang Ren, Zhihuang Huang, Gang Wang, Tianyang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to quantify the overall effect of robot presence on human cognitive task performance and to examine the moderating roles of two key characteristics: interactive capability and anthropomorphic morphology. A total of 10 studies were included for synthesis. We used the standardized mean difference (SMD) to assess task time efficiency (9 studies, n=540) and task accuracy (4 studies, n=129). The meta-analysis results showed no significant overall net effect of robot presence on either task time efficiency or accuracy. However, subgroup analyses revealed a significant differential pattern for task time efficiency: interactive robots were associated with improved efficiency (SMD = -0.31, P=0.03) while non-interactive robots showed the opposite (SMD = 0.22, P=0.009); additionally, non-anthropomorphic robots were associated with decreased efficiency (SMD = 0.47, P=0.0003).These findings indicate that robot presence does not have a uniform impact on human performance; rather, the direction of its effect is bidirectionally moderated by robot characteristics. In particular, interactive capability plays a crucial role in determining whether robots facilitate or inhibit task efficiency, which underscores the importance of designing robot social roles and behaviors in future human-robot collaboration.

Introduction

Human-robot collaboration has become increasingly prevalent across various domains1, from factories2 to hospitals3 and homes4, where robots are being deployed on a large scale to assist human work or achieve human-machine cooperation5. While such deployments aim to improve efficiency and support human partners, substantial evidence suggests that the mere presence of robots can alter human behavior and potentially affect task performance in unexpected ways6.

Addressing this phenomenon, relevant research has found that robot presence may produce dual effects on human task performance7–11, facilitating performance in certain contexts while inhibiting it in others. This phenomenon can be explained by theoretical frameworks originally developed in human-human interaction research. Social facilitation theory12 proposes that the presence of others increases an individual’s physiological arousal level, thereby enhancing their dominant responses, which benefits simple or well-learned tasks but may impair performance on complex or novel tasks. Distraction-conflict theory further suggests13 that the presence of others forces individuals to divide attention between the task and others, and this conflict is a key mechanism underlying performance changes14. Building on this foundation, evaluation apprehension theory provides an additional emotional dimension to explain the aforementioned mechanisms15. This theory posits that individuals, based on past experiences, anticipate that their performance will be evaluated by others, and this evaluative expectation itself triggers stronger physiological arousal responses, thereby exerting additional influence on task performance15. Notably, these theoretical mechanisms fundamentally operate through cognitive processes such as attentional allocation, executive control, and evaluative processing13–15. Tasks that require active information processing, including attention, perception, memory, and decision-making, are therefore particularly susceptible to these presence-related effects16,17, making cognitive task performance the specific focus of this review.

These theoretical mechanisms, though originally established in human-human contexts, have been increasingly applied to human-robot interaction (HRI) research. This extension is grounded in the concept of social presence, which refers to the extent to which an individual feels connected to or aware of the presence of another, whether human or robot18. Robots can generate social presence when they exhibit human-like social behaviours and establish social relationships with humans19. In HRI contexts, social presence manifests when individuals perceive robots as social beings capable of meaningful interaction and experience feelings comparable to those in human interactions20. When such social presence is established, individuals exhibit psychological responses similar to those triggered by human presence, thereby activating the same facilitation and inhibition mechanisms16,17,21–23.

Building on these theoretical foundations, research in HRI has identified two major categories of factors that modulate the strength of social presence and, consequently, the direction of robot presence effects. The first is interactive capabilities, which refer to robots’ ability to actively construct a distinct social role through their behaviors24, such as providing feedback, prompts, or responses as collaborators or evaluators9,25–28. The second is morphological characteristics, particularly the degree of human-likeness8,10,29,30. These two dimensions shape how strongly individuals perceive robots as social entities24,31,32, thereby modulating the strength of social presence and the associated arousal, attention, and evaluative responses, which ultimately determine whether robot presence facilitates or inhibits performance16,22,23,26.

Despite the growing body of empirical research, existing studies vary in task types11,16,22, contextual settings11,17,33, and performance measurements16,33,34, resulting in fragmented and sometimes contradictory findings. This inconsistency may reflect competing theoretical mechanisms operating under different conditions: while social facilitation theory predicts that robot presence can enhance performance through heightened arousal16,35, evaluation apprehension and distraction-conflict theories suggest that robot presence may impair performance by inducing performance anxiety or consuming attentional resources23,26. Yet some studies have found no significant effects of robot presence on cognitive task performance22.

Given these theoretical considerations and the identified moderating factors, this study hypothesized that robot presence would not produce a significant overall effect on cognitive task performance, but that the effects would be moderated by robot interactivity and anthropomorphic morphology. To test these hypotheses, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to quantitatively synthesize evidence on the impact of robot presence on human cognitive task performance. By integrating results across multiple task paradigms, this study aims to clarify the consistency and magnitude of robot presence effects and evaluate social presence-related theories in HRI. The findings can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential mechanisms by which robot presence influences human behavior and offer important empirical evidence for the design and optimization of future human-robot collaborative systems.

Methods

This study strictly followed the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework36. The systematic review protocol has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251080016).

Search strategy

Four databases were used to search for studies on the effectiveness of robot presence on human task performance: Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and PsycINFO. The search was limited to English articles published from January 2015 to July 2025, and all database searches were completed on July 15, 2025. After removing duplicate articles using EndNote X9 (19.0.0.12062), two authors (Ren and Huang) independently screened articles based on titles, abstracts, and keywords for initial selection, then conducted full-text review of articles that passed initial screening to determine final inclusion. When disagreements arose during the full-text review stage, a third author (Wang) was consulted for discussion to reach consensus.

The search strategy employed the following keyword combinations: (”human-robot interaction” OR ”social robot*” OR ”humanoid robot*” OR robot*) AND (”social facilitation” OR ”social inhibition” OR ”presence”) AND (”task performance” OR ”cognitive performance” OR ”cognitive control” OR ”attentional control”). Data extraction

During the review process, two authors (Ren and Huang) systematically extracted important information from each article (see Table2). Disagreements during the extraction process were resolved through discussion with another pair of authors (Wang and Huang). The extracted information included (1) study characteristics (authors and publication year); (2) participant characteristics (sample size and mean age); (3) study design; (4) details of robot presence intervention (interactivity and morphological features); (5) outcome measurements and statistical data.

One study11 did not report subgroup sample sizes, which meant that SMD could not be directly calculated. To address this common issue in meta-analysis, we adopted the equal allocation principle, dividing the total sample size included in the statistics by the number of experimental groups (182/4) to estimate each subgroup sample size. Previous research has shown that this substitute method does not produce significant statistical effects on meta-analysis results5,37. Meanwhile, since this study did not provide specific mean and standard deviation values in the data reporting, we used WebPlotDigitizer38 to extract relevant data from the charts. This program has been proven to have high levels of reliability and validity39,40. Additionally, for the two studies that included similar tasks10,30, we selected data from the ”Modular Arithmetics” condition for extraction, as this arithmetic task, compared to ”Word Recall,” better aligns with the cognitive decision-making characteristics of human-robot collaboration scenarios, requiring faster information processing and higher attention concentration. While most included tasks were established cognitive paradigms (e.g., Stroop, Flanker, Modular Arithmetic), the dragging task in Kim et al.11 was also included because it employed Fitts’s Law41 to manipulate task difficulty. Fitts’s Law conceptualizes goal-directed movement as an information processing task, where the index of difficulty reflects the amount of information that the motor system must process to complete the movement11,41. Robot classification

Robots in the included studies were classified according to two key dimensions: (1) Interactive capability. This dimension was based on whether robots actively construct a distinct social role through their actual behaviors executed within the experimental paradigm24. This classification was based on the objective behaviors of robots as described in each study’s methodology, rather than their design capabilities or participants’ subjective perceptions (this criterion applies to all included robot morphologies, including humanoid, mechanical, and zoomorphic types). Interactive robots position themselves as social agents by directing communicative behaviors toward participants32, such as voice42, gestures43, and eye contact44. These behaviors function as social framing that establishes the robot’s role within the interaction45. Verbally announcing their social role (e.g., evaluator) directly to participants also constitutes active role construction22. The core criterion is that the robot explicitly treats participants as targets of social interaction through at least one documented communicative act, regardless of whether this interaction was continuous throughout the task. In contrast, non-interactive robots lack this active role construction, with their social attributes only passively inferred by users from their ”mere presence.” Consequently, they are typically perceived as passive observers with ambiguous roles, whose behaviors are independent of users and fail to convey clear social intentions46, even when they possess features that could technically enable interaction.

(2) Anthropomorphic morphology. The classification of anthropomorphic robots was primarily based on the presence of human-like features, particularly localized features such as eyes and mouth in the facial region, as well as limbs on the body31. These human-like features are highly inclusive in form, encompassing both realistic physical structures and abstracted or symbolic representations47–49, such as when the literature explicitly describes graphic elements as designs representing human eyes10,11. Furthermore, research has shown that robots’ surface appearance, body manipulators, and facial features positively influence human perception of their anthropomorphism, while mechanical locomotion methods such as wheels and tracks produce negative effects31. Based on these theoretical foundations, this study classified robots possessing human-like facial features (e.g., eyes, mouth) or human-like body structures (e.g., limbs, torso) as anthropomorphic robots. For zoomorphic or mechanical robots, if the original literature only described their animal anatomical features or mechanical structures without mentioning human-like design elements, they were classified as non-anthropomorphic robots.

Outcome measures

The study identified two core outcome indicator domains: (1) Task time efficiency, used to quantify the speed and efficiency with which participants complete specific tasks, encompassing measurement indicators such as reaction time, response time, and task completion time, measured in seconds or milliseconds, where lower values in this indicator domain represent better performance; (2) Task accuracy, used to evaluate the correctness and quality of participants’ performance in specific tasks, encompassing measurement indicators such as correct rate and accuracy rate, where higher values in this indicator domain represent better performance.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality assessment was conducted by two authors (Ren and Huang) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2.0 (RoB 2)50. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with another author (Wang). This tool assesses the risk of bias in studies by evaluating five independent domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (5) bias in selection of the reported result. Based on the assessment, each domain was judged to have a ”low risk of bias,” ”some concerns,” or ”high risk of bias”51. For visualization in Review Manager (Version 5.4), which uses terminology from the first-generation RoB tool, the ”some concerns” judgment corresponds to the software’s ”unclear risk of bias” category and is represented by a yellow indicator. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined based on domain ratings: if all domains were ”low risk,” the overall assessment was ”low risk of bias”; if at least one domain had ”some concerns” but none were ”high risk,” the overall assessment was ”some concerns”; if at least one domain was ”high risk,” the overall assessment was ”high risk of bias”50.

Statistical analysis

We conducted this meta-analysis using RevMan 5.4. The effect sizes for outcomes were expressed as SMD and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), because the included studies reported the effects of robot presence on task efficiency using different analytical methods and measurement units (e.g., ms, s). Additionally, effect size thresholds were defined as 0.2 indicating a small effect, 0.5 indicating a moderate effect, and 0.8 indicating a large effect52. Effect estimates were calculated using the inverse variance random-effects method. The following thresholds were applied as general guidance for interpretation: 0–40% might not be important; 30–60% may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50–90% may represent substantial heterogeneity; 75–100% may indicate considerable heterogeneity. Additionally, between-study variance was assessed using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau ^2$$\end{document} statistic. To assess the robustness of the results, we conducted sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method. Results

Search results

The initial search yielded a total of 4,024 relevant articles. After removing duplicates, 3,905 articles remained for the screening stage. Through title and abstract screening, 25 articles were identified for full-text review, and ultimately 10 articles were included for qualitative analysis. The specific literature screening process is presented according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines53 (see Figure 1).Fig. 1PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the 11 included studies was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool (see Figs. 2 and 3). The majority of studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in key domains. In the domains of the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and missing outcome data, the vast majority of studies showed a low risk of bias, with only Ghiglino et al.23 rated at high risk for the randomization process. However, the ”selection of the reported result” was the domain that raised the most issues, with six studies10,11,22,26,30,34 rated as having some concerns, primarily related to insufficient information regarding pre-registered study protocols. Regarding the overall risk of bias, four studies were ultimately rated as ”low risk,” six as having ”some concerns (unclear risk of bias),” and one study as ”high risk” due to its randomization flaws. In summary, the overall methodological quality of the included studies is moderate. The main source of potential bias identified was in the selective reporting of results, highlighting a need for greater transparency through study pre-registration in future trials.Fig. 2Risk of bias graph.Fig. 3Risk of bias summary.

Discussion

This study conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to quantitatively assess the overall effects of robot presence on human cognitive task performance. Consistent with our first hypothesis, the overall results showed that robot presence did not produce statistically significant overall effects on either task time efficiency or task accuracy. Furthermore, in partial support of our second hypothesis, when robots were categorized according to their interactive behaviors and morphological characteristics, the results revealed significant differential patterns: regarding task time efficiency, the presence of interactive robots was associated with improved task time efficiency, while the presence of non-interactive robots and non-anthropomorphic robots was associated with impaired efficiency. However, anthropomorphic robot presence showed no significant effects, suggesting morphology’s moderating role may be more complex than hypothesized. These patterns are consistent with what social facilitation and social inhibition theories would predict, suggesting that robots may elicit social presence responses similar to those triggered by human presence16,17,22,23. This finding aligns with existing research perspectives, indicating that robots can produce influences in different directions on participants depending on their interactive status and degree of anthropomorphism10. Notably, these two dimensions may shape social presence through distinct pathways: interactivity through behavioral cues that establish social roles24, and morphology through appearance features that trigger attributions of agency31.

The differential effects caused by robot interactivity may stem from the different social roles that robots assume in the task context24. When robots participate in collaboration through responsive verbal or non-verbal means during tasks, they may assume the role of collaborative partners. This active engagement may establish a clearer social context, consistent with conditions under which social facilitation effects are typically observed27. However, when robots explicitly position themselves as ”expert observers” through verbal communication, their interactive behavior may be associated with evaluative contexts16,17,22. Unlike the creativity inhibition effects observed by Geerts et al. in brainstorming tasks21, the meta-analysis results showed improved task execution efficiency. This difference may stem from task nature: the studies in the robot interactivity subgroup primarily used attentional control tasks such as Stroop and Flanker, which require focused processing and inhibition of irrelevant information16,17,22, whereas creative brainstorming relies on divergent thinking54. Although both are cognitive tasks, these contrasting findings suggest that the effect of robot interactivity may vary depending on cognitive task characteristics.

In contrast, non-interactive robots lack the behavioral cues necessary to establish clear social roles. Social presence depends not only on an agent’s mere presence but also on observable behaviors that communicate social intent and engagement20, and such interactive behaviors contribute to the formation of clear mental models of a robot’s social role32. Without such active role construction, non-interactive robots may function primarily as ambiguous social stimuli. Indeed, the lack of interactivity has been shown to significantly alter the patterns of robot influence on human cognitive processes55. According to distraction-conflict theory, ambiguous social stimuli can create competing attentional demands and increase cognitive load14, and robot presence has been associated with evaluative concerns in prior research8. These mechanisms may jointly consume limited cognitive resources, thereby manifesting as performance impairments consistent with social inhibition patterns.

Furthermore, the non-significant effects of anthropomorphic robot presence on task efficiency align with the meta-analysis conclusions of Roesler et al.56. They pointed out that while anthropomorphism can enhance users’ subjective perceptions (such as likability), it has not shown consistent positive effects on objective task performance. This suggests that in contexts lacking strong interaction, human-like appearance alone is insufficient to translate into substantial facilitation of task efficiency.

Compared to anthropomorphic robots, the presence of non-anthropomorphic robots showed significant inhibitory effects on task efficiency. This pattern may relate to unclear social roles: research suggests that the lack of human-like appearance weakens attribution of agency, intentionality, and mentality57. According to social facilitation theory, the facilitative effects of social presence depend on the recognition of others as social agents8,12. Non-anthropomorphic robots, lacking human-like features, may therefore fail to elicit such facilitative responses. Instead, from the perspective of distraction-conflict theory, their presence may function as task-irrelevant stimuli that compete for attentional resources and increase cognitive load58. These results are consistent with the view that non-anthropomorphic robots, due to their limited social attributes, may be more likely to become sources of attentional interference, thereby undermining task efficiency.

Although our study has some significant findings, we acknowledge that limitations still exist. First, the limited number of included studies hindered our ability to enhance the statistical power of the results. Second, despite conducting categorical analyses based on interactivity and anthropomorphism, the considerable diversity in robot types and experimental tasks means that other uncontrolled confounding variables may still affect the stability of results. For example, factors such as experimental environment settings and individual differences among participants may all have moderating effects on robot presence effects. Moreover, although these classifications were theoretically grounded and independently verified, they inherently involve subjective judgment, meaning different researchers might classify the same robots differently, which could affect the reliability of moderator analyses. Future meta-analyses could adopt more standardized classification criteria and include more diverse morphologies (e.g., zoomorphic robots) to address these limitations. Third, potential publication bias may exist, as our assessment revealed selective reporting concerns in the majority of included studies, with insufficient information regarding pre-registered study protocols highlighting the need for greater transparency in future research. Finally, due to significant differences in measurement methods and assessment content for indicators such as subjective experience, psychological states, and physiological arousal in existing studies, this study primarily focused on meta-analysis of objective indicators such as task time efficiency and accuracy. This limited our in-depth understanding of the underlying mechanisms of robot presence effects. Future research needs to expand sample sizes, further control for homogeneity in experimental conditions, and establish standardized multidimensional measurement systems to comprehensively reveal the mechanisms by which robot presence influences human behavior.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis reveals that while the overall presence of a robot has no significant net effect on human cognitive task performance, a robot’s interactive capability is a critical moderator that determines the direction of the effect. Specifically, interactive robots that actively establish a social role tend to produce a social facilitation effect, improving task time efficiency. In contrast, non-interactive and non-anthropomorphic robots are more likely to cause a social inhibition effect, impairing task efficiency. Future research can therefore focus on how specific social roles constructed through interaction moderate performance under various task conditions. Furthermore, establishing standardized subjective measurement systems is crucial for a deeper, quantitative synthesis of the underlying psychological mechanisms.
