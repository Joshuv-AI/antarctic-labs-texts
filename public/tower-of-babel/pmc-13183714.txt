Toward nanomedicine-enabled RNA therapeutics for Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Goknur Kara, Sonia Villapol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common cause of dementia, is driven by intersecting proteopathic and inflammatory processes, including amyloid-β aggregation, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and progressive neuronal loss. Current therapies remain insufficient to address its multifactorial nature. RNA-based therapeutics, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and messenger RNAs (mRNAs), enable precise modulation of disease-relevant pathways. However, their clinical translation in AD is constrained by poor stability, immunogenicity, and limited delivery across the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Nanotechnology has enabled clinically successful RNA delivery in several non-CNS indications, yet nanoparticle (NP)-mediated nucleic acid delivery has not been evaluated in AD clinical trials to date. In this review, we integrate the emerging clinical landscape of CNS-directed RNA therapeutics with the preclinical evidence supporting NP-enabled delivery to AD-relevant targets and cell types, and we highlight design features that enhance stability, BBB transport, endosomal escape, and cellular selectivity. We further delineate the key translational requirements to advance these platforms from proof-of-concept to first-in-human studies, including scalable, reproducible manufacturing; rigorous safety and tolerability assessments; mitigation of innate immune activation; and consistent, quantifiable brain exposure and target engagement. Finally, we discuss next-generation strategies, such as multifunctional, stimulus-responsive nanocarriers and combinatorial RNA payloads, aimed at addressing AD heterogeneity and enabling durable, mechanism-based disease modification.Keywords: ASO, SiRNA, MiRNA, MRNA, Nanoparticle, Drug delivery, BBB, Alzheimer’s disease, Neurodegeneration, Nanotechnology

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60–80% of cases worldwide [1]. Currently, ~ 57 million people live with dementia, of which ~40 million have AD; prevalence is projected to reach 150 million by 2050 [2, 3], placing unprecedented social and economic burdens on healthcare systems. Women are disproportionately affected [4], and both lifestyle factors (e.g., poor diet, sedentary behavior, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, head trauma) and genetic risks contribute to disease onset and progression [5, 6]. Beyond its devastating cognitive, behavioral, and functional impairments, AD imposes an enormous global economic burden exceeding $1 trillion annually [3]. Despite decades of intensive research, treatment options remain limited.

Currently approved therapies fall into two categories: symptomatic drugs (acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs) and memantine), which provide modest and temporary relief, and disease-modifying monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid-β (Aβ) (aducanumab, lecanemab, donanemab). While these antibodies reduce amyloid burden, their clinical effects are modest, their use is constrained by amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), and their delivery requires costly and burdensome infusions with ongoing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) monitoring. Importantly, none of these therapies adequately addresses tau pathology, neuroinflammation, or the multifactorial nature of AD pathogenesis. This persistent gap underscores the need for innovative disease-modifying therapies (DMTs). RNA therapeutics, now a rapidly expanding drug class, offer a programmable, versatile platform to target previously “undruggable” pathways in AD [7].

The repeated failures of traditional small molecules and antibody approaches highlight the need for new therapeutic strategies that target upstream molecular drivers with greater precision and flexibility. RNA therapeutics offer such potential. With more than 20 RNA-based drugs now FDA- or EMA-approved, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and messenger RNA (mRNA)-based vaccines, this drug class has demonstrated clinical viability. RNA molecules can silence, edit, or replace specific genetic signals, expanding the druggable genome far beyond the reach of conventional compounds. However, enthusiasm must be tempered by recognition of key barriers: RNA instability, poor pharmacokinetics, immune activation, and, most critically, the difficulty of delivering these molecules across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) into the central nervous system (CNS). Nanomedicine provides a promising solution by shielding RNA, enhancing circulation, and enabling targeted delivery to the brain.

This review summarizes the status of RNA therapeutics for AD, the emerging role of nanotechnology in overcoming delivery challenges, and the opportunities and limitations of this rapidly evolving field. We highlight where progress has been made, where translation has stalled, and what critical steps are required for RNA drugs to achieve meaningful clinical impact in AD.

Alzheimer’s disease pathology and limitations of current therapies.

Clinically, AD is divided into two main subtypes: early-onset familial AD and late-onset sporadic AD [8]. Familial AD [9, 10], though rare, is caused by autosomal-dominant mutations in APP, PSEN1, or PSEN2, leading to aberrant processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP) and overproduction of the neurotoxic Aβ42 peptide [11]. Sporadic AD, the far more prevalent form, arises after age 65 and lacks a single genetic cause [1, 8], though the ApoE4 allele represents the strongest risk factor, substantially increasing disease susceptibility [11, 12]. ApoE, primarily produced by astrocytes and microglia, plays key roles in lipid transport, neuronal signaling, and neuroinflammation, underscoring its central contribution to AD pathophysiology [13]. Neuropathologically, AD is defined by the extracellular accumulation of Aβ plaques [14] and the intracellular aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau into neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) [15]. These proteinopathies trigger a cascade of deleterious processes, including synaptic dysfunction [16], oxidative stress [17], mitochondrial and bioenergetic impairment [9], neuroinflammation [18], and, ultimately, widespread neuronal death [19]. APP, a glycoprotein involved in neuronal growth and transport, becomes pathogenic when cleaved via the amyloidogenic pathway, generating Aβ oligomers that disrupt synaptic integrity [20, 21]. Downregulation of APP mRNA yields potentially beneficial effects on Aβ biology. Nevertheless, APP modulation influences the production of all its cleavage products, and although Aβ is the main factor in AD, the significance of diminishing other bioactive species warrants careful consideration [22].

Aβ peptides are generated by the sequential proteolytic processing of APP, a type I transmembrane protein with a large extracellular domain, which is expressed predominantly in neurons [23]. APP undergoes two types of cleavage: non-amyloidogenic and amyloidogenic, depending on which secretase enzyme is involved. The non-amyloidogenic pathway occurs under normal physiological conditions. Cleavage by α-secretase releases the extracellular soluble APP domain (sAPPα) from the N-terminus, thereby preventing Aβ formation and demonstrating neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects [24, 25]. In contrast, the amyloidogenic pathway begins with cleavage by β-site APP-cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1), producing a C-terminal fragment that is then processed by γ-secretase to generate Aβ peptides of varying lengths, most notably the most abundant Aβ40 and the more aggregation-prone Aβ42 [14, 26]. A shift towards the production of Aβ42 relative to Aβ40, or an imbalance in the overall synthesis and clearance of Aβ peptides, predisposes to the formation of neurotoxic oligomers (AβO), fibrils (AβF), and plaques [26], which results in synaptic dysfunction, neuronal damage, neurotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation [25]. Studies have demonstrated that ASO-mediated downregulation reduces Aβ levels, is associated with memory improvement, and reduces cytotoxicity [27]. Delivery of an ASO that induces translational blockage of the APP transcript near the β-cleavage site (ODN) successfully reduced Aβ levels while promoting α-secretase over β-secretase cleavage in mice. Interestingly, downregulation of APP was also shown to result in a 40% increase in sAPPα. [28]sAPPα has mainly been linked to neuroprotective roles, and reintroducing sAPPα, whether partially or fully, restored deficits in APP knockout mice, including decreased brain weight, impaired long-term potentiation (LTP), and spatial learning [29]. Considering the catabolic byproducts following APP downregulation underscores the importance of dose-controlled, pathway-aware APP-targeting strategies for chronic therapeutic applications in AD.

Similarly, tau, a microtubule-associated protein essential for axonal stability and synaptic plasticity [30], becomes pathogenic when excessively phosphorylated, leading to tangle formation and disruption of axonal transport [31]. Tau is mainly a neuronal microtubule-associated protein encoded by the MAPT gene, which plays a critical role in microtubule stabilization and axonal transport [32]. Alternative splicing that includes or excludes exons 2, 3, and 10 of the MAPT gene produces six major tau isoforms in the adult human brain. These tau isoforms differ in their protein structures depending on the number of N-terminal inserts (0N, 1 N, or 2 N) and the microtubule-binding region (three-repeat, 3R, or four-repeat, 4R) [33]. The 4 R tau isoform includes exon 10, resulting in 4 microtubule-binding repeats, whereas the 3 R tau lacks this exon. Typically, the human brain maintains a balanced ratio of 3R and 4R tau isoforms; however, this equilibrium is frequently disrupted in various neurodegenerative diseases [34]. Although AD is characterized by the accumulation of both 3R and 4R tau isoforms [35], the contribution of individual isoforms to AD pathology remains largely unexplored [36]. In AD, Tau undergoes abnormal post-translational modifications, most prominently hyperphosphorylation at multiple serine and threonine residues. Pathological phosphorylation of tau occurs at serine 214 (S214), threonine 231 (T231), and serine 262 (S262), thereby decreasing tau's ability to bind microtubules and to self-aggregate into oligomers, leading to synaptic dysfunction and ultimately neuronal cell death [37].

Despite decades of research, AD remains a chronic, progressive, and untreatable condition. Current therapies are limited to two broad categories: symptomatic agents and DMTs [38]. Symptomatic drugs include AChEIs (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, tacrine) [39] and the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist memantine [3]. These agents temporarily improve neurotransmission, providing modest cognitive and functional benefits for 6–12 months [40]. However, they do not halt neurodegeneration and often carry adverse effects such as gastrointestinal disturbances (AChEIs) or over-sedation (memantine). More recently, anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies [41], aducanumab [42], lecanemab [43], and donanemab [44]have been approved and have been used to slow disease progression. Each targets distinct Aβ species, yet their efficacy remains modest [45]. Substantial drawbacks, including ARIA, the need for frequent intravenous infusions, and extensive MRI monitoring, offset clinical benefits [46]. Moreover, treatment costs and logistical challenges limit widespread accessibility. Collectively, these issues underscore the inadequacy of current immunotherapies in delivering durable, safe, and cost-effective disease modification [45]. The persistent failures of clinical trials reflect fundamental challenges in AD drug development. These include the complexity of AD pathophysiology, divergent hypotheses surrounding amyloid and tau biology, and the difficulty of achieving therapeutic concentrations across the BBB. As a result, more than 30 years of attempts to develop effective DMTs have yielded limited success. Approximately 30% of the current AD pipeline still targets Aβ or tau, while the majority is shifting toward novel pathways [38]. Nevertheless, the limited brain penetration of biologics and the narrow efficacy window of existing therapies highlight the need for alternative strategies.

Although many NP-based AD therapies have shown promising results in preclinical studies, they remain far from clinical use. This may be due to poor BBB penetration, inadequate delivery to the brain, and extensive peripheral accumulation of NPs, which hinder their efficacy and raise concerns about potential toxicity [47]. When administered systemically, nanocarriers often adsorb serum proteins to form a "protein corona," which changes their pharmacokinetics, targeting accuracy, and immunogenicity [48]. Moreover, nanomaterials may trigger an autoimmune response, as they can be cytotoxic and potentially cause neurotoxicity. Because the current low targeting efficiency necessitates higher doses to improve treatment, this also increases the risk of toxicity [49, 50]. Moreover, since these nanomaterials compromise BBB integrity, they can provide a route not only for therapeutic drugs but also for toxic substances and pathogens to access the brain.

Furthermore, NPs can disrupt normal cellular metabolism, leading to elevated ROS levels and altered gene expression [50]. Due to the necessity of AD treatment, repeated long-term injections of nanomaterials will inevitably accumulate in the body if not promptly and adequately degraded, particularly in brain tissue, potentially leading to brain damage. Hence, a comprehensive safety evaluation of nanomedicine should be conducted before clinical trials [51]. Moreover, long-term repeated use of nano-drugs may also cause resistance issues; the stimulating effect of nanomedicine on the CNS could lead to addiction [52]. Another point is the complexity of aged human brains, including inflammation and biomarker heterogeneity, as well as the fact that AD is a complex neurodegenerative disease caused by multiple pathogenic factors, which are poorly recapitulated by preclinical models, limiting their translational predictability.

Furthermore, large-scale production of NPs that meet Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards is crucial for clinical use. The high cost of synthesizing nanomaterials remains a challenge, as underdeveloped technology makes production complex and expensive. The high cost of nanomedicine therapies hinders their widespread use in treating AD, significantly impeding the clinical application of nanomedicines. Manufacturing processes should be optimized to ensure consistency, safety, and quality while reducing costs to make these treatments more accessible to patients. RNA-based therapeutics offer a unique opportunity to intervene at multiple levels of AD pathology, from reducing pathogenic APP or tau expression to modulating neuroinflammatory mediators and thus represent a promising next-generation approach, overcoming many of the barriers that hinder conventional therapies. These approaches may provide more flexible, precise, and personalized disease modification, making them an emerging frontier in AD treatment.

RNA therapeutics: current landscape

RNA-based therapies represent a transformative approach to overcoming the limitations of conventional small-molecule drugs, which can act on only a narrow fraction of the genome [53, 54]. Because RNA drugs can be designed to target virtually any transcript or protein, they substantially expand the scope of “druggable” sites, including many that were previously inaccessible [55]. This flexibility, combined with rapid programmability and scalable production, positions RNA as a versatile platform for precision therapeutics [56]. RNA-based therapies have emerged as a transformative class of medicines, with more than 20 drugs now approved by the FDA or EMA. These include ASOs, siRNAs, and mRNA-based vaccines, all of which demonstrate the ability to modulate disease-causing genes and proteins with a level of precision not achievable by conventional small molecules. Clinical successes such as Fitusiran for hemophilia, Inclisiran for hypercholesterolemia [57], Nusinersen for spinal muscular atrophy, and Olezarsen for familial chylomicronemia syndrome highlight the therapeutic breadth of RNA-based modalities [58]. The approval and widespread use of mRNA COVID-19 vaccines further accelerated momentum in the field during the pandemic, underscoring the scalability and versatility of RNA platforms. More recently, RNA-based strategies are beginning to enter the field of neurodegeneration, with promising early results from Mivelsiran (an siRNA targeting APP) [59]and MAPTRx (an ASO targeting tau) [60], suggesting that these approaches may hold therapeutic potential in AD. We will discuss these clinical trials further in Sect. "Challenges of nanoparticle platforms for RNA therapeutics in the brain" of this review.

Despite this progress, RNA-based drugs face significant challenges that limit their broader application, particularly in CNS diseases. RNA molecules are inherently unstable, prone to rapid degradation by nucleases, and exhibit poor pharmacokinetics [61]. Effective delivery into the brain poses an even greater obstacle, as the BBB excludes most systemically administered RNA drugs. Furthermore, RNA molecules may activate innate immune pathways, limiting dosing regimens and raising safety concerns for chronic use. These issues have historically restricted the use of RNA drugs to tissues with more accessible delivery routes, such as the liver, muscle, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) via intrathecal injection.

To overcome these barriers, nanotechnology has become an essential enabling tool. NP carriers, including lipid NPs (LNPs), polymeric NPs, and liposomes, can protect RNA from enzymatic degradation, prolong circulation, and enhance uptake into target tissues. This strategy has already proven successful in several FDA-approved products: Patisiran employs an LNP formulation to achieve efficient delivery to the liver [62], while both the BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccines rely on LNPs for stability and effective translation in humans [63]. Moreover, to date, more than 60 nanoparticle-based drugs have been approved for therapeutic or diagnostic use, with the majority based on polymeric, liposomal, or lipid-based formulations [64]. These advances illustrate the critical role of nanomedicine in translating RNA biology into viable clinical therapies and provide a foundation for adapting RNA-nanoparticle systems to the treatment of AD, where effective and sustained brain delivery remains the key challenge.

RNA therapeutics in AD

For neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, drug delivery is further complicated by the restrictive BBB. Overcoming this obstacle requires advanced delivery platforms that not only protect RNA in systemic circulation but also enable targeted, biocompatible transport to the brain at therapeutic doses while minimizing systemic toxicity [72]. Development of such nanosystems remains a central challenge in realizing the full promise of RNA therapeutics. In AD, RNA dysregulation has been increasingly implicated in disease progression [73]. Abnormal RNA profiles have been identified in the brain, CSF, and serum of AD patients, where they contribute to key pathological mechanisms including Aβ aggregation, tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and synaptic dysfunction [74]. Multiple classes of RNA molecules, including ASOs, siRNAs, miRNAs, mRNAs, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), are being explored for their therapeutic potential. Regulatory RNAs such as miRNAs, lncRNAs, piRNAs, and circRNAs act as key modulators of gene expression at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [75, 76], making them particularly attractive in AD intervention. While there are no approved RNA-based drugs for AD yet, the accumulating evidence for RNA’s involvement in disease pathways provides a strong rationale for their development as novel DMTs in neurodegeneration. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)

ASOs are short, synthetic single-stranded nucleic acids (14–30 nucleotides) designed to bind complementary RNA sequences through Watson–Crick base pairing [88]. By targeting protein-coding mRNAs or non-coding RNAs (e.g., miRNAs, lncRNAs), ASOs modulate RNA function via three main mechanisms [89]: (1) splicing modulation, where ASOs alter pre-mRNA processing by interfering with splicing factor binding [90, 91], (2) enzymatic RNA degradation [92], where RNase H1 or Argonaute 2 (Ago2) cleaves the RNA strand in ASO-RNA duplexes, and (3) steric hindrance, where ASOs use their physical bulk to block cellular machinery, thus preventing a target RNA from being translated into a protein [93] (Fig. 1). These mechanisms allow for either the degradation of pathogenic transcripts or the correction of aberrant splicing events, leading to changes in therapeutic gene expression. Clinically, ASOs are an advancing therapeutic class in neurodegeneration. A leading example is MAPTRx (BIIB080), designed to reduce tau expression by targeting tau mRNA. Phase 1b clinical trial data demonstrated a ~ 50% reduction in CSF tau and phospho-tau with favorable safety, and Phase 2 studies are ongoing. This highlights the promise of ASOs as a disease-modifying strategy in AD. Long-term oligonucleotide therapies raise important safety considerations, particularly given that APP- and tau-targeting strategies will require repeated dosing. The toxicity profile of ASO therapeutics depends on multiple factors, including sequence, chemical modifications, dosage, duration, and route of administration [94]. Reported adverse effects of ASOs include hepatotoxicity, renal toxicity, and hypersensitivity reactions, although these vary across compounds and generations [95]. Mechanistically, ASOs can exhibit nonspecific interactions with plasma proteins, potentially leading to off-target tissue accumulation or interactions with other drugs [96]. Notably, mipomersen, a 2′-O-methoxyethyl-modified ASO targeting apolipoprotein B-100, was associated with hepatotoxicity, which limited its clinical use [97].

Small-interfering RNAs (siRNAs)

siRNAs silence gene expression through the endogenous RNA interference (RNAi) pathway. After processing by Dicer, siRNAs are incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), where Ago2 directs the guide strand to complementary mRNA targets, leading to cleavage and degradation (Fig. 1). This mechanism enables potent and sequence-specific suppression of disease-associated genes. Compared to long double-stranded RNAs, siRNAs are shorter (20–30 nucleotides) and thus less likely to trigger immune responses [98], making them suitable for therapeutic use. Since the first description of RNAi in the late 1990 s, siRNA therapeutics have advanced substantially, with 7 FDA-approved drugs now available for metabolic, cardiovascular, and hematological disorders. These successes validate RNAi as a viable therapeutic strategy and highlight its potential in neurodegeneration. In AD, the leading candidate is Mivelsiran (ALN-APP), which targets APP. Complementing tau lowering, mivelsiran is an investigational, intrathecally delivered RNA interference therapeutic that targets APP to reduce its expression in the CNS, thereby intervening upstream of amyloidogenic processing and Aβ production [60]. The precise oligonucleotide sequences for mivelsiran and MAPTRx (BIIB080/IONIS-MAPTRx) are proprietary and have not been publicly disclosed. Collectively, these modalities exemplify transcript-centric disease-modifying strategies that aim to reset the pathogenic protein burden by constraining synthesis rather than neutralizing downstream aggregates. Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (hATTR) is an autosomal dominant, progressive disorder caused by mutations in the transthyretin (TTR) gene. TTR, primarily synthesized in the liver, functions as a carrier for thyroxine and retinol-binding protein [99, 100]. In hATTR, both mutant and wild-type TTR misfold and form amyloid deposits in peripheral nerves and organs such as the heart and gastrointestinal tract, leading to polyneuropathy and cardiomyopathy [101]. While liver transplantation and TTR stabilizers can slow disease progression, they do not fully prevent amyloid deposition [102]. Patisiran, the first FDA-approved RNAi therapeutic, is a double-stranded siRNA encapsulated in LNPs that enables targeted delivery to hepatocytes [62]. It binds a conserved sequence in the 3′ untranslated region of TTR mRNA, promoting its degradation and reducing circulating TTR protein levels and tissue deposition [103]. Long-term studies (up to 5–7 years) demonstrate sustained efficacy with an acceptable safety profile and no significant off-target effects [104]. However, treatment requires premedication to mitigate infusion-related and LNP-associated inflammatory responses [105].

Givosiran is the first FDA-approved N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)-conjugated RNAi therapeutic for the treatment of acute hepatic porphyria (AHP) [106]. AHP comprises a group of rare inherited disorders of heme biosynthesis, most commonly acute intermittent porphyria [107]. In these conditions, reduced hepatic heme levels lead to upregulation of delta-aminolevulinic acid synthase 1 (ALAS1), the rate-limiting enzyme in the pathway [108]. This results in the accumulation of neurotoxic intermediates, including delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and porphobilinogen (PBG), which contribute to acute neurovisceral attacks and multi-organ complications [109]. Givosiran selectively silences hepatic ALAS1 mRNA, thereby reducing the production of toxic metabolites and significantly lowering the frequency of acute attacks [110]. Prior preventive strategies, such as hemin administration or hormonal suppression, had limited efficacy and tolerability, highlighting the clinical impact of RNAi-based therapies in this disease.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs)

miRNAs are short noncoding RNAs (20–25 nucleotides) that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by guiding the RISC to target mRNAs, leading to translational repression or degradation [111, 112] (Fig. 1). Their ability to simultaneously modulate multiple targets makes them particularly suited for complex diseases such as AD, where numerous pathways converge. In AD, dysregulated miRNAs, including miR-124, miR-132, and miR-195 (Table 2), have been linked to key pathological processes such as Aβ accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation. Although it remains unclear whether these changes are causal or consequential, early alterations in miRNA expression profiles suggest they may actively contribute to disease onset and progression. Therapeutic strategies focus on restoring miRNA homeostasis using mimics to replace downregulated miRNAs [113] or antagomirs to inhibit overexpressed ones. Beyond their therapeutic potential, miRNAs are also being investigated as biomarkers due to their stage- and region-specific expression patterns in the AD brain and their detectability in CSF and serum. Together, these features highlight miRNAs as versatile tools for both intervention and diagnosis in AD [114].

Messenger RNAs (mRNAs)

mRNA therapeutics function by delivering synthetic transcripts that encode proteins, enabling cells to produce therapeutic or antigenic proteins directly [115, 116]. Structurally, mRNA contains a 5′ cap, 3′ poly(A) tail, untranslated regions, and an open reading frame (ORF), which together regulate stability and translation efficiency [117] (Fig. 1). The clinical success of mRNA vaccines for COVID-19, delivered via LNPs, has revitalized interest in mRNA technologies for broader therapeutic applications [118]. mRNA offers several advantages over conventional gene therapy: it can be expressed in non-dividing cells, avoids nuclear entry, and eliminates risks associated with viral vectors [119, 120]. Moreover, proteins are generated in their native form, enabling precise control of expression. For AD, mRNA-based therapies are emerging as promising candidates. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that intracerebroventricular delivery of neprilysin mRNA reduces Aβ accumulation in mice [85], while in vitro-transcribed (IVT) mRNA encoding tau-specific antibodies has shown potential as a novel tau-targeting immunotherapy [86].

Nanotechnology for RNA Delivery to the brain

Effective delivery of therapeutics to the brain remains one of the most formidable challenges in AD treatment. Current drugs such as rivastigmine, galantamine, donepezil, and memantine are administered orally for convenience but are limited by poor pharmacokinetics, non-selective activity, and off-target side effects [121]. These shortcomings are further compounded by the BBB, which blocks nearly all large molecules, including RNA-based drugs, and approximately 98% of small molecules from entering the brain [122]. As a result, only a small fraction of injected NPs reaches the brain, with the majority cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system.

Nanotechnology offers promising strategies for brain therapeutics to overcome these limitations [123]. The physicochemical properties of NPs, including size, structure, and surface charge, critically determine their circulation time, stability, and BBB penetration [124]. NPs ranging from 10–200 nm are considered optimal, as smaller particles are rapidly eliminated by renal filtration, while larger ones are sequestered by the spleen [125]. Surface modifications, such as PEGylation, extend circulation time and shield particles from degradation and immune clearance [126]. Charge is another key determinant: positively charged NPs can cross the BBB via adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT), though excessive cationic charge increases toxicity and immune clearance. Alternatively, receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) provides a more targeted approach, utilizing ligands such as transferrin, lactoferrin, angiopep-2, rabies virus glycoprotein, or mannose to direct NPs across endothelial barriers and into the brain. Beyond passive delivery strategies, external stimuli-responsive systems are emerging as precision approaches for CNS targeting.

Nanoparticle transport across the BBB primarily occurs via AMT [127] and r RMT [128]. AMT relies on electrostatic interactions between cationic NPs and the endothelial membrane, whereas RMT uses ligand-receptor binding to enable selective uptake and transcytosis across brain endothelium [127–131]. In AD, BBB integrity is partially compromised, with evidence of vascular leakage, tight junction disruption, and perivascular accumulation of blood-derived proteins [132]. While this increased permeability may facilitate some passive nanoparticle entry, it does not eliminate the need for active transport mechanisms. BBB dysfunction in AD is heterogeneous and stage-dependent [133], and pathological changes, such as altered interstitial fluid dynamics and vascular damage, may actually impair effective distribution within the brain parenchyma [134]. Consequently, nanocarrier design in AD must account for both impaired barrier function and altered brain microenvironment. Strategies that combine targeted transcytosis with controlled biodistribution and minimal off-target accumulation remain essential, rather than relying solely on passive diffusion through a compromised BBB.

Smart nanocarriers that respond to near-infrared light, magnetic fields, ultrasound, or electrical stimulation enable controlled release of therapeutic cargo at the disease site. In experimental AD models, such systems have shown promise; for example, Prussian blue NPs carrying BACE1 siRNA successfully opened the BBB under near-infrared stimulation and reduced amyloid pathology [135]. Moreover, NPs themselves may exert therapeutic effects by modulating amyloid aggregation, a property that can be tuned by adjusting their size, shape, charge, and surface chemistry [136]. The design of an ideal nanocarrier for AD therapy requires a balance of multiple characteristics: particle sizes between 10–100 nm, efficient BBB penetration, high drug-loading capacity, long-term stability, biocompatibility, biodegradability, low toxicity, and scalability for reproducible manufacturing [137, 138]. When optimized, such nanosystems could provide safe and effective delivery of RNA therapeutics to the brain, enabling disease-modifying strategies that are currently inaccessible with conventional drugs.

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)

LNPs have become one of the most widely studied and clinically validated nanocarrier platforms. Their clinical impact was first established with Doxil, a PEGylated liposomal formulation of doxorubicin approved in 1995 for solid tumors, and has since expanded to nucleic acid delivery [139]. The approval of the siRNA therapeutic Patisiran (Onpattro) in 2018 represented a landmark for RNAi-based medicine, using ionizable amino lipids such as DLin-MC3-DMA to achieve efficient encapsulation, endosomal escape, and reduced cytotoxicity [140].

LNP technology subsequently proved transformative in the development of the BioNTech/Pfizer and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines, which encapsulate mRNA in ionizable lipid, cholesterol, PEGylated lipids [141], and helper lipid mixtures to protect the RNA and enable cytosolic delivery [142]. These successes demonstrate the versatility of LNPs across therapeutic classes [143]. Despite their preclinical [144] and clinical impact, however, nucleic acid delivery efficiency and safety remain limited by physiological barriers, highlighting the ongoing need for optimization in brain-targeted therapies. RNA-based therapeutics delivered through engineered NPs can traverse the BBB and modulate pathogenic mechanisms in AD and related neurodegenerative disorders (Fig. 2). Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs)

SLNs are composed of physiologically compatible lipids, including triglycerides, fatty acids, or waxes, and stabilized by phospholipids with a diameter range of 10–1000 nm [145]. Their solid hydrophobic core enables the encapsulation of both hydrophilic and lipophilic agents, offering a safe and affordable alternative to polymeric systems for CNS applications [146, 147]. SLNs have demonstrated utility in preclinical AD studies, improving the delivery of compounds such as asiatic acid [148], ferulic acid [149], curcumin [150], and quercetin [151], each of which exerts neuroprotective or anti-amyloid effects. Their low toxicity, biocompatibility, and potential to cross the BBB make SLNs an attractive platform for AD drug development.

Liposomes

Liposomes are spherical vesicles formed by phospholipid bilayers surrounding an aqueous core, enabling the encapsulation of both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs. Their versatility has led to widespread clinical use in oncology and infectious disease, and they are increasingly being engineered for CNS delivery. Liposomes show promise in enabling targeted drug delivery to the brain by bypassing efflux transporters or targeting specific cell types [152]. Surface modification with proteins, antibodies, aptamers, or peptides can enhance specificity, while PEGylation extends circulation. In AD models, liposomes have been designed to deliver dual therapeutics (e.g., α-tocopherol and donepezil) [153], as well as diagnostic and therapeutic constructs that selectively bind Aβ oligomers [154]. These advances illustrate their potential as multifunctional agents for both early diagnosis and therapy in AD.

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs)

NLCs are next-generation systems composed of a mixture of solid and liquid lipids, creating structural imperfections that improve drug loading, reduce leakage, and allow sustained release [155]. Compared to SLNs and liposomes, NLCs offer greater stability and lower toxicity while accommodating both hydrophilic and lipophilic compounds [156]. Single- [157, 158] and dual-drug [159, 160] NLCs have been successfully engineered for AD therapies, highlighting their flexibility in combining neuroprotective or anti-amyloid agents.

Collectively, lipid-based nanocarriers represent a clinically validated and highly adaptable strategy for RNA delivery. Their proven success with siRNAs and mRNAs underscores their potential for translation to AD, although optimization for BBB penetration, safety, and long-term tolerability remains essential for clinical application.

Polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs)

PNPs, particularly those based on biodegradable polymers, are widely regarded as versatile carriers for CNS drug delivery. Their ability to encapsulate diverse therapeutic agents, including small molecules, peptides, proteins, and nucleic acids, makes them attractive platforms for treating complex disorders such as AD. PNPs allow controlled drug release, can be modified with targeting ligands to enhance BBB penetration [161, 162], and are often more stable, easier to scale, and achieve higher encapsulation efficiencies (80%–100%) compared to liposomes or micelles [163]. Additional advantages include a high surface-to-volume ratio, which enables lower dosing frequency, improved pharmacokinetics, and reduced off-target side effects [164, 165]. Nonetheless, challenges remain: conventional PNP fabrication often relies on organic solvents, raising environmental and safety concerns, and the stability of encapsulated therapeutics must be carefully managed [163]. Polymers used in biomedical applications can be natural or synthetic. Natural biopolymers such as albumin, gelatin, collagen, chitosan, alginate, hyaluronic acid, and starch offer biodegradability, low immunogenicity, and biocompatibility, though batch-to-batch variability can limit reproducibility [166]. In contrast, synthetic polymers such as polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), polycaprolactone (PCL), and polyethylene glycol (PEG) allow precise engineering of properties to optimize stability, degradation rates, and drug release profiles. Both biodegradable (PLA, PGA, PLGA) and non-biodegradable (polyurethane) variants have been explored for CNS delivery [167].

Below, we list some of the most promising PNPs to be used as an RNA carrier for brain targeting.

Chitosan nanoparticles

Among natural polymers, chitosan has gained particular attention. Its cationic nature enables electrostatic complexation with negatively charged oligonucleotides, stabilizing them against degradation while enhancing cellular uptake. Chitosan can transiently open tight junctions at the BBB and the nasal epithelium [168], making it suitable for intranasal delivery routes. Its mucoadhesive properties further extend drug residence time, providing a promising strategy for direct nose-to-brain delivery of therapeutic oligonucleotides in AD models [169].

Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles

Among synthetic polymers, PLGA is the most extensively used, with both FDA and EMA approval for parenteral administration [170]. PLGA NPs support controlled-release formulations for small molecules, peptides, and nucleic acids, and surface modifications (e.g., PEGylation or surfactant coating with Tween 80) enhance their ability to cross the BBB [171–173]. Incorporation of cationic polymers such as polyethylenimine (PEI) [174, 175], poly-L-lysine (PLL) [176], or amphiphilic cationic lipids [177] improves encapsulation of negatively charged RNAs, including siRNA, miRNA, and mRNA.

Poly(β-amino ester) (PBAE) nanoparticles

Another promising class is PBAEs, biodegradable cationic polymers notable for high gene transfection efficiency and reduced cytotoxicity due to hydrolyzable ester bonds. Their charge-reversible amine groups facilitate electrostatic binding with nucleic acids and promote delivery across cellular membranes. PBAEs can be blended with other polymers such as PEG, PLA, or PCL to generate block copolymers with tunable properties [178]. Preclinical studies demonstrate their potential as carriers for siRNA [179], miRNA [180], and mRNA [181] therapies in neurodegenerative disease contexts. Together, natural and synthetic PNPs provide highly adaptable and effective nanocarriers for CNS drug delivery. Their capacity for chemical customization, combined with established regulatory approval of key polymers such as PLGA, positions them as strong candidates for advancing RNA-based therapies in AD. Future efforts must focus on refining biocompatibility, overcoming production challenges, and improving targeted delivery to maximize their therapeutic potential.

Inorganic nanoparticles

Inorganic NPs, typically ranging from 1–100 nm, have emerged as promising tools for drug delivery and diagnostic applications in neurodegenerative diseases. They are biocompatible and hydrophilic, and compared to organic carriers, they are more stable, while offering unique physicochemical properties suitable for imaging, targeted delivery, and theranostics. The most widely studied inorganic platforms include iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs), carbon-based nanomaterials (CDs), gold NPs (AuNPs), and mesoporous silica NPs (MSNs) [182].

Iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs)

IONPs are well established in biomedicine, with clinical approval for agents such as Feridex and Fereheme. Their magnetic properties enable controlled targeting with external magnetic fields, while their surfaces can be functionalized to improve solubility and biocompatibility [183, 184]. In AD models, transferrin-modified L-arginine–coated IONPs loaded with melittin successfully crossed the BBB via transferrin receptor binding, reducing amyloid plaque burden in 5XFAD mice [185]. Beyond drug delivery, IONPs support multimodal applications, including MRI, hyperthermia, and combined diagnostic–therapeutic formulations [186]. However, their tendency to agglomerate necessitates polymer coatings to improve stability and minimize toxicity [187].

Carbon dots (CDs)

CDs are ultrasmall, fluorescent nanomaterials with excellent water solubility, biocompatibility, and photostability [188]. Their intrinsic photoluminescence enables real-time tracking in vivo, making them highly suitable for drug delivery and theranostics. In AD, Fe₃O₄@CDs loaded with curcumin have been used to label and disrupt amyloid fibrils [189]. CDs are conjugated with diverse drug molecules, including small molecules, peptides, or nucleic acids, through either covalent or noncovalent interactions mediated by surface functional groups [190]. Carbon nitride dots (CNDs) conjugated with memantine demonstrated efficient BBB penetration in zebrafish and suppressed tau aggregation, highlighting their therapeutic promise [191].

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs)

AuNPs exhibit unique optical, electronic, and catalytic properties, along with high biocompatibility and chemical stability [192]. Their large surface area enables strong interactions with proteins, thereby inhibiting Aβ aggregation [136, 193]. Preclinical studies show that 20-nm AuNPs at low doses reduce neuroinflammation, improve mitochondrial function, and rescue cognitive deficits in AD models [194]. In addition, their signal-enhancing capacity has been exploited in biosensor platforms for detecting AD biomarkers [195]. These multifunctional roles underscore their dual potential as therapeutics and diagnostic tools.

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs)

MSNs are characterized by high surface area, tunable pore size, and excellent stability, allowing them to encapsulate diverse bioactive molecules [196]. In AD models, MSNs have been engineered to deliver natural compounds such as curcumin [196], berberine [197], quercetin [198, 199], exhibiting high potential for the treatment of AD. Functionalized MSNs show efficient brain targeting and controlled drug release, with additional potential in imaging and early detection of AD pathology [200]. Together, inorganic NPs provide a versatile toolkit for addressing the dual challenges of therapeutic delivery and biomarker detection in AD. While their unique physicochemical properties have clear advantages, further work is needed to optimize biocompatibility, long-term safety, and reproducibility to enable clinical translation.

ASO nanotherapeutics in AD

The combination of ASOs with nanotechnology has produced multifunctional systems that enhance BBB penetration, improve stability, and enable cell-specific targeting in AD. Preclinical studies show that ASO nanocarriers can modulate Aβ, tau, and α-synuclein while allowing real-time monitoring (Fig. 3). Despite this promise, translation faces major hurdles, including physiological barriers, potential off-target effects, immunogenicity, and manufacturing challenges. ASO nanotherapeutics, therefore, remain an exciting but still experimental strategy that must prove safety and durability before advancing to clinical use.

TREM2-targeted ASOs

Wei et al. engineered liposomes co-loaded with a TREM2-targeting ASO and resveratrol, surface-modified with D-T7 peptides for BBB penetration and MG1 peptides for selective uptake by M1-type microglia [201]. Following intravenous injection in APP/PS1 mice, these nanostructures escaped endo/lysosomal degradation and accumulated in activated microglia within AD lesions. The dual approach restored phagocytic function: resveratrol mitigated oxidative stress by reducing ROS, while TREM2 silencing reprogrammed microglia toward a phagocytic phenotype, synergistically suppressing neuroinflammation.

miR-34a-targeted ASOs

Li et al. developed tetrahedral DNA framework NPs (TDFNs) incorporating functional ASOs, triphenylphosphine (TPP) for mitochondrial targeting, and cholesterol for BBB crossing [207]. In 3xTg-AD mice, bi-daily intravenous injections of TDFNs enabled simultaneous diagnosis and therapy: the fluorescently labeled ASO permitted imaging, while silencing of miR-34a modulated apoptotic pathways. Treatment improved neuronal survival, enhanced learning and memory, and reduced tau aggregation, highlighting the theranostic potential of ASO–DNA nanostructures.

α-Synuclein-targeted ASOs

Although amyloid and tau dominate AD pathology, evidence also implicates α-synuclein (α-syn) in disease progression [208]. Leitao et al. employed an ApoB11 peptide-based nanocarrier to deliver ASOs targeting α-syn [209]. APP/PS1 mice received monthly intraperitoneal injections beginning at 6 months of age. By 9 months, α-syn expression was significantly reduced, correlating with lower Aβ plaque burden, preserved hippocampal neurons, reduced astrogliosis, and improved spatial memory.

siRNA nanotherapeutics in AD

siRNA represents the most widely explored RNA nanotherapeutic strategy for AD. Unlike conventional single-target therapies that have repeatedly failed in clinical trials, siRNA nanomedicines offer the potential for multi-targeted modulation of AD pathology, addressing Aβ, tau, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neurovascular impairment in parallel. A wide variety of nanoplatforms have been engineered to enhance delivery across the BBB, improve stability, and achieve precise cellular targeting. By combining targeted gene silencing with multifunctional nanocarriers, these systems can simultaneously reduce Aβ and tau pathology, modulate microglia, correct metabolic dysfunction, and support regeneration. While preclinical studies consistently demonstrate improvements in pathology and cognition, translation to clinical practice will require overcoming key challenges in delivery efficiency, long-term safety, and large-scale manufacturing.

Targeting Aβ production and aggregation

A major focus of siRNA nanotherapeutics is silencing BACE1, the key enzyme in Aβ production. For example, Yang et al. designed multifunctional PEGylated dendrigraft poly-L-lysine (DGL) NPs modified with lectin and Aβ-binding peptides (KLVFF), co-loaded with BACE1 siRNA and rapamycin [210]. Administered intranasally, this formulation alleviated autophagy dysfunction and reduced Aβ pathology in AD mice. Similarly, Zhou et al. developed galactose-decorated polymeric NPs (Gal-NP@siRNA) stabilized through multiple noncovalent interactions, achieving enhanced circulation, brain accumulation, BACE1 knockdown, and cognitive improvement in APP/PS1 mice [202]. Innovative lipid-based approaches have also been explored: Xu et al. reported lactoferrin-functionalized LNPs co-delivering BACE1 siRNA and α-mangostin, which reduced Aβ production, reprogrammed microglia, and restored cognition in AD mice [211]. Dual-siRNA targeted layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanoparticle systems, which deliver BACE1 siRNA or glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) siRNA along with natural anti-inflammatory compounds like rutin, effectively silenced the target genes, reduced the abnormal Aβ/Tau burden, and restored memory and cognitive function in AD mice [212].

Targeting tau and related signaling pathways

Several nanocarriers extend siRNA targeting to tau-related mechanisms. Ding et al. encapsulated BACE1 siRNA in Prussian blue NPs with photothermal properties, achieving reduced Aβ deposition, inhibition of tau phosphorylation, and improved cognition under near-infrared stimulation [135]. Cai et al. further developed dual-targeting DGL NPs carrying both BACE1 siRNA and a tau phosphorylation inhibitor [213]. Functionalized with T7 and Tet1 peptides for BBB and neuronal targeting, these particles simultaneously reduced amyloid and tau pathology while protecting neuronal integrity. Similarly, leptin-receptor-targeted peptides were used by Kim et al. in 2024 to deliver gamma-secretase-activating protein (GSAP) siRNA, reducing Aβ accumulation and tau hyperphosphorylation while improving cognition in AD models.

Targeting neuroinflammation and microglial dysfunction

Microglia-specific modulation is another promising strategy. Jiang et al. reported an exosome-liposome hybrid nanovesicle co-encapsulating BACE1 siRNA and a TREM2 plasmid [214]. This dual system not only reduced Aβ production but also shifted microglia from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to a phagocytic M2 phenotype, thereby lowering plaque burden and neuroinflammation. Other hybrid nanoplatforms combine gene silencing with synergistic therapeutics, such as high-density lipoprotein-inspired nanoscavengers carrying BACE1 siRNA and curcumin, which were developed to modulate microglial dysfunction [215]. This system promoted Aβ clearance and inhibited neuroinflammation, restoring microglial function by blocking the NF-κB pathway, thereby leading to the rescue of memory deficits in AD mice.

Targeting metabolic and mitochondrial dysfunction

siRNA nanocarriers have also been engineered to address metabolic dysregulation and mitochondrial injury, both of which contribute to AD pathology. Gupta et al. demonstrated that PEG/PEI-functionalized graphene oxide nanosheets carrying GSK3β siRNA ameliorated insulin resistance-induced AD pathology in a sporadic AD rat model [203]. Zhang et al. co-encapsulated magnesium and siRNA against cyclophilin D (CypD) into liposomes modified with MMP9-activatable peptides, restoring mitochondrial function and alleviating neurovascular injury [212]. These studies highlight siRNA nanotherapeutics as versatile modulators of upstream metabolic and bioenergetic defects in AD.

Targeting other signaling pathways

A wide range of novel targets continues to expand the siRNA toolbox. Exosome-based systems modified with targeting ligands enable lesion-specific accumulation of siRNA cargos, where siRNAs could inhibit caspase-3 and BACE1 levels to reduce the apoptosis of neurons and the Aβ plaques after intranasal injection in AD mice [216]. Halloysite nanotubes conjugated with RVG29 peptide successfully delivered Ripk1 siRNA to Aβ plaques, reducing neuronal necroptosis and improving memory in AD mice [217]. In parallel, nanocarriers combining siRNA with regenerative approaches have been developed: Yu et al. co-delivered SOX9 siRNA and retinoic acid using antioxidative ceria-based metal–organic framework (MOF) nanostructures to neural stem cells, enhancing neurogenesis and improving cognition in triple-transgenic AD mice [218].

miRNA nanotherapeutics in AD

miRNAs play essential roles in regulating neuronal function, synaptic plasticity, and neuroinflammation, and their dysregulation contributes to AD pathology. Because many miRNAs are downregulated in AD (e.g., miR-132, miR-124, miR-195) or aberrantly upregulated (e.g., miR-17), restoring or silencing their activity through nanocarrier-mediated delivery has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach. miRNA nanotherapeutics demonstrate strong potential for treating AD by modulating multiple aspects of the pathology, including Aβ production and clearance, tau phosphorylation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation. Advances in nanocarrier design, such as lectin-modified polymers, peptide-functionalized liposomes, and DNA nanostructures, enable improved stability, brain targeting, and cell-specific delivery. While promising in preclinical models, translating miRNA nanomedicines will require further optimization of safety, dosing, and manufacturing for clinical application. Several recent studies highlight diverse nanoplatforms designed to enhance brain penetration, prolong circulation, and achieve cell-specific targeting.

miR-132 restoration

Su et al. developed a nasal delivery system using wheat germ agglutinin (WGA)-modified PEG-PLA NPs encapsulating miR-132 [204]. APP/PS1 mice treated every other day for 30 days showed reduced Aβ expression, increased synaptic proteins (SYN, PSD-95), decreased apoptosis, and improved cognitive performance. WGA modification enhanced BBB penetration and significantly outperformed naked or unmodified miR-132 NPs.

miR-186 restoration

Israel et al. reported a poly(β-L-malic acid) copolymer modified with tri-leucine (PMLA-LLL) and an Aβ-targeting D3 peptide, enabling BBB penetration and neuronal targeting [219]. Tail vein administration of PMLA/LLL/D3/miR-186 in 5XFAD mice led to deposition on Aβ plaques and modulation of multiple AD-related signaling pathways.

miR-124 restoration

miR-124, the most abundant miRNA in the healthy brain, is depleted in AD. Ouyang et al. designed DNA nanoflowers (DFs) generated by rolling circle amplification for miRNA delivery, functionalized with RVG29 for BBB targeting, and co-loaded with rutin [220]. This nanostructure (RDMR) displayed prolonged circulation, high brain uptake, and synergistic inhibition of BACE1 and APP, resulting in decreased Aβ burden and neuroinflammation with cognitive improvement in APP/PS1 mice.

miR-195 restoration

Downregulation of miR-195 promotes Aβ accumulation through dysregulation of APP and BACE1. Su et al. designed dual-modified liposomes functionalized with a mannose-derived compound (MAN) and the TAT cell-penetrating peptide, encapsulating PEI/miR-195 complexes (DPMT@PEI/miR-195). Intravenous delivery enhanced BBB penetration, reduced Aβ and tau pathology, modulated microglial polarization, and significantly improved cognition in APP/PS1 mice [221]. Notably, combination therapy with donepezil further potentiated cognitive benefits compared to either agent alone.

Inhibition of pathogenic miR-17

Aberrant upregulation of miR-17 impairs autophagy and promotes inflammation in AD microglia. Badr et al. engineered mannose-coated lipid NPs (MLNPs) carrying an antagomir against miR-17 [205]. Delivered via intra-cisterna magna injection in 5XFAD mice, Anti-17 MLNPs selectively targeted microglia, reduced inflammation, enhanced autophagy, and decreased Aβ pathology while improving spatial memory and reducing anxiety-like behavior.

mRNA nanotherapeutics in AD

mRNA-based therapies are emerging as a versatile strategy for treating neurodegenerative diseases, including AD. Unlike small molecules or protein therapies, mRNA therapeutics enable transient expression of therapeutic proteins or immunogens within the brain, offering precision control and reduced systemic toxicity. mRNA nanotherapeutics in AD highlight the versatility of this approach, spanning from cell-specific delivery of neurotrophic factors to active immunization against Aβ. Recent advances in nanocarrier design are helping to address long-standing challenges of stability, delivery, and safety within the CNS. Ongoing improvements in mRNA engineering, targeted expression, and LNPs formulations will be critical to translating these promising strategies into effective clinical therapies.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) mRNA delivery

Li et al. developed a PBAE-based nanoplatform to deliver engineered mRNA encoding BDNF directly to the CNS via brain ventricle infusion [206]. To increase stability and transcription efficiency, the BDNF mRNA sequence was optimized with AI-guided engineering. To prevent seizure risk from neuronal overexpression, the construct was modified with neuron-specific miR-124 target sequences in the 3′UTR, enabling selective degradation in neurons. As a result, the mRNA was preferentially translated in astrocytes, which restored BDNF levels and significantly improved memory in AD mice. This approach highlights the potential of mRNA therapeutics for precise, cell-type–restricted delivery of neurotrophic factors.

mRNA vaccine targeting Aβ

In parallel, mRNA vaccines are being investigated for AD prevention. Hovakimyan et al. reported a pilot study of AV-1959LR, an mRNA vaccine encoding the Aβ1–11 epitope, delivered via LNPs using the MultiTEP platform to enhance T-helper (Th) cell responses [87]. In both mice and non-human primates, the vaccine induced strong anti-Aβ antibody responses, with specificity for non-fibrillar regions of Aβ plaques. Antibody titers were equal to or greater than those elicited by the DNA-based vaccine counterpart (AV-1959D). These findings demonstrate the feasibility of mRNA vaccines for generating protective immunity in AD, though further optimization of sequence design and dosing is needed to balance efficacy and minimize LNP- or mRNA-related toxicity.

Synaptic protein mRNA delivery

Studies have shown that neuronal-derived exosomes in patients with AD or preclinical AD display decreased levels of several synaptic proteins, including synaptosome-associated protein 25 (SNAP25), growth-associated protein 43 (GAP43), neurogranin, and synaptotagmin 1 [222]. These proteins together can predict AD 5 to 7 years before symptoms appear, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets for the condition. RVG-modified exosome-based carriers loaded with mRNAs GAP43 and SNAP25 and intravenously administered to 5XFAD mice [223]. The levels of synaptic proteins increased in the brain, restoring synaptic function and thereby enhancing cognitive improvement in AD.

Clinical trials of RNA-based therapeutics in AD

To date, no clinical trials have employed NP-mediated nucleic acid delivery for AD. Instead, current clinical candidates, including siRNA and ASOs, rely on chemical modifications and direct CNS delivery approaches, such as intrathecal administration, to achieve target engagement. Early-phase studies have demonstrated reductions in disease-relevant biomarkers and acceptable safety profiles, although their long-term clinical efficacy remains to be established. These first-in-human gene-silencing strategies targeting amyloid and tau pathology highlight the therapeutic potential of RNA-based approaches, while underscoring the need for improved delivery technologies, particularly nanoparticle-based systems, to enhance brain targeting and broaden clinical applicability (Table 4).

Mivelsiran (ALN-APP)

ALN-APP is the first investigational RNAi therapeutic in development for early-onset AD (clinicaltrials.gov ID: NCT05231785) and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA; NCT06393712). This therapy is designed to reduce APP mRNA levels in the CNS, thereby lowering APP protein production and its downstream cleavage products, including Aβ. It employs Alnylam’s proprietary C16-siRNA conjugate technology, in which a 2′-O-hexadecyl lipid is attached to siRNA, enhancing CNS distribution following intrathecal administration [224, 225]. In a phase 1 double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, early-onset AD patients received single ascending intrathecal doses of 25 or 75 mg. Treatment led to dose-dependent decreases in CSF sAPPα (up to 71%) and sAPPβ (up to 83%) at day 15 in the high-dose group, with only mild to moderate adverse events reported [224]. A follow-up cohort tested single doses of 25, 50, or 75 mg and was monitored over six months. The drug was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events, and achieved sustained reductions in CSF Aβ42 (up to 37.9%) and Aβ40 (up to 55.7%) [59]. These findings provide proof of concept for siRNA-based suppression of APP in humans.

MAPTRx (BIIB080)

MAPTRx is the first ASO therapy to be clinically tested in AD, designed to selectively suppress microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT) mRNA and thereby reduce tau protein expression (NCT03186989). RNA-directed therapeutics provide a mechanistically upstream strategy to attenuate proteopathic cascades by reducing the synthesis of key driver proteins. MAPTRx (BIIB080/IONIS-MAPTRx) is designed to bind an intronic region of MAPT pre-mRNA, resulting in reduced total tau expression, and is expected to decrease both 3R and 4R tau isoforms. MAPTRx is a chemically modified, RNase H-competent ASO designed to bind an 18-nucleotide sequence within intron 9 of MAPT pre-mRNA, leading to RNase H1-mediated degradation of MAPT transcripts and suppression of tau translation [60]. By lowering MAPT mRNA levels at the transcript level, this approach is positioned to reduce total tau broadly (i.e., not restricted to a single aggregated species). It is therefore expected to impact tau across major isoform classes.

In a phase 1b first-in-human trial, monthly intrathecal dosing produced dose-dependent, durable reductions in CSF total tau and phosphorylated tau (p-tau181), with decreases of nearly 50% at 24 weeks in higher-dose groups. Importantly, treatment was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events [60]. A large phase 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of BIIB080 is currently underway (NCT05399888). This study will enroll over 700 participants aged 50–80 years with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD or mild AD, aiming to establish both clinical efficacy and long-term safety.

Challenges of nanoparticle platforms for RNA therapeutics in the brain

Preclinical studies favor intravenous and intranasal routes, whereas clinical trials predominantly use intrathecal delivery (Tables 3 and 4). Intrathecal delivery enables direct administration of oligonucleotides into the CSF, bypassing the BBB, reducing systemic exposure, and allowing less frequent dosing due to prolonged CNS half-life [226, 227]. In contrast, despite promising results in rodent models, intranasal delivery faces significant translational challenges, including anatomical differences, variable CNS exposure, unclear mechanisms of transport, and limited regulatory guidance [228, 229]. Addressing these limitations will require human-relevant in vitro and in silico models [230], advanced imaging to track brain uptake, AI-driven optimization of delivery, and long-term clinical studies assessing safety and efficacy across populations [231]. This reflects key translational barriers: non-invasive approaches exhibit low and variable CNS exposure, anatomical differences, and scaling challenges, making intrathecal administration the most reliable method for consistent target engagement in humans.

Advantages and disadvantages of various nanoparticle formulations for delivering RNA therapeutics to the brain

Lipid-based systems, including LNPs, liposomes, and NLCs, remain among the most advanced carriers for RNA delivery. LNPs have emerged as one of the most suitable platforms due to their low toxicity, scalability, and the ability to improve BBB penetration when modified with specific ligands and PEG. However, they suffer from a short circulation half-life without PEGylation and can trigger immune activation in the brain. Liposomes, already FDA-approved for several drugs, share many of the same strengths but are limited by rapid clearance and potential inflammatory risks if not properly engineered. NLCs offer enhanced stability, sustained release, and greater drug-loading capacity than conventional liposomes, with encouraging preclinical results in AD models, though clinical translation remains limited. PNPs represent another versatile platform. They can be biodegradable, self-assembling, and highly stable, with superior encapsulation efficiency and controlled release properties. When functionalized with ligands, PNPs achieve high BBB penetration, making them attractive for CNS applications. However, their production often involves organic solvents, which may compromise cargo integrity, and, like other nanocarriers, they carry the risk of immune activation. Among natural polymers, chitosan NPs stand out due to their cationic nature, which strongly binds to oligonucleotides, and their mucoadhesive properties, which support intranasal brain delivery. Nonetheless, their positive charge can lead to cytotoxicity and in vivo.aggregation. PBAEs have emerged as a promising class of synthetic biodegradable polymers, combining high transfection efficiency with lower toxicity than other cationic carriers. They can cross the BBB efficiently, but their application remains at the preclinical stage.

Inorganic platforms provide unique theranostic potential. IONPs can be loaded with RNA therapeutics and simultaneously function as MRI contrast agents, with the added benefit of magnetic targeting. However, their ability to generate ROS raises concerns about exacerbating neuroinflammation, and their clearance remains a challenge. Similarly, CDs combine intrinsic fluorescence with high BBB penetration, enabling simultaneous drug delivery and real-time imaging. Despite their promise, CDs remain primarily confined to preclinical research, with concerns about immune activation and limited clinical translation.

Additionally, long-term RNAi therapies raise concerns about off-target effects, immune activation, and cumulative toxicity, highlighting the need for longitudinal safety and efficacy evaluations to support clinical translation. Even biodegradable NPs may accumulate in peripheral organs such as the liver, spleen, and kidneys, primarily due to uptake by the reticuloendothelial system, potentially leading to toxicity if clearance mechanisms are insufficient [48]. Under repeated dosing regimens, incomplete elimination may result in chronic exposure and cumulative tissue damage, including potential neuroinflammatory effects. Quantitative biodistribution studies indicate that only a small fraction of systemically administered NPs reaches the brain. Analysis of published pharmacokinetic datasets using NPs biodistribution coefficients (%ID/g) shows predominant accumulation in the liver (~17.6% ID/g) and spleen (~12.1% ID/g).

In contrast, brain levels remain low (~0.3% ID/g), corresponding to approximately a 50-fold lower exposure in the brain than in the liver [266]. Other organs, including the kidneys, lungs, and heart, typically exhibit intermediate accumulation (< 5% ID/g). These distributions are strongly influenced by physicochemical properties such as size, surface charge, and composition.

While active targeting strategies can enhance functional delivery to the brain, for example, through ligand-modified LNPs, such approaches often improve cellular uptake and transgene expression rather than substantially altering overall biodistribution profiles [267]. Together, these findings highlight a central challenge in CNS nanomedicine: achieving sufficient brain exposure while minimizing off-target accumulation and long-term toxicity in peripheral organs.

Conclusions and future remarks

RNA-based therapeutics have demonstrated remarkable potential to regulate disease-associated proteins, a concept validated by the 2018 approval of Patisiran, the first siRNA therapy for human use. However, translating this success into the brain remains a formidable challenge due to the BBB’s restrictive nature and the inherent difficulties of neuronal transfection.

While siRNA therapies such as Patisiran and Givosiran have proven effective for liver disorders, their application in neurodegenerative diseases like AD faces greater obstacles, fueling skepticism regarding the feasibility of CNS gene therapy [268]. For instance, systemically administered therapeutic antibodies reach the brain at only 0.01%–0.1% of plasma levels [124], underscoring the need for more efficient delivery strategies. Encouragingly, early clinical successes with Mivelsiran (siRNA) and MAPTRx (ASO), delivered intrathecally without nanocarriers, have demonstrated target engagement and biomarker reduction in AD patients. Yet, large-scale safety and efficacy data remain essential before these strategies can be broadly adopted. A deeper understanding of the CNS microenvironment, including receptor expression patterns in AD brains, will be critical for advancing precision delivery systems. Nanotechnology offers powerful solutions to these challenges. Multifunctional lipid-, polymeric-, and inorganic-based nanocarriers can shield RNA payloads, enhance BBB penetration, and achieve cell-specific targeting through ligand functionalization and stimuli-responsive designs. These systems also provide opportunities for controlled release, protection from enzymatic degradation, and co-delivery of multiple therapeutics. Nonetheless, clinical translation faces significant hurdles: ensuring large-scale manufacturing, maintaining long-term stability, minimizing immune activation, and achieving reproducible pharmacokinetics across heterogeneous patient populations. Given the multifactorial nature of AD, future nanotherapeutics must address not only amyloid and tau pathologies but also oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation. Emerging strategies include multifunctional platforms that co-deliver siRNAs, ASOs, or miRNA mimics to target multiple pathological pathways simultaneously. Decorating nanocarrier surfaces with brain-specific ligands and engineering responsive systems that release their cargo in the pathological microenvironment are promising design features for next-generation therapeutics.

Although current AD clinical programs primarily rely on chemically modified oligonucleotides administered intrathecally, NPs-mediated nucleic acid delivery has not yet been evaluated in AD trials. This gap reflects several key translational challenges: achieving reproducible and therapeutically meaningful brain exposure after systemic delivery, particularly when scaling from rodent models to humans, remains difficult; safety and immunogenicity concerns require extensive long-term evaluation, including off-target organ accumulation and potential neuroinflammatory effects; and manufacturing, characterization, and regulatory requirements for complex multifunctional NPs (e.g., GMP scale-up, batch consistency, stability, and quality control) are substantially more demanding than for simpler oligonucleotide approaches.

Nonetheless, elements of this “convergence” are emerging at two levels: (i) clinical validation of RNA delivery platforms in non-CNS indications, and (ii) extensive preclinical evidence demonstrating that nanocarrier-enabled RNA delivery can engage AD-relevant targets and pathways in vivo. Translating this progress into clinical application will require key operational advances, including scalable GMP manufacturing, IND-enabling safety and biodistribution/pharmacokinetic packages with quantitative brain exposure and target engagement benchmarks, strategies to mitigate innate immune activation, and trial designs incorporating mechanism-based biomarkers and patient stratification. Looking ahead, integrating emerging technologies, such as AI-guided nanocarrier optimization and patient-derived neuronal models, could accelerate the development of safe and effective systems tailored to individual patients. By combining the precision of RNA therapeutics with the versatility of nanotechnology, the field is poised to advance toward the first truly effective DMTs for AD. With growing momentum and continued innovation, RNA-based nanomedicine is poised to play a central role in the future of personalized, precise treatments for neurodegenerative diseases. Together, these advances position RNA therapeutics and nanotechnology at a critical inflection point, where overcoming translational barriers could enable truly disease-modifying interventions for AD.
