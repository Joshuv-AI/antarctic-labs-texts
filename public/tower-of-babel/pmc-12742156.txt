Association between body roundness index and kidney stone risk in Chinese adults: an ultrasound-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Wei Zhang, Tianchi Hua, Shengqi Zheng, Shangchun Fei, Yifan Li, Qibing Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveObesity is a key risk factor for kidney stones (KS). The body roundness index (BRI) is a newer measure for assessing body fat distribution. Studies in Western populations have linked BRI to KS risk, but no such research has been conducted in Asian populations. This study aimed to examine the association between BRI and KS in Chinese adults.Materials and methodsThis cross-sectional study included 78 386 Chinese adults who underwent abdominal ultrasound examination. Data on demographic characteristics, clinical history and laboratory parameters were collected. The diagnosis of KS was based on the ultrasound findings. Multivariable logistic regression examined the association between the BRI and KS risk. The analysis incorporated subgroup analyses, dose-response relationship assessment and mediation analysis.ResultsMultivariable-adjusted logistic regression analyses revealed that the BRI was significantly positively correlated with KS risks in both genders, with a more pronounced effect observed in males (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.20) compared with females (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.20). Participants in the highest BRI tertile had a 47% and 23% higher KS risk in males and a 23% higher KS risk in females, respectively, compared with the lowest tertile. Dose-response analysis showed a non-linear relationship between BRI and KS risk in both males and females. Mediation analysis indicated that low-density lipoprotein cholesterol accounted for 7.2% of the association in females, whereas hypertension (14.8%) and uric acid (12.5%) were the primary mediating factors in males. Subgroup analyses confirmed that the association was modified by age and diabetes in females and by diabetes in males.ConclusionThe findings demonstrate a significant association between elevated BRI and increased KS risk, underscoring the necessity of implementing gender-specific strategies and considering mediating factors in KS prevention.

Introduction

Kidney stones (KS) are a common urological condition with a rising prevalence over recent decades and a recurrence rate reaching up to 50% within 5 years.1 2 While many KS patients remain asymptomatic for extended periods, acute stone episodes can lead to hydronephrosis, renal insufficiency and even death.3 Understanding the risk factors associated with KS formation is therefore essential. KS represents a multifactorial disease, with its occurrence and progression influenced by various factors including lifestyle, dietary habits and environmental conditions.1 4 Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome (MetS) are significant risk factors for stone formation.58 Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of these risk factors is crucial, as it provides valuable clinical insights for developing effective preventive strategies and personalised therapeutic approaches.

Obesity is a global health issue linked to increased quality of life and increased risk of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes and cancer.911 Obesity has been identified as a significant risk factor for the formation of KS.12 It may lead to increased urinary excretion of principal stone-forming constituents, including calcium, oxalate, phosphate and uric acid (UA), in KS formation.7 13 14 Epidemiological evidence has demonstrated a higher incidence of KS among obese individuals.15 Body mass index (BMI) has long been a widely recognised indicator of obesity, but it does not provide information about body fat distribution. Thomas et al16 developed the body roundness index (BRI) by incorporating measurements of body height (BH), waist circumference (WC) and hip circumference to estimate visceral adipose tissue and body fat percentage. Studies have found that BRI demonstrates a clear advantage over BMI in terms of diagnostic ability for KS.17 18 Meanwhile, a study by Mao et al19 indicated a positive correlation between BRI and KS risk in the US population, while no relevant studies have been conducted in Asian populations so far. This study aims to explore the link between BRI and KS risk in Chinese adults. Abdominal ultrasonographic examination was employed as a diagnostic tool to determine the presence of KS in study subjects. Through analysing the association between BRI and KS, we aim to provide methodological foundations for KS prevention and early intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Research population

This investigation was conducted at the Health Management Centre of Yangzhou University Affiliated Hospital throughout 2022. From an initial pool of 113 947 eligible participants who underwent health screening, we excluded 35 561 individuals due to incomplete baseline clinical data. The final analytical cohort comprised 78 386 participants (31 808 females and 46 578 males), as shown in figure 1. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with both the ethical standards of the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University (2023-YKL01-Ke13) and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments.

Participant selection process diagram.

Clinical data collection and assessment

The primary outcome variable was the presence of KS. Comprehensive medical histories of all participants were systematically collected by practitioners at the Health Management Centre, encompassing general demographic characteristics including age, gender, surgical history, BH, WC and medical history. The diagnosis of KS was performed using a LOGIQ E9 colour Doppler ultrasound system (GE HealthCare) with a probe frequency of 3.5–5.0 MHz, operated by experienced sonographers.

The primary exposure variable in this study was the BRI, which was calculated according to the previously validated formula: BRI=364.2–365.5×√｛1−[(WC/2π)/(0.5×BH)]2｝. BRI primarily reflects visceral adipose tissue accumulation, with elevated BRI values indicating increased visceral adiposity and a higher predicted risk of MetS.20 In multivariate adjusted models, covariates included age, gender, serum total protein (TP), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), serum creatinine (Scr), UA, diabetes and hypertension.

Laboratory measurements

Blood samples were collected from all participants in the morning after a fasting period of at least 12 hours. Venous blood was drawn for comprehensive biochemical analysis, including measures of liver and kidney function, serum TP, Scr and UA, as well as lipid profiles, TG, HDL-C and LDL-C. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation based on Scr levels, sex and age.

All laboratory measurements were performed using an automated biochemical analyser (C16000, Abbott Laboratories, USA). The instrument was calibrated prior to the tests to ensure accuracy, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with standard protocols.

Statistical methods

Multiple logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between BRI, BRI tertiles and KS prevalence across three progressive models. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 incorporated adjustments for age, hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Model 3 extended these adjustments to include TP, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, Scr and UA, building on Model 2. The variance inflation factor for all screened covariates was less than 5, confirming the appropriateness of including all variables in the final adjusted model analysis. Statistical significance was set at a p value of less than 0.05. The objective was to confirm a robust total association between BRI and KS risk after controlling for all known potential confounders. Having established the presence of a robust association, we further applied the percentile-based non-parametric bootstrap CI method to quantify the proportion of the mediation effect accounted for by the covariates. All analyses were performed using EmpowerStats 4.2 (www.empowerstats.com; X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA).

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Gender-stratified analysis of the association between body roundness index and kidney stone risk

Considering the potential impact of sex-related physiological and metabolic differences on KS risk, we performed gender-stratified analyses for male and female populations. A significant positive association between BRI and KS prevalence was identified in both gender groups, as demonstrated in subsequent analyses. This association was particularly pronounced in the male population after full adjustment in model 3 (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.12, 1.20). When stratifying BRI into tertiles, compared with the lowest tertile, participants in the highest tertile demonstrated significantly elevated KS risk, with a 47% increase in males (OR 1.47, 95% CI 1.34, 1.62) and a 23% increase in females (OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.03, 1.48), as shown in table 2.

In the adjusted analysis, the association between BRI and KS risk was differentially affected by gender. Among female participants, this association weakened after adjustment for potential confounders. In contrast, male participants demonstrated a consistent association between increased BRI and KS risk, which remained significant after adjustment for multiple covariates.

Dose-response relationship between body roundness index and risk of kidney stone stratified by gender

The relationship between BRI and KS was examined using generalised additive model regression and smooth curve fitting. The analysis revealed a linear relationship between BRI and the presence of KS (figure 2). Table 3 shows that the relationship between BRI and KS risk varied with genders. In females, a statistically significant association was observed below the threshold of 2.02 (OR 4.82, 95% CI 1.90, 12.25, p=0.0009), whereas no significant association was observed above this threshold (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.00, 1.16, p=0.0552). In contrast, the association remained statistically significant on both sides of the threshold in males, with a stronger effect below the inflection point of 3.69 (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.24, 1.44, p<0.0001) compared with above it (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01, 1.12, p=0.0225). The likelihood ratio tests confirmed the statistical significance of these threshold effects (p<0.001 for both genders).

Investigating the mediating factors between body roundness index and the risk of kidney stones

Mediation analysis revealed the underlying mechanisms between BRI and KS risk (figures3 4). In females, LDL-C demonstrated a significant mediating effect, accounting for 7.2% (95% CI 1.3% to 20.9%) of the total effect. In males, multiple significant mediators were identified: hypertension showed the strongest mediating effect at 14.8% (95% CI 10.6% to 20.6%), followed by UA at 12.5% (95% CI 8.5% to 17.2%), HDL-C at 5.6% (95% CI 0.6% to 10.9%), TG at 4.0% (95% CI 2.4% to 6.1%) and LDL-C at 3.9% (95% CI 1.2% to 7.1%). Notably, Scr exhibited a small negative mediating effect of −1.0% (95% CI −2.2% to −0.1%), as shown in online supplemental table 1.

Discussion

Previous studies have highlighted the crucial role of obesity in KS formation.7 21 22 The BRI, a novel anthropometric measure reflecting overall body fat distribution, has demonstrated superior predictive capability for MetS compared with conventional anthropometric indicators.20 This study aimed to investigate the relationship between BRI and KS prevalence among Chinese adults, stratified by gender. Our findings revealed a significant association between elevated BRI values and increased KS risk, with notable gender differences: each unit increase in BRI was associated with an 11% and 16% higher risk of KS in females and males, respectively. Our results indicate that the incidence rate among females is catching up with that of males, with recent epidemiological data showing a narrowing gender gap in the incidence of KS.23 24 However, obesity, and particularly body fat distribution, likely remains a stronger driving force for KS risk in males than in females. Research suggests that sex hormones play a role in fat distribution and metabolism. Testosterone level in males is associated with a more centralised fat deposition in the abdominal region, a pattern linked to increased metabolic risk.25 Increased abdominal fat is associated with a higher risk of UA stone formation, whereas females tend to accumulate more fat in the hips and thighs, which may offer a certain degree of protection.26 27

Through the dose-response relationship and threshold effect analysis, we proved that there was a non-linear correlation between BRI and KS risk in both males and females. These findings emphasise the potential utility of BRI as a predictive indicator for KS risk and underscore the importance of considering gender-specific patterns and threshold effects in KS risk assessment and management strategies.

Subgroup analyses in the current study revealed that the association between BRI and KS risk was modified by gender, age, hypertension and diabetes status. In females, the association was significantly stronger among participants aged under 60 years compared with those aged 60 years and above, whereas age showed no significant modifying effect in males. This finding, which emphasises the importance of BRI management in younger and middle-aged females, is consistent with the results reported by Gillams et al.24 In the stratified analyses by comorbidities, we observed that among individuals without hypertension or diabetes, the association was more pronounced in males than in females. These findings are supported by existing literature.28 29 However, further prospective cohort studies are needed to establish the causal relationships.

Gender analysis revealed different mediating factors between BRI and KS risk. In females, LDL-C mediated 7.2% (95% CI 1.3% to 20.9%) of the BRI-KS association. As a known cardiovascular risk factor, LDL may influence KS risk through vascular health, inflammatory responses or metabolic pathways. Previous studies reported a possible link between oxidised LDL-C and KS.30 Gender differences in LDL-C levels31 may explain its mediating role. In males, hypertension and UA mediated 14.8% (95% CI 10.6% to 20.6%) and 12.5% (95% CI 8.5% to 17.2%) of the BRI-KS relationship, respectively. Hypertension remains more common in males32 and may promote KS by increasing intrarenal pressure, altering urinary solute excretion and raising stone formation risk.33 34 KS patients also show higher hypertension risk.35 36 UA, the end product of purine metabolism, associates with KS and other diseases.3739 Fu et al40 found gender-specific effects of UA on heart failure outcomes, with UA predicting long-term prognosis only in males. A study in China41 also noted a dose-response relationship between UA and male gender, which was absent in females. Sex hormone differences contribute to UA’s role in KS risk for both genders.42 43 BRI demonstrated predictive value for KS risk with notable gender-specific variations. This insight enables more precise formulation of targeted preventive strategies. In males, where BRI showed stronger associations with KS risk, interventions should prioritise obesity management and metabolic risk factor control. In females, the identification of LDL-C as a mediating factor point underscores the importance of lipid management, offering dual benefits for both cardiovascular and renal health. Such gender-stratified findings support the development of more effective, population-specific prevention approaches.

In this study, KS were diagnosed using ultrasound, which provides higher objective accuracy than traditional questionnaire-based screening. Questionnaire screening typically relies on self-reported data, which can be influenced by motivation, misunderstanding and recall inaccuracies.44 45 This approach may lead to underdiagnosis of KS in asymptomatic patients or those with atypical presentations. CT is the most accurate method for detecting KS, but it has limitations such as radiation exposure and its high cost, with CT scan fees being approximately twice that of ultrasound examinations.46 Due to its safety and practicality, ultrasound is more suitable for population screening and early detection. According to European Association of Urology guidelines, ultrasound is recommended as the first-line exam for evaluating patients with suspected KS.47

As a cross-sectional analysis, this study cannot establish causation. Future studies should employ prospective cohort designs or randomised controlled trials, incorporating cross-sectional imaging to quantify visceral adipose tissue volume. This approach will enable further investigation into threshold effects of the BRI on KS risk across genders, thereby validating current findings and elucidating underlying causal mechanisms. Additionally, to enhance the generalisability and external validity of the results, subsequent studies should incorporate larger sample sizes, multicentre data and more diverse populations.

In summary, our research provides evidence for the relationship between BRI and KS risk, which is significantly modulated by gender, with diabetes and age emerging as key modifying factors. These insights contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the multifactorial aetiology of KS and may guide the development of gender-specific preventive strategies.

Conclusion

The results indicate that an elevated BRI is positively correlated with an increased risk of KS, with notable gender-specific differences. In the prevention and treatment of KS, it is crucial to consider these gender differences and to focus on managing mediating factors such as LDL-C, hypertension and UA.
