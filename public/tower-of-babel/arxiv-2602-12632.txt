Additively Competitive Secretaries
Mohammad Mahdian, Jieming Mao, Enze Sun, Kangning Wang, Yifan Wang

Abstract
In the secretary problem, a set of secretary candidates arrive in a uniformly random order and reveal their values one by one. A company, who can only hire one candidate and hopes to maximize the expected value of its hire, needs to make irrevocable online decisions about whether to hire the current candidate. The classical framework of evaluating a policy is to compute its worst-case competitive ratio against the optimal solution in hindsight, and there the best policy – the “ 1 / e 1/e law” – has a competitive ratio of 1 / e 1/e . We propose an alternative evaluation framework through the lens of regret – the worst-case additive difference between the optimal hindsight solution and the expected performance of the policy, assuming that each value is normalized between 0 0 and 1 1 . The 1 / e 1/e law for the classical framework has a regret of 1 − 1 / e ≈ 0.632 1-1/e\approx 0.632 ; by contrast, we show that the class of “pricing curves” algorithms can guarantee a regret of at most 1 / 4 = 0.25 1/4=0.25 (which is tight within the class), and the class of “best-only pricing curves” algorithms can guarantee a regret of at most 0.190 0.190 (with a lower bound of 0.171 0.171 ). In addition, we show that in general, no policy can give a regret guarantee better than 0.152 0.152 . Finally, we discuss other objectives in our regret-minimization framework, such as selecting the top- k k candidates for k > 1 k>1 , or maximizing revenue during the selection process.

Introduction

In the domain of online decision making, people constantly face irrevocable decisions amid an uncertain future, where information about that uncertain future unfolds gradually. Central to this area is the theory of optimal stopping – selecting one time to stop in order to maximize the associated reward. The
prophet inequality
and the
secretary problem
are two of the most prominent models of optimal stopping, each spawning numerous variants and fostering dedicated research in recent years.

In both the prophet inequality and the secretary problem,
n
values are observed sequentially by a decision maker, who needs to select one value by making irrevocable decisions on whether to take the current value or not. In the prophet inequality, the values are drawn from independent prior distributions, which are known upfront to the decision maker. In the secretary problem – a metaphor for a company trying to hire the best secretary – the values are instead unknown and adversarial, but their arrival order is uniformly random. Traditionally, in both frameworks, the quality of a stopping rule (i.e., an algorithm or a policy) is measured through the
competitive ratio
– the worst-case ratio between the (expected) performance of the algorithm and the (expected) optimum in hindsight. The competitive ratio of the optimal algorithm in the prophet inequality is
1/2
, and that in the secretary problem is
1/e
.

In this work, we depart from the traditional competitive-ratio approach, and examine a different evaluation framework through the notion of
regret
. Concretely, we assume that every value is within a certain known range, without loss of generality normalized to
[0,1]
, and naturally define the regret as the worst-case
additive difference
between the hindsight optimum and the performance of the algorithm.

In fact, this framework of evaluating optimal stopping problems via regret has been proposed decades ago by Hill and Kertz, where they showed that the optimal regret is
1/4=0.25
for the prophet inequality
[
31
]
, and further examined various related settings
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, there has not been any work in the literature that addresses the secretary problem in this additive-evaluation framework. Consequently, we explore the regret minimization objective for the secretary (random order) setting.

Why is this additive-evaluation framework valuable, especially in light of the extensive research on the competitive ratio of the secretary problem? We start by noting that our choice of studying this framework is influenced by its simplicity and elegance. We believe that our framework has the potential to inspire the development of novel algorithms, which, in turn, may offer practical utility or provide insights in real-world scenarios. Indeed, in many potential application domains, the (rough) knowledge about the range of possible values is available. The standard algorithm for the secretary problem – the “
1/e
law” – has a regret of
1-1/e\approx 0.632
in our framework,

Additionally, in many situations, especially when the values correspond to money (which is inherently additive), minimizing the additive regret makes more sense than maximizing the competitive ratio. Regret as a benchmark is also robust when making multiple independent decisions, where the sum of regrets is still a meaningful measurement. We believe that our work serves as a starting point for a future line of research, on re-examining classical online decision models from this different perspective of regret minimization.

Preliminaries

Throughout our work, we focus on the following definition of the secretary problem. An adversary specifies
n
values
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
with
1\geq x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\dots\geq x_{n}\geq 0
. Each value
x_{i}
arrives at time
t_{i}
drawn from
U[0,1]
, the uniform distribution supported on the interval
[0,1]
. Note that
t_{i}
’s are almost surely distinct. An online algorithm observes each value in the increasing order of their arrival time, and needs to immediately decide whether to pick the observed value or to discard it. An algorithm can only pick one value, and for consistency, we assume that an algorithm always picks a value of
0
at the end if it has not picked any value yet. (Alternatively, we can always modify an algorithm so that it never skips the last value.)

Let
\mathsf{Alg}(\vec{x})
be the expectation of the picked value of the algorithm
\mathsf{Alg}
when the values are
\vec{x}
. Our objective is to minimize the worst-case
regret
, which is defined as

\mathsf{regret}\coloneqq\sup_{\vec{x}}\mathsf{regret}(\vec{x}),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathsf{regret}(\vec{x})\coloneqq x_{1}-\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{Alg}(\vec{x})].

We note that values arriving at random time drawn from
U[0,1]
is equivalent to values arriving in a uniformly random order, since the time vector beyond the order does not provide any useful information. An algorithm that uses this information in its description can simply freshly draw the vector of arrival time from
(U[0,1])^{n}
. We note that the step to simplify the analysis has been done in related work, such as that of
[
17
,
16
]
.

\displaystyle~=~\left(\sum_{k=i}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k}\left((1-\theta_{k})^{k}-(1-\theta_{k+1})^{k}\right)\right)+\left(\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\frac{\theta_{j}}{j-1}\cdot(1-\theta_{j})^{j-1}\right)

\displaystyle~=~\sum_{k=i}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k}\left((1-\theta_{k})^{k}-(1-\theta_{k+1})^{k}+\theta_{k+1}(1-\theta_{k+1})^{k}\right)

\displaystyle~=~\sum_{k=i}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k}\left((1-\theta_{k})^{k}-(1-\theta_{k+1})^{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle~=~\frac{1}{i}\cdot(1-\theta_{i})^{i}-\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k}.

Now we calculate the regret of our algorithm by plugging in these formulas.

\displaystyle\mathsf{regret}(\vec{x})~=~

\displaystyle(x_{1}-0)\cdot\Pr[A_{0}]+\sum_{i=2}^{n}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\Pr[A_{i}]

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle x_{1}\cdot\theta_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\left(\frac{1}{i}\cdot(1-\theta_{i})^{i}-\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k}\right)

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle x_{1}\cdot\theta_{1}+\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{i}\cdot(1-\theta_{i})^{i}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\sum_{k=i+1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k}\right),

which matches the lemma statement.
∎

Next, we further relax the expression provided by

to a relatively simple form. We define the following.

Definition 4.3
.

We use

(where

) to present a relaxation of

:

\displaystyle R_{q}(\vec{x})~:=~

\displaystyle x_{1}\cdot\theta_{1}+\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{i}\cdot(1-\theta_{i})^{i}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\sum_{k=i+1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k}\right)

\displaystyle~\geq~

\displaystyle\mathsf{regret}(\vec{x}),

where the inequality holds by observing that we define

by dropping some negative terms in.

With the definition of

, we give the following

4.4
, which suggests that the hardest instance
\vec{x}
that maximizes the relaxed regret function

has a simple structure.

Lemma 4.4
.

For fixed
n,x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{q-1}
, if the pricing curve satisfies that

is non-increasing and convex, the function

is maximized when

Proof.

We calculate
\frac{\partial R_{q}(\vec{x})}{\partial x_{k}}
for

. Recall that

\displaystyle R_{q}(\vec{x})~=~

\displaystyle x_{1}\cdot\theta_{1}+\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{i}\cdot(1-\theta_{i})^{i}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\sum_{k=i+1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{k-1}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k}\right).

We have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R_{q}(\vec{x})}{\partial x_{k}}~=~

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\left[\left((x_{1}-x_{k})\cdot\frac{1}{k}-\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{k(k-1)}\right)\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k}\right]

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle-\left((x_{1}-x_{k})-\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{k-1}\right)\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k-1}\cdot\theta^{\prime}_{k}-\frac{1}{k}\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k},

where
\theta^{\prime}_{k}
denotes
g^{\prime}(x_{k})
. We note that
\frac{\partial R_{q}(\vec{x})}{\partial x_{k}}
does not depend on
x_{k^{\prime}}
for

.

For
k\in[q,n]
and

Recall that we defined
\theta_{k}=g(x_{k})
and
\theta^{\prime}_{k}=g^{\prime}(x_{k})
. Then, we have

\displaystyle u_{k}(x_{k}):=-k\cdot\left((x_{1}-x_{k})-\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{k-1}\right)\cdot\theta^{\prime}_{k}-(1-\theta_{k})

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial R_{q}(\vec{x})}{\partial x_{k}}=\frac{1}{k}\cdot u_{k}(x_{k})\cdot(1-\theta_{k})^{k-1}.

We next examine the condition of
\frac{\partial R_{q}(\vec{x})}{\partial x_{k}}=0
, which is equivalent to
u_{k}(x_{k})=0
. Note that have

u^{\prime}_{k}(x)=(k+1)\cdot g^{\prime}(x)-k\cdot\left((x_{1}-x)+\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{k-1}\right)\cdot g^{\prime\prime}(x)<0.

The inequality holds because we have
g^{\prime}(x)<0
and
g^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0
, which is true because we assumed

is strictly decreasing and convex, and

is the inverse function of

, and

(x_{1}-x)-\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{k-1}\geq(x_{1}-x_{q-1})-\frac{q-2}{k-1}(x_{1}-x_{q-1})\geq 0.

Therefore, function
u_{k}(x)
is decreasing within
[f(1),x_{q-1}]
. Additionally, notice that

\displaystyle u_{k}(f(1))=-k\cdot\left((x_{1}-f(1))-\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot\frac{1}{k-1}\right)\cdot g^{\prime}(f(1))~\geq~0.

Therefore, either equation

has a unique solution within
[f(1),x_{q-1}]
, or
u_{k}(x)>0
for every
x\in[f(1),x_{q-1}]
. Let

be the unique solution of
u_{k}(x)=0
within
[f(1),x_{q-1}]
. If the solution does not exist, we define
z_{k}=x_{q-1}
. Then,
\frac{\partial R_{q}(\vec{x})}{\partial x_{k}}(x)
is non-negative for
x\in[f(1),z_{k}]
, and is non-positive for

.

We further observe that

\displaystyle u_{k+1}(z_{k})-u_{k}(z_{k})

\displaystyle~=~-(x_{1}-z_{k})\cdot g^{\prime}(z_{k})+\left(\frac{k+1}{k}-\frac{k}{k-1}\right)\cdot\sum_{i=2}^{q-1}(x_{1}-x_{i})\cdot g^{\prime}(z_{k})\geq 0,

i.e.,
u_{k+1}(z_{k})\geq u_{k}(z_{k})
. If
u_{k}(z_{k})=0
, then we have
u_{k+1}(z_{k})\geq 0
, and there must be
z_{k}\leq z_{k+1}
, because function
u_{k+1}(x)
is decreasing. Otherwise, we have
z_{k}=x_{q-1}
, and
u_{k+1}(x_{q-1})>0
, so

should hold. Therefore, in both cases, we have
z_{k}\leq z_{k+1}
.

Finally, we finish our proof by showing the following claim: given vector

,

.

•

w_{q}=w_{q+1}=\dots=w_{n}
.

.

Appendix A
Further Results with Regret Minimization Objective

So far, we have discussed regret minimization for the secretary problem. In this section, we further discuss other combinatorial models with regret minimization as the objective.

A.1
Multiple-Choice Secretary Model

We first extend the discussed single-choice secretary model to the multiple-choice secretary variant, in which the algorithm is allowed to choose
k
values among
n
sequentially arriving numbers, and the regret minimization objective refers to the difference between the sum of top-
k
values and the sum of the chosen values, under the assumption that each arriving value falls between
[0,1]
. For the multiple-choice secretary model, when the objective is to maximize the competitive ratio,
[
39
]
gives the following result.

Proposition A.1

(
[
39
]
)
.

For the multiple-choice secretary problem with the objective of optimizing the competitive ratio, there exists a
(1-O(k^{-1/2}))
-competitive algorithm.

Note that when each arriving value falls between
[0,1]
, the sum of top-
k
values is at most
k
. Therefore, as a direct corollary, the
(1-O(k^{-1/2}))
-competitive algorithm immediately gives an
O(\sqrt{k})
regret bound.

Theorem A.2
.

For the multiple-choice secretary problem with regret minimization as the objective, there exists an algorithm with regret of
O(\sqrt{k})
.

Theorem

A.3
below provides a matching lower bound for the multiple-choice secretary problem.

Theorem A.3
.

For the multiple-choice secretary problem with regret minimization as the objective, every algorithm has
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
worst-case regret.

Theorem

A.3
follows from a
(1-\Omega(k^{-1/2}))
-competitive lower bound claimed in
[
39
]
. However, the proof of such lower bound is not explicitly stated in
[
39
]
. Below we provide a direct proof of
Theorem

A.3
for completeness.

To prove
Theorem

A.3
, consider the following random instance with
2k
arriving values. (Without loss of generality, assume
\sqrt{k}
is an integer and a multiple of
20
.)

•

Case 1: the input contains
k
ones,
k-\sqrt{k}/5
zeros, and
\sqrt{k}/5
one halves.

•

Case 2: the input contains
k-\sqrt{k}/5
ones,
k
zeros, and
\sqrt{k}/5
one halves.

•

The random instance takes Case 1 as input with probability
0.5
, and takes Case 2 as input with probability
0.5
.

It is intuitive that no algorithm can distinguish those two cases very well before incurring a regret of
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
. We will formalize this intuition in the proof below.

Proof of
Theorem

A.3
.

We use Yao’s minimax principle to prove the lower bound. Specifically, it is sufficient to show that every deterministic algorithm suffers a regret of at least
\sqrt{k}/1600
when the input comes from the random instance above.

Consider the following simplification of the problem: the first half of the input (first
k
values) is revealed to the algorithm at once. The algorithm then determine the strategy of picking values from this first half of the input. Clearly the optimal deterministic algorithm should pick all arriving ones and reject all arriving zeros. It remains to determine the number of one halves to pick. Therefore, the optimal deterministic algorithm can be defined as a function
w(a,b)\colon[0,k]\times[0,\sqrt{k}/5]\to[0,\sqrt{k}/5]
, i.e., when the first half of the input contains
a
ones and
b
one halves, the optimal deterministic algorithm decides to take
w(a,b)\leq b
one halves.

Define
P_{1}(a,b)
to be the probability that the first half of the input contains
a
ones and
b
one halves when the input comes from Case 1. Similarly, define
P_{2}(a,b)
to be the probability that the first half of the input contains
a
ones and
b
one halves when the input comes from Case 1. Note that the optimal strategy in hindsight is to pick no one half when the input comes from Case 1, and all
b
one halves when the input comes from Case 2. Either incorrectly picking a one half in Case 1 or missing a one half in Case 2 suffers a regret of
\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, the regret of the optimal deterministic algorithm is at least

\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{k}\sum_{b=0}^{\sqrt{k}/5}0.5\cdot P_{1}(a,b)\cdot\frac{1}{2}w(a,b)+\sum_{a=0}^{k-\sqrt{k}/5}\sum_{b=0}^{\sqrt{k}/5}0.5\cdot P_{2}(a,b)\cdot\frac{1}{2}(b-w(a,b))

\displaystyle\geq~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\cdot\sum_{a=0}^{k}\sum_{b=0}^{\sqrt{k}/5}\min\{P_{1}(a,b),P_{2}(a,b)\}\cdot\max\{w(a,b),b-w(a,b)\}

\displaystyle\geq~

\displaystyle\sum_{b=0}^{\sqrt{k}/5}\frac{b}{8}\cdot\sum_{a=0}^{k}\min\{P_{1}(a,b),P_{2}(a,b)\}

\displaystyle\geq~

\displaystyle\sum_{b=\sqrt{k}/20}^{\sqrt{k}/5}\frac{\sqrt{k}}{160}\cdot\sum_{a=0}^{\frac{k}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{k}}{10}}\min\{P_{1}(a,b),P_{2}(a,b)\}

(3)

where the second inequality uses the fact that
\max\{w(a,b),b-w(a,b)\}\geq\frac{b}{2}
.

Next, we show that
P_{1}(a,b)\leq P_{2}(a,b)
, and further simplify the term of
\min\{P_{1}(a,b),P_{2}(a,b)\}
in
Eq.

3
to
P_{1}(a,b)
. Note that

P_{1}(a,b)=\frac{{k\choose a}\cdot{\sqrt{k}/5\choose b}\cdot{k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose k-a-b}}{{2k\choose k}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{2}(a,b)=\frac{{k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose a}\cdot{\sqrt{k}/5\choose b}\cdot{k\choose k-a-b}}{{2k\choose k}}.

Therefore,

\frac{P_{1}(a,b)}{P_{2}(a,b)}~=~\frac{{k\choose a}\cdot{k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose k-a-b}}{{k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose a}\cdot{k\choose k-a-b}}~=~\frac{(a+b)!\cdot(k-\sqrt{k}/5-a)!}{(k-a)!\cdot(a+b-\sqrt{k}/5)!}~=~\prod_{u=a+b-\sqrt{k}+1}^{a+b}\frac{u}{u-2a-b+k}.

When
a\leq\frac{k}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{k}}{10}
, there must be
2a+b\leq k
, i.e.,
\frac{u}{u-2a-b+k}\leq 1
. Therefore,
P_{1}(a,b)\leq P_{2}(a,b)
when
a\leq\frac{k}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{k}}{10}
.

Now we use the above observation to further simplify
Eq.

3
. We have

(
3
)

\displaystyle=~\frac{\sqrt{k}}{160}\cdot\sum_{b=\sqrt{k}/20}^{\sqrt{k}/5}\sum_{a=0}^{\frac{k}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{k}}{10}}\min\{P_{1}(a,b),P_{2}(a,b)\}

\displaystyle=~\frac{\sqrt{k}}{160}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[b\geq\sqrt{k}/20\land a\leq k/2-\sqrt{k}/10~\mid\text{input is Case 1}\right]

\displaystyle\geq~\frac{\sqrt{k}}{160}\cdot\left(1-\mathbf{Pr}\left[b<\sqrt{k}/20~\mid\text{input is Case 1}\right]-\mathbf{Pr}\left[a>k/2-\sqrt{k}/10~\mid\text{input is Case 1}\right]\right).

(4)

Now, we assume the input is Case 1, and upper bound the probability that

and
a>k/2-\sqrt{k}/10
separately. For simplicity, we omit the condition “input is Case 1” in the following calculation.

We first show the

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}[b=u]~=~\frac{{\sqrt{k}/5\choose u}\cdot{2k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose k-u}}{{2k\choose k}}~<~\frac{{\sqrt{k}/5\choose u+\sqrt{k}/20}\cdot{2k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose k-u-\sqrt{k}/20}}{{2k\choose k}}~=~\mathbf{Pr}[b=u+\sqrt{k}/20],

(5)

where the inequality holds because the combination number
{n\choose m}
is single-peaked with respect to
m

m=n/2
. Then, we have
{\sqrt{k}/5\choose u}<{\sqrt{k}/5\choose u+\sqrt{k}/20}
and
{2k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose k-u}<{2k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose k-u-\sqrt{k}/20}

and

On the other hand, note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}[b=u]~=~\frac{{\sqrt{k}/5\choose u}\cdot{2k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose k-u}}{{2k\choose k}}~=~\frac{{\sqrt{k}/5\choose\sqrt{k}/5-u}\cdot{2k-\sqrt{k}/5\choose k-3\sqrt{k}/20+u}}{{2k\choose k}}~=~\mathbf{Pr}[b=\sqrt{k}/5-u].

5

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[b<\sqrt{k}/20\right]~

\displaystyle=~\sum_{u=0}^{\sqrt{k}/20-1}\mathbf{Pr}[b=u]

\displaystyle\leq~\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{u=0}^{\sqrt{k}/20-1}\left(\mathbf{Pr}[b=u]+\mathbf{Pr}[b=u+\sqrt{k}/20]\right)

\displaystyle=~\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{u=0}^{\sqrt{k}/10-1}\mathbf{Pr}[b=u]

\displaystyle=~\frac{1}{4}\cdot\sum_{u=0}^{\sqrt{k}/10-1}\left(\mathbf{Pr}[b=u]+\mathbf{Pr}[b=\sqrt{k}/5-u]\right)

We further simplify the above inequality to

\displaystyle 0.8\cdot\frac{4^{m}}{\sqrt{\pi\cdot m}}~<~{2m\choose m}~<~\frac{4^{m}}{\sqrt{\pi\cdot m}}.

A.2
Revenue Maximization

The last model we consider is the
revenue
maximization variant of the online stopping problem, in contrast with welfare maximization in earlier sections. Here, a seller is selling one item to
n
buyers that arrive sequentially. Buyer
i
has value
x_{i}\in[0,1]
. At the time that buyer
i
arrives, the seller needs to post a price
p_{i}
. If
x_{i}\geq p_{i}
, buyer
i
buys the item, and the seller gets
p_{i}
as revenue, ending the process. As before, the goal is to minimize the
regret
, defined as the difference between (sometimes the expectation of) the best possible revenue
\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}
, and the expected revenue collected by the seller.

There are different modelling choices about values and arrivals. The values can be picked by an adversary, or can be drawn stochastically from same/different known distributions. Similarly, the arrival order can be uniformly random or chosen by an adversary. For the positive result of the problem, we show that there exists an algorithm that achieves
1/e
regret even in the strongest adversary setting.

Theorem A.4
.

For revenue maximization variant of online stopping problem, there exists an algorithm that achieves
1/e
regret when buyers have adversarial values.

To prove
Theorem

A.4
, we present an algorithm – randomized uniform price (
Algorithm

4
) – that can achieve optimal regret of
1/e
in all of these revenue maximization settings. This algorithm was first discussed in the unpublished work of
[
40
]
for regret minimization for
welfare
with adversarial arrival order. We further observe that the same algorithm also gives the same regret guarantee for the revenue maximization setting.

Let
\alpha\sim U[-1,0]
, and set uniform price
p\leftarrow e^{\alpha}

for

\tau=0\to 1
where
x_{i}
arrives at
t_{i}=\tau

do

if

x_{i}\geq p

then

Pick
x_{i}
and
exit

else

Skip
x_{i}

end if

end for

Algorithm 4

Randomized Uniform Price

Now, we show that
Algorithm

4
is the desired algorithm for
Theorem

A.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

A.4
.

Let
z:=\max\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be the optimal revenue in hindsight. There are two cases:

•

Case (1):
z\leq 1/e
, and the regret is at most
z\leq 1/e
.

•

Case (2):
z>1/e
. In this case, the item is sold if and only if
z\geq p
, which is equivalent to
\alpha\leq\ln z
. The seller gets revenue of
p=e^{\alpha}
if the item is sold. Therefore, the expected revenue of
Algorithm

4
is

\int_{-1}^{\ln z}e^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha=z-\frac{1}{e}.

Therefore, the regret is
z-(z-1/e)=1/e
.
∎

For the negative result of the problem, we provide a matching
1/e
regret lower bound. We also show the hard instance only contains a single stochastic buyer. Therefore the extra stochastic or random order assumption does not make the problem easier.

Theorem A.5
.

For revenue maximization variant of online stopping problem, there exists an instance in which no algorithm can achieve a regret strictly better than
1/e
when buyers have adversarial values.

Proof.

Consider a buyer with valuation
x\sim\mathcal{D}
, where the cumulative distribution function of the distribution
\mathcal{D}
is

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0&\qquad&x\in\Big[0,\frac{1}{e}\Big)\\
&1-\frac{1}{ex}&\qquad&x\in\Big[\frac{1}{e},1\Big)\\
&1&\qquad&x=1\end{aligned}\right.

Observe that an optimal strategy for the seller is to use any price in
[1/e,1)
and get expected revenue of

p\cdot(1-F_{\mathcal{D}}(p))~=~\frac{1}{e}.

On the other hand,

\mathbb{E}[x]~=~\int_{0}^{1}(1-F(x))\,\mathrm{d}x~=~\frac{2}{e}.

By linearity of expectation, the optimal regret for the seller is
2/e-1/e=1/e
.
∎
