Barriers to randomized trials of integrated care for adolescent mothers in the Caribbean: lessons from the THRIVE trial and the Project Amai in Barbados
Authors: Hada Fongha Ieong, Mariah Cobbin, Pilar Tlachi, Connor Chidsey, Ryan Flaks, Arlene Ross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adolescent mothers in the Caribbean represent a high-need, under-served population facing overlapping reproductive, mental health, and social challenges. Despite the urgency of these needs, few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) target this group using culturally tailored, integrated care models. The Teen motHers’ ReproductIve and behaVioral health intErvention (THRIVE) was conceptualized in Barbados in April 2024 as a randomized trial to evaluate such a model. However, as of today, THRIVE has not launched due to prolonged Institutional Review Board (IRB) delays and cultural sensitivities surrounding adolescent pregnancy—barriers that reflect broader systemic challenges common across low- and middle-income country (LMIC) contexts. In response, a parallel initiative—the Project Amai—was launched as a community-driven, service-based intervention outside the traditional RCT framework. Prioritizing cultural responsiveness, youth engagement and leadership, and low-barrier access, Amai reached a cohort of marginalized adolescent mothers and achieved high retention, program graduation, and improvements in agency and well-being within 8 months. This commentary contrasts the stalled progress of THRIVE with the rapid implementation of Amai to examine how institutional, infrastructural, and cultural factors shape the feasibility of equity-focused trials. We draw lessons for trialists working in under-resourced settings and suggest that advancing diversity, equity, and inclusion in trials requires adaptive strategies—including pragmatic designs, regional ethics collaboration, and broader outcome measures in the Caribbean. These insights contribute to emerging models of inclusive global health research and offer actionable guidance for designing trials and programs that are not only methodologically rigorous but also socially just and accessible to those most in need.

Background

Adolescent mothers in the Caribbean face significant health and social challenges, such as repeat unintended pregnancies, depression, intimate partner violence, school dropout, and economic insecurity [1, 2]. These challenges are further exacerbated by poverty, limited access to integrated health services, and societal stigma, which contribute to long-term disadvantages for both mothers and children [3]. Despite these pressing needs, adolescent mothers remain underrepresented in Caribbean trials, with limited research addressing their needs.

Structural and regulatory barriers, alongside cultural stigma around adolescent pregnancy, often impede the inclusion of adolescent mothers in research [4, 5]. Many interventions fail to effectively address the unique needs of this group, as they are designed without their inclusion or without consideration for the particular challenges these mothers face [6, 7]. In response to this gap, a number of initiatives have emerged in the region, though clinical trials specifically targeting adolescent mothers are still rare. Notable exceptions, such as in Jamaica, have focused on reproductive health interventions and have contributed to improving maternal well-being and social reintegration [6, 8]. However, the wider Caribbean remains underexplored in this area.

In Barbados, two projects aim to fill this gap: Teen motHers’ ReproductIve and behaVioral health intErvention (THRIVE), a randomized controlled trial (RCT; ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07149948) designed to deliver community-based integrated care to adolescent mothers, and Project Amai, a community-driven, low-barrier service model supporting young mothers. THRIVE has faced delays [9] due to regulatory and ethical challenges [10], while Amai has engaged participants effectively, improving well-being and agency through reproductive health education, mental health support, and vocational training [11]. Both offer valuable insights into the evolving approaches to inclusive research, particularly in resource-limited settings [12].

This commentary examines lessons from THRIVE and Amai, showing how research and community care can better support the research in the adolescent mother population in the Caribbean. The insights drawn from these projects will also offer valuable lessons for global approaches to supporting under-served adolescent populations.

Community-based, integrated care approach in the Caribbean

Integrated care involves the coordinated delivery of health, behavioral, and social services to address complex needs, particularly for populations facing barriers to care [13]. Although first developed in high-income countries, the model has gained relevance in LMICs, where it helps improve access, continuity, and equity in service delivery for marginalized groups [14, 15].

For adolescent mothers in the Caribbean, community-based integrated care provides an opportunity to access critical support services outside traditional clinical settings, such as schools or community organizations. This model is particularly important given the intersecting challenges these young mothers face—including early parenting, educational disruption, perinatal mental health risks, and economic insecurity—often compounded by cultural stigma and limited access to formal services [2, 16].

Programs such as Jamaica’s Women’s Centre of Jamaica Foundation exemplify integrated care, combining education, reproductive health, and psychosocial support to improve school reintegration and maternal well-being [4, 10]. Similar initiatives in Trinidad and Guyana incorporate peer mentorship and life skills training, though rigorous evaluation remains limited [6, 12, 17].

In Barbados, both THRIVE and Amai were grounded in this community-based, integrated care model, offering reproductive, behavioral health, and vocational support. While THRIVE employed an RCT design to measure outcomes, its implementation was delayed by regulatory and ethical hurdles. In contrast, Amai prioritized rapid, low-barrier service delivery outside the constraints of formal research. These complementary initiatives illustrate different strategies for meeting the needs of under-served adolescent mothers within a shared framework of integrated community-based care.

THRIVE: a randomized trial delayed

THRIVE is a 12-month RCT to evaluate a culturally responsive, integrated care model for adolescent mothers in Barbados. Led by medical students in collaboration with community organizations, the trial is designed to provide essential healthcare services, including the measurement and interpretation of vital signs, alongside reproductive health education, psychoeducation on parenting and mental health, and vocational readiness support. The intervention targets adolescent mothers aged 10–25 years who became pregnant during adolescence and are at increased risk of mood-related symptoms and socioeconomic instability. Eligible participants are identified through referrals from community organizations and screened for depressive symptoms using the PHQ-2 and PHQ-9.

A total of 50 participants is expected to be enrolled in Phase 1 of the trial and randomized into either a treatment group (receiving the THRIVE intervention) or a control group (receiving standard care provided by local clinics and community partners). Randomization is conducted via a third party using Excel-generated sequences. The intervention is delivered flexibly through in-person sessions or virtual meetings, depending on participant preferences and availability. Each participant in the intervention group will receive seven biweekly sessions. These sessions are structured to include a 5-min health check, a 40-min psychoeducation module, and a 15-min assessment and planning component.

THRIVE incorporates a comprehensive evaluation strategy, utilizing validated instruments to assess both physical and psychosocial outcomes. Measures include vital signs and physical health checks, depression and anxiety symptoms (using the CES-D and STAI scales), parenting attitudes and stress (measured with the AAPI and Parental Stress Scale), perceived social support (using the Postpartum Support Questionnaire), reproductive behaviors (including contraceptive and condom use), and demographic and contextual risk factors such as school dropout, repeat pregnancy, and the intergenerational transmission of adolescent pregnancy.

Despite strong community need and a well-structured intervention, THRIVE has yet to launch as of end-2025. It remains stalled in the pre-implementation phase due to a series of interrelated barriers that reflect broader structural challenges for equity-focused research in LMICs.

One major obstacle was prolonged IRB approval. In Barbados, as in many other Caribbean nations, ethical review systems are under-resourced and often cautious, particularly for research involving vulnerable populations and sensitive topics, such as minors, pregnancy, or mental health. THRIVE underwent multiple rounds of IRB feedback regarding consent procedures, safeguarding, and data governance. While these concerns are crucial, the lack of centralized research ethics support contributed to delays exceeding 12 months. Similar delays have been reported in other LMIC contexts, where regulatory systems struggle with the capacity to provide timely ethical oversight [15].

A second barrier arose from scarce institutional resources. THRIVE was initiated within a regional medical school that lacked a dedicated clinical trials unit. Limited administrative infrastructure, a lack of protected research time, and high staff turnover led to a loss of momentum over time. As the timelines extended, key faculty members were diverted into clinical, teaching, or administrative roles, which further weakened the trial’s leadership and continuity. This is a common challenge in LMICs, where academic institutions may struggle to support investigator-initiated trials, particularly during lengthy pre-launch phases [9].

Third, stigma surrounding adolescent pregnancy complicated early recruitment efforts. In Barbados, as in much of the Caribbean, adolescent mothers often face moralizing attitudes and stigma from schools, health professionals, and family [18]. Potential participants expressed concerns about confidentiality, visibility, and being judged—especially in a randomized study requiring formal consent and monitoring. This highlights a broader tension: while RCTs aim to protect vulnerable groups through ethical oversight, their procedural demands may unintentionally deter participation from the very populations they are designed to support [19].

Taken together, these barriers reflect deeper structural issues: equity-focused trials often fail to account for the local challenges that can hinder their success. Delays, disengagement, and mistrust reflect systemic misalignments between research and community realities. Rather than framing the delays in THRIVE as a failure of planning or commitment, it is more productive to view them as evidence of the structural reinforcements needed for such trials to succeed. Without adaptive ethics systems, sustained institutional support, and stigma-sensitive recruitment strategies, even well-designed interventions can remain out of reach for the populations they are intended to serve.

Amai: a community-centered initiative

Where THRIVE struggled to launch within institutional constraints, Amai took root quickly—driven by community partnerships and a flexible, participant-centered model.

Amai is a community-centered 8-month program aimed at supporting young mothers aged 13–24, addressing their biopsychosocial needs and enhancing emotional resilience and vocational readiness. Launched in partnership with the non-profit organization Rotary Club of Barbados, Amai was intentionally designed with a flexible, participant-driven structure to meet the needs of young mothers. The name Amai, derived from a Zimbabwean word meaning “mother,” reflects its core mission to empower young women through personal development and practical skills. Sessions are held at the Mount of Praise Wesleyan Holiness Church Annex— a familiar, accessible space that fosters engagement and comfort.

The program combines life skills workshops, group mental health sessions, and vocational training in cosmetology, event planning, and entrepreneurship. On-site childcare, meals, and a small stipend remove common participation barriers, ensuring young mothers can attend consistently. Although formal quantitative evaluations have not yet been conducted, participant feedback and observational data point to meaningful improvements in emotional resilience, confidence, and skill development. Responding to continued community need, Amai has already begun its second cohort in 2025.

First, Amai’s consistent engagement and high retention demonstrate its relevance and impact. Most participants attended sessions regularly throughout the 8-month program—an uncommon outcome for interventions targeting young mothers, who often face pressures like childcare, financial strain, or emotional stress. The program’s accessibility and wraparound supports allowed participants to prioritize attendance without logistical burdens.

Second, the completion of core modules and vocational certifications stands out. Most participants not only attended but completed training in areas such as cosmetology, event planning, and restaurant services—providing them with immediately marketable skills to support financial security and long-term stability.

Third, participant feedback highlighted improvements in self-esteem, emotional resilience, and confidence. Many felt more equipped to regulate their emotions, communicate effectively, and envision a future. One participant shared:I’ve learned how to communicate, manage my emotions, dress for interviews... and how to pay attention to my body. None of this would have happened without this program.

These early gains, though not yet quantified, reflect shifts in self-esteem and self-efficacy that influence long-term well-being and socioeconomic mobility [20].

Fourth, Amai’s ability to adapt in real time based on participant feedback contributed to its success. While the program followed a core framework, it was intentionally flexible—evolving to meet changing needs and informed by both verbal and non-verbal cues. By prioritizing responsiveness over rigid structure, Amai fostered stronger participant connections and sustained engagement.

Amai illustrates that when traditional research pathways are delayed or constrained, community-driven initiatives can still produce meaningful impact [21]. While its success does not negate the value of RCTs or IRBs, it underscores the need for a broader understanding of what counts as evidence. To truly include marginalized populations in future research, systems must integrate both qualitative insights and quantitative methods. This more adaptive, inclusive approach can ensure that programs like Amai are evaluated rigorously while remaining grounded in community realities and ethical responsiveness.

Lessons from THRIVE and Amai and recommendations

To advance equity, diversity, and inclusion in RCTs in the Caribbean, particularly within LMICs, it is essential to rethink traditional approaches and develop more adaptive and community-centered models. The experiences from the THRIVE trial and the Amai initiative highlight key lessons for overcoming barriers that often prevent marginalized populations from being fully represented and benefiting from clinical research. They underscore the need for flexibility, community involvement, and contextual sensitivity in improving RCT equity. The following actionable recommendations are designed to improve the inclusion and equity of RCTs while maintaining scientific rigor in the Caribbean and similar LMIC settings.

Streamline IRB processes with contextual sensitivity

To address the prolonged delays that often hinder RCTs in LMICs [10, 15], such as those encountered in the THRIVE trial, research teams must work to streamline the IRB processes while maintaining rigorous ethical standards. Caribbean research systems can reduce these bottlenecks by adopting regional ethics review models like the Caribbean Public Health Agency (CARPHA) Ethics Review Committee, which has successfully minimized redundancies and improved governance capacity across multiple islands [22, 23]. This regional model facilitates a faster review process, reducing the overall timeline for trial implementation without sacrificing ethical rigor. To further expedite this process, trialists should work closely with local and regional ethics bodies to anticipate the challenges specific to marginalized populations, such as concerns about consent for minors or vulnerable groups, and align research protocols accordingly. Implementing this approach would ensure that trials not only move faster but also retain the necessary cultural sensitivity and relevance.

Foster ongoing community engagement throughout the research lifecycle

One of the key lessons from the Project Amai is the importance of continuous community engagement throughout the research lifecycle, from study design to implementation and evaluation [5, 12]. While THRIVE attempted to incorporate community input, it was often limited to early stages, resulting in disconnects between the research and community needs. Amai, on the other hand, achieved better outcomes by ensuring local communities were involved in co-designing the intervention and in guiding its implementation, allowing for more responsive adaptation to the needs of participants. To foster similar success in Caribbean RCTs, it is crucial for researchers to institutionalize community engagement in every phase. This includes ongoing feedback loops from participants and community leaders, ensuring that their needs, preferences, and cultural contexts are central to the trial design and its execution [6, 24]. Establishing Community Advisory Boards (CABs) and working with local leaders, youth facilitators, and service users from the very beginning will strengthen participation, reduce attrition, and improve the relevance and acceptability of the intervention, ultimately leading to more sustainable outcomes.

Adopt adaptive trial designs to accommodate local realities

Traditional trial designs are often rigid and ill-suited to the dynamic and, at times, unpredictable conditions of LMIC settings. In the Caribbean, where logistical, political, and social conditions can shift rapidly, the ability to adapt and respond to real-time challenges is critical [25]. Both the THRIVE and Amai initiatives showed that trials with a more flexible design—such as stepped-wedge trials, hybrid models, or trials-within-cohorts—offer more adaptability in these settings [12, 26]. These adaptive models allow for continuous monitoring and real-time adjustments to intervention strategies, making it possible to respond to changing community needs or external challenges without compromising scientific integrity. For instance, if a certain intervention proves less effective or feasible in a particular area, trial teams can adjust the protocol to better suit the local context or target population. Moreover, adaptive trial designs offer opportunities to incorporate real-time feedback from participants, allowing interventions to evolve concurrently with implementation. Research teams should prioritize training and capacity-building in adaptive trial designs for investigators and institutions in LMICs to improve trial responsiveness and feasibility.

Integrate biopsychosocial and qualitative outcomes into trial evaluation

Traditional RCTs often prioritize clinical endpoints such as health metrics or disease outcomes, but this approach overlooks the broader, biopsychosocial aspects of participants’ lives. In the context of marginalized groups such as adolescent mothers in Caribbean settings, it is essential to consider not only biological and clinical outcomes, but also the psychological and social dimensions of well-being [27]. The Amai initiative showed that psychosocial outcomes, such as self-efficacy, peer support, and vocational engagement, are vital indicators of success. Future Caribbean RCTs should expand their evaluation frameworks to integrate biopsychosocial measures, which account for the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors in shaping health and well-being [17]. This could include collecting qualitative data through surveys, interviews, and focus groups to assess emotional resilience, coping strategies, and social support networks [21]. By incorporating these broader indicators, trials can provide a more comprehensive understanding of intervention impacts and ensure that the experiences of marginalized populations are accurately captured and reflected in the research.

Strengthen institutional support for investigators and research teams

The THRIVE trial experienced significant delays due to limited institutional support for investigators, particularly early-career researchers, who often faced burnout or disengagement because of the slow pace of initial review and trial implementation [14, 15]. This highlights the importance of strengthening institutional support structures in LMICs, where research capacity can be limited. Universities, research institutions, and funding agencies in the Caribbean must prioritize the creation of comprehensive research support frameworks that provide protected research time, mentorship, and administrative support [14]. Offering research coordinators, data management resources, and statistical expertise will significantly reduce the workload of investigators and improve the continuity of research activities. By empowering investigators with these resources, it is possible to prevent burnout, increase retention of talent, and ultimately ensure the success and longevity of equity-focused research projects.

Broaden stakeholder collaboration for policy advocacy and institutional capacity building

Building a robust, equitable research ecosystem in the Caribbean will require collaboration among a wide range of stakeholders, including local governments, international organizations, research institutions, and community-based organizations [9, 28]. This collaboration should focus not only on the implementation of individual research projects but also on creating a policy environment that supports long-term, sustainable change in research practices. Stakeholders should advocate for the establishment of policies that incentivize the inclusion of marginalized populations in trials, encourage adaptive trial designs, and fund community-engaged research [15]. Furthermore, regional partnerships should aim to strengthen institutional capacity by adopting training programs for local researchers and improving infrastructure for research governance [22]. These efforts will build a more resilient, contextually relevant research ecosystem that is better equipped to address the health disparities facing marginalized populations in the Caribbean and similar LMIC settings.

Conclusion

THRIVE and Amai illustrate two contrasting pathways: the rigor of an RCT mired in institutional barriers, versus the responsiveness of a community-driven program delivering immediate impact without trial infrastructure. Neither path is sufficient alone. For equity-focused research to thrive in LMICs, trials must be both scientifically sound and socially accessible. This requires flexible ethics systems, institutional investment, and community co-leadership. Rigor and responsiveness are not mutually exclusive; when combined, they can transform research from an academic exercise into a vehicle for advancing global health equity and driving real change for those most excluded from health systems.
