Effective coverage of maternal and newborn health services across the antepartum and peripartum continuum in primary health care in Ethiopia
Authors: Gizachew Tadele Tiruneh, Tamar Chitashvili, Bezawit Mesfin Hunegnaw, Tsegaye Shewangzaw, Biruk Bogale, Nebreed Fesseha, Takele Yeshiwas, Netsanet Belete, Nesredin Nursebo, Dessalew Emaway
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite progress in improving access to and use of maternal and newborn health (MNH) services in Ethiopia, challenges persist in ensuring equitable, high-quality care.MethodsThis study assessed the effective coverage cascade across antepartum and peripartum care (PPC) using formative implementation science research data obtained during July–August 2024. A stratified multistage sampling method recruited 1,922 women with infants (0–11 months), including 1,118 from agrarian and 804 from pastoral regions. The study included facility assessments at 67 primary care facilities and 329 client exit interviews.Service contact was defined as attending at least one antenatal care (ANC) contact and delivering in a health facility. Intervention-adjusted coverage accounted for receipt of key interventions, while readiness- and quality-adjusted coverage incorporated facility input and quality index scores from facility survey readiness assessment and exit interviews. Sampling weights were applied, and household and facility data were linked using ecological methods —that is, by aligning data from the same woreda rather than linking records at the individual level.ResultsMean facility readiness scores for ANC and PPC were 74% and 80%, respectively. The mean care experience score from exit interviews was 83%, while antepartum and peripartum service provision scores were 60% and 61%. Household survey data showed that fewer than three-quarters of women received recommended ANC interventions, while 82% of women received peripartum and newborn interventions. Most facilities had uterotonics, newborn resuscitation bags, and essential maternal health services. However, gaps in trained staff, diagnostics, emergency transport, and dignified care persisted. Missing services—including ultrasound, deworming, maternal depression screening, and postpartum family planning counseling—highlighted critical service delivery challenges.The effective coverage cascade shows significant drop-offs across the continuum of care. For ANC, 81% of women made initial contact, only 63% received care in facilities with essential inputs, 49% received essential interventions, and only 26% ultimately received quality services. A similar pattern was observed for peripartum services: about 74% of women delivered at a health facility, only 59% received PPC in a facility with essential inputs, 49% received essential interventions, and just 26% received quality services. Regional disparities were evident across the cascade.ConclusionWhile MNH service access has expanded, substantial cascade losses and inequities persist, particularly between agrarian and pastoral regions. Addressing these challenges requires equity-focused strategies to enhance readiness, improve service quality, integrate care, and strengthen accountability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02241-5.Keywords: Antepartum care, Effective coverage, Ethiopia, Maternal and newborn health, Peripartum care

Introduction

Over the last two decades, Ethiopia has made significant investments and progress in improving access to and use of maternal and child health (MCH) interventions, leading to reductions in maternal and child morbidities and mortalities [1–3]. Key initiatives such as expanding primary healthcare infrastructure, deploying mid-level health professionals, providing free ambulance services, and implementing the Health Extension Program (HEP) have enhanced access to MCH services [4–6]. However, despite these advancements, challenges remain in ensuring equitable delivery of quality MCH services across the continuum of care [7].

The antepartum, intrapartum, and immediate postpartum periods represent critical windows for delivering life-saving maternal and newborn health (MNH) interventions. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that effective delivery of essential interventions during these stages can significantly reduce maternal and neonatal mortality [8]. In this context, effective coverage has emerged as a vital strategy for achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), recognizing that without deliberate attention to quality and equity—particularly in MNH—UHC risks becoming an empty promise, as more people now die from poor-quality care than from lack of access [9, 10].

Effective coverage in MNH is defined as the proportion of those in need who receive care that goes beyond mere contact, including adherence to clinical guidelines, delivery in well-equipped and prepared facilities, and the provision of respectful, person-centered care. This aspect is crucial for improving health outcomes and preventing unnecessary deaths [9, 11]. For example, high-quality antenatal care (ANC) can reduce neonatal deaths by up to 34% [12]. Moreover, women who receive effective ANC are more likely to utilize subsequent maternal health services, such as facility-based delivery and postnatal care (PNC), thereby strengthening continuity of care and improving outcomes across the maternal and newborn care continuum [7, 13].

However, studies from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have revealed substantial drop-offs in the MNH service cascade, where contact coverage is relatively high, but quality and continuity of care remain insufficient to ensure optimal care and health outcomes [14–16]. This pattern is evident in Ethiopia, where many women initiate care but fail to receive comprehensive, high-quality services across the continuum [7, 17–21]. Quality-adjusted coverage consistently lags behind crude coverage estimates, with persistent socioeconomic disparities [22, 23].

Addressing these gaps requires a paradigm shift from a sole focus on service coverage to a more comprehensive approach that ensures services are high-quality, equitable, and effective in improving maternal and newborn health outcomes. This paper examines the effective coverage cascade of MNH services in Ethiopia during the antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum periods, with a focus on geographic inequalities. It explores disparities and gaps in service utilization, quality, and patient-reported care outcomes across regions to inform tailored, needs-based solutions. The findings aim to inform evidence-based strategies for measuring and improving MNH service delivery and maternal and newborn survival in Ethiopia and similar settings.

Methods

Settings

In 2010, the Government of Ethiopia restructured its health system into three tiers—primary, secondary, and tertiary care—to effectively deliver health services across the continuum. The primary tier is the foundation of the health system and typically comprises one primary hospital, 4–5 health centers, and 20–25 health posts per woreda (i.e., district), serving a population of approximately 100,000 people. Primary health care services encompass promotive, preventive, curative, and rehabilitative care delivered through static facilities (health posts, health centers, and primary hospitals), at home or in the community, and through outreach services. These services are implemented via the HEP and involve the engagement of community volunteers. Health posts, the most peripheral units, provide non-diagnostic MCH services such as ANC counseling, iron-folic acid (IFA) supplementation, PNC follow-up, immunization, family planning, and health education. Health centers offer basic diagnostic and curative MCH services, including urine and blood tests during ANC, skilled delivery, basic obstetric and newborn care, PNC, child illness management, and referral services. Primary hospitals serve as referral facilities for health centers and deliver comprehensive MCH services, including management of obstetric and newborn complications, cesarean sections, blood transfusions, and advanced diagnostic and inpatient care.

Since November 2023, with funding from the Gates Foundation, JSI has been implementing the “Strengthening Service Delivery in Ethiopia (SSD)” project to enhance the quality of the primary health care (PHC) services to improve MCH. The project conducted a population-linked facility formative assessment from July to August 2024 to inform program design and establish benchmarks for monitoring implementation outcomes. The study area includes 20 woredas—16 agrarian and 4 pastoral—across seven regions of Ethiopia: Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Sidama, Central, Somali, and Southwest Ethiopia. These sites were purposively selected to represent both agrarian and pastoralist contexts, capturing the diversity of health service delivery and utilization across Ethiopia. Selection was conducted in consultation with regional health bureaus and the Ministry of Health, guided by national maternal and child health priorities and the presence of other development partners. Pastoral communities in Afar and Somali regions are known for their sparse populations and mobility, as they traverse large areas in search of pasture and water for their herds. Additionally, many parts of these regions are hard to reach due to poor infrastructure. While the agrarian woredas have better infrastructure and access to health services.

Design

This study utilized formative implementation science research data to examine the effective coverage cascade across ANC and peripartum care (PPC), including health services provided during labor, delivery, and the immediate postpartum period.

Sampling strategy

A stratified multistage sampling technique, stratified by agrarian and pastoral regions, was employed to recruit women with infants aged 0–11 months. The survey also included linked facility assessments and client exit interviews. A two-stage stratified cluster sampling technique was used for household selection. The list of kebeles and their corresponding catchment populations was obtained from the intervention woredas. In the first stage, 94 kebeles were randomly selected as primary sampling units—61 in agrarian and 33 in pastoral regions. Sample sizes within each kebele were proportionally allocated based on the population size of each kebele.

An updated list of households with women having infants aged 0–11 months, along with their unique household identifiers, was retrieved from the family folders at the respective kebele health posts. This list served as the sampling frame for data collectors. In the second stage, a systematic random sample of households was drawn from this frame, proportional to the kebele clusters.

All primary hospitals and sampled health centers in the project intervention areas were also enumerated. Within each selected facility, four antepartum and postpartum clients who sought care during the data collection period were recruited for exit interviews.

Sample size

The required sample size to monitor the program’s effectiveness was calculated using StatCalc, based on a comparative cross-sectional household study design. A double population proportion formula was employed to assess the large-scale implementation of the SSD investment on household maternal and child health care behaviors and practices. The calculation assumed a 95% confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96), a 5% margin of error, a design effect of 1.5, 80% power, and a two-sided alpha error of 0.05. We aimed to detect a 15-percentage point increase in the uptake of skilled delivery and basic vaccinations by the project’s end, with stratification between pastoral and agrarian settings. Accordingly, a sample of 1,922 mothers of children aged 0–11 months was recruited, with 1,118 from agrarian and 804 from pastoral regions.

The sample size for facilities and client exit interviews was determined using a stratified approach to ensure representativeness across the 20 intervention woredas. All health centers in pastoral areas and all primary hospitals (located in agrarian regions) were included in the sample. For health centers in agrarian areas, 53 out of 102 across the 16 woredas were selected. This sampling approach ensured adequate power to detect meaningful differences in service readiness and client experience, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 10% margin of error, and a 50% expected prevalence for key facility readiness indicators [24]. Within each selected facility, four client exit interviews were conducted to evaluate the quality index, encompassing both care experiences and providers’ provision of evidence-based clinical recommendations in ANC and PPC. Accordingly, a total of 236 women (105 ANC and 131 peripartum clients)—200 from agrarian areas and 36 from pastoral areas—who visited 67 health facilities, including five hospitals and 62 health centers, for ANC, delivery, or PNC services on the day of the survey, participated in the study.

Data collection

The questionnaires for the regions of Afar, Amhara, Central Ethiopia, Sidama, and Southwest Ethiopia were administered in Amharic; those for the Somali region were in Af-Somali; and the Oromia region’s questionnaires were in Afaan Oromo.

We deployed 53 data collectors and supervisors, conducting six days of training followed by a one-day field test in Addis Ababa from July 1–6, 2024. The questionnaires were administered to mothers in their homes. The interviews gathered data on sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, gender-related measures, care-seeking behaviors across the continuum of maternal and newborn health, essential newborn care, family planning, and immunization services (Additional file 1). Facility readiness assessments and exit interviews were conducted through key informant interviews with facility heads and providers, direct observations of facility infrastructure, and interviews with clients visiting the facility on the day of the survey (Additional files 2 and 3).

Data collection was facilitated using a web-based mHealth platform (SurveyCTO) via tablets and smartphones, enabling Global Positioning System (GPS) data collection. During the data collection phase, field supervisors provided continuous oversight. Survey supervisors and coordinators monitored and supervised the fieldwork to ensure data quality, which included randomly revisiting selected households to validate responses. The project’s Monitoring and Evaluation team implemented a central data quality assurance procedure, involving daily preliminary analysis of data submitted to the central server, prompt resolution of field-reported issues, and real-time communication of necessary adjustments to the entire data collection team.

Measurements and data analysis

Antepartum and peripartum service contact coverage was measured as the proportion of the target women with 0–11 months of age children who had at least one antenatal care visit during pregnancy and delivered in the institution from a health care provider during the last pregnancy, respectively (Table 1). It was calculated from mothers’ self-reports during the household survey. To estimate readiness-adjusted and quality-adjusted coverages, we linked the readiness and quality index measures calculated from the facility readiness and exit interviews from facility assessments [25–27].

Effective coverage cascade measures and definitions

Proportion of women with a live birth in the last year who received ANC and PPC from a health facility, where the required inputs were available, adjusted for contact coverage. The readiness index for each intervention reflected the availability of essential amenities, equipment and supplies, medicines and commodities, diagnostics, and human resources required to deliver the service according to standards. It was measured on the day of the facility assessment and expressed as a percentage score.

Antepartum readiness index comprises the following eight items: blood pressure measuring machine, hemoglobin test, urine protein test, iron and folic acid (IFA) tablets, injectable antibiotics, ANC guidelines, ANC job aid, and trained personnel on ANC.

Peripartum readiness index consists of 19 items: manual vacuum extractor, vacuum aspirator or dilation and curettage kit, gloves, blood pressure apparatus, baby scale, newborn bag and mask, suction apparatus, examination light, tetracycline eye ointment prophylaxis, injectable uterotonics, magnesium sulfate (MgSO4), injectable antibiotics, skin disinfectant, alcohol hand rub or clean water, intravenous (IV) fluids, emergency transport, skilled birth attendant present, trained on basic emergency and obstetric care, and trained on newborn resuscitation.

Intervention coverage was defined as the proportion of women with a live birth in the past year who reported receiving key interventions during their most recent pregnancy and peripartum periods. Intervention-adjusted coverage referred to the same proportion, but was adjusted using the antepartum and peripartum intervention index derived from mothers’ self-reports in the household survey, based on nationally recommended interventions.

Antepartum intervention index: Includes weight measurement, blood pressure measurement, urine tests, blood tests, ultrasound examinations, IFA supplementation, deworming, and counseling on danger signs of pregnancy, breastfeeding, maternal nutrition, place of delivery, and postpartum family planning.

Peripartum intervention index: Includes breastfeeding within one hour of birth, baby weighing, immediate postpartum checkups for the mother and newborn, and immediate drying and wrapping of the baby after delivery, according to national standards.

Input-adjusted coverage was measured, accounting for the facility readiness score to offer antepartum and peripartum services and interventions. Facility readiness was measured from facility audits based on the availability of essential medicines, trained personnel, medical equipment, and guidelines, following the WHO Service Availability and Readiness Assessment guide [28]. Table 1 presents the items used to measure facility readiness for ANC and PCC. Eight and 19 items were used to measure readiness for ANC, and PCC, respectively, and each item had scores of yes/no or 1 and 0 values. Percentage scores were then generated for the facility readiness index. Then we multiplied these readiness scores by the contact coverage to get input-adjusted coverage.

For intervention-adjusted coverage, we measured the intervention index based on the nationally recommended interventions for ANC and PPC. ANC services included physical examinations, laboratory tests, counseling, and treatment. Specific items such as weight measurement, blood pressure measurement, urine tests, blood tests, ultrasound examinations, IFA supplementation, tetanus toxoid and diphtheria vaccination, deworming, and counseling on danger signs of pregnancy, breastfeeding, maternal nutrition, place of delivery, and postpartum family planning were included. Similarly, for PCC, the intervention index was measured based on key interventions such as breastfeeding within one hour of birth, baby weighing, postpartum checkups, and immediate drying and wrapping of the baby after delivery. Each item was scored as 1 (received) or 0 (not received), and a percentage index score was generated. Intervention-adjusted coverage was then obtained by multiplying the input-adjusted coverage by the intervention index score.

For quality-adjusted coverage, we measured the quality index based on mothers’ experiences of care and the provision of care, using data from exit interviews conducted at health facilities (detailed in Additional file 4). The experiences of care for both ANC and PNC clients were assessed through 15 items covering effective communication, dignity and respect, and emotional support. Provision of ANC and PPC was measured using 23 and 14 items, respectively. Each item was scored as 1 or 0, and a quality index score was generated by multiplying the experiences of care and the provision of care. Finally, we calculated quality-adjusted cascade coverage by multiplying the intervention-adjusted coverage by the quality index score.

Data were analyzed using Stata version 15.1. Weighted samples were used in the analysis as appropriate. Service contact coverage and intervention coverage were weighted using the household survey weights, while the facility readiness and quality index measures were weighted using the facility assessment. Then we linked the household and facility datasets using ecological linking techniques [29]. First, the health facility assessment and client exit interview datasets were linked at the health facility level. These were subsequently linked to the household dataset at the woreda level, after aggregating individual-level data. To assess statistically significant losses across the ANC and PPC effective coverage cascades (contact, readiness-adjusted, intervention-adjusted, and quality-adjusted), we conducted paired t-tests comparing sequential cascade stages at the woreda level. The tests estimated mean differences between consecutive steps and assessed whether observed declines were statistically greater than zero. Analyses were conducted separately for ANC and PPC cascades, using two-sided tests with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Ethiopian Public Health Association’s Research Ethics Review Committee (Ref. #: EPHA/OG/382/24, June 28, 2024). Written permission to conduct the study was sought from the regional health bureaus, zonal health departments, and relevant institutions.

Informed verbal consent was obtained from all participants after providing them with comprehensive information about the research to ensure they fully understood the study in accordance with the Fifth Edition of the National Research Ethics Review Guideline. If the respondent was younger than 18 years old, consent was sought from her husband or guardian. The consent form was approved by the Ethics Review Committee (Ref. #: EPHA/OG/382/24, June 28, 2024), and voluntary participation was ensured throughout the study. All participants were informed about the study’s purpose, benefits, and potential risks, and they were made aware of their right to opt out or decline to answer any questions. When a respondent agreed to participate after reviewing the consent information, the interviewer documented consent by marking the questionnaire and providing a digital signature below the consent statement. Interviews proceeded only after consent was given and properly documented. The researchers ensured that all information obtained from participants was kept confidential and private to respect their privacy and maintain the integrity of the study.

Results

Background characteristics of respondents

The mean age of respondents was 28 years in agrarian regions and 29 years in pastoral regions. Most household survey respondents were married. A little more than one-third of mothers were unable to read and write, with wide regional disparities—83% of pastoral women were illiterate, compared to 33% in agrarian areas. Religion also varied widely, with 89% of pastoral women identifying as Muslim, while 58% of agrarian women identified as Christian. Similar religious differences were observed among exit interview respondents, where 80% of pastoral women identified as Muslim, compared to 51% of agrarian women identifying as Christian. Just over half of the respondents (53%) lived within an hour’s walking distance of the nearest health center or hospital (Table 2).

Service contact coverage

Most women (81%; 95% CI: 76–85%) had at least one ANC contact, though this varied significantly between agrarian and pastoral communities. Among agrarian women, 89% attended at least one ANC contact, compared to 57% of pastoral women. Approximately three-fourths (74%; 95% CI: 69–79%) of mothers delivered at health institutions, with 81% of agrarian and 53% of pastoral women giving birth in a facility. Postnatal care within 48 h was received by about one-fifth (21%) of mothers, with coverage higher among agrarian women (24%) than pastoral women (14%).

Service delivery readiness

The mean ANC service readiness score across health facilities was 74%. While most facilities had urine testing and blood pressure measurement services available, a significant number lacked key trained ANC staff in the past two years and did not provide hemoglobin testing (Fig. 1).

The mean readiness score for PPC was 80%, with agrarian facilities scoring higher (81%) than pastoral ones (71%). The mean reported care experience score among women who participated in the exit interviews was 82%, with high ratings for respectful treatment (94%) and emotional support (96%). Intervention coverage

The household survey indicated that slightly less than three-quarters of the recommended ANC interventions were received by women during their last pregnancy, with no wide regional variations. Most women received weight and blood pressure measurements, urine and blood tests, as well as counseling on breastfeeding and maternal nutrition. However, a significant proportion missed ultrasound examinations, deworming medications, and PPFP counseling (Fig. 2).

Approximately 82% of the recommended peripartum and newborn interventions were provided to women and their newborns during their last pregnancy. However, peripartum and newborn care practices varied across regions. For example, early initiation of breastfeeding within one hour of birth was reported in 75% of agrarian communities but only 41% of pastoral communities (Fig. 3).

Effective coverage cascades and inequalities across regions

About 81% of women aged 15–49 years with a live birth in the past year reported having at least one ANC contact; however, readiness-adjusted coverage dropped to 63%, intervention-adjusted coverage to 49%, and quality-adjusted coverage to just 26%. The paired t-test analysis revealed substantial and statistically significant losses across multiple stages of both the ANC and PPC effective coverage cascades. For ANC, the largest declines occurred between contact and readiness-adjusted coverage (18% points (PP), p < 0.001) and between readiness-and intervention-adjusted coverage (17 PP, p < 0.001), highlighting major gaps in facility preparedness and delivery of essential interventions. The final step—from intervention- to quality-adjusted coverage—showed only a small and statistically insignificant decline (1.5 PP, p = 0.54). For PPC, losses were similarly large and highly significant at all stages, with coverage decreasing by 15 PP from contact to readiness (p < 0.001), 14 PP from readiness to intervention delivery (p < 0.001), and an additional 23 PP from intervention to quality-adjusted coverage (p < 0.001) (Table 7).

Discussion

This study indicates that, despite relatively high initial contact coverage for both antepartum and peripartum, effective care coverage rates are substantially lower, with wide regional variations. Stepwise declines were observed across both cascades, with the largest losses occurring between initial contact and readiness-adjusted coverage, followed by further reductions at the intervention-adjusted stage. While ANC quality-adjusted coverage showed only minor declines, PPC quality coverage remained low. In agrarian woreda, the most pronounced drop was at the quality-adjusted stage, followed by intervention-adjusted and readiness-adjusted stages, whereas in pastoral settings, the largest gap occurred at the contact coverage, with a more gradual decline across subsequent stages.

The findings on effective coverage cascade losses are consistent with previous research, indicating that high service contact rates do not necessarily equate to high-quality or effective care [19, 23, 30]. Studies in LMICs have similarly highlighted significant gaps between contact coverage (service utilization) and the actual quality of MNH services [23, 25, 31–33]. In Ethiopia, while institutional delivery rates have improved, earlier research has documented persistent deficiencies in MNH care quality, with incorrect or inconsistent delivery of critical clinical interventions, such as postpartum hemorrhage management, neonatal resuscitation, and postnatal danger sign counseling [4]. Emerging evidence underscores that simply increasing service access is not enough; interventions must be high-quality, evidence-based, and consistently delivered to drive meaningful improvements in MNH outcomes [25, 31].

Despite seeking care at health facilities, a significant proportion of women missed essential services such as ultrasound examinations, deworming medication, antepartum screening for maternal depression, and counseling on PPFP and breastfeeding. Similarly, previous studies have reported widespread gaps in MNH care, with major deficiencies in diagnostic testing, counseling, essential interventions, thermal care, breastfeeding support, and postnatal danger sign counseling [19, 23, 30]. A multi-country analysis in Africa found that while ANC attendance was relatively high, fewer than half of pregnant women received a comprehensive package of ANC services, including blood pressure monitoring, syphilis testing, and IFA supplementation [32]. Likewise, a systematic review of PPC in LMICs revealed that despite increasing facility births, critical interventions—such as uterotonics for postpartum hemorrhage prevention, essential newborn care, and breastfeeding support—were inconsistently provided [33]. The present study reinforces these findings, demonstrating that the greatest losses in effective coverage occur in the transition from basic service contact to the receipt of quality-adjusted care. These gaps represent missed opportunities to improve maternal and newborn survival outcomes, highlighting the urgent need for structured quality-improvement initiatives that enhance service delivery across the continuum of care.

Facility readiness and quality index scores for antenatal and peripartum services were moderate, yet critical shortages of trained staff, essential diagnostic tools, and emergency transport severely constrained service quality. The absence of key resources—such as hemoglobin tests, suction apparatus, vacuum extractors, and reliable emergency transport—posed major barriers, while gaps in physical and audio privacy and limited involvement of women in decision-making further compromised care. Comparative studies from sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America highlight similar challenges, with poor service readiness, inadequate provider competency, and limited access to essential diagnostics contributing to service gaps [31, 32]. In Ethiopia, persistent shortages of supplies, inadequate provider training, and weak referral systems continue to undermine maternal and newborn care [34, 35]. Strengthening service readiness, expanding comprehensive, high-impact, evidence-based antenatal and peripartum clinical interventions, and addressing critical quality gaps—particularly in counseling, diagnostics, and postpartum care—are essential to improving maternal and newborn health outcomes. Investments in facility infrastructure and provider capacity are also crucial to ensuring high-quality, dignified care experiences for women and newborns [33, 36].

Regional disparities further exacerbate challenges in MNH service delivery, with women in pastoralist areas consistently experiencing lower service coverage compared to those in agrarian settings. Although variations decrease across readiness-, intervention-, and quality-adjusted coverage levels, significant gaps remain. These findings suggest that while access is a major barrier—particularly in pastoralist regions—the health system’s performance in delivering quality care is relatively equitable, though uniformly low, once women engage with services. Studies in similar socio-economic contexts have reported comparable patterns, where rural and nomadic populations face disproportionately high barriers to quality maternal and newborn care due to weak health infrastructure and geographic inaccessibility [37, 38]. Structural barriers such as inadequate healthcare facilities, long travel distances, low educational attainment, economic constraints, and socio-cultural factors further widen these inequities [18, 23, 36]. Addressing these disparities requires targeted interventions that prioritize early service uptake in pastoral communities, such as expanding mobile health services, strengthening community health worker engagement, and implementing policies that promote equitable resource allocation to underserved areas and ensure quality of care in both settings.

The concept of effective coverage—which accounts for service inputs, quality, and content—has been increasingly recognized as a critical metric for evaluating healthcare performance [25]. Recent studies have highlighted a strong demand for effective coverage measures, and the positive policy environment in Ethiopia encourages the implementation of such measures [39]. Currently, the Ministry of Health of Ethiopia has developed a national guide for measuring and improving effective coverage, providing a framework and guidance for planning, implementing, and utilizing country-led effective coverage measurements for priority health programs and services [27]. Consequently, the implementation of effective coverage cascade measurements for continuous improvement is crucial to achieving Sustainable Development Goals and national health targets.

This study has several strengths, including the triangulation of household surveys, exit interviews, and facility assessments to provide a comprehensive analysis of effective MNH service coverage cascades. The inclusion of both agrarian and pastoral settings allows for a nuanced understanding of regional disparities, contributing valuable insights for targeted health system improvements. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The study relies on self-reported data from mothers, which may introduce recall and social desirability biases. While exit interviews are valuable for capturing client perspectives on aspects such as respect, communication, and satisfaction, they are limited in accurately assessing the technical quality of care, especially for complex intrapartum practices like the administration of AMTSL and the use of the partograph. Such interventions may not be fully observed or understood by clients, and interviews conducted within or near health facilities may further contribute to overreporting of positive experiences. The absence of direct clinical observation constrained our ability to capture the full spectrum of peripartum care and limited the validity of quality estimates. Although we attempted to mitigate these limitations through interviewer training, assurance of confidentiality, and triangulation with service delivery records, future studies could benefit from incorporating direct clinical observations of intrapartum care to improve the accuracy of quality assessments and strengthen the estimation of quality-adjusted coverage in low-resource settings.

The small sample sizes for facility readiness assessments and evaluations of service provision and experiences of care—particularly in pastoral communities—may have influenced the observed estimates. Another limitation is the lack of data on user adherence and patient-reported outcomes, which are crucial for shedding light on the full effective coverage cascade from contact coverage to outcome-adjusted coverage to understand the complete trajectory from contact coverage to care outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study provides critical evidence to inform policies and interventions aimed at strengthening MNH services in Ethiopia. It offers a nuanced understanding of gaps in service coverage, facility readiness, intervention delivery, and user experience (quality) across pastoral and agrarian communities, allowing for the tailoring of interventions to address the most critical gaps—utilization in pastoral settings and quality in both agrarian and pastoral communities.

Conclusion

While Ethiopia has expanded access to MNH services across the PHC system, substantial losses along the effective coverage cascade persist, highlighting ongoing quality gaps and enduring inequalities. Women in pastoralist areas continue to experience lower service coverage, and although quality-adjusted care is relatively equitable, it remains uniformly low compared to agrarian settings. Inadequate diagnostics, limited emergency transport, and insufficient provider capacity reduce the impact of interventions. Addressing these issues requires equity-focused strategies to enhance healthcare readiness, expand comprehensive maternal health services, improve care quality, integrate maternal and newborn care, and strengthen health system accountability. Ensuring respectful, high-quality care and increasing access to essential diagnostics are critical to better MNH outcomes. These findings highlight the urgent need for sustained investment in health system strengthening and policy reforms to ensure all pregnant women, mothers, and their newborns receive life-saving, quality maternal and newborn care, regardless of location.

Maternal and newborn health services involve complex interactions between clients and the health service delivery system. Measuring contact coverage of MNH services overestimates their benefits, as only half to two-thirds of women receive quality care [9, 40]. The health service coverage cascade helps visualize the care journey of mothers and newborns, identifying key losses to target for maximum impact. Expanding the use of effective coverage metrics in routine monitoring can provide policymakers with actionable insights to improve service quality and equity. This highlights the need for a national health system to regularly measure quality and outcomes, alongside service coverage, to improve MNH outcomes and achieve UHC and Sustainable Development Goals. Integrating effective coverage assessments into national health information systems, strengthening facility-level accountability, and implementing targeted, data-informed continuous improvement interventions are essential to ensuring both access and effective delivery of care. Furthermore, to enhance the utility of the effective coverage cascade for evidence-based decision-making, it is critical to strengthen routine data systems to capture user adherence-adjusted and outcome-adjusted coverage, alongside reliable intervention coverage metrics. Integrating client experience and outcome measures into existing digital reporting platforms and complementing these with periodic validation through population-based surveys or longitudinal tracking can significantly improve the completeness, accuracy, and actionability of coverage data, enhancing responsiveness and accountability for high-quality, equitable maternal and newborn care for all.
