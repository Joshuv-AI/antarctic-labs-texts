Multicellular Exosomes as Therapeutic Modulators of Neuroinflammation: Mechanisms and Clinical Prospects
Authors: Jiayou Qiu, Mingbo Fan, Chenghu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuroinflammation is a fundamental pathological driver of diverse central nervous system (CNS) disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, and traumatic brain injury. This complex process is characterized by persistent neuronal damage and the orchestrated response of glial and peripheral immune cells. Intercellular communication during these inflammatory responses is increasingly attributed to exosomes, 30–150 nanometer (nm) extracellular vesicles that deliver bioactive lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. These vesicles, secreted by neurons, glia, and mesenchymal stem cells, facilitate the sophisticated signaling networks necessary for maintaining neural homeostasis and immune regulation. However, while the individual roles of these vesicles are emerging, a comprehensive synthesis of how multicellular exosomal crosstalk collectively modulates the neuroinflammatory landscape remains elusive. This review systematically evaluates the bidirectional exchange of exosomes among diverse CNS cell populations and their specific roles in regulating inflammatory cytokine expression and immune cell phenotypes. We further examine the molecular mechanisms through which exosomal cargoes promote neural repair, alongside a critical analysis of current challenges in isolation, targeted delivery, and safety. By integrating these multicellular perspectives, this review provides a theoretical framework for utilizing exosomes as precision therapeutic modulators. Ultimately, it highlights the potential of exosome-based interventions to transform clinical outcomes for neuroinflammatory diseases.

Introduction

Neuroinflammation is now widely acknowledged as a key pathological mechanism underlying the onset and progression of numerous CNS disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, stroke, traumatic brain injury, and multiple sclerosis.1 The neuroinflammatory response involves the activation and functional engagement of resident CNS cells, predominantly microglia and astrocytes, as well as oligodendrocytes and neurons, together with the recruitment of peripheral immune cells into the CNS. This intricate cellular crosstalk triggers the release of a broad range of inflammatory mediators, including cytokines, chemokines, and reactive oxygen species, which can exacerbate neuronal injury and contribute to disease progression.2 Although inflammation serves as a protective response under physiological conditions, chronic or excessive neuroinflammation is detrimental and is tightly linked to neuronal dysfunction, synaptic loss, and impaired tissue repair.3

Conventional anti-inflammatory treatments, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids, have demonstrated limited effectiveness in managing CNS diseases. This is primarily attributable to their limited capacity to traverse the blood-brain barrier (BBB), accompanied by the risk of substantial side effects and non-specific immunosuppression. Therefore, there is an urgent need for the development of novel therapeutic strategies that can selectively target neuroinflammatory pathways and reestablish homeostasis within the central nervous system. Traditional anti-inflammatory agents, including NSAIDs and corticosteroids, have demonstrated only modest benefits in the treatment of central nervous system disorders. This is due in part to inadequate CNS specificity and, for many compounds, limited BBB permeability, together with the risk of significant systemic adverse effects and nonspecific immunosuppression. Accordingly, there is an urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches that can precisely target neuroinflammatory pathways and restore central nervous system homeostasis.

Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles of approximately 30–150 nm in diameter that have gained considerable attention as mediators of intercellular signaling. Released by virtually all cell types, exosomes are commonly found in numerous biological fluids, such as cerebrospinal fluid, blood, and urine.4 Exosomes encapsulate a broad spectrum of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, messenger RNAs (mRNAs), and microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby influencing the function and phenotypic behavior of recipient cells. In the context of neuroinflammation, exosomes have attracted considerable interest for their potential to exert immunoregulatory effects, regulate inflammatory signaling pathways, and promote tissue repair.5 Accumulating evidence indicates that exosomes originating from various CNS cell types play distinct roles in the CNS microenvironment. Neuron-derived exosomes have been reported to modulate synaptic activity and inflammatory signaling, thereby potentially protecting neurons from inflammatory damage. Astrocyte-derived exosomes are involved in maintaining neural microenvironment homeostasis and may exert neuroprotective or immunomodulatory effects via the transfer of bioactive cargo, including anti-inflammatory molecules.6 Exosomes derived from oligodendrocytes facilitate myelin repair and regeneration, a process of particular relevance in demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis. Microglia-derived exosomes exhibit context-dependent dual roles in neuroinflammation, either amplifying or attenuating inflammatory responses according to their molecular cargo and the surrounding microenvironment. Moreover, exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) hold substantial promise as cell-free therapeutic candidates by their immunomodulatory and neuroprotective properties in neuroinflammatory disorders.7

Despite their promising therapeutic potential, exosomes still face several major obstacles on the path to clinical translation. The molecular mechanisms through which exosomes modulate neuroinflammation remain incompletely defined, and substantial technical barriers continue to hamper the isolation, purification, and the molecular and functional characterization of exosomes from diverse cellular sources. Moreover, challenges related to targeted delivery to specific brain regions, large-scale and reproducible manufacturing, and long-term safety must be overcome before exosome-based therapies can be implemented in clinical settings8,9 (Figure 1). Exosomes

Definition and Characteristic

Exosomes, a small extracellular vesicle subtype, has diameters varying from about 30 to 150 nanometers. Initially identified in 1983, exosomes were originally considered to serve as a means for cellular waste transportation. However, Emerging evidence suggests that exosomes are vital mediators of intercellular communication.10 They are produced by numerous cell types, including neurons, glial cells, immune cells, and mesenchymal stem cells. Exosomes are formed within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) and are subsequently released into the extracellular space through the fusion of MVBs with the plasma membrane11 (Figure 2). Structurally, exosomes are phospholipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles that harbor a diverse repertoire of bioactive cargo, including proteins, lipids, DNA, mRNA, miRNA, and long non-coding RNA (lncRNA). The molecular cargo of exosomes reflects the physiological state of their parental cells and can modulate recipient cell function.12

Complex Functions

Exosomes play diverse roles in both physiological and pathological settings. Owing to their central role in cell-to-cell communication, exosomes transfer proteins, nucleic acids, and other bioactive cargo to recipient cells, a process of particular relevance to the nervous and immune systems.13,14 In the context of immune regulation, exosomes exhibit context-dependent effects, either promoting immune cell activation and inflammatory responses or exerting immunosuppressive functions that contribute to immune homeostasis. Tumor-derived exosomes, for instance, may suppress immune cell activity and thereby facilitate immune evasion.14 Beyond immune modulation, exosomes participate in inflammatory signaling by delivering bioactive mediators, including cytokines and chemokines, and can either exacerbate or attenuate inflammation depending on their molecular cargo. Moreover, exosomes are involved in the regulation of apoptosis and have been implicated in tissue repair and regeneration.15 For instance, exosomes derived from stem cells have been identified to promote tissue repair and attenuate cell death, underscoring their therapeutic potential for regenerative therapeutic applications. By virtue of their ability to mirror the physiological or pathophysiological state of parental cells and their detectability in body fluids, exosomes are increasingly recognized as valuable biomarkers in multiple disease such as cancer and neurological disorders.16,17 Moreover, their favorable biocompatibility and low immunogenicity render them attractive carriers for targeted drug delivery.

Taken together, exosomes are essential extracellular vesicles that serve as critical regulators of cell-to-cell communication, immune regulation, and the modulation of inflammatory processes. They also hold considerable promise for disease diagnosis and therapy. As exosome research advances, their roles and mechanisms in neurological diseases will become increasingly well defined, offering new insights and strategies for the prevention and treatment of these disorders.18

Mechanisms of Neuroinflammation

Key Cellular Participants

Neuroinflammation is a multifaceted pathological process in the CNS that occurs in response to injury, infection, toxic stimuli, or neurodegeneration, involving tightly coordinated interactions among resident neural cells, vascular components, and infiltrating immune cells. While acute neuroinflammation may initially serve protective roles by facilitating pathogen clearance and tissue repair, persistent or dysregulated inflammation can disrupt CNS homeostasis and drive progressive neuronal injury.19 Neurons are not only major targets of inflammatory damage but also active contributors to inflammatory signaling through the release of cytokines, chemokines, neurotransmitters, and damage-associated signals. Among glial populations, astrocytes and microglia are the principal regulators of neuroinflammation. Astrocytes maintain metabolic support, ionic homeostasis, neurotransmitter recycling, and BBB integrity, but under pathological conditions they become reactive and secrete mediators that may either aggravate inflammation or promote neuroprotection and repair, depending on the context. As resident innate immune cells of the CNS, microglia play a central role in immune surveillance by continuously monitoring the neural microenvironment and responding rapidly to injury or infection through phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and cytokine production. However, excessive microglial activation can amplify inflammatory injury and contribute to neuronal dysfunction. Oligodendrocytes, although not primary immune effector cells, are highly vulnerable to inflammatory insults, and their damage can result in demyelination and impaired axonal conduction.20 Furthermore, disruption of the BBB allows peripheral immune cells, including T cells, B cells, monocytes, and neutrophils, to infiltrate the CNS, thereby intensifying local inflammation and tissue damage. Collectively, neuroinflammation represents a highly dynamic and context-dependent process shaped by extensive crosstalk among neural, immune, and vascular elements.21

Mechanistic Pathways

Neuroinflammation is driven by multiple interconnected mechanisms. Following CNS injury or pathogen invasion, resident glial cells, together with infiltrating immune cells, produce a wide range of inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). These factors amplify inflammatory cascades and contribute to neuronal dysfunction, cellular injury, and apoptotic cell death.22 Under normal conditions, CNS homeostasis is maintained by a dynamic balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory signals, including cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β). During neuroinflammation, however, this balance becomes disrupted, leading to sustained pro-inflammatory activity and inadequate anti-inflammatory control, thereby exacerbating tissue injury.23

In addition, neuroinflammation can compromise BBB integrity, permitting the infiltration of peripheral immune cells and blood-borne inflammatory mediators into the CNS, thereby amplifying the extent and severity of the inflammatory response.24 Persistent inflammatory activation may ultimately lead to neuronal and glial cell death, including apoptosis and necrosis, resulting in neurological dysfunction and structural damage within the CNS25,26 (Figure 3). Collectively, neuroinflammation is driven by the coordinated interplay of diverse cellular populations and molecular cascades. The release of inflammatory mediators, disruption of cytokine homeostasis, oxidative stress, and BBB breakdown interact to promote CNS injury and disease progression. Further insight into these mechanisms will be instrumental in guiding the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for neurological disorders.

Neuron-Derived Exosomes

Exosomes released by neurons contribute significantly to the regulation of synaptic function and plasticity. They transport bioactive cargo, including synaptic proteins, receptors, and regulatory miRNAs, thereby modulating synaptic signaling and neuronal connectivity.29 Reported protein cargo associated with neuronal and synaptic function includes glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored prion protein, glutamate receptor subunits GluR2/3, postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95), synapsin-1, neuroligin-1, and synaptosomal-associated protein, 25kDa (SNAP-25).30 In addition, miR-132, miR-134, and miR-212 have been implicated in the regulation of synaptic plasticity.31 Neuron-derived exosomes also modulate glial activity and inflammatory signaling, thereby influencing neural homeostasis and neuroinflammatory responses in a context-dependent manner.32

Astrocyte-Derived Exosomes

Astrocytes are key regulators of the CNS microenvironment, and their exosomes are involved in neuroprotection and immune modulation in a context-dependent manner. Astrocyte-derived exosomes can deliver neurotrophic factors, antioxidant enzymes, and anti-inflammatory molecules to neurons and other glial cells, thereby supporting neuronal survival and repair.33 They also participate in the modulation of immune responses by modulating the activation state of microglia and, potentially, peripheral immune cells, thus contributing to CNS immune homeostasis.

Oligodendrocyte-Derived Exosomes

Oligodendrocytes are specialized glial cells responsible for the formation and long-term maintenance of myelin sheaths in the CNS. Exosomes derived from oligodendrocytes contain myelin-related proteins, lipids, and other bioactive molecules that may contribute to myelin maintenance, repair, and regeneration.34 These exosomes can be taken up by neurons and other glial cells, thereby supporting intercellular communication, remyelination, and protection against inflammatory injury, which may be of particular relevance in demyelinating diseases, including multiple sclerosis.

Microglia-Derived Exosomes

Microglia constitute the resident immune cell population of the CNS, and microglia-derived exosomes are important mediators of neuroinflammatory signaling. Depending on the activation state of the parent cells, these exosomes may contain pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory cargo, including cytokines, miRNAs, and other immunoregulatory molecules.35 Through modulation of the local inflammatory microenvironment, they can influence neuronal survival and function. Exosomes derived from pro-inflammatory microglia may aggravate neuronal injury, whereas those associated with anti-inflammatory phenotypes may support neuroprotection and tissue repair.36

MSC-Derived Exosomes

Mesenchymal stem cell–derived exosomes exhibit notable immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects. They contain a variety of bioactive molecules, including anti-inflammatory mediators, regulatory miRNAs, and growth factors, enabling them to modulate excessive immune responses, attenuate inflammation, and support tissue repair.37 Owing to their ability to modulate immune cell activity, protect neurons, and promote tissue repair and regeneration, MSC-derived exosomes hold considerable promise as therapeutic agents for a range of neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases (Figure 5).38,39 Figure 5Roles of exosomes in neuroinflammatory state. Taken together, neuron-, glial cell-, and mesenchymal stem cell–derived exosomes exhibit distinct molecular cargo profiles and biological functions that contribute to the regulation of neuroinflammation and neural repair. Their capacity to modulate immune responses, support regenerative processes, and preserve CNS homeostasis highlights their promise as both biomarkers and therapeutic candidates for neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative disorders.40 Further investigation into the specific mechanisms underlying these effects, particularly those mediated by MSC-derived exosomes, will be critical for facilitating clinical translation and improving therapeutic outcomes in CNS diseases.

Inhibition of Pro-Inflammatory Cytokine Expression

Exosomes, particularly mesenchymal stem cell–derived exosomes, modulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β. MSC-derived exosomes can attenuate inflammatory responses by delivering regulatory miRNAs, proteins, and other bioactive molecules that influence major pathways involved in cellular signaling, such as NF-κB.44 In addition, engineered stem cell-derived exosomes are reported to target other inflammatory pathways, including Janus kinases and signal transducers and activators of transcription, JAK/STAT.45 Through the transfer of these regulatory cargos to recipient cells, exosomes can interfere with signaling networks that promote the generation of inflammatory mediators, thus leading to the attenuation of neuroinflammation and limiting injury to neurons and glial cells.46 This inhibition may reduce the overall inflammatory burden within the CNS and help protect neurons and glial cells from further damage.47

Promotion of Anti-Inflammatory Cytokine Secretion

In addition to inhibiting pro-inflammatory signaling, some exosomes have been shown to p stimulate the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10 and TGF-β.48 These factors contribute to the resolution of inflammation, support tissue repair, and promote immune homeostasis within the CNS. Mechanistically, exosomes may exert these effects through the transfer of regulatory miRNAs, proteins, and other bioactive molecules that modulate anti-inflammatory signaling in recipient cells.15

Regulation of Immune Cell Activity and Suppression of Excessive Inflammatory Responses

Exosomes can modulate the activity of multiple immune-related cell types, including microglia, astrocytes, and, under pathological conditions, infiltrating peripheral immune cells.49 By shaping immune cell activation states, immunomodulatory exosomes may help suppress excessive or persistent inflammatory responses that damage neural tissue. For instance, they may attenuate pro-inflammatory microglial activation and promote anti-inflammatory or repair-associated microglial functions, thereby limiting neurotoxicity and supporting tissue repair.50 These changes may create a more favorable microenvironment for neuronal survival and recovery, contributing to the resolution of neuroinflammation.

Promotion of Neural Repair and Regeneration

Beyond their immunomodulatory effects, exosomes are increasingly recognized for their roles in neural repair and recovery. By transferring neurotrophic and other regulatory cargo to injured neurons and glial cells, they may support axonal growth, synaptic plasticity, and remyelination.38,40 Such reparative effects are particularly well documented in stem cell-derived exosomes, which hold substantial therapeutic potential in established preclinical models of neurodegenerative disorders and CNS injury. Through their neuroprotective and pro-repair activities, stem cell-derived exosomes may attenuate neuronal death, promote axonal remodeling, and support synaptic plasticity, thereby supporting to functional recovery in the injured CNS51,52(Figure 6). To sum up, certain exosomes, particularly those with immunomodulatory properties, may alleviate neuroinflammation through multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, enhancement of anti-inflammatory cytokine secretion, regulation of immune cell activity, and support of neural repair and regenerative processes. These multifaceted effects highlight the therapeutic promise of exosomes for neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative disorders, while ongoing research continues to evaluate their clinical applications and optimize their use as biotherapeutic agents.

Applications of Exosomes in Neuroinflammation-Related Diseases

Exosomes are increasingly viewed as promising therapeutic candidates for a range of neuroinflammation-related CNS disorders because of their immunomodulatory properties, support neural repair, and transfer bioactive cargo to recipient cells.43 In addition, their versatility and ability to be programmed for localized delivery render them promising candidates for the development of novel therapies for diseases for disease such as multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and traumatic brain injury.53

Alzheimer’s Disease

In Alzheimer’s disease, certain exosomes, particularly stem cell-derived or engineered exosomes, have suggested therapeutic potential in preclinical studies by promoting amyloid-beta (Aβ) clearance and modulating neuroinflammatory responses.54 Because Aβ accumulation is a central pathological feature associated with neuronal injury and inflammation, exosome-mediated reduction of Aβ burden may help attenuate disease-related neurodegenerative processes. Mechanistically, these effects may involve the transfer of proteins, enzymes, miRNAs, and other regulatory cargo that facilitate Aβ degradation or clearance, together with anti-inflammatory molecules that suppress inflammatory signaling in the brain.55 Through these combined actions, exosomes may help preserve neuronal and synaptic function and support cognitive outcomes in experimental models of Alzheimer’s disease.

Brain Injury

Following traumatic brain injury or stroke, exosomes, particularly stem cell–derived exosomes, have emerged as promising mediators of neural repair and immunomodulation in preclinical studies.22 By transferring neurotrophic and anti-inflammatory cargo to injured brain tissue, they may enhance the survival of neurons, promote glial repair, facilitate synaptic plasticity, and suppress excessive inflammatory responses.56 Collectively, these effects may help limit secondary injury and contribute to functional recovery after CNS injury.

Multiple Sclerosis

In multiple sclerosis, a chronic autoimmune demyelinating disorder of the CNS, exosomes—particularly those with immunomodulatory properties—have shown therapeutic potential in preclinical studies.55 By transferring regulatory RNAs, proteins, and other bioactive cargo, these exosomes may modulate autoreactive immune responses, suppress neuroinflammation, and support oligodendrocyte precursor cell differentiation and maturation. Through these mechanisms, exosomes may promote remyelination and contribute to functional recovery.8 In addition, some exosomes may deliver trophic and myelin repair-associated factors that further support restoration of white matter integrity. Although these findings remain largely preclinical, they highlight the potential of exosome-based strategies for multiple sclerosis treatment9,50 (Figure 7). Their ability to modulate pathological processes, regulate immune responses, and support tissue repair highlights their dual role as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic agents.56 By harnessing their natural capacity for intercellular communication and their potential for delivering bioactive molecules to target cells, exosomes may pave the way for more effective therapeutic strategies for a wide range of neurological disorders57,58 (Figure 8). Prospects and Challenges

Status and Advantages

Exosomes have several characteristics that make them attractive therapeutic platforms for neuroinflammatory diseases. Current evidence suggests that these exosomes exhibit low immunogenicity and favorable biocompatibility, which may reduce the possibility of immune-mediated graft rejection and treatment-related adverse effects. In addition, exosomes have shown the capacity to traverse the BBB, supporting their use as delivery vehicles for the central nervous system. As nanoscale carriers, they can encapsulate and transport diverse bioactive cargo, including proteins, nucleic acids, and small-molecule compounds40 (Table 2). Challenges

Despite these promising features, there are still significant challenges that need to be pointed out before exosome-based therapies can be extensively applied in clinical practice. The isolation and purification of exosomes remain technically demanding, with current methods often resulting in low yield or heterogeneous populations. Directing exosomes to specific tissues or cell types in the CNS continues to represent a major hurdle. Furthermore, the exact mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of exosomes remain incompletely understood, necessitating further in-depth research to elucidate their biological functions and optimize their clinical use (Table 3). Continued investigation is essential to uncover how exosomes interact with recipient cells, regulate signaling pathways, and influence disease progression. Future Directions

Looking ahead, the exploration of innovative exosome-based therapies for neuroinflammation is emerging as a highly promising field of research. Efforts should focus on improving exosome isolation and characterization techniques, engineering exosomes for targeted delivery, and unraveling the molecular mechanisms underlying their actions. Taken together, progress in these areas will support the clinical translation of exosome-based therapies and may provide new therapeutic opportunities for patients with neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases.

Conclusion

In summary, exosomes represent pivotal mediators of intercellular communication within the neuroinflammatory milieu, orchestrating complex signaling events among neurons, glia, and peripheral immune cells. The bidirectional exchange of exosomal cargoes not only regulates inflammatory cytokine expression and immune cell phenotypes but also contributes to neural repair and the restoration of CNS homeostasis. Despite significant advances in elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying exosome-mediated crosstalk, challenges remain in the areas of exosome isolation, targeted delivery, and safety assessment. Continued research integrating multicellular perspectives will be essential for overcoming these obstacles and maximizing the therapeutic efficacy of exosomes. Ultimately, exosome-based strategies offer a promising avenue for precision therapeutics capable of markedly improving clinical outcomes across a spectrum of neuroinflammatory disorders.
