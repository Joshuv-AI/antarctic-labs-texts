Anterior Chamber Depth and the Long-Term Safety of Implantable Collamer Lens Surgery: A Case for Re-Evaluating Age-Stratified Indication Criteria
Authors: Keizo Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeImplantable Collamer Lens (ICL) surgery is mostly performed in patients aged 20–40, with current minimum anterior chamber depth (ACD) eligibility of approximately 2.8–3.0 mm. A shallow chamber carries two distinct long-term risks: corneal endothelial cell loss, determined principally by the endothelium-to-implant distance, and angle closure, determined by progressive anterior segment crowding. This static criterion accounts for neither of the two dynamic factors that reduce effective ACD over decades: age-related crystalline lens thickening, and vault-related anterior displacement of the implant. We propose age-stratified ACD values, anchored to the empirical 2.4 mm threshold for primary angle closure in East Asians, as risk-stratification factors.MethodsWe conducted a structured narrative review of ACD and primary angle-closure disease in East Asians including Japanese data; age-related anterior segment biometry; vault dynamics; and long-term ICL outcomes, integrated into a quantitative model presented as a conservative estimate rather than a definitive predictor.ResultsThe 2.4 mm threshold is confirmed in East Asian populations and consistent with Japanese population-based biometry. ACD shallows at approximately 0.011–0.012 mm/year; vault contributes a further functional reduction of approximately 0.25–0.40 mm. A patient implanted at age 20 with an initial ACD of 2.8 mm may approach 2.0 mm by age 60. Age-stratified minimum values of 3.2, 3.1 and 3.0 mm for the third, fourth and fifth decades preserve a margin above 2.4 mm; sensitivity analysis across lens-extraction ages of 45–60 years yields 3.0–3.2 mm. Independently, ten-year data in eyes with ACD below 2.8 mm show endothelial loss at approximately twice the physiological rate.ConclusionCurrent criteria reflect the eye’s status at a single time point. We suggest age-stratified ACD values be used as risk-stratification factors alongside gonioscopy and anterior segment imaging, not as standalone exclusion criteria, in parallel with established corneal refractive surgery criteria. Long-term validation is warranted.Keywords: implantable collamer lens, anterior chamber depth, age-stratified criteria, long-term safety, refractive surgery

Introduction

Phakic intraocular lens (pIOL) implantation, in particular the posterior-chamber Implantable Collamer Lens (ICL; STAAR Surgical, Nidau, Switzerland), is now an established option for the correction of moderate to high myopia in eyes that are not candidates for corneal refractive surgery. The introduction of the V4c model with a 360 µm central port (KS-AquaPORT) substantially improved the safety profile by eliminating the need for peripheral iridotomy and reducing the incidence of pupillary block and anterior subcapsular cataract relative to earlier designs. ICL has accordingly experienced rapid global uptake among younger patients seeking refractive correction.

Despite these advances, ICL surgery remains structurally distinct from other intraocular implantation procedures. Hip arthroplasty, coronary stenting, and even cataract surgery with intraocular lens implantation share a common surgical principle: a defined anatomical space is created or vacated before the implant is introduced. ICL surgery, by contrast, places a foreign body within an essentially fixed-volume anatomical compartment—the posterior chamber bordered by the iris, ciliary body, and the anterior surface of the crystalline lens. The crystalline lens is preserved, the anterior chamber is non-expansile, and the implant occupies space that was previously shared by the iris, lens, and corneal endothelium.

A shallow anterior chamber carries two long-term risks that act through different mechanisms, on different structures, and are supported by evidence of differing maturity. The first is corneal endothelial cell loss, which is principally determined by the distance between the corneal endothelium and the anterior surface of the implant. The second is angle closure, which is determined by progressive crowding of the anterior segment over time. Both arise from the same anatomical starting point, but they are not interchangeable. The quantitative model developed in this review addresses the second; the first is considered separately in Long-Term Outcomes Data and the Latency of Risk, where direct measurement rather than modelling is available.

Patients undergoing ICL surgery are typically myopic, and myopic eyes tend to have somewhat deeper anterior chambers and wider angles than emmetropic or hyperopic eyes of comparable age. Angle closure is correspondingly uncommon in this population, and this selection effect reduces the absolute baseline angle-closure risk. Three qualifications nevertheless apply. First, the framework developed here takes the individual patient’s measured preoperative ACD as its input and makes no population-level assumption about how deep myopic chambers are; a highly myopic eye with a deep chamber is unaffected by it, and the framework engages only with the minority of candidates who are myopic and nonetheless have a shallow measured chamber. The rarity of a phenotype within a population does not establish the safety of that phenotype in the individuals who have it, and rarity may be precisely why existing short- and mid-term cohorts, which are not powered for it, have not detected a signal. Second, narrow angles do occur in myopic eyes, and gonioscopy is recommended in ICL candidates for that reason.1 Third, the premise that ICL candidates are uniformly highly myopic is becoming less secure: the FDA-approved labelling for the EVO ICL extends to a spherical equivalent of −3.0 D,2 and the Japanese Ophthalmological Society Guidelines for Refractive Surgery designate phakic IOL surgery for myopia of 6 D or greater while providing for cautious application in moderate myopia between 3 D and 6 D.3 As the candidate pool extends toward moderate myopia, it will include a greater proportion of shallower anterior chambers—that is, the protective deepening attenuates precisely in the range into which indications are expanding.

Current eligibility criteria require a minimum anterior chamber depth (ACD) of approximately 2.8–3.0 mm. This requirement reflects an appropriate concern that insufficient anterior segment volume may predispose to vault complications, anterior chamber angle narrowing, and corneal endothelial cell loss. However, this criterion is fundamentally a single time-point assessment. It does not address what is arguably a more important question for the patient: what is the patient’s ACD trajectory over the subsequent decades during which the ICL will remain in place?

ICL surgery is most commonly performed on patients in their twenties and thirties. Cataract surgery, the natural endpoint at which an ICL is removed, typically occurs in the sixties and seventies. The interval between ICL implantation and cataract surgery may therefore exceed 30–40 years. Over this span, two well-documented physiological and surgical phenomena reduce the effective ACD: (1) age-related thickening of the crystalline lens with corresponding ACD shallowing, and (2) vault-induced anterior displacement of the ICL with respect to the lens surface, which functionally reduces the space between the ICL and the corneal endothelium.

In this review, we synthesize the available evidence on these dynamic factors and their cumulative effect on ACD over time. We anchor this synthesis to the empirically derived 2.4 mm ACD threshold below which primary angle closure becomes substantially more prevalent in East Asian populations, and we incorporate Japanese population-based epidemiologic data demonstrating that primary angle-closure disease is a clinically important concern in this population. From this synthesis, we suggest age-stratified minimum ACD values that more rigorously reflect the long-term safety profile of ICL surgery, and we outline a clinical pathway for redirecting patients who do not meet these values to alternative refractive procedures. To our knowledge, although the constituent observations have each been individually documented, this integrated framework has not previously been formalized. We present it as a proposal for discussion and empirical validation rather than as a definitive clinical recommendation.

ICL Among Implantable Devices: A Conceptual Comparison

To clarify the distinctive features of ICL surgery, we briefly compare it to other implantable interventions. In total hip arthroplasty, the femoral head is resected and the acetabulum is reamed, creating a precisely shaped cavity to accommodate the prosthesis. In coronary stenting, balloon angioplasty mechanically dilates the stenotic lumen prior to stent deployment, exploiting the vessel’s compliance to accommodate the device. In cataract surgery, the lens nucleus and cortex are removed by phacoemulsification, leaving a capsular bag into which the intraocular lens is inserted. In each setting, the implant resides in a space that was created, expanded, or vacated as part of the procedure, and the implant therefore does not displace functional adjacent tissue.

ICL implantation operates differently. The collamer lens is placed in the ciliary sulcus or onto the zonules behind the iris and in front of the crystalline lens. The crystalline lens remains in situ; the iris remains in its physiological position; the corneal endothelium is unchanged. No tissue is removed, no compartment is enlarged, and no anatomical space is vacated. The ICL therefore occupies space that was previously shared by the existing intraocular structures, with the consequence that those structures must accommodate the new presence.

This conceptual distinction has practical implications. Long-term complications of ICL implantation—corneal endothelial cell loss, anterior subcapsular cataract formation, and elevated intraocular pressure—each reflect the close proximity of the implant to non-regenerating or slowly regenerating ocular tissues. Once an ICL is implanted, these structures must coexist with the device for the entire duration of its retention, which in young patients may exceed three or four decades.

The 2.4 mm Threshold and East Asian Populations

Primary angle-closure disease (PACD) is a leading cause of irreversible blindness, with substantially higher prevalence in East Asian populations than in populations of European descent. Anatomic crowding of the anterior segment, with shallow ACD as a principal feature, is the central biometric risk factor.

In a landmark population-based analysis, Aung and colleagues (2005) examined 2032 East Asian subjects (942 Mongolians and 1090 Chinese Singaporeans) drawn from two glaucoma surveys with the explicit aim of quantifying the relationship between ACD and the risk of peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS).4 In the Mongolian cohort the authors identified a clear threshold at an ACD of 2.4 mm: above this value, PAS were uncommon; below this value, the rate of PAS rose rapidly. In the Singaporean Chinese cohort, the relationship was more linear, with PAS prevalence increasing across the entire range of ACD values, although still concentrated at the shallower end.

This finding has been reproduced and extended. ACD measurement has been shown to be a robust screening tool for primary angle-closure glaucoma in East Asian populations,5 and shallow ACD remains a principal biometric determinant of angle closure across diverse East Asian populations.6,7 The 2.4 mm value derives from the Mongolian sample and is empirically defined; whether the same threshold applies precisely to Japanese or other East Asian populations has not been the subject of a directly comparable threshold study. In the absence of population-specific evidence to the contrary, however, 2.4 mm represents a reasonable conservative working threshold for estimating long-term angle-closure risk in East Asian patients undergoing ICL surgery.

Importantly, ICL implantation has itself been shown to narrow the anterior chamber angle and to reduce ACD measured anterior to the implant.8 Studies using anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) and swept-source OCT have demonstrated that, after ICL implantation, parameters such as the angle opening distance, trabecular-iris space area, and trabecular-iris contact area shift in the direction of greater angle closure risk.9 For a young patient whose preoperative ACD is at the lower bound of current eligibility criteria, the postoperative anatomical configuration may approach a borderline state from the outset.

Japanese Population-Based Data

Although the 2.4 mm threshold derives from Mongolian and Chinese Singaporean samples, primary angle-closure disease is a clinically important entity in Japan, supporting the relevance of this threshold for Japanese patients undergoing ICL surgery.

The Tajimi Study, a population-based survey of 3021 Japanese residents aged 40 years or older, reported a prevalence of primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) of 0.6% and an overall glaucoma prevalence of 5.0%.10 The Kumejima Study, conducted on a southwestern Japanese island, reported a higher PACG prevalence of 2.2%, approximately 3.7 times that of Tajimi.11 The Hisayama Study, in a general Japanese population, has likewise documented substantial glaucoma burden in this population.12 Asia-wide systematic reviews place the prevalence of PACG in East Asian populations at approximately 1–2%, several-fold higher than in populations of European descent.6

Biometrically, the anterior chambers of Japanese eyes resemble those of other East Asian populations. In the Kumejima Study, individuals with PAC or PACG had mean ACDs centered around 2.4–2.5 mm, consistent with the threshold derived by Aung and colleagues. While the precise inflection point of PAC risk in Japanese populations has not been determined by a directly comparable threshold study, the consistency of available biometric and epidemiologic data with the broader East Asian pattern supports the use of 2.4 mm as a clinically conservative working threshold.

The anterior segment characteristics of the myopic candidate pool, and their bearing on the applicability of this threshold to ICL patients, are addressed in the Introduction.

Age-Related Changes in Anterior Segment Biometry

The progressive shallowing of the anterior chamber with age is well established. The dominant mechanism is age-related thickening of the crystalline lens, which extends anteriorly and reduces the space between the corneal endothelium and the lens. Because ICL surgery does not arrest this process, the trajectory of native lens thickening directly determines the trajectory of effective ACD over time.

Cross-Sectional Estimate of ACD Aging

Sun and colleagues used anterior segment OCT to evaluate 388 healthy Korean subjects aged 30–89 years.13 In a linear mixed-effects model adjusted for axial length and sex, the authors derived an ACD slope of −0.0119 mm/year (P < 0.0001). The same study reported a small but significant positive slope for lens vault, an increase in iris cross-sectional area, and reductions in anterior chamber area, all consistent with progressive crowding of the anterior segment with age. The cross-sectional design of this study limits its ability to model individual trajectories, and the authors themselves called for longitudinal validation.

Longitudinal Confirmation

More recent longitudinal observations have provided direct support for the cross-sectional findings. In a Japanese cohort followed prospectively, ACD was observed to decrease at a rate consistent with the cross-sectional estimate, with a faster rate of narrowing in younger participants. The biphasic kinetics—faster shallowing in pre-presbyopic adults, slower thereafter—are consistent with the underlying mechanism of accelerated lens thickening in mid-adulthood. This pattern is highly relevant to ICL patient selection: patients undergoing ICL surgery are concentrated in the second and third decades of life, the age range during which ACD shallowing proceeds most rapidly.

Lens Thickening and ACD Shallowing

Praveen and colleagues measured lens thickness in a large Indian cohort and reported, in a multiple regression model, that lens thickness increased with age and that each increase in lens thickness was associated with a corresponding decrease in ACD.14 This relationship has been reproduced in subsequent cohorts of cataract surgery candidates. Across populations and methodologies, the rate of lens thickening in adults ranges from approximately 0.012 to 0.027 mm/year, and the corresponding ACD shallowing ranges from approximately 0.008 to 0.014 mm/year. For the purposes of a conservative model, we adopt the central estimate of −0.012 mm/year as representative of average adult ACD shallowing, while recognizing that pre-presbyopic patients may experience faster reduction.

Lens Biometry at Cataract Surgery

Empirical data on lens thickness and ACD at the time of cataract surgery establish the natural endpoint of the ACD trajectory. In a large Chinese cohort of cataract surgery candidates, the mean ACD was approximately 3.0 mm and the mean lens thickness was approximately 4.5 mm, with a strong inverse correlation between the two.15 Across populations and methodologies, the mean ACD in cataract surgery candidates clusters near 3.0 mm. This value, observed in eyes without ICL implants, defines the natural endpoint of the ACD trajectory in normal aging. An ICL eye approaching cataract surgery would, all else being equal, show a shallower ACD because of the additional vault-induced compression discussed below.

Vault and the Functional Reduction of ACD

Vault is defined as the perpendicular distance between the posterior surface of the ICL and the anterior surface of the crystalline lens. An adequate vault prevents direct mechanical contact between the implant and the lens and is essential for the prevention of anterior subcapsular cataract formation. The current consensus range for safe vault is approximately 250–750 µm, with values consistently below 250 µm associated with increased cataract risk and values consistently above 750–1000 µm associated with iridocorneal angle narrowing and elevated intraocular pressure.

The clinical relevance of vault for the present analysis is its impact on the effective ACD measured anterior to the ICL. The ICL is displaced anteriorly relative to the crystalline lens by an amount equal to the vault, with the consequence that the residual space between the ICL anterior surface and the corneal endothelium is the preoperative ACD minus a vault-related contribution. Postoperative measurements consistently demonstrate a reduction in ACD measured to the ICL surface of approximately 0.25–0.45 mm relative to preoperative values, depending on the achieved vault.

The 2.4 mm angle-closure threshold is defined from the corneal endothelium to the anterior surface of the crystalline lens, whereas the effective ACD in the model developed below is measured from the corneal endothelium to the anterior surface of the implant. These are different reference planes. Because the implant surface lies anterior to the natural lens surface, an ICL eye is more crowded at any given nominal ACD than a phakic eye, and comparison against a threshold defined at the natural lens plane is therefore conservative rather than permissive: it cannot cause the model to underestimate risk.

Vault is not static. Schmidinger and colleagues reported a continuous reduction in vault over 73 months, most pronounced in the first 6 months.16 A 10-year longitudinal analysis of V4c outcomes demonstrated exponential decay in both vault and endothelial cell density over the decade.17 Long-term observational data confirm a continuous, slow vault reduction over the lifespan of the implant; however, this is partially offset by the simultaneous anterior displacement of the ICL caused by the thickening lens, so that the net vault-related effective ACD reduction does not return to preoperative values.

For the purposes of the integrated model presented below, we adopt a representative vault-related effective ACD reduction of approximately 0.30 mm. This value reflects the central tendency of postoperative ACD reduction across reported series and incorporates the recognition that vault is dynamic but persistent.

Two qualifications apply. First, cumulative shallowing is the product of rate and duration. If highly myopic eyes reach cataract surgery earlier because lenticular change is accelerated in those eyes, a shorter duration may be accompanied by a higher rate, and the product need not fall. The literature does not permit a firm statement on this point: reports of nuclear cataract development describe increased lens thickness with anterior chamber shallowing, whereas other work has found lenticular myopic eyes to have thinner lenses, and the myopic shift of nuclear sclerosis is attributed principally to refractive index rather than to thickness.18 We therefore note only that shortening the duration while holding the rate fixed is an asymmetric assumption, and that the interval to lens extraction should not be credited to the safety margin without this qualification. Second, the availability of refractive lens exchange is not a neutral consideration: exchange of a clear but presbyopic crystalline lens for a pseudophakic optic remains debated on optical grounds, since presbyopic correction depends on multifocal or extended-depth-of-focus optics and dissatisfaction related to visual quality, dysphotopsia, and neuroadaptation is documented.19,20 This is discussed further in Clinical Implications and Triage to Alternative Procedures.

Long-Term Outcomes Data and the Latency of Risk

Existing long-term outcome data for ICL surgery are reassuring at intermediate intervals but are notably limited at the timescales most relevant to the present concern. A 10-year follow-up study of V4c-implanted eyes reported excellent visual outcomes and exponential decay patterns for both endothelial cell density and vault, suggesting continuing—if slowly decelerating—reduction over time.17 Cohort studies with 10–12 year follow-up have reported visually significant cataract in approximately one-fifth of high-myopic eyes with ICL.21 A 10-year cohort of V4 ICL recipients showed lens opacities in over half of eyes at 10 years, although many were not visually significant.22 Meta-analyses of V4c outcomes report endothelial cell loss in the range of 5% over 10 years.

The Endothelial Axis: An Independent and Already Measurable Signal

The two risks distinguished in the Introduction differ not only in mechanism but in the maturity of the evidence supporting them. The angle-closure axis is mechanistic and operates over a horizon that has not yet been observed. The endothelial axis, by contrast, is already measurable, and it is already showing an accelerated rate.

In the ten-year V4c cohort of Chung and colleagues—in which the inclusion criteria specified an anterior chamber depth below 2.8 mm—endothelial cell density decayed at 0.012 per year, that is, approximately 1.2% annually.17 The AAPPO/APMS consensus panel records the natural age-related endothelial loss as approximately 0.5–0.6% per year.1 Shallow-chamber ICL eyes are therefore losing endothelium at roughly twice the physiological rate over a decade. This is an observed finding rather than a projection, and it is independent of any consideration of the angle.

The two axes converge on the same clinical conclusion from different directions. Both originate in a shallow anterior chamber; both accumulate over the same decades; neither is captured by a criterion that is evaluated once, at the time of surgery.

The Latency of Risk

The most thorough long-term V4c data remain limited to the first decade after implantation. Cumulative ACD reduction over 30–40 years has not been directly measured in any reported series. ICL surgery is approved on the basis of trials and post-market surveillance with follow-up periods substantially shorter than the implant’s expected duration in young patients. The patients we are most concerned about—those implanted in their twenties—will only begin entering the typical age window for cataract surgery in the late 2040s and beyond. The conservative inference is that current data do not yet exclude long-term complications driven by cumulative anatomical encroachment.

Discussion

The Proposed Framework and Its Methodological Precedent

The constituent observations that motivate this proposal are not new. Each of the following is established in the peer-reviewed literature: (i) the importance of preoperative ACD as a screening criterion; (ii) the empirical 2.4 mm threshold for primary angle-closure in East Asians;4 (iii) the ACD shallowing rate of approximately 0.012 mm/year;13 (iv) the vault-related effective ACD reduction following ICL implantation;16 and (v) the recognition that long-term ACD reduction may compromise pIOL safety, leading some authorities to suggest an upper age limit of approximately 50 years for pIOL implantation.

What has not previously been published, to our knowledge, is the integration of these observations into an explicit, quantitative, age-stratified ACD framework for ICL eligibility that uses the 2.4 mm angle-closure threshold as the safety floor and is calibrated to the expected duration of implant retention.

The method employed here is not itself novel. Age-stratified derivation of an eligibility parameter, by projecting an assumed annual rate of change across the expected implant interval to a defined target at the age at which lens extraction becomes likely, is the established basis of the minimum endothelial cell density requirement for phakic IOLs. The ANSI Z80.13 standard defines minimum ECD by age band, derived so as to leave a defined endothelial reserve at 72 years of age, assuming an acute surgical loss and a continuing annual loss. The FDA-approved labelling adopts a more stringent age-dependent requirement, stratified jointly by age and across three anterior chamber depth ranges, targeting a defined reserve at 75 years.1,2 The contribution of the present review is to observe that this same logic has not been applied to the anterior chamber itself, notwithstanding that the anterior chamber is subject to a comparable, well-characterised, monotonic age-related change. We present the framework as a structured proposal intended to invite empirical validation and prospective evaluation in long-term cohorts.

Clinical Implications and Triage to Alternative Procedures

If the proposed values were adopted as risk-stratification factors, several practical consequences would follow. A proportion of younger patients currently considered ICL candidates—particularly those in their twenties with ACD between 2.8 and 3.2 mm—would be reclassified as marginal. This is consistent with the principle that the safety margin required is greater for patients with longer expected implant duration.

The FDA-approved indication for the EVO/EVO+ Visian ICL continues to specify an anterior chamber depth of 3.00 mm or greater, measured from the corneal endothelium to the anterior surface of the crystalline lens; this requirement was retained when the US age indication was expanded in 2026.2 The value proposed here for the fifth decade—3.0 mm—is identical to the approved labelling; the values for the third and fourth decades exceed it by 0.2 and 0.1 mm respectively. This additional margin in younger patients does, by design, exclude some eyes that the current label would admit, and that exclusion is intended. It is not an arbitrary tightening. The rationale is the lifelong thickening of the crystalline lens and the consequent shallowing of the anterior chamber: the longer the implant is retained, the more years of lens growth the eye must absorb, and the greater the structural margin required at the outset. The FDA labelling itself embodies the principle that a shallow anterior chamber demands a protective margin; the present proposal does not depart from that principle but applies it consistently across the interval of implant retention. On the same reasoning, the proposal raises a question about the long-term basis on which practice has moved below a labelling requirement that has not itself been revised.

The AAPPO/APMS consensus addresses this question directly and, we would suggest, compatibly.1 Consensus Statement 3.3 permits off-label implantation at an ACD between 2.6 and under 2.8 mm on the basis of several reported series, while stating in the same sentence that long-term safety is not yet established for this range and recommending that it be considered cautiously with an open angle confirmed on gonioscopy. The studies cited in support of that statement report follow-up of one to two years. Within that window, substantial structural change is documented: the angle-opening distance at 500 µm was reduced by approximately 50% nasally and 48% temporally following implantation in shallow chambers.1 A halving of the angle-opening distance without adverse events at two years describes the latency of risk rather than its absence. The consensus permits these implantations while explicitly declining to assert long-term safety; the present review concerns the horizon that the consensus leaves open.

Importantly, the clinical implication of the proposed values is not to exclude patients from refractive correction in isolation, but to position them alongside established corneal refractive surgery criteria within a single unified framework. We suggest the following decision logic: refractive surgery is considered only when the patient meets the safety criteria of at least one available procedure. SMILE and LASIK are eligible when central corneal thickness (CCT), residual stromal bed (RSB), and topographic profile are all within established safety margins, and when the manifest refraction falls within the comfortable range of contemporary corneal refractive surgery.23–25 ICL is eligible when the patient meets the proposed age-stratified ACD value (≥ 3.2 mm in the twenties, ≥ 3.1 mm in the thirties, ≥ 3.0 mm in the forties), assessed together with gonioscopy and anterior segment imaging.

This produces four possible scenarios summarized in Table 3. When only one of the two pathways is eligible, that pathway is the appropriate choice. When both pathways are eligible, the choice should be made jointly by surgeon and patient after a balanced discussion of the trade-offs—including reversibility, dependence on accommodation, recovery profile, and long-term anatomical considerations. When neither pathway is eligible, refractive surgery should not be performed; the patient should continue with optical correction (spectacles or contact lenses) and be reassessed at intervals if biometric parameters are expected to change.

This unified framework has two important consequences. First, it preserves the conventional indication boundaries for corneal refractive surgery rather than displacing them. We make no proposal to extend SMILE or LASIK beyond their established comfortable ranges; in practice this places approximately the −8.00 D mark as a soft upper limit for routine corneal refractive surgery, beyond which patients have historically been directed toward ICL.26 Second, in the high-myopia range above this boundary, where corneal refractive surgery is generally not appropriate, the proposed age-stratified ACD values become the principal consideration in whether refractive surgery is offered at all. Patients with very high myopia who meet the age-stratified values remain candidates for ICL; patients with very high myopia who do not meet them are best counseled toward continued optical correction rather than into a surgical pathway whose long-term safety margin we judge to be insufficient.

Refractive magnitude and structural reserve are two independent axes rather than a sequence, and the former does not stand proxy for the latter. Refractive magnitude and corneal parameters determine which procedures are optically and biomechanically feasible; anterior chamber depth, angle status, and endothelial cell density determine whether an intraocular implant is structurally appropriate for that individual over the intended implant duration. As approved indications extend toward moderate myopia—to −3.0 D on the FDA labelling,2 and to the 3 D to under 6 D range addressed with caution in the Japanese guidelines3—refractive magnitude becomes progressively less informative about anterior segment dimensions. A framework that routes patients principally by dioptric power will therefore not, on its own, secure structural reserve, and the two axes should be assessed in parallel. This is consistent with the approach already taken in the FDA labelling, which stratifies the endothelial requirement jointly by age and by ACD band independently of refractive magnitude.1,2

For LASIK, RSB is conventionally calculated as CCT minus flap thickness (typically 100–120 µm with current femtosecond platforms) minus ablation depth. The historical minimum RSB recommendation of 250 µm has been progressively superseded by a contemporary preference for at least 300 µm, supported by ectasia case-control data and by the percent tissue altered (PTA) framework, which identifies PTA greater than 40% as an independent ectasia risk factor.27 For SMILE, in which no flap is created, RSB is calculated as CCT minus cap thickness (commonly 120 µm under current US labeling) minus lenticule thickness; a minimum RSB of approximately 250–280 µm is widely accepted, with some surgeons targeting higher values for added safety.

Three illustrative scenarios may help to clarify the practical use of the framework. First, a 22-year-old patient with a manifest spherical equivalent of −7.50 D, ACD 2.95 mm, and CCT 540 µm. This patient is eligible for SMILE with adequate RSB, but does not meet the proposed twenties ACD value (≥ 3.2 mm). The framework directs the patient to corneal refractive surgery. Second, a 25-year-old patient with a manifest spherical equivalent of −5.00 D, ACD 3.30 mm, and CCT 545 µm. This patient is eligible for both SMILE and ICL. The choice should be made jointly by surgeon and patient after a discussion of the trade-offs—reversibility of ICL vs the absence of an intraocular implant with corneal surgery, dry eye risk, recovery profile, and the long-term considerations laid out in this review. Third, a 26-year-old patient with a manifest spherical equivalent of −12.00 D, ACD 3.05 mm, and CCT 510 µm. This patient is outside the comfortable range for corneal refractive surgery and does not meet the proposed twenties ACD value. Under the framework, refractive surgery is not advised; continued optical correction with periodic biometric reassessment is the appropriate recommendation.

The increasing use of refractive lens exchange for presbyopia bears on the assumed implant duration. Exchange of a clear crystalline lens for a pseudophakic optic is not itself an uncontested proposition. Accommodation is lost definitively, presbyopic correction depends on multifocal or extended-depth-of-focus optics, and dissatisfaction related to visual quality, dysphotopsia, and neuroadaptation is documented.19,20 In a nationwide survey conducted by the Survey Working Group of the Japanese Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgery, reduced contrast sensitivity was the leading objective reason for multifocal IOL explantation among 50 eyes, and patient satisfaction improved significantly following exchange to a monofocal lens.28 This is not confined to earlier lens designs: a 2025 series using contemporary extended-depth-of-focus lenses reported dissatisfaction in 22.8% of eyes, with 5.9% remaining dissatisfied despite intervention,29 and a 2025 series spanning bifocal, trifocal, and EDOF designs reported an overall self-reported dissatisfaction rate of 18.5%.30 Successive generations of optic have improved this trade-off without abolishing it. The point here is narrow, and other considerations relating to lens exchange lie outside the scope of this review: the availability of refractive lens exchange should not simply be credited to the safety margin of an ICL decision made decades earlier.

Surveillance and Early Intervention

For patients implanted at the lower margin of the proposed values, structured postoperative surveillance is essential. We suggest annual AS-OCT measurement of ACD and angle parameters, particularly the angle opening distance, together with serial intraocular pressure measurement and vault assessment. A documented ACD below 2.6 mm, a substantially narrowed angle opening distance, or symptomatic intraocular pressure elevation should prompt discussion of ICL explantation. In patients still retaining accommodation, ICL removal alone may be appropriate. In patients beyond the typical onset of presbyopia, ICL removal at the time of cataract surgery (or refractive lens exchange where indicated) is the more usual pathway.

The reversibility of ICL implantation is a genuine and material constraint on the trajectory described in this review, and we return to it in the Conclusion. Surveillance is protective only if it is specified: it requires knowing which patients to monitor, from what age, against which parameters, and at what threshold to act. The AAPPO/APMS consensus recommends ICL removal for excessive vault causing narrowing of the angle and decreasing aqueous flow, but does not define which patients are at sufficient risk to warrant surveillance for that outcome.1 A duration-calibrated framework of the kind proposed here is intended to serve that specifying function as much as any exclusionary one.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant explicit acknowledgment, and they are the reason the model is presented as a conservative estimate rather than a predictor.

First, the 2.4 mm threshold derives from a Mongolian sample; the Singaporean Chinese sample in the same study showed a continuous gradient rather than a sharp threshold. Whether the Mongolian or Singaporean pattern more accurately reflects Japanese populations has not been directly determined, and population-specific validation is warranted.

Second, the ACD shallowing rate is averaged from cross-sectional and short-term longitudinal studies; extrapolation across 30–40 years assumes broad stability of this rate, which the available evidence supports but does not definitively confirm.

Third, vault dynamics are heterogeneous across patients and may evolve over time; our adoption of a single representative value (0.30 mm) for vault-related effective ACD reduction is necessarily a simplification.

Fourth, the model does not incorporate age-related change in the ciliary body, and we are unable to determine the direction of this effect from current evidence. It has been suggested that such change might be compensatory, displacing the implant posteriorly and causing the model to overestimate ACD reduction. The available measurements do not clearly support that direction. Strenk and colleagues demonstrated by magnetic resonance imaging in subjects aged 22 to 83 years that the diameter of the unaccommodated ciliary muscle ring decreases significantly with advancing age, while the lens equatorial diameter does not correlate with age.31 In a subsequent series spanning 22 to 91 years, the same group confirmed a marked age-related decrease in ciliary muscle diameter in phakic eyes, unaltered by intraocular lens implantation, with a consequent reduction in circumlental space.32 If the structures forming the ciliary sulcus narrow centripetally with age, a plate-haptic implant fixated within that sulcus would be expected to be compressed and displaced anteriorly rather than posteriorly, in which case the mechanism would aggravate rather than compensate. We do not assert this: Strenk and colleagues measured the ciliary muscle ring rather than the sulcus itself, and no longitudinal measurement of implant position in ICL eyes over decades exists. The uncertainty here is bidirectional, and it is among the reasons the model should be read as a conservative estimate.

Fifth, the model assumes cataract surgery at age 60. The sensitivity analysis in Sensitivity to the Assumed Age at Lens Extraction examines this assumption across the range of 45 to 60 years; patients who remain phakic into their seventies would experience longer cumulative reduction than the base case.

Sixth, the framework takes each patient’s measured ACD as its input and therefore inherits the measurement variability of the device used. Anatomic values obtained by different instruments are not interchangeable, and the proposed values should be applied with a single, consistent measurement modality.

Finally, the proposed framework has not yet been evaluated against patient-level outcome data; validation in a large, prospectively followed cohort would be the appropriate next step.

Future Research

Several lines of inquiry would substantially strengthen the evidence base for age-stratified ACD values. First, longitudinal AS-OCT biometric data covering 20–40 years of follow-up after ICL implantation are needed; the absence of such data is the central uncertainty in the present argument. Second, population-specific determination of the angle-closure threshold in Japanese and other regional cohorts would refine the safety floor used in the model. Third, individualized prediction of lens thickening rate may enable patient-specific risk modeling that improves on the population-average model presented here. Fourth, direct longitudinal measurement of implant position, and of the ciliary sulcus dimensions in which the implant sits, would resolve the bidirectional uncertainty discussed in Limitations. Finally, prospective registries of ICL recipients with structured long-term follow-up would, over time, generate the data needed to evaluate the proposed framework empirically.

Conclusion

Current ICL eligibility criteria specify a minimum preoperative ACD of approximately 2.8–3.0 mm. This value is a reasonable point estimate of the safety floor at the time of surgery but does not capture the longitudinal trajectory of ACD over the decades during which the implant remains in place. Two well-documented dynamic factors—age-related crystalline lens thickening and vault-related anterior displacement of the implant—jointly reduce the effective ACD by approximately 0.50–0.78 mm over a 20–40 year retention period.

Anchoring this trajectory to the empirically derived 2.4 mm threshold for primary angle-closure disease in East Asian populations yields age-stratified minimum ACD values of 3.2 mm for patients in their twenties, 3.1 mm for those in their thirties, and 3.0 mm for those in their forties. A patient implanted at age 20 with an initial ACD of 2.8 mm—within the current eligibility range—would, by this model, approach an effective ACD of approximately 2.0 mm by age 60, well below the 2.4 mm threshold. Sensitivity analysis across assumed ages at lens extraction from 45 to 60 years yields values between 3.0 and 3.2 mm.

These values are proposed as risk-stratification factors, not as standalone exclusion criteria. They are intended for use alongside gonioscopy and AS-OCT or UBM assessment of the angle, and a documented open angle on gonioscopy is a necessary part of the assessment that no ACD value can replace. A value falling below the proposed threshold should prompt structured counselling, documented angle assessment, and consideration of alternatives, rather than automatic exclusion.

Two further points limit the trajectory described here. The ICL can be monitored and, if necessary, exchanged or explanted, and this materially limits the worst case the model describes. This qualification is real and we do not diminish it. We would add only that explantation is itself intraocular surgery, and that where progressive crowding is the reason for it, the resulting procedure in a shallow chamber is not equivalent to a routine one; and that monitoring is protective only where it is specified in advance—which is a function the present framework is intended to serve.

The proposal is not that ICL surgery should be abandoned. ICL implantation remains an excellent intervention for appropriately selected patients. Rather, the proposal is that current eligibility criteria should be re-examined in light of the cumulative consequences of physiological and surgical factors that act over decades, and that the resulting age-stratified ACD values be used in parallel with the established safety criteria for corneal refractive surgery within a single unified framework. When both pathways are eligible, the choice rests on shared decision-making. When only one is eligible, that pathway is the appropriate choice. When neither is eligible, refractive surgery should not be performed, regardless of patient preference. We submit this framework for discussion and ideally for validation through long-term cohort studies.
