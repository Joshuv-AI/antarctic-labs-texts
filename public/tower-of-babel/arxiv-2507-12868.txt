The Preheating Stage on The Starobinsky Inflation after ACT
Apriadi Salim Adam

Abstract
In this paper, we reinvestigate the Starobinsky inflation and its reheating features after the ACT results. To make the Starobinsky model satisfy ACT with 68 % 68\% CL, the number of e-folds increases to > 70 >70 while the reheating temperature drops to 10 4 10^{4} GeV. The preheating stages and the reheating temperature need to be adjusted drastically in favor of the lower temperature. We obtain that the preheating stage of Starobinsky inflation should be mixed between tachyonic and non-tachyonic preheating to favor the analytical estimation, PBH abundance, and the low reheating temperature at once. For that purpose, a varied momentum is necessary to obtain the favored results.

Case 1

As a starting point, the left panel of Fig.
1
shows that the energy transfer from the inflaton field
\phi
to the particle production of
\chi

3.5

m_{\chi}^{2}+3\xi M^{2}\gg 3k_{\phi}k_{\chi}.

(3.8)

In the conventional theory of inflation, the inflaton should have vanished in the early universe as it decayed into smaller particles. This ensures that there is no inflaton today. However, Starobinsky inflation is rather special. As this inflation is gravity-driven, after the reheating process, it allows the scalaron (or gravity) to remain if all decay channels do not exist. Consequently,
m_{\chi}
does not need to be smaller than
M
. As we will shortly see, however, these conditions are disfavored.

Suppose the condition
2m_{\chi}>M
holds, the perturbative decay of the scalaron to
\chi
is kinematically blocked. In this case, the production of
\chi
can only occur via parametric resonance during preheating. However, since this form of preheating is extremely inefficient (see the left panel of Fig.
1
), it implies that, by the end of the preheating stage, the scalaron would predominantly remain without any viable decay channels. As a consequence, the universe would lack a sufficient number of particles, contradicting current observational data. Therefore, the condition
2m_{\chi}>M
is not a realistic scenario.

On the other hand, if
2m_{\chi}<M
, the perturbative decay is kinematically allowed. Thus, the reheating temperature can be evaluated from this decay, although it can also be determined from the decay of the daughter fields of
\chi
, which is equally possible. We will discuss this case in the section
8
.

For

\chi

30

(3.9)

where

\begin{split}&\bar{m}\simeq\sqrt{\sqrt{6}|k_{\phi}k_{\chi}|\frac{\tilde{\phi}}{M_{P}}}\end{split}.

(3.10)

Analytically, by calculating Eq. (

\delta\rho_{\text{(Case 1)}}\simeq 0.26\times\xi^{3/2}M^{4}\sim 10^{-21}\xi^{3/2}M_{p}^{4}.

(3.11)

In this calculation, to achieve the same energy as inflaton (

On the fermionic preheating

In this part, we will discuss the possibility that, instead of the scalar
\chi
, the scalaron is coupled with fermions. So far, we have only discussed the bosonic preheating, even though fermionic preheating is equally possible. This type of preheating has already been discussed in, e.g., Refs.
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
. In this section, we will qualitatively discuss the possibility of this type of preheating.

If the scalaron is only coupled with certain fermions, at a large coupling
[
33
]
, the preheating is similar to the bosonic case. However, it is heavily suppressed by the Pauli exclusion principle, in contrast to the Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) that can occur during bosonic preheating. In this case, the inflaton energy transfer during resonance is mostly converted into the expansion of the universe. Fermion production becomes less suppressed once the universe expands. However, even after the end of preheating, the potential of the scalaron remains quadratic (
\propto\phi^{2}
), which still favors a matter-dominated regime. Fermions are more likely to be produced at the end of the preheating phase due to the looseness of the Pauli blocking.

Even if tachyonic preheating occurs, fermionic particle production remains heavily suppressed, unlike in the bosonic case. This means fermionic preheating is less efficient, even compared to Case 1 of bosonic preheating. Consequently, the duration of preheating is expected to exceed our analytical prediction. Finally, we safely said that preheating via this mode is strongly disfavored.

8
Reheating scenario

In this section, we will discuss the possible reheating scenario in this model. It is noted that the reheating stage is the condition where the cosmological model can be closely linked to SM particle physics. However, it turns out that calculating the reheating temperature is not straightforward. We will see in the following subsection that several mechanisms can appear, and we will discuss which mechanism is the most favored.

8.1
The Perturbative decay of the scalaron to scalar
\chi

The most traditional depiction of the reheating temperature is based on the simple perturbative decay of the inflaton into light and relativistic particles. It is assumed that the energy of these light particles is converted into radiation. If we consider the scalaron decaying to
\chi
via the Lagrangian

\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\phi}{M_{p}}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\chi\partial_{\nu}\chi,

(8.1)

the decay rate, within approximation
m_{\chi}\ll M
, can be written as

\Gamma(\phi\rightarrow\chi\chi)\simeq\frac{3}{128\pi}\frac{M^{3}}{M_{p}^{2}},

(8.2)

and the reheating temperature can be easily evaluated as

T_{\text{reh}}=\left(\frac{90}{g^{*}\pi^{2}}\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{M_{p}\Gamma},

(8.3)

where
g^{*}\sim 100
is the SM degrees of freedom. As
M
is well-constrained by ACT, it is clear that the reheating temperature can be obtained as

T_{\text{reh}}\approx 3.14\times 10^{9}\text{GeV}.

(8.4)

However, this value is 5 orders of magnitude larger than our prediction after applying the ACT constraint. In this case, the reheating temperature from this perturbative mode is strongly discouraged.
One should note that this value could be enhanced by BEC effects, which may increase the reheating temperature.

8.2
The Perturbative decay of the scalaron to fermion
\psi

As previously remarked, the perturbative decay of the scalaron to
\chi
is heavily suppressed by ACT results. In that case, the interaction between the scalaron and fermions is described by the Dirac Lagrangian in the Einstein frame, which reads

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dirac}}=e^{-3\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\frac{\phi}{M_{p}}}\bar{\psi}(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m_{\psi})\psi,

(8.5)

where
\bar{\psi}
and
\psi
are antifermion–fermion pairs,
\gamma^{\mu}
is the Dirac gamma matrix, and
m_{\psi}
is the mass of the fermions. Due to the kinetic term of the Dirac Lagrangian being on-shell, the interaction term of the scalaron with
\bar{\psi}\psi
is controlled by

3\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\frac{m_{\psi}}{M_{p}}\phi\bar{\psi}\psi.

(8.6)

Straightforwardly, we obtain the decay rate as

\Gamma(\phi\rightarrow\bar{\psi}\psi)\simeq\frac{3}{4\pi}\frac{m_{\psi}^{2}M}{M_{p}^{2}},

(8.7)

and the reheating temperature as

T_{\text{reh}}\simeq 8.07\times 10^{-4}m_{\psi}.

(8.8)

By using the ACT reheating temperature constraint of
10^{4}
GeV,
m_{\psi}\sim 10^{8}
GeV could satisfy the prediction.

8.3
Remarks on the perturbative mode

In our discussion about the perturbative reheating by scalar
\chi
and fermion
\psi
, it is shown that the scalar mode is disfavored by the ACT. Furthermore, as it is enhanced by BEC, the reheating temperature from this mode could be larger than
\sim 10^{9}
GeV. On the other hand, the reheating mode via fermions is more favorable, as it can decay to SM particles due to their chiral nature
[
42
]
.

In Ref.
[
43
]
, it is shown that both reheating mechanisms are rather oversimplified, as they neglect the violation of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem (see Refs.
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
). Additionally, these mechanisms overlook the effects of preheating and can only be significant if preheating is inefficient. This regime slowly transitions to a radiation-dominated regime, and the perturbative decay channel opens only gradually. It is implied that perturbative reheating via bosons is strongly disfavored due to the high reheating temperature, which contradicts the low reheating temperature prediction. Also, during the matter-dominated regime, all dominant particle production is mostly heavy non-relativistic
[
40
]
. On the other hand, fermionic reheating suffers not only from the same problem but also from the Pauli exclusion principle, which further suppresses their production. Even though this mode is more favorable than the bosonic one, its reheating scenario is still not convincing.

In addition, Ref.
[
30
]
, which discusses inflation with a non-minimal coupling model, shows that perturbative reheating is strongly disfavored. This is because achieving a favorable reheating temperature requires a violation of unitarity (i.e.,
\xi\gg 1
).

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we reinvestigate Starobinsky inflation, particularly the preheating stage, following the release of the ACT results. These results suggest a significant increase in the e-fold number (
N\simeq 75
) and constrain the reheating temperature to
T_{\text{reh}}\sim 10^{4}
GeV if we adopt the
68\%
confidence level. Consequently, the preheating and reheating mechanisms must be modified to align with these new results.
Such a low reheating temperature affects the entire preheating stage, not just the reheating mechanism. In addition, the direct perturbative decay from the scalaron to scalar
\chi
is strongly disfavored if the preheating mechanism is dominant and the high reheating temperature is implied. On the other hand, reheating via the decay of the scalaron to fermions
\psi
is also disfavored if the preheating stage is dominant, although less severely, due to the possibility of achieving a lower reheating temperature and its chirality nature.

Ultimately, the reheating mechanism is realized through a specific preheating process. During resonance, the oscillating scalaron produces the scalar
\chi
. While fermion production is also possible, it is heavily suppressed due to the Pauli exclusion principle. During the preheating stage, perturbative decay of the scalaron and
\chi
occurs but proceeds very slowly and does not contribute adequately to the reheating temperature. At the end of the preheating stage, the final burst of particle production from the scalaron to
\chi
, followed by the immediate decay of
\chi
into fermions
\psi
, becomes the sole contributor to the reheating temperature. The reheating mechanism via this mode is found to be consistent with our chosen parameters.

In the preheating stage, we consider two mechanisms, which we refer to as Case 1 and Case 2, distinguished by the presence of tachyonic conditions. Case 1 describes the preheating stage when the tachyonic condition is not applied. In this mode, preheating is shown to be inefficient. Consequently, the preheating stage lasts much longer than the analytical prediction (
\gg 10^{5}\pi/M
). On the contrary, Case 2 exhibits purely tachyonic conditions. In this mode, the preheating stage ceases rapidly due to efficient particle production (
\ll 10^{5}\pi/M
). Neither the numerical prediction from Case 1 nor that from Case 2 alone satisfies the analytical calculation. For this reason, the realistic preheating mechanism may involve a combination of both Case 1 and Case 2. To accommodate this idea, at least one momentum,
k_{\phi}
or
k_{\chi}
, should not be fixed but instead follow a time-dependent function that disrupts the tachyonic conditions. This is referred to as the
ceased-out mechanism

[
30
]
.

The disruption of the tachyonic conditions can occur through two mechanisms, based on the following question: At which stage does tachyonic preheating occur—during the early or late phase of preheating?
This question arises particularly when we consider PBH production during preheating. It has been shown that the appropriate PBH production occurs during the early stage of preheating. Based on this idea, it is clear that a preheating stage where tachyonic condition intervenes on the late stage is strongly disfavored.

We have also considered the favored parameters in our model based on our discussion in the previous sections. In our model,
y
should be small to avoid instant reheating, which would lead to a high reheating temperature. The non-minimal coupling
\xi

\xi=1

\chi

\frac{1}{2}M

m_{\chi}
should be higher than the electroweak scale (

), as
\chi
