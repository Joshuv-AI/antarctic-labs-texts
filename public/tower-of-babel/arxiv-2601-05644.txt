The Maximum Clique Problem under Adversarial Uncertainty: a min-max approach
Immanuel Bomze, Chiara Faccio, Francesco Rinaldi, Giovanni Spisso

Abstract
We analyze the problem of identifying large cliques in graphs that are affected by adversarial uncertainty. More specifically, we consider a new formulation, namely the adversarial maximum clique problem, which extends the classical maximum-clique problem to graphs with edges strategically perturbed by an adversary. The proposed mathematical model is thus formulated as a two-player zero-sum game between a clique seeker and an opposing agent. Inspired by regularized continuous reformulations of the maximum-clique problem, we derive a penalized continuous formulation leading to a nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization problem. We further introduce the notion of stable global solutions, namely points remaining optimal under small perturbations of the penalty parameters, and prove an equivalence between stable global solutions of the continuous reformulation and largest cliques that are common to all the adversarially perturbed graphs. In order to solve the given nonsmooth problem, we develop a first-order and projection-free algorithm based on generalized subdifferential calculus in the sense of Clarke and Goldstein, and establish global sublinear convergence rates for it. Finally, we report numerical experiments on benchmark instances showing that the proposed method efficiently detects large common cliques.

Introduction

Denote by
[a\!:\!b]
the set of all integers in the interval
[a,b]
. Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
be an undirected, loopless graph on vertices
V=[1\!:\!n]
with edge set
E\subset\binom{V}{2}
, and
{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}
be the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{G}
, defined as

[{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}]_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\{i,j\}\in E_{\mathcal{G}}\,,\\
0&\text{ else. }\end{cases}

(1)

Given any nonempty subset
S
of
V
, we denote with
\mathbf{x}(S)
its associated characteristic vector, defined as
\mathbf{x}(S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum\limits_{i\in S}\mathbf{e}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
i
-th column of the
n\times n
identity matrix
{\mathsf{I}}_{n}
.
A clique in
\mathcal{G}
is a subset of nodes
C\subseteq V
that are all mutually connected. It is maximal if there does not exist any larger clique
D
such that
C
is strictly contained in
D
. The classical maximum clique problem consists in finding a maximal clique in
\mathcal{G}
such that its cardinality (number of vertices) is maximum. Given the graph
\mathcal{G}
, the maximum clique cardinality is indicated by
\omega(\mathcal{G})
.

The maximum clique problem has practical applications in a wide range of fields including social network analysis, scheduling, financial networks and telecommunications. Therefore, despite being an NP-hard problem
12
, several solution methods have been explored (see, e.g.,
2
;
24
) due to its practical significance.

In
16
, Motzkin and Straus provided a connection between the maximum clique problem and the solutions of the nonconvex quadratic (so, continuous) optimization problem

z^{*}:=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{V}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathbf{x}

(2)

where
\Delta^{V}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1\}
and
\mathbf{e}
is the all-ones vector. In particular, they proved that given
C\subseteq V
a maximum clique in
\mathcal{G}
, the characteristic vector
\mathbf{x}(C)
is a global maximizer of (
2
), with the clique number
\omega(\mathcal{G})
encoded in its optimal value:
z^{*}=1-\frac{1}{\omega(\mathcal{G})}
. A drawback linked to the Motzkin-Straus formulation is the existence of spurious solutions
18
;
17
, i.e., local (global) solutions of (
2
) that are not characteristic vectors and have no connection to a clique in the graph.

In order to deal with this issue, a regularized approach was hence proposed by Bomze in
6
:

\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{V}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}[{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathsf{I}}_{n}+{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}]\mathbf{x}

(3)

where
{\mathsf{I}}_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix. This encoding guarantees that any local (global) solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}
to problem (
3
) corresponds to a maximal (maximum) clique
C=\text{supp}(\mathbf{x}^{*})=\{i\in V:x_{i}>0\}
and vice versa. This one-to-one correspondence is ensured by the regularization term
{\|\mathbf{x}\|}_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{I}}_{n}\mathbf{x}
.

The role of the regularization term was further generalized in
11
, where the authors analyzed different variants of the regularized continuous formulation (
3
), and established conditions that guarantee the equivalence between the regularized formulation and the maximum clique problem, both in terms of local and global solutions.
In
20
, the Motzkin-Straus formulation was extended to two clique relaxation models,
s
-defective clique and
s
-plex, in terms of maximizing a continuous cubic function over a polyhedral set described by two variables
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
. In
5
, the authors provided a regularized version of the cubic continuous formulation for the maximum
s
-defective clique problem and applied some tailored variants of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm to their formulation.

In scenarios where the exact structure of
\mathcal{G}
cannot be precisely determined, the problem data, that is
{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}
, can be considered given subject to some uncertainty. To model this, one viable approach is to account for continuous perturbations in the entries of
{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}
within a predefined uncertainty set (e.g., a ball, an ellipsoid, a spectrahedron, or a box).
This model makes sense if the link strengths on edges vary, as explored in the literature, e.g.,
by
3
, where the authors focus on relevant applications in social network analysis.

In this paper, we consider an unweighted graph
\mathcal{G}
and analyze the scenario where the perturbation is discrete, meaning that some edges may be added to or removed from
\mathcal{G}
, i.e., some entries of
{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}
may switch from zero to one. In many modern applications, such uncertainty is adversarial in nature, as different agents have conflicting goals regarding the detection or obscuration of the underlying graph structure. This dynamics can be hence naturally modeled as a two-player game between a clique seeker and a network adversary. Formally, we have a family of possible graph structures
\mathcal{G}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{m}
, each representing a possible configuration of edges that may occur due to adversarial intervention.
The seeker’s goal is thus to maximize the connectivity of the selected nodes, while the adversary chooses a graph configuration
\mathcal{G}_{i}
that minimizes this objective by removing/masking critical edges or by including fake ones. We thus define the
backbone network
, as the set of edges that are stable, i.e., included in all the graph configurations and the
adversarial maximum common clique problem
as the seeker’s task of finding the largest subset of vertices forming a clique in the backbone network (i.e., a clique with edges in every configuration), without explicitly knowing all the graph structures. We hence have an adversarial interaction between the two players, and, whenever the seeker selects a subset of vertices, the adversary responds by revealing the worst possible graph configuration
\mathcal{G}_{i}
among the available ones, that is, the one in which the induced subgraph on the selected subset of vertices is least connected. This problem formulation may appear in several real-world settings:

•

Communication Networks
7
;
13
:
The backbone network consists of key routers that have stable connections, while additional links are available depending on congestion or failures. Users hence attempt to identify the most connected subset of nodes, while the network owner dynamically modifies link availability to obscure this information.

•

Cybersecurity and Privacy
19
:
A hidden clique represents a group of critical servers or key actors in a communication network. Attackers attempt to reveal the structure by probing connections, while defenders limit information leakage through controlled link availability.

•

Social Network Analysis
22
;
25
;
26
:

Influential subgroups in a social network form hidden cliques, and malicious users try to identify these communities to feed them with fake news and rumors to manipulate public opinion or generate revenue on their sites (e.g., clickbait). The platform provider may hence intentionally obfuscate certain relationships to prevent manipulation or unwanted discoveries.

As we will see in the next subsection, a natural way to extend the Motzkin – Straus formulation to this case would lead to an adversarial max-min optimization problem. However, unlike the classical maximum-clique case, the solutions of this max-min problem do not, in general, correspond to characteristic vectors of common cliques and even non-feasible patterns may appear. Thus, in order to guarantee the discrete-continuous equivalence, we need to introduce a suitable continuous reformulation that generalizes the above described regularization to the adversarial case.

1.1
Preliminary formulation

We denote the finite universe of all regularized adjacency matrices of graphs of order
n
by

\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}:=\{{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathsf{I}}_{n}+{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{G}\mbox{ is a graph of order }n\}\,;

In other words,
\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}
is composed of all
{\mathsf{U}}={\mathsf{U}}^{\top}
such that
{\mathsf{U}}-\frac{1}{2}{\mathsf{I}}_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
is a symmetric binary matrix with zero diagonal. Reverting the dependence on
\mathcal{G}
, we may write
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{{\mathsf{U}}}=(V,E_{\mathsf{U}})
if and only if
{\mathsf{U}}={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathsf{I}}_{n}+{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

For the theoretical analysis to follow, we may hence consider a general (finite) uncertainty set
{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}
. We consider the following robust optimization problem:

\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{V}}\min_{{\mathsf{U}}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}\,,

(4)

a continuous, generally nonsmooth, nonconvex, piecewise quadratic optimization problem over the simplex polytope. Finiteness of
{\mathcal{U}}
and compactness of
\Delta^{V}
ensure the existence of all extrema considered. It is important to highlight that the seeker does not have direct access to the set of all configurations
\mathcal{U}=\{{\mathsf{U}}_{1},\ \dots,\ {\mathsf{U}}_{m}\}
. If this were the case, the seeker might easily calculate the backbone network adjacency matrix
{\mathsf{A}}_{\mathcal{G}_{bb}}
(considering the Hadamard product of all the available adjacency matrices) and then solve the classic max-clique problem given in (
3
).

Since
{\mathsf{U}}
will be indefinite for all
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, Sion’s theorem cannot be applied, and there is only a general min-max inequality. Indeed, the min-max problem

\min_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{V}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}

corresponds to selecting the lowest
\omega(\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{U}})
across all graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\mathsf{U}}
generated by
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, which is obviously a different problem. The solution to this problem may be complicated by the fact that there is more than one minimal
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, but if solved, it amounts to selecting those edges for removal such that the resulting graph has the smallest possible clique number.

As an illustrative case, let us consider the following example. Let
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{K}_{4}
be a complete graph with four nodes and choose
{\mathcal{U}}=\{{\mathsf{U}}_{1},{\mathsf{U}}_{2},{\mathsf{U}}_{3},{\mathsf{U}}_{4}\}
as shown in Figure
1
. It is easy to prove that
\min_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{V}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}=\frac{3}{4}\,.

Figure 1:
original graph
\mathcal{G}
, and edge configurations
\mathcal{G}({\mathsf{U}}_{1})
,
\mathcal{G}({\mathsf{U}}_{2})
,
\mathcal{G}({\mathsf{U}}_{3})
,
\mathcal{G}({\mathsf{U}}_{4})
that may occur due to adversarial intervention.

In this example, the largest common cliques are the single vertices and, in this case,
\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}\mathbf{x}={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}
for all
i\in[{1}\!:\!{4}]
and for each
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_{i}
, the characteristic vector associated with a single vertex
i\in V
. However, if
\mathbf{x}^{\top}=\frac{1}{4}[1,1,1,1]
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}\mathbf{x}=\frac{5}{8}>{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}
for all
i\in[{1}\!:\!{4}]
. Hence, in general, the characteristic vector
\frac{1}{|C|}\sum\limits_{i\in C}\mathbf{e}_{i}

of the largest common clique
C
is not an optimal solution to the problem (
4
).

In the previous example, we observed that for an optimal solution
\mathbf{x}
to the problem
(
4
)
, its support
\text{supp}(\mathbf{x})
need not be a common clique for all the modifications of
\mathcal{G}
. This motivates the following strategy: adding (in fact, subtracting, as we are maximizing in
\mathbf{x}
) a term penalizing deviations from the complete graph
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. This will enable a complete characterization of (even) global solutions in terms of maximum common cliques across
{\mathcal{U}}
. Our proof strategy for obtaining this result is inspired by
11
. However, major changes are necessary due to nonsmoothness.

1.2
Contributions

The contributions of this paper are therefore twofold:

•

First, we introduce a novel penalized game-theoretical program that extends the regularized continuous maximum-clique reformulation to adversarial settings. This nonsmooth and nonconvex mathematical model introduces a penalty-based mechanism and a semicontinuous relaxation that together enforce the combinatorial structure of the maximum common clique across all available graph realizations.
Within this framework, we further define the new concept of
stable global solution
, i.e., a solution that persists when the parameters of the penalized model are slightly perturbed.
We establish a rigorous equivalence result showing that stable global solutions of the penalized continuous program correspond exactly to the maximum common cliques in the discrete model and vice versa. This result provides the first exact continuous characterization of the maximum clique problem under adversarial uncertainty, thus extending the meanwhile classical correspondence to an adversarial context.

•

Second, we develop a first-order and projection-free algorithm tailored to the nonsmooth and nonconvex structure of the proposed formulation, which is particularly well suited to the underlying adversarial setting
1
1

1

as we will see, the seeker, in the considered framework, interacts with the adversary only through an oracle that returns information related to suitably defined worst-case graph realizations for a given node selection.
. Furthermore, this kind of methods can be easily embedded into a global optimization algorithmic framework based on multistart or local search/basin hopping strategies (see, e.g.,
10
;
14
;
15
for further details on these classes of algorithms). Since the proposed formulation is nonsmooth, the general theory of differentiation for Lipschitz-continuous functions plays a crucial role in the algorithmic development. Contributions in this area were made by Clarke and Goldstein, as presented in
1
. In particular, Goldstein’s foundational work
9
on nonsmooth unconstrained optimization problems inspired some of the theoretical tools needed to analyze our approach. Thanks to those tools, we thus establish global convergence sublinear rates for the proposed method. Specifically, we demonstrate that employing a Goldstein-like subdifferential with fixed accuracy leads to an approximate Goldstein stationary point, whereas increasing accuracy guarantees convergence to a Clarke stationary point. Extensive numerical experiments further demonstrate that our projection-free algorithm, even when embedded into a quite basic multistart framework, consistently identifies large common cliques of adversarially perturbed networks.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we develop a continuous, nonsmooth formulation of the maximum common clique problem, and we provide an equivalence between the stable global minimizers of the proposed formulation and the maximum common cliques. In Section
3
, a new projection-free algorithm is introduced to compute local solutions to the nonsmooth formulation. Section
4
reports some experimental results, and in Section
5
, we delineate the conclusions of the paper.

\displaystyle y_{i}=0\iff x_{i}=0\,;

In other words, we look at the

By relaxing the integrality in the previous problem, we have

\begin{split}\max&\quad F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\

We observe that, in this case,

\displaystyle y_{i}=0\iff x_{i}=0\,;

\displaystyle y_{i}=1\implies x_{i}\geq\varepsilon\,.

\bm{\lambda}
in (

), which in turn implies via

and (

\begin{split}\sigma&=\sigma\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathbf{h}}^{x}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\lambda})+\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\mu}-\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\phi}\\
&=\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathbf{h}}^{x}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\lambda})+\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\delta}-\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\phi}\\
&=\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathbf{h}}^{x}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\lambda})+\mathbf{y}^{\top}\bm{\delta}-\varepsilon\mathbf{y}^{\top}\bm{\phi}\\

(18)

Moreover, since

are quadratic forms (homogeneous of degree 2), we have by Euler’s theorem

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum\limits_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum\limits_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}[\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathbf{h}}^{x}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\lambda})+\mathbf{y}^{\top}{\mathbf{h}}^{y}(\mathbf{y}|\bm{\lambda})]={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}(\sigma-\mathbf{e}^{\top}\bm{\theta})\,,

the last equation coming from (
18
). Finally, feasibility of
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\nu)
means
G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\nu
but on the other hand, for all

we have

by (

),
and at least one such
\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}>0
exists since

. So

, which establishes

under the KKT conditions.

2.2
The role of common cliques and their characteristic vectors

Let

be a nonempty subset of nodes, and let

defined by

\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}(S):=\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}:\text{supp}(\mathbf{x})=\text{supp}(\mathbf{y})\subseteq S\}\,.

We consider the system of all common cliques across

\begin{split}\Delta^{0}_{\varepsilon}&:=\bigcup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{U}}}\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}(C)\\

(19)

As usual, we will call an element

Proposition 1

Let

), (
16
), and (
17

\beta\geq\frac{|{\mathcal{U}}|}{2}-1

Proof

The identities in Lemma

suggest already the choice of dual variables

(i,{\mathsf{U}})\in C\times{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x}(C),\mathbf{y})]_{i}

.
Therefore

for any
\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta^{\mathcal{U}}
and for all

. Moreover, observe that

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=G(\mathbf{x}(C),\mathbf{y})=g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x}(C),\mathbf{y})={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbf{x}(C)^{\top}{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}(C)-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}

so that defining

\bm{\phi}=\bm{\delta}=\bm{\psi}:=\mathbf{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathbf{o}

i\in C

. In this way, it remains to show that

for all
i\in V\setminus C
.
Define

which counts the edges in
\mathcal{G}({\mathsf{U}})
linking
C
to an outside vertex

. Then for all
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
we have

[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x}(C),\mathbf{y})]_{i}=2\left[\beta(1-\frac{{\rm deg}_{\mathsf{U}}(i|C)}{|C|})-\frac{{\rm deg}_{\mathsf{U}}(i|C)}{|C|}\right]\,,

which for large enough

{\rm deg}_{\mathsf{U}}(i|C)=|C|
, i.e., if
C\cup\{i\}
is a clique in
\mathcal{G}({\mathsf{U}})
as well, in which case

[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x}(C),\mathbf{y})]_{i}=-2
and
[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x}(C),\mathbf{y})]_{i}-\sigma=-\frac{1}{|C|}
. But since
C
is a maximal common clique, for all

such that

is not a clique in
\mathcal{G}({\mathsf{U}})

{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{-}\quad\mbox{ with }\quad{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}:=\{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}:{\rm deg}_{\mathsf{U}}(i|C)<|C|\}\,\mbox{ and }\,{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{-}:={\mathcal{U}}\setminus{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}\,,

then

for all

if

1
.
Let us now provide
the quantitative estimate for large enough

, and a suitable choice for
\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta^{\mathcal{U}}

17

\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta^{\mathcal{U}}
(and above defined

\text{supp}(\bm{\lambda})\subseteq C

For any
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}

and thus, since

\displaystyle\beta(1-\frac{{\rm deg}_{\mathsf{U}}(i|C)}{|C|})-\frac{{\rm deg}_{\mathsf{U}}(i|C)}{|C|}-\nu-\frac{1}{2\gamma}|C|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{|C|}({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}+\beta)

and therefore

\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}}2\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}\left[\beta(1-\frac{{\rm deg}_{\mathsf{U}}(i|C)}{|C|})-\frac{{\rm deg}_{\mathsf{U}}(i|C)}{|C|}-\nu\right]\geq\frac{1}{|C|}(1+2\beta)\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}\,.

On the other hand,

\frac{1}{|C|}\,\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{-}_{i}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}=\frac{1}{|C|}-\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}\,,

so that

\frac{1}{|C|}(1+2\beta)\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}-\frac{1}{|C|}\,\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{-}_{i}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}=\frac{2}{|C|}(1+\beta)\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}-\frac{1}{|C|}\,.

Multiplying by

we arrive at the condition

As we know
{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}\neq\emptyset

\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{i}^{+}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}\geq\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{U}}|}

Therefore this condition can be satisfied if

or

. Other choices of

with smaller

may refine this estimate, replacing

with

.

). In the sequel we will show that in contrast to this general situation, here they are indeed sufficient even for local optimality
in the restricted problem
\min_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{0}_{\varepsilon}}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\begin{split}\min&\quad G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
&\text{ s.t. }(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}(C)\,.\end{split}

Lemma 2

Let

for all

g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=-\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|}_{2}^{2}-1-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}=G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,.

Proof

Since

is a clique in
\mathcal{G}({\mathsf{U}})
, the principal submatrix

. Thus we have
\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{2}-{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1-{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}

12

\displaystyle g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{\top}[\beta(\overline{{\mathsf{E}}}-{\mathsf{U}})-{\mathsf{U}}]\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=0-\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathsf{U}}\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-1-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\,.

Hence the result.

be a common clique. Then there is a unique local minimizer (hence strict and global) of (

(\mathbf{x}(C),\mathbf{y})
with
\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

(b) Moreover, any KKT-point

3
A projection-free algorithm for the continuous min-max reformulation

In this section, we present a first-order, projection-free algorithm specifically designed for our problem. In particular, we develop a new subdifferential notion based on the classical Clarke and Goldstein subdifferentials discussed in
1
and study its theoretical properties.
Afterwards, we implement a Frank-Wolfe algorithm that takes advantage of this new subdifferential, proving sublinear convergence rates for it. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt in the literature to prove convergence rates for the non-convex case. In the convex (or concave max-min) case, a Frank-Wolfe method was introduced and analyzed in
23
, but without giving any convergence rate.

3.1
Preliminaries

Before introducing the algorithm, we clarify how first-order information of the nonsmooth function

G
is exploited. Since
G
is the pointwise maximum of a finite set of locally Lipschitz continuous functions
g_{\mathsf{U}}
, it is itself Lipschitz continuous in the domain
\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}
, with Lipschitz constant
L
equal to:

L:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}},\\
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}\end{subarray}}\left\|\left(2\left(-(1+\beta){\mathsf{U}}+\beta\overline{{\mathsf{E}}}\right)\mathbf{x},\;-\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbf{y}\right)\right\|>0\,.

(25)

This property allows us to apply the generalized subdifferential framework for Lipschitz functions introduced in
1
. In the present context, the constant
L
dominates the Lipschitz constants of the gradients related to the quadratic functions
g_{\mathsf{U}}
defining
G
. As a result, even though
L
is defined as a function Lipschitz constant, it can be safely substituted for the gradient Lipschitz constant of the functions
g_{\mathsf{U}}
throughout the analysis.

To understand the subdifferential notion adopted in the algorithm, it is necessary to clarify which set of matrices is supposed to be available at each iteration.
In particular, knowing the set of active matrices would allow us to calculate the Clarke subdifferential, a standard tool in convex analysis.

Definition 2
(Clarke subdifferential)

The
Clarke subdifferential
of
G
at a point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, denoted by
\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, can be expressed as:

\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\textnormal{conv}\left\{\nabla g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\}.

This expression follows directly from the standard characterization of the Clarke subdifferential of a pointwise maximum of finitely many continuously differentiable functions (see, e.g.,
(
1
, Theorem 3.23 and Corollary 3.5)
). In fact,
\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is a (nonempty) polytope, because
{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is finite.

However, an algorithm that relies only on the Clarke subdifferential to solve problem (
11
) cannot provide meaningful theoretical convergence results due to the non-differentiability of the function

G
. Therefore, we assume that for any point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, the algorithm has access to the set
{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
approximating the active set with a tolerance
\delta
, in a sense to be defined below, rather than the exact active set
{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. This assumption reflects realistic scenarios where exact function evaluations or full knowledge of the underlying structure are unavailable. From a computational perspective, this relaxation is also justified because the exact maximizers of
G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
may not be numerically distinguishable when multiple
g_{\mathsf{U}}
yield similar values.

Definition 3
(Approximately active matrices)

Let
\delta>0
be a small parameter and
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{V}_{\epsilon}
.

Recall the (nonempty and finite) set of active matrices at a point
{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
defined in (
21
).
Now define
the set of approximately active matrices (again nonempty and finite) at the same point as

{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\left\{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}:g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\delta\right\}\,.

Approximating the active set with a tolerance
\delta
therefore provides a stable and implementable mechanism; to this end, we will now construct a new approximate set of active matrices
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
which will contain only matrices in
{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
that become active within a certain neighborhood of
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

To be precise, the process to obtain
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is the following. Given a point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, use as neighborhood the Euclidean ball
B_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
of radius
\eta\geq 0
around
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and take
\eta\leq\frac{\delta}{2L}
, then the only functions that may become active in
B_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
are those in
{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
(the result is straightforward due to the Lipschitz continuity of the functions
g_{\mathsf{U}}
and
G
). Finding exactly which
\delta
-active functions become active for at least one point
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})
within
B_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
would require solving a non-linear optimization problem, with a high computational cost. For this reason, as proposed in
9
, we decide to approximate each function
g_{\mathsf{U}}
using its first-order Taylor expansion around
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, which produces a reliable estimate as long as
\eta
is sufficiently small.
To this end, let us consider the following system of linear inequalities in
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})
, focussing on a certain fixed
\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \langle\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\rangle\leq&g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+L\eta^{2},\\
&\forall{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,,\end{aligned}\\
\|(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{n}}\,,\\
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\in\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}\,.\end{cases}

(26)

The first constraint tests whether
g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}
becomes approximately active under the linearized model, while the remaining constraints ensure that the perturbed point
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})
remains within
B_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\cap\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}
.

Definition 4
(approximate local active set)

Given parameters
\delta>0
and
\eta\leq\frac{\delta}{2L}
, and a point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}
, we define the
approximate local active set
at
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
as

{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\left\{\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\ :\ \exists\,(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\in\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}\ \text{satisfying system~\eqref{system:my_B}}\,\right\}.

As in
9
, we infer that
{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
for any
\eta\geq 0
: indeed, taking
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
in system (
26
), it is immediate to see that all matrices active in
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
satisfy the system. In particular we have
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\neq\emptyset
.

We can finally define the exact subdifferential used in the algorithm.

Definition 5
(
\eta
-subdifferential)

\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\textnormal{conv}\left\{\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\}

In fact, this set is again a (nonempty) polytope as also
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is finite.
In Proposition
5
we summarize some relevant properties regarding the set given in Definition
5
, showing that in fact it has the characteristics of a subdifferential. This result establishes the link between the approximate subdifferential and the Clarke subdifferential, and is key for interpreting both the theoretical analysis given in this section and the numerical results given in the next one.

Proposition 5

We list below a set of useful properties of the
\eta
-subdifferential:

1.

The mapping
\partial_{\eta}G
is upper semicontinuous.

2.

Let
\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
denote the Clarke subdifferential of
G
. Then:

\bigcap_{\eta>0}\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\partial_{0}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,.

Proof

1.

Let
(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\to(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and let
{\mathbf{h}}_{k}\in\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
be such that
{\mathbf{h}}_{k}\to{\mathbf{h}}
. We must show that
{\mathbf{h}}\in\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.
The relation
\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
means that there exists
(\mathbf{s}_{k},\mathbf{t}_{k})\in B_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\cap\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}
satisfying system (
26
) at
(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
. Because
\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}
is compact and
\eta
is fixed, the sequence
(\mathbf{s}_{k},\mathbf{t}_{k})
admits a convergent subsequence with limit
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\in B_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\cap\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}
. By continuity of all
g_{\mathsf{U}}
and their gradients, the inequalities in (
26
) remain valid in the limit for all
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, which implies
\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

Finally, since
\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\to\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
for all
\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, and convex combinations are preserved under limits, we obtain
{\mathbf{h}}\in\textnormal{conv}\{\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\}=\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. Therefore,
\partial_{\eta}G
is upper semicontinuous.

2.

First, it is easy to see that
\partial_{0}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.
Indeed, imposing
\eta=0
in system (
26
) forces
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
by the second inequality.
Consequently, the first inequality becomes

0\leq g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\quad\forall\ {\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

The only matrices
\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
satisfying this condition are those that are active at
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
,
which shows that
{\mathcal{U}}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})={\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and hence
\partial_{0}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

We now show that

\bigcap_{\eta>0}\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\partial_{0}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

It is sufficient to prove that there exists
\bar{\eta}>0
such that

\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\partial_{0}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
for all
\eta\leq\bar{\eta}
.
Let
\bar{\delta}>0
be such that
{\mathcal{U}}^{\bar{\delta}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})={\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.
Such a value exists since each
{\mathsf{U}}
is either active or strictly inactive,
in which case
g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})<G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, and the difference

G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is strictly positive.
Setting
\bar{\eta}=\frac{\bar{\delta}}{2L}
,
the Lipschitz continuity of the functions
g_{\mathsf{U}}
implies that,
for all
\eta\leq\bar{\eta}
, the only functions that can become active
within
B_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
are those already active at
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.
Hence,
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})={\mathcal{U}}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
for all
\eta\leq\bar{\eta}
,
and therefore
\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\partial_{0}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

3.2
Algorithmic scheme

Having introduced the theoretical tools required to handle the non-smoothness of
G
, we now turn to the design of the optimization algorithm. We propose a projection-free first-order method that extends the classical Frank–Wolfe algorithm to the nonsmooth and adversarial setting by incorporating the subdifferential
\partial_{\eta}G
.
The proposed algorithm is proved to converge to first-order stationary points of
G
, and in our experiments it empirically converges to maximal common cliques in the underlying backbone networks.

Given that our feasible domain is a polytope, a projection-free optimization strategy, such as the Frank-Wolfe method, is especially suited to this setting
8
. One of the main advantages of the Frank-Wolfe method is that it avoids costly projections: instead of computing a projection onto the feasible set at each iteration, it indeed solves a linear minimization problem over the same set. This feature makes the method particularly attractive in high-dimensional scenarios. Additionally, the Frank-Wolfe method tends to produce sparse iterates, as each point is a convex combination of a small number of extreme points of the feasible region. The main steps of the algorithm are reported in Algorithm
1
. In our context, we assume that the seeker, at each iteration, can query a
first-order adversary oracle
with a feasible point. The oracle then returns either the matrices or the function gradients that belong to the approximate local active set at that point, and these are then used to construct the corresponding
\eta
-subdifferential employed by the algorithm.

Algorithm 1

Frank-Wolfe for the adversarial min-max problem

1:

Set parameters
\varepsilon,\,k_{\max},\,\delta,\,\eta,\,\xi
and choose an initial point
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}
.

2:

for

k=0,\dots,k_{\max}

do

3:

Compute
\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
using the
first-order adversary oracle

4:

Compute
((\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}),\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{k})\in\text{LMO}_{\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}}(\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k}))

5:

Set
\mathbf{d}_{k}=(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k})-(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})

6:

if

\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{k}\geq-\xi

then

STOP

7:

end

if

8:

Update
(\mathbf{x}_{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{k+1})=(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}
, with
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1]
chosen via an Armijo line search

9:

end

for

The algorithm starts from a feasible initial point
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}
and iteratively refines it. At step 3,
the first-order adversary oracle is thus queried with the point
(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
and computes the set
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
following the framework presented in the previous subsection, that is by suitably solving system (
26
) for the
\delta
-active matrices in the point
(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
. The set
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
is then used to define
\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
. Step 4 invokes a Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO), defined by:

\min_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}}\max_{{\mathbf{h}}\in\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})}{\mathbf{h}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{k}).

(27)

The LMO is modeled after the classical Frank-Wolfe algorithm: in the standard setting, the oracle indeed minimizes a linear approximation of the objective function over the original feasible set. Here, we follow the same reasoning, but we replace the gradient with the
\eta
-subdifferential
\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
in the approximation. This generalization preserves the central intuition of Frank–Wolfe (that is, moving toward a feasible point that minimizes a linear approximation of the objective), so the structure and purpose of the LMO remain fully aligned with the classical step.

Since min-max problems are difficult to solve directly, we reformulate (
27
) as a minimization problem. In particular, recalling the definition of
\partial_{\eta}G
as a convex combination of gradients, it is possible to write
{\mathbf{h}}
by baricentric coordinates with respect to the gradients of the active functions in
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{h}}

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{h}}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}},{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}\cdot 2\left(-(1+\beta){\mathsf{U}}+\beta\overline{{\mathsf{E}}}\right)\mathbf{x}_{k},\;-\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbf{y}_{k}\right)\,,

where
r=|{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})|
, and
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{+}
satisfies
\sum_{\mathsf{U}}\lambda_{\mathsf{U}}=1
.

Therefore, the inner maximization problem reduces to a linear program over the standard simplex, whose optimal value is necessarily attained at one of the simplex’s vertices. The LMO can thus be written as the following LP:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mu}

\displaystyle\mu-\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{y}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\mu\geq\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{k})\quad\forall\,{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})

In order to quantify progress, we define the LMO gap as follows.

Definition 6
(
\eta
gap function)

Given a point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}
, the
\eta
gap in
\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}
is defined as:

c_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=-\min_{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}}\max_{{\mathbf{h}}\in\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\mathbf{h}}^{\top}(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{y}).

It holds that
c_{\eta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq 0
for all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}
.

Finally, we compute the step size using an Armijo line search; to this end, given parameters
\omega\in(0,1)
and
\sigma\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, and starting from a base step size
\omega^{0}=1
, we test
the condition

G((\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})+\alpha\mathbf{d}_{k})\leq G(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})-\sigma\alpha c_{\eta}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})

(28)

for
\alpha=\omega^{m}
by increasing the integer
m\geq 0
. We then set
\alpha_{k}=\omega^{m}
for the smallest
m
for which (
28
) is satisfied.

3.3
Theoretical convergence guarantees

Note:
In the remainder of this section, the explicit notation
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is no longer necessary for the scope of the proofs. To simplify the presentation, we will instead use a single variable
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
to denote the concatenated coordinates.

The next result provides a convergence rate for Algorithm
1
. In particular, we show that, choosing
\eta\leq\frac{\delta}{2L}
, for any small enough
\delta
, the algorithm will give an
(\eta,\xi)
-Goldstein stationary point, which we define as follows.

Definition 7
(
(\eta,\xi)
-Goldstein stationary point)

A point
\mathbf{z}^{\star}\in\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}
is called an

(\eta,\xi)
-Goldstein stationary point for the minimization of
G
over

\Delta^{V}_{\varepsilon}
if it satisfies the following condition:

c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}^{\star})\leq\xi.

In other words,
\mathbf{z}^{\star}
is
(\eta,\xi)
-Goldstein stationary whenever the
LMO certificate
c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}^{\star})
does not exceed
\xi
.
This notion matches the stopping criterion of Algorithm
1
, which terminates
as soon as
c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\leq\xi
.

More specifically, the algorithm will satisfy the stopping condition and give an
(\eta,\xi)
-Goldstein stationary point after a finite number of iterations, which can be estimated as follows.

Theorem 3.1

Let
G(\mathbf{z}^{*})=\min\limits_{\mathbf{z}\in\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}}G(\mathbf{z})
and let
\{\mathbf{z}_{k}\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Fix
\eta\leq\frac{\delta}{2L}
, where
L
is the Lipschitz constant of
G
defined in (
25
) and let
c^{*}_{k}=\min\limits_{i\in[0:k]}c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{i})
. Assume that the step size in the algorithm is determined using an Armijo line search with fixed parameter
\sigma
as in (
28
). Finally, observe that
D:=\text{diam}\left(\Delta^{V}_{\epsilon}\right)^{2}=n+1>1
, and let
\rho=\frac{D([G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{\sigma}.

Then,

c^{*}_{k}\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{(k+1)\eta}.&\text{if }k<\frac{\rho(1-\sigma)}{\eta}-1\,,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{(k+1)(1-\sigma)\eta}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular,

\lim_{k\to\infty}c^{*}_{k}=0\quad\text{at a rate of }\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k\eta}}\right),

that is, Algorithm
1
reaches an
(\eta,\xi)
-Goldstein stationary point in

O(1/(\eta\xi^{2}))
iterations.

Proof

First, recall that the quantity
c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
corresponds to:

c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})=-\nabla g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{k},

(29)

where
\mathbf{d}_{k}
is the chosen descent direction and
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
is the approximate active matrix that provides the steepest descent.

As mentioned before, our LMO selects a direction
\mathbf{d}_{k}
by solving a min-max problem. Since the inner problem maximizes a linear function over the convex hull of gradients
\nabla g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
, such a maximum is hence attained at an extreme point of this convex set. Therefore, there must exist a matrix
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
such that equality (
29
) holds, by the fundamental theorem of linear programming.

Adopting an Armijo line search, we can write for every
k
:

G(\mathbf{z}^{*})-G(\mathbf{z}_{0})\leq G(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})-G(\mathbf{z}_{0})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left[G(\mathbf{z}_{i+1})-G(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right]\leq-\sigma\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{i})\,.

(30)

At each iteration
k
, there are two possibilities:

Case 1:
There is a matrix
{\mathsf{U}}
common to both
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
and
{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})
. Then, the following descent condition holds:

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})

\displaystyle=g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})

(31)

\displaystyle\leq g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})+\alpha_{k}\nabla g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{k}+\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}L}{2}\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}

(32)

\displaystyle\leq G(\mathbf{z}_{k})-\alpha_{k}c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})+\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}L}{2}\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}\,,

(33)

where (
31
) follows from the fact that
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})
, while (
33
) derives from
g_{\mathsf{U}}\leq G
.

We can combine the descent condition with the Armijo line search, as done in
4
: let
\alpha_{k}
be the accepted Armijo step and
\tilde{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\omega}
the previous (rejected) trial. Since
\tilde{\alpha}
was rejected, it must hold:

G(\mathbf{z}_{k}+\tilde{\alpha}\mathbf{d}_{k})>G(\mathbf{z}_{k})-\sigma\tilde{\alpha}c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k}).

Combined with the descent bound at
\tilde{\alpha}
, the following relation can be obtained:

G(\mathbf{z}_{k})-\tilde{\alpha}c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})+\frac{L}{2}\tilde{\alpha}^{2}\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}\,\geq\,G(\mathbf{z}_{k}+\tilde{\alpha}\mathbf{d}_{k})\,>G(\mathbf{z}_{k})-\sigma\tilde{\alpha}c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\,,

which after simple rearrangements yields

\tilde{\alpha}>\frac{2(1-\sigma)c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})}{L\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}}\,.

Since
\tilde{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\omega}
, accounting for the upper bound at 1, we get:

\;\alpha_{k}\geq\min\!\Big\{1,\;\frac{2\omega(1-\sigma)\,c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})}{L\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}}\Big\}.

Case 2:
The active set
{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})
does not contain any matrix from
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
. We now show that in this case
\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|=\|\mathbf{z}_{k+1}-\mathbf{z}_{k}\|>\eta
, therefore obtaining
\alpha_{k}>\frac{\eta}{\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|}
.

Suppose by contradiction that
\|\mathbf{z}_{k+1}-\mathbf{z}_{k}\|\leq\eta
. The hypothesis
\eta\leq\frac{\delta}{2L}
implies that
\|\mathbf{z}_{k+1}-\mathbf{z}_{k}\|\leq\frac{\delta}{2L}
, and byLipschitz continuity of the functions
g_{\mathsf{U}}
, only the functions in
{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
can be active functions in
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}
, so
{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
. Let us choose a matrix
\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})
. We will now show that for every
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
the inequality

\langle\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})-(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\rangle\leq g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+L\eta^{2}

(34)

holds. This would automatically imply that
\bar{\mathsf{U}}
satisfies system (
26
), so

\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})\,\cap\,{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
, providing a contradiction.

Fix
\bar{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})
and
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
. By definition of activity at
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}
,

g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})\geq 0.

(35)

Apply the first-order Taylor expansion of each
g_{\mathsf{U}}
around
\mathbf{z}_{k}
with the remainder bounded by the Lipschitz continuity of the gradients: for any
{\mathsf{U}}
there exists a remainder
r_{\mathsf{U}}
with

g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})=g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})+\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla g_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k}),\mathbf{d}_{k}\rangle+r_{\mathsf{U}},\qquad|r_{\mathsf{U}}|\leq\tfrac{L}{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}.

Subtract the expansions for
\bar{\mathsf{U}}
and
{\mathsf{U}}
and use (
35
):

0\leq\big(g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\big)+\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})-\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k}),\mathbf{d}_{k}\rangle+(r_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}-r_{{\mathsf{U}}}).

Rearranging gives

\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})-\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k}),\mathbf{d}_{k}\rangle\leq g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})+(r_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}-r_{{\mathsf{U}}}).

(36)

Using the remainder bounds
|r_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}|,|r_{{\mathsf{U}}}|\leq\tfrac{L}{2}\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}
we get

r_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}-r_{{\mathsf{U}}}\leq|r_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}|+|r_{{\mathsf{U}}}|\leq L\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}\leq L\eta^{2}.

Substitute into (
36
) to obtain:

\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})-\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k}),\mathbf{d}_{k}\rangle\leq g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})+L\eta^{2}.

(37)

The last inequality coincides with (
34
) for
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{z}_{k}
and
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})=\mathbf{z}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}
, providing the required contradiction and therefore the lower bound
\alpha_{k}>\frac{\eta}{\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|}
.

Combining the two cases and noting that for small enough
\delta
it holds
\omega>\delta
, we obtain a general lower bound for
\alpha_{k}
:

\alpha_{k}\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{\eta}{\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|},\frac{\eta(1-\sigma)c_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})}{\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}}\right\}.

(38)

We note that
\max\{\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|,\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|^{2}\}
is bounded by the constant
D>1
due to its definition in Theorem
3.1
, then combining this with the previous inequality (
38
), reversing the inequality (
30
) and recalling the definition of
c^{*}_{k}
in the statement of Theorem
3.1
, we obtain:

G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})\geq(k+1)\sigma c_{k}^{*}\min\left\{1,\frac{\eta}{D},\frac{\eta(1-\sigma)c_{k}^{*}}{D}\right\}.

(39)

Now, assuming
\eta\leq\frac{\delta}{2L}<D
(reasonable for small
\delta
), we obtain:

•

If
\frac{\eta}{D}<\frac{\eta(1-\sigma)c_{k}^{*}}{D}
(and thus
(1-\sigma)c^{*}_{k}>1
), then (
39
) implies

c^{*}_{k}\leq\frac{D[G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{(k+1)\sigma\eta}.

Observe that setting
\rho=\frac{D[G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{\sigma}
, we necessarily have

1<(1-\sigma)\frac{D[G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{(k+1)\sigma\eta}\quad\mbox{and thus}\quad k<\frac{\rho(1-\sigma)}{\eta}-1\,.

•

Otherwise,
(1-\sigma)c^{*}_{k}\leq 1
and (
39
) yields

c^{*}_{k}\leq\left(\frac{D[G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{(k+1)\sigma(1-\sigma)\eta}\right)^{1/2}{=\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{(k+1)(1-\sigma)\eta}}\;.}

This proves the assertion.

This result yields a convergence rate for the stopping criterion of Algorithm
1
. We now show that, if the algorithm is run with decreasing values of
\eta
, there exists at least one sequence converging to a Clarke stationary point.

Definition 8
(Clarke dual gap)

Given a point
\mathbf{z}\in\Delta^{V}_{\epsilon}
, the Clarke dual gap on
\Delta^{V}_{\epsilon}
is defined as:

c(\mathbf{z}):=-\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{V}_{\epsilon}}\max_{{\mathbf{h}}\in\partial G(\mathbf{z})}{\mathbf{h}}^{\top}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{z}).

First, it is necessary to recall the definition of
Clarke stationary points
for minimization of Lipschitz continuous functions over a compact set, which is linked to the
Clarke subdifferential
.

Definition 9
(First-order stationary point)

A point
\mathbf{z}^{*}
is called a
Clarke stationary point
for a locally Lipschitz function
G
to be minimized over the set
\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}
if

\max_{{\mathbf{h}}\in\partial G(\mathbf{z}^{*})}{\mathbf{h}}^{\top}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\geq 0\quad\forall\ \mathbf{w}\in\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}.

Theorem 3.2

Let
G(\mathbf{z}^{*})=\min\limits_{\mathbf{z}\in\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}}G(\mathbf{z})
and let
\{\mathbf{z}_{k}\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Assume that the algorithm is run with a sequence
\eta_{k}=\frac{\eta_{0}}{(1+k)^{\tau}}
, with
0<\eta_{0}<\frac{\delta}{2L}
and
0<\tau<1
. As in Theorem
3.1
,
L
is the Lipschitz constant (
25
) of
G
, the step size is determined using an Armijo line search with fixed parameter
\sigma
and
D:=\text{diam}\left(\Delta^{V}_{\epsilon}\right)^{2}=n+1>1
. Fixing
\rho=\frac{D[G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{\sigma\eta_{0}}
, let
c^{*}_{k}=\min\limits_{i\in[0:k]}c_{\eta_{i}}(\mathbf{z}_{i})
.
Then,

c^{*}_{k}\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{\tau}}}.&\text{if }k<\sqrt[1-\tau]{\rho(1-\sigma)(1-\tau)+1}-1\,,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{(1-\sigma)\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{\tau}}}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(40)

In particular,

\lim_{k\to\infty}c^{*}_{k}=0\quad\text{at a rate of }\mathcal{O}\left(k^{\frac{{1-\tau}}{2}}\right).

Moreover, there exists a limit point of the sequence
\{\mathbf{z}_{k}\}
that is a Clarke stationary point of the problem
\min\limits_{\mathbf{z}\in\Delta_{\epsilon}^{V}}G(\mathbf{z})
.

Proof

Following the exact same reasoning as in Theorem
3.1
, we derive a slight modification of (
39
):

G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})\geq\sigma c_{k}^{*}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\min\left\{1,\frac{\eta_{i}}{D},\frac{\eta_{i}(1-\sigma)c_{k}^{*}}{D}\right\}.

(41)

Assuming, as in Theorem
3.1
, that
\eta_{0}\leq\frac{\delta}{2L}<D
, we can simplify the minimum term, obtaining:

G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})\geq\sigma c_{k}^{*}\min\left\{\frac{\eta_{0}}{D},\frac{\eta_{0}(1-\sigma)c_{k}^{*}}{D}\right\}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{\tau}}.

We finally obtain the two cases as in Theorem
3.1
:

•

If
(1-\sigma)c^{*}_{k}>1
, then

c^{*}_{k}\leq\frac{D[G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{\sigma\eta_{0}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{\tau}}}.

•

Otherwise,

c^{*}_{k}\leq\left(\frac{D[G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{\sigma(1-\sigma)\eta_{0}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{\tau}}}\right)^{1/2}.

Putting
\rho=\frac{D[G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-G(\mathbf{z}^{*})]}{\sigma\eta_{0}}
gives (
40
).

Note that, for
\tau=0
, we would retrieve (
39
), while for
0<\tau<1
, we have

\frac{(k+1)^{1-\tau}-1}{1-\tau}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{\tau}}\leq\frac{(k+1)^{1-\tau}}{1-\tau}

for
k
sufficiently large, which guarantees the rate of convergence for
c^{*}_{k}
.

To conclude, we prove that there exists a limit point
\mathbf{z}^{*}
of the the sequence
\{\mathbf{z}_{k}\}
generated by the algorithm such that
c(\mathbf{z}^{*})=0
. By Definition
9
, this implies that
\mathbf{z}^{*}
is a Clarke stationary point. 3

Since
c^{*}_{k}\to 0
, we can extract from the sequence generated by the algorithm a subsequence
\{\mathbf{z}_{h}\}
converging to
\mathbf{z}^{*}
such that
c_{\eta_{h}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})\to 0
.

Because any matrix
{\mathsf{U}}
is either active or inactive in
\mathbf{z}^{*}
, there exists
\delta^{*}>0
such that
{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta^{*}}(\mathbf{z}^{*})={\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}^{*})
.
All functions
g_{\mathsf{U}}
are Lipschitz continuous with a common constant
L
, hence we may choose a radius
r^{*}<\frac{\delta^{*}}{2L}
such that for every
\mathbf{z}\in B_{r^{*}}(\mathbf{z}^{*})
:

{\mathcal{U}}^{\bar{\delta}}(\mathbf{z})={\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}),\qquad\text{where }\bar{\delta}=\delta^{*}-2Lr^{*}>0.

In particular, no function inactive at
\mathbf{z}^{*}
can become active inside
B_{r^{*}}(\mathbf{z}^{*})
:

{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z})\subset{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}^{*})\qquad\forall\,\mathbf{z}\in B_{r^{*}}(\mathbf{z}^{*}).

We now restrict the subsequence
\{\mathbf{z}_{h}\}
to the indices for which
\mathbf{z}_{h}\in B_{r^{*}}(\mathbf{z}^{*})
and we prove that for such
\mathbf{z}_{h}
,

{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{h})={\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{h}).

Fix such an index
h
. Since
{\mathcal{U}}^{\bar{\delta}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})={\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})
, any function
g_{\bar{{\mathsf{U}}}}
that is not active at
\mathbf{z}_{h}
satisfies

g_{\bar{{\mathsf{U}}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})<-\bar{\delta},\qquad\forall\,{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})

From system (
26
), a matrix
\bar{{\mathsf{U}}}
belongs to
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{h})
only if

\langle\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})-\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h}),\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{h}\rangle\leq g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})-g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})+L\eta^{2}\leq-\bar{\delta}+L\eta^{2}\;\;\;\forall{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\delta}(\mathbf{z}_{h})

Since
\mathbf{z}
is restricted by
\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{h}\|\leq\eta
, we can bound the left-hand side as

\left|\langle\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})-\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h}),\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{h}\rangle\right|\leq\|\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})-\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})\|\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{h}\|\leq 2L\eta\,.

Hence,

\langle\nabla g_{{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})-\nabla g_{\bar{\mathsf{U}}}(\mathbf{z}_{h}),\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{h}\rangle\geq-2L\eta\,.

If
\eta
is chosen such that

2L\eta<\bar{\delta}-L\eta^{2}\,,

no inactive
{\mathsf{U}}
satisfies the inequality defining
{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{h})
. Therefore, for all
\eta\leq\eta^{*}=\sqrt{1+\frac{\bar{\delta}}{L}}-1
, we have

{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{h})={\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})\qquad\text{for all such }\mathbf{z}_{h}.

We thus obtain the chain of inclusions:

{\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{h})={\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})\subset{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}^{*}),\quad\forall\,\mathbf{z}_{h}\in B_{r^{*}}(\mathbf{z}^{*}),\,\forall\,\eta\leq\eta^{*}\,.

Consequently, whenever
\eta_{h}\leq\eta^{*}
,

c_{\eta_{h}}(\mathbf{z}_{h})=c(\mathbf{z}_{h})\geq c(\mathbf{z}^{*}).

Since the left-hand side converges to
0
, we conclude that
c(\mathbf{z}^{*})=0
, as claimed.

This proves that
\mathbf{z}^{*}
satisfies the first-order stationarity condition in the Clarke sense (Definition
9
), therefore there exists an accumulation point of the iterates produced by the algorithm that is a Clarke stationary points.

This result shows that, if Algorithm
1
is executed with progressively smaller values of
\eta
, there exists at least a subsequence converging to a
Clarke stationary point
. It should be noted that this theoretical result does not guarantee convergence to a global or even a local minimum. However, as will be shown in the next chapter, the algorithm empirically converges to local minima in practice. This observation suggests that coupling the method with a multistart or similar global-search strategy may yield solutions related to large common cliques.

Each experiment included 10 test points and 50 matrices
{\mathsf{U}}
. The initial points were randomly chosen in the domain
\Delta^{V}_{\epsilon}
. The maximum number of iterations was set to
k_{\text{max}}=1000
. The stepsize
\alpha
was computed using the Armijo rule with parameters
\omega=0.8
and
\sigma=0.4
. The stopping threshold
\xi
was set to 0.001. For the function
G
, we used
\gamma=1
, while the parameter
\beta
was computed according to Theorem
2.1
, with
\epsilon=0.001
when
n<1000
, and
\epsilon=0.0001
otherwise. Finally, we set
\delta=0.01
while
\eta
was updated accordingly to Theorem
3.2
, with
\eta_{0}=\frac{\delta}{2L}
, where
L
is the Lipschitz constant computed as in (
25
). We note that with the graphs and parameters used, the algorithm converged to a maximal common clique in all experiments.

We further highlight that the adversary oracle requires solving system (
26
) multiple times, which is the most computationally demanding part of the algorithm. In order to mitigate this cost, one may include a control condition that allows this step to be skipped when appropriate. In particular, if at a given iteration the algorithm reaches a feasible point
\mathbf{z}_{k}
for which all matrices
g_{\mathsf{U}}
are active, then
{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})={\mathcal{U}}_{\eta}(\mathbf{z}_{k})={\mathcal{U}}
. In this case, the computation becomes significantly simpler: system (
26
) no longer needs to be solved, since
\partial_{\eta}G(\mathbf{z}_{k})
coincides with
\partial G(\mathbf{z}_{k})
, and the LMO reduces to computing the Clarke dual gap
c(\mathbf{z}_{k})
directly.

Empirically, we have observed that this scenario arises very often, and it appears to be influenced by the choice of the problem parameters (in particular this is the case for large values of
\beta
). As a result, after a few iterations the algorithm tends to give an iterate with all
g_{\mathsf{U}}
active, and keeps generating points that preserve this property. A more refined analysis of this phenomenon could provide sharper theoretical guarantees and is left for future investigation.

The results hence highlight that the proposed approach consistently delivers strong overall performance. Variability across runs remains modest, and although the algorithm falls short of the maximum common clique by a bunch of vertices, it reliably converges to large and meaningful solutions. In summary, the results confirm that the approach effectively captures the structure imposed by the backbone and shows stable behavior across different perturbation levels.

5
Conclusion

Apparently for the first time in literature, we generalize the continuous formulation of the Maximum-Clique problem to a game theoretic setting with adversarial uncertainty. Considering stable global solutions, we establish a one-to-one correspondence with maximum common cliques under this discrete uncertainty on the underlying graph structure.
The two main tools for our continuous formulation are a penalty term and semi-continuity constraints, along with a min-max approach for dealing with the discrete uncertainty, even in presence of (exponentially) many components.

As we are facing a difficult NP-complete problem, this is a hard global optimization task. We thus develop a first-order and projection-free algorithm, tailored to our nonsmooth and nonconvex formulation, that can be easily embedded into a global optimization algorithmic framework based on multistart or local search/basin hopping strategies.
To this end we use a seemingly novel concept for approximate subdifferentials. While we provide a complete convergence theory, our numerical experience shows promising results.

Future lines of research may include
to establish an equivalence between local solutions to our or a related model and common maximal cliques;
from an algorithmic point of view, one can employ other variants of projection-free procedures, improving further the efficiency of the search direction determination.
