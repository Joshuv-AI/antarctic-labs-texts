Stratospheric wave predictability enhances surface forecasts over North America
Authors: Xiuyuan Ding, Baoqiang Xiang, Gang Chen, Liping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advances in subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) forecasting have broad socioeconomic benefits. Stratospheric variability has been identified as a major source of surface predictability on S2S timescales, yet its influence on forecasting North American cold extremes has not been fully understood. Here, using hindcasts from a coupled S2S forecast system, we identify a wave-1–like pattern among leading sources of predictability in the lower stratosphere during extended winters. This mode, characterized by strong stratospheric wave activity, improves second-week temperature prediction over North America by 15% (2 to 28%). It also increases the likelihood of North American cold extremes by 50%. Prediction skill for these extremes increases by 28% in forecasts conditioned on the wave-1–like stratospheric mode, across regions inhabited by over 69% of the Canadian population and over 30% of the U.S. population. These skill enhancements are attributed to the vertical wave coupling between the stratosphere and troposphere, distinct from the well-known downward influence of sudden stratospheric warmings (SSW). By conditioning forecasts on stratospheric states, our findings highlight strong stratospheric waves as windows of opportunity for skillful winter S2S predictions.

INTRODUCTION

Subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) forecasts, generally referring to predictions made 2 weeks to 2 months ahead, bridge the gap between weather forecasts and climate predictions. Skillful S2S prediction could offer important information with considerable socioeconomic value, as this time range is critical for proactive planning and risk management related to extreme weather and climate events such as heatwaves, cold spells, and heavy precipitation [e.g., (1–3)]. In particular, recent cold extremes over North America have attracted much public attention and scientific debate (4–6). The exceptional cold wave in February 2021 was suggested to be related to a sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) in January (7, 8). However, numerical forecasts suggest only a limited influence from the SSW (5, 6). While notable progress has been made in winter forecasts [e.g., (9–11)], S2S predictions have remained challenging.

The extratropical winter stratosphere has emerged as a promising prospect for improving S2S prediction skills of surface fields [e.g., (12–15)] due to its longer timescales and extended predictability compared to the troposphere (16–18). SSWs, characterized by a rapid breakdown of the polar vortex and a reversal of zonal-mean zonal winds, are the most prominent examples. Several modeling studies found an increase in prediction skill for North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) and surface air temperature (SAT) forecasts initialized during SSWs (13, 19, 20). Given these surface implications, it is essential to understand the predictability of the stratosphere. On average, SSWs can be predicted deterministically 10 days in advance in idealized dynamical core models (21) and S2S forecast systems (17, 22), but can vary widely between individual SSW events (23–25). However, the canonical downward impacts of SSWs over North America are often less robust than over the North Atlantic–European sector and can depend strongly on event type and regional circulation structure (26, 27).

Beyond zonal-mean circulation, the planetary wave component of stratospheric variability has received increasing attention for its link to North American (NA) cold spells [e.g., (28–30)]. Cohen et al. (4) argued that polar vortex stretching in the lower stratosphere, which features planetary wave reflection, is linked to NA cold extremes such as the February 2021 Texas cold spell. Earlier studies showed that suitable stratospheric wind profiles can form reflecting surfaces that redirect upward-propagating planetary waves back toward the troposphere (31–33), providing a dynamical pathway for stratospheric influence distinct from annular-mode coupling. Downward wave propagation is commonly diagnosed by negative eddy heat flux in the lower stratosphere. Extremely negative zonal-mean heat flux is linked to concurrent positive NAO and NA coldness on the surface (34, 35). Several observational studies reported a linkage between regional wave reflection over North America and the NA coldness (26, 36–40). Focusing on the leading mode of stratospheric wave variability rather than heat flux, Ding et al. (41, 42) found that enhanced wave activity precedes NA cooling with a lag of 10 days in both observations and climate models. The surface signals and timescales of these events differ from those of SSWs. Idealized numerical experiments also generally corroborate the role of vertical wave coupling behind the downward influence from stratospheric waves (29).

Despite the growing evidence that stratospheric wave variability affects NA cold extremes, its influence on surface forecast skill has not been systematically quantified. During a cold spell associated with wave reflection, nudging the stratosphere in subseasonal forecasts showed improved surface predictions (43). The predictability of stratospheric waves is also less well established. A case study suggests that the predictable limit of a March 2007 negative eddy heat flux event was about 7 days (44). In S2S models, the deterministic predictability of these events appears similar to that of SSWs (13). However, the predictability of strong stratospheric wave events and regional wave reflection events remains unclear.

The present study will disentangle predictable patterns in the lower stratosphere on S2S timescales. The patterns of the most predictable modes (MPMs) resemble the known leading modes of stratospheric variability, thus offering physical insights for the impact of stratospheric planetary waves on surface predictability. In particular, forecasts conditioned on a wave-1–like MPM exhibit increases in surface prediction skill.

RESULTS

Stratospheric MPMs and their predictability

We begin with the prediction of the 100 hPa geopotential height anomaly (Z100) in the lower stratosphere during extended winters (November–March). The Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory (GFDL) Seamless System for Prediction and Earth System Research (SPEAR) S2S system (Materials and Methods) demonstrates statistically significant correlation skills in Z100 over most of the northern extratropics within the first 30 days (Fig. 1, A to C). Forecast skill decreases progressively with increasing lead time, while correlations remain robust over the polar region even during days 31 to 40. Over the midlatitudes, higher skill is retained over North America than over Eurasia. At a 3-pentad (days 11 to 15) lead, the system continues to exhibit strong performance, with anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC) values exceeding 0.6 (fig. S1G). The root mean square error (RMSE) begins to plateau around 4 pentads (days 16 to 20), indicating that stratospheric prediction remains skillful for roughly 20 days after initialization.

To understand the source of stratospheric predictability, we perform the average predictability time (APT) analysis (Materials and Methods) on the pentad-mean Z100 in the 20-year SPEAR hindcasts (2000–2019). The leading modes identified in the APT analysis are termed MPMs and are ranked by their predictability. To corroborate the important role of the MPMs in predictions, the hindcast anomalies are reconstructed using the spatial patterns and time series of the 10 leading MPMs. Compared to the unfiltered (raw) model, the reconstructed anomalies show a fairly comparable prediction skill of Z100 (fig. S1), confirming that the leading MPMs largely capture the S2S predictability sources in the lower stratosphere.

Figure 1 (D to F) shows the spatial patterns (Materials and Methods) of the three leading MPMs. The first two MPMs (MPM1 and MPM2) resemble the Northern Annular Mode (NAM) pattern, characterized by opposite-signed anomalies between the polar region and midlatitudes (Fig. 1, D and E). While their associated time series are uncorrelated by construction, the spatial patterns are not mutually orthogonal (fig. S2). Regression analysis in SPEAR hindcasts suggests that MPM1 is associated with more persistent stratospheric signals while MPM2 is more strongly linked to the troposphere (fig. S3), suggesting that the two modes may reflect different aspects of vertical coupling and timescales of NAM-like variability. However, their detailed relationship warrants future investigation. For the third MPM (MPM3), pronounced positive geopotential height anomalies are present over North America, accompanied by negative anomalies over Eurasia (Fig. 1F). Its dipole structure closely matches the climatological wave-1 pattern, allowing it to represent wave-1 variability. This pattern also resembles those associated with strong stratospheric wave activity and potential regional wave reflection (29). Hereinafter, we refer to the first two modes as NAM-like MPM, and the third mode as wave-1–like MPM. To be consistent with the NAM convention, a negative NAM-like MPM represents a weakened polar vortex. The NAM-like MPMs exhibit the highest skill across all modes at leads of 3 to 9 pentads (Fig. 1G). Their dominance is more evident at longer leads, suggesting that the zonal mean component is most predictable in the lower stratosphere on S2S timescales. The wave-1–like MPM also shows relatively high skill at 3 to 6 pentads compared to MPM4-MPM10.

A Fourier-based error decomposition elucidates the contrasting predictabilities across individual MPMs (Materials and Methods). Figure 1H shows the normalized forecast error of Z100, decomposed into contributions from the zonal mean and from individual zonal wavenumber components. The total forecast error saturates after approximately 6 pentads, indicating a limit to stratospheric predictability consistent with prior literature (18, 45). In contrast, the zonal-mean error continues to grow, implying a longer predictability horizon. The slow growth rate in zonal-mean error explains the high predictability of the NAM-like MPM, which primarily captures zonal-mean variability. Among wave components, the wave-1 error exhibits a slower growth rate than wave-2 and wave-3 errors, consistent with the relatively higher predictability of the wave-1–like MPM. A further decomposition of wave errors into phase and amplitude contributions shows that the total wave error is dominated by phase error (fig. S4), consistent with results in a perfect model experiment (18).

The NAM-like and wave-1–like MPMs dominate subseasonal stratospheric predictability in boreal winter (Fig. 1G). We project daily ERA5 Z100 onto MPM patterns to obtain a historical record of MPM time series. Because the two NAM-like MPMs have similar spatial structures (fig. S2), their projections onto ERA5 are highly correlated and effectively reduce to a single NAM-like index (Materials and Methods); we therefore use their combined index when defining NAM-like MPM events. Using a −1.5 standard deviation (SD) threshold, we identify NAM-like MPM events in ERA5. These events exhibit surface signatures similar to those associated with SSWs (fig. S6). Because the downward influence of SSWs has been extensively documented (13–16, 20), we will focus on the less explored wave-1–like MPM and its implications for subseasonal surface predictability.

Influence of the wave-1–like MPM on surface prediction

Of particular interest is whether the wave-1–like MPM in the stratosphere can affect surface conditions as well as their predictability. To address this, we start by compositing SAT and 500 hPa geopotential height (Z500) anomalies following the wave-1–like MPM. We select wave-1–like MPM events using a 1.5 SD threshold in ERA5. During weeks 2 and 3, a ridge anomaly over Alaska and a downstream trough anomaly act together to induce substantial cold anomalies over North America (Fig. 2A), reminiscent of the Alaskan Ridge weather regime (26, 46). There is a roughly 1-week lag between the stratospheric anomalies and surface coldness. Warm anomalies are observed over northern Eurasia and eastern Europe in weeks 1 and 2. This evolution resembles the life cycle of strong stratospheric wave events and regional wave reflection events, characterized by a rapid drop in temperature over North America (40, 41). SPEAR hindcasts generally capture these key features (Fig. 2B), including the NA cold anomalies and the Eurasian warm anomalies in weeks 2 to 4, albeit with weaker amplitudes likely due to ensemble averaging that reduces internal variability. To ensure a fair comparison between hindcasts and reanalysis, the composites used ERA5 data during 2000–2019, consistent with the SPEAR hindcast period. Analyzing the full ERA5 period (1950–2021) yields very similar results (fig. S7).

To show the stratospheric influence on forecast skills, we assess forecasts conditioned on the wave-1–like MPM and corresponding control forecasts (Materials and Methods). Differences in skill quantify the influence on surface forecasts. In forecasts conditioned on the wave-1–like MPM, central Canada and the U.S. Midwest show substantial increases in week 2 prediction skills (Fig. 3, C and G), coinciding with the regions of strongest cold anomalies following wave-1–like MPM events. Over North America (40°–60°N, 70°–110°W), all metrics indicate improved prediction skills, with 15% ACC (2 to 28%) and −14% RMSE (−22 to −6%) improvements passing bootstrap significance tests (Fig. 3 and fig. S8). The consistency of the cold anomalies in both ERA5 and SPEAR likely contributes to these improvements. Central Asia also shows enhanced week-2 skill, although the composite cold anomalies there are weaker (Fig. 2). The ACC improvements over North America persist into week 3, but RMSE increases, suggesting that the model continues to capture the spatial pattern of the anomalies while their amplitude becomes less reliably predicted at longer lead times (fig. S9). In contrast, ACC decreases appear over northern Eurasia in week 2 and become more widespread by week 3. This reduction is consistent with decreased agreement in anomaly signs between forecasts and reanalysis: Grid points with opposite anomaly signs contribute negative covariance, thereby reducing spatial correlation (fig. S10).

Moving beyond mean temperature anomalies, we examine the temperature distribution. We focus on the NA land region for the wave-1–like MPM, given its more pronounced surface anomalies in this area (Fig. 2). Both ERA5 and SPEAR show a clear shift towards colder conditions (Fig. 4, A and B), statistically significant at the 95% confidence level based on a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Across the 39 events in ERA5, 77% of events show negative temperature anomalies over North America in week 2, and 64% in week 3, implying the existence of diversity in surface signals associated with the wave-1–like MPM. Despite event-to-event variability, the probability of cold extremes increases following wave-1–like MPM events. We quantify this change using a risk ratio, defined as the probability of cold SAT anomalies following wave-1–like MPM events divided by the probability in the control distribution. Thus, a risk ratio of 1 indicates no change, while a risk ratio of 1.5 indicates that cold extremes are 1.5 times as likely, corresponding to a 50% increase in risk. In ERA5, wave-1–like MPM events increase the risk of SAT anomalies colder than −1 and −1.5 SDs by 50 and 50%, respectively. SPEAR shows a similar increase in risk of 40% and 30% for the same thresholds. The increased cold-extreme risk persists into week 3 in ERA5 but becomes more modest in SPEAR (fig. S11).

We further assess the forecast skill of cold extremes conditioned on the MPM. Extreme cold days are defined as days when local SAT falls at least 1 SD below climatology. Figure 4C compares the predicted and observed regionally averaged frequencies of cold extremes, with perfect forecasts lying along the dashed diagonal lines. To avoid spuriously inflated correlations in cases without cold extremes, the control skill distribution is estimated via bootstrapping applied to samples in which the observed cold extreme frequency exceeds 0.4 days per week. Forecasts of NA cold extremes conditioned on the wave-1–like MPM lie more closely to the diagonal than the control distribution, indicating improved consistency between predicted and observed event frequency. The resulting correlation skill (0.74) far exceeds the control skill (0.58), representing a 28% relative skill improvement. Rather than implying a universal forecast improvement, this result reflects a conditional increase in skill associated with wave-1–like MPM.

Together, we demonstrated that the wave-1–like MPM leads to substantial skill increases in predictions of both mean and extreme temperatures over North America, representing windows of opportunity for NA cold spell forecasting. Thus, it requires further examination of the physical processes behind these downward influences.

Physical processes behind stratospheric downward influence

Wave-1–like MPM events feature rapid growth of the stratospheric wave-1 pattern beginning about 5 days before onset (Fig. 5, A and B). Compared to the trough anomaly, the anomalous ridge over North America has larger amplitudes. This wave-1 development is accompanied by positive eddy heat flux anomalies over Eurasia and the Pacific and negative anomalies over eastern North America at 100 hPa (Fig. 5, C and D), indicative of anomalous downward wave propagation and potential regional wave reflection over North America. An examination of the vertical wave structure shows consistent results, with the initial westward-tilting structure of the geopotential height anomalies shifting to an eastward tilt (fig. S12). This is qualitatively similar to the vertical wave coupling during strong stratospheric wave events (29). Following the onset, the tropospheric NA ridge weakens and retrogrades westward into the Alaskan region, giving way to a trough over eastern North America (Fig. 5, E and F). This tropospheric evolution resembles the weather regime transition from a Pacific Trough regime to an Alaskan Ridge regime during regional wave reflection events (40). The Alaskan Ridge regime is known to favor widespread low temperatures over North America (26, 46). Thus, it might explain the cold temperatures following the wave-1–like MPM.

Can the model reproduce the key processes behind the coupling of the wave-1–like MPM? Overall, the ensemble mean in SPEAR (blue lines) agrees well with ERA5 (black lines) (Fig. 5). The time lags between this series of processes are worth noting. In particular, a precursory tropospheric ridge peaks before the stratospheric ridge over North America reaches its maximum (Fig. 5, B and F). Then, negative eddy heat flux anomalies occur concurrently with the stratospheric ridge, preceding the deepening of the tropospheric trough over North America (Fig. 5, D and F). This sequence represents the coupling between the stratosphere and the troposphere associated with the wave-1–like MPM, providing a physical basis for the NA coldness and for increased forecast skills. We note, however, that this diagnosis is based on composites and does not establish a causal relationship among these processes. Addressing causality requires targeted experiments, such as nudging experiments (43, 47) or idealized modeling frameworks (34), which can isolate dynamic processes more rigorously.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified predictable modes in the lower stratosphere using APT applied to a GFDL coupled S2S forecast system. While previous studies have linked stratospheric wave variability to NA cold extremes, we explicitly demonstrate that strong stratospheric wave activity leads to substantial forecast skill gains. The benefited region encompasses more than 69% of the Canadian population and over 30% of the U.S. population, underscoring the societal relevance of these findings. These estimates are based on the Gridded Population of the World, Version 4 (GPWv4) dataset, calculated as the population within the target region (40°–60°N, 250°–290°E) relative to national totals.

Two types of modes dominate 100 hPa predictability as follows: zonally symmetric NAM-like patterns and a zonally asymmetric wave-1–like pattern. Their extended predictability arises from the slow growth of amplitude errors relative to phase errors, as well as the slower error growth of the wave-1 component compared with higher wave numbers. Differences in horizontal phase speed likely contribute, as larger-scale waves tend to evolve more slowly. However, stratospheric predictability is also governed by processes such as vertical wave propagation from the troposphere, wave-mean flow interaction, and wave-wave interactions. In this study, we focus on the lower stratosphere because previous work has shown that lower-stratospheric anomalies can serve as important precursors for tropospheric variability (16, 48, 49). We note that examining higher stratospheric levels may provide additional insight into the vertical structure of predictability and its surface impacts, given recent evidence that the vertical structure of stratospheric variability can provide added value for understanding U.S. cold-air outbreaks (28).

In particular, the wave-1–like mode, associated with enhanced stratospheric wave activity, precedes widespread cold anomalies across North America and elevates the risk of NA cold extremes by 50%. Hindcasts conditioned on the wave-1–like MPM show a 15% (2 to 28%) enhancement in week-2 SAT forecast skill over central Canada and the Midwest, regions most affected by the ensuing cold anomalies. This mode also improves the prediction of cold extreme frequency by 28%, quantified as a relative increase in the correlation between predicted and observed regional cold extreme frequency compared to the control forecasts. In ERA5, 138 (39) wave-1–like MPM events are identified during 1950–2021 (2000–2019).

The wave-1–like MPM events identified in this study provide evidence that downward wave coupling exerts a quantifiable influence on surface prediction over North America. Previous studies have long recognized stratospheric wave reflection as a pathway for surface influence distinct from the canonical downward effects of SSWs and NAM-like variability (31–33). However, its potential role in NA cold extremes has only recently received increased attention. NAM-like MPM events are characterized by a stronger annular polar-cap response and sustained positive zonal-mean eddy heat flux anomalies before onset (figs. S16 and S17), consistent with upward wave driving and polar vortex weakening. In contrast, wave-1–like MPM events exhibit only modest polar-cap height anomalies but show a pronounced regional wave structure, a shorter pulse of upward wave activity, regionally negative eddy heat flux, and changes in vertical wave tilting consistent with downward wave coupling (Fig. 5 and fig. S12). Following these events, the zonal-mean stratospheric wind decreases with altitude above 10 hPa, suggesting a potential reflecting surface that redirects planetary waves downward (fig. S18) (31). However, the zonal-mean eddy heat flux remains positive (fig. S17), differing from canonical wave reflection events characterized by negative zonal-mean heat flux. Nevertheless, these features suggest that the wave-1–like MPM is associated with a downward wave coupling pathway that is particularly relevant to NA surface variability and prediction. A definitive assessment of the potential regional wave reflection warrants future studies.

To place these events in the context of existing classifications, we compare the timing of wave-1–like MPM events with previously identified 10 hPa strong wave events (29) and wave reflection events (40). Over the period 1980–2021 (December–March), when all event records are available, and using a ± 15-day window centered on the onset of wave-1–like MPM events, we find that approximately 50% of MPM events coincide with 10 hPa strong wave events, while about 17% coincide with wave reflection events (fig. S13). These results indicate that wave-1–like MPM events partially overlap with previously identified categories but are not equivalent to them.

Although the present analysis focuses on a single index, MPM3, this does not imply that only wave amplitude is relevant on S2S timescales. The spatial structure of the mode encodes both amplitude and phase information of the planetary waves. Wave-1 phase errors continue to grow beyond ∼15 days (fig. S4), indicating that phase evolution remains an important component on S2S timescales. We focus on MPM3 because it exhibits relatively long predictability and has a clear linkage to surface anomalies and forecast skill. Although MPM4 has some spatial similarity to MPM3, its predictability is substantially weaker (Fig. 1). In addition, composites based on MPM4 show little evidence of the negative eddy heat flux anomalies seen for MPM3 (fig. S14).

Surface forecasts can benefit from both the long predictability of the stratosphere and its downward influence. To illustrate this combined prospect, we examined week-3 surface skill in forecasts initialized before the onset of the wave-1–like MPM (days −5 to −1). These forecasts show skill enhancements broadly consistent with the week-2 improvements seen when forecasts are initialized after the MPM (fig. S15). However, skill gains might be sensitive to model biases in the stratosphere and its coupling, such as the excessively strong stratospheric polar vortex present in SPEAR (50).

To assess the robustness of our findings across forecast systems, we extended the APT analysis beyond SPEAR to ECMWF, UKMO, and ISAC hindcasts from the S2S database. Despite differences in model formulation, ensemble size, hindcast period, and initialization schedule (table S1), these systems show broadly consistent leading predictable stratospheric modes, including NAM-like modes and a wave-1–like mode (figs. S19 and S20). We further examined surface prediction skill following wave-1–like MPM events in these additional systems. Because the different S2S systems use different initialization schedules, we selected, for each system, the hindcast initialization closest to the onset of the wave-1–like MPM events and evaluated the corresponding week-3 SAT skill. The results broadly support enhanced NA SAT skill following wave-1–like MPM events (Fig. 6). These results support the interpretation that predictable stratospheric wave variability is not unique to SPEAR and may provide a relevant source of subseasonal surface prediction skill across forecast systems. However, a more systematic multimodel assessment remains an important next step, particularly to better understand intermodel differences and the role of different stratospheric representations in shaping wave-related surface predictability.

In principle, if the stratosphere were more accurately represented and predictable at longer leads, surface prediction skill could extend further. Given the devastating impacts of winter cold extremes, improved S2S prediction of these events can directly benefit proactive planning and risk management in critical sectors, including transportation, energy planning and management, and public health.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Model hindcasts and verification data

The dynamical forecasts used here are provided by SPEAR (51), a fully coupled model developed at the GFDL. SPEAR includes the AM4 atmospheric model, LM4 land model, MOM6 ocean model, and SIS2 sea ice model. The horizontal resolution is around 50 km for the atmosphere and 100 km for the ocean. The model lid height of AM4 is 1 hPa. Initial conditions are generated through nudging. Atmospheric fields are nudged to the National Centers for Environmental Prediction Global Forecast System analysis data, and the sea surface temperature is nudged to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration Optimum Interpolation 1/4 Degree Daily Surface Temperature Analysis (OISST, version 2) (52). In this study, we analyze hindcasts initialized during extended winters (November–March) from 2000–2019. Hindcasts are initialized every 5 days and integrated for 45 days, with 10 ensemble members per initialization. More details on the SPEAR S2S prediction system can be found in Xiang et al. (10).

The verification data are from the fifth generation of atmospheric reanalysis from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ERA5) (53). The daily data we analyzed are regridded to a horizontal resolution of 1.5° × 1.5°. We interpolate the SPEAR data to the same grid.

To ensure consistency in the verification, when comparing with hindcasts during 2000–2019, ERA5 anomalies are calculated by removing the daily climatology. For the longer 1950–2021 analysis, anomalies are computed by removing the time mean and the first two harmonics of the full-year climatology after detrending. For hindcasts, anomalies are defined relative to the model climatology at an equivalent lead time for all forecasts initialized on the same day of the year.

To assess the robustness of our results, we analyze hindcasts from ECMWF, UKMO, and ISAC in the S2S Prediction Project Database (54) (table S1). For each model, all available hindcasts are used to perform the APT analysis. Surface prediction skill is evaluated only for forecast periods that overlap with the common analysis period of 2000–2019.

APT analysis

To identify predictable patterns in the stratosphere, we adopt the APT analysis (55, 56), a method widely used in forecast datasets (57, 58). For computational efficiency, the analysis is performed on pentad (5-day) means of geopotential height anomalies at 100 hPa (Z100) north of 20°N. We choose to focus on the lower stratosphere because of its importance in the surface response induced by the stratosphere (16, 48, 49).

A standard measure of predictability called the Mahalanobis signal can be defined asP(τ)=1−στ2σclim2(1)where στ2 represents the hindcast variance of Z100 at lead time τ, σclim2 the climatological variance that defined as the Z100 variance of all ensemble hindcasts from different lead times. Thus, P(τ) is near 1 at the shortest leads and decreases to near 0 as the hindcast variance approaches the climatological variance. The APT is then defined as twice the sum of P(τ) from lead time 1 to finite lead times (lead 2 to 9 pentads in the present application)APT=2∑τ=1∞(1−στ2σclim2)(2)

Through a series of derivations, it can be shown that maximizing Eq. 2 leads to a generalized eigenvalue problem2∑τ=1∞(Σˆclim−Σˆτ)q=λΣˆclimq(3)where Σˆclim denotes the climatological covariance matrix and Σˆτ is the hindcast covariance matrix at lead time τ. The eigenvalue λ gives the APT value. Eigenvectors q that satisfy Eq. 3 can be used to derive spatial patterns p associated with each component by regressing qTx(τ,t,e) with x(τ,t,e) (anomalies at lead time τ, start time t, and ensemble member e), which gives p=Σˆclimq. The eigenvalues and their associated eigenvectors are ordered such that the first component maximizes APT. Thus, the spatial patterns p are also referred to as predictable patterns or MPMs. The modes are ordered by their APT values, so that MPM1 maximizes APT, followed by subsequent modes that maximize APT subject to being uncorrelated with the preceding components in the climatological covariance metric.

This distinction is important for interpreting the MPMs. The generalized eigenvalue problem constrains the projection vectors to satisfy qiTΣˆclimqj=0 for distinct modes (i≠j). Equivalently, the projected component time series qiTx and qjTx are uncorrelated under the climatological covariance. However, the displayed MPM maps are regression patterns, p=Σˆclimq, and these regression patterns are not required to be orthogonal in physical space. Thus, different MPMs may share some spatial features even though their associated time series are statistically separated by construction.

APT analysis is therefore analogous to empirical orthogonal function (EOF) analysis only in the broad sense that both methods decompose a field into ordered spatial patterns and associated time series. The key difference is that EOF analysis identifies orthogonal patterns that maximize explained variance, whereas APT analysis identifies components that maximize predictability integrated over the selected lead-time window. An MPM therefore need not be a leading variance mode, and MPM spatial patterns need not be mutually orthogonal.

To ensure that Eq. 3 has a well-defined solution, we first reduce the spatial dimension by performing an EOF analysis on Z100 and retaining the leading 10 modes, accounting for approximately 81% of the total variance. The APT analysis is then applied in this reduced EOF space using the corresponding PCs, so that the effective spatial dimension is the number of retained EOF modes, which is smaller than the temporal dimension. Results are not sensitive to the number of EOFs used. In principle, the APT optimization is defined in the full spatial field; here, it is performed in a reduced EOF space by projecting the data onto the leading EOF modes, such that the optimization is over linear combinations of these modes. Because EOFs form an orthogonal basis, this reduction does not affect the solution provided that sufficient modes are retained.

We obtain the time series of APT modes in ERA5 by regressing ERA5 Z100 anomalies onto each SPEAR-derived MPM pattern individually. These projected time series represent the evolution of the SPEAR-derived predictable structures in observations, allowing us to define ERA5 events that closely resemble each MPM. Defining MPM events in ERA5 facilitates both hindcast validation against reanalysis and forecast skill assessment. Because the two NAM-like MPMs have similar spatial structures with a correlation coefficient of 0.67 (fig. S2), their projections onto ERA5 using single-pattern regression yield highly correlated MPM1 and MPM2 indices (0.92). This remains true when using multiple linear regression (0.99). The linear combination of the two modes is also highly consistent between single-regression and multiple-regression approaches (0.97), indicating that the two modes collapse into a single NAM-like degree of freedom in reanalysis. We therefore define NAM-like MPM events using the combined normalized index of MPM1 and MPM2. These events are defined as days when the combined index is below −1.5, corresponding to weak polar vortex conditions. Wave-1–like MPM events are defined as the days when the normalized third MPM index exceeds 1.5, which shows strong stratospheric wave activity. Consecutive above-threshold days are treated as part of the same event, and no minimum-duration threshold is imposed. While no interval criterion is applied, individual wave-1–like MPM events are separated by at least 6 days. Results are not sensitive to the chosen threshold. Day 0 refers to the first day that meets the event definition. Day −5 denotes 5 days before day 0, and day 5 indicates 5 days after day 0. While SPEAR hindcasts only span 20 years, MPM events are defined in ERA5 from 1950 to 2021. In ERA5, 138 (39) wave-1–like MPM events are identified during 1950–2021 (2000–2019).

To examine impacts on prediction skills, we select SPEAR hindcasts initialized within 5 days after the observational event onset (days 0 to 5), yielding 41 wave-1–like MPM hindcasts. Following Domeisen et al. (13), the forecast anomalies are compared with those of a control population of forecasts to assess skill. For example, for each forecast within 5 days after a wave-1–like MPM event, the control is taken from the same day of the year for other years within the dataset, provided it does not fall into opposite-phased wave-1–like events (<−1.5). In other words, the control forecasts represent a neutral stratospheric state that bears little resemblance to the wave-1–like MPM. This procedure constructs a large control sample with a distribution that closely matches the event forecasts in terms of seasonality. There are 679 control hindcasts for wave-1–like MPM. A corresponding control population for reanalysis is built using the dates of the control forecasts, for comparison with observational MPM event anomalies.

Skill measures

In this study, forecast skill is evaluated using a range of commonly used measures. One such metric is the correlation coefficient rr=∑t=1T(Xmod−Cmod)(Xobs−Cobs)∑t=1T(Xmod−Cmod)2·∑t=1T(Xobs−Cobs)2(4)where X is a time-dependent variable and C is its climatology. Subscripts mod and obs denote the model ensemble mean and the reanalysis, respectively. T is the number of events or time steps for which r is evaluated.

To evaluate the spatial skill of the anomaly pattern, we apply the ACCACC=∑t=1T∑s=1Sw·(Xmod−Cmod)(Xobs−Cobs)∑t=1T∑s=1Sw·(Xmod−Cmod)2·∑t=1T∑s=1Sw·(Xobs−Cobs)2(5)where the covariance and variance terms are spatially weighted by the cosine of latitude w and averaged over S grid points.

An alternative is to measure the correlation between the observations and ensemble mean forecasts within each subselected ensemble, termed subset correlation (SSC) (59)SSC=∑t=1T∑s=1Sw·(Xmod−[Cmod])(Xobs−[Cobs])∑t=1T∑s=1Sw·(Xmod−[Cmod])2·∑t=1T∑s=1Sw·(Xobs−[Cobs])2(6)where [Cmod] and [Cobs] represent the mean within each subset. Charlton-Perez et al. (59) demonstrate that subsets of forecasts can show inflated ACC even when the actual skill is identical using statistical models. Thus, SSC complements ACC as an additional skill measure. We also evaluate the RMSE and the pattern correlation.

To assess the significance of the correlation coefficient r, we use the effective degrees of freedom in the Student’s t-test to account for the autocorrelation in the time series, which would otherwise cause substantial biases (60). In Figs. 3 and 4, a two-sided 1000-sample bootstrap resampling with replacement is used to estimate the confidence interval for the prediction skills.
