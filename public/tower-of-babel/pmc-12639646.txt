Construction and validation of a risk prediction model for symptom cluster distress in brain tumor patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Li Ying, Su Yangmei, Xiang Qing, Duan Yuyu, Zou Daili, Luo Huidan, Zhou Zhihuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain tumor patients experience high rates of symptom cluster distress (SCD), severely impacting quality of life and recovery. Nurse-led predictive tools are lacking.ObjectiveTo explore the determinants of SCD in brain tumor patients, analyze the risk indicators of SCD, construct a nomogram model, and validate the model.MethodsUsing a convenience sampling method, 300 brain tumor patients admitted to the Department of Neurosurgery at a tertiary-grade A cancer hospital from April 2023 to January 2025 were selected as the study subjects. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors for SCD. Patients were categorized into a low symptom distress (SD) group (SD < 4 scores) and a high SD group (SD ≥ 4 scores) based on the presence of symptom-related distress. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to investigate the factors associated with SCD in brain tumor patients. The risk prediction nomogram model for SCD in these patients was developed using R software (version 4.3.1) with the rms package. The prediction effect and degree of fit of the nomogram model were evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and the Hosmer–Lomoshow goodness-of-fit test.ResultsA prediction model was constructed based on eight influencing factors—age, payment method, disease duration, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), financial toxicity (COST), self-efficacy (GSES), and medical coping mode (MCMQ). The model’s area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.813 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.765–0.862, P < 0.05), with a sensitivity of 0.717, specificity of 0.791, optimal cutoff value of 0.750, and Youden index of 0.508. The calibration curve analysis results indicated that the calibration curve of the column graph model for predicting SCD in brain tumor patients was a straight line with a slope close to 1. According to the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, the incidence of SCD in brain tumor patients predicted by the pillar-line model did not differ significantly from the actual incidence (P = 0.061).ConclusionThe model constructed in this study has a good prediction effect, which can provide a reference for medical staff to quickly identify the risk of SCD in brain tumor patients and take timely preventive management measures, thereby improving the effectiveness of symptom management.Keywords: Brain tumor, Symptom cluster distress, Symptom distress, Influencing factors, Prediction model, Nursing intervention

Background

Brain tumors, which are common diseases of the central nervous system with a global incidence rate of approximately 3.9 per 100,000 and a mortality rate of 4.1 per 100,000 [1], have emerged as a major threat to human health. The clinical trajectory of these patients is frequently complicated by dynamic symptom clusters—interconnected groups of co-occurring symptoms arising from elevated intracranial pressure, tumor localization, and therapeutic interventions [2]. The presence of these symptom clusters intensifies the physical burden on patients and profoundly affects their psychological well-being, social functioning, and overall quality of life, culminating in what is termed symptom distress (SD) [3]. Therefore, symptom cluster-based management is crucial in clinical practice. Early identification and precise risk stratification of high-risk populations enable proactive intervention during the optimal therapeutic window, thereby significantly improving symptom management outcomes.

Advancements in medical technology have extended the survival periods of brain tumor patients; however, the high costs associated with long-term treatment have led to significant financial burdens (termed “financial toxicity”) [4]. Patients with higher levels of financial toxicity often experience more severe symptom clusters, including physical symptoms such as pain, fatigue, and nausea, as well as psychological symptoms such as depression and anxiety [5, 6]. This economic pressure affects treatment adherence and efficacy while exacerbating psychological distress, creating a vicious cycle through the “financial stress–symptom exacerbation–treatment obstruction” pathway [7]. Multiple studies have confirmed that among cancer survivors [6] and primary brain tumor patients [8], higher levels of financial toxicity are not only positively correlated with anxiety and depression severity but are also significantly associated with greater overall symptom burden and lower health-related quality of life scores, indicating that financial toxicity has emerged as an independent prognostic indicator [6, 8]. Therefore, addressing financial toxicity is critical for improving comprehensive supportive care for brain tumor patients.

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to complete specific tasks or cope with situations [9]. The self-management theory suggests that this belief serves as the core internal driver for patients to persistently and effectively manage symptoms [10]. Individuals with high self-efficacy are more inclined to adopt proactive strategies such as actively participating in rehabilitation training and adhering to medication regimens, thereby effectively controlling symptoms, reducing distress, and improving quality of life [11]. Conversely, those with low self-efficacy—lacking confidence in coping—tend to experience intensified uncertainty and fear about their condition, which exacerbates symptom burden. Research [12] indicates that differences in self-efficacy lead to divergences in the cognitive appraisal of symptoms: high self-efficacy individuals perceive symptoms as surmountable challenges, while low self-efficacy individuals view them as threats to health. This cognitive divergence directly influences the perceived intensity of SD. In terms of emotional regulation, those with high self-efficacy demonstrate stronger emotional resilience, experiencing fewer negative emotions such as anxiety and depression when facing symptoms, or recovering more quickly from them [13]. Positive emotional states further alleviate SD by enhancing immunity and symptom tolerance, forming a positive cycle in disease coping. Enhancing self-efficacy is therefore essential for empowering patients to manage their symptoms effectively and achieve better health outcomes.

Medical coping styles refer to the cognitive and behavioral strategies individuals adopt when facing illness and treatment, including patterns such as problem-facing, seeking social support, and avoidance [14]. Based on Lazarus and Folkman’s coping theory [15], positive coping strategies like proactive problem-solving and accessing social support help patients enhance their adaptability to illness and improve their quality of life. In contrast, negative coping styles such as avoiding reality or denying the condition may exacerbate SD and reduce quality of life [16]. Research indicates that patients using positive coping styles typically hold higher expectations for treatment [17]. However, uncomfortable experiences during treatment, physical suffering caused by the illness, and slow recovery often lead to psychological disappointment. When patients excessively focus on their disease state, negative emotions may emerge, further worsening SD. Conversely, moderate avoidance can buffer psychological conflicts, reduce stress load [18], and play a positive role in alleviating SD. Therefore, an in-depth exploration of the coping mechanisms of brain tumor patients and identification of the adaptive characteristics of different coping styles are of critical clinical significance for optimizing supportive care plans and improving patient outcomes.

Current research on symptom clusters in patients with brain tumors primarily focuses on predicting individual symptoms, such as fatigue [19] or anxiety [20], or identifying symptom clusters [21], with limited attention to risk-stratified prediction models based on symptom clusters. To address this critical gap, in this study, we developed and validated a patient-reported symptom cluster risk prediction model for brain tumor patients. Furthermore, we elucidated the mechanistic roles of financial toxicity, self-efficacy, and medical coping style in symptom clusters, aiming to empower nursing professionals with evidence-based tools for personalized symptom management interventions.

Methods

Study participants and procedures

This cross-sectional study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit 300 brain tumor patients from Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (Guangzhou, China) between April 2023 and February 2025. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of brain tumor according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors (5th edition) [22]; (2) age of patients 18–80 years; (3) awareness of their cancer diagnosis; (4) adequate verbal communication and reading comprehension skills to independently complete assessment scales; and (5) willingness to participate with written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included (1) severe clinical conditions impairing communication (e.g., coma or critical illness); (2) comorbid end-stage systemic diseases (e.g., NYHA class IV cardiac dysfunction, respiratory failure) or severe psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia); and (3) recurrent tumors or distant metastases.

Data collection

From April 2023 to January 2025, data were prospectively collected in the neuro-oncology department of the hospital after obtaining ethical approval from the institutional review board. The research team screened potential participants using the predefined eligibility criteria. Detailed explanations of the study’s objectives, methodology, and significance were provided to eligible patients and/or their legal guardians, followed by written informed consent acquisition. Clinical and demographic data were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system, while additional variables were collected through structured face-to-face interviews. To ensure data reliability, researchers received standardized training on questionnaire administration, all questionnaires underwent completeness verification immediately after completion, and questionnaires were collected on-site to minimize missing data.

Of 325 distributed questionnaires, 300 were returned with valid responses, yielding an effective response rate of 92.3%.

Measurements

General information questionnaire

General Information Questionnaire: Sex, age, marital status, education, monthly income, payment method, disease duration, employment status post-diagnosis, and Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS). The KPS scale comprises 11 hierarchical levels with scores ranging from 0 to 100 points, with 10-point increments. A score of 0 indicates mortality, while a score of 100 represents normal physiological functioning without limitations [24]. This validated assessment tool demonstrates a positive correlation between higher scores and improved physical functional capacity, serving as a reliable indicator of patient performance status.

M.D. Anderson symptom Inventory-Brain tumor module

The MD Anderson Symptom Inventory for Brain Tumor (MDASI-BT), a disease-specific patient-reported outcome measure originally developed by Armstrong et al. (2006) [25], systematically evaluates symptom burden and its multidimensional impacts on daily functioning. The Chinese version of this instrument, translated from the original English version by Shuying Pu et al. (2021) [26], has demonstrated robust psychometric properties through validation studies, achieving exceptional reliability with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.920. The inventory comprises two clinically meaningful components: (1) a 22-item symptom severity module assessing both generic cancer-related symptoms (e.g., fatigue, pain, and nausea) and brain tumor-specific manifestations (e.g., visual disturbances, motor weakness, and seizures) over a one-week recall period, and (2) a six-domain functional interference module evaluating current impairments in affective, physical, and social functioning domains. Utilizing a standardized 0–10 numerical rating scale, the severity subscale specifically captures symptom intensity within the preceding 24 h, whereas the interference subscale reflects real-life functional limitations. A score of SD ≥ 4 indicates the presence of significant symptom distress. Higher composite scores on both subscales correlate with greater clinical severity, providing clinicians with quantifiable metrics for symptom management and rehabilitation planning in neuro-oncology care.

Comprehensive score for financial toxicity

The Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity (COST), a validated patient-reported outcome tool for assessing cancer-related financial burdens, was originally developed by De Souza et al. [27] in 2014. Translated from the original English version by Huihui Yu et al. (2017) [28], the Chinese version was linguistically and culturally adapted, demonstrating excellent psychometric properties with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.889, which indicates strong internal consistency and cross-cultural applicability. This 12-item instrument comprises three multi-item domains: (1) financial strain (4 items), (2) financial coping capacity (4 items), and (3) psychosocial impact (3 items), supplemented by a standalone global financial status item. Utilizing a 5-point Likert scale (0="completely disagree” to 4="completely agree”), the total score ranges from 0 to 44, with lower scores reflecting greater financial toxicity. Notably, a validated threshold score of ≤ 26 points has been established through ROC analyses to identify patients with clinically significant financial toxicity [27].

General self-efficacy scale

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), originally developed by Schwarzer’s research team [29] as a psychometrically robust instrument for measuring perceived coping competence, was cross-culturally adapted for Chinese populations by Caikang Wang et al. [30] through rigorous forward-backward translation protocols. The scale comprises 10 items employing a 4-point Likert scale, with each item scored from 1 to 4 points. The theoretical total score ranges from 10 to 40 points, and it is interpreted as follows: 1–10, low self-efficacy; 11–20 points, relatively low self-efficacy; 21–30 points, high self-efficacy; and 31–40 points, high self-efficacy. Psychometric validation studies of the Chinese GSES demonstrated satisfactory reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.87.

Medical coping modes questionnaire

The Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire (MCMQ), originally developed by Feifel’s team [14] in 1987, was cross-culturally adapted into a Chinese version by Shen Xiaohong’s research group [31]. This 20-item instrument employs a Likert 4-point scoring system (1–4), yielding a total score ranging from 20 to 80 across three dimensions—confrontation (8 items), avoidance (7 items), and resignation (5 items). Higher scores on each dimension indicate patients’ stronger propensity toward the corresponding coping strategy. Reliability analysis of the Chinese version demonstrated Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.69 for confrontation, 0.60 for avoidance, and 0.76 for resignation. To align with the research design, the coping modes were dichotomized: the confrontation dimension was classified as an active coping strategy, while the avoidance and resignation dimensions were merged to reflect passive coping characteristics, thereby enhancing the clinical interpretability of distinct coping patterns.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp). Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s independent t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as the median (interquartile range, IQR; P25–P75) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons performed using the chi-square test. Logistic regression analyses identified independent risk factors. A nomogram prediction model was developed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation), with calibration curves plotted to evaluate model calibration and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve calculated to assess predictive accuracy. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05.

Results

The general information of brain tumors patients

The study included 300 patients, with 142 males (47.33%) and 158 females (52.66%). The age range was 18–76 years (mean 48.51 ± 13.23). Regarding marital status, the study population was distributed as follows: 180 married (60%), 120 unmarried/divorced/widowed (40%). Education: 98 primary school or below (32.67%), 78 junior high school (26%), 59 senior high school or technical secondary school (19.66%), and 65 college or above (21.67%). Monthly income categories: <5,000RMB (141 cases, 47%), 5,000–10,000 RMB (74 cases, 24.67%), and ≥ 10,000RMB (85 cases, 28.33%). Payment methods: commercial insurance (75 cases, 25%), medical insurance (73 cases, 24.33%), new rural cooperative medical scheme (82 cases, 27.34%), and self-payment (70 cases, 23.33%). Disease duration: ≤1 month (84 cases, 28%), 1–6 months (53 cases, 17.67%), 6–12 months (66 cases, 22%), and > 12 months (97 cases, 32.33%). Employment status post-diagnosis: no impact on retirement age (215 cases, 71.67%), delayed retirement (7 cases, 2.33%), early retirement (5 cases, 1.67%), and unemployment/resignation (73 cases, 24.33%). KPS scores were: ≤30 (72 cases, 42.0%), 40–70 (88 cases, 29.33%), and 80–100 (140 cases, 46.67%). Among the 300 brain tumor patients included in this study, 252 cases (84.0%) experienced varying degrees of symptomatic distress. The mean overall SD score was 3.37 ± 2.59, with 152 patients (50.66%) meeting the criteria for high symptomatic distress. Analysis of clinical symptom distribution revealed that emotional distress, fatigue, and disturbed sleep were the most prevalent manifestations, followed sequentially by sadness, headache, drowsiness, dry mouth, lack of appetite, irritability, and numbness/tingling (Table 1).

Univariate analysis of SCD in brain tumor patients

Our study categorized 300 patients into high symptom distress (SD ≥ 4) and low symptom distress (SD < 4) groups based on the presence of significant symptom distress. Univariate analysis was performed to compare differences between the two groups and screen for potential predictive variables. The results revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in age, monthly income, payment method, disease duration, KPS, COST, GSES, and MCMQ between the two groups. These variables were identified as potential predictors for the high symptom distress group and were included in the predictive model (Table 1).

Discussion

The high-risk prediction model for SCD in patients with brain tumors demonstrates favorable predictive performance

This study employed ROC curves to evaluate the model’s predictive efficacy. The AUC is interpreted as follows: 0.5–0.7 indicates low predictive power, 0.7–0.8 represents moderate predictive power, 0.8–0.9 suggests good predictive power, and >0.9 reflects excellent predictive power [32]. The developed model achieved an AUC of 0.813, with a sensitivity of 0.717, specificity of 0.791, optimal cutoff value of 0.750, and Youden index of 0.508, indicating robust predictive performance. A calibration curve was generated to assess the model’s goodness of fit, revealing a slope close to 1, which signifies strong agreement between predicted and actual probabilities of high SD occurrence. These results demonstrate that the model exhibits satisfactory discrimination and calibration. The nomogram-based high SD prediction model translates complex regression equations into a visual tool, enabling clinicians to calculate the individualized probability of high SD for each patient with brain tumors through a simple scoring system, thereby enhancing clinical utility and accessibility.

Analysis of the predictors of SCD in brain tumor patients

In this study, 84.0% of brain tumor patients experienced SD, with 50.66% suffering from severe symptom burden. The symptom incidence rate reached 100%, with these symptoms primarily including emotional distress, fatigue, disturbed sleep, sadness, headache, drowsiness, dry mouth, lack of appetite, irritability, and numbness/tingling, which aligns with previous findings [6, 11, 16]. However, SD levels varied significantly among patients, possibly due to differences in disease progression, surgical approaches, chemotherapy regimens, financial toxicity, self-efficacy, medical coping modes, and sociodemographic characteristics [6–8, 11–13, 16–18].

This study demonstrated that financial burden is a significant predictor of symptom clusters in brain tumor patients. According to previous studies, patients with heightened subjective perceptions of economic pressure are more prone to developing high symptom burden profiles, with household monthly income showing a significant negative correlation with symptom cluster severity [6, 8, 33]. These findings align with previous multinational cohort studies. Surveillance data from the U.S. National Cancer Institute indicate that low-income cancer patients exhibit a 37.2% higher SD index compared to middle-high income groups, potentially mediated by social determinants such as disparities in health literacy, limited healthcare accessibility, and deficiencies in social support networks [34, 35]. Based on this evidence, we recommend implementing a precision stratified management strategy in clinical nursing practice: Conduct “symptom-economic dual-dimensional assessments” for economically vulnerable brain tumor patients, prioritizing the identification of individuals with gaps in health insurance literacy and insufficient social support systems. The intervention framework should integrate multidisciplinary resources, including (1) policy navigation: Systematically organize region-specific medical assistance policies and establish charity foundation referral pathways; (2) digital health tools: Develop symptom management applications integrated with pharmacoeconomic decision-support modules; (3) peer support networks: Create mutual aid alliances for brain tumor patients experiencing financial hardship to facilitate resource sharing; (4) continuity of care: Establish dynamic monitoring mechanisms for post-discharge financial burdens. By constructing a multidimensional support system that integrates “assessment-education-support-follow-up” as a cohesive framework, this approach aims to substantially enhance equity in healthcare delivery for this vulnerable population.

According to self-management theory, self-efficacy serves as an intrinsic driver for patients to implement effective and sustained symptom management behaviors [10].

Evidence-based studies confirmed that patients’ self-efficacy levels demonstrate dual clinical effects: showing significant positive correlations with symptom management adherence while reducing physio-psychological symptom burden by 42% [36]. The mechanisms operate through three pathways—① promoting proactive coping strategies (enhanced treatment adherence and increased self-care behaviors); ② improving mental health indicators (reduced anxiety/depression scale scores); ③ strengthening disease control beliefs (elevated self-management efficacy), thus establishing a “confidence-behavior-symptom relief” virtuous cycle. We recommend implementing a three-tier clinical intervention system: (1) standardized assessment: Integrate the GSES into routine admission evaluations and establish a dynamic monitoring database; (2) precision management: For the low-efficacy group (scoring at most the 50th percentile), deploy a tripartite “clinician-family-social worker” support program including cognitive behavioral training (twice weekly), family empathy support workshops (monthly sessions), and community resource navigation services; (3) systematic competency development: Create a “Symptom Management Capacity Enhancement Module” featuring three core components: symptom identification (AI-powered intelligent alert systems), medication decision-making (interactive pharmacoeconomic evaluation tools), and stress management (VR relaxation training). This system comprises an integrated framework to optimize intervention continuity and clinical outcomes.

Our study revealed that patients employing passive coping strategies are more likely to fall into high-risk groups for SD, a finding consistent with previous research [37, 38]. Such maladaptive responses may trigger self-doubt, frustration, and anger in patients, heightening susceptibility to psychiatric comorbidities like depression while compromising their capacity for proactive symptom cluster self-management. Targeted nursing interventions should prioritize delivering positive psychological reinforcement, providing informational and psychological support, and implementing strategies to bolster self-confidence. These approaches can foster positive emotional states, mitigate stress perception, and ultimately facilitate effective symptom cluster management in this vulnerable population [13, 14].

Our study instituted that older patients are more prone to symptom distress. With aging, physiological functions (e.g., nervous and immune systems) gradually decline, leading to reduced tolerance for tumor-related symptoms. Additionally, side effects from treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy are more likely to exacerbate symptoms [39]. Furthermore, older adults are more susceptible to psychological distress, such as depression and anxiety, which further intensifies symptom burden [40]. Prolonged duration of illness was also identified as a risk factor for symptom cluster distress, consistent with previous studies [41, 42]. Over time, patients experience deterioration in physical function, while emotional and social well-being are significantly impacted, increasing vulnerability to symptom clusters [42]. These findings highlight the need for healthcare providers to enhance attention and care for elderly brain tumor patients and those with long-term illness, promptly identifying physical and psychological issues, addressing their physiological and psychological needs, and reducing the risk of symptom cluster distress.

Our study indicated that a KPS score ≥ 80 serves as a protective factor against SD, consistent with previous research [19, 43, 44]. Patients with KPS ≥ 80 demonstrate better tolerance to treatment side effects and actively engage in rehabilitation training [43]. They are also more capable of mobilizing social support systems and maintaining positive psychological states [19]. Research further showed that patients with higher KPS scores enjoy better quality of life, where favorable physical-mental conditions and symptom management form a virtuous cycle, ultimately improving overall health outcomes [44]. This suggests that nursing staff should emphasize functional status assessments and develop personalized intervention plans accordingly.

Self-payment brain tumor patients face increased vulnerability to SD, as financial burdens heighten the risk of anxiety and depression—psychological issues that exacerbate the symptom burden [45, 46]. Economic constraints may lead patients to opt for low-cost rather than high-quality medical services, or even reduce or discontinue treatments, resulting in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes and poor symptom control. These findings highlight the need for healthcare providers to enhance attention toward self-payment brain tumor patients, offering social support to alleviate financial pressures and improve care continuity.

Clinical practice

In terms of the concept of symptom management, this study attempts to shift the thinking of symptom management for brain tumor patients from generalized management of the entire population to precise management of high-risk groups, so as to assist in the optimization of the allocation of future medical care resources. At the same time, it aims to improve patients’ experience and quality of life while saving care costs. The nomogram model constructed in this study was utilized to evaluate the impact of various factors on SCD in brain tumor patients and calculate the occurrence probability of such distress. When the predicted probability ≥ 0.750, it indicates a high risk of SCD in brain tumor patients, and nursing staff should enhance their attention to these patients and implement targeted management promptly. When the predicted probability approaches the critical threshold, nursing professionals should remain vigilant and develop proactive intervention plans. As SCD constitutes a dynamic process, nursing teams should conduct long-term dynamic tracking and assessment of brain tumor patients to identify high-risk individuals at an early stage. Preventive management measures should be promptly implemented to improve the effectiveness of symptom management.

Based on multiple risk factors (such as age, payment method, disease duration, KPS, financial toxicity, self-efficacy, medical coping modes, etc.), it is recommended to construct an individualized risk assessment system, prioritize the allocation of refined resources (such as psychological support and symptom education) for high-risk groups, and design multidimensional intervention strategies. Specifically, integrate social support programs for the economic burden, enhance patients’ self-management ability through empowerment education, and customize personalized support plans in combination with the medical coping model. Through precise stratified management, optimize the allocation of resources, reduce unnecessary costs, and use the saved resources for intensive interventions for high-risk groups (such as extended home care services), thus balancing the improvement of patients’ quality of life and the sustainability of the medical system. In addition, it is necessary to promote the construction of an interdisciplinary collaboration network, integrate the efforts of multiple parties such as nursing, medical care, and social work, implement the comprehensive “social-psychological-biomedical” care model, and ultimately provide a replicable and precise path for optimizing oncology nursing practice and global chronic disease management.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the sampling was restricted to brain tumor patients from a single tertiary cancer hospital in Guangdong Province, which limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Future multi-center studies across diverse regions in China are needed to validate the results of this study. Second, the small sample size across specific brain tumor subtypes may introduce potential confounding effects. Subsequent research should employ larger cohorts with refined tumor classification (e.g., glioma, meningioma, and metastatic tumors) to optimize the predictive model. Additionally, the exclusion of disease-related variables due to incomplete data collection highlights the need for enhanced data acquisition protocols. Future investigations will implement structured data collection frameworks and longitudinal tracking to minimize missing values and strengthen analytical rigor. Finally, while internal validation demonstrated model reliability, external validation using independent cohorts from different geographic or institutional settings is essential to confirm the model’s robustness and clinical applicability.

Conclusions

The risk prediction model for SCD in brain tumor patients constructed in this study has demonstrated good prediction accuracy, consistency, and favorable clinical practicability. It can be used for the early identification of high-risk patients with self-reported SCD among brain tumor patients. Moreover, factors such as self-efficacy, financial burden, and coping styles are important predictors. In the future, based on our work, the model will be optimized through the continuous input of big data, and a risk early warning system for SD in brain tumor patients, as well as decision-making intervention plans, will be developed to facilitate the precise symptom management of brain tumor patients.
