Hierarchically assembled curcumin-vitamin E-zinc thermo-gel for multi-synergistic prevention and treatment of radiation-induced oral mucositis
Authors: Juan Duan, Muxi Chen, Mengyan Wang, Rui He, Ke Li, Lei Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation-induced oral mucositis (RIOM) substantially compromises the efficacy of cancer treatment and patient survival, while current therapeutic approaches exhibit limited efficacy in addressing its multifactorial pathogenesis. Curcumin possesses multi-targeted pharmacological properties relevant to RIOM, including antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and mucosal repair effects, rendering it an ideal therapeutic agent. This study developed a curcumin-based intelligent-responsive integrated therapeutic platform (Cur-VE-Zn@gel) via a hierarchical assembly strategy. Specifically, curcumin was covalently bound to vitamin E to form the active center, while its coordination with Zn2+ facilitates self-assembly into stable polymeric structures. This design not only significantly enhanced the biosecurity of curcumin but also amplified its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and tissue repair capabilities, while exhibited stable DNA repair characteristics, thus establishing a comprehensive radioprotective barrier. This bioactive complex was further incorporated into an adhesive thermosensitive hydrogel consisting of chitosan and poloxamer, which enabled targeted delivery to oral mucosa and bypassed the low systemic bioavailability of curcumin. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrated that the Cur-VE-Zn@gel platform effectively modulated DNA damage repair, oxidative stress, inflammatory cascades, and epithelial regeneration, thereby achieving synergistic multi-targeted therapy for the suppression of RIOM. Ultimately, this biomaterial-based strategy successfully addresses the key pharmacological limitations of curcumin through structural modification and targeted delivery, providing a highly promising, safe, and effective approach for the clinically challenging management of RIOM.Keywords: Radiation-induced oral mucositis, Curcumin, Vitamin E (VE), Zinc (zn), Thermosensitive hydrogel

Introduction

Radiation-induced oral mucositis (RIOM) remains a dose-limiting inflammatory complication characterized by mucosal injury in cancer patients undergoing chemoradiotherapy. Over 70 % of patients undergoing hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) develop RIOM [1], while its incidence reaches approximately 100 % of head and neck cancer (HNC) patients receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy [2,3]. Clinically, RIOM is defined by oral mucosal ulceration, erosion, hemorrhage, and necrosis [4]; it often induces severe pain that forces patients to interrupt or discontinue critical cancer treatment [5], directly compromising overall therapeutic efficacy and patient survival rates. Notably, up to 8 % of HNC patients may develop chronic, painful mucosal lesions post-radiotherapy [3,6], resulting in long-term oral dysfunction and severely impairing quality of life. Sonis’ five-stage model [7] offers a conceptual framework for elucidating the pathogenesis of RIOM, emphasizing that the development of RIOM involves interrelated dynamic stages such as DNA damage, oxidative burst, dysregulated inflammatory cascades, microbial dysbiosis, and impaired tissue repair. Despite this mechanistic insight, current clinical management strategies such as growth factors, antimicrobial mouthwashes, analgesics, and investigational agents [4,[8], [9], [10], [11]] offer limited efficacy for alleviating RIOM. Neither single-agent therapies nor interventions targeting isolated pathways can comprehensively address the interconnected pathological processes underlying RIOM. Effective therapeutic strategies must therefore simultaneously modulate oxidative stress, suppress inflammation, regulate microbiota, and promote mucosal repair. Consequently, there is an urgent and well-recognized clinical need to develop a novel integrated therapeutic platform, one that can concurrently target multiple key pathological mechanisms of RIOM while enabling precise drug delivery to the lesion site (see Scheme 1).

Synthesis of curcumin-based complex and radiation protection strategies for oral mucosa.

Curcumin, the primary bioactive component of Curcuma longa L. (turmeric) [12,13], exhibits robust multi-targeted pharmacological characteristics relevant to RIOM, including potent antioxidant [14,15], anti-inflammatory [16,17], antimicrobial [12], and wound healing activities [18,19]. Its potential to modulate multiple pathways associated with Sonis’ five-stage model theoretically renders it a promising therapeutic candidate, and numerous studies support its efficacy in mitigating RIOM [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. However, clinical translation of curcumin is hindered by critical limitations: extremely poor aqueous solubility, rapid systemic clearance, and substantial hepatic first-pass metabolism, resulting in negligible systemic bioavailability following oral administration [25]. Furthermore, some in vitro studies have demonstrated that while curcumin reduces the viability of tumor cells, it also impairs the viability of normal (non-cancerous) cells, regardless of their tissue origin [12,26,27]. For instance, Rujirachotiwat et al. found that 30 μM curcumin significantly reduced the viability of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) [28]. Graboeska et al. reported that 2.5 μM and 5 μM curcumin inhibited the proliferation of endothelial cells (ECs) and human vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), with 10 μM curcumin exerting cytotoxic effects [29]. Additionally, Lin et al. observed that curcumin facilitates cellular ROS scavenging at low concentrations (5–10 μM); however, 20 μM curcumin may elevate intracellular ROS levels, thereby suppressing cellular activity [30,31]. Collectively, these findings indicate that curcumin exhibits a biphasic dose-response within a relatively narrow concentration window: it enhances cellular function at low concentrations, while inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, promoting ROS release, and triggering apoptosis at high concentrations.

To unlock the full therapeutic potential of curcumin against RIOM and address its inherent limitations, thereby enhancing clinical applicability, this study innovatively proposes an intelligent responsive integrated therapeutic platform via a hierarchical assembly strategy, designed to improve biosafety and biological efficacy. First, curcumin was covalently conjugated to vitamin E (VE), a nutrient also beneficial for RIOM [[32], [33], [34]], to form a curcumin-VE complex, which serves as the core active unit and structural framework. Next, zinc ions (Zn2+) act as “molecular glue”, driving self-assembly via coordination with the β-diketone moieties of Cur-VE units [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]] to form a stable curcumin-VE-Zn (Cur-VE-Zn) polymer complex. This complex further amplifies curcumin's antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-repairing bioactivities while establishing a multifunctional synergistic therapeutic microenvironment to target multiple pathological stages of RIOM. Additionally, to address poor systemic bioavailability and enable targeted, sustained drug delivery to mucosal lesions, a local oral mucosal intervention strategy was adopted: the Cur-VE-Zn complex was further incorporated into a hydrogel system with excellent biocompatibility, mucoadhesion, and thermosensitivity. Thermosensitive hydrogels based on poloxamer P407 exhibit well-defined temperature-responsive characteristics, facilitating a reversible sol-gel transition within a physiologically relevant temperature range [40,41]. The incorporation of P188 has been demonstrated to extend and stabilize the phase transition profile of P407, thereby improving its thermal stability and performance consistency under biological conditions [42]. Moreover, the integration of chitosan significantly enhances the mechanical integrity and mucoadhesive capacity of the poloxamer-based hydrogel system, making it more suitable for biomedical applications such as wound healing or drug delivery [43]. Therefore, the intelligent P407/P188/Cs hydrogel, based on poloxamer (P407/P188) and chitosan (Cs), undergoes rapid sol-gel phase transition at physiological temperature and demonstrates strong mucoadhesive properties. This characteristic facilitates easy administration and enables prolonged retention on the oral mucosa despite salivary flow and oral movements.

This study describes the development and evaluation of a novel integrated therapeutic platform. With the “molecular synergistic unit” (Cur-VE-Zn complex) as the cargo and a stimuli-responsive hydrogel as the carrier, the Cur-VE-Zn@gel platform effectively overcomes the clinical limitation of curcumin through precise structural modification and localized delivery. This work offers robust support for developing efficient, translatable biomaterial-based therapies for RIOM.

Materials and methods

Main materials

Curcumin was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. α-Tocopheryl succinate (VE-SA), Poloxamer F127, and Poloxamer F68 were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) and 4-Dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) were acquired from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Zinc acetate dihydrate and chitosan were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of Cur-VE-Zn complex

Accurately weigh VE-SA (1 mmol, 0.5308 g) and DCC (2 mmol, 0.4127 g), then mix and dissolve the solids in dichloromethane (DCM, 45 mL) under a nitrogen atmosphere at room temperature for 30 min. Separately, curcumin (1 mmol, 0.368 g) and DMAP (0.5 mmol, 0.061 g) were dissolved in DCM (5 mL) using ultrasound, and the resulting solution was slowly added to the previously prepared mixture under stirring. React at 4 °C under nitrogen protection for 7 h, then filter. The filtrate is purified by silica gel column chromatography (petroleum ether: ethyl acetate = 3:1) and evaporated to obtain Cur-VE dry powder. Dissolve Cur-VE dry powder (4.4g) in anhydrous ethanol(300 mL) and zinc acetate dihydrate (2.2g) in anhydrous ethanol (100 mL) under nitrogen, mix at 60 °C, and react for 1.5 h until stable precipitation forms. The reaction is then stopped, and the Cur-VE-Zn complex is obtained after vacuum drying.

Synthesis of Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel

Ultrasonication was employed to disperse Tween 80 (100 μL) in deionized water (4650 μL). Subsequently, each of curcumin (20 mg) and the Cur - VE - Zn complex (20 mg) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 250 μL). Both the two solutions were then slowly added to the deionized water to prepare the curcumin and Cur - VE - Zn complex formulations. To the above formulation, glacial acetic acid (28.6 μL) was added, and the volume was fixed to 5 mL to reach a concentration of glacial acetic acid of 0.1 mol L−1. Dissolve a certain amount of chitosan in the formulation, and stir magnetically at room temperature for 1 h to obtain a 0.5 % chitosan solution. Dissolve Poloxamer F127 and F68 in a 20:5 mass ratio in the chitosan solution, and allow to swell at 4 °C for 24 h to obtain clear and viscous curcumin (Cur@gel) and Cur-VE-Zn gel (Cur-VE-Zn@gel). Prepare a blank gel with pure water as a control (gel).

Characterization of Cur-VE-Zn complex

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet Nexus670, USA) was used to characterize the characteristic absorption peaks and functional groups. Analyze the binding energy and valence states of the Cur-VE-Zn complex using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo ESCALAB Xi+, USA). Observe the structure and elemental distribution of the Cur-VE-Zn complex using Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS, Zeiss Sigma 300, Germany and Oxford UlLTIM MAX 40, UK) mapping [44].

Rheological testing of gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel

Perform rheological analysis of the thermosensitive gels using a rheometer (NETZSCH Kinexus Lab+, Germany) to evaluate the gelation temperature [45]. Place 1 mL of gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel samples in the rheometer, set a constant frequency of 1 Hz, maintain a strain value of 0.1 %, and heat from 15 to 40 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min. Rheological properties were measured under specified conditions. Storage modulus (G’, Pa) and loss modulus (G”, Pa) were plotted as functions of temperature to generate modulus/complex viscosity–temperature curves, which were utilized to evaluate the gelation temperature of the hydrogels.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel

The prepared gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel were frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently subjected to vacuum freeze - drying to yield lyophilized samples. The samples were then fractured, sputter - coated with gold, and their internal structure and microscopic morphology were observed using SEM (Zeiss Sigma 300, Germany) at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV [46].

Adhesion of gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel

Measure the adhesion of gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel using a lap shear test [47]. Remove hair and oil from fresh pig skin and cut into 60 mm × 15 mm pieces. Apply hydrogel (100 μL) evenly to the surface of two pig skin pieces, covering an area of 15 mm × 15 mm. The samples were equilibrated at 37 °C for 1 h, after which their adhesion was measured using a universal testing machine (zwickLine Z2.5 TN+, Germany). To simulate the moist oral environment, pig skin specimens of uniform size were immersed in artificial saliva for 1 h. Subsequently, the adhesion measurement procedure was repeated to assess the adhesion strength under moist conditions.

In vitro studies

HaCaT cell: The human keratinocyte cell line (HaCaT) was obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere. The cell line was routinely authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Cell Viability Assay: Seed HaCaT cells in a 96-well plate. Co-culture the cells with curcumin and Cur-VE-Zn complex at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 200 μM for 24 and 48 h, respectively. After incubation, incubate with Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Japan) solution at 37 °C for 1–2 h. Measure the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Multiskan FC1510, USA) to determine cell viability [48].

Scratch Assay: Seed HaCaT cells in a 6-well plate and incubate for 24 h. After X-ray irradiation, the cells were co-cultured with 10 μM curcumin and Cur-VE-Zn complex for 24 h. Scratch the cells with a 1 mL pipette tip and culture in DMEM for 24 h. The width of the scratch was observed at 0, 12, and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus IX83, Japan). Quantitative analysis of the scratch area was carried out using Image-J(FIJI) [48].

Transwell Assay: The cells were irradiated and treated in the same manner as the previous experiment. Digest and resuspend the cells in DMEM to obtain a cell suspension at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/mL. Use a Transwell chamber to divide the 24-well plate into upper and lower chambers. A 200-μL aliquot of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while complete medium (500 μL) was introduced into the lower chamber. After a 24-h incubation period, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cell migration was then observed under an inverted microscope (Olympus IX83, Japan), and the number of migrated cells was recorded [48].

γ-H2AX and Nrf2 Expression: The cells were irradiated and treated in the same manner as the previous experiment. Cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton-X100 at room temperature, with a blocking buffer for 1 h. The cells were incubated with monoclonal antibodies against γ-H2AX and Nrf2 (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. Wuhan, China), followed by iFluor™ 488 Conjugated Goat anti-rabbit IgG polyclonal antibody (Huaanbio Technology Co., Ltd. Shandong, China). Fluorescence images were captured via a high-throughput multiparameter cell dynamic analysis system, and quantitative analysis was carried out on these images.

ROS Level Measurement: The cells were irradiated and treated in the same manner as the previous experiment. Digest, centrifuge, and wash the cells, then incubate with DCFH-DA dilution at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Measure ROS levels using flow cytometry [48].

Inflammatory Cytokine Levels: The cells were irradiated and treated in the same manner as the previous experiment. Measure IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels using an ELISA kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Wuhan, China), and determine the absorbance at 450 nm [48].

In vivo studies

Experimental Animals: The protocol of animal experiments has been reviewed by the experimental animal ethics committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University and conformed to the ethical requirements for animals, ethics record number: 20240618001. SPF grade 8-week-old male BALB/C mice weighing 21–23 g were purchased from Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China), animal production license number: SCXK (Sichuan) 2020-030. The mice were housed at the Animal Experiment Center of West China School of Basic Medical Sciences & Forensic Medicine, Sichuan University, and randomly divided into Normal, X-ray, X-ray + gel, X-ray + Cur@gel, and X-ray + Cur-VE-Zn@gel groups, with 10 mice in each group.

Establishment of Animal Model: The RIOM model was established using a single high-dose radiation. After 7 days of acclimatization, the mice were anesthetized with 0.3 % sodium pentobarbital (3.5 mL kg−1 body weight) and fixed in a supine position in a lead box, exposing the head and neck. The rest of the body was shielded with 5 mm thick lead plates to isolate ionizing radiation. Local head and neck radiotherapy was administered to the mice using an X-RAY small animal precision irradiator (Radsource,USA) at a dose of 25 Gy [48].

Intervention Methods: The Normal and X-ray groups were given regular feed and water without any additional treatment. In addition to being provided with regular feed and water, the mice in the X-ray + gel, X-ray + Cur@gel, and X-ray + Cur-VE-Zn@gel groups received hydrogel (100 μL) twice daily at consistent time points.

Ulcer Area in Mouse Tongue Tissue: The tongue tissue was excised, cleaned with 1 % acetic acid, stained with 1 % toluidine blue for 5 min, and washed with 1 % acetic acid. The ulcer area, mucositis area, and total tongue area were analyzed using Image-J(FIJI) [48].

Histopathological evaluation and Mucosal Epithelial Thickness: Following toluidine blue staining, the tongue tissue was rinsed twice with phosphate - buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, it was fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24–48 h and then sectioned sagittally. The tissue specimens underwent dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning procedures. After that, the sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin. photos from all groups were analyzed by calibrated and blinded oral medicine professional based on the method described by Lima et al. [49,50]. Microscopic observation (Leica DM2000, Germany) was carried out to measure the thickness of the mucosal epithelium.

TUNEL Assay in Mouse Tongue Tissue: After fixation, dehydration, embedding, and sectioning, the tongue tissue was treated with Proteinase K working solution at 37 °C. Fifty microliters of the TUNEL reaction mixture was added to the tissue sections. The sections were then incubated in a dark chamber at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining was performed, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. After that, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Microscopic observation (P250 FLASH, 3dhistech Ltd., Hungary) was carried out, and images were captured for further analysis [44].

Expression of CD31, γ-H2AX, 8-OHDG, and Nrf2 in Mouse Tongue Tissue: After fixation, dehydration, embedding, sectioning, dewaxing, and antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase was blocked. Bovine serum albumin was added for blocking at room temperature for 20 min. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with diluted primary antibodies (Nrf2: antibody dilution = 1:200, γ-H2AX: antibody dilution = 1:200, 8-OHDG: antibody dilution = 1:200, CD31: antibody dilution = 1:200) at 4 °C overnight. After rewarming, wash with PBS, incubate with secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min, wash with PBS, and perform DAB staining. Counterstain the nuclei with hematoxylin, dehydrate, clear, and mount. Observe under a microscope, randomly select 3 fields per section for imaging, and analyze the number of positive cells using Image-J(FIJI) [51].

Measurement of SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, and MDA in Mouse Tongue Tissue: Tongue tissues were homogenized in ice-cold saline and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The activities of SOD, CAT, GSH-Px and the level of MDA in the supernatants were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturers' protocols. Specifically, hydroxylamine, ammonium molybdate, colorimetric, and thiobarbituric acid methods were employed, respectively [51]. All results were normalized to the total protein content measured by a BCA protein assay kit.

Proteomics: Proteins were reduced (10 mM TCEP, 37 °C, 60 min), alkylated (40 mM IAM, RT, 40 min), and digested overnight with trypsin (1:50, 37 °C). Peptides were desalted (HLB columns), dried, and quantified (NanoDrop). LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC-Astral system (DIA mode, 1.5 kV). Data were processed (Spectronaut™ 18/DIA-NN; FDR ≤0.01, XIC ≤75 ppm) with peak area-based quantification. Differentially expressed proteins (P < 0.05, FC > 1.2 or <0.83) were annotated via GO/KEGG.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 26.0 software. Continuous data with normal distribution was presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For datasets with homogeneous variance, t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed, followed by Tukey's method used for multiple comparisons. For data with heterogeneous variance, Welch's test was applied, followed by Games-Howell's method for multiple comparisons. Repeated measures ANOVA was used for repeated measurement data. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided alpha level of 0.05.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the Cur-VE-Zn complex

The Cur-VE core unit was synthesized via esterification of curcumin with D-α-Tocopherol Succinate (VE-SA) using a DCC/DMAP catalytic system. Simultaneously, zinc ions coordinated with the β-diketone moiety of curcumin, resulting in the formation of the Cur-VE-Zn complex [39,[39], [39], [40], [41],52,53](Figure S1). The result of Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) (Fig. 1A) indicated that the Cur-VE-Zn complex possesses characteristic absorption peaks of both the Cur-Zn and Cur-VE complexes. Infrared spectral analysis revealed characteristic red shifts in the conjugated C=O and C=C stretching vibrations of curcumin (originally at 1628 and 1598 cm−1) to1622/1597 cm−1 in the Cur-Zn complex and 1618/1589 cm−1 in Cur-VE-Zn complex [35,39,54]. Notably, these shifts were absent in the Cur-VE complex, indicating an interaction between zinc ions and the β-diketone moiety of curcumin in the complexes. Additionally, the absorption peak of the Cur-VE-Zn complex at 1755 cm−1 attributed to the ester group and absorption peaks at 2926 and 2846 cm−1 belonged to symmetric and asymmetric -CH2 stretches. These vibrational modes were also present in the Cur-VE complex and were confirmed to originate from the vitamin E succinate (VE-SA) moiety, demonstrating the presence of VE-SA in the complex [53,55,56]. X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) revealed characteristic binding energy peaks for C 1s (284.78 eV), O 1s (531.06 eV), and Zn 2p (1022.72 eV) (Fig. 1B), suggesting the presence of carbon (C), oxygen (O), and zinc (Zn) elements in the curcumin-VE-Zn complex. The subpeak of O 1s at 531.06eV was attributed to Zn-O, while which at 532.15eV belonged to C=O (Figure S2), and the high-resolution Zn 2p spectrum fitted two spin-orbit binding energy characteristic peaks for Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2 at 1022.72 eV and 1045.66 eV (Fig. 1C). The fine spectra of O 1s and Zn 2p suggested that Zn2+ exists in the complex [57,58], potentially stabilized in an oxidized state by accepting lone pair electrons from the O in the curcumin ligand [59,60]. In addition, the elemental distribution of C, O, and Zn in the Cur-VE-Zn complexes was characterized through energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Figure S3). All the aforementioned results demonstrate the successful synthesis of the curcumin-VE-Zn complex.

Cell viability

The biosafety of curcumin and the Cur-VE-Zn complex was evaluated in vitro using human immortalized keratinocytes (HaCaT) as a model for oral mucosal epithelial cells. Cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with varying concentration gradients of curcumin, curcumin-VE-Zn complex, and the intermediate products: curcumin-Zn complex and curcumin-VE complex, followed by 24- and 48-h incubation periods. The results of Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (Fig. 2A) revealed that 10 μM curcumin exhibited no significant effect on HaCaT cell viability within 24 h. However, curcumin at concentrations ranging from 20 to 200 μM markedly suppressed cellular activity after 24 h, with this inhibitory effect becoming more pronounced following 48 h of exposure. The results were consistent with previous findings [30,61], further substantiating the cytotoxic effects of high-dose curcumin on HaCaT cells. At 10 μM of Cur-Zn exhibited increased cytotoxicity compared to free curcumin, demonstrating that zinc ion coordination enhances the inhibitory effect of curcumin. Conversely, covalent conjugation with vitamin E (VE) significantly reduced cytotoxicity, with 20 μM Cur-VE complex promoting cell proliferation and Cur-VE-Zn further enhancing this effect on cell viability. The IC50 values for Cur, Cur-Zn, Cur-VE, and Cur-VE-Zn were determined to be 18.4 μM, 13.91 μM, 41.27 μM, and 50.04 μM, respectively (Figure S4). These data indicate that Zn2+ coordination potentiates the inhibitory potency of curcumin against HaCaT cells, while the incorporation of VE elevated the IC50 value, indicating a reduced inhibitory effect and thus widening the concentration range over which curcumin exerts its bidirectional regulatory activity. This effect likely results from the ability of VE to maintain membrane integrity and promote cellular homeostasis [32,33].

The Cur-VE-Zn complex improves cell migration and anti-inflammatory

To determine the in vitro radioprotective properties of curcumin and the Cur-VE-Zn complex, we intervened on post-irradiated HaCaT cells at the “safe dose” (10 μM) of curcumin. A single 20 Gy X-ray dose was administered to HaCaT cells to simulate a scenario of oral mucosa suffering from a high-dose radiation injury, with subsequent assessment of cellular migration capacity through both scratch wound healing and Transwell migration assays. In the scratch assay (Fig. 2B and C), the healing rates of cell scratches at 12 and 24 h showed consistent trends that radiation inhibiting cell migration. Contrary to expectations, curcumin failed to ameliorate the impaired cell migration induced by X-ray irradiation. Rather, an exacerbation of radiation-induced suppression of HaCaT cell migratory capacity was observed upon curcumin treatment. The Cur-VE-Zn complex restored the migration ability of irradiated HaCaT cells. The Transwell assay (Fig. 2D and E) further demonstrated the Cur-VE-Zn complex's capability to improve cell migration ability after radiation. In epithelial tissue repair, re-epithelialization serves as a critical process for restoring structural integrity and physiological function. This reparative mechanism depends on the coordinated migration of epithelial cells to the injury site, followed by their proliferation and subsequent differentiation into functionally specialized cell types, ultimately reestablishing polarized, fully functional epithelial tissue [[62], [63], [64]]. The performance of the Cur-VE-Zn complex in cell migration assays confirmed its potential superiority over free curcumin in improving oral mucosal re-epithelialization after radiation damage.

RIOM, as a model of inflammatory injury disease, is characterized by the release of abundant inflammatory factors under radiation exposure. These factors exacerbate tissue damage and destruction through positive feedback mechanisms, ultimately leading to the formation of ulcers. Therefore, the concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines in HaCaT cells following radiation exposure and subsequent treatment with either curcumin or the Cur-VE-Zn complex were quantitatively assessed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Fig. 2F–H). The results indicated that the inhibitory effect of Cur-VE-Zn on radiation-induced upregulated IL-6 was not as significant as that of free curcumin. However, for the level of IL-β, Cur-VE-Zn demonstrated a more superior improvement effect. Notably,10 μM curcumin promoted the level of TNF-α in HaCaT cells after radiation.

In vitro DNA damage repair and antioxidant capacity of the Cur-VE-Zn complex

Among the five stages of RIOM, the initial stage acts as the “initiator” of the whole dynamic process. X-rays can induce DNA damage in the instant of contact with the organism, and at the same time release excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) to mediate oxidative stress, which triggers a biological cascade reaction within a few seconds and enters the phase of primary damage, ultimately leading to cell damage and apoptosis [65]. At this stage, while substantial biological damage occurs at the tissue level, the mucosa maintains an absence of clinically detectable lesion [3]. Therefore, mitigation of radiation-induced DNA damage and oxidative stress represents a critical therapeutic strategy for preventing RIOM initiation or progression. Consequently, this study systematically evaluated the radioprotective effects of both curcumin monomer and the Cur-VE-Zn complex on X-ray irradiated HaCaT cells, with particular emphasis on their modulation of DNA damage and oxidative stress.

The phosphorylated histone variant γ-H2AX, a well-established biomarker of DNA double-strand breaks, was quantitatively analyzed in HaCaT cell nuclei using immunofluorescence microscopy. This analysis evaluated temporal patterns of γ-H2AX expression at 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-X-ray irradiation to evaluate radioprotective effects mediated by curcumin and the Cur-VE-Zn complex (Fig. 3A–D). Compared with the normal group, X-ray exposure significantly induced γ-H2AX foci formation in HaCaT cells, serving as molecular evidence of radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks, but the damage from a single irradiation didn't appear to accumulate over time, and the expression of γ-H2AX at 24 h in the nuclei of all groups was lower than at 6 h. The level of γ-H2AX in the curcumin group was lower than that in the radiation group at 6 h and 12 h time points, whereas seemed to be higher than that in the X-ray group after 24 h of intervention, suggesting that the effect of curcumin in alleviating radiation-induced DNA damage is not stable and may exacerbate DNA damage at 24 h. The Cur-VE-Zn complex demonstrated excellent and stable performance in repairing DNA damage during our experiment. The content of γ-H2AX in HaCaT cell nuclei was significantly lower than that in the radiation group at 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h, indicating that the Cur-VE-Zn complex does not cause DNA damage to cells and can reverse DNA damage induced by X-rays. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant radiation-induced increase in intracellular ROS levels in HaCaT cells. We found that curcumin did not markedly reduce radiation-induced ROS levels, while Cur-VE-Zn exhibited significantly enhanced ROS-scavenging capacity, resulting in pronouncedly decreased intracellular ROS levels compared to those in the curcumin group (Fig. 3E and G). Additionally, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) expression analysis (Fig. 3F and H) revealed that both curcumin and Cur-VE-Zn significantly upregulated radiation-suppressed Nrf2 expression. Previous studies have established that curcumin exerts antioxidant effects through direct free radical neutralization or Nrf2 pathway modulation. However, its effect on ROS levels is biphasic [31,66,67]: at low concentrations, curcumin induces low levels of ROS that promote conformational modification of Keap1 or directly interact with Keap1, facilitating Nrf2 nuclear translocation and binding to the antioxidant response element (ARE) on DNA, activating the endogenous antioxidant system to eliminate ROS. In contrast, at high concentrations, curcumin induces mitochondrial dysfunction, disrupts the electron transport chain (ETC), and generates ROS that exceeds the scavenging capacity of the antioxidant system it induces. This biphasic effect explains why 10 μM curcumin, despite significantly enhancing Nrf2 expression, failed to counteract radiation-induced ROS generation. In contrast, the Cur-VE-Zn complex substantially scavenged ROS, indicating that its potent antioxidant activity not solely through the pathway regulating Nrf2 expression.

Notably, the Cur-VE complex exhibited comparable cytotoxic amelioration effects to the Cur-VE-Zn complex. Therefore, we measured outcomes including cell migration, DNA damage repair, ROS levels, and inflammatory factor levels following incubation with the Cur-VE complex in an in vitro radiation model. We found that the Cur-VE complex failed to significantly mitigate radiation-induced ROS elevation or Nrf2 expression suppression (Figure S5C, D, and F), exhibited a reduced antioxidant capacity compared to curcumin. this may be attributed to the covalent modification occupying one of the active sites of curcumin responsible for countering oxidative stress [68]. However, it exhibited superior efficacy relative to free curcumin in facilitating DNA repair (Figure S5A, E), enhancing cell migration (Figure S6), and suppressing inflammation (Figure S7), though its effectiveness remained inferior to the Cur-VE-Zn complex. Research has indicated that metal-phenolic networks can enhance the biological activity of polyphenols, with curcumin-metal complexes formed through the conjugation of curcumin with metal ions significantly augmenting the biological activity of curcumin, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and antiviral activities [35,37]. Consequently, we posit that during the radioprotective action of Cur-VE-Zn, the covalent conjugation between curcumin and VE serves as the pivotal factor in enhancing the biosafety of free curcumin, and the resulting Cur - VE complex constitutes a fundamental active unit and structural scaffold with excellent biosafety. Meanwhile, the introduction of zinc ions, through the “polyphenol-metal conjugation effect”, comprehensively elevates the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, DNA damage repair, and cell migration regulatory capabilities of the Cur - VE complex.

In vitro experiments provided preliminary validation of the hypothesis that the synthesized Cur-VE-Zn complex possesses superior biocompatibility relative to curcumin monomer, as evidenced by significantly reduced IC50. Meanwhile, HaCaT cells treated with the Cur-VE-Zn complex after X-ray irradiation demonstrated better DNA damage repair, ROS elimination, inflammation suppression, and migration capabilities. These results suggest that the Cur-VE-Zn complex can alleviate the dose threshold associated with curcumin's bidirectional effects and possesses superior radioprotective properties.

Characterizations and in vivo biosafety assessment of hydrogels

Inspired by the aforementioned results, the radioprotective efficacy of curcumin monomers and the Cur-VE-Zn complex was systematically evaluated using a RIOM animal model. The oral bioavailability of curcumin is severely limited due to its poor solubility, instability in gastric acid, a significant first-pass effect and rapid metabolism. To overcome these limitations, various advanced delivery systems have been investigated, including liposomal/nanoparticulate curcumin, cyclodextrin complexes, and mucoadhesive films or gels. Our study bypasses these drawbacks by employing an in situ oral mucosal delivery approach. To achieve targeted delivery of the Cur-VE-Zn complex to the oral mucosa, increase the contact time between the complex and the oral mucosa, and enhance the therapeutic effect, we designed and synthesized a thermosensitive hydrogel based on poloxamer and chitosan, loaded with pure water, curcumin, and Cur-VE-Zn complex to form the blank gel (gel), curcumin gel (Cur@gel), and Cur-VE-Zn complex gel (Cur-VE-Zn@gel).

The temperature responsiveness of the hydrogel was observed using the 45° tilting method (Fig. 1D). At low temperatures (4 °C), the hydrogel exhibited an unstable fluid state, while at physiological temperature (37 °C), it formed a stable solid state. This property allows the hydrogel to be sprayed onto wounds during administration [69,70]. The results of gelation time indicated that the hydrogel could rapidly undergo a sol-gel transition under physiological conditions, forming a non-flowing solid hydrogel that meets application requirements (Fig. 1E). The gelation temperature of the hydrogels was further explored using a rheometer and revealed that the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G”) of gel, Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel remained stable before the gelation temperature (the intersection point of G′ and G″), with G’ < G″, indicating a liquid sol state. While after reaching the gelation temperature, G′ rises faster than G″, resulting in G’ > G″, and the hydrogel existed in a solid state. The gelation temperatures for gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel were 30.18 °C, 29.62 °C, and 30.61 °C (Fig. 1F–H), suggesting that hydrogels could adhere to the oral mucosa in a solid state under oral temperature. SEM images revealed the microstructure of the hydrogel, which featured a porous network structure that provided an excellent environment for the loading and storage of the Cur-VE-Zn complex, and the porous structure of the hydrogel was not altered after loading the carrier (Fig. 1I and S8). After adhering to the oral mucosa, the complex could be continuously released through the porous network structure of the hydrogel [71], thereby extending the intervention time of the Cur-VE-Zn complex. The adhesion of gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel was measured using lap shear tests [47](Fig. 1J and K). These hydrogels exhibited good adhesion in a dry environment, but their adhesive properties were adversely affected by the moist oral environment due to saliva. When artificial saliva was used to simulate the oral environment, the adhesion of hydrogels decreased but remained higher than that of common excipients, meeting the requirements for adhesion to the oral mucosa [72,73]. The swelling ratio and degradation rate results also demonstrated that these hydrogels had excellent swelling and degradation properties (Figure S9, Figure S10). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading capacity (DLC) of Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel were quantified by high-speed centrifugation coupled with UV spectrophotometry. Cur@gel exhibited an EE of 81.81 % and a DLC of 11.95 mg/g, while Cur-VE-Zn@gel showed an EE of 73.27 % and a DLC of 10.27 mg/g (Figure S11). The release kinetics of both formulations assessed via dialysis and UV spectrophotometry (Figure S12). Based on the gel swelling and degradation properties, the release process of the gels was divided into three stages. In the first stage (0–4 h), cumulative release reached 53.42 % (Cur@gel) and 39.3 % (Cur-VE-Zn@gel), exhibiting a “burst release effect,” which was attributed to rapid dissolution and diffusion of surface-adsorbed drug molecules [74]. The release in the second stage (4–12 h) was governed by the combined effects of internal drug diffusion and gel degradation, resulting in sustained but slower release of Cur and Cur-VE-Zn compared to the first stage [75]. In the pslateau stage (12–48 h), minimal release resulted solely from gel degradation. To further investigate the advantages of the gel in prolonging the contact time between Cur-VE-Zn and the oral mucosa, in vivo oral imaging was performed in mice (Figure S13, 14). The Cur-VE-Zn complex were labeled with indocyanine green (ICG) to prepare Cur-VE-Zn-ICG@gel, which were administered to the murine oral cavity. Fluorescence imaging was acquired at 0.5 h, 1 h, 1.5 h, 2 h, 2.5 h, and 3 h. Relative to unencapsulated Cur-VE-Zn complex, the gel significantly extended the retention time of the functional substances in the oral cavity.

A comprehensive biosafety assessment was performed for both Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel. During the evaluation period, normal mice received daily hydrogel administration (0.2 mL day−1). Terminal analyses included complete hematological profiling and histopathological examination of major organ systems. Throughout the study duration, no mortality or clinically significant adverse effects were observed in any treatment group. Blood test results showed that Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel did not cause significant changes in the blood indices of mice (Figure S15). Biochemical indicators of liver function, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total protein (TP), and albumin (ALB), as well as kidney function indicators, including creatinine (CREA) and uric acid (UA), showed no significant changes (Figure S16). Histopathological analysis of the major organs demonstrated clear organ structures without significant morphological differences or organic lesions, indicating that Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel did not cause organ damage or toxic effects (Figure S17). All of these toxicity tests demonstrated the high biosafety of the hydrogels. These findings suggest that the poloxamer–chitosan hydrogel-based delivery platform is capable of enabling effective localized therapy.

Radioprotective effects of Cur-VE-Zn@gel on oral mucositis in mice

We administered a radiation dose of 25 Gy to the head region of mice to simulate the acute injury induced by a single high dose of ionizing radiation. At 10:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. daily, 100 μL of gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel were applied to the oral cavity of the mice, with the intervention continuing for 7 consecutive days. By monitoring the body weight of the mice throughout the experimental cycle, we found that the body weight of the mice in all groups decreased dramatically after X-ray irradiation, but by the 5th day, the rate of weight loss began to slow in the Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel groups, with the most significant mitigation observed in the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group (Fig. 4B and Figure S18).

Toluidine blue staining allowed for the visual observation of oral mucositis and/or ulcer formation in the mice. Post-X-ray irradiation, multiple toluidine blue-positive areas and extensive mucositis were observed in the tongue tissue of the mice. The use of gel alone and Cur@gel both reduced the occurrence of ulcers and mucositis to some extent, but the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group showed almost no ulcers or mucositis (Fig. 4C and E-F). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated the mitigating effects of Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel on radiation-induced tissue damage at the microscopic level. The tongue tissue of normal mice exhibited intact epithelial structure, orderly cell arrangement, and no inflammatory cell infiltration in the epithelium and lamina propria. In contrast, the X-ray and gel groups exhibited loss of epithelial integrity, substantial shedding of the keratinized epidermis, disordered cellular arrangement, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration within the lamina propria. Both Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel demonstrated significant reductions in these pathological alterations. Notably, the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group displayed a higher density of basal layer cells, more organized cellular alignment, diminished inflammatory infiltration, and a tissue morphology more closely resembling normal mouse tongue tissue compared to the Cur@gel group (Fig. 4D). Measurement of the epithelial thickness of the tongue tissue revealed that radiation reduced the thickness of the mucosal epithelium, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel was more effective than Cur@gel in increasing the thickness of the mucosal epithelium reduced by radiation (Fig. 4G). Histopathological evaluation of the tongue tissue based on epithelial integrity and inflammatory status (Figure S19B) revealed that the radiation-treated groups exhibited progressively lower scores in the blank gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel groups, indicating a corresponding enhancement in radioprotective efficacy on the oral mucosal tissue. In summary, through body weight monitoring, observation of oral mucositis and/or ulcer formation, and histopathological examination, we found that Cur-VE-Zn@gel enhanced the radioprotective capacity of the oral mucosa by mitigating radiation-induced mucosal damage, thereby alleviating mouse weight loss, exhibiting superior efficacy compared to Cur@gel alone.

Cur-VE-Zn@gel reduces apoptosis and inflammation, enhances angiogenesis in vivo

Further TUNEL assays revealed that radiation caused apoptosis in the epithelial cells of the mouse tongue tissue. Application of gel alone failed to ameliorate epithelial cell apoptosis, while Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel reduced the number of apoptotic cells, suggesting that both had inhibitory effects on radiation-induced apoptosis. However, Cur@gel exhibited limited inhibitory capacity, while Cur-VE-Zn@gel was more effective (Fig. 5A and B). Through quantification of cleaved caspase-3 expression in tissues (Figure S20), it was observed that, compared to the radiation group, the blank gel, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel groups exhibited a progressive reduction in the positive expression of this key marker of apoptosis, further verifying the ability of these gels to improve radiation-induced apoptosis as indicated by the TUNEL results. CD31, a marker primarily expressed in endothelial cells, is associated with angiogenesis during tissue repair. Reduced CD31 expression implies endothelial cell damage and dysfunction, disruption of endothelial junctions, increased vascular permeability, attenuated angiogenesis, and delayed wound healing [76]. Western blot and immunohistochemical measurement of CD31 (Fig. 5C and D, Figure S21) expression and distribution in the tongue tissue of the mice revealed a significant decrease in CD31 expression in the X-ray group 7 days post-irradiation, indicating inhibited angiogenesis. The gel group showed a slight increase in CD31 expression, but the difference was not significant. In contrast, Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel significantly improved the radiation-suppressed CD31 levels, promoted endothelial cell proliferation, increased angiogenesis, and accelerated tissue repair [77], with the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group showing a more pronounced increase in CD31 expression, suggesting a faster promotion of tissue and mucosal repair post-radiation. These findings were further confirmed by Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. S19 A and C), which revealed extensive areas of disorganized blue collagen fibers in the irradiation group, suggesting that radiation impedes tissue repair and promotes fibrosis. In contrast, the Cur-VE-Zn@gel treatment group demonstrated the most outstanding regeneration effect with collagen deposition levels closest to those of normal tissue. Additionally, we measured the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the tongue tissue of the mice. The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly elevated in the X-ray irradiated group, and the use of gel alone did not reduce the expression of these cytokines, while Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel could inhibit the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, the inhibitory effect of Cur@gel was not significant compared to that of Cur-VE-Zn@gel (Fig. 5E, F, and G, Figure S21). These results suggest that Cur-VE-Zn@gel also inhibits radiation-induced oral mucosal injury by decreasing apoptosis, increasing angiogenesis, and exerting anti-inflammatory effects.

In vivo DNA damage repair and antioxidant capacity of the Cur-VE-Zn@gel

Based on the findings from in vitro studies, we continued to focus on the analysis of DNA damage and oxidative stress levels in animal models. Immunohistochemical examination of γ-H2AX expression revealed that the proportion of γ-H2AX positive epithelial cells was up to 100 % in the X-ray group and the gel treatment group. In contrast, the expression level decreased in the Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel groups, with the lowest expression observed in the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group, which indicated that Cur-VE-Zn@gel has the strongest ability to withstand DNA damage induced by ionizing radiation in animal models (Fig. 6A and B, Figure S21). 8-OHDG, an oxidative adduct generated by the direct attack of ROS on the 8th carbon atom of guanine in DNA molecules, serves as a widely recognized biomarker for ROS-induced DNA oxidative damage in tissues and indirectly reflects tissue ROS levels and oxidative stress status [78,79]. Examination of 8-OHDG levels in mouse tongue tissues revealed that the application of hydrogels could effectively alleviate radiation-induced oxidative stress, with the lowest proportion of 8-OHDG positive cells observed in the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group, which indicated that Cur-VE-Zn@gel had a more pronounced efficacy in ameliorating oxidative damage in tongue tissues compared to Cur@gel (Fig. 6A and C). The expression trend of Nrf2 in animal models was consistent with the in vitro studies, and the application of Cur@gel and Cur-VE-Zn@gel increased Nrf2 levels in tissues, while the highest expression was observed in the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group (Fig. 6A and D). The performance of the antioxidant enzyme system in the tongue tissues further corroborated the antioxidant capacity of Cur-VE-Zn@gel. The activities of SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px were significantly higher in the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group than in the X-ray, gel and Cur@gel groups, while the level of the lipid peroxidation product MDA was significantly lower in the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group (Fig. 6E, F, G and H). These results suggest that in vivo, the Cur-VE-Zn complex retains a superior ability to inhibit radiation-induced DNA damage and oxidative stress compared to free curcumin, providing tissues with enhanced resistance to radiation.

Potential mechanisms underlying radioprotective effect of Cur-VE-Zn@gel

To further validate the aforementioned phenotypic results and preliminarily investigate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the radioprotective effects of Cur-VE-Zn@gel, DIA quantitative proteomic analysis was performed to screen for differentially expressed proteins across the Normal, X-ray, Cur@gel, and Cur-VE-Zn@gel groups, using fold change (FC) thresholds of >1.2 or < 0.83 and a significance criterion of P < 0.05. The screened differentially expressed proteins were grouped into upregulated and downregulated categories (Fig. 7A, B and C) and subjected to functional annotation, and GOBP signaling pathways related to cell proliferation, apoptosis, DNA damage repair, inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and tissue damage repair were finally enriched (Fig. 7D). These GOBP pathways revealed that in the comparison between the X-ray group and the Normal group, the upregulation of multiple apoptosis-related signaling pathways indicated the occurrence of radiation-induced apoptosis. The upregulation of inflammatory response signaling pathways, regulation of AIM2 inflammasome complex assembly signaling pathway, IL-1 mediated signaling pathway, and pathways related to leukocyte migration and proliferation further supported radiation-induced inflammatory damage. Studies have shown that defects in Fe-S cluster synthesis can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, and DNA damage [80]. When cells are under oxidative stress, such as exposure to ultraviolet radiation, ionizing radiation, and chemical toxins, the upregulation of Fe-S cluster assembly can enhance the activity of intracellular antioxidant enzymes, facilitating the clearance of excess ROS, reducing oxidative damage, and maintaining normal cell structure and function [81,82]. The downregulation of pathways related to Fe-S cluster assembly in the X-ray group suggested a potential imbalance in the regulation of oxidative stress levels in tissues after radiation. In the comparison between the Cur-VE-Zn@gel group and the X-ray group, it was found that Cur-VE-Zn@gel may exert its antioxidant effects by promoting glutathione generation, reduce inflammatory responses through modulating TLR4, IL-1, and IL-12 pathways, adjust the cell cycle and facilitate DNA repair to mitigate radiation-induced DNA damage, and upregulate the VEGF pathway to promote tissue repair. These findings further corroborated previous phenotypic studies, demonstrating that Cur-VE-Zn@gel exerts radioprotective capabilities by promoting DNA damage repair, exerting antioxidant effects, suppressing inflammatory injury, and enhancing tissue regeneration. Compared to the X-ray group, pathways related to reduced apoptosis, antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and promotion of DNA damage repair were also enriched in the Cur@gel group. Collectively, our data indicated that Cur-VE-Zn@gel mediates synergistic therapeutic efficacy via pleiotropic mechanisms involving multiple pathways and molecular targets.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a novel comprehensive therapeutic platform for treating RIOM with complex pathological mechanisms. Curcumin and VE form the core active unit through covalent binding, and the addition of Zn2+ drives the self-assembly of this unit into stable polymers, forming a multi-functional ionizing radiation defense barrier. This barrier exhibits superior DNA damage repair, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and mucosal repair properties via synergistic effects. The utilization of an adhesive thermosensitive hydrogel enables the precise delivery of functional substances to mucosal wounds, overcoming bioavailability limitations. In vitro studies further confirms that curcumin inhibits the activity of normal epithelial cells. Notably, in radiation-exposed epithelial cells, curcumin is found to exacerbate the suppression of cell migration induced by ionizing radiation and aggravate DNA damage. In contrast, the Cur-VE-Zn complex exhibits more potent and stable activity in DNA damage repair, antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and promoting cell migration, along with lower cytotoxicity. In a murine RIOM model, local application of Cur-VE-Zn@gel provided significant protective and healing effects, effectively alleviating mucosal damage by reducing DNA damage, inflammation, and oxidative stress, while robustly accelerating tissue repair. Proteomic analysis revealed the complex's unique radioprotective mechanism, which involves multi-directional regulation of multiple pathways, extending beyond the capability of curcumin alone. In conclusion, our biomaterial-based comprehensive RIOM therapy platform successfully transforms curcumin from a promising yet flawed molecule into a clinically viable, safe, and highly effective multi-target therapy for RIOM, and demonstrates high technical feasibility. This approach may contribute to improving the overall efficacy of cancer treatment regimens. But it should be pointed out that, future studies are warranted to evaluate the long-term safety and potential chronic toxicity of this platform, a necessary step for its broader clinical application.
