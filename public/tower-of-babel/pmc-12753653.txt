AI powered multi feature fusion framework for retrieving images using color, texture and shape descriptors
Authors: Kommu Naveen, R. M. S. Parvathi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The exponential expansion of digital visual material requires sophisticated and efficient picture retrieval technologies. The article presents a new framework using AI to integrate form descriptors, texture patterns, and color histograms to improve Content-Based Image Retrieval. Because they use various feature sets, earlier CBIR systems generally have semantic mismatches and poor retrieval accuracy. We overcome these limits using deep feature fusion with color moments, GLCM for texture analysis, and Fourier and Hu moments for form extraction. SVM classifiers and CNNs are used to add acquired features to a learnt similarity space. This improves semantic matching and retrieval. Attention-guided weighting adjusts feature type importance based on the query image environment. After training and validation on Corel-1 K and Caltech-101 benchmark datasets, the method beats standard CBIR models in accuracy, recall, and mAP. Experimental data shows that the hybrid AI-enhanced CBIR model has a maximum accuracy of 92.3% on Corel-1 K and performs consistently across all categories and noise levels. Improved, more scalable image retrieval systems that combine deep learning-based optimization with low-level visual descriptors will assist in digital asset management, medical imaging databases, and multimedia search engines.

Introduction

The proliferation of digital cameras and mobile devices has led to the daily generation of millions of images, which constitute a substantial portion of the world’s information stored in image format1. Retrieving visually similar images efficiently has become more challenging as image databases grow in size and complexity at a rapid pace2. AI based Content-Based Image Retrieval (CBIR) is an approach that shows promise; it finds images not by textual annotations but by their visual content3. Manual tagging and annotation can be time-consuming, subjective, and inconsistent; hence, CBIR has been studied for the last 20 years as a potential replacement. Due to differing interpretations, annotators may assign labels that are vague or incomplete, which can make retrieval tasks more difficult4. On the other hand, CBIR utilizes quantifiable visual features to determine the similarity between two images: one from the query and one from the database5.

The semantic gap—when robots extract low-level elements like color, texture, or form and humans perceive high-level semantic meaning—is a major CBIR difficulty6. Traditional CBIR algorithms handle texture and color equally, even though the human visual system prioritizes texture in object detection7. Due to representational power issues, early feature-based systems failed to overcome the semantic gap despite their interpretability and computational speed. CBIR has improved substantially because to CNNs and other deep learning (DL) advances8. CNNs can capture complicated patterns, textures, and spatial connections by learning abstract and hierarchical representations from raw image data, unlike human modelers9. Deep models have improved retrieval accuracy across datasets10.

Research gap

The latest deep learning-based CBIR systems have drawbacks. Occlusion, intra-class variability, geometric modifications including scaling and rotation, and real-time processing are common in large-scale contexts11. Their inflexibility un answering various concerns is another negative. System retrieval accuracy decreases when features are not dynamically prioritized according to the query or feature weighting is static12. Many current systems start with DL or manual low-level features. In complicated retrieval settings with plenty of content variance, separate techniques generally fail13. Most hybrid approaches use context-independent methodologies like fixed-weight fusion or early/late feature concatenation14. Many CBIR models may revise feature importance during retrieval. In heterogeneous picture databases, static fusion approaches lack query image semantics, therefore similarity comparisons fail15. Most deep learning-based CBIR systems fail in low-resource contexts because they demand a lot of processing power and labeled data16. Caching, indexing, and response time generally prioritize retrieval accuracy above computational efficiency17. As data quantities expand, image retrieval systems must be fast, scalable, and accurate. Modern methods seldom index or cache large datasets for low computational cost18.

Novelty

A hybrid CBIR framework that combines deep learning-based representations with handcrafted color, texture, and shape is the study’s key breakthrough. This framework uses adaptive, attention-guided fusion. Our method improves semantic alignment and retrieval by altering feature significance depending on query picture context19. Current models use CNNs with classical descriptors such color moments, HSV histograms, GLCM, Hu, and Fourier moments to ensure interpretability, which is vital to the discipline. A support vector machine (SVM) classifier may help arrange feature space for improved discrimination20. Even with big datasets, cache-optimized indexing may speed up retrieval. This work aims to improve CBIR system computational efficiency via design, development, and assessment. The adaptive multi-feature fusion and cache-optimized retrieval pipeline of the proposed AI-CBIR framework improve semantic accuracy and computational efficiency over current CBIR and deep retrieval systems. AI-CBIR uses an Adaptive Attention-Guided Fusion (AAGF) mechanism to dynamically adjust the contribution of each modality based on query context and feature relevance, unlike conventional deep retrieval models that use convolutional feature embeddings or single-modality representations. Thus, the system captures low-level visual granularity and high-level semantic correspondence. A cache-optimized indexing technique improves retrieval performance and scalability, solving latency and throughput issues in big picture databases. The method uses attention-weighted similarity mapping to increase feature discriminability and minimize feature space redundancy.

Literature survey

Kayhan and Fekri-Ershad (2021) recommended weighting color and texture characteristics to increase content-based image retrieval accuracy. GLCM, LNDP, and MLBP are utilized to extract texture features. These characteristics show grayscale images’ spatial and structural distinctions. Parallel quantized color histograms reveal global color distributions. The authors compare query and database photographs using Canberra distance separately on color and texture feature sets. On the Corel-1 K and Corel-10 K benchmark datasets, the system surpassed typical retrieval approaches in recall and accuracy. For powerful feature extraction, Alghamdi et al.22 suggested a hybrid CBIR architecture with Color Moments (CMs), Color Histogram, Ranklet Transform, RGB, and HSV color spaces and a Graph-based Gray-Level Co-Occurrence Matrix (HGGLCM). SVM classification and Squared Euclidean Distance retrieval are used in the system. Our technique beat the best at the time, averaging 22.3% recall and 91.7% accuracy on the Oxford Flower dataset and 18.4% on the Corel-1k dataset. Issues like high feature dimensionality, restricted rotation and scale invariance, and others continue.

A major CBIR difficulty is the semantic gap between low-level visual cues and high-level semantic comprehension. To solve this issue, Bosco et al.23 combined TriCLR (RGB, YCbCr, and L*a*b*) and Histogram of Local Binary Patterns (HistLBP) color and texture data. This enhances retrieval accuracy with several color and texture characteristics. The semantic gap exists because the technique focuses on attributes and lacks context awareness, while outperforming conventional ones. Kumar co-developed hybrid CBIR optimization24. This design merges color, texture, and local descriptors via adaptive feature selection. Dynamically optimizing weighting and choosing attributes enhances discrimination and decreases redundancy. Improved indexing and caching benefit large picture databases. Testing indicated 35% faster processing and 8% better accuracy. Responding took 45.2 milliseconds and mAP 0.91. It handles complicated queries well but might be stronger.

Wei et al.25 developed HybridHash to recover multiple pictures. Self-attention and convolutional neural networks are used. They store local texture and global contextual information in their stage-wise architecture using local self-attention and convolutional blocks. Interaction modules let feature levels communicate, improving feature representation. The CIFAR-10, NUS-WIDE, and ImageNet benchmark datasets show that HybridHash retrieves data better than deep hashing. However, its binary hash design may make it less accurate for fine-grained retrieval applications, and it hasn’t been evaluated on Corel-1 K and Caltech-101 CBIR benchmarks. Narwal et al.26 propose a hybrid CBIR architecture that combines filtering and classification to improve retrieval. This approach removes photos that seem different in the search space using color moment-based filtering. It then extracts texture and color data such color histograms and Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix. Extreme Learning Machines aggregate these qualities for identification. On the Wang image dataset, low-level descriptors and lightweight classifiers scored 96.25%. The method is accurate and fast, however it uses handmade features and can’t handle huge or complex datasets, making it less useful in a wider variety of CBIR situations.

Sadiq et al.27 suggested combining more input image portions using three convolutional neural networks—NASNetMobile, DenseNet121, and VGG12. The ensemble improves feature richness and retrieval accuracy by using each design’s unique qualities. The technique outperformed single-model CBIR algorithms on benchmark datasets in accuracy and durability. Learning from many networks enhances semantic matching. In real-time or with limited resources, deep ensemble structures may increase computational weight and inference time, making the model less efficient. Hu and Bors (2023) developed a new CBIR method28. Co-attention improves database visual-query picture semantic alignment. Their technique properly shows the link between question and candidate image qualities using deep convolutional features and a co-attention module. By concentrating on related areas, our technique improves semantic similarity estimates and retrieval efficiency. Experimental findings show the method outperforms deep learning-based CBIR models in benchmark dataset object identification. This works when there are several factors. However, co-attention processes demand more computer power, making them harder to deploy on low-resource devices or in real time. Kim et al.29 use CNNs and an SVM classifier to create a hybrid model for face emotion recognition that uses dynamic facial expression data. CNN classifiers extracted motion characteristics from dense facial motion flows, whereas SVM classifiers used geometric landmark expression sequence flows. The software correctly recognized seven facial emotions in CK + and BU4D: contempt, disgust, fright, excitement, sadness, and amazement. BU4D and CK + databases recognized the suggested method 94.69% and 99.69%, respectively. The suggested strategy has superior outcomes on the BU4D and CK + databases. An innovative photo classification method by Marzouk et al.30 uses CNNs, SVMs, and a modified bag-of-visual-words model. SIFT-ORB-BoVW improves classifier performance using ORB descriptors and SIFT. This research implies that convolutional neural networks and support vector machines may assist feature extractors learn visual language from images. We compared the suggested method to BoVW. The proposed method outperformed the most sophisticated BoVW models in testing.

Sikandar et al.31 suggest a CBIR system that uses both DL and machine learning. A K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier that uses a Euclidean distance metric would examine features from ResNet50 and VGG16 to determine how similar the pictures are. The system demonstrated an impressive ability to distinguish between objects with flawless accuracy when tested on the Corel-1 K, Corel-5 K, and Corel-10 K datasets. Even though the method is easy to implement and works well, its efficacy depends on how sensitive KNN is to feature dimensionality. The model’s dependency on Euclidean distance may not work well with many datasets. The model lacks any sophisticated methods for combining and weighing variables that change based on the query.

Rastegar et al.32 employed Generalized Discriminant Analysis (GDA) to reduce the number of data dimensions and developed a deep convolutional neural network architecture specifically for CBIR. The design became better thanks to a useful feedback loop. It enhances retrieval by extracting features based on the user’s input. The system performed well, achieving mAPs of 96.8%, 95.62%, and 98.78% on the Oxford-Tehran (OT), Corel-1000, and Caltech-101 datasets, respectively. With interactive refinement, this method gets over the semantic gap. Additionally, the GDA stage complicates processing, which may slow down real-time retrieval. User input may not be functional in procedures that are entirely automated.

Content-based image retrieval (CBIR) originally relied on handcrafted low-level descriptors — colour histograms/moments and MPEG-7 descriptors for colour, Gabor/LBP/GLCM for texture, and edge/contour or region-based measures for shape — because these features are computationally cheap and interpretable and map directly to visual appearance33. Early and foundational surveys show how these descriptors formed the backbone of CBIR systems and why MPEG-7 became a de-facto reference for standardized colour descriptors.

Because individual feature types capture complementary aspects of images, many successful classical systems fuse colour, texture, and shape to improve retrieval accuracy and robustness. Fusion strategies include feature concatenation, weighted score fusion, and dimensionality-reduction on the concatenated space (PCA, LDA)34. Empirical studies and method papers demonstrate that carefully weighted multi-feature fusion consistently outperforms any single descriptor, especially on datasets with high intra-class variation; recent work continues to refine fusion (e.g., combining SIFT/SURF/HOG or using learned weighting) to reduce redundancy while preserving discriminability.

Over the past decade DL has reshaped CBIR: convolutional neural networks learn compact, semantically rich embeddings that close much of the “semantic gap” between low-level features and human perception. Recent decade surveys summarize that learned global descriptors (CNN feature vectors), deep metric learning, and hybrid systems (handcrafted + learned) now dominate state-of-the-art retrieval benchmarks; nevertheless, handcrafted colour/texture/shape descriptors remain useful as complementary channels (e.g., for fine-grained texture or when training data are limited). The literature also emphasizes evaluation best practices (mAP, precision@k, retrieval speed) and the importance of public benchmarks for reproducibility35.

Xiaoting Zhang et al.38 suggested the Plaid fabric image retrieval based on deep local and global features with attention mechanism. This research introduces an innovative approach to picture retrieval for plaid textiles using a convolutional neural network (CNN) with an attention mechanism, based on the properties of plaid fabrics. To completely describe the plaid fabric pictures, global and local deep features are recovered by combining the attention mechanism with the CNN branches. An orthogonal fusion module is then utilized to fuse the features. The feature extraction and fusion tasks are optimized using a unique training technique that reduces the quantity of training data. When trying to strike a compromise between retrieval efficiency and accuracy, the Annoy algorithm is used as a similarity measuring tool. Over 44,000 fabric samples were obtained from the manufacturer to construct the picture database, which serves as a standard for verifying the suggested approach. At rank five, experiments reveal a recall of 57.1% and a precision of 77.5%, with an average accuracy of up to 0.758.

P. Deekshita et al.39 proposed the Hybrid CBIR Framework Using Vision Transformers and Genetic Algorithm for Enhanced Image Retrieval. Vision Transformers (ViT) are included in the suggested system to effectively capture complex, global visual figures across many picture categories. Displaying its exceptional retrieval capability and computational efficiency, the system attains an F1-score of 95.12%, a decreased error rate of 0.62%, and an accuracy of 99.38% in retrieval.

Tariq Mahmood et al.40 discussed the Recent Advancements and Future Prospects in Active DL for Medical Image Segmentation and Classification. Covering topics such as segmentation, picture capture, augmentation, registration, and classification, this article presents the most recent deep learning-based applications in medical image analysis. Convolutional neural network (CNN), deep belief network (DBN), stacked autoencoder (SAE), and recurrent neural network (RNN) are four state-of-the-art DL models covered in the article. The process of selecting studies included perusing reputable academic sources, gathering relevant literature and suitable indicators for analysis, with an emphasis on techniques to segmentation and classification that rely on DL, and assessing performance metrics. This study sheds light on the challenges faced by researchers and physicians in their pursuit of a reliable cancer prognostic framework that makes use of cutting-edge deep-learning algorithms. In order to overcome obstacles and progress the area of medical image analysis, future horizons are being investigated.

Tariq Mahmood et al.41 deliberated the Breast cancer diagnosis with MFF-HistoNet: a multi-modal feature fusion network integrating CNNs and quantum tensor networks. To tackle the multi-grading issues of breast images and greatly improve diagnosis accuracy, this research suggests a powerful Multi-Modal Feature Fusion Network for Histopathology (MFF-HistoNet). By fusing a convolutional neural network (CNN) with a quantum tensor network (QTN), MFF-HistoNet is able to compress model parameters, allowing for deeper global features while reducing model parameters. The data improvement approach reduces color interference and guarantees a balanced training set. The GLCM approach is used with LBP and Gabor filtering to extract spatial, dimensional, and directional local cell shape features from histopathology images. By making use of the BreaKHis dataset, MFF-HistoNet is able to classify eight distinct subtypes of breast cancer, simplify its models without sacrificing their capacity to capture important spatial correlations, and ultimately improve computational efficiency. With a remarkable accuracy of 98.8% at the picture level and 98.4% at the patient level under 100 × magnification, and 98.1% and 98.9% under 40 × magnification, the MFF-HistoNet al.gorithms demonstrate the benchmark performance, surpassing previous models while decreasing resource needs.

Open challenges and gaps relevant to a multi-feature fusion framework are well documented: (1) effective fusion rules that adapt per-query or per-dataset rather than fixed global weights, (2) computational and memory costs of high-dimensional concatenated feature spaces, (3) robustness to domain shift and image degradations, and (4) explainability of learned embeddings when combined with interpretable handcrafted descriptors. Recent papers propose dynamic weighting, compact hashing/quantization, and joint end-to-end training of multi-stream networks (each stream specialised for colour, texture, or shape) as promising directions to combine interpretability with performance36,37.

Proposed methodology

Overall structure of CBIR

The suggested AI-CBIR system uses hybrid feature fusion. This method uses CNN-derived deep semantic features and manually created low-level features. Low-level qualities include color, texture, and shape. An attention-guided adaptive weighting strategy enhances fusion by dynamically adjusting feature type relevance based on the query image context. Figure 1 illustrates the general AI-CBIR framework, comprising several essential steps. The user uploads the query image as the initial step in CBIR. Every image in the database will undergo the identical sequence of processing as the query image. Online processes are often implemented when the user submits the query image, while offline processes are used to apply the same procedures to dataset photos before the query is submitted. By using AI-CBIR, the similarity between the query image and the database images may be efficiently quantified. In a multi-stage attention-driven process, the AI-CBIR framework’s Adaptive Attention-Guided Fusion (AAGF) mechanism dynamically modifies color, texture, and shape descriptor contributions. First, each feature modality is embedded into a vector that captures its discriminative properties. Each descriptor’s contextual significance is assessed by an attention encoder by assessing inter-feature correlations and the semantic link between query and database pictures. Normalizing the attention ratings to provide proportionate priority to each modality allows the fusion layer to enhance the most informative characteristics for a retrieval instance. As the model learns cross-modal relationships, this real-time adaptive weighting refines feature importance.

Color Moments show the statistical distribution of colors; Texture features show how the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) represents spatial intensity patterns; and Shape features use Hu Moments and Fourier Descriptors. Hu Moments, on the other hand, are constant geometric descriptors that describe the shape’s structure. However, Fourier Descriptors may convert object outlines into frequency-based representations that don’t change whether scaled, rotated, or moved. CNNs also gather high-level semantic information about images and utilize it to create deep feature vectors. Attention-Guided Feature Fusion ensures that various components work together effectively within the system. This part uses the query context to dynamically weight each feature set, ensuring that the most important ones are prioritized. In architecture, shape and texture are often the most essential elements, whereas in nature, color and texture frequently take precedence.

The Similarity Learning Module employs a CNN-based deep embedding and an SVM classifier to work with the fused feature vector. The CNN learns to discover patterns in similar pictures and uses them to construct strong and abstract feature representations. The SVM is also learning how to use these representations to judge images. Using both supervised and unsupervised learning enhances the model’s ability to distinguish between images that are visually or semantically similar. The proposed AI-CBIR framework uses hierarchical memory management and data locality concepts to expedite feature access and similarity calculation in the cache-optimized indexing process. Feature vectors from colour, texture, and shape descriptors are cache-aligned during indexing to reduce query memory latency. An adaptive Least Recently Used (LRU) replacement strategy stores frequently used feature clusters in high-speed cache buffers, keeping the most relevant descriptors accessible for computation. The indexing structure uses hash-based partitioning and tree-based reference to minimize search depth and speed nearest-neighbor retrieval. Maintaining spatial coherence between related feature entries and prefetching data into cache lines reduces I/O cost and computational time.

Feature extraction module

Feature extraction and selection convey picture semantics, making them the most important parts of AI-CBIR. Global characteristics incorporate color, texture, form, and geographical data to complete the image. Local features are discovered by dividing the picture into smaller sections or recognizing edges, blobs, and corners. Local characteristics stay generally intact when object size, location, or orientation changes. Color properties must be determined using color spaces. The most common color spaces used in CBIR are LAB, YCbCr, RGB, and HSV. Color moments are durable in these color spaces because they are unaffected by translation, rotation, and scale changes. This characteristic makes color spaces strong. Regions and contours are two standard methods for obtaining shape information. Region-based extraction examines the entire region, whereas boundary-based extraction focuses solely on the edges of the region. Fourier Descriptors (FDs) can show the forms of objects in pictures on a frequency scale. After finding the contour, the outline of an object becomes a string of complicated integers. The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) can be used to transform these into short, unchanging shape descriptors. Translation, scaling, or rotation do not change the seven mathematical properties known as Hu Moments. These come from times in pictures. One reason pattern recognition employs them is that they can precisely show how shapes are made. The suggested CBIR model is used to encode the global form structure of each image’s objects, which is how Hu Moments are calculated. Because of their high computing efficiency and strong discriminative strength, these qualities are valuable for real-time applications. CNN deep feature

The proposed model utilizes a transfer learning framework to extract deep features from two well-known CNN architectures, VGG16 and ResNet50, as shown in Fig. 3. These models can quickly and easily extract high-level semantic features from the vast ImageNet dataset without requiring a fresh start with training. VGG16 excels at capturing spatial hierarchies and fine-grained textures due to its consistent design and deep stack of 3 × 3 × 3 convolutional layers. Small picture embeddings are made from feature maps from the “fc1” layer in this study. ResNet50 has a deeper and more efficient learning architecture because it utilizes residual connections, which make training more stable and help mitigate the vanishing gradient problem. This is achieved by leveraging features from the “avg_pool” layer of ResNet50, as that global representation contains a wealth of semantic and contextual information.

To properly merge the two architectures, the input layers of VGG16 and ResNet50 are modified to accommodate pictures of size 224 × 224 × 3. The 224 × 224 × 3 design does not include input, pooling, fully connected, softmax, or classification layers. Adding a GlobalMaxPooling2D layer to both models to turn the feature maps into one-dimensional vectors instead. During the preprocessing phase, the RGB photographs in the database are changed to BGR format according to the model inputs. Later, features are collected from both models. ResNet50 produces a vector with 2048 dimensions, while VGG16 generates one with 512 dimensions. These vectors are combined to create a final feature descriptor, leveraging the semantic robustness of ResNet50 and the detailed textural representation of VGG16.

Conclusion

The AI-CBIR system can process large datasets and deliver accurate results with rapid response times and semantic alignment. This uses attention-guided fusion and SVM-based similarity learning. Handmade and deep characteristics merge perfectly in this product. There are still issues even if the model works well on orderly (Corel-1 K) and challenging (Caltech-101) picture collections. Domain-specific datasets may be tougher to adapt to without fine-tuning pre-trained CNNs. Dynamic fusion may be computationally costly for large datasets. Cache-optimized retrieval may also fail with unexpected or variable query patterns. The purpose of cache-optimized retrieval is to improve things. Pre-trained CNN architectures like VGG16 and ResNet50 can improve data discovery in domain-specific datasets like medical or satellite photographs without fine-tuning. Even with cache-optimized indexing, large and changing image repositories can increase processing time and memory usage. Attention-guided feature fusion and similarity calculation across high-dimensional vectors are causing problems. Future work will focus on real-time responsiveness, combining textual or contextual metadata into more complete and easier-to-use multi-modal queries for image retrieval, and self-supervised learning to make the system more adaptable to different domains.
