Special Lagrangian submanifolds in K3-fibered Calabi-Yau 3-folds
Shih-Kai Chiu, Yu-Shen Lin

Abstract
. We construct special Lagrangian submanifolds in collapsing Calabi-Yau 3 3 -folds fibered by K3 surfaces. As these 3 3 -folds collapse, the special Lagrangians shrink to 1 1 -dimensional graphs in the base, mirroring the conjectured tropicalization of holomorphic curves in collapsing SYZ torus-fibered Calabi-Yau manifolds. This confirms predictions of Donaldson and Donaldson-Scaduto in the Calabi-Yau setting. Additionally, we discuss our results in the contexts of the Thomas-Yau conjecture, the Donaldson-Scaduto conjecture, and mirror symmetry.



1.
Introduction

A fundamental problem in complex and symplectic geometry is to find
special Lagrangian submanifolds in compact Calabi-Yau manifolds. Given
a homology or Hamiltonian isotopy class, the existence of a special
Lagrangian representative remains a widely open question. The
important works of Schoen-Wolfson
[
53
]
and Wolfson
[
67
]

highlight the complexities involved in the variational approach to
this problem. On the other hand, the Thomas-Yau conjecture
[
62
]

suggests a link between the convergence of Lagrangian mean curvature
flow (LMCF) and stability conditions, although Neves’ seminal work

[
51
]
shows that singularities along LMCF are, to some extent,
inevitable. Note that Neves’ examples do not contradict the
aforementioned conjecture, as they do not satisfy the almost
calibrated condition required by the conjecture, a notion originally
introduced in M.-T. Wang’s pioneering
work
[
66
]
. Joyce
[
30
]
has since refined the original
conjecture significantly by identifying key additional structures and
possible singularities. We refer the reader to Y. Li’s work

[
41
]
, which covers recent developments and introduces new ideas
along these lines.

Another promising approach is to look for special Lagrangian
submanifolds in certain homology classes, such as vanishing cycles or
special fibration structures. Hein-Sun
[
27
]
produces first
examples of special Lagrangian vanishing spheres in dimension three
and higher; see Collins-Gukov-Picard-Yau
[
8
]
for an analogous
result in the non-Kähler setting. A recent breakthrough on the
Strominger-Yau-Zaslow conjecture, due to Y. Li
[
41
]
, is the
construction of special Lagrangian torus fibrations in generic regions
of Fermat quintics near the large complex structure limit. A common
theme in both examples is the analysis of metric behavior under
degenerations of the metric or the complex structure of the ambient
Calabi-Yau manifold.

The main goal of this paper is to present a new gluing construction of
special Lagrangian submanifolds in compact Calabi-Yau 3-folds. Let
X

be a compact Calabi-Yau 3-fold with a Lefschetz K3 fibration

\pi:X\to Y
, where the base is
Y=\mathbb{P}^{1}
. For simplicity,
assume each singular fiber contains exactly one singular point. Let

\Omega
be a holomorphic volume form on
X
, and fix reference
Kähler metrics
\omega_{X}
on
X
and
\omega_{Y}
on
Y
, suitably
normalized. For small
t>0
, consider the Kähler class

(1.1)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{t}\pi^{*}[\omega_{Y}]\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R}),

and let
\tilde{\omega}_{t}
denote the unique Calabi-Yau metric
[
68
]

in this class. We denote the ambient Calabi-Yau manifolds as

(X,\tilde{\omega}_{t},\Omega)
, and note that the rescaled metrics

t\tilde{\omega}_{t}
has diameter
\sim 1
and converge in the
Gromov-Hausdorff sense to the generalized Kähler-Einstein metric

\tilde{\omega}_{Y}
on
Y
as
t\to 0
. See Tosatti
[
63
]
for a
comprehensive survey.

Next, we describe the topological setup for our construction. Let

S\subset Y
denote the discriminant locus of the fibration, and
suppose
y_{0},y_{1}\in S
, contained in an open set

U
. Assume the vanishing cycles of
y_{0}
and
y_{1}
in each regular
fiber over
U
are identical up to a sign, and denote the vanishing
cycle in
X_{y}
for each
y\in U
by
L_{y}
. Then the quadratic
differential
\phi=\alpha\otimes\alpha
, where

\alpha(y)=\int_{L_{y}}\Omega
, is well-defined and holomorphic in

U
. We define a path
\gamma\subset U
connecting
y_{0}

and
y_{1}
as admissible if

•

it is a geodesic with respect to the flat metric on
U
induced
by the quadratic differential
\phi
, and

•

each
L_{y}
for
y\in\gamma
can be represented by a special
Lagrangian
2
-sphere.

See Section
5.1
for more details about quadratic
differentials and admissible paths. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an admissible path with phase
\theta
connecting
two points
y_{0},y_{1}
in the discriminant locus
S\subset Y
. Then
for sufficiently small
t>0
, there exists a special Lagrangian

3
-sphere
\tilde{L}_{\gamma,t}
with phase
\theta
in the
Calabi-Yau manifold
(X,\tilde{\omega}_{t},\Omega)
. Let
g_{t}
denote
the induced metric on
\tilde{L}_{\gamma,t}
. As
t\to 0
,

(\tilde{L}_{\gamma,t},tg_{t})
converges in the Gromov-Hausdorff
sense to
\gamma\subset(Y,\tilde{\omega}_{Y})
.

By the nature of adiabatic limits, Theorem
1.1
constructs
special Lagrangian spheres that are elongated and thin, with their
“ends” sharpening as
t\to 0
. This stands in contrast to the more
rounded shape of the special Lagrangian vanishing spheres described in

[
27
]
.

In a similar vein, we can consider admissible loops

\gamma:S^{1}\to Y
, which satisfy the following conditions:

•

There exists a smooth fibration
\pi:L_{\gamma}\to\gamma
whose
fibers are smooth special Lagrangian
2
-cycles, and

•

The parallel transport along
\gamma
fixes

[L_{y}]\in H_{2}(X_{y},\mathbb{Z})
.

•

\gamma
is a geodesic with phase
\theta
with respect to the
quadratic differential associated with the fibers of

\pi:L_{\gamma}\to\gamma
.

•

There exists a horizontal lifting of
\gamma
that satisfies an
integrability condition.

See Definition
5.4
for full details. We note
that a similar integrability condition was discussed by Donaldson
during the 2023 Simons Collaboration on Special Holonomy in Geometry,
Analysis and Physics Annual Meeting.

It follows from the definition that for an admissible loop
\gamma
,
the submanifold
L_{\gamma}
is a mapping torus of some
orientation-preserving fiber diffeomorphism, and the topological
obstructions to perturbing
L_{\gamma}
into a special Lagrangian
submanifold vanish, i.e.,

\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\theta}\Omega)|_{L_{\gamma}}=0
and

[\tilde{\omega}_{t}|_{L_{\gamma}}]=0
. Hence, we have the following
result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
\gamma:S^{1}\to Y
is an admissible loop with phase

\theta
. Then for sufficiently small
t>0
there exists a special
Lagrangian
L_{\gamma,t}
with phase
\theta
in the Calabi-Yau
manifold
(X,\tilde{\omega}_{t},\Omega)
. Let
g_{t}
denote the induced
metric on
L_{\gamma,t}
. As
t\to 0
,
(L_{\gamma,t},tg_{t})

converges in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense to

\gamma\subset(Y,\tilde{\omega}_{Y})
.

As a corollary, we could construct special Lagrangians which are
mapping tori. However, note that the integrability condition imposes
significant constraints on the loop in the moduli space of special
Lagrangian
2
-cycles. It is currently unclear whether examples of
admissible loops exist when the
2
-cycles have higher
genus. Nevertheless, when the
2
-cycles are topologically
S^{2}
, the
integrability condition is automatically satisfied. We can then
construct examples of admissible loops, and consequently special
Lagrangian
S^{1}\times S^{2}
, using a doubling construction (see
Example
6.3
).

We remark that the notion of admissible paths dates back at least to
Klemm-Lerche-Mayr-Vafa-Warner
[
32
, Section 3]
, although they
did not attempt to construct the corresponding special Lagrangians. A
related picture appears in Smith
[
56
, Section 1.4]
, where
admissible paths are studied in the context of Fukaya categories. We
anticipate that our work could connect to the results of
Bridgeland-Smith
[
2
]
and Haiden-Katzarkov-Kontsevich
[
23
]

by providing a more geometric framework for their ideas.

In the
G_{2}
setting, similar constructions of associative
submanifolds in Kovalev-Lefschetz fibrations have been predicted by
Donaldson
[
13
]
and later extended by
Donaldson-Scaduto
[
14
]
. Their program suggests that, in the
adiabatic limit, associative submanifolds should correspond to certain
graphs, known as gradient cycles, in the base
S^{3}
of the
fibration. The concept of gradient cycles generalizes the notion of
admissible paths. Generically, these gradient cycles are trivalent
graphs. Very recently, Esfahani-Li
[
17
]
proved a conjecture of
Donaldson-Scaduto by constructing special Lagrangians in
A_{2}
-type

\mathrm{ALE}\times\mathbb{C}
, whose three ends are asymptotic to
special Lagrangian
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. It would be intriguing to
combine our result with theirs to construct the first examples of
compact special Lagrangians corresponding to trivalent graphs in the
base.

One advantage of our setting, compared to the
G_{2}
case, is that
examples of admissible paths are more readily available. In
Section
6
, we present several examples,
including special Lagrangian
3
-spheres, evidence of wall-crossing
phenomena of Thomas-Yau stability, and the aforementioned examples of
special Lagrangians
S^{1}\times S^{2}
. We also explore connections to
mirror symmetry and the Donaldson-Scaduto conjecture.

We now explain the main ideas of this paper. In Section
2
,
we provide an overview of basic results in special Lagrangian
geometry. In particular, in Section
2.3
, we discuss in
detail the quadratic term that appears in the deformation
problem. One key case is when both the ambient manifold and the
submanifold are cylindrical. This will play a crucial role in
Section
5
.

In Section
3
, we give a refinement of Y. Li’s gluing
construction of the Calabi-Yau metrics
\tilde{\omega}_{t}

[
40
]
. The result roughly says that away from the singular
fibers,
\tilde{\omega}_{t}
is modeled by a “semi-Ricci flat” metric,
while near the singular points of the fibration,
\tilde{\omega}_{t}
is
modeled by a rescaled copy of
\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
, which is a
Calabi-Yau metric on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
with tangent cone

\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
at infinity.

Given an admissible path
\gamma
connecting

y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathcal{S}
, we construct the approximate special
Lagrangians
L_{\gamma,t}
as follows. We take the union of the
special Lagrangian vanishing
2
-spheres
L_{y}
in
X_{y}
for

y\in\gamma
away from
y_{0},y_{1}
. Topologically, this produces a
cylinder
S^{2}\times I
. Near each endpoint
y_{i}
of
\gamma
, we glue
in a scaled copy of the special Lagrangian thimble
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

inside
(\mathbb{C}^{3},\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}})
. See
Proposition
3.1
for its existence. The special
Lagrangian thimble consists of the vanishing special Lagrangian

2
-spheres in the standard Lefschetz fibration

(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\mapsto z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}
.

The original result of
[
40
]
yields large errors in our gluing
construction due to non-optimal estimates for
\tilde{\omega}_{t}
. We
refine the result in
[
40
]
by defining suitable weighted spaces
for better control near the transition region and the region far away
from the singular fibers. This bridges the gap between the gluing
construction in
[
40
]
and the a priori estimates in the work of
Hein-Tosatti
[
28
]
.

The core of the paper lies in the deformation of
L_{\gamma,t}
to
genuine special Lagrangians, detailed in Sections
4

and
5
. One challenge arises from

L_{\gamma,t}
’s degeneracy near its two “ends”. In
Section
5
, we define appropriate weighted
spaces to control the inverse of the Hodge-Dirac operator, employing a
parametrix method inspired by
[
40
]
.

Section
4
addresses the inversion of the Hodge-Dirac
operator on the special Lagrangian thimble
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
. The
equation

(1.2)

\displaystyle\eta\in\Lambda^{1}\mapsto(d+d^{*})\eta=(f,\omega)\in\Lambda^{0}\oplus\Lambda^{2}

involves right-hand terms with polynomial decay, which complicates
applying the usual linear theory due to
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
’s slow
volume growth. To resolve this, we combine the parametrix method from
Székelyhidi
[
59
]
with Hein’s solution
[
21
]
for the
Poisson equation on complete manifolds, improving the solution’s
growth from exponential to polynomial.

Finally, we address difficulties posed by the “base direction” of
the inverse of
d+d^{*}
on
L_{\gamma,t}
as
t\to 0
. The norm of the
inverse of
d+d^{*}
along the base direction depends linearly on the
diameter of
L_{\gamma,t}
. Unlike prior gluing constructions such the
twisted connected sum construction of
G_{2}
manifolds

[
33
]
[
10
]
, our initial errors are not small enough to
offset this linear dependence’s impact on the quadratic term.

To overcome this, we carefully define the weighted spaces in
Section
5
, ensuring that the base direction
is treated separately in the invertibility result (see
Proposition
5.10
). To resolve the issue due to large
quadratic term, we perform the iteration scheme in the range, rather
than the domain, of the linearized operator
d+d^{*}
. Modulo non-linear
effects, the quadratic term differentiates the base direction and we
obtain the necessary quadratic estimates required for the iteration
scheme. These points are discussed in more detail in
Section
5.5
.

2.
Special Lagrangian submanifolds

In this section, we review the notion of special Lagrangian
submanifolds, discuss their basic properties, and introduce existing
constructions that will be useful in later sections. We start by
defining what is meant by a Calabi-Yau manifold in this paper.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a complex manifold with complex dimension
n
.
X
is
Calabi-Yau if there exist a Kähler form
\omega
and a
nowhere-vanishing section
\Omega\in H^{0}(K_{X})
, called the
holomorphic volume form, such that
\omega
and
\Omega
satisfy the
following complex Monge-Ampère equation:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{n}}{n!}=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^{n}\Omega\wedge\overline{\Omega}.

Note that it follows from (
2.1
) that
\omega
is
Ricci-flat.

Let
L\subseteq X
be an oriented Lagrangian submanifold. It follows
from (
2.1
) that we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Omega|_{L}=e^{i\theta_{L}}\operatorname{vol}_{L},

where
\operatorname{vol}_{L}
is the induced volume form on
L
and

\theta_{L}
is an
S^{1}
-valued function. Note
\nabla\theta_{L}
is
well-defined. By direct computation, one can show that the mean
curvature
\mathbf{H}
of
L
is given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=J\nabla\theta_{L}.

We say
L
is zero-Maslov if there exists a lifting of
\theta_{L}
to

\mathbb{R}
. Any such lifting, which we will still denote by

\theta_{L}
, is called a grading of
L
.
L
is graded if
L
is
zero-Maslov and a grading
\theta_{L}
is fixed.

Harvey and Lawson introduced an important class of calibrated
submanifolds in Calabi-Yau manifolds called special Lagrangians
[
25
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

A Lagrangian submanifold
L
in a Calabi-Yau manifold

(X,\omega,\Omega)
is
special Lagrangian
if

\Omega|_{L}=e^{i\theta}\operatorname{vol}_{L}
for some constant

\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. Equivalently, this means that

\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\theta}\Omega)|_{L}=0
and
L
is calibrated
by the calibration form
\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\Omega)
in
the sense of Harvey-Lawson.

Let
L
be a special Lagrangian submanifold. Being calibrated,
L
is
an area minimizer within its homology class. Thus
L
is minimal,
i.e.
\mathbf{H}=0
. Note that this can also be concluded using
(
2.3
).

Very few constructions of compact special Lagrangian submanifolds are
known. In the following we shall review some existing constructions
that will be useful in later sections.

2.1.
Fix loci of anti-holomorphic, anit-symplectic involutions

It is elementary to show the following. Given
(X,\omega,\Omega)
a
Calabi-Yau manifold and
\iota:X\to X
such that

\iota^{2}=\operatorname{Id}
,
\iota^{*}\omega=-\omega
, and

\iota^{*}\Omega=\overline{\Omega}
, then each connected component of
the fix locus
\operatorname{Fix}(\iota)
is a special Lagrangian
submanifold.

Using this, Bryant
[
3
]
constructed special Lagrangian tori in
certain quintic
3
-folds. We will later use this to construct a
special Lagrangian thimble (see Proposition
3.1
).

2.2.
Hyperkähler rotations

Let
(X,\omega,\Omega)
be a Calabi-Yau
2
-fold. Denote
g
the
corresponding Ricci-flat metric and write

(2.4)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}=\operatorname{Re}\Omega,\hskip 14.22636pt\omega_{2}=\operatorname{Im}\Omega,\hskip 14.22636pt\omega_{3}=\omega.

Define
J_{i}
by

(2.5)

\displaystyle g(\cdot,\cdot)=\omega_{i}(\cdot,J_{i}\cdot),\mbox{ for }i=1,2,3.

Then
J_{i}
are integrable complex structures satisfying quaternionic
relations. In particular, the underlying space
\underline{X}
admits
a family of complex structures parametrized by
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, called
the twistor line.

Explicitly, these complex structures are given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle J_{\zeta}=\frac{i(-\zeta+\bar{\zeta})J_{1}-(\zeta+\bar{\zeta})J_{2}+(1-|\zeta|^{2})J_{3}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}},\hskip 8.53581pt\zeta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}.

Let
\omega_{\zeta}=g(J_{\zeta}\cdot,\cdot)
be the corresponding
Ricci-flat Kähler form. The holomorphic symplectic
2
-form

\Omega_{\zeta}
with respect to the compatible complex structure

J_{\zeta}
is given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\zeta}=-\frac{i}{2\zeta}\Omega+\omega_{3}+\frac{i}{2}\zeta\overline{\Omega}.

It is straightforward to check that a submanifold
L
in

\underline{X}
is a holomorphic curve with respect to
J_{0}
if and
only if
L
is special Lagrangian with respect to

(\omega_{e^{i\theta}},\Omega_{e^{i\theta}})
for each

e^{i\theta}\in S^{1}
, the equator of the twistor line.

In particular, we have the following well-known lemma via the
Riemann-Roch theorem (see for instance
[
34
, Lemma 2.1]
):

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(X,\omega,\Omega)
be a K3 surface with
[\omega]\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
. If

[L]\in H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is a homology class with
[C]^{2}\geq-2
,
then there exists a special Lagrangian representative in the
homology class
[L]
with respect to
(\omega,\Omega)
.
Moreover, we have the following:

•

If
[L]^{2}=-2
and
[L]
is represented by a smooth special Lagrangian, then its special Lagrangian representative is unique.

•

If
X
is generic in its deformation family such that
[\omega]
is Kähler and
[L]^{2}=2g-2
, then
[L]
can be represented by a smooth special Lagrangian surface of genus
g
. Moreover, the smooth representatives are paramatrized by the complement of a divisor in
\mathbb{P}^{g}
.

Proof.

Let
e^{i\theta}\in S^{1}
be given by

[\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\theta}\Omega)].[L]=0
. Consider
the hyperkähler rotation
(X_{\theta},\omega_{\theta},\Omega_{\theta})

where

(2.8)

\displaystyle\omega_{\theta}=\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\Omega),\>\>\>\>\Omega_{\theta}=\omega+i\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\theta}\Omega).

Since
[\Omega_{\theta}].[L]=0
, we have

PD[L]\in H^{1,1}(X_{\theta},\mathbb{R})\cap H^{2}(X_{\theta},\mathbb{Z})
. It follows that
PD[L]
is represented by a line
bundle, also denoted by
L
, on
X_{\theta}
. By the Riemann-Roch
theorem for surfaces and Serre duality, we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle h^{0}(L)+h^{0}(L^{*})\geq\chi(\mathcal{O})+\frac{1}{2}[L].([L]-K_{X_{\theta}})\geq 2+\frac{1}{2}\cdot(-2)=1.

So either
L
or
L^{*}
has a nontrivial section
s
, whose zero set
is represented by a holomorphic curve
L
in
X_{\theta}
with

[L]^{2}\geq-2
, which is special Lagrangian with respect to the
original hyperkähler structure
(X,\omega,\Omega)
.

Suppose
[L]
is represented by a smooth special Lagrangian
L

and another (possibly singular) special Lagrangian
L^{\prime}
. Then they
must have the same phase

\theta=\operatorname{Arg}\int_{[L]}\Omega
. After hyperkähler
rotation,
L,L^{\prime}
are holomorphic curves in the same K3 surface

X_{\theta}
. By adjunction formula,
L
is a smooth rational
curve in
X_{\theta}
. Recall that the intersections of distinct
holomorphic cures are non-negative. Since
L.L^{\prime}=[L]^{2}=-2
and
L

is smooth,
L^{\prime}
must contain
L
as a component. Then
L^{\prime}-L
is an
effective divisor in
X_{\theta}
with
[L^{\prime}-L]=0
. If

L^{\prime}-L\neq 0
, then
\int_{L^{\prime}-L}\omega_{\theta}>0
and we reach a
contradiction. This shows the uniqueness part of the lemma.

Finally, for the last part of the lemma, we may assume

[\omega]\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
after a scaling. Assume that
X

is generic in the sense that
[L]=[L_{1}]+[L_{2}]
implies

\operatorname{Arg}\int_{[L_{1}]}\Omega\neq\operatorname{Arg}\int_{[L_{2}]}\Omega
, then
[L]
is represented
by an irreducible special Lagrangian from the earlier
discussion. In particular, it is represented by a base point free
divisor if
[L]^{2}\geq 0
and thus
[L]
can be represented by a
smooth special Lagrangian by the Bertini theorem. If

[L]^{2}=2g-2\geq 0
and
[L]
is represented by a smooth special
Lagrangian surface or
[L]^{2}=-2
, then by hyperkähler rotation
and adjunction formula,
L
has genus
g
. By the Riemann-Roch
theorem, the linear system
|L|
in
X_{\theta}
is

\mathbb{P}^{g}
.
∎

2.3.
Deformations of special Lagrangian submanifolds

McLean
[
47
]
showed that first-order deformations of a compact
special Lagrangian submanifold
L
are unobstructed, and the local
moduli space is parametrized by
H^{1}(L,\mathbb{R})
. A good reference
for a precise treatment of McLean’s theorem, as well as its
generalizations, can be found in Marshall’s
thesis
[
46
]
. The main focus in this subsection is the
nonlinear counterpart of McLean’s theorem, that is to deform
approximate special Lagrangian submanifolds to genuine ones. This is
done through a contraction mapping argument, and a key step involves
estimates for the quadratic term. See,
e.g.,
[
4
]
[
30
]
[
35
]
[
36
]
. See also

[
8
, Section 5]
for a good exposition.

Let
(X,\omega,\Omega)
be a compact Calabi-Yau
n
-fold. Let

f:L\to X
be a real
n
-dimensional compact submanifold. Assume
that
\omega|_{L}
is exact and
\int_{L}\operatorname{Im}\Omega=0
. Furthermore, we assume that
|\omega|_{L}|_{g_{L}}<1
, so that for
any local orthonormal frame
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
of
L
, the vector
fields
e_{1},Je_{1},e_{2},Je_{2},\ldots,e_{n},Je_{n}
form a local frame of

TX|_{L}
. For a
1
-form
\eta
on
L
, taking the symplectic dual
produces a vector field
V
along
L
with values in
TX
, via

\omega(V,\cdot)=\eta
. Note that if
L
is Lagrangian, then
V
is
a normal vector field. Using the Riemannian exponential map, we define
the deformation map
f_{\eta}:L\to X
by

. Here

is the orthogonal

\displaystyle F:\Lambda^{1}(L)\to\Lambda^{0}(L)\oplus\Lambda^{2}(L)

defined by the formula

\displaystyle F(\eta)=*f_{\eta}^{*}\operatorname{Im}\Omega+f_{\eta}^{*}\omega.

From this we see that

. The linearization of

\displaystyle DF_{0}(\eta)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}F(t\eta)=L_{V^{\perp}}\omega+*(L_{V^{\perp}}\operatorname{Im}\Omega)|_{L}

where

denotes the Lie derivative along the vector field

.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\eta\in\Lambda^{1}(L)
. The linearization
DF_{0}
of

at
the origin satisfies

(2.13)

\displaystyle DF_{0}(\eta)=(d+d^{*})\eta+E(\eta).

The error term

E(\eta)=d\iota(V^{\perp}-V)\omega+*(L_{V^{\perp}}\operatorname{Im}\Omega)|_{L}-d^{*}\eta

satisfies

(2.14)

\displaystyle\|E(\eta)\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\leq C(\|\omega|_{L}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}+\|\operatorname{Im}\Omega|_{L}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}})\|\eta\|_{C^{1,\alpha}},

with the constant
C>0
depending on the Riemann curvature tensor of

(X,\omega)
and the second fundamental form of
L
.

Proof.

This is essentially a pointwise calculation. Fix a local orthonormal
frame
e^{1},e^{2},\ldots,e^{n}
on
L
and let
f^{j}=-Je^{j}
. Define

\omega_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{j}\wedge f^{j}
and

\Omega_{0}=\Pi_{j=1}^{n}(e^{j}+\sqrt{-1}f^{j})
. Define the dual vector
field
V_{0}
of
\eta
with respect to
\omega_{0}
by

\omega_{0}(V_{0},\cdot)=\eta
. Since
\Omega
is closed,

L_{V}\operatorname{Im}\Omega=d\iota(V)\operatorname{Im}\Omega
. We
then compute

(2.15)

\displaystyle\iota(V)\operatorname{Im}\Omega

\displaystyle=\iota(V)(\operatorname{Im}\Omega-\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle+\iota(V-V_{0})\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0}

\displaystyle+\iota(V_{0})\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0}.

Note that
\iota(V_{0})\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0}=-*\eta
by a
standard pointwise computation, so

d^{*}\eta=-*d*\alpha=*d\iota(V_{0})\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0}
. It
remains to estimate

and
\Omega-\Omega_{0}
. Write

\Omega=u(z)\Omega_{0}
. Then we have

\Omega\wedge\overline{\Omega}=|u(z)|^{2}\Omega_{0}\wedge\overline{\Omega}_{0}
, so

\omega^{n}=|u(z)|^{2}\omega_{0}^{n}
by the normalization
(
2.1
) of the Calabi-Yau condition. Expanding
around
\omega_{0}
, we see that

(2.16)

\displaystyle|u|^{2}=1+O(|\omega-\omega_{0}|)

in arbitrary orders. Locally we can write

. Then

(2.17)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\Omega-\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0}

\displaystyle=|u|(\cos\theta\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0}+\sin\theta\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{0})-\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0}

\displaystyle=((|u|-1)\cos\theta+(\cos\theta-1))\operatorname{Im}\Omega+\sin\theta\operatorname{Re}\Omega.

It follows that

(2.18)

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{Im}\Omega-\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0}\|_{C^{k}}\leq C_{k}(\|\omega-\omega_{0}\|_{C^{k}}+\|\sin\theta\|_{C^{k}}),

where the constants

depend on the
(X,\omega,\Omega)
and
the second fundamental form of
L
. A direct computation then shows
that

(2.19)

\displaystyle\|V-V_{0}\|_{C^{k}}\leq C_{k}\|\omega-\omega_{0}\|_{C^{k}}\|\eta\|_{C^{k}}.

Finally, for the difference
V^{T}=V-V^{\perp}
, we have

(2.20)

\displaystyle g(V^{T},e_{i})=g(V,e_{i})=g(JV,Je_{i})=\omega(V,Je_{i})=\eta((Je_{i})^{T}).

Since

\|(Je_{i})^{T}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}\leq C\|\omega|_{L}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}
, the
result follows.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

From now on, we will write

\displaystyle=\mathrm{(I)}+\mathrm{(II)}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{(I)_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\ell}(F(\eta_{\ell})-DF_{0}(\eta_{\ell}))\,d\ell

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|\eta_{1}|_{C^{1,\alpha}}+|\eta_{0}|_{C^{1,\alpha}})(|\eta_{1}|_{C^{1,\alpha}}+|\eta_{0}|_{C^{1,\alpha}})|\eta_{1}-\eta_{0}|_{C^{1,\alpha}}.

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\ell}(DF_{0}(\eta_{\ell})-(d+d^{*})\eta_{\ell})\,d\ell

\tilde{\omega}_{t}
the unique Calabi-Yau metric in it.

In this section, we refine the construction of Calabi-Yau metrics

\tilde{\omega}_{t}
on
X
, building upon Y. Li’s work in
[
40
]
. The
original result,
[
40
, Theorem 4.1]
, introduces substantial
initial errors in our gluing construction, as the weighted spaces used
there do not offer precise enough control over the metric behavior in
the transition from singular fibers to the semi-Ricci flat region.

On the other hand, Hein-Tosatti
[
28
]
obtained smooth
asymptotics of the Calabi-Yau metrics
\tilde{\omega}_{t}
at a fixed
distance from the singular fibers with respect to the background
metric
\omega_{X}
, which seems to provide better control in these
regions compared to
[
40
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Even when combining the results of
[
40
]
and
[
28
]
,
additional refined estimates are still required in the transition
region. Our approach introduces a new weight away from the singular
fibers that is compatible with the weighted norms from
[
40
]
in
the transition region. This method bridges the gap between the two
previous results.

The main result of this section is Theorem
3.7

below. See also Propositions
3.8
and
3.9

for applications of the improved estimates. We note that the necessary
estimates, prior to being reformulated in terms of weighted norms,
were already obtained in
[
40
, Section 2]
. Therefore, we will
focus on explaining the modifications necessary to adapt Li’s results
to our new weighted norms.

3.1.
Calabi-Yau metrics on
\mathbb{C}^{3}

A main ingredient of Li’s gluing construction is a Calabi-Yau metric

\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, whose tangent cone at
infinity is given by the Calabi-Yau cone

\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. This Calabi-Yau metric

\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
was constructed independently by
Y. Li
[
39
]
, Székelyhidi
[
59
]
and
Conlon-Rochon
[
11
]
using similar but different
techniques. Li
[
40
, Remark 9]
observed that this metric is
unique within its “asymptotic class”. Székelyhidi
[
60
]

later proved that such metric is unique up to automorphism of

\mathbb{C}^{3}
and scaling.

Following the exposition in
[
40
]
, let
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
be the
coordinates of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Define the functions

(3.3)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=(|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}+|z_{3}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}},

\displaystyle\tilde{y}

\displaystyle=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2},

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\sqrt{|\tilde{y}|^{2}+\sqrt{R^{4}+1}}.

Then the map
\tilde{y}:\mathbb{C}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}
defines the
standard Lefschetz fibration. Alternatively, we can think of

\mathbb{C}^{3}
as a hypersurface in
\mathbb{C}^{4}
. Note that the
central fiber
\{z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}=0\}
is biholomorphic to

\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
equipped with the flat
cone metric
\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}R^{2}
. Any smooth fiber is
biholomorphic to

\{1=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{3}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
, which admits
the Eguchi-Hanson metric

\omega_{EH}=\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\sqrt{R^{4}+1}
.

The Calabi-Yau metric

\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}=\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\phi_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
has normalization

(3.4)

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}^{3}=\frac{3}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\sqrt{-1}dz_{i}\wedge d\bar{z}_{i},

whose asymptotics at infinity is given by

(3.5)

\displaystyle\phi_{\infty}=\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{y}|^{2}+\sqrt{R^{4}+\rho},\>\>\>\phi_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}=\phi_{\infty}+\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}.

For
K\gg 0
, the function
\rho
is uniformly equivalent to the
distance function to the origin on
\{\rho>K\}
. Thus the metric

\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
admits three distinct behaviors, together with
transition behaviors in the overlapping regions. In the large compact
set
\{\rho<K\}
, the metric is uniformly equivalent to a Euclidean
metric. When
\rho>K
but
R\lesssim\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}
, we are
near the vanishing cycles
\{R=|\tilde{y}|^{\frac{1}{4}}\}
. Note
that each vanishing cycle is represented by a special Lagrangian
S^{2}

with respect to the rescaled Eguchi-Hanson metric in the fiber. In
this region, the Calabi-Yau metric
\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
is modeled
on the warped product metric

\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}|\tilde{y}|^{2}+|\tilde{y}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{EH}
. When
\rho>K
but
R\gtrsim\rho
,

\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
is modeled on

\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\left(|\tilde{y}|^{2}+R^{2}\right)
, the
product cone metric on
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

The actual analysis for estimating the error term

\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
thus requires the doubly weighted spaces

C^{k,\alpha}_{\delta,\tau}(\mathbb{C}^{3},\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}})
first
introduced in
[
59
]
. Let
\kappa>0
be a fixed small
number. Define a weight function
w
by

(3.6)

\displaystyle w=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $R\geq 2\kappa\rho$},\\
R/(\kappa\rho)&\text{if $R\in(\kappa^{-1}\rho^{\frac{1}{4}},\kappa\rho)$},\\
\kappa^{-2}\rho^{-\frac{3}{4}}&\text{if $R<\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{-1}\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}$}.\end{cases}

We now define the Hölder seminorm of a tensor
T
by

(3.7)

\displaystyle{}_{0,\alpha}=\sup_{\rho(z)>K}\rho(z)^{\alpha}w(z)^{\alpha}\sup_{z\neq z^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in B(z,c)}\frac{|T(z)-T(z^{\prime})|}{d(z,z^{\prime})^{\alpha}}.

Here
c>0
is such that the ball
B(z,c)
has bounded geometry and is
geodesically convex. The expression
|T(z)-T(z^{\prime})|
is defined by
parallel transporting
T(z^{\prime})
along a geodesic connecting
z
and

z^{\prime}
.

The weighted norm of a function
f
is then defined by

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\delta,\tau}}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\rho<2K)}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sup_{\rho>K}\rho^{-\delta+j}w^{-\tau+j}|\nabla^{j}f|

\displaystyle+[\rho^{-\delta+k}w^{-\tau+k}\nabla^{k}f]_{0,\alpha}.

Equivalently, the weighted norms can also be defined as

(3.9)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\delta,\tau}}=\|\rho^{-\delta}w^{-\tau}f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\rho^{-2}w^{-2}\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}}}.

Similar weighted norms can also be defined for tensors. For example,
for a
2
-form
\omega
, one can define

(3.10)

\displaystyle\|\omega\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\delta-2,\tau-2}}=\|\rho^{-\delta}w^{-\tau}\omega\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\rho^{-2}w^{-2}\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}}}.

The error term
\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
can then be estimated as

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\delta,0}}\leq C(k,\alpha,\delta)

for all
\delta^{\prime}>-1
(see, e.g.,
[
40
, Eq. (15)]
). By unwinding
the definition of the weighted norms, we see that

(3.12)

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}|_{\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}}\leq C(k,\alpha,\delta^{\prime})\rho^{\delta^{\prime}-\frac{k}{4}}

for any
-1<\delta^{\prime}<0
. In particular,
\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
has
at most subquadratic polynomial growth. This enables us to prove the
following:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\iota:\mathbb{C}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}^{3}
be the anti-holomorphic
involution
\iota(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=(\bar{z}_{1},\bar{z}_{2},\bar{z}_{3})
. Then
\iota^{*}\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}=-\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
. In particular, the real locus

(3.13)

\displaystyle L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}=\{(\tilde{y},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\in\mathbb{C}^{4}\mid\tilde{y},z_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\>\tilde{y}=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}\}

is a special Lagrangian submanifold in

(\mathbb{C}^{3},\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}})
.

Proof.

First we note that the leading asymptotic
\phi_{\infty}
and the
distance function
\rho
are both invariant under
\iota
. Consider
the Calabi-Yau metric
-\iota^{*}\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
which
satisfies

(-\iota^{*}\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}})^{3}=\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}^{3}
. Note
that

(3.14)

\displaystyle-\iota^{*}\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}-\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}=\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}(\phi_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\circ\iota-\phi_{\mathbb{C}^{3}})=\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}(\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\circ\iota-\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}).

Since
\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\circ\iota-\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
has
subquadratic polynomial growth, by
[
7
, Theorem 1.3]
, we have

\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\circ\iota-\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}=0
. So

\iota^{*}\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}=-\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
.
∎

Proposition
3.1
can also be proved using elementary
methods such as integration by parts, given that the error

\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
actually decays at infinity. The same method
can also be applied to show that for each Calabi-Yau metric
constructed in
[
59
]
and
[
6
]
, there exists a special
Lagrangian submanifold.

Before moving on, we take the opportunity to discuss horizontal
differentiation. For details, see
[
40
, p.1021]
. Near the
thimble
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
, the Calabi-Yau metric

\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
is roughly the warped product metric

\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\partial\bar{\partial}|\tilde{y}|^{2}+|\tilde{y}|^{1/2}\omega_{EH}
. Thus fiberwise differentiation
introduces a factor of
|\tilde{y}|^{-1/4}
. However, the horizontal
differentiation introduces a factor of
|\tilde{y}|^{-1}
, due to the
fact that a lift of
\partial_{\tilde{y}}
with respect to the
Euclidean metric is given by

(3.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\bar{z}_{i}}{2|z|^{2}}\partial_{z_{i}}.

An application of this principle which is crucial to this paper is
the following:

Proposition 3.2
.

Fix
-1<\delta^{\prime}<0
. For
\rho>0
sufficiently large and

R<\kappa^{-1}\rho^{1/4}
(i.e., we are in vicinity of the
vanishing cycles or equivalently close to
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
), write

(3.16)

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}-(\omega_{\mathbb{C}}+R^{2}\omega_{EH})=(f,f)+(b,b)+(f,b),

where
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
is the Euclidean metric on

\mathbb{C}_{\tilde{y}}
,
(f,f)
stands for the fiber directions,

(b,b)
stands for the base directions, and
(f,b)
stands for the
mixed directions. Then we have

(3.17)

\displaystyle|(f,f)|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}})},|(b,b)|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}})}

\displaystyle\leq C(k)\rho^{\delta^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{k}{4}},

\displaystyle|(f,b)|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}})}

\displaystyle\leq C(k)\rho^{\delta^{\prime}-\frac{5}{4}-\frac{k}{4}}.

Proof.

By (
3.12
), we have

\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}-(\omega_{\mathbb{C}}+R^{2}\omega_{EH})=\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
with

\|\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\delta^{\prime},0}}\leq C(k,\alpha,\delta^{\prime})
for any
\delta^{\prime}>-1
. The estimates for

(f,f),(b,b)
follow from the definition of the weighted norms,
while for
(f,b)
, we additionally apply the principle of horizontal
differentiation.
∎

3.2.
The approximate solutions

We now describe Li’s construction of the approximate solutions

\omega_{t}
. In the following, we will pretend that the Lefschetz
fibration
\pi:X\to Y
only has one singular fiber over
0\in Y
,
whose singular point is given by
P\in X
. This assumption is purely
for notational simplicity. We can choose local coordinates

\mathfrak{z}_{1},\mathfrak{z}_{2},\mathfrak{z}_{3}
in a neighborhood

U_{1}
around
P
and a local coordinate
y
on
Y
such that

y=\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{2}+\mathfrak{z}_{2}^{2}+\mathfrak{z}_{3}^{2}
. We
define

r=(|\mathfrak{z}_{1}|^{2}+|\mathfrak{z}_{2}|^{2}+|\mathfrak{z}_{3}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}
. We extend
r
to a function on
X

by requiring
r=O(1)
outside the coordinate neighborhood.

The generalized Kähler-Einstein metric
\tilde{\omega}_{Y}
on
Y
can
be described as follows. Given the local coordinate
y
on
Y
, we
write
\Omega=dy\wedge\Omega_{y}
. Then

(3.18)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{Y}=\pi_{*}(\sqrt{-1}\Omega\wedge\overline{\Omega})=\sqrt{-1}A_{y}dy\wedge d\bar{y},

where
A_{y}=\int_{X_{y}}\Omega_{y}\wedge\overline{\Omega}_{y}
is a
Lipschitz function
[
40
, Lemma 2.1]
. It follows that in these
local coordinates, the holomorphic volume form is given by

(3.19)

\displaystyle\Omega=\sqrt{A_{0}}d\mathfrak{z}_{1}\wedge d\mathfrak{z}_{2}\wedge d\mathfrak{z}_{3}(1+O(\mathfrak{z})).

Each fiber
X_{y}
admits a Calabi-Yau metric, denoted as

\omega_{SRF}|_{X_{y}}
, satisfying

(3.20)

\displaystyle\omega_{SRF}|_{X_{y}}=\omega_{X}|_{X_{y}}+\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\psi_{y},\>\>\>\>(\omega_{SRF}|_{X_{y}})^{2}=\frac{1}{A_{y}}\Omega_{y}\wedge\overline{\Omega}_{y}.

Here the potential function
\psi_{y}
satisfies

\int_{X_{y}}\psi_{y}\omega_{X}^{2}=0
. For
0<|y|<\epsilon_{1}\ll 1
,

\omega_{SRF}|_{X_{y}}
is also obtained by a gluing construction by
replacing a neighborhood of
P
in
X_{0}
by a scaled copy of the
Eguchi-Hanson metric

\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}(\sqrt{r^{2}+|y|})
. See
Proposition
3.30
below.

One can thus consider the semi-Ricci flat metric

(3.21)

\displaystyle\omega_{SRF}=\omega_{X}+\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\psi+\frac{1}{t}\pi^{*}\tilde{\omega}_{Y},

where
\psi=\psi_{y}
is a global function on
X
. Note that

\omega_{SRF}
is neither smooth nor necessarily positive-definite. It
is however a good description of the collapsing Calabi-Yau metric

\tilde{\omega}_{t}
away from the singular fibers.

The approximate solutions
\omega_{t}
are obtained by gluing scaled
copies of the metric
\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
and the semi-Ricci flat
metric
\omega_{SRF}
together. It is instructive to follow

[
40
, p.1014]
to identify the “quantization scale”. To do so,
note that near
y=0
, the semi-Ricci flat metric has the following
asymptotic:

(3.22)

\displaystyle\omega_{SRF}\sim\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}(\sqrt{r^{2}+|y|}+\frac{1}{t}A_{0}|y|^{2}).

Let
F_{t}:U_{1}\to\mathbb{C}^{3}
denote the coordinate change

(3.23)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{z}_{i}=\left(\frac{t}{2A_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}z_{i},\>\>\>\>r=\left(\frac{t}{2A_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{6}}R,\>\>\>\>y=\left(\frac{t}{2A_{0}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\tilde{y}.

It follows that

(3.24)

\displaystyle\sqrt{r^{4}+|y|}+\frac{1}{t}A_{0}|y|^{2}=\left(\frac{t}{2A_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\sqrt{R^{4}+|\tilde{y}|}+|\tilde{y}|^{2}\right)\sim\left(\frac{t}{2A_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\phi_{\infty},

which is the scaled asymptotic Kähler potential of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

We now turn to describing the Calabi-Yau metrics on the K3 fibers near

y=0
, following
[
40
, Section 2.2]
. See also
[
57
]
. As
indicated before, near the vanishing cycle in
X_{y}
, the Calabi-Yau
metric is modeled on the scaled Eguchi-Hanson metric

EH_{y}=\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\sqrt{r^{2}+|y|}
. Thus
r
can be
thought of as a smoothing of the distance function to the vanishing
cycle. Given this, we define the weighted norm of a function
f
on

X_{y}
as

(3.25)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta}(X_{y})}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(X_{y}\setminus\{r>c\},\omega_{X})}+\sum_{j\leq k}\sup_{X_{y}\cap U_{1}}r^{-\beta+j}|\nabla^{j}_{EH_{y}}|

\displaystyle+\sup_{d_{EH_{y}}(x,x^{\prime})\ll r(x),x,x^{\prime}\in X_{y}\cap U_{1}}r(x)^{-\beta+\alpha+k}\frac{|\nabla_{EH_{y}}^{k}f(x)-\nabla_{EH_{y}}^{k}f(x^{\prime})|}{d(_{EH_{y}}(x,x^{\prime})^{\alpha}},

where we use parallel transport along the unique minimal geodesic
connecting
x,x^{\prime}
to measure the difference of two tensors. The
constant
c>0
is chosen small enough so that
\{r<c\}
is contained
in the coordinate neighborhood
U_{1}
.

Equivalently, we can also define the weighted norm as

(3.26)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta}(X_{y})}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(X_{y}\setminus\{r>c\},\omega_{X})}+\|r^{-\beta}f\|_{C^{k},\alpha(X_{y}\cap U_{1},r^{-2}EH_{y})},

and we can define similar norms for tensors. For example, if
\omega

is a
2
-form on
X_{y}
, then we define

(3.27)

\displaystyle\|\omega\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta-2}(X_{y})}

\displaystyle=\|\omega\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(X_{y}\setminus\{r>c\},\omega_{X})}+\|r^{-\beta}f\|_{C^{k},\alpha(X_{y}\cap U_{1},r^{-2}EH_{y})}.

Let
1\ll\Lambda_{1}\ll\epsilon_{1}^{-1/4}
be a large number. By a
flow along the vector field orthogonal to the fibers with respect to
the Riemannian metric induced by
\omega_{X}
, we have a fiberwise
preserving diffeomorphism

(3.28)

\displaystyle G_{0}:\{x\in X||y|<\epsilon\}\setminus\{r<\Lambda_{1}|y|^{1/4}\}\cong\big(X_{0}\setminus\{r<\frac{1}{2}\Lambda_{1}|y|^{1/4}\}\big)\times\{|y|<\epsilon_{1}\},

and write
G_{0,y}=G_{0}|_{X_{y}}
. Using this, we can write down the
approximate Calabi-Yau metric
\omega_{y}^{\prime}
on
X_{y}
as follows. Let

\gamma_{1}(s)
be a cutoff function such that

(3.29)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(s)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if }s>2,\\
0&\mbox{ if }s<1,\end{cases}

and
\gamma_{2}=1-\gamma_{1}
. Define

(3.30)

\displaystyle\omega_{y}^{\prime}=\omega_{X}|_{X_{y}}+\sqrt{-1}\left\{\gamma_{1}\left(\frac{r}{|y|^{1/6}}\right)G_{0,y}^{*}(\psi_{0}-c_{0})+\gamma_{2}\left(\frac{r}{|y|^{1/6}}\right)\sqrt{r^{2}+|y|}\right\}.

This glues the Eguchi-Hanson metric to the orbifold Calabi-Yau metric
modeled on
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. It can be shown that for

\epsilon_{1}
sufficiently small,
\omega_{y}^{\prime}
is a genuine Kähler
metric for
|y|<\epsilon_{1}
. Furthermore, the Ricci potential of

\omega^{\prime}_{y}
with respect to

\frac{1}{A_{y}}\Omega_{y}\wedge\overline{\Omega}_{y}
is sufficiently small
for perturbing
\omega^{\prime}_{y}
to the Calabi-Yau metric

\omega_{SRF}|_{X_{y}}
. In sum, we have

Proposition 3.3

(
[
40
, Proposition 2.4]
)
.

Let
-2<\beta<0
and
0<\epsilon_{1}\ll 0
. There is a unique
potential function
\psi_{y}^{\prime}
with
\int_{X}\psi_{y}^{\prime}\omega_{y}^{\prime 2}=0

such that

(3.31)

\displaystyle\omega_{SRF}|_{X_{y}}=\omega_{y}^{\prime}+\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\psi_{y}^{\prime},

with the estimate

(3.32)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{y}^{\prime}\|_{C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\beta}(X_{y})}\leq C(k,\alpha,\beta)|y|^{-\frac{1}{6}\beta+\frac{2}{3}}.

We remark that this estimate is crucial for showing the existence of
the special Lagrangian vanishing sphere in
X_{y}
. In particular, it
provides an estimate for the vanishing cycle’s deviation from being
special Lagrangian in
X_{y}
. This will be useful later in Section 5.2.

When considering deformation of
\omega_{SRF}|_{X_{y}}
as the complex
structure varies in
y
, especially when
|y|<\epsilon_{1}
, it is
helpful to use the reference metric
\omega_{X}|_{X_{y}}
instead.

Given
|y^{\prime}|<\epsilon_{1}
, consider the trivialization

(3.33)

\displaystyle G_{y^{\prime}}:\{x\in X:|\pi(x)-y^{\prime}|<\epsilon_{2}|y^{\prime}|\}\to X_{y^{\prime}}\times\{y\in Y:|\pi(x)-y^{\prime}|<\epsilon_{2}|y^{\prime}|\}

defined in a similar manner as before. For
|y-y^{\prime}|<\epsilon_{2}|y^{\prime}|
,
this induces the diffeomorphism
G_{y^{\prime},y}:X_{y}\to X_{y^{\prime}}
, which
depend smoothly on
y
. We have the following:

Lemma 3.4

(
[
40
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

The potential functions
\psi_{y}
in (
3.20
) satisfy the
following. For
-2<\beta<0
,

(3.34)

\displaystyle\|G_{y^{\prime},y}^{*}\psi_{y^{\prime}}-\psi_{y}\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta}(X_{y})}\leq C(k,\alpha,\beta)|y-y^{\prime}||y^{\prime}|^{{-\frac{1}{4}(\beta+2)}}

when
|y^{\prime}|<\epsilon_{1}
and
|y-y^{\prime}|<\epsilon_{2}|y^{\prime}|
. When

|y^{\prime}|\geq\epsilon_{1}
and
|y-y^{\prime}|<\epsilon_{2}|y^{\prime}|
, we have a better estimate

(3.35)

\displaystyle\|G_{y^{\prime},y}^{*}\psi_{y^{\prime}}-\psi_{y}\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta}(X_{y})}\leq C(k,\alpha,\beta)|y-y^{\prime}|.

Here
|y-y^{\prime}|
means the distance
d_{\omega_{Y}}(y,y^{\prime})
when one of
y,y^{\prime}
is
outside the coordinate neighborhood.

We choose a partition of unity
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
on the base
Y
such that

•

0\leq\chi_{i}\leq 1
and
\chi_{i}
has uniform
C^{k}
-bound with respect to the metric
\frac{1}{t}\omega_{Y}
for any
k
;

•

\chi_{0}=1
on
\{|y|\leq t^{\frac{6}{14+\tau}}\}
for some

-2<\tau<0
, and the support of
\chi_{0}
is contained in

\{|y|\leq 2t^{\frac{6}{14+\tau}}\}
;

•

for
\geq 1
,
\chi_{i}
has support centered at
y_{i}\in Y

with diameter of scale
t^{\frac{1}{2}}
and is contained in

\{|y|>t^{\frac{6}{14+\tau}}\}
.

•

The number of non-vanishing
\chi_{i}
at a point
y
is bounded independent of
t
.

With all the above notations, for
t>0
sufficiently small, the
approximate solution
\omega_{t}
is given by

(3.36)

\displaystyle\omega_{t}

\displaystyle=\omega_{X}+\frac{1}{t}\tilde{\omega}_{Y}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}G^{*}_{y_{i}}\psi_{y_{i}}+\chi_{0}\left(c_{0}+\gamma_{1}\left(\frac{r}{t^{1/10}+t^{1/12}\rho^{\prime 1/6}}\right)G^{*}_{0}(\psi_{0}-c_{0})\right.\right.

4.
Analysis on the special Lagrangian thimble

In this section, we discuss the analysis on the local model for the

inside

-forms has a bounded right inverse
from its image with respect to suitable weighted spaces. We begin by

4.1.
Geometry of the special Lagrangian thimble

We will use the notations introduced in
Section

. Recall that we embed
\mathbb{C}^{3}
into

\mathbb{C}^{4}
as the hypersurface given by the total space of the
trivial Lefschetz fibration

(4.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{y}=\pi(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2},

and
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
is the real locus of the above hypersurface. To
understand the asymptotic geometry of
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
, it is enough
to understand the Calabi-Yau metric
\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
near

L
. In the region when
|\tilde{y}|\gg 0
and near

L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
, the radial distance
\rho
is equivalent to

|\tilde{y}|
. So we will write
\tilde{y}
and
\rho
interchangeably
when as functions on
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
when their values are
large. Similarly, we will identify
R
with
\rho^{1/4}
as functions
on
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
. By making these function equal to

on a
compact set containing the origin, we may assume that
R,\rho
are
smooth.

Define the semi-flat metric

(4.2)

\displaystyle\omega_{EH\times\mathbb{C}}=i\partial\bar{\partial}\phi_{EH\times\mathbb{C}},

where

(4.3)

\displaystyle\phi_{EH\times\mathbb{C}}=\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{y}|^{2}+\sqrt{R^{4}+|\tilde{y}|}.

Thus on each fiber
\pi^{-1}(\tilde{y})
,
\omega_{EH\times\mathbb{C}}

restricts to the Eguchi-Hanson metric whose distance is scaled up by
the factor
|\tilde{y}|^{1/4}
.

We would like to know the error going from

\omega_{EH\times\mathbb{C}}
to
\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
. By direct
differentiation, we see that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\nabla^{k}(\phi_{\infty}-\phi_{EH\times\mathbb{C}})=\nabla^{k}\left(\frac{|\tilde{y}|-\rho}{\sqrt{R^{4}+\rho}+\sqrt{R^{4}+|\tilde{y}|}}\right)=O(\rho^{-1-k})

for
\rho\gg 0
near
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
. On the other hand, we have
(
3.12
). It follows that

i\partial\bar{\partial}\phi_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}^{\prime}
gives the dominant error
going from
\omega_{EH\times\mathbb{C}}
to

\omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}
. We now restrict to
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
. write

g_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}
for the induced metric of
\tilde{\omega}_{t}
on

L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
, and write

g_{0}=g_{EH\times\mathbb{C}}|_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}
. Note that up to
negligible errors, the metric
g_{0}
is exactly the warped product
metric

, where
g_{S^{2}}
is the
round metric on
S^{2}
. We have the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
g
be the induced metric on
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
. Then for any

\delta^{\prime}>-1
, we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle|g-g_{0}|_{C^{k}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}},g)}\leq C(k,\delta^{\prime})\rho^{\delta^{\prime}-1/2-k/4}

A few things can be deduced from the asymptotic geometry. First, the
volume growth of the special Lagrangian thimble is
\rho^{3/2}
, so we
are in the situation strictly between asymptotically cylindrical and
asymptotically conical. Second, note that we can perform the following
conformal change of coordinates:

(4.6)

\displaystyle g_{0}=d\rho^{2}+\rho^{1/2}g_{S^{2}}=\rho^{1/2}(d\rho^{2}/\rho^{1/2}+g_{S^{2}})=R^{2}(ds^{2}+g_{S^{2}}),

where

. It follows that the rescaled metric

is asymptotically translation invariant in the sense of

[

]
. However, this metric is not asymptotically
cylindrical in the strong sense, since the rate of convergence of

R^{-2}g_{0}=ds^{2}+g_{S^{2}}
as

is only
polynomial instead of exponential. We will see that the standard
Fredholm theory for manifolds with ends is insufficient for
establishing the required mapping properties for the Hodge-Dirac
operator

.

4.2.
Weighted spaces

Lockhart
[
42
]
considered the Fredholm theory on manifolds
with ends, where the metrics, after conformal change, are
asymptotically translation invariant. One can easily check that the
metric
g_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
on the special Lagrangian thimble

L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
is admissible in the sense of
[
42
]
by
taking derivatives of the conformal factor
R^{2}
. Lockhart then
defined weighted Sobolev spaces by introducing the exponential weight
function

, and showed that the Hodge-Laplace operator and
Hodge-Dirac operator acting on forms are Fredholm if the exponential
weight avoids a discrete set in
\mathbb{R}
. In our case the discrete
set is
\{\pm\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\}
, where
\lambda_{i}
are the
eigenvalues of the Hodge-Laplace operator acting on functions on

S^{2}
. One can similarly define weighted Hölder spaces and show
analogue results. For an

-form
u
, define

(4.7)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}=\|e^{-\beta s}\rho^{-\delta}R^{-r}u\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{R^{-2}g}},

and define

to be the space of
r
-forms such that the above norm is finite. Note
that we use
\rho
for the polynomial weight function instead of
R
,
which is used in Lockhart
[
42
]
. Since
\rho=R^{4}
, our
weights differ from theirs by a constant multiple.

We will write

C^{k,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})=C^{k,\alpha}_{0,\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
when the exponential weight
is trivial. Following
[
42
]
, we have

Proposition 4.2
.

The map

\Delta:C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})\to C^{k-2,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta-1/2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
is Fredholm if
and only if
\beta\neq\pm\sqrt{\lambda}
for all eigenvalues

\lambda
of the Laplacian acting on functions on
S^{2}
. For

\beta<0
, the map is injective. For
\beta>0
with

\beta<\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
, where
\lambda_{1}
is the first
eigenvalue, the map has a one-dimensional kernel, is surjective, and
has a bounded right inverse.

Similarly, the map

d+d^{*}:C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))\to C^{k-1,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta-1/4}(\Lambda^{0}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})\oplus\Lambda^{2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))

is Fredholm if and only if
\beta\neq\sqrt{\lambda}
for all
eigenvalues
\lambda
of
S^{2}
.

Proof.

Lockhart proved the weighted Sobolev version of the Fredholm
property. See
[
42
, Theorem 5.11]
. To get the weighted
Hölder version, one can argue similarly as in Marshall’s
thesis
[
46
, Theorem 6.9]
. Since the kernel is finite
dimensional, there exists a complement closed subspace

\mathcal{A}\subset C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
so that

C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})=\mathcal{A}\oplus\operatorname{ker}\Delta
. Since
\Delta
has closed range, it
follows from the open mapping theorem that there exists a bounded
right inverse from its range. That the map is injective when

\beta<0
follows from integration by parts. Now, suppose that

0<\beta<\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
. By self-adjointness, we have

\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{coker}\Delta_{\beta,\delta}=\operatorname{dim}\ker\Delta_{-\beta,-\delta-3}=0
.
By the index jump formula
[
43
]
, we have

2\operatorname{dim}\ker\Delta_{\beta,\delta}=2
.

The statement about
d+d^{*}
is entirely similar.
∎

Proposition
4.2
immediately implies the following:

Proposition 4.3
.

For
0<\beta<\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
, the Hodge-Dirac operator

(4.8)

\displaystyle d+d^{*}:C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta+1/4}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))\to C^{k-1,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(\Lambda^{0}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})\oplus\Lambda^{2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))

is injective. In particular, there is a constant

C(\beta,\delta)>0
such that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|\eta\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta+1/4}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C\|(d+d^{*})\eta\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}

for all
\eta\in C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))
.

Proof.

Let

\eta\in C^{k,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))

such that
(d+d^{*})\eta=0
. By elliptic regularity,
\eta
is
smooth. Since
H^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})=0
, by the Poincaré lemma
there exists

u\in C^{k+1,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta+1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
such that

\eta=du
. It follows that
u
is harmonic. By
Proposition
4.2
,
u
is constant. So
\eta=0
.
∎

In our application, the right hand side lies in

C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}
for some
\delta<0
. But by
Proposition
4.2
the map

d+d^{*}:C^{1,\alpha}_{\delta+1/4}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))\to C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})\oplus C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(\Lambda^{2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))
is not
Fredholm. To remedy this, one could enlarge the space on the left hand
side by defining

\tilde{C}^{1,\alpha}_{0,\delta+1}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))
to be
the closure of

C^{1,\alpha}_{0,\delta+1/4}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))
in

C^{1,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta+1/4}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))
with
respect to the norm

(4.10)

\displaystyle\|\eta\|_{\beta}=\|\eta\|_{C^{1,\alpha}_{\beta,\delta+1/4}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}+\|(d+d^{*})\eta\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{0,\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}.

Here
\beta>0
. One can then argue as in

[
42
, Theorem 5.10]
to show that the definition of the space

\tilde{C}^{1,\alpha}_{0,\delta+1/4}(\Lambda^{1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))
does not depend on
the choice of
\beta>0
, and all the norms
\|\eta\|_{\beta}
are
equivalent. Then the map

(4.11)

\displaystyle d+d^{*}:\tilde{C}^{2,\alpha}_{0,\delta+1/4}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})\to C^{0,\alpha}_{0,\delta}(\Lambda^{0}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})\oplus\Lambda^{2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))

is Fredholm and in particular has a bounded right inverse from its
image. The control of exponential growth for the solutions is quite
coarse, which prevents us from performing the iteration scheme in
Section
5
.

The remainder of this section is dedicated to improving the above
mapping properties of the Hodge-Dirac operator, ensuring that the
solutions actually have polynomial growth.

4.3.
Analysis on cylinders

Let
M
denote a closed Riemannian manifold. The goal of this
subsection is show that the Hodge-Dirac operator
d+d^{*}
, acting on

1
-forms on
\mathbb{R}\times M
, is invertible with respect to
suitably defined subspaces. This result builds on the works of
Walpuski
[
65
, Appendeix A]
and
Székelyhidi
[
59
, Section 5]
, both of which, in turn, build on
Brendle
[
1
]
, we begin by formally inverting the Hodge-Dirac
operator.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
denote the
\mathbb{R}
coordinate, and let

x\in M
. Any
1
-form on
\mathbb{R}\times M
is of the form

(4.12)

\displaystyle f(t,x)\,dt+\eta(t,x),

where
\eta
has values in
\Lambda^{1}(M)
. It is clear that
dt
lies
in the kernel of
d+d^{*}
. Suppose that
(d+d^{*})(f\,dt+\eta)=0
. By
direct computation, we have

(4.13)

\displaystyle\tilde{d}f-\eta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}\eta

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-f^{\prime}+\tilde{d}^{*}\eta

\displaystyle=0.

Here tilde denotes the operators on
M
and prime is the derivative
with respect to
t
. We will adopt these notations in the rest of this
subsection. For any forms
F(t,x)\in\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}\times M)
,
we define the Fourier transform

(4.14)

\displaystyle\hat{F}(\xi,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\xi t}F(t,x)dt.

Thus, formally taking the Fourier Transform of the above equations, we get

(4.15)

\displaystyle\tilde{d}\hat{f}-i\xi\hat{\eta}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}\hat{\eta}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-i\xi f+\tilde{d}^{*}\eta

\displaystyle=0.

From the first and third equations we deduce

(4.16)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Delta}+\xi^{2})\hat{f}=0.

Here
\Delta_{H}=\tilde{d}^{*}\tilde{d}
is the Hodge-Laplace operator. It
follows that
\hat{f}
must be a linear combination of derivatives of
the Dirac delta function. Taking the inverse Fourier transform, we
conclude that
f
is constant. It follows that
\eta
is a harmonic

1
-form on
M
independent of
t
.

Given the above heuristics, we now define the relevant subspaces. Let

C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
be the set of
elements in
C^{k,\alpha}(\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
that are
“fiberwise
L^{2}
-orthogonal to harmonic forms on
M
”. More
precisely, let

u\in C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
:

•

If
u=u(t,x)
is a function, then
\int_{M}u(t,\cdot)=0
.

•

If
u=f(t,x)\,dt
, then
\int_{M}f(t,\cdot)=0
.

•

If
u=\eta(t,x)
is a
1
-form tangent to
M
, then

\eta(t,\cdot)
is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to harmonic
1
-forms on
M
.

•

If
u=\mu(t,x)\wedge dt
is a
2
-form with
\mu
tangent to

M
, then
\mu
is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to harmonic
1
-forms on
M
.

•

If
u=\omega(t,x)
is a
2
-form tangent to
M
, then

\int_{M}\omega(t,\cdot)=0
.

The main result of the subsection is the following.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
K^{2}
be the space of closed
2
-forms. The Hodge-Dirac operator

(4.17)

\displaystyle d+d^{*}:C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))\to C^{k-1,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times M)\oplus K^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times M))

is an isomorphism, with the inverse
P=(d+d^{*})^{-1}
having
operator norm
\|P\|\leq C
, where
C
depends on
M
. Moreover, if

(4.18)

\displaystyle(d+d^{*})\varphi=\psi

where the support of
\psi
lies in
(-R,R)\times M
for some

R>0
, then
\varphi
has exponential decay.

We prove this proposition by establishing the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

\chi\in C^{\infty,ave}(\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times M)\oplus K^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
such that the Fourier transform
\hat{\chi}

has compact support away from
0\in\mathbb{R}
. Then we can find

f\,dt+\eta\in C^{\infty,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M)
such
that

(4.19)

\displaystyle(d+d^{*})(f\,dt+\eta)=\chi.

Moreover,
f\,dt+\eta
has exponential decay in
t
, i.e., for any

a>0
, there exist a constant
C
such that

\|f\,dt+\eta\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(|t|>A)}\leq C(1+A)^{-a}
. A
similar statement for the Hodge-Laplacian

\Delta=(d+d^{*})^{2}:C^{1,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\to C^{0,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
also holds.

Proof.

Write
\chi=g+dt\wedge\mu+\nu
, where

g=g(t,x)\in C^{\infty,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
,

\mu=\mu(t,x)\in C^{\infty,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(M))
, and

\nu=\nu(t,x)\in C^{\infty,\alpha,ave}(K^{2}(M))
. The equations we want
to solve are

(4.20)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}-\tilde{d}f

\displaystyle=\mu,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}\eta

\displaystyle=\nu,

\displaystyle-f^{\prime}+\tilde{d}^{*}\eta

\displaystyle=g.

Taking Fourier transform, the equations become

(4.21)

\displaystyle i\xi\hat{\eta}-\tilde{d}\hat{f}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu},

\displaystyle\tilde{d}\hat{\eta}

\displaystyle=\hat{\nu},

\displaystyle-i\xi\hat{f}+\tilde{d}^{*}\hat{\eta}

\displaystyle=\hat{g}.

These imply that

(4.22)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Delta}+\xi^{2})\hat{f}=i\xi\hat{g}-\tilde{d}^{*}\hat{\mu},

which we can solve since
\tilde{\Delta}+\xi^{2}
is
invertible. Taking the inverse transform, we can solve for
f
. To
solve for
\eta
, we simply take the inverse Fourier transform of
the first equation. The second equation is then automatically
satisfied using the closed condition. That
f
and
\eta
have
exponential decay in
t
is follows from the fact that
\hat{f}
and

\hat{\eta}
have compact support.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that
(d+d^{*})\varphi=0
for some

\varphi\in C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
. Then
\varphi=0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\varphi
is nonzero. By elliptic regularity,
\varphi

is smooth. Then there exists

\chi\in C^{\infty,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
with

\hat{\chi}
having compact support such that

\int\langle\varphi,\chi\rangle\neq 0
. Applying
Lemma
4.5
, we can find
h
such that

\Delta h=\chi
. We then compute

(4.23)

\displaystyle\int\langle\varphi,\chi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\langle\varphi,\Delta h\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\langle\Delta\varphi,h\rangle=0,

which is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

For
\varphi\in C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
, we have

(4.24)

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}\leq C\|(d+d^{*})\varphi\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}.

In particular,
d+d^{*}
has closed range.

Proof.

By the Schauder estimates, we have

(4.25)

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}\leq C\|(d+d^{*})\varphi\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}+C\|\varphi\|_{C^{0}}.

To obtain the bound for the inverse, it is enough to show that

(4.26)

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{C^{0}}\leq C\|(d+d^{*})\varphi\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}.

Suppose for contradiction that there exist

\varphi_{i}\in C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
with

\|\varphi_{i}\|_{C^{0}}=1
and

\|(d+d^{*})\varphi_{i}\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}<1/i
. By the Schauder
estimates above, after passing to a subsequence, we have

\varphi_{i}\to\varphi
in
C^{k,\alpha^{\prime}}
for some

\varphi\in C^{k,\alpha^{\prime},ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
. Here
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
. It follows that
(d+d^{*})\varphi=0
. By Lemma
4.6
, we see that
\varphi=0
,
a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

It is enough to show that the map

(4.27)

\displaystyle d+d^{*}:C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))\to C^{k-1,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times M)\oplus K^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times M))

is surjective, since Lemma
4.6
implies that
this map is injective. Let

\psi=(f,\omega)\in C^{k-1,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times M)\oplus K^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
. We cannot apply
Lemma
4.5
directly, since the forms in
Lemma
4.5
do not form a dense set.

Instead, we proceed as follows. By Hodge theory, we can find

\zeta\in C^{k,\alpha}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
such that

d\zeta=\omega
,
d^{*}\zeta=0
and

\|\zeta\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}\leq C
, with the constant
C>0
depending
on
\omega
. We can then find a sequence of smooth
f_{i}
’s and a
sequence of smooth
\zeta_{i}
’s such that their Fourier transforms
have compact support,
f_{i}\to f
and
\zeta_{i}\to\zeta
locally
uniformly, and

\|f_{i}\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}+\|\zeta_{i}\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}<C_{1}
for
some constant
C_{1}>0
depending on
\psi
. By
Lemma
4.5
, there exist

\eta_{i}\in C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
such
that
(d+d^{*})\eta_{i}=\psi_{i}=(f_{i},d\zeta_{i})
, while by
Proposition
4.7
,

\|\eta_{i}\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}\leq C\|\psi_{i}\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}\leq CC_{1}
. By passing to a subsequence, we can take a limit

\eta_{i}\to\eta
in

C^{k,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times M))
so that

(d+d^{*})\eta=\psi
with

\|\eta\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}\leq C\|\psi\|_{C^{k-1,\alpha}}
. This
completes the proof.
∎

4.4.
Approximating the Green function

The goal in this subsection is to construct the following Green type
functions on
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
:

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
N>10
. Then there exists a smooth function

G:L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that (1)
G=O(\rho^{1/2})
, (2)

\Delta G=O(\rho^{-N})
, and (3)
\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\Delta G=1
. Moreover,

\|dG\|_{C^{1,\alpha}_{\delta^{\prime}+1/4}}<\infty
.

Note that
\rho^{1/2}
is exactly the Green function for the warped
product metric
g_{0}=d\rho^{2}+\rho^{1/2}g_{S^{2}}
, since the Green
function is given by
\rho^{2-\nu}
, where
\nu=3/2
is the volume
growth. This can also be seen directly from computing the Laplace
operator

(4.28)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{0}}=\partial_{\rho}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\rho}\partial_{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{1/2}}\Delta_{S^{2}}.

By Proposition
4.1
, we have

(4.29)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}(\rho^{1/2})=\Delta_{g_{0}}(\rho^{1/2})+(\Delta_{g}-\Delta_{g_{0}})(\rho^{1/2})=O(\rho^{-2+\delta^{\prime}})

for
\rho\gg 0
, where
\delta^{\prime}
is in
Proposition
4.1
. To improve the decay on the right hand
side, we need to subtract suitable functions from the left hand
side. This is the content of the following lemma:

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
f\in C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
. Then for
A>0
sufficiently
large, there exist
u_{1}\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+1/2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
and

u_{1}\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+2}(\pi(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))
such that

\Delta(u_{1}+u_{2})=f
for
\rho>A
. Moreover,

(4.30)

\displaystyle\|u_{1}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+1/2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})},

\displaystyle\|u_{2}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(\pi(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))},

where
C
does not depend on
A
.

Proof.

We follow closely the strategy in
[
59
, Proof of
Proposition 22]
. First construct a collection of cutoff
functions
\chi_{i}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\sum_{i}\chi_{i}=1

on
\{\rho>A\}
. Choose a large
B>0
. Equip
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
the metric

\tilde{g}=B^{-2}\tilde{y}^{-1/2}d\tilde{y}^{2}=B^{-2}ds^{2}
. We then
cover
\{\rho>A\}
by disks
B_{\tilde{g}}(\tilde{y}_{i},2)
of
radius
2
with respect to
\tilde{g}
with center
\tilde{y}_{i}
such
that the disks
B_{\tilde{g}}(\tilde{y}_{i},1)
are disjoint. We can
then define cutoff functions
\chi_{i}
supported on

B_{\tilde{g}}(\tilde{y}_{i},2)
and equal to
1
on

B_{\tilde{g}}(\tilde{y}_{i},1)
. Scaling back, we get cutoff functions

\chi_{i}
with support in
B(\tilde{y}_{i},2B\tilde{y}_{i}^{1/4})
and are
equal to
1
on
B(\tilde{y}_{i},B\tilde{y}_{i}^{1/4})
. Here the metric
is
d\tilde{y}^{2}
. Note that we have

|\nabla^{l}\chi_{i}|_{d\tilde{y}^{2}}=O(B^{-l}\tilde{y}_{i}^{-l/4})
. These
cutoff functions will be used to decompose the right hand side
f

to functions defined on the cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
. Similarly, we construct cutoff functions
\tilde{\chi}_{i}
which
are equal to
1
on the support of
\chi_{i}
, and are supported in

B(\tilde{y}_{i},3B\tilde{y}_{i}^{1/4})
. The cutoff functions

\tilde{\chi}_{i}
are used to transfer the solution of the Poisson
equation on the cylinder back to
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
.

For each
i
, we have the following decomposition:

(4.31)

\displaystyle\chi_{i}f=f_{i}+f_{i}^{\prime},

where

f_{i}^{\prime}(\tilde{y})=\operatorname{Vol}(S^{2}_{\tilde{y}})^{-1}\int_{S^{2}_{\tilde{y}}}\chi_{i}f(\tilde{y},z)
is the
fiberwise average of
\chi_{i}f
. Here the metric on
S^{2}_{\tilde{y}}

is the pullback of round metric on
S^{2}
by the scaling map. It
follows that
f_{i}=\chi_{i}f-f_{i}^{\prime}
can be viewed as a function
defined on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
, with zero fiberwise average with
respect to the product structure. We define the base direction of

f
to be
f_{0}=\sum_{i}f_{i}^{\prime}
.

Since the metric
g_{L}
is equivalent to

\tilde{y}_{i}^{1/2}(ds^{2}+g_{S^{2}})
on the support of
f_{i}
,

\Delta\sim\tilde{y}_{i}^{-1/2}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}}
. There
exists a unique
u_{i}
on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
such that

\Delta_{\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}}u_{i}=\tilde{y}_{i}^{1/2}f_{i}
, with

\|u_{i}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times S^{2})}\leq C\tilde{y}_{i}^{1/2}\|f_{i}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times S^{2})}
.

To see that
\tilde{\chi}_{i}u_{i}
defines an approximate right inverse,
we estimate

(4.32)

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}}(\tilde{\chi}_{i}u_{i})-\tilde{y}_{i}^{1/2}f_{i}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times S^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\tilde{\chi}_{i}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times S^{2})}\|u_{i}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times S^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq CB^{-2}\|f_{i}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times S^{2})}.

By letting
B\gg 0
, we can make the constant on the right hand
side less than
1/100
. Rescaling back to the metric
g_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}
and
multiplying by
\tilde{y}_{i}^{-\delta-1/2}
, we get

(4.33)

\displaystyle\|\Delta(\tilde{\chi}_{i}u_{i})-f_{i}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}}\leq\frac{1}{100}\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}}.

We now turn to the base direction
f_{0}
. The PDE at infinity is given by

(4.34)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{0}}u_{0}=f_{0}.

Since
f_{0}
only depends on the base direction, the above PDE reduces to the ODE

(4.35)

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2\rho}u_{0}^{\prime}=f_{0}.

Recall that the fundamental solutions are
h_{1}=1
and

h_{2}=\rho^{1/2}
. From these we can calculate the Wronskian

W=h_{1}h_{2}^{\prime}-h_{2}h_{1}^{\prime}=-(1/2)\rho^{-1/2}
. A particular solution
is then given by

(4.36)

\displaystyle u_{p}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-h_{1}(\rho)\int\frac{h_{2}(\rho)f_{0}(\rho)}{W(\rho)}d\rho+h_{2}(\rho)\int\frac{h_{1}(\rho)f_{0}(\rho)}{W(\rho)}d\rho

\displaystyle=\int 2\rho f_{0}(\rho)d\rho-2\rho^{1/2}\int\rho^{1/2}f_{0}(\rho)d\rho.

Thus a general solution is given by
u_{0}=c_{1}h_{1}+c_{2}h_{2}+u_{p}
. We may
choose the initial conditions
u_{0}(\rho_{0})=u_{0}^{\prime}(\rho_{0})=0
for
some
\rho_{0}<A
. Then from the above formula we have

(4.37)

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+2}}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}},

with

(4.38)

\displaystyle\|\Delta u_{0}-f_{0}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}}\leq\|(\Delta-\Delta_{0})u_{0}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}}\leq CA^{-1/2+\delta^{\prime}}\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}}.

By letting
A\gg 0
we can make the constant on the right hand side less than
1/100
.

We now define the approximate right inverse

(4.39)

\displaystyle Pf=u_{0}+\sum_{i}\tilde{\chi}_{i}u_{i}.

From the above estimates we have

(4.40)

\displaystyle\|\Delta(Pf)-f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}}\leq\frac{1}{50}\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}}.

Thus we can write down a genuine right inverse

(4.41)

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}f=P\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(I-\Delta P)^{k}f.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.8
.

Let
f=\Delta(\rho^{1/2})
. Then we have

\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{-3/2+\delta^{\prime}}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}<\infty
. By
Lemma
4.9
, there exists
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
with

\|u_{1}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta^{\prime}-1}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta^{\prime}}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}
and

\|u_{2}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta^{\prime}+1/2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta^{\prime}}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}
such that
\Delta(u_{1}+u_{2})=f

for
\rho>A
. Set
G=2/\pi(\rho^{1/2}-(u_{1}+u_{2}))
. So (1) and
(2) are satisfied. To prove (3), we integrate by parts and see that

(4.42)

\displaystyle\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\Delta G

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\int_{B(0,\rho)}\Delta G

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\int_{\partial B(0,\rho)}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\rho}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\frac{\rho^{1/2}}{2\pi}\int_{S^{2}}\frac{\partial\rho^{1/2}}{\partial\rho}

\displaystyle=1.

The third equality holds because
\partial_{\rho}u_{i}
’s all have fast enough
decay. This completes the proof.
∎

4.5.
Inverting the Laplacian on
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Proposition 4.10
.

For
\delta<0
sufficiently close to
0
, there exists a bounded
map

(4.43)

\displaystyle P:C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})\to C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+1/2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})\oplus C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+2}(\pi(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}))

such that

.

The strategy for proving Proposition
4.10
is to apply
Hein’s result
[
21
, Theorem 1.5]
. We state the case relevant to
our argument.

Proposition 4.11

(
[
21
, Theorem 1.5]
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a complete, noncompact Riemannian manifold of
dimensions
n>2
. Suppose that
(M,g)
satisfies the

\operatorname{SOB(\beta)}
condition with
\beta\leq 2
. Let

f\in C^{0,\alpha}(M)
satisfy
|f|\leq C(1+r)^{-\mu}
for some

\mu>2
, with
\int_{M}f=0
. Then there exists

u\in C^{2,\alpha}(M)
such that
\Delta u=f
. Moreover,

\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}<\infty
. Here

is the distance function to
a fixed point.

For the definition of the
\operatorname{SOB}(\beta)
condition, see

[
21
, Definition 1.1]
. Using the asymptotic geometry discussed
in the first subsection, it is easily seen that the special Lagrangian
thimble

satisfies the
\operatorname{SOB}(\beta)

condition with
\beta=3/2
. Thus Proposition
4.11

applies. However, Proposition
4.11
cannot be applied
directly to prove Proposition
4.10
for the following
reasons:

•

The right hand side in Proposition
4.11
does not
decay faster than quadratically.

•

The right hand side
f
does not necessarily satisfy

\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}f=0
.

•

Proposition
4.11
does not immediately give the
effective bounds needed for the right inverse. It does give an

L^{\infty}
bound, but we need decay in
L^{\infty}
to get higher

In the remainder of this subsection, we deal with the points above,
proving Proposition
4.10
. Let

f\in C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
with
\delta<0
. By
Lemma
4.9
, there exist
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
with

\Delta(u_{1}+u_{2})=f
when
\rho>A
, where
A
is a fixed large
number. Let
f_{1}=f-\Delta(u_{1}+u_{2})
. Then
f_{1}
has compact
support. To apply Proposition
4.11
, we need to modify
f_{1}

so that it has average
0
. For this we set

f_{2}=f_{1}-(\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}f_{1})\Delta G
, where
G
is from
Proposition
4.8
. It follows that
\int_{L}f_{2}=0
.

At this point we can apply Proposition
4.11
: there exists

\tilde{u}\in C^{0,\alpha}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})
with

\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}|\nabla\tilde{u}|^{2}<\infty
such that

\Delta\tilde{u}=f_{2}
. Define

(4.44)

\displaystyle u=\left(\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}f_{1}\right)G+u_{1}+u_{2}+\tilde{u}.

Then we have
\Delta u=f
. The coefficient in front of
G
can be estimated as

(4.45)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}f_{k+1}\right|\leq\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}|f_{k+1}|\leq\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\rho^{\delta+k\delta^{\prime}}\|f_{k+1}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta+\delta^{\prime}}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C(k)\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}.

It remains to show that

\|\tilde{u}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+1/2}}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}}
for some uniform constant
C
. Given
that Proposition
4.11
already gives an
L^{\infty}
bound, we
still need to show that
\tilde{u}
has fast decay in order to apply
the (weighted) Schauder estimates. For this, we will follow the
strategy laid out in the proof of
[
26
, Theorem 4.1]
. First we
prove the following:

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\Delta u=f
with
\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}|\nabla u|^{2}<\infty
and

f=O(\rho^{-N})
for
N>100
. Define

. Then for all
sufficiently large
\rho>0
, we have
Q_{\rho}\leq C\rho^{-N+3/2}

for a constant
C
depending on the
L^{\infty}
bound of

.

Proof.

We write
B_{\rho}=B(0,\rho)
,
B_{\rho}^{c}=L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\setminus B_{\rho}
, and let

(4.46)

\displaystyle\bar{u}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Vol}(\partial B_{\rho})}\int_{\partial B(\rho)}u

be the
L^{1}
average of
u
on
\partial B(0,\rho)
. Integrating by parts gives

(4.47)

\displaystyle Q_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}^{c}}u\frac{\partial u}{\partial\rho}-\int_{B_{\rho}^{c}}uf

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}^{c}}(u-\bar{u})\frac{\partial u}{\partial\rho}+\int_{\partial B_{\rho}^{c}}\bar{u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\rho}-\int_{B_{\rho}^{c}}uf

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}^{c}}(u-\bar{u})\frac{\partial u}{\partial\rho}+\int_{B_{\rho}^{c}}(\bar{u}-u)f

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}(u-\bar{u})^{2}}\sqrt{\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\rho}\right)^{2}}+\int_{B_{\rho}^{c}}(\bar{u}-u)f

\displaystyle\leq C\rho^{1/4}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}|\nabla u|^{2}+\int_{B_{\rho}^{c}}(\bar{u}-u)f

\displaystyle=-C\rho^{1/4}\partial_{\rho}Q_{\rho}+\int_{B_{\rho}^{c}}(\bar{u}-u)f

\displaystyle\leq-C\rho^{1/4}\partial_{\rho}Q_{\rho}+C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\rho^{-N+3/2}.

Integrating the above inequality from
\rho/2
to
\rho

(
\rho\gg 0
), we see that

(4.48)

\displaystyle Q_{\rho}\leq C\rho^{-N+3/2}.

∎

Once we have Lemma
4.12
at hand, we can argue
similarly as in Step 3 in the proof of
[
26
, Theorem 4.1]
. The
conclusion is that we have
|\tilde{u}|\leq C\rho^{-N+3/2}
for some
uniform constant

. This together with the weighted Schauder
estimates implies that that

(4.49)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{-N+3/2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C\|\tilde{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C\|f_{k+1}\|_{L^{\infty}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}.

Here the last constant
C
depends on
k
. By choosing

large
enough, we can use the basic property of the weighted norms to
conclude that

(4.50)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\delta+1/2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq\|\tilde{u}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{-N+3/2}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}.

This completes the proof Proposition
4.10
.

We now turn to inverting the Hodge-Dirac operator acting on

Proposition 4.13

Fix
\delta<0

The rest of this subsection is devoted to the proof of

Lemma 4.14
.

Let

5.
The main construction

In this section, we give the main construction of this paper, proving
Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Let

(X,\tilde{\omega}_{t},\Omega)
be a Calabi-Yau 3-fold as in
Section
3
. Recall that there exists a Lefschetz map

\pi:X\to Y=\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and the discriminant locus is denoted by

S
. For simplicity of notation we assume that for each
y\in Y
, the
fiber
X_{y}
contains at most one singular point. We first discuss the
topological conditions needed for the main construction.

5.1.
Quadratic differentials

Let
U\subset Y
be a connected open set and

[L_{y_{*}}]\in H_{2}(X_{y},\mathbb{Z})
for some fixed

y_{*}\in U\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
[L_{y_{*}}]
is fixed up to
sign by the monodromy along any loop within
U\setminus\mathcal{S}
. Denote by
[L_{y}]
be the parallel transport of

[L_{y_{*}}]
along a path within
U\setminus\mathcal{S}
. For
simplicity, we may omit the subscript
y
when no confusion
arises. For each
y\in U\setminus\mathcal{S}
, define

\alpha(y)=\int_{[L_{y}]}\Omega
. In a local coordinate
y
on
Y
,
if we write
\Omega=dy\wedge\Omega_{y}
, then we can write

\alpha(y)=(\int_{[L_{y}]}\Omega_{y})\,dy
.
\alpha
is a nowhere
vanishing
1
-form on
U\setminus\mathcal{S}
, well-defined up to
sign. To get rid of the sign ambiguity, we define the quadratic
differential
\phi=\alpha\otimes\alpha
on
U\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
Locally
\phi=f(y)\,dy\otimes dy
for some holomorphic function

f(y)
.

An important class of examples is when
[L_{y}]
is the homology class
of the vanishing cycle for some
y_{0}\in U\cap\mathcal{S}
. If

|U\cap\mathcal{S}|\geq 2
, we will further assume the vanishing
cycles from different singular fibers are the same up to sign via
parallel transport along paths in
U\setminus\mathcal{S}
. Then we
have
[L_{y}]^{2}=-2
and the monodromy around
y_{0}
sends
[L_{y}]
to

-[L_{y}]
by the Picard-Lefschetz formula. In this case, one can
naturally extend the quadratic differential over
U
.

Lemma 5.1
.

When
[L]
is a vanishing cycle,
\phi
extends to a holomorphic
quadratic differential over each
y_{i}\in U\cap\mathcal{S}
with a
simple zero at
y_{i}
.

Proof.

By a local computation (see e.g.
[
38
, Proposition 2.2]
), we
see that the integral
\alpha=\int_{[L_{y}]}\Omega_{y}
is locally a
holomorphic
1
-form, and
\alpha(y)\to 0
as
y\to y_{i}
. From the
Picard-Lefschetz formula the monodromy around each
y_{i}
sends

[L_{y}]
to
-[L_{y}]
and thus
\phi
is well-defined holomorphic
quadratic differential on
U
. It follows from the removable
singularity theorem of holomorphic functions that
\phi
is
well-defined on
U
. That
\phi
has simple zero again follows from
a local computation, noting the fact that the diameter of the
vanishing cycle
L_{y}
scales as
|y|^{1/4}
.
∎

The quadratic differential
\phi
defines a flat metric

g_{\phi}=|f(y)dy^{\otimes 2}|
on
U
, whose cone angle at each

y_{i}\in U\cap\mathcal{S}
is
3\pi
. Given two points

y_{0},y_{1}\in U
, the geodesic connecting them with respect to

g_{\phi}
minimize the functional

(5.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}|\sqrt{f(y)}dy|

among the paths
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow U
such that

\gamma(0)=y_{0}
and
\gamma(1)=y_{1}
. Locally the geodesics
satisfy the constraint

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{f(y)}\frac{dy}{ds}=e^{i\theta}

after parametrization by arc length
s
, for some

\theta\in S^{1}
. This
\theta
is called the phase of the geodesic. It
is well-known that geodesics of the same phase form a foliation of

U
. Moreover, for any path
\gamma\subset U
, we always have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}|\sqrt{f(y)}dy|\geq\left|\int_{\gamma}\sqrt{f(y)}dy\right|,

and equality holds if and only if (
5.2
) holds along

\gamma
. In the physics literature (see
[
55
]
), the length of

\gamma
is known as the mass of
\gamma
, and this inequality is
referred to as the BPS inequality. The solutions to this inequality,
i.e. the geodesics, are called BPS solitons.

Our focus is on geodesics that connect two points in
\mathcal{S}

when
[L]
is the vanishing cycle, or closed geodesics when
[L]
is
any cycle. In either case, the phase of the geodesic is given by the
phase of the central charge

(5.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\sqrt{f(y)}dy,

where
\gamma
is any path homotopic to the geodesic, making the phase
a topological property.

The following definition is equivalent to the notion of gradient
cycles as discussed in
[
13
]
[
14
]
, but here it is
reformulated to align with classical results concerning quadratic
differentials.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
U\subset Y
be a
connected open set such that the vanishing cycles
L_{y}
of
each
y_{i}\in S\cap U
match up to sign in
X_{y}
, for
y\in U
. Let

\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow U
be a path connecting
y_{0},y_{1}\in S
.
We say
\gamma

(1)

\gamma
is the geodesic with respect to the quadratic

(2)

For each

, The vanishing cycle

can be
represented as a smooth special Lagrangian

in

.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to U
be a geodesic with respect to
\phi
with

\theta

\operatorname{Arg}\phi(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})=2\theta

\operatorname{Im}\big(e^{-i\theta}\Omega|_{L_{\gamma}}\big)=0
.

Proof.

The geodesic condition reads

(5.5)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Arg}\alpha(\gamma^{\prime})=\theta.

On the other hand, since
L_{y}\subset X_{y}
is special Lagrangian,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Arg}\Omega_{y}|_{L_{y}}=\operatorname{Arg}\int_{L_{y}}\Omega_{y}.

It follows that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Arg}\Omega|_{L_{\gamma}}=\operatorname{Arg}\Omega_{y}|_{L_{y}}+\operatorname{Arg}\gamma^{\prime}=\operatorname{Arg}\alpha(\gamma^{\prime})=\theta.

∎

Definition 5.4
.

A loop
\gamma:S^{1}\rightarrow Y

satisfying the

(1)

[L]
is fixed by the parallel transport along
\gamma

5.2.
The approximate solutions

Let

3.7

For simplicity, let us assume

(5.9)

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(s)=\gamma_{1}(s/t^{9/20})\gamma(s)+\gamma_{2}(s/t^{9/20})s,

\displaystyle W:\left(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\cap\left\{|\tilde{y}|<2\left(\frac{t}{2A_{0}}\right)^{-\frac{2}{3}}t^{\frac{9}{20}}\right\}\right)\to L_{\gamma,t}\cap\{|y|<2t^{\frac{9}{20}}\}.

\displaystyle|\nabla\chi_{0}^{\prime}|_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\leq Ct^{1/6+1/20}

\displaystyle|\tilde{\eta}_{2}|_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}

\displaystyle|\nabla\chi_{0}^{\prime}|_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}|\tilde{\eta}_{2}|_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\leq Ct^{23/60}.

It follows in the
\mathcal{B}
norm, the cutoff error is bounded by

\displaystyle\|d\chi_{0}^{\prime}\wedge P_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\psi_{0}\|_{\mathcal{B}}

Since

, by making

sufficiently small, we have

(5.44)

\displaystyle\|(d+d^{*})P_{0}\psi-\psi_{0}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\frac{1}{100}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

Now we turn to dealing with
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}
. On each

\omega_{i}\in C^{\infty,ave}(K^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}))

for the preimage of
(y_{i}^{1/2}f_{i},\omega_{i})
under the
Dirac operator on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
obtained using

4.4
. Scaling back, we see that

\displaystyle\|P_{y_{i}}\psi\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}\leq y_{i}^{1/4}\|\psi\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}.

We use the cutoff functions

\chi_{i}^{\prime}=\gamma_{2}\left(\frac{|y-y_{i}|}{\Lambda_{3}t^{1/2}}\right)
to
piece them together:

(5.46)

\displaystyle P_{1}\psi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\chi}_{i}P_{y_{i}}\psi_{i}.

By letting
\Lambda_{3}\gg 0
and
t
sufficiently small, we get

(5.47)

\displaystyle\|(d+d^{*})P_{1}\psi-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\frac{1}{100}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}

and

(5.48)

\displaystyle\|P_{1}\psi\|_{\mathcal{C}}\leq C\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

Finally, we deal with

. Equip

\tilde{\gamma}
with the induced metric from

. We define the average

of

along
{\tilde{\gamma}}

along
\tilde{\gamma}

(5.49)

\displaystyle u(s)=-\operatorname{Vol}(L_{s})^{-1}\int_{0}^{s}\operatorname{Vol}(L_{s})(f_{\tilde{\gamma}}-\bar{f}_{\tilde{\gamma}})\,ds,

where

\tilde{\gamma}

Note that this is well-defined since

\tilde{\gamma}

To estimate

\displaystyle\operatorname{Vol}(L_{s})^{-1}\int_{t^{9/20}}^{y}\operatorname{Vol}(L_{y})^{-1}|f_{\tilde{\gamma}}|\frac{dy}{t^{1/2}}\leq C\operatorname{Vol}(L_{s})^{-1}\int_{t^{9/20}}^{y}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}y^{-1/2}dy\leq C\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

\displaystyle=d^{*}_{\tilde{\gamma}}P_{\tilde{\gamma}}\psi+(d^{*}-d^{*}_{\tilde{\gamma}})P_{\tilde{\gamma}}\psi

\displaystyle=f_{\tilde{\gamma}}-\bar{f}_{\tilde{\gamma}}+(d^{*}-d^{*}_{\tilde{\gamma}})P_{\tilde{\gamma}}\psi.

t

sufficiently small, we have

(5.53)

\displaystyle\|d^{*}(P_{\tilde{\gamma}}\psi)-(f_{\tilde{\gamma}}-\bar{f}_{\tilde{\gamma}})\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\frac{1}{100}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

It remains to bound

. We need the following lemma,
exploiting the fact that

.

Lemma 5.12
.

(5.54)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\tilde{\gamma}\cap\{y>t^{9/20}\}}\operatorname{Vol}(L_{y})f_{\tilde{\gamma}}\right|\leq Ct^{\frac{9}{40}}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

Using the fact that

(5.55)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{|y|<t^{9/20}}f+\int_{|y|>t^{9/20}}f.

By Fubini’s theorem, the second term on the right becomes

(5.56)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\gamma}\cap\{|y|>t^{9/20}\}}\int_{L_{y}}f

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{\gamma}\cap\{|y|>t^{9/20}\}}\operatorname{Vol}(L_{y})f_{\tilde{\gamma}}.

The first term on the right can be estimated as

(5.57)

\displaystyle\int_{|y|<t^{9/20}}f

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{1/2}\int_{|\tilde{y}|<2t^{-2/3+9/20}}\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\delta}(L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})}|\tilde{y}|^{\delta}

∎

Using the above Lemma

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{\tilde{\gamma}}\leq Ct^{1/2+9/40}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}

It follows that

(5.59)

\displaystyle\|\bar{f}_{\tilde{\gamma}}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq Ct^{9/40}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

In sum, for

sufficiently small, we have

(5.60)

\displaystyle\|(d+d^{*})P_{\tilde{\gamma}}\psi-f_{\tilde{\gamma}}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\frac{1}{100}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

Finally, define

(5.61)

\displaystyle\|(d+d^{*})P\psi-\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\frac{1}{20}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

It follows that we can write down the right inverse of the

(5.62)

\displaystyle(d+d^{*})^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle=P\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-(d+d^{*})P)^{j}\psi

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}+\eta_{2},

where

(5.63)

\displaystyle\eta_{1}

\displaystyle=(P_{0}+P_{1})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-(d+d^{*})P)^{j}\psi

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle=P_{\gamma}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-(d+d^{*})P)^{j}\psi.

We have the following estimates:

(5.64)

\displaystyle\|\eta_{1}\|_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}},

\displaystyle\|\eta_{2}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(\gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

when

5.5.
Deforming to special Lagrangians

We now set up the deformation of the approximate solutions
L_{\gamma,t}

to special Lagrangians for all sufficiently small

.

As in Section
2.3
, let
\eta\in\Lambda^{1}
, and let

(5.65)

\displaystyle\eta=\tilde{\omega}_{t}(V,\cdot)

(5.66)

\displaystyle F:\Lambda^{1}

\displaystyle\to\Lambda^{0}\oplus\Lambda^{2},

\displaystyle F(\eta)

\displaystyle=(*f_{\eta}^{*}\operatorname{Im}\Omega,f_{\eta}^{*}\tilde{\omega}_{t}).

Then

.

Let us write

(5.67)

\displaystyle F(\eta)=F(0)+(d+d^{*})\eta+Q(\eta),

\displaystyle N(\psi)=-F(0)-Q(P\psi).

Lemma 5.13
.

\tilde{\omega}_{t}
is exact on
L_{\gamma,t}
, and

\int_{L_{\gamma,t}}*\operatorname{Im}\Omega=0
. Consequently,

F(0)\in C^{\infty,ave}\oplus K^{2}

Proof.

That

is exact follows from the fact that

. To show that

\int_{L_{\gamma,t}}*\operatorname{Im}\Omega=0
, first note that

\operatorname{Im}\Omega|_{L_{\gamma,t}}
is supported on

\{|y|<2t^{9/20}\}
. Thus we have

(5.69)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{L_{\gamma,t}}\operatorname{Im}\Omega\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{L_{\gamma,t}\cap\{|y|<2t^{9/20}\}}\operatorname{Im}\Omega\right|

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{1/2}\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\cap\{|\tilde{y}|<2t^{-13/60}\}}|(\operatorname{Im}\Omega-\operatorname{Im}\Omega_{0})|_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}|

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{1/2+1/20+13\delta/60}\int_{L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\cap\{|\tilde{y}|<2t^{-13/60}\}}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}|\tilde{y}|^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{1/2+1/20+13\delta/60}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}(t^{-13/60})^{3/2+\delta}

\displaystyle=C\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}t^{9/40}.

Here the third line of the above inequality follows from the
definition of the weight norm. By
Proposition
5.9
,
\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq C

for some uniform constant

. Since for all sufficiently small

,
L_{\gamma,t}
are isotopic,

∎

Lemma 5.14
.

For
\eta\in\Lambda^{1}
, we have

.

Proposition 5.15
.

For

\displaystyle\|Q(P\psi_{1})-Q(P\psi_{0})\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq C(t)\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{0}\|_{\mathcal{B}},

0

Region I.

We refer the reader to the previous subsection for notations involved

Since the admissible loop

Lemma 5.18

∎

We now state an analogous result of Proposition
5.10

Lemma 5.19
.

For

(5.87)

\displaystyle P:\psi=(f,\omega)\in C^{\infty,ave}(\Lambda^{0})\oplus C^{\infty}(E^{2})\to C^{\infty}(\Lambda^{1})

(5.88)

\displaystyle\|\eta_{1}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(L_{\gamma,t})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\psi\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(L_{\gamma,t})},

\displaystyle\|\eta_{2}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(\gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{-1/2}\|\psi\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(L_{\gamma,t})},

and the constant
C>0
does not depend on
t
.

Proof.

The overall strategy follows very closely the proof of
Proposition
5.10
. We first need an analogue of
Lemma
5.11
. The presence of higher genus fibers poses
no extra difficulties, as we have
\omega\in E^{2}
. In fact, the
proof is slightly easier as the thimble
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
does not
appear here.

We now explain the construction of the parametrix in the loop case.
Since the thimble
L_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
does not appear as local
models, the analysis is much easier. However, there is an extra
complication in the loop case due to the presence of higher genus
fibers. In the following we will assume the content of the proof of
Proposition
5.10
.

Let us fix
y^{\prime}\in\gamma
and consider a neighborhood
U
of
y^{\prime}

with size
t^{1/2}
with respect to
\omega_{Y}
. Then we can identify

\pi^{-1}(U)\cap L_{\gamma,t}
with the geometry

\mathbb{R}\times L_{y^{\prime}}
, where
L_{y^{\prime}}
is equipped with the
metric induced from the Calabi-Yau metric on the K3 fiber

X_{y^{\prime}}
. The error between the actual induced metric on

\pi^{-1}(U)\cap L_{\gamma,t}
and
\mathbb{R}\times L_{y^{\prime}}
is of
order
O(t^{1/2})
, as explained in the proof of the previous
proposition.

We now let
(f,\omega)
be given by the analogue of
Lemma
5.11
, supported in

\pi^{-1}(U)\cap L_{\gamma,t}
. To apply
Proposition
4.4
, we need

(5.89)

\displaystyle(f,\omega)\in C^{0,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times L_{y^{\prime}})\oplus\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times L_{y^{\prime}})).

The function part
f
can be modified by taking out the fiberwise
average with respect to the geometry
\mathbb{R}\times L_{y^{\prime}}
. This
fiberwise average can then be dealt with in a way similar to the
proof of Proposition
5.10
.

To deal with the
2
-form part
\omega
, write
\partial_{y}
for the
horizontal lifting of
\gamma^{\prime}
. Then
\omega
, given by the analogue
of Lemma
5.11
, can be written as

(5.90)

\displaystyle\omega=t^{-\frac{1}{2}}dy\wedge\mu+\nu,

where
\mu
and

are
1
-form and
2
-form tangent to the fiber

L_{y^{\prime}}
. Since the
2
-form

is exact, we clear have

\nu\in C^{0,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times L_{y}))
. On the
other hand, by construction (the analogue of
Lemma
5.11
), we have

(5.91)

\displaystyle\int_{L_{y}}\langle\nu,h\rangle=0

for all harmonic

-forms

and
y\in U
. To satisfy the assumption in
Proposition
4.4
, we define

(5.92)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}=\mu-\left(\sum_{i}\int_{L_{y^{\prime}}}\langle\mu(y),h_{i}\rangle\right)h_{i},

where
\{h_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{H}^{1}(L_{y^{\prime}})

(5.93)

\displaystyle t^{-1/2}dy\wedge\tilde{\mu}\in C^{0,\alpha,ave}(\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times L_{y^{\prime}})),

and we can apply Proposition
4.4
. The result
is that using cutoff functions, we can construct a parametrix
similar to the proof of Proposition
4.4
.

Now we claim that the fiberwise average part

(5.94)

\displaystyle t^{-1/2}dy\wedge\left(\sum_{i}\int_{L_{y^{\prime}}}\langle\mu(y),h_{i}\rangle\right)h_{i}

is much smaller and can be ignored in the iteration. This boils down
to the fact that the metric deviation between

and

is

. Using this, a straightforward computation then yields

(5.95)

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{L_{y^{\prime}}}\langle\mu(y),h_{i}\rangle\right\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(L_{y^{\prime}})}\leq Ct^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\omega\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(L_{\gamma,t})}

for a constant

∎

To complete the deformation setup, we need the following estimate for

Lemma 5.20
.

For

\displaystyle\|Q(P\psi_{1})-Q(P\psi_{0})\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}\leq C^{\prime}t^{1/2}\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{0}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}},

where

.

The proof essentially follows from Proposition
2.7

6.
Examples and further discussions

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
reduce the problem of
finding special Lagrangian submanifolds to establishing the existence
of admissible paths and loops, which are geodesics with respect to
certain (locally defined) quadratic differentials. In this section, we
will construct explicit examples of admissible paths and loops,
leading to new examples of special Lagrangian submanifolds. We will
also relate of our work to the Thomas-Yau conjecture, mirror symmetry,
and the Donaldson-Scaduto conjecture.

6.1.
Special Lagrangian vanishing spheres

Let
\phi
be a quadratic differential on an open set
U
of a Riemann
surface. We first
recall the following basic result of quadratic differentials:

Lemma 6.1
.

[
58
, Theorem
14.2.1, Theorem 18.2.1]
Assume that
U
is a simply
connected. Then we have the following:

(1)

There exists at most one smooth geodesic connecting any given
two zeros of the quadratic differential. Moreover, there exists no
geodesic loop in
U
.

(2)

Any two zeros of the quadratic differential are connected by a
sequence of geodesics possibly of different phases. Moreover, if

y_{0},y_{1}\in U
such that the distance
dist(y_{0},y_{1})
is less than
the distance of
y_{0}
to
\partial U
, then there exists a smooth
geodesic connecting
y_{0},y_{1}
.

The second part of the lemma can be viewed as an analogue of

[
62
, Theorem 4.3]
. Given this, we are now ready to present
examples of admissible paths corresponding to vanishing cycles.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
be a projective family of
Calabi-Yau
3
-folds over a disc
\mathbb{D}
. Denote by
X_{s}
the
fiber over
s\in\mathbb{D}
. Assume that

•

X_{s}
is smooth for each
s\neq 0
.

•

X_{0}
is smooth except a point where it has a conifold singularity.

•

For all
s\in\mathbb{D}
,
X_{s}
admits a Lefschetz K3
fibration
\pi_{s}:X_{s}\rightarrow Y_{s}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Then there exist
s_{0}>0
and
t_{0}(s)>0
such that
X_{s}
admits a
special Lagrangian vanishing sphere with respect to the unique
Ricci-flat metric in the class

[\omega_{X_{s}}+\frac{1}{t}\omega_{Y}]
for all
t<t_{0}(s)
and

|s|<s_{0}
.

Proof.

From Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to construct an admissible
path on the base
Y_{s}
of
X_{s}
for
s
sufficiently small.

By identifying the bases
Y_{s}
of the K3 fibrations
X_{s}
with a
fixed
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, we may assume that the discriminant loci in

Y_{s}
vary holomorphically with respect to
s
. Near the conifold
singularity in
X_{0}
, the fibration

\pi_{s}:X_{s}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is locally given by

\{(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},y):z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}=y(y-y_{1}(s))\}\mapsto y
after
a suitable choice of coordinates, where
y_{1}(s)\rightarrow 0
as

s\to 0
. Then
y_{0}=0,y_{1}
are two critical values of

\pi_{s}
. Denote by
y_{0}=0,y_{1}(s),\ldots,y_{N}(s)
the critical values
of
\pi
. Define

\displaystyle l_{j}(s)=\inf_{\gamma}\int_{\gamma}|\alpha|,

where the infimum is taken over all admissible paths
\gamma

connecting
y_{0}
to
y_{j}
and
\alpha
is the locally holomorphic

1
-form defined in a neighborhood of
\gamma
corresponding to the
vanishing cycle of
y_{0}
. In other words,
l_{j}(s)
is the distance
from
0
to
y_{j}(s)
with respect to the one coming from the
quadratic differential
\alpha\otimes\alpha
. For
s
small enough,
we may assume that
l_{1}(s)<l_{j}(s)
for all
j>1
. From Lemma

6.1
(2), there exists a piecewise
smooth geodesic
\gamma
connecting
y_{0},y_{1}(s)
(possibly with
different phases along each smooth component) that minimizes the
length with respect to the metric defined by

\alpha\otimes\alpha
. Let
y_{*}=\gamma(t_{*})
be a non-smooth point
of such length minimizing path if there exists any. The assumption

l_{1}(s)<l_{j}(s)
guarantees that
y_{*}\neq y_{j}
and thus
y_{*}
is not
a critical value of
\pi_{s}
. Therefore, there exists a neighborhood
of
y_{*}
such that any two points can be connected by a smooth
geodesic with respect to the flat metric which strictly realizes the
shortest distance. In particular, one can replace

\gamma|_{[t_{*}-\epsilon,t_{*}+\epsilon]}
by a smooth geodesic
connecting
\gamma(t_{*}\pm\epsilon)
for small enough
\epsilon>0
,
which contradicts the definition of
\gamma
. Therefore,
\gamma
is
a smooth geodesic. From the explicit local equation, one sees that
the vanishing cycles corresponding to these two critical values are
the same up to sign and thus Definition
5.2

(1) and (2) hold.

It is known that the versal deformation of the singularity modeled
by
z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}=0
is given by

z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}=y
. Therefore, there exists a neighborhood

\mathcal{V}
of
(s=0,y=0)
in
\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}

such that there exists a smooth special Lagrangian
2
-sphere in

(X_{s})_{y}
for all
(s,y)\in\mathcal{V}
by combining the works of
Chan
[
5
]
and Spotti
[
57
]
. See also

[
27
, Corollary A.2]
. Now it suffices to take
s_{0}
small enough
such that the distance between
y=0,y_{1}(s)
is smaller than the
distance from
y=0
to

\partial(\mathcal{V}\cap\{s\}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})
for all

|s|<s_{0}
. Then Definition
5.2
(3) can be
achieved.

Finally we remark that such a family
\chi\rightarrow\mathbb{D}

can be constructed as follows: For instance, let
W
be a Fano

3
-fold such that
-K_{W}
is very ample. An anti-canonical pencil

P
of
W
uniquely determines a hypersurface
X_{P}
of bi-degree

(1,-K_{W})
in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times W
and vice versa. By the Bertini
theorem and adjunction formula, such generic hypersurfaces are
smooth and fibers are anti-canonical. Let

\mathcal{D}\subseteq|-K_{W}|\cong\mathbb{P}^{N}
be the set
parametrizing to singular anti-canonical divisors. Then the generic
element of
\mathcal{D}
corresponds to anti-canonical divisors with
exactly one nodal singularity
[
64
, Corollary 2.10]
. Now choose a
generic point of
\mathcal{D}
and
P
is the pencil determined by
the tangent line of
\mathcal{D}
, then
X_{P}
has a conifold
singularity and a double cover of
X_{P}
ramified at two generic
smooth fibers is a Calabi-Yau
3
-fold
X_{0}
with a conifold
singularity. A generic complex deformation of
X_{0}
yields a family
described in the proposition.

∎

In projective Calabi-Yau manifolds near a conifold singularity,
Hein-Sun
[
27
]
also constructed special Lagrangian vanishing
spheres modeled on the zero section of
T^{*}S^{n}
with respect to the
Stenzel metric. Due to the nature of adiabatic limits, The special
Lagrangian spheres in Proposition
6.2

are elongated and thin, with their “ends” sharpening as
t\to 0
. This contrasts with the more rounded shape of the special
Lagrangian vanishing spheres described in
[
27
]
.

Proposition
6.2
can be generalized to
higher dimensions. The construction of the geodesics remains
unchanged. By
[
27
]
, smooth special Lagrangian spheres exist in
the Calabi-Yau fibers above the geodesic, assuming the conditions in
Proposition
6.2
. This establishes the
existence of the admissible path. The refinement of
[
40
]
in
Section
3
can be generalized to higher dimensions using
the Calabi-Yau metrics on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
constructed by
Székelyhidi
[
59
]
and Conlon-Rochon
[
11
]
, and
Hein-Sun’s result
[
27
]
on Calabi-Yau metrics with conical
singularities (see also
[
7
]
). Finally, perturbing the
approximate solutions to genuine special Lagrangians follows the same
analysis in Sections
4
and
5
.

It is a long-standing question how far the special Lagrangian
vanishing spheres can persist in the complex moduli of Calabi-Yau
manifolds. Near the adiabatic limit, this question can be reduced to
studying the persistence of admissible paths. The admissible path
constructed in Proposition
6.2
persists
in
Y_{s}
as a geodesic, i.e., satisfies Definition
5.2
(1)(2), as long as
l_{1}(s)<l_{j}(s)
. Notably, this
condition is more relaxed than the usual requirement of a small
neighborhood near the conifold, typically expressed as

l_{1}(s)\ll l_{j}(s)
. However, the geodesic
\gamma_{s}:[0,1]\to Y_{s}

fails to satisfy the condition in Definition
5.2
(3) when there exists a point

along the geodesic

\gamma_{s}
such that the special Lagrangian vanishing cycle in

(X_{s})_{y}
degenerates. This phenomenon is linked to the concept of
surgery triples, as described by Donaldson-Scaduto
[
14
]
. See
Section
6.5
below.

6.2.
Special Lagrangian
S^{1}\times S^{2}

We now turn to constructing explicit examples of admissible loops,
leading to examples of
S^{1}\times S^{2}
by Theorem

.

Example 6.3
.

Choose
P
to be a generic anti-canonical pencil of

\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{3}
and
X_{P}
is the corresponding
bi-degree
(1,4)
hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{3}

with a Lefschetz fibration
X_{P}\rightarrow P
. Consider
X
, a
double cover of
X_{P}
ramified over two smooth fibers

y_{*},y^{\prime}_{*}\in P
. Then
X
is a compact Calabi-Yau
3
-fold admitting
a quartic fibration structure and the base
Y
of
X
is a double
cover of
P
ramified over
y_{*},y^{\prime}_{*}
. By abuse of notation, we will
still denote by
y_{*},y^{\prime}_{*}
the corresponding points over
y_{*},y^{\prime}_{*}

in
Y
.

For
y^{\prime}_{*}
being sufficiently close to
y_{*}
in

, there exists a
neighborhood

of
y_{*},y^{\prime}_{*}
that is diffeomorphic to
an annulus where the fibration over
U
is topologically
trivial. Choose a
(-2)
-class
[L]
in
H_{2}(X_{y_{*}},\mathbb{Z})

with vanishing pairing with the hyperplane class on the quartic

X
. Such
(-2)
-classes exist in quartic K3 surfaces by a theorem
of Eichler
[
16
, §10]
(see also
[
20
, Lemma 3.5]
).

Choose a generic quartic K3 surface

with holomorphic volume form

\Omega
such that

\int_{[L_{1}]}\Omega/\int_{[L_{2}]}\Omega\notin\mathbb{R}_{+}
if

[L_{1}]+[L_{2}]=[L]
. This can be achieved by the Torelli theorem of
polarized K3 surface
[
22
, Chapter 6, Remark 3.7]
. Choose
P

to be a generic pencil passing though the point corresponding to
S

and denote by
y_{*}\in P
be the point. Then
[L]
can be realized as
a smooth special Lagrangian sphere in
X_{y_{*}}
by Lemma
2.3
and thus can be realized as smooth special Lagrangian

S^{2}
for
y\in P
near
y_{*}
. In particular, if we choose
y^{\prime}_{*}

sufficiently close to
y_{*}
, then
[L]
can be represented by a
smooth special Lagrangian
S^{2}
in
X_{y}
for
y\in U
. The
triviality of the fibration over
U
allows us to define a quadratic
differential on
U
via the construction in Section
5.1
. The holomorphic volume form on the fibration over

U
is of the pull back of

\frac{1}{\sqrt{(y-y_{*})(y-y^{\prime}_{*})}}dy\wedge\Omega_{y}
, where
y

serves as a local coordinate on
P
. Therefore, the distance between

y_{*},y^{\prime}_{*}
is less than the distance from
y_{*}
to
\partial U
when

y^{\prime}_{*}
is sufficiently close to
y_{*}
by straight-forward
calculation. Considering the universal cover of
U
equipped with
the pull-back quadratic differential, we can find a geodesic
connecting the lifting of
y_{*},y^{\prime}_{*}
in the same fundamental domain
as established in Lemma
6.1
. We claim
that the geodesic concatenates with its

-mirror image
form a closed geodesic in

. It suffices to show that the
concatenation is smooth at
y_{*},y^{\prime}_{*}
. Notice that the holomorphic
volume form in a tubular neighborhood is of the form

, for some suitable coordinates

on
U
such
that
y(y_{*})=0
. From the double cover structure of

\sigma

and

6.3.
Thomas-Yau conjecture

We now recall the stability condition introduced by Thomas
[
61
]
,
which is also referred to as Thomas-Yau stability in

[
41
]
. Following the exposition in
[
44
]
, we have the
following definition:

Definition 6.4
.

Let
(X,\omega,\Omega)
be a Calabi-Yau
manifold. We say a compact zero Maslov Lagrangian submanifold
L
is
unstable if it is Hamiltonian isotopic to a graded Lagrangian
connect sum
L_{1}\#L_{2}
of compact graded Lagrangians
L_{1}
and

L_{2}
, with variations of their Lagrangian angles less than
2\pi
,
so that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Arg}\int_{L_{1}}\Omega\geq\operatorname{Arg}\int_{L_{2}}\Omega.

The Lagrangian
L
is called stable if it is not unstable.

Given the above definition, we are ready to state the following
conjecture, originally proposed by Thomas
[
61
, Conjecture 5.2]

(see also
[
62
]
]), which draws an analogy to slope stability and
Hermitian Yang-Mills connections on vector bundles:

Conjecture 6.5
.

Let
L
be a compact zero Maslov Lagrangian in

(X,\omega,\Omega))
. Then there exists a unique special Lagrangian
in the Hamiltonian isotopy class of
L
if and only if it is stable.

Y. Li
[
41
]
proved the conjecture under technical assumptions in
the setting of Calabi-Yau Stein manifolds. Notably, Li’s results are
variational in nature, and make explicit use of holomorphic curves to
establish both existence and non-existence results.

Soon after
[
61
]
, Thomas-Yau
[
62
]
proposed a stronger version
of the conjecture, relating the notion of stability to convergence of
Lagrangian mean curvature flow (LMCF) starting from

L
. Joyce
[
31
]
has since significantly updated the Thomas-Yau
conjecture. The expectation is that once
L
is equipped with a
“bounding cochain”, singularity formation of LMCF should be
well-understood, and the flow could be continued beyond the formation
of singularities.

More recently, Lotay-Oliveira
[
44
]
proved the Thomas conjecture
and a version of the Thomas-Yau conjecture for
S^{1}
-invariant compact
Lagrangians in the Gibbons-Hawking ansatz. In the same setting,
Lotay-Oliveira
[
45
]
also showed that finite time singularities
of LMCF are neck pinches, thus proving various conjectures in

[
31
]
by demonstrating how “unstable” Lagrangians decompose into
unions of special Lagrangians under the flow. Our main results,
Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
, can be viewed as higher
dimensional generalizations of the
S^{1}
-invariant setting found in

[
44
]
[
45
]
.

We now make connections of the Thomas-Yau conjecture with our
geometric setup. Let
(X,\Omega)
be a Calabi-Yau
3
-fold with a
Lefschetz K3 fibration
\pi:X\to Y
, where
Y=\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Suppose that there exist admissible paths

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
in an open set
U\subset Y
such that
\gamma_{1}

goes from
y_{0}
to
y_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
goes from
y_{1}
to
y_{2}
. Let

\psi
denote the angle between
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
. In a
conformal coordinate
y
near a simple zero of the quadratic
differential, any geodesic emanating from
y_{1}
with phase

\theta\in[0,2\pi)
is of the form

(6.2)

\displaystyle y(t)=\left(\frac{3}{2}e^{i\theta}t\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}.

Thus
\operatorname{Arg}y^{\prime}(0)=\frac{2}{3}\theta
. Now, let

\bar{\gamma}_{1}
be
\gamma_{1}
with orientation reversed. Thus the phase

\bar{\theta}_{1}
of
\bar{\gamma}_{1}
satisfies

\theta_{1}=\bar{\theta}_{1}-\pi\mod 2\pi
. From the local expression of
geodesics we see that

(6.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Arg}\bar{\gamma}_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{2}{3}\theta_{2}+\psi,

or equivalently

(6.4)

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}_{1}=\theta_{2}+\frac{3}{2}\psi.

It follows that

(6.5)

\displaystyle\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}

if and only if

(6.6)

\displaystyle\psi<\frac{2}{3}\pi.

By Theorem
1.1
, for sufficiently small
t>0
, there exist
special Lagrangians
\tilde{L}_{\gamma_{1},t}
and

\tilde{L}_{\gamma_{2},t}
in the collapsing Calabi-Yau
3
-fold

(X,\tilde{\omega}_{t},\Omega)
. Thus the stability of the Lagrangian
connect sum
\tilde{L}_{\gamma_{1},t}\#\tilde{L}_{\gamma_{2},t}
is
determined by the angle
\psi
between
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.

We have the following lemma, which can be found in
[
55
]
:

Lemma 6.6
.

In the setting above, there exists
a unique smooth geodesic from
y_{0}
to
y_{2}
if and only if the
angle
\psi
between
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
is less than

\frac{2\pi}{3}
.

Proof.

If the angle between the geodesics is larger than

6.4.
Mirror symmetry and DHT Conjecture

Here we anticipate that adiabatic limits discussed in this paper is
mirror to the Tyurin degeneration from the viewpoint of homological
mirror symmetry. Let
\pi:X\rightarrow Y

3
-fold

Harder-Kartzarkov
[

conjectured that there exists a Tyurin

\check{X}_{t}\rightsquigarrow\check{X}_{0}=\check{Y}_{1}\coprod_{\check{D}}\check{Y_{2}}

i.e.
\check{X}_{t}

\check{X}_{0}

\displaystyle N_{\check{D}/\check{Y}_{1}}\otimes N_{\check{D}/\check{Y}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\check{D}}.

Moreover, the homological mirror symmetry holds

\displaystyle\mbox{Fuk}(X_{0})\cong\mbox{Per}(\check{X}_{0}),

where

6.5.
Donaldson-Scaduto Conjecture

We begin by recalling the following folklore conjecture, which serves
as part of the motivation behind tropical geometry:

Conjecture 6.8
.

Let
(X_{t},g_{t},C_{t})
be a
1
-parameter family of compact Calabi-Yau
manifolds
(X_{t},g_{t})
admitting special Lagrangian fibration
structures and with bounded diameters, and let
C_{t}
be a
holomorphic curve in
X_{t}
. Assume that
X_{t}
are collapsing, i,e.,

(X_{i},g_{i})\xrightarrow{GH}(B_{\infty},g_{\infty})
, where

B_{\infty}
is an integral affine manifold equipped with a Hessian
type metric outside of a codimension two locus. Then
C_{t}
converges
to a tropical curve on
B_{\infty}
.

A more geometric scenario arises when
X_{t}
admits a special
Lagrangian fibration,
X_{t}\to B_{t}
. In this case,
B_{t}
naturally
inherits the structure of an affine manifold with singularities and a
Hessian-type metric. As the special Lagrangian fibration collapses, it
is expected that
B_{t}\xrightarrow{\text{GH}}B_{\infty}
, while the
structure of the affine manifold with singularities is preserved. In
this setting, tropical curves correspond to images of graphs, with
each edge mapped to an affine line segment in terms of the complex
affine structure. Meanwhile, the affine lines with respect to the
complex structure are gradient flow lines of a symplectic area
functional. This folklore conjecture has been proved only in specific
cases, such as algebraic curves in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
with incidence
relations
[
48
]
, and for torus fiber bundles
[
52
]
, but no
results have yet been established for compact

\operatorname{SU}(n)
-Calabi-Yau manifolds.

In the setting of Conjecture
6.8
, one could also ask
whether the special Lagrangian submanifolds in
X_{t}
collapse to
tropical-like objects. For example, Mikhalkin reconstructed
Lagrangians from tropical curves
[
49
]
. It is interesting to
know if these Lagrangians can be made special.

Motivated by tropical geometry, Donaldson-Scaduto considered another
geometric setup where the roles of special Lagrangians and holomorphic
submanifolds are reversed, leading the following conjecture:

Conjecture 6.9
.

[
14
]

Let
X
be a Calabi-Yau
3
-fold with a K3-fibration

\pi:X\rightarrow Y
. Let
\omega_{X},\omega_{Y}
be Kähler forms on

X
and
Y
, respectively. Denote by
\tilde{\omega}_{t}
the unique
Calabi-Yau metric in the Kähler class

[\omega_{X}]+\frac{1}{t}\pi^{*}[\omega_{Y}]
, for
t\ll 1
. If
L_{t}
is a

1
-parameter family of special Lagrangian submanifold with respect
to
\tilde{\omega}_{t}
, then
L_{t}
Gromov-Hausdorff converges to a
graph in
Y
where each edge is a geodesic introduced in Section

5.1
, or, equivalently, a gradient flow line of
certain area functional.

Remark 6.10
.

The original conjecture of Donaldson-Scaduto conjecture concerns

G_{2}
-manifolds. The Calabi-Yau setting considered here can be
thought of as an
S^{1}
reduction of the original conjecture.

As a sanity check, we observe the following: the Leray-Serre spectral
sequence for
\pi:X\rightarrow Y
degenerates at its
E_{2}
-page. In
particular, we have

H^{3}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{1}(Y,R^{2}\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z})
and any homology
class in
H_{3}(X,\mathbb{Z})
can be represented by a
3
-cycle which
projects onto a graph with generic fibers that are
2
-cycles in the
K3 fibers. Such graphs in Conjecture
6.9
are referred to as
gradient cycles in
[
14
]
: each edge is decorated by a
2
-cycle in
the K3 fiber and is the gradient flow line of the area functional of
that
2
-cycle. Moreover, the decorated
2
-cycles associate to edges
adjacent to a fixed vertex sum up to zero, which is the analogue to
balancing conditions in tropical geometry. In particular, the
admissible paths and admissible loops considered here are the simplest
examples of gradient cycles. In a similar context, these graphs are called spectral
networks in the work of
Gaiotto-Moore-Neitzke
[
19
]
.

An important problem in tropical geometry is the realizability of
tropical curves, i.e. determining whether there exists a holomorphic
curve that realizes a given tropical curve. This leads to the
following conjecture:

Conjecture 6.11
.

[
14
]

Given a Calabi-Yau
3
-fold with Lefschetz K3 fibration

\pi:X\rightarrow Y
and a gradient cycle, there exists a

1
-parameter family of special Lagrangian
L_{t}
in

(X,\tilde{\omega}_{t})
such that
L_{t}
converges to the gradient
cycle as
t\rightarrow 0
.

While Conjecture
6.11
remains far from reach at the moment,
Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
provide the first examples
that offer support for it. Recently, S. Esfahani and Y. Li constructed
a local model of special Lagrangian pair of pants in the product

ALE\times\mathbb{C}
for star graphs
[
17
]
, where
ALE
refers
to
ALE
-gravitational instantons. Building on the techniques
developed in this paper, we expect to construct some special Lagrangian
3-spheres in K3-fibered Calabi-Yau
3
-folds, which, under the adiabatic
limit, should Gromov-Hausdorff converge to gradient cycles that
include trivalent vertices. This represents a key step toward
advancing Conjecture
6.11
.
