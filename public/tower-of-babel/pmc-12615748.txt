Habitat distribution of major wildlife in the Sanjiangyuan National park under climate scenarios based on maxent modeling
Authors: Xiangxiang Hu, Yaya Shi, Bao Song, Liangbai Hu, Qiang Zhao, Dongdong Pang, Jixin Yin, Yan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sanjiangyuan National Park (SNP), the first national park established in China, plays a pivotal role in conserving biodiversity on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. This study investigates the habitat suitability and projected future distribution of four key species: the Tibetan antelope (Pantholops hodgsonii), kiang (Equus kiang), Tibetan gazelle (Procapra picticaudata), and wild yak (Bos mutus). Utilizing 358 occurrence records and the MaxEnt model, we predicted current and future suitable habitats under different climate scenarios for the years 2050 and 2070. Our findings indicate that suitable habitats are significantly influenced by factors such as elevation, temperature, and vegetation health. Among the species studied, the Tibetan gazelle exhibits the most extensive suitable habitat, followed by the kiang, Tibetan antelope, and wild yak. Specifically, the Tibetan antelope’s habitat is anticipated to shift northward, while the kiang and wild yak may experience further habitat fragmentation, thereby increasing risks to their populations. Additionally, the results highlight the importance of integrating climate considerations into future conservation planning to ensure the long-term survival of these species. By identifying regions with optimal environmental conditions, conservation strategies can be more effectively directed to maintain and improve these habitats, thereby preserving biodiversity within the Sanjiangyuan region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23536-4.

Introduction

Global climate change has emerged as a major environmental challenge, significantly affecting species distribution patterns worldwide. Understanding how species respond to these shifting environmental conditions is crucial for developing effective conservation strategies1. In recent decades, species distribution models (SDMs) have become vital tools in conservation biology, providing insights into potential species responses to climate change and aiding in the formulation of informed conservation plans. These models have evolved substantially, incorporating more sophisticated algorithms and improved data integration, allowing researchers to anticipate shifts in species distributions under various climate scenarios2–4. The advancements in SDMs underscore the importance of understanding how global climate change influences species distribution patterns5. Nestled at the heart of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the Sanjiangyuan region is an ecologically vital area, serving as the source of the Yangtze, Yellow, and Mekong Rivers. Spanning 363,000 km²—roughly half the size of Qinghai Province—this region, recently designated as Sanjiangyuan National Park with an area of 123,100 km², plays a critical role in water regulation and biodiversity conservation6. Recently upgraded to Sanjiangyuan National Park, this area encompasses 123,100 km² and plays a crucial role in water regulation and biodiversity conservation7. The region’s diverse habitats, shaped by its challenging climate and high altitude, support a wide range of wildlife species, including the snow leopard, Tibetan antelope, and wild yak8. Climate factors, especially temperature and precipitation, significantly influence species distribution and habitat suitability. The region’s elevation gradients determine the availability of essential food resources for maintaining biodiversity9. However, the Sanjiangyuan region faces increasing pressures from climate change and human activities, highlighting the urgent need for effective conservation measures.

In conservation biology, species distribution models (SDMs) quantify the relationship between species occurrence and environmental variables, making them essential tools for predicting species diversity patterns6. Various models have been developed for this purpose, including the genetic algorithm for rule-set production (GARP), bioclimatic modeling (BIOCLIM), CLIMEX, and ecological niche factor analysis (ENFA). While each of these models has its strengths, Maximum entropy (MaxEnt) stands out due to its high accuracy and robust performance. MaxEnt can handle both continuous and categorical environmental variables, making it ideal for predicting species distributions even with limited data. Its outputs are easy to interpret and provide valuable insights into the impacts of climate change and land-use dynamics on biodiversity10. Therefore, we chose MaxEnt as the primary analytical tool for this study.

Despite the widespread use of SDMs, most current studies focus on individual species, neglecting the broader landscape-level impacts on multiple species distributions11. This research aims to fill that gap by developing MaxEnt models for key wildlife species in the Sanjiangyuan region, including Tibetan antelope, Tibetan wild ass, Tibetan gazelle, and wild yak. These species were selected as they represent key large herbivores unique to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau’s alpine ecosystems, serving as flagship and umbrella species for broader biodiversity conservation efforts. With conservation statuses ranging from Least Concern (Tibetan wild ass) to Near Threatened (Tibetan antelope and gazelle) and Vulnerable (wild yak) on the IUCN Red List, they are highly sensitive to environmental changes and provide valuable insights into ecosystem health. By comparing species distribution predictions under different climate scenarios, we aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of how climate change affects biodiversity in this critical region. Our multi-species modeling approach will help identify biodiversity hotspots, contributing to more effective conservation planning12–14.

Habitat suitability modeling (HSM), synonymous with SDM, is a statistical approach that combines species occurrence data with environmental variables such as climate, soil, and topography to create a correlative model. This model reflects the environmental conditions that meet a species’ ecological requirements and helps identify potential habitats15,16. HSM has broad applications in conservation biology, supporting the identification of habitats for endangered species and providing insights into biological invasions. By generating ecological niche models, HSM allows researchers to predict new distribution areas under future environmental changes, thereby facilitating the development of conservation and management strategies17,18.

In this study, we developed MaxEnt models for the Tibetan antelope, Tibetan wild ass, Tibetan gazelle, and wild yak. During model construction, we considered key environmental factors such as climate and topography, enabling us to predict species distributions under multiple climate scenarios. By doing so, we assess the impact of environmental variables and climate change on the habitat suitability and distribution patterns of these species. Our research provides a comprehensive view of how climate change may affect biodiversity in the Sanjiangyuan region, contributing to the global effort to conserve key wildlife species in vulnerable ecosystems.

Materials and methods

To improve clarity of the methodology, Fig. 1 illustrates the full workflow applied in this study, structured into three major stages: data processing, experimental design, and model assessment. In the data processing stage, species occurrence records were resampled to a 1-km resolution, pseudo-absence points were generated, and environmental variables were organized into natural and human factors. In the experimental design stage, pseudo-point construction and correlation analysis were conducted to minimize collinearity, followed by factor filtering. The MaxEnt model was then implemented with parameter tuning, and the results were output for subsequent evaluation. Finally, in the model assessment stage, model performance was tested using multiple evaluation metrics (AUC, TSS, Boyce), and the parameter set with the highest index scores was selected to determine the optimal model configuration. This stepwise framework ensures transparency in data preparation, model construction, and validation, and helps readers clearly trace how methodological consistency was maintained throughout the habitat suitability modeling process.

Study area

The Sanjiangyuan District, located between 31°39′ and 36°12′ N and 89°45′ and 102°23′ E in southern Qinghai Province, China, has an average altitude ranging from 3500 to 4800 m (Fig. 2). As the core region of the Tibetan Plateau, it features predominantly mountainous terrain and a continental plateau climate with distinct hot and cold seasons as well as wet and dry periods. The landscape is interspersed with numerous glaciers, making it one of the areas with the highest concentration and altitude of glaciers globally, and includes a diverse array of wetland types. Sanjiangyuan National Park, the first of its kind in China, covers an extensive area of 123,100 km², representing 31.16% of the entire Sanjiangyuan region. It includes three major zones: the sources of the Yangtze River, the Yellow River, and the Lancang River, and serves as a vital freshwater source for China and Southeast Asia, making it a critical zone for monitoring global climate change impacts.

The region is rich in biodiversity, home to 69 nationally protected animal species, including the bharal and Tibetan gazelle (Procapra picticaudata). However, in recent decades, the Tibetan Plateau’s ecological environment, including the Sanjiangyuan region, has experienced significant degradation due to climate change and severe soil erosion, resulting in over 20% of its biological species being classified as threatened, characterized by grassland desertification, wetland shrinkage, and permafrost thaw, driven by climate change and severe soil erosion. These changes have resulted in over 20% of its biological species being classified as threatened. Sanjiangyuan National Park plays a crucial role in preserving ecological functions, natural landscapes, and biodiversity, holding both national and global significance. It serves as an ecological security barrier in Western China, supporting numerous endangered and endemic wildlife species. As a pioneering model for nature conservation, the park aims to showcase ecological cultural heritage on the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau.

Occurrence point

During July and August 2023, we conducted extensive field investigations in Sanjiangyuan National Park, documenting wildlife occurrence records using GPS device. Our research focused on four key species: the Tibetan antelope (Pantholops hodgsonii), the Tibetan wild ass (Equus kiang), the Tibetan gazelle (Procapra picticaudata), and the wild yak (Bos mutus). Throughout these field surveys, we collected 54 presence points for the Tibetan antelope, 158 points for the Tibetan wild ass, 128 points for the Tibetan gazelle, and 18 points for the wild yak.

These meticulously gathered data are crucial for constructing accurate habitat distribution models using MaxEnt under various climate scenarios. Given that MaxEnt modeling relies heavily on the quality and spatial accuracy of input data to effectively predict potential habitats and distribution patterns, we implemented Biomod2 to generate pseudo-absences. This approach allowed us to mitigate the impact of spatial autocorrelation and reduce bias arising from overly clustered occurrence points. By ensuring appropriate spacing between data points, we enhanced the robustness and reliability of our predictions. Detailed information, including the relevant code, can be found in Supplementary Material (Appendix S1), Sect. ❝Materials And Methods❞.

Environmental variables and selection

In our study, the selection of environmental influencing factors for model development was guided by previous research to ensure their relevance and effectiveness in predicting species distributions. We categorized these factors into three groups: natural factors, anthropogenic factors, and bioclimatic factors.The natural factors selected include elevation, slope, aspect, NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), lakes, and distance to the nearest rivers and roads. These factors have been widely recognized in previous studies as critical determinants of species distribution, influencing temperature, water availability, vegetation health, and habitat conditions19. Specifically, elevation, slope, and aspect are key components for assessing topographical variations that affect local microclimates, while NDVI is an important indicator of vegetation health and productivity, which is crucial for understanding habitat suitability.Elevation, slope, and aspect data were sourced from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) (http://www.gscloud.cn/), and NDVI data was obtained from the MODIS satellite (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/). Data on lakes and river proximity were derived from the HydroSHEDS dataset (http://hydrosheds.cr.usgs.gov/). The selection of these variables aligns with established findings in the literature, where similar parameters have proven effective in modeling the habitat preferences of a wide range of species.Details regarding the specific environmental variables used in the study are further provided in Table 1.

To enhance the reliability and accuracy of the model, we utilized the variance inflation factor (VIF) in R to address multicollinearity issues among the predictor variables. VIF measures the degree of linear correlation between a predictor variable and other predictor variables. In ecological modeling, especially species distribution models (SDMs), high multicollinearity can significantly affect model stability and predictive power20,21. Therefore, we calculated the VIF values to identify and remove variables with VIF values greater than 10, ensuring the independence of input variables and the credibility of the model results. This step not only helps reduce noise from redundant variables but also improves the model’s performance in predictions, ultimately leading to more accurate and robust habitat distribution predictions for the species22. For different species, including Pantholops hodgsonii, Equus kiang, Procapra picticaudata, and Bos mutus, we used the variance inflation factor (VIF) to eliminate variables with high multicollinearity for species distribution modeling. In each species model, we calculated the VIF values for all predictor variables and removed those with VIF values greater than 10. This process ensured that the input variables in each model had low multicollinearity, thereby improving model stability and predictive accuracy. The results of the correlation analysis and VIF values are presented in Appendix 1.

Model selection and evaluation

Model selection

The MaxEnt model, developed by Phillips et al. in 2006, is a niche model based on the principle of maximum entropy. This model utilizes actual species occurrence data to analyze the characteristics of various environmental variables (such as climate and topography) that constrain species distribution. By doing so, it seeks the most probable distribution under these constraints, resulting in a spatial distribution and suitability of the species’ habitat.

The MaxEnt model is straightforward, fast, and has excellent predictive capabilities. It requires as few as five species occurrence points for modeling and includes a self-validation function that can generate the area under the ROC curve (AUC) to evaluate the accuracy of the model and the importance of different environmental variables23. Therefore, the MaxEnt model is widely used in habitat evaluation, suitability and potential spatial distribution prediction, biological invasion studies, and the assessment of the potential impacts of climate change on species distribution. Especially in situations where “absence data” for wild animals is difficult to accurately collect, the MaxEnt model shows significant advantages in simulating and assessing animal habitats24.

Parameter settings

During the operation of the MaxEnt model, the algorithm continuously adjusts the weight of each environmental variable to maximize the likelihood of the presence data set, ultimately ensuring convergence to the maximum probable distribution. Specifically, we randomly selected 25% of the distribution data for the model test dataset (Test data), with the remaining 75% used as the training dataset (Training data).

For the model parameters, we set the maximum number of iterations to 10,000, allowing sufficient computational time for the algorithm to converge. The maximum background value was also set to 10,000, ensuring a robust background sampling that supports the model’s predictive accuracy. We employed the Jack-knife Test method to assess the contribution of each environmental variable, which helps in understanding their relative importance in predicting species distribution. The model outputs were generated using the logistic output format, which provides probability values ranging from 0 to 1, indicating the likelihood of species occurrence in various environmental conditions.

We took care to retain the default settings for other parameters to maintain the integrity of the model and to allow for a standardized comparison with other studies. The model’s prediction results are expressed as the probability of species occurrence, where higher values indicate more suitable environmental conditions for the species’ survival. This approach effectively reflects the actual distribution of species, making the MaxEnt model an essential tool for studying niches and species distributions.

Model evaluation

To evaluate the performance of the model, we utilized the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). The AUC values range from 0 to 1, where an AUC value of 0.50 indicates that the model performs no better than random prediction, whereas an AUC value of 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination25. We considered the model with the highest test AUC value to be the best performer. Additionally, we used response curves to illustrate the impact of the most significant ecogeographical variables on the MaxEnt model18.AUC is a commonly used metric for model evaluation, measuring the model’s predictive ability by calculating the true positive rate and false positive rate. A higher AUC value indicates that the model can more accurately distinguish between positive and negative samples26. In our study, we followed the generally accepted classification of AUC values for model adequacy: AUC values between 0.7 and 0.8 indicate acceptable performance, values between 0.8 and 0.9 are considered excellent, and values above 0.9 suggest outstanding model performance. By comparing the AUC values of multiple models, we could identify which model had the strongest predictive capability under different environmental conditions. The use of response curves further helped us understand the importance and impact of various ecogeographical variables in the model, providing a basis for optimizing the model.

To address the limitations of AUC, especially with small sample data and unevenly distributed occurrence points, we also incorporated TSS (True Skill Statistic) and Boyce’s index as additional evaluation metrics. TSS and Boyce’s index provide a more comprehensive assessment of model accuracy by considering sensitivity and specificity, allowing us to validate the model’s performance more effectively for datasets with fewer occurrence points. By including TSS and Boyce’s index, we aimed to strengthen the robustness of our evaluation, ensuring a balanced assessment that captures both predictive accuracy and ecological relevance, especially under conditions where occurrence data may be limited or unevenly spread. These additional metrics offer a more nuanced understanding of the model’s predictive capabilities and are particularly beneficial in supporting the reliability of the MaxEnt model for small-sample datasets.

Results

Model performance

In this section, we assess the model’s performance in predicting the distribution of four species: Pantholops hodgsonii, Equus kiang, Procapra picticaudata, and Bos mutus. The AUC values for all species distribution models exceeded 0.8, indicating high predictive accuracy. Specifically, the mean AUC for Pantholops hodgsonii was 0.905, for Equus kiang it was 0.875, for Procapra picticaudata it was 0.846, and for Bos mutus it was 0.920, as illustrated in Fig. 2. These findings demonstrate that our models provide robust predictions for the distribution of these species.

In addition to AUC, we evaluated model performance using the True Skill Statistic (TSS) and Boyce’s index. The TSS and Boyce results further underscore the model’s robustness in predicting species distribution. For Pantholops hodgsonii, the TSS was 0.816 and Boyce’s index was 0.908, indicating excellent model performance. Equus kiang had a TSS of 0.781 and Boyce’s index of 0.770, while Procapra picticaudata showed a TSS of 0.791 and a Boyce index of 0.855. Finally, Bos mutus achieved a TSS of 0.734 and a Boyce index of 0.839. These results align with the high AUC values, reinforcing the predictive accuracy and reliability of the models in capturing the distribution patterns of each species effectively.

Importance of environmental factors

In further analysis of the most influential factors for each species, we found that for Pantholops hodgsonii, the most critical variable was Bio18, which contributed 20.9% to the habitat suitability model (as shown in Table 2). This suggests that precipitation during the warmest quarter significantly affects the distribution of Pantholops hodgsonii, likely due to its influence on vegetation growth and water availability, which are crucial for the species’ survival and reproduction. The jackknife test of variable importance confirmed the significant role of Bio18, further underscoring its contribution to the model.

The response curve for Pantholops hodgsonii to Bio18, shown in Fig. 1, indicates a strong negative correlation between Bio18 and habitat suitability. As precipitation during the warmest quarter increases, habitat suitability for Pantholops hodgsonii decreases sharply. This trend suggests that the species prefers areas with lower precipitation during this period, with an optimal value around 120 mm, which may be associated with specific vegetation types or other ecological factors beneficial to the species.

For Equus kiang, the most important factor was Dem (elevation), contributing 33.6% to the habitat suitability model. The jackknife test also indicated the substantial influence of elevation on habitat suitability. The response curve for Equus kiang to Dem, shown in Fig. 3, demonstrates a strong negative relationship. Habitat suitability declines as elevation increases beyond a certain point, indicating that Equus kiang prefers mid-elevation ranges. This preference is likely due to the availability of optimal foraging grounds and escape terrain from predators. We determined the optimal elevation of around 4200 m based on the habitat suitability response curve, which indicated that this elevation maximizes habitat quality and foraging opportunities for the species.

Procapra picticaudata’s habitat suitability was predominantly influenced by Bio5, contributing a substantial 47.3%. The response curve for Procapra picticaudata to Bio5, shown in Fig. 4, reveals a positive relationship. Habitat suitability increases with the maximum temperature of the warmest month, suggesting that Procapra picticaudata thrives in warmer conditions, with an optimal temperature around 16 °C, possibly due to improved forage quality and availability.

For Bos mutus, NDVI was the most critical factor, contributing 64.6%. NDVI, labeled as Vg in our analysis, reflects vegetation health and coverage, suggesting that Bos mutus habitats are strongly dependent on areas with dense and healthy vegetation, providing necessary forage and cover. The response curve for Bos mutus to NDVI, shown in Fig. 4, indicates that the optimal NDVI value is around 0.5, highlighting the importance of high-quality vegetation for this species. The response curves for each of these factors illustrate the model’s performance in predicting habitat suitability for each species based on these key environmental variables.

Habitat suitability maps

Based on the suitability distribution maps and the suitability proportion, we found that the suitable habitat areas for the four species within the Sanjiangyuan research area are as follows: Pantholops hodgsonii (Tibetan antelope) has a suitable area of 19,719.913 (7.02% of the total area) square kilometers, primarily concentrated in the central and southeastern parts of the research region (Fig. 5-A). The threshold for suitability is 1.550, categorizing the areas into high, moderate, and low suitability. Equus kiang (kiang) has a suitable area of 31,094.99033(11.07% of the total area) square kilometers, widely distributed across the eastern and southern parts (Fig. 5-B). Its threshold value is 1.000, indicating that regions above this threshold represent high suitability. Procapra picticaudata (Tibetan gazelle) has the largest suitable habitat area, covering 50,556.01714 square kilometers(18% of the total area), spanning most of the central and northern regions (Fig. 5-C). With a threshold value of 1.000, the habitats above this limit are classified as highly suitable.Bos mutus (wild yak) has a suitable area of 14,648.29005(5.21% of the total area) square kilometers, with habitats relatively scattered in the western and southern parts (Fig. 5-D). The threshold for Bos mutus is 1.840, with suitable areas reflecting varying degrees of habitat quality.

The total area of the Sanjiangyuan research region is 280,878.786672 square kilometers. From these data, it is evident that the Tibetan gazelle has the largest suitable habitat area, accounting for 18.00% of the total research area, followed by the kiang with 11.07%, the Tibetan antelope with 7.02%, and the wild yak with the smallest suitable habitat area, accounting for 5.21%.

Future suitable maps

In this study, we utilized the Maxent model to map the suitable distribution areas of four species in 2023: Pantholops hodgsonii, Equus kiang, Procapra picticaudata, and Bos mutus. The current climate scenario’s distribution of suitable areas for these species is depicted in the provided figures. However, considering the ongoing and future impacts of climate change, we extended our analysis to predict changes in these suitable areas under future climate scenarios, specifically SSP245 and SSP585, for the years 2050 and 2070.

Through analyzing the variations under these future climate scenarios, we observed that while the suitable areas for the four species fluctuate across different scenarios, the overall trend indicates a reduction in their suitable habitats. This general decline highlights the potential adverse effects of climate change on these species’ distributions.

The detailed analysis results are outlined as follows: For Pantholops hodgsonii, the distribution of suitable areas under the SSP126 and SSP585 scenarios for 2050 and 2070 is illustrated in Figs. 6-A, 6-B, 6-C, and 6-D. Under the SSP126 scenario, the suitable area decreases by 2050 but still retains a considerable proportion (Fig. 6-A). By 2070, the area further diminishes (Fig. 6-C). Specifically, compared with the current distribution (1.97 × 10^4 km²), the suitable habitat area is projected to shrink by 45.7% in 2050 and 49.2% in 2070 under SSP126. Under the SSP585 scenario, suitable areas decline less sharply by 2050 (34.5% reduction) but undergo a dramatic contraction by 2070 (72.1% reduction) (Figs. 6-B and 6-D; Table 3). In addition, the overlapped suitable areas of the four species under future scenarios were mapped (Fig. 7), which highlights the contraction of co-distributed habitats, further indicating the increasing conservation challenges for P. hodgsonii and sympatric species.

Change in center of gravity

For Pantholops hodgsonii, under the SSP126 scenario, the suitable area for 2050 shows a notable northward shift of approximately 22,773 m from its current position. This shift suggests that the species will need to adapt to new habitats further north to survive. By 2070, this shift continues, albeit slightly reduced to 14,809 m, indicating a gradual but persistent movement of suitable areas over time(as shown in Fig. 7). In contrast, the SSP585 scenario projects an even more substantial displacement of the suitable area. By 2050, the suitable habitat moves 26,787 m from its current position, reflecting the immediate and significant impacts of a high-emission scenario. By 2070, the shift remains considerable at 23,550 m, further highlighting the extensive habitat alteration due to severe climate change. These changes underline the significant impact of climate change on the distribution of suitable habitats for Pantholops hodgsonii, potentially leading to challenges in migration, adaptation, and survival if the species cannot cope with the rapid environmental changes.

For Equus kiang, the results demonstrate a similar pattern of habitat displacement under future climate scenarios. Under the SSP126 scenario, the suitable area shifts by 41,722 m by 2050, indicating a significant relocation of the species’ preferred habitat. This northward movement continues, with the suitable area moving 31,188 m by 2070, suggesting ongoing adjustments to new environmental conditions. The SSP585 scenario, representing a more severe climate change impact, predicts an even more drastic shift in suitable areas. By 2050, the suitable habitat moves 86,252 m, showing a dramatic change in distribution due to increased temperatures and altered precipitation patterns. By 2070, the shift remains substantial at 22,358 m, indicating continuous habitat transformation. This trend of significant habitat displacement emphasizes the urgency of addressing climate change impacts on the species’ distribution, as such large-scale shifts could lead to habitat fragmentation, reduced genetic diversity, and increased competition for limited resources, ultimately threatening the long-term survival of Equus kiang.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the habitat suitability and spatial distribution of Pantholops hodgsonii, Equus kiang, Procapra picticaudata, and Bos mutus under current and projected climate scenarios in the Sanjiangyuan region. Our results emphasize that environmental variables—including temperature, precipitation, vegetation condition, and topography—are key determinants of habitat suitability. These findings are consistent with previous research highlighting the strong role of climate and vegetation in shaping the distribution of high-altitude ungulates3,27. In particular, the projected contraction of suitable habitats under the SSP585 scenario suggests that intensifying climate change will pose substantial risks to these iconic plateau species.

The reduction of suitable areas for Tibetan antelope and Tibetan gazelle aligns with findings from other alpine ecosystems, where rising temperatures and altered precipitation regimes have been shown to reduce forage availability and shift species elevational ranges28,29. Similarly, the decline of suitable habitats for the wild yak highlights the vulnerability of cold-adapted megafauna, echoing patterns observed for musk ox and reindeer in Arctic regions30. These parallels suggest that large herbivores adapted to extreme environments may be disproportionately affected by global warming, making proactive conservation strategies essential.

Beyond species-level implications, our results have broader ecological significance. The Sanjiangyuan region, known as the “Water Tower of Asia,” supports unique alpine ecosystems that are crucial for regional biodiversity and ecosystem services. The projected habitat loss under high-emission scenarios not only threatens the survival of individual species but may also disrupt trophic interactions and ecosystem stability. For instance, reduced overlap in the habitats of Tibetan antelope and kiang could alter grazing dynamics and competitive balances within alpine grasslands. From a conservation perspective, our findings support the urgent need to strengthen adaptive management in Sanjiangyuan National Park. Establishing and maintaining ecological corridors could help mitigate habitat fragmentation, particularly for wide-ranging species like the wild yak and kiang. Similar recommendations have been made in other mountainous regions, where connectivity has proven critical for facilitating species movements in response to climate change31,32. Moreover, integrating our climate-based projections with land-use change scenarios would provide a more comprehensive assessment of future threats, as human activities such as infrastructure development and overgrazing continue to reshape plateau landscapes. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. While the Maxent model is robust for species distribution modeling, it does not account for biotic interactions, such as predation or competition, which may influence realized distributions. Additionally, the current study excluded anthropogenic variables that can strongly affect habitats. Future research should therefore incorporate both ecological and human-driven processes, as well as evaluate the effectiveness of different conservation strategies under climate change scenarios. By combining ecological modeling with socio-ecological data, future work can provide more nuanced guidance for sustaining both wildlife populations and ecosystem functions in the Tibetan Plateau.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of habitat suitability and distribution for four key alpine species—Pantholops hodgsonii (Tibetan antelope), Equus kiang (kiang), Procapra picticaudata (Tibetan gazelle), and Bos mutus (wild yak)—within the Sanjiangyuan research area under current climatic conditions. Our findings highlight the differential influence of environmental variables on each species’ habitat suitability, emphasizing the critical roles of temperature, precipitation, vegetation condition, elevation, and terrain features.

For the Tibetan antelope, precipitation during the warmest quarter (Bio18) and terrain aspect were primary determinants of habitat suitability, reflecting the species’ reliance on sufficient water availability and favorable microclimates. The kiang’s distribution was largely influenced by elevation and seasonal precipitation (Bio15), consistent with its preference for mid-altitude grasslands. For the Tibetan gazelle, maximum temperature of the warmest month (Bio5) and vegetation health were dominant factors, suggesting adaptation to warmer conditions and high-quality forage. The wild yak’s habitat suitability was strongly linked to NDVI and aspect, underscoring its dependence on dense, healthy vegetation and specific terrain orientations.

The spatial analysis of suitable habitats revealed notable differences among species. The Tibetan gazelle occupies the largest suitable area, followed by the kiang, Tibetan antelope, and wild yak. While the Tibetan gazelle benefits from relatively continuous habitat, the wild yak’s suitable areas are highly fragmented, indicating the need for targeted conservation measures to enhance connectivity. Grasslands and unused lands constitute the majority of suitable habitats, providing essential forage and shelter for these large herbivores, particularly in semi-arid alpine regions.

Our results provide valuable guidance for conservation planning in the Sanjiangyuan region. Conservation efforts should be species-specific, focusing on the protection of critical habitats and maintenance of environmental conditions that support survival and reproduction. For example, habitat protection and restoration in central and southeastern regions should prioritize the Tibetan antelope, while the eastern and southern regions should be managed to maintain mid-altitude grasslands suitable for the kiang. Additionally, addressing habitat fragmentation, particularly for the wild yak, is essential to ensure long-term species persistence.

In summary, this study demonstrates the importance of integrating species-specific environmental requirements with spatial distribution data to inform effective conservation strategies. The insights gained here are crucial for anticipating the impacts of climate change on alpine wildlife and for developing adaptive management plans that safeguard biodiversity in the Sanjiangyuan region.
