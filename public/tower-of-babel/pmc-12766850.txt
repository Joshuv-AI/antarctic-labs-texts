Simulation-based training intervention using artificial intelligence to improve clinical bronchoscopy performance: a pre–postintervention study
Authors: Kristoffer M Cold, Arman Arshad, Christian Kildegaard, Christian B Laursen, Lars Konge, Anders B Nielsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDoes a simulation-based training intervention with an artificial intelligence (AI) navigation system improve their clinical bronchoscopy performance? And can the AIs outcome measures be used to evaluate clinical performance?DesignPre–postintervention study.SettingOdense University Hospital of Southern Denmark, pulmonary endoscopy suite.ParticipantsNine bronchoscopists (4 experienced, >500 bronchoscopies and 5 intermediates, 10–500 bronchoscopies).Primary outcome measuresDiagnostic completeness (DC), structured progress (SP), procedure time (PT) and procedure efficiency (DC/PT).ResultsThe primary outcome measures showed no statistically significant difference between the pre- and postintervention bronchoscopies DC: 53% versus 59%, p=0.16, SP: 29% versus 32%, p=0.35 and PT: 219 s versus 181 s, p=0.22. The experienced outperformed the intermediates regarding DC: 73% versus 43%, p<0.001, SP: 47% versus 13%, p<0.001 and procedure efficiency: 533 s/full inspection versus 274 s/full inspection, p<0.001 but not on PT: 189 s versus 208 s, p=0.53).ConclusionsDC, SP and PT showed no statistically significant difference after a simulation-based training intervention. DC, SP and procedure efficiency differentiated between experienced and intermediate bronchoscopists and can be used to evaluate clinical bronchoscopy performance.

Introduction

The leading cause of cancer mortality globally is lung cancer.1 Several countries have implemented screening programmes to decrease the mortality,2 and guidelines towards standardisation have been recommended.3 With increasing screening programmes, the number of flexible diagnostic bronchoscopies will increase and warrant training of more bronchoscopists.4 Novice bronchoscopists have higher complication rates with increased patient discomfort and exhibit lower yields of positive biopsy samples.5 An expert panel suggested the use of simulation-based training in bronchoscopy, to spare patients the initial part of novices’ learning curve.6 In bronchoscopy, the use of virtual reality (VR) simulators and bronchoscopy phantoms has proven effective,79 and potentially superior to clinical apprenticeship training.10 11 Measuring the transfer of simulation-based training to improved clinical performance is highly warranted,12 but only four studies have done so in bronchoscopy.7 A newly developed bronchial identification navigation system based on artificial intelligence (AI) has shown very promising effects for training of novice bronchoscopists13 (online supplemental video 1). It does so by tracking and providing feedback on whether the segments are fully, systematically and efficiently inspected with validated performance metrics in a simulated setting.14 The AI fuses the best of current training modalities, by merging VR-simulator feedback metrics with haptic and tactile input from bronchoscopy phantoms. Thereby, it could revolutionise bronchoscopy training,15 as intermediate and experienced bronchoscopists, also perform better with the AI than without it in a simulation-based setting.16 Additionally, a systematic review on AI in bronchoscopy concluded that no clinical AI systems have been tested17 and AI systems have improved the performance in other endoscopic disciplines.1820 An AI simulation-based training intervention and its performance metrics—which pertain to the rate, order and efficiency of segment inspection—could be transferable to a clinical setting, even for intermediate and experienced bronchoscopists.

Study aim

Does clinical bronchoscopy performance for intermediate and experienced bronchoscopists improve after a simulation-based training intervention using an AI navigation system? And can the AI’s simulation-based performance metrics be used to assess performance in a clinical setting?

Material and methods

Study design

The study was conducted as a single-centre prospective pre–post intervention trial at the Department of Respiratory Medicine, Odense University Hospital, Denmark (figure 1). The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for simulation research (online supplemental material 1).21 The study was deemed exempt from approval by the local ethics committee (F-23068101), as ethical approval is not required for simulation-based training studies in Denmark because they are regarded as quality assurance programmes. All patients gave informed consent that their bronchoscopy reporting could be used within this quality assurance programme. Each bronchoscopist was a consultant or resident pulmonologist. They performed three diagnostic bronchoscopies pre- and postintervention.

Simulation-based training intervention

Between the pre- and postintervention bronchoscopies, the bronchoscopists were referred to complete a simulation-based training intervention at the local simulation centre within the same hospital. They used a bronchial identification navigation system based on AI (Ambu BPS, Prototype version AmbuBPS-trainingGUIDE V.0.0.1, Ambu, online supplemental video 1) with a single-use bronchoscope (aScope 5, Ambu, Denmark), which was the same as used for the clinical bronchoscopies, on a Koken Bronchoscopy Model (Bronchoscopy Training Model LM-092, Koken Co., Tokyo, Japan) as it was used in previous studies13 14 16 22 and is the most thoroughly investigated AI tool in bronchoscopy.17 The phantom has 18 segments, with 10 on the right side (Right Bronchus (RB)1–10) and 8 on the left side, as there is no segment 7 and segment 1+2 was considered fused together (Left Bronchus (LB)1+2–10 without LB7). To complete the training programme, the bronchoscopist had to perform two bronchoscopies without AI assistance meeting the mastery learning criteria set by a previous study.22 Mastery learning is a training modality, where you train until proficiency targets are met, and its use was suggested by a CHEST expert panel.23 The AI rated the bronchoscopists on the following three outcome measures. Diagnostic completeness (DC): proportion of inspected segments,22 proficiency target: 18/18 segments. Structured progress (SP): number of structured progressions22—proficiency target: 18/18 progressions. Procedure time (PT): time from visualising the carina to extraction of the scope13—proficiency target: <120 s. The bronchoscopists were instructed to perform according to the outcome measures for their clinical bronchoscopies, by visiting all segments, in a structured order as efficiently as possible, following the department’s instructions. The time spans between preprocedure bronchoscopies, the intervention and postprocedure bronchoscopies should not exceed 2 weeks to ensure retention from the intervention. Time spent training was monitored and logged by the coauthor CK for all bronchoscopists and registered through their informed consent forms (online supplemental material 2).

Evaluation of clinical bronchoscopies

An overview of the complete evaluation workflow—including video recording, blinded annotation, segment adjudication and calculation of outcome measures—is visually summarised in figure 1. The pre- and postintervention bronchoscopies were recorded, so they could be rated by two annotators who were trained in lung anatomy by an expert thoracic surgeon with a PhD in assessment of flexible bronchoscopy (LK).24 The raters were blinded to the bronchoscopies being pre- or postintervention. Each video was initially annotated by one annotator, who extracted the frames necessary to describe when the scope was viewing and/or entering bronchial segments. The first annotator added labels to all displayed bronchial segments in the selected frames, including information about the name of the bronchial segment and boxed the size of the bronchial segments (figure 1—blue annotation). Subsequently, the same frames were annotated with boxes and labels by a second annotator, who was blinded to the annotations of the first annotator (figure 1—green annotation). Each video was rated by the annotators on a scale of 1–3 (1=easy anatomy, 2=known anatomical variations, 3=difficult unknown anatomy). The labels from the first and second annotators were compared for the number of labels, the specific labels used and the size and location of the labels per frame. Any inconsistencies were discussed and corrected. The annotation process was based on the same 18 bronchial segments.

All labels were assessed by an engineer trained in lung anatomy, who selected videos displaying abnormal anatomy (including videos rated 2 or 3 by the annotators) for review by one or two expert consultants (LK and AA) to ensure accurate labelling of the displayed anatomy. Finally, the labels were corrected according to the advice from the experts.

Known anatomical variances (eg, RB9 and RB10 fused together) were rated as both visited if the joint segment took up >80% of the image. Unclassifiable anatomical variances were indicated as ‘other’ and were included in the analyses.

Based on the size of the annotation of the bronchial segments, it was determined whether a segment was visited. The primary outcome measures were based on an 80% cut-off, that is, the box covering the segment must take up ≥80% of the image size to be regarded as visited. DC80, SP80, procedure efficiency80 (= mean intersegmental time80 (MIT80)=DC80/PT), inspection time80 (time spent with a segment ≥80% of the image size), navigation time80 (time spent with a segment taking up <80% of the image size) and PT (navigation time +inspection time) and visualised segments20 (the box covering the opening of a segment takes up ≥20% of the image). The secondary outcome measures were the same but corresponding to a 10% and 30% cut-off for visualised segments and 70% and 90% for DC and its relating outcome measures. Revisits80 (revisited segments at the 80% threshold) were also included as a secondary outcome measure. As anatomical variations exist, segments were also registered as missing or blocked. DC and SP would therefore be divided with the number of available segments for the corresponding bronchoscopy. Example: 10 visited segments to the 80% level, but two missing segments and one blocked segment (DC80=10/(18-2-1) = 67%). MIT (=PT/DC) is a measure of procedure efficiency and has proven discriminatory ability on performance in previous studies.162528 The same outcome measure was added as a primary outcome measure, but as DC80 was used as a fraction, the name was changed to procedure efficiency80, as the fraction (PT/DC80) is translatable to time if all segments had been visited based on the same segment inspection efficiency.

Evaluation of usability of clinical outcome measures

The validity of using the outcome measures DC80, SP80 and procedure efficiency80 was conducted using Messick’s validity framework,29 recommended by the American Educational Research Association.30 Those three outcome measures were chosen as they have proven discriminatory abilities in previous, simulation-based studies.162527

Bronchoscopists (study participants, power and patients)

Experienced bronchoscopists (>500 bronchoscopies) or intermediate bronchoscopists (10–500 bronchoscopies) were included as study participants. The group intervals were based on previous assessment studies of the outcome measures.26 31 Along with informed consent to participate in the study, basic demographics of all bronchoscopists were collected: job position (consultant or resident), bronchoscopies performed, years of bronchoscopy experience and bronchoscopies performed within last 6 months (online supplemental material 3). To ensure the individual bronchoscopist was not identifiable, their sex and age are known to the authors but were not published. We aimed to recruit 4 intermediate bronchoscopists (10–500 bronchoscopies performed), as a similar study showed a significant training effect in cataract surgery for 4 intermediate operators.32 We aimed to include at least 4 experienced bronchoscopists (>500 bronchoscopies performed), to allow for comparison between the 2 groups. Intermediate bronchoscopists performed the clinical bronchoscopies independently; however, in accordance with the study design, a senior clinician was present to supervise in case a complication occurred but was not permitted to provide feedback or interfere with the navigational bronchoscopy performed as part of this study. Patients were referred for a diagnostic bronchoscopy due to malignant suspicion on a CT-thorax.

Patient and public involvement

None

Statistical analysis

Mixed-model, repeated measures one-way ANOVA (ANalys Of VAriance) was used to examine the effect of the training intervention, by comparing performance according to the primary and secondary outcome measures between the pre- and postintervention bronchoscopies. As all bronchoscopists had performed >10 bronchoscopies before inclusion to the study, we expected the case-to-case learning effect to be negligible. The outcome measures between the bronchoscopies performed by intermediates and experienced bronchoscopists were compared using a linear mixed model with experience level as a factor. R (R V.4.1.2, Vienna, Austria) was used for statistical testing and figures.

Results

From 8 November 2023 to 9 February 2024, 9 bronchoscopists (5 intermediate and 4 experienced) completed the study (online supplemental material 3) for bronchoscopists’ demographics), making 54 bronchoscopies eligible for analysis (27 pre-intervention and 27 postintervention, figure 1). The bronchoscopists spent a mean of 40 min (SD: ±15 min) to complete the simulation-based training intervention, with a mean of 6.3 days (SD: ±7.9 days) between the training intervention and first postintervention bronchoscopy (online supplemental material 3). There was no statistically significant difference in the primary outcome measures, between the pre- and postintervention bronchoscopies, DC80: 53% versus 59% of available segments, p=0.16 (figure 2), SP80: 29% versus 32% of available progressions, p=0.35 (online supplemental figure 1), PT: 218 s versus 181 s, p=0.22, procedure efficiency80: 544 s/DC80 versus 402 s/DC80, p=0.09, navigation time80: 187 s versus 157 s, p=0.25, segment inspection time80: 32 s versus 24 s, p=0.29, visualised segments20: 77% versus 80% of available segments, p=0.42 (table 1). There were neither any statistically significant difference in the secondary outcome measures, between the pre- and postintervention bronchoscopies (online supplemental material 4). Neither of the primary nor secondary outcome measures were statistically significant when dividing the groups into intermediates and experienced (online supplemental material 5). There were no significant correlations between time spent training during the AI simulation-based training intervention and the primary outcome measures. The intermediates compared with the experienced bronchoscopists had a lower DC80: 43% versus 73% of available segments, p<0.001, lower SP80: 18% versus 47% of available progressions, p<0.0001, lower procedure efficiency80: 533 s/DC80 versus 274 s/DC80, p<0.001, higher navigation time80: 188 s versus 152 s, p=0.005, lower segment inspection time80: 20 s versus 37 s, p=0.040 but not a statistically significant lower PT80: 208 s versus 189 s, p=0.53 (table 2). In general, SP80 correlated significantly with DC80 (Pearson’s correlation, P-value; 0.82, p<0.001), with procedure efficiency80 (0.50, p<0.001) and DC80 with procedure efficiency80 (0.56, p<0.001) (online supplemental material 6). All outcome measures for all bronchoscopies are available as online supplemental material 7.

Discussion

This is the first study using DC80, SP80 and procedure efficiency80 as clinical outcome measures. The outcome measures did not improve statistically significantly from the intervention. The experienced outperformed the intermediate bronchoscopists, providing validity evidence for those outcome measures.

Experienced bronchoscopists

Several systematic reviews have found simulation-based training to be effective when learning bronchoscopy79; however, few are transfer studies,12 measuring transferability to improved clinical performance and all studies have focused on training of novices.11 Experienced and intermediate bronchoscopists perform better with the AI system enabled in a simulated setting than without.16 We therefore performed this as the first study measuring the effect of a simulation-based training intervention with the AI on intermediate and experienced bronchoscopists,12 but we did not find any significant effects on clinical performance (table 1 and online supplemental material 4). Multiple factors could cause or influence this finding. Experienced bronchoscopists are on a less steep part of their learning curve than novices and therefore less likely to improve their performance after a relatively small intervention (intervention training time: 40±15 min). The training effect decreases with experience,16 which is supported by a similarly designed study32 and proposed through figure 3. Experienced bronchoscopists are also less likely to change their operating patterns. Fitts and Posner’s motor learning theory divides learning into three phases: cognitive, associative and the autonomous phase.33 The experienced bronchoscopist is at the autonomous phase, where bronchoscopy skills become automatic, requiring minimal cognitive effort.33 Adapting to the training intervention would require the experienced bronchoscopist to take a step back into the cognitive and associative phases. However adapting from autonomous techniques is difficult.34 It is true even for the most competitive environments, as the high jumpers took years to transition to the Fosbury Flop, after Dick Fosbury won the Olympic gold medal in 1968 in Mexico City.34 The previously ubiquitous straddle jump could still be seen 20 years after in Olympic games in Seoul in 1988.

General efficacy model of a simulation-based training intervention based on experience level. Previous studies have detected a decreased effect (ie, smaller gain between the dots on the Y-axis) of similar simulation-based training interventions (illustrated by the two red arrows of similar size) with experience (indicated on the X-axis). In the current study, the training intervention was too small, experience level too high and/or the number of participants too low to detect a statistically significant improvement in clinical performance after the simulation-based training intervention.

Learning correct navigational techniques is important in the beginning of the learning curve. Colt et al25 used a VR simulator to train novice bronchoscopists in navigational skills, making them able to outperform experienced bronchoscopists on a bronchoscopy phantom by having a higher DC. DC is the most frequently used performance measure when learning and assessing bronchoscopy skills,7 9 highlighting the consensus that a diagnostic bronchoscopy should start with a full and systematic inspection of the bronchial tree.12 Previous studies have shown that even when experienced bronchoscopists are instructed to inspect all segments, like in our study, several are missed both in a simulated and clinical setting.112527 31 35 This is supported by our study (DC80 experienced: 73%±18% proportion of available segments, figure 2), with segments also being not visualised (visualised segments20 experienced; 87%±13% proportion of available segments) (online supplemental material 4).

This is the first study to provide validity evidence for these three performance measures in a clinical setting: DC80, SP80 and procedure efficiency80. They are based on already established outcome measures from a simulation-based setting (Messick’s validity framework Aspect 1: content),13 16 22 they can be assessed based on rigid annotation by specially trained annotators (Aspect 2: response process), they correlate with one another (Aspect 3: internal structure, online supplemental material 6) and the experienced outperformed the intermediate bronchoscopists (Aspect 4: correlation to other variables, table 2). While this study aids in investigating bronchoscopy patterns in intermediate and experienced bronchoscopists, further studies and consensus statements should be made to standardise how thoroughly segments should be inspected/visited and to set a pass-fail standard (Aspect 5: consequence of testing). Only about half of the segments were visited (pre-intervention vs postintervention DC80: 53% vs 59% of available segments) with minimal differences to the DC90 and DC70 levels (table 1 and online supplemental material 4). A clinical AI application could help in setting standards and ensuring uniform inspection in bronchoscopy. Especially as the AI system has proven helpful for experienced bronchoscopists in a simulated setting, as they have a higher DC with the AI enabled16 and the AI can reliably assess performance.14 A future clinical application of the AI system could therefore help in ensuring a thorough and systematic bronchoscopy while also certifying the bronchoscopists to visit all segments in a systematic order as efficiently as possible.36

Intermediate bronchoscopists: A similar study using the same study design with a VR-cataract surgery simulator did not find the experienced to improve clinical performance either.32 However, both the novices and intermediate surgeons did.32 Their differences in performance were more likely to be detectable as cataract surgery is a more complex procedure than a diagnostic bronchoscopy making the learning curve prolonged and thereby increasing the effect of a training intervention in the middle part (figure 3). Including complete novices in our study would have increased the possibility of detecting an improvement (figure 3), but as they are initially trained at a different hospital, it was not possible for our study. In ANalys Of VAriance (EBUS), bronchoscopists performed better in a clinical setting when being randomised to simulation-based training than to traditional clinical apprenticeship training.10 Simulation-based training allows for practising in a safe environment and going through multiple cases in a short amount of time. The American and European Respiratory Societies therefore suggest and have implemented simulation-based training and certification courses in EBUS. Surgical residents also show better clinical performance from simulation-based training with mastery learning on hernia repair than traditional apprenticeship training.37 The question regarding simulation-based training in bronchoscopy is therefore rather how than if to ensure bronchoscopists develop the correct techniques in the beginning of their learning curve.12 38 The SP score has been identified as a performance measure with high validity evidence,7 26 and the first score being evaluated according to Messick’s validity framework in bronchoscopy.26 The SP80 score in our study was low (18%±13% of available progressions), indicating a low degree of systematic inspection. SP80 correlated significantly with a higher DC80 (Pearson’s r, P-value; 0.82, p<0.001). This finding is supported by previous studies, as bronchoscopists who have a more systematic inspection also visit more segments.7 13 14 16 26 It is important to teach novices the correct techniques at the beginning of the learning curve, so they do not develop bad habits. Simulation-based training has proven useful in doing so, as the bronchial segments are named after an intended orderly progression through the bronchial tree, with SP being the best indicator to differentiate bronchoscopy proficiency in a simulated setting.7 13 14 16 26

Strengths and limitations: This study is the most thorough evaluation of clinical bronchoscopy performance, using video recordings and two blinded, trained annotators with disagreements being sorted by expert raters. Based on our intervention we did not detect a statistically significant increase in performance, which could be attributed to the limitations of this study. All the primary outcome measures seemed to improve from the intervention, but none were statistically significant. These findings may be due to chance, or the study could be susceptible to a Type II error, thereby being underpowered. Unfortunately, we could only provide one bronchoscopy simulator and would recruit all bronchoscopists at one department limiting the sample size. In a previous study, novices had a mean of 114 min to complete the training intervention,13 and the low training intervention time was expectedly lower for our study (40±15 min), which could indicate a too small intervention to improve performance (figure 3), as there might also have been too long time between the intervention and first postintervention bronchoscopy (6.3±7.9 days) diminishing the intervention’s effect. The effect of the training intervention could be maximised in future studies by introducing just-in-time simulation training where participants perform a perfect simulated bronchoscopy immediately prior to each clinical procedure. We would then expect the simulation-based performance (DC80=100%, SP80=100% and PT<120 s) to be better transferable to the clinical setting than in the current study (postintervention mean: DC80=59%, SP80=32%, PT=181 s). Some training is better than no training and comparative effectiveness and cost-analysis could have been more elaborately addressed with this study. We chose to use the AI system as it is superior to expert instructions even for more skilled bronchoscopists39 and has shown to be very useful in previous training studies.13 16 22 Procedural volume does not guarantee expertise40 and other assessment tools could, therefore, have been used in categorising expertise.35 No international consensus exists on defining experience level based on a set number of procedures, leading to different studies using different classifications.16 26 28 However, by gathering validity evidence for the primary outcome measures, the categorisation was useful in collecting validity evidence for Aspect 4: relationship to other variables (experience), which is the most frequently used source of validity evidence.41 Comparison with other assessment tools such as Bronchoscopic Skills and Tasks Assessment Tool (BSTAT) could have collected further validity evidence for Aspect 4.

Conclusions

A 40-min simulation-based training intervention using mastery learning did not improve DC, SP or PT significantly. The experienced outperformed the intermediate bronchoscopists, indicating DC, SP and procedure efficiency to be reliable outcome measures to evaluate bronchoscopy performance.
