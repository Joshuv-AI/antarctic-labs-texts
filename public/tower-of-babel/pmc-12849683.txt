Concomitant infectious foci in community-acquired bacterial meningitis: the clinical impact of concomitant spondylitis and endocarditis
Authors: Takafumi Hosokawa, Shoji Ogawa, Taiki Sawai, Yukiyo Yoshimoto, Yoshitsugu Nakamura, Shin Ota, Takahiko Hirose, Hideto Nakajima, Shigeki Arawaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity-acquired bacterial meningitis has been linked to concurrent infectious foci in certain cases. However, reports specifically addressing concomitant infectious foci in patients with community-acquired bacterial meningitis, particularly in recent years, remain scarce. Moreover, the distinct characteristics of various types of concomitant infectious foci in bacterial meningitis cases have yet to be fully elucidated.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 35 consecutive patients diagnosed with community-acquired bacterial meningitis. We assessed the presence, distribution, and frequency of concomitant infectious foci, followed by an evaluation of the clinical characteristics of patients with these infections. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the period of onset: the early-period group (2004–2013) and the late-period group (2014–2024).ResultsAmong the 35 patients, 20 (57%) presented with concomitant infections. Based on the site of infection, 7 patients (20%) had sinusitis, 4 (11%) had otitis, 3 (9%) had pneumonia, 8 (24%) had spondylitis, and 4 (12%) had endocarditis. The total number of patients with spondylitis and/or endocarditis was 11 (31%). A higher proportion of patients with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis belonged to the late-period group (9 of 11, 82%) compared to those without these infections (9 of 24 38%).ConclusionsSpondylitis and endocarditis were observed as concomitant infectious foci in patients with community-acquired bacterial meningitis, and their occurrence appeared more frequent than in earlier reports. In addition, the data from our cohort indicate a possible increasing trend in recent years, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Bacterial meningitis is an acute condition characterized by inflammation of the arachnoid and pia mater surrounding the brain and spinal cord, caused by bacterial infection. Despite advances in medical care, its prognosis remains unfavorable, with reported mortality rates ranging from 15% to 35% and rates of long-term sequelae between 10% and 30%. [1] The incidence of bacterial meningitis varies significantly across different geographical regions. In high-income countries, particularly developed nations, incidence rates have declined, reaching 0.9 per 100,000 population, as observed in 2014 in the United States and the Netherland.[2, 3]

Community-acquired bacterial meningitis has been associated with concomitant infectious foci in certain cases. Sinusitis, otitis, and pneumonia are relatively common concomitant infections [1, 3, 4], whereas concomitant spondylitis [5] and endocarditis [6], though infrequent, have also been reported. The presence and type of these concomitant infections play a crucial role in determining treatment strategies and clinical outcomes, highlighting their significance in medical practice. In developed countries, including Japan, rapid societal transformations such as accelerated aging are markedly progressing. Consequently, the demographic and clinical characteristics of bacterial meningitis patients may also be evolving, with potential changes in the presence, location, and frequency of concomitant infectious foci. However, reports specifically addressing concomitant infectious foci in patients with community-acquired bacterial meningitis, particularly in recent years in Japan, remain scarce. Moreover, the distinct characteristics of various types of concomitant infectious foci in bacterial meningitis cases have yet to be fully elucidated.

This study aims to investigate the presence, distribution, and frequency of concomitant infectious foci in patients with community-acquired bacterial meningitis. Additionally, we analyzed the clinical characteristics of bacterial meningitis patients with these concomitant infectious foci.

Methods

Patients dentification

This retrospective study was conducted between January 2004 and December 2024 at Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University Hospital, a tertiary university hospital with 894 beds and 29 clinical departments, and at Hirakata Municipal Hospital, a 602-bed regional general hospital. Potential cases were initially screened using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. From these, we excluded patients with hospital-acquired meningitis, with a neurosurgical device and those who had undergone neurosurgery or had head traumas within 1 month prior to onset. After applying these exclusion criteria, only patients fulfilling the predefined diagnostic criteria for community-acquired bacterial meningitis were included.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinical features, such as a headache, fever, and signs of meningeal irritation; (2) positive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings, including pleocytosis ( > 5mm3, mainly neutrophilic), elevated protein concentrations ( > 45 mg/dL), and a reduced ratio of CSF glucose to serum glucose ( < 0.60), with at least one of these abnormalities being sufficient for inclusion; (3) a negative CSF stain, culture, or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for viruses, mycobacteria, and fungi; and (4) a positive CSF culture, smear, or PCR for bacterial pathogens or a good specific response to anti-bacterial therapy. [7] Patients with positive CSF soluble antigen alone or positive blood cultures alone were not considered to meet criterion (4) solely on this basis. However, in patients who fulfilled criterion (4) by other means (i.e., those diagnosed by a good specific response to antibacterial therapy), the bacteria identified by these tests were regarded as the causative pathogens.

While assessing the presence, location, and frequency of concomitant infectious foci, we excluded secondary infections affecting other organs that were not present at admission but emerged more than one week after hospitalization from this definition. For example, patients who had no respiratory symptoms or pulmonary opacities on chest CT at admission but developed these findings three weeks later were not classified as having concomitant infectious foci.

To evaluate baseline comorbidities, the Charlson Comorbidity Index was calculated for all patients. The Charlson Comorbidity Index assigns weighted points to chronic diseases, with higher scores indicating greater comorbidity burden and mortality risk. Scores of 0–1 were considered mild, 2–3 moderate, and ≥4 severe. [8] Effects on clinical outcome measured with the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS; 1 = death, 2 = vegetative state, 3 = severe disabilities, 4 = moderate disabilities, 5 = no/mild disabilities) were assessed. [9] Patients were divided into two groups based on the onset period: the early-period group (January 2004 to December 2013) and the late-period group (January 2014 to December 2024).

This study was conducted according to the 2013 Helsinki Declaration, and the Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University Ethics Committee approved the study protocol and the need for informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective study and the data were collected without individual patient identifiers (Approval number #2020–190-1)．

Statistical analyses

Given the small number of cases, we adopted a descriptive rather than inferential statistical approach for between-group comparisons. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. All analyses were performed using the JMP software version 15.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, the USA).

Regarding sex, age, and underlying diseases showed no notable differences between the two groups when compared descriptively. However, cases from the late period (2014–2024) were more frequently observed in the group with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis. In addition, both the duration from symptom onset to admission and the duration from symptom onset to initiation of antibiotic therapy tended to be longer in patients with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis than in those without these conditions.

In terms of laboratory findings, patients with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis tended to show higher rate of positive blood cultures compared to those without. Concerning the causative pathogens, Staphylococcus aureus was more commonly identified in patients with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis.

Regarding treatment, the duration of antibiotic therapy tended to be longer in the group with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis. Surgical intervention was also more frequently observed in this group. Specifically, surgery was performed in five patients with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis, compared with four in the group without these complications. The types of procedures reflected the underlying infectious foci or related complications.

With regard to prognosis, patients with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis generally had longer hospital stays, whereas Glasgow Outcome Scale scores at discharge were broadly similar between groups.

Discussion

Our study suggests that concomitant spondylitis and endocarditis may occur with relatively high frequency in patients with community-acquired bacterial meningitis, with their incidence appearing higher than what has been described in previous reports. In this study, the incidence of concomitant spondylitis reached 23%. Although reports on the prevalence of spondylitis in bacterial meningitis are scarce, particularly from large-scale epidemiological studies, a large-scale study conducted in the Netherlands between 2006 and 2018 documented a prevalence of only 0.5% among bacterial meningitis patients. [5] The incidence of concomitant endocarditis in our study was 11%. While data on the frequency of endocarditis in bacterial meningitis remain limited, especially from large-scale epidemiological studies, a previous large-scale study conducted in the Netherlands between 2006 and 2012 reported a prevalence of merely 2% among bacterial meningitis patients. [6] In contrast, the incidence of concomitant sinusitis, otitis, and pneumonia in patients with bacterial meningitis was comparable to previous reports. In our study, the incidence of concomitant sinusitis, otitis, or at least one of these conditions in bacterial meningitis was 20%, 11%, and 26%, respectively. Although studies specifically examining the incidence of sinusitis alone in bacterial meningitis are limited, previous research has reported an incidence of concomitant otitis ranging from 15% to 21% [10–13] and an incidence of at least one of sinusitis or otitis between 20% and 34% [1, 3, 14]. Similarly, in the present study, the incidence of pneumonia among bacterial meningitis patients was 9%, aligning with prior reports indicating a prevalence of 8% to 19%.[1, 3, 15–20]

Our study indicated a higher frequency of concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis in the late-period group (2014–2024) compared to the early-period group (2004–2013). This observation is consistent with the findings that the incidence of these infections in our study was higher than that reported in previous studies completed prior to the end of our study period. [5, 6] It also aligns with reports describing increased incidences of pyogenic spondylitis and infective endocarditis in some recent cohorts. [21–23] However, owing to the limited sample size and the observational nature of our study, we refrain from asserting a causal relationship between these background epidemiologic reports and the pattern observed in our cohort.

In addition, we investigated factors that may have influenced concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis. Patients with spondylitis and/or endocarditis tended to have longer durations from onset to hospital admission, and consequently longer durations from onset to initiation of antibiotic therapy, compared with those without these complications, in a descriptive comparison. No notable differences were observed in age, sex, or underlying comorbidities, including the Charlson comorbidity index. Previous studies have reported that a longer interval from onset to antibiotic treatment is associated with higher mortality [24], and our findings suggest that severe concomitant infections such as endocarditis or spondylitis may also be more likely to occur in this context. However, although the frequency of endocarditis and spondylitis appears to have increased compared with earlier reports, it remains unclear whether this increase can be fully explained by prolonged pre-hospital intervals, as precise comparisons of the time from onset to admission and from onset to antibiotic initiation between our cohort and previous studies are not available.

At the same time, the proportion of patients who underwent spinal MRI to detect spondylitis and echocardiography to detect endocarditis was higher in the late-period group than in the early-period group. Therefore, the observed rise in concomitant infectious foci may reflect not only a true epidemiological trend but also improved diagnostic capabilities. Furthermore, it is possible that the actual prevalence of spondylitis and endocarditis as concomitant infections in bacterial meningitis is even higher than previously reported. Further studies are warranted to clarify these aspects and to underscore the importance of systematic spinal MRI and echocardiographic.

In our study, bacterial meningitis accompanied by concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis demonstrated a less pronounced elevation in CSF cell count and neutrophil ratio compared to bacterial meningitis without these conditions. This observation can be explained by previous reports indicating that bacterial meningitis with a modest increase in CSF cell count is associated with excessive bacterial growth, bacteremia, sepsis, and other systemic complications. [25–28] Consistent with the findings in our study, bacterial meningitis with concomitant endocarditis has been shown to exhibit lower CSF inflammatory markers, including a reduced white blood cell count. [6] In contrast, no large-scale studies have specifically examined the CSF findings in bacterial meningitis with concomitant spondylitis. However, as this condition has been reported to predominantly result from hematogenous spread of bacteria [5], it is likely associated with bacteremia, which, in turn, may correlate with a modest increase in CSF cell count. Moreover, some cases of this condition are believed to arise from spondylitis triggering an inflammatory response in the CSF through the development of a paraspinal or epidural abscess, in which case the inflammatory reaction in meningitis is suggested to be mild. [5] Taken together, these observations suggest that in cases of bacterial meningitis with mild cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities, clinicians should not be reassured by the seemingly benign presentation but should exercise heightened vigilance for potential concomitant infection foci and conduct thorough investigations to ensure timely diagnosis and appropriate management.

Our data further suggest that in bacterial meningitis, Staphylococcus aureus is predominantly responsible for cases with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis, in agreement with prior studies. [29] In our cohort, Staphylococcus aureus was identified as the causative agent in 9% of all bacterial meningitis cases. However, this prevalence increased to 27% when considering only those with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis. Although recent studies have reported a lower overall prevalence of Staphylococcus aureus meningitis, ranging from 1% to 3% [3], they similarly demonstrate that Staphylococcus aureus is more frequently identified in cases complicated by concomitant spondylitis (30%) [5] and endocarditis (33%). [6] Thus, the trend observed in our study—namely, the higher frequency of Staphylococcus aureus among patients with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis—is consistent with the existing literature. From the perspective of Staphylococcus aureus meningitis, although our study identified only three cases, all were associated with either spondylitis or endocarditis-two with spondylitis and one with endocarditis. The high prevalence of these concomitant conditions in our cohort is consistent with earlier reports, which have noted rates of 39–60% for spondylitis and 25–56% for endocarditis. [29–35] These findings underscore the importance of considering the possibility of concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis, particularly when Staphylococcus aureus is the implicated pathogen.

Our results also indicate that among patients with bacterial meningitis, those with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis have a longer duration of antibiotic therapy and tend to have a higher frequency of surgical intervention compared to those without these concomitant conditions, with the types and specifics of the surgeries differing between the groups as expected. Regarding the duration of antibiotic therapy, the treatment period in our cohort was generally longer than the commonly recommended durations, and we acknowledge that this may limit generalizability. However, the observation that patients with meningitis complicated by concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis received longer courses of antibiotics is consistent with previous reports. For meningitis without systemic infections, international recommendations suggest 10 to 14 days of antibiotic treatment [36], whereas international guidelines recommend approximately 6 weeks for most cases of vertebral spondylitis [37] and infectious endocarditis [38], including infections caused by S. aureus. Specifically, in cases of meningitis complicated by endocarditis, Lucas MJ et al. recommend a treatment duration of 4 to 6 weeks. [6] Regarding surgical intervention, an increased risk for surgical intervention has also been reported in patients with bacterial meningitis who develop concomitant spondylitis [5] and endocarditis [6]. Moreover, while our findings did not indicate an association between the presence of spondylitis and/or endocarditis with poor prognosis in bacterial meningitis, previous studies have suggested that the presence of endocarditis may contribute to adverse outcomes.[6]

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, it is a retrospective analysis based on cases from only two hospitals, with a limited sample size. As a result, the extent to which these findings—especially a recent increase in the prevalence of spondylitis and/or endocarditis as concomitant infections in bacterial meningitis patients—can be extrapolated to the broader Japanese population remains unclear. Although our study suggests a recent increase in the prevalence of these complications, the relatively small sample size means that the possibility of random error cannot be excluded. For example, the proportion of staphylococcal meningitis in our cohort is higher than in recent reports, as noted earlier, which cannot be ruled out as reflecting sampling variation; because Staphylococcus aureus is more likely to cause concomitant spondylitis or endocarditis, the frequency of these complications cannot be ruled out as being overestimated. In addition, due to the limited number of cases, comprehensive statistical analyses, including multivariate analysis, could not be performed. Second, caution is required when extrapolating these findings to other developed nations or the global context, as the pathogen distribution in Japan differs substantially from that in Europe and North America. In particular, national guidelines indicate that meningococcal meningitis is markedly less frequent in Japan, whereas non-pneumococcal streptococcal meningitis is relatively more common. [39] Since meningococcal disease is known to cause fewer secondary focal infections, applying our results directly to Western populations cannot exclude the possibility of overestimating these concomitant infections. Future multicenter prospective studies across multiple regions with larger sample sizes are warranted.

Third, the criteria used to define cases in this study have certain constraints. Patients without typical symptoms such as headache, fever, or meningeal irritation were excluded, although bacterial meningitis can occur without these features according to the 2016 ESCMID recommendations. [40] Thus, atypical presentations may have been missed. Conversely, several patients were diagnosed based on a favorable clinical response to antibacterial therapy despite lacking microbiological confirmation. This inclusion was necessary to capture clinically convincing cases in which pathogen identification was difficult due to prior antibiotic use or technical limitations, and in fact, more than half of the patients had CSF cultures performed after antibiotic administration. Although this approach may have allowed a few non-bacterial meningitis cases, it was essential for capturing the full clinical spectrum of suspected cases. These methodological considerations should be noted when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

Spondylitis and endocarditis may occur as concomitant infectious foci in community-acquired bacterial meningitis, potentially higher frequency than previously reported, and their incidence appears to have been increasing in recent years. Bacterial meningitis with concomitant spondylitis and/or endocarditis may tend to show a relatively milder increase in cerebrospinal fluid cell count and neutrophil proportion, and Staphylococcus aureus appears to be more frequently observed as the causative pathogen. In such cases, we maintain a high index of suspicion for these infectious foci and perform comprehensive diagnostic evaluations to facilitate prompt identification and optimal management.
