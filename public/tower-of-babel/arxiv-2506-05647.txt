Learning to Weight Parameters for Data Attribution
Shuangqi Li, Hieu Le, Jingyi Xu, Mathieu Salzmann

Abstract
We study data attribution in generative models, aiming to identify which training examples most influence a given output. Existing methods achieve this by tracing gradients back to training data. However, they typically treat all network parameters uniformly, ignoring the fact that different layers encode different types of information and may thus draw information differently from the training set. We propose a method that models this by learning parameter importance weights tailored for attribution, without requiring labeled data. This allows the attribution process to adapt to the structure of the model, capturing which training examples contribute to specific semantic aspects of an output, such as subject, style, or background. Our method improves attribution accuracy across diffusion models and enables fine-grained insights into how outputs borrow from training data.



1
Introduction

Tracing how specific training examples influence the outputs of generative models—known as data attribution—is critical for transparency, copyright protection, and data governance. Diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Ramesh et al. 2022
)
, in particular, complicate this task due to their iterative, stochastic generation process, which obscures direct associations between training data and outputs. Gradient-based attribution methods
(
Charpiat et al. 2019
;
Pruthi et al. 2020
)
have emerged as a practical solution, estimating influence by comparing gradients between training and test examples. More recently, kernel-based methods
(
Park et al. 2023
;
Georgiev et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2023
)
have extended this idea
by leveraging a Hessian matrix computed based on a linearized model,
offering a scalable way to approximate influence in a single pass without retraining, with strong empirical performance across a range of models.

Interestingly, as we will show in
section

3.1
, kernel-based attribution methods implicitly assign different importance to different parts of the network. While not their primary focus, this aligns with a well-established understanding of a model’s internal mechanisms: Different layers tend to encode different aspects of the output and may rely on different subsets of the training data
(
Ronneberger et al. 2015
;
Sclocchi et al. 2025
)
. For example, in diffusion models, early layers typically capture low-level visual features, such as texture and style, while deeper layers and attention blocks tend to model more holistic semantics. The gradients extracted from these isolated components hence vary not only in magnitude but also in attribution
signal,
suggesting that a uniform treatment of the network may overlook important and functionally distinct signals. To further verify this, we measure attribution performance using gradients from individual parameter groups in a Stable Diffusion model
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
. As shown in Figure
1
, several up-block layers yield significantly higher Linear Datamodeling Scores (LDS)
(
Park et al. 2023
)
than others, and self-attention layers consistently outperform cross-attention layers in attribution relevance.

These results point to a broader insight: both the location and the functional role of parameters within the network
impact attribution quality—a source of signal that remains largely unmodeled. However, exploiting this signal is nontrivial. The relative importance of different network components is not fixed; it can vary substantially across architectures, training procedures, and datasets.

To this end, we propose a data-driven method that learns to weight gradients from different parameter groups for the purpose of data attribution. Instead of relying on architectural assumptions or manual heuristics, our approach optimizes weights directly to enhance attribution quality. Thus the learned weighting adapts to the specific structure and behavior of the model, capturing which network components are most informative for tracing outputs back to the training data. Crucially, we train the weights without requiring any ground-truth attribution labels.
By leveraging attribution rankings and controllable semantic variations in synthetic images—we create a supervision signal that enables the model to learn which parameter groups specialize in different semantic aspects of the output. This self-supervised formulation allows our method to scale across architectures and datasets while also supporting fine-grained, semantic-specific attribution. Our experiments demonstrate that learning parameter importance significantly enhances the effectiveness of gradient-based attribution methods across multiple datasets and model architectures. Furthermore, our approach enables fine-grained capabilities, separating attribution for style, subject, and background in the generated images.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We identify a key limitation in existing data attribution methods: Their failure to explicitly account for the varying roles of different network components.

•

We empirically demonstrate that attribution quality varies significantly across parameter groups in diffusion models, motivating the need for adaptive attribution strategies.

•

We propose a self-supervised method that learns to weight the gradients from different parameter groups of the network, without requiring ground-truth attribution labels.

•

Our approach improves attribution accuracy across multiple benchmarks, supports semantic disentanglement (e.g., subject vs. style), and generalizes across architectures and datasets.

2
Background and Related Work

Data Attribution.

The goal of data attribution is to trace a given model output back to its influential training examples. Specifically, a data attribution method
\tau(x,\mathcal{D})
is a function
\tau:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
that, for any sample
x\in\mathcal{X}
and a training set
\mathcal{D}
of size
N
, assigns a score to each training example
x^{n}\in\mathcal{D}
indicating its contribution to a pre-specified model output function, e.g., the loss function
\mathcal{L}(x;\theta^{*}(\mathcal{D}))
, where
\theta^{*}(\mathcal{D})
represents the model parameters optimized on
\mathcal{D}
.

Various attribution methods have been developed. One line of research consists of sampling-based methods, including
empirical influence functions
(
Feldman and Zhang 2020
)
,
Shapley value estimators
(
Jia et al. 2019
;
Ghorbani and Zou 2019
;
Covert and Lee 2021
)
, datamodels
(
Ilyas et al. 2022
)
, and unlearning-based methods
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Isonuma and Titov 2024
)
. However, such methods require training numerous models and are often prohibitively costly for practical-sized models.
In contrast, gradient-based methods that exploit the information in the gradient with respect to the model parameters are computationally more favorable. In this context, TracIn
(
Charpiat et al. 2019
;
Pruthi et al. 2020
)
computes the similarity between the gradient from a sample of interest and from the training examples; Influence Functions
(
Koh and Liang 2017
;
Schioppa et al. 2022
;
Mlodozeniec et al. 2024
)
and TRAK
(
Park et al. 2023
)
go beyond a simple gradient agreement strategy but introduce computation-intensive matrix inversion terms; TRAK
(
Park et al. 2023
)
projects the gradient into a lower-dimensional space to alleviate this issue.
In this work, we develop an approach able to improve
gradient-based attribution methods.

Attribution in Diffusion Models.

Our experiments focus on diffusion models, a class of generative models that have gained popularity due to their ability to produce high-quality, diverse images
(
Ramesh et al. 2022
;
Saharia et al. 2022
;
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
.
These models typically require multiple steps of denoising to generate an image, using a noise prediction network.
The rise of powerful diffusion models also raises ethical and privacy issues due to the inadvertent use of copyrighted, toxic, and private images in their training
(
Carlini et al. 2023
;
Somepalli et al. 2023
;
Birhane et al. 2021
)
.
This trend highlights the critical need for effective attribution methods that can trace back the origin of generated images to their training data.
Journey TRAK
(
Georgiev et al. 2023
)
made the first attempt to extend TRAK to multi-step diffusion models, providing attributions for each step. D-TRAK
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
improved gradient estimation by exploring various options of model output function.
Lin et al. 2024
proposed the Diffusion Attribution Score based on a new output function specifically derived for diffusion models.

Parameter Importance Heterogeneity.

The heterogeneous nature of parameter importance is considered mainly in the field of model pruning
(
Sun et al. 2023
;
Ma et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Jaiswal et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2020
)
, which aims to identify and remove redundant parameters to improve model inference efficiency.
The parameter importance heterogeneity has also been considered in the field of machine unlearning, knowledge editing, and quantization, in the form of knowledge localization
(
Yu et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023
;
Wang and Veitch 2025
)
and mixed-precision optimization
(
Kim et al. 2023
;
Cui and Wang 2024
)
.
To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to explicitly address parameter heterogeneity in data attribution.

3
Parameter Heterogeneity in Data Attribution

In this section, we first review the theoretical basis of common attribution methods, highlighting their assumptions about parameter importance, and then provide empirical evidence supporting our claim of parameter heterogeneity in attribution for diffusion models.

3.1
Parameter Heterogeneity in Previous Work

Gradient-based attribution methods typically compute the influence of training examples on generated outputs by analyzing gradient signals. A prominent approach involves gradient similarities. For instance, TracIn
(
Charpiat et al. 2019
;
Pruthi et al. 2020
)
computes the dot product between the gradient vector from a sample of interest (
x_{\text{query}}
) and the gradient vectors from training samples (
x^{n}
), i.e.,

\tau_{\text{TracIn}}(x_{\text{query}},x^{n})=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(x_{\text{query}};\theta)^{\top}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(x^{n};\theta),

(1)

where
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}
denotes the gradient of the loss function w.r.t. the model parameters
\theta
. This formulation inherently treats all parameters uniformly, assuming equal contribution to the attribution score.

However, this uniform weighting assumption overlooks the functional specialization within complex models. For example, in the UNet architectures common in diffusion models, different components (e.g., attention vs. convolutional layers, shallow vs. deep layers) serve distinct roles. Deeper layers typically capture more holistic semantics, while shallower layers focus on style and texture. This structural heterogeneity strongly suggests that gradients originating from different parameter locations carry varying types and amounts of attribution information, making uniform weighting suboptimal.

More sophisticated methods, such as the kernelized approach TRAK
(
Park et al. 2023
)
, implicitly deviate from uniform weighting. Specifically, TRAK estimates the influence of the training samples by reweighting the (projected) parameter gradients using a kernel function. This is expressed as

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{TRAK}}(x_{\text{query}},x^{n})

\displaystyle=\left(P^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(x_{\text{query}};\theta)\right)^{\top}\cdot\left(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\cdot P^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(x^{n};\theta),

(2)

\displaystyle\text{where }\mathbf{\Phi}

\displaystyle=[\mathbf{\phi}(x^{1}),\dots,\mathbf{\phi}(x^{N})]^{\top},\quad\mathbf{\phi}(x^{i})=P^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(x^{i};\theta).

(3)

where
P
is a random matrix for projection, and the inverse term
\left(\mathbf{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}
can be considered as an
implicit
re-weighting mechanism of the parameter contributions. While this formulation introduces non-uniform weighting, the effect is entangled via the projection and kernel structure—it is not explicitly designed to capture which parameter groups are most informative for attribution. Moreover, the kernel inversion step can be computationally expensive, which limits scalability.

3.2
Attribution Strength across Locations and Functionalities

In this section, we first demonstrate that attribution strength varies widely across layers and parameter functions. Here, we define attribution strength as the effectiveness of gradients from a given parameter group in identifying influential training examples. To understand how attribution strength varies throughout the network, we fine-tuned a Stable Diffusion model on ArtBench-2
(
Liao et al. 2022
)
using LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
and performed attribution using D-TRAK
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
. We partitioned the
LoRA parameters into 128 consecutive groups based on their location
within the UNet architecture. We evaluate the attribution strength of each group by computing Linear Datamodeling Score

(LDS)
(
Park et al. 2023
)
, using only gradients from that specific parameter group.

We hypothesize that the variability in attribution strength of different parameter groups also extends to distinct semantic elements in the generated image, such as subject, style, or background.
To investigate semantic specialization, we synthesized a dataset of 600 images, evenly divided across three attribute types: Subject, style, and background. For each attribute, we defined 10 categories (e.g., Pokemon Bulbasaur for subject, blacklight painting for style, beach for background), and collected 20 unique images per category. Each image was labeled according to a single attribute type, making the dataset balanced but semantically disentangled.

We fine-tuned a text-to-image latent diffusion model on this dataset, then generated new images using prompts that combined subject, style, and background (e.g., “A [style] of a [subject], [background]”). For each generated image, we treated the 20 training samples that matched its subject, style, or background as the ground-truth contributors for that respective attribute. Attribution accuracy was evaluated using recall@10—the fraction of top-10 attributed training samples that matched the ground-truth set for the targeted semantic element.

Key Takeaway:

As shown in Section
3.2
, attribution strength varies significantly across different layers and functional components of the network. In Section
3.3
, we have further demonstrated that different parameter groups could specialize in attributing distinct semantic elements of the image. These findings highlight a largely underexplored opportunity in data attribution: Determining the proper parameter importance for each parameter groups in different contexts. In the next section, we introduce our proposed method that builds on these insights—optimizing importance weights directly from data to improve attribution accuracy, enable fine-grained control, and support semantics-specific attribution (e.g., disentangling content from style).

4
Learning to Weight Parameters for Data Attribution

4.1
Parameter-Weighted Data Attribution: Formulation

Let the model parameters
\theta
be partitioned into
M
disjoint groups,
\theta=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M}\}
, such as individual tensors or layers. For an input
x
, let
g_{j}(x)
denote the gradient-derived feature vector for parameter group
\theta_{j}
. Attribution methods typically use the concatenated gradient features
g(x)=[g_{1}(x),\dots,g_{M}(x)]
to compare a query example
x_{\text{query}}
with a training example
x^{n}
.

We introduce a learnable, non-negative weight vector
w=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{M}\}
, where each
w_{j}
scales the contribution of the corresponding parameter group. The reweighted query feature is thus

\tilde{g}(x;w)=[w_{1}g_{1}(x),\dots,w_{M}g_{M}(x)].

(4)

Then, the
parameter-weighted attribution score
between
x_{\text{query}}
and
x^{n}
is

\widetilde{\tau}(x_{\text{query}},x^{n};w)=\text{Sim}(\tilde{g}(x_{\text{query}};w),g(x^{n});K),

(5)

where
\text{Sim}(\cdot,\cdot;K)
is a similarity function (e.g., a dot product or a kernel-based metric) with a kernel
K
. Although the weights
w
are applied only to the query-side gradient
\tilde{g}(x_{\text{query}};w)
, this is sufficient when using a dot product similarity metric since weighing either side effectively modulates the contribution of each parameter group.

This formulation is compatible with both simple and kernelized attribution methods. For example, applying it to TRAK yields

\widetilde{\tau}_{\text{TRAK}}(x_{\text{query}},x^{n};w)=\tilde{g}(x_{\text{query}};w)^{\top}\cdot K\cdot g(x^{n}),

(6)

where
K=(\Phi^{\top}\Phi+\lambda I)^{-1}
is the kernel derived from projected gradient features. Additionally, setting the kernel
K
to the identity matrix
I
would derive the similarity metric used in TracIn (Eq.
1
).

This unified framework allows any gradient-based attribution method to benefit from learned parameter importance weights, improving both interpretability and performance.

4.2
Self-Supervised Weight Learning

Our goal is to learn the weighting parameters
w
that maximize data attribution performance over a given set of training samples. Ideally,
the direct supervision would be a loss minimizing the discrepancy between the parameter-weighted attribution scores
\widetilde{\tau}(x_{\text{query}},x^{n};w)
and the ground-truth attribution scores
\tau^{*}(x_{\text{query}},x^{n})
for a training set of queries. However, obtaining such ground-truth scores is generally infeasible. Therefore, we propose a self-supervised approach that learns
w
without requiring ground-truth labels. Our idea is based on a simple assumption: The current top-scoring training examples are likely to be more influential than the bottom-scoring ones. Thus, we treat this ranking as a weak supervision signal and train the parameter importance weights
w
to amplify the gap between the top
k
and bottom
k
attribution scores.

Specifically, for a given query sample
x_{\text{query}}
and the full training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{x^{1},\dots,x^{N}\}
, we first compute the vector of all parameter-weighted attribution scores
\widetilde{\tau}(x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D};w)=[\widetilde{\tau}(x_{\text{query}},x^{1};w),\dots,\widetilde{\tau}(x_{\text{query}},x^{N};w)]
. Let
S_{\text{top-k}}(x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D},w)
denote the top
k
scores in
\widetilde{\tau}(x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D};w)
, and
S_{\text{bot-k}}(x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D},w)
denote the bottom
k
scores. We define a self-supervised loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSL}}
based on the difference between the average top
k
and bottom
k
scores, normalized by the
l_{2}
norm of the entire score vector. That is,

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSL}}(w;x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D},k)=-\frac{1}{\|\widetilde{\tau}(x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D};w)\|_{2}}\frac{1}{k}\left(\sum_{s\in S_{\text{top-k}}(x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D},w)}s-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S_{\text{bot-k}}(x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D},w)}s^{\prime}\right).

(7)

The normalization prevents the optimization from trivially minimizing the loss by simply scaling up all attribution scores. Instead, it focuses the objective on increasing the
relative difference
between the top and bottom scores compared to the overall score vector magnitude. Minimizing this loss thus encourages
w
to amplify the relative importance of influential examples.

To encourage the learned weights
w
to generalize beyond a single query, we learn
w
by minimizing the expectation of this loss over a distribution of query samples
\mathcal{Q}
(e.g., a set of randomly generated images). Additionally, we add a regularization term to
prevent overfitting. Altogether, we have

w^{*}=\arg\min_{w}\mathbb{E}_{x_{\text{query}}\sim\mathcal{Q}}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSL}}(w;x_{\text{query}},\mathcal{D},k)]+\lambda\|w\|_{2}.

(8)

4.3
Fine-Grained Attribution with Learned Weights

Because our method is entirely data-driven—requiring neither ground-truth labels nor handcrafted heuristics—it can be readily extended to fine-grained data attribution tasks.
In this case, we aim to isolate the influence of training data on specific aspects of the generated content such as style, or subject. Our framework achieves this by learning specialized weight sets (
w_{\text{style}}
,
w_{\text{subject}}
, etc.) using appropriately curated sets of queries.

The core idea is to construct a query set
\mathcal{Q}
in which the target attribute remains consistent across the samples, while other attributes vary. This setup encourages the self-supervised objective to assign higher weights to parameter groups responsible for attributing the invariant attribute. For instance, to learn a style-specific weight,
w_{\text{style}}
, we construct
\mathcal{Q}
by generating images with prompts of
a few specific styles
(e.g.,“
watercolor
”, “
pencil sketch
”) while randomizing subject and background, making the style the most consistent signal across the queries. When optimizing the weights
on this curated set, the self-supervised loss encourages higher weights for parameter groups that consistently contribute to the shared style. In contrast, the parameters tied to the varying elements (like subject or background) receive less consistent updates, and are therefore down-weighted.

By repeating this process for different attributes, we obtain weights that target specific facets of the image. Figure
3
shows that these specialized weights are more focused and distinct from the general-purpose ones, highlighting the flexibility of our approach. As can be seen in the figure, while the overall weights tend to emphasize deeper layers (e.g., mid block), the specialized weights learned using the curated query sets are more focused on fewer, highly relevant parameters.

5
Experiments

In this section, we first present experimental results on standard data attribution tasks where we show that our proposed framework can be integrated with various existing attribution methods to improve their performance across different datasets. Next, we demonstrate that fine-grained attribution is achievable by learning specialized weights through curated query sets, resulting in weights tailored to specific attributes such as subject, style, or background. Finally, we show that these learned weights can also support parameter pruning, reducing the computational cost of attribution and enabling higher-dimensional projections for improved accuracy.

5.4
Parameter Pruning with Learned Weights

Parameter weights can also be used for pruning to reduce attribution costs. We adopt a simple strategy: retaining only gradients from the top-weighted parameters (e.g., top 50% or 25% by weight magnitude) during attribution.
In the experiment shown in Table
3
, weights are learned once at the 26k projection dimension and reused across all dimensionalities. This strategy reduces query time while maintaining strong attribution performance: even with 50% or 75% of parameters pruned, LDS scores (24.1% and 23.4%) remain comparable to or better than the unweighted baseline (23.5%). Pruning also enables higher projection dimensions, which consistently improve accuracy.

6
Conclusion

We introduced a self-supervised method to learn parameter importance, improving gradient-based data attribution. Our approach enables general and fine-grained attribution by identifying model parts responsible for specific semantic elements (e.g., style, subject, background) and can also guide parameter pruning for computational efficiency. A key limitation is the reliance on potentially noisy top-scoring examples as training signals; future work could explore better signals, broader architectural adaptability, and applications beyond diffusion models. Overall, our findings highlight the value of explicitly learning parameter importance, opening new directions for scalable, interpretable, and task-specific data attribution.
