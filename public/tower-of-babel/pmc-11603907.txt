Multiple insect pollination contributes to differential phenotypic selection on floral traits in Stellera chamaejasme
Authors: Shu-Fan Sun, Yan-Bao Li, Jing Leng, Ding-Hai Zhang, Wen-Liu Zhang, Bo Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFlower morphology is believed to be primarily shaped by the most important pollinators when more than one pollinating species visits the flowers. However, floral adaptation to the selection mediated by multiple pollinators has received little attention. The plant Stellera chamaejasme has evolved a pollination syndrome matching both lepidopteran and thrips. In this study, to test the relative importance of different pollinators to the plant’s reproductive success and clarify how phenotypic selection on floral traits relates to different functional groups of pollinators, we determined the relative contributions of different pollinating insects to plant reproductive fitness through pollinator manipulation experiments and investigated the associated phenotypic selection on floral traits.ResultsLepidopterans and thrips had comparable contributions to the reproductive fitness of S. chamaejasme. Among lepidopterans, the fitness contribution was distinctly higher for moths than for butterflies. Corolla opening size (i.e., corolla entrance) was subject to positive directional selection mediated mainly by lepidopterans, with thrips exerting opposing directional selection for this trait. The significant correlational selection between tube length and corolla opening size suggested that thrips and lepidopteran pollinators favored either the broad-entranced short flowers or the narrow-entranced long flowers.ConclusionsMost likely, a functionally integrated adaptation to accommodate a variety of pollinators led to the floral design of S. chamaejasme, which in turn contributed to the diversity of flowers.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Floral divergence, Phenotypic selection, Pollinator-specific adaptation, Pollination, Reproductive fitness, Stellera chamaejasme

Introduction

Plant-pollinator relationships have been a central topic in the study of floral evolution since Darwin [1, 2]. It is widely believed that floral trait evolution is driven to a large extent by pollinator-mediated selection [2, 3]; and therefore, understanding the role of pollinator selection in floral adaptative diversification is one of the more fascinating and relevant themes in the field of evolutionary ecology in angiosperms [4, 5]. Community surveys demonstrate that except in some highly specialized pollination systems, the majority of plants have multiple species of pollinators [3, 6, 7], and their pollinator assemblages generally vary spatially [8, 9] and temporally [10–12]. In these types of generalized pollination systems, pollinator assemblages may change flower traits in different ways if they are made up of different types of pollinators or if they pollinate plants in different ways [7, 13, 14]. Many studies have shown that flower traits have changed over time in response to changes in pollinators [15, 16] and differences in space across a wide range of regions [9, 14, 17].

The evolution of flowers is generally expected to be a reflection of the interactions mediated mainly by dominant and effective pollinators in a population [3, 7], i.e., Stebbins’ Most Effective Pollinator Principle [6, 18]. However, based on theoretical models of phenotypic selection, Aigner [19] argued that pollinator importance in selecting for floral specialization is not necessarily coupled with their effectiveness in pollination, as is also documented in few studies [20]; and instead, the role of a pollinator in floral selection depends on whether its interaction with variable traits may result in the associated difference in plant fitness. Additionally, growing evidence reveals that apart from pollinators as primary selective agents, various abiotic and biotic non-pollinator also may exert strong selection on floral traits as agents, such as herbivore, non-pollinator visitors, predator, and environmental factors [21]. Therefore, floral traits, in many cases, represent a compromised adaptation to multiple selection pressures imposed by various pollinators and non-pollinator agents [22].

Stellera chamaejasme, a multistemmed perennial herb in Thymelaeaceae, is obligately outcrossing (i.e., self-incompatible) and completely depends on insects for pollination [23]. The flowers of S. chamaejasme are scented and generally are considered to evolve a butterfly- or moth-pollination syndrome mainly due to a long-tubed morphology (Fig. 1) [23]. However, a recent study evidenced that the plant might be pollinated not only by lepidopterans but also by thrips [24], and its flowers also display characters of thrips-pollination syndrome like enclosed floral morphology with a narrow entrance. In the light of dual pollination syndromes in S. chamaejasme, coupled with multi-insect pollination environment, we presume that the plant species may serve as an ideal model for the study on floral evolution driven by multiple pollinator selections. Given that there exists much genetic variation in floral traits and different pollinator guilds (i.e., pollinator functional groups) differ in pollination efficiency and fitness contribution to the plant, we hypothesize that the plant flowers exhibit pollinator-differential phenotypic selections. Specifically, we predict that a large flower production is favorable to the selection mediated by both pollinator guilds, as the trait determines the plant’s capacity of mating opportunity [25–27], and that floral structural traits, like corolla opening and floral tube, may be under differential selection mediated by different pollinators, provided that these trait expression exert a variable influence on pollination efficiency among pollinator functional groups. Considering the differences in circadian activity and body size among pollinating insects, we conducted pollinator manipulation experiments, which involved the separation of diurnal and nocturnal pollination and the separation of different functional groups (i.e., lepidopterans and thrips), to determine relative importance of different pollinators to the plant’s reproductive success and their selection on floral traits.

Results

Relative contributions of different pollinators to the reproductive fitness ofS. chamaejasme

Contributions of diurnal and nocturnal pollination

In two years, both seed number per inflorescence and seed set per flower were the highest for open-pollinated individuals, intermediate for nocturnally pollinated individuals, and the lowest for diurnally pollinated individuals (Figs. 2 and 3). In the year 2020, the seed number per inflorescence and seed set per flower of nocturnal pollination were 4.34 ± 0.88 (mean ± SE, n = 38) and 0.14 ± 0.03 (mean ± SE, n = 38), respectively; and both items were significantly greater than those of diurnal pollination (Fig. 2). In 2021, we did not detect a significant difference between nocturnal and diurnal pollinations in both experiments (P = 0.28; P = 0.34) (Fig. 3). Both seed number per inflorescence and seed set per flower under diurnal pollination were much lower than was under open pollination in both years (Figs. 2 and 3), while either of items was not significantly different between nocturnal and open pollinations except for the seed set per flower in 2020 (Fig. 2).

Different pollinators’ contributions

Both seed number per inflorescence and seed set per flower by diurnal thrips pollination were greater than half of that by diurnal open pollination (i.e., pollinated by all diurnal insects) (Fig. 3); that is, the diurnal thrips pollination treatment contributed more to plant fitness than did the diurnal butterfly treatment. For nocturnal pollinators, the seed number per inflorescence and seed set per flower for the nocturnal thrips pollination treatment were 0.90 ± 0.25 (mean ± SE, n = 49) and 0.04 ± 0.01 (mean ± SE, n = 49), respectively, both of which were less than half of that by nocturnal open pollination (P = 0.002; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). That is, nocturnal moths presumably made more contribution to plant fitness than did nocturnal thrips. We did not detect a statistically significant difference between diurnal and nocturnal thrips pollination treatments (P = 0.14; P = 0.2) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

For plants that can be diurnally and nocturnally pollinated, the pollinator assemblage is generally different between daytime and nighttime [7, 10, 12, 28]. In such a pollination system, variable pollinator-mediated selection is expected due to different pollination efficiency [29], and thereby significantly influence floral evolution [7, 28].

The relative contributions of different pollinators to reproductive success of S. chamaejasme

Individuals of S. chamaejasme exposed to nocturnal pollinators had higher seed production than those exposed to diurnal pollinators in one of two years, suggesting that nocturnal pollination tend to have an overall greater contribution to the plant fitness relative to diurnal pollination. Additionally, these results suggested between-year variation in pollinator service and their relative importance to reproduction success of S. chamaejasme.

Thrips had not been highly recognized as efficient pollinators due to their limited capability of movement until that growing evidence revealed their effectiveness in pollination for a few angiosperm plants [30–34]. In the case of S. chamaejasme, the study provided more compelling evidence for the effectiveness of thrips pollination. Our results showed that thrips abundance had a direct positive effect on the plant fitness, and the fitness by thrips pollination was roughly half of that under open pollination, indicating considerable contribution of thrips to the plant reproduction. Diurnal thrips made a slightly higher contribution to the plant fitness than nocturnal ones. One reason is probably that thrips movement among plant individuals is more frequent in the daytime than at night [35], thereby leading to a higher efficiency of cross-pollination in the daytime, since the species has a mating system of obligate xenogamy [23]. The previous studies have documented that thrips foraging often exhibits a distinct circadian rhythm [35, 36], which varies with environment, the species and even different sexes (e.g., Thrips major) [33]. In addition, a brood-pollination mutualism was evidenced between the thrips and S. chamaejasme flowers [24], that is, the flowers could provide pollinating thrips with breeding sites as a reward. In the mutualistic system, the thrips, generally as the pests, were not found to consume the seeds (or ovules) of the host plants, and it was argued that a mechanism of protecting seeds from thrips consumption plays a vital role in maintaining their mutualistic relationship. Evidently, it is predictable that thrips pollination efficiency is closely associated with their foraging and egg-laying behaviors, during which the factors facilitating thrips to switch among plant individuals, such as chemical regulation and weather conditions (e.g., wind), will contribute to pollination success and the evolution of the mutualism [24]. Among lepidopterans, the nocturnal moth pollination generated a much greater contribution to reproductive fitness than the diurnal butterflies. This finding concurs with a previous study that showed the white-flowered populations of S. chamaejasme are preferentially visited by moths rather than butterflies [23]. The field observation during the present study also suggested that butterflies were present at a very low frequency in the population.

According to Stebbins’ Most Effective Pollinator Principle [6, 18], flower morphology is expected as a reflection of its main pollinators, and in turn the most effective pollinators for a species can be inferred from its floral design [28, 29]. In the present study, considering two functional groups of pollinators and their comparable fitness contributions to the plant, it is not surprising that flowers of S. chamaejasme exhibit pollination syndrome characters matching both lepidopterans and thrips, such as long corolla tube with a narrow opening, and a relatively compact inflorescence as well [23, 24, 32]. Spatiotemporal variation in the densities of important pollinators as selective agents plays an important role in preventing the evolution of a highly specialized pollination system [19, 29]. Therefore, it is likely that the alternation of pollinator importance among different functional groups, due to seasonal and interannual fluctuation in pollinator occurrence, plays a role in driving and maintaining the evolution of multi-insect pollination syndromes in species like S. chamaejasme.

Pollinator-mediated selection on floral traits ofS. chamaejasme

Flower production is a trait directly tied to plant fitness itself [4, 37], which determines a plant’s capacity for mating opportunities through both ovules and pollen [26, 27]. Therefore, this trait is prone to directional selection because the individuals with more flowers have the potential to obtain either higher female fitness through seed set or higher male fitness through pollen siring success on other plants [25, 38, 39]. As expected, and also being consistent with other studies on selection of floral traits [7], the flower production of S. chamaejasme was subject not only to positive directional selection but also to significant nonlinear convex selection (Fig. 6A). This indicates that the selection overall favored the individuals with many more flowers owing to an advantage in mating capacity. Nevertheless, the plant’s female fitness did not linearly increase as flower number increased; that is, the fitness would be saturated and even decline when the flower production reached a certain limit (Fig. 6A). This can be attributed to a trade-off between flower production and resource-limited seed development [40]. In addition, even in the absence of resource limitation, it is also unlikely that seed production linearly increases with flower production because the large inflorescences of plants with plenty of flowers rarely receive a proportionally high rate of probing by pollinators in natural populations [41].

Significant nonlinear selection for flower production (A) and correlational selection on corolla tube and opening (i.e., entrance) (B) in S. chamaejasme under open multi-pollinator pollination

Flower production can not only be selected due to direct effect on the reproductive capacity, but also be selected by pollinator as agents [4, 26, 27]. Firstly, the individuals with a greater flower production have a larger floral display, and thus stand a better chance of mating by promoting pollinator attraction [37, 42]. Secondly, the individuals with more flowers may extend their flowering duration, and hence enhance the probability of being visited, particularly in a harsh environment with highly stochastic pollinator visits [43], like arctic or subarctic habitats [44] and the alpine grassland of this study. Thirdly, flower number can be selected by other related traits, such as floral scent probably in the case of S. chamaejasme, which may play an important role in floral selection by pollinator perception [45].

A meta-analysis on floral trait selection showed that pollinator-mediated selection tends to be much stronger on pollination efficiency traits than on other traits like floral attraction and phenology [21]. For plants with tubular flowers, floral structural traits, especially corolla entrance size and tube length, are expected to undergo pollinator-mediated selection because of their effect on pollination efficiency through mechanical interaction with pollinating insects [2, 17, 46–48]. We found that corolla entrance diameter of S. chamaejasme flowers was under positive directional selection; that is, the flowers with a broad entrance had a fitness advantage over the flowers with a narrow entrance. Presumably, it is likely that the pollinating lepidopterans serve as one of main selective agents for corolla opening size. Considering overall narrow entrance of the flowers, we suspect that a relatively broader opening may facilitate lepidopteran insects inserting their long proboscis into floral tubes, and thereby promote pollination efficiency, potentially reducing the loss of imported and exported pollen during pollination. Alternatively, corolla entrance size may be correlated with another trait under selection we did not measure.

When more than one guild of pollinators visits flowers of a plant, floral traits are subject to different selection pressures [7, 10]. We found that the female fitness of S. chamaejasme was strongly associated with the interplay between corolla opening size and thrips number, indicating that the flowers with a smaller corolla opening, when foraged by many more thrips, tend to obtain a comparable fitness to the flowers with a larger opening that are foraged by fewer thrips. It is unclear to us how larval thrips within a flower in themselves affects seed set in the self-incompatible S. chamaejasme; however, the flowers with more resident hatched larvae at least reflect more visits by adult thrips for laying eggs in tubes [24]. Regardless, our results implies that thrips probably exert a selection on corolla opening size in the opposite direction to that by lepidopterans. In addition, the combination of tube length and corolla entrance diameter was subject to negative correlational selection (Fig. 6B), indicating that either the short-tubed flowers with a broad entrance or the long-tubed flowers with a narrow entrance are favored in the current population. Apparently, the corolla entrance size, combined with tube length, is undergoing differential selection by different functional pollinator groups, which is likely to reflect divergent adaptation of floral construction to both types of pollinating insects (i.e., lepidopterans and thrips), as is showed in Fig. 7. Other studies, like in Parrya nudicaulis [44], also have shown negative correlational selection of multiple floral traits, invoking divergent pollinator guilds. We suggest that the short-tubed flowers with a broad entrance are better fitted to lepidopteran pollination, whereas the long-tubed flowers with a narrow entrance are more suitable for thrips pollination. Selection for the broad-entranced short flowers could promote pollination effectiveness (i.e., pollen transfer and deposition) of the long-tongued insects as discussed above. Selection for the small-entranced long flowers in thrips could be attributed to two reasons. One is that thrips generally prefer a closed habitat and are sensitive to desiccation [49] and thus favor the flowers with a narrow entrance, and the other is that the long-tubed flowers can provide a large space for brooding their larvae as documented a previous study [24]. Nevertheless, as a pest, it is also probable that the thrips exerts an effect on floral trait selection in a way that has not been found, such as by reducing the plant fitness.

Phenotypic selection is predicated on the variation of fitness-associated traits [14], and if the variation is genetic, adaptive phenotypic evolution will occur on these traits [50]. In the present study, the strong phenotypic selections were observed for floral traits of S. chamaejasme, reflecting vast variation of the target traits in the current population. However, we did not obtain direct evidence of pollinator-mediated selection on the floral traits, and the full understanding of the findings need to further study on the selection mechanism (i.e., selective agents). As for floral trait variation in S. chamaejasme, it may be attributed to some extent to obligate outcrossing of the species [23]. Besides, in the ecological context, multi-pollinator divergent selection or selection fluctuation probably plays a critical role in maintaining phenotypic variation of the species, as is also suggested in P. nudicaulis [44]. Furthermore, our finding on divergent selection for floral traits provided support for the viewpoint that floral efficiency traits, like corolla opening and tube length in S. chamaejasme, are more likely to contribute to pollinator-mediated diversification than other traits [21].

Conclusion

Since Darwin, plant flowers have been recognized to be complex adaptation with selection acting on suites of traits [51]. The flowers of S. chamaejasme exhibits a dual pollination syndrome, for lepidopterans and thrips with the characters of long tube and enclosed morphology, respectively. We found that two functional pollinator groups had comparable contributions to reproductive success of the plant. As expected, the two target floral traits of corolla entrance size and tube length, were subject to strong selection likely mediated by multiple pollinators. The flowers were under phenotypic selection for either relatively short tubes with a broad entrance or relatively long tubes with a narrow entrance, probably reflecting floral divergent adaptation to lepidopteran and thrips pollinations. As such, we propose that such floral design of S. chamaejasme in current populations, i.e., enclosed tubular morphology with a narrow entrance, presents a compromise for the selection imposed by two pollinator guilds, namely a functionally integrated adaptation to the multi-pollinator environment. Additionally, it has been indicated that pollinator interactions in their effects on plant fitness, besides fitness trade-offs for floral specialization to one versus the other pollinator, play a key role in floral adaptive evolution in multi-pollinator environment [19]. It is therefore necessary to clarify the interplay between lepidopterans and thrips in their contributions to reproductive success of S. chamaejasme in attempts to shed light on floral syndrome evolution of the species.

Materials and methods

Study site and material

This study was conducted in Qilian Mountain National Nature Reserve, Tianzhu County, Gansu Province, China. The study area has a typical plateau climate with low barometric pressure and strong solar radiation. The long-term annual average temperature is around 0 ℃, the annual average precipitation is approximately 416 mm, and the frost-free period is 3–4 months. The alpine meadow is the main type of vegetation, dominated by Elymus nutans, Kobresia humilis and Polygonum viviparum [52, 53]. Stellera chamaejasme is one of main poisonous species distributed in the degraded grassland. The studied population is located in an alpine meadow near the Tianzhu field station of Gansu Agricultural University (37°12′50′′N, 102°47′33′′E; 3100 m a.s.l) in the reserve. This population flowered from late June to late July. Most individuals have an average of 10 capitate inflorescences, with each inflorescence producing approximately 20–30 white flowers (Fig. 1A) and each flower containing a single ovule. A voucher specimen of the plant, which was collected in 2019, has been deposited in the herbarium of the Northwest Institute of Eco-Environment and Resources of Chinese Academy of Sciences (LZD, 0050602).

Pollinator manipulation experiments

In the present study, we conducted pollinator manipulation experiments in two flowering seasons of S. chamaejasme. In 2020 and 2021, we determine relative contributions of diurnal and nocturnal pollinators to reproductive fitness of the plant through two experiments. In both experiments, two types of bags were employed for pollination manipulation, i.e., coarse-meshed (5–6 mm) bags and fine-meshed (0.15–0.2 mm) bags. The coarse-meshed bags (Fig. 1E) may exclude lepidopteran pollinators and all other large pollinators and allow thrips to forage and move among flowers; and the fine-meshed bags (Fig. 1D) may exclude all pollinators (both lepidopteran insects and thrips) from foraging on the inflorescences [24].

Experiment I

We labeled over 120 budded inflorescences on different individuals (i.e., one inflorescence on each plant) with plastic tags in early flowering seasons of 2020 and 2021. These individuals were roughly similar in plant size and the labeled inflorescences flowered basically synchronously, such that they could obtain similar opportunities of pollination. In each season, we performed three pollination treatments on the labeled inflorescences, with the inflorescences randomly and equally assigned to each treatment. In treatment I, the inflorescences were bagged with the fine-meshed bags only in the daytime (i.e., bags were applied at 0700 h and removed at 1900 h); that is, the inflorescences could be pollinated only by nocturnal insects (i.e., nocturnal open pollination), and thus, their mean fitness was estimated as the contribution of nocturnal pollination for the total of inflorescences. In treatment II, the inflorescences were bagged with the fine-meshed bags only during nighttime (i.e., bags were applied at 1900 h and removed at 0700 h the next day); that is, the inflorescences could be pollinated only by diurnal insects (i.e., diurnal open pollination), and their mean fitness was estimated as the contribution of diurnal pollination for the total of inflorescences. In treatment III, the inflorescences were under open pollination, which served as the control.

Experiment II

Concurrently with Experiment I in 2021, we additionally labeled 100 synchronously budded inflorescences and carried out two pollination manipulation treatments, with the treatment III of Experiment I in the same year as the control (i.e., open pollination). The labeled inflorescences were randomly and equally assigned to each treatment. In treatment I, the inflorescences were bagged with coarse-meshed bags in the daytime and with fine-meshed bags during the nighttime (i.e., bagged with the coarse-meshed bags at 0700 h and then with the fine-meshed bags at 1900 h). That is, the inflorescences were expected to be pollinated only by diurnal thrips, and thus, their mean fitness was determined by the contribution of daytime thrips pollination. In treatment II, the inflorescences were bagged with fine-meshed bags in the daytime and with coarse-meshed bags during the nighttime. As such, the inflorescences were expected to be pollinated only by nocturnal thrips, and their mean fitness was therefore determined by the contribution of nighttime thrips pollination. In the experiment, while it is possible that other small bees or flies apart from thrips have access to flowers bagged with the coarse bags, multi-year observations and the previous study showed no other insects except thrips were observed to visit the bagged flowers, and even under open pollination of S. chamaejasme [52].

Using this approach, we could estimate the contribution of butterfly pollination by subtracting seed set under daytime thrips pollination (i.e., treatment I in Experiment II) from seed set under diurnal open pollination (i.e., treatment II in Experiment I of 2021), and estimate the moths’ contribution by subtracting seed set under nighttime thrips pollination (i.e., treatment II in Experiment II) from seed set under nocturnal open pollination (i.e., treatment I in Experiment I of 2021). The manipulation of bag placement and removal on inflorescences was repeated until all flowers on each inflorescence had wilted. After seed maturation (i.e., approximately ten days after flower wilting), we collected all treated inflorescences and counted seed number of each inflorescence as an estimate of female fitness. In addition, we calculated seed set per flower by dividing seed number of each inflorescence by its flower number, which was counted soon after the inflorescence was labelled at the beginning of the experiments. In the species, the seed set per flower (seed number / flower number) was also equal to the fruit set, as each flower has a single ovule.

Trait measurements and thrips quantification

In the mid-flowering season of 2021, we randomly selected over 130 individuals in the population as candidates for phenotypic selection, and simultaneously counted the number of inflorescences on each plant (i.e., branches) as a proxy of plant size. Since the individuals generally have many inflorescences, which do not flower synchronously and thus have a long flowering period, it is difficult to assess the total female fitness at the individual level. Therefore, we labeled a fully flowering inflorescence on each plant (i.e., an inflorescence on which all flowers had opened), which served as the focal inflorescence used to estimate the fitness component of the plant, followed by counting flower number on the inflorescence. For floral morphometrics, we selected another unlabeled fully flowering inflorescence of each plant to avoid damage to the focal inflorescence when measuring floral traits. For each candidate plant, we measured three floral traits of 4–5 freshly opened flowers: corolla entrance diameter (i.e., corolla opening size), corolla tube length and floral width (Fig. 1B, C), and the mean value of each trait was calculated as the trait value of the plant. All traits were measured by using digital calipers (GB/T21389-2008, 0–150 mm, Guilin, China) with ± 0.01 mm of error.

To detect the effects of thrips abundance and their interplay with floral traits on reproductive fitness of S. chamaejasme, we quantified the visitation of thrips on the focal inflorescences. Because adult thrips movement is difficult to observe, it is difficult to directly quantify how many adults have foraged in the inflorescences. One of main purposes of adult thrips foraging is to lay eggs and brood their larvae in the flowers [24]. Therefore, we counted the number of larvae hatched in flowers as a proxy for visitation rate of adult thrips. Furthermore, the flowers of the plant open successively over two weeks from the margin to the top of the capitulum [54]. To estimate as accurately as possible thrips occurrence in the whole flowering period of an inflorescence, we sampled five different-positioned flowers (i.e., two marginal, two middle and one top flowers) at their own stages of wilting, and counted larvae number in petri dish with a magnifier by tearing open floral tubes lengthways. The total number of larvae in the sampled flowers were calculated as the estimate of adult visitation to the inflorescence. Finally, we counted seed number of each focal inflorescence as an estimate of female fitness.

Data analysis

The difference in seed number per inflorescence among different pollination treatments was tested by a generalized linear model (GLM) with a negative binomial error structure (log link function) in both 2020 and 2021, and the difference in seed set per flower was determined by a GLM with a quasibinomial distribution (logit link function) in both years. The multiple comparison among treatments was performed with “Tukey contrasts” by using “glht” function in the “multcomp” package of R [55].

Multivariate regression analysis was used to estimate the strength and pattern of selection on the floral traits [56], with plant fitness as the dependent variable and floral traits as independent variables. Before the analyses, the plant fitness, i.e., seed number was relativized (individual seed number / population mean), and the values of each trait were standardized (i.e., mean = 0 and standard deviation = 1) by subtracting the population mean from the individual trait value and dividing by the standard deviation ((individual trait value ‒ population mean)/standard deviation).The number of collected individuals (i.e., sample size) approached recommended levels for measuring nonlinear selection [44, 57]. We therefore employed the most complete regression model (i.e., the full general linear model) to estimate different selection gradients [4]. The full model included linear and quadratic terms for each floral trait (i.e., flower number, corolla tube length, and corolla entrance diameter) and the products of pairs of these traits, in which floral width was not included due to its significant correlation with other measured traits. Considering the effect of resource availability on plant size and thus on both floral traits and fecundity, we also introduced the number of inflorescences (i.e., as the proxy of plant size) in the model in an attempt to account for maternal effects on trait selection [38, 58], and in the model, only the linear term of the variable was included. In the regression model, a significant partial regression coefficient of the linear term of each trait serves as an indicator of directional selection on the trait, the doubled coefficient of a trait squared as a measure of nonlinear selection [59], and the coefficient of the product of trait pairs as a measure of correlational selection (i.e., selection on the combination of the two traits) [4, 50]. Unfortunately, the errors (i.e., residuals) of the full model were not normally distributed, and there were no significant terms except linear and quadratic terms of flower number. Considering that selection coefficients are not believed to be affected by a poorly fit model while the statistical significances are [56, 60, 61], we established GLM with a negative binomial error structure (i.e., Model 2) to obtain the statistical significances of the selection coefficients, since the seed number (i.e., the fitness) had a much greater variance relative to its mean. To maintain consistence of the models, this GLM model was completely identical in structure to the model estimating selection coefficients except that the original seed number, instead of the relativized one, was used as dependent variable.

To determine the effects of thrips and their interaction with floral traits on the fitness, we established another GLM with a negative binomial error structure by introducing thrips number (i.e., larva no.) in the Model 2 as explanatory variable. That is, this model included linear and quadratic terms for thrips and each floral trait and their pair products, and thrips number was standardized before analysis like floral traits. Based on the GLM, we further established a minimum adequate model (i.e., Model 3) with the “step” function to obtain the final coefficients for the terms relevant to the plant fitness.

Finally, we performed a diagnosis for the statistically significant coefficients in Model 2 and Model 3 by using the added-variable plots. The plots for linear terms of Model 2 served as a visual illustration of directional selections on floral traits. Additionally, we graphically illustrated significant nonlinear and correlational selections by using generalized additive models (GAMs) with the “mgcv” package in R [61, 62]. These models can fit a spline regression for the quadratic terms representing nonlinear selection in the models of selection gradient analysis, and the nonlinear and correlational selections could be visually illustrated with the “plot.gam” and “vis.gam” functions, respectively. For all data analyses, R version 4.3.1 [55] was used.
