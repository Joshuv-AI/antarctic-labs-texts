WA-NET: enhanced boundary-aware segmentation of skin lesions via frequency-spatial feature fusion and attention-guided edge refinement
Authors: Gefeng Hu, Wen Zhu, Xinyi Liao, Qingbo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin cancer remains a major public health concern due to its high morbidity and mortality rates. While automatic segmentation techniques have improved diagnostic accuracy, they continue to face challenges such as artefacts, small lesion detection, and poor contrast between lesions and surrounding tissue. To overcome these limitations, we propose WA-NET, a novel skin lesion segmentation network that integrates a Boundary Refinement module (BRM) and an Enhanced Wavelet Transform (EWT) module. The BRM employs independent edge detection branches to enhance boundary representation, particularly in low-contrast regions. The EWT module adaptively fuses multi-scale, multi-directional sub-band features in the frequency domain to better capture texture and structural details. Furthermore, a composite loss function combining binary cross-entropy, Dice loss, and edge-supervised loss is introduced to improve both global segmentation accuracy and local boundary precision. WA-NET achieves state-of-the-art performance on three benchmark datasets—ISIC2017 (DSC: 0.9395, SE: 0.9357, ACC: 0.9573), ISIC2018 (DSC: 0.9458, SE: 0.9460, ACC: 0.9610), and PH2 (DSC: 0.9517, SE: 0.9593, ACC: 0.9638)—demonstrating strong robustness and superior boundary segmentation under challenging imaging conditions.Keywords: Skin lesion segmentation, Medical image Segmentation, Deep learning, Boundary refinementSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, the rapid advancement of computer science and imaging technologies has established digital images—owing to their intuitive visual representation—as a vital medium for perceiving and interpreting information1. Within this context, medical image segmentation has emerged as a fundamental technique in medical image analysis. It leverages computational algorithms to accurately identify regions of interest at the pixel or voxel level and classify them into specific categories2. The primary objective is to delineate anatomical structures from complex backgrounds or adjacent tissues3, thereby providing objective and quantifiable image-based support for clinical diagnosis, treatment planning, and biomedical research.

Accurate feature extraction is central to medical image segmentation because segmentation quality directly influences clinical decision-making4. Medical images present diverse characteristics, including fine-grained local details and global structural patterns. However, boundaries are often indistinct, target regions vary considerably in shape, and foreground–background imbalance remains a common challenge5. In addition, medical images are susceptible to heterogeneous noise, low contrast, and significant inter-individual variability introduced by differences in imaging devices and acquisition protocols6. These factors necessitate the development of tailored segmentation methods that can robustly extract relevant features and address the diverse complexities inherent in medical imaging data.

Traditional methods predominantly rely on manual annotation, which is inefficient and makes it difficult to depend solely on clinicians for accurate interpretation7. Conventional segmentation approaches largely analyze low-level pixel features—such as color, grayscale, texture, and edges—to achieve segmentation8. However, when applied to complex and diverse medical images, these methods struggle to capture high-level semantic information. They mainly learn low-level features, which hinders their ability to distinguish between normal tissues and regions of interest, thereby limiting precise delineation of target structures. Despite these drawbacks, traditional approaches laid a solid foundation for subsequent developments9,10.

With the continual progress of machine learning and deep learning technologies, segmentation methods have rapidly evolved through the integration of domain knowledge and computational advances. Deep learning models emulate the human brain’s capacity to extract meaningful patterns from data through hierarchical feature learning11. In recent years, deep learning has achieved remarkable success across a variety of fields.

The earliest application of neural networks to segmentation was the fully convolutional network (FCN), which replaced fully connected layers of conventional CNNs with convolutional layers12. Although this marked a significant step forward, FCN-based results were often coarse and suffered from substantial feature loss. As a result, they were inadequate for the high precision demands of medical imaging. Building upon the FCN architecture, U-Net was introduced with optimised skip connections that alleviate spatial information loss during downsampling13. U-Net not only reduces the number of network parameters but also enables multi-scale feature fusion, significantly improving segmentation accuracy. It has since become a seminal model in medical image segmentation, with many variants achieving excellent performance across diverse segmentation tasks.

To further enhance multi-scale feature extraction, U-Net++14 refines the original U-Net by introducing dense skip connections at each layer, thereby improving its ability to segment complex structures and enhancing the flexibility of feature fusion. However, skip connections in U-Net architectures may still propagate irrelevant information. To address this limitation, Attention U-Net integrates an attention mechanism to dynamically emphasise informative regions while suppressing redundant ones during feature fusion15.

More recently, the Transformer architecture has attracted considerable attention due to its success in natural language processing, motivating its adoption in segmentation tasks16,17. Hybrid models such as TransUNet18, Swin-UNet19, and other Transformer-enhanced variants combine U-Net’s strong local feature extraction capabilities with the Transformer’s global modelling strength. This integration allows the network to capture both fine-grained spatial structures and long-range semantic dependencies. Consequently, these models achieve more precise and comprehensive feature representation, thereby improving both segmentation accuracy and efficiency20.

In the specific context of skin cancer analysis, boundary information plays a particularly critical role. To better exploit such information, Lin et al.21 proposed an edge-guided decoder network (CTO), which incorporates the Sobel edge detection operator to extract boundary features. This approach enhances edge representations on feature maps and fuses foreground–background information via an edge-guidance module, leading to more accurate boundary localisation. Collectively, these methods aim to ensure the effective transmission of salient features through hierarchical feature superposition, thereby strengthening feature representation across the entire network.

At present, most mainstream segmentation methods primarily rely on feature extraction in the spatial domain22. These approaches typically adopt encoder–decoder architectures and employ spatial-domain techniques such as multi-scale feature extraction and attention mechanisms to capture shape and structural information. However, such methods are highly sensitive to noise and struggle to capture complementary frequency-domain information. Relying solely on spatial features limits the representational capacity of segmentation models. By transforming an image from the spatial to the frequency domain, texture and frequency-related information can be effectively captured, thereby enhancing relevant features while suppressing noise and artefacts23. Common frequency-domain feature extraction techniques include the Fourier Transform and the Wavelet Transform. While the Fourier Transform provides only global frequency information, the Wavelet Transform offers both high-frequency details and low-frequency structural contours. Furthermore, its multi-scale decomposition makes it particularly well suited to image processing tasks.

This study recognises that although existing segmentation methods have achieved notable progress, they predominantly focus on spatial-domain feature extraction. In contrast, relatively limited effort has been devoted to incorporating frequency-domain information to capture fine-grained morphological and textural characteristics. To address this gap, we propose a strategy that integrates edge information through multi-level feature fusion, thereby combining global representations with local lesion details across multiple scales to achieve more precise segmentation.

Related work

Medical image segmentation has traditionally relied on spatial-domain features. However, critical information such as texture and edges is often more salient in the frequency domain. Consequently, fusing spatial- and frequency-domain features while enhancing boundary representations has emerged as an effective strategy to improve segmentation accuracy24.

Frequency-domain feature extraction

Spatial-domain information alone may fail to capture complex structural details in medical images. The wavelet transform, with its strong time–frequency localization and multi-scale decomposition capabilities, has been shown to effectively extract texture features25. Integrating spatial and frequency features can therefore yield richer, more discriminative representations.

Recent studies have incorporated wavelet transforms into deep learning frameworks. Liu et al.26 proposed a multi-stage wavelet CNN, replacing standard downsampling and upsampling with discrete and inverse wavelet transforms (DWT/IWT). MARIA et al.27 introduced a deep adaptive wavelet network with trainable wavelet filters within a CNN. Zhao et al.28 developed WRANet, a residual attention network that propagates low-frequency information during downsampling to capture high-level features while attenuating high-frequency components. More recently, Spectral U-Net29 employed spectral decomposition via Wave-Block and iWave-Block modules, streamlining the fusion of wavelet components.

While these approaches demonstrate the utility of frequency-domain analysis, most employ fixed or heuristic fusion strategies for sub-band features. They lack adaptive mechanisms to dynamically weigh the importance of different frequency components according to input content.

Edge reinforcement

Blurry and indistinct boundaries remain a persistent challenge in medical image segmentation30. Yan et al.31 proposed CCT-Net, which uses a local coding module and cross-shaped window self-attention to enhance local feature extraction while capturing long-range dependencies. CSAP-UNet32 addresses boundary blur by introducing a Boundary Enhancement Module (BEM) that processes low-level features from CNN (ResNet) and Transformer (DeiT) pathways in parallel, improving edge localization.

These studies demonstrate the value of explicit boundary modeling. However, edge refinement is often treated as a separate or late-stage process, limiting the integration of boundary information across multiple feature scales.

Encoder–decoder architectures

Enhancements to encoder–decoder frameworks, particularly U-Net, have focused on encoder feature extraction, skip connection optimization, and decoder fusion strategies33. ARCUNet34 incorporates residual convolutions and attention mechanisms to accelerate convergence and suppress irrelevant features. MRP-UNet35 constructs multi-scale feature representations with residual structures and skip connections, augmented with dilated convolutions to expand receptive fields. CoTransUNet36 combines fine-grained local and long-range features through a correlation-enhancement module, improving decoder reconstruction.

Recent networks further improve lesion segmentation. SGNet37 adopts lightweight attention-guided designs, while ARU-Net-MD38 leverages multi-scale dense connections with attention refinement. DSU-Net39 integrates CNN and Transformer branches in a dual-stage U-Net to capture both local details and global context. BDFormer40 employs boundary-aware transformers to explicitly enhance lesion edges.

Despite these advances, most architectures primarily operate within the spatial domain. Deeply integrated, parallel frequency–spatial analysis within encoder-decoder frameworks remains underexplored.

Our approach

Building upon these insights, we propose WA-NET, a frequency–spatial parallel architecture. It integrates an Enhanced Wavelet Transform (EWT) and a Boundary Refinement Module (BRM).

The EWT module performs spatial convolution and wavelet-based frequency decomposition simultaneously. It employs a channel–spatial attention mechanism to dynamically fuse multi-scale sub-band features. In parallel, the BRM generates edge maps at each encoding level, reinforcing boundary perception throughout the network.

During decoding, wavelet-domain features are fused with spatial-domain backbone features. This cross-domain interaction maximizes feature complementarity and addresses both texture representation and boundary ambiguity in skin lesion segmentation.

Methodology

This section presents the proposed WA-NET model from two perspectives: the network architecture design and the formulation of the loss function.

WA-NET is designed as a task-driven solution to the intrinsic challenges of skin lesion segmentation, which include irregular shapes, low contrast, blurred boundaries, and heterogeneous textures. Conventional U-Net variants, relying primarily on spatial-domain convolutions, often struggle with these issues, leading to under-segmentation or boundary smoothing.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce two core modules into a U-Net-based framework: the Enhanced Wavelet Transform (EWT) and the Boundary Refinement Module (BRM). The EWT leverages wavelet-domain decomposition to explicitly separate frequency components, thereby enhancing the representation of subtle boundaries and textures. The BRM provides fine-grained edge supervision to reinforce the model’s sensitivity to complex lesion contours. This dual-pathway design, fusing frequency and spatial domains with explicit boundary awareness, constitutes a principled approach tailored to the problem, rather than an incremental modification.

Architecture of WA-NET

WA-NET consists of two core components: the Enhanced Wavelet Transform (EWT) module and the Boundary Refinement Module (BRM), both embedded within a U-Net-based encoder–decoder architecture.

Difference from CBAM. Although the design of EWT’s dual attention is inspired by CBAM, there are two major differences. First, in CBAM the attention is applied directly on convolutional feature maps in the spatial domain, whereas in EWT it is specifically applied to frequency-domain sub-bands obtained by wavelet decomposition. This allows EWT to explicitly differentiate and reweight directional edge features (LH, HL, HH) and structural contours (LL). Second, instead of treating all channels equally as in CBAM, EWT reorganizes the attention-refined features back into frequency groups, preserving the interpretability of wavelet sub-bands. Thus, EWT not only learns “where to look” and “what to emphasize,” but also explicitly leverages frequency priors that are absent in conventional CBAM.

Overall architecture

An overview of the WA-NET architecture is shown in Fig. 1. The network follows a symmetrical encoder–decoder structure.

Overall structure of WA-NET.

The encoder comprises five stages, each containing two 33 convolutional layers with ReLU activation. The first four stages use 22 max pooling for downsampling. At each stage, features are refined by BRMs, generating an edge map via a 33 convolution and sigmoid activation. The edge map is concatenated with the original feature map and further refined through a 33 convolution followed by channel–spatial attention.

The refined features from the first four encoder stages are passed through EWT modules. Haar wavelet decomposition extracts four frequency-domain sub-bands, which are weighted using channel–spatial attention. The sub-band features are aligned with corresponding encoder features via a feature alignment module. A dynamic fusion module then adaptively combines the features using a 11 convolution with learnable weights.

The decoder mirrors the encoder. Each stage performs bilinear upsampling of the feature map, concatenates it with the corresponding fused encoder features via skip connections, reduces channel dimensionality using a 11 convolution, and refines the result with two 33 convolutions.

For boundary processing, edge maps from all five BRMs are upsampled to the input resolution via bilinear interpolation. Each map is refined using 33 convolutions and fused using softmax-normalized learnable weights. Efficient Channel Attention generates the final edge map, which serves as both an auxiliary output and a regularization signal.

During training, binary edge labels are generated by applying the Sobel operator to ground-truth masks. These labels supervise the BRM-predicted edge maps via binary cross-entropy loss. The edge branch is a lightweight convolutional sub-network trained to produce soft edge predictions that align with anatomical structures.

Experimental setup and implementation

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness and robustness of WA-NET, segmentation experiments were conducted on three publicly available skin lesion datasets: ISIC 2017, ISIC 2018, and PH2. These datasets encompass diverse imaging conditions, lesion types, and visual characteristics, enabling a thorough assessment of the model’s generalization capability.

The proposed WA-NET model comprises five encoding layers. All layers except the last incorporate the Enhanced Wavelet Transform (EWT) module. The EWT performs multi-scale decomposition using fixed Haar wavelet kernels, separating low-frequency (LL) structural information from high-frequency (LH, HL, HH) texture details. A channel–spatial attention mechanism then adaptively emphasizes salient frequency components while suppressing redundant information. This enhances the model’s sensitivity to critical texture features. After wavelet decomposition, the number of channels in each feature map quadruples. These frequency-domain features are fused with backbone features during decoding, significantly improving multi-scale representation capability.

In addition, a Boundary Refinement Module (BRM) is embedded at the output of each encoder layer. The module first extracts edge-related features to generate boundary probability maps, which are then concatenated with the original feature maps and further enhanced using a channel–spatial attention mechanism. During decoding, edge features are integrated via a multi-level Edge-guided fusion to guide segmentation predictions, resulting in clearer and more coherent lesion boundaries. In summary, the EWT module focuses on frequency-domain texture modeling, while the BRM strengthens boundary perception; their synergistic operation equips WA-NET with superior modeling capability for images with blurred boundaries and complex textures.

A multi-stage data augmentation strategy was employed to maximize sample diversity and improve robustness against complex transformations and clinical imaging variations. The augmentation pipeline includes:

Synchronous geometric transformations: applied to both images and masks, including random horizontal/vertical flips, rotations (, , , ), and random cropping (retaining 80%–100% of the area) to enhance rotational invariance and multi-scale feature learning.

Experiments were implemented in PyTorch and accelerated using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 GPU. The Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of and a batch size of 8 was used. Mixed-precision training improved efficiency and stability. A multi-loss joint optimization strategy was adopted, with the edge loss weight set to 0.7. Training was conducted for 100 epochs, with five-fold cross-validation on each dataset to enhance robustness.

Segmentation performance was evaluated using Dice coefficient, Accuracy, and Sensitivity. These metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of WA-NET and a solid basis for comparison with other state-of-the-art methods.

Ablation study

To evaluate the individual contributions of each module within WA-NET, we conducted an ablation study using three model variants: Base+EWT, Base+BRM, and Base+EWT+BRM (without edge loss). Table 2 summarises the quantitative results across all datasets using DSC, SE, and ACC metrics. In the ablation study, the ‘Base’ refers to the backbone architecture derived from the classical U-Net. Specifically, it retains the fundamental encoder-decoder structure with five encoding stages (each consisting of two 3x3 convolutional layers followed by 2x2 max pooling) and corresponding decoder stages (using bilinear upsampling and feature concatenation), along with the standard skip connections. Conclusion

In this paper, we propose WA-NET, a novel medical image segmentation network that effectively integrates frequency-domain and spatial-domain features. WA-NET leverages Haar wavelet decomposition to extract multi-scale, multi-directional sub-band features and employs an attention mechanism to adaptively weight the fusion of these frequency components, thereby capturing their relative importance across different bands. A Boundary Refinement Module is embedded within each encoder layer to enhance edge sensitivity through multi-stage processing, including edge feature extraction, boundary map prediction, feature fusion, and attention-based enhancement. The generated edge maps not only guide the training process but also directly improve the accuracy of final segmentation boundaries.

Regarding the loss function, we design a composite objective combining binary cross-entropy, Dice loss to mitigate class imbalance, and edge supervision loss, which collectively balances global segmentation accuracy with local boundary precision and enhances structural segmentation capability.

Importantly, the proposed WA-NET is not intended as a simple incremental modification of U-Net, but as a task-driven design tailored to the intrinsic challenges of skin lesion segmentation. By explicitly addressing issues such as irregular lesion morphology, low contrast, and blurred or ambiguous boundaries, WA-NET demonstrates that frequency–spatial feature fusion combined with boundary refinement provides a principled and effective solution for enhancing segmentation accuracy in complex medical imaging scenarios.
