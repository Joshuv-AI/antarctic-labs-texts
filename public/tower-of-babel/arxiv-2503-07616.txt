An Alternate Method for Finding Particular Solutions of Nonhomegeneous Differential Equations
Ashot Djrbashian, Milena Safaryan

Abstract
. In this short note we are presenting a method of finding particular solutions of nonhomegeneous linear equations. This approach is different from methods of undetermined coefficients or variation of parameters presented in virtually every standard Differential Equations textbook.



1.
Introduction

Let

(1)

a_{n}y^{(n)}+a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+a_{1}y^{\prime}+a_{0}y=q(t)

be an
n
th order linear nonhomogeneous differential equation with constant coefficients. As it is well known, the general solution to this equation consists of the sum of the general solution of the homogeneous part and a particular solution of the equation (1). For this and all other facts used in this note we can refer, for example, to classical textbook [1]. While finding the general solution of the homogeneous part is reduced to solving an algebraic polynomial equation of the order
n
and is quite standard, finding the particular solution of the equation (1) creates sometimes difficulties. There are two standard methods for that purpose which are described in every textbook. They are the methods of undetermined coefficients and the method of variation of parameters. In this note we present another method which allows to reduce solution to repetitive solutions of first order equations. Moreover, we show that in some very special cases, this method could be applied to find particular solutions of equations with variable coefficients.

2.
Differential Operator Approach

Let us start with the simple case of second order equation:

(2)

y^{\prime\prime}+ay^{\prime}+by=q(t),

Using the standard notation
Df=\frac{df}{dt}
we can re-write this equation in the form

(3)

\left(D^{2}+aD+bI\right)y=q(t),

where
I
is the identity operator. The characteristic polynomial of the given differential operator is:

p(r)=r^{2}+ar+b,

which can be factored as follows:

(r-r_{1})(r-r_{2}),

where
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are the roots of the polynomial. Accordingly, the equation (3) can be written as

(D-r_{1}I)(D-r_{2}I)y=q,

where the product of the differential operators is understood as composition of operators.

Expanding the last equation we get

(4)

(D-r_{1}I)(D-r_{2}I)y=D((D-r_{2}I)y)-r_{1}((D-r_{2}I)y)=q.

Now, let us denote
(D-r_{2}I)y=\phi.
With that notation the above equation (4) becomes

(D-r_{1}I)\phi=q.

This means that finding a particular solution to the nonhomogeneous equation is reduced now to solving two first order constant coefficient linear equations consequently.

So, we start by solving the "outer" equation first:
y^{\prime}-r_{1}y=q(t).
By the standard method, solution is given as (we omit integration constants in the process of solution because we are just looking for particular solutions)

(5)

\phi(t)=e^{r_{1}t}\int e^{-r_{1}t}q(t)dt.

Now, using this solution, we can solve the "inner" equation
y^{\prime}-r_{2}y=\phi(t).
It is given, as above, by the formula

(6)

y=e^{r_{2}t}\int e^{-r_{2}t}\phi(t)dt.

Combining solutions (5) and (6) we get the particular solution of the equation (2)-(3)

(7)

y_{p}=e^{r_{2}t}\int e^{(r_{1}-r_{2})t}\left(\int e^{-r_{1}t}q(t)\,dt\right)dt.

From the demonstration for solution of the second order equation it is clear that the same approach will work for equations of any order. Moreover, the solution is independent from the type of "outside force" function
q
. Hence, this approach can substitute both the method of undetermined coefficients and the method of variation of parameters.
Below we will demonstrate this approach on a number of examples, including the case of third order equation.

3.
Examples

1.
The case of distinct real roots.
To solve the differential equation

(8)

y^{\prime\prime}+5y^{\prime}+6y=2t+5

we first solve the corresponding characteristic equation
r^{2}+5r+6=0
and find
r_{1}=-2,r_{2}=-3
.
Using the formula above, we see that the particular solution is given by

y_{p}(t)=e^{-3t}\int e^{3t}e^{-2t}\left(\int e^{2t}(2t+5)dt\right)dt.

Calculating the inside integral gives (here and everywhere below we disregard integration constants)

\int e^{2t}(2t+5)dt=te^{2t}+2e^{2t}.

Continuing the process, now we have to calculate the "outer" integral

\int e^{t}(te^{2t}+2e^{2t})dt=\int(te^{3t}+2e^{3t})dt

=\frac{te^{3t}}{3}+\frac{5e^{3t}}{9}.

Finally, putting everything together, we have the final answer

y_{p}(t)=\frac{t}{3}+\frac{5}{9}.

Substitution into the original equation (8) shows that this is indeed a solution of the non homogeneous equation.

2.
The case of distinct real roots, where the right-hand side is a solution of the homogeneous equation.
Let us now consider the same equation, where the outside force is one of the solutions of the homogeneous equation.

(9)

y^{\prime\prime}+5y^{\prime}+6y=2e^{-2t}

Here we have now

y_{p}(t)=e^{-3t}\int e^{t}\left(\int e^{2t}2e^{-2t}dt\right)dt

=e^{-3t}\int 2te^{t}dt=e^{-3t}2(t-1)e^{t}=2e^{-2t}(t-1).

Once again, simple substitution into (9) confirms that this is the correct solution. Note here, that unlike the case of solving by the method of undetermined coefficients, we did not need to guess the form of the solution: The formula gave us the correct solution without any guessing.

3.
The case of repeated roots.
Here we will consider the case of a third order equation with two repeated roots:

(10)

y^{\prime\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime}+y=e^{5x}.

Here the characteristic polynomial for the homogeneous equation is:

r^{3}-r^{2}-r+1=(r-1)^{2}(r+1).

This means the differential equation is equivalent to:

(D-I)(D-I)(D+I)(y)=e^{5x}.

We need to solve three linear differential equations of the first order:
1. For
(D-I)y=e^{5x}
, the solution is

y_{1}=\frac{e^{5x}}{4}.

2. For
(D-I)y=\frac{e^{5x}}{4}
we have

y_{2}=\frac{e^{5x}}{16}.

3. For
(D+I)y=\frac{e^{5x}}{16}
, solving the linear differential equation gives the final answer

y_{p}=\frac{e^{5x}}{96}.

Once again, simple check establishes correctness of this solution.

4.
The case of complex roots.
More interesting cases occur when we have our characteristic polynomial with complex roots. In this case, our differential equation can be represented as:

(D+(a+bi)I)(D+(a-bi)I)(y).

Let us examine this type of differential equation and derive the general formula for particular solutions when the right-hand side involves
\sin x
or
\cos x
.

Consider, for example, the equation:

(D+(a+bi)I)(D+(a-bi)I)(y)=\cos x.

As we know,
\cos x
can be expressed using Euler’s formula:

\cos x=\frac{e^{ix}+e^{-ix}}{2}.

This substitution simplifies our work, as the integration of complex exponentials is analogous to the integration of real exponentials. Proceeding as before:

(D+(a+bi)I)(y)=\frac{e^{ix}+e^{-ix}}{2}.

Multiplying through by
e^{(a+bi)x}
and following the method used for real coefficients, we find:

y=\frac{e^{ix}}{2(a+(b+1)i)}+\frac{e^{-ix}}{2(a+(b-1)i)}.

Now, we solve:

(D+(a-bi)I)(y)=\frac{e^{ix}}{2(a+(b+1)i)}+\frac{e^{-ix}}{2(a+(b-1)i)}.

Proceeding as before, we obtain:

y=\frac{e^{ix}}{2(a^{2}+b^{2}-1+2ai)}+\frac{e^{-ix}}{2(a^{2}+b^{2}-1-2ai)}.

After simplifications and substituting back for the trigonometric functions, we arrive at the particular solution:

y_{p}=\frac{1}{(a^{2}+b^{2}-1)^{2}+4a^{2}}\left[(a^{2}+b^{2}-1)\cos x+2a\sin x\right].

5.
The case when the undetermined coefficients method does not apply.

Consider the example (borrowed from [2])

(11)

y^{\prime\prime}+4y^{\prime}+4y=e^{-2t}\ln t.

For this kind of righ-hand side functions only the variation of parameters method can be applied. Also, pay attention, that the characteristic equation has repeated root
r=-2.
Now, re-writing in operator form, we get

(D^{2}+4D+4I)y=D(D+2I)y+2(D+2I)y=e^{-2t}\ln t.

Denoting
\phi=(D+2I)y
we arrive to the first order equation

\phi^{\prime}+2\phi=e^{-2t}\ln t

which has the solution
\phi=e^{-2t}(t\ln t-t).
Now, on the last step we solve the equation

y^{\prime}+2y=e^{-2t}(t\ln t-t)

which gives us the final solution

y_{p}=\frac{1}{4}t^{2}e^{-2t}(2\ln t-3).

It is important also to notice, that in this particular case (and any other case when variation of parameters method is used) we completely avoided the necessity of calculating the Wronskian of the homogeneous equation. Additionally, there is no need to memorize the two formulas that lead to final solution in cases when the variation of parameters method is used.

6.
The case of variable coefficients.
From demonstration at the beginning of the Section 2, it is clear that it is impossible to factor equations into a product of two (or more) lower degree equations in the case when coefficients are variable. Moreover, the notion of characteristic equation and characteristic roots does not apply here. As it is well known, and described in any standard Differential Equations textbook, the only more or less productive method of solving variable coefficients equations is the method of power series. Obviously, the Cauchy-Euler equation is one lucky exception. Because of difficulty of finding these kind of solutions, the case of nonhomogeneous equations is not discussed at all. Below we will show that in some limited special cases the approach described above will work here too.

Let us consider the expression

(12)

(D+ax^{n}I)(D+bx^{m}I)

and see for which real values of
a,b,n,m
this expression might take the form
D^{2}-cx^{k}I.
Expanding the expression in (12) (with the use of the product rule) we get

D^{2}+(ax^{n}+bx^{m})D+(anx^{n-1}+bmx^{m-1}+abx^{n+m})I

and the necessary condition turns out to be
n=m,b=-a.
This means that equations of the form
y^{\prime\prime}-a^{2}x^{2n}y=q
could be factored as

(D+ax^{n}I)(D-ax^{n}I)y=q(x).

From here we can proceed exactly as in the case of constant variable coefficients and have
D(D-ax^{n}I)y+ax^{n}(D-ax^{n}I)y=q.
Notation
\phi=(D-a^{n}xI)y
reduces our equation to the first order linear equation

\phi^{\prime}+ax^{n}\phi=q(x).

A particular solution to this equation is given by the formula

\phi=\exp\left\{-\frac{ax^{n+1}}{n+1}\right\}\int\exp\left\{\frac{ax^{n+1}}{n+1}\right\}q(x)dx.

On the second, and last step of the solution, we need to take this function and solve the second first order equation
y^{\prime}-ax^{n}y=\phi.
Once again, the solution is standard and is given by the formula

y_{p}=\exp\left\{\frac{ax^{n+1}}{n+1}\right\}\int\exp\left\{-\frac{ax^{n+1}}{n+1}\right\}\phi(x)dx.

Concluding remarks
.
As demonstrated, the approach using linear differential operators effectively eliminates the need for guessing solutions and provides a systematic method for analytically solving differential equations. That said, it is quite possible that this method is not completely new. However, all our efforts to find a reliable reference where this was done before, were unsuccessful. We will appreciate if anybody could provide that kind of information.
