Pedigree structure, inbreeding, and survival analysis in an Irish Wolfhound cohort
Authors: Carlotta Ferrari, Francesca Bernini, Stefano P. Marelli, Marcello Poli, Maria G. Strillacci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Irish Wolfhound (IW) is a giant dog breed characterized by a relatively short lifespan, a high prevalence of heritable disorders, and extensive relatedness among lineages. Despite the recognized importance of inbreeding and population structure in purebred dogs, relatively little is known about how pedigree structure and demographic parameters influence survival patterns in large multinational IW populations. The aim of this study was to investigate pedigree structure, inbreeding, demographic parameters, and survival patterns in a multinational cohort of Irish Wolfhounds. An integrated pedigree comprising 2,343 individuals was reconstructed from the five-generation pedigrees of 96 reference dogs previously included in a genomic study. Pedigree completeness, ancestor redundancy, parental contribution, genealogical recurrence, generation interval, inbreeding coefficients, and effective population size (Ne) were evaluated. Longevity and survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier estimators and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsWithin the reconstructed pedigree, analyses revealed heterogeneous genealogical depth and substantial pedigree interconnectedness. The pedigree-based Ne estimate was 90.1 and remained similar (85.9) when a more restrictive pedigree-depth criterion was applied. Most recorded deaths occurred between 6 and 10 years of age. Females showed significantly lower mortality risk and slightly greater longevity compared with males; no significant cohort effects were detected. After adjustment for pedigree depth, pedigree-based inbreeding coefficients were not significantly associated with survival. Individuals with limited ancestral information often showed low or zero inbreeding coefficients despite reduced survival estimates, suggesting that missing pedigree data may underestimate relatedness and autozygosity.ConclusionsOverall, the results highlight the importance of pedigree completeness for accurate interpretation of inbreeding and survival analyses in highly interconnected populations. Integrating pedigree and genomic information may improve the assessment of population structure and health dynamics in purebred dogs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-026-00158-1.

Background

The Irish Wolfhound is a giant dog breed characterized by rapid growth, a relatively short lifespan, and a high prevalence of heritable diseases, including neoplastic, cardiac, and musculoskeletal conditions [1]. Among these, osteosarcoma and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) represent two of the most extensively documented breed-associated conditions and are considered major contributors to morbidity and mortality in the breed [1–3]. Both conditions have been linked to a substantial heritable component, with osteosarcoma showing high heritability estimates in pedigree-based studies of the breed and DCM being recognized as an inherited cardiac disease in IWs [2, 3]. Understanding the demographic and genetic factors underlying survival variability in this breed is therefore of considerable scientific and practical importance. In this context, pedigree-based analyses provide a fundamental framework for investigating population structure, inbreeding, and genealogical dynamics in domestic species. In closed or semi-closed breeding systems, such as those typical of purebred dogs, the repeated use of a limited number of breeding individuals can lead to pedigree collapse, reduced effective population size, and increased relatedness among individuals [4, 5]. These processes can affect both genetic diversity and the accumulation and distribution of deleterious alleles within the population. Consequently, inbreeding is expected to increase genome-wide homozygosity and may expose recessive deleterious variants, potentially affecting survival and health, a principle long established across animal and plant species [6, 7]. However, the reliability of pedigree-derived inbreeding estimates is strongly dependent on pedigree completeness and genealogical depth.

In parallel, demographic parameters such as generation interval and effective population size (Ne) provide key insights into the dynamics of genetic variation and the long-term sustainability of the population. In structured breeding systems, unequal reproductive contributions of specific sires and dams, often referred to as the ‘popular sire effect,’ may further shape population structure and accelerate loss of diversity [5]. Although several studies have investigated specific diseases and lifespan in Irish Wolfhounds, relatively limited information is available regarding the combined relationship between pedigree structure, demographic parameters, inbreeding, and survival patterns in large multinational cohorts [1, 3, 8]. In particular, few studies have jointly explored how genealogical structure and unequal ancestral contributions may influence longevity and the distribution of causes of death within the breed. Integrating pedigree-derived information with life-history data may therefore provide useful insights into how population structure shapes survival and disease distribution in Irish Wolfhounds.

The present study builds on a previously characterized cohort of 96 Irish Wolfhounds collected across 23 countries and subjected to genomic analyses [9], further integrating pedigree and life-history data from a multinational sample. Specifically, the aims were to: (i) describe the pedigree structure of the dataset; (ii) evaluate longevity and survival patterns across birth cohorts and sex; (iii) assess whether inbreeding was associated with survival after accounting for pedigree depth; and (iv) explore whether highly influential sires and dams were associated with recurrent patterns of specific causes of death within their descendant lineages.

Methods

Data collection and pedigree reconstruction

The pedigree dataset was derived from a cohort of 96 purebred Irish Wolfhounds (41 males and 55 females) previously included in a genomic study, which served here as the reference population. The dogs were sampled from 23 countries worldwide (Figure S1), representing a geographically diverse multinational subset of the breed [9]. Given the international interconnectedness of the Irish Wolfhound population, geographic origin was not assumed to imply genealogical independence; however, genomic relatedness among the 96 reference dogs was previously assessed through pairwise genome-wide IBD analyses, which showed that close relationships were uncommon within the sampled cohort [9].

For each individual, genealogical and life-history information was retrieved from the Irish Wolfhound Database (IWDB; https://iwdb.org), a free, open-access, volunteer-maintained resource launched in 2016 to consolidate worldwide pedigree, life-history, and health information on Irish Wolfhounds. Birth and death dates and causes of death are provided by owners and breeders, and the database is estimated to contain records for the large majority of Irish Wolfhounds ever registered.

Although deeper genealogical information was occasionally available for individual lineages, the overall completeness of both genealogical (parentage) and life-history data declined substantially beyond the fourth ancestral generation. Therefore, pedigree records were retrieved up to five generations for each of the 96 reference dogs, representing the maximum depth that ensured sufficiently homogeneous and reliable data availability across the study population.

To ensure subject anonymity, the 96 individuals retained the identification codes used in the genomic analysis, allowing direct linkage between the reconstructed pedigrees and the original reference subjects. All ancestors were instead assigned alphanumeric codes. To avoid ambiguity arising from homonymous individuals, dates of birth were incorporated into the coding procedure, thereby ensuring a unique identifier for each dog.

Pedigree structure and completeness analysis

An in-house R script was developed to merge the individual five-generation pedigree records into a single integrated pedigree dataset, enabling population-level analyses of pedigree structure, ancestor overlap, and genealogical completeness across the 96 Irish Wolfhounds included in the study. Although pedigree information was initially retrieved up to five generations for each reference dog, overlap among individual pedigrees allowed deeper ancestral relationships to be reconstructed for some individuals in the merged dataset. Thus, analyses based on the merged pedigree could extend beyond five generations as a consequence of pedigree overlap. The resulting integrated pedigree comprised 2,343 dogs. Individuals lacking both recorded parents within this reconstructed pedigree were operationally classified as pedigree founders. This category may include both individuals with genuinely unknown parentage and boundary individuals whose parents fall outside the reconstructed pedigree.

Pedigree completeness was assessed using two complementary metrics: equivalent complete generations (gen_equiv) and longest ancestral path, calculated using the genealogical information available in the integrated pedigree. Path-weighted genealogical recurrence

The recurrence of sires and dams across the pedigrees of the 96 IWs was evaluated using two complementary approaches: direct parental contribution and path-weighted genealogical recurrence, allowing recorded reproductive output within the reconstructed pedigree to be distinguished from broader patterns of ancestral recurrence. The path-weighted genealogical recurrence score integrates the frequency of ancestral occurrence with genealogical proximity across the pedigrees of the 96 reference dogs.

Direct parental contribution

To quantify the parental contribution of each sire and dam within the reconstructed pedigree, the number of recorded direct offspring was calculated from the integrated pedigree dataset by counting occurrences as a parent. Individuals were ranked in descending order of offspring number to identify the most represented parents within the reconstructed pedigree.

where g is the generation number relative to the proband (e.g., g = 1 for parents, g = 2 for grandparents). For each ancestor, weights (w) were summed across all occurrences, thereby capturing both frequency of occurrence and genealogical proximity.

The objective of these metrics was not to estimate formal genetic contributions or founder representation, but rather to describe the genealogical structure of the reference pedigrees by identifying ancestors that recur most frequently, are shared by the largest number of probands, and occur at shorter genealogical distances from the proband. For this descriptive purpose, simple path-based metrics were considered more appropriate than classical marginal-contribution approaches.

The generational weighting follows the expected halving of genealogical contribution with each successive generation. However, unlike classical marginal-contribution approaches such as that described by Boichard et al. (1997) [12], weights were summed directly across all ancestor occurrences without correction for contributions already accounted for by other ancestors. The resulting path-weighted genealogical recurrence score is therefore used here as a descriptive measure integrating ancestral recurrence and genealogical proximity, rather than as an estimate of the proportion of genetic material attributable to a given ancestor [12].

Finally, to distinguish ancestors broadly distributed across the reference pedigrees from those concentrated within fewer pedigrees, the mean path weight per proband was calculated. Sex information was used to stratify the results and separately visualize top-ranking male and female ancestors.

Generation interval, inbreeding and effective population size

The generation interval was estimated as the mean age of parents at the birth of their recorded offspring within the reconstructed pedigree. Parental ages were calculated as the difference between the date of birth of each offspring and that of its recorded sire or dam (in years). Mean sire and dam ages at offspring birth were estimated separately, and the overall generation interval was computed as their average. Additionally, generation interval was estimated across the four classical gametic pathways (sire–son, sire–daughter, dam–son, dam–daughter), and the mean of these pathway-specific values was also reported.

The inbreeding coefficient (F) was calculated from the pedigree-based kinship matrix using the kinship2 package in R [13].

Effective population size (Ne) was estimated from the individual rate of inbreeding increase, following pedigree-based methods described by Gutiérrez et al. (2009) [14]. where Fi is the inbreeding coefficient and ti is pedigree depth, approximated here by the number of equivalent complete generations (gen_equiv). Individuals with non-missing Fi and gen_equiv > 1 were included in the primary analysis. The mean individual rate of inbreeding increase (ΔF̄) was used to estimate effective population size as Ne = 1/(2ΔF̄). Because pedigree-based F = 0 values may be less informative when ancestral information is limited, a secondary sensitivity analysis was performed using a more restrictive pedigree-depth criterion (gen_equiv ≥ 2), irrespective of whether F was zero or greater than zero. This analysis was used to evaluate the sensitivity of the Ne estimate to pedigree depth while retaining zero inbreeding coefficients supported by more informative pedigrees.

Longevity and survival analyses

Longevity was defined as age at death (in years) and was calculated only for dogs with both recorded birth and death dates. A total of 1,052 dogs met these criteria. Dogs were grouped into three birth cohorts: pre-1990, 1990–2005, and post-2005. Longevity was assessed descriptively by cohort and sex using boxplots and proportional distributions of age at death.

Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier estimators and log-rank tests, as implemented in the survival package in R [15]. Age in years was used as the time scale, and death was coded as the event of interest. Because not all dogs without death dates could plausibly be considered alive, censoring was assigned using the following criteria: (i) dogs born up to and including 2016 without a recorded death date were excluded from survival analysis; (ii) dogs born from 1 January 2017 onward without a recorded death date were treated as right-censored (event = 0; this cutoff was based on the observed mean longevity of about 8 years, assuming that individuals below this age were unlikely to have completed their lifespan at the time of data collection); (iii) dogs with both birth and death dates were coded as events (event = 1). Survival was compared across birth cohorts using global and pairwise log-rank tests, with pairwise comparisons adjusted using the Holm method. Sex effects were tested both globally and within cohorts. Additional survival analyses were conducted using Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate the association between pedigree-based inbreeding and mortality risk. Inbreeding was first modelled as a continuous variable and subsequently as a categorical variable (F > 0 vs. F = 0), adjusting for pedigree depth (gen_equiv), birth cohort, and sex. To further assess the influence of pedigree completeness on the interpretation of zero inbreeding coefficients, individuals with F = 0 were classified according to pedigree depth (gen_equiv < 2 vs. gen_equiv ≥ 2), and survival was compared among F = 0 individuals with limited pedigree depth, F = 0 individuals with higher pedigree depth, and individuals with F > 0. Finally, the categorical F = 0 versus F > 0 comparison was repeated after restricting the analysis to individuals with gen_equiv ≥ 2.

Cause of death analysis

Causes of death were analysed to explore potential associations with inbreeding patterns, with a particular focus on the contribution of top sires or dams. Cause of death information was not available for all individuals and was therefore analysed on a subset of the population. To ensure consistency, reported causes of death were standardised by harmonising heterogeneous labels referring to the same condition (e.g., “Splenic torsion”, “Torsion of spleen”, “Torsion – splenic”). To reduce dimensionality and improve interpretability, causes of death were grouped into broader categories based on pathophysiological similarity, while age-at-death classes were defined descriptively relative to the observed lifespan distribution of the breed (mean age at death: approximately 8 ± 2 years). Age-at-death categories included early mortality (< 6 years), intermediate age (6–10 years), and old age (> 10 years), whereas causes of death categories (COD, n. 16) comprised Acute/Trauma, Age-related (dogs < 10 years and reported in IWDB as “Old age or Weak rear”), Autoimmune, Cancer, Cardiac, Gastrointestinal, Immunity/Infection, Musculoskeletal, Neurological, Organ-failure, Osteosarcoma, Renal/Urinary, Reproductive, Respiratory, Missing, Old_age (dogs > 10 years if reported in IWDB as “Old age or Weak rear”) and Other. The “Missing” category included cases lacking both a recorded cause and date of death, whereas “Other” referred to cases explicitly reported as unknown or involving dogs found dead without a defined diagnosis (e.g., found dead). These categories were then used to investigate mortality patterns within the pedigrees originating from the 96 reference dogs. In particular, the occurrence of specific causes of death was evaluated in relation to the contribution of top-ranking sires/dams, to identify potential clustering of conditions within highly influential lineages.

Results

Pedigree structure and completeness analysis

For the 96 reference IWs, pedigrees were reconstructed up to the fifth generation, yielding a dataset of 2,343 individuals, including 2,247 related individuals. Among these, 716 (30.5%) were founders (272 males and 444 females), while 1,627 (69.5%) had both parents known (727 males and 900 females). The birth year distribution of the integrated pedigree showed relatively few records in earlier decades, followed by a progressive increase in the number of registered individuals in more recent years. To account for sparse data in earlier years, individuals born before 1970 were grouped into a single category for presentation. The oldest of the 96 reference dogs was born in 2013 (Fig. 1).

The number of distinct ancestors per pedigree (ancestor list, ALC) ranged from 39 to 62, indicating variable levels of pedigree collapse and genealogical redundancy among individuals (Fig. 2). Pedigree depth varied across individuals, with the longest ancestral path ranging from 0 to 14 generations (mean 3.87 ± SD 4.02). The number of equivalent complete generations (gen_equiv) ranged from 0 to 7.87 (mean 2.20 ± SD 2.04). Overall, 518 individuals had gen_equiv > 4, and 278 exceeded 5 equivalent complete generations, indicating that a substantial proportion of the dataset was supported by relatively deep and informative pedigrees. When focusing on the reference population of 96 IWs, pedigree depth was considerably higher, with a mean gen_equiv of 6.36 (± SD 0.62) and a mean longest ancestral path of 11.57 generations (± SD 1.83).

Parental and genealogical contribution – top 10

Parental contribution was unevenly distributed among individuals. Among the top 10 contributors, the most represented sires included DOG_1474 (n = 13 offspring), DOG_1753 (n = 11), and DOG_2246 (n = 9), with several others contributing between 7 and 8 offspring (Fig. 3A). In contrast, dams showed lower reproductive output, with the top female (DOG_0264) contributing 6 offspring and the remaining top dams contributing between 4 and 5 offspring.

Genealogical contribution analysis showed a heterogeneous distribution of ancestor influence (Fig. 3B; Table S1). Among sires, DOG_1753 and DOG_1774 were the most represented ancestors, each with n_paths = 49 and appearing in 34 and 33 pedigrees (n_probands), respectively. These individuals showed the highest contribution values (0.52% and 0.42%). DOG_1780, although present in fewer pedigrees (n_probands = 18), also ranked among the top contributors with a total weight of 2.09.

Among dams, DOG_0513 was the top-ranking ancestor, appearing in 24 pedigrees (n_probands) and across 31 genealogical paths (n_paths), with a contribution of 0.31%. Other top-ranking females, such as DOG_1024 and DOG_1550, showed similar values of n_paths and n_probands, whereas individuals such as DOG_1525 and DOG_1798 were present in a larger number of pedigrees (n_probands = 21 and 22, respectively) but with lower total weights (1.09 and 1.03) (Table S1).

Cumulative contribution values indicated that the top 10 male ancestors accounted for 3.87% of the total genealogical contribution, while the top 10 female ancestors accounted for 10.59% (Fig. 3B).

Several individuals were consistently identified as top contributors under both parental and genealogical approaches. Among sires, DOG_1753, DOG_1774, DOG_1780, and DOG_0321 ranked among the top contributors in both analyses. Among dams, this overlap was limited to DOG_1550 and DOG_1024.

Generation interval, inbreeding and effective population size

The estimated mean sire age at offspring birth was 3.66 years, whereas the corresponding mean dam age was 3.98 years, yielding an overall mean generation interval of 3.81 years. Implausibly high paternal ages were inspected manually. Given that artificial insemination may occur in the breed, generation interval was calculated both including all valid parental ages and after excluding extreme paternal ages as a sensitivity check. To avoid distortion by extreme values, parental ages exceeding 10 years were excluded (n = 35) from the filtered analysis, considering the average lifespan of the breed. After filtering, the mean sire age was 3.54 years and the mean dam age was 3.98 years, resulting in an overall generation interval of 3.76 years.

A total of 1,790 of 2,343 individuals had F = 0, whereas 553 had F > 0 (Fig. 4). Across the entire reconstructed pedigree, mean F was 0.009, with a maximum observed value of 0.31. Among individuals with F = 0, 687 had gen_equiv ≥ 2, whereas 1,103 had more limited pedigree depth (gen_equiv < 2). The primary Ne analysis included 1,301 individuals with gen_equiv > 1 and yielded a mean individual rate of inbreeding increase of 0.0055, corresponding to an estimated Ne of 90.08. In the sensitivity analysis applying the more restrictive criterion of gen_equiv ≥ 2, 1,240 individuals were retained, including 687 individuals with F = 0. The mean individual rate of inbreeding increase was 0.0058, corresponding to an estimated Ne of 85.86.

Longevity and survival analyses

Longevity, defined as age at death (in years), was calculated for 1,052 dogs with both recorded birth and death dates. Individuals were grouped into three birth cohorts: pre-1990 (n = 79; 44 males, 35 females), 1990–2005 (n = 492; 210 males, 282 females), and post-2005 (n = 481; 208 males, 273 females).

Boxplot distributions showed broadly overlapping lifespan patterns across cohorts, with most deaths occurring between approximately 6 and 10 years of age. Median longevity was slightly higher in females than in males across all cohorts, with a tendency toward higher upper quartiles in females, particularly in the 1990–2005 and post-2005 cohorts (Fig. 5A).

Fig. 5(A) Longevity distribution by cohort and sex; (B) Proportional age-at-death distributions by cohort and sex

(A) Longevity distribution by cohort and sex; (B) Proportional age-at-death distributions by cohort and sex

Proportional age-at-death distributions confirmed these patterns, with peak frequencies concentrated between 7 and 9 years in all cohorts (Fig. 5B). Female distributions showed a modest shift toward older ages, particularly in the post-2005 cohort, where a higher proportion of individuals reached ages above 9–10 years. Early-age mortality (< 5 years) was limited across cohorts and sexes, with only sporadic occurrences.

The final dataset for survival analysis included 1,267 dogs, which is larger than the subset used for descriptive longevity (n = 1,052) because individuals without a recorded death date were included as right-censored observations. Survival was compared across birth cohorts using global and pairwise log-rank tests, with pairwise comparisons adjusted using the Holm method. Sex effects were tested both globally and within cohorts. All individuals in the pre-1990 (n = 79) and 1990–2005 (n = 492) cohorts had recorded death events, whereas in the post-2005 cohort, 215 individuals were right-censored and 481 had recorded deaths.

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no statistically significant differences among the three birth cohorts (global log-rank P = 0.20). Pairwise comparisons were also non-significant after Holm correction (Fig. 6A).

Survival analysis by cohort and sex. (A) Survival curves stratified by birth cohort; (B) Survival curves stratified by both birth cohort and sex

When sex was analysed across the full survival dataset, a significant difference in survival was detected (P = 0.0002), indicating lower mortality risk in females, consistent with the Kaplan–Meier curves. Within-cohort analyses showed that this effect varied across cohorts: no significant difference was observed in the pre-1990 cohort (P = 0.679), a non-significant trend was detected in the 1990–2005 cohort (P = 0.06), and a significant difference was observed in the post-2005 cohort (P < 0.0007) (Fig. 6B).

Cox proportional hazards analysis showed no significant association between pedigree-based inbreeding and survival when F was modelled as a continuous variable after adjustment for pedigree depth, birth cohort, and sex (HR = 2.03, 95% CI: 0.16–25.16, P = 0.580). Categorical analyses similarly showed no overall difference in survival between individuals with F > 0 and F = 0 (Figure S2). However, when individuals with F = 0 were distinguished according to pedigree depth, those with limited pedigree information (gen_equiv < 2) showed a significantly higher mortality risk than F = 0 individuals with greater pedigree depth (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.03–1.43, P = 0.018). No significant association between F status and survival was observed after restricting the analysis to individuals with gen_equiv ≥ 2.

Cause of death analysis

Across the entire dataset (n = 2,343), the largest proportion of individuals was classified within the “Missing” category (n = 1,291), reflecting the absence of sufficient mortality information. Among dogs with available records, intermediate age-at-death represented the most frequent category (68.16%), followed by old age (18.35%) and early mortality (13.50%). Similar patterns were observed in both sexes, although females showed a higher proportion of old-age individuals (22.71% vs. 12.77% in males), whereas early mortality was slightly more frequent in males (15.58% vs. 11.86% in females). Intermediate age remained the predominant category in both sexes, accounting for 65.42% of females and 71.65% of males.

Cause of death categories (16 grouped COD; n = 707, corresponding to approximately 30% of the dataset), after exclusion of the “Missing” category, showed a heterogeneous distribution across the population (Fig. 7). Osteosarcoma represented the most frequent single category (17%), followed by other cancer-related conditions (14%), together accounting for approximately 31% of all recorded causes of death, followed by cardiac diseases (11%) and respiratory conditions (10%). Gastrointestinal, immunity/infection, and musculoskeletal causes each accounted for approximately 9% of recorded deaths, whereas neurological, organ-failure, autoimmune, and renal/urinary conditions were comparatively rare (Fig. 7A).

Sex-specific distributions were broadly similar, although some differences were observed. Females showed proportionally higher frequencies of old-age, cancer, and immunity/infection causes, whereas males presented slightly higher proportions of acute/trauma, gastrointestinal, and age-related conditions. Osteosarcoma represented one of the most frequent causes of death in both sexes, and the autoimmune condition was observed only in males (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive overview of pedigree structure, genealogical contribution, and survival patterns in a multinational cohort of IWs, integrating pedigree-based and life-history indicators within a five-generation framework derived from the 96 reference individuals. The reconstructed pedigree revealed substantial variability in pedigree completeness and genealogical depth, with evidence of both deeply documented lineages and individuals characterized by limited ancestral information. The wide distribution observed for ancestor list counts, longest ancestral paths, and equivalent complete generations reflects the heterogeneous quality typical of large canine pedigree databases assembled from multiple sources and countries [5]. Despite this variability, a substantial proportion of individuals showed relatively informative pedigrees, with 518 dogs exceeding four equivalent complete generations and 278 exceeding five. Pedigree reconstruction therefore captured considerable genealogical information for part of the population, particularly within the IW reference group, which exhibited markedly greater pedigree depth, with mean gen_equiv values above six generations and ancestral paths extending beyond eleven generations on average. These findings indicate the presence of well-documented and historically traceable lineages within the dataset. Nevertheless, pedigree completeness remained heterogeneous across the overall population. As highlighted by Leroy (2011) [5], pedigree depth strongly influences the accuracy of genealogical parameters, particularly inbreeding coefficients and estimates of genetic diversity, since shallow or incomplete pedigrees systematically underestimate relatedness and autozygosity [5, 16]. In the present dataset, several individuals showed limited ancestral information, including founder-like pedigrees and gen_equiv values below one generation, conditions likely leading to underestimation of pedigree-based inbreeding coefficients [5, 16]. Similar limitations have been reported in other canine populations, where differences in studbook organization, historical registration practices, and international exchange of breeding animals contribute to uneven pedigree informativeness across lineages [5].

Parental and genealogical contribution

Parental contribution was markedly uneven, with a limited number of sires accounting for a large proportion of offspring. When extended to genealogical contribution, this imbalance persisted, although with distinct patterns between males and females.

Some sires combined wide distribution across pedigrees with high genealogical recurrence, indicating a strong and pervasive influence across the population. Others, despite appearing in fewer pedigrees, showed relatively high per-lineage contributions, suggesting more localized but still relevant genetic impact. Female ancestors showed a more heterogeneous pattern, with some individuals broadly distributed but contributing less intensively, and others exhibiting more balanced one-to-one relationships between occurrence and distribution. Overall, cumulative contribution profiles indicated that male genealogical influence was more diffuse, whereas female contribution tended to be more concentrated among fewer individuals. These patterns are consistent with asymmetric reproductive use between sexes, commonly observed in pedigree dog populations, where a limited number of sires disproportionately shape population structure through the “popular sire effect” and long-term lineage persistence [16, 17].

Generation interval, inbreeding and effective population size

The estimated mean parental age at offspring birth within the reconstructed pedigree was approximately 3.8 years. This metric provides a description of reproductive timing within the study pedigree but should not be interpreted as the classical population generation interval, which is defined as the average age of parents at the birth of replacement breeding offspring. Direct comparison with generation interval estimates reported in other canine pedigree studies should therefore be made with caution [16, 18, 19]. The modest reduction observed after excluding extreme paternal ages suggests that, although relatively uncommon, the use of older sires through artificial insemination and frozen semen may influence demographic estimates within the breed. In the present dataset, 48 dogs were generated from matings in which the sire–offspring age difference exceeded 10 years, involving a total of 35 distinct sires, a pattern compatible with prolonged reproductive use through artificial insemination and frozen semen. Such practices may extend the genetic influence of specific lineages far beyond the natural reproductive lifespan of individual dogs, potentially reinforcing existing pedigree structure and slowing effective genetic turnover within the population. In breeds already characterized by substantial genealogical interconnectedness and moderate effective population size, repeated use of older sires may also contribute to persistence and wider dissemination of potentially deleterious variants or lineage-associated disease predispositions across generations [17]. In fact, among the 35 distinct sires used in artificial insemination, death dates were available for only 21 individuals, with a mean lifespan of 8.6 years. Cause-of-death information was available for 17 sires, including three cases of osteosarcoma, two additional cancer-related deaths, and four individuals classified within the old-age category (> 10 years, up to 12 years), alongside other causes broadly consistent with those observed across the overall population. While no direct causal relationship can be inferred, these findings highlight the importance of considering long-term lineage persistence when evaluating genetic diversity and health management strategies in the breed.

The pedigree-based effective population size estimate was approximately 90 in the primary analysis (Ne = 90.08) and remained relatively stable when a more restrictive pedigree-depth criterion was applied in the sensitivity analysis (Ne = 85.86). This limited variation indicates that the estimate was not strongly affected by the specific pedigree-depth threshold applied. Nevertheless, pedigree completeness remains an important consideration when interpreting pedigree-derived inbreeding parameters. In the present dataset, a high proportion of individuals had F = 0, particularly among individuals with limited genealogical depth. However, zero pedigree-based inbreeding coefficients were also observed among individuals with more informative pedigrees, emphasizing that F = 0 should not be interpreted as synonymous with founder status or absence of underlying relatedness. Rather, it indicates that no inbreeding was detected within the available reconstructed ancestry. As previously reported in pedigree-based canine studies, incomplete or shallow genealogies may underestimate relatedness and autozygosity [5, 17], and pedigree-based inbreeding coefficients should therefore be interpreted in relation to the amount of genealogical information available. The pedigree-based Ne estimates obtained in the present study should consequently be regarded as indicative rather than definitive estimates of the effective population size of the breed. The similar values obtained in the primary and sensitivity analyses support the relative robustness of the estimate to the pedigree-depth criteria examined here, while not eliminating the broader limitations associated with heterogeneous pedigree completeness. Together with the substantial pedigree interconnectedness, repeated ancestral loops, and unequal reproductive patterns observed in the reconstructed pedigree, these results are consistent with a structured breeding population. However, pedigree-based Ne estimates alone are insufficient to accurately quantify the effective population size of the worldwide Irish Wolfhound population [20].

Longevity and survival patterns

Descriptive analyses of longevity revealed largely overlapping lifespan distributions across cohorts, with most deaths occurring between 6 and 10 years, consistent with previous reports for the breed [20]. Although survival distributions were broadly similar across birth cohorts, females consistently showed slightly greater longevity and lower mortality risk compared with males. This pattern was further supported by Kaplan–Meier analyses and Cox proportional hazards models, in which sex remained significantly associated with survival after adjustment for pedigree-based inbreeding, pedigree depth, and birth cohort. Sex-related differences in longevity have been described in several canine populations and may reflect a combination of physiological, hormonal, and disease-related factors influencing mortality risk across the lifespan [8]. No statistically significant differences in survival were observed among birth cohorts, suggesting relative temporal stability in the observed mortality patterns. However, cohort effects should be interpreted cautiously, as the most recent cohort included a substantial proportion of right-censored individuals that had not yet completed their lifespan at the time of data collection. Pedigree-based inbreeding was not significantly associated with survival. Although increased homozygosity is generally expected to negatively affect fitness and lifespan through inbreeding depression and the expression of deleterious recessive variants [6, 7, 21], the present analyses highlight the importance of pedigree completeness when interpreting pedigree-derived inbreeding coefficients. In particular, zero or low F values derived from shallow pedigrees may reflect limited ancestral information rather than genuinely low genealogical relatedness. This may reduce the ability of pedigree-based F to capture associations with survival, particularly in highly interconnected populations. Similar limitations have been reported in pedigree-based studies of domestic dogs, where incomplete genealogies can obscure underlying population structure and reduce the accuracy of demographic and genetic parameters [5, 16]. This interpretation is further supported by the genomic analyses previously reported for the reference population, which identified substantial levels of homozygosity despite heterogeneous pedigree-based inbreeding estimates [9]. Thus, low pedigree-based inbreeding coefficients should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of low genomic homozygosity, particularly when ancestral information is incomplete.

Cause of death analysis

The cause-of-death analysis highlighted the complexity of mortality patterns within the breed, while also emphasizing the limitations imposed by incomplete historical records. More than half of the individuals in the integrated pedigree lacked sufficiently detailed mortality information and were therefore classified as “Missing”. Nevertheless, among dogs with available information, most individuals were classified within the intermediate age-at-death category, consistent with lifespan estimates commonly reported for giant breeds and IWs in particular [1, 8]. Females showed a higher proportion of long-lived individuals, whereas males were more frequently represented among early-mortality cases, in agreement with the sex-related survival differences observed in the Kaplan–Meier analyses. The high frequency of neoplastic diseases observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports identifying Irish Wolfhounds as one of the breeds most predisposed to osteosarcoma and other malignancies. In particular, osteosarcoma alone accounted for 17% of all recorded deaths, while neoplastic conditions overall represented approximately one-third of known mortality cases. This is in line with previous pedigree-based studies reporting high heritability estimates for osteosarcoma in the breed and suggesting a substantial genetic contribution to disease susceptibility [2].

Beyond neoplastic conditions, a considerable proportion of deaths was also associated with cardiac, respiratory, and gastrointestinal disorders. Cardiac diseases, including forms compatible with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), represented one of the most frequent categories, consistent with the recognized predisposition of IWs to inherited cardiac disease [3]. Respiratory conditions, including recurrent pneumonia, were also common and have previously been associated with reduced lifespan in the breed [8]. Similarly, gastrointestinal causes included several cases compatible with gastric or splenic torsion/volvulus, conditions frequently reported in giant breeds [22, 23].

Interestingly, a substantial proportion of deaths also fell within the immunity/infection category, including post-surgical and post-partum complications. Although descriptive, this pattern may suggest reduced resilience to inflammatory or infectious challenges in at least part of the population. In highly homogeneous breeds such as the IW, long-term accumulation of homozygosity may contribute not only to disease-specific predisposition but also to broader reductions in physiological robustness and immune competence [6, 21]. This hypothesis remains speculative in the present dataset, but it is consistent with the high genomic homozygosity observed across the reference population and warrants further investigation integrating genomic diversity, immune-related loci, and longitudinal clinical data [9].

At the same time, interpretation of the cause-of-death data should consider that these records were collected from the Irish Wolfhound Database, where information is voluntarily provided by owners and breeders. In many cases, the reported causes of death are based on veterinary diagnoses; however, the level of diagnostic detail is not uniform across all records. Consequently, some deaths, particularly in older dogs, may be classified using broader categories or lack detailed diagnostic information, which could influence the distribution of specific causes of death within the dataset.

Among the 96 reference IWs, nine individuals were reported as deceased, with a mean age at death of approximately 8 years (range: 3.5 years, autoimmune disease, to nearly 12 years, old age). From this subset, two individuals with contrasting and well-defined mortality phenotypes were selected as illustrative cases to qualitatively explore the distribution of phenotypic patterns within genealogical lineages: IWF_002, which reached nearly 12 years and was classified as old age (Fig. 8A), and IWF_006, which died of osteosarcoma at 10 years of age (Fig. 8B). In both pedigrees, ancestors sharing the same phenotypic category (osteosarcoma or old age/longevity) were highlighted to illustrate how these traits may be distributed across ancestral lineages, without implying direct causality. In the pedigree of IWF_002, the only dog highlighted in blue had a recorded accidental cause of death, having died in an accident at 5.5 years of age. In the context of these two dogs, the numbers of unique ancestors were lower than the theoretical maximum of 62 expected for a five-generation pedigree, with a total of 53 and 52 distinct ancestors observed, respectively. This indicates the presence of pedigree collapse, further supported by ancestor loss coefficients (ALC) of about 85.5% and 83.9%, reflecting moderate genealogical redundancy.

The seven-generation pedigree reconstructions of IWF_002 and IWF_006 illustrated the occurrence of the respective phenotypic categories within their ancestral lineages. In the pedigree of IWF_002 (Fig. 8A), selected as an example of exceptional longevity, several ancestors were classified within the old-age category (> 10 years), reflecting the presence of long-lived individuals across the pedigree. At the same time, intermediate and early-mortality cases were also present, reflecting heterogeneous survival patterns within the lineage. Similarly, in the pedigree of IWF_006 (Fig. 8B), osteosarcoma- and cancer-related cases were distributed across both paternal and maternal branches, illustrating the presence of neoplastic conditions across multiple ancestral lines rather than confinement to a single lineage. In both pedigrees, multiple ancestral loops and repeated ancestors were observed, with four consanguineous matings identified in the pedigree of IWF_002 and five in IWF_006, indicating substantial pedigree interconnectedness and ancestor recurrence. These patterns highlight that repeated ancestors and consanguineous loops make it difficult to determine whether the observed phenotypes are associated with specific lineages or with the overall pedigree structure itself. Moreover, a more complete availability of death dates and cause-of-death records across the pedigree would likely provide a more accurate and comprehensive representation of phenotype distribution within these genealogical lineages. This limitation also prevented extension of the same pedigree-based visualization approach to the top sires and dams identified under both parental and genealogical contribution analyses, as incomplete mortality information for several top contributors precluded a comprehensive assessment of potential associations between specific lineages and causes of death. Interestingly, the two representative individuals also differed in pedigree-based and genomic inbreeding coefficients [9]. IWF_006, affected by osteosarcoma, showed higher pedigree-based and genomic homozygosity estimates (F = 0.049; FHOM = 0.107) compared with the long-lived individual IWF_002 (F = 0.023; FHOM = 0.046). However, both individuals exhibited similarly high FROH values (0.323 and 0.299, respectively). Notably, neither individual showed ROH segments longer than 16 Mb, although several consanguineous matings were observed within the recent pedigree structure of IWF_006. This is consistent with the observation that genomic homozygosity does not reflect extremely recent close inbreeding alone, but rather a combination of more recent lineage interconnectedness superimposed on an already highly homogeneous breed background. Both individuals belonged to the subgroup of 41 dogs in which genomic inbreeding was primarily driven by ROH between 8 and 16 Mb, supporting the hypothesis that a substantial proportion of homozygosity in the breed reflects cumulative shared ancestry distributed across generations rather than isolated recent consanguineous mating [9].

Limitations and strengths

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. Pedigree completeness and availability of life-history information were heterogeneous across individuals, and death dates and cause-of-death records were frequently unavailable. This variability may affect the accuracy of pedigree-based inbreeding estimates and limit the interpretation of mortality patterns across lineages.

Pedigree information was initially retrieved up to five generations for each of the 96 reference dogs, although overlap among individual pedigrees allowed deeper ancestral relationships to be reconstructed for some individuals in the integrated pedigree. Accordingly, some pedigree measures, such as the number of unique ancestors and ALC, were explicitly restricted to five generations, whereas gen_equiv and longest ancestral path were calculated using the genealogical information available in the integrated pedigree. Despite accounting for pedigree depth in the analyses, heterogeneous genealogical completeness should still be considered when interpreting pedigree-based inbreeding and relatedness estimates, particularly for individuals with limited ancestral information.

In addition, genomic inbreeding measures were available only for the subset of 96 reference dogs, preventing direct comparison between pedigree-based and genomic estimates across the wider reconstructed dataset. Similarly, the lineage-based analyses of mortality patterns were primarily descriptive and therefore do not allow causal inference regarding associations between specific ancestors and disease occurrence.

Among the 96 genomically characterized reference dogs, only nine individuals had recorded deaths. These individuals were considered specifically for the illustrative lineage-based analysis, from which two dogs with contrasting and well-defined mortality phenotypes (osteosarcoma and exceptional longevity) were selected as representative examples. These pedigree reconstructions were intended to illustrate how sufficiently complete pedigree and phenotypic information may allow mortality-related patterns to be traced across genealogical lineages, rather than to provide a formal predictive analysis.

At the same time, the reconstruction of extended pedigrees from the 96 reference IWs provided a valuable framework for exploring genealogical structure across multiple generations. By integrating pedigree, survival, and cause-of-death information, this approach allowed qualitative investigation of potential connections between ancestral lineages and recurrent phenotypic patterns, providing a basis for future studies integrating more complete phenotypic and genomic information.

Conclusions

The present study provides an integrated overview of pedigree structure, demographic parameters, inbreeding, and survival patterns in a multinational Irish Wolfhound cohort reconstructed from 96 reference individuals previously characterized at the genomic level. The results revealed substantial pedigree interconnectedness, heterogeneous pedigree completeness, and uneven parental representation and genealogical recurrence, consistent with the structured breeding dynamics typical of purebred dog populations.

Pedigree-based Ne estimates were relatively consistent across the pedigree-depth criteria examined, but should be interpreted as indicative rather than as definitive estimates of the effective population size of the breed. The extensive genomic homozygosity previously reported in the reference population [9] provides complementary evidence of substantial shared ancestry within the breed.

Longevity analyses identified significant sex-related differences, with females showing slightly greater lifespan and lower mortality risk than males, whereas pedigree-based inbreeding coefficients were not significantly associated with survival.

These findings highlight the importance of pedigree completeness when interpreting pedigree-derived parameters in highly interconnected populations. Neoplastic conditions, particularly osteosarcoma, represented a major cause of death, together with cardiac, respiratory, and gastrointestinal disorders. Qualitative pedigree reconstructions illustrated recurrence of phenotypic patterns within interconnected lineages, although incomplete mortality data limited definitive interpretation. Overall, this study provides a comprehensive characterization of genealogical structure, demographic patterns, and survival in Irish Wolfhounds, establishing a useful basis for future investigations of population dynamics and breed health.
