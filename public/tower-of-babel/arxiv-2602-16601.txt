Error Propagation and Model Collapse in Diffusion Models: A Theoretical Study
Naïl B. Khelifa, Richard E. Turner, Ramji Venkataramanan

Abstract
Machine learning models are increasingly trained or fine-tuned on synthetic data. Recursively training on such data has been observed to significantly degrade performance in a wide range of tasks, often characterized by a progressive drift away from the target distribution. In this work, we theoretically analyze this phenomenon in the setting of score-based diffusion models. For a realistic pipeline where each training round uses a combination of synthetic data and fresh samples from the target distribution, we obtain upper and lower bounds on the accumulated divergence between the generated and target distributions. This allows us to characterize different regimes of drift, depending on the score estimation error and the proportion of fresh data used in each generation. We also provide empirical results on synthetic data and images to illustrate the theory.

Setting

We study a recursive training procedure in which the training data in each round is a combination of fresh samples from the true data distribution and synthetic samples generated by the current diffusion model. Let
p_{\mathrm{data}}
denote the (unknown) true data distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We refer to each training round as a generation, and denote the model distribution at the end of generation
i
by
\hat{p}^{i}
for
i\geq 0
, with
\hat{p}^{0}=p_{\mathrm{data}}
. At each generation

•

a proportion
\alpha

, and

•

a proportion
(1-\alpha)
of synthetic samples drawn from the current model

We emphasize that the samples are not labeled as fresh or synthetic. This captures a realistic setting where the training is agnostic to how the samples were generated.
Therefore, at the population level, the effective training distribution for generation

q_{i}:=\alpha\,p_{\mathrm{data}}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{p}^{i}.

(1)

A score network is trained on samples from

, which is then used to produce the next synthetic distribution
\hat{p}^{i+1}
. This defines the recursion

\hat{p}^{i}\;\xrightarrow{\text{mix with }p_{\mathrm{data}}}\;q_{i}\;\xrightarrow{\text{train model}}\;\hat{p}^{i+1}.

(2)

The goal of this work is to answer the following question:

In this recursive setting, how do errors accumulate over generations and how does the divergence between
\hat{p}^{i}
and

Learning Target

At a high-level, training a diffusion model is equivalent to learning the score function of
p_{\mathrm{data}}
corrupted with Gaussian noise, at a range of noise levels
(
49
;
14
)
.
Although the intention is to learn
p_{\mathrm{data}}
, the score network at generation
i
is trained on samples drawn from the mixture
q_{i}
. At the population level, the identifiable target of training is therefore the score of
q_{i}
, not the score of
p_{\mathrm{data}}
. This mismatch between the intended target and the learned target is a fundamental source of error under recursive training. Furthermore, the score network does not error learn the score of
q_{i}
perfectly (due to finite-sample and function approximation errors). This score matching error propagates through the stochastic differential equation (SDE) defining the diffusion model (the “reverse SDE” in (
8
)), because of which the learned distribution
\hat{p}^{i+1}
will differ from
q_{i}
. Characterizing this error propagation is crucial for understanding the divergence of
\hat{p}^{i+1}
from
p_{\mathrm{data}}
as
i
grows.

We track the evolution of the recursion using two quantities based on the
\chi^{2}
-divergence (defined in (
5
)):

•

Accumulated divergence
, measuring the divergence between the
i
-th generation model and the true data distribution:

D_{i}:=\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i}\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{data}}).

(3)

•

The
intra-generation
divergence, measuring the error introduced in one training round:

I_{i}:=\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i+1}\,\|\,q_{i}).

(4)

We work primarily with the
\chi^{2}
-divergence because, in the perturbative regime relevant for our analysis, it is equivalent to the KL divergence up to universal constants (see empirical evidence in Figure
4
, where our bounds holds for both divergences), while yielding a particularly convenient algebra under the refresh step
q_{i}=\alpha p_{\mathrm{data}}+(1-\alpha)\hat{p}^{i}
that makes the cross-generation recursion explicit.

Main contributions

The recursion induced by training on real-synthetic mixtures involves two competing effects: error
mitigation
from the fresh data used in each step, and error
accumulation
from imperfect score learning and sampling. Our contributions quantify both terms and their interaction across a growing number of generations.

1.
Lower bound for intra-generation divergence
:
We provide a lower bound (Proposition
3.3
) for
I_{i}
that, for small score errors, is the product of two diffusion-specific terms: (i)
observability
of errors at the endpoint of the diffusion path (captured by a coefficient
\eta_{i}
), and (ii) the energy of the path-wise score error.

\underbrace{\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{\,i+1}\|q_{i})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Intra-Generational}\\
\text{Divergence}\end{subarray}}\;\gtrsim\;\underbrace{\eta_{i}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Observability}\\
\text{of Errors}\end{subarray}}\,\cdot\,\underbrace{\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Score Error}\\
\text{Energy}\end{subarray}}

To our knowledge, this is the first lower bound for diffusion models quantifying the discrepancy between the learned distribution and the target.

2.
Intra-generation Divergence Control via Score Error:

Combining the above lower bound with a standard upper bound (Proposition
3.1
),
and proving an equivalence between the score error energies on the ideal-path and the learned-path (for small errors),
we show in Theorem
3.4
that in this regime
I_{i}
is equivalent to the score error energy (up to constants):
\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{\,i+1}\|q_{i})\;\asymp\;\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}
.

3. Long-horizon regimes and a discounted accumulation law.

We analyze the accumulated divergence
D_{N}
for small per-generation score errors, and identify a dichotomy as the generation
N
grows (Proposition
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
): (i) if the score error energies do not decay fast enough (e.g. a nonzero error floor so that
\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{\star,k}^{2}=+\infty
), then
D_{N}
cannot vanish with growing
N
; (ii) if
\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{\star,k}^{2}<\infty
, then
D_{N}
remains stable (uniformly bounded) with growing
N
.
Moreover, the accumulated divergence after a finite number of generations
N
admits an interpretable geometrically-discounted decomposition up to a bias term:

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{N+1}\|\,p_{\text{data}})+C_{\textrm{bias}}\;\asymp\;\sum_{k=0}^{N}\underbrace{(1-\alpha)^{2(N-k)}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Forgetting}\\
\text{(real data)}\end{subarray}}\cdot\hskip-5.0pt\underbrace{\varepsilon_{\star,k}^{2}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{ Accumulation}\\
\text{(synthetic data)}\end{subarray}},

which shows that errors from
m
generations in the past are suppressed by a factor
(1-\alpha)^{2m}
, quantifying the effect of
\alpha
, the proportion of fresh data used in each training round. Qualitatively, this is consistent with empirical and theoretical work in a variety of settings showing that incorporating fresh data in each training round can mitigate model collapse, e.g.
(
25
;
59
;
8
;
17
)
.

We also provide experiments on simulated data and images to illustrate the validity of our bounds. Our results are relevant in the regime where the energy of the path-wise score error (defined in (
10
)) is small for all but a few generations. This regime is of interest since we seek to quantify how the intra-generation and accumulated divergences evolve when the score is estimated with high accuracy in each generation. However, we note that the number of samples for the score error to be accurately estimate may grow exponentially in the dimension
d
(see (
12
)).

Background and Model Assumptions

Forward diffusion

To define the score-matching objective and the subsequent sampling procedure, we associate to each
q_{i}
a forward diffusion process on a truncated time interval
[t_{0},T]
, with
0<t_{0}<T
. We consider the following variance-preserving Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
(
34
;
49
)
:

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{i}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{i}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t},

(6)

where
(\mathbf{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. The process is initialized with
\mathbf{X}_{0}^{i}
drawn from a suitable distribution
q_{i,0}
.
Denote by
q_{i,t}:=\Law(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{i})
the time-
t
marginal of the forward process, with population score
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},t):=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q_{i,t}(\mathbf{x})
.

Reverse-time generation and learning

Under standard regularity conditions
(
3
;
28
)
, the time reversal of (
6
) solves the reverse-time SDE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star}=\big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star}-\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star},s)\big]\,\mathrm{d}s+\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{s},

(7)

integrated backward from
s=T
to
s=0
. Here
(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
is another Brownian motion on the same probability space. If we run the reverse SDE starting from
\mathbf{Y}_{T}^{i,\star}\sim q_{i,T}
, then
\mathbf{Y}_{0}^{i,\star}\sim q_{i,0}
.
In practice, the score
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}
is unknown and is approximated by a learned network
\mathbf{s}_{\theta_{i}}
trained on samples from
q_{i}
in (
1
). Moreover, since the score function is often ill-behaved and challenging to estimate as
t\to 0

(
48
;
35
)
, as done in practice we truncate the learned process at
s=t_{0}>0
. We choose
q_{i,0}
such that
q_{i,t_{0}}=q_{i}
. Plugging the learned score
\mathbf{s}_{\theta_{i}}
into the reverse dynamics yields the learned reverse process:

\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s}^{i}=\big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s}^{i}-\mathbf{s}_{\theta_{i}}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s}^{i},s)\big]\mathrm{d}s+\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{s},

(8)

with initialization
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
.
The next generation model is thus denoted
\hat{p}^{i+1}:=\Law(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{t_{0}}^{i})
. Finally, denote the corresponding path laws on the path-space
C([t_{0},T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}:=\Law\big((\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star})_{s\in[t_{0},T]}\big),\quad\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}:=\Law\big((\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s}^{i})_{s\in[t_{0},T]}\big),

so that the time-

marginals of the ideal and the learned reverse processes satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\sim q_{i}\ \text{ under }\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star},\ \text{ and }\ \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{t_{0}}^{i}\sim\hat{p}^{\,i+1}\ \text{under }\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}.

(9)

With some abuse of notation, we use
q_{i},\hat{p}^{i+1}
to both the laws and the densities with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

We define the pointwise score error

\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t):=\mathbf{s}_{\theta_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},t),

and its path-wise energies

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star},s)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right],

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}\!\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s}^{i},s)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right].

(11)

These quantities control the intra-generation divergence

defined in (

(

;

;

\mathcal{D}_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d}}{\sim}q_{i}

\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\;\lesssim\;\mathrm{polylog}(n_{i})\,n_{i}^{-1}\,(1/t_{0})^{d/2}.

(12)

Similar bounds, relying on different assumptions, have been derived in various works

Our results are relevant in the regime where

Our main goal is to quantify how the score estimation error (

Model Assumptions

The proportion

of fresh samples used for training is strictly positive.
We will assume

admits a density with respect to Lebesgue measure and the model distribution

is absolutely continuous with respect to

. This is a standard assumption in analyses of diffusion models

)
.
In addition to the score estimation error, there are two other sources of error in practice. One is due to the learned reverse process (
8
) being initialized with

\mathbf{Y}_{T}^{i,\star}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
rather than
rather than
\mathbf{Y}_{T}^{i,\star}\sim q_{T}
used for the ideal process in (

\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
converges to 0 exponentially fast in

We do not quantify these errors as our main goal is to obtain lower bounds on the intra-generation and accumulated errors. Obtaining error propagation bounds taking these additional sources of error into account is a direction for future work.

Intra-generation divergence bounds

We now analyze the
intra-generational
mechanism by which score estimation error in generation
i
translates into mismatch between the sampling output
\hat{p}^{\,i+1}
and the training distribution
q_{i}
in (

).
Our goal is to relate the one-step divergence
I_{i}
defined in (
4
)
to the pathwise score-error energies defined by (
10
)-(
11
).

Key Technical Ideas and Girsanov Quantities

To quantify how local score errors propagate to the final samples, we use a change-of-measure argument based on Girsanov’s theorem
(
26
;
37
)
. This is a key result in the theory of diffusion models that shows how the drift mismatch between the ideal and learned reverse-time processes (score error in our setting) controls the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the path-space measure
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
. Define the error martingale

\displaystyle M_{t}^{i}:=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star},s)\cdot\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{s},

(13)

and its quadratic variation

\displaystyle\langle M^{i}\rangle_{t}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star},s)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(14)

We also define
Z_{t}^{i}:=M_{t}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{t}
. Under mild and standard assumptions (
A1
and
A2
below), Girsanov’s theorem states that
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}\ll\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
and

\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}=\exp(Z_{T}^{i})
.
Crucially, the likelihood ratio of the terminal marginals
R_{i}:=\hat{p}^{i+1}/q_{i}
is obtained by projecting this path density onto the terminal state (Proposition
B.1
):

R_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}):=\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})}{q_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\Big[e^{Z_{T}^{i}}\;\Big|\;\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\Big].

(15)

This relation is central to our analysis as it translates functional score errors (captured by
Z_{T}^{i}
) into probabilistic drift. A key challenge in our analysis is obtain a good lower bound on the conditional expectation in (
15
). We begin with an upper bound that follows from a standard application of Girsanov’s theorem.

3.1
Upper Bound via Change of Measure

Assume there exists
i_{0}\geq 1
such that for all generations
i\geq i_{0}
, the following conditions hold.

(A1)

Finite drift energy along learned paths.

\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}\!\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s}^{i},s)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]<\infty.

(A2)

Martingale property of the Girsanov density.

The exponential
e^{Z_{T}^{i}}
associated with the drift difference between (
7
) and (
8
) defines a true martingale on
[t_{0},T]
so that the Girsanov change of measure holds. (A more precise version in stated in Appendix
B.1
.)

Assumptions
A1
-
A2
impose no restriction on the form of the score error beyond finite quadratic energy and are standard in diffusion theory. They yield the following well-known upper bound relating sampling error to the pathwise score-estimation energy
(
12
;
14
;
7
)
.

Proposition 3.1

(Intra-generational upper bound)
.

Under
A1
–
A2
,
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}\ll\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
and, by data processing inequality (marginalization to time

),

\mathrm{KL}(\hat{p}^{\,i+1}\|q_{i})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}\|\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}.

(16)

For completeness, we give the proof in Appendix

.

3.2
Lower Bound Via Error Observability

The upper bound of Proposition
3.1
measures the entire path-space score error. Obtaining a divergence lower bound is more challenging as we have to to work with the marginal likelihood ratio given by (
15
).

First Challenge: Observability of Errors

It is possible that the path-space score error
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}>0
, but
\mathrm{KL}(\hat{p}^{\,i+1}\|q_{i})=0
, i.e., a non-zero path-wise error may leave no trace at the end point
t_{0}
if it is orthogonal (in
L^{2}
) to all observables at
t_{0}
. This phenomenon is intrinsic, so we need an observability condition linking path-wise perturbations to the endpoint. We quantify the visibility of score errors at
t_{0}
through the following coefficient.

Definition 3.2

(Observability of Errors Coefficient)
.

Recalling the definition of
M_{T}^{i}
in (
13
),

\eta_{i}:=\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\big(\mathbb{E}[M_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\big)}{\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}}\;\in\;[0,1],

(17)

with the convention
\eta_{i}:=0
if
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}=0
. The denominator in (
17
) equals
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}(M_{T}^{i})
, by Itô isometry
(
37
)
.

The leading term in our divergence lower bound (Proposition
3.3
) is proportional to
\eta_{i}
. Hence the bound is informative whenever
\eta_{i}>0
.

When is
\eta_{i}>0
in practice?
By definition,

\eta_{i}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\big[M_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\big]=0\ \ \mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}\text{-a.s.},

i.e.,
\eta_{i}=0
when the drift-mismatch martingale
M_{T}^{i}
is
conditionally orthogonal
to all terminal observables. This cancellation is highly non-generic: in common parametric score models (e.g. neural networks with smooth activations (e.g. SiLU, GeLU)), due to random initialization and stochastic optimization noise we expect
\eta_{i}>0
.
The experiments discussed in Appendix
G
suggest that in realistic data-driven setups,
\eta_{i}>0
holds across generations; see Figure
7
(10-dimensional Gaussian Mixture) and Figure
9
(CIFAR-10 dataset). More generally, a simple and practically relevant sufficient condition is
state dependence
of the score perturbation function
\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)
:
time-only (or purely path-orthogonal) perturbations can be averaged out by the conditional expectation, whereas perturbations coupled to the sample state
\mathbf{x}
typically leave a detectable imprint on the endpoint. In particular, any nontrivial state-dependent affine component yields observability: if
\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{w}\,\mathbf{x}+\xi(t)
with
\mathbf{w}\neq 0
and
\xi
independent of
\mathbf{x}
, then
\eta_{i}>0
.
This dichotomy is illustrated empirically in Figure
2

Error Accumulation Across Generations

Theorem

shows that when the score error is observable (i.e.,

A key observation is that the fresh samples introduced in each generation contracts the accumulated divergence. Indeed, a simple calculation (Lemma

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}_{i}\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{data}})\;=\;(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i}\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{data}})

as

\sum_{i\geq 0}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}=\infty

D_{i}
and cannot converge to

\sum_{i\geq 0}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}<\infty

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We analyzed recursive training of diffusion models with a fixed proportion
\alpha>0
of fresh data in each training round. We derived lower and upper bounds on the intra-generation and accumulated divergences, in terms of the path-wise energy of the score error in each generation. An essential component of our lower bound is the observability coefficient in (
17
), which quantifies the fraction of the path-wise error energy that manifests in the learned distribution.
The paper focuses on the setting where the score error energy in each generation is small. An important direction for future work is to establish bounds in the large error regime. Obtaining a lower bound in this regime, even for one generation, is challenging because the ratio between the learned and ideal densities (given by the projection of the path-wise ratio in (
15
)) cannot be linearized with reasonable control of the remainder term. Another direction for further work is to taking into account the discretization error due to the diffusion being implemented in discrete-time. Finally, a key open question is: is there is a limiting distribution to which the model converges when recursively trained, and if so, how does it depend on
\alpha
and
p_{\mathrm{data}}
?

(resp.
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{\,i}

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}

\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{i}

T

\mathbf{s}_{\theta_{i}}

\hat{p}^{i+1}

The drift discrepancy is given by the score error:

\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t):=\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},t)

A1

Finite drift energy.
The learned path measure satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s},s)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s]<\infty

A2

Martingale property.

From (
13

s\in[t_{0},T]

M_{s}^{i}:=-\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{u}^{i,\star},u)\cdot\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{u},\qquad\langle M^{i}\rangle_{s}:=\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{u}^{i,\star},u)\|^{2}_{2}\mathrm{d}u,\qquad Z_{s}^{i}=M_{s}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{s}.

Discussion on Assumptions
A1
and
A2
.

These assumptions ensures that the Girsanov transformation yields a valid probability measure (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[U_{T}]=1
).
While
U_{s}
is guaranteed to be a local martingale by construction, different sufficient conditions can be found in the literature to guarantee the full martingale status. The most common ones are:

•

Novikov’s condition

(
39
)
:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star},s)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]<\infty.

•

Beneš Condition
(
6
)
: the drift discrepancy satisfies a linear growth bound

\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)\|_{2}\leq C(1+\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}),\qquad C>0.

In the context of diffusion models parameterized by neural networks, this holds if the network weights are finite and the activation functions are Lipschitz (e.g., ReLU, SiLU) or bounded (e.g., Tanh, Sigmoid), provided the domain is effectively bounded or the growth is controlled.

•

Stopping Time Condition.
Finally, another popular assumption relies on stopping times. Formally, the exponential process
(U_{s})_{s\in[t_{0},T]}
is always a
local martingale
. This implies the existence of a non-decreasing sequence of stopping times
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
such that
\tau_{n}\uparrow T
almost surely as

n

(U_{s\wedge\tau_{n}})_{s\in[t_{0},T]}

\tau_{n}:=\inf\left\{s\in[t_{0},T]:\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{u}^{i,\star},u)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\geq n\right\}\wedge T.

(24)

By definition, the accumulated energy up to time
\tau_{n}
is bounded by
n
. Consequently, the conditional Novikov criterion is trivially satisfied for the stopped process:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau_{n}}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{i,\star},s)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\right]\leq e^{n/2}<\infty.

This guarantees that
\mathbb{E}[U_{\tau_{n}}]=1
for all
n

A2
is therefore equivalent to the condition that the sequence is uniformly integrable, allowing the equality to hold in the limit:
\mathbb{E}[U_{T}]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[U_{\tau_{n}}]=1
. This stopping time condition has been used in previous analyses of the convergence properties of diffusion models
(
14
;
7
)
.

,
U_{s}
is a nonnegative local martingale under
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
and hence a
supermartingale. By Assumption
A2
,
(U_{s})_{s\in[t_{0},T]}
is in fact
a (uniformly integrable) martingale, so in particular
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[U_{T}]=U_{t_{0}}=1
.
Therefore, we can define the probability measure
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
by the Radon-Nikodym derivative:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}((\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,i})_{s\in[t_{0},T]})=U_{T}=\exp\left(-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,i},s)\cdot\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,i},s)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Under this new measure
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
, the process
\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s}:=\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{s}+\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{u},u)\mathrm{d}u
is a standard Brownian motion (by the Girsanov theorem), and the coordinate process
\mathbf{Y}
satisfies the SDE of the learned diffusion
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{i}
. Thus,
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
is precisely the law of the learned process.

Step 2: KL Divergence Calculation.

The Kullback-Leibler divergence is defined as
\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}\|\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star})=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}[\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}]
.
We express the log-density ratio in terms of the process
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}
(governed by
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
). Under
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
, we can write
\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{s}=\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s}-\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s},s)\mathrm{d}s
, where
\hat{\mathbf{B}}
is a standard Brownian motion under
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
.
Substituting this into the log-likelihood ratio:

\displaystyle\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}((\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s})_{s\in[t_{0},T]})

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s},s)\cdot\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s},s)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot(\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s}-\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathrm{d}s)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s},s)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s},s)\cdot\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s},s)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Taking expectation under
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
:

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}\left[\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}\left[-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{s},s)\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right].

The first term is the expectation of a stochastic integral. Under Assumption
A1
, the integrand is square-integrable, so the stochastic integral is a true martingale starting at 0, and its expectation vanishes. The second term is exactly
\frac{1}{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}
.

Step 3: Marginalization (Data Processing).

Let
\pi_{t_{0}}:C([t_{0},T])\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the projection map
\omega\mapsto\omega(t_{0})
. The marginal distributions are push-forwards:
\hat{p}^{i+1}=(\pi_{t_{0}})_{\#}\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}
and
q_{i}=(\pi_{t_{0}})_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
.
By the data processing inequality (contraction of KL under push-forward):

\mathrm{KL}(\hat{p}^{i+1}\|q_{i})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}\|\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star})=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}.

∎

B.2
Relating Marginals via Girsanov’s Theorem

Proposition B.1

(Marginal Ratio Representation)
.

Under Assumptions
A1
and
A2
, the Radon-Nikodym derivative between the path measures is given by
e^{Z_{T}^{i}}
, where
Z_{T}^{i}=M_{T}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}
. Consequently, the marginal likelihood ratio satisfies:

R_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})\;:=\;\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})}{q_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\Big[e^{Z_{T}^{i}}\;\Big|\;\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\Big],\quad q_{i}\text{-almost everywhere.}

(25)

Proof.

As shown in Appendix
B.1
, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\;=\;\exp\left(Z_{T}^{i}\right)
.
Next, we restrict this relation to the marginals at time
t_{0}
. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be any bounded measurable test function acting on the data space. We compute the expectation of
f
under the generated distribution
\hat{p}^{i+1}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\hat{p}^{i+1}}[f(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}[f(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{t_{0}}^{i})]

(Definition of marginal)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[f(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\right]

(Change of measure)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[f(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})\,e^{Z_{T}^{i}}\right]

\displaystyle\text{(Definition of }Z_{T}^{i}\text{)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[f(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[e^{Z_{T}^{i}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right]

\displaystyle\text{(Tower property)}.

Comparing the first and last lines, we identify
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[e^{Z_{T}^{i}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]
as the density ratio
\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}}{q_{i}}
.
∎

Appendix C
Moment Control and Observability Lemmas

Proposition
B.1
expresses the density ratio as the conditional expectation of
\exp(Z_{T}^{i})
given
\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}
, where
Z_{T}^{i}=M_{T}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}
. The score error functional
Z_{T}^{i}
is defined on the entire path, but only its projection onto the terminal state
\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}
affects the marginal divergence.
The first lemma in this section lower bounds the second moment of this projection.

Lemma C.1

(Observability of Errors Transfer)
.

Assume
A1
and
A4
. Assume
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}<\infty
. For any
\theta\in(0,1)
, the conditional expectation of the pathwise error satisfies the lower bound:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[Z^{i}_{T}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]^{2}\Big]\;\geq\;(1-\theta)\,\eta_{i}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\;-\;\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}

(26)

Proof.

Recall the definition
Z_{T}^{i}=M_{T}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}
. We condition on the terminal state
\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}
under the ideal measure
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
. Using Young’s weighted inequality for any
\theta\in(0,1)
,
(a-b)^{2}\geq(1-\theta)a^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)b^{2}
, we have:

\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[Z_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\;\geq\;(1-\theta)\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[M_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\;-\;\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}.

Taking expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
yields:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[Z_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\right]\;\geq\;(1-\theta)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[M_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\right]}_{\text{(Signal)}}\;-\;\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\right]}_{\text{(Noise)}}.

(27)

Analysis of the Signal Term:

Since
M^{i}
is an Itô integral with square integrable adapted integrand (
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}<\infty
by assumption) it is a martingale with
M_{t_{0}}^{i}=0
hence
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[M_{T}^{i}]=0
. By the tower property of conditional expectation:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[M_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[M_{T}^{i}]\;=\;0.

Thus, the second moment of the conditional expectation is equal to its variance:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[M_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\right]\;=\;\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[M_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right).

By Definition
3.2
, the observability coefficient is
\eta_{i}:=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[M\mid Y])/\mathbb{E}[\langle M\rangle]
. Therefore:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[M_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\right]\;=\;\eta_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}]\;=\;\eta_{i}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}.

Analysis of Noise Term:

Applying Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectation,

\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\right].

Taking the outer expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}
and using the tower property:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right)^{2}\right]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\right]

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2+\gamma}]^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4},\quad\text{by }\ref{ass:quadratic-variation-concentration}

Substituting the results for these two terms back into (
27
) gives the result.
∎

The next lemma controls the second and fourth moments of
Z_{T}^{i}
.

Lemma C.2

(Second and Fourth moment bound for the Girsanov log-density)
.

Assume
A4
. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[(Z_{T}^{i})^{2}]\leq 2\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

(28)

Moreover, in the regime where
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\leq 1
there exists a constant
C_{Z4}<\infty
such that, uniformly in
i\geq i_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[Z_{T}^{i\,4}\big]\;\leq\;C_{Z4}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

(29)

Proof.

Second Moment Bound.

Using
(a-b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
on
Z_{T}^{i}=M_{T}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}
, we have:

(Z_{T}^{i})^{2}\leq 2(M_{T}^{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2}.

Taking expectations under
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
and applying Itô’s isometry (
\mathbb{E}[(M_{T}^{i})^{2}]=\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}
) and Assumption
A4
:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[(Z_{T}^{i})^{2}]\leq 2\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

(30)

Fourth Moment Bound.

Recall that
Z_{T}^{i}=M_{T}^{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}
. Using the elementary inequality
(a-b)^{4}\;\leq\;8a^{4}+8b^{4}
we obtain

Z_{T}^{i\,4}\;\leq\;8\,|M_{T}^{i}|^{4}\;+\;8\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\Big)^{4}\;=\;8\,|M_{T}^{i}|^{4}\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{4}.

(31)

By the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality
(
10
;
41
)
for continuous martingales with exponent
p=4
, there exists a constant
C_{4}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[|M_{T}^{i}|^{4}\big]\;\leq\;C_{4}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2}\big]\

By Assumption
A4
for
\gamma>\max\{2,\frac{4}{\delta-1}\}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2}\big]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2+\gamma}\big]^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\;\leq\;K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4},

hence

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[|M_{T}^{i}|^{4}\big]\;\leq\;C\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

(32)

Similarly, by Assumption
A4
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{4}\big]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2+\gamma}\big]^{\tfrac{4}{2+\gamma}}\;\leq\;K_{\gamma}^{\tfrac{4}{2+\gamma}}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{8}

Thus,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}]\leq 8K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}+\frac{K_{\gamma}^{\tfrac{4}{2+\gamma}}}{2}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{8}

Observing that, whenever
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\leq 1
, one has that
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{8}\leq\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}
, yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\big[(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}\big]\;\leq\;C\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4},

(33)

which proves the claim.
∎

Appendix D
Proof of The Intra-Generational Lower Bound (Proposition
3.3
)

From Proposition
B.1
, recall that the ratio
\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}}{q_{i}}
is given by

R_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})\;:=\;\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})}{q_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\Big[e^{Z_{T}^{i}}\;\Big|\;\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\Big],\quad q_{i}\text{-almost everywhere.}

To obtain a lower bound, we write
e^{Z_{T}^{i}}=1+Z_{T}^{i}+\psi(Z_{T}^{i})
and control the remainder term
\psi(Z_{T}^{i})
under the assumptions of the proposition. To this end, we will use two lemmas proved in Appendix
C
. The first, Lemma
C.1
controls the second moment of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\Big[Z_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\Big]
. The second, Lemma
C.2
, controls the second and fourth moments of
Z_{T}^{i}
.

We will prove the following version of Proposition
3.3
with explicit constants.

Proposition D.1

(Intra-generational lower bound with explicit constants)
.

Assume
A1
–
A4
, with associated parameters
(\gamma,K_{\gamma})
and
(\delta,C_{\delta})
. Let
C_{\mathrm{BDG}}(r)
denote a Burkholder–Davis–Gundy
(
10
;
41
)
constant such that for any continuous martingale
M
,

\mathbb{E}\!\big[|M_{T}|^{r}\big]\leq C_{\mathrm{BDG}}(r)\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\langle M\rangle_{T}^{r/2}\big].

(34)

Then, for every generation
i\geq i_{0}
satisfying the perturbative condition
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\leq 1
, one has

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{\,i+1}\|q_{i})\;\geq\;\frac{1}{4}\,\eta_{i}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\;-\;C\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4},

(35)

with the explicit choices

C:=\frac{K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}}{8}\;+\;\frac{C_{Z4}}{4}\;+\;\frac{1}{4}\,\Big(C_{\mathrm{BDG}}(4p^{\prime})\,K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2p^{\prime}}{2+\gamma}}\Big)^{\!1/p^{\prime}}\,C_{\delta}^{1/p},

(36)

where
p=\frac{1+\delta}{2}
and
p^{\prime}=\frac{\delta+1}{\delta-1}
.
In particular, defining the explicit perturbative threshold

\kappa_{i}:=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\eta_{i}}{8C}\Big\},

(37)

whenever
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\leq\kappa_{i}
we obtain the clean lower bound

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{\,i+1}\|q_{i})\;\geq\;\frac{c}{2}\,\eta_{i}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}=\frac{1}{8}\,\eta_{i}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}.

(38)

Proof.

The proof proceeds in six steps: we expand the density ratio using Taylor’s theorem, apply Young’s inequality to isolate the leading-order term, invoke the observability lemma for the signal, control the remainder using exponential integrability, and combine all bounds.

1. Exponential expansion with controlled remainder.

Define the remainder function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\psi(z):=e^{z}-1-z=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}.

(39)

From Proposition
B.1
(Marginal Ratio Representation), we have

R_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})=\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})}{q_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star})}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\bigl[e^{Z_{T}^{i}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\bigr].

(40)

Applying the decomposition
e^{Z_{T}^{i}}=1+Z_{T}^{i}+\psi(Z_{T}^{i})
and taking conditional expectations:

\displaystyle R_{i}-1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[e^{Z_{T}^{i}}-1\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[Z_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\psi(Z_{T}^{i})\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}].

We introduce the notation:

\displaystyle\bar{Z}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[Z_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\quad\text{(conditional mean of log-likelihood ratio)},

(41)

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\psi(Z_{T}^{i})\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\quad\text{(conditional mean of remainder)}.

(42)

Thus we have the fundamental decomposition:

R_{i}-1=\bar{Z}_{i}+\Psi_{i}.

(43)

2. Quadratic lower bound via Young’s inequality.

For any
\nu\in(0,1)
, we apply the weighted Young’s inequality in the form
(a+b)^{2}\geq(1-\nu)a^{2}-(\frac{1}{\nu}-1)b^{2}
:

(R_{i}-1)^{2}=(\bar{Z}_{i}+\Psi_{i})^{2}\geq(1-\nu)\bar{Z}_{i}^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{\nu}-1\right)\Psi_{i}^{2}.

(44)

Taking expectations with respect to
q_{i}
(equivalently, with respect to
\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}
under
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
):

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i+1}\|q_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}}[(R_{i}-1)^{2}]\geq(1-\nu)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}}[\bar{Z}_{i}^{2}]}_{\mathrm{Signal}}-\left(\frac{1}{\nu}-1\right)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}}[\Psi_{i}^{2}]}_{\mathrm{Remainder}}.

(45)

We proceed to bound each term separately.

3. The signal term—applying observability.

Since
\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}\sim q_{i}
under
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}}[\bar{Z}_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\left[\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[Z_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\bigr)^{2}\right].

(46)

Invoking Lemma
C.1
, for any
\theta\in(0,1)
:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\left[\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[Z_{T}^{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}]\bigr)^{2}\right]\geq(1-\theta)\,\eta_{i}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

(47)

For the noise term: by Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectations,

\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}]\bigr)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}].

(48)

Taking outer expectations and applying the tower property:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}]\bigr)^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2}]\leq K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4},

(49)

where the final inequality uses Assumption
A4
.

4. The remainder term—exponential moment control.

We bound
\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}}[\Psi_{i}^{2}]
using the exponential integrability condition (Assumption
A3
). By Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectations (since
x\mapsto x^{2}
is convex):

\Psi_{i}^{2}=\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z_{T}^{i})\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}]\bigr)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z_{T}^{i})^{2}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}].

(50)

Taking expectations over
\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}\sim q_{i}
and applying the tower property:

\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}}[\Psi_{i}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\psi(Z_{T}^{i})^{2}].

(51)

Observe that, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the bound

0\leq\psi(z)=e^{z}-1-z\leq\frac{z^{2}}{2}e^{z_{+}},\quad z_{+}:=\mathrm{max}(z,0),

(52)

Using the bound (
52
):

\psi(Z_{T}^{i})^{2}\leq\frac{(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}}{4}e^{2(Z_{T}^{i})_{+}}=\frac{(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}}{4}\Big(\mathbf{1}_{Z_{T}^{i}\leq 0}+e^{2Z_{T}^{i}}\mathbf{1}_{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0}\Big)

(53)

5. Controlling the exponential factor (with only
2p^{\prime}\leq 2+\gamma
).

From (
52
), it remains to control the “positive” contribution

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\Big[(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}e^{2Z_{T}^{i}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0\}}\Big].

Define the Hölder conjugates
p:=\frac{1+\delta}{2}
and
p^{\prime}:=\frac{\delta+1}{\delta-1}
that are so that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. Hölder’s inequality yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\Big[(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}e^{2Z_{T}^{i}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0\}}\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\Big[|Z_{T}^{i}|^{4p^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0\}}\Big]^{1/p^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\Big[e^{2pZ_{T}^{i}}\Big]^{1/p}.

(54)

Since
2p=1+\delta
, Assumption
A3
gives

\sup_{i\geq i_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\big[e^{2pZ_{T}^{i}}\big]=\sup_{i\geq i_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\big[e^{(1+\delta)Z_{T}^{i}}\big]\leq C_{\delta}.

(55)

On the other hand, recall
Z_{T}^{i}=M_{T}^{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}
so that, on the event
\{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0\}
, we have
M_{T}^{i}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}
i.e.
\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\leq 2M_{T}^{i}
hence also

0\leq Z_{T}^{i}=M_{T}^{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}\leq M_{T}^{i},\qquad\text{on }\{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0\}.

Therefore,

|Z_{T}^{i}|^{4p^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0\}}\leq|M_{T}^{i}|^{4p^{\prime}}.

(56)

By the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality (
34
) with
r=4p^{\prime}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\big[|M_{T}^{i}|^{4p^{\prime}}\big]\leq C_{\mathrm{BDG}}(4p^{\prime})\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\big[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2p^{\prime}}\big].

Since, by definition of
\rho
and
\gamma
in Assumption
A4
, one has that
2p^{\prime}\leq 2+\gamma
, Lyapunov monotonicity yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\big[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2p^{\prime}}\big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\big[\langle M^{i}\rangle_{T}^{2+\gamma}\big]^{\frac{2p^{\prime}}{2+\gamma}}\leq K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2p^{\prime}}{2+\gamma}}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4p^{\prime}}.

Combining with (
56
) gives

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\Big[|Z_{T}^{i}|^{4p^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0\}}\Big]\leq C_{\mathrm{BDG}}(4p^{\prime})\,K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2p^{\prime}}{2+\gamma}}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4p^{\prime}}.

(57)

Plugging (
55
) and (
57
) into (
54
) yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}\!\Big[(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}e^{2Z_{T}^{i}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{T}^{i}\geq 0\}}\Big]\leq\Big(C_{\mathrm{BDG}}(4p^{\prime})\,K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2p^{\prime}}{2+\gamma}}\Big)^{1/p^{\prime}}\,C_{\delta}^{1/p}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

Returning to (
52
) and using also
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{T}^{i}\leq 0\}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(Z_{T}^{i})^{4}]\leq C_{Z4}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}
(Lemma
C.2
),
we conclude that for some constant
C^{\prime}
depending only on
(\delta,C_{\delta},\gamma,K_{\gamma})
,

\mathbb{E}_{q_{i}}[\Psi_{i}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\star}}[\psi(Z_{T}^{i})^{2}]\leq C^{\prime}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4},

(58)

with an admissible explicit choice

C^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{4}\,C_{Z4}+\frac{1}{4}\Big(C_{\mathrm{BDG}}(4p^{\prime})\,K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2p^{\prime}}{2+\gamma}}\Big)^{1/p^{\prime}}\,C_{\delta}^{1/p}.

5. Combining all bounds.

Substituting (
47
) and (
58
) into (
45
):

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i+1}\|q_{i})

\displaystyle\geq(1-\nu)\left[(1-\theta)\eta_{i}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}\right]-\left(\frac{1}{\nu}-1\right)C^{\prime}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

(59)

Expanding and collecting terms:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i+1}\|q_{i})

\displaystyle\geq(1-\nu)(1-\theta)\eta_{i}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}-\left[\frac{1}{4}(1-\nu)\left(\frac{1}{\theta}-1\right)K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+\left(\frac{1}{\nu}-1\right)C^{\prime}\right]\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

We choose
\nu=\theta=\frac{1}{2}
to balance the terms:

•

Leading coefficient:
(1-\frac{1}{2})(1-\frac{1}{2})=\frac{1}{4}

•

First error coefficient:
\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot(2-1)\cdot K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}=\frac{1}{8}K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}

•

Second error coefficient:
(2-1)\cdot C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}

Thus:

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i+1}\|q_{i})\geq\frac{1}{4}\eta_{i}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}-\left[\frac{1}{8}K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+C^{\prime}\right]\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

(60)

Setting

C:=\frac{1}{8}K_{\gamma}^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+C^{\prime},

(61)

we obtain the desired bound:

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i+1}\|q_{i})\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\eta_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}-C\cdot\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}.

(62)

6. Clean form in the perturbative regime.

When the error is sufficiently small, specifically when

\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\leq\frac{c\cdot\eta_{i}}{2C}=\frac{\eta_{i}}{8C},

(63)

the quartic error term satisfies
C\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{4}\leq\frac{c}{2}\eta_{i}\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}
. Therefore:

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i+1}\|q_{i})\geq c\cdot\eta_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}-\frac{c}{2}\cdot\eta_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}=\frac{c}{2}\cdot\eta_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}.

(64)

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark D.2

The constants

Proof of Theorem

Under Assumptions

-

58

A3
and
A4

∎

F.1
Preliminary Results

We first quantify how much the discrepancy contracts purely due to mixing with fresh data (the “refresh” step).

Lemma F.1

(Exact Contraction)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the mixture
q_{i}
satisfies:

\chi^{2}(q_{i}\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{data}})\;=\;(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i}\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{data}})

(74)

Proof.

Pointwise, the density ratio satisfies:

\frac{q_{i}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}}-1\;=\;\alpha+(1-\alpha)\frac{\hat{p}^{i}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}}-1\;=\;(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{\hat{p}^{i}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}}-1\right).

Squaring both sides and integrating with respect to
p_{\mathrm{data}}
yields the result.
∎

Next, we show that the innovation error
I_{i}
cannot be completely hidden by the contraction.

Lemma F.2

(Lower Bound Recursion)
.

D_{i+1}+(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{i}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha}{2}I_{i}.

(75)

Proof.

We decompose the likelihood ratio of the next generation
\hat{p}^{i+1}
against the target
p_{\mathrm{data}}
:

\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}}-1\;=\;\underbrace{\left(\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}}{q_{i}}-1\right)\frac{q_{i}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}}}_{=:A}\;+\;\underbrace{\left(\frac{q_{i}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}}-1\right)}_{=:B}.

Then
D_{i+1}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[(A+B)^{2}]
. Using the algebraic inequality
(A+B)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}A^{2}-B^{2}
:

D_{i+1}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[A^{2}]\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[B^{2}].

1.
Term B:
By Lemma
F.1
,
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[B^{2}]=\chi^{2}(q_{i}\|p_{\mathrm{data}})=(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{i}
.

2.
Term A:
We have:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[A^{2}]=\int\left(\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}}{q_{i}}-1\right)^{2}\frac{q_{i}^{2}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}^{2}}p_{\mathrm{data}}=\int\left(\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}}{q_{i}}-1\right)^{2}\frac{q_{i}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}}q_{i}.

Since
q_{i}=\alpha p_{\mathrm{data}}+(1-\alpha)\hat{p}^{i}\geq\alpha p_{\mathrm{data}}
, we have
q_{i}/p_{\mathrm{data}}\geq\alpha
. Thus:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[A^{2}]\;\geq\;\alpha\int\left(\frac{\hat{p}^{i+1}}{q_{i}}-1\right)^{2}q_{i}\;=\;\alpha I_{i}.

Substituting these back yields
D_{i+1}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}I_{i}-(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{i}
. Rearranging gives the result.
∎

(Reverse Hölder bound for
R_{i}
)
.

Assume
A3
with
\delta>1
. Recall
q_{i}:=\alpha p_{\mathrm{data}}+(1-\alpha)\hat{p}^{\,i}

Proof.

By the marginal projection identity (

and
\zeta\in(0,1)
and take
\zeta_{i}\equiv\zeta
in
Proposition
F.5
. Its proof (see (
100
)) yields

\|A_{i}\|_{L^{2}(p_{\mathrm{data}})}^{2}\leq B_{i}\Big(I_{i}+2^{\delta}\rho^{-(\delta-1)}(C_{\delta}+1)\Big)+\rho^{2}\bigl(1+(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{i}\bigr)+K_{\delta}\,\zeta^{\frac{\delta-1}{1+\delta}},

(115)

where

B_{i}=\alpha+(1-\alpha)\Bigl(1+\sqrt{D_{i}/\zeta}\Bigr).

Under (
109
), we have the uniform bound

B_{i}\leq\alpha+(1-\alpha)\Bigl(1+\sqrt{D_{\max}/\zeta}\Bigr)=:B_{\max}<\infty,\qquad i\geq i_{0},

and also
\rho^{2}(1+(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{i})\leq\rho^{2}(1+(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{\max})
.
Therefore, for all
i\geq i_{0}
,

\|A_{i}\|_{L^{2}(p_{\mathrm{data}})}^{2}\leq\bar{C}_{A}\,I_{i}+\bar{c}_{A},

(116)

with

\bar{C}_{A}:=B_{\max},\qquad\bar{c}_{A}:=B_{\max}\,2^{\delta}\rho^{-(\delta-1)}(C_{\delta}+1)+\rho^{2}\bigl(1+(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{\max}\bigr)+K_{\delta}\,\zeta^{\frac{\delta-1}{1+\delta}}.

Step 2: Replace
I_{i}
by
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}
.

By Theorem
3.4
in the perturbative regime
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\leq\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\eta_{i}}{8C}\Big\}
,

\frac{1}{8}\underline{\eta}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\leq I_{i}\leq 4\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2},\qquad i\geq i_{0}.

Combining with (
114
)–(
116
) gives, for all
i\geq i_{0}
,

\frac{\alpha}{8}\underline{\eta}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}\leq\|A_{i}\|_{L^{2}(p_{\mathrm{data}})}^{2}\leq 4\,\bar{C}_{A}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}+\bar{c}_{A}.

(117)

Step 3: Discounted upper bound (with bias).

From (
113
), (
117
), and recalling
\|T_{i}-1\|_{L^{2}(p_{\mathrm{data}})}=(1-\alpha)\sqrt{D_{i}}
,

\sqrt{D_{i+1}}\leq(1-\alpha)\sqrt{D_{i}}+2\sqrt{\bar{C}_{A}}\,\varepsilon_{\star,i}+\sqrt{\bar{c}_{A}}.

Iterating for
N\geq i_{0}
yields

\sqrt{D_{N}}\leq(1-\alpha)^{N-i_{0}}\sqrt{D_{i_{0}}}+2\sqrt{\bar{C}_{A}}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{N-1}(1-\alpha)^{N-1-i}\varepsilon_{\star,i}+\sqrt{\bar{c}_{A}}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{N-1}(1-\alpha)^{N-1-i}.

The geometric sum is bounded by
\alpha^{-1}
, and by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{N-1}(1-\alpha)^{N-1-i}\varepsilon_{\star,i}\leq S_{N}^{1/2},

where
S_{N}
is defined in (
110
).
Hence,

\sqrt{D_{N}}\leq(1-\alpha)^{N-i_{0}}\sqrt{D_{i_{0}}}+2\sqrt{\bar{C}_{A}}\,S_{N}^{1/2}+\frac{\sqrt{\bar{c}_{A}}}{\alpha}.

Squaring and using
(u+v+w)^{2}\leq 3(u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2})
gives

D_{N}\leq 3(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}+12\bar{C}_{A}\,S_{N}+\frac{3\bar{c}_{A}}{\alpha^{2}}.

Now, using that
\frac{3\bar{c}_{A}}{\alpha^{2}}\leq\max\{D_{\max}\left(1+\frac{2(1-\alpha)^{2}}{1-(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right),\frac{3\bar{c}_{A}}{\alpha^{2}}\}
, we obtain the upper bound in (
111
),

D_{N}\leq 3(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}+12\bar{C}_{A}\,S_{N}+\max\{D_{\max}\left(1+\frac{2(1-\alpha)^{2}}{1-(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right),\frac{3\bar{c}_{A}}{\alpha^{2}}\}.

Discounted Lower Bound.

We establish the lower bound via induction on
N
.

Claim:
For all
N\geq i_{0}+1
, the following inequality holds:

D_{N}+(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}+2\sum_{k=i_{0}+1}^{N-1}(1-\alpha)^{2(N-k)}D_{k}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}S_{N}.

(118)

Base Case (
N=i_{0}+1
):

For
N=i_{0}+1
, the sum is empty and
S_{i_{0}+1}=\varepsilon_{\star,i_{0}}^{2}
. Lemma
F.2
alongside Theorem
3.4
yields:

D_{i_{0}+1}+\gamma D_{i_{0}}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha}{2}I_{i_{0}}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}\varepsilon_{\star,i_{0}}^{2}\;=\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}S_{i_{0}+1},

which proves the base case.

Inductive Step:

Assume the claim holds for some
N\geq i_{0}+1
. Multiply the assumed inequality (
118
) by
(1-\alpha)^{2}
:

(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{N}+(1-\alpha)^{2(N+1-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}+2\sum_{k=i_{0}+1}^{N-1}(1-\alpha)^{2(N+1-k)}D_{k}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}(1-\alpha)^{2}S_{N}.

(119)

From Lemma
F.2
, the single-step recurrence at generation
N
gives:

D_{N+1}+(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{N}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}\varepsilon_{\star,N}^{2}.

(120)

Adding (
119
) and (
120
) together yields:

D_{N+1}+2(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{N}+(1-\alpha)^{2(N+1-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}+2\sum_{k=i_{0}+1}^{N-1}(1-\alpha)^{2(N+1-k)}D_{k}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}(\varepsilon_{\star,N}^{2}+(1-\alpha)^{2}S_{N}).

By definition,
\varepsilon_{\star,N}^{2}+(1-\alpha)^{2}S_{N}=S_{N+1}
. Moreover, the term
2(1-\alpha)^{2}D_{N}
perfectly absorbs into the summation, extending its upper limit to
N
:

D_{N+1}+(1-\alpha)^{2(N+1-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}+2\sum_{k=i_{0}+1}^{N}(1-\alpha)^{2(N+1-k)}D_{k}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}S_{N+1}.

This completes the induction.

Isolating
D_{N}
via Uniform Boundedness:

To isolate
D_{N}
, we must bound the accumulated history on the left side of (
118
). From (
109
), we know that
\sup_{i\geq i_{0}}D_{i}\leq D_{\max}
. Consequently, the summation is bounded by a geometric series:

2\sum_{k=i_{0}+1}^{N-1}(1-\alpha)^{2(N-k)}D_{k}\;\leq\;2D_{\max}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(1-\alpha)^{2j}\;=\;2D_{\max}\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{1-(1-\alpha)^{2}}.

We can rewrite the left side of (
118
) to match the target format by extracting
\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}
:

D_{N}+(1-\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16})(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}+2\sum_{k=i_{0}+1}^{N-1}(1-\alpha)^{2(N-k)}D_{k}+\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}S_{N}.

Substituting the geometric upper bound and bounding
(1-\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16})(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}\leq D_{\max}
yields:

D_{N}+D_{\max}\left(1+\frac{2(1-\alpha)^{2}}{1-(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right)\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}\Big(S_{N}-(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}\Big).

Taking
C_{\mathrm{bias}}=\max\{D_{\max}\left(1+\frac{2(1-\alpha)^{2}}{1-(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right),\frac{3\bar{c}_{A}}{\alpha^{2}}\}
yields the left-hand inequality of (
111
):

D_{N}+C_{\mathrm{bias}}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha\underline{\eta}}{16}\Big(S_{N}-(1-\alpha)^{2(N-i_{0})}D_{i_{0}}\Big).

G.1
First Experimental Setup: 10-dimensional Gaussian Mixture

Ground-truth distribution.

We consider a mixture of five isotropic Gaussians in
\mathbb{R}^{10}
:

p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{5}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{10}),

(121)

where the means
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{5}
are placed at the origin
(0,0)
and at
((-4,-4),(-4,4),(4,-4),(4,4))
and
\sigma=0.6
. This configuration creates a well-separated mixture with analytically tractable score functions.

Exact Gaussian closure for GMM data.

Contrary to realistic data distributions (e.g. images), the Gaussian initialisation for
p_{\text{data}}
in this experiment is motivated by the close-form expressions it yields:

•

Explicit score expression.
The true score of
p_{\text{data}}
can be written explicitly as:

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{5}\underbrace{\left(\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{10})}{\sum_{j=1}^{5}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{10})}\right)}_{\text{Posterior probability }\pi_{k}(\mathbf{x})}\left(-\frac{\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)

(122)

•

Marginals explicit expression.
Because the forward diffusion is linear with additive Gaussian noise, each mixture component remains Gaussian at every diffusion time, and all time marginals are therefore Gaussian mixtures with explicitly known parameters.
Moreover, both the ideal and learned reverse-time SDEs preserve Gaussianity at the component level.
Consequently, for every generation
i
, the generated distribution
\hat{p}^{\,i}
and the training mixture

q_{i}=\alpha p_{\text{data}}+(1-\alpha)\hat{p}^{\,i}

are exactly Gaussian mixtures.
This yields closed-form expressions for the score, drift mismatch, and quadratic variation, ensuring that all theoretical quantities appearing in the bounds are well-defined and can be evaluated with high numerical precision.

Score estimation via kernel density.

For the Gaussian mixture, rather than training neural networks, we use soft kernel density estimators (KDE)
(
45
)
with bandwidth
h=\sigma=0.6
to approximate the score function from samples. Given

N
training samples
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, the estimated score at diffusion time
t
is:

\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot(a_{t}\,\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x})}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad w_{j}(\mathbf{x},t)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-a_{t}\mathbf{x}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right),

(123)

with
a_{t}=e^{-t/2}
,
\sigma_{t}^{2}=a_{t}^{2}h^{2}+(1-a_{t}^{2})
and the constant of proportionality chosen to ensure
\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}(\mathbf{x},t)=1
. This provides a smooth, differentiable score estimate that captures the structure of the training
distribution while introducing controlled approximation error.

Recursive training protocol.

We implement the iterative retraining procedure described in Section
1
:

1.

Generation 0:
Sample
N=100{,}000
points from
p_{\mathrm{data}}

and construct the initial score estimator
\hat{s}_{0}
.

2.

Generation

:
Form the training mixture

q_{i}=\alpha\,p_{\mathrm{data}}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{p}^{i}
by combining

\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor
fresh samples from
p_{\mathrm{data}}
with

(N-\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor)
synthetic samples from
\hat{p}^{i}
.

3.

Score update:
Construct the new score estimator
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{i+1}

from the training mixture
q_{i}
.

4.

Sampling:
Generate
\hat{p}^{i+1}
by running the reverse diffusion
SDE with drift
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{i+1}
for 500 Euler-Maruyama steps from
T=4
to
t_{0}=0.02
.

We repeat this process for 20 generations across three fresh data fractions

\alpha\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}
, with 10 independent runs per configuration to estimate variance.

Divergence estimation.

We estimate the
\chi^{2}
divergence
\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i}\|p_{\mathrm{data}})
using

\hat{p}^{i}
versus

.
Specifically, we train a 3-layer MLP with SiLU activations for 30 epochs on balanced
datasets, then estimate:

\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i}\|p_{\mathrm{data}})\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\left[\left(\frac{\hat{p}^{i}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})}-1\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\left[(e^{\hat{r}(\mathbf{x})}-1)^{2}\right],

(124)

where

is the learned log-density ratio. A similar procedure is used to estimate

and

Score error computation.

For the 10-dimensional Gaussian mixture experiment, the score error

\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)=\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{s}_{q_{i}}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},t)

is computed as follows. The learned score
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta_{i}}
is obtained via

h=0.6
fitted to the
training samples from
q_{i}
. The target score
\mathbf{s}_{q_{i}}^{\star}
of the mixture

q_{i}=\alpha\,p_{\mathrm{data}}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{p}^{i}

\mathbf{s}_{q_{i}}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\alpha\,p_{\mathrm{data},t}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{data}}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},t)+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{p}^{i}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{i}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\alpha\,p_{\mathrm{data},t}(\mathbf{x})+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{p}^{i}_{t}(\mathbf{x})},

where
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{data}}^{\star}
is the exact score of the Gaussian mixture

\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{i}

\hat{p}^{i}

Score error and score energy.

For each generation
i

\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x},t)
at time

and for sample
\mathbf{x}
as the difference between the learned model and the true model at this generation given by,

\mathbf{s}_{q_{i}}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\alpha\,p_{\mathrm{data},t}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{data}}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},t)+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{p}^{i}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{i}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\alpha\,p_{\mathrm{data},t}(\mathbf{x})+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{p}^{i}_{t}(\mathbf{x})}.

\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{i+1}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{t}^{i},t)-\mathbf{s}_{q_{i}}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{t}^{i},t)\|^{2}_{2}\,dt\right],

Observability coefficient.

We estimate, for all generations

\hat{\eta}_{i}=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[M_{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{\star,i}])/\mathrm{Var}(M_{i})
,
using the following method:

1.

Run
N=50{,}000
reverse trajectories using the target score
\mathbf{s}_{q_{i}}

\mathbf{Y}^{\star,(j)}_{s-\Delta t}=\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,(j)}+\Big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,(j)}-\mathbf{s}_{q_{i}}^{\star}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,(j)},s)\Big]\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\mathbf{z}_{s}^{(j)},

where

At each step, we compute the score error

\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,(j)},s)=\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta_{i}}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,(j)},s)-\mathbf{s}_{q_{i}}^{\star}(\mathbf{Y}_{s}^{\star,(j)},s)

2.

Train a regression MLP to predict

Compute

Experimental setup.

We train diffusion models on
28\times 28
grayscale images from Fashion-MNIST (
N=50{,}000
training samples). The score network is a U-Net architecture with:

•

Residual blocks with group normalization and SiLU activations

•

Sinusoidal time embeddings projected through a 2-layer MLP

•

Dropout regularization (
p=0.1
) to prevent overfitting

•

Exponential moving average (EMA) of weights with decay
0.99

The
\varepsilon
-prediction objective.

In the standard DDPM formulation
(
29
)
, the forward diffusion process adds
Gaussian noise to a data sample
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
according to a variance schedule

\{\bar{\alpha}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. The noisy sample at time
t
is given by

\mathbf{x}_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{z},\quad\text{where }\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d}).

Here,
\mathbf{z}
is the
noise variable
—a standard Gaussian
vector that represents the stochastic component added during the forward process.
The neural network
\varepsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
is trained to predict this
noise from the corrupted sample
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, yielding the denoising objective:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathrm{Unif}(t_{0},T),\,\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},\,\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})}\Big[\|\varepsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)-\mathbf{z}\|^{2}_{2}\Big].

This is equivalent to score matching, since the score of the noisy distribution satisfies

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=-\mathbf{z}/\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}
, and thus
\varepsilon_{\theta}
implicitly parameterizes the score function.

Recursive training protocol.

We implement the iterative retraining procedure with fresh data fractions

\alpha\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}
:

1.

Generation 0:
Train the base model on real Fashion-MNIST data
for 200 epochs with AdamW optimizer (learning rate
2\times 10^{-4}
,
weight decay
10^{-4}
) and cosine learning rate annealing.

2.

Generation
i\to i+1
:
Construct the training mixture

q_{i}=\alpha\,p_{\mathrm{data}}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{p}^{i}
by combining

\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor
real images with
(1-\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor)

synthetic samples generated via DDPM sampling
(
29
)
from the current model.

3.

Fine-tuning:
Train the new model on
q_{i}
for 100 epochs,
with early stopping if validation loss plateaus for 50 epochs.

4.

Sampling:
Generate synthetic data using 1000-step DDPM
sampling with the EMA model weights.

\alpha
value, tracking all theoretical quantities
throughout the process.

Metric computation.

Computing the quantities from our theoretical framework requires careful adaptation
to the high-dimensional image setting.

•

Global divergence
D_{i}=\chi^{2}(\hat{p}^{i}\|p_{\mathrm{data}})
:

In the CIFAR-10 (resp. Fashion-MNIST) experiments we evaluate distributional drift using a proxy
\chi^{2}
-divergence computed in a learned feature space rather than pixel space.
Concretely, for each generation
i
we draw
M
synthetic samples
\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{\mathrm{gen}}\sim\hat{p}^{\,i}
from the EMA-smoothed DDPM sampler and
M
real samples
\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{\mathrm{real}}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
from the CIFAR-10 (resp. Fashion-MNIST) training set (we use
M=1000
in our implementation). We pass images through a fixed auxiliary classifier
c(\cdot)
trained once on CIFAR-10 (resp. Fashion-MNIST), and extract penultimate-layer embeddings
f(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\{f^{(m)}_{\mathrm{gen}}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\{f^{(m)}_{\mathrm{real}}\}_{m=1}^{M}
denote generated and real features. We then fit Gaussian densities in feature space,

p_{f}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p},\Sigma_{p})
using
\{f^{(m)}_{\mathrm{gen}}\}
and
q_{f}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q},\Sigma_{q})
using
\{f^{(m)}_{\mathrm{real}}\}
, with empirical mean and covariance (regularized as
\Sigma\leftarrow\Sigma+\epsilon I
for numerical stability, taking a small
\epsilon
). The divergence proxy is the Monte Carlo estimate of
\chi^{2}(p_{f}\|q_{f})
under
q_{f}
:

\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{feat}}(\hat{p}^{i}\|p_{\mathrm{data}})\;:=\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\frac{p_{f}(f^{(m)}_{\mathrm{real}})}{q_{f}(f^{(m)}_{\mathrm{real}})}-1\right)^{2}\qquad f^{(m)}_{\mathrm{real}}\sim q_{f},

where the density ratio is evaluated via Gaussian log-likelihoods using a Cholesky factorization of
\Sigma_{p}
and
\Sigma_{q}
. This metric captures how far the generated distribution has drifted from the real data distribution along discriminative directions encoded by the classifier features.

•

Score error energy
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}
and
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}
:

We subsample 50 timesteps uniformly from
[0,T]
and accumulate

\|\varepsilon_{\theta_{i+1}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)-\varepsilon_{\theta_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\|^{2}_{2}

along sampling trajectories, where
\varepsilon_{\theta_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
denotes the learned
i
-th model according to the
\varepsilon
-prediction mechanism described above. For
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}
, trajectories are
sampled using the new model
\theta_{i+1}
; for
\varepsilon_{\star,i}^{2}
,
trajectories use the previous model
\theta_{i}
.

•

Observability coefficient
\eta_{i}
:
Exactly as in the
10
-dimensional Gaussian multivariate mixture model, we run paired reverse trajectories using the previous model’s score, recording terminal samples

\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}
and the discretized error martingale

M_{i}\approx-\sum_{t}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star},t)\cdot\Delta B_{t}
. A regression network
is trained to predict
M_{i}
from classifier features of
\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}
, yielding

\hat{\eta}_{i}=\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{Y}_{t_{0}}^{i,\star}))/\mathrm{Var}(M_{i})
.

Heatmap.

Once again, the procedure here is exactly the same as the one described in the
10
-dimensional Gaussian multivariate mixture model. Theorem
4.2
predicts that score errors propagate with
geometric decay:

\mathrm{Contrib}[i,n]\;\approx\;(1-\alpha)^{2(N-1-i)}\varepsilon_{i}^{2},

(127)

where
\varepsilon_{i}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\|\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{k}-\mathbf{s}_{q_{k}}\|^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}t
is the path-space score error at generation
k
. The factor
(1-\alpha)^{2(N-1-i)}
captures the mitigation induced by fresh data injection: when
\alpha
is large, errors decay rapidly;
when
\alpha
is small, errors persist across many generations. As illustrated in Figure
10
the conclusions of this experiment match the theory, suggesting it also holds in higher dimensions.
