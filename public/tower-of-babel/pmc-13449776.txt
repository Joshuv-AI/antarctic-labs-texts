Multiple inducible DNA double-strand breaks using a restriction endonuclease in Arabidopsis thaliana mitochondria
Authors: Moira Rodriguez, Ana Martinez-Hottovy, Alessandra Meza, Alan C. Christensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant mitochondrial genomes are associated with important agronomic traits, such as cytoplasmic male sterility, which would be a desirable trait to control. However, plant mitochondrial genomes are currently neglected as a target for genome editing in plants, due to their recalcitrance to CRISPR/Cas9 editing, and because the understanding of DNA repair mechanisms in plant mitochondrial genomes is still limited. To further study double-strand break repair (DSBR) and to advance the ability to make targeted DNA breaks in plant mitochondrial genomes, we developed an Arabidopsis thaliana line containing a nuclear encoded T-DNA expression cassette carrying a restriction enzyme that is targeted to the mitochondria and has its expression induced with application of β-estradiol. The restriction enzyme chosen, KasI, has 35 recognition sites throughout the A. thaliana mitochondrial genome, with cut sites being in essential coding regions, non-functional DNA, and within or near non-tandem repeats. Following a single induction of this nuclear-encoded, mitochondrial-targeted restriction enzyme, we were able to decipher the consequences on the structure of the mitochondrial genome, informing on required homology lengths for DNA repair, as well as the consequences on individual cut sites, comparing non-functional DNA, coding DNA, and repeat region repair choices. This research provides a unique tool to study DNA repair in plant mitochondrial genomes, following multiple simultaneous double-strand breaks in a plant with an intact repair system. This tool can also be used to further characterize DNA repair proteins that act in plant mitochondria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09166-6.

Background

Plant mitochondrial genomes are housed in an organelle that produces many DNA damaging agents through cellular respiration, yet these genomes are still reliably inherited. These genomes tend to be very large compared to mitochondrial genomes in animals and fungi, even though the number of genes they encode has been minimized. Plant mitochondrial genomes rearrange frequently and have a remarkably low rate of synonymous substitutions [19]. Plant mitochondria are seemingly missing the DNA repair pathways for conventional mismatch repair and nucleotide excision repair, so this evidence, plus the unique characteristics of plant mitochondrial genomes, leads to the hypothesis that they use double-strand break repair (DSBR) mechanisms more frequently than in other organelles [4–6]. It is difficult to study how double-strand breaks (DSBs) are repaired due to plant mitochondria’s recalcitrance to targeted DNA editing methods such as CRISPR-Cas9. While protein-based editor systems, such as mitoTALENs, have recently been deployed successfully in making targeted deletions in the mitochondrial genome they require complex and time-consuming construction, and can usually only target one location at a time [1, 2, 12, 28]. DNA damaging agents specific to organellar DNA like ciprofloxacin can be applied to create many breaks throughout the genome, but this occurs in seemingly random locations and works by hindering other processes of mitochondrial DNA metabolism (DNA gyrase in the case of ciprofloxacin [30]). Genetic approaches can be taken by mutating nuclear-encoded, mitochondrial targeted DNA repair genes and studying the outcome on the DNA to better understand mechanisms,however, this approach is limited by the identification and characterization of these proteins acting in plant mitochondria. Due to these difficulties, having a system in which multiple DSBs can be made simultaneously in known locations throughout the mitochondrial genome of plants in vivo, without hindering other proteins potentially involved in mitochondrial DNA processes, would be greatly beneficial to the field of plant mitochondrial genetics and advance our knowledge of the mechanisms of DNA repair in plant mitochondria.

To accomplish directed DNA DSBs in plant mitochondria, a nuclear-encoded, mitochondrial-targeted restriction enzyme was used. The chosen enzyme, KasI (derived from the bacterium Kluyvera ascorbata), has multiple recognition sites in the Arabidopsis thaliana mitochondrial genome, both in essential gene coding regions, and in presumably nonessential and non-functional DNA. Some of the cut sites are located within or near genomic repeats. KasI recognizes the sequence G/GCGCC, leaving a 5′ GCGC overhanging end, and a total of 35 recognition sites for this enzyme are located throughout the A. thaliana mitochondrial genome (Table 1). These KasI sites make up a total of 0.05% of the total mitochondrial genome size. Another level of control over the enzyme cutting comes from expression of the enzyme being driven by a non-plant hormone inducible promoter. In this case, KasI is driven by an estrogen-based receptor XVE system that is highly regulated and induced by low concentrations of β-estradiol, a human hormone that plants do not possess a receptor for [34] (Fig. 1). This inducibility grants control as to exactly when in development the DSBs should occur, and allows a choice of which tissues to induce DSBs in. Zuo et al. Materials and methods

Cloning KasI

The protein sequence of KasI was obtained from GenBank (accession ADX97306.1), and codon optimized with A. thaliana codon preferences using the GeneArt DNA synthesis tool (ThermoFisher). To avoid difficulties in bacterial transformation, an mCBC intron that interrupts the coding sequence for the KasI enzyme was inserted to prevent expression in bacteria. An intron prevents expression of the enzyme while in E. coli and Agrobacterium tumefaciens, and some data suggest that an intron in a transgene improves expression [3, 18]. The sequence was synthesized with a mitochondrial targeting peptide from the alternative oxidase 1 A (Aox1A) protein and attL sites were included on both ends (Wynn and Christensen [32]). An LRClonase reaction was performed to transfer the KasI construct into pER8, a binary plant transformation vector (Accession number AF309825) [24]. The pER8 + KasI plasmid was transformed into chemically competent E. coli Top10 OneShot (Invitrogen) by mixing 1μL of LRClonase reaction mixture with 50μL of cells, incubating on ice for 30 min, heat shocking at 42 °C for 30 s, followed by recovery at 37 °C in 250 mL SOC medium while shaking at 225 rpm. The culture was then plated on LB plates containing spectinomycin. After one day of growth at 37 °C, positively transformed colonies were identified with PCR and used for a plasmid prep. The purified plasmid was used to transform electrocompetent A. tumefaciens c58c1 cells. Briefly, 50 ng of DNA was mixed with 100μL competent cells and transferred to an electroporation cuvette. This cuvette was then pulsed at 2400 V, and 900μL of SOC medium was immediately added. This mixture was recovered at 30 °C, 225 rpm for 1 h, then diluted and plated on LB plates containing spectinomycin, gentamicin, and rifampicin. Positively transformed colonies were identified with PCR and used for plant transformation.

Plant transformation

Transformation was done using Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Col-0. A. tumefaciens c58c1 containing the pER8 vector containing the KasI construct, as confirmed by PCR and growth on plates with the antibiotics rifampicin, gentamycin, and spectinomycin were inoculated in 50 mL YEBS media (1 g/L yeast extract, 5 g/L beef extract, 5 g/L sucrose, 5 g/L Bacto-Peptone, and 0.5 g MgSO4, pH 7.0) supplemented with antibiotics rifampicin, gentamicin, and spectinomycin. This inoculate was grown at 28 °C while shaking at 225 rpm. After two days of growth, the 50 mL culture was scaled up to 500 mL with 450 mL fresh YEBS media and antibiotics. The 500 mL culture was grown at 28 °C while shaking at 225 rpm for about 8 h. 100 μl Silwett L77 was added to the culture prior to floral dipping. Floral dipping was done by submersing flowers of A. thaliana plants into the 500 mL culture of A. tumefaciens for about 20 s while swirling. The excess media was allowed to drip off the plants for several seconds before covering the entire plant in a Ziploc plastic bag overnight to encourage the bacteria to infiltrate the ovules of the flowers. Once the bag was removed the next day, plants were placed back into the growth chamber and allowed to grow normally, and seeds were collected [8].

Hygromycin selection

T0 seeds from Agrobacterium-dipped plants were collected and surface sterilized using chlorine gas. The seeds were plated, along with positive and negative controls, on 1/2 × MSA plates (pH 5.6, no sucrose) supplemented with 30ug/ml of the antibiotic hygromycin B. After plating, the plates were covered in foil to reduce light exposure and vernalized for 3 days at 4℃. After vernalization, the foil was removed, and the plates were moved to the growth chamber for 3 days. Following 3 days of growth, the plates were wrapped again in foil and kept in the growth chamber for 3–4 days to allow for etiolation. Once the foil was removed, green seedlings with extended hypocotyls were transferred to 1/2 × MSA plates without hygromycin for recovery, and then ultimately transferred to soil. Since hygromycin inhibits cytosolic protein synthesis, which is required for etiolation, successfully transformed seedlings resistant to hygromycin have longer hypocotyls than untransformed seedlings [10].

Plant growth and induction

Plants, whether on plates or on soil, were grown under a 16 h light, 8-h dark day cycle at 22℃. Plants grown on soil were watered 3 times per week. For induction with β-estradiol, 4-week-old seedlings were either sprayed or painted with 100 μM β-estradiol in H20 with 0.1% Silwett-L77, on both the adaxial and abaxial surfaces of all leaves. For one whole tray of plants (18 pots, 2 seedlings per pot) 90 mL of spray was applied. Trays remained in the chemical fume hood until the leaves were dry and then placed back into the growth chamber for 48 h before leaf collection.

Primers used in qPCR reactions

Results were analyzed with the double-delta method [16]. Outliers from triplicates causing the standard deviation to be over 1.0 were removed and the average Cq was recalculated. The Cq value for each sample reaction was normalized to cox1 by subtracting the cox1 Cq value from the Cq value of the same sample (ΔCq). Then, the Cq values were normalized to the wild type Cq values by subtracting the wild type Cq value from the induced samples from the same primer amplification (ΔΔ Cq). The ‘relative expression’ of each repeat was calculated by the equation: 2^-(ΔΔCq). To check for significant differences in the ‘relative expression’ of each repeat form as compared to wild type, an unpaired t-test on the ΔCq values was performed.

Mitochondrial DNA enrichment

Mitochondrial DNA enrichment followed the general protocol from [27], with some modifications. Either whole trays or individual induced plants were collected and ground to a fine powder with liquid nitrogen in a mortar and pestle. Chilled grinding buffer (350 mM mannitol, 30 mM MOPS, 1 mM EDTA, 50 μM PVPP, 11.2 μM L-cysteine,pH 7.6) at a volume 2 ml per gram of original plant tissue was added to the plant powder and further homogenized. The plant slurry was strained through cheesecloth and then spun at 5,000 g in a chilled centrifuge for 3 min. The supernatant was filtered using a 30 mL syringe with a 5 μM filter. The filtrate was divided into 1.7 ml microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged in a chilled centrifuge at 17,600 g for 6 min. The resulting supernatant was discarded, and each pellet was resuspended in 500μL of wash buffer (300 mM mannitol, 20 mM MOPS, 1 mM EDTA; pH 7.2). This mixture was centrifuged at 17,600 g for 1 min, and the supernatant was again discarded. The final pellets were resuspended in 30μL TE buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8), [27]. DNA from the resuspended pellets was extracted using an SPRI bead-based protocol [21].

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Mitochondrial-enriched DNA samples were sent to SeqCenter in Pittsburgh, PA for both Oxford Nanopore sequencing (1.2Gbp minimum reads) and Illumina Whole Genome Sequencing (2Gbp minimum reads).

For Illumina sequencing data, FASTQ paired-end reads were interleaved, trimmed using fastp (5 bp from beginning of read, quality score 20 in 4 bp windows at 3’ end) and aligned using the BWA-MEM algorithm against the A. thaliana reference genome (GCF_000001735.4) with the 620 kb of mitochondrial sequence on chromosome 2 removed [14, 29]. Duplicate reads were removed with Picard, and the resulting BAM file was sorted and indexed with SAMtools [9]. SAMtools depth was used to create a text file with the read depth at each individual base within the mitochondrial genome. This data was plotted using Microsoft Excel to view read depth across the genome. SNPs were called using BCFtools mpileup [9]. To view the sequence at the 35 KasI cut sites, a BED file was created containing the reference coordinates of all 35 sites which was then used to extract the most common sequence at each of the sites in the sample reads. IGV was used to view the read depth in the mitochondrial genome to view drop-offs at the cut sites, SNPs, and structural variants [20].

Allele frequencies and depth were also measured using Perbase v 0.9.0 [26]. The Perbase output was analyzed using Excel. Read depth ratios from the KasI induced plants and the uninduced plants were calculated for each base. The 6 bp KasI sites, as well as the adjacent 6 bp on either side, were separated from the rest of the positions, and non-overlapping 50 bp windows were used to calculate average read depth per window.

For Nanopore sequencing data, reads shorter than 1000 bp were filtered out. Reference-based alignment was performed with minimap2 [15]. Reads that mapped to the mitochondrial genome with minimap2 were extracted and used for de novo assembly of the mitochondrial genome using Flye, and the resulting genome structure was visualized with Bandage [13, 31]. The structures produced by Bandage were then reconstructed in Excel in maps that included the locations of repeats, coding sequences, and recombination breakpoints. Sequencing statistics are given in Additional file 1.

Results

Positively transformed KasI plants were identified by selection on hygromycin plates, as evidenced by their extended hypocotyls. This was also confirmed by PCR amplification of the KasI sequence. Positive plants were transferred to soil, and seeds were collected. The progeny were grown and confirmed by PCR to still carry the KasI inducible expression cassette and were subsequently sprayed with β-estradiol for induction. The induced plants had no distinguishable phenotypic differences from wild type.

A common way of checking for mitochondrial genomic stress or instability is to perform a qPCR analysis on intermediate-sized mitochondrial repeats. An increase in DNA damage, either through directly causing DSBs, or by mutating a DNA repair gene, consistently increases the amount of recombination between intermediate repeats [7, 22, 23]. This recombination can be captured by qPCR with primers designed to amplify the original, or ‘parental’ conformations of the repeat, and by using different combinations of these same primers to amplify the recombined forms of the repeats. This allows the detection of mitochondrial genomic disruption, either in DNA integrity or DNA repair pathways. One intermediate repeat that is particularly sensitive to mitochondrial DNA damage or defective repair in A. thaliana mitochondria is repeat L, which is 249 bp in size [7, 22, 23]. Repeat recombination qPCR using repeat L performed on mitochondrial-enriched, β-estradiol induced DNA from KasI plants showed a significant increase in repeat recombination of intermediate repeat L when compared to wild type plants with the same treatment (Fig. 2). De novo assembly of the Nanopore sequencing reads also revealed a higher level of structural variation in the mitochondrial genome in KasI induced samples compared to uninduced plants. The A. thaliana Col-0 reference mitochondrial genome structure can isomerize under normal conditions due to the recombination of the two large repeat pairs, which is illustrated in our Nanopore assemblies of the uninduced genomes (Fig. 4a, b and c). Depending on the current orientation of the two large repeat pairs, the genome can be mapped into one large continuous circle, or as a ‘Fig. 8’ structure. However, following induction of KasI the mitochondrial genomes had much more entangled assemblies as illustrated by the Bandage graphs, with much more representation of intermediate-sized repeats at the locations of breakage junctions (Figs. 5, 6, 7 and 8). Intermediate sized repeats C1/C2, V1/V3, and Q1/Q2 were sites of crossover in almost all of the induced KasI sample assemblies. Comparing the coordinates of these repeats to the KasI sites reveals that it is likely these repeats were mobilized to repair nearby KasI cut sites. Repeat V has KasI sites about 500 bp away, repeat C has KasI sites about 83 bp away, and repeat Q has KasI sites 1.6 kb away. Both repeat V-1 and V-3 are in the 5’UTR of the two copies of the atp6 gene. Q-1 is located in the 3’UTR of the atp1 gene, and Q-2 is in a noncoding region. Discussion

This research represents a proof-of-concept for a novel method to study multiple targeted DSBs in plant mitochondrial genomes using a nuclear-encoded, mitochondrial-targeted, inducible restriction enzyme, KasI. Multiple lines of evidence confirmed that KasI was induced and actively cutting the target sites in the mitochondrial genome. Quantitative PCR assays of repeat L revealed ectopic recombination indicating that genomic stress was occurring in the induced plants compared to the noninduced plants also carrying the KasI T-DNA expression cassette. Illumina sequencing revealed a drop-off in read depths at the KasI target sites. Nanopore sequencing showed increased entanglement of the genomes of induced plants, with repeats C, Q, and V having recombined. The evidence shows that cutting occurred at the KasI sites following induction, and that the damage was not all successfully repaired, reflected in the lower read depth in the vicinity of the cut sites. Ectopic recombination at intermediate repeat L was seen by qPCR, and at intermediate repeats C, V and Q by nanopore sequence assembly. These all indicate a significant level of damage to the mitochondrial genome in 48 h between induction and DNA extraction.

One interesting question that arises is why other intermediate-sized repeats that are distanced similarly to a KasI cut site do not become mobilized in the same way as repeats C, V, and Q. For example, repeat D-1 is about 1 kb away from a cut site, but this repeat overlaps with the cox2 coding sequence. The cut site near the D-1 repeat is in the cox2 intron, and since there is only one copy of cox2 present in the genome, mitochondria that do not accurately repair the cox2 gene will most likely be eliminated by selection on mitochondrial function. Similarly, repeat A-1 is only 300 bp away from a cut site, but this repeat overlaps with an exon for the rps3 gene, and selection likely eliminates inaccurately repaired copies from the population of mitochondria. Repeat T-2 is close to a cut site (~ 200 bp away), and is also close to the gene nad4L. The other copy of the T repeat (T-1) overlaps with the 5’UTR of the atp1 gene, so recombination of repeats T-1 and T-2 could potentially impede expression of two genes, and again, we predict a selective disadvantage.

Another interesting repeat to focus on is the DD-1 and DD-2 repeat, which have KasI sites within the repeat itself, but this repeat does not seem to be a site of crossover according to the de novo assembly. The DD repeat is slightly smaller (128 bp) than the repeats that are seen in crossover sites of the assembly, so perhaps the homology at this repeat is not long enough to participate in recombination. Another explanation could be that the excision that results after the DSB is too long, so no homologous sequence remains after cutting and excision and thus no recombination can occur.

Repeat L was observed to recombine via qPCR, but it is not one of the intermediate repeats that makes a new connection in the KasI induced de novo assemblies. We speculate that although repeat L recombines, it is perhaps not forming a stable enough isoform to appear as a new connection in the de novo assembly [11, 17].

Future work with this construct could include following the progeny of induced plants to better understand how these ‘scrambled’ genomes are inherited in the progeny. Any other restriction enzyme of interest could replace KasI in this construct, allowing for different targets to be cut. For example, to understand how many simultaneous DSBs the mitochondrial genome can endure, KasI could be replaced with AluI, which has 2,078 recognition sites within the mitochondrial genome of A. thaliana ecotype Col-0. Comparing the difference in genome stability between 35 cuts and over 2,000 cuts would inform the upper limits of homologous recombination repair in plant mitochondrial genomes. The KasI restriction enzyme has two neoschizomers (restriction enzymes that recognize the same site, but leave a different type of DSB end), PlutI and SfoI. PlutI leaves a 3’ overhang at the recognition site, while SfoI leaves a blunt end. Being able to compare repair choices based on the type of end left at the double-strand break would lend more insight into how the mechanisms of DSB proceed in the plant mitochondria. The KasI restriction enzyme line could also be combined with DNA repair mutant backgrounds to further characterize the exact mechanism of different DNA repair proteins. Since we now have a baseline showing how the mitochondrial genome deals with 35 simultaneous DSBs in a wild type background, any differences seen in a mutant could be attributed to the actions of that mutation. In addition, this inducible system could be used in any transformable plant system, allowing for the expansion of mitochondrial genome editing in plants.

Overall, this research provides a novel tool to induce multiple simultaneous DNA double-strand breaks in plant mitochondrial genomes. This adds knowledge into how plant mitochondrial genomes deal with multiple, simultaneous DSBs in genes, repeats, and junk DNA. This method allows for checking damage response in the mitochondrial genome in an otherwise wild type background, which is not possible with all other methods currently available to cause mitochondrial damage. It also adds a novel method to the field and provides a tool to continue studying DNA repair.
