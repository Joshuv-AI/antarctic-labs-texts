Development of infection control and preventive nursing assessment tool for central line–associated bloodstream infection in selected hospitals in China: an exploratory sequential mixed-method study
Authors: Xiuwen Chi, Jianping Yang, Tuyong Liang, Juan Guo, Huishi Huang, Jialing Liang, Lijuan Wu, Zhen Huang, Jordan T Salvador
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundExtended hospital stays, higher hospital costs, and increased mortality are common central line–associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI) outcomes. Nurses’ roles in preventing CLABSI are vital. Improving patient outcomes requires action to ensure intensive care unit (ICU) nurses’ strict adherence with nursing practice guidelines.AimThe aim of this study was to develop and validate a nursing assessment tool to enhance CLABSI prevention and infection control in Chinese ICUs.Study designWe used an exploratory sequential mixed-method design using a three-stage sequential procedure: phase 1, an exploratory descriptive design involving interviews with 15 nurse experts and analysis using the Colaizzi method; phase 2, tool development and evaluation, including rigorous assessment of internal consistency and reliability using Cronbach’s α and split-half test; and phase 3, a descriptive design applying the newly developed tool to 183 registered nurses in China. We evaluated demographic variable effects using one-way analysis of variance and independent samples t-tests.ResultsThe six themes of the CLABSI assessment tool covered the entire central venous catheter insertion and maintenance processes. The themes’ weighted mean values were 4.57, 4.51, 4.49, 4.38, 4.37, and 3.86 for innovation for maintenance, innovation for replacement of infusion sets, innovation for cannulation procedure, internal environment, recipient, and external environment, respectively. The overall weighted mean of the tool was 4.43.ConclusionsThe CLABSI assessment tool was highly accepted by ICU nurses. Most items align with current evidence-based guidelines. Some items reflect interventions proven effective in clinical practice, even exceeding guideline recommendations.Keywords: Blood, China, Infections, Intensive care, Nurses

Introduction

Previous research shows that evidence-based practices (EBPs) are often not fully implemented in clinical settings [1]. For example, guidelines suggest replacing infusion devices at least every 7 days, but many hospitals replace them every 24 h. When EBP programs are not effectively promoted, healthcare providers often hesitate to apply clinical guidelines due to a lack of confidence, making implementation difficult.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines help reduce central line–associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI) rates, but adherence remains inconsistent [2,3]. No studies have implemented all 14 recommendations. While intensive care unit (ICU) professionals and professional societies endorse checklists for catheter insertion [4], most bundles focus on technical innovation, overlooking operational and human factors [3].

Background

CLABSIs are bloodstream infections that occur in patients with an indwelling central line, confirmed by laboratory testing on the day of infection or the day prior [5]. The CDC began collecting ICU bloodstream infection data in the 1970s and issued recommendations in 1991 to reduce intravascular infections, but CLABSI rates remained high throughout the 1990s. However, in the past two decades, CLABSI rates have declined as these infections are now largely preventable [6].

In the USA, CLABSI rates decreased by 46% between 2008 and 2013, but around 12,219 CLABSIs still occur annually in acute care ICUs [7]. Despite numerous guidelines, such as the Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Intravascular CLABSI [8] and Practice Guidelines for Central Venous Access [9], infections persist, indicating that guidelines alone are insufficient.

In China, the 2024 National Healthcare Services and Quality Safety Report indicated that the incidence of CLABSI among inpatients in 1306 tertiary general hospitals remained at 0.23‰ in 2023 [10], highlighting gaps in prevention efforts.

Aims and objectives of study

We aimed to develop and validate an evidence-based nursing assessment tool to enhance CLABSI prevention and infection control in Chinese ICUs. We introduced external facilitators, analysed recipients’ perspectives on the innovation, and explored ICU organizational contexts. Through qualitative interviews and expert consultation, we identified EBPs and developed a reliable, context-adaptive tool. Following the spiral progression of the i-PARIHS (integrated Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services) framework, the study initially included a single ICU and later expanded to 12 ICUs, assessing the tool’s acceptability among ICU nurses [11].

Design and methods

Design

This study used an exploratory sequential mixed-method approach, combining qualitative and quantitative analyses to explore nursing practices for preventing CLABSI in ICUs [12]. The design aimed to leverage qualitative research for understanding the ‘why’ and ‘how’ of nursing behaviour, followed by quantitative methods to validate and test the developed tool [13,14]. The core questions of this three-phase research design were (1) what are the EBPs of nurses in preventing CLABSI in the ICU as the basis for developing a preventive nursing tool in a selected hospital in China; (2) what intervention development variant can be identified from the phase 1 qualitative findings; and (3) what are the nurses’ levels of evidence-based care to prevent CLABSI occurrences in the selected hospitals’ ICUs in China? Participants and sampling

Participants in each phase differed. In phase 1, the general ICUs of nine teaching hospitals, located in three cities (X, Y, and Z) and affiliated with our university, were selected. Nurse experts, including educators, clinical practitioners, managers, leaders, and advocates (N = 15), were included in the qualitative interviews if they (1) were registered nurses; (2) had at least 10 years of experience in education, practice, and research; and (3) were willing to share expertise and perspectives. ICU nurses with a mental illness diagnosis and those unwilling to participate in this study were excluded. For the semi-structured interviews, purposive sampling methods and snowballing techniques were used to enrol participants.

In phase 2, for the tests of validity and reliability of the newly developed tool, 6 nursing experts and 51 registered nurses were recruited. The nursing experts were required to have (1) at least 10 years’ working experience; (2) major expertise in nursing; (3) a master’s degree in nursing; and (4) willingness to participate in this study.

Lastly, in phase 3, registered nurses (N = 183) who worked in 12 ICUs were surveyed quantitatively. Using Slovin’s formula, the sample size was computed at a 95% confidence level (0.05 alpha level).

Data collection

From June to September 2023, researchers collected the phase 1 data. The semi-structured interview guide is shown in Supplementary Table S1. To prevent irregularities, all interviewers were trained and participated in a workshop before conducting interviews. Every interview was conducted after obtaining permission from the participants. To put the participants at ease, the researchers conveyed all important information on the investigation and answered all the participants’ questions about the study. The interviewees were contacted in the ICU. The interview duration ranged from 30 to 45 min. All information was meticulously recorded, and field notes were documented [15,16]. Each participant was expected to offer distinctive and insightful perspectives to expand the reader’s knowledge base [17]. Data saturation was confirmed, and data collection stopped when no new themes or information emerged throughout several consecutive interviews [15]. On the same day as the interview, the recordings were transcribed and entered into a database after being transcribed verbatim.

In phase 2, we asked six nurse specialists to check the survey tool distributed via WeChat (Tencent Inc., Shenzhen, China). The tool was tested for validity and reliability in a pilot study with ICU nurses (N = 51). We included a link (https://www.wjx.cn/vm/rwJ7DQs.aspx) to the online survey created on the Wenjuanxing platform (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd., Changsha, China). The link, along with all study details, was distributed to participants via WeChat questionnaires throughout the data collection period. In addition, multiple expert roundtable discussionswere conducted to refine and finalize the draft items before pilot testing.

A descriptive cross-sectional study of ICU nurses from 12 hospitals in Guangdong province was conducted as part of this study’s third phase. All eligible ICU nurses in the participating hospitals were invited to participate; participation was voluntary. The link (https://www.wjx.cn/vm/rwJ7DQs.aspx) to the survey and all study details were included in the WeChat questionnaires.

Data analysis

In phase 1 (qualitative design), data were analysed using Colaizzi’s data analysis method [18]. The approach comprises seven steps: (1) reading and re-reading of narratives – researchers reviewed the collected narratives multiple times to deeply understand participants’ experiences and identify patterns, meanings, and themes; (2) significant statement extraction – key statements reflecting important aspects of participants’ experiences were identified and extracted for further analysis; (3) formulating meanings – the extracted statements were analysed to uncover their deeper meanings, going beyond surface-level descriptions; (4) organization of thematic clusters and themes – similar meanings were grouped into thematic clusters, creating a structured framework that accurately represented the data; (5) integration of findings into exhaustive descriptions – the themes were combined into a detailed and coherent description, capturing the complexity of participants’ experiences; (6) description of the fundamental structure of the phenomenon – a conceptual framework was developed, outlining the core elements and relationships within the phenomenon; and (7) participants’ validation – participants reviewed and validated the findings to ensure the interpretations accurately reflected their experiences. Principles of credibility, confirmability, dependability, and transferability were applied for the rigour of the study: credibility was enhanced through triangulation, member checking, and the use of a researcher’s reflective journal; dependability, by maintaining detailed records of the research process and data analysis steps; reliability, by strictly adhering to the established methodology and ensuring the study’s consistency; and transferability was supported by providing detailed background information, allowing the findings to be applied or referenced in similar contexts.

In phase 2, the qualitative data analysis findings informed the development of the survey concepts, constructs, and tool items. Concepts were derived from the themes, while constructs and items were developed from the formulated meanings presented in Supplementary Table S2. The instrument subsequently underwent two distinct forms of validation: face validity and content validity. Face validity was assessed to ensure that the questionnaire items were clear, comprehensible, and easily interpretable by the target population of ICU nurses. Consistent with contemporary perspectives on face validation, particular attention was given to the potential for misunderstanding or misinterpretation of questionnaire items. The initial draft of the instrument was reviewed by a panel of six nursing experts. The experts evaluated each item from the perspective of the intended respondents, focussing on clarity of wording, comprehensibility, and potential ambiguity. They also provided feedback on grammatical accuracy, sentence structure, spelling, and overall readability. In addition, formatting aspects, including font size and layout, were evaluated to ensure that the questionnaire items were presented in a way that facilitated accurate interpretation by respondents. Based on the experts’ suggestions, minor revisions were made to improve the clarity and readability of several items prior to quantitative validation.

Content validity was then assessed using the content validity index (CVI) [19]. The same six experts independently rated the relevance of each of the 44 items on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant). The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion of experts assigning a rating of 3 or 4 to each item. Items with an I-CVI > 0.79 were retained, those between 0.70 and 0.79 were revised, and those <0.70 were eliminated. The scale-level content validity index/average (S-CVI/Ave) was computed as the mean of all I-CVI values, with ≥0.90 indicating excellent content validity. Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability testing. An item reduction process was conducted based on the I-CVI results. Of the initial 44 items, those with an I-CVI > 0.79 were retained, items scoring between 0.70 and 0.79 were revised according to expert recommendations, and items with an I-CVI < 0.70 were eliminated. This systematic screening ensured that only items demonstrating adequate relevance and clarity were retained in the final version of the instrument.

In phase 3, the data were reported using weighted means and percentages. The proportion of demographic profiles to the total population was expressed as a percentage. To determine the items’ level of acceptance among ICU nurses, the weighted average of the six survey components was calculated. Weighted means were used to evaluate nurses’ adoption of the survey items. To interpret the results, the standard 5-point Likert scale values with their predetermined range and interpretation are as follows [9]: highly acceptable, 4.21–5.0; acceptable, 3.41–4.20; moderately acceptable, 2.61–3.40; unacceptable, 1.81–2.60; and highly unacceptable, 1.0–1.80. The analysis included eight variables: age, sex, marital status, educational qualifications, post designation, years of ICU work experience, monthly income, and job position. Each variable was divided into subgroups, with sample size (N) and percentage (%) indicated. The effects of different demographic variables on the total score (expressed as mean ± standard deviation) were examined using one-way analysis of variance and independent samples t-tests, which were carried out using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Phase 1 – qualitative findings

Phase 1 identified six major themes and 44 theme clusters representing clinical nursing practices related to central venous catheter management. The six themes were as follows: (1) innovation for cannulation procedure; (2) innovation for maintenance; (3) innovation for replacement of infusion sets; (4) recipient; (5) internal environment; and (6) external environment. These themes informed the development of survey constructs and tool items in phase 2.

A detailed presentation of the qualitative findings is provided in the Supplementary Materials. Supplementary Table S2 summarizes the major themes, theme clusters, formulated meanings, and representative significant statements that formed the basis of tool development.

Phase 2 – tool development

Multiple roundtable discussions were held while developing the tool’s final components. For face validation, the experts suggested ways to enhance survey questions and improve the survey items. The initial instrument consisted of 44 items derived from the qualitative phase. Based on expert evaluation using the I-CVI, 33 items (I-CVI > 0.79) were retained, 5 items (I-CVI = 0.70–0.79) were revised according to expert feedback, and 6 items (I-CVI < 0.70) were deleted. Following revision and item elimination, the final version of the nursing assessment tool consisted of 38 items. The S-CVI/Ave was 0.93, indicating excellent content validity.

The newly developed tool has two sections. The initial part involves participants’ demographic profiles, while the latter comprises six survey components related to the six major themes. In Supplementary Table S3, the development process of the survey tool is summarized in a matrix, including information on the number of items, their definitions, and the equivalence of each survey component.

In the pilot study (N = 51), the instrument demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.881. According to the criteria proposed by George and Mallery [20], a Cronbach’s α between 0.80 and 0.89 indicates good reliability. The split-half reliability coefficients were 0.713 and 0.857 for parts 1 and 2, respectively. In the final validation sample (N = 183), the internal consistency further improved, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.959, which is considered excellent reliability based on established criteria [20]. The split-half reliability coefficients for parts 1 and 2 were 0.940 and 0.928, respectively. These findings indicate that the finalized 38-item instrument demonstrates excellent internal consistency in assessing ICU nurses’ practices for preventing CLABSI.

Phase 3 – quantitative design

A total of 183 registered nurses from 12 hospitals participated in the quantitative descriptive study. All eligible ICU nurses in the participating hospitals were invited to take part in the survey. As shown in Table I, key findings include that only ‘job position’ showed significant effects on total scores (F = 8.51, P < 0.001). The head nurse group had the highest total score (178.80 ± 8.70), while the ‘other position’ group scored the lowest (144.29 ± 30.15). No significant differences in total scores were observed for other variables, including age (F = 0.06, P = 0.939) and sex (t = 0.17, P = 0.863). Educational qualifications (F = 2.19, P = 0.091), post designation (F = 2.52, P = 0.06), and years of ICU experience (F = 2.42, P = 0.092) approached the 0.05 significance threshold. This analysis quantifies associations between total scores and demographic characteristics, highlighting the pronounced impact of job position and providing a foundation for targeted interventions and future research design.

Discussion

CLABSI is strongly associated with prolonged length of stay, increased readmission, and higher mortality, underscoring the importance of improving EBPs [21]. Previous large-scale interventions have shown mixed effectiveness depending on baseline infection rates and implementation fidelity [22,23]. In this context, our study systematically synthesized key EBPs from ICU nursing practice in China and developed a structured assessment tool grounded in real-world clinical experience. Compared with the existing guideline-based approaches [2,8,24], this study extends current knowledge by integrating a multi-centre mixed-method design. Through qualitative interviews and quantitative validation, we developed a nursing-focussed CLABSI assessment tool with excellent psychometric properties. The instrument demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.881 in pilot testing and 0.959 in final validation), supporting its reliability for evaluating ICU nursing practices.

Six thematic constructs were identified, reflecting comprehensive CLABSI prevention practices. Among these, ‘innovation for cannulation procedure’ achieved a high acceptance level (4.49). Although catheter insertion decisions are typically physician-led, nurse-driven interventions – such as prompting timely catheter removal – have been shown to reduce unnecessary catheter use and CLABSI incidence [25]. Some guideline-recommended items, including ultrasound guidance and catheter lumen selection [2], were excluded during expert consultation due to inconsistent applicability or evolving evidence [26]. Notably, certain practices (e.g. double fixation) are widely adopted in clinical settings despite not being explicitly recommended, highlighting the gap between guidelines and real-world practice.

‘Innovation for maintenance’ received the highest score (4.57), emphasizing the central role of routine care. Practices such as saline flushing aligned with National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommendations [24], while chlorhexidine-based bathing demonstrated effectiveness in reducing infection rates [27]. However, variability in product availability and cost considerations affect implementation. Emerging alternatives (e.g. Chlorhexidine Gluconate (CHG) foam) suggest potential improvements in cost-effectiveness and compliance, although these were not directly evaluated in this study. Hand hygiene remains a cornerstone of infection prevention [2,24,28], and expert consultation reaffirmed the necessity of both hand disinfection and glove use in appropriate contexts.

‘Innovation for replacement of infusion sets’ (4.51) reflects strong nurse acceptance despite variability in institutional policies. Although guidelines provide general recommendations [2], actual replacement intervals often rely on clinical experience. Ongoing trials examining optimal replacement timing (e.g. 24 h vs 96 h) may provide stronger evidence in the future [29]. In addition, discrepancies between recommended and actual disinfection practices persist (e.g. inadequate contact time), highlighting persistent challenges in EBP implementation [30].

The ‘recipient’ and ‘internal environment’ domains (4.37 and 4.38, respectively) underscore the influence of contextual and organizational factors. Resource limitations, including the cost of consumables, can affect adherence to standardized practices. Meanwhile, institutional measures such as checklists, training, and quality control indicators are critical for sustaining infection prevention efforts [2]. These findings reinforce the importance of aligning organizational support with clinical practice.

The ‘external environment’ received the lowest score (3.86), reflecting limited access to advanced training and broader system-level support. This suggests that strengthening regional and national training systems may further enhance CLABSI prevention capacity.

Overall, the tool demonstrated high acceptability (weighted mean: 4.43), indicating that ICU nurses are generally compliant with CLABSI prevention bundles. Importantly, several items incorporated into the tool are derived from clinical experience rather than formal guidelines, suggesting that frontline practices can complement and extend existing recommendations. This balance between guideline adherence and practice-based innovation represents a key strength of the developed instrument.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. First, in the qualitative investigation into CLABSI factors, only the participants’ experiences and insights were included. Second, the sample size was small and limited due to the study’s qualitative nature. For example, small subgroup sample sizes (e.g. master’s degree holders, N = 2; associate nursing professors, N = 9) may reduce statistical power. Therefore, future studies should expand the sample sizes to enhance robustness. Third, the results may not apply to other healthcare settings because the investigation only included nurses from one province. In addition, owing to geographical distribution limitations, some degree of selection bias might have occurred. Consenting individuals may also have a higher level of interest in the research topic, resulting in self-selection bias and affecting the generalizability of the findings. However, despite potential sampling bias, we sought to maximize the credibility and validity of the study through diversified sampling and transparent reporting.

Implications and recommendations for practice and further research

Future studies should validate the tool across multiple regions and healthcare settings to assess its broader applicability. Expanding participant representation – including nurse managers, infection control specialists, and policymakers – would strengthen the tool’s relevance and generalizability. The tool can also be used to identify key gaps in EBP implementation and guide targeted training programs. ICU nurse leaders should promote engagement in EBP and quality improvement initiatives. Particular attention should be given to items that diverge from or extend beyond guideline recommendations as these may represent areas for further investigation or innovation. In addition, structured training programs led by provincial and national health authorities are recommended to enhance ICU nurses’ competencies in CLABSI prevention. Integrating the tool into routine clinical evaluation and education may further improve compliance and outcomes.

In conclusion, this study developed a CLABSI assessment tool suitable for ICUs in China that was highly accepted by ICU nurses. Most items align with current evidence-based guidelines. Some items reflect interventions proven effective in clinical practice, even exceeding guideline recommendations. For ICUs with high CLABSI rates, the tool is a valuable reference; for ICUs with low incidence, it may provide feasible strategies to further optimize infection control and reduce CLABSI rates.
