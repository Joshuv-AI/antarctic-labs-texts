Body fat percentage in relation to semen characteristics, testicular volume, and reproductive hormones: a cross-sectional study
Authors: N Brix, A Ernst, A Gaml-Sørensen, L H Arendt, G Toft, S S Tøttenborg, K S Hougaard, J P E Bonde, C H Ramlau-Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSTUDY QUESTIONIs body fat percentage associated with semen characteristics, testicular volume, and reproductive hormone levels?SUMMARY ANSWERHigher body fat percentage was associated with poor semen characteristics and impaired reproductive hormone levels but not with testicular volume.WHAT IS KNOWN ALREADYHigh BMI is associated with poor semen characteristics and impaired reproductive hormone levels. However, it remains unsettled whether body fat percentage is related to semen characteristics, testicular volume, and hormone levels; whether body fat percentage is a better measure of adiposity than BMI and waist-to-height ratio and hence stronger associated with semen characteristics, testicular volume, and reproductive hormones; and whether a high body fat percentage is associated with semen characteristics, testicular volume, and reproductive hormones, even among men considered normal weight according to BMI.STUDY DESIGN, SIZE, DURATIONIn this cross-sectional study, we used data from the Fetal Programming of Semen Quality (FEPOS) Cohort nested within the Danish National Birth Cohort. During 2017–2019, 1058 (19%) of 5697 invited men participated in FEPOS at the age of 18 years and 9 months.PARTICIPANTS/MATERIALS, SETTING, METHODSThe participants had their body fat percentage measured by bioelectrical impedance and had measured their BMI and waist-to-height ratio. They provided a semen and a blood sample, self-measured their testicular volume, and filled in a questionnaire on health behavior. Body fat percentage was analyzed as categories (<8%[low], 8–19% [normal, reference], 20–24% [high], ≥25% [very high]) and as a linear variable (in SDs). Relative differences in the outcomes in percent (with 95% confidence intervals) were estimated using negative binomial regression models. We further explored whether body fat percentage was stronger associated with impaired male fecundity than BMI and waist-to-height ratio, and whether an association for body fat percentage was present even among men considered normal weight according to BMI (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2).MAIN RESULTS AND THE ROLE OF CHANCECompared to normal body fat percentage (8–19%), high (20–24%) and very high (≥25%) body fat percentages were associated with lower total sperm count (high: −23% [−35%, −9%]; very high: −15% [−35%, 13%]; continuous, per SD-increase in BMI: −8% [−13%, −2%]), and tendencies toward lower semen volume and concentration were observed. No associations were observed for the remaining semen characteristics and testicular volume. A higher body fat percentage was associated with lower sex hormone-binding globulin and testosterone levels, and higher free androgen index, LH, and estradiol levels. The results for low body fat (<8%) were generally in the opposite direction but less consistent. The associations with total sperm count, semen volume, and semen concentration were stronger for body fat percentage than for BMI and waist-to-height ratio. Further, the associations for body fat percentage with total sperm count and poorer normal morphology were even stronger among men considered normal weight according to BMI, although the associations for the reproductive hormones attenuated.LIMITATIONS, REASONS FOR CAUTIONThe participation rate was low (19%) in FEPOS, which we tried to counter with selection weights. Even though body fat percentage measured by bioelectrical impedance constitutes a better measure of body fat than BMI, there may be misclassification. Data were collected cross-sectionally, which poses a risk of reverse causation. However, a sensitivity analysis using BMI at 11 years of age as the exposure indicated minimal risk of reverse causation.WIDER IMPLICATIONS OF THE FINDINGSIf our associations reflect causality, they suggest that a higher percentage of adipose tissue might adversely affect semen production and hormonal balance.FUNDINGThis article is part of the ReproUnion collaborative study, co-financed by the European Union, Interreg V ÖKS (20200407). Introduction

Over the last decades, the world-wide prevalence of overweight and obesity has rapidly increased, and currently up to a third of the world’s population is affected (Chooi et al., 2019). This increase has largely paralleled reports of secular trends toward reduced sperm counts (Levine et al., 2023) and increasing infertility rates (The World Bank, 2023) and use of ARTs (Nyboe Andersen and Erb, 2006). Currently, infertility affects 17.5% of couples globally (World Health Organization, 2023), with male factor contributing to the inability to conceive in up to 50% of childless couples (Practice Committee of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine, 2015).

Overweight and obesity may affect male reproductive health via changes in hormonal levels, increased reactive oxygen species and scrotal temperature, accumulation of endocrine-disrupting chemicals, and epigenetic alterations (Mintziori et al., 2020). In epidemiological studies, overweight and obesity are usually measured in terms of the BMI indicated by the intervals of 25.0–29.9 and ≥30.0 kg/m2, respectively (World Health Organization, 2025). Using this definition, couples with overweight or obese men are at increased risk of prolonged time-to-pregnancy (Ramlau-Hansen et al., 2007b) and decreased probability of livebirth per ART cycle than couples with normal weight men (Campbell et al., 2015). These findings may partly owe to impaired semen quality, because high BMI has been associated with lower semen volume, total sperm count, concentration, and vitality, as well as lower proportion of spermatozoa with normal motility and morphology (MacDonald et al., 2010; Sermondade et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2017; Salas-Huetos et al., 2021).

However, BMI is not an ideal measure of body fat, as, e.g. BMI can be high due to accumulated muscle mass in a lean person (Gallagher et al., 1996; Rothman, 2008). A more direct measure of body fat, such as body fat percentage, can be obtained using bioelectrical impedance (Rothman, 2008). Only a few studies have investigated body fat percentage in relation to semen characteristics, with some reporting no association (Ehala-Aleksejev and Punab, 2015; Tsao et al., 2015; Chang et al., 2018; Hansen et al., 2023; Uzun et al., 2024). The studies were, however, without detailed confounder adjustment, small, or based on a selected group of men.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the associations between body fat percentage and markers of male fecundity (semen characteristics, testicular volume, and reproductive hormones) using the large Fetal Programming Of Semen quality (FEPOS) cohort with detailed information on potential confounders. In secondary analyses, we further explored: (i) whether body fat percentage was a stronger risk factor for impaired male fecundity than BMI and waist-to-height ratio, thereby exploring whether body fat more precisely measures adiposity, and (ii) whether an association for body fat percentage is present even among men considered normal weight according to BMI.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study is based on the Danish National Birth Cohort (DNBC) (Olsen et al., 2001). The DNBC includes around 100 000 mother–child pairs. The mothers were recruited by their general practitioner at their first antenatal meeting during 1996–2002. They gave information on health, lifestyle, and social factors in computer-assisted telephone interviews scheduled during and after pregnancy and in several postnatal follow-up questionnaires. In a sensitivity analysis, we used information on their sons’ height and weight measured by the parents at the 11-year follow-up.

The FEPOS cohort was established as a sub-cohort within the DNBC (Keglberg Haervig et al., 2020). Young men were eligible for being invited to the FEPOS cohort if they lived in proximity to Aarhus or Copenhagen, Denmark, their mothers had participated in the two pregnancy interviews in the DNBC and provided a blood sample during pregnancy (n = 21 623). Between March 2017 and December 2019, we continually invited young men fulfilling the inclusion criteria when they turned 18 years and 9 months to participate (n = 5697). The young men were encouraged to decline participation in case of sterilization, previous chemotherapy or if only one or no testicles had descended into the scrotum. In total, 1174 (21%) returned the web-based FEPOS questionnaire. Of these, 1058 (19%) participated also in the clinical examination, at either the Department of Occupational Medicine, Aarhus University Hospital, Aarhus, Denmark or at the Department of Occupational and Environmental Medicine, Bispebjerg and Frederiksberg Hospital, Copenhagen, Denmark (Supplementary Fig. S1). At the clinical examination, we evaluated height, weight, waist circumference, and body fat percentage, and the young men provided a semen and a blood sample and self-measured their testicular size.

Body composition: body fat percentage, BMI, and waist-to-height ratio

At the clinical examination, the weight and body fat percentage were measured using the TANITA MC 780 MA P bioimpedance analyzer (Tanita, Tokyo, Japan). Height was measured in cm using a Seca portable stadiometer 0–230 cm (Seca, GmBH & Co Kg, Hamburg, Germany) in a standardized manner. Waist circumference was measured with a Seca measuring tape (Seca, GmBH & Co Kg, Hamburg, Germany) in cm at the level of the umbilicus, midway between the lower border of the rib cage and the iliac crest. Body fat percentage was used as the main exposure variable. It was divided into four categories (<8% [low], 8–19% [normal, reference], 20–24% [high], ≥25% [very high]) based on the equation by Gallagher et al. (2000) to be comparable to the WHO BMI groups: underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese. Similar cut-points for categorization of body fat percentage have also been derived and used by others (Romero-Corral et al., 2008; Pasco et al., 2014). For secondary analyses, BMI was derived from height and weight and waist-to-height ratio from waist circumference and height.

Semen characteristics, testicular volume, and reproductive hormones

The men provided a semen sample either at home or at the clinical examination. If collected at home, they were instructed to keep the semen sample close to their body. The men were encouraged to be sexually abstinent for 48–72 h prior to semen sampling. The semen samples were analyzed according to the recommendations by the World Health Organization (WHO) (World Health Organization, 2010) and cross-referenced with the Bjorndahl et al. 2022 checklist (Bjorndahl et al., 2022). Semen volume was derived by weighing of the sample. Then, the sample was incubated at 37°C for liquefaction. Semen concentration was derived by counting after standard dilution using a BLAUBRAND® Improved Neubauer Hemocytometer (BLAUBRAND®, Wertheim, Germany). Total sperm count was derived by multiplying semen volume and sperm concentration. Sperm motility was analyzed manually and divided into percentages of progressive motile, non-progressive motile and immotile sperm. Semen volume, sperm concentration, total sperm count, and motility assessment were analyzed locally at each clinical site, while sperm morphology was analyzed at the Reproductive Medicine Centre, Skaane University Hospital, Malmö, Sweden. Men reporting spillage of the semen sample were excluded from analyses of semen volume and total sperm count (n = 182), and men with azoospermia were excluded from analyses of motility and morphology (n = 17).

After thorough oral and written instructions, testicular volume was self-measured by the participants at the clinical examination using a Prader Orchidometer (Bayer AG, Leverkusen, Germany). This method has previously been found to have moderate to good agreement with an expert examiner (Ramlau-Hansen et al., 2007a).

Non-fasting venous blood samples were drawn at the clinical visit using VACUETTE® SAFETY Blood Collection Set + Holder (Greiner Bio-One GmbH, Kremsmünster, Austria) and stored in CryoPure Tubes (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) at −80°C until analysis. Timing of blood sampling is shown in Table 1. Reproductive hormones were measured at the Department of Clinical Biochemistry at Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark: testosterone (reference range: 8.3–31 nmol/l) and estradiol (reference range: 30–160 pmol/l) were measured using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, while sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG, reference range: 15–180 nmol/l), FSH (reference range: 2.0–12.5 IU/l), and LH (reference range: 2.0–10.5 IU/l) were measured using immunoassays (Cobas® 8000 e602; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Testosterone refers to total testosterone while free androgen index (FAI) was derived as: (testosterone/SHBG) × 100%. In total, <5 participants had FSH below the limit of detection (LOD), <5 had LH below the LOD, and 15 had estradiol below the LOD. Values below the LOD were imputed as ‘LOD/√2’, which has been shown not to introduce bias if <10% of samples are below the LOD (Lubin et al., 2004).

Covariates

Potential confounding factors were identified by use of Directed Acyclic Graphs (Greenland et al., 1999). They included highest educational level of the parents as a measure of socioeconomic status, maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and first trimester smoking, birth weight for gestational age, as well as smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity of the young men themselves (Supplementary Fig. S2). Information on highest educational level of the parents, maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, and smoking were obtained from the first interview in the DNBC scheduled at 12 weeks of gestation. Birth weight for gestational age was based on birth weight, gestational age, and sex obtained from the Danish Medical Birth Register (Bliddal et al., 2018). It was derived as z-scores based on the reference material developed by Marsal et al. (1996) and categorized into small for gestational age (<10th percentile), appropriate for gestational age (10th–90th percentile), and large for gestational age (>90th percentile). Highest educational level of the parents was based on the International Standard Class of Occupation and Education codes (ISCO-88 and ISCED). To improve precision, we also adjusted the analyses for precision variables, i.e. variables strongly associated with the studied outcomes even though these were expected to be unrelated to the exposure. Hence, the analyses of semen characteristics were further adjusted for spillage, abstinence time, and place where the semen sample was collected. The analyses of motility were also further adjusted for the interval from collection to analysis of semen sample (Makler et al., 1979). The analyses of testicular volume were only adjusted for abstinence time. The analyses of reproductive hormone levels were only adjusted for timing of blood sampling. The covariates were categorized as shown in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Multiple imputation with chained equations was used to impute missing information on the exposure variables (body fat percentage, BMI, and waist-to-height ratio [<1.0%]) and covariates (n = 80 [7.6%]) in 50 imputation datasets (Supplementary Data File S1). The multiple imputation analyses described below were performed on each imputed dataset, and then, the estimates were combined using Rubin’s rule (White et al., 2011).

In descriptive analyses, covariates and precision variables were tabulated against categories of body fat percentage for the complete cases (n = 978). Then, the markers of fecundity (semen characteristics, testicular volume, and reproductive hormone levels) were tabulated against categories of body fat percentage for the complete cases. To explore for departures of linearity, we analyzed the markers of fecundity as unadjusted flexible functions of body fat percentage using restricted cubic splines in the multiple imputed datasets (n = 1058).

In the primary analyses using the imputed datasets (n = 1058), we used a multivariable negative binomial regression model to obtain mean ratios of the outcomes across levels of body fat percentage with normal body fat percentage (9–18%) as the reference. These ratios were converted to relative differences (with 95% confidence intervals): (ratio − 1) × 100%. Potential confounders were all categorical and included as indicator variables. The negative binomial regression model accounts for the overdispersed outcome data and has previously shown a good fit to the data in the FEPOS cohort. To ensure optimal model fit, sperm motility was analyzed as non-progressive + immotile sperm (referred to as non-progressive motility). For the present study, we inspected the model assumptions by plotting the observed outcome distributions against the model-based distribution in QQ plots as well as plotting the standardized deviance residuals against the predicted mean. The plots showed acceptable model fit.

We conducted two secondary analyses. First, we analyzed body fat percentage, BMI, and waist-to-height ratio as a linear term (in SDs) to examine whether body fat percentage was stronger associated with impaired male fecundity than BMI and waist-to-height ratio (n = 1058). Second, we estimated the associations for body fat percentage (in SD) among normal weight men (BMI: 18.5–24.9 kg/m2) both with and without further adjustment for BMI as a continuous variable.

We conducted two sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated the primary analysis as a complete case analysis (n = 978) to compare the results with those from the multiple imputation analysis (n = 1058). Second, to explore the possibility of reverse causation, we used BMI based on the 11-year follow-up in the DNBC as the exposure of interest and compared the results with those for adult BMI based on the (cross-sectional) FEPOS clinical examination.

To account for selective non-participation, we employed selection weights (Hernan et al., 2004). Selection weights were estimated as the inverse probability of selection based on multivariable logistic regression of participation (yes, no) on the following explanatory variables: maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, maternal alcohol consumption during pregnancy, maternal smoking during pregnancy, maternal age at birth, parental time-to-pregnancy including use of medically assisted reproduction, highest educational level of the parents, and the participant’s region of living.

We used robust SEs to account for clustering of siblings and the use of selection weights. According to the requirements of regulations (GDPR, Regulation (EU), 2016/679 of 25 May 2018), all percentiles were reported as pseudo-percentiles based on an average of the five nearest observations around the corresponding percentile. All data analyses were performed in STATA MP 17.0.

The establishment of the FEPOS cohort was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee for Copenhagen and Frederiksberg (No. H-16015857). The Steering Committee of the DNBC permitted the recruitment and data collection (Ref. no. 2016-08) and approved the conduction of this specific study (Ref. no. 2020-27 and 2018-09). Results

Descriptive analysis

In total, 9% (n = 89) had low body fat percentage of <8%, 75% (n = 731) normal body fat percentage of 8–19%, 11% (n = 111) high body fat percentage of 20–24%, and 5% (n = 47) very high body fat percentage of ≥25%. Participants with higher body fat percentages, i.e. ≥20%, had parents with lower education and mothers with higher pre-pregnancy BMI who more often smoked in the first trimester, and the men themselves had higher birth weight, BMI, and waist-to-height ratio, and were currently less often smoking, less frequently drinking alcohol, less frequently exercising, reported spillage more often, and had shorter time from ejaculation to analysis than participants with lower body fat percentages (Table 1).

The distributions of the semen characteristics, testicular volume, and reproductive hormones are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S1.

Primary analysis

Compared to having a normal body fat percentage, high or very high body fat percentage were associated with lower total sperm counts, SHBG, and testosterone levels, but higher levels of LH, estradiol, and FAI (Fig. 1). Tendencies toward lower semen volume and concentration were observed with higher body fat percentage, although the confidence intervals crossed the null. No consistent patterns were observed for motility, morphology, testicular volume, and FSH. For low body fat percentage, the individual estimates were generally in the opposite direction of those for high and very high body fat percentages, but all confidence intervals crossed the null.

Secondary analyses

In the first secondary analysis, we included body fat percentage in SD (1 SD = 5.7%) as a linear term in the regression model (Table 2) as the unadjusted spline plots were reasonably compatible with linearity (Supplementary Fig. S3). As in the main analysis, a higher body fat percentage was associated with lower total sperm count, SHBG, and testosterone, but higher levels of LH, estradiol, and FAI. Only tendencies were observed for semen volume and sperm concentration (Table 2), with confidence intervals crossing the null. When BMI in SD (1 SD = 3.5 kg/m2) and waist-to-height ratio in SD (1 SD = 0.05) were used as measures of exposure, the results for the reproductive hormones were comparable, but the associations for total sperm count and the tendencies for semen volume and sperm concentration attenuated (Table 2).

In the second secondary analysis, we restricted the analysis to men considered normal weight according to their BMI (BMI: 18.5–24.9 kg/m2): the adjusted relative difference per 1-SD increase in body fat percentage was even stronger for total sperm count (−13% vs −8%) and normal morphology (−7 vs −1%), while only tendencies toward lower semen volume, sperm concentration, and proportion of motile spermatozoa were observed (Table 3), whereas the findings for reproductive hormone levels were generally attenuated. In this analysis, the results remained essentially the same after further adjustment for BMI as a continuous variable (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

In the first sensitivity analysis, we repeated the main analysis with restriction to complete cases (n = 978, 92.4%), and the results remained essentially the same (Supplementary Fig. S4). In the second sensitivity analysis, the association for childhood BMI at 11 years was comparable to those for adult BMI (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

We found that body fat percentage was inversely and exposure-dependently associated with total sperm count and with similar tendencies observed for semen volume and concentration, although with less precision. Higher body fat percentage was also associated with lower SHBG and testosterone levels. Conversely, with increased body fat percentage, we observed higher estradiol and LH levels as well as higher FAI. Body fat percentage was stronger associated with semen volume, total sperm count, and concentration than BMI and waist-to-height ratio were. It may therefore be a better measure of the underlying effective exposure, which may be the amount of adipose tissue. Of note, higher body fat percentage was even stronger associated with total sperm count and normal morphology after the analysis was restricted to normal weight men (according to BMI: 18.5–24.9 kg/m2) albeit the associations for hormone levels attenuated.

The relative difference in total sperm count of −15% for very high body fat percentage (≥25% body fat) corresponds to a change in the absolute mean from 105 to 89 million (mill). Similarly, the relative difference of −23% in total sperm count for high body fat percentage (20–24% body fat) corresponds to a decrease from 105 to 81 mill. While the mean total sperm count was well above the WHO lower reference limits of 39 mill (Cooper et al., 2010), these reductions may be clinically relevant for the smaller proportion of men with borderline semen quality. The other semen characteristics generally remained above the WHO lower reference limits for all exposure groups (Supplementary Table S1). Estimates for semen volume and concentration were smaller and less precise but pointed in the same direction, whereas estimates for the remaining semen characteristics were close to null. Although the relative differences were modest, even small population-level shifts could potentially increase the proportion of men falling below clinical threshold.

The main strengths of the present study include the large study population, use of bioelectrical impedance to measure body fat percentage, comparison with other measures of body composition (BMI and waist-to-height ratio), possibility to explore reverse causation, detailed adjustment for prospectively collected confounders, and use of selections weights. We have not used a strict statistical significance criterion that adjusts for multiple testing as this comes at a high risk of Type 2 errors. Instead, we have reported all associations irrespective of their statistical significance, fully acknowledging this increases the likelihood of spurious, chance findings (Type 1 errors). We counteracted this by looking for consistency and biological coherence.

Only 19% of the invited men participated in FEPOS. This implicates a potential risk for selection bias if participation is related to both the exposure and outcome. However, most men at 19 years of age are probably unaware of their fecundity. Further, a validation study of selection bias within the FEPOS cohort found negligible selection bias under realistic scenarios of differential non-participation using a multidimensional bias analysis approach (Gaml-Sorensen et al., 2022). Finally, we used selection weights to accommodate the risk of selection bias (Hernan et al., 2004).

Measurement of body fat percentage by bioelectrical impedance is not a perfect measure of body fat. A validation study found a correlation of 0.852 between measurement of body fat percentage by bioelectrical impedance compared to the golden standard, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (Verney et al., 2015). Some of the measurement error may be due to bioelectrical impedance’s sensitivity to variation in hydration status (Schierbauer et al., 2023). In this context, the hydration status in our participants could be affected as the measurement were done non-fasting without instruction to abstain from exercise prior to measurement. However, this measurement error is most likely non-differential causing bias toward the null, and hence, cannot explain the observed non-null associations. Another source of error is the wide day-to-day variations in semen characteristics although this is also not expected to introduce systematic error (Stokes-Riner et al., 2007).

As we collected information on both exposure and outcomes during the clinical visit (i.e. cross-sectionally), we cannot exclude reverse causation. In a sensitivity analysis, we used childhood BMI at 11 years (measured around 8 years before collecting the outcome information) as the exposure to exclude the possibility of reverse causation. Reassuringly, the results, when childhood BMI was used as the exposure, were similar to those when using adult BMI (Supplementary Table S2). Hereby, reverse causation seems less likely.

The associations between body fat percentage and semen characteristics might be confounded by lifestyle and social factors. In the present study, we had more detailed information on lifestyle factors of the participants (physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption) and the mother (smoking and BMI) as well as information of socioeconomic status, than in previous studies (Ehala-Aleksejev and Punab, 2015; Tsao et al., 2015; Chang et al., 2018; Hansen et al., 2023). This allowed for more detailed confounder adjustment. Still, we cannot exclude that our results were affected by residual confounding.

The low participation rate may also affect the generalizability. To counter this, we reweighted the data using selection weights to create a pseudopopulation that is comparable to all invited men in terms of socioeconomic position and lifestyle factors.

To our knowledge, the associations between body fat percentage and measures of semen characteristics have only been investigated in five previous studies. Tsao et al. (2015) reported no association between body fat percentage and sperm concentration, motility and morphology adjusted for BMI, smoking, age, and markers of cardio-metabolic health in a cross-sectional study of 7630 young healthy Taiwanese men working in the same private company (Tsao et al., 2015). However, almost 75% of the men had a high or very high body fat percentage according to our definition (Tsao et al., 2015). Ehala-Aleksejev et al. (2015) studied 260 fertile Estonian men and found that high body fat percentage, measured by a bioimpedance analyzer, was associated with lower total sperm count and semen volume (Ehala-Aleksejev and Punab, 2015). Chang et al. (2018) studied 125 infertile men and found that high body fat percentage was associated with lower semen volume, poorer motility, and lower percentage of sperm with normal morphology; however, only the abstract was available in English (Chang et al., 2018). In a cross-sectional study, Hansen et al. (2022) studied 2289 Danish military recruits and found that body fat percentage measured by dual X-ray absorptiometry was associated with only lower total sperm count (Hansen et al., 2023). Uzun et al. (2024) used data on 121 young men to develop a machine learning prediction model for testicular function based on anthropometrics and metabolic risk factors; however, body fat percentage was not associated with semen characteristics in the prediction model (Uzun et al., 2024).

We observed a disrupted hormonal profile in terms of lower SHBG and total testosterone and higher LH and estradiol levels with increasing body fat percentage. The free androgen index increased with fat percentage probably owing to the low SHBG concentration among participants with high body fat percentage. Similar directions of associations for reproductive hormonal profiles have previously been reported for BMI (Salas-Huetos et al., 2021).

From a biological perspective, body fat percentage may better capture the underlying effective exposure, adiposity, than BMI, a composite measure of body weight and height that is also affected by other factors such as muscle and bone mass. This is empirically supported by our data as body fat percentage appeared to be stronger associated with impaired semen characteristics than BMI and waist-to-height ratio. The importance of body fat percentage was further supported by the stronger associations between body fat percentage and total sperm count and normal morphology among the normal weight (BMI 18.0–24.9 kg/m2) men, although the associations for the reproductive hormones attenuated.

Future research should replicate the associations between body fat percentage and semen characteristics among normal weight men, while exploring the role of leptin. While the associations between body fat percentage and semen quality were stronger for normal weight men, the associations for the hormonal levels were attenuated, thereby questioning the biological coherence. This calls for replication in future studies. Based on evidence from animal studies, we speculate that leptin, a satiety hormone positively correlated with body fat mass (Ostlund et al., 1996), may pose a potential biological explanation for our finding: normal-weight rodents treated with leptin presented with poorer semen characteristics, suggesting that leptin may be one mediator of the possible adverse effect of body fat on male reproductive health (Almabhouh et al., 2020). Unfortunately, we had no information on leptin levels, to further explore this hypothesis.

In conclusion, we found that higher body fat percentage was exposure-dependently associated with impaired semen characteristics and disrupted hormonal profiles. Body fat percentage was stronger associated with impaired semen characteristics than BMI and waist-hip circumference were, suggesting that body fat percentage is a better measure of the true exposure, adiposity. The associations for body fat percentage were even stronger among normal weight men with regards to semen characteristics. If our associations reflect causality, they suggest that a higher percentage of adipose tissue might adversely affect semen production and hormonal balance.
