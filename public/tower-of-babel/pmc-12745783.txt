Promoters of cooperation in evolutionary games
Authors: Jakub Svoboda, Krishnendu Chatterjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEvolutionary games on graphs are a famous framework describing dynamics over networks for many applications, from biology to physics. Individuals reside on nodes of the network, collect payoffs from pairwise interactions with neighbors, and, based on payoffs, update their strategies. Despite the wide applicability of the model, the only known networks that provably promote cooperation do so only for weak selection. We present networks that promote the spread of cooperative behavior in medium and strong selection. Our networks are robust against small changes of the parameters of the game. Moreover, the networks ensure that the spread of cooperation happens fast. Due to the broad applicability of evolutionary games, our networks are significant in many domains.

Evolutionary games provide a broad and flexible mathematical framework for a wide range of problems in biology, e.g., in ecology and evolution (1, 2), frequency-dependent selection (3), and social dynamics (4, 5) as well as in the social evolution (6, 7). In evolutionary games, agents adopt simple strategies, the payoff from pairwise interactions of strategies is given by the payoff matrix. Payoffs translate to fitness, depending on the selection strength, and fitness determines reproductive success. This simple yet elegant and powerful mathematical framework models applications from diverse disciplines.

The most studied class of payoff matrices for social dilemmas is Prisoners’ dilemma, where there are two actions, namely, cooperation and defection (8). In Prisoners’ dilemma, the cooperative behavior produces the highest average payoff, but a selfish individual can obtain a higher payoff by significantly lowering the payoff of the cooperative player. Donation games represent an important subclass of Prisoners’ dilemma where a cooperator (C) pays cost c for the other player to receive benefit b, and a defector (D) does not pay any cost nor provide any benefit. For such games, the key parameter is β=b/c the benefit-to-cost ratio, and this single parameter quite elegantly captures the essence of donation games. While mathematically, cooperation is never favored, in the nature we observe collaborative behavior across various domains (9–15). There are several directions of research to explain the collaborative behavior (6), e.g., repeated interaction as studied under direct reciprocity (16, 17); the notion of reputation in indirect reciprocity (18, 19); and the role of population structure (20–22). In this work, we focus on the last item.

In evolutionary games on graphs, see refs. 2 and 3, the population structure is represented by a graph. Every vertex is occupied by a single individual that follows a simple strategy in donation games, C or D. The edges represent the interactions. The process evolves over multiple rounds. In one round, every individual plays a donation game with all neighbors and collects a payoff from the games. The payoff Px of individual x is then translated to fitness by an exponential function esPx, where s is the parameter for the strength of selection. One individual is randomly chosen to update its strategy, the new strategy is selected among the neighbors proportionally to the fitness. Eventually, one strategy replaces the other, this event is called fixation and we are interested in the fixation probability of cooperators.

Evolutionary games on graphs have been widely studied from many perspectives. Ohtsuki et al. (22) examine which parameters and graphs influence the spread of cooperation. Traulsen et al. (4) show that human behavior in the real world can be modeled by evolutionary games. Many works (21, 23, 24) empirically examine natural graphs that approximate the social networks (scale-free graphs). Despite the sustained efforts, the existence of graphs that promote cooperative behavior for a wide range of parameters is unknown. Allen et al. (25) show structures promoting cooperation for β>1 in weighted graphs and the limit of weak selection (s tends to 0). Fotouhi et al. (26) present structures promoting cooperation for β≥3/2 and the limit of weak selection. Svoboda and Chatterjee (27) present structures promoting cooperation for spatial games (representing the limit of strong selection).

A lot of theoretical works are interested in weak selection (25, 26, 28) where the fitness is equal to 1+sPx for payoff Px and small s. Besides the relevance of weak selection, a technique developed in ref. 22 allows the efficient computation of the fixation probabilities, which is not known for general selection strength. In our setting, weak selection is the limit of s→0, in that case, esPx≈1+sPx. However, the fitness esPx for individual x with payoff Px is the only function that satisfies all natural axioms for the fitness and generalizes to higher selection strengths, see ref. 29. The focus on the weak selection also means that the fixation probability of one randomly placed cooperator in the graph full of defectors (or the other way around) is always close to 1n, where n is the size of the network. Hence, in the regime of weak selection, a graph promotes cooperation if the fixation probability of a randomly placed cooperator into the graph of defectors exceeds 1n.

While for weak selection, previous works compare the ability of the network to promote cooperation against the neutral case, in general, the comparison is between the ratio of fixation probabilities of cooperators vs. defectors. We define ratio promoters, these are graphs on which one randomly placed cooperator fixates with significantly higher probability than one defector placed among cooperators. While the basic definition of promoters of cooperation is with respect to parameter values of s and β, in addition, we consider the robustness property that requires promotion of cooperation for a range of parameter values. We say that a promoter is (ε,γ)-robust if it is a promoter for all β≥1+ε and all s≥γ. In other words, we consider robustness with respect to the parameters of selection strength s and benefit-to-cost ratio β.

Given the previous results in the literature, there are several long-standing open questions: (a) do promoters of cooperation exist for medium and strong selection for 1<β<2 (for β>2 the cycle graph is a promoter); and (b) do unweighted promoters of cooperation exist for weak selection and for 1<β<1.5? Moreover, since the existence of promoters has been open with respect to the selection strength, in particular, the notion of robust promoters of cooperation have not even been considered in the literature, though this is a desirable property of promoters of cooperation.

In this work, we present a graph family C(n,d) parameterized by the number of vertices n and the maximum degree d. First, for all β>1 and s>0, we show that there exists d such that C(n,d) are promoters of cooperation. Thus, we answer questions (a) and (b) above in the affirmative. Second, we show that for all ε>0 and γ>0, there exists d such that C(n,d) are (ε,γ)-robust promoters of cooperation. In addition, we show that C(n,d) guarantees high fixation probability if cooperators start as a small randomly distributed fraction of the population. Finally, we empirically evaluate the behavior of C(n,d) for small population size n and compare it to the behavior of many well-known graphs from the literature.

Results

Model.

Games on graphs model the spread of strategies over networks. Every vertex (or node) of the network represents an individual and the edges of the network represent interactions between individuals. We examine the spread of cooperation in donation games, a well-studied instance of a Prisoner’s dilemma. In these games, there are two strategies: cooperation and defection. In every pairwise interaction, the cooperator pays a cost of 1 for a neighbor to gain β, where β>1 represents the benefit-to-cost ratio. The defector pays nothing and provides no benefit. Hence, for a defector, the payoff is β times the number of neighboring cooperators, and for a cooperator, the payoff is β times the number of neighboring cooperators minus the number of neighbors.

The process consists of four repeating steps: First, every individual x plays the donation game with all neighbors and collects payoff Px. Second, the payoff is transformed to fitness, which is equal esPx, where s is a parameter that denotes the strength of selection. Third, one of the vertices, x, is randomly selected for a strategy update (or death). Finally, among all neighbors of x, vertex u is selected randomly proportionally to the fitness (it is esPu) and x adopts the strategy of u (or u reproduces to replace x). This corresponds to death–Birth (dB) updating (22) with a payoff-to-fitness function that generalizes the weak selection (29). The model is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Eventually, one of the strategies spreads over the whole network, we call this event fixation. For graph G and donation game parameterized by benefit-to-cost ratio β and selection strength s, we denote ρC(G,β,s) the probability that one cooperator placed uniformly at random in the network G full of defectors spreads over the whole network. We call this quantity fixation probability of cooperators. Similarly, we denote ρD(G,β,s), the probability that one defector placed in the network G full of cooperators spreads over the whole network, we refer to this quantity as the fixation probability of defectors. Moreover, we consider density fixation probability, ρC(G,β,s,p), which is the probability that cooperators fixate on G when initially every vertex is a cooperator with probability p and a defector otherwise.

While ratio promoters consider the ratio of fixation probability for a single mutant, we consider two other variants: a) instead of comparing ratio of fixation probability we compare against the neutral case as baseline (corresponding to ρC(G,β,0) in our notation); and b) instead of a single mutant we examine a small fraction of initial mutant population. These variants give rise to the following notions of promoters. Neutral case promoter is a graph family where the fixation probability of cooperators divided by 1n goes to infinity in the limit of large population size. Density promoter is a graph family where for every constant p, density fixation probability (ρC(G,β,s,p)) approaches 1 in the limit of large population size. To summarize, the ratio promoter ensures that one cooperator fixates with higher probability than one defector. Neutral case promoter ensures that one cooperator fixates with higher probability than 1n and is used mainly in weak selection. Density promoter ensures that if a small fraction of the population consists of cooperators, then cooperators fixate almost surely.

Robustness with respect to β and s.

The above notion of promoter requires the promotion of cooperation for specific values of the parameters β and s. We also consider the notion of robustness, which requires that the promotion of cooperation happens for a range of parameter values β and s. Formally, given constants ε>0 and γ>0, a graph family G is an (ε,γ)-robust with respect to a property if G satisfies the property for all β≥1+ε and all s≥γ. In particular, we are interested in the existence of (ε,γ)-robust ratio promoters, neutral promoters, and density promoters.

The notion of robustness is with respect to the parameters of the Prisoners’ dilemma. In our model, we focus on the death–Birth updating, where the death is followed by a reproduction. The Birth–death updating explored mainly in the context of constant selection usually changes the mutant promotion properties of graphs (in constant selection), see refs. 30–32 and seems to be impossible for frequency-dependent selection (22). There are different ways to treat the degree heterogeneity with respect to payoffs: Payoffs can be either accumulated or averaged by the degree. For the averaged payoffs, the promotion of cooperation is harder to achieve (21, 29, 33, 34), and we do not attempt it here. Finally, we note that our construction is robust toward the initial placement of the mutant. Since the process is death–Birth, every individual is replaced with the same rate, if the parent migrates, then it is even more likely that the mutant arises in a big vertex, which we show is the ideal starting position for a lone cooperator. For details, see ref. 35.

We comment on three aspects related to previous results of the literature: First, previous works (22, 25, 26) consider promotion of cooperation in the setting of weak selection. In weak selection, the notion of promoter of cooperation coincides with our notion of neutral case promoter, i.e., the small influence of the payoff for reproductive success can change the fixation probability only slightly above 1n. Second, previous works (22, 25, 26) for weak selection also considers the least benefit-to-cost ratio, referred to as β∗, such that promotion of cooperation is guaranteed for all β above β∗. While this was done in the setting of weak selection, our notion of (ε,γ) robustness extends it to all selection strength, in particular, for weak selection, setting β∗=1+ε coincides with the previous notions of the literature. Third, McAvoy and Allen (36) show that in the weak selection, the notion of β∗ might not paint the full picture regarding the fixation probability. Our definition of ratio promoters also requires the ratio between the fixation probability of cooperators and the fixation probability of defectors to grow with increasing size of the population. There might be better graphs that promote the cooperation more but have higher β∗.

Promoter Graph Family.

In this work, our main contribution is to present cooperation promoters, a graph family that provably promotes cooperation. We present the graph structure below.

For a given n and d, promoter of cooperation C(n,d) consists of two types of vertices, namely, path vertices and support vertices. The path vertices are further classified into big vertices and bridge vertices. First, the path vertices form a line (or a path) that starts with a bridge vertex, followed by two big vertices, then a bridge vertex, then two big vertices, and the pattern repeats. Every vertex in the path has an edge to the vertex before and after it. The bridge vertices have no other neighbors. Every support vertex is a leaf: It is only connected to one big vertex and has no other neighbors. Every big vertex has d−2 support vertices as neighbors. We call pair neighborhood the pair of two big vertices and all of their neighbors. The structure of the promoter, together with the sketch of the spread of cooperation is described in Fig. 2.

Namely, the path vertices forming a path, a big vertex with many neighbors, the support vertices connected to the big vertex, and a bridge vertex connecting two consecutive big vertices. The initial cooperator needs to appear in a big vertex; otherwise, the cooperation dies out with high probability. (B) This panel shows the cooperator in the big vertex converting all its support neighbors to cooperation. This event happens with significant probability. (C) When the cooperator in the big vertex has many cooperating neighbors, it can spread to the neighboring big and bridge vertex. (D) Afterward, the cooperators have a high chance of spreading over the whole pair neighborhood, which consists of two connected big vertices and all their neighbors. (E) The cooperators in one pair neighborhood are more likely to claim one more pair neighborhood than to lose one, which means the spread of cooperation is favored.

We intuitively describe the spread of cooperation, and from this, we derive the bound for d, the maximal degree. When a new cooperator is placed into a big vertex in an otherwise empty pair neighborhood, the spread of cooperation has two components: combinatorial and payoff-based. First, the combinatorial spread ensures that all d−2 support vertices are converted into cooperation without the big vertex being replaced by a defector. This happens with probability proportional to 1d and the payoff of the cooperator does not play any role, since the replaced defectors do not have any other neighbors. Second, the payoff-based spread describes how the big vertex, now with d−2 cooperating neighbors, converts the rest of the pair neighborhood. The payoff from donation game of this big vertex is (d−2)(β−1)−2, which means the fitness ises((d−2)(β−1)−2).

In the case that the neighboring big vertex updates its strategy, the cooperator in the big vertex competes with d−1 defectors with payoff 0 (and fitness 1). To have a high chance of spreading, the fitness of a cooperator, es((d−2)(β−1)−2), needs to be at least of order d, we select e3d6, which translates to[1]s((d−2)(β−1)−2)≥3+6logd.

We choose the minimal d to satisfy the equation. Note that for any given constants β>1 and s>0, there exists a constant d to satisfy Eq. 1. After all the neighbors of a cooperator in a big vertex are cooperators, the whole pair neighborhood is claimed with high probability.

When at least one pair neighborhood belongs to cooperators, the ratio between claiming another pair neighborhood and losing a pair neighborhood is higher than 1, which means the spread of cooperators over pair neighborhoods can be modeled by a biased Markov chain. The claim that the pair neighborhoods are more likely to be claimed by cooperators uses the following facts: First, the defector claims a big vertex in a pair neighborhood held by cooperators with probability at most e−3d−6 (from Eq. 1), since the neighboring cooperator in the big vertex has at least d−1 cooperating neighbors, and the bridge vertex can have at most 2 cooperating neighbors. Second, the cooperation spreads to the neighboring big vertex with probability proportional to 1de−s, since bridge vertex with fitness es(β−2) competes with d−1 defectors with fitness 1. Third, the lone cooperator in a big vertex spreads over the pair neighborhood with probability roughly 1d2. Fourth, if this spread over the pair neighborhood fails, the abandoned cooperators in support vertices help the defectors to resit or to spread against cooperators. However, the defectors replace these misplaced cooperators quickly, relative to the probability of adverse spread. Finally, carefully bounding all the facts gives the desired inequality for the ratio.

Analytical results for time and mutation.

We present two important results related to promoters of cooperation (see SI Appendix for more details).

First, in many cases of evolutionary processes and evolutionary games, the time to fixation can be large, e.g., exponential in the size of the population (37, 38) or proportional to the weights of edges (25). However, fast fixation time, which is polynomial in the population structure, is desirable. We show that for our graph family the fixation time, i.e., the expected number of steps until fixation, for cooperators, is at most quadratic (a low-degree polynomial) in the size of the population. This result has important implications for the effective rate of evolution (39) (SI Appendix).

Second, we examine the spread of cooperation in C(n,d) in the presence of mutation. Our main result shows that the ratio ρCρD is high and also the time to fixation is fast. The above two facts are the main reasons that our structure for a low mutation rate quickly reaches and maintains high cooperator density. We explore this aspect in more detail in the simulation results.

Discussion

Our work presents an important advancement in the area of evolutionary games on graphs by presenting population structures that promote cooperation for any selection strength and benefit-to-cost ratio exceeding 1. Moreover, the structures enjoy many desirable properties, such as robustness with respect to the parameter values and they ensure a fast spread of cooperation within the quadratic number of steps in the size of the network. We discuss several related lines of research and possible extensions of our work.

First, in the area of evolutionary processes on graphs, the most basic case is the constant (or frequency-independent) fitness. This direction has been widely explored in the context of the Moran process (20). The most important research results establish the existence of promoters for advantageous mutants (41, 42) (called amplifiers). Another important aspect is the time to fixation (39), previous research shows an interesting tradeoff between fixation probability and fixation time (43). Amplifiers with fast fixation time have also been established in ref. 44. However, the mathematical techniques for these works do not extend to the more general model of evolutionary games, which represent frequency-dependent selection.

Second, the study of evolutionary games on graphs where the fitness depends on the composition of the population is referred to as frequency-dependent selection. This topic has also been extensively studied (22, 45). From the perspective of computational efficiency, hardness results (or impossibility of efficient computational methods) have been established for strong selection in refs. 37 and 38. In the weak selection regime, efficient computational methods exist (22, 25). The existence of promoters or cooperation for weak selection and β≥3/2 has been shown in ref. 25, and (26) present natural graphs and empirical results in this regime. These results do not extend to medium or strong selection to the best of our knowledge. There are also promoters of cooperation for spatial games (27), however, this represents a deterministic case which is the limit of strong selection, and this result does not apply to the stochastic evolutionary games on graphs. On one hand, the existence of promoters of cooperation for medium and strong selection has been open; on the other hand, the absence of efficient computational methods implies that an exhaustive search to find desirable population structures is not a feasible method. Hence mathematical and analytical techniques were developed to establish the existence of graphs that promote cooperation which our work achieves.

Finally, there are several extensions to evolutionary games, such as direct reciprocity (8, 16) with crosstalks (46), indirect reciprocity (18) in the presence of noise and imperfect information (47, 48). Our model does not capture these scenarios. Extending our results to more general models is an important direction for future work. There are also extensions of evolutionary games on graphs where every individual interacts with a subset of neighbors, this area represents Network Exchange Theory (49). The extensions of our results to such scenarios are also another possible direction for future work. Finally, there is a wide simulation-based research in the area of evolutionary games on graphs, see refs. 50–52. While these works present impressive numerical results, they do not provide analytical guarantees. In contrast, our work presents an analytical guarantee for promotion of cooperation. Exploring our structures, or networks inspired by our structures with mathematical guarantees, in thorough simulation-based studies is a practical direction of future work.

Randomly Placed Cooperator.

In SI Appendix, we show that one randomly placed cooperator in C(n,d) has a constant fixation probability (that depends on d). Concretely, we show that for all n, s>0, β>1, and all d that satisfies Eq. 1, we have[2]ρC(C(n,d),β,s)≥310d3.

The proof of Eq. 2 requires two statements. The first is that one randomly placed cooperator spreads over one pair neighborhood (two neighboring big vertices and all their neighbors) with probability at least[3]35d3.

The second is that the cooperators that occupy at least one pair neighborhood are twice as likely to spread to another pair neighborhood than to lose one pair neighborhood. These two statements prove Eq. 2, since when cooperators occupy at least one pair neighborhood, we can model the number of pair neighborhoods occupied by cooperators as a Markov Chain with positive bias.
