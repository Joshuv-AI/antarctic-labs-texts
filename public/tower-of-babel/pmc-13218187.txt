Unveiling the burden of long covid in hospital and community settings: findings from the Post-Acute Sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 Network (PASCNET) cohort study in Italy’s pandemic epicentre
Authors: Laura Antolini, Maria Grazia Valsecchi, Anna Bussi, Giuseppe La Piana, Elisabetta Pagani, Maria Grazia Pascarella, Andrea Patroni, Ivan Pellegrino, Anna Pozzi, Mario Sorlini, Marianna Ticozzelli, Marco Villa, Mario Zappa, Antonio Giampiero Russo, Claudio Lucifora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPost-COVID-19 condition (PCC) has emerged as a major public health concern. We aimed to estimate the 1-year incidence of PCC in adults with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection in Lombardy, Italy, comparing community-managed and hospitalised patients and to assess the prognostic value of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Researching COVID to Enhance Recovery (RECOVER) score to support estimation of long-term PCC prevalence.DesignRetrospective-prospective observational cohort study enrolling patients infected between 1 March 2020 and 31 December 2022. The study visit was conducted between 16 January and 23 December 2024.SettingMulticentre study involving seven public hospitals and general practitioners across Lombardy.ParticipantsRandomly sampled adults aged 18–70 years with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection. Hospitalised patients (HP) were admitted for COVID-19; general practitioner patients (GPP) were managed in the community. The total sample comprised: 1162 (546 HP, 616 GPP).InterventionThis is an observational study with no active intervention.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcome: 1-year incidence of PCC retrospectively assessed at the study visit.Secondary outcomes: symptom profiles, long-term PCC prevalence at the study visit and predictive value of the NIH RECOVER score.ResultsMedian age was 57.1 years in HP and 42.9 years in GPP; 66.1% of HP and 47.7% of GPP were male. PCC developed in 280 patients (223 HP, 57 GPP). The 1-year cumulative incidence was 39.9% in HP (95% CI 35.9% to 44.1%) and 9.1% in GPP (95% CI 7.1% to 11.7%). The NIH RECOVER score was associated with PCC at 1 year (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.21). Model-based long-term PCC prevalence was 31.8% in HP and 6.3% in GPP.ConclusionsPCC remained frequent and heterogeneous, particularly among previously HP. In this cohort, the NIH RECOVER score showed prognostic value for estimating longer-term PCC burden. These findings underscore the need for structured long-term follow-up across both hospital and primary care settings.

Introduction

4 years after the first wave of SARS-CoV-2, a sizeable minority of survivors remain affected by ‘long COVID’, also termed post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC) or post-COVID-19 condition (PCC), a chronic, multisystem illness with symptoms that linger, relapse or newly emerge after the acute phase.

Current definitions hinge on two temporal anchors. Public health practice often uses a ≥4 week threshold in registries and surveys; for example, a nationwide Israeli analysis of 1.9 million PCR-tested individuals classified long covid as symptoms persisting or appearing beyond 4 weeks from diagnosis.1 European clinical series and WHO guidance favour a stricter ≥12-week minimum: cardiology groups diagnose long covid when manifestations persist for at least 12 weeks from onset,2 and the Swedish PCC registry assigns International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code U09.9 no earlier than 90 days after infection.3 The US National Institutes of Health Researching COVID to Enhance Recovery (RECOVER) programme initially adopted a 12-week approach and later developed a 37-symptom machine-learning score to identify PASC at the first study visit, at least 6 months after infection.4

Electronic health record studies have extended follow-up further. Among 273 618 US survivors, 57% had at least one of nine hallmark symptom clusters within 6 months, and more than one-third remained symptomatic between 3 and 6 months.5 In an insurance-based cohort, excess cardiovascular and pulmonary risks persisted through the first post-infection year.6 A population-based analysis also showed higher healthcare utilisation in the 6 months after infection than in uninfected comparators.7

Even individuals who were never hospitalised may experience long-term sequelae, although most mild cases in an Israeli cohort improved within 12 months.1 Despite heterogeneity, several points have become clear. Long covid is multisystem, with fatigue, post-exertional malaise, ‘brain fog’, dysautonomia-like palpitations, dyspnoea, chest or throat pain, gastrointestinal disturbances, myalgia and dysosmia/dysgeusia among the most reproducible features.4 8 Risk rises with acute-phase severity but is not confined to the critically ill; more than half of non-hospitalised US patients reported at least one long covid feature.5 Follow-up clinics report that only 23% of patients are completely symptom-free at 12 months.2

Taking together, these findings support a pragmatic definition of PCC as symptoms, signs or measurable impairments that begin during or after confirmed or probable infection, persist or recur beyond the acute phase and are not explained by alternative diagnoses. This approach accommodates the 90-day threshold used in surveillance while acknowledging the relapsing-remitting course observed clinically.

Recent USA estimates suggest that 3.6% of adults currently experience PCC, with about two-thirds reporting activity-limiting symptoms.9 Fatigue remains common; in a highly vaccinated Danish sample, fatigue scores increased by 3% and post-exertional malaise nearly doubled between 2 and 18 months after infection. The burden was consistently higher among previously hospitalised participants.10

Published Italian follow-up studies have largely focused on previously hospitalised cohorts, including multicentre and single-centre studies with 12-month or longer follow-up after hospital discharge.1113 By contrast, fewer data are available on PCC in community-managed patients and on direct comparison between hospital and primary care settings within the same regional framework. This gap is especially relevant in Lombardy, one of the earliest and hardest-hit pandemic epicentres in Europe, where both hospital and primary care services had to absorb the long-term consequences of SARS-CoV-2 infection. The Post-Acute Sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 Network (PASCNET) study was designed to address this gap by estimating the 1-year incidence of PCC in adults with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection in Lombardy, separately in hospitalised and community-managed patients, and by assessing the prognostic value of the NIH RECOVER score to support estimation of longer-term PCC prevalence at the study visit.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This multicentre retrospective-prospective observational study enrolled adults aged 18–70 years residing in Lombardy with a first confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection between 1 March 2020 and 31 December 2022, spanning three pandemic waves (wave 1: 1 January 2020 to 31 July 2020; wave 2: 1 August 2020 to 31 January 2021; wave 3: 1 February 2021 to 31 December 2022). Two samples were defined. Hospital patients (HP) were admitted primarily for COVID-19, identified by prespecified International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes and hospitalised in one of seven public hospitals (Aziende Socio-Sanitarie Territoriali (Agenzie di Tutela della Salute (ASST)) and Istituti di Ricovero e Cura a Carattere Scientifico (IRCCS)) within participating ATS territories. General practitioner patients (GPP) had a recorded positive SARS-CoV-2 test, resided within participating ATS territories at diagnosis and enrolment, were never hospitalised for COVID-19 and were registered with IML (Iniziativa Medica Lombarda) general practitioners at the time of infection.

Patients and data collection

Eligible individuals were sampled and contacted by hospitals or general practitioners (GPs) and were enrolled after written informed consent, up to budget caps. Exclusions were death before enrolment, major comorbidities at infection or admission per-protocol and loss of Lombardy residency before enrolment (online supplemental table 1 presents the complete list of inclusion and exclusion criteria).

The index date for all time-to-event analyses was the documented date of confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection that made the participant eligible for the study. For GPP, this corresponded to the date of the positive SARS-CoV-2 test recorded in GP records; for HP, it corresponded to the acute COVID-19 episode documented in hospital records and leading to admission. The study visit was the single in-person follow-up assessment, performed between 16 January and 23 December 2024.

At the study visit, we collected both retrospective data covering the first year after the index infection and contemporaneous clinical data at the time of the visit. Data were collected in hospitals for HP and in GP offices for GPP.

This included patient-reported information on the onset of PCC-related symptoms during the first year after infection, together with review of hospital or GP medical records to document conditions relevant to possible PCC aetiology or severity, including the date of PCC occurrence.

PCC was clinically defined as symptoms arising after infection or initial recovery, lasting at least 2 months and affecting daily functioning. The symptoms considered are listed in online supplemental table 2A. Recovery from PCC was assessed on the basis of resolution of the relevant symptoms, as documented in clinical records and verified with the patient at the study visit; the date of recovery was collected when available. Information on SARS-CoV-2 vaccination before infection was also recorded at the study visit. Vaccination in Lombardy began on 27 December 2020, towards the end of the second wave.

At the study visit, participants underwent a standardised reassessment of PCC-related symptoms together with vital signs, first-level laboratory testing and first-level instrumental assessment. Vital signs included heart rate (beats per minute), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (mm Hg), respiratory rate (breaths per minute), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2, %) and body temperature (°C). All participants, irrespective of prior medical history, also underwent a fasting laboratory panel on the same day, including coagulation, inflammatory, haematological, metabolic, renal, liver and lipid parameters. Pulse oximetry was measured at the study visit; electrocardiography was prescribed and recorded as normal or abnormal, and additional specialist or second-level instrumental examinations were requested according to the symptom profile.

The updated NIH RECOVER score14 was calculated from PCC symptoms collected both during the first year after infection (retrospectively) and at the study visit, as shown in online supplemental table 2B.

Data were entered into a dedicated electronic database compliant with the General Data Protection Regulation; records were pseudonymised and accessible only to authorised staff.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in setting the research question, selecting outcome measures, designing the study, planning the analyses or disseminating the results.

Statistical design and analysis

The planned sample sizes (1260 for HP and 600 for GPP) reflected practical considerations on feasibility and the need to achieve acceptable width of CIs for PCC incidence at 1 year from infection. Different scenarios for the cumulative incidence of PCC at 1 year were considered, ranging from 10% to 40% and a desired width of the 95% CI was set in the range from 0.04 to 0.07.

Descriptive analyses of demographic and clinical characteristics, symptoms at infection, symptoms at PCC onset, symptoms at the study visit and other clinical information were conducted overall and stratified by cohort (HP or GPP). Frequencies of major characteristics and symptom types were compared between cohorts using the χ2 test.

The cumulative incidence of PCC during the first year after infection and the probability of recovering from PCC were estimated according to Kaplan-Meier and compared between cohorts with the log-rank test.

Among patients who developed PCC within 1 year after infection, the proportion of those who did not recover during that first year was used to estimate PCC prevalence at approximately 1 year after infection (retrospective component). Stratified estimates by infection wave, age group and sex were also provided.

We assessed the prognostic value of the NIH RECOVER score for identifying probable PASC cases in the first year after infection. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% CIs. Sensitivity and specificity of the proposed cut-off were calculated against clinical evaluation. We then fitted a logistic model for PCC occurrence within 1 year after infection to evaluate the RECOVER score as a predictor, expressed as ORs and 95% CIs, together with cohort (HP or GPP); interactions between score and wave and between score and cohort were also tested after adjustment for age, sex, number of comorbidities, education level, SARS-CoV-2 vaccination before infection, alcohol use and smoking at infection. Model results were then used to estimate PCC prevalence at the study visit. First, the RECOVER score was recalculated using symptoms recorded at the study visit. Second, the logistic model was used to predict each participant’s long-term probability of PCC based on the updated score. Long-term PCC prevalence was estimated by averaging these individual predictions.

Finally, the proposed RECOVER score cut-off14 was applied to symptoms recorded at the study visit to derive an alternative estimate of long-term PCC prevalence.

Results

We analysed 1162 participants: 616 GPP and 546 HP (online supplemental figure 1). As described in table 1 (and in online supplemental table 3 by wave), GPP were younger than HP (median 42.9 years, IQR 32.7–54.6 vs 57.1 years, IQR 49.7–62.9) and more often female; the absence of comorbidity was more frequent in GPP (72.3%) than in HP (26.1%). Education and employment levels were higher in GPP (university level 40.3% vs 12.5%; employed 70.9% vs 59.5%). Both cohorts were predominantly Caucasian (94%). GPP were more often single; smoking was more prevalent among GPP (16.1% vs 9.0%), while alcohol use was more commonly reported by HP (61.2% vs 17.2%). Almost all participants vaccinated before infection belonged to wave 3 (210 of 215 vaccinated participants). Median time from infection to the study visit was approximately 4.2, 3.7 and 3.0 years for infections in waves 1, 2 and 3, respectively.

As shown in online supplemental tables 4 and 5 (by wave), at SARS-CoV-2 infection diagnosis both cohorts had high prevalences of influenza-like and respiratory problems. Typical symptoms such as anosmia and ageusia were less frequent in GPP than in HP (25.5% vs 46.0%; co-occurrence was 16.9% vs 36.6%). Musculoskeletal and neurological symptoms were more frequent in GPP (39.4% vs 29.9%), while cardiovascular symptoms were less common overall but slightly more frequent in HP (18.7% vs 12.3%).

During the first year after infection, 280 participants developed PCC (57/616 GPP, 9.3%; 223/546 HP, 40.8%). Considering only the 270 participants with a known date of PCC occurrence, the 1-year cumulative incidence was 9.1% (95% CI 7.1% to 11.7%) in GPP and 39.9% (95% CI 35.9% to 44.1%) in HP (p<0.0001). Incidence was already high by 90 days (8.1% vs 38.7%, respectively), suggesting early onset. Across waves, GPP incidence declined, whereas HP incidence remained relatively stable, consistent with period-related factors such as changing variants and vaccination coverage among community cases. The CI width in HP slightly exceeded the planned target because the enrolled HP sample was smaller than planned (table 2).

As shown in table 3 (and online supplemental table 6), symptoms correlates of PCC during the first year after infection differed by cohort. Overall, the odds of PCC were about sevenfold higher in HP than in GPP (OR 6.77, 95% CI 4.9 to 9.3). In GPP, musculoskeletal symptoms, neurological symptoms and headache were strongly associated with PCC (OR 5.1, 95% CI 2.5 to 10.1; OR 5.4, 95% CI 2.8 to 10.2; OR 16.7, 95% CI 8.1 to 33.9). In HP, musculoskeletal and cardiovascular symptoms were prominent correlates (OR 5.9, 95% CI 3.8 to 9.1; OR 5.6, 95% CI 3.2 to 10.1). Respiratory symptoms were common but associated with lower odds of PCC in both cohorts (OR 0.14, 95% CI 0.1 to 0.3 in GPP; OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.29 to 0.60 in HP). Patients with PCC typically reported two or more symptom classes, whereas nearly half of those without PCC had symptoms confined to a single class in both cohorts. In both GPP and HP, the odds of PCC were approximately halved in patients vaccinated before infection.

At 1 year from infection, estimated prevalence of PCC was 1.9% in GPP and 30.2% in HP, as 12 and 165 patients with PCC did not resolve it, respectively.

At the study visit, when asked about recovery of pre-infection autonomy in daily life, 93.3% of GPP and 75.1% of HP reported recovery; however, this proportion was lower among those whose PCC had not resolved than among those whose PCC had resolved or who had never developed PCC. Symptom frequencies reported at the study visit were consistently higher among participants with unresolved PCC, with persistent neurological symptoms notably present among HP without prior PCC, suggesting residual subclinical burden (online supplemental table 7).

Finally, symptoms were adapted (online supplemental table 2B) to calculate the RECOVER score both for symptoms during the first year (table 3) and for symptoms at the study visit (online supplemental table 7). At 1 year, median RECOVER scores were 4 (IQR 2–10) in GPP with PCC versus 1 (IQR 0–5) without PCC, and 7 (IQR 3–13) in HP with PCC versus 0 (IQR 0–5) without PCC (table 3). The ≥11 cut-point had high specificity but limited sensitivity in both cohorts (GPP 94.1% and 24.6%; HP 87.6% and 35.4%). AUCs were 0.74 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.80) in GPP and 0.78 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.82) in HP. At the study visit (online supplemental table 7), updated RECOVER scores were higher among those with unresolved PCC than among those whose PCC had resolved in both cohorts, and the median score was 0 among participants who never developed PCC.

In the logistic model fitted to all 1162 patients (online supplemental table 8A), each one-point increase in RECOVER score increased the odds of 1-year PCC (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.21), and the cohort effect was strong (HP vs GPP OR 5.61, 95% CI 3.6 to 8.7). Because the interaction between wave and score was significant, the logistic model was also fitted separately by wave (online supplemental table 8B), with RECOVER score ORs per point of 1.13 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.19), 1.15 (95% CI 1.09 to 1.22) and 1.27 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.34) for waves 1, 2 and 3, respectively. In wave 3, vaccination emerged as a strong protective factor for PCC occurrence (OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.50). Model-based long-term prevalence, using updated RECOVER scores at the study visit, was 6.3% in GPP and 31.8% in HP (table 4); applying the ≥11 cut-point at the study visit yielded alternative estimates of 3.1% and 5.9%, respectively.

Discussion

Long-term sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection have emerged as a major, heterogeneous public health challenge. In this multicentre retrospective-prospective cohort study, which included both community-managed patients and survivors of hospital admission, the 1-year incidence of PCC was 9.1% among individuals managed in primary care and 39.9% among previously hospitalised patients. At 2–4 years after infection, model-based PCC prevalence remained 6.3% in GPP and 31.8% in HP.

Strengths and limitations

This study has notable strengths. It combined hospital and community-managed cohorts within the same regional network, allowing direct comparison of PCC burden across care settings. Recruitment was multicentre, participants were sampled through routine care pathways, and data were collected in a standardised pseudonymised database. In addition, the study integrated retrospective ascertainment of the first year after infection with clinical follow-up at the study visit and included infections occurring across three pandemic waves. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, PCC ascertainment during the first year partly relied on patient recall and review of available clinical records, which may have introduced some misclassification, particularly for symptom onset and recovery dates. Second, participation required written informed consent and attendance at an in-person study visit, which may have introduced selection bias, as individuals with persistent symptoms may have been more likely to participate, whereas frailer patients may have been under-represented. Third, generalisability is limited by the restricted age range, protocol-based exclusions and the absence of an uninfected comparator. Finally, because eligibility was anchored to a documented confirmed SARS-CoV-2 episode, prior asymptomatic or otherwise undocumented infections cannot be completely excluded.

Symptom profiles and PCC risk trajectories

Respiratory symptoms were predominant at presentation in both settings, yet anosmia, ageusia and severe dyspnoea were far more frequent among inpatients, whereas headache and neurological manifestations prevailed in community cases. OR estimates showed that, in primary care patients (GPP), neurological and musculoskeletal clusters and headache increased the odds of PCC by 5-fold to 16-fold.

Respiratory symptoms appeared protective, producing a ‘respiratory paradox’. Several mechanisms may underlie the attenuated evolution to PCC in those with acute respiratory presentations. Marked dyspnoea and desaturation, affecting 67% of hospitalised patients and more than 60% of community patients, may have triggered earlier, more aggressive interventions (eg, supplemental oxygen and systemic corticosteroids) that curtailed systemic inflammation. Alternatively, a distinct immunopathological pattern may be operative: respiratory-dominant illness could reflect viral replication largely confined to the airways, with a strong local innate response and limited endothelial dysfunction, whereas early headache, diffuse myalgias or neurological disturbances, which predicted PCC, may suggest multisystem immune activation and a higher likelihood of persistent autoreactivity. In addition, patients with respiratory disease routinely undergo post-discharge imaging and pulmonary function testing, facilitating early detection and treatment of fibrotic sequelae and facilitating respiratory rehabilitation programmes that may hasten functional recovery and attenuate long covid symptoms.

Comparative estimates

Cross-study comparison is hampered by heterogeneity of design, yet our incidence and persistence estimates in hospitalised survivors fall within the 30%–50% range reported by the RECOVER consortium4 14 15 and Danish cohorts,10 while the 9% observed in ambulatory cases is close to the 8%–12% range reported in an Israeli population study,1 although it exceeds estimates from telephone surveys in the UK and the USA.

Additionally, a nationwide US Veterans Affairs electronic health record cohort found that the burden of PASC increased with the severity of acute infection, from 44.5 per 1000 persons among non-hospitalised patients to 217.1 per 1000 among hospitalised patients and 360.2 per 1000 among those admitted to intensive care at 6 months.16 In our data, PCC incidence in community-managed patients declined across waves, from 23.1% in wave 1 to 7.8% in wave 3, whereas risk in previously hospitalised patients remained substantially higher, ranging from 35.5% to 43.5% across waves.

Health system impact

Applying the PCC prevalence estimates derived from PASCNET to official demographic and surveillance data suggests a substantial current care burden in Lombardy. The region had 10 012 054 residents at 31 December 2023,17 and official surveillance data indicate that more than 4.3 million confirmed SARS-CoV-2 cases had recovered in Lombardy by early 2025.18 If approximately 5% required hospitalisation, this translates into about 217 257 post-discharge survivors and 4 127 892 community-managed survivors. Using the prevalences estimated in our study, 31.8% among former inpatients and 6.3% among non-hospitalised cases, the pool of residents living with PCC and potentially needing structured follow-up reaches about 330 000 (70 000 previously hospitalised and 260 000 non-hospitalised), or 3.3 per 100 inhabitants. These estimates were obtained by adapting our symptom data to the items used in the RECOVER study. In our cohort, the score as a continuous variable was significantly associated with the odds of PCC in the first year after infection, supporting its use for model-based estimation at the study visit. However, the proposed cut-point had low sensitivity against clinical evaluation of PCC in the first year and substantially underestimated cases in both cohorts. Applying the cut-point at the study visit yielded lower 2–4 years prevalence estimates of 5.9% in HP and 3.1% in GPP, corresponding to approximately 141 000 residents with unresolved symptoms (13 000 previously hospitalised and 128 000 non-hospitalised), or 1.5% of the population. Although the interval is wide, every scenario places the current burden between roughly 148 000 and 330 000 residents. Published RECOVER reports also documented a substantial and heterogeneous long covid burden in US adults over time,4 14 15 supporting the broader external relevance of our findings. Importantly, although individual risk was substantially higher among previously hospitalised patients, the absolute number of affected individuals may still be large in the community because non-hospitalised survivors are far more numerous.

Conclusions

Our results consistently show a substantially higher PCC burden among previously hospitalised patients than among patients managed in primary care. The 1-year cumulative incidence was 39.9% in HP compared with 9.1% in GPP, and the prevalence of unresolved PCC at approximately 1 year from infection was 30.2% versus 1.9%, respectively. This difference persisted at longer follow-up, with model-based PCC prevalence at the study visit estimated at 31.8% in HP and 6.3% in GPP. These findings are clinically plausible, as hospitalised patients were older, had a higher burden of comorbidity and experienced more severe acute disease. At the same time, community-managed survivors remain highly relevant for public health because they are far more numerous and may still contribute to a substantial absolute number of PCC cases.

Operational use of the RECOVER score as the fulcrum of a two-phase pathway would require integration of the algorithm into electronic health records. Simulations based on PASCNET indicate that lowering the primary care threshold from 11 to 7 points would double sensitivity from 25% while maintaining specificity around 80%; the same inpatient threshold would increase sensitivity to approximately 60% without increasing false-negative rates beyond 20%. Detecting previously unrecognised PCC could help general practitioners refer patients to specialised hubs, reducing clinical under-ascertainment and limiting downstream pressure on waiting lists and fee-for-service spending. In Lombardy, where our model-based estimates suggest that approximately 1 in 30 residents may currently live with chronic post-COVID-19 sequelae, sustainable management will require automated symptom surveillance in primary care and a second-level network sized to the projected caseload over the next 5 years.
