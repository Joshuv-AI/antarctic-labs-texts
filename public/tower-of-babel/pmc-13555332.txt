The Association Between Mindful Parenting and Children’s Peer Social Competence in Chinese Families: A Moderated Mediation Model
Authors: Hongyu Li, Wei Li, Jie Deng, Huai Deng, Xinyu Wen, Yunxi Ping
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionParenting practices are closely associated with children’s peer social competence. As a positive parenting approach, mindful parenting is relevant to children’s emotional development and social adjustment. However, the pathways linking mindful parenting to children’s peer social competence remain insufficiently understood.PurposeIn the context of Chinese culture, this study examined the association between mindful parenting and peer social competence among preschool children and investigated the mediating role of children’s emotion regulation and the moderating role of the parent-child relationship.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 531 families of kindergarten children. Parents completed the Mindfulness in Parenting Questionnaire (MIPQ), Child-Parent Relationship Scale (CPRS), Social Competence With Peers Questionnaire (SCPQ), and Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ).ResultsAll study variables were at moderate levels and showed substantial individual variation. Mindful parenting was positively associated with peer social competence (beta = 0.672, p < 0.001). Children’s emotion regulation mediated this association, accounting for 33% of the total effect, and parent-child closeness moderated the association between mindful parenting and emotion regulation (beta = 0.031, p < 0.05).ConclusionMindful parenting was associated with peer social competence through the mediating role of children’s emotion regulation, and this indirect association varied according to parent-child closeness. The findings suggest that strengthening parents’ mindful awareness and parent-child closeness may support more adaptive emotional responses and, in turn, children’s peer interactions, providing a basis for family-based interventions.

Introduction

Peer social competence is central to preschool children’s social development. Its quality is relevant not only to children’s current social adjustment but also to their subsequent development. Peer social competence refers to children’s ability to perceive, adapt to, coordinate, and manage relationships with peers of the same or a similar age,1 and encompasses dimensions such as social initiative, peer rejection, and peer avoidance.2 A substantial body of research has linked strong peer social competence to emotional well-being, higher-quality interpersonal relationships, and cognitive development.3–7 Conversely, children with limited peer social competence may be more vulnerable to adverse social environments and negative relationships, placing them at risk of short- or long-term difficulties in social adjustment and related behavioral problems. The preschool years are widely recognized as a critical period for the development of peer social competence. Skills acquired through peer interaction during this period provide an important foundation for children’s later participation in diverse social settings and interpersonal collaboration.8 Nevertheless, the antecedents of preschool children’s peer social competence within specific cultural contexts remain incompletely understood and warrant context-sensitive investigation.

Family systems theory and social learning theory identify the family as children’s primary living environment and the earliest context of socialization.9,10 Family education is therefore widely regarded as central to children’s physical and psychological development, with particular attention directed toward parents’ role in this process. Because parents are the relational figures with whom children interact most frequently and extensively at home, parenting practices are closely associated with children’s early acquisition of social habits, skills, and behaviors.11,12 Extensive empirical evidence indicates stable positive associations between positive parenting practices and children’s social competence and peer acceptance.13–15 Positive parenting is also associated with higher-quality parent-child interactions and secure attachment.16,17 In addition, parents’ own social competence, the quality of parent-child communication, and parental attitudes toward social interaction may be linked to the development of children’s peer social competence through parenting practices and parent-child interactions.18 Although traditional parenting research has established the importance of positive parenting for child development, comparatively less attention has been paid to parents’ psychological states during parenting. In recent years, mindful parenting, a parenting framework emphasizing parental awareness and acceptance, has attracted increasing attention and offers a new theoretical and practical perspective on the psychological processes underlying high-quality family education.

Mindful parenting refers to parents’ intentional, nonjudgmental awareness and acceptance of present-moment experiences during everyday interactions with their children.19 It emphasizes sustained attention to parent-child interactions, awareness of one’s own emotions and automatic reactions, nonjudgmental acceptance of the child’s behavior, and self-compassion regarding one’s limitations as a parent.20 Unlike general positive parenting, mindful parenting places greater emphasis on parents’ awareness of their own thoughts and feelings and of their children’s needs, while drawing on emotion regulation and values-based intentions to cultivate calm and caring parenting.21 This approach may interrupt automatic reactions under parenting stress and shift parenting from reactive to responsive, thereby supporting positive parent-child relationships and child well-being.22 Intervention studies conducted outside China have linked mindfulness training to lower parenting stress, stronger emotion regulation, less oppositional parenting, and improved parent-child interaction.23–25 Neuroscientific research has also associated mindfulness training with stronger prefrontal regulation of amygdala activity and greater emotion-regulation capacity.26,27 Parents with higher levels of mindfulness may respond less punitively to children’s negative behavior and use more guiding and empathic responses, thereby providing a safe and supportive scaffold for emotion regulation.22 This evidence offers a cognitive-neuroscientific account of how mindful parenting may be associated with a shift from reactive to responsive parenting. From a Vygotskian sociocultural perspective, children’s higher psychological functions initially emerge through co-regulation with caregivers and are gradually internalized as self-regulatory capacities. Mindful parenting may therefore be associated not only with the quality of parent-child interaction but also with children’s emotion regulation and, indirectly, their social adjustment in peer contexts.28,29

The literature reviewed above indicates that evidence concerning the association between mindful parenting and children’s peer social competence has been drawn primarily from Western cultural settings. Evidence regarding its applicability and effect size in Chinese cultural contexts remains limited. Unlike Western families, which tend to emphasize individual autonomy, Chinese parents’ understanding of mindful principles such as acceptance and nonjudgment may be shaped by Confucian concepts including moderation and self-reflection. The relevant pathways may therefore differ from those observed in Western samples. Furthermore, existing research on mindful parenting has focused mainly on parents’ mental health or improvements in parent-child relationships.25 Empirical evidence extending this work to specific indicators of children’s social development, particularly peer social competence, remains comparatively limited, and the mechanisms underlying this association require further investigation. Accordingly, the following hypothesis was proposed in the Chinese cultural context: H1: Parental mindful parenting is positively associated with children’s peer social competence and significantly predicts peer social competence.

H1: Parental mindful parenting is positively associated with children’s peer social competence and significantly predicts peer social competence.

Emotion regulation has been identified as a core correlate and predictor of children’s peer social competence and is highly relevant to healthy child development.30–32 Emotion regulation refers to the ability to identify and understand emotions, manage emotional responses, and express emotions, encompassing processes involved in modulating, maintaining, and communicating affective states.33 Extensive research has documented a strong association between emotional and social competence. Children who regulate their emotions effectively, understand others’ perspectives, and express emotions in socially acceptable ways are better able to establish and maintain positive interpersonal relationships and tend to demonstrate stronger peer social competence.34–37 Children’s emotion regulation is also closely associated with parenting practices.38 On the one hand, higher levels of mindful parenting are associated with more supportive parental responses, lower emotional lability and negativity in children, and fewer emotional symptoms.39 On the other hand, the heightened emotional awareness and accepting parent-child attitudes characteristic of mindful parents may be associated with children’s emotion-regulation abilities.40 Previous studies have separately established associations among parental mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, and children’s peer social competence.41–44 However, few studies have incorporated emotion regulation into a mediation framework linking mindful parenting and peer social competence or systematically tested the full pathway from mindful parenting to emotion regulation and then to peer social competence. Related work has shown that children’s emotion regulation mediates the association between parental mindfulness and child resilience.45 Although these findings do not directly address peer social competence, they suggest that emotion regulation may serve as an intermediary linking parenting characteristics to children’s social adjustment. The following hypothesis was therefore proposed: H2: Parental mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, and children’s peer social competence are positively correlated, and children’s emotion regulation mediates the association between parental mindful parenting and children’s peer social competence.

H2: Parental mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, and children’s peer social competence are positively correlated, and children’s emotion regulation mediates the association between parental mindful parenting and children’s peer social competence.

Importantly, within the family system, the relevance of mindful parenting to child outcomes may vary across relational contexts. Positive development depends not only on the presence of protective factors but also on the level of risk factors.46 As a protective resource for child development, mindful parenting may be translated into children’s emotion-regulation capacity differently depending on the quality of the parent-child relationship. The parent-child relationship is among the most fundamental interpersonal relationships experienced by children. It is an emotional bond rooted in the parent-child relationship and is closely connected to parenting practices.47 It is commonly conceptualized in terms of closeness, conflict, and dependence.48 Because the dependence dimension does not always meet acceptable psychometric standards, many studies retain only closeness and conflict. Previous research indicates that the parent-child relationship moderates associations between parenting practices and children’s emotional problems.44,49 Parent-child closeness has been found to moderate the association between authoritative parenting and children’s externalizing behavior, whereas parent-child conflict has moderated the association between authoritarian parenting and children’s internalizing behavior. A growing body of research similarly identifies the parent-child relationship as an important moderator of associations between parenting practices and child development.50 However, few studies have compared the moderating roles of closeness and conflict in a Chinese cultural context. Examining their potentially distinct roles may therefore extend theory and help identify targets for tailored family interventions. Accordingly, the following hypothesis was proposed: H3: Parent-child closeness and parent-child conflict each moderate the association between mindful parenting and children’s emotion regulation, with different patterns of moderation.

H3: Parent-child closeness and parent-child conflict each moderate the association between mindful parenting and children’s emotion regulation, with different patterns of moderation.

In summary, this study developed a moderated mediation model to address three sequential questions: Is mindful parenting positively associated with children’s peer social competence? Does emotion regulation mediate the association between mindful parenting and peer social competence? Do different dimensions of the parent-child relationship differentially moderate the association between mindful parenting and emotion regulation?

This study sought to make three contributions. First, it extended research on mindful parenting to preschool children in the Chinese cultural context, responding to calls for greater attention to cross-cultural variation and providing empirical evidence regarding the applicability and boundary conditions of Western theories of mindful parenting in Chinese families. Second, it examined whether children’s emotion regulation mediates the association between mindful parenting and peer social competence and whether parent-child closeness and conflict moderate the association between mindful parenting and emotion regulation. In doing so, it sought to clarify the mechanisms underlying the association between mindful parenting and children’s peer interactions. Third, by distinguishing how the association operates through emotion regulation and when it is stronger or weaker across parent-child relationship contexts, this study provides evidence that may inform a shift from general advocacy of mindful parenting to family interventions tailored to relationship characteristics. The following sections present the methods, results, and discussion, followed by the study limitations and directions for future research.

Method

Participants

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, as well as the ethical standards of the American Psychological Association. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling. Drawing on personal networks and relevant research projects, the researchers surveyed families from 18 kindergartens across southwestern, northeastern, southeastern, northwestern, and central China. The sample included urban and rural settings and all three kindergarten grade levels. Although participants were recruited from multiple Chinese provinces, the sampling procedure was not probabilistic; the generalizability of the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and examined using random sampling in future research. Ethical approval was obtained from the university’s academic ethics committee before data collection. Trained graduate students in psychology and education served as research assistants. Before distributing the questionnaires, the research assistants explained the study purpose and the confidentiality and anonymity of the data to parents in person, answered their questions, and obtained written informed consent from all participants. During questionnaire completion, standardized guidance was available, but the research assistants did not influence participants’ responses. Of the 550 questionnaires distributed, 19 were classified as invalid because completion time was less than 120 seconds or the same response option was selected throughout. A total of 531 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding a valid response rate of 96.5%. Item-level missing data were replaced with the respondent’s mean for the completed items on the relevant scale when fewer than 5% of scale items were missing. Questionnaires with 5% or more missing data were to be excluded; no questionnaire met this criterion. Although the scales did not contain separate attention-check items, data quality was assessed using a combination of reverse-scored items and completion-time screening. Instruments

Mindfulness in Parenting (MIPQ)

Mindful parenting was assessed using the Mindfulness in Parenting Questionnaire (MIPQ) developed by McCaffrey et al51 The MIPQ consists of two dimensions: mindful discipline and being in the moment with the child. The mindful discipline dimension includes 15 items and assesses parents’ nonreactivity, parenting awareness, and goal-oriented focus during parenting. A sample item is: “Did you consider your feelings before disciplining your child?” The being in the moment with the child dimension includes 13 items and assesses present-moment attention, awareness of the child’s emotions, and unconditional acceptance during parent–child interactions. A sample item is: “Did you carefully listen and tune into your child when you two were talking?”

The MIPQ uses a four-point scoring method, in which participants evaluate their parenting practices and interactions with their child on a scale ranging from 1 to 4: 1 = Infrequently, 2 = Sometimes, 3 = Often, and 4 = Almost always. The total score is calculated by summing the scores of all items, with higher scores indicating higher levels of parental mindful parenting. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the total scale and the two dimensions were 0.96, 0.89, and 0.84, respectively.

Child–Parent Relationship Scale

The parent–child relationship was assessed using the Child–Parent Relationship Scale (CPRS) developed by Pianta.52 The original scale consists of three dimensions: Closeness, Conflict, and Dependence. In the present study, the Dependence dimension did not meet the acceptable standards in the reliability and validity tests and was therefore removed. This is also consistent with the current common practice of using the CPRS as a two-dimensional measure. Closeness is considered the positive aspect of the parent–child relationship and is assessed using items such as “If my child is upset, he/she will seek comfort from me” and “My child spontaneously shares information about himself/herself with me.” Conflict is considered the negative aspect of the parent–child relationship and is assessed using items such as “My child and I always seem to be struggling with each other” and “Dealing with my child drains my energy.” The scale uses a five-point scoring method, in which parents evaluate their relationship with their child on a scale ranging from 1 to 5: 1 = Definitely does not apply, 2 = Not really, 3 = Neutral/Not sure, 4 = Applies somewhat, and 5 = Definitely applies. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the total scale, Closeness dimension, and Conflict dimension were 0.68, 0.74, and 0.86, respectively.

Social Competence with Peers Questionnaire

Children’s peer social competence was assessed using the Social Competence With Peers Questionnaire (SCPQ) originally developed by Howes1 and subsequently localized and revised by Chinese scholar Zhang Yuan. The scale consists of four dimensions: social proactivity, verbal and nonverbal communication competence, social communication difficulties, and prosocial behavior. The social proactivity dimension includes 4 items and assesses children’s willingness and actions to initiate social interactions. A sample item is: “The child actively introduces himself/herself to new peers.” The verbal and nonverbal communication competence dimension includes 4 items and assesses children’s ability to communicate, explain, and make evaluations. A sample item is: “The child can make an appropriate evaluation of his/her appearance and abilities.” The social communication difficulties dimension includes 3 items and assesses difficulties in social expression and interaction. A sample item is: “The child never expresses his/her own opinions.” The prosocial behavior dimension includes 4 items and assesses children’s concern for others and empathy. A sample item is: “The child is willing to help others.” The SCPQ uses a five-point scoring method, in which respondents evaluate the child’s behavior on a scale ranging from 1 to 5: 1 = Completely inconsistent, 2 = Relatively inconsistent, 3 = Uncertain, 4 = Relatively consistent, and 5 = Completely consistent. The total score is calculated by summing the scores of all items after reverse scoring where appropriate, with higher scores indicating higher levels of peer social competence. Items 3 and 14 were reverse scored. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the total scale and the four dimensions were 0.911, 0.83, 0.80, 0.78, and 0.81, respectively.

Emotion Regulation Questionnaire

Children’s emotion regulation was assessed using the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) developed by Zheng.53 The questionnaire consists of four dimensions: emotion recognition and understanding, emotion regulation, emotional expression, and emotion utilization. The emotion recognition and understanding dimension includes 6 items and assesses children’s ability to perceive, distinguish, and interpret their own and others’ emotions. A sample item is: “The child can recognize when others are happy or angry.” The emotion regulation dimension includes 7 items and assesses children’s ability to regulate, control, and adaptively manage their own emotions. A sample item is: “When having a conflict with peers, the child can control his/her anger.” The emotional expression dimension includes 5 items and assesses children’s ability to express their emotions appropriately. A sample item is: “The child can express his/her emotions and thoughts in an appropriate way.” The emotion utilization dimension includes 3 items and assesses children’s ability to use emotional information to guide behavior and achieve goals. A sample item is: “When the teacher is in a bad mood, the child can take the initiative to ask about and show concern for the teacher.” The ERQ uses a five-point scoring method, in which parents evaluate children’s daily behavior on a scale ranging from 1 to 5: 1 = Never, 2 = Occasionally, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Usually, and 5 = Always. The total score is calculated by summing the scores of all items, with higher scores indicating stronger emotion regulation ability in children. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the total questionnaire and the four dimensions were 0.926, 0.81, 0.79, 0.77, and 0.76, respectively.

Data Analysis

First, SPSS 26.0 was used to conduct descriptive statistics and correlation analyses. Second, according to different demographic variables, independent-samples t tests and one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) were performed using SPSS 26.0 to examine differences in mindful parenting, parent–child relationship, children’s peer social competence, and children’s emotion regulation ability. Third, the bootstrap method was used to test the mediating effect. Fourth, the PROCESS macro was employed to examine the moderated mediation effect.

Results

Common Method Bias Test

Harman’s single-factor test was used to examine potential common method bias in this study. The results showed that 11 factors had eigenvalues greater than 1. The variance explained by the first factor was 34.25%, which was below the critical threshold of 50%.54 These results indicate that common method bias was not a serious concern in this study.

For children’s gender, independent-samples t tests were performed on mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, peer social competence, parent-child closeness, and parent-child conflict. No significant gender differences were observed in mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, or peer social competence (p > 0.05), and all effect sizes were small (Cohen’s d = 0.073–0.135).

For children’s age, primary caregiver, and parents’ educational level, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons was conducted. Age-related differences emerged in children’s emotion regulation and peer social competence (p < 0.05), although the effect sizes were small (partial eta squared = 0.022 and 0.011, respectively). Further pairwise comparisons indicated that children aged 5–6 years scored significantly higher than those aged 3–4 years on both emotion regulation and peer social competence, suggesting an upward developmental trend in these abilities with age.

Differences were also identified across primary-caregiver groups in mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, and peer social competence (p < 0.05), with small effect sizes (partial eta squared = 0.013–0.037). Specifically, children whose primary caregiver was their mother obtained higher scores in emotion regulation and peer social competence than those primarily cared for by grandparents. Moreover, mothers serving as primary caregivers reported higher levels of mindful parenting than fathers and grandparents.

Parental educational level was associated with significant differences in mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, and peer social competence (p < 0.001). Effect sizes were moderate for peer social competence (partial eta squared = 0.107), emotion regulation (partial eta squared = 0.089), and mindful parenting (partial eta squared = 0.123). Overall, higher parental education corresponded to higher scores across these variables. Pairwise comparisons further revealed that parents with a bachelor’s or postgraduate degree reported higher levels of mindful parenting, and their children showed higher levels of emotion regulation and peer social competence, than families with lower parental educational attainment. Parent-child closeness and conflict also differed significantly by parental educational level (p < 0.05), with small effect sizes for closeness (partial eta squared = 0.024) and conflict (partial eta squared = 0.022). Post hoc tests indicated that parent-child closeness scores were significantly higher among parents with bachelor’s or postgraduate degrees than among those with lower educational attainment. Parent-child conflict scores were significantly higher among parents with a high school or technical secondary school education than among parents with postgraduate degrees.

Discussion

Using a sample of Chinese families, this study examined the associations among mindful parenting, the parent-child relationship, children’s emotion regulation, and preschool children’s peer social competence within a moderated mediation model. The findings supported the study hypotheses: (1) parental mindful parenting was positively associated with children’s peer social competence and emotion regulation; (2) mindful parenting was indirectly associated with peer social competence through children’s emotion regulation; and (3) parent-child closeness moderated the association between mindful parenting and children’s emotion regulation, such that the positive association was stronger at higher levels of closeness.

Based on data from 531 Chinese families, the results showed that parents’ mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, and peer social competence were all at moderate levels. No obvious extreme values were observed, suggesting that the data provided an appropriate basis for subsequent hypothesis testing. With respect to gender differences, no significant differences were found between boys and girls in mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, or peer social competence. This suggests that these variables are not determined by children’s gender itself during the preschool period. This finding differs from that of Moreira and Cristina Canavarro,55 who reported more pronounced associations among mindful parenting and emotion regulation in girls when examining adolescent samples. One possible explanation is that gender differences in socioemotional development may become more salient during adolescence, whereas such differences may be less stable or less differentiated during early childhood. In the preschool stage, children’s emotion regulation and peer interaction patterns are still undergoing rapid development and remain highly dependent on parenting contexts. Therefore, mindful parenting may play a broadly promotive role in the emotion regulation and peer social competence of preschool children regardless of gender. This further highlights the importance of mindful parenting in early childhood and its foundational role in children’s emotion regulation and peer social development.

With regard to children’s age, the scores indicated that children aged 5–6 years performed significantly better than those aged 3–4 years in emotion regulation and peer social competence. This finding is consistent with the developmental characteristics of children’s psychology, suggesting that preschool children’s emotion regulation and peer social competence gradually improve with age.56,57 However, no significant differences were found between children aged 4–5 years and the other age groups. A possible explanation is that emotion regulation and peer social competence develop progressively during the preschool period, and developmental differences become more evident when the age gap is larger. This finding suggests that early childhood education should respect the developmental pace of children and provide gradual, developmentally appropriate guidance to support their social and emotional growth.

Regarding differences by primary caregiver, mothers scored significantly higher than fathers and grandparents in mindful parenting, children’s emotion regulation, and peer social competence. In China, traditional cultural expectations have often positioned mothers as the primary caregivers.58 Compared with other caregiver groups, mothers tend to be more involved in childrearing, more sensitive to children’s needs, and more attentive to children’s emotional responses. This finding is consistent with Tao et al59 Future family education programs may therefore consider strengthening mindful parenting support for fathers to facilitate their involvement in childcare. Grandparents may also benefit from brief, practical family-education programs, such as collaborative parenting workshops for parents and grandparents. Such programs could introduce the core principles of mindful parenting and practical strategies for communication and emotion coaching, helping family members establish a more consistent and stable parenting environment.

Regarding differences in parental educational level, the scores of children’s peer social competence, children’s emotion regulation, parental mindful parenting, and close parent–child relationship were significantly higher among families in which parents had a bachelor’s degree or a postgraduate degree than among those with lower educational levels. This suggests that parents with a bachelor’s degree or above may have developed relatively clear group advantages in mindful parenting and in supporting children’s emotional and social development.

For conflictual parent–child relationship, parents with a high school or technical secondary school education scored significantly higher than those with a postgraduate degree. In general, parents with higher educational levels tend to demonstrate higher levels of mindfulness in parenting practices.60 They may be more capable of recognizing and responding to children’s physical and psychological developmental needs, thereby influencing children’s emotion regulation and peer social competence. This finding suggests that family education guidance should pay particular attention to parents with relatively lower educational levels. Mindful parenting should be introduced in more accessible, life-oriented, and practical ways, so as to lower the learning threshold and make evidence-based parenting knowledge more available to a wider range of families.

The present study found a positive association between mindful parenting and children’s peer social competence, consistent with Wang et al43 This finding suggests that mindful parenting characteristics are relevant to the complex social skills children use in peer interactions. Mindful parenting may be associated with greater parental self-awareness, more adaptive parenting responses, greater empathy toward oneself and one’s child, and more positive social interactions with children.21 Mindful parenting should therefore be viewed not merely as an abstract educational concept but as a family characteristic with practical relevance to young children’s social development. Through its associations with the quality of parent-child interaction, children’s emotional stability, and patterns of social behavior, mindful parenting may be linked to children’s adjustment within peer groups. Overall, this study extends the literature and offers a basis for supporting mindful parenting practices in efforts to strengthen young children’s peer social competence.

Children’s emotion regulation partially mediated the association between parental mindful parenting and children’s peer social competence. This finding provides empirical clarification of a pathway linking mindful parenting to children’s social development and identifies emotion regulation as an important intermediary in this association.

This finding is generally consistent with previous research,61 which suggests that mindfulness training is associated with children’s empathic responding, emotion recognition, emotional communication, anger management, and interpersonal functioning. Emotion socialization theory likewise proposes that children’s emotion regulation does not develop in isolation but is gradually constructed through observation, co-regulation, and emotion-related conversations in daily interactions with primary caregivers. Higher parental mindful parenting is associated with stronger emotion regulation in children.44 Specifically, parents with higher levels of mindful parenting may be more likely to respond to children’s emotional expressions with awareness, acceptance, and nonjudgment and to offer constructive guidance and supportive strategies when children encounter emotional difficulties. Such responses may support the development of children’s emotion regulation.39,62 In turn, children with stronger emotion regulation may be better able to manage their emotions during peer interactions, interpret others’ emotional cues accurately, display more prosocial behavior, and show less aggression and withdrawal, thereby experiencing greater peer acceptance.63 The positive association between children’s emotion regulation and peer social competence was also significant in the mediation model. Peer interaction involves frequent emotional exchanges, including joy when sharing success, frustration following rejection, and anger or sadness during conflict.31,57,64 Children who regulate emotions effectively may be more likely to use adaptive social strategies, such as seeking comfort, reappraising situations, and expressing needs calmly, rather than aggression or withdrawal. Thus, support for mindful parenting may be associated with stronger emotion regulation and, in turn, greater peer social competence.

The association between mindful parenting and children’s emotion regulation varied according to the parent-child relationship.40 In this process, relationship quality functioned as a moderator of the extent to which parental mindful parenting was associated with children’s emotion-regulation ability. A high-quality parent-child relationship may provide an emotionally secure context for this association, whereas a poor relationship may weaken it. Parent-child closeness positively moderated the association between mindful parenting and emotion regulation. One possible explanation is that a close parent-child relationship provides emotional support and positive feedback for parents and children and is associated with fewer negative emotions during parenting.65 A positive parent-child relationship may allow parents to practice mindful parenting more naturally and respond to children with intentional, nonjudgmental awareness and acceptance. Within this relational context, children’s emotion regulation may be more strongly supported.66

In contrast to parent-child closeness, the moderating effect of parent-child conflict was not statistically significant (beta = −0.022, p > 0.05). Although this finding may appear unexpected, it is theoretically plausible for three reasons. First, the central mechanism of mindful parenting concerns parents’ cognitive processing of parenting situations and their patterns of emotional response rather than the direct elimination of parent-child conflict. Research suggests that mindful parenting primarily buffers parents’ immediate responses after conflict occurs rather than reducing the overall level of conflict.67 Second, developmental research indicates that positive and negative dimensions of family relationships do not operate as symmetrical opposites. In this study, closeness strengthened the association between mindful parenting and children’s emotion regulation, whereas conflict did not show a comparable moderating role. This pattern suggests that the relevance of mindful parenting may depend more strongly on the activation of positive relational resources. Mindful parenting may itself constitute a psychological resource for managing conflict; when this resource is strong, individual differences in conflict may add little to its association with children’s emotion regulation. Third, in Chinese family culture, parent-child conflict often carries a moral connotation and may be interpreted as evidence of inadequate parenting. The nonjudgmental acceptance emphasized in mindful parenting may help parents reinterpret conflict as a normal feature of parent-child interaction. Among parents with higher levels of mindful parenting, the meaning of conflict may therefore be reframed, making the frequency of conflict less important than how parents notice and respond to it. Intervention programs may consequently need to be tailored to family relationship patterns. For families characterized by high closeness, daily mindful-awareness exercises and nonjudgmental listening may reinforce existing strengths. In families characterized by high conflict, relational repair may need to precede the introduction of mindful parenting practices.

The contributions of this study can be summarized in three areas. First, it extends research on the correlates of mindful parenting. Whereas previous studies have focused mainly on general child adjustment outcomes, such as internalizing and externalizing problems, the present study examined peer social competence as a specific and important indicator of social functioning. The findings link mindful characteristics within the family microsystem to the quality of children’s interactions with peers outside the family. The study further tested a pathway from mindful parenting through children’s emotion regulation to peer social competence, spanning parental characteristics, children’s intrapersonal capacities, and interpersonal functioning. This pathway provides empirical evidence regarding how family parenting characteristics may be associated with broader social contexts through children’s internal regulatory processes. Second, the study identifies asymmetric roles for different dimensions of the parent-child relationship. Parent-child closeness positively moderated the association between mindful parenting and children’s emotion regulation, whereas the moderating effect of parent-child conflict was not significant. This asymmetry suggests that closeness may function as a facilitating resource that strengthens the association between mindful parenting and children’s emotion regulation, whereas conflict may not add explanatory value when mindful parenting is already operating as a family resource. The finding refines current understanding of the relational conditions under which mindful parenting is associated with child outcomes. Third, the study provides empirical support for mindful parenting theory in a Chinese cultural setting. Using data from 531 Chinese families of kindergarten children, the study systematically examined the pathway linking mindful parenting to children’s peer social competence. Mindful parenting was positively associated with children’s emotion regulation and peer social competence in Chinese families, and the observed pattern was broadly consistent with findings from Western settings. These results provide preliminary evidence of cross-cultural consistency and add evidence from a non-Western context to the literature on mindful parenting interventions.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, when examining demographic differences in mindful parenting, parent-child closeness, parent-child conflict, children’s emotion regulation, and peer social competence, this study did not fully account for other potential confounders, such as family socioeconomic status and regional variation. Future studies should include these factors to provide a more comprehensive account of the associations among the study variables. Second, the cross-sectional design cannot clarify temporal or causal relationships and may be vulnerable to common-source bias. Although Harman’s single-factor test did not indicate serious common method bias, the test has recognized limitations and cannot fully rule out systematic error arising from social desirability or response-consistency tendencies. Experimental or longitudinal designs are therefore needed to examine the relevant mechanisms more rigorously. Third, although the convenience sample included families from several Chinese provinces, it was not a probability sample and may not adequately represent the broader population. The generalizability of the findings should be interpreted cautiously and assessed in future studies using stratified random sampling.

Conclusion

Using cross-sectional data from 531 Chinese families with preschool children, this study examined the association between mindful parenting and children’s peer social competence and reached the following conclusions:

First, mindful parenting was positively associated with children’s emotion regulation, peer social competence, and parent-child closeness.

Second, children’s emotion regulation partially mediated the association between mindful parenting and peer social competence; mindful parenting was indirectly associated with stronger peer social competence through higher levels of children’s emotion regulation.

Third, the parent-child relationship moderated the association between mindful parenting and children’s emotion regulation. Parent-child closeness positively moderated this association, such that the positive association between mindful parenting and emotion regulation was stronger at higher levels of closeness. The moderating effect of parent-child conflict was not statistically significant, suggesting a limited moderating role in this association.
