Belted and Ensembled Neural Network for Linear and Nonlinear Sufficient Dimension Reduction
Yin Tang, Bing Li

Abstract
We introduce a unified, flexible, and easy-to-implement framework of sufficient dimension reduction that can accommodate both linear and nonlinear dimension reduction, and both the conditional distribution and the conditional mean as the targets of estimation. This unified framework is achieved by a specially structured neural network — the Belted and Ensembled Neural Network (BENN) — that consists of a narrow latent layer, which we call the belt, and a family of transformations of the response, which we call the ensemble. By strategically placing the belt at different layers of the neural network, we can achieve linear or nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction, and by choosing the appropriate transformation families, we can achieve dimension reduction for the conditional distribution or the conditional mean. Moreover, thanks to the advantage of the neural network, the method is very fast to compute, overcoming a computation bottleneck of the traditional sufficient dimension reduction estimators, which involves the inversion of a matrix of dimension either p p or n n . We develop the algorithm and convergence rate of our method, compare it with existing sufficient dimension reduction methods, and apply it to two data examples.



Keywords:
Autoencoder, Convergence rate, Covering numbers, Deep learning, Probability characterizing family.

Introduction

Sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) is a methodology that extracts a low-dimensional sufficient predictor from the high-dimensional observed predictor. It is a powerful tool for data visualization, regression diagnostics, enhancement of regression accuracy, and assessment of conditional independence. See, for example,
Li, 1991
and
Cook, 1994
,
Li, 2018
, and
Li and Kim, 2024
. SDR has been developed into many branches, but the four most important branches can be characterized as follows. According to the target of estimation, there are methods that target the conditional distribution (
Li, 1991
,
Cook and Weisberg, 1991
) and those that target the conditional mean (
Cook and Li, 2002
;
Cook and Li, 2004
,
Xia et al., 2002
). According to the nature of the extracted predictor, there are linear and nonlinear SDR methods (
Lee et al., 2013
and
Li and Song, 2017
). A computational bottleneck in scaling up these methods for big data is that they involve inversion of matrices with dimensions equal to the sample size or the dimension of the predictor, which can be very slow for large and/or high-dimensional data. In this paper we propose a deep-learning-based SDR methodology that is flexible enough to accommodate all of the above four branches, that is fast to compute because it avoids the inversion of large matrices, and that is highly accurate due to the richness of the deep learning family.

To properly motivate our method, we first give an overview of the four frameworks mentioned above.
Let
{\bf X}
be a random vector of covariates in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
, and
Y
be the response in
{\mathbb{R}}
. The general goal is to find a transformation
{\bf f}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
, where
d<p
, such that

\displaystyle Y\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,{\bf X}|{\bf f}({\bf X});

(1)

that is,
Y
and
{\bf X}
are independent given
{\bf f}({\bf X})
. The function
{\bf f}({\bf X})
is called the sufficient predictor of
Y
as it contains all the information in
{\bf X}
about
Y
.

When
{\bf f}
in (
1
) is a linear function, the dimension reduction problem becomes

\displaystyle Y\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,{\bf X}|{\bf B}^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}{\bf X},

(2)

which is known as the linear SDR problem. See
Li, 1991
and
Cook, 1994
.
The identifiable object in (
2
) is the subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
spanned by the columns of
{\bf B}
, since the relation (
2
) is not affected if we right multiply
{\bf B}
by a nonsingular square matrix. The smallest such space is called the
central subspace
, denoted by
{\cal{S}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y|{\bf X}}}
. This is the objective of estimation for linear SDR. Many methods are developed for linear SDR, which are summarized, for example, in
Li, 2018
;
Ma and Zhu, 2013
.
When
{\bf f}
is a nonlinear function, problem (
1
) is the nonlinear SDR problem and the identifiable (infinite-dimensional) object is the
\sigma
-field generated by
{\bf f}({\bf X})
. The smallest such
\sigma
-field is called the
central
\sigma
-field
, which is the objective of estimation for nonlinear SDR.
Lee et al., 2013
and
Li and Song, 2017
developed estimation procedures for nonlinear SDR based on the reproducing-kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS).

A closely related SDR problem is one in which conditional mean
E(Y|{\bf X})
is of primary interest. In this setting, we assume

\displaystyle E(Y|{\bf X})=E[Y|{\bf f}({\bf X})].

(3)

When
{\bf f}
is a linear function, the above problem (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle E(Y|{\bf X})=E(Y|{\bf B}^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}{\bf X}).

(4)

This is the SDR problem considered by
Cook and Li, 2002
;
Cook and Li, 2004
. As in (
4
), this relation is determined by the column space of
{\bf B}
, and the smallest such space is called the
central mean subspace
. The forward regression methods proposed by
Xia et al., 2002
in fact target this problem. It is also useful to consider the problem (
4
) when
{\bf f}
is a nonlinear function. The smallest
\sigma
-field that satisfies (
3
) is called the
central mean
\sigma
-field
. Note that problem (
3
) is meaningful only when the response is a random vector
{\bf Y}
because, when the response is a scalar
Y
, we can always set
{\bf f}({\bf X})=E(Y|{\bf X})
to satisfy (
3
).

One can bridge the difference between the SDR problems (
1
) and (
3
) by the use of an
ensemble
. Suppose
\{g(\cdot,t):t\in I\}
, where
I
is a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
, is a family of transformations of
Y
that uniquely determines the distribution of
Y
. Then

\displaystyle Y\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,{\bf X}|{\bf f}({\bf X})\ \Leftrightarrow\ E[g(Y,t)|{\bf X}]=E[g(Y,t)|{\bf f}({\bf X})]\quad\mbox{for all $t\in I$}.

Such a class of functions is called a probability determining class, and we call it an
ensemble
in our context.
The above equivalence means that we can recover the central subspace (or
\sigma
-field) by estimating the central mean subspace (or sub-
\sigma
-field) for every member of the ensemble. This idea was first used by
Zhu and Zeng, 2006
, who propose a Fourier transform method to estimate the central mean space in linear SDR problems.

Yin and Li, 2011
formally introduces the ensemble estimator, and proposes various ensembles to turn any method for estimating the central mean subspace into one that estimate the central subspace.
See also
Li et al., 2008
,
Fertl and Bura, 2022
, and
Zeng and Zhu, 2010
.

From the above discussion, we need the following ingredients to conduct linear or nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction:

1.

a family of functions to model the sufficient predictor
{\bf f}({\bf X})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
;

2.

a family of functions of
Y
(the ensemble) to characterize the conditional distribution of
Y
given
{\bf X}
;

3.

a regression operation that links the above two families.

For linear SDR, the first family is the linear family
{\bf B}^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}{\bf X}
; the second family is any ensemble; the regression operation can be the forward regression or inverse regression. For RKHS-based nonlinear SDR procedures mentioned earlier, both the first and the second families are RKHS’s, and the regression operation is implemented by a linear operator known as the
regression operator
.

In this paper, we introduce a deep-learning framework (see, for example,
Goodfellow et al., 2016
and

Yuan et al., 2020
) for linear and nonlinear SDR, where the first family is implemented by neural network, the ensemble is any distribution-determining class, and the regression operation that links the two is also implemented by neural network. Specifically, at the population level, we minimize

\displaystyle E\|{\bf g}(Y)-({\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}})({\bf X})\|^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},

(5)

where
{\bf g}(Y)
is a vector-valued function of
Y
that plays the role of the ensemble,
{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}
is a neural network mapping from
{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
used to model the sufficient predictor, and
{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}
is another neural network that maps the sufficient predictor into the ensemble space. We call
{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}
the dimension reduction neural network, and
{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}
the ensemble neural network. Since the output of
{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}
is of dimension
d
, narrower than the other layers, we call
{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}
the
Belted and Ensembled Neural Network
(BENN).

We can see immediately the neural network framework is flexible enough to accommodate all four settings mentioned earlier. If we place the belt as the first hidden layer without an activation function, then the above objective function becomes

E\|{\bf g}(Y)-{\bf f}({\bf B}^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}{\bf X})\|^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},

which corresponds to the linear SDR problem (
2
). If we place the belt in the first hidden layer as above and further take
g
to be the identity mapping
g(Y)=Y
, then (
5
) becomes

E[Y-f({\bf B}^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}{\bf X})]^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},

which corresponds to conditional mean linear SDR problem (
4
). If we place the belt in the middle of the neural network, then the objective function (
5
) takes its original form, which corresponds to the nonlinear SDR problem (
1
). If we place the belt in the middle and take
g
to be the identity mapping, then (
5
) becomes

E[Y-f^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}({\bf X})]^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},

which
corresponds to the conditional mean nonlinear SDR problem (
3
).

Several recent papers have pioneered the neural network approaches to sufficient dimension reduction.
Kapla et al., 2022
proposes a neural-network-based linear SDR method that corresponds exactly to the conditional mean linear SDR described above. Thus, our proposed BENN framework can be regarded as an extension of their approach.
Liang et al., 2022
proposes a stochastic neural network (StoNet) for nonlinear SDR. Each hidden layer of StoNet involves auxiliary Gaussian noises, and the output of the last hidden layer is used as the sufficient predictor. Since the sufficient predictor produced by the StoNet depends on the random errors in each hidden layer, it is not a deterministic function of
{\bf X}
(or deterministic
\sigma
-field) as postulated in the original SDR problem (
1
).
Sun and Liang, 2022
further generalizes the StoNet to a kernel-expanded stochastic neural network (K-StoNet).
Huang et al., 2024
introduces a nonlinear SDR method based on neural networks by maximizing the distance covariance between the sufficient predictor
{\bf f}({\bf X})
and the response
Y
, and add a penalty term for identifiability. For further related developments, see
Chen et al., 2024
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce our method at the population level, describe its mathematical structure and the intuitions behind it. In Section
3
, we discuss the various important special cases of our framework and its relations with some existing methods. In Section
4
, we develop the numerical procedure to implement our method at the sample level. In Section
5
, we derive the convergence rate of the proposed estimator, and in Section
in the Supplementary Material, we show that this rate is faster than that of the neural network regression without dimension reduction. In Section
6
, we compare by simulation our proposed method with several existing linear and nonlinear SDR methods based on neural networks or RKHS. In Section
7
, we apply our method to a data application. To save space, all proofs, some additional simulations, and another data application are presented in a separate Supplementary Material.

2
Sufficient dimension reduction via BENN

2.1
General sufficient dimension reduction through ensembles

Let
(\Omega,{\cal{F}},P)
be a probability space,
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\bf X}}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\bf X}}})
and
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}},{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})
be measurable spaces, where
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\bf X}}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
and
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
, and
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\bf X}}}
and
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
are Borel
\sigma
-fields. Let
{\bf X}:\Omega\to\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\bf X}}}
and
Y:\Omega\to\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
be random variables measurable with respect to
{\cal{F}}/{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\bf X}}}
and
{\cal{F}}/{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
, respectively.
As is stated in the Introduction, in sufficient dimension reduction, we postulate the conditional independence (
1
) and, under this hypothesis, seek to estimate the statistic
{\bf f}({\bf X})
, or any random vector that has a one-to-one relation with
{\bf f}({\bf X})
. We call (
1
) the general sufficient dimension reduction problem as
{\bf f}
can be a linear or a nonlinear functions of
{\bf X}
. When
{\bf f}
is linear, (
1
) is called the linear SDR problem; when
{\bf f}
is nonlinear, (
1
) is called the nonlinear SDR problem. The condition (
1
) can be restated in terms of the characteristic family–or the ensemble–as we define below.

Definition 1

We say that a function class
{\cal{G}}
characterizes the distributions on
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
if, for any two random variables
Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
taking values in
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
,
E[g(Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})]=E[g(Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})]
for all
g\in{\cal{G}}
implies that
Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\overset{{\cal{D}}}{=}Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, where
\overset{{\cal{D}}}{=}
indicates equal in distribution. Such a function class is called an ensemble.

To be more specific, in this paper we consider the parametric family of functions

\displaystyle{\cal{G}}=\{g(\cdot,t):t\in I\},

(6)

where
I
is a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
. Many parametric families are characteristic. For example, when
Y
is a scalar,

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=&\,\{{g(y,t)}=y^{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}:t\in\mathbb{N}\},\hskip 82.38774pt{\cal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=\{{g(y,t)}=I(y\leq t):t\in{\mathbb{R}}\},\\
{\cal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{3}}=&\,\{{g(y,t)}={\sin(ty),\cos(ty)}:t\in{\mathbb{R}}\},\hskip 21.68121pt{\cal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}=\{{g(y,t)}=\kappa(t,y):t\in{\mathbb{R}}\},\end{split}

(7)

where
{\cal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
defines the general moment functions;
{\cal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
defines the cumulative distribution function;
{\cal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{3}}
defines the characteristic function; and
{\cal{G}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}}
, with a universal kernel
\kappa
such as the Gaussian radial basis function, defines an injective embedding of a probability into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. The above examples were used in
Yin and Li, 2011
as ensembles that characterize the central subspace. The idea of using a family of functions to characterize the central subspace for sufficient dimension reduction can be traced back to
Zhu and Zeng, 2006
, where a Fourier transform is used to recover the central subspace from a collection of central mean subspaces.
Li et al., 2008
uses a family of linear transformations to perform sufficient dimension reduction for multivariate responses. See also
Fertl and Bura, 2022
and

Zeng and Zhu, 2010
.

With the characteristic family (which we call ensemble)
{\cal{G}}
, we have the following equivalence:

\displaystyle Y\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,{\bf X}|{\bf f}({\bf X})\ \Leftrightarrow\ E[g(Y,t)|X]=E[g(Y,t)|{\bf f}({\bf X})]\quad\mbox{for all {$t\in I$}}.

Note that the right-hand side can be rewritten as
(h_{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf f})({\bf X})
for some
h_{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. So, at the population level, we can find
{\bf f}({\bf X})
by the minimizing the objective function

\displaystyle\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{I}}E\{[g({\bf Y},t)-(h_{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf f})({\bf X})]^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\}d\mu(t)

(8)

over a sufficiently rich family of
h_{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf f}
, where
\mu
is a finite measure on
I
. To implement this optimization through the neural network family is the basic idea underlying our proposal.

2.2
Neural network

We first give a definition of the neural network.

Definition 2

A function
{\bf f}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
is called a neural network with structural parameters
(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},{\bf k},d)
, where
{\bf k}=(k_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}})
, if it has the form

\displaystyle{\bf f}={\bf A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}+1)}}\circ\sigma\circ{\bf A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}})}}\ldots\circ\sigma\circ{\bf A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(2)}}\circ\sigma\circ{\bf A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}

where, for each
l=1,\ldots,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}+1
,
{\bf A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l-1}}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l}}}}
(with the convention
k_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}=p
and
k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}+1}}=d
) is the affine function

\displaystyle{\bf A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}}({\bf x})={\bf W}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}}{\bf x}+{\bf b}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}},\quad{\bf W}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l}}\times k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l-1}}}},\quad{\bf b}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l}}}},

and
\sigma
is an activation function. If, furthermore,
k_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=\ldots=k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}}=r_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
, then we call
{\bf f}
a neural network with structural parameters
(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},r_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},d)
.

In the above, for each
l=1,\ldots,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
, the function
\sigma\circ{\bf A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}}
is called the
l
-th layer of the neural network, and the last affine transformation
{\bf A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}+1)}}
is called the fully-connected layer. The structural parameters
p
and
d
are called the input and output dimensions;
l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
is called the depth or the number of layers of the neural network;
{\bf k}
is called the width vector, with each
k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l}}
called the width of the
l
-th layer. Furthermore, for
l=1,\ldots,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}+1
,
{\bf W}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}}
and
{\bf b}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(l)}}
are called the weight matrix and bias vector in the
l
-th layer, respectively, and the elements of them are called the weights and biases. In the special case where
k_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=\cdots=k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}}
,
r_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
is called the width of the neural network. We next define the function class of neural networks.

Definition 3

The collection of all neural networks with structural parameters
(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},{\bf k},d)
and with weights and biases belonging to
[-B,B]
for some
B>0
is called the neural network function class

{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{NN}}}(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},{\bf k},d,B)
. In the special case where
k_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=\cdots=k_{\scriptscriptstyle{l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}}}
, we denote this class by
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{NN}}}(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},r_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}},d,B)
.

2.3
The belt and ensemble of the neural network

The function class we use to estimate the functions
h_{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf f}
is the composition of two neural networks, where all the hidden layers and the last layer are allowed to go to infinity with the sample size
n
except one layer, which we call the “belt”. The output of our neural network is usually of high dimension that allows to go to infinity with
n
, so we could capture all the features of the conditional distribution in the limit. The following definition gives an explicit description of this structure.

Definition 4

Let

{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}
and
{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}
be two neural networks with structural parameters
(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},d)
and
(d,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},m)
, respectively,
where
{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=(k_{\scriptscriptstyle{11}},\ldots,k_{\scriptscriptstyle{1l_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}})
and
{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=(k_{\scriptscriptstyle{21}},\ldots,k_{\scriptscriptstyle{2l_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}}})
. We call the function

\displaystyle{\bf h}={\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(E)}}\circ{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(D)}},

a belted and ensembled neural network (BENN). The class of all such functions, with all weights and biases belonging to
[-B,B]
for some
B>0
, is called BENN-class, and will be written as
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{BENN}}}(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},d,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},m{,B})
. In the special case where the vector
{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
(or
{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})
contains the same integer
r_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
(or
r_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
), we write the BENN-class as
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{BENN}}}(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},r_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},d,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},r_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},m{,B})
.

Without a proper context the function
{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}\circ{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}
is nothing but the composition of two neural networks. Nevertheless, the concepts of belt and ensemble will bring useful insights into how our method works for sufficient dimension reduction.

The width of the belt,
d
, is the number of sufficient predictors we need to describe the regression relation. In practice, we usually choose
d
to be a small and fixed (i.e. not increasing with
n
) integer, while
m
can be a large integer. The middle layer with
d
neurons is called the “belt”, as it is usually much narrower than the layers before or after it, which constitutes the “belly”. The sub-neural network
{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
functions as a dimension reducer that brings down the original dimension of
p
of
{\bf X}
to a much lower number
d
, which is the dimension of the sufficient predictor. The superscript (D) indicates “dimension reduction”. The sub-neural network
{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}
performs the fitting of the family of the transformations of
Y
(or the ensemble). It attempts to capture all the information of the condition distribution of
Y|{\bf X}
. For this purpose
m
is usually choose to be a large integer and in theory it increases with the sample size
n
. The superscript (E) of this sub-neural network indicates “ensemble regression”. The next corollary shows that the
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{BENN}}}
family is a special case of the
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{NN}}}
-family. Its proof is straightforward and is omitted.

Corollary 1

Under Definitions
3
and
4
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{BENN}}}(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},d,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},m,B)={\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{NN}}}(p,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}+l_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}+1,({\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},d,{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}),m,B).

Our use of a narrow “belt” — that is,
d
is a small integer that does not depend on
n
–is consistent with statistical regression relations that are most commonly encountered in practice. Indeed, in most regression settings, the predictor vector
{\bf X}
, appears only in a few features of the conditional distribution of
Y|{\bf X}
. For example, in the commonly used nonparametric mean regression model for a scalar response
Y
, we assume

\displaystyle Y=f({\bf X})+\epsilon,

where
\epsilon
is an error independent of
{\bf X}
. In this case,
{\bf X}
only appears in the conditional mean
E(Y|{\bf X})
and nowhere else in the conditional distribution of
Y|{\bf X}
. So
d=1
. In the regression model with heteroscedasticity, we assume

\displaystyle Y=f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}({\bf X})+f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}({\bf X})\epsilon,

where
\epsilon\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,{\bf X}
. In this case,
{\bf X}
only appears in the conditional mean and conditional variance of
Y
given
{\bf X}
; so
d=2
. To take advantage of the low-dimensional structure through which
{\bf X}
enters into the conditional distribution is the main point of nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction.

The output of a BENN is fed into least-squares procedure that fits a finite collection of transformations of
Y
. In other words, the function
{\bf f}
in (
8
) is replaced by the neural network
{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}
; the functions
\{h_{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}:t\in I\}
is replaced by the neural network
{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}
, which has an
m
-dimensional output. Each of the components of
{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}}
, say
({\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}})_{\scriptscriptstyle{\ell}}
, represent a function that estimates
h_{\scriptscriptstyle{t_{\scriptscriptstyle{\ell}}}}
, where
t_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,t_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}
are points in
I
. The set of
m
functions

\displaystyle\{({\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(E)}}}})_{\scriptscriptstyle{\ell}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf g}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mbox{\tiny{(D)}}}}:\ell=1,\ldots,m\}

is the belted ensemble of neural networks that will be used as the proxy of
h_{\scriptscriptstyle{t}}{\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}}{\bf f}
in (
8
). The integral
\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{I}}\cdots d\mu(t)
will be replaced by numerical integral or sum, depending whether
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure or counting measure. The ensemble size
m
is allowed to go to infinity with
n
so that the numerical integral converges to the true integral as the sample size tends to infinity.

3.2
Relation with autoencoder

Apart from the mentioned paper by
Kapla et al., 2022
, another precursor of our method is what is known as the autoencoder for unsupervised dimension reduction.

Kramer, 1991
introduced a special type of neural network, the auto-associative neural network, to conduct nonlinear principal component analysis. They constructed a neural network, with a narrow layer in the middle, to fit
{\bf X}
itself. Since this is using a function of
{\bf X}
as the predictor to fit
{\bf X}
as the response, the authors call their method the “autoencoder”. The low-dimension random vector in the narrow layer is used as the nonlinear principal components of
{\bf X}
.
See also
Bourlard and Kamp, 1988
and
Hinton and Zemel, 1993
for related developments. Moreover,
Zhong et al., 2023
generalizes the autoencoder method to nonlinear functional principal component analysis by constructing a transformed functional autoassociative neural network, where the functions are expanded under a B-spline basis.

The narrow layer in the autoencoder plays the role of the belt in our estimator. The difference between our method and the autoencoder is that we use a belted network to fit a class of functions of
Y
rather than
{\bf X}
itself. Thus, the autoencoder is a unsupervised dimension reduction method, whereas BENN-SDR is a supervised dimension reduction method. The common point is that both methods use a narrow layer in a neural network as the vehicle for dimension reduction. In a sense, the relation between our proposed BENN-SDR and the autoencoder resembles the relation between nonlinear SDR based on reproducing kernel Hilbert space (
Lee et al., 2013
) and the kernel principal component analysis (
Sch

¨
o

lkopf et al., 1998
).

. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g(t,{\bf x})=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}E\left[s(t,Y)|{\bf X}={\bf x}\right]=E\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}s(t,Y)|{\bf X}={\bf x}\right]=-E\left[Y\sin(tY)|{\bf X}={\bf x}\right].

Note that

\displaystyle E\left[|Y\sin(tY)||{\bf X}={\bf x}\right]\leq E\left[|Y||{\bf X}={\bf x}\right].

Assuming the right-hand side is a continuous function of
{\bf x}
, then, by Assumption
3
, it is bounded by a constant. Hence the absolute value of the derivative
\partial s({\bf x},t)/\partial t
is bounded by a constant independent of
{\bf x}
and
t
. By the same argument, the same can be said about
g(y,t)=\sin(ty)
.
These imply the Lipschitz condition in Assumption
4
.

We will use
h_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,h_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}
to denote the components of
{\bf h}
, and use
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{0,1}},\ldots,f_{\scriptscriptstyle{0,d}}
to denote the components of
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}
.

Assumption 5

For each
m=1,2,\ldots
, the functions
\{h_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}:j=1,\ldots,m\}
are Lipschitz continuous with a universal constant
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{h}}
; that is, for all
{\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
and all
j=1,\ldots,m
and all
m=1,2,\ldots
,

\displaystyle|h_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}({\bf x})-h_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}({\bf x}^{\prime})|\leq L_{\scriptscriptstyle{h}}\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\|.

Assumption 6

The functions
\{f_{\scriptscriptstyle{0,k}}:k=1,\ldots,d\}
are Lipschitz continuous with a universal constant
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{f}}
; that is, for all
l=1,\ldots,d
and for all
{\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
,

\displaystyle|f_{\scriptscriptstyle{0,k}}({\bf x})-f_{\scriptscriptstyle{0,k}}({\bf x}^{\prime})|\leq L_{\scriptscriptstyle{f}}\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}\|.

In addition, when investigating the convergence rate, we set the activation functions in all neural networks to be the ReLU function; that is,
\sigma(x)=x^{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}\equiv\max\{0,x\}
.

5.2
Convergence rate

Since the goal of nonlinear SDR is the extract the sufficient predictor
\hat{\bf f}({\bf X})
, ideally, to assess the accuracy of nonlinear SDR, one should target the distance between
\hat{\bf f}
and
{\bf f}
in (
1
). However, note that the identifiable parameter in (
1
) is the
\sigma
-field generated by
{\bf f}({\bf X})
: if
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}({\bf X})
is a one-to-one transformation of
{\bf f}({\bf X})
then (
1
) continues to hold for
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
. Thus it is meaningless to compare the functions
{\bf f}
and
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
themselves. Instead, we should use a numerical measurement of the difference of two
\sigma
-fields, if this is possible.

Consider two statistics
{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
that are measurable with respect to
\sigma({\bf X})
and are of the same dimension. Moreover, suppose that
{\cal{A}}
is the smallest
\sigma
-field such that
{\bf Y}\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[-0.50003pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-4.49997pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.49994pt}\;\,{\bf X}|{\cal{A}}
and that
{\cal{A}}
is generated by
{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
. Then,
\sigma({\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})={\cal{A}}
if and only if

\displaystyle E[g(Y,t)|{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}]=E[g(Y,t)|{\bf X}].

(16)

So it is reasonable to measure the distance between the
\sigma
-fields generated by
{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
by the conditional expectations
E[g(Y,t)|{\bf X}]
and
E[g(Y,t)|{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}]
. This motivates us to use the following criterion to measure the accuracy of the estimated central
\sigma
-field:

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}=E\left[\iint\left|E[g(Y,t)|{\bf X}={\bf x}]-\hat{f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}({\bf x},t)\right|^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}d\mu(t)dP_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\bf X}}}({\bf x})\right]

(17)

where

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}({\bf x},t)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}\hat{f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}({\bf x})I(t_{\scriptscriptstyle{j}}\leq t<t_{\scriptscriptstyle{j+1}}),

(18)

and
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure on
I
. In (
17
), the outer expectation is taken with respect to the observed data
\{({\bf X}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}),\ldots,({\bf X}_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}},Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}})\}
, which appears in the expression only through the symbol “hat” in
\hat{f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}
. From (
12
) we see that
\hat{f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}({\bf x},t)
is the truncated least squares estimate of
g(Y,t)
; so it can be viewed as the best estimate of the conditional mean
E[g(Y,t)|{\bf X}]
. The criterion (
17
) is consistent with our consideration that leads to (
16
): the central
\sigma
-field plays the role of
{\cal{A}}
; the family
\{f({\bf X},t):t\in I\}
plays the role of
{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
, and the estimated family
\{\hat{f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}({\bf X},t):t\in I\}
plays the role of
{\bf T}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
. The convergence rate of
{\cal{L}}
is given by the next theorem. In the following, we use
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\lesssim b_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
to represent
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\leq cb_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
where
c
is a constant that does not depend on
n
. If
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\lesssim b_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
and
b_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\lesssim a_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
, then we write
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\asymp b_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}
.

Theorem 2

Suppose that

1.

{\bf t}=(t_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,t_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}})^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}
satisfies Assumption
1
;

2.

the ensemble
\{g(y,t),t\in I\}
satisfies Assumption
2
with
B_{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}\geq 1
;

3.

the support of
{\bf X}
satisfies Assumption
3
;

4.

the conditional expectation function
s({\bf x},t)
defined in (
13
) satisfies Assumption
4
;

5.

{\bf s}
has the form (
15
), where
{\bf h}=(h_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,h_{\scriptscriptstyle{m}})
and
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{0}}=(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{0,1}},\ldots,f_{\scriptscriptstyle{0,d}})
satisfy Assumptions
5
and
6
, respectively.

Let
{\cal{L}}
be defined by (
17
) and (
18
), where
\hat{\bf f}^{\scriptscriptstyle{*}}
is the minimizer defined by (
10
) and (
11
). Let
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},N_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},N_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
be positive integers,
B_{\scriptscriptstyle{w}}
be a sufficiently large number such that
B_{\scriptscriptstyle{w}}\geq B_{\scriptscriptstyle{y}}
and
B_{\scriptscriptstyle{w}}\asymp n
, and let the parameters
l_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},r_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},l_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},r_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
in (
11
) be

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=12L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}+14,\quad r_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=\max\{4p\lfloor N_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/p}}\rfloor+3p,12dN_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}+8d\},\\
&\,l_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=12L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}+14,\quad r_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=\max\{4d\lfloor N_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/d}}\rfloor+3d,12mN_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}+8m\}.\end{split}

(19)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal{L}}\lesssim&\,m^{\scriptscriptstyle{-2}}+n^{\mbox{\tiny$-1$}}(L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}N_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}+L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}N_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}m^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})(L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\log N_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}+L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\log m+L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\log N_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\\
&\,+L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\log n+L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\log n)+N_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-4/p}}L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-4/p}}+N_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-4/d}}L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-4/d}}.\end{split}

(20)

While the detailed proof of the theorem is given in the Supplementary Material, we outline here the main ideas and the important references used. We use the general result
in
Györfi et al., 2002
to link the convergence rate of nonparametric regression with the covering number of the class of nonparametric functions used, and use

Shen, 2024
to develop the covering numbers specific to structure of our BENN family. We use
Shen et al., 2020
to analyze the bias caused by approximating Lipschitz functions from within a deep learning family. We follow the ideas employed by
Bagirov et al., 2009
,
Bauer and Kohler, 2019
and
Kohler and Langer, 2021
to decompose the mean squared regression error.

5.3
Optimal rates of tuning parameters

Note that the convergence rate in Theorem
2
explicitly depends on the neural network parameters
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},N_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},N_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
and ensemble size
m
. This allows us to derive the optimal choice of these tuning parameters to optimize the convergence rate in Theorem
2
.

Theorem 3

Suppose that the conditions in Theorem
2
are satisfied and that the tuning parameters
m,L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},N_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},N_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
are of the form

\displaystyle m\asymp n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\alpha}},\quad L_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\asymp n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}},\quad L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\asymp n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}}},\quad N_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\asymp n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}}},\quad N_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\asymp n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}}}

(21)

for some
\alpha,\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}>0
. When
d\leq p-2
, the optimal rate of
{\cal{L}}
up to a log factor in (
20
) is

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}\lesssim n^{\scriptscriptstyle{-\frac{2}{p+2}}}\log n,

(22)

and this rate can be achieved by the following choice of the tuning parameters

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{p}{(p+2)(d+2)},\quad\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}+\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}=\frac{p}{2p+4},\quad\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}+\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=\frac{pd}{(2p+4)(d+2)}.

(23)

Under the optimal choice of tuning paramaters, we have
m\asymp n^{\scriptscriptstyle{\frac{p}{(p+2)(d+2)}}}
, which is always of a smaller order of magnitude than
n
. That means we do not need to perform as many as
n
transformations on
Y
. In comparison, in a kernel nonlinear SDR method, such as GSIR (
Lee et al., 2013
), we effectively applied
n
transformations on
Y
:
\kappa(\cdot,Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}),\ldots,\kappa(\cdot,Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}})
.

Furthermore, we show that the convergence rate of BENN is faster than that of the neural network regression without dimension reduction. Due to space limit, we place this result in the Supplementary Material.

6
Simulations

In this section we compare our BENN method with several existing nonlinear and linear dimension reduction methods, including a kernel based method and three other neural network based methods. We will denote the regression model of
Y
versus
{\bf X}
by roman letters A, B, C, …, and the models for the distribution of
{\bf X}
by roman numbers
I
,
II
,
III
, …. To assess accuracy, we use the distance correlation (
Székely et al., 2007
) for nonlinear SDR, and the distance between two projection matrices for linear SDR. For each model setting below, before the full-scale application of BENN, we first conduct preliminary experiments on two extra datasets with sample size
n=5000
to pick a reasonable number of iterations to avoid severe overfitting. This iteration number is then fixed throughout the rest of the simulations for this model. Our simulation studies contain four settings: nonlinear SDR without heteroscedasticity, nonlinear SDR with heteroscedasticity, nonlinear SDR with heteroscedastity alone, linear SDR with heteroscedasticity.
Due to space limit, we present the second setting in this section and place all the other settings in Section
the Supplementary Material.

For the setting of nonlinear SDR with heteroscedasticity, we consider a setting where
{\bf X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
with
p=50
and
d=2
. The regression model is

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}:\quad Y=[\sin((X_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}+X_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})\pi/10)+X_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}]+[2\sin^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}((X_{\scriptscriptstyle{3}}+X_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}})\pi/10)+X_{\scriptscriptstyle{3}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}]\epsilon,

where

{\bf X}
,
and

\displaystyle\textup{IV}:\quad X_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\ldots,X_{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}\ \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\ N(0.2,0.5).

The sufficient predictors for

are any one-to-one function of

\displaystyle{\bf f}({\bf X})=[f_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}({\bf X}),\ f_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}({\bf X})]=[\sin((X_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}+X_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})\pi/10)+X_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},\ 2\sin^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}((X_{\scriptscriptstyle{3}}+X_{\scriptscriptstyle{4}})\pi/10)+X_{\scriptscriptstyle{3}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}].

{\bf X}
