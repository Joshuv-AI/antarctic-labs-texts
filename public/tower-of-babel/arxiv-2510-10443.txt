Thermal Deformations in Super-Eddington Magnetized Neutron Stars: Implications for Continuous Gravitational-Wave Detectability
Hong-Bo Li, Yacheng Kang, Ren-Xin Xu

Abstract
Rapidly rotating neutron stars (NSs) are promising targets for continuous gravitational-wave (CGW) searches with current and next-generation ground-based GW detectors. In this Letter, we present the first study of thermal deformations in super-Eddington magnetized NSs with column accretion, where magnetic fields induce anisotropic heat conduction that leads to crustal temperature asymmetries. We compute the resulting mass quadrupole moments and estimate the associated CGW strain amplitudes. Our results show that Galactic magnetized NSs undergoing super-Eddington column accretion can emit detectable CGWs in upcoming observatories. Assuming a 2-yr coherent integration, the Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer could detect such CGW signals from rapidly spinning NSs with spin periods P ≲ 20 ​ ms P\lesssim 20\,\rm ms , while the LIGO O5 run may detect systems with P ≲ 6 ​ ms P\lesssim 6\,{\rm ms} . These findings suggest that super-Eddington magnetized NSs could represent a new class of CGW sources, providing a unique opportunity to probe the NS crust and bridge accretion physics with GW astronomy.



I
Introduction

Ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULXs) are extragalactic objects with X-ray luminosities (
L_{\mathrm{X}}
) significantly exceeding
{10^{39}\,\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}
, and in some cases reaching up to
{10^{42}\,\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}
, well above the Eddington limit for a 10 solar mass black hole
(
P. Kaaret et al. 2017
;
S. N. Fabrika et al. 2021
;
A. King et al. 2023
)
. These sources are generally found in active star-forming regions and are typically associated with young, luminous high-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs)
(
T. P. Roberts et al. 2002
;
Y. Gao et al. 2003
;
K. Kovlakas et al. 2020
)
. Traditionally, ULXs have been interpreted as accreting stellar-mass
(
M. C. Begelman et al. 2006
;
J. Poutanen et al. 2007
)
or intermediate-mass black holes
(
E. J. M. Colbert & R. F. Mushotzky 1999
;
J. M. Miller 2006
;
T. J. Maccarone et al. 2007
;
F. Koliopanos et al. 2017
)
. However, this interpretation was challenged by the discovery of coherent X-ray pulsations from
M82 X-2

(
M. Bachetti et al. 2014
)
, which exhibits an X-ray luminosity of
{L_{\mathrm{X}}\simeq 1.8\times 10^{40}\,\rm erg\,s^{-1}}
and a pulse period of
1.37\,\rm s
. Since black holes lack a solid surface, they are unlikely to generate such X-ray pulsations
(
P. Kaaret et al. 2017
)
. Instead, the pulsating ULXs are naturally explained by accretion onto magnetized neutron stars (NSs), where infalling matter is channeled along magnetic field lines onto the poles, producing rotating hot spots and pulsed X-ray emission
(
F. D. Seward & P. A. Charles 2010
)
. These systems, known as ULX pulsars (ULXPs), extend the observed population of NS accretors reaching super-Eddington luminosities. The observed properties of currently known ULXPs are summarized in Table

.

Notably, recent population synthesis studies further suggest that NSs in low- and intermediate-mass X-ray binaries (LMXBs and IMXBs) can reach ULX luminosities during intense mass-transfer phases
(
A. R. King et al. 2001
;
Y. Shao & X.-D. Li 2015
;
G. Wiktorowicz et al. 2019
;
M. Quast et al. 2019
;
D. Misra et al. 2020
)
. However, the detailed physical origin of the extreme luminosities observed in ULXPs are still under debate. Proposed models span a wide range of NS surface magnetic field strengths, from
10^{11}\,\rm G
, where the luminosity may be boosted by strong outflows and geometrical beaming
(
M. Middleton & A. King 2016
;
M. J. Middleton & A. King 2017
;
A. King & J.-P. Lasota 2019
;
J.-P. Lasota & A. King 2023
;
K. Kovlakas et al. 2025
)
, to magnetar-like fields of
10^{14}\,\rm G
, which can suppress radiation pressure
(
M. M. Basko & R. A. Sunyaev 1976
;
H. Tong 2015
;
S. Dall’Osso et al. 2016
;
K. Y. Ekşi et al. 2015
;
K. Kovlakas et al. 2025
)
and confine accretion columns above the magnetic poles
(
A. A. Mushtukov et al. 2015
;
H. Inoue 2020
)
. In addition to magnetic field strength, several other factors may also significantly affect accretion dynamics in ULXPs, including non-dipolar magnetic field configurations
(
G. L. Israel et al. 2017a
;
S. S. Tsygankov et al. 2018
)
, enhanced neutrino cooling
(
A. A. Mushtukov et al. 2018
;
A. A. Mushtukov et al. 2019
;
A. Asthana et al. 2023
;
A. A. Mushtukov et al. 2025
;
H.-B. Li et al. 2025
)
, and photon bubble instabilities
(
J. Arons 1992
;
M. C. Begelman 2006
;
L. Zhang et al. 2021
)
. In view of this, electromagnetic observations alone may not allow us to fully infer the intrinsic properties of these super-Eddington magnetized NSs.

On the other hand, rapidly rotating NSs can emit continuous gravitational wave (CGW) via an oscillating mass quadrupole moment, arising from various possible mechanisms such as elastic strain
(
B. Haskell et al. 2006
;
N. K. Johnson-McDaniel & B. J. Owen 2013
;
F. Gittins et al. 2020
;
J. A. Morales & C. J. Horowitz 2022
;
J. A. Morales & C. J. Horowitz 2025
)
, magnetic deformation
(
A. Melatos & D. J. B. Payne 2005
;
B. Haskell et al. 2008
;
K. Fujisawa et al. 2022
)
, and
r
-mode instabilities
(
N. Andersson et al. 1999
;
Y. Levin 1999
;
P. Arras et al. 2003
;
R. Bondarescu et al. 2009
)
. In this context, super-Eddington magnetized NSs may also represent promising CGW targets. In particular, magnetically channeled column accretion from the companion can induce non-axisymmetric crustal deformations in rapidly spinning NSs, generating significant CGW signals. Even in the absence of a detection, stringent upper limits on CGW amplitudes from these super-Eddington magnetized NSs can provide meaningful constraints on their accretion dynamics and internal structure.

In this Letter, we present the first quantitative study of thermal deformations in super-Eddington magnetized NSs with column accretion, focusing on crustal temperature asymmetries induced by magnetically modulated anisotropic heat conduction. We compute the resulting mass quadrupole moments and evaluate the detectability of the associated CGW emission with current and next-generation ground-based GW detectors, including the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-wave Observatory
(
B. P. Abbott et al. 2016
, LIGO;)
, the Einstein Telescope
(
M. Branchesi et al. 2023
, ET;)
, and the Cosmic Explorer
(
V. Srivastava et al. 2022
, CE;)
. Our results indicate that Galactic magnetized NSs undergoing super-Eddington column accretion can produce detectable CGWs in upcoming observatories, representing a new class of CGW sources. Assuming a 2-yr coherent integration, the ET and CE could detect such CGW signals from rapidly spinning NSs with spin periods
P\lesssim 20\,\rm ms
, while the LIGO O5 run may detect systems with
P\lesssim 6\,{\rm ms}
. These findings suggest that super-Eddington magnetized NSs provide a unique opportunity to probe the NS crust and bridge accretion physics with GW astronomy.

This Letter is organized as follows. Section
II
describes the construction of the accretion column model above these super-Eddington magnetized NSs. In Section
III
, we calculate their thermal crustal deformations. Section
IV
discusses the detectability of the associated CGW signals, and Section
V
presents our conclusions.

II
Structure of the accretion column

Super-Eddington accretion onto magnetized NSs generates radiation pressure-dominated shocks above the NS surface, leading to the formation of accretion columns
(
H. Inoue 1975
;
H. R. Takahashi & K. Ohsuga 2017
;
H. Inoue 2020
;
A. Inoue et al. 2020
;
T. Kawashima & K. Ohsuga 2020
;
A. Inoue et al. 2023
)
. The structure and temperature of these columns are primarily determined by the accretion rate and the optical depth of the inflowing material. The governing dynamical equations can be expressed as
(
H. Inoue 1975
;
H. Inoue 2020
)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\varepsilon}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}(\frac{\varepsilon}{r_{\rm D}}-\frac{GM}{r^{2}})\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\rho}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3\rho}{4\varepsilon}(\frac{\varepsilon}{r_{\rm D}}+3\frac{GM}{r^{2}})\,,

(2)

where
\varepsilon
and
\rho
denote the radiation energy per unit mass and the mass density within the accretion column, respectively;
M
is the NS mass; and the photon diffusion length
r_{\rm D}
is given by
r_{\rm D}=1.4\times 10^{5}\left(\frac{\dot{M}}{10^{17}\rm{g\;s}^{-1}}\right)\;\rm{cm}
, where
\dot{M}
is the mass accretion rate onto the NS.

Before quantitatively analyzing thermal deformations in super-Eddington magnetized NSs, we should first solve Equations (
1
) and (
2
) to determine the physical properties of the accretion columns. Taking a NS model with
M=1.4\,M_{\odot}
and
R=10^{6}\,\rm cm
as an example, we present in Figure
1
the radiation energy, mass density, pressure, and temperature of accretion columns as functions of the column height for different mass accretion rates. Details of the numerical treatments are provided in Appendix
A
. One can find that an extremely high mass accretion rate results in a taller accretion column. Moreover, the temperature at the base of the column remains largely insensitive to the accretion rate, as the radiation pressure is counterbalanced by the magnetic pressure. The characteristic temperature at the column base is approximately
4\times 10^{9}\,\rm K
, which exceeds that typically observed in most known LMXB systems
(
H. Schatz et al. 1999
)
.

B=10^{12}\,\rm G
, we define the normalization parameters as
\varepsilon_{0}=GM/R
,

We now investigate the thermal structure of magnetized NSs undergoing super-Eddington column accretion and derive the resulting temperature perturbations. Assuming that the magnetized NS is spherically symmetric and in a steady-state thermal equilibrium, the heat flux driven by column accretion obeys Fourier’s law as

{\bf F}=-\kappa\nabla T\,,

(3)

where
\kappa
denotes the thermal conductivity. In our previous work
(
H.-B. Li et al. 2025
, see Section 3 of)
, we discussed in detail the relevant heating and cooling processes—including accretional heating and neutrino emission—used to compute
{\bf F}
, along with the corresponding thermal conductivity
\kappa
. Importantly, the boundary condition is set by the characteristic temperature at the base of the accretion column. The presence of internal toroidal magnetic fields in super-Eddington magnetized NSs can introduce anisotropy in the thermal conductivity, thereby generating significant temperature gradients across the crust
(
U. R. M. E. Geppert et al. 2004
;
U. Geppert et al. 2006
;
D. Page et al. 2007
;
D. N. Aguilera et al. 2008
)
.

Following
E. L. Osborne & D. I. Jones 2020
, we compute the temperature perturbations in these super-Eddington magnetized NSs with column accretion. This approach is valid for magnetic field strengths
B\lesssim 10^{13}\,\rm G

(
E. L. Osborne & D. I. Jones 2020
;
T. J. Hutchins & D. I. Jones 2023
)
. Note that in this section, we adopt the DH NS equation of state from
F. Douchin & P. Haensel 2001
for calculating temperature perturbations, which yields a NS mass of
M\simeq 1.84\,M_{\odot}
and radius
R\simeq 1.17\times 10^{6}\,\rm cm
. These values differ from the typical NS model adopted in Section
II
. Further details on the calculations of magnetic-field-induced perturbations in the thermal structure are provided in Appendix
B
. Our results, shown in Figure
2
, illustrate the dependence of the relative temperature perturbation
\delta T/T
on magnetic field strength. An increase in magnetic field strength leads to more prominent temperature asymmetries within the crust. Compared with typical values in most LMXBs
(
E. L. Osborne & D. I. Jones 2020
, see e.g.,)
, the temperature perturbations in super-Eddington magnetized NSs can be enhanced by up to four orders of magnitude. This significant amplification arises from the combination of a higher base temperature at the accretion column foot and the characteristically stronger internal magnetic fields.

As noted by
G. Ushomirsky et al. 2000
, electron capture rates in the NS crust are highly sensitive to temperature, particularly when it exceeds
2\times 10^{8}\,\rm K
. Elevated temperatures can enhance electron capture transitions relative to cooler regions, thereby inducing compositional and structural asymmetries. Consequently, large-scale temperature perturbations in magnetized NSs undergoing super-Eddington column accretion can induce elastic deformations in the crust. When such asymmetries are misaligned with the NS rotation axis, they lead to non-axisymmetric density distributions and a resulting mass quadrupole moment
Q_{22}
. In this Letter, we apply the self-consistent numerical approach developed by
H.-B. Li et al. 2025
to compute the mass quadrupole moment
Q_{22}
. A brief overview of the methodology is presented in Appendix
C
. We find that
Q_{22}
increases with the strength of the internal magnetic field
B
. Specifically, for the cases illustrated in Figure
2
,
Q_{22}
increases from
Q_{22}\simeq 0.4\times 10^{38}\,\rm g\,cm^{2}
at
B=10^{12}\,\rm G
to
Q_{22}\simeq 1.2\times 10^{38}\,\rm g\,cm^{2}
at
B=10^{13}\,\rm G
.

IV
CGW detectability

Rapidly rotating, super-Eddington magnetized NSs with asymmetric deformations can generate CGWs via quadrupole radiation
(
M. Zimmermann & E. Szedenits 1979
;
P. Jaranowski et al. 1998
)
. To assess the detectability of such signals, we express our results in terms of the stellar ellipticity, defined as
\epsilon\equiv\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{15}}\frac{Q_{22}}{I_{\rm zz}}
, where
I_{\rm zz}\simeq 10^{45}\,\rm g\,cm^{2}
is the principal moment of inertia. The corresponding GW strain amplitude
h_{0}
is given by
(
B. P. Abbott et al. 2019
;
B. T. Reed et al. 2021
)

h_{0}=\frac{4\pi^{2}G}{c^{4}}\frac{I_{\rm zz}\epsilon\,f^{2}_{\rm GW}}{d}\,,

(4)

where
f_{\rm GW}=2\nu
is the GW frequency corresponding to a magnetized NS with spin frequency
\nu
, and
d
is the source distance. In this Letter, we suggest that the ellipticity resulting from the temperature-induced asymmetry can be approximately expressed as
\epsilon\simeq 2\times 10^{-7}\,B_{12}
, where
B_{12}
is the surface magnetic field strength in units of
10^{12}\,\rm G
. We employ Equation (
4
) to assess the detectability of CGWs from all known ULXPs in Table
1
. Unfortunately, we find that the expected GW strain amplitudes
h_{0}
from these sources lie below the sensitivity of LIGO’s O5 run
(
B. P. Abbott et al. 2016
)
for a 2-yr coherent integration
(
A. Watts et al. 2008
)
, and are far below the detection capabilities of ET and CE. This non-detectability arises primarily from two factors. First, except for Swift J0243.6
+
6124, all detected ULXPs are extragalactic, leading to substantial GW signal attenuation due to their large distances. Second, the spin frequencies of these ULXPs are insufficiently high to place the resulting GW signals within the peak sensitivity range of current and next-generation detectors.

Although the results above may seem discouraging given the current detected ULXPs, recent population synthesis studies suggest that there could be
\sim 7-20
NS ULXs with helium-star donors in our Galaxy
(
Y. Shao & X.-D. Li 2015
;
Y. Shao et al. 2019
;
D. Misra et al. 2020
;
S.-J. Gao et al. 2022
;
L. Li et al. 2024
, MW;)
. Notably, a Galactic detached pulsar + helium star binary, PSR J1928
+
1815, with the NS spin period of
P=10.55\,{\rm ms}
, has recently been discovered by the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical radio Telescope
(
Z. L. Yang et al. 2025
, FAST;)
. Such nearby NS + helium star binaries in our Galaxy may represent an early stage preceding intense mass transfer. Once they enter the super-Eddington accretion phases, they could significantly contribute to both the Galactic ULX population and the detectable CGW signal.

More importantly, these Galactic NS ULXs can be spun up (recycled) during their accretion phases, ultimately evolving into millisecond pulsar
+
white dwarf systems, which are observed as intermediate-mass binary pulsars (IMBPs). Consequently, the observed spin periods of IMBPs can serve as empirical upper bounds on the spin frequencies of magnetized NSs in their earlier super-Eddington accretion phases. We find that several known IMBPs exhibit NS spin periods shorter than
P\simeq 20\,{\rm ms}
; for instance, PSR J1614
-
2230 has an exceptionally short spin period of
P\simeq 3\,{\rm ms}

(
T. M. Tauris et al. 2011
;
T. M. Tauris et al. 2012
;
L. Li et al. 2024
)
, supporting the potential existence of Galactic NS ULXs with spin periods below
P=20\,{\rm ms}
. In view of this, Figure
3
presents the predicted CGW strain amplitudes for Galactic NS ULXs with various fixed spin periods and their corresponding detectabilities. Assuming a 2-yr coherent integration time, we find that the predicted Galactic NS ULXs with spin periods shorter than
20\,\rm ms
could be detected by next-generation ground-based GW observatories, including the ET and CE. The LIGO O5 run may detect Galactic NS ULXs with
P\lesssim 6\,{\rm ms}
. Although no such rapidly spinning NS ULXs have been observed in our Galaxy to date, our results highlight their potential as promising CGW sources, should a Galactic NS ULX system with
P\lesssim 20\,{\rm ms}
indeed exist. We encourage future population studies to further explore the occurrence and properties of these fast-spinning super-Eddington magnetized NSs.

V
Conclusion

In this Letter, we present the first study of thermal deformations in super-Eddington magnetized NSs undergoing column accretion and investigate their implications for CGW emission. Focusing on radiation-pressure-dominated accretion columns, we compute their base temperatures and demonstrate how magnetically induced thermal perturbations produce localized asymmetries. These asymmetries can generate a finite mass quadrupole moment, potentially leading to observable CGW signals. Given the strong dependence of detectability on the spin period, our results indicate that next-generation ground-based GW detectors, such as the ET and CE, could observe such CGW signals from Galactic NS ULXs with spin periods
P\lesssim 20\,\rm ms
, while the upcoming LIGO O5 run can only detect the fast-spinning super-Eddington magnetized NSs with
{P\lesssim 6\,{\rm ms}}
.

Future work incorporating more realistic models of internal magnetic field configurations
(
U. Geppert et al. 2004
;
U. Geppert et al. 2006
;
J. A. Pons & U. Geppert 2007
;
J. A. Pons et al. 2025
)
and updated microphysics of the accreted crust
(
A. F. Fantina et al. 2018
;
A. F. Fantina et al. 2022
)
will enable more accurate predictions of thermal deformation and CGW amplitudes. Such refinements will not only improve detectability estimates for future GW detectors but also help optimize search strategies, offering deeper insights into the internal structure of magnetized NSs. Further dedicated studies in this direction are encouraged.

Appendix A
Numerical treatment of accretion column structure

Assuming one-dimensional, steady-state super-Eddington column accretion, the structure of the accretion column is determined by the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy
(
H. Inoue 1975
;
H. Inoue 2020
, see e.g.,)
. Equation (
1
) describes the evolution of the energy density, balancing the gravitational acceleration
(GM/r^{2})
against radiative dissipation; while Equation (
2
) governs the density stratification, where the compressive effects of gravity are balanced by radiative pressure. Together, the two equations jointly define the vertical structure of the accretion column and the associated energy transport in the polar regions.

At the top of the polar accretion columns, the mass density and radiation energy density are set as follows
(
H. Inoue 1975
;
H. Inoue 2020
)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm top}

\displaystyle=7\rho_{\rm F}(r_{\rm S})\,,

(A1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\rm top}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}\frac{GM}{r_{\rm S}}\,.

Here,
\rho_{\rm F}
denotes the matter density in the free-fall region at the top boundary location
r_{\rm S}
. The position
r_{\rm S}
is iteratively adjusted to ensure consistency with the bottom boundary condition. Specifically, we numerically integrate the dynamical equations inward from an initial guess for
r_{\rm S}
toward the stellar surface. At the surface, the bottom boundary is defined by the balance between gas pressure and magnetic pressure:

p_{*}=\frac{\rho_{*}\varepsilon_{*}}{3}=\frac{B^{2}_{*}}{8\pi}\,,

(A2)

where the subscript “
*
” denotes quantities evaluated at the stellar surface.

Appendix B
Temperature perturbation calculation

Following the methodology of
E. L. Osborne & D. I. Jones 2020
, we assume a spherically symmetric, steady-state temperature profile for magnetized NSs undergoing super-Eddington column accretion. From energy conservation, the heat flux
\mathbf{F}
is related to the net heat generation rate per unit volume
Q
via

\nabla\cdot{\bf F}=Q\,.

(B1)

Assuming that the magnetic-field-induced variation in the net heating rate depends only on temperature
(
J. A. Pons & U. Geppert 2007
;
E. L. Osborne & D. I. Jones 2020
)
, the perturbation equation can be written as

\nabla\cdot{\bf\delta F}=\frac{{\rm d}Q}{{\rm d}T}\delta T\,.

(B2)

Owing to the spherical symmetry of the background magnetized NS, we expand the perturbed quantities
\delta T
and
\mathbf{\delta F}
in tensor spherical harmonics
(
E. L. Osborne & D. I. Jones 2020
)
:

\displaystyle\delta T(r\,,\theta\,,\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell m}\delta T_{\ell m}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta\,,\phi)\,,

(B3)

\displaystyle\bf\delta F

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell m}U_{\ell m}\hat{\bf r}Y_{\ell m}+V_{\ell m}\nabla Y_{\ell m}\,,

(B4)

where
\hat{\bf r}
is the radial unit vector. Substituting Equations (
B3
) and (
B4
) into Equation (
B2
) yields the perturbed thermal structure equations:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}U_{\ell m}}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{r}U_{\ell m}+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}V_{\ell m}+\frac{{\rm d}Q}{{\rm d}T}\delta T_{\ell m}\,,

(B5)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}(\delta T_{\ell m})}{{\rm d}r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{{\rm d}\kappa}{{\rm d}T}\frac{{\rm d}T}{{\rm d}r}\delta T_{\ell m}-\frac{U_{\ell m}}{\kappa}\,,

(B6)

accompanied by the algebraic relation,

V_{\ell m}=-\kappa\delta T_{\ell m}+\kappa\omega^{*}r\frac{{\rm d}T}{{\rm d}r}\psi_{\ell m}\,,

(B7)

where
\omega^{*}\approx 0.02B_{12}/T_{9}
, with
B_{12}=B/10^{12}\,\rm G
, and
T_{9}=T/10^{9}\,\rm K
. A detailed description of the toroidal magnetic field structure
\psi_{\ell m}
can be found in Equation (33) of
E. L. Osborne & D. I. Jones 2020
. In this Letter, we consider only the quadrupole component, i.e.,
\ell=m=2
. The boundary conditions follow those in
G. Ushomirsky et al. 2000
, namely, the absence of temperature perturbations at both the bottom and the surface of the NS crust.

Appendix C
Crust deformation calculation

When a star deviates from perfect sphericity, it develops multipole moments, defined as

Q_{\ell m}\equiv\int_{0}^{R}\delta\rho_{\ell m}(r)\,r^{\ell+2}\,{{\rm d}r}\,,

(C1)

where
(l,m)
denotes the harmonic mode of the density perturbation
\delta\rho_{\ell m}(r)
;
R
is the stellar radius. Following
G. Ushomirsky et al. 2000
and
H.-B. Li et al. 2025
, the fiducial quadrupole moment
Q_{22}
can be expressed as

Q_{22}\equiv\int\left.\frac{\partial\ln\rho}{\partial\ln T}\right|_{p}\frac{\delta T}{T}\,\rho\,r^{4}\,{\rm d}r\,,

(C2)

Equation (
C2
) shows that the quadrupole moment of magnetized NSs undergoing super-Eddington column accretion is directly determined by their crustal temperature perturbations. In this context, the relative temperature variation
\delta T/T
acts as the source term in the perturbation equations, which are coupled and described in detail by Equations (36–47) of
H.-B. Li et al. 2025
.
