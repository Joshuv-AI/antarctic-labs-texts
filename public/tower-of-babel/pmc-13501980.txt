The Goldilocks state of transcriptional readthrough for chimeric splicing
Authors: Guillaume F Chanfreau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProper transcription termination coupled to pre-mRNA cleavage and polyadenylation is an important step in gene expression as it generates the 3'UTR of mRNAs and defines transcription units. Defective termination (transcriptional readthrough) can suppress transcription of downstream genes by transcriptional interference but can also promote the production of chimeric transcripts, which contain the exons of upstream genes spliced to exons of downstream genes. This review will summarize current findings of chimeric splicing between exons of adjacent genes generated by transcriptional readthrough and the conditions that promote these events. Chimeric splicing can be facilitated by inactivation of factors that promote transcription termination or of specific splicing factors, particularly those involved in 3'-splice site recognition. This effect is due to the tight coupling between recognition of the terminal intron 3'-splice site and of the downstream polyadenylation sites, and its impact on transcriptional termination. Production of chimeric transcripts is also increased in cellular conditions that perturb transcription termination, such as cellular stress, treatment with the chemotherapeutic agent Imatinib or viral infections. However, not all conditions that promote transcriptional readthrough result in chimeric splicing, suggesting that the production of chimeric mRNAs requires a “Goldilocks” state of alteration of the transcriptional machinery that allows for transcriptional readthrough without a general reduction of splicing efficiency.Keywords: Transcription termination, transcriptional readthrough, chimeric splicing, SF3B1, U2AF

Transcriptional readthrough and impact on downstream gene regulation

Transcription termination is an essential step in gene expression. Termination of transcription by RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) at most mRNA genes is coupled to cleavage and polyadenylation (CPA) (reviewed in [1]). CPA generates the mature 3' end of most eukaryotic mRNAs and the selection of the polyadenylation site (PAS) will determine the length of the 3'UTR (Figure 1A), which can have major consequences on the fate of the mRNA (reviewed in [2]). The importance of proper termination has been highlighted by studies showing that defective termination (transcriptional readthrough; Box 1) can interfere with expression of downstream genes, as initially reported [3](Figure 1B). This phenomenon called transcriptional interference is based on the model that the elongating RNA polymerase complex coming from an upstream gene prevents assembly of productive initiation complexes on the downstream gene promoter (Figure 1B). Transcription interference is more likely to occur in compact genomes in which transcription units tend to be densely placed close to each other but can also be detected in larger, less dense genomes including in mammalian cells [4,5]. Transcriptional interference is not always a problem, as this mechanism is used to regulate expression of metabolic genes by upstream promoters that generate non-coding (nc) RNAs [6,7]. In addition, transcriptional interference was recently shown to provide the molecular basis for the expression of single olfactory receptor proteins from gene arrays in olfactory neurons of some ant species [8]. Besides modulating transcription at downstream promoters, transcriptional readthrough can also lead to the production of transcripts containing the information from multiple transcription units, also known as polycistronic RNAs (Box 1), when the RNA polymerase transcribes exonic regions of downstream genes (Figure 1C). I will review below evidence of polycistronic transcription that arises from transcriptional readthrough with the concomitant production of chimeric mRNAs containing information from multiple genes, and the conditions in which this phenomenon is favored.

Discovery of genes that exhibit transcriptional readthrough and intergenic chimeric splicing

In the late 1990s, the first examples of intergenic splicing between exons belonging to adjacent genes resulting in so-called chimeric mRNAs (Box 1) were published [9,10] (reviewed in [11]). Chimeric mRNAs carrying the information from two separate genes can be generated by a number of processes, including genomic rearrangements at the DNA level or trans-splicing between two distinct mRNAs [12]. For the purpose of this review, I will limit the discussion to chimeric transcripts generated from adjacent transcription units, when the RNA polymerase fails to terminate and transcribes through two adjacent genes, resulting in polycistronic transcription (Box 1), also called “Read-In” transcription (Box 1), as illustrated in Figure 1(C). RNAs produced by splicing of exons belonging to adjacent genes have been called in the literature “Transcription-Induced Chimeras” (TIC [13]); “Fusion Transcripts of Adjacent Genes” (FuTAGs [14]); or “cis-Splicing of Adjacent Gene (cis-SAGe [15]) transcripts. In this review, I will refer to those transcripts simply as chimeric mRNAs. Early genomic studies based on cDNA and expressed sequenced tags (EST) analyses performed in the early 2000s showed that chimeric mRNAs produced from adjacent genes can be detected genome-wide [11,13]. These studies based on EST analyses lacked the depth that was later provided by high throughput RNA sequencing studies, and many of the events detected were based on the detection of single sequences, raising questions about the significance or frequency of these events. Nevertheless, these early studies suggested that some level of transcriptional readthrough can occur genome-wide, which can be sufficient for some genes to produce chimeric mRNAs. This model was consistent with the observation that gene pairs that produced chimeric mRNAs tend to be located closer to each other than the typical human gene pair, as the median distance between genes that generate chimeric transcripts was found to be around 8.5 kb, as opposed to 48 kb for the average gene pair in human genome [11]. The fact that chimeric mRNAs were detected at higher frequency for gene pairs that are within a short distance supported the model that they were generated by transcriptional readthrough, rather than by trans-splicing. These early studies estimated that around 5% of adjacent genes found in the human genome might produce chimeric mRNAs [13].

With the multiplication of genomic approaches and that of RNA sequencing datasets, it became apparent that chimeric transcripts could be detected in a variety of cells and contexts. Because many RNA sequencing efforts focused on the characterization of the transcriptome of cancer cell lines, the production of chimeric intergenic spliced transcripts first appeared to be a hallmark of cancer cells (4,15–20]; reviewed in [12,21]). However, other studies have shown that chimeric mRNAs can be detected generated in a variety of contexts and in normal mammalian cells or tissues [5,22–24]. The level of transcriptional readthrough and chimeric transcripts detected seems to vary extensively between genomes and appears to be anticorrelated with the level of compactness of the genomes. While transcriptional readthrough and chimeric transcripts have been detected in many mammalian cells lines and healthy tissues [5], chimeric transcripts are typically not detected in plants and yeast transcriptomes, unless perturbations of termination are experimentally introduced (see below). This phenomenon might be linked to the level of density of the transcription units within genomes. In compact genomes such as the yeast S. cerevisiae, transcription units are located close to each other, which possibly requires a very tight transcription termination process for most genes. In large genomes such as mammalian cells, larger intergenic distances may allow for more flexibility in the termination process which may result in the background level of readthrough detected by RNA sequencing studies.

In general, mRNAs generated by intergenic cis-splicing tend to exclude the last exon of the upstream gene and the first intron in the downstream gene [4], as illustrated in Figure 1C. This splicing pattern can be rationalized by mechanistic studies that demonstrated a reciprocal coupling between splicing of terminal introns and PAS recognition, and which showed that recognition of the 3' splice site (3'SS) of the terminal intron and that of the downstream PAS are functionally linked (Figure 2A) [25]. More generally, efficient recognition of downstream PAS impacts the splicing of terminal introns [26,27]. Thus, a defect in usage of the last 3'SS of the upstream gene will lead to inefficient recognition of the poly(A) site of the first transcription unit, and in a defect in termination. The spliceosome will then use the first 3'SS transcribed from the downstream gene when it emerges from the elongating RNA polymerase (Figure 1C), instead of 3'SS of the terminal intron of the upstream gene. While most intergenic splicing events identified involve known exons and splice sites, some chimeric mRNAs are produced through splicing of sequences of adjacent genes that are not spliced in a normal context. In S. cerevisiae, perturbation of termination induces intergenic splicing events between adjacent genes that do not naturally contain introns [28]. The reason for this phenomenon is that the upstream mRNA can contain a sequence that resembles a 5'SS, and the downstream mRNA contains sequences that match a branchpoint and a 3'SS (Figure 1D). When transcribed separately, these two mRNAs do not undergo splicing because the upstream RNA lacks a branchpoint or 3'SS, and the downstream RNA lacks a 5'SS. However, when these three splicing signals are contained in the same mRNA due to transcriptional readthrough, it reconstitutes a functional substrate for splicing and generates chimeric mRNAs (Figure 1D). These results show that hidden splicing signals can be present in transcription units, and that transcriptional readthrough can reveal the presence of hidden splicing signals and produce chimeric mRNAs from otherwise splicing-silent genes.

Chromatin-associated proteins that impact transcriptional readthrough and chimeric mRNA production

While chimeric spliced mRNAs can be detected in cancer cells and to some extent in normal cell lines, experimental perturbations of the gene expression machinery can lead to an increase in the production of these chimeric mRNAs (Figure 2B). A key feature of chimeric cis-splicing is the fact that the RNA polymerase needs to transcend the boundaries of genes and in some cases the heterochromatin barrier that separates two active genes to continue transcription into neighbor genes. Consistent with this idea, genomic regions prone to transcriptional readthrough tend to exhibit increased chromatin accessibility in intergenic regions [5]. One of the first factor identified as a gatekeeper for chimeric splicing is the chromatin insulator factor CTCF. Reduction in the levels of CTCF was shown to increase the level of some chimeric RNAs [15,29]. It is difficult to rationalize why reducing CTCF increases levels of chimeric mRNAs considering the multiple roles of CTCF [30] and the pleiotropic effects of its depletion. However, given the role of CTCF in establishing boundaries between euchromatin and heterochromatin, it is possible that reducing CTCF levels may relax these boundaries and allow the transcriptional machinery to extend through some heterochromatin regions normally present between genes, allowing a higher level of transcriptional readthrough.

In addition to CTCF, the histone methyltransferase SETD2 was also found to be involved in preventing transcriptional readthrough [4]. SETD2 associates with RNA polymerase II and mediates transcription-coupled trimethylation of histone H3 at residue 36 (H3K36me3). While inactivation of SETD2 is typically associated with increased cryptic transcription, mutations or experimental depletion of SETD2 were found to promote transcriptional readthrough in cancer cells [4]. Although inactivation of SETD2 was not formally linked to the production of chimeric mRNAs in that study, the authors found that the production of chimeric mRNAs correlated well with the levels of transcriptional readthrough [4], suggesting that inactivation of SETD2 might indeed promote the production of chimeric mRNAs. In a distinct study, SETD2 inactivation was shown to result in an increase in chromatin accessibility and in changes in splicing patterns, although no specific analysis of chimeric mRNAs was performed [31]. One model to explain the increase in transcriptional readthrough when SETD2 is depleted would be that SETD2 depletion promotes an increase in chromatin accessibility near the 3'-end of genes, facilitating readthrough by the RNA polymerase. Alternatively, it is possible that the impact of SETD2 on readthrough and chimeric splicing might be mediated through its functions on coupling splicing to transcription [32]. Surprisingly, SETD2 depletion did not impact production of chimeric mRNAs expressed from an artificial reporter system [33]. It is possible that the lack of impact of SETD2 depletion on chimeric mRNA production in that system was due to the fact that the reporters used were expressed from transiently transfected plasmids, which may not reconstitute the normal chromosomal chromatin environment that is normally impacted by SETD2 function in other studies that demonstrated a role for SETD2 function in limiting transcriptional readthrough [4].

Cleavage and polyadenylation efficiency influences the production of chimeric mRNAs

Beyond factors involved in chromatin, it seems logical to postulate that any perturbation that reduces the efficiency of transcription termination would lead to transcriptional readthrough and to the formation of chimeric mRNAs, assuming no concomitant effect on splicing efficiency. However, several studies have shown that disruption of recognition of the PAS which leads to transcriptional readthrough is correlated with decreased splicing efficiency [34–36]. Moreover, genomic analyses have shown that although transcriptional readthrough can be frequently detected in human tissues, it is generally associated with lower splicing efficiency [5]. Therefore, the production of chimeric mRNAs requires a “Goldilocks” alteration of the gene expression machinery, in which termination needs to be perturbed sufficiently to allow the RNA polymerase to transcribe into downstream genes, but without too severe of an impact on splicing, which would otherwise prevent the splicing between exons of adjacent genes and the production of chimeric mRNAs. In addition, the production of chimeric mRNAs requires a very specific dysregulation of splicing, in which the terminal 3'SS of the upstream gene exon(s) are not recognized, such that the 5'SS of these upstream exons can be spliced to the 3'SS of the exons from the downstream gene (Figure 1C).

The main factors that are known to impact the efficiency of transcriptional termination are subunits of the CPA machinery, and multiple studies have shown that depleting or inactivating CPA factors can increase the production of chimeric mRNAs (Figure 2B). In mammalian cells, a reporter system designed to identify factors that control the production of chimeric cis-spliced RNAs showed that reducing the level of CPSF1 (a subunit of the main CPA factor CPSF previously known as CPSF160) increased the production of chimeric mRNAs [33]. Consistent with these data obtained in mammalian cells, mutation of the Arabidopsis thaliana cleavage and polyadenylation factor FPA was shown to promote transcriptional readthrough and the production of chimeric RNAs [37,38]. Low levels of chimeric RNAs can also be detected in another A. thaliana CPA mutant pcfs4, which affects a homologue of the CPA factor Pcf11. In the yeast S. cerevisiae, inactivation of Ysh1, the endonuclease subunit of the CPA machinery (homologue of the CPSF73 endonuclease subunit) or of the RNA binding protein Rna15 resulted in termination defects and polycistronic transcripts, with the production of chimeric mRNAs produced by splicing of RNA sequences from adjacent genes [28]. Taken together, these studies show that in a variety of organisms and genomes, disruption of the CPA machinery can lead to increase of chimeric splicing, most likely because of the transcriptional readthrough induced by depletion of these CPA factors. In mammalian cells, the increase in chimeric mRNAs upon CPA factors depletion might be explained by the impact of this depletion on the recognition of the 3'SS of the terminal intron (Figure 2A). Reduction in the levels of CPA factors and of the recognition of the downstream PAS would lead to inefficient recognition of the 3'SS of the last intron of the upstream gene, promoting splicing of the 5'SS of the last intron to the 3'SS of the first intron in the downstream gene (Figure 1C). It is worth noting that reduction of the levels of mammalian CPA factors other than CPSF1 did not seem to reduce the production of chimeric mRNAs in the reporter screen mentioned above [33]. Whether this reflects insufficient depletion of the corresponding factors, a lack of sensitivity due to the plasmid-based expression of the reporter mentioned above, or a differential impact of the different CPA subunits on readthrough and chimeric splicing remains to be established.

The coupling between terminal 3' splice site recognition and CPA-mediated termination explains the specific impact of reducing splicing factors involved in 3' splice site recognition on chimeric splicing

The role of splicing factors in the production of chimeric mRNAs is complicated and somewhat counterintuitive, as one would expect that inactivation of, or reduction in the levels of most splicing factors would reduce the production of chimeric spliced mRNAs because of general splicing defects. However, given that recognition of the 3'SS also enhances poly(A) site usage and termination (Figure 2A), partial inactivation of factors involved in 3'SS recognition may result in an increase in transcriptional readthrough and a higher potential for production of chimeras. Therefore, two opposite effects of reducing the activity of splicing factors are expected, depending on whether or not they are involved in the process of 3'SS recognition. Interestingly, the reporter screen study mentioned above analyzed the impact of knocking down a number of splicing factors on the production of chimeric mRNAs and only a very small number of factors were found to specifically affect production of the chimeric mRNAs [33]. Reduction in the levels of SR family protein SRRM1 (also known as SRm160) reduced the production of chimeric mRNAs expressed from the reporters or from endogenous genes [33]. While the impact of SRRM1 depletion seemed to be specific to chimeric mRNAs, SRRM1/SRm160 is known to act not just as a splicing coactivator, but also function to stimulate 3’-end processing, and it is also a component of the splicing-dependent exon-junction complex (EJC), which binds mRNAs following splicing [39]. Given the pleiotropic functions of SRRM1/SRm160, it is difficult to propose a specific mechanism which would explain the decrease of chimeric transcripts production upon SRRM1depletion and it remains to be determined whether the splicing, 3’−end processing or EJC functions of SRRM1 are involved in enhancing chimeric splicing production.

More significantly, two key splicing factors involved in 3'SS recognition were shown to limit the production of chimeric mRNAs. Reducing the levels of splicing factor SF3B1 resulted in an increase in chimeric transcripts expressed from the reporter system mentioned above, and the authors showed that reducing SF3B1 levels also increased chimeric mRNAs production from endogenous loci [33]. More recently, depletion of the splicing factor U2AF1 was also shown to increase the production of chimeric mRNAs from closely adjacent genes [40]. Both U2AF1 and SF3B1 are required for 3'SS recognition (Figure 2A). While no mechanistic model was proposed to explain why SF3B1 or U2AF1 depletion increased chimeric mRNAs levels in the studies mentioned above, the specific effect of the depletion of these factors on chimeric mRNA production can be rationalized by the aforementioned coupling between terminal 3'SS and downstream PAS recognition (Figure 2A). Defect in recognition of the terminal 3' splice sites of the upstream mRNA will negatively impact recognition of the downstream PAS, leading to higher levels of transcriptional readthrough and more opportunity for chimeric splicing. This model would explain why only proteins involved in 3'SS recognition were found to limit chimeric mRNA splicing.

Perturbations of transcription termination caused by stress or viral infections can induce chimeric splicing

As mentioned earlier, transcriptional readthrough can be detected in normal, healthy human tissues [5,41]. However, cellular stress can cause extensive inhibition of Pol.II termination in mammalian cells [42–45], resulting in the production of 3'-extended RNAs called DoGs, because transcription occurs Downstream Of Genes (Box 1; reviewed in [46]). Based on these studies, one might expect that stress-induced transcriptional readthrough might promote the widespread production of chimeric mRNAs. However, this does not seem to be the case. First, DoGs do not always extend into genes located downstream. In addition, transcriptional readthrough is generally associated with inefficient splicing [53442], which would limit the production of chimeric spliced transcripts even if the RNA polymerase extends sufficiently into downstream genes. Nevertheless, at least one study reported that stress-induced transcriptional readthrough can promote the production of some chimeric mRNAs [47]. These observations show that perturbation of transcriptional termination is a necessary, yet not sufficient condition to cause chimeric splicing, providing further evidence for the “Goldilocks” model of transcriptional readthrough proposed above. Interestingly, a recent study showed that treatment of chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) cells with the chemotherapeutic agent imatinib also promoted transcriptional readthrough [48]. While readthrough transcripts generally show inefficient splicing [53448], imatinib treatment did not trigger intron retention for the readthrough mRNAs but rather resulted in the production of chimeric transcripts for many adjacent genes [48]. Thus, imatinib treatment seems to result in a unique molecular signature, which perturbs transcription termination, while at the same time allowing for efficient splicing resulting in the production of chimeras. The molecular mechanism involved in this effect is currently unknown but should provide unvaluable mechanistic insights.

Another example of major stress condition experienced by cells is virus infection, which can result in widespread defective transcription termination of transcription by the host RNA polymerase in infected cells (reviewed in [49]). One of the best documented examples is lytic infection by Herpes Simplex Virus 1 (HSV-1) which was found to disrupt termination of transcription of most cellular genes, resulting in extensive intergenic transcription [47,50,51]. The mechanism proposed to explain the disruption of RNA Polymerase II termination upon HSV-1 infection is the interaction of the HSV-1 immediate early protein ICP27 with CPSF [51], which inhibits CPA-mediated termination at many host genes. HSV-1-induced termination defects resulted in an associated increase in aberrant splicing that includes the production of chimeric mRNAs, but interestingly, the intergenic splicing events detected upon HSV-1 infection do not seem to correlate with those triggered by cellular stress that induces DoGs [47]. One of the differences between HSV-1-induced transcriptional readthrough and stress-induced DoG production is that HSV-1 infection promotes a strong increase in chromatin accessibility downstream of poly(A) sites [47]. The mechanism involved in this increase in chromatin accessibility is unclear, but it would explain the higher level of transcriptional readthrough triggered by HSV-1 infection, as the RNA polymerase would more easily transcribe downstream if not hindered by the chromatin barrier. Cellular infection by Influenza virus was also shown to disrupt transcription termination by the host RNA polymerase II [52–54]. As described above for HSV-1 infection, Influenza-mediated inhibition of Pol.II termination can result in the production of chimeric transcripts [53]. In the case of Influenza, the NS1 viral protein interacts with the CPSF30 subunit, inhibiting CPSF30 function [55] and resulting in an inhibition of CPA-mediated termination. Thus, perturbation of RNA Polymerase II termination has been extensively documented for two viruses, with CPSF being the main complex targeted by these viruses to promote this effect.

From an evolutionary perspective, the induction of chimeric splicing of host mRNA genes through inhibition of host RNA polymerase termination may correspond to a general strategy to facilitate virus replication or infection. Considering that some of the chimeric mRNAs are not functional and/or might encode truncated proteins with dominant negative functions, it is tempting to speculate that some viruses have evolved to promote the production of chimeric host-derived mRNAs, some of which may generate proteins with dominant negative functions that could perturb the host cell metabolism to facilitate viral replication. In favor of this idea, expression of the chimeric transcript linking the adjacent IFNAR2 and IL10RB human genes was found to be strongly correlated with COVID-19 infectivity and severity in human patients [56]. Chimeric IFNAR2-IL10RB transcripts were found to produce fusion proteins expressed at the cell surface [56], which inhibited Interferon antiviral response by sequestering type I Interferons away from normal receptors [56]. It is not clear whether or not SARS-Cov2 infection actually increase production of these fusion proteins. However, this observation shows that chimeric transcripts generated by readthrough can indeed play a role in promoting viral infection by acting as dominant negative versions, in support of the aforementioned hypothesis.

Biological functions and cellular fate of chimeric transcripts produced by transcriptional readthrough

One of the major biological question that arises from the detection of chimeric mRNAs generated through splicing of readthrough products is whether or not they encode functional protein products or correspond to functional ncRNAs. Analysis of chimeric fusion mRNAs in non-cancer tissues and cells estimate that only about 20% of mRNAs produced could generate functional proteins [22]. Some chimeric mRNAs yield protein products that can be detected by mass spectrometry [57], but these chimeric mRNAs originate from genes located on different chromosomes, so these are not generated by transcriptional readthrough and are not relevant to this discussion. In addition to the aforementioned chimeric IFNAR2-IL10RB transcripts [56], limited examples of potentially functional chimeric transcripts have been described, such as those generated from adjacent UGT genes, which were proposed to contribute to the diversity of the human UGT proteins [58]. Some genetic analyses have reported that loss of function of some chimeric mRNAs can generate cell proliferation defects. For instance, knocking down the ADCK4-NUMBL readthrough chimeric mRNA reduced cell proliferation rate and cell motility in prostate cells, and reduced cell migration in astrocytes but without impact on proliferation [59]. The IFNAR2-IL10RB chimeric transcripts characterized in [56] also show that fusion proteins expressed from chimeric transcripts can modulate the response to viral infection.

While some chimeric mRNAs could bear functional significance (Figure 2C), others may not because the information content of upstream and downstream genes may not have evolved to maintain a functional open-reading frame when fused together through splicing. Arguing against functionality, most chimeric mRNAs produced by intergenic cis-splicing do not maintain the ability to produce functional proteins [22]. In these cases, translation is expected to stop early due to premature translation termination codons (PTCs) which is predicted to trigger targeting of the chimeric mRNAs by nonsense-mediated decay (NMD; Figure 2C). Interestingly, depletion of splicing factor U2AF1 was found to increase the level of chimeric mRNAs containing translational frame-shifts and decrease the levels of in-frame chimeric RNAs [40], but the mechanism involved to explain this differential effect is unknown. Surprisingly, data obtained from different experimental systems suggest that chimeric mRNAs containing premature termination signals seem to escape degradation by NMD. In plants, chimeric RNAs generated through inactivation of the CPA factor FPA do not increase in mutant backgrounds defective for NMD [37]. Similarly, in the yeast S. cerevisiae, chimeric mRNAs resulting from inactivation of CPA factors are unaffected by concomitant inactivation of the key NMD factor Upf128. The most likely explanation is that these chimeric mRNAs are in general not exported or not translated at high level. In support of the hypothesis of inefficient export, chimeric mRNAs generated by HSV-1 infection were shown to be mainly absent from cytoplasmic fractions [47]. More generally, mRNAs generated from transcriptional readthrough tend to be found primarily in the nucleus [4]. Systematic analysis of read-in transcription also showed that mRNAs that are prone to be produced by read-in transcription are not translated well [42]. These studies suggest that mRNAs generated by transcriptional readthrough and chimeric splicing may not be exported well out of the cytoplasm, which would allow them to escape translation-mediated quality control pathways such as NMD. The reason for the increased nuclear retention of these transcripts is unclear and should be investigated, as it might point to a quality control mechanism that recognizes aberrant splicing and prevents mRNA from being exported.

Transcriptional readthrough and chimeric splicing – the tip of the iceberg?

Chimeric mRNAs produced by transcriptional readthrough and splicing of exons from adjacent genes have been described for over 30 years but may have been under-reported for technical or biological reasons. First, the detection of chimeric splicing requires specific bioinformatic pipelines (compared in [60]) which are not always used systematically in the bioinformatics analysis of high throughput RNA sequencing data sets. Therefore, it is possible that some chimeric splicing events may have escaped detection because of a lack of specific bioinformatics analysis. For instance, several studies have analyzed the impact of mutations of splicing factors in various cancers [61], but these studies have not reported the production of chimeric mRNAs induced by these mutations, most likely because of the lack of specific focus on intergenic or chimeric splicing events during the bioinformatics analysis of the sequencing data. Interestingly, many splicing factors mutations associated with cancer involve proteins involved in 3' SS recognition, including SF3B1 and U2AF161, the depletion of which was found to increase the level of chimeric transcripts [40]. Because chimeric mRNAs seem to be more highly expressed in cancer cells, and because several oncogenic mutations affect factors involved in 3'SS recognition, it seems logical to think that chimeric mRNAs might be more abundant in cancer cells carrying oncogenic mutations of these splicing factors, but this remains to be carefully analyzed using specific bioinformatics pipelines devoted to chimeric transcripts analysis.

The mechanistic basis for the production of chimeric transcripts through transcriptional readthrough is not yet fully understood. It has become clear that just promoting transcriptional readthrough is not sufficient to generate chimeric mRNAs, and that efficient recognition of the 3'SS of terminal exons coupled with that of the PAS of upstream genes is critical to prevent transcriptional readthrough and avoid chimeric mRNA production. The main system that has been used to identify factors that modulate chimeric splicing has been based on plasmid-borne reporters [33]. Although this system has proved valuable to identify proteins that control chimeric splicing, it has limitations since the plasmids used do not recapitulate the native chromosomal context, as well as the diversity of distances, poly(A) sites, and chromatin environments that can lead to the production of chimeric transcripts [44]. Genomic screens using chromosomal based reporters might identify additional factors that regulate the production of chimeric transcripts. It also remains to be understood if the mechanisms that give rise to chimeric transcripts in a normal context are related to those involved in stress-induced transcriptional readthrough or following treatment with Imatinib. Finally, the functional significance of chimeric mRNAs remains to be carefully studied in future studies to establish whether these encode functional proteins, contribute to cellular transformation (reviewed in [21]), and/or correspond to a widespread strategy used by viruses infection to disrupt cellular metabolism.
