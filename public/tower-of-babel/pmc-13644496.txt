Harnessing the central dogma: Enzyme-driven gene diversification strategies for in cellulo continuous directed evolution
Authors: Liang Ma, Kai Chen, Yuan Lin, Yihan Deng, Siyu Ma, Yihan Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn cellulo continuous directed evolution offers a powerful route to optimize biomolecules with enhanced or novel functions by enabling rapid, iterative exploration of vast fitness landscapes. At the heart of this approach lie intracellular gene diversification strategies, which have undergone notable innovations in recent years. Although a growing list of techniques has emerged—often tailored to distinct host organisms—they share overarching design principles rooted in the central dogma. By examining how genetic mutations are introduced and propagated through nucleic acid–processing and mutating enzymes, we gain a unifying framework for comparing and adapting these strategies. Here, we review the latest intracellular gene diversification methods within this conceptual lens, highlighting their commonalities, their mechanistic underpinnings, and their adaptability. We then discuss key opportunities for future development, aiming to guide the design of next-generation in cellulo continuous directed evolution systems with greater efficiency, broader host applicability, and finer control over mutagenesis.Keywords: Continuous directed evolution, Central dogma, Intracellular gene diversification, Enzyme-centric perturbation, Fitness landscape

Introduction

Directed evolution has become an indispensable tool for engineering biomolecules with enhanced or novel functionalities, impacting fields such as enzyme engineering, protein therapeutics, gene editing and delivery, and high-sensitivity biosensors, among others [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Traditional directed evolution protocols involve constructing a mutant library in vitro, transforming this library into microbial cells (e.g., Escherichia coli (E. coli) or Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae)) for expression, and then screening or selecting mutants based on observed phenotypic or functional changes [6,7]. Mutations that confer improved function are enriched and used to create the next-generation library, a cycle repeated multiple times to refine the desired traits (Fig. 1a). However, these processes are often labor-intensive, time-consuming, and restricted to a narrow range of host organisms due to transformation barriers. They also limit the scale and depth of evolutionary searches, reducing the ability to explore broader fitness landscapes [8].

To address these constraints, substantial advances have been made across multiple fronts, broadening the scope and boosting the efficiency of directed evolution. These innovations include: (1) high-throughput or more precise library construction and delivery methods, as well as new intracellular gene diversification techniques for in cellulo continuous directed evolution [7,9,10]; (2) expansion of host platforms, such as to include industrial microbial strains, mammalian cells, and cell-free systems [[11], [12], [13]]; (3) advanced functional screening and selection platforms, incorporating automation and high-throughput detection [14]; and (4) computational strategies, notably machine learning-based approaches and in silico evolution [[15], [16], [17]]. Collectively, these developments have markedly increased the success rate and impact of directed evolution endeavors.

Among these breakthroughs, in cellulo continuous directed evolution has emerged as a highly promising approach. To address conceptual ambiguity in the field, we define “in cellulo continuous directed evolution” in a practical and inclusive manner. Specifically, “in cellulo” refers to systems that enable intracellular gene diversification, encompassing both virus-based strategies and classical intracellular mutagenesis approaches. The term “continuous directed evolution” captures two distinct yet complementary dimensions: the continuity of gene diversification and the continuity of functional evaluation. Conventionally, continuous directed evolution denotes an autonomous process in which genes of interest are continuously diversified and selected, facilitating iterative optimization of biomolecules with minimal manual intervention (Fig. 1b) [10]. In practice, many in cellulo directed evolution systems still require varying degrees of human intervention, particularly in the evaluation of variant functionality, as certain traits (e.g., fluorescence intensity or the performance of biosensors) are not easily encoded into synthetic gene circuits. Nevertheless, advances in automation technologies (e.g., automated liquid handling robotics, passaging systems and flow cytometry) are making such processes increasingly compatible with continuous automated workflows [18]. Accordingly, in cellulo directed evolution systems capable of continuous mutagenesis within a target sequence—while not yet fully autonomous but compatible with automation-enabled continuous evolution—are also included within the scope of continuous directed evolution in this review.

Systems for in cellulo continuous directed evolution have been implemented in a diverse array of organisms (referred to as chassis cells), including phages, bacteria, yeast, and mammalian cells [9,10,19]. Among these, E. coli stands out for its high compatibility with in cellulo continuous directed evolution. This is attributable to its ease of cultivation, simple genetic background, efficient genome engineering and gene diversification, diverse selection marker repertoire, and the capacity to directly couple phenotype with growth, enabling seamless integration into high-throughput automated platforms. Nonetheless, the broader applicability of E. coli is constrained by its inability to perform complex post-translational modifications on proteins, limited capacity to express or correctly fold eukaryotic proteins, and its failure to recapitulate the metabolic environments of industrially relevant hosts. Moreover, its prokaryotic nature makes it distinct from mammalian cells in terms of chromatin structure, signaling pathways, and intracellular context, thereby limiting its applicability in evolving mammalian-relevant proteins [13,20]. Yeast, as a eukaryotic microorganism, exhibits slower growth than E. coli, yet it offers key eukaryotic features such as proper protein folding, secretion, and glycosylation. In addition to enabling the evolution of yeast-native proteins, these capabilities make yeast a usable platform for evolving mammalian-related proteins. Mammalian cells offer the most physiological environment for evolving therapeutic proteins or probing mammalian-specific functions. Yet, their use is hindered by high cultivation costs, low throughput, and limited compatibility with automation. Additionally, their complex genome organization and efficient DNA repair mechanisms pose significant barriers to mutagenesis, rendering some microbial strategies ineffective. Furthermore, the highly interconnected gene networks introduce further complications during selection due to redundancy and feedback regulation [13,20]. Accordingly, chassis cells bring shared advantages and challenges to in cellulo continuous directed evolution systems. The choice of chassis should be guided by the nature of the target protein or pathway, the desired evolutionary pressure, the robustness and convenience of the evolution platform, and practical considerations such as cost and scalability (Table S1).

Intracellular gene diversification lies at the heart of in cellulo continuous directed evolution, enabled by recent advances in genetic engineering and synthetic biology. Once an appropriate chassis is selected, the choice of intracellular gene diversification strategy becomes a critical factor in ensuring the success of a continuous evolution campaign, and is evaluated based on factors like mutation rate, mutation range, spectrum uniformity, mutagenesis controllability, and orthogonality to the host (Fig. 1c, 1d). Most intracellular gene diversification strategies introduce mutations through modifications to genetic carriers (DNA or RNA) or by perturbing fundamental processes underlying genetic information transfer (e.g., replication, transcription, and reverse transcription). The central dogma, which outlines the flow of genetic information [21], offers a unifying framework to classify these approaches. Specifically, three loops within the central dogma—DNA→DNA→DNA, DNA→RNA→DNA, and RNA→RNA→RNA—can direct how mutations are introduced and propagated (Fig. 1e) [22]. The DNA→DNA→DNA loop involves DNA replication, the most commonly exploited target for intracellular gene diversification [7]. The DNA→RNA→DNA loop integrates transcription and reverse transcription, prompting novel strategies that harness or perturb these processes [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. Finally, the RNA→RNA→RNA loop, relating to RNA replication, is gaining more attention given the prevalence of RNA viruses (e.g., SARS-CoV-2 [28]) and associated mutagenesis strategies [[29], [30], [31]].

In this review, we apply the central dogma as a guiding framework to systematically examine current intracellular gene diversification strategies, discussing the underlying mutagenesis mechanisms, performance characteristics, advantages, limitations, and demonstrated applications (Table S1). Our focus is on enzyme-centric perturbations—targeting DNA polymerase, nucleoside deaminase, RNA polymerase, reverse transcriptase, and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase—that directly modify nucleic acid substrates. By offering a comparative overview, we aim to help researchers select methods suited to specific experimental objectives and inspire new intracellular gene diversification strategies that empower novel in cellulo continuous directed evolution endeavors.

Intracellular gene diversification through perturbation at the DNA level

Introducing mutations at the DNA level is the most direct and intuitive method of intracellular gene diversification, reflecting the historical roots of in vitro directed evolution. By design, these DNA-focused strategies can be broadly classified into three categories: error-prone DNA polymerase–mediated mutagenesis (Fig. 2a), base editor–driven mutagenesis (Fig. 3a, 3b), and DNA-level combinatorial mutagenesis (Fig. 4a). In this section, we organize and discuss each category according to its mechanistic principle, rather than chronological order. Although certain recombination-based approaches also induce mutations in genomic DNA, our focus here is on techniques that generate mutagenic changes within cells prior to any recombination step—an essential feature of in vivo continuous evolution. Methods involving the construction of mutant libraries in vitro followed by their delivery into cells for homologous recombination are reviewed comprehensively elsewhere [19].

Intracellular gene diversification through error-prone DNA replication

Leveraging viral DNA polymerases

Many DNA viruses encode orthogonal and autonomous DNA polymerases with higher error rates than host polymerases, making them appealing for directed evolution. For instance, Shoulders and colleagues established a directed evolution system in mammalian cells by employing an error-prone adenoviral DNA polymerase mutant (e.g., F421Y/D827A) in a trans-complemented format to replicate an adenoviral genome (with deletions in the DNA polymerase and protease genes) harboring the gene of interest [32]. The F421Y mutation compromises the polymerase’s proofreading, whereas D827A reduces nucleotide selection fidelity, collectively yielding a mutation rate of approximately 3.7 × 10⁻⁵ substitutions per base pair per viral passage—a rate orders of magnitude higher than the typical human somatic mutation rate of ∼10⁻⁹ substitutions per base pair per replication cycle [33], over genes of interest up to 7 kb in length (Fig. 2b). However, a pronounced mutation bias was observed. The main challenge with virus-based mutagenesis lies in cell cytopathic effects from uncontrolled viral replication, which can limit the range of genes of interest.

Orthogonal replicons as linear plasmids

To circumvent cytopathic effects, virus-derived replication systems can be engineered into controllable, linear plasmids that eliminate lytic cycles. This design enables stable, long-term replication in host cells, as exemplified by BacORep [34] and EcORep [35]. In BacORep (established in Bacillus thuringiensis, Fig. 2c), the error-prone GIL16 DNA polymerase mutant replicates a linear plasmid derived from the temperate phage GIL16 (where phage structural and lysis genes are replaced by the gene of interest). The mutation coverage spans the entire plasmid (up to ∼15 kb), with a mutation rate of ∼6.8 × 10⁻⁷ substitutions per base pair per generation. All 12 base substitution types can occur, yielding a relatively balanced mutation spectrum. BacORep has been successfully applied in generating strong promoters and increasing methanol assimilation. Similarly, EcORep (Fig. 2d) is founded on a synthetic replication operon in E. coli—engineered from the lytic phage PRD1—that encodes an error-prone DNA polymerase mutant and multiple other genes related to replication. This system replicates a linear replicon containing the gene of interest and PRD1 inverted terminal repeats, with genes of interest up to 16.5 kb and a mutation rate of ∼10⁻⁶ substitutions per base pair per generation. The mutation spectrum includes both base transitions and transversions with a relatively balanced distribution, except for a slightly lower proportion of G:C to C:G mutations (below 5%). EcORep was validated by evolving tigecycline resistance and GFP fluorescence. While both systems share core principles, BacORep retains replication components on the linear plasmid itself, providing additional flexibility for mutagenesis control.

Exploiting naturally occurring linear DNA plasmids

Linear plasmids also occur naturally in certain microorganisms. For example, the linear DNA plasmids pGKL1 and pGKL2 in Kluyveromyces lactis replicate similarly to bacteriophage φ29 [36]. OrthoRep capitalizes on these plasmids by relocating the protein-primed DNA polymerase (TP-DNAP1) gene from pGKL1 into the host genome (Fig. 2e) [37]. The error-prone TP-DNAP1 mutant expressed from the genome replicates the cytoplasmic linear plasmid p1, which replaces its original polymerase gene with the gene of interest and selection markers. Iterative engineering refined TP-DNAP1 variants’ mutation rates and spectra [38], with TP-DNAP1-4-2 at ∼1 × 10⁻⁵ substitutions per base pair per generation (transition-biased), and BadBoy2 reaching ∼1.4 × 10⁻⁴ while maintaining a more balanced spectrum. With these enhanced variants, OrthoRep has demonstrated robust performance across multiple evolution experiments [3,[38], [39], [40]], including evolving drug-resistant malarial dihydrofolate reductase and evolving the tryptophan synthase b subunit from Thermotoga maritima.

Targeted artificial DNA replisome (TADR)

Another design-oriented method employing viral DNA polymerases is TADR (Fig. 2f). It uses ΦX174 CisA nickase to define a replication origin and a hyperactive Rep helicase fused to an error-prone T5 DNA polymerase mutant to induce mutations. The CisA nickase binds a 30-bp initiation sequence on the plasmid, creates a single-strand nick, and recruits Rep helicase to unwind the DNA. The fused T5 polymerase then synthesizes a new mutated strand, displacing the original non-template strand. Although most mutations occur between the CisA initiation and termination sites, inefficient termination can extend the mutated region across the plasmid. TADR increases the mutation rate by ∼2.3 × 10⁵-fold relative to baseline, with over 60% of mutations being base transitions, ∼20% A > T transversions, and the remainder comprising other base transversions. Currently demonstrated in the self-evolution of TADR for a higher mutation rate and the evolution of tetA for resistance to tigecycline in E. coli, TADR may be adapted for other chassis cells [41].

Host-derived error-prone DNA polymerases

In addition to viral or virus-like elements, host-derived error-prone DNA polymerases have been engineered for intracellular mutagenesis. One example uses E. coli Pol I (Fig. 2g) [42]: an error-prone Pol I mutant is expressed from a plasmid carrying a pSC101 origin (Pol I–independent), thereby introducing mutations during replication of a high-copy ColE1 plasmid (Pol I–dependent) encoding the gene of interest. This approach achieves ∼8.1 × 10⁻⁴ mutations per bp per generation, mainly transitions. However, even after optimizing culture conditions, the host genome mutation rate still rises by several dozen-fold [42]. The system was used in engineering TEM-1 β-lactamase for hydrolyzation of a third-generation lactam antibiotic, aztreonam.

Building on Pol I-mediated mutagenesis, EvolvR (Fig. 2h) offers a targeted approach to reduce off-target effects [43]. EvolvR fuses a Cas9 nickase (nCas9) with an error-prone Pol I mutant and directs it to a specific locus using a sgRNA. After nCas9 introduces a nick, the error-prone Pol I rewrites up to 350 bp around that site. The nCas9-PolI3M (i.e., D424A/I709N/A759R) variant exhibits ∼2.4 × 10⁻⁶ mutations per bp per generation, though the rate drops with increasing distance from the PAM site. EvolvR was initially developed in E. coli and later adapted to yeast (yEvolvR) [44], and was used to identify novel mutations to E. coli rpsE that confer spectinomycin resistance.

Perturbations to the host polymerase III and DNA repair systems

Other approaches focus on host polymerase III (e.g., Pol III in E. coli) or mismatch repair to induce widespread mutations. Keasling et al. developed Feedback-regulated Evolution of Phenotype (FREP) [45], which uses mutD5 (a dnaQ mutant encoding Pol III’s ε subunit) to hamper proofreading and reduce mismatch repair, thereby increasing the mutation rate genome-wide. A feedback circuit linked to the gene of interest regulates mutD5 expression, allowing fine-tuned mutation rates. FREP was employed in increasing tyrosine and isoprenoid production in E. coli.

Such Pol III fidelity perturbations can be combined with additional methods, as illustrated by E. coli mutator strain XL1-Red [46]. XL1-Red carries mutations mutS, mutD, and mutT, compromising mismatch repair, Pol III proofreading, and the hydrolysis of 8-oxodGTP, respectively. This system raises mutation rates in both plasmid and genomic DNA but suffers from a biased spectrum and a reduced cell fitness, limiting its utility in continuous directed evolution.

Intracellular gene diversification through DNA base editing

Base editing has gained significant attention for its potential in correcting genetic disorders [47] and has likewise proven useful in directed evolution. By enabling direct modification of nucleotide bases, base editing can drive heritable changes without requiring full double-strand breaks or template-based repair. In continuous directed evolution engineering endeavors, base editing systems have achieved iterative cycles of mutagenesis, and the editing enzyme has also been migrated into various systems to facilitate efficient mutagenesis.

Leveraging B cell somatic hypermutation

A well-known natural example is somatic hypermutation (SHM) in B lymphocytes. During antibody affinity maturation, activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) deaminates cytidine (C) to uracil (U) on single-stranded DNA, which can then be read as thymidine (T) upon replication or removed by uracil-DNA glycosylase (via base excision repair), thereby introducing a spectrum of mutations (Fig. 3a, 3b) [48]. Tsien et al. tapped into this process by integrating a fluorescent protein gene into the genome of B lymphocytes, seeking to evolve non-antibody proteins [49]. Although they used lentiviral random integration, the enriched variant was primarily detected at the Ig heavy-chain locus, indicating that targeting the gene of interest to naturally SHM-prone regions could boost mutation efficiency. Building on this concept, Mehta et al. developed CODE-HB, a continuous directed evolution strategy, which uses CRISPR to integrate the gene of interest and an SHM enhancer into B cells, achieving mutation rates of about 5 × 10⁻⁵ – 9 × 10⁻⁵ substitutions per base pair per generation [50]. Other studies have used CRISPR to insert human-derived antibodies or protein-binding domains into murine heavy-chain loci, then performed in vivo affinity maturation in mice [[51], [52], [53]].

Genome-wide base editing

Beyond B cell somatic hypermutation, various base-editing strategies have emerged for more general mutagenesis. Several methods induce genome-wide mutations with minimal targeting specificity, such as MutaEco (by fusing Petromyzon marinus cytidine deaminase (PmCDA1) with a subunit of E. coli RNA polymerase) [54], rBE (by fusing a nonspecific single-stranded DNA binding protein with APOBEC (a cytidine deaminase) in S. cerevisiae) [55], and Helicase-AID (by fusing DNA helicase with AID in both E. coli and S. cerevisiae) [56]. rBE and Helicase-AID were both exemplified in evolution to increase β-carotene productivity in S. cerevisiae. While these approaches effectively introduce random mutations that can benefit applications such as metabolic pathway evolution, rapidly and accurately identifying specific functional mutations can be challenging.

Targeted base editing with CRISPR and helicase fusions

To improve targeting, CRISPR-based platforms like CRISPR-X and targeted AID-mediated mutagenesis (TAM) recruit AID variants to defined genomic loci using nuclease-inactive dCas9 plus sgRNA [57,58], achieving a mutation rate of ∼1 × 10⁻³ mutations per base pair per generation. TAM directly fuses the AID variant to dCas9, while CRISPR-X recruits AID via protein-RNA interactions using MS2-tagged sgRNA and MCP-tagged AID (Fig. 3c). However, each sgRNA typically mutates only ∼100 bp near the PAM, making these systems ill-suited for continuous evolution unless multiple sgRNAs are used. These platforms were exemplified by evolving spectrum-shifted GFP variants, bortezomib-resistant PsmB5 variants, and imatinib-resistant ABL mutations. Helicase-assisted continuous editing (HACE) addresses this limitation by fusing a hyperactive AID mutant to a more processive helicase variant. When sgRNA-guided nCas9 creates a nick at a targeted genomic locus and exposes a single-stranded DNA region, the HACE editor loads onto the region and unwinds the DNA, allowing mutations to extend ∼1 kb from the nicked site (Fig. 3d) [59]. HACE evolved MEK1 inhibitor–resistance mutations, identified SF3B1 variants that result in mis-splicing, and uncovered functional variants on the enhancer element of CD69. Meanwhile, CoMuTER in S. cerevisiae leverages Cas3’s helicase activity for cytidine deamination over spans up to 55 kb [60]. In this system, Cas3 cuts the non-target strand at the crRNA designated site and translocates along the strand in the 3′–5′ direction to unwind the DNA. The resulting single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) is subjected to cytidine deamination by the fused base editor, significantly expanding the range of targeted mutagenesis. CoMuTER demonstrates the suitability for pathway optimization by doubling the production of lycopene.

T7RNAP-based base editing platforms

An alternative targeting strategy uses T7 RNA polymerase (T7RNAP) specificity, as exemplified by MutaT7 [61], which fuses T7RNAP to cytidine deaminase. During transcription of T7 promoter-driven genes by the T7RNAP, the fused cytidine deaminase induces continuous mutations within the transcribed region, with a length that can span a multi-kb DNA sequence (Fig. 3e). MutaT7 was initially developed in E. coli, utilizing a fusion protein of rAPOBEC1 and T7RNAP for mutagenesis [61]. To enhance the mutation rate, Kim et al. replaced rAPOBEC1 with PmCDA1 fused to T7RNAP, creating an enhanced MutaT7 (eMutaT7) system for inducing mutations in E. coli [62]. To reduce deleterious off-site mutations, Fernández et al. utilized a dCas9 system to delimit the boundaries of DNA diversification, naming the approach T7-DIVA [63]. To expand the mutation spectrum, Shoulders et al. fused TadA7.10 or TadA8e with T7RNAP, developing MutaT7A→G and eMutaT7A→G for A > G mutagenesis in E. coli. They demonstrated that these systems could be combined with MutaT7C→T to enable all types of base transitions [64]. Similarly, Kim et al. developed eMutaT7transition [65], and Zhang et al. introduced T7-DualMuta [66], both employing analogous designs. More recently, Shoulders et al. engineered the MutaT7GDE system by fusing a substrate-promiscuous general deaminase with T7RNAP, achieving comprehensive coverage of all transition mutations through a single construct [67]. In yeast, Smolke and Yuan independently developed TRIDENT [68] and MutaT7trans [69], respectively, based on a PmCDA1-T7RNAP fusion protein. Both systems also investigated the optimization of the mutational spectrum by modulating DNA repair factors. In mammalian systems, Chen et al. used AID mutants fused to T7RNAP, resulting in the TREACE system, which enables mutagenesis in mammalian cells [70]. These systems have frequently been exemplified through the evolution of drug resistance mutations.

Emerging glycosylase-based strategies

Although base deaminases are most common, glycosylases represent another enzyme class with potential for base-editing–based continuous evolution [[71], [72], [73]]. TaGTEAM [74], for instance, fuses a single-chain Tet repressor (scTetR) to the yeast 3-methyladenine DNA glycosylase (Mag1p). Upon binding a 240 × tetO array, Mag1p excises bases and triggers error-prone repair, creating transitions, transversions, and occasional deletions (Fig. 3f). While still at an early stage, glycosylase-based approaches may ultimately broaden the mutation repertoire for intracellular mutagenesis.

Intracellular gene diversification through DNA-level combinatorial mutagenesis

A multitude of intracellular gene diversification strategies rely on error-prone DNA polymerases or base editors, but no single technique fully surpasses the others in every respect. Consequently, some methods combine multiple mechanisms into “combinatorial mutagenesis”. Among these, MP6 stands out as a well-optimized system in E. coli [75]. It compromises replication fidelity through five mechanisms: (1) dnaQ926: a dominant-negative variant of DNA polymerase III’s proofreading domain; (2) dam overexpression: disrupts DNA methylation, impairing mismatch repair; (3) seqA expression: adjusts MP gene expression to extend the mutagenesis dynamic range; (4) emrR overexpression: blocks export of mutagenic nucleobases, affecting base selection; (5) CDA1 + Ugi: primarily induce C > T transitions, while inhibiting uracil-DNA glycosylase to disrupt uracil-excision repair. With this suite of modifications, MP6 attains a mutation rate of ∼6.2 × 10⁻⁶ substitutions per base pair per generation in both the E. coli genome and intracellular plasmids, combines a balanced and broad mutation spectrum, and is easily regulated by arabinose (Fig. 4a).

A prominent application of MP6 is in phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE), which originally used a simpler mutagenesis plasmid (MP1) encoding dnaQ926 and error-prone Pol V for translesion synthesis and mutagenesis [76]. The current PACE system integrates MP6 to mutate M13 phage genomes that carry the gene of interest linked to pIII expression, ensuring that only beneficial mutations promote phage propagation. By controlling lagoon flow rates and constantly replenishing healthy bacterial hosts, PACE manages off-target mutations and maximizes evolutionary efficiency (Fig. 4b). This integration of MP6 further refines the mutation process, demonstrating substantial success across multiple directed evolution applications [10,75,77], including evolving T7 RNA polymerase, genome editing tools, and protein-protein interactions.

Overall, these DNA-level strategies—from orthogonal error-prone polymerases to base editors and combinatorial mutagenesis systems—provide a diverse toolkit for tailoring mutagenesis rates, spectra, and targeting specificity. Researchers can choose or combine approaches according to their goals, paving the way for increasingly sophisticated continuous directed evolution experiments.

Intracellular gene diversification through error-prone transcription or reverse transcription

Leveraging reverse transcription

Several intracellular mutagenesis strategies target the genetic information loop involving RNA transcription and reverse transcription. Because reverse transcription exhibits a higher mutation rate—a phenomenon well-documented in retroviruses like human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)—this process has naturally drawn attention for directed evolution. One example [23] incorporates the rtTA protein into an HIV genome modified with eight tetO binding sites in its LTR promoter and disrupted Tat–TAR elements, rendering the virus doxycycline-dependent. Mutations in rtTA, introduced by the HIV reverse transcriptase, accumulate during viral replication and are enriched for those that enhance doxycycline sensitivity or transcriptional activity (Fig. 5a) [23]. However, such approaches often affect cellular viability and raise biosafety concerns, leading to a narrow spectrum of possible targets.

Transcription- or reverse transcription–based intracellular gene diversification. (a) Retrovirus-based continuous directed evolution in human T cells [23]. Error-prone reverse transcriptase of HIV introduces mutations into the target gene carried on a modified HIV genome. (b) Ty1-based in cellulo continuous evolution (ICE) in yeast [24]. The gene of interest is inserted into the Ty1 retrotransposon, undergoing repeated transcription, reverse transcription, and reintegration, thereby accumulating mutations. (c) Retron-based evolution [25] in E. coli. An error-prone T7RNAP transcribes the retron cassette (containing homologous sequences to the gene of interest), and mutated msDNA complexes edit and integrate into the gene of interest. (d) CRAIDE [27] system in S. cerevisiae. An error-prone T7RNAP variant introduces mutations in a chimeric gRNA (cgRNA), which then mediates homologous recombination at the target gene locus.

A potential way to mitigate these challenges is to engineer retroviral replication components within the host cell, enabling controlled self-replication without full-blown cytopathic effects. Because retrotransposons and retroviruses share similar replication mechanisms, the Ty1-based gene mutagenesis strategy in yeast aligns with this concept [24]. In this system, the gene of interest is integrated into the Ty1 retrotransposon, which undergoes repeated cycles of transcription, reverse transcription, and reintegration. Each cycle introduces new mutations via the error-prone reverse transcriptase, gradually accumulating diversity in the gene of interest (Fig. 5b). This system has been applied to evolve Ura3p substrate specificity, Spt15p mutants for 1-butanol tolerance, and pathways for xylose catabolism.

Exploiting error-prone transcription

In contrast, relatively few intracellular mutagenesis strategies specifically exploit error-prone RNA transcription. Most such approaches rely on error-prone T7RNAP. One example involves retrons, specialized bacterial genetic elements. In a retron-based E. coli directed evolution platform, an error-prone T7RNAP variant transcribes a retron cassette containing a guide sequence and a targeting sequence (homologous to the gene of interest), introducing mutations into the RNA transcripts. During reverse transcription, these mutated transcripts form msDNA complexes that can edit homologous genomic regions, diversifying the gene of interest (Fig. 5c) [25]. While the entire retron cassette is subject to transcription-induced mutations, only the targeting sequence is integrated into the gene of interest. This retron system can edit a genomic region of about 10 amino acids. Because the mutation rate is only ∼190 times higher than background, a subsequent system called the retron-mediated genome editing system (REGES) incorporates additional optimizations [26]. Inspired by eMutaT7 [62], REGES fuses a cytidine deaminase PmCDA1 with error-prone T7RNAP, combining mutation events from retron reverse transcription, PmCDA1-mediated deamination, and error-prone transcription. REGES’s in cellulo evolution ability was exemplified by evolving a counter-selection gene sacB.

Another RNA transcription-based mutagenesis system is CRISPR- and RNA-assisted in vivo directed evolution (CRAIDE) in S. cerevisiae (Fig. 5d) [27]. CRAIDE is based on intracellular targeted gene repair, regulated by (d)Cas9 and donor-coupled chimeric gRNA (cgRNA) containing a homology region to the target site. An error-prone T7RNAP variant transcribes cgRNAs and introduces mutations into the cgRNAs. During the repair of CRISPR-induced breaks, the mutated cgRNAs serve as templates, facilitating the integration of mutations into the genome. Mutagenesis spans the entire cgRNA transcribed by the error-prone T7RNAP, with the homology region reaching lengths of up to several hundred bp (e.g., 670 bp). CRAIDE was validated through the evolution of novel functional variants of an auxotrophic marker gene and resistance to a toxic amino acid analogue.

Intracellular gene diversification through error-prone RNA replication

RNA replication–based mutagenesis has deep historical roots, though its development picked up momentum only more recently. In the 1960s, Spiegelman demonstrated an early form of directed evolution by using RNA replicase Qβ to amplify a self-replicating RNA molecule in vitro [78]. Over 74 serial transfers, the average length of these RNA products was reduced by ∼83%, yet they replicated more rapidly. Although these shortened RNAs had no specific function, Spiegelman’s work is widely regarded as the first directed evolution experiment [78]. In recent years, intracellular gene mutagenesis based on RNA replication engineered from RNA virus [79] has facilitated the development of a panel of directed evolution systems (Fig. 6a), exemplified by VEGAS [29], PROTEUS [30], and REPLACE [31].

RNA replication–based intracellular gene diversification. (a) Error-prone alphavirus genomic replication [79]. Negative-strand synthesis involves nsP4 and nsP1–3, whereas positive-strand synthesis is driven by nsP1, nsP2, nsP3, and nsP4. (b) VEGAS based on Sindbis virus [29]. The gene of interest replaces structural genes, and its activity governs the expression of structural proteins, linking viral propagation to the target gene’s function. PROTEUS implements a similar design, but replaces SSG with VSV-G to form virus-like particles [30]. (c) REPLACE system [31]. Sindbis-derived replicative RNA stably resides in mammalian cells, undergoing basal error-prone replication by nsP4. Additional mutagenesis arises from nucleoside analogs (e.g., molnupiravir), allowing flexible tuning of mutation rates and spectrum. In cells, molnupiravir is converted to β-d-N4-hydroxycytidine (NHC) triphosphate, which exists in two tautomeric forms: Imino-M and Amino-M. During viral RNA replication, Imino-M can replace U to pair with A, while Amino-M can replace C to pair with G. This process enables the introduction of all four base transition mutations during RNA replication.

VEGAS and PROTEUS

VEGAS replaces the structural protein genes in Sindbis virus with a gene of interest, while the viral structural proteins are supplied in trans from the host genome (Fig. 6b) [29]. The virus’s RNA-dependent RNA polymerase introduces mutations across the entire viral RNA genome containing the gene of interest, with a reported mutation rate of ∼10⁻⁴ per base per replication cycle. It was reported that these mutations cover all 12 base substitutions relatively evenly. Moreover, structural protein expression is controlled by the gene of interest’s activity, ensuring variants with improved function produce more virus particles and thereby become enriched. VEGAS was used to evolve transcription factors, GPCRs, and active-state nanobodies. However, VEGAS may induce cell cytopathic effects, generate recombinant viral particles and present other potential drawbacks [80].

Built on Semliki Forest Virus, PROTEUS follows a similar principle but addresses certain biosafety concerns by replacing structural proteins with VSV-G to produce virus-like particles [30]. PROTEUS displays a biased mutation spectrum (∼80% A > G and ∼10% U > C substitutions), and like VEGAS, can induce cytopathic effects in host cells. PROTEUS was used in generating a more sensitive TetON-4G tool for gene regulation and intracellular nanobody evolution.

REPLACE

REPLACE tackles these limitations more effectively and introduces additional refinements. Analogous to BacORep [34] and EcORep [35] that implement an orthogonal DNA replication system for mutagenesis, REPLACE modifies the Sindbis virus genome so it can stably replicate in mammalian cells as an RNA molecule harboring the gene of interest [31]. Mutations arise via two mechanisms: error-prone RNA replication and controlled mutagenesis through nucleoside analogs (e.g., molnupiravir). This dual mechanism can push the overall mutation rate above 10⁻³ mutations per base per day for a gene of interest up to ∼5 kb. After the addition of nucleoside analogs, error-prone RNA replication yields an evenly distributed spectrum of all four nucleotide transitions (A > G, G > A, U > C, and C > U) and occasional transversions (A > U, A > C) (Fig. 6c). REPLACE has shown superior performance in multiple directed evolution experiments, including blue-shifting GFPs, engineering transcriptional regulators, MEK1 Darwinian adaptation, and dominant-negative KRAS escape, thanks to its controllable mutation rate, broad mutation spectrum, and reduced cytopathic effects [31]. Overall, REPLACE’s inducible mutagenesis and reduced cytotoxicity make it uniquely suitable for long-term mammalian cell evolution compared to VEGAS and PROTEUS. Moreover, as exemplified by REPLACE, the controllable mutagenesis of replicative RNA via molnupiravir and other compounds may serve as a broadly applicable strategy across diverse replicative RNA or RNA virus-based evolution systems.

Conclusion and future perspectives

In recent years, in cellulo continuous directed evolution has achieved remarkable success, driven by the refinement of intracellular gene diversification strategies [6,7,9,13]. However, the creation of a universal platform suitable for all experimental scenarios remains a formidable challenge. To advance the field, future efforts should prioritize innovations that enhance mutagenesis control, diversify mutation types, and expand host compatibility [81]. Below, we outline key opportunities and emerging directions.

Refining DNA replication-based mutagenesis

Orthogonal replication systems like OrthoRep in yeast [38,39] demonstrate the power of iterative polymerase engineering to optimize mutation rates and spectra. Applying similar strategies—such as high-throughput screening of DNA polymerase mutants or rational design informed by structural studies—could further unlock the untapped potential of systems like BacORep [34] and EcORep [35]. For example, replacing the PRD1 DNA polymerase in EcORep with variants exhibiting higher processivity or mutation rate and more balanced mutation spectra could expand its utility in E. coli. Similarly, integrating feedback-regulated mutagenesis circuits [45] into BacORep might enable dynamic control over mutation rates during long-term evolution experiments.

Diversifying mutation type

While base editor-empowered mutagenesis strategies like MutaT7 and CRISPR-X enable transition mutations (C > T or A > G), transversion mutations—critical for accessing larger areas of sequence space—remain underrepresented. Recent advances in glycosylase-based editors, such as AYBE [72], CGBE [82], CABE [83], GYBE [84], and TBE [71,73,85,86], now enable all kinds of base transversions. Integrating these tools into continuous evolution platforms (e.g., coupling glycosylase-based editors with T7RNAP in MutaT7 systems or Cas3 in CoMuTER systems) could further diversify mutational outcomes and broaden host compatibility. Additionally, exploring novel enzyme classes, such as T7RNAP or reverse transcriptase mutants with unbiased mismatch incorporation, may further broaden the spectrum of accessible mutations.

Extending orthogonal DNA replication to mammalian systems

Orthogonal DNA replication platforms (e.g., BacORep [34], EcORep [35], and OrthoRep [38,39]) have proven effective in microbial hosts but remain underexplored in mammalian cells. Adapting viral replication origins (e.g., Epstein-Barr virus, adenovirus or herpesvirus) into stable episomes, paired with engineered error-prone polymerases, could circumvent the high fidelity of mammalian DNA replication machinery [87]. Success would require addressing challenges like low mutation rate, biased mutation spectra, immune recognition of viral components and ensuring compatibility with non-dividing cells. Lessons from yeast linear plasmids (pGKL1/2) [37] and adenoviral systems (e.g., controlled replication via trans-complementation) [32] may guide initial designs.

Demystifying transcription/reverse transcription–based systems

Retron-like and CRAIDE platforms offer great potential for targeted mutagenesis but require a deeper mechanistic understanding. For example, clarifying how retron-derived msDNA integrates into host genomes and how RNA repair donors are incorporated into genomic loci under (d)Cas9 guidance in the CRAIDE system could provide insights to enhance editing efficiency. Parallel efforts to port these systems into mammalian cells could further broaden their applicability.

Advancing RNA replication–based strategies

RNA replication systems (e.g., REPLACE [31]) excel in mammalian cells but face limitations in microbial hosts. Reviving early concepts like Qβ replicase–driven evolution [78] from E. coli or engineering RNA virus like brome mosaic virus [88] in S. cerevisiae—where the replicase’s inherent error rate (∼10⁻⁴ substitutions/base/infection [89]) could be augmented with nucleoside analogs—might bridge this gap. Additionally, engineering RNA-dependent RNA polymerases to favor transversions would address the current bias toward transitions in systems like PROTEUS [30] and REPLACE [31].

Convergence with computational tools

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI), including machine learning (ML), is poised to transform in cellulo continuous directed evolution by bridging the gap between mutagenesis design and functional outcomes. Predictive models trained on high-throughput mutational data, such as deep mutational scanning or phage display libraries, can forecast the fitness effects of mutations, enabling systems like EvolvR or MutaT7 to prioritize regions of sequence space most likely to yield functional gains. Beyond prediction, closed-loop systems integrating real-time sequencing, robotic automated phenotype evaluation, and reinforcement learning could dynamically adjust mutation rates or selection pressures, minimizing redundant experiments and accelerating adaptive walks toward target phenotypes [18,90]. In silico directed evolution [16,17] adds another dimension: computational frameworks now simulate evolutionary trajectories by combining structure prediction (e.g., AlphaFold [91]), molecular dynamics, and fitness modeling. Platforms like EVOLVEpro [15] design mutant libraries enriched with functional variants before experimental validation, dramatically reducing the need for exhaustive screening. Hybrid workflows could leverage these in silico-optimized sequences as starting points for further refinement—for example, using OrthoRep or REPLACE to evolve computationally pre-screened variants. Active learning loops could further refine these models, training them on continuous directed evolution data (e.g., sequence-function data from PACE evolution experiments) to iteratively improve predictive accuracy. Challenges persist, such as modeling epistatic interactions or conformational dynamics, but advances in large language models for proteins [92] and generative AI (e.g., diffusion models for protein design [93]) are rapidly closing these gaps. Together, these tools promise to accelerate evolution campaigns while conserving resources, exemplifying the power of merging computational and experimental paradigms.

In summary, advancements in the aforementioned directions are poised to transform intracellular gene diversification strategies, fueling in cellulo continuous directed evolution and enabling breakthroughs across diverse biological and biotechnological applications.
