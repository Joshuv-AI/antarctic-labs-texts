Factors associated with psychological distress among newly diagnosed patients with gastric cancer: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Hyunju Ji, Ji Yea Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the factors associated with psychological distress in patients newly diagnosed with gastric cancer.MethodsThis study used a descriptive cross-sectional design. The participants were 173 patients newly diagnosed with gastric cancer who had not yet initiated any treatment. They were recruited from a tertiary hospital in South Korea between December 2023 and September 2025. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires including the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire, Supportive Care Needs Survey–Short Form 34, M.D. Anderson Symptom Inventory–Gastrointestinal Cancer Module, and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson's correlation, and hierarchical multiple regression.ResultsThe mean psychological distress score was 12.8 ± 7.4 and 46.2% of participants were classified as having clinically significant psychological distress. Psychological distress was significantly positively correlated with illness perception, unmet care needs, and symptom severity. Hierarchical regression analysis revealed that illness perception (β = 0.41, P < 0.001), unmet care needs (β = 0.28, P < 0.001), and symptom severity (β = 0.16, P = 0.009) were significantly associated with psychological distress, collectively accounting for 52.6% of its variance.ConclusionsPsychological distress is common in newly diagnosed patients with gastric cancer. Customized nursing interventions aimed at addressing patients’ illness perceptions, managing symptoms, and meeting supportive care needs even before treatment initiation may be beneficial for alleviating psychological distress and promoting better psychological adjustment.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks fifth worldwide in terms of incidence and mortality, with approximately 76% of cases occurring in Asian populations.1 The five-year survival rate for Stage I GC is approximately 68% to 81%, whereas that for Stage II or more advanced stages decreases substantially to below 60%.2 The primary treatment for GC is surgery, which may lead to dietary restrictions and gastrointestinal symptoms due to reduced gastric volume.3 Unfavorable prognoses and treatment-related changes in this population may intensify anxiety and depressive symptoms, which constitute the most prevalent forms of psychological distress.4, 5, 6 A study that clustered patients with cancer according to their psychological distress levels found that patients with GC tended to be in the highly psychologically distressed group.7 It is important to recognize and address the psychological distress experienced by this population.

Psychological distress among cancer patients has been associated with multiple adverse outcomes, such as treatment non-adherence, mood disturbances, impaired immune function, and diminished quality of life.8, 9, 10 The National Cancer Institute emphasizes that the diagnostic and pretreatment phases can represent a high-risk period for psychological distress.11 During this time, patients experience new symptoms, diagnostic procedures, confirmation of malignancy, and concerns regarding impending treatment or hospitalization, which increase their uncertainty and emotional strain. However, most studies assessed patients during or after treatment,12, 13, 14, 15 potentially overlooking the initial psychological impact that emerges at the time of diagnosis. Therefore, understanding the underlying factors of psychological distress among newly diagnosed patients with GC is essential.

Physical symptoms are one of the most direct contributors to psychological distress among patients with cancer because the physical burden of the disease leads to increased emotional strain.16 A systematic review consistently reported that physical symptoms, particularly symptom severity, were the most reliable factors associated with psychological distress.17 However, only limited studies have examined symptoms in newly diagnosed GC patients before treatment.18 One potential reason is that GC often presents with no or nonspecific symptoms at diagnosis,19 while more distinct gastrointestinal symptoms commonly emerge after surgery.20 Nonetheless, evidence from some studies indicates symptoms do exist before treatment,18,21 underscoring the importance of acknowledging symptoms even before treatment due to their potential impact on patients’ psychological distress.

Illness perception refers to a patient's cognitive and emotional understanding of their illness.22 Cancer patients demonstrate varying degrees of illness perception, thereby reflecting differences in how they interpret the severity, controllability, and emotional impact of the disease.13 Those who perceived their illness as threatening or life-disruptive tended to experience greater psychological distress.13 From another perspective, illness perception affects how individuals manage their condition and use coping strategies, thereby influencing their levels of psychological distress.23 This perspective is also aligned with the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation, which proposes that individuals' interpretations of an illness shape their responses to the health threat and, ultimately, their emotional outcomes.24 Within this framework, perceiving cancer as more threatening, chronic, or uncontrollable may be associated with greater psychological distress. This theoretical perspective has also been applied in patients with newly diagnosed GC, in whom illness perception was significantly associated with psychological distress.21 Taken together, these findings suggest that illness perception may play an important role in psychological distress among patients with GC.

Unmet care needs are another important factor contributing to increased psychological distress among patients with cancer. Multiple domains of unmet care needs—informational, psychological, and physical—were significantly associated with distress among patients with advanced cancer.15 A longitudinal study found that the level of unmet needs among cancer patients was highest at baseline and six months after diagnosis and gradually declined over time.25 Another study reported that patients with GC experience significant unmet care needs, particularly in the informational domains, from the time of diagnosis.14 These findings suggest that addressing unmet care needs early in the disease trajectory may help reduce psychological distress among patients newly diagnosed with GC.

However, research that has simultaneously examined the associations between symptom severity, illness perception, unmet care needs, and psychological distress in patients newly diagnosed with GC is lacking. Although one study investigated the relationship between symptoms, illness perception, and psychological distress in GC, the symptom assessment tool used was not validated, whereas unmet care needs were not included.21 Therefore, investigating the factors associated with psychological distress among patients newly diagnosed with GC is essential to enhance the understanding of this critical psychological response and provide a foundation for developing future interventions to reduce psychological distress.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional design was used to explore the influence of symptom severity, illness perception, and unmet care needs on psychological distress in patients newly diagnosed with GC. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Study participants and setting

A total of 173 patients newly diagnosed with GC participated in this study. The term “newly diagnosed patients with GC” refers to individuals who have been diagnosed with GC but have not yet undergone any treatment, such as gastrectomy or endoscopic resection. Participants were recruited from the GC Center of Ajou University Hospital, a tertiary hospital in Suwon, South Korea. The hospital has approximately 1200 inpatient beds and has a Grade 1 certification for GC treatment from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service. Data were collected from December 2023 to September 2025 using written self-administered questionnaires.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients diagnosed with GC and those who had not yet undergone any treatment, such as gastrectomy or endoscopic resection, after diagnosis. Patients were recruited during their first outpatient visit to the hospital's GC Center, at which time they completed a self-reported questionnaire. This initial visit typically occurred approximately two to three weeks after patients were informed that their health screening results were suspicious for GC. The exclusion criteria were as follows: prior history of any cancer diagnosis, inoperable cancer status, and difficulty in understanding the study aim or survey questions, and having ≥ 20% missing responses on the main study variables.26

The sample size was estimated using G∗Power version 3.1.9.7. The calculation was conducted for a multiple linear regression model with nine predictor variables. The significance level was set at 0.05, statistical power at 0.90, and the effect size at 0.15.27 Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 141 participants. In line with a previously published study that applied a conservative dropout assumption of 20%,27 the target number of questionnaires was increased to 177 to compensate for potential non-response or incomplete data. A total of 173 completed questionnaires were included in the final analysis.

Measures

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

Socio-demographic variables included age, sex, marital status, living status with a caregiver, educational level, and employment status, whereas clinical information included hospital department, comorbidities, and reason for cancer detection. Comorbidities were assessed using multiple responses and included cardiovascular, renal, liver, and respiratory diseases, as well as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, benign prostatic hyperplasia, and other conditions. The reasons for cancer detection were categorized as national cancer screening, private health screening, symptom onset, and others.

Psychological distress

Psychological distress was evaluated using the Korean version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)28 developed by Zigmond and Snaith.29 The HADS contains two domains, anxiety (seven items) and depression (seven items). Each item was rated on a scale of 0–3, with a higher total score indicating a higher distress level. Cronbach's α values were reported as 0.86–0.96 in the Korean validation study,28 and 0.91 in this study. Depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and psychological distress were classified using cutoff scores of ≥ 5 for the depression subscale, ≥ 7 for the anxiety subscale, and ≥ 13 for the total score, based on thresholds validated for patients with cancer.30

Symptom severity

Symptom severity was measured using the Korean version of the M. D. Anderson Symptom Inventory-Gastrointestinal Cancer Module (MDASI-GI),31 developed by Wang et al.32 The instrument assessed the severity of 18 symptoms experienced during the last 24 h, comprising 13 core cancer-related symptoms and 5 gastrointestinal symptoms. Each item was rated on an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (“not present”) to 10 (“as bad as you can imagine”), with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The total symptom severity score was calculated as the mean of all the item scores. Cronbach's α was 0.80 for the original32 and Korean versions31 of the MDASI-GI, and 0.89 in this study. The use of MDASI-GI was permitted after paying processing and licensing fees.

Illness perception

The Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (B-IPQ) was used to assess the participants' illness perception22 and translated into Korean by Seong and Lee.33 The B-IPQ consists of eight items that assess the perceived impact of the illness on life, expected duration, personal control, belief in treatment effectiveness, symptom experience, concerns regarding the illness, understanding of the illness, and emotional responses. Each item was rated on a 0 (“not at all”) to 10 (“extremely”), with higher total scores suggesting greater perceived illness threat. Reverse scoring was applied to the three items measuring personal control, belief in treatment effectiveness, and understanding of the illness. The test–retest reliability in the original study ranged from 0.42 to 0.75 22 and the Korean version demonstrated a Cronbach's α of 0.77.33

Two of the reverse-coded items (personal control and belief in treatment effectiveness) showed negative item–total correlations (r = −0.27 and r = −0.03, respectively) and reduced the overall internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.49). After excluding these items, Cronbach's α increased to 0.70, indicating acceptable reliability. This approach is consistent with the methodological recommendations that reverse-coded items can reduce internal consistency and that items showing negative item–total correlations may be excluded to enhance reliability.34,35 All subsequent analyses were conducted using the revised six-item version of the B-IPQ. Although two B-IPQ items were excluded from the analyses, a sensitivity analysis showed that the association between illness perception and psychological distress was similar when the total score was calculated using all eight items (Supplementary Table S1).

Unmet care needs

Unmet care needs were measured using the Korean version of the Supportive Care Needs Survey–Short Form 34 (SCNS-SF34)36 developed by Boyes et al.37 The SCNS-SF34 comprises five domains: physical and daily living (five items), psychological (10 items), sexuality (three items), patient care and support (five items), and health systems and information (11 items). Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from one (“not applicable”) to five (“high need”), with higher scores indicating greater levels of unmet need. Standardized total scores were calculated on a scale of 0–100 points. Cronbach's α values in the original study ranged from 0.86–0.96,37 and the Korean version reported a McDonald's Omega of 0.96.36 Cronbach's α was 0.96 in this study.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Ajou University (IRB No. #AJOUIRB-SB-2023-553) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Eligible participants were informed regarding the purpose, data collection method, anonymity of the data, their right to withdraw from the study, benefits, and harms of the study by the research assistant. Those who were interested were provided with a consent form. After reading the consent form and asking research assistant any questions regarding the study, those who agreed to participate completed the questionnaire.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the R software (version 4.5.1). With an overall missing rate of 0.13% (17 items), missing values were handled using simple imputation based on the mean score of each scale.38 Descriptive statistics, such as means, standard deviations, and frequencies, were used to summarize the participants' general characteristics, levels of symptom severity, illness perception, unmet care needs, distress, and prevalence of psychological distress. Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach's α coefficients. Cases in which a scale showed low internal consistency, item–total correlations were reviewed, and items with inconsistent relationships with the total score were excluded to improve reliability, consistent with the recommendations of Tavakol and Dennick.34 Based on this analysis, the B-IPQ was modified by excluding the two items that showed inconsistent correlations. Pearson's correlation coefficients were computed to examine the relationships between symptom severity, illness perception, unmet care needs, and psychological distress.

Prior to performing regression analysis, the assumptions of linearity, independence of errors, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, and multicollinearity were examined. The Durbin–Watson, Breusch–Pagan, and Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated that all assumptions were satisfied (all P > 0.05), and variance inflation factors ranged from 1.08 to 1.64, suggesting no multicollinearity issues. Subsequently, a hierarchical multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with psychological distress. In Model 1, socio-demographic and clinical covariates known from prior research to influence psychological distress among cancer patients—age, sex, marital status,39 educational level,11 occupation,40 reasons for cancer detection16,17—were included as covariates. The reasons for cancer detection were included as a covariate and dichotomized as symptom onset versus not symptom onset (national cancer screening, private health screening, or other). Model 2 additionally incorporated symptom severity, illness perception, and unmet care needs. All statistical tests were interpreted at a significance level of P < 0.05.

Discussion

Main findings

This study revealed that psychological distress is a prevalent concern among patients with newly diagnosed GC and is associated with physical symptom burden, high levels of illness perception, and unmet supportive care needs.

Approximately half of newly diagnosed patients with GC experience clinically significant psychological distress, representing a higher prevalence than that reported in previous studies on digestive cancers (approximately 20% to 30%).41 This may be because the cut-off score we used in this study was not specifically validated for Asian people. One study pointed out that a different cut-off score is needed for identifying psychological distress in other cultures, given that cultural differences in emotional expression and willingness to report psychological symptoms may influence distress screening results.42 Another explanation may be that this rate reflects a family-oriented culture in Asia, in which family relationships play a central role in illness experiences and care. In such settings, patients may experience distress not only from the cancer itself, but also from concerns about burdening their family members, as family-centered values may heighten perceived responsibility toward loved ones and, in turn, increase emotional burden.43,44 Further studies are needed to investigate psychological distress across different cultural contexts.

At the same time, the high prevalence of psychological distress observed in the present study may reflect the timing of assessment at diagnosis, when distress is often most pronounced. A longitudinal study of women with newly diagnosed breast cancer found that psychological distress levels were the highest shortly after diagnosis and gradually declined during follow-up.45 Therefore, a systematic assessment and early intervention for psychological distress should begin at the time of cancer diagnosis. Specifically, depression was more prevalent than anxiety (64.7% vs. 38.7%), consistent with previous studies using the same instrument in patients with gastrointestinal cancer.41,46 These findings may stem from patients’ lack of experience in coping with serious illnesses, diminished confidence, as well as pervasive negative thoughts and fears regarding their conditions.47

Symptom severity was associated with distress, although most participants reported only mild symptoms. Among these, drowsiness and fatigue were the most severe symptoms reported, whereas vomiting and difficulty swallowing were the least severe. Consistent with our findings, a previous study using the same instrument reported that drowsiness and fatigue were among the most severe preoperative symptoms, while pain, diarrhea, sleep disturbance, and bloating were the most severe symptoms during the first postoperative week among patients with GC.48 After surgery, patients commonly experience physical symptoms, including not only pain but also gastrointestinal symptoms resulting from reduced gastric volume.49 Consequently, substantial evidence has accumulated on postoperative physical symptoms in patients with GC,49 whereas preoperative symptoms have received relatively limited attention. However, our findings suggest that relatively mild psychological or physical discomfort is more prevalent in the preoperative phase, and that even mild preoperative symptoms may be associated with psychological distress at the time of diagnosis, highlighting the need for early and continuous assessment of symptoms and patient education focused on self-management strategies. For example, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) Clinical Practice Guidelines recommend that pain assessment and management be incorporated from the initial phase of cancer care and continued throughout the disease trajectory, with attention to psychosocial distress and the impact of symptoms on patients’ daily functioning and well-being.50 This approach may help reduce psychological distress in patients with GC.

Higher levels of illness perception were significantly associated with greater psychological distress in patients with GC. This variable exhibited the strongest standardized effect in the regression model, emphasizing that patients who perceived their illness as more threatening, chronic, or uncontrollable were more likely to experience psychological distress. The strong association between illness perception and psychological distress could be understood through the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation, which posits that individuals' interpretations of illness are closely related to their emotional outcomes.24 This interpretation is also supported in newly diagnosed GC21 and breast cancer51 that applied the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation framework and likewise demonstrated significant associations between illness perception and psychological distress. Considering that cancer is a long-term illness, rather than an acute condition, it is important to shift from a negative or catastrophic interpretation of the illness to a more balanced perspective that acknowledges suffering and growth.52 Nurses are crucial in fostering cognitive and emotional shifts in clinical practice. For example, they can evaluate patients’ physical and psychological statuses, clarify misconceptions regarding their illness, and redefine cancer-related beliefs through reflective discussions. This supportive interaction enables patients to reinterpret their health status constructively, thereby alleviating psychological distress and fostering a sense of personal control.53

This study demonstrated that unmet care needs were significantly associated with higher levels of psychological distress among patients newly diagnosed with GC. The highest unmet needs were identified in the health systems and information domains, whereas unmet needs related to physical and sexual aspects were comparatively low. This finding is consistent with that of a study conducted on patients with GC.14 This pattern may reflect the participants' early stage in the cancer trajectory, when prominent physical symptoms may not yet have emerged. After a cancer diagnosis, patients often seek comprehensive information regarding their disease, such as causes, prognosis, expected symptoms, and treatment options, to support informed decision-making.54 Studies have shown that many cancer patients struggle to communicate with health care professionals regarding their illness or treatment, because they are often unsure of whom to approach or whether it is appropriate to express certain concerns.55 Therefore, nurses should conduct ongoing assessments of patients’ information needs, provide appropriate education customized to each phase of the cancer trajectory, and facilitate opportunities for peer relationships to enhance shared understanding.54

In the final regression model, none of the socio-demographic covariates —including age, sex, marital status, educational level, and occupation—was significantly associated with psychological distress. This implies that background characteristics may play a relatively limited role in explaining psychological distress during the early phase of the cancer trajectory. Previous studies have reported higher levels of psychological distress among patients with cancer who are younger, female, unmarried, or have experienced school or job loss, possibly because of differences in socioeconomic status, coping patterns, and broader life circumstances.56, 57, 58 However, much of this evidence has been derived from populations undergoing active treatment or long-term follow-up. At the early phase of diagnosis, individuals may have limited opportunity to mobilize coping strategies or to benefit from socioeconomic resources, which could attenuate the influence of background characteristics observed in later phases of the cancer trajectory. This temporal distinction may partly explain why socio-demographic characteristics were not significantly associated with psychological distress in the final model. Further research specifically targeting patients at the point of diagnosis is warranted to better understand psychological distress patterns unique to this early period.

Notably, the reason for cancer detection was not associated with psychological distress, despite symptom severity showing a significant relationship with distress. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as the proportion of patients whose cancer was detected due to symptom onset was relatively small in the present sample, which may have limited the ability to detect differences according to detection pathway. In South Korea, approximately 70% of the population is covered by voluntary private health insurance,59 leading to a high proportion of cancers being detected through health screening. This unique contextual factor should be taken into account when interpreting these findings.

Implications for nursing practice and research

High prevalence of psychological distress in patients newly diagnosed with GC before treatment initiation suggests that psychological distress assessment should begin at the time of diagnosis rather than after treatment starts. In nursing practice, nurses should assess symptom severity, illness perception, and unmet care needs comprehensively, as all three factors were significantly associated with psychological distress in this study. Early nursing interventions should therefore extend beyond symptom monitoring and management to include individualized information, emotional support, and communication that help patients develop a realistic and balanced understanding of their illness. Particular attention should be paid to unmet needs related to health systems and information, as these were most prominent in this population. From a research perspective, future research should focus on developing nursing intervention programs that aim to reduce psychological distress starting at diagnosis by improving symptom control, reshaping illness perceptions, and addressing patients' unmet care needs. Further studies are needed to expand current knowledge by investigating whether psychological distress before treatment initiation influences patients’ health outcomes after treatment, such as cancer coping and adaptation.

Strengths and limitations

This study had several limitations. First, it was not possible to verify the temporal relationships among the variables because it was based on a cross-sectional design. Second, the participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other clinical contexts. Third, two reverse-coded B-IPQ items showed poor internal consistency and were excluded from the analyses, resulting in the use of a revised six-item score. Although the sensitivity analysis comparing the original eight-item and revised six-item scores demonstrated a robust association with psychological distress, this may limit comparability with studies using the B-IPQ and has potential implications for construct validity. Fourth, although the relationship between illness perception and psychological distress may be interpreted within the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation, the overall study was not fully guided by a theoretical framework integrating all study variables. This may limit the extent to which the observed associations can be interpreted within a unified explanatory model. In addition, the prevalence of psychological distress should be interpreted with caution, as HADS-based classification may vary according to the thresholds applied across studies. Despite these limitations, this study highlights the need for systematic assessment and management of psychological distress after cancer diagnosis. In South Korea, psychological distress screening has not been integrated into hospital accreditation or national cancer care quality evaluations. These findings may provide useful evidence for the development of institutional protocols and national guidelines that include structured psychological distress evaluations and supportive care.

Conclusions

Psychological distress is common among newly diagnosed GC patients, affecting approximately half of the patients before treatment initiation. Higher symptom severity, high levels of illness perception, and unmet supportive care needs are associated with psychological distress. These findings highlight the importance of early psychosocial assessment during the diagnostic and pretreatment phases. Customized interventions that address symptoms, help patients develop more accurate and adaptive illness perceptions, and meet supportive care needs may help reduce psychological distress and promote psychological adjustment. Incorporating these strategies into routine care could improve patient well-being, facilitate treatment adherence, and enhance quality of life. Future research should examine the effectiveness of such interventions on disease trajectory.
