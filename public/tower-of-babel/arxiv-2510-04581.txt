Can LLMs Detect Ambiguous Plural Reference? An Analysis of Split-Antecedent and Mereological Reference
Anh Dang, Rick Nouwen, Massimo Poesio

Abstract
Our goal is to study how LLMs represent and interpret plural reference in ambiguous and unambiguous contexts. We ask the following research questions: (1) Do LLMs exhibit human-like preferences in representing plural reference? (2) Are LLMs able to detect ambiguity in plural anaphoric expressions and identify possible referents? To address these questions, we design a set of experiments, examining pronoun production using next-token prediction tasks, pronoun interpretation, and ambiguity detection using different prompting strategies. We then assess how comparable LLMs are to humans in formulating and interpreting plural reference. We find that LLMs are sometimes aware of possible referents of ambiguous pronouns. However, they do not always follow human reference when choosing between interpretations, especially when the possible interpretation is not explicitly mentioned. In addition, they struggle to identify ambiguity without direct instruction. Our findings also reveal inconsistencies in the results across different types of experiments.

1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have been shown to reach human-like competence in a wide range of natural language tasks
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Brown et al. 2020
. Communication between humans and AI systems is becoming more ubiquitous, given their remarkable language understanding and generation capacity. An important factor in effective communication is the ability to handle ambiguity in language. Although humans are very good at resolving ambiguity based on context and common sense knowledge, LLMs are designed with a strong focus on accuracy. In fact, they have been shown to struggle with capturing language ambiguity
Liu et al. 2023
;
Wildenburg et al. 2024
;
Stengel-Eskin et al. 2023
;
Karamolegkou et al. 2025
. They are often unable to detect or generate ambiguous expressions. Since ambiguity is always present in communication, this inadequacy can lead to misunderstanding and the inability of LLMs to fully comprehend the use of language in context.

In this work, we focus on the ambiguity in coreference resolution. A lot of work has been done on building coreference resolution systems
Yu et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2017
;
Bohnet et al. 2023
. More recent efforts to evaluate LLMs have shown that they can resolve coreference to some extent, yet it is still a challenging task
Brown et al. 2020
;
Pradhan et al. 2012
;
Gan et al. 2024
. In actual communication, anaphoric expressions can sometimes be ambiguous. This means that it is not always clear to which referent the anaphoric expression refers. For example, when we say
Jane looked at Mary and she smiled
, the pronoun
she
possibly refers to Jane or Mary. Without further context, it is impossible to disambiguate the pronoun.

Some research has been conducted on how LLMs process anaphoric expressions. However, most of the work focuses on singular reference and specific cases of ambiguity such as gender-ambiguous pronouns
Webster et al. 2018
;
Emami et al. 2019
. No research has been done specifically on the processing of plural reference. In this paper, our aim is to study how LLMs conceptualize plural reference in both ambiguous and unambiguous cases. We find plural reference particularly interesting because it is highly dependent on how humans conceptualize plurality. In psycholinguistic research, formulating plural expressions is a complex process because they introduce ambiguity to the discourse
Cokal et al. 2023
;
Koh and Clifton Jr 2002
;
Moxey et al. 2012
;
Moxey et al. 2004
;
Asher and Wang 2003
;
Patson 2014
. Our goal is to study
how LLMs produce, interpret, and represent plural reference in unambiguous and ambiguous contexts
.

We design a set of experiments, including next-token prediction and prompting, to compare how comparable LLMs are to humans in producing and interpreting ambiguous and unambiguous plural reference. We ask the following research questions.

RQ1:

Do LLMs exhibit human-like preferences when representing plural reference?

RQ2:

Are LLMs able to detect ambiguity in plural anaphoric expressions and identify possible referents?

Our main findings are as follows: (1) LLMs follow human preference when choosing between interpretations in ambiguous cases where the possible referents are directly mentioned and struggle to do so when they are not present in the input; (2) LLMs fail to identify ambiguity without direct instruction.

2
Background

There is a body of work in psycholinguistics that shows that plural reference is more difficult to process and interpret than singular reference because the antecedents of plural anaphoric expressions are usually underspecified
Patson 2014
, leaving more possibilities to interpret them. For example, in the pair of sentences below, the pronoun
they
is more likely to refer to three people in 1(a) than 1(b). In 1(b),
they
may also refer to the group of Tom and Dick.

\ex

.
Ṫom, Dick, and Harry went to a restaurant last night. They ate a pizza.
.̱ Tom and Dick went to a restaurant with Harry last night. They ate a pizza.

Here, we focus specifically on the cases where there is an interaction between singular and plural reference. These are cases where singular and plural expressions are both probable.
Moxey et al. 2012
find that the degree of overlap in roles between objects affects whether they are grouped as a plural entity. For example, in
sentences 2(a) and 2(b),
waitress
is more related to
waiter
than
customer
in that they are more likely to perform the same action. As such,
they
is more likely to be the continuation of 2(a) than 2(b).

\ex

.
Ṫhe waiter and the waitress were in the restaurant. __ (
He/She/They
)
.̱ The waiter and the customer were in the restaurant. __ (
He/She/They
)

More recently,
Cokal et al. 2023
investigate how humans interpret singular and plural pronouns in
contexts where there is a whole-part relation between entities.
When two singular objects are introduced to the discourse, and they are combined into a unified object, the new unified object is known as a
mereological
object
Poesio et al. 2006
. It has been found that when more than one object is present, a plural expression is preferred and the use of a singular expression can cause processing difficulty
Moxey et al. 2004
;
Sanford and Moxey 1995
;
Gordon et al. 1999
. However, there are cases where a singular reference may become salient as a new entity is created by combining the two antecedents.

\ex

.
Ṫhe engineer attached the
engine
to the
boxcar
and sent __ (
it/them
)
.̱ The engineer detached the
engine
from the
boxcar
and sent __ (
it/them
)

Consider the two sentences above. In 3(a), through the verb
attached
, the engine and the boxcar are combined, introducing a new singular object to the discourse. This new object is a
train
, though it is never explicitly mentioned. Now, a singular or plural reading is both appropriate. The pronoun following the verb
sent
can be
it
or
them
, with
it
referring to one of the parts (that is, the engine or the boxcar) or the new object (that is, the train). In 3(b), however, the verb
detached
prevents the formation of the mereological entity, as such, when
it
is used, it can only refer to one of the objects.

Them
is used to refer to the group of two objects.
Cokal et al. 2023
found that humans prefer using
it
more in 3(a) than 3(b). Moreover, they are more likely to consider the mereological object rather than the constituents to be the referent of
it
. This suggests that when entities are joined, a new unified
group
entity is created, making
it
a preferred antecedent for singular pronouns. In the following sections, we study how LLMs produce and interpret pronouns in these ambiguous cases.

3
Ambiguity in LLMs

Ambiguity is
when an expression can be interpreted in different ways in a context.
The use of everyday language is full of ambiguity, and it is shown that ambiguity indeed facilitates effective communication by minimizing effort
Piantadosi et al. 2012
. With LLMs, recognizing ambiguity can be challenging, as they are not trained for this purpose and do not have access to pragmatic contexts.

An emerging body of research has been done on how LLMs handle ambiguity in input.
Stengel-Eskin et al. 2023
focus on semantic parsing. They prompt LLMs with ambiguous sentences with two possible interpretations and ask them to respond with the logical parses that correspond to the interpretations. They found that LLMs are unable to capture the possible interpretations in zero-shot contexts but perform much better in the few-shot contexts.
Sedova et al. 2024
look at entity ambiguity. They show that while LLMs are aware of the possible readings, they often struggle with explicitly verbalizing the ambiguity.
Liu et al. 2023
use a prompting-based approach to test the ability of LLMs in disambiguating, recognizing, and modeling different types of linguistic ambiguity. They found that even
GPT-4
still struggles with capturing ambiguity.

In the domain of coreference resolution, some studies have considered coreference to be one of the main sources of ambiguity
Wildenburg et al. 2024
;
Kamath et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Gan et al. 2024
. However, the types of ambiguity they cover are quite limited
Wildenburg et al. 2024
;
Kamath et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
or strongly focus on unambiguous reference
Gan et al. 2024
. The instances related to coreference account for a small number in their datasets. As such, in this work, we aim for a more extensive investigation of different types of plural reference ambiguity.

4
Methods

We use two types of experiments to study how LLMs process ambiguous plural references. For both experiments, we test a number of recent autoregressive decoder-only LLMs, namely
Qwen2.5-7B

Team 2024
,
Aya-Expanse-8B

Dang et al. 2024
and
Llama-3.1-8B

Touvron et al. 2023
. We evaluate the ability of LLMs to process ambiguous plural anaphoric expressions along the following dimensions. These capabilities are tested using a combination of prompting and next-token prediction tasks.

Production

is the ability to predict the correct pronoun for the referents. We test whether LLMs are aware of the pronouns for all possible antecedents. For example, in mereological contexts, are they able to identify that both singular (mereological object) and plural pronouns (constituents) are appropriate?

Interpretation

means being able to directly specify the possible antecedents of the pronoun. Several prompting experiments will be used to evaluate this ability.

Detection

refers to the ability to identify whether the pronoun can have multiple possible antecedents. This ability can be demonstrated through the extent to which LLMs can provide meta-linguistic judgment of the ambiguity.

Dataset

The dataset of this task consists of 44 pairs of mereological and non-mereological sentences. Half of the dataset is adapted from the material from
Cokal et al. 2023

Before running the main experiment, we want to ensure that the model is aware of the mereological object formed by attaching the two objects. We then look at the top 15 tokens with the highest probabilities. If the mereological object is one of those tokens, then the sentence prefix is qualified. 6
Interpretation

While Section 5 aims at testing whether LLMs are able to produce correct pronouns in unambiguous and ambiguous contexts, in this Section we look at how LLMs interpret these pronouns. Do they interpret them differently in ambiguous contexts? Do they notice possible ambiguity? To do this, we use various prompting techniques to study the extent to which LLMs can detect ambiguity and generate possible referents for the pronouns.

Previous studies have found that while LLMs may be aware of the potential ambiguity in input, they are sometimes unable to transform this knowledge into a textual explanation
Stengel-Eskin et al. 2023
;
Karamolegkou et al. 2025
. Taking into account such a possibility, we use two prompting directions. In Prompt 1 (
P1
), we ask LLMs to
provide all possible referents for a pronoun.
In Prompt 2 (
P2
),
LLMs have to choose the referent for the pronoun among those in a supplied list.
Some of the listed referents suggest ambiguity,
to see to what extent the LLMs can make use of them to detect ambiguity.
In Prompt 3 (
P3
), we ask LLMs if the mereological object can be the referent.
Finally, we ask if the LLMs think that the pronoun
it
is ambiguous (
P4
).
All of our prompts are few-shot.
See Appendix
A
for details about our prompt designs and how we code LLMs’ responses.

For baseline comparison, we also prompt LLMs on the simple reference dataset. The experiments are run on two LLMs, namely
Aya-Expanse-8B
and
Qwen2.5-7B
.

6.1
Reference Identification

Unambiguous Reference

As a baseline, we first test whether LLMs can choose the correct antecedent for simple unambiguous reference using the dataset described in Section 5.1. The results are not surprising. The LLMs reach 100% accuracy. This shows that in unambiguous contexts, LLMs are fully able to choose the correct antecedents for pronouns.

Ambiguous Reference

We report the results of
P1
and
P2
for the mereological dataset. When being asked to list all possible referents for
it
, the LLMs show somewhat different patterns.
Aya-Expanse-8B
consistently chooses one of the constituents as the only referent in both mereological and non-mereological contexts.
Qwen2.5-7B
is better at recognizing that the pronoun is ambiguous and that both objects can be the referent (50%). However, both rarely mention the mereological object as a possible referent (11.3%).

Figure
2
(top, left) shows the percentage of each answer choice in mereological and non-mereological contexts. When LLMs are forced to choose an antecedent for
pronoun
it
, they generally prefer to link

it
to the first object. Notably, for
Qwen2.5-7B
, the number of times the LLMs choose the first object is much higher
in the non-mereological cases than in the mereological cases. However,
Aya-Expanse-8B
gives nearly equal preference for the first object and the mereological object. The preference for the second object is quite similar in both cases. As a result, the percentage of choosing the mereological object is higher in the mereological cases (
Aya-Expanse-8B
: 37.3%,
Qwen2.5-7B
: 22%) than the non-mereological cases (22.8%). This shows that LLMs give higher preference to the mereological object in mereological contexts. However, they seem to be more likely to link the pronoun
it
to the constituent in both cases.

Another answer choice that we are also interested in is
D. any of the above objects
. If the LLMs choose
D
, they think that all objects are equally likely to be the referent for the pronoun
it
. In fact, if the models understand that
it
is ambiguous, this option has to be the most-chosen one. The low percentage of this option shows that, when being asked, the LLMs tend to specify an object as a referent instead of accepting the ambiguity. This shows that when the LLMs are instructed towards choosing a single referent, they often fail to identify the ambiguity that is present.

A surprising result is that
Aya-Expanse-8B

7
Discussion

In the above experiments, we assess LLMs’ ability to identify different types of coreference ambiguity using probability-based and prompting-based approaches. In the split-antecedent references, LLMs show a strong alignment with human preference
Moxey et al. 2004
;
Moxey et al. 2012
. When the roles assigned to the antecedents indicate that they may be involved in the same action, they are more likely to be referenced as a plural entity. However, in mereological contexts, the results are more complex. On the one hand, LLMs show preference for the mereological object following the verb, as shown in the
Object Production
experiment. On the other hand, the
Interpretation
experiment shows the lack of self-consistency of LLMs between prompting strategies and hints about ambiguity and the mereological objects. Without hints of ambiguity, they almost never recognize the ambiguity when being prompted with
P1
and
P2
, although these prompts do not exclude the possibility that the pronoun is ambiguous. They also do not explicitly mention the mereological object as a referent except when it is provided in the prompt. Generally, LLMs prefer to link
it
to the first constituent.

However, in
P2
and
P3
, where the mereological objects are presented in the prompts, they are chosen or listed much more frequently (40%–60%). This leads to the possibility that LLMs may exhibit this psycholinguistic phenomenon when direct instruction is provided. This hypothesis is complemented by the results of the
Object Production
experiment where some LLMs (
Aya-Expanse-8B and Qwen2.5-7B
) give higher probabilities to mereological objects in mereological contexts. This possibility aligns with
Stengel-Eskin et al. 2023
and
Karamolegkou et al. 2025
, who find that LLMs are better at producing different interpretations in few-shot contexts. Taken together, these results may imply that LLMs may not be able to apply their knowledge of ambiguity without specific instruction. In other words, they sometimes know the possible interpretations, but struggle to consistently show them in different types of instruction. This may be the case, as LLMs successfully detect ambiguity between the constituents in
P4
. However, the results of
P3
, where the difference between mereological and non-mereological contexts is not considerable, the mention of the mereological objects may not mean that the LLMs actually recognize the joined object. As mentioned before, the effect of frequency may be present. This suggests that the LLMs may be able to detect potential referents when they are mentioned in the input but fail to take into account the mereological object, a more complex referent. This suggests that while LLMs broadly capture the basic findings about plural pronoun preferential interpretation from classic papers such as
Moxey et al. 2004
;
Moxey et al. 2012
, their preferences differ from those captured in more recent literature such as
Cokal et al. 2023
.

We also observe that, while the LLMs perform quite similarly in the next-word prediction task, they exhibit different patterns in prompting tasks.
Qwen2.5-7B
seems to align more with humans. The model indeed chooses the mereological objects more in mereological contexts.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we conduct a set of experiments to investigate whether LLMs are able to capture different interpretations of ambiguous plural reference and whether their representations of reference are comparable to how humans do in psycholinguistic literature. Our mixed findings contribute to a larger picture of the degree to which LLMs possess knowledge of ambiguity and how this knowledge is used.

9
Limitations

Our study provides new insights into how LLMs process text with different types of coreference ambiguity. Yet it has a few limitations that need to be noted. First, in the mereological cases, we think that the frequency of the constituents and the mereological object affect its likelihood to be chosen by the LLMs in both the next-token prediction and prompt tasks. For example, in the sentence
The shoemaker stripped the leather from the sole and inspected it __
, the mereological object
shoe
is much more common than the
leather
or the
sole
. Although we try to select the verb that can be done on both constituents and the mereological objects, this effect of frequency is likely to be present, leading the LLMs to choose the mereological object as the referent without knowledge of the mereological relation. Secondly, the LLMs that we tested are rather small (around 7-8B). Investigations into larger LLMs are important as they may exhibit higher competency in interpreting ambiguous pronouns. In addition, we rely on prompting results, which may not always reflect the representation and reasoning capability of the LLMs. Future research may use recent mechanistic interpretability approaches for a more thorough and dimension-wise investigation of how LLMs represent ambiguous references
Syed et al. 2024
;
Vig et al. 2020
;
Hanna and Mueller 2025
. Such methods involve interventions on the LLMs’ hidden activations, which potentially uncover the process underneath the LLMs’ generation.

In addition, as pointed out by the reviewers, we may be quite strict in evaluating LLMs’ ability to provide metalinguistic judgements about ambiguity because even humans can still have difficulty identifying it. We did not conduct the prompting experiments on humans, so we do not know their preferences. We acknowledge this as one of the limitations of the paper.

10
Ethical Considerations

The study uses only publicly available linguistic stimuli from previous research and synthetic data generated by LLMs. It does not include any data from human participants.
