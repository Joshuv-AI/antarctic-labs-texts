Association between prognostic nutritional index and clinical outcomes in patients with pyogenic liver abscess: a retrospective multicenter study
Authors: Jianguo Wang, Zhenyi Lin, Lintao Chen, Zi Yang, Daqing Li, Yanxin Wan, Rongqian Wu, Yu Zhang, Zhaoqing Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & aimsDespite advances in therapeutic strategies, pyogenic liver abscess (PLA) remains a life-threatening bacterial infection associated with substantial mortality. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), a marker of immuno-nutritional status, has been shown to predict outcomes in various diseases. However, its prognostic value in patients with PLA remains unclear.MethodsA total of 405 patients diagnosed with PLA were enrolled in this study. Based on the median PNI value at admission, patients were stratified into the high-PNI group (PNI ≥ 37.15) and low-PNI group (PNI < 37.15). Clinical characteristics, laboratory data, imaging findings, presumed clinical route of PLA, previous hepatobiliary/pancreatic procedure history, treatment modalities, and clinical outcomes were analyzed to evaluate the association between PNI and prognosis.ResultsPatients in the low-PNI group were significantly older than those in the high-PNI group (59.07 ± 13.30 years vs. 54.20 ± 13.92 years, P < 0.001) and a higher prevalence of fever (92.08% vs. 85.15%, P = 0.028) was seen in patients with low PNI. The low-PNI group also had a higher frequency of SIRS at admission (32.51% vs. 17.33%, P < 0.001), higher rate of abscess formation with a diameter > 10 cm (16.75% vs. 6.44%, P < 0.001) and a higher frequency of gas formation in abscess (18.22% vs. 6.93%, P = 0.001). Additionally, patients with low PNI had significantly higher complication rates; particularly for formation of pleural effusion and abdominal effusion. Moreover, the length of hospital stay was significantly prolonged (15.70 ± 8.94 days vs. 13.46 ± 7.16 days, P = 0.006), and treatment costs were higher (4130.76 ± 3227.45 USD vs. 3388.60 ± 2975.95 USD, P = 0.017) in the low-PNI group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that low PNI was associated with prolonged temperature recovery time (OR = 1.589, 95% CI: 1.022–2.470, P = 0.040) and prolonged hospital stay (OR = 1.580, 95% CI: 1.033–2.415, P = 0.035).ConclusionPNI may serve as a useful marker for early clinical risk stratification in patients with PLA.Clinical TrialNot applicable.Keywords: Pyogenic liver abscess, Prognostic nutritional index, Clinical outcomes, Prognostic assessment

Introduction

Pyogenic liver abscess (PLA) is a severe liver disease caused by bacterial infection, with typically presented symptoms such as acute abdominal pain, fever, nausea, vomiting, and weight loss. The etiology of PLA is multifactorial, and is often associated with biliary tract and intra-abdominal infections. Previous interventions, such as surgical or endoscopic procedures, may also contribute to the development and severity of PLA [1]. Despite the advancements in antibiotic therapy, and the drainage procedures, PLA remains as a disease which is associated with high mortality and morbidity. Prognosis is particularly poor in elderly patients, immunocompromised individuals, and those with comorbidities [1–4]. Delayed diagnosis and treatment of the disease may lead to abscess rupture, acute liver failure, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, which can progress to sepsis and death [1, 5, 6]. Therefore, early identification of high-risk patients and prompt implementation of personalized treatment strategies are crucial for achieving favorable clinical outcomes.

Nutritional status is a key prognostic factor influencing disease outcomes. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), a comprehensive indicator combining serum albumin concentration and peripheral blood lymphocyte count, was initially developed to assess the nutritional status of patients with gastrointestinal diseases [7]. PNI has been increasingly applied in the prognostic assessment of various clinical conditions by reflecting the immune functions related with the nutritional status of the patients [8–10]. The decrease in PNI value indicates malnutrition and immunosuppression, which impair the host response to infection and thereby adversely affect disease prognosis [11].

Although PNI is a well-established prognostic marker for patients with malignant, infectious, and cardiovascular diseases, further research is needed to clarify its role in PLA [12–14]. The aim of this study was to elucidate the role of PNI in determining the clinical outcomes of PLA patients and to evaluate its utility as an easily applicable tool for identifying high-risk individuals, thereby facilitating the development of personalized treatment modalities to improve prognosis in this patient population.

Methods

Study group & data collection

Patients with pyogenic liver abscess (PLA) treated between January 2014 and February 2023 at Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital and the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University were retrospectively analyzed. All patients were diagnosed with PLA and met the following selection criteria: Inclusion criteria included age ≥ 18 years old, clinical diagnosis of PLA with complete clinical data (basic information, laboratory results, imaging data, etc.) and measurable prognostic nutritional index (PNI) at initial admission. Exclusion criteria included severe complications at admission (multiple organ failure, decompensated liver cirrhosis), atypical clinical manifestations precluding definitive PLA diagnosis, and no treatment or interrupted treatment after admission. The diagnosis of PLA was established based on a combination of clinical manifestations, laboratory evidence of infection, and radiological findings on abdominal ultrasound or computed tomography. Microbiological evidence from blood or pus cultures, when available, and clinical response to antibiotic therapy and/or drainage were also considered supportive diagnostic evidence. Cases in which PLA could not be definitively confirmed after chart review were excluded.

The collected clinical data included basic characteristics (age, gender, underlying diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, smoking and drinking history, and the presumed clinical route of PLA, previous hepatobiliary/pancreatic surgery or intervention), clinical manifestations (admission symptoms including fever, abdominal pain, hepatomegaly and fatigue, vital signs such as body temperature), serum biochemical indicators (white blood cell count, lymphocyte count, serum albumin, C-reactive protein [CRP], procalcitonin [PCT], liver function enzymes including ALT, AST, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, international normalized ratio [INR], prothrombin time [PT]), and imaging findings (abdominal ultrasound and computed tomography to determine abscess location, size and gas involvement), treatment modality, and clinical outcomes.

SIRS at admission was defined as meeting at least two of the following criteria: body temperature > 38 °C or < 36 °C, heart rate > 90 beats/min, respiratory rate > 20 breaths/min or PaCO₂ <32 mmHg, and WBC count > 12 × 10⁹/L or < 4 × 10⁹/L. ICU admission referred to ICU care during hospitalization due to clinical deterioration or the need for higher-level monitoring and supportive care, and was recorded separately from baseline severity.

The presumed clinical route of PLA was determined according to available clinical diagnoses, imaging findings, microbiological data, biliary disease records, evidence of intra-abdominal or distant infection, and clinical course. The presumed clinical route of PLA was classified into five mutually exclusive categories: biliary, postoperative/procedure-related, portal venous or direct extension, hematogenous, and cryptogenic/undetermined. When more than one potential route was present, the final category was assigned according to the most clinically plausible dominant route. Cases without an identifiable clinical route after chart review were classified as cryptogenic/undetermined.

Previous hepatobiliary/pancreatic surgery or intervention history was recorded as an independent baseline variable. The row “Any previous hepatobiliary/pancreatic surgery or intervention” represented unique patients with at least one documented previous hepatobiliary or pancreatic procedure. Detailed procedure subcategories were summarized descriptively and were not mutually exclusive, because some patients had undergone more than one previous procedure or intervention. Previous hepatic surgery/intervention did not include liver abscess drainage or puncture, which was listed separately. Biliary stent placement/removal or cholangioscopy was also listed separately when specifically documented.

Clinical outcomes were systematically analyzed based on time to body temperature normalization, treatment response (cure or improvement), hospitalization costs, length of hospital stay and mortality rate. This study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [15]. The Medical Ethics Committee of Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study.

PNI calculation

The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) was calculated according to the following formula: PNI=serum albumin (g/L) + 5×total lymphocyte count (×10⁹/L) [16, 17]. In this study, the median value of PNI was 37.15. Therefore, all patients were divided into two groups according to the PNI value: the high-PNI group (PNI ≥ 37.15) and the low-PNI group (PNI < 37.15).

The treatment strategies & definition of clinical outcomes

Antibiotic therapy was used as the initial treatment. Conservative treatment was defined as antibiotic therapy and supportive medical management without percutaneous drainage or surgical intervention during hospitalization. Percutaneous drainage was considered in patients with large abscesses, poor response to antibiotics, or localized complications. An abscess diameter greater than 5 cm was regarded as an important factor supporting drainage. Treatment decisions were made by the attending physicians based on radiological findings, including abscess size, the location of abscess formation, the degree of liquefaction in the content, and clinical response to the treatment and the overall condition of the patient.

Poor response to antibiotic therapy was assessed according to clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings of the patients; including persistent symptoms such as fever and inadequate improvement in serum inflammatory markers, and imaging findings on radiology. Surgery was considered for patients who were unsuitable for percutaneous drainage, had drainage failure, or developed abscess rupture.

Cure was defined as complete symptom relief, normalization of body temperature and serum biochemical parameters, and imaging evidence of abscess resolution or marked regression. Improvement was defined as symptom alleviation, body temperature ≤ 37.0 °C, improved serum biochemical parameters, and imaging evidence of abscess regression. Prolonged hospital stay was defined as hospitalization for more than 14 days. Prolonged time to temperature normalization was defined as more than 7 days to reach normal body temperature.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent-sample t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed with the chi-square test (χ²). Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed for defining the significant variables and variables with a p value < 0.10 were subsequently entered into the model. The multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the prognostic factors. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic clinical characteristics

A total of 405 patients with pyogenic liver abscess (PLA) were enrolled in this study. The patients were grouped according to the median PNI value, with 202 patients in the high-PNI group and 203 patients in the low-PNI group. There were no differences between the groups in terms of gender distribution, smoking and drinking history, or underlying diseases such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension. The mean age of the patients was 56.64 ± 13.81 years, and patients in the low-PNI group were significantly older than those in the high-PNI group (59.07 ± 13.30 years vs. 54.20 ± 13.92 years, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Age-stratified distribution of prognostic nutritional index (PNI) among patients with pyogenic liver abscess (PLA) at admission

Regarding the presumed clinical route of PLA, 143 patients (35.31%) were classified as biliary, 81 (20.00%) as postoperative/procedure-related, 10 (2.47%) as portal venous or direct extension, 3 (0.74%) as hematogenous, and 168 (41.48%) as cryptogenic/undetermined. The distribution of the presumed clinical route of PLA did not differ significantly between the high-PNI and low-PNI groups. Previous hepatobiliary/pancreatic surgery or intervention history was documented in 167 patients (41.23%), with comparable proportions between the two groups. Detailed procedure subcategories are shown descriptively in Table 1 and were not mutually exclusive. Clinically, fever was higher in the low-PNI group (92.08% vs. 85.15%, P = 0.028), whereas the prevalence of symptoms including abdominal pain, hepatomegaly, and fatigue was comparable between the two groups. In addition, body temperature at admission did not differ significantly between groups. However, SIRS at admission was significantly more common in the low-PNI group than in the high-PNI group (32.51% vs. 17.33%, P < 0.001). ICU admission was rare overall (2/405, 0.49%), and both cases occurred in the low-PNI group (Table 1).

Laboratory findings and imaging findings

Regarding laboratory findings, the low-PNI group exhibited a significantly higher neutrophil count (9.67 ± 5.64 × 10⁹/L vs. 8.23 ± 4.90 × 10⁹/L, P = 0.007) and procalcitonin level (12.05 ± 23.06 ng/mL vs. 3.02 ± 9.06 ng/mL, P < 0.001). Serum albumin level was notably lower in the low-PNI group (26.98 ± 3.85 g/L vs. 35.06 ± 4.45 g/L, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the incidence of deterioration in liver function (elevated AST: 34.98% vs. 25.74%, P = 0.043; elevated DBIL: 54.19% vs. 41.58%, P = 0.011; INR > 1.2: 44.33% vs. 18.81%, P < 0.001) was higher in the low-PNI group. Imaging findings revealed that the incidence of abscesses with a diameter > 10 cm was significantly higher in the low-PNI group than in the high-PNI group (16.75% vs. 6.44%, P < 0.001). Additionally, the incidence of gas formation was also higher in the low-PNI group (18.22% vs. 6.93%, P = 0.001) (Table 1).

Complications, treatments, and clinical outcomes

Patients with low PNI had an increased incidence of complications including pulmonary infection (4.93% vs. 2.48%) and portal venous thrombosis (1.33% vs. 0.75%). Notably, the incidences of abdominal effusion (13.30% vs. 6.44%, P = 0.021) and pleural effusion (33.99% vs.15.35%, P < 0.001) were significantly higher in this group. Percutaneous drainage was performed more frequently in the low-PNI group, although the difference was not statistically significant (64.53% vs. 56.44%, P = 0.061). Combination antibiotic therapy was more commonly required in the low-PNI group (62.07% vs. 47.03%, P = 0.002). Among patients undergoing percutaneous drainage, polymicrobial infection was more frequent in the low-PNI group (12.98% vs. 1.75%, P = 0.004). The time to normalization of body temperature was also longer in the low-PNI group (7.75 ± 5.846 days vs. 5.69 ± 5.993 days, P < 0.001). With respect to clinical outcomes, although there was no significant difference in the cure rate between the two groups, the length of hospital stay (15.70 ± 8.94 days vs. 13.46 ± 7.16 days, P = 0.006) and treatment costs (4130.76 ± 3227.45 USD vs. 3388.60 ± 2975.95 USD, P = 0.017) were significantly higher in the low-PNI group (Table 2).

Microbiological data

In microbiological examinations, the pus culture results from the two groups showed that the incidence of Escherichia coli infection was significantly higher in the low-PNI group (25.51% vs. 10.26%, P = 0.008), whereas no significant difference was observed in the rate of Klebsiella pneumoniae between the two groups. Moreover, polymicrobial infections were more prevalent in the low-PNI group (17.35% vs. 5.13%, P = 0.011). In terms of blood culture, the positive blood culture rate in the low-PNI group (32.73% vs. 13.25%, P = 0.002) was significantly higher than that in the high-PNI group (Table 3).

Comparison between conservative treatment and percutaneous drainage

Comparisons of clinical characteristics and outcomes according to treatment modality within each PNI group are shown in Table 4. Among patients with low PNI, those who underwent percutaneous drainage had a longer length of hospital stay (16.40 ± 8.59 days vs. 12.42 ± 8.49 days, P = 0.005) and higher total hospitalization expenses (4389.48 ± 3061.81 USD vs. 2818.07 ± 1619.37 USD, P < 0.001) than those treated conservatively. In contrast, low-PNI patients treated conservatively had a higher prevalence of chronic liver disease than those who underwent percutaneous drainage (15.09% vs. 4.62%, P = 0.016). In the high-PNI group, percutaneous drainage was associated with a longer time to temperature normalization (6.73 ± 6.08 days vs. 2.64 ± 4.97 days, P < 0.001), whereas the difference in length of hospital stay was not statistically significant.

Univariable and multivariable regression analyses

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with prolonged temperature normalization and prolonged hospital stay. Low PNI was significantly associated with prolonged temperature recovery time (OR = 1.589, 95% CI:1.022–2.470, P = 0.040) and prolonged hospital stay (OR = 1.580, 95% CI:1.033–2.415, P = 0.035). Age ≥ 65 years (OR = 1.921, 95% CI:1.157–3.189, P = 0.012) and white blood cell count > 10 × 10⁹/L (OR = 2.482, 95% CI:1.599–3.853, P < 0.001) were also associated with prolonged temperature recovery time. Discussion

This retrospective multicenter study examined the association between PNI and clinical outcomes in patients with pyogenic liver abscess (PLA). A low PNI at admission was associated with delayed temperature normalization, prolonged hospitalization, and higher hospitalization costs. In multivariable analyses, low PNI remained significantly associated with prolonged time to temperature normalization and prolonged length of stay after adjustment for potential confounding factors. These findings suggest that PNI may provide additional information for early clinical assessment in PLA.

Several potential confounding factors should be considered when interpreting the association between PNI and clinical outcomes [18, 19]. These include age, underlying diseases, chronic liver disease, admission severity, inflammatory status, abscess burden, microbiological characteristics, hepatic dysfunction, and treatment-selection factors. In this study, patients with low PNI were older and showed more severe clinical features, including higher rates of SIRS at admission, larger abscesses, gas formation, positive blood cultures, and pleural or abdominal effusion [20, 21]. Moreover, treatment strategies such as percutaneous drainage and combination antibiotic therapy were selected according to clinical severity, which may have introduced treatment-selection bias [22]. Therefore, low PNI should be interpreted as an integrative marker of immunonutritional vulnerability and disease severity rather than as an isolated causal determinant of poor outcomes.

The subgroup analysis according to treatment modality should be interpreted with caution. In patients with low PNI, percutaneous drainage was associated with longer hospitalization and higher costs compared with conservative treatment. This finding does not necessarily indicate that drainage worsened outcomes; rather, patients selected for drainage were likely to have larger abscesses, poorer response to antibiotics, liquefied abscess cavities, or more complicated clinical presentations [22, 23]. Therefore, treatment-selection bias may partly explain the longer hospital stay and higher costs observed in the drainage group. Conversely, the higher prevalence of chronic liver disease among low-PNI patients treated conservatively may reflect physicians’ preference for less invasive management in patients with impaired hepatic reserve or higher procedural risk. These findings highlight the clinical heterogeneity of PLA and support individualized treatment decisions based on both infection severity and host condition [24].

The association between low PNI and poor outcomes in PLA likely reflects the combined effects of malnutrition, impaired immune competence, and heightened systemic inflammation [25–27]. PNI integrates two biologically meaningful components, albumin and lymphocyte count, and it captures both nutritional reserves and host immune capacity [28–30]. Patients with low PNI may have reduced cellular immunity and diminished pathogen clearance, which can facilitate abscess progression and systemic dissemination [31]. In our cohort, the low-PNI group exhibited more prominent signs of severe infection, including higher inflammatory markers, greater prevalence of SIRS at admission, and increased rates of positive blood cultures.

The higher frequency of larger abscesses and gas formation in the low-PNI group may further reflect a greater local infectious burden and impaired host containment of infection. Because PNI combines serum albumin and lymphocyte count, a low PNI may indicate reduced nutritional reserve and weakened cellular immune competence, which could contribute to delayed control of local infection [32, 33]. However, these imaging findings should be interpreted as components of a high-risk clinical profile associated with low PNI, rather than as direct consequences of low PNI itself. These findings are consistent with a higher probability of bacteremia and sepsis. Low PNI represents a vulnerable immunonutritional phenotype in which severe infection is more likely to develop and persist, contributing to delayed defervescence and prolonged hospital stay [34]. Of note, we excluded patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis and observed no significant imbalance in baseline chronic liver disease. This suggests that the prognostic signal of low PNI in this study is more closely related to infection-associated inflammatory stress rather than advanced underlying liver failure.

Albumin biology provides an additional mechanistic link between low PNI and disease severity. Hypoalbuminemia is not only a marker of malnutrition but also a negative acute-phase reactant that decreases during systemic inflammation due to suppressed hepatic synthesis, capillary leakage, increased catabolism, and reduced nutritional intake [35, 36]. Therefore, in PLA, low albumin may simultaneously indicate poor baseline reserves and an intensified inflammatory state. Clinically, hypoalbuminemia may aggravate complications such as pleural effusion and abdominal effusion by impairing oncotic pressure and fluid homeostasis [7, 30, 37]. In parallel, malnutrition and lymphopenia may compromise host immune defense and tissue repair [38, 39]. These processes may create a self-reinforcing cycle in which inflammation worsens nutritional depletion and immune suppression, thereby facilitating persistent infection and delayed recovery. This framework is consistent with the higher rates of pleural and abdominal effusion observed in the low-PNI group and supports the clinical plausibility of PNI as an integrative prognostic marker in PLA.

The prognostic relevance of PNI should also be interpreted in the context of disease background and admission severity. In our cohort, patients with low PNI more frequently met SIRS criteria at admission, suggesting that low PNI was associated not only with immunonutritional vulnerability but also with a more severe early clinical presentation [26, 40]. In addition, the substantial proportions of biliary and postoperative/procedure-related cases further underscore the clinical heterogeneity of PLA. Accordingly, the prognostic role of PNI may be particularly informative in clinically complex patients with previous hepatobiliary or pancreatic disease history.

Beyond global infection severity, we observed distinct microbiological patterns associated with low PNI. Patients with low PNI showed higher proportions of Escherichia coli infection and polymicrobial growth [41], as well as a higher blood-culture positivity rate. These findings may reflect greater susceptibility to invasive infection in the low-PNI group. Previous studies have suggested that E. coli-related liver abscesses are often associated with biliary tract abnormalities and a more complicated clinical course [42]; however, these relationships were not directly tested in our cohort [4]. Therefore, the microbiological differences observed in the present study should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further investigation in future studies [43].

These findings have practical implications for clinical management. Patients with low PNI in our cohort tended to present with larger abscesses, higher incidence of gas formation, and greater complication burden, often requiring combination antibiotic therapy and invasive source control through percutaneous drainage or surgery [33, 44–46]. Accordingly, incorporating PNI into early clinical decision-making may help guide the intensity of monitoring, the breadth of empirical antimicrobial coverage when appropriate, and the timing of intervention, especially for patients whose initial presentation suggests high-risk disease.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and residual confounding, and treatment strategies were not fully standardized across centers. In addition, etiologic classification and some previous intervention variables were derived from chart review rather than from a prospectively standardized coding framework. Furthermore, drainage indications were not uniformly recorded in a structured format, and detailed nutritional parameters such as BMI and prealbumin were not consistently available. Second, although the cohort size was moderate, external generalizability requires confirmation in larger prospective multicenter studies. Third, we did not evaluate whether combining PNI with additional biomarkers, such as immune cell subsets or comprehensive nutritional assessment tools, improves predictive performance. Future studies should prospectively validate the role of PNI in risk stratification and assess whether integrated models can improve individualized management for PLA.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that low PNI was associated with delayed temperature recovery, prolonged hospital stay, and increased treatment costs in patients with PLA. As a simple and readily available biomarker, PNI may serve as an early risk-stratification marker in patients with PLA. Further prospective studies are needed to validate its clinical utility.
