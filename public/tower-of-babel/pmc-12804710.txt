Effects of different powers of repeated low-level red light on form-deprivation myopia inhibition in guinea pigs
Authors: Ling Huang, Mengyuan Gao, Aobo Ma, Yunyan Wu, Jiarui Fei, Guoying Mu, Jiangping Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to determine whether 1.2 mW repeated low-level red light (RLRL) is as effective as 2.0 mW RLRL in inhibiting the development of myopia in the guinea pig model of form-deprivation myopia (FDM), while providing greater safety. Forty-eight guinea pigs were randomized into four groups: normal control (NC), FDM, 1.2 mW RLRL, and 2.0 mW RLRL. FDM was induced in the right eye by fitting it with an opaque mask for 4 weeks. Two RLRL groups underwent the same FDM and received twice-daily RLRL therapy (1.2 mW or 2.0 mW, 3 min each) to the masked eye. Parameters included refraction, axial length (AL), choroidal thickness (ChT), vessel density of the choriocapillaris (VDCC), vessel density of the choroidal layer (VDCL), and the ultrastructure of scleral collagen. TUNEL staining was used to assess retinal cell apoptosis. The average difference of parameters was compared by repeated-measures analysis of variance. 1.2 mW and 2.0 mW RLRL significantly weakened FDM-induced axial elongation (0.217 ± 0.11 mm and 0.233 ± 0.07 mm vs. FDM: 0.553 ± 0.088 mm; P < 0.001) and myopic shift (− 3.458 ± 0.958 D and − 2.792 ± 0.958 D vs. FDM: −6.25 ± 1.422 D; P < 0.001) compared with the FDM group at 4 weeks. Both powers restored ChT and VDCC, preserved scleral collagen structure, and prevented retinal cell apoptosis. No significant inter-dose differences were observed (P > 0.05). Both 1.2 mW and 2.0 mW RLRL effectively slowed down the progression of FDM in the guinea pig model by regulating choroidal thickness, hemodynamics, and scleral remodeling, and were safe. Introduction

Myopia has become a major public health issue in the 21st century1. It is projected that by 2050, nearly half of the global population will have myopia, among whom approximately 10% will be highly myopic and at risk of blindness2. In China, more than 80% of high school students are myopic, of whom over 20% have high myopia3,4.

The primary structural manifestation of myopia is axial elongation of the eyeball. This elongation is closely associated with choroidal thinning and reduction of choroidal blood perfusion (ChBP), which are thought to play a crucial role in scleral remodeling and myopia progression5,6. Specifically, impaired ChBP may influence the transformation of scleral fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, leading to extracellular matrix reorganization and biomechanical weakening of the sclera7,8.

The current mainstream clinical strategies for myopia control primarily include increasing outdoor activity9, orthokeratology lenses10, defocus spectacles11, and low-dose atropine12. While these approaches can be effective, they are generally limited by scholastic stress, poor compliance, high costs, or side effects. This highlights the need for a convenient, non-invasive, and well-tolerated alternative.

Repeated low-level red light (RLRL) therapy was initially used to treat pediatric amblyopia, and recently it has been applied in the prevention and control of myopia13,14. Proposed mechanisms include photobiological effects, hemodynamic changes in the choroid, and improvement of oxygen supply15,16. Clinical studies have shown that RLRL therapy effectively controls myopia progression in children17–20, but its complete safety profile requires further investigation. As a Class III medical device, RLRL therapy still faces scientific challenges, particularly concerning more definitive efficacy and safety. Current protocols mainly use the irradiation power of 2.0 ± 0.5 mW21. A previous randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed that the efficacy and safety of lower RLRL energy levels (0.37 mW, 0.60 mW, and 1.20 mW) were comparable in terms of axial length (AL) and spherical equivalent (SE) after 6 months of treatment22. However, this study did not directly compare the relative efficacy of lower energy levels with the traditional 2.0 mW setting in myopia control or the effects on ocular tissues, such as choroid and sclera. In our study, the intensity of 1.20 mW was chosen, as it was significantly lower than the conventional dose (approximately 40%), and is directly relevant as a standard setting on commercial devices.

Thus, this study aims to compare the efficacy of lower-power (1.2 mW) and conventional power (2.0 mW) RLRL therapy in myopia control, and to comprehensively evaluate ocular biometric parameters, choroidal thickness (ChT), ChBP, scleral remodeling, and safety profiles in a guinea pig model of form-deprivation myopia (FDM).

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Animal Protection Committee of the Shandong Provincial Hospital affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (NO.2022-089). All experiments were conducted in compliance with all relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines and the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) statement for the use of animals in ophthalmic and vision research. Tissue sections were prepared following the Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) of Servicebio, which encompasses pathological tissue sampling, fixation, embedding, paraffin sectioning, and other relevant procedures. To ensure reliability, all examinations were performed by two examiners who were blinded to the group assignments and proficient in ocular anatomy.

Animals and experimental design

The experimental flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Three-week-old male pigmented guinea pigs (Cavia porcellus, English short-hair stock, tricolor strain) with a weight of 120–150 g and a refraction of 3–6 D were used. The animals were reared in transparent plastic cages (20 × 30 × 35 cm) under a 12-hour light-dark cycle (8 AM–8 PM) at 25 °C, and illuminance intensity of ~ 350 lx at cage level (measured with a digital hand-held lux meter: Smartsensor AS803; Shenzhen, China). The animals had free access to standard chow, with fresh vegetables supplied twice daily.

Experimental flowchart. D, diopter; NC, normal control; FDM, form-deprivation myopia; RLRL, repeated low-level red light; AL, axial length; ChT, choroidal thickness; ChBP, choroidal blood perfusion.

Forty-eight male guinea pigs were randomly assigned to the following 4 groups (n = 12 per group): the normal control (NC) group, the FDM group, the 1.2 mW RLRL group, and the 2.0 mW RLRL group. SPSS (Version 27, USA) software was used to generate the randomization sequence. No intervention was administered to the NC group. Form deprivation was achieved by securing an opaque balloon mask over the right eye of the guinea pigs23. In the FDM, 1.2 mW RLRL, and 2.0 mW RLRL groups, each guinea pig was given a mask over its right eye for 4 weeks to induce myopia, while the left eye was untreated. During red-light treatment, the right eye mask was removed, and the animals were exposed to RLRL in a dark environment to eliminate any interference from other light. Each treatment was applied twice a day for 3 min per session, with a minimum interval of 4 h. To minimize potential circadian influences on ocular growth, treatments were administered twice daily at standardized times (09:00–10:00 and 15:00–16:00 h)16. The sample size was determined based on the animal availability and experimental feasibility. No animal was excluded from the experiment due to eye inflammation or other eye diseases.

The red-light irradiation therapy was provided by a light therapy device (Eyerising, Suzhou Xuanjia Optoelectronics Technology, Jiangsu, China). The device comprised 650 nm semiconductor LEDs that delivered low-level red light at a fixed working distance of 3 cm from the corneal plane. The emitted beam had energy densities of 2.0 and 1.2 mW/cm2 and an illuminance of approximately 1600 lx. During the 3-minute therapy, unsedated guinea pigs were gently restrained and tolerated the procedure well. The device was aligned to project a stable, central corneal light spot within the pupillary region, which was then directed onto the central retina.

Biometric measurements

Refractive measurements were performed using a handheld spot retinoscope (Keeler, UK) by the same optometrist in a dark room at 50 cm. Before the examination, the pupil was dilated with 1% cyclopentolate hydrochloride (Alcon, USA) eye drops every 5 min three times. The refraction was measured three times, and the average spherical equivalent (SE) was recorded24.

Axial length (AL) (from the anterior corneal surface to the nerve fiber layer of the retina) was measured with A-scan ultrasonography (Aviso, Quantel Medical, France). Before the measurement of the AL, oxybuprocaine hydrochloride (Santen Pharmaceutical, Japan) was dropped into the guinea pig’s conjunctival sac and achieved topical anesthesia. The tip of the probe touched the center of the cornea and was perpendicular to the plane of the cornea during the measurements. The frequency of the ultrasonic probe emission was 11 mHz. The conduction velocity for the anterior chamber, lens, and vitreous chamber was 1554, 1723.3, and 1540 m/s, respectively. Each eye was measured ten times, and the average value was recorded. The intra-session repeatability of the A-scan ultrasonography was excellent, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.97.

OCT and OCTA analysis

Guinea pigs were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1.5% pentobarbital sodium (37.5 mg/kg, Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) after retinoscopy, and then fixed on a lifting platform16. The Spectralis HRA + OCT (Heidelberg, Germany) was used with a central operating wavelength of 870 nm. OCT scans were centered on the optic disc (Fig. 2A) and were carried out in the horizontal and vertical directions to obtain structural OCT (Fig. 2B). Transverse scaling was corrected to account for ocular magnification effects based on AL and SE25. The selected OCTA images were centered on the optic disc with a 9 × 3 mm² field of view (Fig.2C, green line) and were overlaid with structural OCT images (Fig. 2D- E). ChBP was observed by erythrocyte movement, which was represented as yellow-coded signal points in composite OCT-OCTA images (Fig. 2D− E). Only scans with signal strength over 30 were included in the subsequent analysis.

ChT was measured as the vertical distance between the outer retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) boundary and the scleral boundary. The RPE boundary was automatically identified by the built-in software (Fig. 2B, yellow line) and manually adjusted when misalignment occurred, while the scleral boundary was manually delineated by the examiner (Fig. 2B, blue line). We employed a standard measurement protocol with the optic disc as the central reference point5. This protocol was based on two concentric circles with radii of 600 μm (denoted as “in”) and 1050 μm (denoted as “out”) (Fig. 2B, red lines). ChT measurements were obtained at eight predefined points, which corresponded to the intersections of these circles with the four quadrants (superior, inferior, temporal, and nasal) (Fig. 2A − B). The average values were calculated for analysis.

Based on vascular morphological features at different depths, the examiner manually demarcated the boundaries of these two layers on overlaid OCT-OCTA images to evaluate ChBP. The choriocapillaris (CC) layer was defined as the region extending from Bruch’s membrane to 20 μm below. The choroidal layer (CL; containing medium and large vessels) spanned from 20 μm to 100 μm below Bruch’s membrane. The CC and CL were segmented, in which the defined region of interest (ROI) was confined between the red dotted lines (Fig. 2D − E). Within each ROI, blood perfusion signals (yellow-coded) were identified as pixels with intensities exceeding the mean vitreous signal value. The vessel density of the choriocapillaris (VDCC) and choroidal layer (VDCL) was subsequently calculated as the ratio of perfused vascular pixels to the total number of pixels in the ROI26.

Transmission electron microscopy examination and analysis

Guinea pigs in each group (n = 3) were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of a lethal dose of 4% pentobarbital at 4 weeks. Immediately post-mortem, the eyeballs were removed, and the orbital fat and conjunctiva were removed under a surgical microscope (Topcon, Japan). Mark the cornea and limbus of the eye at 12 o’clock with an indelible ink marker. Eyes were then fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C for 4 h. The cornea and lens were dissected, and the mark remained in the limbal area. The posterior tissues (retina, choroid, and sclera) were extracted with a 6 mm diameter trephine. The sclera strips (< 1 mm³) were cut from the nasal area 1 mm away from the optic nerve with a blade27. These samples were re-fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C for over 2 h, and then put into 1% osmium. After rinsing and dehydration in graded ethanol and acetone, they were embedded in a mixture of pure acetone and embedding solution (epoxy resin) (2:1 at room temperature for 3–4 h, 1:2 at room temperature overnight, and pure embedding solution at 37 °C for 2–3 h). Ultrathin sections (50–60 nm) were cut using an LKB-1 ultramicrotome and double-stained with 3% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. These sections were then imaged via transmission electron microscopy (JEM-1200EX, Japan) at x25000 magnification27. A technician analyzed the diameters and densities of fibers using ImageJ (USA), and each group counted about 300 fibers.

TUNEL assays and safety assessment

Three guinea pigs were randomly selected from each experimental group for histopathological analysis at 4 weeks. Guinea pigs were euthanized, and the eyeballs were removed and fixed as described above. After conventional dehydration and paraffin-embedding, the eyeballs were cut into 5-µm slices. Paraffin sections of the eye tissues were dewaxed and treated with proteinase K, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 20 min. Then, a mixture of TDT enzyme, dUTP, and buffer (2:5:50) was applied to the tissues and incubated in a humid chamber at 37 °C for 1 h. Sections were washed with PBS, and nuclei were stained with DAPI in the dark for 10 min. After washing, slices were fixed with an anti-fluorescence quenching sealing agent. An optical microscope (Nikon, Japan) was used for observation, and ImageJ software (USA) was used to capture images for subsequent analysis. The nuclei stained with DAPI appeared blue under UV excitation (330–380 nm excitation, 420 nm emission), while the kit’s 488 fluorescein was green to mark the positive apoptotic cells (465–495 nm excitation, 515–555 nm emission). To ensure specific identification of apoptotic cells, only those exhibiting discrete, punctate green fluorescence exceeding threefold background tissue fluorescence and co-localized with pyknotic or fragmented nuclei were scored, excluding debris and non-specific signal. The apoptotic index of retinal cells was quantified as the ratio of fluorescein-positive cells to total DAPI-stained nuclei. The cornea and lens were examined by slit lamp biomicroscopy during the whole experiment.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study were presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs), and parametric statistical analyses were used. Change in parameter = Post-treatment value – Baseline value22. We used repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare the changes of refraction, AL, ChT, and choroidal vessel area density, with groups as factors and time as the repeated measures. When sphericity was violated (Mauchly’s test, P < 0.05), the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons used the post-hoc Bonferroni5. Analyses were performed in SPSS statistical software (Version 27, USA). Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Effect of RLRL on the refraction and AL

Figure 3(A − B) shows the changes in refraction and AL of four experimental groups with time. Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of group and time, as well as group × time interactions, for both refraction and AL changes from baseline (all P < 0.001). Compared to the NC group, the FDM group showed a progressive myopic shift in refraction and a progressive increase in AL at both 2 weeks (refraction: −3.354 ± 0.901 vs. −0.625 ± 0.406 D; AL: 0.336 ± 0.069 vs. 0.091 ± 0.056 mm; both P < 0.001) and 4 weeks (refraction: −6.25 ± 1.422 vs. −1.333 ± 0.567 D; AL: 0.553 ± 0.088 vs. 0.154 ± 0.077 mm; both P < 0.001). RLRL treatment (1.2 mW or 2.0 mW) significantly suppressed the changes induced by form deprivation. At 2 weeks, compared to the FDM group, the 1.2 mW RLRL group exhibited a less myopic shift in refraction (− 1.917 ± 0.779 D vs. FDM: −3.354 ± 0.901 D; P < 0.001) and a slower increase in AL (0.102 ± 0.055 mm vs. FDM: 0.336 ± 0.069 mm; P < 0.001). Similarly, the 2.0 mW RLRL group also showed a less myopic shift (− 1.646 ± 0.494 D, P < 0.001) and a slower increase in AL (0.095 ± 0.059 mm, P < 0.001) compared to the FDM group (refraction: −3.354 ± 0.901 D; AL: 0.336 ± 0.069 mm) at 2 weeks. By 4 weeks, refraction in both the 1.2 mW group (− 3.458 ± 0.958 D) and the 2.0 mW group (− 2.792 ± 0.958 D) demonstrated significantly less myopic shift than in the FDM group (− 6.25 ± 1.422; both P < 0.001); AL followed the same trend (1.2 mW: 0.217 ± 0.11 mm; 2.0 mW: 0.233 ± 0.07 mm vs. FDM: 0.553 ± 0.088 mm; both P < 0.001). The 2.0 mW group exhibited a tendency toward less myopic shift compared to the 1.2 mW group at the 4-week follow-up (− 1.646 ± 0.494 D vs. −1.917 ± 0.779 D at 2 weeks; −2.792 ± 0.958 D vs. −3.458 ± 0.958 D at 4 weeks), but the difference was not statistically significant at any time point (all P > 0.05).

Effect of RLRL on the ChT and blood perfusion

The temporal changes of ChT are shown in Fig. 4(A). The main and interaction effects of group, time, and the interactive effects were significant on the change in ChT from baseline, as demonstrated by repeated-measures ANOVA (both P < 0.01). Post hoc comparisons indicated that the FDM group exhibited a significantly greater reduction in ChT compared to the NC group at both 2 weeks (− 14.156 ± 9.94 μm vs. −5.234 ± 5.014 μm) and 4 weeks (− 21.709 ± 11.373 μm vs. −6.5 ± 6.095 μm) (both P < 0.001). By 4 weeks, both the 1.2 mW RLRL (− 9.688 ± 8.994 μm; P < 0.05) and 2.0 mW RLRL (− 8.75 ± 5.863 μm; P < 0.01) therapy groups showed a significantly smaller reduction in ChT compared to the FDM group (− 21.709 ± 11.373 μm). However, the changes in ChT from baseline in the RLRL groups were not significantly different from those in the FDM group at 2 weeks (all P > 0.05). There was no statistical difference in the change of ChT between the two RLRL groups at any time point (all P > 0.05).

VDCC and VDCL changes over time are shown in Fig. 4(B − C) for all study groups. The repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of group and time, as well as a significant group × time interaction on VDCC change from baseline (all P < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons found that the reduction in VDCC from baseline was significantly greater in the FDM group than in the NC group at both 2 weeks (FDM: −0.052 ± 0.011% vs. NC: −0.019 ± 0.007%; P < 0.01) and 4 weeks (FDM: −0.095 ± 0.023% vs. NC: −0.023 ± 0.009%; P < 0.001). At 4 weeks, the 1.2 mW RLRL group showed a significantly smaller reduction in VDCC than the FDM group (− 0.052 ± 0.023% vs. −0.095 ± 0.023%; P < 0.01), although no difference was observed at 2 weeks (P > 0.05). The 2.0 mW RLRL group exhibited a significantly smaller VDCC reduction than the FDM group at both 2 weeks (− 0.021 ± 0.02% vs. −0.052 ± 0.011%; P < 0.05) and 4 weeks (− 0.042 ± 0.021% vs. −0.095 ± 0.023%; P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were detected in VDCC reduction between the two treated groups at either time point (P > 0.05).

For VDCL change, the main effects of group and the group × time interaction did not reach statistical significance (all P > 0.05). Exploratory analyses of the change in VDCL from baseline at each time point revealed no significant differences (all P > 0.05).

Representative transmission electron micrographs of scleral collagen fibers and their diameter distribution histograms are presented in Fig. 5. RLRL treatment at 1.2 mW (84.666 ± 20.512 nm) and 2.0 mW (89.192 ± 20.464 nm) significantly prevented the FDM-induced reduction in fiber diameter relative to untreated FDM controls (P < 0.001 for both). Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of 1.2 mW and 2.0 mW RLRL therapy in an FDM guinea pig model. Our data suggested that both RLRL treatments significantly inhibited axial elongation and myopic shift. Despite no statistically significant differences between the two groups, a trend showed the potential effectiveness of higher power. Through quantitative analysis using OCT and OCTA, we further found that both RLRL treatments effectively prevented the FDM-induced reduction in both ChT and VDCC. Ultrastructural examination of sclera revealed that both RLRL treatments improved scleral biomechanical strength.

The dose-response relationship of phototherapy is still controversial. Our findings suggested there might be an efficacy plateau for RLRL treatment within the 1.2–2.0 mW range over 4 weeks. This observation was consistent with the clinical findings of Zhou et al.22 They found that, within the lower range of 0.37–1.20 mW, all power levels yielded comparable myopia control, although a trend suggested improved outcomes with higher power. A recent randomized crossover trial also showed that 50%-intensity RLRL was non-inferior to full-intensity therapy in reducing AL after 4 weeks (delivered at 0.145 mW and 0.29 mW through a 4-mm pupil, respectively)28. In contrast, Dong et al.29 compared full-power desktop RLRL devices with 10%-power ones (0.03 mW entering a 4 mm pupil) in an RCT. They found that full-power devices were more effective in controlling myopia progression and axial elongation after 6 months of treatment. Notably, the full-power equipment cited in these studies refers to the standard therapeutic device used in our study. A further study examined three RLRL powers (1.8 mW, 0.6 mW, and 0.2 mW)30. It did not directly compare myopia control in pairs, so the dose-response problem was unresolved. Further research is needed to explore potential dose-response relationships and the effects of even higher power intensities of RLRL.

By promoting the structural remodeling of choroidal blood vessels and stroma, RLRL treatment may delay the development of myopia. Our research showed significant reductions in both ChT and ChBP in the FDM guinea pig model, which were effectively reversed following RLRL treatment. Similarly, a randomized crossover trial demonstrated that both 50% and 100% intensity regimens yielded comparable efficacy, significantly increasing ChT and improving vascular parameters after 4 weeks. A secondary analysis of the multicenter RCT by Xuan et al.31 revealed that one-year RLRL treatment could significantly increase ChT, choroidal luminal area, stromal area, total choroidal area, and choroidal vascularity index in children with myopia. Notably, the 2.0 mW RLRL group in this study exhibited the benefits of early intervention, which significantly prevented the decline of VDCC at the 2-week time point. This suggests that higher power densities enable the delivered light energy to reach the critical threshold for initiating therapeutic responses more rapidly32. However, although the higher power conferred an initial advantage, this benefit diminished over time, with the therapeutic outcomes of the two groups converging at the 4-week endpoint. This aligns with the theory that photobiomodulation efficacy is determined by the interplay between power density and treatment duration33. These findings aligned with the clinical findings of Jiang et al.34, whose RCT demonstrated that RLRL safely and dynamically improved choroidal microcirculation in children with myopia. The study reported immediate post-treatment increases in perfusion after single exposures. Cumulative effects reached the peak after 9 months of continuous treatment, and then gradually decreased after cessation, which was related to the frequency of treatment. This progress in time may explain the similar long-term results we have observed in the current research.

The absence of a significant change in VDCL underscores the selective effect of RLRL on the microvasculature. This vascular specificity supports the proposed mechanism that RLRL improves local oxygenation and metabolic supply mainly through hemodynamic regulation at the capillary level13,35,36. Our results confirmed the work of Xiang et al.16 They also found stable parameters (vessel area density, vessel skeleton density, and flux) in the choroidal medium-large vessel layer after 3 weeks of 2.0 ± 0.5 mW RLRL treatment in FDM guinea pigs. Before any detectable ChT changes, there was a significant difference in VDCC in the 2.0 mW RLRL group at 2 weeks. This finding suggests microcirculation changes may mediate initial RLRL effects before structural choroidal thickening occurs. The RCT by Wei et al.37 showed that short-term improvement of ChBP in children following RLRL is neither persistent nor correlated with long-term choroidal thickening. This difference may be explained by the automatic adjustment ability of the choroid, which highlights the complexity of choroidal hemodynamic responses5. The anatomical and physiological differences in choroidal structure between guinea pigs and humans are also important considerations.

Ultrastructural analysis revealed that the scleral collagen fibers in RLRL-treated groups exhibited significantly greater compactness and organization compared to the FDM group. Our results showed that RLRL treatment effectively prevented the reduction of scleral collagen fiber diameter and restored fiber size to about 70–80% of that in the NC group. This structural preservation may be due to RLRL-mediated regulation of the activity of scleral fibroblasts. This regulation may reduce the degradation of collagen, enhance the synthesis of extracellular matrix, and weaken excessive axial elongation15. The controlled axial elongation observed after RLRL treatment further supports the hypothesis that RLRL could reduce pathological scleral thinning38. Scleral reinforcement was consistent with improvements in choroidal microvascular perfusion, which indicates that a potential hemodynamic mechanism may be consistent with that of RLRL. Enhanced ChBP may increase oxygen and nutrition delivered to the sclera and regulate the metabolism of fibroblasts7,39. Notably, our transmission electron microscopy analysis focused on the nasal sclera, a key region for scleral remodeling in FDM40. Future studies extending these analyses to the remaining quadrants (temporal, superior, and inferior) would help confirm the consistency of the observed morphological improvements across the posterior sclera.

The study confirmed the safety of RLRL therapy by histopathological analysis and quantitative apoptosis assays. Both RLRL levels showed their ocular biocompatibility, and revealed a numerical trend of reducing the apoptosis rates of retinal cells at lower dose. Our finding that 1.20 mW is comparably effective to 2.0 mW offers clinicians a viable, potentially safer treatment option. This establishes a new foundation for future trials and safety standards, advancing personalized myopia management. A prior case report reported transient visual impairment that may be caused by individual susceptibility, device variability, or protocol deviations41. RCTs have revealed only mild and reversible adverse events unrelated to treatment17,29. The research results are still limited by short-term follow-up and heterogeneous protocols, which highlights the necessity of establishing standardized power supply parameters and long-term safety evaluation.

While the focused beam primarily targeted the central retina, we observed significant choroidal thickening in the unilluminated peripapillary region. This suggests that the therapeutic effects of RLRL extend beyond the directly irradiated area, which could be partly explained by a bystander effect42,43. Furthermore, regionally specialized photoreceptor and neuronal densities confer region-specific responses across the retina44,45. The study of Li et al.30 showed that localized delivery of RLRL to the peripheral retina is more effective than that to the central retina in inhibiting myopic axial elongation in guinea pigs. This may demonstrate that the retina exhibits differential responses to RLRL based on the spatial characteristics of light exposure. The precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Several limitations exist. Firstly, the rapidly induced FDM guinea pig model is different from multifactorial human myopia progression (such as reduced outdoor activity and near work). This may lead to overestimation of the short-term efficacy of RLRL. Secondly, the limited power range and 4-week duration limited the determination of the optimal treatment thresholds or long-term safety evaluation. Thirdly, while the sample size (n = 12/group) meets conventional standards, it may lack the ability to detect all clinically relevant differences. Fourthly, our mechanistic assessment does not include molecular pathway analysis. Consequently, the precise molecular pathways remain to be fully elucidated, and better methods are needed to study upstream signaling in the sclera. Finally, while our safety assessment was based on histological examinations, functional evaluations such as electroretinography would provide deeper insights into retinal safety.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that both 1.2 mW and 2.0 mW RLRL therapies may effectively inhibit the development of myopia in the FDM guinea pig model by regulating choroidal thickness and hemodynamics, and scleral remodeling. Both doses showed comparable efficacy despite a numerical trend in the 2.0 mW group. Further long-term research and more samples are needed to confirm these findings.
