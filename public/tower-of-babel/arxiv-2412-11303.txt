Regularized Dikin Walks for Sampling Truncated Logconcave Measures, Mixed Isoperimetry and Beyond Worst-Case Analysis
Minhui Jiang, Yuansi Chen

Abstract
We study the problem of drawing samples from a logconcave distribution truncated on a polytope, motivated by computational challenges in Bayesian statistical models with indicator variables, such as probit regression. Building on interior point methods and the Dikin walk for sampling from uniform distributions, we analyze the mixing time of regularized Dikin walks. Our contributions are threefold. First, for a logconcave and log-smooth distribution with condition number κ \kappa , truncated on a polytope in n defined with m m linear constraints, we prove that the soft-threshold Dikin walk mixes in O ~ ​ ( ( m + κ ) ​ n ) \widetilde{O}((m+\kappa)n) iterations from a warm initialization. It improves upon prior work which required the polytope to be bounded and involved a bound dependent on the radius of the bounded region. Moreover, we introduce the regularized Dikin walk using Lewis weights for approximating the John ellipsoid. We show that it mixes in O ~ ​ ( ( n 2.5 + κ ​ n ) CLOSE \widetilde{O}((n^{2.5}+\kappa n) . Second, we extend the mixing time guarantees mentioned above to weakly log-concave distributions truncated on polytopes, provided that they have a finite covariance matrix. Third, going beyond worst-case mixing time analysis, we demonstrate that soft-threshold Dikin walk can mix significantly faster when only a limited number of constraints intersect the high-probability mass of the distribution, improving the O ~ ​ ( ( m + κ ) ​ n ) \widetilde{O}((m+\kappa)n) upper bound to O ~ ​ ( m + κ ​ n ) \widetilde{O}(m+\kappa n) . Additionally, per-iteration complexity of regularized Dikin walk and ways to generate a warm initialization are discussed to facilitate practical implementation.

Introduction

Sampling from high-dimensional distributions under constraints is an important computational challenge in fields such as Bayesian statistics, computer science, and systems biology. In Bayesian statistics, posterior sampling is crucial for estimation and uncertainty quantification. There, posterior distributions are often truncated on a subset of
n
, rendering standard sampling algorithms for smooth distributions such as Langevin algorithms or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo ineffective. Examples of such posteriors include ordered exponential family models, censored data models, and ordered linear models
[
GSL92
]
. In addition, Bayesian probit regression models
[

]
, isotonic regression
[
ND04
]
, spatial probit models
[

]
, and tobit models
[

]
all require sampling from truncated normal distributions as a subroutine. In computer science, efficient volume computation of convex bodies relies heavily on sampling from distributions truncated on these bodies
[

,
LV06b
,
LV17
,
CV18
,
KV24
]
. In systems biology, constraint-based modeling of human metabolic networks uses polytopes to define solution spaces, making high-dimensional polytope-constrained sampling essential for these biological tasks
[
WFGP04
,
LNP12
,
SN16
,
HCT+17
,

]
. To address these needs, many sampling algorithms are developed and analyzed. Although uniform sampling on convex bodies or polytopes is well understood for algorithms such as Ball walk, hit-and-run
[

]
, and Dikin walks
[

]
, significant challenges remain to develop and understand new algorithms that leverage additional structure in non-uniform distributions.

One important motivating family of distributions in Bayesian statistics is the SUN (unified skew normal) distribution
[

,

]

Previous experiments show that sampling from SUN distributions through the “i.i.d. sampling” significantly outperforms directly sampling via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
[
AFDZ23
]
. However, while the proposed algorithm works well for small sample size

and relatively large feature dimension

, it becomes computationally prohibitive for datasets with large sample size

The challenge in sampling from SUN distributions with moderate or large

\mu
and covariance matrix
\Sigma

\pi(x)\propto\mathbb{I}_{\left\{x\geq 0\right\}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}{(x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}\right].

(1)

When
N
reaches the scale of thousands, given that each iteration costs at least
N^{2}
additions or multiplications, it is an open question whether sampling from a truncated Gaussian distribution in
N
can be done in less than
N^{3}
total number of additions or multiplications.

With the above motivation in mind, in this paper, we study mixing time guarantees of regularized Dikin walks for sampling strongly logconcave and log-smooth distributions truncated on polytopes. Dikin walks form a family of Markov chain sampling algorithms inspired by interior point methods in optimization, which has already shown promising performance for sampling truncated uniform distributions
[
KN12
]
. Regularized Dikin walks are their variants to tackle non-uniform distributions. However, the existing mixing time guarantees such as those in
[
MV23
]
match neither those in truncated uniform sampling nor those in unconstrained sampling when these scenarios are treated as special cases. As we explain at the end of this section after introducing appropriate notations, we improve the existing mixing time analysis and compare its computational performance to previous works. Through theoretical understanding of the mixing time, we also develop a new isoperimetric inequality that combines the Euclidean metric and the Hilbert metric on a convex set, which enables us with a better mixing time analysis.

1.3
Main Contributions

We investigate soft-threshold Dikin walk and our extension to more general regularized Dikin walks for sampling from a logconcave and log-smooth distribution truncated on a convex set. Our contributions are three-fold.

First, we improve the analysis on the soft-threshold Dikin walk proposed in
[
MV23
]
, upper bounding the mixing times for a wider range of shape constraints under the strongly logconcave and strongly log-smooth regime. For polytope constraints specifically, we show it mixes in
\widetilde{O}\left((m+\kappa)n\right)
iterations from a warm initialization. One improvement to
[
MV23
]
is that we no longer require the polytope to be bounded. For truncated Gaussian sampling, where an affine transformation ensures
\kappa=1
, we reduce the mixing time upper bound from
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
in
[
KV24
]
to
\widetilde{O}(n)
when
m=o(n)
, matching the state-of-the-art mixing time analysis in unconstrained logconcave sampling using Random Walk Metropolis. To reduce the dependency on
m
when
m\gg n
, we use regularized Dikin walk with regularized Lewis metric and show the mixing time is
\widetilde{O}(n^{2.5}+\kappa n)
.

Second, we extend our mixing time analysis on the regularized Dikin walk to the weakly logconcave distributions, where we only require the target distribution
\Pi
to have a finite covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\pi}
. For polytope constraints specifically, we show that soft-threshold Dikin walk mixes in
\widetilde{O}\left(mn+\eta\beta n\right)
, where
\eta
is the largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\pi}
. Using regularized Dikin walk with regularized Lewis metric, we achieve a mixing time of
\widetilde{O}(n^{2.5}+\eta\beta n)
when
m>n
.

Meanwhile, our regularized Dikin walk runs with only zeroth-order information of the negative log-density
f
. This is because we add a fixed regularization term to the local metric, which does not depend on first- or second-order information of
f
. In contrast,
[
KV24
]
relies on constructing a barrier function depending on
f
and add the corresponding Hessian to the local metric, which restricts the target distributions that their algorithm can be applied to.

Third, for truncated distributions on a polytope, going beyond worst-case analysis, we demonstrate that soft-threshold Dikin walk achieves a mixing time dependent on the number of constraints that a high-probability ball intersects, rather than the total number of constraints
m
, improving over the above
O(mn)
upper bound.

Related Works

The problem of sampling from distributions truncated on convex sets has been widely studied. The simplest case of uniform sampling on a convex body has seen significant progress in algorithm design and mixing time analysis in the last few decades. In this section, we first discuss this progress on uniform sampling in Section

2.1
Uniform Sampling over Convex Bodies

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms are by far the most popular sampling algorithms for uniform sampling on convex bodies. We first introduce the so-called “general-purpose” samplers, using the terminology from
[
KV24
]
, which are designed for sampling from a general convex body
K\subseteq\real^{n}
. It assumes having access to the convex body only through a membership oracle which checks whether
x\in K
. Thus, the convergence of general-purpose samplers is measured by how many times the oracle is called to achieve certain error tolerance. Two famous examples of general-purpose samplers are ball walk and hit-and-run.

For uniform sampling over a convex body
K\subseteq\real^{n}
, assume that the convex body is in the isotropic position, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}_{K}(x)=0
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{K}(xx^{\top})=I
, then
[
KLS97
]
shows that the mixing time of ball walk to be
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}/\psi_{n}^{2})
under warmness, where
\psi_{n}
is the smallest isoperimetric constant for any isotropic logconcave distributions over
n
, also known as Kannan-Lovasz-Simonovits (KLS) constant. Hit-and-run has also been studied for decades (see
[
Lov99
]
,
[
LV06b
]
,
[
CE22
]
). For hit-and-run on isotropic
K
,
[
CE22
]
proved a mixing time of
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}/\psi_{n}^{2})
under warmness. Using the current lower bound for the KLS constant
\psi_{n}\gtrsim\log^{-\frac{1}{2}}(n)
(see
[
Che21
]
,
[
KL22
]
,
[
JLV22
]
,
[
Kla23
]
), the mixing time for both ball walk and hit-and-run is
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
for isotropic convex bodies.

One potential drawback for general-purpose samplers like ball walk or hit-and-run is that, when the convex body is not isotropic, an affine transformation needs to be computed to bring the convex body to near-isotropic positions. This preprocessing step is called rounding. For a convex body
K\subseteq\real^{n}
, the state-of-art rounding algorithm from
[
JLLV21
]
has an oracle complexity of
\widetilde{O}(n^{3})
. So for a general non-isotropic convex body, the oracle complexity to get the first approximate sample is
\widetilde{O}(n^{3})
.

General-purpose samplers like ball walk and hit-and-run only use the membership oracle to access
K
. In practice, we often have richer information of the target distribution. For example, when sampling uniformly from a polytope
K=\left\{x|Ax-b>0\right\}
. To exploit the structures of the linear constraints, a class of structured samplers called Dikin walk was proposed. Motivated by interior point methods in convex optimization, in Dikin walk, a local ellipsoid (often defined according to the Hessian of a barrier function) is used as the proposal distribution. The local ellipsoid can automatically adjust its radius based on its closeness to the boundary of
K
, making it possible to circumvent the rounding procedure for non-isotropic target distributions.

Using Dikin walk to sample uniformly from a convex body,
[
KN12
]
proved a mixing time of
\widetilde{O}(mn)
using logarithmic barrier. In the regime where
m\gg n
, the linear dependency of mixing times upon
m
is often undesirable. A solution to this problem is to put weights on different constraints, thus tuning the mixing time dependency upon
m
and
n
:
[
CDWY18
]
proved mixing times of
\widetilde{O}(m^{0.5}n^{1.5})
and
\widetilde{O}(n^{2.5})
using Vaidya weights and approximate John weights respectively.

To better exploit the geometry induced by the polytope structure, instead of Dikin walk where each proposal is based on an ellipsoid in the Euclidean space,
[
LV17
]
designed geodesic walk, where a Riemannian metric is defined by the Hessian of the logarithmic barrier, and the proposal is determined by solving a differential equation to follow the geodesic on the manifold. This allows the sampler to take larger step while maintaining a low rejection rate.
[
LV17
]
achieved a mixing time of
\widetilde{O}\left(mn^{3/4}\right)
, which improved the
\widetilde{O}(mn)
mixing time of plain Dikin walk in
[
KN12
]
. Later,
[
LV18
]
further improved the mixing time to
\widetilde{O}(mn^{2/3})
by using RHMC (Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo), where a larger step can be taken since the Metropolis filter is eliminated while the stationary distribution is maintained throughout following the differential equations. To deal with the case
m\gg n
,
[
GKV24
]
recently redesigned RHMC where the Riemannian metric is based on a hybrid barrier (of Lewis-weights barrier and logarithmic barrier), and the mixing time is proved to be
\widetilde{O}(m^{1/3}n^{4/3})

3.1
Properties of local metrics

We define a few properties that a local metric
G
can satisfy. Since we do not restrict the convex set upon which the target distribution is truncated to be bounded/compact, we first need to extend the cross-ratio distances defined on convex bodies to unbounded convex sets.

Definition 3
.

Assume
K\subseteq\real^{n}
is an open and convex set. Fix
x,y\in K
, we define the cross-ratio distance
d_{K}(x,y)
under three scenarios:

•

If the line
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 1.29167pt\hskip 0.58333pt\rule[5.59721pt]{8.64674pt}{0.43057pt}}{xy}
intersects
\partial K
(the boundary of
K
) with
p,q
, and in the order
p,x,y,q
, then

d_{K}(x,y):=\frac{\left\|p-q\right\|_{2}\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}}{\left\|p-x\right\|_{2}\left\|q-y\right\|_{2}}.

•

If one of
\{p,q\}
is at infinity, then we delete the two distances involving that point. In other words, if
p=\infty
, then
d_{K}(x,y):=\frac{\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}}{\left\|q-y\right\|_{2}}
; If
q=\infty
, then
d_{K}(x,y):=\frac{\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}}{\left\|p-x\right\|_{2}}
.

•

If both
\{p,q\}
are at infinity, then
d_{K}(x,y):=0
.

It is worth noting that the above three scenarios can be summarized in an equivalent definition: For (possibly unbounded) convex set
K
, fix
x,y\in K
, the cross-ratio distance is defined by
d_{K}(x,y)=\underset{R\to\infty}{\lim}d_{K\cap\mathbb{B}(0,R)}(x,y)
. It is worth noting that for all
x\in\partial K
,
y\in K
and
y\neq x
, we have
d_{K}(x,y)=+\infty
. We just avoid this technical issue by requiring
K
to be an open subset of
n
in this paper. Our mixing time result can be extended to non-open
K
easily, because the Lebesgue measure of the boundary
\partial K
of a convex set
K
is
0
.

In theorem statements and proofs, we introduce a few properties of a local metric
G
, following
[
KV24
]
.

Definition 4

(
[
LLV20
,
KV24
]
)
.

A matrix function
G:K\to\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
is said to satisfy

•

Strong self-concordance (SSC) if
G
is positive-definite on
K
and

\left\|G(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{D}G(x)[h]G(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{F}\leq 2\left\|h\right\|_{G(x)},\quad\forall x\in K,h\in\real^{n}.

•

Lower trace self-concordance(LTSC) if
G
is positive-definite on
K
and

\displaystyle\trace\left\{G(x)^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{2}G(x)[h,h]\right\}\geq-\left\|h\right\|_{G(x)}^{2},\quad\forall x\in K,h\in\real^{n}.

We say it satisfies strongly lower trace self-concordant (SLTSC) if for any PSD matrix function
\overline{G}
on
K
it holds that

\trace\left\{\left(\overline{G}(x)+G(x)\right)^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{2}G(x)[h,h]\right\}\geq-\left\|h\right\|_{G(x)}^{2},\quad\forall x\in K,h\in\real^{n}.

•

Average self-concordance (ASC) if for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
r_{\epsilon}>0
such that for
r\leq r_{\epsilon}
,

\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(x,\frac{r^{2}}{d}G(x)^{-1})}\left(\left|\left\|z-x\right\|_{G(z)}^{2}-\left\|z-x\right\|_{G(x)}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{2\epsilon r^{2}}{n}\right)\geq 1-\epsilon.

We say it satisfies strongly average self-concordant (SASC) if for any
\epsilon>0
and any PSD matrix function
\overline{G}
on
K
it holds that

\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(x,\frac{r^{2}}{d}[G(x)+\overline{G}(x)]^{-1})}\left(\left|\left\|z-x\right\|_{G(z)}^{2}-\left\|z-x\right\|_{G(x)}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{2\epsilon r^{2}}{n}\right)\geq 1-\epsilon.

Note that we make the ASC definition stricter than it was in
[
KV24
]
, by requiring the difference in local norms to be bounded on both sides. This modification does not have a major impact on the results, because the verification of ASC is based on Taylor approximation as in
[
KV24
]
, which automatically ensures that both sides are controlled.

3.5
Practical Matters

Finally, since our main results rely on the existence of warm starts and focus on the mixing time analysis which ignores per-iteration cost, we devote the rest of this section to discuss per-iteration complexity and how to obtain a warm initialization.

Per-Iteration Complexity

In our main results (Theorem
1
,
2
and
3
), we assume that in each step, we can compute the local metric
G(x)
, its inverse
G(x)^{-1}
and its determinant
\det G(x)
exactly. The mixing times in Theorem
1
,
2
and
3
are given without detailing per-step complexity. Under a computational model where arithmetic operations
\{+,-,\times,\div,\sqrt{\quad}\}
over real numbers can be done exactly, the number of arithmetic operations needed for soft-threshold metric is
O(\max\left\{m,n\right\}n^{\omega-1})
. For regularized Dikin walk with regularized Lewis metric, we also need to compute the Lewis weights defined in Eq. (
9
), which has to be done approximately. The high-accuracy solver for Lewis-weights in
[
FLPS22
]
can be applied with complexity
\widetilde{O}(\max\left\{m,n\right\}n^{\omega-1})
. Detailed per-step complexity analysis is provided in Appendix
C.1
.

-Warm Start

and

) assume an

-warm initial distribution

. For the truncated target distribution in Eq. (

), finding a suitable
\mu_{0}
with small

3.6
Main Lemmas and Proof Outline

Now we provide the outline and the necessary lemmas for establishing mixing times of soft-threshold Dikin walk for sampling truncated logconcave distributions.

We bound the mixing time by finding a lower bound for the conductance of the transition kernel
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.91359pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{T}}
, which is the transition kernel
\mathcal{T}
after lazification, and the connection between the mixing time and conductance can be found in
[
LS93
]
. For the sake of completeness, we list it as Lemma
1
. Since the lazification
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.91359pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{T}}
at most shrinks the conductance by a factor of
2
, we only need to bound the conductance of the transition kernel
\mathcal{T}
before lazification.

To bound the conductance, we first bound the overlap between transition kernels
\mathcal{T}_{x}
and
\mathcal{T}_{y}
(measured in total-variation distance) for close enough (measured in the local metric
G(x)
)
x
and
y
in
K
. This transition overlap result is summarized as Lemma
2
.

Lemma
2
shows that two subsets of
K
with bad conductance
A_{1}^{\prime}
and
A_{2}^{\prime}
are far away in local metric
G
. To continue to get a lower bound of conductance, we need to show that
K\setminus(A_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime})
is also large if both
A_{1}^{\prime}
and
A_{2}^{\prime}
are large, so we need a certain type of isoperimetric inequality. Since we need to consider both the logconcave distribution and the polytope which it is truncated on, we design a mixed metric of Euclidean and cross-ratio distances, and the isoperimetric inequality under this mixed metric is listed as Lemma
3
.

We first introduce the important concepts of (
s
-)conductance of Markov chains. Given the transition kernel
\mathcal{T}
of a Markov chain, and
\Pi
be its stationary distribution, we define its conductance
\Phi
and
s
-conductance
\Phi_{s}
to be (we require
0<s<1/2
):

\Phi:=\underset{0<\Pi(A)\leq\frac{1}{2}}{\inf}\frac{\int_{A}{\mathcal{T}}_{u}(A^{c})\Pi(du)}{\Pi(A)},\quad\Phi_{s}:=\underset{s<\Pi(A)\leq\frac{1}{2}}{\inf}\frac{\int_{A}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A^{c})\Pi(du)}{\Pi(A)-s}.

(17)

Lemma 1

(
[
LS93
]
,
[
Lov99
]
)
.

For a reversible lazy Markov chain defined by the transition kernel
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
, let
\Pi
denote its unique stationary distribution and
\widetilde{\Phi}
,

denote its conductance,

\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\mu_{0})-\Pi\right\|_{TV}\leq\sqrt{M}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\Phi}^{2}\right)^{k}.

(18)

One can achieve a similar result using the notion of

-conductance:

\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\mu_{0})-\Pi\right\|_{TV}\leq Ms+M\left(1-\frac{\widetilde{\Phi}_{s}^{2}}{2}\right)^{k}\leq Ms+M\exp\left(-\frac{k\widetilde{\Phi}_{s}^{2}}{2}\right).

(19)

Since Lemma
1
is well-known and its proof can be found in

In controlling the transition overlap in Lemma

Lemma 3
.

Suppose

d^{\prime}(x,y)=\max\left\{d_{K}(x,y),\log(2)\sqrt{\alpha}\|x-y\|_{2}\right\}
and the corresponding distance between two sets

d^{\prime}(S_{1},S_{2}):=\underset{(x,y)\in S_{1}\times S_{2}}{\inf}d^{\prime}(x,y)
. Note that here

refers to the cross-ratio distance defined over

.

The reason we need Lemma
3
is that, to use Lemma

to bound the conductance, we need a suitable isoperimetric inequality for the local metric
G
. The conventional wisdom is to use the isoperimetric inequality for cross-ratio distance
d_{K}
over the polytope
K

[

,
KV24
]

[

\lambda I
simply can not be bounded by cross-ratio distance. In fact, for unbounded convex set

, we can always find some

such that the Euclidean distance
\beta\left\|y-x\right\|_{2}
is of higher order of magnitude than
d_{K}(x,y)
.

We defer the proof of Lemma
3
to Section
4.1
. Isoperimetric inequality under either metric (cross-ratio distance/ Euclidean distance) is already well-known, while their combination is not trivial in that the new distance

for two sets

may not be achieved when either

or

is achieved. In the proof, we use a localization argument proposed in
[

]
to reduce the

In order to extend the mixing time results to weakly logconcave distributions as in Theorem
2

K

d_{K}
in Definition

does not satisfy triangle inequality, thus
d_{K}
is not a metric. To circumvent this inconvenience, we use the Hilbert metric

over a convex set

d^{\mathcal{H}}_{K}(x,y):=\log\left(1+d_{K}(x,y)\right),

(21)

where

is the cross-ratio distance defined in the extended sense for possibily unbounded convex sets as in Definition

for close

by definition, thus changing

to

would not impact the application of the isoperimetric inequality to bound mixing times. Now we give the new isoperimetry for weakly logconcave distributions.

Lemma 4

Suppose
\Pi

\Pi
has a bounded covariance matrix

[

The proof of Lemma

Now we introduce stochastic localization, following the notation in

Based on the discussion in Appendix

If

. Let

be defined as

\rho(x):=\frac{\mathfrak{d}(x,S_{1})}{\mathfrak{d}(x,S_{2})+\mathfrak{d}(x,S_{1})},

where we define

. We bound

, the modulus of the gradient of

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\rho(x)-\rho(y)\right|}{\mathfrak{d}(x,y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{d}(x,y)}\frac{\left|\mathfrak{d}(y,S_{2})\left[\mathfrak{d}(x,S_{1})-\mathfrak{d}(y,S_{1})\right]+\mathfrak{d}(y,S_{1})\left[\mathfrak{d}(y,S_{2})-\mathfrak{d}(x,S_{2})\right]\right|}{\left[\mathfrak{d}(x,S_{2})+\mathfrak{d}(x,S_{1})\right]\left[\mathfrak{d}(y,S_{2})+\mathfrak{d}(y,S_{1})\right]}

As a result, the modulus of the gradient of

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\rho(x)\right|:=\underset{d(x,y)\to 0^{+}}{\lim\sup}\frac{\left|\rho(x)-\rho(y)\right|}{d(x,y)}\leq\frac{1}{\mathfrak{d}(S_{1},S_{2})},\forall x\in K.

\displaystyle\Pi(S_{3})

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\mathfrak{d}(S_{1},S_{2})\int_{S_{3}}\left|\nabla\rho(x)\right|\Pi(dx)

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{=}\mathfrak{d}(S_{1},S_{2})\int_{K}\left|\nabla\rho(x)\right|\Pi(dx)

\displaystyle\overset{(iii)}{\geq}\mathfrak{d}(S_{1},S_{2})\int_{0}^{1}\Pi^{+}\left\{x\in X|\rho(x)>t\right\}dt

\displaystyle\overset{(iv)}{\geq}\mathfrak{d}(S_{1},S_{2})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{6\max\left\{1,\psi_{n}\right\}}\Pi\left\{\rho(x)>t\right\}\cdot\Pi\left\{\rho(x)\leq t\right\}dt

\displaystyle\overset{(v)}{\geq}\frac{\mathfrak{d}(S_{1},S_{2})}{6\max\left\{1,\psi_{n}\right\}}\Pi(S_{1})\Pi(S_{2}).

Inequality

is ASC and LTSC.

Next we prove the metric inequality, it is obvious that
G(x)\succeq\beta I
. Since
H
is
\bar{\nu}
-symmetric with
\bar{\nu}=\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2})
. Thus, by Fact
4
, we have the following metric inequality

\displaystyle\min\left\{\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(x)}^{2},\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(y)}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\left\|y-x\right\|_{H(x)}^{2},\left\|y-x\right\|_{H(y)}^{2}\right\}\beta\left\|y-x\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot(\log m)^{c_{2}}\cdot n^{3/2}d_{K}(x,y)^{2}+\beta\left\|y-x\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
C>0
is some absolute constant. Moreover, since
H
is
\bar{\nu}
-symmetric and
G(x)\succeq H(x)
, we have
E(x,G(x),1)\subseteq E(x,H(x),1)\subseteq K
. Thus Corollary
3
is proved by inserting
C_{E}=\beta
and
C_{K}=(\log m)^{c_{2}}n^{3/2}
.

∎

5.2
Extension to Weakly Logconcave Distributions

In this section, we prove Theorem
2
and its corresponding Corollaries
4
and
5
. Actually, the only task in Section
5.2
is to prove Theorem
2
. This is because the required properties of the local metric
G
in Theorem
2
are the same as in Theorem
1
. Those properties of both soft-threshold metric (Definition
1
) and regularized Lewis metric (Definition
2
) are already proved in Section
5.1
. Thus Corollaries
4
and
5
can be derived directly from Theorem
2
. The proof follows similar conductance arguments from the proof of Theorem
1
in Section
5.1
, except that we use the isoperimetric inequality over weakly logconcave measures.

proof of Theorem
2
.

Set the step-size
r
indicated in Lemma
2
, we try to bound the conductance of the Markov chain: For any partition
K=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
, we will prove the following inequality:

\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du\geq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n\left(C_{K}+\eta{C_{E}}\right)}}\min\left\{\Pi(A_{1}),\Pi(A_{2})\right\},

(42)

where
C
is some absolute constant. Let
K=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
be any measurable partition, and we define two bad conductance subsets of
A_{1},A_{2}
to be:

A_{1}^{\prime}:=\left\{x\in A_{1}\bigg|\mathcal{T}_{x}(A_{2})<\frac{1}{10}\right\},\quad A_{2}^{\prime}:=\left\{x\in A_{2}\bigg|\mathcal{T}_{x}(A_{1})<\frac{1}{10}\right\}.

We can assume
\Pi(A_{1}^{\prime})>\frac{1}{2}\Pi(A_{1})
and
\Pi(A_{2}^{\prime})>\frac{1}{2}\Pi(A_{2})
, otherwise Eq. (
42
) can be proved the same way as in the proof of Theorem
1
. Then for any
x\in A_{1}^{\prime}
and
y\in A_{2}^{\prime}
, by definition of
A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime}
we have

\left\|\mathcal{T}_{x}-\mathcal{T}_{y}\right\|_{TV}\geq\mathcal{T}_{x}(A_{1})-\mathcal{T}_{y}(A_{1})=1-\mathcal{T}_{x}(A_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{y}(A_{1})\geq 1-\frac{1}{10}-\frac{1}{10}>\frac{4}{5}.

So by Lemma
2
and the symmetry between
x
and
y
we have

\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(x)}>\frac{r}{10\sqrt{n}},\quad\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(y)}>\frac{r}{10\sqrt{n}},

thus according to our assumption of
G
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}}{100n}

\displaystyle<C_{K}d_{K}(x,y)^{2}+C_{E}\left\|y-x\right\|_{2}^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle=C_{K}\left[e^{d^{\mathcal{H}}_{K}(x,y)}-1\right]^{2}+C_{E}\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\left[e^{\mathfrak{d}(x,y)}-1\right]^{2}+C_{E}\frac{{\eta}}{(\log 2)^{2}}\mathfrak{d}(x,y)^{2}.

Recall that we use the Hilbert metric
d^{\mathcal{H}}_{K}
instead of the cross-ratio distance
d_{K}
to apply the mixed isoperimetric inequality for weakly logconcave distributions (Lemma
4
). The last inequality holds since we define
\mathfrak{d}(x,y)=\max\left\{\frac{\log 2}{\sqrt{\eta}}\left\|y-x\right\|_{2},d^{\mathcal{H}}_{K}(x,y)\right\}
. Consider the case that
\mathfrak{d}(x,y)\leq 1
, then according to the fact
e^{x}\leq 1+2x
for
x\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}}{100n}<4C_{K}\mathfrak{d}(x,y)^{2}+C_{E}\frac{{\eta}}{(\log 2)^{2}}\mathfrak{d}(x,y)^{2}.

(44)

As a result, for all
x\in A_{1}^{\prime}
and
y\in A_{2}^{\prime}
we have

\mathfrak{d}(x,y)\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{r}{20\sqrt{n\left(C_{K}+C_{E}\eta\right)}}\right\}\geq\frac{r}{20\sqrt{n\left(C_{K}+C_{E}\eta\right)}},

where the last inequality holds since we assumed
C_{K}\geq 1
,
n\geq 1
and we can always set the step-size
r\leq 1
.
Now we are ready to control the ergodic flow from
A_{1}
to
A_{2}
, using the same argument in the proof of Theorem
1
(Eq. (
38
)) we have

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{x}(A_{2})\pi(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{20}\Pi(K\setminus(A_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime})).

(45)

Now we apply Lemma
4
over the target distribution
\Pi
, and we have

\Pi(K\setminus(A_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime}))\geq\frac{1}{6\max\left\{1,\psi_{n}\right\}}\mathfrak{d}(A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime})\Pi(A_{1}^{\prime})\Pi(A_{2}^{\prime})

(46)

Now insert Eq. (
46
) into Eq. (
45
), and we have

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{x}(A_{2})\pi(x)dx

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\frac{\Pi(A_{1}^{\prime})\Pi(A_{2}^{\prime})}{120\max\left\{1,\psi_{n}\right\}}\cdot\frac{r}{20\sqrt{n\left(C_{K}+C_{E}\eta\right)}}

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\geq}\frac{\min\left\{\Pi(A_{1}),\Pi(A_{2})\right\}}{8\cdot 120\max\left\{1,\psi_{n}\right\}}\cdot\frac{r}{20\sqrt{n\left(C_{K}+C_{E}\eta\right)}},

where inequality
(i)
follows by the lower bound for
\mathfrak{d}(A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime})
in Equation (
44
), and inequality
(ii)
holds because we assumed
\Pi(A_{i}^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{2}\Pi(A_{i})
for
i=1,2
and
\Pi(A_{1})+\Pi(A_{2})=1
.

Combining the situations, we have proved an lower bound on the conductance
\Phi
of the transition kernel
\mathcal{T}
. There exists a universal constant
c
, and since the step-size
r
is also an universal constant,
n\geq 1
and
C_{K}\geq 1
, we have

\Phi\geq\frac{c}{\max\left\{1,\psi_{n}\right\}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\left(C_{K}+C_{E}\eta\right)}}\geq\frac{c}{\psi_{n}\sqrt{n(C_{K}+C_{E}\eta)}},

(47)

where the last inequality holds since
\psi_{n}=\Omega(1)
. Since the conductance
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.91359pt}{0.43057pt}}{\Phi}
for the lazified transition kernel
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.91359pt}{0.43057pt}}{\mathcal{T}}
satisfies
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{\hskip 2.05pt\hskip 0.0pt\rule[8.12498pt]{4.91359pt}{0.43057pt}}{\Phi}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Phi
. According to Lemma
1
, there exists a universal constant
C>0
, for any error tolerance
\epsilon>0
and
M
-warm initial distribution
\mu_{0}
, the mixing time satisfies:

T_{\text{mix}}(\epsilon;\mu_{0})\leq\frac{8}{\Phi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\epsilon}\right)\leq C\psi_{n}^{2}\cdot n\left(C_{K}+C_{E}\eta\right)\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\epsilon}\right).

∎

5.3
Violated Constraints in a High Probability Ball

In Section
5.3
, we prove Theorem
3
. The idea is similar to prove the conductance lower bound for the Markov chain
\mathcal{T}
in Theorem
1
. The difference is that we needed to cut off the corner of
K
that is too close to the remaining
(m-\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta})
linear constraints. Since this corner has a low probability mass, it does not impact the mixing time much. We need the concept of
s
-conductance
\Phi_{s}
(see Eq. (
17
)) to enable the cutoff.

We first give the statement and the proof of Lemma
11
, which controls the ergodic flows from
A_{1}
to
A_{2}
.

Lemma 11
.

Assume our target distribution
\Pi
with density
\pi(x)\propto\mathbf{1}_{K}(x)e^{-f(x)}
with twice differentiable
f
to be both
\alpha
-convex and
\beta
-smooth as in Eq. (
2
). Let
\mathcal{T}
denotes the transition kernel in Eq. (
7
) defined using the soft-threshold metric in Definition
1
. Then there exists a step-size
r>0
such that for any partition
K=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
, for any
R>0
and
\delta>0
, we have

\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du\geq\frac{r}{32000\sqrt{\left[\kappa+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}}+\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}\right]n}}\min\left\{\Pi(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}),\Pi(A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})\right\},

(48)

where the definitions of
\mathcal{B}_{R}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}
can be found in Section
3.4
.

Proof.

For any
R>0
and
\delta>0
, recall the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}
, we denote
[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}]
to be the following subset of
[m]:=\left\{1,2,\dots,m\right\}
,

[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}]:=\left\{i|\,\exists\,x\in\mathcal{B}_{R}^{\delta}\text{ such that }a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i}\leq 0\right\}.

The distance of any point
x
to the
i
-th face
\left\{z|a_{i}^{\top}z-b_{i}=0\right\}
equals
\frac{\left|a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i}\right|}{\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}}
, thus for all
x\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
, we have

\frac{a_{i}a_{i}^{\top}}{(a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i})^{2}}\preceq\frac{\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{(a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i})^{2}}I_{n}\overset{(\text{I})}{\preceq}\frac{\alpha}{n\delta^{2}}I_{n}\text{ for }i\in[m]\setminus[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}],

where inequality (I) holds since for any
x\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
, the distance of
x
to any face
i\in[m]\setminus[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}]
is greater than
\delta\sqrt{\frac{n}{\alpha}}
. Otherwise, there exists
z
such that
\left\|z-x\right\|_{2}\leq\delta\sqrt{\frac{n}{\alpha}}
satisfying
a_{i}^{\top}z-b_{i}\leq 0
for some
i\in[m]\setminus[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}]
, which implies
z\notin\mathcal{B}_{R}^{\delta}
. Meanwhile, we also have
z\in\mathcal{B}_{R}^{\delta}
because
\left\|z-x^{\star}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|x-x^{\star}\right\|_{2}+\left\|x-z\right\|_{2}\leq(R+\delta)\sqrt{\frac{n}{\alpha}}
. This is a contradiction.

Now we can control the soft-threshold metric
G(x)
for all
x\in K\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\beta I+\sum_{i\in[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}]}\frac{a_{i}a_{i}^{\top}}{(a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i})^{2}}+\sum_{i\in[m]\setminus[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}]}\frac{a_{i}a_{i}^{\top}}{(a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle\preceq\left[\beta+\frac{\alpha(m-\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta})}{n\delta^{2}}\right]I+\sum_{i\in[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}]}\frac{a_{i}a_{i}^{\top}}{(a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i})^{2}}.

Then we can control the
\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(x)}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{B}_{R}\cap K
:

\displaystyle\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(x)}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(i)}}{\leq}\left[\beta+\frac{\alpha(m-\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta})}{n\delta^{2}}\right]\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}d_{K}^{2}(x,y)

(49)

\displaystyle\leq\left[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}(\log 2)^{2}}+\frac{\kappa}{(\log 2)^{2}}\right]d^{\prime}(x,y)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\left[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}}+{\kappa}\right]d^{\prime}(x,y)^{2},

where inequality
(i)
holds due to the
\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}
-symmetry of the logarithmic metric
H(x):=\sum_{i\in[\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}]}\frac{a_{i}a_{i}^{\top}}{(a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i})^{2}}
(see the proof of Lemma
10
in Section
5.1
).
d^{\prime}(x,y)
is the combination of Euclidean and cross-ratio distances defined in Lemma
3
.

As the routine for bounding conductance of a transition kernel, we consider the subsets of
A_{1},A_{2}
with bad conductance. We define:

A_{1}^{\prime}:=\left\{u\in A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}\bigg|\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})<\frac{1}{10}\right\},\quad A_{2}^{\prime}:=\left\{u\in A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}\bigg|\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{1})<\frac{1}{10}\right\}.

We first consider the case
\Pi(A_{i}^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Pi(A_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})
for
i=1
or
i=2
. Since
\Pi
is the stationary distribution of the transition kernel
\mathcal{T}
, we can assume
\Pi(A_{1}^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Pi(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})
without loss of generality. we have

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du

\displaystyle\geq\int_{A_{1}\setminus A_{1}^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du\overset{(i)}{\geq}\frac{1}{10}\Pi(A_{1}\setminus A_{1}^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{20}\Pi(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{20}\min\left\{\Pi(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}),\Pi(A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})\right\},

where inequality
(i)
holds because we assumed
\Pi(A_{1}^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Pi(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})
. So Eq. (
48
) is proved where we insert
r=10^{-5}
,
n\geq 1
and
\kappa\geq 1
.

Next we deal with the case
\Pi(A_{i}^{\prime})>\frac{1}{2}\Pi(A_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})
for both
i=1
and
i=2
. For any
x\in A_{1}^{\prime}
and
y\in A_{2}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{T}_{x}-\mathcal{T}_{y}\right\|_{TV}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{T}_{x}(A_{1})-\mathcal{T}_{y}(A_{1})

\displaystyle=1-\mathcal{T}_{x}(A_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{y}(A_{1})>\frac{4}{5},

According to the transition overlap we proved in Lemma
2
, and by symmetry between
x
and
y
, we have

\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(x)}>\frac{r}{100\sqrt{n}}\text{ and }\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(y)}>\frac{r}{100\sqrt{n}}.

Since we assumed
x,y\in\mathcal{B}_{R}\cap K
, Eq. (
49
) holds, thus we have

\displaystyle d^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left\|y-x\right\|_{G(x)}}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}}+\kappa}}\geq\frac{r}{200\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}}+\kappa}}.

(50)

Then the LHS of Eq. (
48
) can be bounded by:

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du+\int_{A_{2}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{1})\pi(u)du\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{A_{1}\setminus A_{1}^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du+\int_{A_{2}\setminus A_{2}^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{1})\pi(u)du\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{20}\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R}\setminus(A_{1}^{\prime}\cap A_{2}^{\prime})).

Now we define
\Pi_{R}(\cdot)
to be the probability measure
\Pi(\cdot)
constrained on
\mathcal{B}_{R}\cap K
. In other words,
\Pi_{R}(C):=\frac{\Pi(C)}{\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R}\cap K)}
for all Borel sets
C\subseteq K\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}
. And we define
d^{\prime}_{R}(x,y)
to be the mixed metric whose cross-ratio metric is (see definition in Lemma
3
) restricted on
\mathcal{B}_{R}\cap K
:

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{R}(x,y):=\max\left\{d_{K\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}}(x,y),\log 2\sqrt{\alpha}\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}\right\}.

where we changed the cross-ratio distance from over
K
to over
K\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}
. It is easy to verify that
d_{K\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}}(x,y)\geq d_{K}(x,y)
because
K\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}\subseteq K
, so we also have:
d^{\prime}_{R}(x,y)\geq d^{\prime}(x,y)
. Countinuing to lower bound the LHS of Eq. (
48
), we apply isoperimetric inequality (Lemma
3
) under the metric
d^{\prime}_{R}(x,y)
:

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{20}\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R}\setminus(A_{1}^{\prime}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}))=\frac{1}{20}\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R})\Pi_{R}(\mathcal{B}_{R}\setminus(A_{1}^{\prime}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\frac{1}{20}\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R})d_{R}^{\prime}(A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime})\Pi_{R}(A_{1}^{\prime})\Pi_{R}(A_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\geq}\frac{1}{20}\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R})d^{\prime}(A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime})\Pi_{R}(A_{1}^{\prime})\Pi_{R}(A_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\overset{(iii)}{\geq}\frac{1}{80\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R})}d^{\prime}(A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime})\Pi(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})\Pi(A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{80}d^{\prime}(A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime})\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R})\Pi_{R}(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})\Pi_{R}(A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})

\displaystyle\overset{(iv)}{\geq}\frac{1}{160}d^{\prime}(A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime})\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{R})\min\left\{\Pi_{R}(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}),\Pi_{R}(A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{160}d^{\prime}(A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime})\min\left\{\Pi(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}),\Pi(A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})\right\},

where inequality
(i)
is the application of our new isoperimetric inequality (Lemma
3
) over the constrained metric
d_{R}^{\prime}(x,y)
, inequality
(ii)
is due to our argument that
d^{\prime}(x,y)\leq d_{R}^{\prime}(x,y)
, inequality
(iii)
holds because of the assumptions
\Pi(A_{i}^{\prime})>\frac{1}{2}\Pi(A_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})
for
i=1,2
, and inequality
(iv)
is due to the fact that
\Pi_{R}(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})+\Pi_{R}(A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})=1
.

Now insert the upper bound of
d^{\prime}(x,y)
in Eq. (
49
), we have

\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du\geq\frac{r}{32000\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}}+\kappa}}\min\left\{\Pi(A_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R}),\Pi(A_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{R})\right\}.

∎

Now we can give the proof of Theorem
3
, where we apply Lemma
11
setting
R
to be
R_{o}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2M}\right)
, and
\delta>0
is still an arbitrary parameter that can be tuned by the user:

proof of Theorem
3
.

We set
\Upsilon:=R_{o}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2M}\right)
, then by definition of
R_{o}
(see Definition
5
), we have

\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{\Upsilon})\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{2M}.

For any partition
K=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
, we have
\Pi(A_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\Upsilon})\geq\Pi(A_{i})-\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{\Upsilon})
for
i=1,2
. Apply Lemma
11
where we set
R:=\Upsilon
, we have the result as:

\int_{A_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{u}(A_{2})\pi(u)du\geq\frac{r}{32000\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{\Upsilon}^{\delta}+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}}+\kappa}}\min\left\{\Pi(A_{1})-\frac{\epsilon}{2M},\Pi(A_{2})-\frac{\epsilon}{2M}\right\}.

We set
s:=\frac{\epsilon}{2M}
, then the
s
-conductance of our transition kernel
\mathcal{T}
before lazification satisfies:

\Phi_{s}\geq\frac{r}{32000\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{\Upsilon}^{\delta}+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}}+\kappa}}.

(51)

After lazification, the
s
-conductance under our new transition kernel
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{x}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{x}(\cdot)+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{T}_{x}(\cdot)
satisfies:
\widetilde{\Phi}_{s}=\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{s}
. Using the mixing time bound for lazy Markov chains in Eq. (
19
), we obtain

\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\mu_{0})-\Pi\right\|_{TV}\leq Ms+M\left(1-\frac{\widetilde{\Phi}_{s}^{2}}{2}\right)^{k}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+M\exp\left(-\frac{k\widetilde{\Phi}_{s}^{2}}{2}\right).

To ensure
\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\mu_{0})-\Pi\right\|_{TV}\leq\epsilon
, we only need to ensure
M\exp\left(-\frac{k\widetilde{\Phi}_{s}^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. That is,
k\geq\frac{2}{\widetilde{\Phi}_{s}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2M}{\epsilon}\right)
. Insert the lower bound of
\Phi_{s}
as in Eq. (
51
) and the fact that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{s}=\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{s}
, we only need to ensure:

k\geq\frac{8\times 32000^{2}}{r^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2M}{\epsilon}\right)\left(\kappa+\frac{m}{n\delta^{2}}+\mathcal{M}_{\Upsilon}^{\delta}\right)n,

(52)

where the step-size
r
is also an absolute constant, and the desired mixing time is proved. To prove that Eq. (
52
) still suffices for
\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\mu_{0})-\Pi\right\|_{TV}\leq\epsilon
to hold if we replace
\Upsilon
with
\widehat{\Upsilon}:=\widehat{R_{o}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2M}\right)
, we only need to prove
\mathcal{M}_{\Upsilon}^{\delta}\leq\mathcal{M}_{\widehat{\Upsilon}}^{\delta}
. This is easy since
\mathcal{M}_{R}^{\delta}
is clearly an increasing function in
R
, and we have
\Upsilon\leq\widehat{\Upsilon}
(see the concentration inequality in Eq. (
12
)).
∎

6
Discussions

This work opens several avenues for future extensions. In terms of warm initial distributions, although we proposed the uniform distribution on a certain ball in Appendix
C.2
, it still has one major limitation. The warmness parameter
M
scales exponentially with the dimension
n
, thus introducing an extra
n
-factor in the mixing times. To mitigate this, Gaussian cooling
[
CV18
]
emerges as one promising direction. This method constructs a sequence of distributions to sample from, each serving as a warm start for the next. For truncated sampling,
[
KV24
]
explores a Gaussian cooling procedure under a different framework than ours. Adapting Gaussian cooling techniques to our framework could significantly reduce the warmness dependency.

Moreover, Theorem
1
and
2
may be generalized to sample logconcave distributions truncated on convex bodies defined by nonlinear constraints. For instance, in the case of quadratic constraints,
[
KV24
]
constructs the barrier function, whose Hessian satisfies various self-concordance properties in Definition
4
. By leveraging this structure, our results may yield mixing time bounds for sampling log-concave distributions truncated on ellipsoids.

Finally, our regularized local metric could inspire new mixing time analyses for higher order algorithms such as geodesic walk and RHMC.

(Proposal Overlap)

(70)

where inequality

For the SSC, it is well-known that the unregularized logarithmic metric

Our proof used the fact that the unregularized logarithmic metric

is invertible

Lemma 15
.

If

are a symmetric matrices, and if
0\preceq A\preceq C
, then we have

\left\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}BA^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{F}\leq\left\|C^{\frac{1}{2}}BC^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{F}.

Proof.

Note that
\trace\left\{P^{\frac{1}{2}}BPBP^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}=\left\|P^{\frac{1}{2}}BP^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
for any symmetric PSD matrix
P
. So we only need to prove
\trace\left\{A^{\frac{1}{2}}BABA^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\leq\trace\left\{C^{\frac{1}{2}}BCBC^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
. This is easy to verify:

\displaystyle\trace\left\{A^{\frac{1}{2}}BABA^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\leq\trace\left\{A^{\frac{1}{2}}BCBA^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}=\trace\left\{C^{\frac{1}{2}}BABC^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\leq\trace\left\{C^{\frac{1}{2}}BCBC^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}.

•

\displaystyle d_{K}(x,y)
