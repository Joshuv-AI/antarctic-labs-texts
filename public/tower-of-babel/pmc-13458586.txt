Analysis of purines and pyrimidines across biospecimens: influence of storage temperature and duration
Authors: Rachel Wurth, Coleman Turgeon, Jean Lacey, Zinandré Stander, Dimitar Gavrilov, Patricia L. Hall, Dietrich Matern, Matthew Schultz, Silvia Tortorelli, Devin Oglesbee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purines and pyrimidines are key cellular components for the development, proliferation, and repair of cells and tissues, whose circulating levels are associated with various diseases. Biospecimen processing delays can significantly induce hypoxanthine and inosine levels, among other changes, in blood. We developed a liquid chromatography tandem mass-spectrometry method to quantify metabolites of the purine and pyrimidine pathways and investigated the impact of delayed sample processing on single donor plasma and dried blood spots (DBS). Plasma hypoxanthine increased above the normal reference range to an average 20-fold increase across individual donors when whole blood was kept refrigerated 64 h prior to processing. The same trend was observed in dried blood spots (DBS) prepared from refrigerated whole blood. Plasma inosine also increased above the reference range after a 48-hour delay in processing refrigerated whole blood. In contrast, hypoxanthine remained stable in DBS for up to 48 h, regardless of the card storage temperature. Therefore, DBS is a feasible alternative specimen type to measure hypoxanthine if frozen shipping, delayed centrifugation, or storage interval is a concern. Understanding the impact of specimen handling conditions is essential for successful biomarker discovery, especially when new biomarkers are to be introduced into the clinical laboratory.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54454-8.

Introduction

Purines and pyrimidines have several essential physiological functions1. Nucleotide bases derived from purines (e.g. adenine and guanine) and pyrimidines (e.g. cytosine, thymine, and uracil) are necessary for transcription and translation among other cellular processes. Various cofactors (i.e., nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, and flavin adenine dinucleotide) and other key analytes (adenine triphosphate (ATP) and uridine triphosphate) for energy metabolism are derived from both purines and pyrimidines2,3. Pyrimidines are also structural components of vitamins and contribute to the glycosylation of lipids and proteins, which are important in intracellular and extracellular signaling pathways4. Additionally, purines have important, specialized roles as neurotransmitters5. The diverse and essential functions of these compounds in cellular and tissue processes have resulted in many studies of these analytes across a variety of entities, and their quantification in plasma and urine is important for the diagnosis and monitoring of inborn errors of purine and pyrimidine metabolism as well as other related conditions6–8.

Purine metabolism consists of three pathways: de novo biosynthesis, salvage, and catabolic (Fig. 1)2,6. Pathogenic variants that increase or decrease the activity of certain enzymes in these pathways, result in abnormal accumulation of purines, pyrimidines, and related intermediates (Table 1). Xanthinuria type I was the first inborn error of purine metabolism to be described in 195410. Since this initial report, more than 30 different inborn errors of purine and pyrimidine metabolism have been described, with three new disorders described in the last fifteen years11–13. In addition to implications in inherited metabolic disease, variants in the DPYD gene, which encodes dihydropyridine dehydrogenase, the rate limiting enzyme in pyrimidine catabolism, can increase an individual’s risk of toxicity to chemotherapy agents 5-fluorouracil and capecitabine that are used to treat various cancers, including breast and colorectal cancer14,15.

There are several challenges to quantifying purine and pyrimidine analytes in human blood specimens. Purine nucleotide phosphorylase (PNP) is a cytoplasmic enzyme that will rapidly degrade deoxyinosine and deoxyguanosine in whole blood, therefore care must be taken when preparing calibrator and quality control materials in biospecimen matrix as required for diagnostic testing16. Interference by bacterial enzymes is an additional pre-analytical variable that has been shown to cause false positive and false negative results17,18. In terms of analytical methodology, liquid or gas chromatography tandem mass spectrometry is required to detect purine and pyrimidine analytes that are structurally similar and exhibit a broad concentration range in biological fluids19. The stability of these analytes can also differ under the same sample preparation method (i.e. dihydrouracil and dihydrothymidine can degrade to beta-ureidopropionic acid and beta-ureidoisobutyric acid under specific pH conditions), which can limit the number of analytes that can be evaluated in a single analytical method20. The lack of commercially available isotopically labeled internal standards has also hindered quantification of many purine and pyrimidine analytes20.

Additionally, several studies have shown purine analytes, hypoxanthine, inosine, and adenosine-monophosphate (AMP), are highly sensitive to pre-analytical sample handling. Specifically, a recent study by Nagana Gowda et al. found that delaying plasma separation from whole blood past 4-hours following sample collection led to a marked elevation in hypoxanthine and inosine concentrations21. However, the use of pooled donor whole blood did not permit the determination of inter-individual variability for these analytes. These observations have considerable implications in the field of untargeted metabolomics analysis, a popular, exploratory methodology used to discover novel metabolite biomarkers and develop hypotheses regarding metabolic mechanisms of disease. Both hypoxanthine and inosine have been reported previously as possible biomarkers for diseases including obstructive sleep apnea, ischemic brain edema, and breast cancer22–24. Given the proposed potential utility of these analytes as biomarkers for various conditions, and the important role that untargeted metabolomics findings may play in the development of clinical testing, thorough characterization of the impact of pre-analytical bias in the measurement of these analytes is warranted.

The purpose of this study was to determine the impact of pre-analytical variables (specimen collection tube type, lipemia, icterus, and delays in processing whole blood at different temperature conditions) on the accurate quantification and stability of most clinically relevant purine and pyrimidine metabolites, beyond hypoxanthine and inosine, in single donor biospecimens. Additionally, we investigated the impact of delayed processing on another blood-derived specimen type, dried blood spots (DBS). We hypothesize that hypoxanthine will be stable in DBS prepared immediately after sample collection and could be a feasible blood-derived sample type to investigate disease-related changes to this purine analyte18. These findings will provide a more comprehensive characterization of the stability of many clinically relevant purine and pyrimidine analytes in plasma, in contrast to clinical laboratory diagnostic profiles, as well as highlight the importance of specimen handling in research studies aiming to translate novel biomarkers into the clinical laboratory.

Methods

Materials

Isotopically labeled internal standards were purchased from several vendors (Supplemental Table S1). Bilirubin, Intralipid emulsion, ammonium acetate, and ammonium formate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Methanol was purchased from EDM chemicals. Acetonitrile (ACN) and formic acid (98–100% purity) were purchased from Fisher Scientific.

Study participants

Written informed consent was obtained from each study participant. The experimental protocols and collection of study specimens were approved by and carried out in accordance with the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB #15-005393). All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines.

Study specimens

To evaluate acceptable sample collection tube type, whole blood was collected from five non-fasting donors in vials containing EDTA and no additive to generate serum. The percent difference in the measured concentration of purine and pyrimidine analytes was calculated between the two specimen types.

The impact of lipemia on the quantification of purines and pyrimidines was determined by spiking three concentrations (low = 250 mg/dL, medium = 500 mg/dL, and high = 1000 mg/dL) of triglycerides from stabilized soybean oil lipid emulsion (Intralipid) into EDTA plasma.

To evaluate the impact of icterus on purine and pyrimidine quantification, three concentrations (low = 12.5 mg/dL, medium = 25 mg/dL, and high = 50 mg/dL) of bilirubin were spiked into EDTA plasma.

The impact of delayed sample processing on the quantification of purine and pyrimidine analytes was determined by collecting EDTA whole blood from three non-fasting donors and preparing specimens according to the stability study scheme (Fig. 2). All donors were evaluated individually without pooling specimens. Briefly, three tubes from each donor were mixed, and two Whatman Protein Saver 903 filter paper blood spot collection cards (PerkinElmer), one stored at room temperature and one stored at 4 °C after drying for 1 h, were created immediately after sample collection. To determine whether purine and pyrimidine metabolite concentrations change when whole blood processing is delayed, protein saver cards were created, and plasma was collected following centrifuging at 1800 rpm for 10 min for the reference (0 h) timepoint. Fourteen aliquots (1 mL) from each donor were made from the residual whole blood, and stored at 4 °C or room temperature, prior to creating protein saver cards or separating plasma for evaluation at the remaining time points.

Imprecision studies

Three concentration levels of analyte were spiked into EDTA plasma (low, medium, and high) and evaluated (n = 20 each) in a single batch to determine intra-assay percent coefficient of variation (CV). Inter-assay percent CV was evaluated by analyzing the three levels of analyte in EDTA plasma, each over 20 batches.

Preparation of plasma calibrators and controls

The calibrator solutions were prepared in 1mM ammonium acetate with the final concentration of analyte in each solution provided in Supplemental Table S1. The working standard solution was serially diluted (1:2) in artificial serum (SeraCare Life Sciences) to create a 6-point calibration curve. Two levels of quality control were spiked with working standard solution and stored at − 70 to − 80 °C prior to analysis.

Plasma sample preparation

Working internal standard (50 µL), 1mM ammonium acetate (100 µL) and 50 µL of plasma were added to a deep-well polypropylene plate with an ultra-centrifugal filter. Following centrifugation at 1400xg for 20 min, the total eluent was transferred to a deep well polypropylene plate with 200 µL of water. The prepared samples were covered with a disposable pierceable mat and 5 µL was subject to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Preparation of DBS calibrators and controls

Six DBS calibrators were created by spiking whole blood to achieve hypoxanthine concentrations of 0, 10, 50, 100, 250 and 500 µM onto a Whatman Protein Saver 903 filter paper blood spot collection card (PerkinElmer) and dried for a minimum of 1 h. Two DBS QC samples, one low (naïve EDTA whole blood) and one high (EDTA whole blood spiked with 50 µM hypoxanthine), were also created. The spotted cards were stored at − 70 to − 80 °C in a Zip-Loc bag with desiccator prior to analysis.

DBS sample preparation

A single 4.8 mm DBS punch was added to a polytetrafluoroethylene membrane filter plate (Sigma Aldrich) along with working internal standard (250 µL). Samples were rotated at room temperature for 2 h at 120 rpm, then centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 2 min to collect the filtrate in a polypropylene microtiter plate. Filtrate was dried with ultra-high purity nitrogen gas without heat and reconstituted with 200 µL of 80:20 ACN: H2O. The samples prepared were covered with a disposable pierceable mat and 1 µL was subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Plasma liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) procedure

Prepared sample (5 µL) was injected into a 100 µL sample loop using a Sciex PAL HTS-xt autosampler coupled to a Sciex API 5500 tandem mass spectrometer with a Shimadzu LC-20AB HPLC Pump and CBM-20 A Controller. Purine and pyrimidine analytes were chromatographically separated from each other by gradient elution using a HPLC C182 column (Phenomenex, Luna 5µ, 150 × 3.0 mm, #00F-4252-Y0). Mobile phases A (CLR water/ 1mM ammonium acetate) and B (1:1 1mM ammonium acetate: methanol) were delivered with a total flow of 0.400 mL/ min using the following program: hold at 0% B for 1 min, ramp to 100% B for 6 min, hold at 100% B for 3 min and return to 0% B for 4 min. The MS was operated in positive electrospray ionization mode to monitor select reaction monitoring (SRM) transitions for each analyte and isotopically labeled internal standard (Supplemental Table S1).

DBS LC-MS/MS procedure

Prepared sample (1 µL) was injected into a 20 µL sample loop using a CTC RSI 850 autosampler coupled to a Sciex API 5500 + tandem mass spectrometer with Shimadzu LC40-B xr pumps. Gradient elution using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) (Waters XBridge BEH Amide 2.5 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm column XP, #186006091). Mobile phases A (CLR water / 1% FA / 10 mM ammonium formate) and B (acetonitrile / 0.25% FA) were delivered with a total flow of 0.350 mL/ min using the following program: ramp from 92% B to 85% B over 7 min, then 85% B to 50% B over 4.5 min, then from 50% B to 30% B over 30 s, hold at 30% B for 30 s, then ramp to 92% B and hold for 2.5 min. The MS was operated in positive electrospray ionization mode to monitor transitions for hypoxanthine and 1,1,2,2-d4-Ethanolamine internal standard (Supplemental Table S1).

Comparison of sample collection tube type on plasma purine and pyrimidine measurement

Quantification of six analytes was found to be significantly different (percent difference more than +/− 20%) between serum and EDTA plasma collected from the same donor (Table 1). The concentrations of deoxyguanosine, guanosine, hypoxanthine, inosine, and N-carbamoyl-β-alanine were greater in serum compared to EDTA plasma. The most dramatic elevations were hypoxanthine and inosine with an average percent difference across all five donors of 108% and 174%, respectively. Values for adenine are not reported, since adenine was added as an additional reportable analyte after the initial development and validation of the method and after EDTA plasma was determined to be the preferred specimen type.

Lipemia and Icterus do not significantly interfere with purine and pyrimidine quantification in plasma

Nearly all analytes fell within the acceptance criteria of +/- 20% difference from the baseline specimen concentration at each level of triglycerides and bilirubin (Supplemental Table S2). The small number of measurements across all the analyte concentration and triglyceride and bilirubin levels that deviated beyond +/− 20% difference from baseline (n = 5 for lipemia and n = 5 icterus) likely did not indicate a pattern of interference in the quantification of these analytes, but rather the differences reflect imprecision of the method.

Hypoxanthine, inosine, and dihydrouracil plasma concentrations are affected by delayed processing of whole blood

As anticipated, we found a significant increase in the concentration of hypoxanthine in plasma when there was a delay in processing refrigerated whole blood (Fig. 3). When plasma was aliquoted within 4 h, there was no appreciable change in the concentration of hypoxanthine from the baseline value. Slight deviations were observed at the 8-hour time point (5 to 8 µM; <2 to 5 µM; 3 to 6 µM, baseline to 8 h for donors 1, 2, and 3, respectively), and beyond 8 h the concentration of hypoxanthine in plasma increased dramatically in a time-dependent manner. All donors would be flagged as abnormal with hypoxanthine elevated above the normal reference of our laboratory’s clinical diagnostic assay (≤ 15 µM, Fig. 3). In contrast to findings from Nagana Gowda et al., we did not observe any elevation in hypoxanthine in whole blood kept at room temperature21.

Plasma inosine levels from refrigerated whole blood remained congruent with baseline levels for all donors until 32 h (Fig. 4). After 32 h, two of the three donors had a time dependent increase in the plasma concentration of inosine, above the normal reference range (≤ 2 µM, Fig. 5). Interestingly, the maximum concentration of inosine observed in this study (9 µM) was far less than the concentrations reported previously21.

Hypoxanthine concentration in dried blood spots

Since hypoxanthine was altered the greatest when delaying the separation of plasma from refrigerated whole blood, we focused our analysis of DBS on the quantification of hypoxanthine. Additionally, the impact of delayed sample processing on the concentration of hypoxanthine in DBS has not been previously reported. When blood spots are prepared immediately after sample collection, the concentration of hypoxanthine does not considerably differ from baseline (measured at 1 h to allow sufficient time for the card to dry prior to analysis), regardless of whether the protein saver card is stored at 4 °C or room temperature (Fig. 5A). This suggests that DBS could be a more reliable specimen type to evaluate hypoxanthine when trying to eliminate any logistical challenges associated with shipping frozen specimens. However, if whole blood is kept at 4 °C for more than 8 h prior to preparing the blood spot, a considerable increase in hypoxanthine concentration is observed, which was expected given the results in plasma. Additionally, blood spots derived from whole blood kept at room temperature did not indicate significant change in the concentration of hypoxanthine over time, until the 64-hour time point where an increase in concentration was observed for all donors (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

Anomalous accumulation of certain purine (hypoxanthine, inosine, AMP) as well as related analytes (ATP) under cold storage has been documented over several decades21,37–40. The clinical diagnostic assay performed by our laboratory quantifies 18 purine and pyrimidine analytes in plasma, in addition to hypoxanthine and inosine, to monitor and diagnose inborn errors of purine and pyrimidine metabolism and other diseases. We found that nearly all these metabolites remained either below the limit of detection of the assay, or within the normal reference interval. However, it is possible that higher baseline concentrations of these analytes may have yielded different results. Since all donor plasma specimens evaluated in this study were within the normal reference interval for all quantified analytes at baseline, and we did not add higher concentrations of analyte into any of the specimens, we cannot determine how these pre-analytical conditions may affect higher concentrations, which may be an area for future investigation.

Aligning with previous findings, hypoxanthine increased significantly, above the normal reference interval of our clinical diagnostic assay, in plasma from whole blood kept refrigerated for longer than 8 h. Additionally, we report an expected similar trend of increasing hypoxanthine concentrations in DBS prepared from refrigerated whole blood. However, while plasma inosine increased above the normal reference interval when whole blood was kept refrigerated longer than 32 h, the deviations from baseline are less than a similar study conducted by Nagana Gowda et al.21. The difference may be attributed to the use of pooled plasma in previous studies, where it is possible that one outlying donor could impact the increase in the observed pooled concentration.

With such large elevations of hypoxanthine, we anticipated downstream metabolites of hypoxanthine (xanthine, and uric acid) to be increased as well. Circulating levels of XOR are quite low under normal physiological conditions in humans and may explain the accumulation of hypoxanthine without an increase in downstream metabolites41. Another possible explanation could be the inosine cycle (Fig. 1), which is a cyclic relationship between hypoxanthine, IMP, and inosine42,43. This cycle helps maintain intracellular PRPP, which is a critical precursor for de novo purine biosynthesis and essential for maintaining appropriate balance of the nucleotide pool, by hydrolyzing PRPP during the conversion of hypoxanthine to IMP by HPRT6,43. IMP is then converted to inosine by IMP-specific 5’-nuclotidase, before PNP forms hypoxanthine from inosine to start the cycle again. Furthermore, ATP is an activator of this cycle43. Since ATP is thought to accumulate in the plasma of whole blood due to the glycolytic activity of RBCs, this provides further support that the activity of the inosine cycle in whole blood that is not processed in a timely manner results in the elevation of plasma hypoxanthine and inosine21,44. However, it is unclear why the increase in concentration of these analytes is so much greater at refrigerated temperatures when compared to room temperature38. The hypoxic conditions that can occur in prolonged (> 14 day) refrigeration of packed red blood cell units for transfusion has been suggested to activate adenosine monophosphate deaminase, which drives purine deamination and the formation of hypoxanthine40. However, it is unclear whether this increase in enzyme activity would be expected under storage lengths of hours rather than days.

Dihydrouracil was the only other analyte besides hypoxanthine and inosine to have concentrations above the normal reference interval (Supplemental Figure S1). This elevation was only observed in plasma samples from whole blood stored at room temperatures for more than 32 h. DPD is responsible for catalyzing the reduction of uracil to dihydrouracil, and is highly active in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, which may drive the increase in dihydrouracil observed as the whole blood specimens are left at room temperature45.

It is important to note that the specimen handling conditions evaluated in this study (Fig. 2) would not be acceptable for daily clinical testing. The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines on sample handling and processing state that serum or plasma must be separated from the packed cell layer within 2 h of collection46. This is general guidance, and the appropriate specimen handling time may be even less than CLSI recommendations as it is dependent on the analytes evaluated and should be determined during the method development and validation process. Although not applicable to clinical quantification of purine and pyrimidine analytes, this study highlights the importance of considering the impact of specimen handling on research studies. The exact details on specimen processing are infrequently reported in the methods section of manuscripts, making it challenging to critically appraise biomarker discovery studies for any pre-analytical bias that may be introduced by delayed sample processing. At a minimum, describing whether controls and case specimens were processed and handled identically will provide greater confidence in the potential diagnostic or prognostic relevance of novel biomarkers. Ultimately, untargeted metabolomic analysis can be a powerful discovery and hypothesis generating tool, but conclusions derived from poorly handled specimens will not yield diagnostically relevant biomarkers and the consequences of this pre-analytical bias cannot be easily corrected. This observation is likely a contributing factor to the lack of reproducibility and successful translation of novel biomarkers discovered using metabolomics47.

In conclusion, we provide a comprehensive evaluation of the storage related changes to clinically relevant purine and pyrimidine metabolites in individual donor biospecimens using a laboratory developed clinical diagnostic assay. We found that EDTA plasma is preferred over serum for the accurate quantification of purines and pyrimidine metabolites. In agreement with previous findings, prompt separation of plasma from whole blood, and storage of plasma at -70 to -80 °C until analysis is necessary to avoid erroneous elevations of hypoxanthine and inosine. Furthermore, these findings highlight the importance of considering the impact of specimen handling and storage on research studies, especially if the goal is to discover novel biomarkers that will be used for future clinical diagnostic testing. Based on review of the literature, we propose low circulating levels of XOR and the inosine cycle as putative mechanisms for the elevation of hypoxanthine and inosine in refrigerated whole blood. Alternatively, we show that for the measurement of hypoxanthine, DBS is a viable specimen type if whole blood plasma cannot be processed in a timely manner and shipped frozen, given that the DBS card can be prepared immediately after sample collection or directly from a finger or heel-stick.
