Implicit Strategic Optimization: Rethinking Long-Horizon Decision-Making in Adversarial Poker Environments
Boyang Xia ∗, Weiyou Tian ∗, Qingnan Ren, Jiaqi Huang, Jie Xiao, Shuo Lu, Kai Wang, Lynn Ai, Eric Yang, Bill Shi †

Abstract
Training large language model (LLM) agents for adversarial games is often driven by episodic objectives such as win rate. In long-horizon settings, however, payoffs are shaped by latent strategic externalities that evolve over time, so myopic optimization and variation-based regret analyses can become vacuous even when the dynamics are predictable. To solve this problem, we introduce Implicit Strategic Optimization (ISO), a prediction-aware framework in which each agent forecasts the current strategic context and uses it to update its policy online. ISO combines a Strategic Reward Model (SRM) that estimates the long-run strategic value of actions with iso-grpo , a context-conditioned optimistic learning rule. We prove sublinear contextual regret and equilibrium convergence guarantees whose dominant terms scale with the number of context mispredictions; when prediction errors are bounded, our bounds recover the static-game rates obtained when strategic externalities are known. Experiments in 6-player No-Limit Texas Hold’em and competitive Pokémon show consistent improvements in long-term return over strong LLM and RL baselines, and graceful degradation under controlled prediction noise.

Introduction

Training large language model (LLM) agents for adversarial games is a natural testbed for sequential decision-making and strategic interaction, bridging classic online learning/game dynamics and modern agent training
[
17
,
10
]
. In repeated games, multiplicative-weights and regret-based dynamics yield uncoupled learning with guarantees, and empirical play converges to equilibrium notions such as (coarse) correlated equilibrium
[
16
,
15
,
19
,
6
]
. Optimistic and regularized variants further sharpen convergence in games and saddle-point problems, with refined guarantees toward Nash equilibrium in structured settings
[
29
,
33
,
14
,
1
]
.

Many strategic environments are
long-horizon
: action payoffs are mediated by latent strategic externalities (e.g., reputation, opponent adaptation, meta-game effects) that unfold over time and are not directly observable. Learning in
time-varying
games is often analyzed via dynamic regret/tracking bounds scaling with path-length or variation
[
36
,
2
,
39
,
12
,
18
,
5
]
. Yet variation can be large even when dynamics are highly predictable; optimistic analyses show that accurate forecasts can drastically reduce regret
[
29
,
12
]
. As in Example 1, existing bounds may still grow linearly with the horizon even under deterministic strategic externalities, where constant regret is achievable—suggesting that agents must
forecast future strategic returns
.

In parallel, large-scale systems achieve strong equilibrium-approximating behavior in long-horizon, imperfect-information games, including poker agents (DeepStack, Libratus, Pluribus)
[
26
,
8
]
, DeepNash for Stratego
[
28
]
, and population-based training such as AlphaStar
[
35
]
. LLM-centric agents further combine language models with planning/search/opponent modeling (e.g., Cicero, PokéChamp, LATS), and benchmarks like GameBench probe strategic reasoning across games
[
4
,
21
,
38
,
13
]
. Despite empirical success, most methods optimize episodic objectives or assume stationary payoffs, leaving limited guarantees that tie
prediction accuracy under evolving strategic externalities
to regret and equilibrium convergence.

Motivated by the central role of predictive models in economic and industrial decision-making
[
20
,
34
,
27
,
3
]
, we ask:

How does an agent’s ability to accurately predict future strategic returns affect long-run performance and equilibrium convergence in long-horizon games?

We take a
prediction-aware
perspective and introduce
Implicit Strategic Optimization
(ISO), which makes explicit what agents can forecast about latent strategic externalities and how such forecasts shape learning dynamics. Our main contributions are:

•

Prediction-aware game framework (ISO).

We formalize long-horizon games with a latent context driving strategic externalities: players receive private context predictions, play mixed strategies conditioned on predictions, and update both policies and predictors online. We define contextual regret benchmarks and context-conditioned equilibrium notions.

•

Algorithm and theory with explicit dependence on mispredictions.

We propose
iso-grpo
, a context-conditioned optimistic learning rule. Via a contextual Regret bounded by Variation in Utility (RVU) analysis, we show regret and equilibrium convergence scale with the number of context mispredictions; under bounded prediction error, we recover static-game rates as if strategic externalities were known.

•

Empirical validation in complex games.

We evaluate ISO/
iso-grpo
in 6-player No-Limit Texas Hold’em and competitive Pokémon, showing improved long-term strategic returns over strong LLM and RL baselines and graceful degradation under controlled prediction noise.

Related Works

2.1
GRPO for LLM Agents

Reinforcement learning (RL) is widely used to train and align LLM agents under outcome-based or verifiable rewards, with PPO
[
30
]
as a common baseline but requiring a learned critic at substantial LLM-scale cost. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
31
]
removes the critic by estimating advantages through group-relative reward comparisons, enabling scalable RL from verifiable rewards; subsequent work improves optimization/robustness in episodic regimes, including Token-Regulated GRPO
[
25
]
, Scaffolded GRPO
[
37
]
, and Training-Free GRPO
[
9
]
. Existing GRPO-based methods, however, typically target short-horizon objectives and do not explicitly model evolving strategic context in long-horizon competitive interactions.

2.2
Long-Horizon Adversarial Games and Strategic Learning for LLM Agents

Strategic learning in adversarial multi-agent settings spans self-play in perfect-information games
[
32
]
and equilibrium-seeking methods for imperfect-information domains such as poker, where RL and counterfactual regret minimization yield superhuman systems (e.g., Libratus, Pluribus)
[
7
,
8
]
. Population-based game-theoretic training, including Policy Space Response Oracles (PSRO), further addresses non-stationary opponents and is supported by platforms like OpenSpiel
[
23
,
22
]
, while recent work studies adversarial interaction directly among language agents
[
11
]
. Long-horizon LLM-agent training adds challenges from latent strategic externalities and opponent adaptation, motivating frameworks that combine contextual regret, optimism, and equilibrium convergence ideas
[
16
,
24
]
; our work contributes by extending GRPO toward prediction-aware strategic optimization in such long-horizon adversarial settings.

Assumption 3.1

(Finite context set)

Why finite

Assumption

This assumption is theoretically central because it converts long-horizon difficulty into

Linear-in-context loss model (analysis convenience).

For each player

\phi^{j}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

Given a joint mixed strategy

c^{j}(\mathbf{w},Z)\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\mathbf{w}}\!\left[\left\langle\phi^{j}(\mathbf{a}),\,Z\right\rangle\right].

(1)

This bilinear structure is not the core contribution; it is a clean modeling choice that
makes “predicting context” directly useful for “predicting losses,” and connects naturally
to optimistic online learning (Section

Assumption 3.2

(Bounded costs)
.

For any
j\in[J]

\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}

\big|\langle\phi^{j}(\mathbf{a}),Z\rangle\big|\leq 1

When
\mathbf{w}=w^{j}\otimes\mathbf{w}^{-j}

\Phi^{j}(\mathbf{w}^{-j})[:,k]\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}^{-j}\sim\mathbf{w}^{-j}}\!\left[\phi^{j}(a_{k}^{j},\mathbf{a}^{-j})\right].

(2)

Then the induced per-action loss vector for player

\ell^{j}(\mathbf{w}^{-j},Z)\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \Phi^{j}(\mathbf{w}^{-j})^{\top}Z\penalty\ \in\penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{K}.

(3)

(Linearization)
.

For any player

c^{j}(\mathbf{w},Z)\penalty\ =\penalty\ \left\langle w^{j},\penalty\ \ell^{j}(\mathbf{w}^{-j},Z)\right\rangle.

(4)

Interaction protocol with private context predictions.

The game unfolds over
T
rounds. At each round
t
:

1.

Nature selects a context
Z_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}
.

2.

Each player
j
observes a private

\hat{Z}_{t}^{j}\in\mathcal{Z}

(e.g., from an online multiclass predictor).

3.

Player
j

\hat{Z}_{t}^{j}

inducing joint play
\mathbf{w}_{t}
.

4.

After play, player
j
observes feedback sufficient to construct
\ell_{t}^{j}:=\ell^{j}(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{-j},Z_{t})
.

The only “long-horizon uncertainty” we explicitly model is the
hidden strategic regime

Z_{t}
;
learning difficulty will be tied to how well players can predict it.

Prediction errors.

We quantify prediction quality by the number of mispredictions

L_{T}^{j}\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{1}\!\left\{\hat{Z}_{t}^{j}\neq Z_{t}\right\}.

(5)

Prediction-aware regret benchmark (not dynamic regret).

Let
\mathscr{F}^{z}=\{t\in[T]:Z_{t}=z\}
be the subsequence of rounds at which context
z
is realized.
Define the
best per-context static comparator

\pi_{\star}^{j}(z)\in\arg\min_{w\in\Delta_{K}}\sum_{t\in\mathscr{F}^{z}}\left\langle w,\ell_{t}^{j}\right\rangle.

(6)

This comparator corresponds to an oracle that
perfectly anticipates the realized strategic regime

Z_{t}

and plays the best fixed action distribution for each regime.
Our contextual external regret is

\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{j}\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(\left\langle w_{t}^{j},\ell_{t}^{j}\right\rangle-\left\langle\pi_{\star}^{j}(Z_{t}),\ell_{t}^{j}\right\rangle\Big).

(7)

Crucially, (
7
) is
not
dynamic regret: the comparator does not track an arbitrary sequence,
but only switches when the underlying strategic regime switches.
Thus
\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{j}
measures the cost of
imperfect strategic anticipation
(imperfect prediction of
Z_{t}
),
rather than penalizing all non-stationarity via variation/path-length terms.

Where a “variation term” can still matter.

Our theory will separate (i) the cost of
mispredictions

L_{T}^{j}
and (ii) the difficulty of learning
within

each realized context subsequence
\mathscr{F}^{z}
.
Large cross-context shifts do not directly harm the bound unless they induce mispredictions.
However, if losses vary adversarially
within the same context

z
, the within-context term can still dominate;
this is precisely what the contextual RVU analysis in Section
4
controls.

4
Prediction-aware Learning

Goal and decomposition.

Given the model in Section
3
, the remaining uncertainty is the latent context
Z_{t}
.
Players observe only predictions
\hat{Z}_{t}^{j}
, which may be wrong.
Our goal is to design an
uncoupled
learning rule that
(i) guarantees no-regret with respect to the prediction-aware benchmark (
7
),
and (ii) makes the dependence on prediction quality explicit:

fewer context mistakes

\Rightarrow

easier long-horizon learning
.

4.1
Algorithmic principle: “one learner per context”

Why separate learners by context.

A central design choice is to maintain a separate online learner for each context
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Conceptually, each context defines a “subgame” (a regime-conditioned loss sequence).
If the prediction is correct, the player updates and plays in the correct subgame.
If the prediction is wrong, the player pays a localized “mistake cost” but does
not

pollute the learners associated with other contexts.
This realizes the intended factorization:long-horizon game
\penalty\ \approx\penalty\
(a small set of near-static problems) + (a small number of context mistakes).

4.2
Algorithm:
iso-grpo
(context-conditioned optimistic updates)

High-level description.

For each context
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, player
j

(e.g., optimistic MWU / GRPO-style update) producing a distribution
\rho_{t,z}^{j}\in\Delta_{K}

and an optimistic prediction object
\Psi_{t,z}^{j}

At round
t

\hat{Z}_{t}^{j}
,
and routes

.

(player

Instantiation.

The analysis below only requires a standard optimistic stability inequality (an RVU-type one-step bound).
Thus
iso-grpo
can be instantiated with optimistic MWU in theory,

4.3
Contextual RVU: only count variation
within
a context

Why standard variation bounds are pessimistic.

Classical dynamic-regret analyses bound regret by a path-length/variation term over the
entire
horizon.
This can be overly pessimistic in long-horizon strategic settings:
large regime changes can be
highly predictable
and should not be paid for if correctly anticipated.
Our key refinement is therefore:

compute variation only on the subsequences corresponding to the same realized context
.

Within-context variation proxy.

For each context
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, let
\mathscr{F}^{z}=\{t:Z_{t}=z\}
and let
t_{z}(1)<t_{z}(2)<\cdots

be its ordered elements.
Define the within-context variation (one convenient proxy) as

\mathsf{Var}_{T}^{j}(z)\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum_{r\geq 2}\left\|\ell_{t_{z}(r)}^{j}-\ell_{t_{z}(r-1)}^{j}\right\|_{\infty}.

(8)

This term ignores cross-context shifts entirely; it measures only how adversarially losses evolve
inside

the same strategic regime.

.

–
3.2
.

\eta\in(0,1]
and an optimistic update rule

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{j}\penalty\ \leq\penalty

(9)

Interpretation of (
9
).

Term (B) is the key: regime shifts do not directly appear, only the number of times the player routes play using the wrong regime.
Thus:
predictable long-horizon (small

)

Term (C) is the

This cleanly answers the “corner case” concern: prediction errors dominate when within-context sequences are stable/predictable,
but an adversary can still force difficulty by making losses vary rapidly without changing the context label.

Choosing
\eta

A standard choice is
\eta\asymp\sqrt{\frac{m\log K+L_{T}^{j}}{\sum_{z}\mathsf{Var}_{T}^{j}(z)+1}}
,
yielding sublinear regret whenever
L_{T}^{j}=o(T)
and the within-context variation grows sublinearly.

4.4
Equilibrium convergence consequence

From regret to equilibrium (coarse correlated equilibrium).

When every player achieves sublinear contextual regret (
7
),
the empirical distribution of play converges to an approximate coarse correlated equilibrium (CCE)
in the standard way for uncoupled no-regret dynamics.
Concretely, letting
\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{w}_{t}
denote the time-average joint distribution,
the CCE violation scales with the average regret.

Corollary 4.2

(Approximate CCE with prediction dependence)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
4.1
, if all players
j\in[J]

iso-grpo
, then

\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{T}
is an
\varepsilon_{T}
-approximate CCE with

\varepsilon_{T}\penalty\ \leq\penalty\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{j},

(10)

and
\varepsilon_{T}\to 0
whenever each
\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{j}=o(T)
.
In particular, via (
9

the cumulative mistake counts

and the within-context terms

5
Experiments

5.1
Experimental Setup

Tasks and environments.

We evaluate
Implicit Strategic Optimization
(ISO) in two long-horizon games:
(i)
6-player No-Limit Texas Hold’em (NLHE)
in a custom environment with heterogeneous agents (LLMs, RL, and rule-based bots). We use standard rules with
100BB
stacks and blinds
0.5BB/1BB
. Actions are {
fold, check, call, bet, raise
} with a continuous sizing component; for stability and reproducibility, we discretize bet/raise sizes into a small set of common sizes (e.g., pot fractions and all-in) while retaining strategic richness.
(ii)
competitive Pokémon (Gen 1 OU)
via Pokémon Showdown, using a fixed ladder-consistent ruleset (species clause) and a diverse opponent pool to reflect metagame uncertainty.

Observation and action interface.

For LLM agents, we use a structured-text state encoding: (i) public state (street, pot, betting history, stacks), (ii) private information (hole cards; team/HP), and (iii) a constrained schema listing only legal actions and admissible moves. This standardizes prompts and makes comparisons across agents consistent.

Training pipeline.

ISO-LLM follows three stages.
SFT:
we fine-tune
qwen2.5-0.5b
on curated expert gameplay (NLHE hand histories and solver-labeled actions at key subgames such as river; Pokémon high-ELO replays and standard OU archetypes).

SRM:
we train a strategic reward model to predict
long-horizon strategic value
from trajectories, using delayed-return supervision so that locally suboptimal actions can be assigned high strategic value.

GRPO:
starting from the SFT policy, we apply GRPO using SRM as the reward signal to optimize long-horizon value under diverse opponent interactions.

Baselines.

We compare ISO-LLM with SFT-LLM, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama-3.1-70B, PPO, and CFR.

Metrics and diagnostics.

We report Long-Term Return (LTR), Win Rate, Exploitability, and Strategic Sacrifice Rate (SSR). To localize gains, we additionally measure: (i) street-wise value decomposition, (ii) pot-size conditioned EV, (iii) negative-immediate / positive-long-horizon action rates (NLHE “sacrifice”), and (iv) SRM calibration (rank correlation between SRM scores and realized long-horizon outcomes).

Evaluation protocol.

Unless stated otherwise, NLHE results average over
10,000
hands with standard errors across multiple seeds and seating permutations. For Pokémon, Elo is computed from large match sets against a mixed opponent pool and reported after rating convergence. Full details are in Appendix
C
.

Conclusion

Recent progress on uncoupled learning for long-horizon games enables principled modeling of non-stationary strategic interaction, but existing analyses largely treat non-stationarity as purely adversarial and ignore agents’ ability to
predict
evolving strategic externalities. We introduced
Implicit Strategic Optimization
(ISO), a prediction-aware framework that factorizes long-horizon difficulty into (i) forecasting a latent strategic context and (ii) learning conditioned on that context. We proposed
iso-grpo
, a context-conditioned optimistic update rule that routes actions by predicted context and updates by realized context, yielding regret and equilibrium-convergence guarantees whose dominant degradation scales with context mispredictions. Empirically, ISO improves long-term strategic return in 6-player No-Limit Texas Hold’em and competitive Pokémon, highlighting a practical decoupling between episodic win rate and long-run strategic value.

Looking ahead, three directions are especially promising: (1) weaken feedback assumptions (e.g., bandit/partial feedback) while retaining prediction-dependent guarantees; (2) extend ISO beyond finite context sets to continuous or large context spaces; and (3) study strategic settings where players’ predictions are jointly inferred or socially coupled, and design learning rules that account for this interaction.

Impact Statement

This work aims to advance machine learning by developing prediction-aware learning mechanisms for long-horizon, multi-agent decision-making. By explicitly modeling and accounting for prediction uncertainty, the proposed system can support more robust reasoning and adaptation in complex decision environments where future conditions or strategic contexts must be estimated rather than observed.

Such capabilities may benefit the design of AI systems for planning, coordination, and decision support in dynamic and uncertain settings. We do not anticipate immediate negative societal impacts arising specifically from this work; as with general-purpose learning systems, responsible deployment and domain-specific oversight remain important considerations.

A.2
Proof sketch for Proposition
4.1
(Contextual RVU)

We outline a sufficient argument based on a standard optimistic MWU/RVU inequality;
the same structure applies to other optimistic updates.

Step 1: Decompose regret by realized contexts.

Let
\mathscr{F}^{z}=\{t:Z_{t}=z\}
. Then

\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{j}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{t\in\mathscr{F}^{z}}\Big(\langle w_{t}^{j},\ell_{t}^{j}\rangle-\langle\pi_{\star}^{j}(z),\ell_{t}^{j}\rangle\Big).

(14)

For each
z
, compare the realized actions
w_{t}^{j}
to the best fixed comparator for that same subsequence.

Step 2: Separate “correct routing” rounds from mispredicted rounds.

Let
\mathscr{G}^{j}:=\{t:\hat{Z}_{t}^{j}=Z_{t}\}
be correctly predicted rounds and
\mathscr{B}^{j}:=\{t:\hat{Z}_{t}^{j}\neq Z_{t}\}
be mistakes.
Then
|\mathscr{B}^{j}|=L_{T}^{j}
.
On correct rounds, play is produced by the learner corresponding to the realized context; on mistake rounds, play comes from an incorrect learner.

Step 3: RVU bound within each context subsequence.

Fix
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and restrict attention to rounds in
\mathscr{F}^{z}
.
On those rounds where the player routes correctly (
t\in\mathscr{F}^{z}\cap\mathscr{G}^{j}
),
the per-context optimistic update (e.g., optimistic MWU) yields a standard RVU inequality of the form

\sum_{t\in\mathscr{F}^{z}\cap\mathscr{G}^{j}}\Big(\langle w_{t}^{j},\ell_{t}^{j}\rangle-\langle\pi_{\star}^{j}(z),\ell_{t}^{j}\rangle\Big)\leq\frac{\log K}{\eta}+\eta\,\mathsf{Var}_{T}^{j}(z),

(15)

where
\mathsf{Var}_{T}^{j}(z)
is any admissible within-context variation proxy; (
8
) is one such choice.

Step 4: Pay a uniform penalty for mistake rounds.

On mistake rounds
t\in\mathscr{B}^{j}
, we upper bound the instantaneous regret crudely using bounded losses.
By Assumption
3.2
, each coordinate of
\ell_{t}^{j}
lies in
[-1,1]
, hence

\langle w_{t}^{j},\ell_{t}^{j}\rangle-\langle\pi_{\star}^{j}(Z_{t}),\ell_{t}^{j}\rangle\leq 2.

(16)

A standard way to integrate this into an RVU-style bound for the routed learner is to charge an additional

\frac{2}{\eta}
per mistake (equivalently, to treat each mistake as breaking the potential decrease argument).
Summing over
L_{T}^{j}
mistakes yields the
\frac{2}{\eta}L_{T}^{j}
term in (
9
).

Step 5: Sum over contexts.

Summing the per-context bounds over
z\in\mathcal{Z}
gives

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log K}{\eta}\,m+\frac{2}{\eta}\,L_{T}^{j}+\eta\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathsf{Var}_{T}^{j}(z),

(17)

which matches (
9
).

Appendix B
Extension to Infinite Context Sets

Assumption B.1

(Additional regularity for infinite context sets)
.

We include a short derivation to make the argument self-contained. The proof is
RVU-style, with the only substantive change being that all quantities are
conditioned on the realized context sequence.

Notation.

At round
t
, the learner observes
c_{t}\in\mathcal{C}
and samples an action

a_{t}\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid c_{t})
. Let
r_{t}(a,c)\in[0,1]
be the reward and

a_{t}^{\star}(c)\in\arg\max_{a}r_{t}(a,c)
be an optimal action under context
c
.
Define the (random) instantaneous regret

\Delta_{t}\;:=\;r_{t}(a_{t}^{\star}(c_{t}),c_{t})-r_{t}(a_{t},c_{t}),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{T}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}].

(18)

We write
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]
for conditional expectation w.r.t. the history

\mathcal{H}_{t-1}:=(c_{1},a_{1},r_{1},\ldots,c_{t-1},a_{t-1},r_{t-1})
.

A contextual RVU decomposition.

Let
\hat{r}_{t}(a,c)
be the model prediction used by the algorithm and define
the
prediction error
(under the realized context) as

\varepsilon_{t}\;:=\;\hat{r}_{t}(a_{t},c_{t})-r_{t}(a_{t},c_{t}),\qquad\varepsilon_{t}^{\star}\;:=\;\hat{r}_{t}(a_{t}^{\star}(c_{t}),c_{t})-r_{t}(a_{t}^{\star}(c_{t}),c_{t}).

(19)

Then by adding and subtracting
\hat{r}_{t}(\cdot,c_{t})
we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\bigl[\hat{r}_{t}(a_{t}^{\star}(c_{t}),c_{t})-\hat{r}_{t}(a_{t},c_{t})\bigr]}_{\text{(I) optimistic gap}}\;+\;\underbrace{\bigl[\varepsilon_{t}-\varepsilon_{t}^{\star}\bigr]}_{\text{(II) prediction error}}.

(20)

Taking
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]
on both sides yields the same decomposition in
expectation, now conditioned on
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
(and hence on
c_{t}
).

Bounding the prediction error term.

Assume the algorithm maintains a context-conditional error bound

|\varepsilon_{t}|\leq\beta_{t}(c_{t}),\qquad|\varepsilon_{t}^{\star}|\leq\beta_{t}(c_{t}),

(21)

for some predictable radius
\beta_{t}(\cdot)
(e.g., derived from concentration
or a calibrated score model). Then

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\varepsilon_{t}-\varepsilon_{t}^{\star}]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\varepsilon_{t}|+|\varepsilon_{t}^{\star}|]\;\leq\;2\,\beta_{t}(c_{t}).

(22)

Bounding the optimistic gap term.

The RVU argument controls term (I) via a stability / one-step improvement
inequality induced by the update rule. Concretely, suppose the policy update
satisfies, for each realized context
c_{t}
,

\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\hat{r}_{t}(a_{t}^{\star}(c_{t}),c_{t})-\hat{r}_{t}(a_{t},c_{t})\right]\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\eta}\Bigl(\Phi(\pi_{t},\!c_{t})-\Phi(\pi_{t+1},\!c_{t})\Bigr)\;+\;\eta\,\mathbb{V}_{t}(c_{t}),

(23)

where
\eta>0
is a stepsize,
\Phi(\cdot,c)
is a context-conditional potential
(e.g., negative entropy / Bregman divergence), and
\mathbb{V}_{t}(c)
is the
context-conditional variance proxy that appears in RVU-style bounds.

Putting everything together.

Combining (
20
), (
22
), and (
23
)
and summing over
t=1,\ldots,T
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Phi(\pi_{t},c_{t})-\Phi(\pi_{t+1},c_{t})\Bigr]\;+\;\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{V}_{t}(c_{t})\bigr]\;+\;2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\beta_{t}(c_{t})\bigr].

(24)

The potential term telescopes under standard regularity conditions on

\Phi(\cdot,c)
, giving an
O(\Phi/\eta)
contribution. Choosing
\eta
in the
usual RVU manner and substituting the bound on
\sum_{t}\mathbb{V}_{t}(c_{t})
yields
the claimed regret rate in Proposition 5 (up to constants), with an additional
additive term governed by the cumulative context-dependent prediction radius

\sum_{t}\beta_{t}(c_{t})
.

We briefly outline how the preceding contextual analysis can be extended when

\mathcal{C}
is infinite (e.g., continuous).

Why the finite-context argument breaks.

In the finite case, one can often take a union bound over all contexts to make
(
21
) hold uniformly. When
\mathcal{C}
is infinite, such a
uniform bound is generally impossible without additional structure.

Covering-number approach.

Assume
(\mathcal{C},d)
is a metric space and the prediction model is
Lipschitz in
c
:

|\hat{r}_{t}(a,c)-\hat{r}_{t}(a,c^{\prime})|\leq L\,d(c,c^{\prime}),\qquad|r_{t}(a,c)-r_{t}(a,c^{\prime})|\leq L_{r}\,d(c,c^{\prime}).

(25)

Let
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},d,\epsilon)
be the covering number. One can prove
a high-probability version of (
21
) on an
\epsilon
-net and then
transfer it to all contexts using Lipschitzness, leading to an error radius of
the form

\beta_{t}(c)\;\lesssim\;\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},d,\epsilon)+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{t}}}\;+\;(L+L_{r})\epsilon,

(26)

where
n_{t}
is an effective sample size (or information gain term, depending on
the estimator). Plugging this
\beta_{t}(\cdot)
into
(
24
) yields regret bounds with an additional complexity term
depending on
\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},d,\epsilon)
.

Function approximation view.

Alternatively, one may model the context dependence via a parametric class

\hat{r}_{t}(a,c)=\langle\theta_{t},\phi(a,c)\rangle
(linear) or a kernel / neural
predictor, replacing covering numbers with an effective dimension (e.g.,
elliptical potential / information gain). The resulting regret bound takes the
same form as (
24
), with
\sum_{t}\beta_{t}(c_{t})
controlled by
the complexity of the function class.

Takeaway.

In infinite-context settings, the analysis typically reduces to controlling the context-conditional prediction radius
\beta_{t}(c)
via capacity measures such as covering numbers or effective dimension; the rest of the RVU-style argument remains unchanged.

Appendix F
Environment

F.1
LLM Poker Environment

We constructed a multi-agent poker environment, and the code will be released upon acceptance. We evaluate our method in the heterogeneous multi-agent poker environment designed for long-horizon decision-making under imperfect information.
The environment supports mixed populations of agents, including rule-based policies, conventional RL agents, and LLM-based agents, all interacting through a unified action interface.

Figure 7
:
Overview of the LLM Poker environment. The environment supports heterogeneous agents, a unified action gateway, and memory mechanisms for long-horizon strategic interaction.

At the beginning of each hand, a game configuration specifies the number of players, blind structure, stack sizes, and betting rules.
Players proceed through standard poker stages (pre-flop, flop, turn, river), with action legality enforced by the environment.

F.2
Observations, Actions, and Memory

Each agent observes its private cards, public cards, pot size, stack sizes, player positions, and the full betting history.
Actions follow standard no-limit poker operations (check, call, bet, raise, fold), with bet sizing constrained by game rules.

To support long-horizon analysis, the environment maintains both within-hand memory (action history and intermediate states) and cross-hand memory (aggregated behavioral statistics).
For prediction-aware evaluation, we additionally log a discrete context label
Z_{t}
at each decision point, which is not revealed to agents before acting but is used to measure prediction accuracy and misprediction counts in our experiments.
