A review of artificial intelligence in healthcare supply chains: untapped potential?
Authors: Oscar Espinosa, Sergio Basto, Angélica Ordóñez, Martha-Liliana Arias, Dino Sepúlveda, Mahmood F Bhutta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSupply chain management (SCM) is a central function of operational strategy and provides assurance of continuous delivery of services and products across different industries. While Artificial Intelligence (AI) has transformed SCM in other industries through predictive analytics, its application within healthcare remains poorly defined. We conducted a systematic review to analyse the extent of AI adoption, the specific methodologies employed, and the empirical evidence of its impact on healthcare SCM.MethodsWe performed a systematic review following Cochrane guidelines across MEDLINE (PubMed), LILACS, and SciELO databases up to May 2024. We screened for studies describing AI applications in healthcare SCM. Because of the heterogeneity of the included studies, we applied a narrative synthesis framework. Quality of reporting and evidence maturity were assessed using the STROBE tool. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024528856).FindingsFrom 8,697 initial records, only 13 met the inclusion criteria. The narrative synthesis revealed a significant conceptual gap: most “AI” applications (N = 6) relied on theoretical fuzzy multi-criteria decision making (MCDM) and optimisation logic, rather than data-driven machine learning techniques common in other sectors. The literature is concentrated on supplier selection (N = 4) and logistics simulation. Crucially, none of the studies reported application in real-world settings. STROBE assessment indicated a low maturity of evidence, with consistent lack of reporting on bias and external validity.DiscussionThe integration of AI in healthcare SCM is in a theoretical stage. There is a marked disconnect between the computational potential of AI and its actual deployment. Current research is dominated by mathematical simulations of supplier selection rather than operational implementation. The lack of empirical evidence and the reliance on theoretical models highlight a significant “untapped potential” that requires a shift from simulation to real-world validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14559-2.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Healthcare, Supply chains, Systematic review

Introduction

Countries share a common agenda of improving and strengthening health systems to achieve Universal Health Coverage (UHC) [1]. UHC relies on three major axes: a population covered by public or private insurance (pooled funds), financial protection of this population for receiving health interventions, and a list of health services covered by those funds [2]. Health systems should aspire to be efficient, equitable, and sustainable for achieving UHC [3, 4], and to achieve this, operational efficiency is essential [5, 6].

Supply chain management (SCM) is defined as the activities through which decision-makers in the value chain plan and manage the process of procurement, conversion and delivery of goods and services to clients upstream and downstream of the operational unit, integrating supply and demand management within and across companies [7]. Some authors include activities normally associated with marketing and production within the scope of SCM, and demand and sales forecasting, although inclusion of these is contested in the literature [7, 8]. SCM is viewed as a central function of operational strategy and assurance of continuous delivery of services and products across different industries, complementing manufacturing chains or service provision chains [9].

Service industries pose specific obstacles for the implementation of best SCM practices. Despite services becoming a driving force in the world economy, the specificities of intangibility and heterogeneity of the product, simultaneity of consumption, and perishability make many usual SCM practices (most of them developed specifically for the production of tangible goods) not directly applicable to the provision of services [9]. In the case of health, these challenges can be even more pronounced: the provision of health services requires high diversity of pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical medical supplies; provision is heterogeneous with interventions ranging from public health measures (e.g. universal vaccination against SARS-CoV-2) to equipment required for procedures only performed at centralised specialist hospitals, and procurement may be undertaken at individual, institutional, regional or national level according to the type of product and the underlying structure for healthcare provision in a particular country. Finally, the sector has exposure to public opinion, and so can be subject to political controversy [10, 11].

SCM has been shown to be a cardinal necessity in the health sector in different areas: for example, the supply chain for personal protective equipment (PPE), vaccines and essential medicines was proven critical during the recent COVID-19 pandemic in many countries [12–14]. Methods of SCM are not only relevant for optimising supplies to the healthcare industry, but also for helping health systems manage entire care pathways, as in the case of rare diseases [15] or home-based healthcare [16].

Despite the relevance of good SCM practices in health service provision, there are barriers to achieving implementation compared with other industries. Optimal implementation of SCM practice is anchored in principles of sharing information, risks and benefits [17], which may be especially hard to address in the health system context, where there is a range of certainty related to intervention effectiveness and risks, and confidential financial arrangements [18]. Moreover, the final user (patient) although part of service production, is not directly involved in the procurement or selection of production factors, and most purchases are conducted at scale by providers. In particular, key differences in the deployment of commercial vs. healthcare SCM practices include a core emphasis on effectiveness rather than efficiency, as well as on the local provision of supplies, with limited implementation of sourcing strategies.

Various approaches have been exploited for improving SCM in different industries, and algorithms and mathematical optimisation tools are common, particularly in productive industries. Recently, novel methods of Artificial Intelligence (AI) have been adopted for SCM, and the characteristics and dependence on interoperability, business analytics, and transmission of information make SCM particularly suitable to adoption of AI methods.

The scope of AI is broad and, although theoretically conceptualised since the mid-20th century, many of the methods cover what has in recent years been termed machine learning and deep learning [19, 20], including artificial reasoning, pattern identification, and inference techniques for solving problems through optimisation [21]. In this respect, application of AI in SCM seems worthwhile, as it can for example help in the design and development of supply chain networks, supplier selection, and inventory, workforce and demand planning [8].

AI has been progressively introduced into SCM, and there is increasing literature published on this topic [22–24]. AI has been successfully applied to SCM in areas such as inventory management, algorithms for optimising parcel delivery, production chain maintenance in agricultural and food processing industries, and field service operations [25]. In addition, supply chain risk assessment has been identified as a domain with significant potential for enhancement through AI algorithms [26, 27].

Health systems could benefit from use of AI in their process of service design, logistics, provision and delivery to catalyse adoption of best SCM practices, resulting in more equitable health systems. This is especially relevant in the case of Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), which often operate with fewer resources for the deployment of best SCM practices and delivery of health. Despite the existence of scattered initiatives on application of AI for SCM in health, there has been no review of those experiences, nor attempts to synthesise results of such use.

While the theoretical potential of AI in general supply chains is well-documented, a clear divergence exists in the healthcare sector between proposed computational models and their actual deployment. The term AI in this context comprises specific sub-fields relevant to operations research, including machine learning for demand forecasting, natural language processing for procurement contract analysis, and deep learning algorithms for complex logistics optimisation. However, the extent to which these specific techniques have transitioned from mathematical formulation to warehouse or health facility implementation remains unclear.

Therefore, this review addresses a critical gap in the literature: the lack of empirical evidence regarding the real-world operationalisation of AI in healthcare SCM. We move beyond a broad descriptive overview to critically assess the maturity of current research. Specifically, we address three research questions:

In which specific domains of healthcare SCM (e.g. supplier selection, inventory, logistics) are AI models currently being concentrated?

In which specific domains of healthcare SCM (e.g. supplier selection, inventory, logistics) are AI models currently being concentrated?

What specific AI taxonomies and methodological approaches (e.g. fuzzy logic, neural networks) are being utilised to address these problems?

Methods

We conducted a systematic literature review following Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group guidelines [28], a methodology recognised for its rigour in mapping existing evidence and identifying research gaps. The search strategy was unrestricted by study design, publication date, or language, and included studies published up to May 2024. Indexed terms (MeSH and DeCS) were adapted to each electronic database (MEDLINE via PubMed, LILACS, and SciELO). The complete search strategy is provided in Appendix A.

Records were managed using Mendeley to remove duplicates, and the screening process was conducted in Rayyan software. Studies were excluded if they addressed AI applications exclusively in non-healthcare supply chains or healthcare supply chains without the use of AI methods. Two reviewers (SB and AO) independently and blindly screened titles and abstracts, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (OE).

While we acknowledge that AI methods (e.g. genetic algorithms, neural networks) are theoretically transferable across industries, we excluded non-health SCM studies to isolate the specific maturity and implementation barriers within the healthcare sector. Healthcare SCM operates under distinct constraints (such as high regulatory scrutiny, biological perishability (e.g. blood, vaccines), and zero-error tolerance for critical patient supplies) which create a unique adoption environment distinct from general manufacturing or retail.

Eligible articles underwent full-text review and, where appropriate, data were extracted. A list of excluded studies with reasons for exclusion is presented in Appendix B. Reference lists of included studies were examined to identify additional relevant publications (snowball sampling). Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (SB and AO) using a standardised extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel and subsequently verified by a third reviewer (OE). Extracted data were summarised descriptively.

Given the heterogeneity of AI models, study designs, and reported outcomes, as well as the absence of standardised implementation metrics, a narrative synthesis framework was applied. This approach enabled the systematic classification of AI techniques proposed for healthcare supply chain management, a critical appraisal of methodological quality, and a structured comparison between theoretical claims and empirical evidence, thereby moving beyond descriptive aggregation to examine the gap between computational potential and real-world deployment.

Finally, the transparency, completeness, and quality of reporting of the articles were assessed using the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist [29]. For studies not involving human participants, the checklist was adapted or items marked as not applicable to ensure alignment with the scope and objectives of the review. While STROBE is primarily designed for observational studies, we selected and adapted it due to the absence of a validated quality checklist specifically for theoretical AI supply chain models. We utilised STROBE as a proxy to evaluate the transparency and completeness of reporting (specifically focusing on the clear description of data sources, variable definitions, and discussion of limitations) which are critical quality indicators regardless of the underlying modelling technique (Appendix C).

Results

Search results, and characteristics and quality of included studies

A total of 8,679 articles were identified in the indexed electronic databases. An additional 18 articles were identified through snowball sampling. Of these (8,697), 109 (1.2%) were excluded as duplicates, and 8,588 were examined by title and abstract. Of these, 61 (0.71%) articles were evaluated in full text, of which only 13 were included in this review. The PRISMA diagram summarising the process is presented in Appendix D, and a matrix with main fields of analysis in Appendix E.

Of the 13 included studies, one study was published in 2016 [30], one in 2022 [31], six in 2023 [32–37] and five in 2024 [38–42] (Table 1). Six studies [30, 31, 34, 40–42] did not report a specific country of application of the proposed model, but where reported, India [33, 38], and China [36, 37] were the most frequent countries, with two studies from each (Table 1).

For quality assessment, most of the items of the STROBE instrument were fulfilled across the articles included. After excluding items that were not applicable, 91.7% of items on the checklist were fulfilled. All articles provided information about the background, objective and study design, as well as the variables, data sources, study size, quantitative variables, statistical methods, outcomes and main results. Less often reported were bias (with 23% of items not reported), setting (8% not reported), generalisability (8%) and funding (8%).

The STROBE quality assessment revealed implications beyond mere reporting compliance. The consistent lack of reporting on bias suggests a low level of evidence maturity. The high prevalence of theoretical simulations without external validation implies that while these models are mathematically sound, their reliability in complex, unpredictable real-world hospital environments remains unproven. Outcomes of application of the STROBE instrument checklist are provided in Appendix C.

Types and domain of models

No dominant type of AI model was identified across the studies. Six articles [31, 34, 35, 38, 39, 41] described a multimodal approach to analysis, and one article [40] did not employ a specific model but instead simulated an entire supply chain in the metaverse (Table 2). Authors reported use of Large Language Models in three studies [34, 36, 37]. A critical pattern emerged regarding the nature of the AI employed. Contrary to the prevailing trend of data-driven machine learning in other sectors, our results show a dominance of theoretical optimisation and fuzzy multi-criteria decision making (MCDM) methods in healthcare SCM. As shown in Table 2, these traditional operational research techniques constitute the majority of the AI-labelled studies, whereas data-intensive approaches such as deep learning or neural networks were notably scarce. This indicates that current research is focused more on expert-based logic systems rather than learning from large-scale operational data.

Comparison with other algorithms or models was found in 11 studies [31–35, 37–42], with most comparisons made against more basic or previous similar models. One article [33] compared against expert opinion. Performance metrics or sensitivity analyses were carried out in eight [31, 34, 35, 37–39, 41–42] articles, but none of these included external data for assessment of over-fitting.

Frameworks and models for adoption of AI in healthcare supply chains

Two studies described critical factors related to diffusion of AI for supply chains in healthcare [32, 33]. Despite using different methodological approaches, both articles stated that a key factor was the intensity and dimensions of the technology employed, in particular their adoption by users in preparation for new developments. A second dimension identified was environmental/institutional factors and government support, in particular existence of competitive pressure among different actors, and how regulatory norms can shape adoption. In this regard, government support was recognised as especially relevant in LMICs, where the private health sector can be too small to embrace risk related to adoption of recent technologies. Both articles highlighted the potential role of AI in resilience and efficiency in healthcare supply chains, yet none provided an empirical evaluation or a specific case study.

Two articles analysed the type of supply chain model to adopt using AI algorithms. The first [35] discussed the application of blockchain as a platform for improving healthcare supply chains, particularly in relation to user data management and supplier selection, providing a multi-criteria decision-making framework. The second article [36] studied how AI can help to identify and select the optimal healthcare supply chain mode, on attributes related to efficacy and efficiency. Both relied on modelling methods, but neither applied these models to case studies.

One study [40] developed a conceptual model of a healthcare supply chain in the metaverse, adopting the Cyber-Physical-Social System (CPSS) framework. This framework integrates interconnected digital, physical, and social structures, including the Internet of Things (IoT), cloud computing, AI, and blockchain. Every facet, process, and element within the metaverse is outfitted with sensors and IoT devices to consistently capture and store the data they produce. Those data are transmitted to the main cloud server where they undergo further refinement, involving a multitude of processes including AI, machine learning, deep learning, and natural language processing. The authors used a conceptual approach without actual deployment of the model, and listed barriers to implementation of a system of this nature, including technological and regulatory barriers.

Supplier selection

Four articles [31, 34, 38, 42] addressed supplier selection, and shared a similar approach, relying on AI techniques to apply MCDM, most of them with fuzzy-type multi-model deployments. These models were used to obtain an optimal solution for maximum value among different suppliers, using a variety of value dimensions (indicators).

One article [31] developed a multi-step methodology comprising four parts as follows: Fuzzy Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory (FDEMATEL) to determine interrelationships among different indicators of suppliers; Fuzzy Best-Worst Method (FBWM) for pair-wise comparison of suppliers; Fuzzy Analytic Network Process (FANP) for assessing the weight of indicators, and Fuzzy Vlse Kriterijumsk Optimizacija Kompromisno Resenje (FVIKOR) for identifying the optimal solution for maximum value of selected suppliers.

A second article [34] used a Multi-Stage Decision-Making Framework (MSDMF) comprising four sequential stages: computing scores of suppliers based on several indicators, then using Stochastic Fuzzy Best–Worst Method (SFBWM) and a Multi-Objective Model (MOM) for supplier selection and order allocation decisions; then a data-driven Fuzzy Robust Stochastic (FRS) optimisation approach to efficiently treat hybrid uncertainty of the problem; and finally, using Chebyshev Multi-Choice Goal Programming with Utility Function (CMCGP-UF) to obtain the optimal solution for selection and allocation of suppliers.

The third article [42] used a mixed approach of Stochastic Multicriteria Acceptability Analysis (SMAA) and the Multi-attributive Border Approximation Area Comparison (MABAC) method, to assess indicators expressed by Belief Distributions (BDs). In this method, the global ignorance in BDs and the uncertain criteria weights are considered by judging the feasible space to derive the final rank of healthcare suppliers for quick delivery and higher certainty of delivery. The fourth article [38] examined supplier selection using Probabilistic Hesitant Fuzzy (PHF) data sets, generalised Dombi operators, and an MCDM model called Measurement of Alternatives and Ranking according to Compromise Solution (MARCOS) for consensus-building in a group decision-making scenario.

Each of these four articles complemented the theoretical explanation of the models with an applied exercise for implementation in the selection of suppliers. Relevant factors considered for supplier selection were costs, environmental performance, quality, system reliability and agility. Two additional and critical factors highlighted were the availability and reliability of supplies. Despite the variety of models used, all of them represented implementation of quantitative MCDM models, and none used semi-qualitative/qualitative models. Finally, the four articles relied on the modelling of different scenarios for selection of suppliers, but none of them applied the selection model to a real-life scenario.

Risk and resilience of logistics and distribution of supplies

Four articles [30, 37, 39, 41] addressed use of AI systems on logistics and distribution in healthcare supply chains. One article [30] deployed a risk assessment model for supply of pharmaceutical excipients, using an AHP-fuzzy comprehensive evaluation model, to assess risk based on a set of indicators in the supply chain, using as an example polysorbate 80. This was the only included article to focus on risk assessment in the supply chain, and the authors did not apply this model to a real-life setting.

Two articles analysed AI models for enhancing the availability of critical supplies for blood and vaccines. In the first example [39] the authors analysed logistics related to supply of blood, its storage, transfer among facilities and adaptability to face shortages. They used a possibilistic mixed-integer linear programming algorithm for distribution and localisation of stock, solving the model system with lexicographic and Torabi-Hassini methods. The solution achivied a decrease of 10% in overall delivery time and 15% in total cost but did not provide any real-world data. The second case [37] used a combination of blockchain technology and AI to model a vaccine supply chain. The model incorporated a two-player dynamic game to estimate the level of consumers’ difficulty in obtaining a certified vaccine source, and the magnitude of the production planning and demand forecasting, but also did not present real-world data. This paper was the only one to apply a differential game approach to develop a dynamic game model, and to consider combining AI and blockchain technology.

A fourth article [41] used machine learning to model the supply chain of medical goods. The authors proposed a multi-task learning model with task-specific regularisation for optimising time and quantity of delivery for supplies and proposed a neural network model optimised for predicting shipped quantities of medical supplies and days to delivery. The model included hyperparameter tuning and allowed both optimisation aims (quantity and time of delivery) to be identified as critical factors in the optimisation of a supply chain in healthcare. Again, this model was not applied to a real-life scenario.

Discussion

Our review shows that the application of AI models and techniques within healthcare supply chains is a relatively recent development, with almost all publications appearing in the last four years. Notably, much of the literature focuses on supplier selection and logistics management, two well-established topics in supply chain literature within and outside of healthcare. Included studies that evaluated broad application of AI for supply chain management were theoretical, and whereas they described barriers and facilitators for integrating AI techniques they did not evaluate the actual status of use of AI in healthcare supply chains, or describe examples where AI has already been implemented.

In terms of using AI for supplier selection, a classical approach was observed, predominantly structured along MCDM methods, which are well-established in the non-healthcare supply chain literature. These studies commonly employ fuzzy AI models to optimise supplier selection across multiple criteria to maximise value. We found no empirical evidence on how the integration of AI techniques with MCDM would improve supply chains. The included studies on logistics displayed greater heterogeneity, addressing specific supply challenges such as vaccine and blood distribution.

Our findings highlight a critical conceptual ambiguity in the literature regarding the definition of AI in healthcare SCM. While the term implies advanced data-driven autonomy, the majority of reviewed studies rely on fuzzy MCDM and traditional optimisation logic. Unlike the industrial sector, where machine learning is widely deployed for predictive analytics, healthcare SCM research appears to remain grounded in theoretical ‘expert systems’. This reliance on symbolic AI (logic-based) rather than sub-symbolic AI (data-driven) explains the lack of scalability and real-world implementation found in our review. Researchers are optimising theoretical models rather than training algorithms on actual hospital data.

Several of the included studies stated that AI can optimise flow of critical medical supplies; however, none of them applied the proposed solutions in real-life scenarios, or compared them with standard practice. Our contribution is not merely reaffirming the potential of AI, but diagnosing the root cause of its stagnation: a methodological conservatism that favours safe, theoretical fuzzy models over the riskier, data-heavy implementation of modern machine learning.

Previous studies have suggested that AI integration in healthcare SCM improves supply chain resilience, enabling rapid and effective responses to fluctuating demand for essential supplies [12, 13], a subject much discussed following the COVID-19 pandemic, where reliability and availability of healthcare supplies became a prominent topic. AI has demonstrated value in SCM for tasks like inventory, planning, and logistics optimisation, reducing costs and improving accuracy in resource allocation. Given the scale of healthcare procurement, and that value for money is critical (particularly where public expenditure is concerned), use of AI in healthcare therefore offers much potential. For example, the National Health Service (NHS) Supply Chain in England purchases around 592,000 different types of medical products from around 80,000 different suppliers [43], and has recently announced it is exploring use of AI to improve operational efficiency [44]. In other European countries there has been a recent trend towards centralised procurement for medical technology, and again AI could optimise this [45].

It is therefore notable that we found so few studies of AI in healthcare SCM, and those we did find provided no evidence or outcomes of real-world application. A lack of standardised approach and framework for the application of AI in healthcare SCM can also hinder broader adoption, and future research should look at AI application to healthcare-specific requirements [17, 22], as well as demonstrating real-world application.

We should also recognise that SCM in healthcare can present specific obstacles, such as coordinating multiple stakeholders or multiple health providers with sometimes conflicting priorities, and often a need to meet legal requirements on public expenditure. This context differs from manufacturing sectors where AI applications are more predictable and controlled [46]. Furthermore, procurement systems between health providers differ, and may run on legacy technology or software unable to handle AI operations, and existing human resources may lack technical expertise [47]. Regulations and processes for healthcare purchasing differ between countries, meaning that procurement technologies that work in one country may need adapting to apply to another. In addition, some medical products are subject to monopoly providers and/or lack interoperability [48], and so without ready availability of clinical end-users in procurement decisions, application of AI may be limited by actual or perceived risk, held by procurement specialists of purchasing equipment not fit for purpose.

We should further recognise that AI has potential to evaluate metrics in healthcare procurement other than price and logistics, including risk of labour abuse or environmental harm. For example, the NHS in England has introduced a requirement for all suppliers to provide data on environmental performance (carbon footprint) of their products [49], and so AI could be used to evaluate supplier responses. However, there are many caveats to carbon footprinting, and using a database or algorithm to compare products for purchasing decisions is fraught with difficulty [50]. Health procurement teams in Norway have used data on the country of origin of medical products to assess risk of labour abuse in manufacture (where overall 49% of medical products were found to come from countries at high risk of abuse [51]), and here AI could also assist in evaluating that risk. Again, that comes with caveats, because country of origin is a proxy for actual worker rights infringement, and without granular data specific to the product, that risk is theoretical rather than necessarily real.

Our findings have important implications for healthcare managers and policymakers. First, the current dominance of theoretical and fuzzy-based models suggests that decision-makers should be cautious when adopting AI-labelled tools that lack empirical validation. Instead, there is a need to prioritise investments in data infrastructure, interoperability, and analytics capabilities that enable the deployment of data-driven AI solutions in real-world settings. Second, healthcare organisations should promote pilot implementations that test AI applications under operational conditions, particularly in areas such as inventory management, demand forecasting, and logistics coordination. Finally, policymakers and procurement agencies play a key role in fostering AI adoption by creating regulatory frameworks and incentives that support experimentation, data sharing, and cross-institutional collaboration, especially in resource-constrained settings.

In conclusion, the published literature identifies few studies of the application of AI to healthcare SCM, and no evidence on outcomes from real-world evaluation. Given the scale and complexity of healthcare supply chains, and the increasing range of metrics to evaluate, this represents an untapped potential.
