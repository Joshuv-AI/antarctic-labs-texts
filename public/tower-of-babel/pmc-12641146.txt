Standardizing Methodologies in Prosthodontics to Improve Research Reliability and Peer Review Efficiency: A Perspective‐Based Proposal
Authors: Seyed Ali Mosaddad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSignificant variability in research methodologies within prosthodontics limits the comparability of findings and reduces the strength of evidence‐based conclusions. The establishment of a Prosthodontic Research Methodology Council (PRMC) is proposed to develop standardized, consensus‐based methodological frameworks aimed at improving research reliability and peer review efficiency.MethodsLimitations in current prosthodontic research designs and methodological inconsistencies were reviewed through a narrative search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science using keywords related to prosthodontic research methodology, standardization, and systematic reviews/meta‐analyses. Relevant meta‐research studies and consensus‐based initiatives in dentistry and medicine, including CONSORT, PRISMA, and STROBE frameworks, were analyzed. Insights from these initiatives were used to conceptualize the PRMC structure, governance, and phased implementation strategy, incorporating Delphi consensus methods for harmonizing research protocols.ResultsThe proposed PRMC model consists of a multidisciplinary expert consortium representing academia, clinical practice, industry, and journal editorial boards. A four‐phase implementation plan is outlined: (1) formation of a steering committee and identification of priority research areas, (2) development of consensus‐based methodological guidelines, (3) pilot implementation of pre‐study methodological reviews in selected journals, and (4) widespread adoption across international research networks. Expected outcomes include enhanced reproducibility, improved comparability of findings, reduced heterogeneity in systematic reviews, and greater efficiency in peer review processes.ConclusionThe PRMC framework provides a structured approach to address methodological variability in prosthodontics. Through harmonized study designs and strengthened international collaboration, this initiative is expected to improve the reliability of research outcomes and facilitate the integration of high‐quality evidence into clinical practice.

Introduction

Despite a rapidly growing body of research in prosthodontics, studies investigating similar questions frequently report conflicting results. The significant variability in methodological approaches across studies on similar topics has been considered the root of this issue. This inconsistency hampers the ability to draw meaningful and reliable conclusions, as the lack of uniformity in research design leads to challenges in comparing findings [1]. As a result, many studies are accompanied by extensive limitations and cautionary statements, undermining the overall strength of the evidence base in the field [2]. As an illustrative contrast, Taghva et al. [3] evaluated the influence of implant depth on the three‐dimensional positional accuracy of digital impressions from angulated implants and observed that reduced scanbody exposure did not necessarily compromise accuracy in partially edentulous jaws. Conversely, Gimenez‐Gonzalez et al. reported that diminished scanbody visibility decreased scan accuracy and recommended using longer ones for deep implant scanning [4]. This contrast exemplifies how differences in study design and methodological parameters can yield conflicting results within the same research domain, underscoring the need for methodological standardization.

Methods

This manuscript is based on a narrative review approach, drawing upon relevant literature in prosthodontics and related biomedical fields to identify methodological gaps and potential solutions. Sources were identified through targeted searches in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science using keywords such as ‘prosthodontics,′ ‘research methodology,′ ‘standardization,′ ‘consensus guidelines,′ and ‘reporting frameworks.′ Priority was given to meta‐research studies, systematic reviews, and consensus‐based initiatives that exemplify methodological challenges or successful standardization strategies. The proposal for the Prosthodontic Research Methodology Council (PRMC) was developed by synthesizing these findings, benchmarking against established frameworks in medicine and dentistry (e.g., CONSORT, PRISMA, STROBE), and adapting key elements through a conceptual modeling process. The central idea was inspired by repeated observations while conducting and reviewing systematic reviews, where conclusions were frequently limited by high heterogeneity of included studies and the need for cautious interpretation. This recurring problem emphasized the necessity of initiating a standardized methodological framework in prosthodontics, with the intention of progressively moving toward an ideal standard and assessing its impact in future investigations.

Illustrative Examples of Methodological Variability

Concrete examples in the prosthodontics field [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10] highlight how inconsistent methods impede cumulative knowledge. First, in clinical full‐arch implant scenarios, a recent systematic review on digital versus conventional impressions showed high heterogeneity; conclusions varied depending on scanning strategy, implant angulation, and accuracy metrics, underscoring the need for standardized protocols [11]. Second, randomized trials of occlusal splints for temporomandibular disorders (TMD) use divergent diagnostic criteria, splint designs, comparators, follow‐up intervals, and pain scales; a 37‐trial systematic review rated the overall certainty very low and did not show a clinically meaningful pain reduction, largely because outcome measures and reporting were inconsistent [12]. Third, laboratory wear testing lacks harmonized protocols: reviews show chewing simulators vary widely in motion profiles, loads, antagonists, cycle counts, and media, undermining reproducibility and often precluding meta‐analysis [13]; similarly, in another study evaluating the amount of enamel wear induced by different antagonistic ceramic crown materials in the posterior region, a short‐term follow‐up period was forced to select to minimize methodological heterogeneity; however, this approach limited the applicability of the findings for predicting long‐term material performance [14].

Recent analyses highlight the substantial methodological variability within prosthodontic research. Hammel et al. [15] conducted a methodological assessment of 185 systematic reviews of in vitro dental studies and reported that approximately 68% were characterized by high heterogeneity and low quality of evidence. In implant prosthodontics specifically, Vardić et al. [16] performed a meta‐research study of 117 randomized controlled trials and identified more than 70 distinct outcome measures, making cross‐study comparisons highly challenging. These findings, as examples showing only the tip of the iceberg, emphasize the magnitude of methodological inconsistency in prosthodontics [17] along with suboptimal reporting or analysis of heterogeneity [18].

Current Challenges

It is increasingly evident that research in medical fields [19], and in particular, prosthodontics suffers from inconsistent methodologies, with authors from diverse regions and institutions employing different experimental designs, protocols, and evaluation criteria for studies on comparable topics. Although these studies undergo rigorous peer review, the inherent differences in methods often prevent the drawing of firm conclusions and necessitate careful interpretation of results. Consequently, the research community frequently encounters limitations such as “no firm conclusions can be drawn” or “results must be interpreted with caution,” which are becoming all too familiar and tiresome.

The current peer‐review process, while essential, is not effective in addressing this issue comprehensively [20]. For example, if a study has limitations in its design, it might be rejected by one journal but could still be published in another. This practice does not resolve the underlying methodological issues but merely shifts the problem to a different platform. Therefore, rejecting flawed papers after the fact does not solve the problem; instead, it perpetuates the cycle of variable and unreliable research.

Lessons from Other Fields

Methodology standardization has been successfully addressed in other areas of medicine and dentistry, offering valuable insights for prosthodontics. For example, in periodontology, the 2018 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri‐Implant Diseases unified diagnostic criteria globally, enabling consistent study designs and comparable outcomes across institutions [21]. Menne et al. [22] recently evaluated 190 systematic reviews in dentistry that included animal studies and found that the overall methodological quality was predominantly suboptimal, with 70% rated as critically low and 23% as low according to AMSTAR‐2 criteria. Although modest improvements were noted in more recent publications and those in higher‐impact journals. This suggests that the implementation of structured methodological tools and stricter editorial standards can drive measurable improvements in research quality, reinforcing the potential benefits of adopting consensus‐based frameworks in prosthodontics.

Beyond dentistry, several large‐scale medical initiatives demonstrate the effectiveness of consensus‐driven methodology frameworks. Oncology trials have adopted centralized protocols via initiatives like the NCI′s Cancer Therapy Evaluation Program (CTEP), which harmonizes eligibility criteria, endpoints, and reporting standards, resulting in improved research reproducibility and translational impact [23]. The Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium (CDISC) [24] has developed globally recognized data and methodology standards, mandated for FDA submissions, streamlining study protocols and ensuring interoperability. The IDEAL framework [25] provides a structured evaluation process for surgical innovations, enhancing rigor and transparency. Core Outcome Sets (COS), created using Delphi consensus methods, define minimum outcomes for clinical trials, improving comparability and the quality of systematic reviews [26]. The PCORI Methodology Standards [27] establish evidence‐based requirements for comparative effectiveness research, ensuring scientific rigor and stakeholder relevance. Finally, the ACCORD reporting guidelines [28] aim to standardize the reporting of consensus methods in biomedical research, increasing transparency and methodological reliability. These examples demonstrate that a structured, consensus‐driven approach to methodology standardization is feasible and beneficial.

Introduction of PRMC

To address these challenges, the formation of an expert consortium, which could be named the Prosthodontic Research Methodology Council (PRMC), is proposed to develop and standardize methodologies for key areas of investigation within prosthodontics. This group would establish evidence‐based, consensus‐driven guidelines for study design. In cases where standardized methodologies are not yet available, researchers should be able to submit their proposed methods to the PRMC through the journal′s platform for review and refinement before proceeding with their studies.

The pre‐study review process would follow a structured, multi‐step workflow designed to ensure methodological rigor before data collection begins (Figure 1). First, researchers would submit a standardized methodology proposal to the PRMC through a dedicated online platform. Next, a multidisciplinary panel of PRMC experts—including specialists in prosthodontics, biostatistics, digital dentistry, and clinical research—would evaluate the proposal based on predefined methodological standards. Feedback would be provided to the authors, who could revise and resubmit their protocols until they meet the required criteria. Once approved, the PRMC would issue a formal certification confirming methodological compliance, which could then be referenced when submitting the study to journals. This collaborative process ensures early identification and correction of potential design flaws while fostering harmonized protocols across institutions and countries.

Prosthodontic Research Methodology Council (PRMC): Structure and Governance

The PRMC would function as an independent, multidisciplinary consortium of experts representing academia, clinical practice, industry, journal editorial boards, and international dental organizations. Members would be nominated based on demonstrated expertise and research impact, ensuring diversity in geography, institutional affiliation, and methodological backgrounds. A rotating leadership model would distribute responsibilities evenly, with an elected chairperson serving fixed‐term appointments.

Decision‐making would follow a consensus‐driven process, using Delphi methodology to evaluate and approve proposed methodological frameworks. Subcommittees would focus on specific research domains such as digital dentistry, biomaterials, implant biomechanics, and patient‐reported outcomes. Funding could be obtained from professional associations, research councils, and industry partnerships, with strict policies to avoid conflicts of interest and ensure transparency. To enhance credibility, PRMC decisions and guidelines would be openly published, allowing peer scrutiny and broad community input. To ensure that the established guidelines remain relevant and effective, a periodic review and update process is also recommended. This would allow the methodologies to evolve in response to advancements in the field and feedback from the research community, ensuring that they continue to meet the highest standards of scientific rigor.

Implementation Strategy

Establishing the PRMC and a pre‐study review framework would involve a phased approach: Phase 1 (0–12 months): Formation of an interim steering committee, stakeholder mapping, and identification of priority research domains with the highest variability (e.g., digital impression accuracy, wear testing protocols, and mechanical testing). Phase 2 (12–24 months): Drafting of consensus‐based methodological guidelines for the identified priority areas using a Delphi process, with open consultation across professional bodies, academic institutions, and journal editorial boards. Phase 3 (24–36 months): Pilot implementation of pre‐study reviews within selected journals to refine workflows, evaluate feasibility, and gather feedback from authors and reviewers. Phase 4 (36–48 months): Full‐scale adoption of the PRMC framework across partner journals and research networks, supported by dissemination of guidelines, workshops, and training materials.

Potential Challenges and Limitations

To facilitate the adoption of PRMC recommendations, foreseeable barriers and corresponding mitigation strategies are outlined below.

Perceived Limitations on Creativity and Academic Freedom

Challenge

Some researchers may view standardization as prescriptive or constraining.

Mitigations

PRMC guidance will specify minimum “Core Methods” required for comparability while explicitly encouraging “Innovation Modules” that extend beyond the core. Departures from the core will be allowed under a comply‐or‐justify model, with a brief, pre‐registered Deviation & Rationale statement and clear documentation of novel procedures. An Innovation Registry will catalog approved deviations to accelerate knowledge transfer and avoid stifling new ideas. A scheduled sunset/review cycle (e.g., 18–24 months) will update standards based on emerging evidence so guidance evolves rather than ossifies.

Editorial and Reviewer Workload

Challenge

Introducing pre‐study methodological review and standard checklists may be perceived as an additional burden.

Mitigations

A single, author‐facing methodological dossier (standardized forms, checklists, and templates) will streamline submissions and enable editorial triage. Journals adopting PRMC certification can rely on the certification to reduce later revision cycles, shifting effort upstream. A shared pool of trained methodological reviewers (coordinated across partner journals) and brief calibration webinars will promote consistent, efficient assessments. Lightweight automation (structured fields for outcomes, sample size rationale, and analysis plans) will simplify checks without adding narrative workload. Adoption will be phased to allow workflow adjustment and evaluation.

Cultural, Institutional, and Resource Heterogeneity Across Countries

Challenge

Differences in regulatory processes, ethics oversight, clinical pathways, language, and access to equipment can hinder uniform adoption.

Mitigations

PRMC will establish regional working hubs and translations of guidance, alongside resource‐stratified tiers (e.g., Tier A/B/C) that define acceptable alternatives when high‐cost instruments or software are unavailable. Standards will prioritize equipment‐agnostic specifications (e.g., performance tolerances, calibration steps) rather than brand‐specific requirements. Capacity‐building (open templates, training materials, and workshops) and equity provisions (fee waivers or micro‐grants for low‐resource groups) will support inclusive adoption while preserving methodological rigor.

Governance, Conflicts of Interest, and Sustainability

Challenge

Diverse stakeholder interests and funding sources could raise concerns about bias and long‐term maintenance.

Mitigations

Governance will be transparent, with public comment on draft standards, diverse representation (geography, institution, and discipline), and strict COI policies (disclosure, recusal rules, and independent oversight). Sustainability will rely on a mixed funding model (societies, public agencies, philanthropy) with a firewall preventing funders from influencing methodological content. A monitoring & evaluation plan will track adoption metrics (e.g., protocol completeness, outcome harmonization, time‐to‐decision) to inform iterative improvements.

Collectively, these measures aim to balance standardization with innovation, minimize editorial burden through upstream rigor, respect international diversity through stratified recommendations, and ensure robust, transparent governance.

Scope of Application of PRMC Methodology Standards

While not all prosthodontic investigations are suitable for methodological standardization, several study designs can greatly benefit from harmonized protocols, particularly when experimental conditions can be controlled. Randomized controlled trials assessing implant‐supported restorations, prosthetic designs, or abutment configurations represent the highest priority, as predefined variables facilitate comparability across studies. Similarly, in vitro laboratory investigations—such as those evaluating mechanical features, optical properties, wear patterns, and bond strength—are ideal candidates for standardization given their inherently controlled environments. Moreover, digital dentistry workflow studies would significantly benefit from structured, replicable methodologies. Within these domains, the PRMC could develop consensus‐based templates and outcome definitions to ensure consistent data acquisition, enabling more reliable evidence synthesis.

Relevance to Digital Dentistry

Digital dentistry is one of the fastest‐evolving areas within prosthodontics and would benefit significantly from methodology standardization. Diverse workflows, ranging from intraoral scanning techniques to CAD/CAM software algorithms and additive versus subtractive manufacturing protocols, lead to highly variable study outcomes. For instance, accuracy comparisons among intraoral scanners often differ based on scan path, calibration strategies, and processing algorithms [29]. Similarly, studies on 3D‐printed versus milled restorations lack uniformity in build orientation, resin post‐processing, and testing protocols, which hinders meaningful comparisons [7]. In parallel, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and robotics into prosthodontics is expanding rapidly, yet current investigations often lack standardized evaluation metrics for algorithm performance, validation datasets, and clinical applicability [30]. Without methodological guidance, future research risks reproducing the same heterogeneity and interpretive limitations already seen in digital workflows. Since AI applications are still in their early stages within dentistry, this is an ideal moment to establish clear methodological guidelines, ensuring that research in this area develops on a reliable and comparable foundation. Establishing harmonized guidelines through the PRMC would promote reproducibility, facilitate meta‐analyses, and accelerate the safe integration of emerging technologies into clinical practice.

Integration with Existing Reporting Guidelines

The proposed PRMC framework would complement, rather than replace, existing international reporting guidelines such as CONSORT (for randomized controlled trials) [31], STROBE (for observational studies) [32], and PRISMA (for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses) [33]. While these guidelines focus primarily on how research findings should be reported, they do not provide standardization of the methodologies used during study design and data collection. As a result, studies can still differ substantially in protocols, outcome definitions, and analytical methods even when adhering fully to reporting checklists. These successful initiatives demonstrate that adopting structured, consensus‐based frameworks can significantly improve the quality, consistency, and comparability of research outcomes. The PRMC would build on these principles by establishing consensus‐based methodological standards tailored to prosthodontic research, thereby harmonizing study designs and methodology stages. In doing so, PRMC recommendations would enhance the quality and comparability of prosthodontic studies, ultimately improving the utility and interpretability of findings reported under CONSORT, STROBE, and PRISMA frameworks.

Conclusion

Establishing the consensus‐based methodological guidelines or the pre‐study review process would allow researchers to correct major design flaws before conducting their studies. It would also enable the peer‐review process to focus on reporting deficiencies and minor issues rather than fundamental design problems. Such an approach would save time and resources, enhance the quality and reliability of research findings, and mitigate the frustration associated with frequent limitations in study results. This approach could also be particularly efficient in the rapidly evolving field of digital dentistry. The editorial board of the relevant journals is encouraged to consider this proposal and take the lead in developing PRMC, potentially in collaboration with other relevant journals and communities in the field. This initiative could greatly improve the quality and impact of prosthodontics research, ultimately benefiting both practitioners and patients alike.
