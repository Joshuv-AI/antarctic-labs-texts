Commentary beyond the ATP site: Cryo-EM views of allosteric PI3Kα inhibition
Authors: Benjamin G. Paetzel, John E. Burke
Published: 2026

Activating mutations in the P110α catalytic subunit of class IA phosphoinositide 3 kinase alpha (PI3Kα) are major drivers of cancer and overgrowth disorders, with PIK3CA encoding it being the 2nd most frequently mutated gene in human cancer1. Oncogenic mutations occur throughout P110α at important regulatory sites, with two hotspot regions: H1047R in the kinase domain and E542K/E545K in the helical domain2. Although these mutations all result in PI3Kα hyperactivation, they do so through distinct mechanisms by mimicking or enhancing conformational states sampled by wild-type PI3Kα during its activation cycle3. The H1047R mutation reorients the C-terminus, allowing for the kα12 helix in the C-terminus to engage with the membrane, ultimately promoting a membrane-engaged conformation with increased access to the PIP2 lipid substrate4,5. Conversely, the E542K/E545K mutations disrupt the autoinhibitory interaction between the P110α helical domain and the nSH2 domain of the P85α regulatory subunit3,6. The development of the ATP-competitive, PI3Kα-selective inhibitor alpelisib marked a major milestone in the development of targeted cancer therapies, becoming the first PI3Kα-selective inhibitor to receive U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in 20197. However, while alpelisib is the most widely used FDA-approved PI3Kα isoform-selective inhibitor, its clinical efficacy is limited by treatment-induced hyperglycemia and compensatory insulin feedback, which can reactivate PI3Kα signalling8. This spurred development of mutant-selective PI3Kα inhibitors9, 10, 11, most notably RLY-2608 and STX-478, with the goal of inhibiting oncogenic PI3Kα while sparing wild-type PI3Kα in normal tissues to avoid this metabolic toxicity9,10. These compounds exploit a cryptic allosteric pocket that is preferentially accessible in conformations enriched by the H1047R oncogenic mutant, and each exhibit selectivity for H1047R over wild-type. Critically, both RLY-2608 and STX-478 have demonstrated anti-tumor activity with reduced metabolic dysfunction in pre-clinical studies. X-ray crystal structures of both RLY-2608 and STX-478 were previously solved9,10; however, comparison of crystal and cryo-EM structures of H1047R PI3Kα revealed that the C-terminus is ordered by crystal contacts, obscuring the conformational dynamics of this region6,12. The full structural basis for RLY-2608 and STX-478 mutant selectivity and inhibitory mechanisms remained incompletely defined, highlighting the need for further structural investigation of these allosteric inhibitors.

Recently, Liu et al.13 reported high-resolution cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of PI3Kα oncogenic mutants H1047R, E542K and E545K bound to the allosteric inhibitors RLY-2608 or STX-478. RLY-2608-bound H1047R, E542K and E545K complexes were determined at overall resolutions of 2.84, 2.89 and 3.01 Å, respectively, and STX-478-bound complexes determined at 3.31, 2.86 and 3.01 Å, respectively. Comparative structural analyses were then performed against previously reported structures, including both X-ray and cryo-EM of wild-type and oncogenic mutants bound to both allosteric and orthosteric inhibitors. This work provides unique additional structural information for how allosteric inhibitors target PI3Kα. Direct comparisons among their newly determined inhibitor-bound H1047R, E542K, and E545K structures were further performed to assess both shared and mutation-specific conformational responses to RLY-2608 and STX-478. These structural analyses were complemented with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, quantitative analyses of the solvent accessible surface area (SASA) of the catalytic and activation loops, lipid kinase activity assays, and quantification of AKT phosphorylation (pAKT) to further validate the proposed mechanisms of inhibitor binding and mutant selectivity. Cell viability assays across a panel of PIK3CA-mutant cancer cell lines is consistent with previous work showing enhanced response for STX-478 and RLY-2608 against cell lines containing an H1047R mutant.

As previous structures exist for PI3Kα bound to both RLY-2608 and STX-478, one of the critical questions is what unique information is provided by this new study. This work allows for a tightly controlled experiment looking at how two allosteric inhibitors that bind in a similar site propagate conformational changes in different PI3Kα hotspot mutants using a shared cryo-EM approach. It also provides the first view of STX-478 bound in the absence of an ATP-competitive inhibitor, as the previous co-crystal structures were determined with inavolisib occupying the orthosteric site. Notably, there are some major shifts in how both STX-478 and RLY-2608 bind compared to previous crystal structures, with both compounds withdrawn towards kα10 in the cryo-EM structures. This change is dramatically more pronounced for STX-478, where the benzofuran moiety vacates a sub-pocket formed by kα2 and kα9, amounting to a major repositioning of the compound rather than a local adjustment. As kα2 also lines the ATP site, orthosteric occupancy in the previous crystal structure may contribute to this difference. There will likely still be some uncertainty in absolute positioning, particularly for STX-478, and higher-resolution structures will be required to resolve this ambiguity.

RLY-2608 binding to the H1047R mutant induces both local rearrangements surrounding the allosteric pocket and global allosteric changes affecting key regulatory elements. Locally, they observed disordering of the C-terminal segment of the activation loop, along with a pronounced rearrangement of the catalytic DRH motif. Globally, they observed a reorientation of the phosphate-binding loop (P-loop) away from the catalytic site and repositioning of all three membrane-binding loops. They also observed reorganization of the P110α–p85α interface, specifically enhanced contacts between the P110α ABD and P85α and between the P85α nSH2 and P110α, while iSH2–p110α contacts are weakened. While the allosteric changes observed upon RLY-2608 binding to H1047R are largely conserved in E542K/E545K, some mutant-specific differences were present. These were centred on the activation loop, where they observed a displacement of F937 relative to its position in H1047R, as well as mutant-specific side-chain orientations of D939 that generate distinct hydrogen-bonding networks within the allosteric site. Pronounced mutant-specific differences were also present in the P-loop as well as the C2 domain loops, which are well ordered in H1047R but are poorly resolved in E545K, suggesting altered local flexibility in the C-lobe.

Binding of STX-478 to the H1047R mutant elicits both local rearrangements to the allosteric site and long-range restructuring of catalytically critical elements. Locally, the N-terminal segment of the activation loop unwinds from a helix to an extended conformation, opening the allosteric site. Notably, and in contrast to RLY-2608, the DRH motif retains a conformation close to that in the BYL-719-bound structure, indicating that the two compounds reshape the activation loop in ways that make distinct demands on the catalytic loop. Globally, STX-478 binding induces shifts in the kα8 and kα11 helices, a repositioning of the P-loop, and substantial rearrangements across all three membrane-binding regions. Where RLY-2608 weakens iSH2 engagement, STX-478 strengthens the P110α–p85α interface throughout, enhancing polar contacts across the ABD–iSH2, C2–iSH2, helical–nSH2 and kinase–nSH2 interfaces, and forming new salt bridges between E601 in the iSH2 C-terminal tail and K941/K942 in the kinase domain that are absent from both unliganded and BYL-719-bound H1047R. The E542K/E545K–STX478 complexes show very weak density for the P85 and the ABD, which is different from the almost complete loss of density in these regions seen in previous E542K and E545K cryo-EM structures12. So STX-478 may partially stabilise the iSH2, but cannot completely overcome the helical domain mutation disengagement of the P85 subunit and ABD domain that occurs in the E542K/E545K mutants. Mutant-specific differences were also evident at the allosteric site itself. In H1047R, D915 rotates towards the amine of the STX-478 pyrimidine ring and K941 to form a salt bridge network that stabilises the inhibitor, whereas in both E542K and E545K an outward rotation of D915 eliminates this interaction, with the pyrimidine amine instead making weaker contacts with R951 and the catalytic loop backbone. Mutant specific conformations were also observed at the P-loop, with a 3.0 Å Cα displacement of S773 in the E545K mutant relative to H1047R, as well as significant reorganization in the RBD.

Collectively, the findings from Liu et al.13 advance our understanding of how allosteric inhibitors exploit the unique conformational landscapes of oncogenic PI3Kα mutants to achieve selective inhibition. Their comparative structural analyses with previously published structures provide useful structural insights for better understanding the distinct inhibitory mechanisms of RLY-2608 and STX-478. Notably, these analyses reveal key differences in the allosteric changes elicited by STX-478 and RLY-2608, particularly at the activation and catalytic loops, while also demonstrating conserved allosteric effects across oncogenic mutants, including remodeling of membrane-binding regions and key elements of the kinase domain. Additionally, their detailed characterization of the allosteric binding pocket provides a valuable framework for future drug discovery. In particular, the conserved features of this pocket across distinct oncogenic mutants suggest a common vulnerability for therapeutic targeting.

Despite the important mechanistic insights provided by Liu et al.13, a complete understanding of how STX-478 and RLY-2608 achieve mutant-selective inhibition has yet to emerge. An important limitation of the current study is that all structural analyses were performed using PI3Kα in its solution state, which likely represents a primarily inactive conformation. Because PI3Kα activation is coupled to membrane association and cooperative interactions with receptor phosphotyrosine motifs and Ras–GTP, it remains unclear whether the allosteric mechanisms described here are fully preserved in the physiologically active membrane-bound state. As allosteric inhibitors alter the conformation of membrane binding regions, there is also the chance that these compounds alter membrane affinity for both WT and mutant versions of PI3Kα. The recent determination of cryo-EM structures of PI3Kα–KRas complexes embedded in lipid nanodiscs4 opens exciting opportunities to further investigate membrane complexes of PI3Kα, and explore how RLY-2608 and STX-478 modulate these membrane bound forms. Despite these remaining questions, Liu et al.13 provide valuable structural insights of how RLY-2608 and STX-478 inhibit oncogenic mutants of PI3Kα.
