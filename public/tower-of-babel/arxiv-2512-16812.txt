Efficient Monte-Carlo sampling of metastable systems using non-local collective variable updates
Christoph Schönle, Davide Carbone, Marylou Gabrié, Tony Lelièvre, Gabriel Stoltz

Abstract
Monte-Carlo simulations are widely used to simulate complex molecular systems, but standard approaches suffer from metastability. Lately, the use of non-local proposal updates in a collective-variable (CV) space has been proposed in several works. Here, we generalize these approaches and explicitly spell out an algorithm for non-linear CVs and underdamped Langevin dynamics. We prove reversibility of the resulting scheme and demonstrate its performance on several numerical examples, observing a substantial performance increase compared to methods based on overdamped Langevin dynamics as considered previously. Advances in generative machine-learning-based proposal samplers now enable efficient sampling in CV spaces of intermediate dimensionality (tens to hundreds of variables), and our results extend their applicability toward more realistic molecular systems.



I
Introduction

Monte-Carlo simulations promise to give access to the equilibrium properties of physical systems even when the numerical simulation of their relaxation dynamics is prohibitively expensive. In practice, however, standard Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods (MCMC) rely on physically-inspired local moves and struggle to equilibrate across metastable states, as does molecular dynamics.

Enhanced sampling algorithms go beyond the naive Markov Chain Monte Carlo approach to tackle metastability, see Ref.
23
for a review in the context of molecular dynamics. A first class of methods originated from the observation that the metastability is removed at sufficiently high temperatures, which led to the development of algorithms like parallel tempering, annealed importance sampling, and sequential Monte Carlo
50
;
38
;
15
;
56
;
51
. While known to work on a wide range of problems, they spend a substantial amount of computation time at temperatures not of direct interest and generally require a careful choice of parameters (e.g. for parallel tempering the temperature ladder and the frequency of exchange attempts).

Another important category of algorithms makes use of a collective variable (CV), a lower-dimensional representation of the system that is supposed to capture the metastability of the system. A ‘good’ CV that resolves the metastability can be used for biasing simulations to bridge modes, using enhanced sampling algorithms such as umbrella sampling
53
and related histogram methods
28
;
48
, thermodynamic integration
27
or free energy adaptive biasing methods
12
;
22
;
29
;
4
. However, identifying a suitable CV in low dimension is by no means trivial and thus poses the main limitation to this class of methods.

In recent years, another approach has emerged, which is exploiting generative machine learning models. Among the various generative models, normalizing flows
42
are particularly well-suited for the purposes of MCMC sampling since their tractable probability density allows to employ them as a non-local proposal density of physical configurations, where any imperfections in training are corrected for via a Metropolis-Hastings accept/reject scheme or self-normalized importance sampling
41
;
20
. Thanks to the impressive flexibility of neural networks, proofs of concept have been made in different domains ranging from molecular systems to field theories in quantum chromodynamics
35
;
41
;
19
;
24
;
1
. However, the limiting factor to scale these approaches appears to be the ability of the generative model to be precise enough either when the dimension of the problem becomes of the order of a few thousands or when the distribution has singularities
14
;
20
;
47
;
2
.Other recent approaches make use for example of transformers
5
and diffusion models
34
;
6
, but these do not give easy access to the underlying probability density and are therefore less suitable for MCMC sampling than normalizing flows.

A promising approach is therefore to combine the strength of CV-enhanced sampling and generative models (normalizing flows) with a CV of ‘intermediate’ dimension (from a dozen to a few hundred degrees of freedom). Within this dimensionality, identifying a CV such that it resolves the metastability will be much easier than finding a one to three-dimensional representation required by most classical CV-based approaches. Conversely, a normalizing flow in an ‘intermediate’ dimension can be trained to much higher accuracy than if one attempted to learn the distribution of the entire system. Since the overall goal is still sampling full configurations of the system, the question arises how to employ such a proposal sampler in CV space to obtain an unbiased sampler for the full distribution. This has been addressed in multiple works
3
;
40
;
8
;
9
;
39
, in particular under the names of “Hybrid Nonequilibrium Molecular
Dynamics” (HNMD)
8
;
9
and “Nonequilibrium candidate Monte Carlo” (NCMC)
40
in the context of molecular dynamics, but also in a Bayesian setting
26
. The central idea is always to drive the system from one value of the CV to another, a procedure referred to as steering in the following, and then accept or reject the resulting proposal in the full-space. A similar procedure has also been used in the simulation of glassy system with shear deformations
13
. In a previous article
47
, we described such an algorithm with driven dynamics in continuous time
and identified various time-discretizations for which we proved reversibility with respect to the equilibrium measure. Notably, we
also related this to the Jarzynski and the Jarzynski–Crooks equalities

25
;
11
, clarifying the algorithm design. In a parallel work
52
, one version of these algorithms was applied to a toy molecular system, using a normalizing flow as the CV sampler and including an adaptive protocol to train it alongside the MCMC procedure.

However, both these works considered only overdamped Langevin dynamics for the steering and required the CV to be a subset of the Euclidean coordinates giving the conformation of the system. In the present article, we generalize these works by presenting an algorithm for a non-linear collective variable and using general underdamped Langevin dynamics. While in spirit this algorithm already fits into the paradigm of some of the previous works like NCMC and HNMD, we present here an algorithm that is readily implemented and treat all technical details arising from the non-linear CV and general Langevin dynamics. We explicitly prove that the associated Markov chains are reversible with respect to the target measure and present its application to several example systems of increasing complexity. In all of them, we see that the deterministic (Hamiltonian) steering dynamics exhibit the best performance, with an improvement of up to two orders of magnitude compared to overdamped dynamics.

II
Sampling with a non-linear collective variable

II.1
Target distribution and collective variable

We begin this section by outlining the sampling problem at hand.
Our goal is to sample from the Boltzmann–Gibbs probability measure

\displaystyle\nu(\mathrm{d}q)=Z_{q}^{-1}e^{-\beta V(q)}\mathrm{d}q

(II.1)

over positions
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension
d
, with the energy function
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the inverse temperature
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}>0
and the normalizing constant
Z_{q}
. Let us assume that a collective variable (CV)
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
with
\ell<d
has been identified and we have a ‘good’ sampler
\rho
to propose moves in CV space. As mentioned in the introduction, the question of how to use these two ingredients to construct an unbiased MCMC sampler in the full space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has previously been addressed in literature
3
;
40
;
8
;
9
;
39
, but it is not obvious how to employ these schemes if the map
\xi
is non-linear. Notably, the geometry of the level sets
\Sigma(z)
defined as

\displaystyle\Sigma(z)=\left\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,\xi(q)=z\right\}

(II.2)

must be taken into consideration. We present an algorithm which explicitly treats the case of a non-linear CV in the following section. Much of the notation throughout this paper is based on Refs.
31
;
32
and we refer the reader to those for an in-depth introduction on the mathematical concepts used here.

Even though we are only interested in sampling
\nu(\mathrm{d}q)
, it will turn out to be helpful to introduce also an auxiliary momentum variable
p
and define the Boltzmann–Gibbs measure in phase space
(q,p)
by

\displaystyle\mu(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p)=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta H(q,p)}\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p

(II.3)

where
Z
is the normalizing constant,
{H(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}p^{\top}M^{-1}p+V(q)}
is the Hamiltonian, and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a symmetric positive definite mass matrix.

II.2
The algorithm

To explain the algorithm, we first present its overall structure in
Section

II.2.1
, followed by the necessary ingredients and considerations in
Sections

II.2.2
,
II.2.3
and
II.2.4
.

II.2.1
Overall structure

The algorithm consists in iterating over
n\in\mathbb{N}
the following steps:
Starting from a state
Q_{n}
with collective variable value
Z_{n}:=\xi(Q_{n})
, generate
Q_{n+1}
as follows:

Step 1
:

Propose a move in the collective variable space:

\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}\sim\rho(Z_{n},z^{\prime})\,{\rm d}z^{\prime},

where
\rho(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes a Markov kernel with probability density
\rho(z,z^{\prime})
to jump to
z^{\prime}
starting at
z
.

Step 2
:

Generate a new
\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})=\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}
by steering the system in CV space. To this end, (i) specify a schedule to constrain the positions and velocities in CV space, (ii) initialize the position
q^{0}=Q_{n}
and sample an initial momentum
p^{0}
and (iii) use this to follow underdamped Langevin dynamics in the RATTLE discretization (all of this to be specified below). These dynamics yield a final state
\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}
and a quantity
\mathcal{W}_{n+1}
that is identified as a work.

Step 3
:

Draw a random variable
U_{n+1}
with uniform law over
[0,1]
and

•

If
U_{n+1}\leq\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1}))\displaystyle\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}
,

accept the proposal:
Q_{n+1}=\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}
.

•

Otherwise, reject the proposal:

Q_{n+1}=Q_{n}
.

We explain the various ingredients required for
Step 2
in the next three subsections. The idea to sample a system by following some dynamics followed by an accept/reject step that is governed by a work is closely related to the Jarzynski and the Jarzynski–Crooks equalities

25
;
11
.

Remark II.1
.

In all of the numerical examples in this work, we will actually use an independent sampler in the CV space, i.e.
\rho(z,z^{\prime})
is only a function of
z^{\prime}
.

II.2.2
Schedule

To specify the constrained dynamics under which the system evolves from the value of the CV at the previous iteration
Z_{n}
to the newly proposed value
\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}
, choose first the number of intermediate steps
K_{T}
associated with this jump. With the fixed timestep
\Delta t
, this sets the physical transition time
T=K_{T}\Delta t
. In practice, we will choose
K_{T}
as a function of the Euclidean distance
\Delta z=|\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}-Z_{n}|
of the proposed jump.

Introduce a schedule
(z^{(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
with
t_{k}=k\Delta t
whose endpoints are

\displaystyle z^{(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=Z_{n}

(II.4)

\displaystyle z^{(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}(t_{K_{T}})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}

(II.5)

as well as a velocity schedule

(v_{z}^{(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
with zero initial and final velocity,

\displaystyle v^{(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}_{z}(t_{0})=v_{z}^{(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}(t_{K_{T}})=0.

(II.6)

We require that the schedules for position and velocity satisfy the following reversibility property with the switching of the endpoints: for any
(Z,\widetilde{Z})
, we need that

\displaystyle z^{(Z,\widetilde{Z})}(t_{k})

\displaystyle=z^{(\widetilde{Z},Z)}(t_{K_{T}-k})

(II.7)

\displaystyle v_{z}^{(Z,\widetilde{Z})}(t_{k})

\displaystyle=-v_{z}^{(\widetilde{Z},Z)}(t_{K_{T}-k}),

(II.8)

for all
k\in\{0,\ldots,K_{T}\}
.
Note that the requirement (
II.8
) precludes the simplest choice for the velocity of
v_{z}(t_{k})=(z(t_{k+1})-z(t_{k}))/\Delta t
. A simple choice is instead a schedule that connects the endpoints on a straight line with a continuously differentiable schedule function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
:

\displaystyle z^{(Z,\widetilde{Z})}(t_{k})

\displaystyle=Z+(\widetilde{Z}-Z)f(k/K_{T})

(II.9)

\displaystyle v^{(Z,\widetilde{Z})}_{z}(t_{k})

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{Z}-Z)\frac{\dot{f}(k/K_{T})}{K_{T}\Delta t}.

(II.10)

The conditions on the schedule translate to conditions on
f
as
f(0)=0
,
f(1)=1
and
\dot{f}(0)=\dot{f}(1)=0
, which are fulfilled for example by

\displaystyle f(\tau)=(1-\cos(\tau\pi))/2.

(II.11)

See
B.4
in
Section

B.2.1
for further comments on the velocity schedule. The assumptions (
II.6
) and (
II.8
) play a crucial role in the proof of the reversibility of the Markov chain
(Q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and we demonstrate in
Section

H.2
how their violation can introduce a bias.

Remark II.2
.

Even though this is not explicitly indicated, the total time
T
can also depend on
(Z,\widetilde{Z})
in which case one requires
T^{(Z,\widetilde{Z})}=T^{(\widetilde{Z},Z)}
.

II.2.3
Initialization of the momentum

For a given position
q
and momentum
p
, the velocity in
\xi
-direction is defined as

\displaystyle v_{\xi}(q,p)=\nabla\xi(q)^{\top}M^{-1}p\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}

(II.12)

where
\nabla\xi(q)=\left(\nabla\xi_{1}(q),\ldots,\nabla\xi_{\ell}(q)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\ell}
.
The initial momentum that is sampled in
Step 2
of the algorithm needs to have zero velocity
v_{\xi}
to agree with condition (
II.6
) of the velocity schedule.
The cotangent space of zero velocity associated with a position
q\in\Sigma(z)
is

\displaystyle T_{q}^{*}\Sigma(z)=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\middle|\,v_{\xi}(q,p)=0\right\},

(II.13)

and the orthogonal projection on
T_{q}^{*}\Sigma(z)
(for the scalar product induced by
M^{-1}
) is given by:

\displaystyle P_{M}(q)=\operatorname{Id}_{d}-\nabla\xi(q)G_{M}^{-1}(q)\nabla\xi(q)^{\top}M^{-1}.

(II.14)

Here,
G_{M}
is the Gram tensor defined as

G_{M}(q)=\nabla\xi(q)^{\top}M^{-1}\nabla\xi(q).

(II.15)

Throughout this paper, we assume that
G_{M}
is invertible on the submanifolds
\Sigma(z)
(for all
z
).
It is easy to check that, for a given
q\in\Sigma(z)
, one has
P_{M}(q)p\in T_{q}^{*}\Sigma(z)
for any
p
and that
P_{M}(q)^{2}=P_{M}(q)
and
M^{-1}P_{M}(q)=P_{M}(q)^{\top}M^{-1}
and thus
P_{M}
is indeed the orthogonal projection, as stated above.

We initialize the momentum
p^{0}
by sampling an unconstrained momentum
\widetilde{p}^{0}
from a centered normal distribution with covariance matrix
\frac{1}{\beta}M
and projecting it onto the required subspace:

\displaystyle p^{0}=P_{M}(q^{0})\widetilde{p}^{0}.

II.2.4
RATTLE dynamics and work

To relax the entire configuration
q
along the chosen CV and velocity schedules, for reasons related to the geometry of the level sets
\Sigma(z)
(see also the reversibility proof in
Section

B.3
), we will follow constrained dynamics with a modified potential energy
\widetilde{V}(q)=V(q)+V_{\mathrm{fix}}(q)
with the so-called Fixman term
18

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{fix}}(q)=\frac{1}{2\beta}\log\left(\det G_{M}(q)\right).

(II.16)

Starting from
(q^{0},p^{0})
, a discretized path
\left((q^{k},p^{k})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}\right)
is generated by iterating on
0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1
a version of the RATTLE dynamics with constraints found in literature
32
(for the sake of readability, we drop the superscript indicating the endpoints of the schedules
z(t)
and
v_{z}(t)
in this subsection). The scheme writes as follows:

\displaystyle p^{k+1/4}=p^{k}

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta t}{4}\gamma_{P}(q^{k})M^{-1}(p^{k+1/4}+p^{k})

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t}{2}}\sigma_{P}(q^{k}){\mathcal{G}}^{k}

(II.17)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle p^{k+1/2}=p^{k+1/4}-\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\nabla\widetilde{V}(q^{k})}+\nabla\xi(q^{k})\lambda^{k+1/2}\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle q^{k+1}=q^{k}+\Delta t\ M^{-1}p^{k+1/2}\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\xi(q^{k+1})=z(t_{k+1})\quad\quad(C_{q})\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle p^{k+3/4}=p^{k+1/2}-\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\nabla\widetilde{V}(q^{k+1})}+\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\lambda^{k+3/4}\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})^{T}M^{-1}p^{k+3/4}=v_{z}(t_{k+1})\quad\quad(C_{p})\end{cases}

(II.18)

\displaystyle p^{k+1}=p^{k+3/4}

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta t}{4}\gamma_{P}(q^{k+1})M^{-1}(p^{k+3/4}+p^{k+1})

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t}{2}}\sigma_{P}(q^{k+1}){\mathcal{G}}^{k+1/2}

(II.19)

where
({\mathcal{G}}^{k})
and
({\mathcal{G}}^{k+1/2})
are independent sequences of i.i.d. centered Gaussian random vectors with covariance matrix
\mathrm{Id}_{d}
. The Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{k+1/2}
(resp.
\lambda^{k+3/4}
) is chosen so that the constraint
(C_{q})
(resp.
(C_{p})
) is satisfied. For
\lambda^{k+3/4}
, this leads to a linear equation with an analytical solution, whereas the solution for
\lambda^{k+1/2}
will in general be obtained numerically using Newton’s methods. For further precisions on this point see
B.3
in the appendix. Note that there is an equivalent formulation of (
II.17
) and (
II.19
) that is more suitable for numerical implementation, which we provide in
Appendix

A
for completeness.
The matrices
\gamma_{P}(q^{k})=P_{M}(q)\gamma P_{M}(q)^{\top}
and
\sigma_{P}(q^{k})=P_{M}(q)\sigma
satisfy the fluctuation-dissipation identity

\forall q\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\sigma_{P}(q)\sigma_{P}(q)^{\top}=\frac{2}{\beta}\gamma_{P}(q),

(II.20)

ensured by defining the scalar diffusion parameter
\sigma
in terms of the scalar damping parameter
\gamma>0
as

\sigma^{2}=\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}.

(II.21)

We stick here for simplicity to scalar damping and diffusion parameters, but generalization to matrices is straightforward.

Assuming that the position and velocity schedules are compatible in the continuous-time limit
\Delta t\to 0
, i.e.
v_{z}(t)=\dot{z}(t)
,
Eqs.

II.17
,
II.18
and
II.19
are discretizations of Langevin dynamics with constraints
(\xi(q^{k}),v_{\xi}(q^{k},p^{k}))=(z(t_{k}),v_{z}(t_{k}))
see Ref.
32
.

These dynamics yield a proposal move in the full configuration space
\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}=q^{K_{T}}
as well as a work
\mathcal{W}_{n+1}:=\mathcal{W}^{K_{T}}
, which we use to compute the acceptance probability in
Step 3
. This work is defined as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{W}^{0}&=0\\
\mathcal{W}^{k+1}&=\mathcal{W}^{k}+\widetilde{H}(q^{k+1},p^{k+3/4})-\widetilde{H}(q^{k},p^{k+1/4}),\end{aligned}\right.

(II.22)

for
k=0\ldots K_{T}-1
, where
\widetilde{H}
denotes the Hamiltonian that is modified by the Fixman term (
II.16
), namely
{\widetilde{H}(q,p)=\tilde{V}(q)+\frac{1}{2}p^{\top}M^{-1}p}
.

For
\gamma=0
, the RATTLE dynamics (
II.17
)-(
II.19
) become deterministic (driven Hamiltonian dynamics) and the only remaining source of randomness in
Step 2
is the initialization of the momentum
p^{0}
. Furthermore, since in this case
p^{k+1/4}=p^{k}
and
p^{k+3/4}=p^{k+1}
, the work simplifies to a telescopic sum and just depends on the endpoints of the path:

\mathcal{W}^{K_{T}}=\widetilde{H}(q^{K_{T}},p^{K_{T}})-\widetilde{H}(q^{0},p^{0}).

Remark II.3
.

When the Gram tensor
G_{M}(q)
does not depend on
q
(an example is given in
Section

III.3
), the Fixman term just adds an overall shift to the energy
V
and need not be considered. In this case, the requirement on the initial and final velocity can also be relaxed to
v_{z}(t_{0})=v_{z}(t_{K_{T}})
and can be non-zero, assuming that the initial momentum
p^{0}
is still sampled from the appropriate subspace (see
B.2
and
B.10
). This is true in particular for the case of a linear CV, which, after a change of basis, can always be seen as representing a subset of
\ell
degrees of freedom. This is the standard setting treated in previous works
3
;
40
;
8
;
9
;
39
, where some degrees of freedom are driven through a non-equilibrium protocol.

The problem of constructing a steered path can be understood as transporting samples from the constrained target measure at the initial CV value
Z_{n}
to the final one
\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}
. Our method is based on a stochastic differential equation (with the auxiliary variable of the momentum), but other dynamics are possible, notably deterministic transformations
8
or a combination of stochastic and deterministic steps, where the underlying SDE is modified by a deterministic ‘escorting’ term
54
. The latter can also be understood as an instance of a stochastic normalizing flow
7
. These approaches are not easy to adapt to our context of a nonlinear CV, however, since they require to transport samples supported on the submanifolds
\Sigma_{z}
.

II.3
Reversibility of the algorithm

Our main theoretical result is the following theorem:

Theorem II.4
.

The MCMC algorithm presented in
Section

II.2
is reversible with respect to the target measure
\nu(\mathrm{d}q)
.

We prove this statement in
Appendix

B
. The basic ingredient of the proof is to introduce a backward version of the dynamics (associated to the forward dynamics) and use a version of the Jarzynski–Crooks equality
11
.

As a consequence of
Theorem

II.4
, the MCMC algorithm admits
\nu
as an invariant measure and, up to checking irreducibility which has to be done on a case-by-case basis, empirical averages over trajectories
(Q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
converge to averages with respect to
\nu
.

Remark II.5
.

In
Section

H.2
, we numerically demonstrate how the omission of the Fixman term or an inappropriate steering schedule can lead to a bias in the simulations, underlining that these two ingredients are indeed necessary for an unbiased algorithm.

II.4
A normalized version of the algorithm

The overall algorithm depends on many parameters:
\Delta t
,
\gamma
,
\sigma
,
M
, and the choice of the schedule
z(t)
and
v_{z}(t)
and transition time
T
. In order to reduce the number of parameters, we will use the following normalized version in the numerical examples: Assuming that the mass matrix
M=M\,\mathrm{Id}
is isotropic, we show in
Appendix

C
that the overall dynamics only depends on the two parameters

\alpha_{1}=\Delta t\gamma/(4M),\quad\alpha_{2}=\Delta t^{2}/(\beta M).

as well as the schedule
z(t_{k})
and the normalized velocities
\widetilde{v}_{z}(t_{k})=\Delta tv_{z}(t_{k})
. To fix these schedules, on can use the version given by (
II.9
)-(
II.10
) with an appropriate function
f
and choose the number of intermediate steps for a proposed jump as
K_{T}=\mathrm{ceil}\left(|\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}-Z_{n}|/\widetilde{v}\right)
, with an effective normalized velocity
\widetilde{v}
. With this, the algorithm is then fully governed by the three parameters
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
and
\widetilde{v}
.

For the parameter choice
\alpha_{1}=1
, the steered dynamics (
II.17
)-(
II.19
) reduce to overdamped Langevin dynamics (see
Appendix

D
). If
\xi
is linear in addition, so that after a suitable change of basis the CV can be identified with the first
\ell
degrees of freedom, the overall algorithm is equivalent to the ‘asymmetric’ algorithm presented in our earlier work
46
(up to a slight change of the definition of the steered path: here we first move the CV and then the remaining degrees of freedom). Therein, we also provided the continuous-time limit for this version of the algorithm together with the corresponding stochastic differential equation (SDE). For the choice of
\alpha_{1}=0
, the steered dynamics correspond to the Verlet scheme (Leapfrog dynamics) in the orthogonal degrees of freedom and are therefore a discretization of Hamiltonian dynamics with a time-dependent Hamiltonian. Note that in this case, the continuous-time limit of the dynamics
\Delta t\to 0
simply corresponds to taking
\alpha_{2}\to 0
and the acceptance of the move in
Step 3
is governed only by the (normalized) physical transition time
T/\sqrt{\beta M}=K_{T}\Delta t/\sqrt{\beta M}=\mathrm{ceil}\left(|\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}-Z_{n}|/\widetilde{v}\right)\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}
(see
Fig.

14
in the appendix for a numerical illustration).

III
Numerical examples

In this section, we apply the proposed CV-guided sampler to examples of increasing complexity, demonstrating in particular the advantage of deterministic underdamped steering dynamics compared to the previously considered overdamped version. We consider four model systems of increasing complexity: a theoretical model-system dubbed ‘Gaussian tunnel’ (
Section

III.1
), the one-dimensional
\phi^{4}
model from statistical physics (

Gaussian tunnel

We first consider a simple model with a linear CV as already introduced in previous work

\displaystyle\nu_{\rm CV}(\mathrm{d}z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(w\,\mathrm{e}^{-z^{2}/2}+(1-w)\,\mathrm{e}^{-(z-b)^{2}/2}\right)\mathrm{d}z,

(III.1)

\displaystyle\nu_{\perp}(\mathrm{d}x^{\perp}|z)

(III.2)

III.2
\phi^{4}
Model

As a second example we consider a field model from statistical physics, the one-dimensional
\phi^{4}
model (also considered previously
46
). It consists of
N
degrees of freedom
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
on a one-dimensional lattice and can be seen as a continuous version of the Ising model for ferromagnetism in solids. The target distribution is the Boltzmann–Gibbs probability distribution
\nu({\rm d}\phi)
with the energy (using the simplified version of the model considered in Ref.
19
)

\displaystyle\beta V(\phi)=\frac{a\beta N}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}(\phi_{i}-\phi_{i-1})^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4aN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-\phi_{i}^{2})^{2},

(III.3)

with Dirichlet boundary conditions
\phi_{0}=\phi_{N+1}=0
. For sufficiently low temperatures
1/\beta
, this system has two well-separated modes characterized by positive and negative magnetization
\overline{\phi}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}/N
concentrated at
\pm\overline{\phi}^{*}
for some
\overline{\phi}^{*}
which depends on
\beta
. We place ourselves in this bimodal phase by setting
\beta=20
and
a=0.1
. In many practical settings, one will be interested in systems with an external field
h
, which corresponds to adding a term
h\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}
to the potential
V(\phi)
. For the purpose of testing the algorithm, however, the case
h=0
is most illustrative since the two modes will have exactly the same weight due to the symmetry
V(\phi)=V(-\phi)
, and one can verify that the algorithm produces no bias with respect to the mode weights.

We use the magnetization
\overline{\phi}
as the collective variable, which is a natural order parameter of the system.
We run the algorithm on the system in a wavelet basis (see
Appendix

E
for details) since this order parameter then naturally emerges as the first component of the system.
To fix the proposal sampler in CV space, we first ran a short simulation using the Metropolis Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA)
44
;
43
, which, due to the local proposal updates of MALA, always stays within one mode. From this data, the mean and standard deviation of the observed mode were then estimated as
\overline{\phi}^{*}=0.79
and
\sigma=0.06
. Consequently, we used as the proposal sampler a Gaussian mixture model with two modes centered at
\pm\overline{\phi}^{*}
and of width
\sigma
.

Since we are again using a linear CV here, we use the same linear positions schedule
z(t_{k})
and constant velocity schedule
v_{z}(t_{k})
which we also used for the Gaussian tunnel. We compare the performance of the sampling algorithm over a grid of parameters
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
and
\widetilde{v}
, using the mode jump cost as a criterion (the average number of calls to the force between transitions between
\overline{\phi}>0
and
\overline{\phi}<0
). Optimal performance is again observed for the deterministic dynamics at
\alpha_{2}=0
(see
Fig.

16
in
Section

H.3
). Notably, as can be seen in
Fig.

4
, there is a performance increase by around two orders of magnitude passing from the overdamped to the deterministic dynamics. This is consistent with the experiments for the Gaussian tunnel. Away from the optimum, we see a white diagonal where no transitions were observed, corresponding to a particularly ‘bad’ physical transition time
T/\sqrt{\beta M}=K_{T}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}
. This might be a resonance effect due to the deterministic dynamics, but it is in any case far away from the parameter range of optimum performance.

For the optimal parameters, we also run a modified experiment where we deliberately bias the CV proposal sampler by putting a larger weight on the mode corresponding to negative magnetization. As we show in
Fig.

5
, this bias is accurately corrected and the obtained samples align perfectly with the target density.

Driving the system from one mode to another corresponds to creating a domain of opposite orientation from either end of the lattice and gradually increasing its size. We indeed observe in
Fig.

6
that ‘good’ transitions with a high probability of acceptance follow one of these two transition paths. The existence of multiple transition paths does not pose a difficulty per se for the algorithm presented here: the acceptance of a proposal move is only determined by the work accumulated along the way and thus the intermediate bimodality is not a problem for accurate sampling of the two modes and their relative weights.

III.3
Dimer in a solvent

We now apply the algorithm on a model with a non-linear collective variable
49
;
16
;
31
. It consists of
N
particles of equal masses
M
in a two-dimensional box of side length
L
with periodic boundary conditions. Two of the particles form a dimer; the remaining particles are designated as solvent particles. The solvent particles interact between them and with the dimer particles via the Weeks–Chandler–Andersen (WCA) pair potential, which is a truncated Lennard-Jones potential:

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{WCA}}(r)=\begin{cases}4\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{r^{*}}{r}\right)^{12}-\left(\frac{r^{*}}{r}\right)^{6}\right]+\epsilon\quad&\text{if }r\leq r_{0},\\
0\quad&\text{if }r>r_{0}.\end{cases}

Here,
r
is the distance between two particles,
r_{0}=2^{1/6}r^{*}
and
\epsilon
and
r^{*}
are positive constants.
The two particles of the dimer interact via a double-well potential

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{D}}(r)=h\left[1-\frac{(r-r_{0}-w)^{2}}{w^{2}}\right]^{2},

(III.4)

with the two positive parameters
h
and
w
. Denoting the particle positions by
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with each
q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the total potential energy is thus given by

\displaystyle V(q)

\displaystyle=V_{\mathrm{D}}(|q_{1}-q_{2}|)+\sum_{3\leq i<j\leq N}V_{\mathrm{WCA}}(|q_{i}-q_{j}|)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{3\leq j\leq N}V_{\mathrm{WCA}}(|q_{i}-q_{j}|).

(III.5)

The minima of
V_{\mathrm{D}}
are at
r=r_{0}
and
r=r_{0}+2w
, giving rise to a compact state and stretched state with an energy barrier of height
h
. Two configurations for the compact and the stretched state are shown in
Fig.

7
.

Figure 7:
Typical configurations for the dimer in the compact and the stretched state.

As a reaction coordinate, we use the normalized bond length

\displaystyle\xi(q)=\frac{|q_{1}-q_{2}|-r_{0}}{2w},

(III.6)

so that the compact and the stretched states correspond to
\xi(q)=0
and
\xi(q)=1
, respectively. As explained in
Section

F.1
, the Gram tensor is given by
G_{M}(q)=(2w^{2}M)^{-1}
. Since it does not depend on
q
, the Fixman term

V_{\mathrm{fix}}(q)=-\frac{1}{2\beta}\log(2w^{2}M)

is therefore a constant that does not change the dynamics and need not be considered. The Lagrange multipliers for the constrained dynamics can be obtained analytically for this system and CV (see
Section

F.2
). We make use of this since it is numerically beneficial, but in principle, one could equally use numerical solvers as explained in
Section

II.2.4
.

We ran numerical experiments for parameters
h=w=2
,
r^{*}=\epsilon=1
and
M=1
with
N=100
particles and a box of length
L=15
, in line with previous works
31
;
32
. For the CV sampler we use a Gaussian mixture model with modes of equal weight at
z=0
and
z=1
and width
\sigma_{\mathrm{prop}}=0.2
. We reject any unphysical proposals with
z<z_{\mathrm{min}}=-\frac{r_{0}}{2w}
and
z>z_{\mathrm{max}}=(L/\sqrt{2}-r_{0})/(2w)
since the distance
|q_{1}-q_{2}|
is always positive and the periodic boundary conditions (torus coordinates) impose a maximum possible distance.

We compare the performance of the algorithm for different parameter values using a mode jump cost that counts the number of calls to the force required to go from a state with
z<0.1
to a state with
z>0.9
or vice versa, in line with the ‘mean residence duration’ used in Ref.
33
.

Once again, we observe a large advantage in using the deterministic algorithm with
\alpha_{1}=0
, whose performance for different values of
\alpha_{2}
and
\widetilde{v}
are shown in
Fig.

8
, while we see almost no mode switches for the overdamped algorithm within the same computational budget (see
Section

H.4
).

In
Fig.

9
, we show the free energy profile obtained by running the algorithm for the optimized parameters until convergence together with the free energy in the absence of solvent particles (see
Section

F.3
for its derivation). As one can see, the presence of the solvent particles increases the likelihood of the compact state (lowering its free energy in comparison with the stretched state). The result agrees almost perfectly with a result obtained from thermodynamic integration (TI) (see Ref.
32
for a theoretical introduction). In thermodynamic integration, the system is sampled under the constraint
\xi(q)=z_{i}
for a sequential series of CV-values
z_{i}
on a grid to estimate the mean force. This demonstrates that the inaccuracy of the proposal potential has been corrected by the algorithm.

Note that this model was also considered by the authors of Ref.
40
, Contrary to our approach, however, they chose the full dimer configuration as the CV,
\xi(q)=(q_{1},q_{2})^{\top}
, and used a proposal kernel that samples a dimer configuration
\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}
conditioned on the current configuration
Z_{n}
with a new distance
|q_{1}-q_{2}|
but the same overall rotation angle.

III.4
Polymer in a solvent

As a final example, we consider a toy molecular system that consists of a 9-bead polymer embedded in a three-dimensional box with 200 solvent particles, similar to a model introduced in Ref.
52
. The polymer beads and the solvent particles all interact via a Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential. Within the polymer, the nearest and second-nearest neighbor distances (and hence the binding angle) of the polymer beads are softly constrained with a harmonic potential. In addition, a bi-modal potential is imposed on the end-to-end distance of the polymer, giving rise to two stable confirmations in an open and a closed state, see
Fig.

10
. Denoting by
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,9}
the polymer coordinates and by
\{q_{i}\}_{i=10,\dots,N}
the solvent coordinates, the total energy is given by

\displaystyle V(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{8}V_{1}(|q_{i}-q_{i+1}|)+\sum_{i=1}^{7}V_{2}(|q_{i}-q_{i+2}|)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq 6\\
i+3\leq j\leq 9\end{subarray}}V_{\mathrm{LJ}}(|q_{i}-q_{j}|)+V_{e}(|q_{1}-q_{9}|)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq 9\\
10\leq j\leq N\end{subarray}}V_{\mathrm{LJ}}(|q_{i}-q_{j}|)

\displaystyle+\sum_{10\leq i<j\leq N}V_{\mathrm{LJ}}(|q_{i}-q_{j}|),

(III.7)

with a Lennard-Jones interaction
V_{\mathrm{LJ}}
, harmonic potentials
V_{\kappa}(r)=\frac{k_{\kappa}}{2}(r-d_{\kappa})^{2}
for
\kappa\in\{1,2\}
and a double-well potential (acting on the end-to-end distance)
V_{e}(r)=\frac{k_{e}}{4}(\frac{r-d_{e}}{a})^{4}-k_{e}(\frac{r-d_{e}}{a})^{2}
with two local minima at
r=d_{e}\pm a\sqrt{2}
. Precise parameter values and implementation details are provided in
Appendix

G
.

We test the algorithm for two different choices of CV: (i) the end-to-end distance,
\xi(q)=|q_{1}-q_{9}|
, a one-dimensional CV, and (ii) the entire polymer chain,
\xi(q)=(q_{1},\ldots q_{9})
, a 27-dimensional CV. For the latter, we define a proposal sampler with the help of a normalizing flow.

When running simple MALA dynamics on this system, mode transitions are only observed very rarely, necessitating the use of an enhanced sampling technique. To obtain a reference dataset to compare against, we ran biased dynamics (removing the potential energy
V_{e}
on the end-to-end distance) and unbiased them via reweighting.

An obvious choice of CV that resolves the bimodality is the end-to-end distance. Since it is one-dimensional, it is easy to construct a proposal distribution in the form of a Gaussian mixture. For simplicity, we extract the position of the modes and their width from short Langevin simulations on the full system (see
Appendix

G
). A rough grid search to optimize the parameters of the algorithm is shown in
Fig.

11
, with the best achieved inverse mode jump cost at around
9\times 10^{-5}
. In contrast, the overdamped algorithm with
\alpha_{1}=1
does not show any transitions within the same parameter range and computational budget, giving an upper limit of the performance of at most
6\times 10^{-7}
; it is thus at least two orders of magnitude worse in performance.

Another possible choice of collective variable in this case is to use the positions of the beads of the entire polymer configuration. Here, a good proposal distribution requires a careful parameterization, for which we use a normalizing flow (see
Appendix

G
). Since our primary interest is in the performance of the algorithm, we trained our proposal sampler on already existing reference data. In practice, one could use adaptive schemes in which the CV-sampler is progressively trained while running the MCMC sampler
19
;
52
. Note that for this choice of CV, the steered dynamics in
Step 2
of the algorithm effectively only acts on the solvent particles, since the schedule already imposes the evolution of the entire polymer. Running the algorithm, we again observe that the deterministic dynamics (
\alpha_{1}=0
) gives good results with frequent mode switches while the overdamped version (
\alpha_{1}=1
) does not work at all. Strikingly, for the optimized parameters, we achieve an overall acceptance rate of 99% with this algorithm. Since the proposals are drawn independently, this corresponds almost to the optimum performance with maximum decorrelation within the polymer degrees of freedom. A rough grid search for the deterministic algorithm is shown in
Fig.

12
together with the marginal distribution of the end-to-end distance. Comparing the optimal mode jump cost obtained with the two choices of CV, we see an advantage of around one order of magnitude in using the positions of all polymer beads over the end-to-end distance between first and last bead as a CV. This only accounts for the calls to the force, however, and not for the effort in learning the proposal sampler in CV space.

IV
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we present an MCMC algorithm with non-local proposals in a collective variable space. This CV space may be high-dimensional, provided a good proposal sampler can still be learned. We generalize related algorithms by considering the underdamped Langevin dynamics and a non-linear collective variable and we prove reversibility of the resulting scheme. We provide a detailed description of the algorithm so that it is readily implementable and we illustrate its performance on a number of examples, ranging from a toy low-dimensional example to molecular systems. We demonstrate a significant performance advantage in using Hamiltonian over overdamped steered Langevin dynamics.

We leave certain questions for future investigations, notably the performance of the algorithm with respect to different choices of CV, as well as the choice of schedule (path) in CV space and the training of the proposal sampler. Furthermore, it would be very interesting to apply this algorithm to more complex molecular systems such as the conformational equilibrium of biomolecules, which is something we leave for future work.

Appendix B
Proof of the reversibility of the algorithm

The objective of this section is to prove the reversibility of the algorithm introduced in Section
II.2
(namely
Theorem

II.4

B.1
Introducing notation

This subsection largely uses tools and results from Ref.
32
. Let us introduce a number of quantities which we will need later on.
A central quantity of interest is the free energy

\xi

\displaystyle F(z)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\int_{\Sigma(z)}Z_{q}^{-1}e^{-\beta V(q)}\delta_{\xi(q)-z}(\mathrm{d}q).

(B.1)

The phase space associated with constraints on the collective variable
\xi
and effective velocity

is denoted as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})=\left\{(q,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\,\Big|\,\xi(q)=z,\,v_{\xi}(q,p)=v_{z}\right\}.

(B.2)

For a fixed position
q
, we denote the affine space of constrained momenta as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\middle|\,v_{\xi}(q,p)=v_{z}\right\}.

(B.3)

Note that
T^{*}_{q}\Sigma(z)=\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(0)
, which was defined in (
II.13
).
The Boltzmann–Gibbs probability distribution can also be defined on these constrained spaces as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mu_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p):=\frac{{\rm e}^{-\beta H(q,p)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{z,v_{z}}}\sigma_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p),\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{z,v_{z}}:=\int_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})}{\rm e}^{-\beta H}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})},\end{cases}

(B.4)

where
\sigma_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p)
is the phase space Liouville measure on
\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})
.

Using the Fixman term
V_{\mathrm{fix}}
introduced in
Eq.

II.16
, it is possible to relate the free energy (
B.1
) to the normalisation of the restricted Boltzmann–Gibbs measure (
B.4
). More precisely, the modified potential
\widetilde{V}(q)=V(q)+V_{\mathrm{fix}}(q)
gives rise to a new Hamiltonian
denoted
\widetilde{H}(p,q)=H(q,p)+V_{\mathrm{fix}}(q)
and an associated constrained measure
\widetilde{\mu}_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}}
with normalizing constant
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{z,v_{z}}
(using the formulas in (
B.4
) with
H
replaced by
\widetilde{H}
).
We then obtain the following reformulation of free energy differences in terms of ratios of the normalizing constants
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{z,v_{z}}
.

Lemma B.1
.

For all
(z,v_{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{z,v_{z}}=(\det M)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(2\pi\beta^{-1}\right)^{\frac{d-\ell}{2}}\int_{\Sigma(z)}e^{-\beta V(q)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}v_{z}^{\top}G_{M}(q)^{-1}v_{z}\right)\delta_{\xi(q)-z}(\mathrm{d}q).

In particular, for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{z_{1},0}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{z_{2},0}}=e^{-\beta(F(z_{1})-F(z_{2}))}.

(B.5)

Proof.

The proof relies on Ref.
32
.
From Sec. 2.3 therein, we restate the following two identities: By the co-area formula, one can show that

\delta_{\xi(q)-z}(\mathrm{d}q)=\left({\rm det}\,M\right)^{-1/2}\big|{\rm det}\,G_{M}(q)\big|^{-1/2}\,\sigma^{M}_{\Sigma(z)}(\mathrm{d}q).

(B.6)

Furthermore, the measure
\sigma_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})}
can be written as a product of two measures:

\sigma_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p)=\sigma^{M^{-1}}_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}p)\,\sigma^{M}_{\Sigma(z)}(\mathrm{d}q),

(B.7)

where we denote
by
\sigma^{M}_{\Sigma(z)}(\mathrm{d}q)
the surface measure on
\Sigma(z)
induced by the scalar
product
\langle q,\tilde{q}\rangle_{M}=q^{\top}M\tilde{q}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and,
for a given
q\in\Sigma(z)
, by

\sigma^{M^{-1}}_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}p)
the
surface measure on the affine
space
\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})
,
induced by the scalar product

\langle p,\tilde{p}\rangle_{M^{-1}}=p^{\top}M^{-1}\tilde{p}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For more precise definitions of these measures, we refer
to Ref.
31
, Sections 3.2.1 and 3.3.2

and the references therein.

Using the above identities, we write

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{z,v_{z}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})}e^{-\beta\widetilde{H}(q,p)}\sigma_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}(z,v_{z})}}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z,v_{z})}e^{-\beta H(q,p)}(\det(G_{M}(q)))^{-1/2}\sigma^{M^{-1}}_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}p)\,\sigma^{M}_{\Sigma(z)}(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma(z)}\int_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}p^{\top}M^{-1}p\right)\sigma^{M^{-1}}_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}p)\;e^{-\beta V(q)}(\det(G_{M}(q)))^{-1/2}\sigma^{M}_{\Sigma(z)}(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma(z)}\left(2\pi\beta^{-1}\right)^{\frac{d-\ell}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}v_{z}^{\top}G_{M}(q)^{-1}v_{z}\right)\;e^{-\beta V(q)}(\det(G_{M}(q)))^{-1/2}\sigma^{M}_{\Sigma(z)}(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=(\det M)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(2\pi\beta^{-1}\right)^{\frac{d-\ell}{2}}\int_{\Sigma(z)}e^{-\beta V(q)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}v_{z}^{\top}G_{M}(q)^{-1}v_{z}\right)\delta_{\xi(q)-z}(\mathrm{d}q).

Equation (
B.5
) follows by taking
v_{z}=0
.
For the last but one equality in the series of equalities above, we use a computation from Sec. 5.1 in Ref.
32
, namely, for fixed
q
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}p^{\top}M^{-1}p\right)\sigma^{M^{-1}}_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})}(\mathrm{d}p)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}v_{z}^{\top}G_{M}(q)^{-1}v_{z}\right)\int_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(0)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}p^{\top}M^{-1}p\right)\sigma^{M^{-1}}_{\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(0)}(\mathrm{d}p)

\displaystyle=\left(2\pi\beta^{-1}\right)^{\frac{d-\ell}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}v_{z}^{\top}G_{M}(q)^{-1}v_{z}\right).

The first equality follows from a change of variable: the space
\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(v_{z})
is composed of momenta fulfilling
0=v_{\xi}(q,p)-v_{z}=\nabla\xi(q)^{\top}M^{-1}\left(p-\nabla\xi(q)G_{M}^{-1}(q)v_{z}\right)
, and therefore, when replacing
p
by
p-\nabla\xi(q)G_{M}^{-1}(q)v_{z}
, the resulting integral is taken over the space
\Sigma_{v_{\xi}(q,\cdot)}(0)
.
∎

Remark B.2
.

When the Gram tensor
G_{M}(q)
is independent of
q
, the introduction of the Fixman term becomes unnecessary and one furthermore has that

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{z_{1},v_{z}}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{z_{2},v_{z}}}=\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{z_{1},v_{z}}}{\mathcal{Z}_{z_{2},v_{z}}}=e^{-\beta(F(z_{1})-F(z_{2}))}

(B.8)

for any velocity
v_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
.

B.2
Properties of the steered dynamics

In this section, we state a number of properties of the RATTLE scheme introduced in (
II.17
)-(
II.19
). Since we follow dynamics for the energy modified by the Fixman term, in principle we would need to put a tilde on all associated measures and quantities, but we omit it here for the sake of readability and just consider the dynamics for a general potential
V
.

B.2.1
Forward steered dynamics

Let us make two remarks concerning the forward steered dynamics (
II.17
)-(
II.19
)

Remark B.3
.

In
Eq.

II.18
, the constraint
(C_{p})
yields a unique well-defined Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{k+3/4}
which can be obtained by solving the linear equation

\displaystyle v_{z}(t_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})^{T}M^{-1}p^{k+1/2}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})^{T}M^{-1}\nabla V(q^{k+1})+\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})^{T}M^{-1}\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\lambda^{k+3/4},

since the Gram tensor
G_{M}(q^{k+1})=\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})^{T}M^{-1}\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})
is invertible by assumption.
In contrast, the constraint
(C_{q})
leads to a non-linear equation for
\lambda^{k+1/2}
, which is solved using Newton’s method. In practice, we never encountered non-convergence of Newton’s method in our numerical experiments. See however Ref.
33
and Sec. 3.2.1 of Ref.
32
) for techniques to deal with this difficulty, using so-called reversibility checks.

Remark B.4
.

Notice that the velocity schedule
(v_{z}^{(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}(t_{k}))_{0\leq n\leq K_{T}}
can in principle be chosen independently of the position schedule
(z^{(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
. It is only in the continuous-time limit that the schedule
z(t)
directly imposes the velocity schedule as
v_{z}(t)=\dot{z}(t)
. Still, certain choices can be numerically beneficial, as explained in Ref.
32
, Sec. 5.4.1
. In (
II.18
), the unconstrained part of the update is given by

\widetilde{q}^{k+1}=q^{k}+\Delta t\ M^{-1}p^{k+1/4}-\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\ M^{-1}\nabla V(q^{k}),

which is followed by a projection with
q^{k+1}=\widetilde{q}^{k+1}+\Delta tM^{-1}\nabla\xi(q^{k})\lambda^{k+1/2}
. The standard choice
v_{z}(t_{k})=\frac{z(t_{k+1})-z(t_{k})}{\Delta t}
ensures that
\xi(\widetilde{q}^{k+1})=z(t_{k+1})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
. However, such a choice is not possible in our context since it would violate the reversibility property (
II.8
).
One simple choice that fulfills the requirements is presented in
Section

II.2.2
. Another canonical choice with a meaningful limit in continuous time that satisfies the reversibility condition is

v_{z}(t_{k})=\frac{z(t_{k+1})-z(t_{k-1})}{2\Delta t},0\leq k\leq K_{T},

requiring just the auxiliary extension of the position schedule to times
t_{-1}
and
t_{K_{T}+1}
. To fulfill the requirement that
v_{z}(t_{0})=v_{z}(t_{K_{T}})=0
, this implies that
z(t_{-1})=z(t_{1})
and
z(t_{K_{T}+1})=z(t_{K_{T}-1})
.

B.2.2
Backward steered dynamics

For given position schedule
(z(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
and velocity schedule
(v_{z}(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
, we define the following backward process. Starting from
(q^{\mathrm{b},0},p^{\mathrm{b},0})\in\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z(t_{K_{T}}),v_{z}(t_{K_{T}}))
, iterate the following procedure on
0\leq k^{\prime}\leq K_{T}-1
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/4}=p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}}-\frac{\Delta t}{4}\gamma_{P}(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}})M^{-1}(p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/4}+p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}})+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t}{2}}\sigma_{P}(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}}){\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}}

(B.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/2}=p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/4}+\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\nabla V(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}})}+\nabla\xi(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}})\lambda^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/2}&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1}=q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}}-\Delta t\ M^{-1}p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/2}\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\xi(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1})=z(t_{K_{T}-k^{\prime}-1})&(C_{q})\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+3/4}=p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/2}+\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\nabla V(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1})}+\nabla\xi(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1})\lambda^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+3/4}&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\nabla\xi(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1})^{T}M^{-1}p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+3/4}=v_{z}(t_{K_{T}-k^{\prime}-1})&(C_{p})\end{cases}

(B.10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1}=p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+3/4}-\frac{\Delta t}{4}\gamma_{P}(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1})M^{-1}(p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+3/4}+p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1})+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t}{2}}\sigma_{P}(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1}){\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/2},

(B.11)

where
({\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}})
and
({\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/2})
are independent sequences of i.i.d. centered Gaussian random vectors with covariance matrix
\mathrm{Id}_{d}
and
\lambda^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/2}
and
\lambda^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+3/4}
are Lagrange multipliers such that the conditions
(C_{q})
and
(C_{p})
are fulfilled.

B.2.3
Relation between processes with opposite endpoints

Thanks to the reversibility property of the schedule (
II.7
)-(
II.8
), we can relate the law of the forward and backward steered dynamics with schedules with swapped endpoints one to another: With the right initialization, the solution of the backward dynamics for a schedule with endpoints
(z,\widetilde{z})
has the same law as the forward dynamics for a schedule with endpoints
(\widetilde{z},z)
up to a momentum flip. We make the statement more precise in the following lemma.

Lemma B.5
.

Consider a forward process
\left((q^{k},p^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}},p^{K_{T}}\right)
for a schedule
(z^{(\widetilde{z},z)}(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
,
(v_{z}^{(\widetilde{z},z)}(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
with initial conditions

(q^{0},p^{0})\sim\mu_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z^{(\widetilde{z},z)}(t_{0}),v_{z}^{(\widetilde{z},z)}(t_{0})\right)}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p),

(B.12)

and a backward process
\left((q^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k+1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}\right)
for a schedule with swapped endpoints
(z^{(z,\widetilde{z})}(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
,
(v_{z}^{(z,\widetilde{z})}(t_{k}))_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}}
and initial conditions

(q^{{\rm b},0},p^{{\rm b},0})\sim\mu_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z^{(z,\widetilde{z})}(t_{K_{T}}),v_{z}^{(z,\widetilde{z})}(t_{K_{T}})\right)}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p).

(B.13)

Then, this backward process has the same law as the momentum flipped version of the forward process,
\left((q^{k},-p^{k},-p^{k+1/4},-p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}},-p^{K_{T}}\right)
.

Proof.

The initial distribution of the forward process satisfies

\mu_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z^{{(\widetilde{z},z)}}(t_{0}),v_{z}^{{(\widetilde{z},z)}}(t_{0})\right)}=\mu_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z^{(z,\widetilde{z})}(t_{K_{T}}),-v_{z}^{(z,\widetilde{z})}(t_{K_{T}})\right)}
by the symmetry properties (
II.7
)-(
II.8
) of the schedule. Since

(q,p)\in\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z,-v_{z}\right)\Leftrightarrow(q,-p)\in\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z,v_{z}\right),

and from the definition of the constrained measure in (
B.4
) with the symmetry of the Hamiltonian
H(q,p)=H(q,-p)
, this implies that
(q^{\mathrm{b},0},p^{\mathrm{b},0})
and
(q^{0},-p^{0})
are indeed drawn from the same distribution. From there, using again the symmetry of the schedule, one can easily verify that the equations describing the forward dynamics with endpoints
(\widetilde{z},z)
for
(q^{k},-p^{k})
are exactly the same as the equations describing the backward dynamics with end points
(z,\widetilde{z})
for
(q^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k})
.
∎

B.2.4
Symplecticity and reversibility of the deterministic flows

Lemma B.6
.

Consider the the numerical flow

\Phi^{k}\ :\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\displaystyle\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z(t_{k}),v_{z}(t_{k})\right)&\to&\displaystyle\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z(t_{k+1}),v_{z}(t_{k+1})\right)\\[10.0pt]
(q^{k},p^{k+1/4})&\mapsto&(q^{k+1},p^{k+3/4})\end{array}\right.

(B.14)

associated with the deterministic part of the forward dynamics (
II.18
) as well as the flow

\Phi^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}}\ :\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\displaystyle\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z(t_{K_{T}-k^{\prime}}),v_{z}(t_{K_{T}-k^{\prime}})\right)&\to&\displaystyle\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z(t_{K_{T}-k^{\prime}-1}),v_{z}(t_{K_{T}-k^{\prime}-1})\right)\\[10.0pt]
(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}},p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/4})&\mapsto&(q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1},p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+3/4})\end{array}\right.

(B.15)

associated with the deterministic part of the backward dynamics (
B.10
).

The flow
\Phi^{k}
is a symplectic map for all
k\in\{0,\ldots,K_{T}-1\}
, and the same holds true for
\Phi^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}}
for all
k^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,K_{T}-1\}
.

To prove this statement, we show that the 2-form
\mathrm{d}q\wedge\mathrm{d}p
is preserved, where
\wedge
denotes the wedge-product (see Ref.
37
for a textbook introduction to symplectic structures). For the simplest version with a constant constraint, this is a standard result found for example in Refs.
21
and
30
.
We follow here the same arguments as in Ref.
30
. The differentials obey:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/4}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathrm{d}(\nabla V(q^{k}))+\mathrm{d}(\nabla\xi(q^{k})\lambda^{k+1/2})

(B.16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k}+\Delta t\ M^{-1}\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}

(B.17)

\displaystyle\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})^{\top}\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}

\displaystyle=0

(B.18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}p^{k+3/4}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathrm{d}(\nabla V(q^{k+1}))+\mathrm{d}(\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\lambda^{k+3/4}).

(B.19)

Denoting the Hessian of
V
by
\nabla^{2}V
, we have
\mathrm{d}(\nabla V(q^{k}))=\nabla^{2}V(q^{k})\mathrm{d}q^{k}
. Taking the wedge product at the endpoints, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+3/4}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathrm{d}(\nabla V(q^{k+1}))+\mathrm{d}(\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\lambda^{k+3/4})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\nabla^{2}V(q^{k+1})\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}+\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}(\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\lambda^{k+3/4})

(B.20)

As in the proof by Ref.
30
, we make use of the following two lemmata.

Lemma B.7
.

Let
\mathrm{d}u
be an arbitrary differential in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
A
be any
n\times n
real symmetric matrix. Then

\mathrm{d}u\wedge(A\mathrm{d}u)=0.

Proof.

Writing this expression component-wise, we have

\mathrm{d}u\wedge(A\mathrm{d}u)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}u_{i}\wedge(A_{ij}\mathrm{d}u_{j})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\mathrm{d}u_{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}u_{j}=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ji}\mathrm{d}u_{j}\wedge\mathrm{d}u_{i}=-\mathrm{d}u\wedge(A\mathrm{d}u)

by the symmetry of
A
and the antisymmetry of the wedge-product, and thus
\mathrm{d}u\wedge(A\mathrm{d}u)=0
.
∎

Lemma B.8
.

Let
\mathrm{d}u
and
\mathrm{d}v
be arbitrary differentials in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, respectively, and let
B
be any
n\times m
real matrix, then

\mathrm{d}u\wedge(B\mathrm{d}v)=(B^{\top}\mathrm{d}u)\wedge\mathrm{d}v.

Proof.

Writing this expression component-wise, we have

\mathrm{d}u\wedge(B\mathrm{d}v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathrm{d}u_{i}\wedge(B_{ij}\mathrm{d}v_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(B_{ij}\mathrm{d}u_{i})\wedge\mathrm{d}v_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(B^{\top}_{ji}\mathrm{d}u_{i})\wedge\mathrm{d}v_{j}=(B^{\top}\mathrm{d}u)\wedge\mathrm{d}v,

which is the desired result.
∎

Since the Hessian is symmetric, the second term in (
B.20
) vanishes by
B.7
. The third term in (
B.20
) also vanishes:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}(\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\lambda^{k+3/4})=\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\mathrm{d}\lambda^{k+3/4}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda^{k+3/4}_{i}\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge(\nabla^{2}\xi_{i})(q^{k+1})\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}=0,

where
\xi_{i}
is the
i
-th component of the CV. Indeed, thanks to
B.8
, we have
\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\mathrm{d}\lambda^{k+3/4}=(\nabla\xi(q^{k+1}))^{\top}\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\lambda^{k+3/4}
which is zero due to (
B.18
). The final term equally vanishes since the Hessian is symmetric and one can thus apply
B.7
to each term of the sum.

We therefore have that

\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+3/4}=\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}.

Using (
B.17
), we can continue (with the same reasoning as before)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q^{k+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}+\Delta t\ M^{-1}\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/4}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathrm{d}(\nabla V(q^{k}))+\mathrm{d}(\nabla\xi(q^{k})\lambda^{k+1/2})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/4}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}(\nabla V(q^{k}))+\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}(\nabla\xi(q^{k})\lambda^{k+1/2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/4}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\nabla^{2}V(q^{k})\mathrm{d}q^{k}+\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\left(\nabla\xi(q^{k})\mathrm{d}\lambda^{k+1/2}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}^{k+1/2}(\nabla^{2}\xi_{i})(q^{k})\mathrm{d}q^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/4}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\nabla^{2}V(q^{k})\mathrm{d}q^{k}+(\nabla\xi(q^{k}))^{\top}\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}\lambda^{k+1/2}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}^{k+1/2}\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge(\nabla^{2}\xi_{i})(q^{k})\mathrm{d}q^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}q^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{k+1/4}.

Following exactly the same arguments, one can equally show that
\mathrm{d}q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1/4}=\mathrm{d}q^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+1}\wedge\mathrm{d}p^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}+3/4}
. This concludes the proof of
B.6
.

In all of the following, we assume that the time-step
\Delta t
is sufficiently small so that, along the trajectory, the forward and backward flow associated with the deterministic parts (
II.18
) and (
B.10
) satisfy a reversibility property, see e.g. Sect. VII.1.4 of Ref.
21
and Sect. 5.4.3 of Ref.
32
, as well as Lemma 2.9 in Ref.
33
where a precise setting is exhibited for which this is satisfied. More precisely, we assume that the following pointwise equality holds for the forward and backward flows
\Phi^{k}
and
\Phi^{{\rm b},k^{\prime}}
introduced in (
B.14
) and (
B.15
). For all the configurations which are visited and all
k\in\{1,\ldots,K_{T}\}
:

\displaystyle\Phi^{{\rm b},K_{T}-k}\circ\Phi^{k-1}=\operatorname{Id}.

(B.21)

B.2.5
Jarzynski–Crooks equality

We are now in position to state the Jarzynski–Crooks formula. We state it here in a slightly more general form than in Ref.
32
, but otherwise follow exactly the presentation there.

Theorem B.9
.

Consider the distribution
\mu_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}(z(t),v_{z}(t))}

and its normalizing constant
\mathcal{Z}_{z(t),v_{z}(t)}
defined in (
B.4
).
Denote by
\left((q^{k},p^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}},p^{K_{T}}\right)
the solution of the forward discretized Langevin dynamics (
II.17
)-(
II.19
) with initial conditions
distributed according to

(q^{0},p^{0})\sim\mu_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z(t_{0}),v_{z}(t_{0})\right)}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p),

(B.22)

and by
\left((q^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k+1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}\right)
the solution of the discretized
backward Langevin dynamics (
B.9
)-(
B.11
) with initial conditions distributed according to

(q^{{\rm b},0},p^{{\rm b},0})\sim\mu_{\Sigma_{\xi,v_{\xi}}\left(z(t_{K_{T}}),v_{z}(t_{K_{T}})\right)}(\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}p).

(B.23)

Then, the following Jarzynski–Crooks identity holds: for any bounded
discrete path functional
\varphi_{[0,K_{T}]}
,

\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{z(K_{T}),v_{z}(t_{K_{T}})}}{\mathcal{Z}_{z(t_{0}),v_{z}(t_{0})}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi_{[0,K_{T}]}\left((q^{k},p^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}},p^{K_{T}}\right)\,{\rm e}^{-\beta\mathcal{W}^{K_{T}}}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi^{\rm r}_{[0,K_{T}]}\left((q^{{\rm b},k},p^{{\rm b},k},p^{{\rm b},k+1/4},p^{{\rm b},k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{{\rm b},K_{T}},p^{{\rm b},K_{T}}\right)\right)},

(B.24)

where
\mathcal{W}^{K_{T}}
is computed according to (
II.22
) with
\widetilde{H}=H
, and

(\,\cdot\,)^{\rm r}
denotes the composition with
the operation of time reversal of paths:

\displaystyle\varphi_{[0,K_{T}]}^{\rm r}

\displaystyle\left((q^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k+1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{[0,K_{T}]}\left((q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k-1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k-3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},0},p^{\mathrm{b},0}\right).

(B.25)

Proof.

The proof is the same as the proof of Ref.
32
, Theorem 5.5
. The only difference lies in the slightly different momentum constraint
(C_{p})
for the forward and backward dynamics. Since we show in
B.6
that the forward and backward deterministic flows are symplectic, the proof goes through in the same way.
∎

B.3
Proof of reversibility

We are now in a position to prove
Theorem

II.4
, namely that the algorithm presented in
Section

II.2
is reversible with respect to the target probability measure
\nu
.
Assume that
Q_{n}
is distributed according to
\nu
. The aim is to prove that
(Q_{n},Q_{n+1})
has the same law as
(Q_{n+1},Q_{n})
. In order to study the law of
(Q_{n},Q_{n+1})
, let us consider a bounded measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Recall that
Z_{n}=\xi(Q_{n})
and
\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}=\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})
. One has:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\varphi(Q_{n},Q_{n+1}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})\mathbf{1}_{U_{n+1}\leq\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},Q_{n})\mathbf{1}_{U_{n+1}>\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})\left[1\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},Q_{n})\left[1-1\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}\right)\right]\right),

where here and in the following,
a\wedge b=\min(a,b)
.
The expectation value is taken over
Q_{n}\sim\nu
(which fixes
Z_{n}
), and all the random variables used to generate
Q_{n+1}
: sampling
\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}
from the density
\rho(Z_{n},\cdot)
, following the steered dynamics to obtain
\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}
and accepting or rejecting the final move. The expectation value over
Q_{n}
can also be understood in two steps:

•

First, sample
Z_{n}\sim\xi\#\nu
, where

(\xi\#\nu)(\mathrm{d}z)=\exp(-\beta F(z))\,\mathrm{d}z

denotes the image of the probability measure
\nu
by
\xi
(see (
B.1
) for the definition of the free energy
F
).

•

Second, sample a full state
Q_{n}
from
\nu(\mathrm{d}q\,|\,\xi(q)=Z_{n})
, where
\nu(\mathrm{d}q\,|\,\xi(q)=z)
denotes the the family of conditional measures

\nu(\mathrm{d}q\,|\,\xi(q)=z)=\frac{Z_{q}^{-1}e^{-\beta V(q)}\delta_{\xi(q)-z}(\mathrm{d}q)}{Z(z)},

where
Z(z)=Z_{q}^{-1}\int_{\Sigma(z)}e^{-\beta V(q)}\delta_{\xi(q)-z}(\mathrm{d}q)=\exp(-\beta F(z))
is the normalizing constant.

By conditioning on the collective variables
(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})
, one gets

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})\left[1\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{z,\widetilde{z}}\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})\left[1\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}),\xi(Q_{n}))}{\rho(\xi(Q_{n}),\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}))}\right)\right]\middle|(\xi(Q_{n}),\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}))=(z,\widetilde{z})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\exp(-\beta F(z))\rho(z,\widetilde{z})\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}\widetilde{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{z,\widetilde{z}}\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})\left[\rho(\xi(Q_{n}),\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}))\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\rho(\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}),\xi(Q_{n}))\right)\right]\middle|(\xi(Q_{n}),\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}))=(z,\widetilde{z})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\exp(-\beta F(z))\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}\widetilde{z}.

The conditional expectation value depends on the full steered path (and not just on the endpoints) via the work defined by (
II.22
), which we can rewrite here as

\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{T}-1}\left(\widetilde{H}(q^{k+1},p^{k+3/4})-\widetilde{H}(q^{k},p^{k+1/4})\right).

(B.26)

Notice that the work of a time-reversed path is the negative of the work of the path without time-reversal:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\left((q^{K_{T}-k},p^{K_{T}-k-1/4},p^{K_{T}-k-3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{T}-1}\left(\widetilde{H}(q^{K_{T}-k-1},p^{K_{T}-k-3/4})-\widetilde{H}(q^{K_{T}-k},p^{K_{T}-k-1/4})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{T}-1}\left(\widetilde{H}(q^{k},p^{k+1/4})-\widetilde{H}(q^{k+1},p^{k+3/4})\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right).

(B.27)

Using the generalized Jarzynski–Crooks identity, one gets, introducing the notation
\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)
for the forward process and
\left((q^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k+1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}\right)
for the backward process as in
Theorem

B.9
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(Q_{n},\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})\left[\rho(\xi(Q_{n}),\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}))\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\rho(\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}),\xi(Q_{n}))\right)\right]\Big|(\xi(Q_{n}),\xi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}))=(z,\widetilde{z})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{0},q^{K_{T}})\Big[\rho(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\left(\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{K_{T}}),\xi(q^{0}))\right)\Big]\Big|(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))=(z,\widetilde{z})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{0},q^{K_{T}})\Big[\left(\exp\left(\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\rho(\xi(q^{K_{T}}),\xi(q^{0}))\Big]\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)\right)\Big|(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))=(z,\widetilde{z})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:Jarz_Crooks_generalized}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}},q^{\mathrm{b},0})\Big[\left(\exp\left(\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k-1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k-3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},0}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\,\rho(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}))\Big]\Big|(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}))=(\widetilde{z},z)\Bigr)\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}(\widetilde{z},v_{z}(t_{K_{T}}))}{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}(z,v_{z}(t_{0}))}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:free_energy_relations}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}},q^{\mathrm{b},0})\Big[\left(\exp\left(\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k-1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}-k-3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},0}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\,\rho(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}))\Big]\Big|(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}))=(\widetilde{z},z)\Bigr)\exp(-\beta(F(\widetilde{z})-F(z)))

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:work_rev_symmetry}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}},q^{\mathrm{b},0})\Big[\left(\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k+1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\,\rho(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}))\Big]\Big|(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}))=(\widetilde{z},z)\Bigr)\exp(-\beta(F(\widetilde{z})-F(z))).

(B.28)

In the last but one step, we used the assumption (
II.6
) that the initial and final velocity is zero,
v_{z}(t_{0})=v_{z}(t_{K_{T}})=0
, and applied the identity (
B.5
).
For the final step, we used the symmetry property (
B.27
) of the work function.

Remark B.10
.

As mentioned in
B.2
, when
G_{M}
does not depend on
q
, there is no Fixman term to be considered and (
B.8
) holds. One can use this equation instead of (
B.5
) in the computation above, and this in turn implies that reversibility can be proven only assuming that the initial and final velocities are equal i.e.
v_{z}(t_{0})=v_{z}(t_{K_{T}})
, but not necessarily zero (see condition (

)).

We can use
B.5
to rewrite the latter expectation value over a backward path as one over a forward path as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}},q^{\mathrm{b},0})\Big[\left(\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{\mathrm{b},k},p^{\mathrm{b},k+1/4},p^{\mathrm{b},k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\,\rho(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}))\Big]\Big|(\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},0}),\xi(q^{\mathrm{b},K_{T}}))=(\widetilde{z},z)\Bigr)\exp(-\beta(F(\widetilde{z})-F(z)))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{K_{T}},q^{0})\Big[\left(\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},-p^{k+1/4},-p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{K_{T}}),\xi(q^{0}))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\,\rho(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))\Big]\Big|(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))=(\widetilde{z},z)\Bigr)\exp(-\beta(F(\widetilde{z})-F(z)))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{K_{T}},q^{0})\Big[\left(\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{K_{T}}),\xi(q^{0}))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\,\rho(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))\Big]\Big|(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))=(\widetilde{z},z)\Bigr)\exp(-\beta(F(\widetilde{z})-F(z))).

(B.29)

In the last step, we used that the work function
\mathcal{W}
is symmetric with respect to momentum reversal since
\widetilde{H}(q,p)=\widetilde{H}(q,-p)
.
Using (
B.28
) and (
B.29
), we get (by just swapping the indices
z
and
\widetilde{z}
in the third equality)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},\widetilde{Q}_{n+1})\left[1\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{z,\widetilde{z}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{K_{T}},q^{0})\Big[\left(\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)\right)\rho(\xi(q^{K_{T}}),\xi(q^{0}))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\wedge\,\rho(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))\Big]\Big|(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))=(\widetilde{z},z)\Bigr)\exp(-\beta F(\widetilde{z}))\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}\widetilde{z}.

\displaystyle=\int_{z,\widetilde{z}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{K_{T}},q^{0})\left[1\wedge\left(\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)\right)\frac{\rho(\xi(q^{K_{T}}),\xi(q^{0}))}{\rho(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{z,\widetilde{z}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\varphi(q^{K_{T}},q^{0})\left[1\wedge\left(\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}\left((q^{k},p^{k+1/4},p^{k+3/4})_{0\leq k\leq K_{T}-1},q^{K_{T}}\right)\right)\frac{\rho(\xi(q^{K_{T}}),\xi(q^{0}))}{\rho(\xi(q^{0}),\xi(q^{K_{T}}))}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1},Q_{n})\left[1\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}\right)\right]\right).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\varphi(Q_{n},Q_{n+1}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(\widetilde{Q}_{n+1},Q_{n})\left[1\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left(\varphi(Q_{n},Q_{n})\left[1-1\wedge\left(\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{n+1})\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\varphi(Q_{n+1},Q_{n})),

which concludes the proof of

II.4
.

Appendix C
Normalization of the steered dynamics (
II.17
)-(
II.19
)

We assume in this section that the mass matrix
M=M\mathrm{Id}
is isotropic and furthermore write the velocity schedule as
v_{z}(t_{k})=\widetilde{v}_{z}(t_{k})/\Delta t
, with a velocity
\widetilde{v}_{z}(t_{k})
normalized by the timestep. The parameterization then simplifies considerably. With the dimensionless momentum
\widetilde{p}=\sqrt{\beta/M}p
, which is of identity covariance under the equilibrium distribution, the steered dynamics (

)-(
II.19
) on
(q,\widetilde{p})
read

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{p}^{k+1/4}=\widetilde{p}^{k}-\frac{\Delta t\gamma}{4M}P_{M}(q^{k})P_{M}(q^{k})^{\top}(\widetilde{p}^{k+1/4}+\widetilde{p}^{k})+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t\gamma}{2M}}P_{M}(q^{k}){\mathcal{G}}^{k}

(C.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\widetilde{p}^{k+1/2}=\widetilde{p}^{k+1/4}-\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\beta M}}\nabla(\beta\widetilde{V}(q^{k}))}+\nabla\xi(q^{k})\widetilde{\lambda}^{k+1/2}&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle q^{k+1}=q^{k}+\frac{\Delta t}{\sqrt{\beta M}}\widetilde{p}^{k+1/2}&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\xi(q^{k+1})=z(t_{k+1})&(C_{q})\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}^{k+3/4}=\widetilde{p}^{k+1/2}-\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\beta M}}\nabla(\beta\widetilde{V}(q^{k+1}))}+\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})\widetilde{\lambda}^{k+3/4}&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\nabla\xi(q^{k+1})^{T}\widetilde{p}^{k+3/4}=\frac{\sqrt{\beta M}}{\Delta t}\widetilde{v}_{z}(t_{k+1})&(C_{p})\end{cases}

(C.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{p}^{k+1}=\widetilde{p}^{k+3/4}-\frac{\Delta t\gamma}{4M}P_{M}(q^{k+1})P_{M}(q^{k+1})^{\top}(\widetilde{p}^{k+3/4}+\widetilde{p}^{k+1})+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t\gamma}{2M}}P_{M}(q^{k+1}){\mathcal{G}}^{k+1/2},

(C.3)

with renormalized Lagrange multipliers
\widetilde{\lambda}^{k+1/2}
and
\widetilde{\lambda}^{k+3/4}
that ensure that the constraints
(C_{q})
and
(C_{p})
are satisfied.
Here, we have deliberately moved
\beta
into the gradients of the potential since it is the quantity
\beta V
that defines the target distribution at equilibrium (note that the correction

is independent of

). With this,

of the algorithm in

and

as well as the two parameters

. Note that while the Fixman term in general depends on the mass

\nabla V_{\mathrm{fix}}(q)
does not.

To explicitly connect the presented algorithm to the previous one from Ref.

and
q_{\perp}=(q_{\ell+1},\dots,q_{d})
. Similarly, one can then write the momenta as
p=(p_{\mathrm{CV}},p_{\perp})
.
Assume once again that

M=M\operatorname{Id}_{d}
(with a slight abuse of notation). Then the Gram tensor is
G_{M}(q)=M^{-1}\operatorname{Id}_{\ell}
, and the projection operator for the momenta is just

\displaystyle P_{M}(q)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{\ell\times\ell}&0\\
0&\mathbf{1}_{(d-\ell)\times(d-\ell)}\end{pmatrix}=P_{M}(q)^{\top}

setting the momenta
p_{\mathrm{CV}}
associated with the CV to zero. With the scalar parameters
\gamma
and
\sigma
, one then has
\gamma_{P}(q)=P_{M}(q)\gamma P_{M}(q)^{\top}=\gamma P_{M}(q)
and
\sigma_{P}(q)=P_{M}(q)\sigma
. Explicitly solving for the Lagrange multipliers, the resulting dynamics read (using the notation
\nabla_{\perp}V
for the partial derivative of
V
with respect to
q_{\perp}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k+1/4}=p_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k}\\
\displaystyle p_{\perp}^{k+1/4}=p_{\perp}^{k}-\frac{\Delta t}{4M}\gamma(p_{\perp}^{k+1/4}+p_{\perp}^{k})+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t}{2}}\sigma{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp}^{k}\end{cases}^{\prime}

(D.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle p_{\perp}^{k+1/2}=p_{\perp}^{k+1/4}-\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k})}&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k+1}=z(t_{k+1})&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle q_{\perp}^{k+1}=q_{\perp}^{k}+\frac{\Delta t}{M}\ p_{\perp}^{k+1/2}&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k+3/4}=Mv_{z}(t_{k+1})&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle p_{\perp}^{k+3/4}=p_{\perp}^{k+1/2}-\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k+1})}&\end{cases}

(D.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k+1}=p_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k+3/4},\\
\displaystyle p_{\perp}^{k+1}=p_{\perp}^{k+3/4}-\frac{\Delta t}{4M}\gamma(p_{\perp}^{k+3/4}+p_{\perp}^{k+1})+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t}{2}}\sigma{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp}^{k+1/2}.\end{cases}

(D.3)

In the following we consider two limiting cases, the overdamped limit and the deterministic limit.

D.1
Overdamped limit

For the choice
\gamma=2
and
M=\frac{\Delta t}{2}
(and therefore
\sigma=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}
), the forward dynamics (
D.1
)-(
D.3
) simplify to overdamped Langevin dynamics

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k+1}=z(t_{k+1})&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle q_{\perp}^{k+1}=q_{\perp}^{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta t}{\beta}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp}^{k}-\displaystyle{\Delta t\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k})}.\end{cases}

(D.4)

In terms of the parameters of the normalized algorithm introduce in
Section

II.4
, this parameter choice corresponds to setting
\alpha_{1}=1
.
Using (
II.22
), the associated work is calculated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{K_{T}}=V(q^{K_{T}})-V(q^{0})+\frac{1}{4\Delta t}\sum_{k=0}^{K_{T}-1}\left(\left\|q_{\perp}^{k}-q_{\perp}^{k+1}+\displaystyle{\Delta t\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k+1})}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|q_{\perp}^{k+1}-q_{\perp}^{k}+\displaystyle{\Delta t\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k})}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

For the overall accept/reject probability in the MCMC algorithm, this gives

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{W}^{K_{T}}\right)=\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\beta V(q^{K_{T}})}}{e^{-\beta V(q^{0})}}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{4\Delta t}\sum_{k=0}^{K_{T}-1}\left\|q_{\perp}^{k}-q_{\perp}^{k+1}+\displaystyle{\Delta t\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k+1})}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{4\Delta t}\sum_{k=0}^{K_{T}-1}\left\|q_{\perp}^{k+1}-q_{\perp}^{k}+\displaystyle{\Delta t\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k})}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)}.

This algorithm is very closely related to the
asymmetric
algorithm from Ref.
46
, with the only difference that forward and backward path have swapped their definitions.

D.2
Deterministic limit

In the deterministic limit, we have
\gamma=\sigma=0
. The forward dynamics then simplify to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k+1}=z(t_{k+1})&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle q_{\perp}^{k+1}=q_{\perp}^{k}+\frac{\Delta t}{M}\ p_{\perp}^{k}-\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2M}\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k})}&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{CV}}^{k+1}=Mv_{z}(t_{k+1})&\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle p_{\perp}^{k+1}=p_{\perp}^{k}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\left(\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k})+\nabla_{\perp}V(q^{k+1})\right).&\end{cases}

(D.5)

This corresponds to the deterministic dynamics for the CV coordinate and Verlet integration for the orthogonal coordinates. The associated work is simply

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{K_{T}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{T}-1}\left(H(q^{k+1},p^{k+1})-H(q^{k},p^{k})\right)

\displaystyle=H(q^{K_{T}},p^{K_{T}})-H(q^{0},p^{0}).

For the overall MCMC algorithm defined earlier, this means that the proposal
\widetilde{Q}_{n+1}=q^{K_{T}}
is accepted with probability the minimum of 1 and

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\beta H(q^{K_{T}},p^{K_{T}})}}{e^{-\beta H(q^{0},p^{0})}}\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}=\frac{e^{-\beta V(q^{K_{T}})}}{e^{-\beta V(q^{0})}}\frac{e^{-\beta\|p^{K_{T}}\|^{2}/(2M)}}{e^{-\beta\|p^{0}\|^{2}/(2M)}}\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}=\frac{e^{-\beta V(q^{K_{T}})}}{e^{-\beta V(q^{0})}}\frac{e^{-\beta\|p_{\perp}^{K_{T}}\|^{2}/(2M)}}{e^{-\beta\|p_{\perp}^{0}\|^{2}/(2M)}}\frac{\rho(\widetilde{Z}_{n+1},Z_{n})}{\rho(Z_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n+1})}

The last equality, where the norm of the momentum is only considered on the orthogonal degrees of freedom, comes from the assumption that the initial and final velocities in CV space are equal. This is the relaxed version of (
II.6
) when there is no Fixman term to be considered, see
II.3
.

Appendix E
Details of the wavelet basis for the
\phi^{4}
model

As mentioned in
Section

III.2
, we use a basis of Haar-wavelets to represent the state of the
\phi^{4}
model in our simulation. For an introduction to wavelet transformations we refer the reader to Refs.
10
;
36
, for example. Here, we only give a very practical definition.
For a one dimensional input signal
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of size
N=2^{n}
, set
\varphi^{(0)}_{i}=\phi_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots N\}
and repeatedly apply the following transformation
n
times for
j\in\{1,\ldots n-1\}
:

\displaystyle\varphi^{(j+1)}_{i}=\frac{\varphi^{(j)}_{2i+1}+\varphi^{(j)}_{2i}}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\overline{\varphi}^{(j+1)}_{i}=\frac{\varphi^{(j)}_{2i+1}-\varphi^{(j)}_{2i}}{\sqrt{2}}

with
i\in\{1,\ldots 2^{n-j}\}
.
This transformation constitutes a bijective linear map between the input
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the wavelet fields
\varphi^{(n)}
and
\{\overline{\varphi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
. The coarsest-scale wavelet field has a single component entry:

\displaystyle\varphi^{(n)}=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2})^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}.

For the
\phi^{4}
model, since
\varphi^{(n)}=\sqrt{N}\cdot\overline{\phi}
, this corresponds to the magnetization
\overline{\phi}
up to a prefactor. In practice, we therefore run the dynamics (
D.1
)-(
D.3
) with
q_{\mathrm{CV}}=\overline{\phi}
and
q_{\perp}
the remaining wavelet components
\{\overline{\varphi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
.

Appendix F
Details of the dimer model

F.1
Computation of the Fixman term

Recall that we use the reaction coordinate defined in (
III.6
):

\displaystyle\xi(q)=\frac{|q_{1}-q_{2}|-r_{0}}{2w}.

We write the gradient of the reaction coordinate
\nabla\xi(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
in the form

\displaystyle\nabla\xi(q)=\frac{1}{2w}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{q_{1}-q_{2}}{|q_{1}-q_{2}|}\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{q_{1}-q_{2}}{|q_{1}-q_{2}|}\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{2w}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}e_{12}\\
-e_{12}\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\right)

with the normalized vector
e_{12}=\frac{q_{1}-q_{2}}{|q_{1}-q_{2}|}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The Gram tensor is then given by

G_{M}(q)=\nabla\xi(q)^{\top}M^{-1}\nabla\xi(q)=\frac{1}{M}|\nabla\xi(q)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2Mw^{2}},

leading to a Fixman term (defined in (
II.16
)) that is constant:

V_{\mathrm{fix}}(q)=\frac{1}{2\beta}\log\det G_{M}(q)=-\frac{1}{2\beta}\log(2w^{2}M).

F.2
Lagrange multiplier for position constraint

From (
II.18
), the enforcement of the position constraint can be rewritten as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \displaystyle q^{k+1}&=\tilde{q}^{k+1}+\Delta t\ M^{-1}\nabla\xi(q^{k})\lambda^{k+1/2},\\
\displaystyle\xi(q^{k+1})&=z(t_{k+1}),\quad\quad(C_{q})\end{aligned}\right.

with
\tilde{q}^{k+1}=q^{k}+\Delta t\ M^{-1}p^{k+1/4}-\ M^{-1}\displaystyle{\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\nabla\widetilde{V}(q^{k})}
.
Inserting the first equation into the second and using the definition
(
III.6
) of
\xi
leads to a quadratic equation for
\lambda^{k+1/2}
with the solution

\displaystyle\lambda^{k+1/2}_{\pm}=\frac{wM}{\Delta t}\left(-e_{12}^{k}\cdot(\tilde{q}^{k+1}_{1}-\tilde{q}^{k+1}_{2})\pm\sqrt{\Delta}\right),

where
e_{12}^{k}=\frac{q_{1}^{k}-q_{2}^{k}}{|q_{1}^{k}-q_{2}^{k}|}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a normalized vector and the discriminant is given by

\Delta=\left[e_{12}^{k}\cdot(\tilde{q}^{k+1}_{1}-\tilde{q}^{k+1}_{2})\right]^{2}-\left[|\tilde{q}^{k+1}_{1}-\tilde{q}^{k+1}_{2}|^{2}-(2wz(t_{k+1})+r_{0})^{2}\right].

We choose the smallest value for
\lambda^{k+1/2}
, which corresponds to choosing
\lambda^{k+1/2}_{+}
if
e_{12}^{k}\cdot(\tilde{q}^{k+1}_{1}-\tilde{q}^{k+1}_{2})>0
and
\lambda^{k+1/2}_{-}
in the opposite case. This choice is consistent with ensuring that the value of the Lagrange multiplier is of order
\Delta t
for small timesteps.

F.3
Free Dimer not interacting with solvent particles

Considering the dimer model and the collective variable introduced in
Section

III.3
, the free energy can be computed analytically for the special case when the solvent particles do not interact with the dimer.
We have

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\int e^{-\beta V(q)}\delta_{\xi(q)-z}(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\int e^{-\beta V(q)}|\nabla\xi(q)|^{-1}\sigma^{M}_{\Sigma(z)}(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(e^{-\beta V_{D}(2wz+r_{0})}(2wz+r_{0})\right)+\mathrm{const.}

\displaystyle=V_{D}(2wz+r_{0})-\frac{1}{\beta}\log(2wz+r_{0})+\mathrm{const.}

where we ignored constants that just shift the free energy. For the second equality, consider that if
q_{1}=0
is fixed, the values of
q_{2}
are constrained to a circle of radius
r=2wz+r_{0}
, which introduces a Jacobian term.

Appendix G
Details of the polymer model

We simulate the polymer system introduced in
Section

III.4
at a temperature
1/\beta=1.3
using the jaxMD package
45
. The system is contained in a three-dimensional box of length
L=7.47
with periodic boundaries, with
N_{\mathrm{solvent}}=200
solvent particles, which yields a solvent density of
N_{\mathrm{solvent}}/L^{3}=0.48
particles per unit volume. The parameters of the polymer potentials are
d_{1}=1
,
d_{2}=1.63
(to fix the bonding angle at 109°),
k_{1}=k_{2}=500
,
d_{e}=4.62
,
a_{e}=0.8
and
k_{e}=9.1
, corresponding to an energy barrier of
7\beta^{-1}
. The Lennard-Jones potential is essentially given by

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{LJ}}=4\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{r^{*}}{r}\right)^{12}-\left(\frac{r^{*}}{r}\right)^{6}\right],

with
r^{*}=\epsilon=1
, and thus very similar to the WCA potential used for the dimer. In practice, however, we use a modified version given by jaxMD that smoothly decays to zero between an onset radius
r_{\mathrm{onset}}=2.0
and a cutoff radius
r_{\mathrm{cutoff}}=2.5
.

To implement
Step 1
of the algorithm in
Section

II.2
, we use an independent proposal sampler in CV space for both choices of CV considered here. For the first CV, i.e. the one-dimensional end-to-end distance, the proposal sampler is given by a Gaussian mixture model with two modes of equal weight and width
\sigma=0.17
at positions
z=3.53
and
z=5.65
.
For the second, 27-dimensional CV, i.e. the Cartesian coordinates of the polymer beads, we use a normalizing flow to propose distances between the polymer beads. These distances are the eight nearest neighbor and seven second-nearest neighbor distances as well as the six distances between the first bead and beads number 4 to 9, adding up to 21 distances in total. To complete such a proposed set of distances to a full proposal of polymer coordinates, one needs to additionally specify the global translation and rotation, as well as six sign values
\eta_{i}\in\{\pm 1\},\,i\in\{1,\ldots 6\}
(explained in the following). The global translation is fixed by always positioning the central bead of the polymer at the center of the box (which we do throughout the simulation) and the global rotation is sampled uniformly. The necessity of the six sign values
\eta_{i}
can be understood from the triangulation process used to determine the Cartesian polymer bead positions. Assuming one has already placed three beads with the desired distances, there will be two possible solutions (if there is a solution) to place the fourth bead at the right distance to the previous three (two intersection points of three spheres), which corresponds to choosing the sign of the dihedral angle. For a given set of 21 distances as in our case, there are six such sign values to choose, and we sample them uniformly at random. We compute the Jacobian of this reconstruction using automatic differentiation to arrive at a probability measure in the CV space of polymer positions
z\in\mathbb{R}^{27}
. The flow will sometimes produce sets of distances for which no solution to the triangulation problem exists and these samples are then directly rejected. Since this restriction only changes the normalization of the flow proposal measure, it does not enter into the overall formula of the accept/reject probability in
Step 3
of the algorithm in
Section

II.2
.
The flow is implemented using the FlowJax package
55
, with one affine whitening layer and 20 blocks of RationalQuadraticSpline transformations
17
with 32 knots on the interval
[-4,4]
and internal neural networks of depth 2 and width 30 (see the reference for further explanations). It was trained on a large dataset of one million samples obtained from a long Langevin simulation over 200 epochs with a batch size of 40,000 and a learning rate of
10^{-3}
using the Adam optimizer with otherwise default parameters.
