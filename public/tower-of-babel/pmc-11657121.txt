Adjustment of the ACEF score by the triglyceride glucose index improves the prediction of clinical outcomes in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention
Authors: Qiao Feng, Maoling Jiang, Xiufen Peng, Hui He, Dongyue Jia, Jie Feng, Zhen Zhang, Shiqiang Xiong, Lin Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ACEF score (age, creatinine, and left ventricular ejection fraction) and the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index have been identified as robust risk prediction models for adverse outcomes post-percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in coronary atherosclerotic heart disease (CHD) patients. This study aimed to assess whether incorporating the TyG index enhances the predictive ability of the ACEF score and enhances risk stratification for CHD patients undergoing PCI.MethodsThis observational cohort study enrolled 1248 patients diagnosed with CHD who underwent PCI at the Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu, China, between May 2018 and December 2022. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to establish the optimal cutoff values for the TyG index and ACEF score. The primary endpoint event was defined as major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs). Cumulative survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method. Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of MACCEs. The predictive ability of the ACEF score and the TyG index was assessed using the area under the ROC curve, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsOver a median follow-up period of 30.60 months, 214 patients experienced MACCEs. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a significant association between elevated ACEF score (> 1.27) and TyG index levels (> 8.82) with a heightened incidence of MACCEs (HR = 2.018, 95%CI 1.593–2.789; HR = 2.057, 95%CI 1.572–2.691; Log-Rank test, both P < 0.001). Multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed that even after adjusting for multiple confounders, both the ACEF score and TyG index remained as predictors of MACCEs (HR = 1.702, 95%CI 1.210–2.394, P = 0.002; HR = 1.575, 95%CI 1.296–1.916, P < 0.001). Additionally, the addition of the ACEF score and TyG index into the conventional risk model significantly improved the ability to predict MACCEs, as evidenced by the increase in the C-statistic value from 0.664 to 0.703, along with notable improvements in continuous NRI (0.391), categorical NRI (0.107), and IDI (0.033) (all P < 0.001).ConclusionThe combination of the TyG index and the ACEF score enhances the predictive ability for long-term MACCEs in patients with CHD undergoing PCI.

Background

Coronary atherosclerotic heart disease (CHD) is a significant contributor to global mortality [1].Cardiovascular diseases account for over 40% of all deaths in China, with the prevalence of CHD rapidly increasing [2, 3]. The significant advancements and widespread adoption of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) technology in contemporary clinical practice have solidified PCI as the primary treatment choice for the majority of patients with CHD [4, 5]. Despite adhering to standardized diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines, some patients undergoing PCI are still at a high risk of experiencing major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) [6, 7]. Hence, further research is essential to promptly and efficiently identify high-risk patients post-PCI to mitigate severe adverse outcomes.

The age, creatinine, and left ventricular ejection fraction (ACEF) score, serving as a simple risk assessment tool, has exhibited strong predictive ability in evaluating the risk of MACCEs among patients undergoing PCI [8–10]. Previous studies have suggested that combining the ACEF score with additional clinical risk factors can provide incremental predictive value for adverse outcomes in patients undergoing PCI [11, 12]. Recent research has confirmed a noteworthy association between insulin resistance (IR) and the onset, progression, and prognosis of CHD [13, 14]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, as a reliable surrogate marker for IR, is closely associated with the severity of coronary artery disease [15, 16] and exhibits strong predictive ability for the risk of MACCEs in patients of different ethnicities and cohorts [17–21].

To the best of our knowledge, previous studies have not investigated whether the inclusion of metabolic biomarkers, like the TyG index, improves the predictive ability of the ACEF score for MACCEs among PCI patients. This study aims to assess the predictive efficacy of the TyG index and ACEF score for MACCEs and to examine the potential incremental value of predictive capacity and risk stratification by integrating the TyG index into the ACEF score.

Methods

Study population

This study enrolled 1248 patients diagnosed with CHD, who underwent PCI at the Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu between May 2018 and December 2022. The exclusion criteria include (1) incomplete critical clinical and follow-up data; (2) a history of coronary artery bypass grafting surgery; (3) congenital heart disease and other cardiac conditions requiring surgical surgery; (4) malignant tumors and other conditions with life expectancies of less than one year.

The research protocol obtained approval from the ethics committee of the Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu and adhered to the principles set forth in the Helsinki Declaration, ensuring that all patients provided written informed consent.

Data collection and definitions

Demographic information, laboratory results, and procedural details were gathered from the electronic medical records system provided by the Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu.

Hypertension was defined as office systolic blood pressure (SBP) values ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure values (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg or a physician-diagnosed condition, irrespective of current antihypertensive medication usage [22]. Diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was established based on the specific criteria: (1) self-reported diagnosis or antidiabetic therapy usage before hospitalization; (2) Fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (3) 2-hour plasma glucose > 11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test; (4) Classic diabetes symptoms and casual plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; and (5) HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [23]. Patient histories of previous PCI, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and stroke were obtained through self-reports and confirmed by medical records.

Venous blood samples were typically taken after overnight fasting (> 8 h), within 24 h of admission. Various blood markers were measured using standard laboratory techniques, including cardiac troponin T (cTnT), serum creatinine (Scr), FBG, triglyceride, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein-C (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein-C (HDL-C), lipoprotein (a), albumin, fibrinogen, D-Dimer, hemoglobin, platelet count, and white blood cells (WBC) count. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) values were assessed using echocardiography during the patient’s hospitalization.

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) was defined as acute myocardial injury detected by abnormal cardiac biomarkers and supported by clinical evidence of acute myocardial ischemia [24], including non-ST elevation myocardial infarction and ST-elevation myocardial infarction identified by electrocardiography [25]. Multivessel disease (MVD) was defined as the presence of two or more coronary arteries with a diameter of 2·5 mm or more and ≥ 70% stenosis, identified through visual estimation or positive coronary physiology testing [26].

The baseline SYNTAX score (bSS) was computed by two experienced interventional cardiologists, utilizing the online tool (http://syntaxscore.com/), relying on the initial coronary angiography results before PCI [27]. They conducted the calculation process without access to the study protocol or the patients’ baseline clinical characteristics. The TyG index was calculated as follows: TyG = ln [fasting triglyceride (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL) / 2] [15]. The ACEF score was computed using the following formula: ACEF score = age (years) / left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) % + 1 (if serum creatinine value > 2.0 mg/dL) [28]. In this study, all laboratory measurements were standardized to international units for calculation.

Endpoints and follow-up definitions

The primary endpoint event was defined as major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs), including all-cause death, cardiac death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, unplanned revascularization, and stroke. All occurrences were thoroughly documented and confirmed through a meticulous review of relevant medical records whenever possible. Regular clinical follow-up visits took place at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after PCI, followed by annual check-ups. Follow-up data were collected from outpatient electronic medical records, telephone follow-ups, or readmission records.

Statistical analysis

All study data were analyzed using R language (version 4.3.1) and SPSS software (version 27.0). The normality of the data was evaluated through probability-probability plots. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), with intergroup comparisons conducted using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and intergroup comparisons were performed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests.

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to identify the optimal cutoff values for the ACEF score and TyG index in predicting the primary endpoint and assessing long-term prognosis. The optimal cutoff values of the ACEF score and TyG index for the primary endpoint were obtained using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis with the area under the curve and Youden’s J statistic. The Kaplan-Meier method was employed to evaluate the incidence of MACCEs in the subgroups defined by the optimal cutoff values, with subgroup differences analyzed using log-rank tests.

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were utilized to determine the risk factors affecting the prognosis of PCI patients. Furthermore, the C-statistic, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) indices were applied to explore the synergistic and incremental effects of the novel clinical risk model that integrates the TyG index and ACEF score in predicting MACCEs. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

The median follow-up time was 30.60 months (25.83 months, 34.87 months), during which 214 patients (17.15%) experienced MACCEs. Patients in the MACCEs group were characterized by older age, a higher prevalence of T2DM, COPD, and previous stroke history compared to those in the non-MACCEs group (all P < 0.05). Moreover, patients in the MACCEs group exhibited elevated baseline levels of heart rate, cTnT, Scr, FBG, triglyceride, TC, LDL-C, fibrinogen, D-dimer, and WBC (all P < 0.05). Conversely, patients in the MACCEs group had lower LVEF and hemoglobin levels (both P < 0.05). Significant differences in clinical diagnoses existed between the groups, with a higher proportion of MACCEs patients being diagnosed with AMI (P = 0.014). Patients in the MACCEs group showed a higher incidence of MVD and a higher level of bSS than non-MACCEs patients. Additionally, the ACEF score and TyG index were significantly elevated in patients with MACCEs (P < 0.001).

Predictive value of the ACEF score and TyG index for the risk of MACCEs

The predictive performance of the ACEF score for MACCEs in patients post-PCI was assessed using ROC analysis, as shown in Fig. 1. The ROC curve determined the optimal cut-off value for the ACEF score to predict MACCEs as 1.27, with a sensitivity of 53.74% and specificity of 66.34%. The area under the curve was 0.621 (95% CI: 0.579–0.663, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the ROC curve illustrated that an elevated TyG index was associated with adverse outcomes, with an area under the curve of 0.614 (95%CI: 0.575–0.653, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Patients with a TyG index above the optimal cutoff value (> 8.82) demonstrated a higher incidence of MACCEs, with a sensitivity of 70.56% and specificity of 48.55%.

Comparison of the cumulative incidence rate of MACCEs in subgroup during follow-up

In the follow-up period, according to the Kaplan-Meier analysis presented in Fig. 3, the cumulative incidence of MACCEs was significantly higher in the high ACEF score group (> 1.27) than in the low ACEF score group (≤ 1.27) among all CHD patients following PCI (HR = 2.108, 95% CI: 1.593–2.789, P < 0.001). Similarly, as Fig. 4 indicated that the cumulative incidence of MACCEs was notably higher in the high TyG index group (> 8.82) compared to the low TyG index group (≤ 8.82) among all CHD patients after PCI (HR = 2.057, 95% CI: 1.572–2.691, P < 0.001).

Synergistic and incremental effects of the TyG index combined with ACEF score on risk stratification for MACCEs

The findings of the univariable Cox regression analyses revealed that age, T2DM, heart rate, Scr, FBG, triglyceride, LDL-C, fibrinogen, hemoglobin, WBC, LVEF, AMI, bSS, TyG index, and ACEF score were all significant risk predictors of MACCEs in PCI patients (all P < 0.05). Additionally, upon adjusting for confounding factors, the multivariable Cox regression analysis identified T2DM, LDL-C, fibrinogen, bSS, ACEF score, and TyG index as significant independent predictors and prediction systems and of MACCEs (all P < 0.05) (Table 2). Considering that the calculation of the ACEF score and TyG index involves age, Scr, LVEF, FBG, and triglyceride levels, to avoid errors due to collinearity, these risk factors were excluded in the multivariable Cox regression analysis.

The synergistic and incremental effects of combining the TyG index with the ACEF score in predicting MACCEs are presented in Table 3; Fig. 5. Compared to the baseline risk model including fibrinogen, LDL-C and bSS (Model 1), the inclusion of the ACEF score (Model 2) raised the C-statistic from 0.700 (95% CI 0.662–0.739) to 0.712 (95% CI 0.675–0.750) (P < 0.001), with notable enhancements in reclassification indices: continuous NRI = 0.217 (95% CI 0.071–0.363, P = 0.004), category NRI = 0.065 (95% CI 0.003–0.126, P = 0.039), and IDI = 0.011 (95% CI 0.003–0.019, P = 0.011). Similarly, adding the TyG index (Model 3) to Model 1 increased the C-statistic to 0.711 (95% CI 0.673–0.749) (P < 0.001), with significant improvements in reclassification indices: continuous NRI = 0.206 (95% CI 0.059–0.352, P = 0.006), category NRI = 0.110 (95% CI 0.047–0.173, P < 0.001), and IDI = 0.020 (95% CI 0.011–0.029, P < 0.001). The study demonstrated that the integration of both the TyG index and ACEF score (Model 4) into the baseline risk model yielded the most substantial incremental effect in predicting MACCEs, with the C-statistic improving from 0.700 (95% CI 0.662–0.739) to 0.728 (95% CI 0.691–0.765) (P < 0.001). Furthermore, this enhancement was supported by significant improvements in the reclassification indices, with continuous NRI improved by 35.6%, category NRI improved by 17.4%, and IDI improved by 3.5% (all P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study identified the ACEF score and TyG index as significant risk prediction models of long-term MACCEs in patients undergoing PCI. The established innovative risk prediction model, which integrates the TyG index with the ACEF score, showed a markedly improved predictive capability value for MACCEs. Hence, the findings of this study may present a novel strategy for improving risk stratification, reducing the incidence of adverse outcomes, and ameliorating the long-term prognosis of PCI patients.

Previous studies have indicated that elevated IR can disrupt the balance in systemic glucose and lipid metabolism, resulting in inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, and cardiovascular remodeling. This metabolism disruption can trigger and further exacerbate the progression of cardiovascular diseases, playing a critical role in the development and progression of CHD, and it is correlated with the severity of coronary artery lesions [29, 30]. The TyG index, recognized as a straightforward, cost-effective, and dependable surrogate marker for IR, exhibits a robust correlation with the established gold standard method for IR assessment in both healthy individuals and those with diabetes [17, 31]. Therefore, investigating the value of the TyG index in risk stratification and prognostic prediction of CHD patients undergoing PCI holds considerable clinical relevance.

This study cohort enrolled patients with CHD undergoing PCI, in which we examined the association between the TyG index and MACCEs. Our analysis revealed that an elevated TyG index was independently associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. However, it should be noted that some researchers have suggested the utilization of the TyG index as a biomarker for prognosis in patients with cardiovascular disease may be influenced by diabetes and hypertriglyceridemia due to shared pathological processes [32]. Consequently, bias could be potentially introduced into the findings of such investigation due to these confounding factors. Our study differs from a recent study [33] that aimed at predicting adverse outcomes in patients with acute coronary syndrome following PCI, in which the researchers simultaneously incorporated both diabetes mellitus and the TyG index into the multivariable risk analysis and risk modeling process. The accuracy of the study’s findings could potentially be impacted by this unwise integration. In our study, T2DM, triglyceride, FBG, and the TyG index were not included simultaneously in the multivariable Cox regression analysis and risk model establishment process. This approach aimed to lessen errors arising from factor interaction and collinearity, ultimately improving the accuracy of the risk model. Conversely, some researchers opt to address this bias by selecting patients with both CHD and T2DM for the study cohort [34, 35]. They have observed that the TyG index, serving as a valuable marker for risk stratification and prognosis, can independently predict the risk of MACCEs in these individuals following PCI. Nevertheless, the generalizability and extensive applicability of risk prediction models could undoubtedly be limited by the precise selection of research participants. In contrast to the aforementioned studies, this study adopts broader inclusion criteria, thus enhancing the model’s applicability, while still reaching comparable conclusions. In this study, the cutoff value for the TyG index was statistically determined to be 8.82, based on Youden’s J statistic. Although our identified optimal cutoff value differs from those reported in previous studies, the overall trend remains consistent. Zhu et al. reported that the optimal cutoff value of the TyG index for predicting adverse cardiovascular prognosis was 8.55 in patients with acute coronary syndrome following PCI, which closely aligns with our findings of 8.82, likely due to the similarity in study populations and the proportion of diabetic patients (34.7% vs. 34.6%) [33]. However, our optimal cutoff value is significantly lower than those determined in studies focused exclusively on PCI patients with diabetes [34, 35]. The differences in study populations, types of CHD, degrees of abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism, and severity of vascular lesions may explain the variation in these results.

Moreover, a growing body of research indicates that the prognostic value of the TyG index for CHD patients falls within the mild-to-moderate range, suggesting challenges in predicting severe cardiovascular events based solely on the TyG index [36]. Prior studies have demonstrated that the incorporation of the TyG index into established risk scoring models like SYNTAX and GRACE scores can substantially enhance the predictive value of MACCEs in PCI patients [35, 37, 38]. However, the complexity of these traditional scoring systems, involving numerous variables and anatomical features of coronary artery lesions, hinders clinical feasibility, thereby constraining clinicians in their utilization. Our study first endeavored to integrate the TyG index into the ACEF score to overcome these limitations and facilitate rapid and precise prognosis assessment for PCI patients. The ACEF score has gained widespread adoption among PCI patients in recent years due to its simplicity, objectivity, and clinical accessibility. Mehmet et al. have demonstrated a strong association between the ACEF score and the severity of coronary artery stenosis [39]. Furthermore, it has been established as a crucial predictor for early or late MACCEs risk in diverse cohorts of CHD patients post-PCI [40–42]. This study further demonstrates that the ACEF score offers significant risk stratification abilities and prognostic value for CHD patients undergoing PCI treatment. PCI patients with a baseline ACEF score > 1.27 exhibit a markedly elevated risk of long-term MACCEs during the follow-up period compared to those with an ACEF score ≤ 1.27. Notably, a high ACEF score serves as a strong predictor of adverse long-term prognoses following PCI in CHD patients. These findings highlight the utility of a straightforward scoring model in effectively assessing the adverse outcomes in PCI patients during routine clinical practice. Furthermore, this study strives to enhance the efficiency and clinical relevance of the scoring model by innovatively introducing the TyG index, based on recognizing the deficiency of cardiovascular metabolic risk factors in the ACEF score, while preserving the model’s simplicity.

In this study, we are delighted to report that the integration of the ACEF score (model 2) and TyG index (model 3) into the established baseline risk model (model 1) significantly enhances the predictive capability for MACCEs risk in PCI patients. Additionally, the combination of the TyG index and ACEF score (model 4) leads to substantial improvements in the C-statistic, NRI, and IDI values compared to model 1. This indicates that enhancing the risk scoring model by adjusting the ACEF score with the TyG index could improve the discriminatory and reclassification capabilities, resulting in a stronger predictive ability for MACCEs in patients undergoing PCI. It is important to note that, although the present study was conducted as a retrospective analysis at a single center in China, our research team endeavored to expand the inclusion criteria and increase the sample size to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the study was carried out at a prominent hospital in the local area, which serves patients from a wide range of regions, which helped to minimize potential selection bias. Therefore, we believe that the findings will be relevant and informative for general CHD populations undergoing PCI in various healthcare settings.

The findings of this study indicate that the utilization of the TyG-ACEF score risk model could rapidly and precise identify high-risk CHD patients after PCI, enabling more targeted prevention or aggressive treatment for clinicians to improve clinical outcomes in PCI patients. For patients with elevated TyG levels, reducing IR treatment appears to be a promising intervention to ameliorate the risk of MACCEs [43]. Nonetheless, as delineated in the 2018 ESC guidelines on myocardial revascularization [44], no single risk model can provide a flawless risk assessment. The predictive ability of risk-scoring models is often constrained by the specific definitions of scores used, application methods, differences in variables included, considerations of endpoint events, and limited external validation. Consequently, clinical decisions should not depend solely on a risk-scoring model but should involve comprehensive discussions within a multidisciplinary clinical team.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, being a single-center observational cohort study, its persuasiveness is less robust compared to a randomized clinical trial. Future enhancements to the study’s reliability necessitate additional prospective, multicenter, and larger-scale cohort studies. Secondly, the baseline assessment of the TyG index and ACEF score could be influenced by medication use and may fluctuate during follow-up, emphasizing the importance of monitoring these risk factors dynamically. Lastly, the study primarily involved CHD patients undergoing PCI, restricting the generalizability of the findings to patients of diverse ethnicities or those undergoing alternative surgical treatments.

Conclusion

Elevated TyG index and ACEF score levels are significantly associated with an increased risk of MACCEs in CHD patients undergoing PCI. The adjustment of the ACEF score by the TyG index offers synergetic and incremental predictive value for the long-term prognosis of PCI patients. Consequently, clinicians can pinpoint high-risk individuals using the TyG-ACEF score model, subsequently reducing the occurrence of MACCEs through the application of aggressive treatment strategies, targeted prevention, and ultimately advancing their clinical outcomes.
