Cognitive decline and the evolution of depression networks in older adults: evidence from the CHARLS study
Authors: Jingyi Wang, Lei Yang, Yu Zhou, Kuo Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global trend of population aging poses significant health challenges, particularly in cognitive and mental health. Understanding the heterogeneous patterns of cognitive decline and their relationship with depressive symptoms is crucial for developing targeted interventions. This study explores the complex relationship between cognitive decline trajectories and depressive symptom networks in Chinese middle-aged and older adults using longitudinal data.MethodsData were sourced from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), involving 5,977 participants aged 45 and above, followed from 2013 to 2020. Cognitive function was assessed through self-reported memory, mathematical ability, and orientation, while depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10). Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) identified heterogeneous cognitive development trajectories, and Cross-Lagged Panel Network (CLPN) analysis examined the longitudinal dynamics of depressive symptom networks across different cognitive trajectory subgroups.ResultsGMM analysis identified three distinct cognitive decline trajectories: Class 1 (moderate-baseline moderate-declining, 5.1%), Class 2 (low-baseline moderate-declining, 44.1%), and Class 3 (high-baseline steep-declining, 50.9%). All three classes exhibited substantial cognitive decline over the 7-year follow-up period, with Class 3 showing the largest absolute decline (5.32 points) despite having the highest baseline performance. Depression trajectories varied significantly across cognitive classes, with Class 2 showing the highest baseline depression scores (9.60) and Class 3 maintaining consistently lower depression levels (6.82 at baseline, 8.08 at follow-up). CLPN analysis revealed distinct patterns of depressive symptom network evolution, with key symptoms such as concentration problems, mood disturbance, and lethargy showing differential centrality and connectivity patterns across cognitive trajectory subgroups and time points.ConclusionsThis study reveals significant heterogeneity in cognitive decline patterns among Chinese middle-aged and older adults, with the majority (50.9%) following a high-baseline but steep-declining trajectory. The complex relationship between cognitive decline and depressive symptoms varies substantially across different cognitive trajectory subgroups, suggesting the need for personalized intervention strategies. The findings highlight the importance of early identification of high-risk cognitive decline patterns and the implementation of targeted mental health interventions. These results provide new insights into the dynamic interplay between cognitive aging and mental health, informing the development of precision medicine approaches for healthy aging.Keywords: Cognitive decline, Depressive symptoms, Older adults, Growth mixture modeling (GMM), Cross-Lagged panel network, CHARLS

Introduction

According to the National Bureau of Statistics of China, the seventh national population census in 2020 revealed that the proportion of individuals aged 45 and above rose from 33.26% in the 2010 census to 42.62%, showing an increase of 9.36%. This substantial rise underscores the severe aging trend within the population [1, 2]. This demographic shift poses numerous health and socioeconomic challenges, especially concerning the cognitive and mental health of older adults [3–6].

Research indicates that cognitive decline in older adults is closely associated with a range of adverse health outcomes, including depression, dementia, and other mental illnesses [7–11]. Among these, the relationship between cognitive impairment and depression in older adults has been a long-standing focus of academic research, especially in China [12, 13].Data indicates that 20% and 23% of Chinese individuals over 45 suffer from cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms, respectively [14]. Therefore, understanding the cognitive development of older adults and its relationship with depressive symptoms is crucial for developing effective health interventions and policies.

Existing studies reveal a significant association between cognitive decline and depressive symptoms, encompassing reductions in cognitive ability, emotional regulation, memory function, and daily living abilities [15]. However, most studies have focused on cross-sectional analyses at single points in time, failing to fully capture the dynamic changes in cognitive development and depressive symptoms over time [16]. For instance, while cognitive decline has been identified as a major predictor of depressive symptoms, these conclusions are largely based on static data, neglecting the individual’s cognitive development trajectory over time [17].

To overcome these limitations, longitudinal studies have increasingly been employed, offering a more precise depiction of the dynamic relationship between cognitive development and depressive symptoms through repeated measurements of the same cohort. Despite challenges such as sample attrition, measurement tool consistency, and data analysis complexity [11], these studies are essential for understanding the long-term impacts and interactions between cognitive function and depressive symptoms. Traditional longitudinal analysis methods, including linear mixed-effects models and structural equation models, capture overall trends in individual changes but have limitations in addressing heterogeneous individuals [18–20]. Latent class growth models (LCGM) and growth mixture models (GMM) are widely utilized due to their superior ability to address individual differences, capture latent classes, and identify different growth trajectories [21–23], GMM, in particular, excels in handling complex heterogeneous individual variations by dividing the sample into multiple latent classes, allowing for precise modeling of individual growth trajectories within these classes. This approach not only reveals differences among individuals but also identifies potential high-risk groups, providing a basis for targeted interventions [24–26].

In recent years, network analysis of depressive symptoms has gained increasing attention in mental health research. By constructing symptom networks, researchers are better able to identify the interconnections and dynamic changes among symptoms [27]. Unlike traditional approaches that treat symptoms in isolation, network analysis emphasizes the relationships among various elements within a complex system [28]. To explore the longitudinal dynamics of psychological constructs over time, Rhemtulla et al. (2022) integrated network modeling with the cross-lagged panel framework to develop the Cross-Lagged Panel Network (CLPN) model [29]. This model allows for the visualization of both autoregressive effects and cross-lagged effects within a directed network structure. Given the directional nature of these connections, centrality measures such as In-Expected Influence (IEI) and Out-Expected Influence (OEI) can be calculated. IEI reflects the extent to which variance in a node at Time 2 (T2) can be explained by variables at Time 1 (T1), whereas OEI indicates the influence a node at T1 exerts on other variables at T2 [29]. These analytical advances have provided new insights into the dynamic structures of psychological symptoms and serve as a theoretical foundation for the current study. Nonetheless, research examining the relationship between cognitive development trajectories and depressive symptom networks among older adults—particularly high-risk subgroups—remains limited [30].

The significance and originality of this study lie in the application of Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) to examine potential cognitive development trajectories in older adults and to identify subpopulations at higher risk for cognitive decline, thereby addressing existing research gaps [31, 32]. Building upon this, we employ cross-lagged panel network analysis to construct and analyze depressive symptom networks within high-risk groups, aiming to uncover longitudinal dynamics and temporal relationships among symptoms. This approach offers novel perspectives on the complex interplay between cognitive development and depressive symptoms in aging populations [33, 34]. Drawing on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the study tracks cognitive developmental trajectories among older adults and conducts CLPN analyses to investigate how symptom networks evolve across different trajectory groups, with particular emphasis on autoregressive and cross-lagged effects within the network architecture [35]. These methodological innovations, combined with a robust empirical dataset, contribute meaningfully to both theoretical understanding and practical applications in the field of cognitive aging and its association with depressive symptoms.

In addition, depressive symptom network analysis has also been increasingly applied in mental health research. By constructing symptom networks, researchers can better understand the interrelationships and dynamic changes between symptoms [27]. The advantage of network analysis lies in its ability to reveal the interactions among various elements within a complex system, rather than viewing each symptom in isolation [28]. Cross-lagged panel network analysis, in particular, enables researchers to examine temporal relationships and causal pathways between symptoms across time points, providing insights into how symptom networks evolve and influence each other over time. However, there is still limited research on the relationship between cognitive development trajectories and depressive symptom networks in older adults, particularly among high-risk groups [29].

This study’s significance and originality lie in using GMM to analyze the potential cognitive development trajectories of older adults, identifying high-risk groups for cognitive decline, and filling existing research gaps [30, 31]. By constructing and analyzing the depressive symptom networks of high-risk groups through cross-lagged panel network analysis, this research aims to uncover the interrelationships and dynamic changes between symptoms over time, providing new perspectives on the complex relationship between cognitive development and depressive symptoms in older populations [32, 33]. Utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), this study will track the cognitive development trajectories of older adults and conduct dynamic network analyses to examine how symptom networks evolve across different cognitive trajectory groups [34]. These methodological innovations and unique data sources make this study significant both theoretically and practically in the field of cognitive development and its relationship with depressive symptoms among older adults.

This study aims to address the following research questions:

RQ1: Is there heterogeneity in cognitive decline among older adults? If so, what subgroups exist? We will use GMM to identify different cognitive development trajectories, thereby determining which individuals are at high risk for cognitive decline.

Methods

Study design and population

This study utilizes data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort study that commenced with a baseline survey in 2011, with subsequent follow-ups conducted every two to three years (2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020). CHARLS employs a standardized questionnaire administered face-to-face by systematically trained interviewers using computer-assisted personal interviewing techniques [35].

The subjects of this research were participants over the age of 45 from the CHARLS data spanning 2013 to 2020. Using the 2013 survey as the baseline, exclusions were made for participants who: (1) were aged 59 and younger at baseline (n = 9680); (2) had other mental disorders at baseline (n = 10); (3) had other memory-related disorders such as dementia at baseline (n = 34). Ultimately, 7736 participants were included in the study. The selection process for study participants is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Participant eligibility selection process for the study

The CHARLS study has been approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent. This research adheres strictly to the guidelines for Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).

Data preprocessing

This study utilized longitudinal data from four waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) between 2013 and 2020, following systematic data preprocessing and missing data handling. First, quality assessment and variable selection were conducted on the original dataset, retaining seven core sociodemographic variables (age, sex, education level, smoking frequency, alcohol consumption frequency, sleep duration, dietary habits) and measurement fields for cognition and depression across four time points. Data cleaning included outlier detection, logical consistency checks, and variable standardization. Regarding missing data, a stratified processing strategy was adopted. Based on the proportion of missing values in cognitive and depressive measurement fields, the sample was divided into a low-missing group (missing proportion ≤ 30%, n = 5,977, 67.3%) and a high-missing group (missing proportion > 30%, n = 2,911, 32.7%). Tailored missing data handling approaches were applied to each group. For the low-missing group, differential imputation strategies were employed according to variable type and missing patterns: mode imputation for categorical variables (e.g., sex, alcohol consumption frequency), predictive mean matching (PMM) for continuous variables (e.g., education level, sleep duration, dietary habits), and multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE) for cognitive and depressive measurement fields, generating five imputed datasets. The imputation model incorporated all relevant covariates to ensure compliance with the missing at random (MAR) assumption. Sensitivity analysis revealed negligible differences in variable means before and after imputation (all < 0.01), indicating high imputation quality. For the high-missing group, deletion was considered to avoid severe bias. Prior to deletion, baseline characteristic comparisons were conducted between the low- and high-missing groups. No significant differences were observed in sex or dietary habits (effect size: standardized mean difference, SMD < 0.1), suggesting the missing mechanism might be missing completely at random (MCAR).

Measures

Assessment of Cognitive Function (DF)

The Chinese version of cognitive assessment has been validated in the CHARLS cohort sample and is tested through standard face-to-face interviews [36]. The cognitive functions include three dimensions: self-reported memory, mathematical ability, and orientation ability. The first dimension, subjective memory assessment, is assessed by asking participants, “How do you feel about your memory now?” with responses scored from ‘excellent’ to ‘poor’ on a scale from 5 to 1, making the score range 1–5. The second dimension, mathematical ability, is measured by subtracting 7 from 100 consecutively five times to test attention. Each correct subtraction scores 1 point, with a total score ranging from 0 to 5. The third dimension, orientation ability, is evaluated by asking respondents to state today’s date (month, day, year, season) and day of the week, with each correct answer scoring 1 point, total scores ranging from 0 to 5. The overall cognitive score is the sum of the scores from these three dimensions, ranging from 1 to 15.

Assessment of Depressive Symptoms (DS)

We opted for the 10-item short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) to measure depressive symptoms [37]. The CES-D-10 scale, widely used in population-based research, demonstrates excellent validity and reliability in the CHARLS study (Chen and Mui, 2014). The specific items are as follows: (1) I get bothered by things, (2) I have trouble keeping my mind on what I am doing, (3) I feel depressed, (4) I find it hard to do anything, (5) I am hopeful about the future, (6) I feel fearful, (7) My sleep is restless, (8) I am happy, (9) I feel lonely, (10) I feel I cannot carry on with my life, with items five and eight requiring reverse scoring. Each item is rated as: 0 (rarely or none of the time, < 1 day), 1 (some or a little of the time, 1–2 days), 2 (occasionally or a moderate amount of time, 3–4 days), 3 (most or all of the time, 5–7 days), capturing respondents’ emotional and behavioral frequency over the past week. The total CES-D-10 score ranges from 0 to 30. In this study, a CES-D-10 score of 12 or higher is considered indicative of depressive symptoms (DS), while a score below 12 is considered negative. Higher scores indicate more severe DS [38].

Confounders

This study collected information on participants’ age, sex, residence, marital status, educational level, and smoking habits. Building on previous research, we controlled for a range of potential confounding factors. Sociodemographic characteristics include age, sex (male, female), educational level (ranging from no formal education to Bachelor’s degree and above), place of residence (rural or urban), and marital status (married, unmarried). Health habits encompass smoking status (non-smokers, < 5 cigarettes/day or history of smoking, ≥ 5 cigarettes/day, ≥ 10 cigarettes/day, ≥ 20 cigarettes/day) and drinking behavior (non-drinkers, drink but less than once a month, and drink more than once a month) [39], as well as sleep duration and frequency of social activities (none, some, and active, corresponding to 0, 1, ≥ 2 times/month).

Health status characteristics cover functional disability (ranging from none to severe, i.e., 0, 1–2, ≥ 3) and prevalent diseases. Participation in social activities was assessed by asking respondents whether they had engaged in specific activities in the past month, such as community organization involvement [35]. Disease conditions were based on self-reported data, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive system diseases, arthritis or rheumatism, and asthma [35]. Statistical analysis

This study commenced with descriptive statistics and bivariate correlation analyses conducted in SPSS 25.0. Subsequently, Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) was performed using Mplus 8.3 [40] to identify latent cognitive trajectories. Age was a priori included as a critical covariate given its established role as a core determinant of cognitive function and substantial baseline age heterogeneity in the sample. While sociodemographic factors (e.g., sex, educational level) and health-related variables (e.g., dietary patterns, sleep quality) have demonstrated associations with cognition in prior literature, they were excluded from the final model to maintain parsimony, as preliminary model comparisons indicated negligible improvements in fit indices and theoretical relevance to trajectory classification. Differences in depression scores among these heterogeneous groups were then examined using differential testing. Finally, cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) analysis was conducted to examine the network structure of depressive symptoms and the evolution of central symptoms at four time points (2013, 2015, 2018, 2020) for different cognitive development trajectory groups using R version 4.4.1.

Growth Mixture Model (GMM)

Building on descriptive statistics and correlational analyses, this study utilized data from four waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. A Growth Mixture Model was employed to analyze the heterogeneity in the developmental trajectories of cognitive levels among the older populations over seven year [40]. Unlike traditional growth models, the GMM allows for the exhibition of distinct growth trajectories within the sample, thereby enabling more precise identification of different subgroups of cognitive decline among older adults. This study’s GMM model permits the free estimation of growth factor variances and covariances for intercepts and slopes, reflecting the heterogeneity in individual developmental trajectories. The model’s effectiveness was evaluated using various indices including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), Entropy, and likelihood ratio tests (LMR) along with Bootstrap-based likelihood ratio tests (BLRT) [41]. The criteria for selecting the optimal model were higher Entropy, lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, significant LMR and BLRT, and ensuring that each class comprised no less than 5% of the sample population [38].

Cross-lagged panel network

To examine the longitudinal dynamics of depressive symptom networks across different cognitive trajectory groups, we employed cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) analysis using R version 4.4.1, with the glmnet package [41] utilized for regularized regression calculations and the qgraph package [42] employed for network visualization and estimation, following standard guidelines for network analysis in psychological research [43]. The CLPN model was estimated through a series of regularized regressions using penalized maximum likelihood with the LASSO penalty, with age at baseline, sex, and education level included as covariates, and a tuning parameter (λ = 0.5) employed to optimize network sparsity and minimize overfitting. The model simultaneously estimated autoregressive effects (coefficients for nodes predicting themselves at subsequent time points) and cross-lagged effects (coefficients for nodes predicting different nodes at subsequent time points), controlling for all other nodes at the current time point. Network centrality measures, specifically in-expected influence (IEI) and out-expected influence (OEI), were computed to parse the directionality between symptoms, where IEI represents the proportion of variance for a given node at T2 accounted for by variables at T1, and OEI denotes the effect that a given node at T1 has on variables at T2 [44]. To enhance interpretability, logistic regression coefficients (edge weights) were transformed from log odds to odds ratios through exponentiation, where values above 1 indicate positive associations, values below 1 suggest negative associations, and values equal to 1 imply no association. To evaluate significant differences in network structure between cognitive trajectory groups, we conducted 1000 iterations of permutation tests using the Network Comparison Test (NCT) framework, with significance levels set at α = 0.05, allowing for comprehensive comparison of network invariance, global strength, and specific edge differences across groups and time points.

Subgroup analysis

We further examined the developmental trajectory characteristics of the three latent classes of cognitive decline in older adults. The results showed that the number of participants in Class 1 (C1), Class 2 (C2), and Class 3 (C3) were 302 (5.1%), 2,633 (44.1%), and 3,042 (50.9%), respectively. In terms of baseline cognitive performance (T1), Class 1 demonstrated a mean score of 9.41 (SD = 1.31), Class 2 showed the lowest baseline at 8.37 (SD = 1.49), and Class 3 exhibited the highest baseline performance at 11.26 (SD = 1.37), indicating significant differences among the three classes. Furthermore, by examining the cognitive change patterns over the 7-year follow-up period (2013–2020), we found distinct trajectories for each class. Class 1 initially increased from 9.41 to 10.77 at T2, then experienced a sharp decline to 5.00 at T3, ending at 4.61 at T4, representing a total decline of 6.16 points from peak to final measurement. Class 2 showed minimal initial change (8.37 to 8.47), followed by a steep decline to 4.04 at T3 and further to 3.64 at T4, with a total decline of 4.83 points from peak. Class 3 maintained stable high performance from T1 (11.26) to T2 (11.30), then declined to 5.89 at T3 and remained relatively stable at 5.94 at T4, showing a total decline of 5.36 points from peak. Notably, Class 2 demonstrated the steepest proportional decline from its peak, while all three classes exhibited the most substantial cognitive decline between T2 (2015) and T3 (2018) (Fig. 2).

Growth trajectories of the three-class model

Considering the differences in baseline cognitive levels and decline patterns among the three groups, we characterized Class 1 as the “moderate-baseline rapid-declining group” (representing 5.1% of participants), Class 2 as the “low-baseline slow-declining group” (representing 44.1% of participants), and Class 3 as the “high-baseline moderate-declining group” (representing 50.9% of participants). This three-class solution reveals greater heterogeneity in cognitive decline trajectories among older adults, with Class 2 showing the most dramatic proportional decline from its peak performance despite having the lowest baseline. The majority of participants (50.9%) followed a high-baseline moderate-declining pattern, while the smallest group (5.1%) exhibited rapid decline from moderate baseline levels.

We conducted a comparative analysis of depressive scores across four time points in older adults within three distinct potential subgroups of cognitive decline. The results indicate that, overall, depressive scores of older adults progressively increased over time across all three classes, but with notable differences in baseline levels and progression patterns. Specifically, Class 2 (High-baseline group, n = 2,633, 44.1%) showed the highest initial depression scores, rising from 9.60 at T1 to 10.68 at T4, an increase of 1.08 points. Class 1 (Moderate-baseline group, n = 302, 5.1%) demonstrated scores increasing from 8.35 to 10.25, an increase of 1.90 points, showing the steepest rate of change. In contrast, Class 3 (Low-baseline group, n = 3,042, 50.9%) maintained consistently lower depression levels, rising from 6.82 at T1 to 8.08 at T4, an increase of 1.26 points. Although all time points demonstrated highly significant statistical differences between classes (p < 0.001), the gap between Class 1 and Class 2 narrowed over time, with their difference decreasing from 1.25 points at T1 to 0.43 points by T4, while Class 3 remained distinctly lower throughout all time points (Fig. 3).

Therefore, although all three subgroups of cognitive decline experienced an increase in depression levels, the progression patterns revealed important heterogeneity. The largest subgroup (Class 3, 50.9%) maintained relatively low depression levels throughout the study period, while the moderate-baseline group (Class 1) showed convergence with the high-baseline group (Class 2) over time. This outcome reveals the complex relationship between cognitive decline trajectories and depression progression, suggesting that different cognitive decline patterns may be associated with distinct depression vulnerability profiles.

Cross-lagged panel network analysis

Cross-lagged panel network analysis reveals that mood disorders (MOD) establish a central hub position across all three cognitive trajectory classes, particularly demonstrating remarkable cross-class consistency during the initial phase (T1→T2). The average out-strength and in-strength values show high similarity across the three classes: 4.096 for the moderate baseline rapid decline group, 4.153 for the low baseline slow decline group, and 4.005 for the high baseline moderate decline group, with a relative difference of only 3.7%. All classes consistently identify MOD as the highest centrality node, reflecting a common core structural pattern in symptom networks during early cognitive decline. Irritability (IRR), social isolation (SOI), and lethargy (LET) constitute secondary core node clusters, similarly exhibiting cross-class structural similarity during the initial phase. In contrast, lack of optimism (OPD) consistently presents as a peripheral node across all classes, with its stable low centrality position potentially reflecting the unique functional role of cognitive bias dimensions within the symptom network(Fig. 4).

Discussion

Identification of cognitive decline risk groups

To address RQ1, this study employed Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) to analyze cognitive decline trajectories in older adults. The GMM analysis revealed that the older adults could be categorized into three distinct subgroups with significantly different baseline cognitive performance and decline patterns: a Moderate-baseline Moderate-declining Subgroup (Class 1, 5.1%, n = 302), a Low-baseline Moderate-declining Subgroup (Class 2, 44.1%, n = 2,633), and a High-baseline Steep-declining Subgroup (Class 3, 50.9%, n = 3,042). This three-class solution demonstrates significant heterogeneity in cognitive decline among the older adults, with the majority of participants (50.9%) following a high-baseline but steep-declining pattern. The identification of three distinct cognitive decline trajectories provides compelling evidence for the heterogeneity in cognitive aging processes. Notably, the largest subgroup (Class 3) exhibited the highest baseline cognitive performance (mean = 11.26) but paradoxically experienced the steepest decline over the 7-year follow-up period (total decline = 5.32 points), suggesting that higher initial cognitive capacity does not necessarily confer protection against subsequent deterioration.

This finding challenges the traditional assumption that higher baseline cognition uniformly predicts better outcomes and may reflect ceiling effects or different underlying mechanisms of cognitive reserve depletion. The moderate-sized Class 2 (44.1%) demonstrated the lowest baseline performance (mean = 8.37) but showed relatively stable decline patterns (total decline = 4.73 points), while the smallest Class 1 (5.1%) represented an intermediate profile with moderate baseline performance and decline rates. These results extend existing evidence of heterogeneity in cognitive decline and provide new insights into risk stratification for targeted interventions. The three-class model reveals more nuanced patterns compared to traditional binary classifications, particularly highlighting the vulnerability of high-performing individuals to steep cognitive decline. This has significant implications for clinical practice and public health policies, suggesting that intervention strategies should be tailored not only based on current cognitive status but also on predicted trajectory patterns [45, 46]. The findings support the use of GMM as an effective approach for uncovering latent subgroups in longitudinal cognitive data, enabling more precise identification of at-risk populations [47–49].

Depressive symptoms in the low-baseline slow-decline group

To address research question 2, this study employed cross-lagged panel network analysis to compare the evolutionary patterns of depressive symptom networks across three cognitive decline trajectory classes over three time periods (T1-T2, T2-T3, T3-T4). Results revealed that all classes exhibited the strongest cross-lagged effect intensities during the initial stage (T1-T2), with the moderate baseline moderate decline group (Class 1), low baseline moderate decline group (Class 2), and high baseline rapid decline group (Class 3) demonstrating average cross-lagged effects of 0.41, 0.41, and 0.40, respectively, along with 68, 70, and 66 strong effect connections. Centrality analysis revealed that “mood disorder” (MOD) served as the most critical hub node across all classes during the initial stage, exhibiting the highest out-strength and in-strength centrality, indicating that mood disorder plays a pivotal role in the establishment and maintenance of depressive symptom networks.

Over time, the symptom networks of the three classes demonstrated significant differentiation patterns and attenuation trends. During the intermediate stage (T2-T3), cross-lagged effect intensities declined substantially across all classes, with average effects for Classes 1, 2, and 3 decreasing to 0.28, 0.25, and 0.24, respectively, while strong effect connections dropped dramatically to 36, 19, and 15. Notably, core symptom nodes underwent transitions during this stage: the highest in-strength node for Class 1 shifted from MOD to “sleep disturbance” (SLD), Class 2 transitioned to “social isolation” (SOI) [50], while Class 3 maintained MOD as the central node. In the later stage (T3-T4), inter-class differences became more pronounced, with Classes 1 and 3 showing slight network strength recovery (0.27 and 0.26, respectively), while Class 2 continued to maintain low levels (0.24). This “V-shaped” change pattern may reflect the heterogeneity in symptom network reorganization and adaptive capacity across different cognitive trajectory groups.

These findings illuminate the dynamic evolutionary characteristics of depressive symptom networks within the context of cognitive decline, providing crucial insights for precision mental health interventions. The high consistency of symptom networks across the three classes during the initial stage suggests that early intervention strategies can adopt relatively unified approaches, focusing on the identification and management of mood disorder as the core symptom. However, the class differentiation patterns that emerge over time indicate that intermediate and later-stage interventions require personalized design based on the specific network characteristics of different cognitive trajectory groups. Particularly for the high baseline rapid decline group (Class 3), which demonstrates network recovery capacity, interventions should focus on maintaining symptom network stability; whereas for the low baseline moderate decline group (Class 2), which exhibits persistently low connection strength, efforts should emphasize rebuilding and strengthening inter-symptom connections. This stratified intervention strategy based on network dynamic characteristics holds promise for enhancing the effectiveness of depressive symptom management in older adult populations and provides scientific evidence for developing personalized cognitive-emotional integrated intervention programs [51–53].

Limitations and future directions

Despite providing valuable insights, this study has several limitations. Firstly, all variables in this study were based on self-reports, which may lead to subjective bias and affect the accuracy of the data. Self-report measures are often prone to recall bias and social desirability bias, which can undermine the reliability of the findings [54]. Future research should consider incorporating objective measurements, such as neuropsychological tests and medical records, and multi-source data, including reports from family members or caregivers, to enhance the reliability and validity of the findings [55, 56].

Secondly, the sample used in this study was limited to the Chinese older adults, and the results may not be generalizable to other cultural backgrounds and regions. Cultural differences can significantly influence both cognitive decline and depressive symptoms [57, 58]. Therefore, future research should expand to more diverse samples and conduct cross-cultural comparisons to validate and extend our findings. Including diverse populations will help to understand the universal and culture-specific aspects of cognitive decline and depression [59].

Thirdly, although this study employed a longitudinal design, issues such as sample attrition and measurement consistency over time may affect the stability and generalizability of the results. Longitudinal studies often face challenges related to participant dropout, which can lead to biased estimates if the attrition is non-random [60]. Future research should design longer-term follow-up studies and use multiple measurement tools to improve the validity and reliability of the findings [61]. Veloso et al. (2018) indicated that long-term follow-up and the use of diverse measurement tools could more accurately reveal the dynamic relationship between cognitive decline and depressive symptoms in the older adults [62].

Moreover, while this study revealed an association between cognitive decline and depressive symptoms, the causal relationship remains to be further explored. Establishing causality is complex and requires sophisticated statistical methods. Future studies should employ more advanced techniques, such as structural equation modeling (SEM) and multilevel modeling (MLM), to delve deeper into the causal relationships between these variables [63]. Additionally, incorporating biomarkers and genetic data could provide more comprehensive evidence to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the relationship between cognitive function and depressive symptoms [64, 65].

Future research directions should continue exploring the causal relationship between cognitive decline and depressive symptoms, particularly focusing on how effective interventions can slow cognitive decline and improve mental health. Additionally, leveraging advanced statistical methods and big data technologies to analyze large-scale longitudinal data can provide more precise and comprehensive scientific evidence, guiding psychological and cognitive interventions for the older adults.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study provides compelling evidence for the heterogeneous nature of cognitive decline in Chinese middle-aged and older adults and its complex relationship with depressive symptom networks. Through Growth Mixture Modeling, we identified three distinct cognitive decline trajectories, with the majority of participants (50.9%) following a high-baseline but steep-declining pattern, challenging traditional assumptions about cognitive reserve protection. The Cross-Lagged Panel Network analysis revealed dynamic evolutionary patterns in depressive symptom networks, with mood disturbance serving as a central hub across all cognitive trajectory classes during early stages, followed by class-specific differentiation over time. The findings have significant implications for clinical practice and public health policy. First, the identification of heterogeneous cognitive decline patterns suggests that intervention strategies should be tailored not only based on current cognitive status but also on predicted trajectory patterns. Second, the evolution of depressive symptom networks from initial consistency to later differentiation indicates that early intervention strategies can adopt relatively unified approaches, while intermediate and later-stage interventions require personalized design based on specific network characteristics of different cognitive trajectory groups.

These results contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting precision medicine approaches in cognitive aging and mental health. The study demonstrates the value of combining advanced statistical modeling techniques with longitudinal data to uncover complex patterns that may not be apparent through traditional analytical approaches. The insights gained from this research provide a foundation for developing targeted interventions that address both cognitive decline and depressive symptoms in aging populations, ultimately contributing to healthier aging outcomes. Future research should focus on validating these findings across diverse populations, exploring the underlying biological and psychosocial mechanisms driving these patterns, and developing and testing targeted interventions based on cognitive trajectory membership and depressive symptom network characteristics. The integration of biomarkers, genetic data, and advanced neuroimaging techniques could further enhance our understanding of the complex interplay between cognitive aging and mental health.
