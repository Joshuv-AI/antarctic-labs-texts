Aligning training with clinical reality: linking case-mix and self-reported confidence to entrustable professional activities in physician-staffed EMS
Authors: Christian Engelen, Tobias Bexten, Kai Schmid, Jens Christian Kubitz, Anne Kamphausen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergency medical services (EMS) physicians must manage a broad spectrum of high-acuity prehospital emergencies. However, current training pathways in Germany are not explicitly competency-based and provide limited structured exposure to low-frequency, high-stakes scenarios. Entrustable professional activities (EPAs) offer a framework to align training with real-world clinical demands. This study aimed to identify key prehospital tasks suitable for an EPA-based curriculum.MethodsWe conducted a multi-method descriptive study combining (1) a retrospective analysis of 4,078 prehospital EMS physician records (Nuremberg, 2017–2021) to assess case-mix and procedural frequencies and (2) an online survey of 134 bavarian EMS physicians assessing self-reported confidence across emergency domains. Tasks were categorized on the basis of frequency and confidence levels and mapped to candidate EPAs.ResultsNontraumatic emergencies were dominated by cardiovascular and neurological conditions, whereas traumatic cases involved primarily extremity injuries. The most frequent interventions were intravenous access, oxygen administration, and ECG acquisition; invasive procedures were rare. Physicians self-reported high perceived confidence in resuscitation, airway management, and analgesia and lower perceived confidence in paediatric, neonatal, obstetric, and mass-casualty scenarios. Physicians with an anaesthesiology background descriptively reported higher perceived confidence across several domains; no inferential testing was performed. Tasks characterized by either high frequency with low confidence or low frequency with high stakes were identified as key targets for EPA development.ConclusionThese findings identify patterns of clinical exposure and self-perceived confidence in the studied EMS context and suggest domains that may warrant further investigation in broader settings. They may inform the preliminary development of candidate EPA-based training frameworks in physician-staffed EMS systems. However, the findings do not demonstrate actual competence deficits, unsafe practices, patient safety effects, or the effectiveness of EPA-based curricula.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12873-026-01753-y.

Introduction

Entrustable professional activities (EPAs) are units of professional practice that can be entrusted to a trainee once sufficient competence has been demonstrated. Since their introduction by ten Cate, EPAs have become a central component of competency-based medical education (CBME), bridging the gap between abstract competencies and observable clinical tasks [1].

Prehospital emergency medicine presents a unique challenge for training. EMS physicians must be prepared to manage a wide range of acute conditions across all age groups, often in resource-limited and time-critical environments. While some skills are performed frequently, others, such as neonatal resuscitation, paediatric emergencies, or prehospital obstetrics, are rare but critical. This variability results in inconsistent exposure during training [2, 3].

In Germany, qualification as an EMS physician requires an additional certification (“Zusatzbezeichnung Notfallmedizin”), typically obtained after at least 24 months of clinical training. However, current regulations focus primarily on less prescriptive procedural counts (e.g., 50 endotracheal intubations performed) and do not comprehensively address the breadth of competencies required for independent prehospital practice. As a result, important but infrequently encountered scenarios may be insufficiently covered.

Simulation-based training programs have been developed to address these gaps, but their availability and implementation remain inconsistent. Consequently, training often relies on opportunistic exposure rather than structured competency development [4–7].

EPAs provide a potential solution by defining concrete, observable tasks that integrate knowledge, skills, and clinical judgment. They enable structured assessment, progressive autonomy, and transparent entrustment decisions. [8, 9] While EPAs have been proposed in emergency medicine, their application in physician-staffed EMS systems remains limited [1, 10].

Meaningful EPA development requires an empirical understanding of which tasks physicians encounter and in which domains preparedness is insufficient. In physician-staffed EMSs, the case mix is highly variable and system dependent, making hospital-based curriculum mapping an inadequate substitute. EPAs developed without reference to local case-mix risk prioritize educationally convenient tasks rather than clinically relevant ones. To date and to our knowledge, no study has systematically linked EMS case-mix data with physicians’ self-perceived confidence in deriving EPA candidates for physician-staffed systems.

This study aimed to identify key prehospital tasks suitable for EPA-based training by systematically linking empirical EMS case-mix data with self-perceived confidence to identify candidate tasks for EPA-based training in a physician-staffed EMS system.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a multi-method descriptive study using two complementary quantitative data sources: a retrospective analysis of the German Interdisciplinary Association for Intensive Care and Emergency Medicine (DIVI) operational protocol for emergency physicians in prehospital emergency medicine, with data collected through a survey based on findings from these records, and an assessment of the self-perceived confidence of EMS physicians.

Retrospective data sources and participants

This retrospective analysis of the DIVI operational protocol for emergency physicians in prehospital emergency medicine covered a total of 4,078 protocols in the period from 2017 to 2021. The DIVI operational protocol for emergency physicians is a standardized protocol template and is available as a handwritten paper form.

The protocols originate from one emergency service of the EMS physician ambulance car of Nuremberg City. The Nuremberg EMS system serves a predominantly urban population and operates four EMS physician response units, collectively managing approximately 24,000 physician-attended emergency calls per year.

Variables and data collection

Data were extracted from handwritten EMS physician protocols using a standardized, study-specific data extraction form developed a priori. The variables included:

Data extraction was performed by trained members of the study team (KS). To ensure consistency and reliability, a subset of records was independently reviewed and extracted by a second investigator (CE). Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Suspected or working diagnoses were recorded as documented in the original EMS protocols and subsequently mapped to predefined diagnostic categories according to the DIVI protocol.

In cases of ambiguous or unclear documentation, classification decisions were made by consensus within the study team.

No imputation of missing data was performed. All the variables were treated as observational data without modification.

Survey design, participants, and data collection

The survey was designed as a cross-sectional, anonymous, web-based questionnaire. The survey comprised a total of 56 items with the following domains: (1) demographic and professional characteristics (age, sex, medical specialty, years of EMS experience, work setting, additional qualifications, and prior paramedic/EMT experience); (2) self-perceived confidence in managing prehospital emergency scenarios (including cardiovascular, neurological, respiratory, traumatic, paediatric, neonatal, obstetric, and mass-casualty incidents); (3) self-perceived confidence in the use of common prehospital medication groups (analgesics, sedatives, antiarrhythmics, antidotes, and others); (4) self-perceived confidence in performing prehospital procedures (airway management, mechanical ventilation, intraosseous access, analgosedation, and others); (5) organizational and legal topics (mass casualty incident management, legal aspects of prehospital care); and (6) primary sources of knowledge for each domain. To maintain a high level of methodological structure, the questionnaire primarily consisted of closed-ended single- and multiple-choice questions.

The questionnaire was developed on the basis of the findings from the retrospective EMS case-mix analysis and was iteratively refined by the study team. The confidence rating scale was adapted from the entrustment framework described by Mulder et al. and informed by Miller’s pyramid of clinical competence [11, 12]. To support content relevance and clarity, the questionnaire underwent an expert content review by experienced EMS physicians. However, no formal validation study or external pilot testing was conducted, and the instrument has not been previously validated in a separate study. The complete questionnaire is included in Supplementary File 1 to enable full appraisal and reproducibility.

Confidence was measured using a 5-point Likert scale with the following response options: 1 = not confident at all, 2 = slightly confident, 3 = moderately confident, 4 = confident, and 5 = extremely confident.

For the EPA classification framework, confidence ratings were dichotomized into low confidence (scores 1–3) (LC) and high confidence (scores 4–5) (HC). This threshold was set a priori: a rating of “moderately confident” was considered insufficient to reflect the level of readiness required for independent unsupervised prehospital practice, which is consistent with the entrustment rationale underlying EPA frameworks. The frequency data from the retrospective analysis were dichotomized at the median.

Furthermore, participants who reported being confident or extremely confident in a given domain were asked to identify the primary source of knowledge contributing to that confidence, selecting from the following predefined categories: (A) Clinical work: hospital-based practice within the participant’s primary medical specialty (e.g., ICU, anaesthesiology, internal medicine ward); (B) Prehospital work: experience acquired directly during EMS physician deployments on the emergency ambulance car; (C) Formal course: structured educational programs with defined curricula, including the mandatory 80-hour Notarzt course, ACLS, ITLS, PALS, or intensive care transport certification; (D) Simulation: scenario-based training using mannequins or high-fidelity simulators outside real patient care; and (E) Self-directed learning: independent study via textbooks, guidelines, or scientific literature. This item was designed as a single-choice question: participants were instructed to select the one category they perceived as most influential for each domain, and only this single response was recorded and analysed.

Recruitment, survey administration, and data protection: Participants were EMS physicians working in Bavaria, Germany, recruited through established professional EMS networks; the survey was open to all eligible physicians within these distribution channels, participation was voluntary, and no incentives were offered. The questionnaire was administered using Google Forms (Google LLC). Informed consent was obtained electronically at the outset of the survey, and participants could proceed to the questionnaire only after providing consent; no identifying personal data were collected. Responses were collected anonymously, IP addresses and other indirect identifiers were not stored, and data handling complied with institutional data protection regulations.

Response rate and completeness

The DIVI protocol dataset comprised 4,078 records as the final analytical sample. All 4,078 records were assigned a sequential case number and included in the analysis. No records were excluded because of incompleteness, as the study treated all the variables as observational data without imputation or exclusion criteria based on missing fields. Missing data were handled transparently and are reported by variable in Table 1.

As recruitment was conducted via open professional established EMS networks, the exact number of individuals who viewed the survey link could not be determined. Therefore, a precise response rate cannot be calculated. All the submitted questionnaires were screened for completeness.

Handling of duplicate responses

No technical restrictions (e.g., IP-based blocking or cookies) were implemented to prevent multiple submissions. However, no duplicate or suspicious entries were identified during data screening.

Data handling and analysis

Responses were exported from Google Forms into a secure database for analysis. Only fully anonymized datasets were used for statistical processing. Self-perceived confidence ratings were measured using a 5-point Likert scale and analysed descriptively.

Data processing and categorization framework

Each task or scenario was treated as a single unit corresponding to a diagnostic category, medication group, prehospital procedure, or organizational/legal domain. These units were defined a priori on the basis of the DIVI protocol structure and adopted as the survey domains, ensuring direct correspondence between both data sources.

The frequency was derived from the retrospective case-mix analysis and expressed as the proportion of protocols in which a given diagnosis or procedure was documented. Both frequency and confidence were dichotomized as described above: frequency at the median of the observed distribution (above = HF; at or below = LF); confidence at a mean Likert score of 4.0 (≥ 4 = HC; <4 = LC), representing the threshold between “moderately confident” and “confident”.

Confidence for each task was calculated as the mean Likert score across all available respondents (n = 134) for the corresponding survey item.

Clinical risk (high-stakes vs. low-stakes) was applied as a secondary dimension for low-frequency tasks. A task was classified as high if (1) mismanagement carried an immediate risk of patient mortality or severe irreversible morbidity and (2) no feasible prehospital alternative could substitute for direct physician competence at the scene. This classification was developed through investigator consensus.

The resulting classification framework, which integrates all the analytical dimensions, is presented as a joint display in Table 6. High-frequency/low-confidence and low-frequency/high-stakes categories were prioritized on the basis of patient safety reasoning: frequent tasks with low confidence generate cumulative risk across many encounters; rare tasks with high stakes carry acute, irreversible consequences if mismanaged. This finding is consistent with the CBME literature on entrustment and simulation-based training [2, 7, 13–15]. HF/HC tasks were included for entrustment transparency; LF/LS tasks were assigned optional status.

Data integration

A convergent analytic approach was used. The retrospective EMS dataset and survey responses were analysed separately and subsequently integrated at the interpretation stage. Triangulation of objective frequency data and subjective confidence ratings was used to derive the final EPA categorization framework.

Statistical analysis

All the analyses were descriptive. Data are presented as the means ± standard deviations or proportions. No inferential statistical testing was performed.

Ethics

The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of PMU—Paracelsus Medical University, Nuremberg, Germany (IRB-2023-06). Survey participants provided informed consent. Retrospective data were analysed in anonymized form.

Results

Case mix

The mean patient age was 60.6 years, with a balanced sex distribution. The age distribution by sex is shown in Fig. 1.

The working/suspected diagnoses specified in the DIVI protocols were divided into nontraumatological and trauma clinical pictures.

Nontraumatic emergencies were predominantly cardiovascular conditions (most commonly ACS, n = 366 (8.97%)) and neurological emergencies (seizures, n = 278 (6.82%); cerebrovascular events, n = 249 (6.11%)). Among the traumatic cases, lower limb trauma was most common (n = 224 (5.49%)), followed by upper limb trauma (n = 166 (4.07%)) and traumatic brain injury (n = 148 (3.63%)). The full diagnostic distributions are presented in Figs. 2 and 3.

Procedural frequencies

The most common procedures were intravenous access (n = 2961 (72.6%)), oxygen administration (n = 879 (21.6%)) and ECG acquisition (n = 556 (13.6%)).

Invasive procedures such as intubation and advanced airway management were infrequent. The results are displayed in Fig. 4; Table 1.

Discussion

By linking retrospective case-mix data with self-perceived confidence among EMS physicians, this study identifies areas in which physicians reported lower confidence. Given that confidence is not a direct measure of competence, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of actual competence deficits or unsafe practices. Cardiovascular and neurological emergencies dominated the case mix, whereas invasive procedures were relatively rare. Although physicians reported high self-perceived confidence in core resuscitation skills, substantial uncertainty remained in the paediatric, neonatal, obstetric, and organizational domains. These findings suggest a potential mismatch between training exposure and clinical demands within the studied context, although caution is needed when generalizing beyond a single urban EMS system and a Bavarian survey sample.

Positioning the findings within EPA-based competency frameworks

EPAs offer a structured approach to address gaps in medical education by clearly defining observable clinical tasks and enabling progressive entrustment. In particular, tasks characterized by either high frequency and low self-perceived confidence or low frequency and high clinical relevance may help identify areas that warrant further investigation and educational consideration. These findings may also contribute to the future development and validation of candidate EPAs but should not be interpreted as evidence of competence deficits or as validated EPA recommendations.

Our findings are broadly consistent with international competency-based medical education (CBME) frameworks, which emphasize workplace-based assessment and simulation for low-frequency, high-stake scenarios. However, given the exploratory nature of this study and the lack of formal EPA development and validation, these observations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Simulation-based training is particularly important in prehospital medicine, where real-world exposure to certain critical events is limited [2, 7, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18]. this constraint is compounded by the fact that current German EMS physician qualifications remain largely course-based and do not explicitly define thresholds for independent practice. This may contribute to a discrepancy between formal certification and perceived readiness and thus a phenomenon that may impact patient safety.

This discrepancy reflects a common limitation of outcome-based curricula: they define learning objectives but not the entrustment threshold for independent prehospital action. Such a discrepancy may create uncertainty about readiness for independent practice in scenarios that demand rapid, unsupervised decision-making; however, the present study did not assess actual clinical performance or patient safety outcomes [4, 7].

Integrating structured experiential learning through structured supervision, simulation, and longitudinal mentorship may help narrow this gap.

Such models have proven effective in international prehospital training (e.g., UK PHEM curriculum, Dutch and Swiss emergency medicine rotations) where exposure and feedback are systematically tracked, although comparative outcome data remain limited [4, 15].

Previous publications suggest the following potential advantages of EPA implementation for the training of EMS physicians [19, 20]:

(A) Curriculum-practice alignment: EPAs can be backmapped from local case mix [2], and (B) Assessment transparency: entrustment scales are intended to formalize readiness for independent practice in critical tasks [21].(C) Targeted simulation: Low-frequency domains can be simulated and reassessed until entrustment is achieved, with workplace-based assessment and longitudinal portfolios;(D) Portability and accountability: EPA sign-offs may provide auditable evidence of competence across systems and could help support patient safety during high-risk transitions [22].

Practical implications

In accordance with previous reports [23] and given the self-perceived confidence in paediatric/neonatal/obstetric emergencies and MCI/legal topics, we proposed an EMS physicians’ EPA set prioritizing high-frequency/low self-perceived confidence and low-frequency/high-clinical-risk tasks. The candidate EPAs generated in this study were derived from quantitative data and expert interpretation only. No formal consensus-building or validation process involving relevant stakeholders was performed. Therefore, the proposed candidate EPAs should be considered preliminary and require further refinement and validation before implementation.

Despite its promise, an EPA-based reform of EMS physician education requires careful design and resource investment [24, 25]:

(A) Consensus development: Stakeholders must agree on a pragmatic national set (typically 15–25 EPAs) to avoid fragmentation and assessment fatigue.(B) Assessor calibration: Without structured rater training, interobserver variability threatens validity.(C) Time and workload: Integrating EPA observations into clinical services adds administrative load; implementation should therefore prioritize high-impact EPAs first. Nevertheless, international experience suggests that these challenges may be mitigated with phased roll-out, faculty development, and institutional support.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The retrospective data were derived from handwritten DIVI records and may be incomplete or subject to documentation bias.

Additionally, the analysis was limited to a single urban EMS location, reducing generalizability (see the Generalizability section).

Furthermore, the survey instrument was developed for the purpose of this study and was not designed as a psychometric scale. Therefore, no formal reliability testing, such as Cronbach’s alpha for grouped confidence domains, was performed. This limits the interpretation of grouped confidence ratings and should be considered when the findings are interpreted. Participation was voluntary, and recruitment was conducted via professional networks, introducing potential selection bias toward experienced and engaged physicians.

Given that the survey was distributed via open channels, a precise response rate could not be calculated, limiting the assessment of nonresponse bias.

Additionally, self-reported confidence is a proxy measure and does not equate to objectively assessed competence; physicians may over- or underestimate their abilities, and confidence ratings may be influenced by specialty background or prior exposure.

The dichotomization of both confidence and frequency variables, while conceptually justified, inevitably results in loss of information and introduces an element of arbitrariness, particularly for domains in which ratings clustered near the cut-off.

Finally, the candidate EPAs were derived from quantitative data and author consensus without a formal stakeholder consensus process such as the Delphi procedure. The proposed EPAs should therefore be considered preliminary and require formal validation before implementation.

The results should be interpreted accordingly, and future studies should consider ordinal or continuous analytical approaches.

The generalizability of these findings is also limited by several structural factors. The case mix derives from a single urban EMS location operating a rendezvous model with predominantly anaesthesiology-trained physicians. This is a unique composition that may have altered the confidence ratings in the airway and procedural domains compared to systems with different staffing profiles. Rural systems, where trauma proportions and transport times differ, may show divergent case-mix patterns. The survey sample was self-selected via professional networks and likely overrepresents experienced, engaged physicians. These findings should therefore be regarded as locally derived and hypothesis-generating rather than representative of German EMS physician practice broadly.

Conclusions

This single-site retrospective analysis and voluntary survey identified patterns of clinical exposure and self-perceived confidence that may inform the preliminary development of candidate EPA domains. In future educational development, such candidate domains may help generate hypotheses about how expectations for supervision, structured learning processes, and simulation-based training could be further defined and evaluated.

Future studies should examine whether EPA-based curricula are feasible, improve objectively assessed competence, and ultimately influence patient safety outcomes.
