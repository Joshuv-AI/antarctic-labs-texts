Endoscopic transnasal and transoral resection of the odontoid process and C1 combined with occipitocervical fusion for osteoradionecrosis of the upper cervical spine: a case report and literature review
Authors: Zhijie Chen, Zhongsheng Bi, Da Liu, Bin Deng, Ming Lu, Yongqin Zeng, Xubiao Zhang, Tao Lin
Published: 2024

Background

Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) of the upper cervical spine (C1 and C2) is a rare adverse event in patients with head and neck cancer who have undergone radiotherapy [1, 2]. ORN of the upper cervical spine is difficult to treat [1, 2]. To the authors’ knowledge, less than 30 cases of ORN of the upper cervical spine have been reported since it was first described in 1999 [3]. ORN of the upper cervical spine can present similarly to metastasis, recurrence, or infection, making it crucial to obtain a histopathological analysis for an accurate diagnosis [2–4]. Here, we describe treatment of a patient with a history of nasopharyngeal carcinoma who developed ORN of the upper cervical spine and review the published literature reporting surgical management of this condition.

Case presentation

In July 2023, a 59-year-old female patient presented to our hospital with persistent neck pain for one month and limited range of neck motion. The patient had undergone radiotherapy for nasopharyngeal carcinoma 15 years ago, with a total dose of 69.96 Gy. No other abnormalities were found on a neurological examination.

Computed tomography revealed the lesion had destroyed the front of C1 and C2, and thickening of the soft tissues in the posterior wall of the nasopharynx (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed enhanced abnormal masses in the clivus, C1, C2, and adjacent nasopharynx posterior wall, basilar invagination (BI), and atlantoaxial subluxation (AAS) (Fig. 1). A mass occurring around the odontoid process was impinging on the medulla oblongata and cervical spine, causing spinal stenosis and degeneration of the spinal cord.

Post-operative pathology of the odontoid mass showed areas of fibrosis and necrosis, granulation tissue, and no evidence of malignancy.

Surgical procedure

The patient underwent combined endoscopic transnasal and transoral resection of the mass occurring around the odontoid process and C1 anterior arch with neuronavigation (Fig. 2). A “U” shaped mucosal flap was created at the posterior pharyngeal wall. Under neuronavigation, the odontoid process was removed using a 3 mm drill. The mucosa was sutured with 4 − 0 absorbable sutures, covered with biological glue, and compressed with iodophor gauze. A piece of fascia with blood vessels was taken from the outer side of the right thigh and sutured onto the nasopharyngeal flap under oral endoscopy. This fascia lata graft was required because the posterior pharyngeal wall mucosa was incised by an electric knife and could not be tightly sutured. For nasal endoscopy, a No.10 Foley catheter was inserted, the balloon was inflated with 6 ml saline, and fixed on the posterior pharyngeal wall.

Occipitocervical fusion (C0C3/C4) was performed within 10 days of endoscopic resection. There were no complications, such as leakage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or infection.

Three days after surgery, the patient’s neck pain was significantly improved. Five days after surgery, the patient was able to walk and was discharged from the hospital. After one year, MR examination showed no abnormal findings. Cervical radiography with a DR device revealed good recovery after occipitocervical fusion (Fig. 1).

Literature review

To better understand surgical management of ORN of the upper cervical spine, the literature published in the PubMed, Ovid MEDLINE, and Embase databases was reviewed. Since 2005, 11 cases of surgical management of ORN of the upper cervical spine (including the present case) have been published. Detailed information of these cases is included in Table 1. Mean patient age was 62.45 years. Mean time from radiotherapy to the onset of ORN was 8.7 years. Mean previous radiotherapy dose was 79.99 Gy. The most common primary cancer was nasopharyngeal carcinoma (6 cases), followed by laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (2 cases). BI and/or AAS were observed in 4 /11 cases. Endoscopic procedures were performed in 4/11 cases, and occipitocervical fusion was performed in 8 /11 cases.

Discussion and conclusions

To the authors’ knowledge, only 97 cases of cervical ORN, including 27 cases of ORN of the upper cervical spine have been published up to 2023 [5]. Currently, there is no consensus on the clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and treatment of ORN of the upper cervical spine. To raise awareness of this condition, we describe a patient with a history of nasopharyngeal carcinoma who developed ORN of the upper cervical spine and review the published literature reporting surgical management.

ORN of the upper cervical spine is most often associated with nasopharyngeal carcinoma, tongue cancer, and hypopharyngeal cancer [5]. In these patients, ORN of the upper cervical spine is usually a complication of high-dose radiation therapy, which can cause necrosis of normal bone tissue due to cell death and vascular damage [3, 6, 7]. Our review of the literature, including the present study, showed patients who developed ORN of the upper cervical spine received a previous mean radiotherapy dose of 79.99 Gy. Accordingly, in the present study, the patient received a previous radiotherapy dose of 69.96 Gy. ORN of the upper cervical spine most commonly affects the clivus, C1, and C2, which is related to the radiotherapy treatment area [5, 8].

Symptoms of ORN of the upper cervical spine vary in severity, type, and rate of progression [5]. Neck pain is the main symptom, with other less common symptoms including headache, kyphosis, fever, epistaxis, dysphagia, cough, bowel/bladder incontinence, dysgeusia, and respiratory distress [5, 6, 9, 10]. Respiratory failure and death can occur [5]. In the present study, the patient had persistent neck pain and headache, with restricted range of motion in the neck and difficulty lifting her head.

Patients with ORN of the upper cervical spine may experience fever, cough, pneumonia, meningitis, leakage of CSF, edema, and sepsis [11]. If a patient has BI and AAS, symptoms may not be relieved with conservative treatment. BI is life-threatening due to the potential for severe dysphagia caused by protrusion of the odontoid process [12]. AAS occurs when there is a fracture in the C1 vertebra [13, 14], leading to persistent neck pain and restricted range of motion in the neck.

There is no consensus on conservative or surgical treatment of ORN of the upper cervical spine. Conservative treatment includes analgesics, antibiotics, physical therapy, and hyperbaric oxygen therapy [3, 10, 15]. Surgical treatment may be more effective than conservative treatment, with a success rate of 77.3% compared to 41.1% [15]. Surgical treatment of ORN of the upper cervical spine varies based on a patient’s condition and the surgeon’s judgment. Occipitocervical fusion is frequently used. Our review of the literature, including the present study, showed 8/11 patients underwent occipitocervical fusion, including 2 patients who underwent occipitocervical fusion after anterior spinal fusion (ASF). Occipitocervical fusion commonly involved C2 to C7, depending on pathology and surgeon’s preference. In a previous report, occipitocervical fusions were performed in 83.3% of postodontoidectomy patients [16]. In 2015, combined endoscopic transnasal and transoral techniques were used to remove necrosis in C1 and C2 in a 56-year-old female with ORN of the upper cervical spine. Subsequently, occipitocervical fusion was completed to prevent severe neurological and functional deficits [14]. In the present study, the patient presented with BI and AAS, and the medulla oblongata and cervical spinal cord were compressed by the odontoid process. Resection of the anterior odontoid process and C1 anterior arch did not alleviate symptoms; therefore, the patient underwent occipitocervical fusion. Intraoperative venous bleeding was rapidly controlled by injecting fluid gelatin into the bleeding site.

Posterior spinal fusion without anterior surgery is an option for ORN of the upper cervical spine with limited bone necrosis or high surgical risk [4, 17]. For large ORN affecting C2 to C4, debridement, decompression, and spinal realignment with fibular bone graft are beneficial [10]. Our review of the literature, including the present study, showed 5/11 patients underwent combined anterior-posterior surgery and 4 /5 of these patients underwent anterior odontoidectomy followed by posterior occipitocervical fusion. 2/11 patients underwent anterior surgery alone; of these, one patient required debridement and removal of the anterior arch of C1 [18], while the other underwent endoscopic transoral debridement of necrosis in C2 and nasopharynx reconstruction with a radial forearm free flap [19].

Lesions of the odontoid process present a significant surgical challenge [20]. In 2005, clinical experience showed that endoscopic transnasal resection was effective in patients with erosion of the odontoid process by rheumatoid arthritis [21]. Prior to this, the microsurgical transoral approach was widely regarded as the “gold standard” for surgically removing lesions of the odontoid process [22]. The endoscopic transnasal approach provides a wider optical field and better exposure for resection of the odontoid process compared to the microsurgical transoral approach, but the distance to the target is shorted with the transoral approach [23]. Due to long operating distance and blind spots, instruments for endoscopic nasal surgery require long-handled drills and endoscope lenses with multiple angles [23]. Our review of the literature, including the present study, showed 1/11 patient was treated with a nasal endoscope, 1/11 patient was treated with a transoral endoscope, and 2/11 patients were treated with combined approaches (including our current patient). The other patients were operated on without an endoscope. Endoscopic transnasal and transoral approaches are both safe and effective for resection of the odontoid process [14, 16, 24]. Patients undergoing the endoscopic transnasal approach are at a higher risk for CSF leaks than patients undergoing the endoscopic transoral approach [16]. If the dura mater is damaged during surgery, it can be sutured under endoscopy and a lumbar drain can promote healing if CSF leakage occurs [25]. Covering pharyngeal ulcers with a pedicled flap after debridement may improve long-term results [19], while antibiotics can help control infection and promote healing [14].

BI may cause the odontoid process and C1 to be exposed above the hard palate, making complete removal difficult with endoscopic transnasal or transoral approaches alone. BI can affect the nasopalatine line, and the hard palate may need to be resected to expose the structures using an endoscopic transnasal approach [26].

Surgical intervention is necessary for ORN of the upper cervical spine to alleviate symptoms, restore anatomy, and prevent complications. Tailored treatment plans and collaboration from a multidisciplinary team are essential. Endoscopic resection combined with occipitocervical fusion is effective, especially in patients with BI and AAS.
