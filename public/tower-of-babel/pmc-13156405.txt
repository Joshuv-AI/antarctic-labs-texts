Efficacy and Safety of Azacitidine Combined With Lisaftoclax in the Treatment of Relapsed/Refractory Acute Myeloid Leukemia and Myelodysplastic Syndrome With Increased Blasts
Authors: Shan Meng, Wanhong Zhao, Hui Zhang, Bo Lei, Wanggang Zhang, Yilin Zhang, Jin Wang, Yinxia Chen, Xingmei Cao, Liufang Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo retrospectively analyze the early efficacy and safety of azacitidine plus lisaftoclax in patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myelodysplastic syndrome with increased blasts (MDS‐IB), providing a reliable clinical reference.MethodsA total of 17 patients admitted to the Department of Hematology between August 1 and December 31, 2025, were enrolled. The treatment was as follows: azacitidine 75 mg/m2 (Days 1–7, subcutaneously) and lisaftoclax 200–600 mg (Days 1–10/14, orally), repeated in 28‐day cycles. Efficacy and safety were evaluated.ResultsAmong nine AML patients, 55.6% achieved complete remission (CR) after one cycle, with 60.0% of responders being MRD‐negative. Among eight MDS‐IB patients, 62.5% achieved CR and 25.0% achieved hematologic improvement, with 40.0% of CR patients being MRD‐negative. The median time to first CR was 1 month for both AML and MDS‐IB patients. In the entire cohort, common Grade 3–4 adverse events included thrombocytopenia/leukopenia (58.8%), lymphopenia (47.1%), and febrile neutropenia (35.3%, AML: 22.2%, MDS‐IB: 50.0%); no treatment‐related deaths occurred. Median follow‐up was 2.1 months; median OS was 1.9 months, and RFS was unevaluable.ConclusionThis regimen induces favorable responses with manageable toxicity, supporting its further investigation, while longer follow‐up and larger prospective studies are required to confirm the long‐term efficacy.Trial Registration: The authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myelodysplastic syndromes with increased blasts (MDS‐IB) are high‐risk myeloid neoplasms characterized by rapid progression and suboptimal treatment outcomes [1]. To date, conventional treatments have often yielded unsatisfactory results, underscoring an urgent unmet need for more effective therapeutic strategies [2].

However, the objective response rate to single‐agent azacitidine remains modest (approximately 30%–40%), and many patients develop resistance or disease progression. Adverse events (AEs), such as myelosuppression, may also compromise treatment adherence. Thus, combination therapies have emerged as a critical approach to enhancing efficacy.

Azacitidine combined with the BCL‐2 inhibitor venetoclax (Aza+Ven) is a cornerstone of first‐line therapy for elderly or unfit newly diagnosed AML patients and has significantly improved overall survival (OS) and quality of life [3]. However, the VERONA trial demonstrated negative results for Aza+Ven in MDS‐IB, leading to its lack of approval for MDS‐IB indication; moreover, Aza+Ven often causes severe myelosuppression in MDS‐IB patients, requiring dose reductions of venetoclax in clinical practice to balance efficacy and safety [4].

Lisaftoclax (APG‐2575), a novel, highly selective BCL‐2 inhibitor independently developed in China [5], exerts antitumor activity by specifically inhibiting the anti‐apoptotic function of BCL‐2 and inducing apoptosis in malignant hematopoietic cells [6]. Unlike venetoclax, lisaftoclax has weak binding affinity for BCL‐xL/BCL‐w, which reduces off‐target hematologic toxicity (e.g., severe thrombocytopenia and prolonged neutropenia) and contributes to its better safety profile. In addition, lisaftoclax has a favorable pharmacokinetic profile with mild myelosuppressive effects, which avoids the need for dose reduction in MDS‐IB patients. Preclinical studies have confirmed that azacitidine can sensitize tumor cells to BCL‐2 inhibition through hypomethylation, creating a synergistic anti‐tumor effect with lisaftoclax; this synergy is more significant in MDS‐IB patients with bone marrow reserve impairment, providing a theoretical basis for the non‐dose reduction of lisaftoclax in this population.

Given the disease heterogeneity between AML and MDS‐IB, and the unmet clinical need for MDS‐IB after the negative VERONA trial [7], this retrospective study aims to evaluate the early efficacy and safety of Aza+Lisa in AML and MDS‐IB subgroups separately, and to explore the clinical value of this regimen in venetoclax‐refractory patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective, single‐arm, single‐center clinical study was designed to describe the early efficacy and safety profiles of azacitidine combined with lisaftoclax in AML and MDS‐IB patients. The study was conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University and was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No. 2025–261). All patients provided written informed consent prior to treatment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national regulatory requirements.

Patient Enrollment

Patients who met the diagnostic criteria for relapsed/refractory AML (according to NCCN Guidelines Version 1.2024) and myelodysplastic syndrome with excess blasts (WHO Classification, fifth edition, 2022), and who had received at least one cycle of treatment with the azacitidine plus lisaftoclax regimen followed by an efficacy evaluation, were enrolled in the study.

Treatment Regimen

Azacitidine for injection: 75 mg/m2, subcutaneously, once daily, on Days 1–7, lisaftoclax tablets: for AML patients: 200 mg/d, orally, D1–3; 600 mg/d, D4–14, once daily; for MDS‐IB patients: 200 mg/d, orally, D1–3; 400 mg/d, D4–14, once daily; repeated in 28‐day cycles. Lisaftoclax dose adjustments were permitted based on therapeutic drug monitoring for patients concurrently receiving strong CYP3A inhibitors like azole antifungals.

Efficacy Assessment

Efficacy assessment was performed at the end of each cycle. For AML patients, efficacy assessment was performed based on NCCN Guidelines Version 1.2024. Briefly, responses were categorized as complete remission (CR), partial remission (PR), no response (NR), and progressive disease (PD). Morphologic leukemia‐free state was defined as bone marrow blasts at less than 5%, and the absence of circulating blasts and extramedullary disease, with no requirements for hematologic recovery. Relapse was defined as the reappearance of leukemic blasts in the peripheral blood, bone marrow blasts at more than 5% (after excluding other causes such as bone marrow regeneration), or evidence of extramedullary disease after achieving CR. The CR rate was calculated for each cycle. Measurable residual disease (MRD) was monitored using eight‐color flow cytometry, and the MRD‐negative rate was calculated among patients who achieved composite complete remission (cCR).

As for patients with MDS‐IB, efficacy was evaluated according to the WHO Classification, fifth edition, 2022. Briefly, CR was defined as bone marrow blasts at less than 5% with no dysplasia in any cell lineage, and peripheral blood counts meeting specific criteria (neutrophils ≥ 1.0 × 109/L, platelets ≥ 100 × 109/L, and independence from red blood cell transfusions). Hematologic improvement (HI) was defined as a sustained and significant increase in erythroid, platelet, or neutrophil counts, or freedom from transfusion dependence, which was considered a clinically meaningful treatment response.

Adverse Event

AEs were monitored and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), Version 5.0.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 10.1.2; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and SPSS Statistics (Version 20.0). Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method. Stratified analysis by disease subtype (AML/MDS‐IB) was performed for all efficacy and safety endpoints, including CR rate, MRD‐negative rate, AE incidence, and median time to first CR.

Results

A total of 17 patients with confirmed R/R AML or MDS‐IB were enrolled between August 1 and December 31, 2025. The cohort comprised 11 males and six females. The male‐to‐female ratio was 3.5:1 in AML and 1:1 in MDS‐IB, respectively, with a median age of 68.5 years (range: 47–76). Subgroup analysis showed the median age was 69 years (AML, range: 47–76) and 67.5 years (MDS‐IB, range: 48–72), with seven males and two females in AML and four males and four females in MDS‐IB, respectively. Notably, the median bone marrow blast proportion was significantly higher in the AML subgroup than in the MDS‐IB subgroup (28.5% vs. 13.5%, p < 0.05). Efficacy

All patients completed at least one cycle of treatment and 41.2% of patients (7/17) completed two treatment cycles. Treatment efficacy was evaluated at the end of each cycle, and the specific outcomes are detailed in Table 2. The median time to first CR was 1 month for both AML patients and MDS‐IB patients. Importantly, for four patients who had been refractory to venetoclax previously, their one‐cycle CR rate was 75.0% (3/4), with MRD‐negative rate of 66.7% (2/3) and the median time to first CR was 1 month in this subgroup, with no difference from other patients and with no dose reduction of lisaftoclax required. Safety

All 17 patients were evaluable for safety. In the whole cohort, the most common Grade 3–4 hematological AEs were thrombocytopenia, leukopenia (58.8%, 10/17), lymphopenia (47.1%, 8/17), febrile neutropenia (35.3%, 6/17), and anemia (29.4%, 5/17). The most common Grade 3–4 non‐hematological toxicities included infection at any site (23.5%) and pneumonia (17.6%). As for different subgroups, there were no statistically significant differences in the overall profile of Grade 3–4 AEs between the AML and MDS‐IB subgroups. Hematological AEs were most frequent, which is consistent with the expected profile of intensive AML/MDS therapy. The incidence and grading of non‐hematological toxicities are summarized in Figure 2. Two patients experienced sepsis, with the causative pathogens being Escherichia coli and Enterobacter cloacae, respectively. Two patients experienced tumor lysis syndrome (TLS), both of whom were classified as biochemical TLS and improved rapidly after symptomatic management. No treatment‐related deaths occurred. Notably, no dose reduction of lisaftoclax was required in any patient due to AEs.

Survival and Disease Progression

The median OS was 2.0 months (range: 1.3–4.8) for AML patients and 2.1 months (range: 0.9–4.0) for MDS‐IB patients, with a median follow‐up of 2.1 months (AML: 2.0 months, MDS‐IB: 2.2 months). Relapse‐free survival (RFS) was unevaluable in both subgroups due to short follow‐up.

Discussion

This study evaluated the Aza+Lisa regimen in AML and MDS‐IB subgroups separately, considering the inherent disease heterogeneity between the two entities: MDS‐IB is characterized by bone marrow dysplasia and impaired hematopoietic reserve, while AML is defined by clonal proliferation of blasts with higher malignant potential. The stratified analysis revealed distinct efficacy and safety profiles between the two subgroups, providing more targeted clinical evidence for the application of Aza+Lisa in myeloid neoplasms.

In terms of early efficacy, the Aza+Lisa regimen demonstrated encouraging response rates. Among nine patients with R/R AML, a cCR rate of 55.6% was observed after one cycle, with 60% responders achieving MRD‐negativity. For the eight patients with MDS‐IB, the initial CR rate was 62.5% (5/8), with an MRD‐negative rate of 40% among responders. Notably, no progression to AML was observed in MDS‐IB patients during treatment, a finding of clinical relevance given the high transformation risk in this group. These early outcomes, though derived from a small sample, indicate that lisaftoclax combined with azacitidine can induce rapid and deep responses, potentially altering early disease trajectory. Zhai et al. [6] previously reported that lisaftoclax in combination with alrizomadlin could overcome venetoclax resistance in AML patients. In our study, we enrolled four patients who had been refractory to venetoclax, and their one‐cycle CR rate was 75.0% (3/4), with MRD‐negative rate of 66.7% (2/3), providing us with a strategy to overcome resistance to venetoclax.

Notably, the 62.5% CR rate of Aza+Lisa in MDS‐IB was significantly higher than the efficacy of Aza+Ven in the VERONA trial (negative results) [7], and the median time to first CR in MDS‐IB was only 1 month, indicating that Aza+Lisa induces rapid and deep responses in MDS‐IB patients. For AML patients, the 55.6% CR rate and 1‐month median time to first CR were comparable to those of Aza+Ven in R/R AML, while the 75% CR rate in venetoclax‐refractory AML highlighted the potential of Aza+Lisa to overcome venetoclax resistance.

The safety profile of Aza+Lisa was manageable, with hematologic toxicities being the main AEs, consistent with intensive therapy for myeloid neoplasms. However, stratified analysis showed a lower incidence of Grade 3–4 febrile neutropenia in AML (22.2%) compared to MDS‐IB (50.0%), which may be attributed to more severe bone marrow microenvironment abnormalities and a less favorable genetic mutation profile for hematopoietic recovery in the MDS‐IB subgroup. The key advantage of lisaftoclax over venetoclax is its weak binding to BCL‐xL/BCL‐w: venetoclax inhibits BCL‐xL to a certain extent, leading to severe thrombocytopenia and prolonged neutropenia in MDS‐IB patients with impaired bone marrow reserve, thus requiring routine dose reductions; in contrast, lisaftoclax avoids off‐target hematologic toxicity, so no dose reduction was needed in MDS‐IB patients in this study, which improved treatment adherence and clinical practicability. In addition, the incidence of Grade 3–4 febrile neutropenia (35.3% overall) in this study was lower than that of Aza+Ven regimens (50%–60%) reported in previous studies, further confirming the better safety profile of lisaftoclax [4, 8].

These early efficacy and safety signals position Aza+Lisa as a promising therapeutic alternative, which may offer a valuable option for patients who are ineligible for failing venetoclax‐based regimens and even serve as a potential first‐line candidate worthy of head‐to‐head evaluation with the Aza+Ven regimen [8]. Notably, the negative results of the VERONA trial have limited the clinical application of Aza+Ven in MDS‐IB, thus leaving an unmet clinical need for effective and well‐tolerated therapeutic regimens in this population. Our study demonstrated that Aza+Lisa yields promising efficacy with manageable safety in MDS‐IB without the need for dose reduction, thereby providing a novel and practical therapeutic option for these patients. For AML patients, Aza+Lisa also acts as an effective alternative for those with venetoclax‐refractory disease, which not only validates its value in overcoming venetoclax resistance but also enriches the therapeutic armamentarium for relapsed/refractory AML. Future research should focus on conducting randomized controlled trials to definitively compare the efficacy and safety of Aza+Lisa with the established Aza+Ven standard. Specific areas of interest include optimizing the dosing and treatment duration of lisaftoclax, evaluating its efficacy in well‐defined molecular subgroups (e.g., IDH1/2‐ or FLT3‐ITD‐mutated AML), and exploring its synergistic effects in combination with other novel agents [9].

Study Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, its retrospective, single‐center design with a small sample size limits its generalizability. Second, the analysis was conducted extremely close to the treatment period (2025), resulting in a very short follow‐up. Consequently, the survival data, particularly the median OS, are preliminary and may change substantially with longer observation, affecting the stability of our conclusions. In addition, although we performed stratified analysis by disease subtype, the small sample size of each subgroup (AML n = 9, MDS‐IB n = 8) may limit the statistical power of the results; future large‐sample prospective studies should enroll more patients in each subgroup to confirm the efficacy and safety of Aza+Lisa. In addition, this study only reported the incidence of common genetic mutations and did not explore the correlation between specific mutation profiles (e.g., TP53, FLT3‐ITD) and treatment response, which warrants further investigation in future studies

Conclusion

In conclusion, this retrospective stratified analysis suggests that the combination of azacitidine and lisaftoclax exhibits promising early antileukemic activity with a manageable safety profile in both R/R AML and MDS‐IB patients. This regimen induces rapid responses (short median time to first CR) in MDS‐IB with no dose reduction required, filling the clinical gap after the negative VERONA trial, and is also effective in venetoclax‐refractory AML.
