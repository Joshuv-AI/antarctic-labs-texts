Letter to the Editor: Surgical Timing and Neurological Outcomes in Traumatic Central Cord Syndrome: A Single-Center Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Zitong Wang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with interest the recent single-center retrospective cohort study evaluating the association between surgical timing and short-term neurological outcomes in traumatic central cord syndrome (CCS). 1 Their finding that surgical timing was not associated with in-hospital mortality, ventilatory requirements, intensive care unit length of stay, ambulatory status, or early motor recovery contributes useful real-world data. However, several considerations warrant caution.

First, the study appears to treat surgical timing primarily as an exposure, while the clinical indication and urgency for surgery may have been major determinants of timing. Patients undergoing ultra-early surgery may have had more severe cord compression, progressive neurological deficits, unstable injuries, epidural hematoma, or concomitant traumatic pathology requiring urgent stabilization. Conversely, patients whose operations were delayed may have been medically optimized, observed for neurological evolution, or selected because they had relatively stable deficits. This is a classic example of confounding by indication. Without adjustment for neurological severity at presentation, degree of canal compromise, cord signal abnormality, fracture-dislocation pattern, or preoperative neurological trajectory, similar postoperative outcomes across timing groups may reflect unequal baseline risk rather than equivalence of treatment timing. Could an apparent absence of benefit from early surgery instead represent early treatment being preferentially applied to patients with a worse initial prognosis?

Second, the choice of short-term motor-score change and discharge ambulatory status as principal neurological outcomes may not fully capture clinically meaningful recovery in CCS. The hallmark disability of CCS is frequently disproportionate upper-extremity and hand dysfunction. A modest improvement in aggregate bilateral upper-extremity motor scores may not translate into restoration of dexterity, grip strength, self-feeding, buttoning, writing, or independence in activities of daily living. Studies of acute spinal cord injury increasingly emphasize that the timing effect may be more evident in upper-limb recovery, particularly among patients with more severe incomplete injuries.2,3 Functional measures such as the Spinal Cord Independence Measure, Functional Independence Measure, detailed hand function assessments, or patient-reported quality-of-life outcomes would have strengthened the clinical interpretation. Is preservation or recovery of a few motor-score points equivalent to meaningful restoration of hand function for a patient with CCS? Probably not.

Third, CCS should not be regarded as a homogeneous syndrome. Its clinical phenotype includes patients with traumatic disc herniation, cervical spondylotic stenosis without fracture, fracture-associated instability, multilevel degenerative compression, and varying degrees of intramedullary injury. These subgroups may differ substantially in the biological rationale for urgent decompression and in their potential responsiveness to surgery. MRI features, such as the length of T2 hyperintensity, hemorrhagic cord injury, residual canal area, and ongoing compression, are established prognostic variables after cervical spinal cord injury. 4 A pooled analysis without stratification by these factors may obscure a timing effect confined to patients with demonstrable persistent compression or severe incomplete neurological injury. Similarly, the possibility that American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale grade C patients derive greater benefit from early surgery than grade D patients deserves explicit exploration, as suggested by prior multicenter data. 2

Finally, the interpretation should remain aligned with the broader evidence base and contemporary guideline recommendations. The 2017 clinical practice guideline suggested that early surgery within 24 hours be considered as an option for adults with traumatic CCS, while acknowledging the low quality of available evidence. 5 More recently, a large propensity-matched cohort study found that early decompression was associated with improved upper-limb motor recovery at one year among patients with CCS, particularly those with AIS grade C injury. 2 Although observational evidence cannot eliminate residual confounding, these findings indicate that a neutral result in a modest single-center cohort should not be interpreted as establishing therapeutic equivalence between early and delayed surgery. Rather, it underscores the need to identify the patients for whom time-sensitive decompression is most likely to matter.

In conclusion, this study usefully highlights the uncertainty surrounding surgical timing in CCS. Nevertheless, confounding by indication, limited functional specificity of neurological outcomes, clinical and radiographic heterogeneity within CCS, and insufficient risk-stratified analyses temper the conclusion that timing does not influence recovery.
