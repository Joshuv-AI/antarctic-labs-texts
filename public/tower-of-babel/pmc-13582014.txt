Volume-inflation registration (INFREG) for morphometric analysis of human focal cortical dysplasia type II
Authors: Yongsheng Zhang, Haojie Han, Fang Chen, Hongen Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeurodevelopmental expansion of human association cortex increases cortical folding complexity, causing registration inaccuracies that confound detection of subtle pathological signatures like focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) in voxel-based morphometry. We introduce INFREG (volume-inflation registration), integrating surface-based deformation with volumetric alignment to minimize sulcal smoothing. Validated on 80 histologically confirmed FCD cases and 85 controls, INFREG outperformed conventional methods (CAT12/Demons/BrainSuite) by (i) superior template to subject alignment (SSIM = 0.95 ± 0.04; GM/WM Dice = 0.93 ± 0.05 vs. 0.70–0.87, all p < 0.001); (ii) sharper templates with 23%–41% lower intensity SD and finer gyral alignment (3.34 ± 0.13 vs. 3.45 ± 0.15 mm, p < 0.01); (iii) reducing false positive FCD Type II clusters by 70%–88% (p < 0.001) in association cortices; and (iv) halving manual verification time (Observer1:101 ± 67 vs.177 ± 114 s; Observer2:75 ± 48 vs.162 ± 102 s; p < 0.001). In 30 typical cases, 90% localized within top 5 clusters, and all 12 VBM-indeterminate cases resolved. INFREG may enhance clinical workflows for FCD type II evaluation.Keywords: brain, registration, inflation, focal cortical dysplasia, voxel-based morphometry

Introduction

Epilepsy, a complex neurological disorder affecting 1% of the global population, carries substantial morbidity, and for those with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), resective surgery can be curative if the epileptogenic lesion is accurately identified.1,2,3 Cortical developmental malformations constitute one of the common etiologies of DRE, accounting for approximately 20% of such cases, with higher rates in pediatric cohorts (40% vs. 12% in adults).4 Imaging findings are inconsistent across FCD subtypes.5 Focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) type II often exhibits a characteristic triad on MRI—gray matter (GM) thickening (60%), white-gray matter boundary (WGB) blurring (74%), and white matter (WM) T2 hyperintensity (63%).6 Conventional visual analysis remains constrained by MRI contrast limitations and inter-reader variability, resulting in undetected cases of up to 80% for type I7 and 21%–33% type II FCD.6

Lesion identification is particularly critical because complete resection of FCD type II is strongly associated with favorable seizure outcomes.7,8 Automated detection algorithms can assist presurgical evaluation by highlighting candidate regions that warrant focused radiological review.9,10,11 Thus, any method that improves lesion detection has the potential to improve surgical candidacy and outcomes.

These limitations of visual diagnosis have driven advancements in computational neuroimaging, particularly voxel-based morphometry (VBM)12 and surface-based morphometry (SBM),13 which enable objective quantification of structural abnormalities using routine clinical MRI protocols. The Morphometric Analysis Program (MAP18),14,15 a widely used VBM pipeline, computes feature maps highlighting GM and WGB irregularities by comparing individual brains to a normative template.16 Despite elevated detection sensitivity, MAP18 suffers from low specificity (6%–10%),9,10 a limitation rooted in its dependence on spatial smoothing to suppress registration-induced false positives—a trade-off that we examine more closely below. When combined with texture analysis, VBM allowed the identification of 9%–15% more epileptogenic foci than visual inspection, although at the cost of reduced interpretability.11 Supervised learning models—including artificial neural network,17,18,19 deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs),20,21 and surface-based classifiers22,23—have recently shown promise for automated FCD detection.24 However, these approaches typically require large, expertly labeled training datasets, can exhibit reduced generalizability, and often function as “black boxes” with limited interpretability for surgical decision making. VBM/SBM retain critical advantages in small-sample reliability and interpretability—essential factors for preoperative planning.25

VBM primarily utilizes registration,26 feature representation, and group-level comparative techniques, whereby iterative coregistration mapping to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI)27 space allows regional outliers in constructed mean templates28 to indicate pathological features. However, the accuracy of this comparison depends critically on the fidelity of the underlying registration. When registration fails—as it often does in anatomically complex regions—the resulting misalignment introduces spurious residuals that mimic true pathology. A fundamental source of false positives in template-based VBM lies in registration errors concentrated in regions of high cortical folding variability. Human association cortices—including lateral temporal, lateral parietal, and dorsomedial prefrontal regions—undergo disproportionate developmental expansion (up to 4-fold),29 developing complex, individually variable folding patterns. This variability in sulcal anatomy causes conventional volumetric registration to systematically fail: deformation fields constrained by continuity requirements cannot independently align opposing sulcal banks, introducing signal residuals at mismatched sulci. Spatial smoothing (typically 6–15 mm FWHM) is widely applied to mitigate these residuals, at the cost of reducing sensitivity to small lesions and blurring fine anatomical detail.30 The cortical folding paradox—the tension between individual gyral fingerprints and population-based registration—remains a fundamental neuroimaging challenge.

In contrast to VBM’s volumetric approach, SBM circumvents the cortical folding paradox by inflating the cortex to align sulcal patterns before performing statistical comparisons, consistently outperforming volumetric methods in spatial normalization accuracy31 and FCD detection.23 However, SBM faces significant barriers to routine clinical adoption: surface reconstruction requires computationally intensive mesh optimization and topological correction; surface-based metrics (e.g., local gyrification index) lack validated diagnostic thresholds; and results must be visualized on inflated surfaces, which is incompatible with the slice-based review paradigm used by radiologists. Consequently, VBM remains dominant in clinical settings, despite its registration limitations.

To overcome this limitation, enhanced registration techniques that preserve sulcal structures are essential, benefiting from group-level feature mean templates that facilitate precise quantification of epileptic malformations. However, despite these advances, no existing suitable method fully bridges the gap between surface-based anatomical precision and voxel-based clinical operability. To address this, we introduce volume inflation registration (INFREG). INFREG extends the cortical surface unfolding principle into the volumetric domain: the cortex is computationally inflated to flatten sulci, registration is performed in this unfolded space where folding variability is eliminated, and the result is deflated back to native voxel space. This enables sulcus discontinuous deformation fields while preserving direct voxel level correspondence with the original 3D MRI—combining SBM’s anatomical fidelity with VBM’s clinical operability. We validate INFREG exclusively on FCD type II, the subtype most commonly associated with MRI visible gray-white junction blurring, and comprehensively benchmark its registration accuracy, template quality, and lesion detection performance against multiple conventional pipelines. In this study, as shown in Figure 1, we (i) detail the INFREG framework, including surface reconstruction, cortical surface registration, volume inflation/deflation, and template-based feature computation; (ii) quantitatively evaluate its registration accuracy against six conventional methods at both voxel and geometric levels; (iii) assess the sharpness and anatomical precision of population mean templates constructed in the inflated domain; and (iv) validate the clinical potential of INFREG-based feature maps for FCD type II detection in 80 patients and 85 controls, including a reading time experiment with two neurologists.

Results

Implementation and characteristics of the volume-inflating method

Cortical surface-based registration improves alignment precision by leveraging a deformable framework for sulcal-gyral matching. We extend this principle through voxel-level expansion of the surface inflation paradigm, enabling direct volumetric registration. We focus more on the WM surface than the GM surface because it is more associated with FCD lesions. Here, we detail implementation strategies, methodological advancements, and computational outcomes.

Cortical surface reconstruction, optimization, and registration

Precise cortical surface reconstruction serves as the foundation for subsequent registration processes. To expedite this computationally intensive step for brain datasets, Vox2Cortex, an open-source deep learning tool, was employed. It achieved reconstruction times of less than 1 min per subject on a single GPU (for each mesh of a hemisphere with 42,016 vertices <8 GB), representing a speedup exceeding 100-fold compared to FreeSurfer and CAT12. However, the initial reconstruction of the WM surface exhibited partial misalignment with the centreline of WGB (the border of WM). As shown in Figure 2, the mean WGB probability of each vertex was adopted to measure the alignment accuracy, and the original extracted surface exhibited an average error of 0.4 (compared to the expected highest probability value of 1). To address this issue, as shown in Figures 2A and S2, we implemented an iterative optimization protocol that appended vertices to the WGB following its gradient, resulting in a reduction of surface fit error by 81.2 ± 2.3% (before vs. after optimization; p < 0.001) across 40 test cases. Similar optimizations have been applied to the GM surface. This strategy, combined with deep-learning-based reconstruction and interpretable correction, improves the efficiency of batch data analysis while preserving anatomical accuracy. The surface registration of directly aligned sulci can be used as the initial boundary of voxel registration. Therefore, we performed surface registration using a surface mean curvature map of the hemispherical GM inner surface extended to a rectangle, similar to the SVreg pipeline of BrainSuite, but with a higher initial alignment and smaller local flattening deformation, as shown in Figures 3 and S2.

Cortical volume-inflation

Then, expand the surface registration to cover the voxels of the entire cortex. Unlike traditional techniques, such as the harmonious expansion algorithm implemented in the BrainSuite, our proposed voxel inflation technique represents a volume deformation-based on cortical expansion and interpolation with intrinsic reversibility. Considering the complex folding pattern of the cortex, it is crucial to maintain local consistency and homotypic integrity throughout the process. As shown in Figure 2, the WM surface expands outward along a gradient of the distance field of WGB while maintaining its local structure and topology. The expanded GM maintained a nearly uniform thickness except near the spinal cord gyrus (thickness ratio over 1.2). Adjacent brain regions retain their inter-regional information (e.g., adjacent regions A, B, and C keep their boundaries intact) while eliminating wrinkles. Swelling of deep WM may cause slight topological distortions (characterized by Jacobi determinants below zero) and thicken WM deep within the clefts and sulci. However, except for the “transmantle sign,” for imaging studies of diseases such as epilepsy or cortical malformations, difficulties are mainly focused on cortical GM proximity, and thus, defects in deep WM conversion have minimal impact on the analysis. Therefore, subcortical structures were not considered in the subsequent processing and analysis in this study.

Computation characteristics in the inflated-volume domain

To elucidate the properties of cortical voxel inflation, Figure 2F demonstrates the application of data smoothing. Volume smoothing reduces mismatches and improves the signal-to-noise ratio of functional signals, such as fMRI imaging signals. However, it risks contaminating folded gyral regions, leading to erroneous activation. Previous studies have shown that sensitivity increases when smoothing occurs in cortical surface domains.32 Trying isotropic 3D Gauss smoothing directly in the voxel expansion space and then reverting to the original fold configuration, we observed preserved sulci morphology next to the intermingled GM with adjacent regions. Switching to folded space, the Gauss kernel morphs into a flattened, anisotropic shape along the gyrus surface, significantly mitigating cross-folding GM crosstalk. After artificial local scribed measurement, and the overall voxel comparison, the gray-white matter contrast loss in the dilated volume domain (normalized gray level difference: 1.4 vs. 0.6 in this case) can be significantly reduced compared to the original voxel domain after filtering with a blur kernel of the same size.

Evaluation of inflate-registration in the voxel domain

Our voxel inflation registration (INFREG) technique demonstrates exceptional accuracy in regular cortical territories and avoids overfitting in aberrant gyral zones, thereby providing an insightful benchmark for scrutinizing anomalous lesions within neuroimaging contexts. Through multifaceted evaluations, juxtaposed with respected volume registration benchmarks including Elastix, Demons, ANTs, BrainSuite, and CAT12, our methodology exhibits superiority across numerous metrics. To avoid losing abnormal features of the patient’s brain, we tested the effect of registering the average template to the patient’s brain and assessed the registration ability of cortical regions outside the lesion zone by image similarity. The evaluation results are shown below by quantitative comparison and intuitive visualization.

Voxel-level evaluation of registration methods

Notably, Elastix’s reliance on spline curve elastic registration and ANTs’ fast SyN configurations for the Slicer platform may lack fine-tuning for peak performance compared with the best performance reported before. BrainSuite’s surface-constrained volume registration encounters pitfalls due to potential inaccuracies or failures in self-reconstructed cortical surfaces, impacting registration quality. Regardless of optimal calibration, traditional volume registration remains inherently constrained by deformation field smoothness (particularly along the sulcal margins), impeding sulco-gyral alignment in various regions, prominently the middle frontal gyrus, portions of the parietal lobe, and medial temporal lobe, as depicted in Figure 5. Figure 4B reveals how our volumetric approach facilitates a discontinuous deformation field across sulci, accommodating intricate gyrification patterns. CAT12’s registration methods constrain the matching capability between different sulci due to deformation constraints imposed across sulcal banks, resulting in smooth deformation. This holistic optimization for global similarity causes overfitting in the extension-type lesion here. Our method enables discrete deformation fields on opposing sulcal banks, permitting freer alignment. Crucially, INFREG maintains deformation continuity and ipsilateral cortical thickness, preventing lesion overfitting.

Comparison of intuitive 3D visualization

2D perspectives often fail to capture the intricacies of folded structures, presenting them as disjointed segments; however, a three-dimensional viewpoint offers superior visibility. Leveraging curve reformatting techniques, we vividly illustrate the sulcal and gyral trajectories by selectively excising varying depths of lateral brain tissue, effectively highlighting the trendlines of these anatomical features. Discrepancies in registration manifest as striking crimson speckles on the three-dimensional rendering of the 1-SSIM composite, pinpointing probable lesion locations.

As shown in Figures 7, S4, and S6, employing curve reformatting visualization, we showcased sulco-gyral registration effectiveness in a representative patient cohort comprising 4 subjects (Subj 3/16/64/10). Here, the 1-SSIM map activation in a normal brain, higher than 0.5 was regarded as an obvious false-positive spot, and the proportion of the volume covering the whole area was calculated as the false-positive volume rate (FPVR). Within the normal hemisphere in 20 test brains, INFREG display notable variances versus Demons and CAT12 in false-positive identifications (FPVR across all cortical regions, INFREG: 3.5 ± 1.3%, Demons: 28.8 ± 7.3%, CAT12: 30.6 ± 6.2%, N = 20), concentrated primarily in the frontal, parietal lobes and medial temporal lobe (FPVR in these regions, INFREG: 6.1 ± 1.8%, Demons: 56.1 ± 15.4%, CAT12: 63.6 ± 14.0%, N = 20). Simultaneously, for Subj16 and Subj10, bearing “extension” subtype pathologies, our approach singularly illuminates genuine positive differential highlights proximal to the lesion site (similarity differences appear only in the red lesion box), underscoring the precision and specificity of our registration technique.

Assessment of mean templates generated by registration

The construction of population-average templates through multi-modal registration of neurotypical brains addresses a fundamental challenge in neuroimaging: disentangling intersubject anatomical variability from methodological artifacts. The clarity of the template sulci can be improved by special fusion strategies,33,34 but it is important as a marker-free assessment of registration ability at the group level. Leveraging OpenNeuro’s curated repository of 80 age-matched T1w MRI scans (18–45 years, balanced sex distribution), we generated intensity-normalized templates encoding four complementary features: T1 normalized-intensity, GM probability, cortical thickness, and WGB probability. As shown in Figure 8, 3 registration frameworks spanning deformation complexity were benchmarked: Elastix (low-DOF), CAT12 (medium-DOF), and our INFREG method (high adaptive deformation)—were comparatively evaluated through both qualitative sulcal alignment assessment and quantitative variance analysis.

Registration performance analysis showed Elastix templates capturing only primary gyral boundaries, while CAT12 and INFREG achieved superior detail preservation of secondary sulci. Quantitative evaluation demonstrated INFREG’s superiority: using normalized GM probability images, it reduced intensity variance by 23%–41% versus CAT12 across neocortical divisions (whole cortex, 0.53 vs. 0.73, p < 0.001; frontal: 0.51 vs. 0.72, p < 0.001; parietal: 0.53 vs. 0.75, p < 0.001; temporal: 0.58 vs. 0.68, p < 0.01; occipital: 0.53 vs. 0.76, p < 0.001; F test, FDR-corrected). While edge contrast in conserved regions (central sulcus: 0.97 ± 0.02 vs. 0.98 ± 0.01) showed method equivalence, INFREG outperformed in high-variability zones (superior frontal: 0.83 vs. 0.71, p = 0.003). Due to well-aligned anatomy and a large amount of incoming or outgoing fibers, the WGB of the central sulcus appears to be wider/brighter, which advantage decreases with the improvement of the registration ability (For example, quantified by mean intensity in central sulcus vs. parietal lobe, elastix: 3.17, CAT12: 1.51, INFREG: 1.23).

Regional normalized S.D. (nS.D.) using medium-DOF registration like CTA12 may indicate specificity of brain regions, revealing a neurobiological gradient: conserved sensorimotor central sulcus (nS.D. = 0.18) versus variable association neocortices (prefrontal lobe: S.D. = 0.74, temporal-parietal association region: nS.D. = 0.68), correlating with Brodmann’s cytoarchitectonic complexity rankings. Thus, a higher nS.D. of GM probability may imply complex cellular architectures to support multimodal information processing. These findings, visually summarized in Figure 5, demonstrate INFREG’s enhanced capacity to resolve both conserved and variable neuroanatomical features through adaptive deformation modeling.

Evaluation of registration in the geometry domain

A pivotal challenge in neuroimaging registration lies in quantifying the spatial discordance of cortical folding patterns across subjects.35 Unlike voxel-based similarity metrics (e.g., normalized mutual information or SSIM), our geometric evaluation framework directly compares anatomical landmarks—gyral/sulcal curves.

We first extracted gyral/sulcal curves via local curvature maxima detection (threshold: κ > 0.35 mm−1). These curves can be evaluated in an unpaired manner without having to distinguish between specific partitions, which may avoid errors in the extraction process. See Figure 9, 6 registration pipelines were benchmarked, including affine registration and classic deformable methods. Quantitatively assessing gyral curve alignment errors of registration across diverse anatomical locations, determining the mean minimum Separation (mmSEP) between any node on the curve and all counterparts, unveils disparities. Crucially, CAT12 and INFREG demonstrated superior capability in superimposing sulcal curves from 80 subjects into coherent curves (mean inter-subject separation<4.0 mm), whereas conventional methods exhibited dispersion artifacts. Detecting FCD lesion by template comparison

The detection of FCD through template comparison necessitates balancing standardization with individual neuroanatomical variability. While generic brain templates provide spatial reference frameworks, our study reveals that conventional subtraction methods (e.g., CAT12 registration and MAP18 protocol) induce voxel-wise false positives due to gyral pattern mismatches, particularly in regions with high sulcal variability. Through comparative analysis of GM and WGB probability residuals using INFREG and MAP18 protocol, we established two distinct feature domains: extension-map capturing GM thickness anomalies and junction-map highlighting WGB distortions.

As demonstrated in Figures 6A–6C, INFREG-generated feature maps exhibit superior noise suppression compared to MAP18 outputs, with residual clusters anatomically constrained to clinically relevant regions. MAP18 can not adopt an appropriate threshold to separate true and false positives under weak lesion characteristics, whereas INFREG can do so more easily, as shown in Figure S4. The volume-rendering shown in Figures 6D and 6E gives a more complete picture of the batch of patients. Here, all the clusters are ordered by volume, denoted by top-k clusters, which could be focused on by the examiner. In these cases, 12 lesions were difficult to localize with MAP18 outputs (could not be easily found within top-10 clusters), while all lesions could be localized by our methods: top-1 cluster (19/30 = 63.3%), top-5 clusters (8/30 = 26.7%), top-10 clusters (3/30 = 10%). Figures S8–S11 show a detailed comparison of each feature map for the 40 cases.

To test the manual verification time of MAP18 and our outputs, two neurologists performed the diagnosis by reading T1/FLAIR scans and feature maps of 30 FCD cases, as introduced in Figure S12. The results show that: without feature maps, 182 ± 97/155 ± 83 s per case; within feature maps of MAP18, 177 ± 114/162 ± 102 s per case; within our feature maps, 101 ± 67/75 ± 48 s per case. This evaluation demonstrated that our methods enabling resource-efficient clinical workflows with (100% –[101 + 75]/[177 + 162] ≈) 50% reduction in manual verification time compared to MAP18 pipelines (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test).

To preliminarily assess whether the improved registration and feature-map specificity observed in the primary Bonn cohort could extend to other clinical settings, we applied both MAP18 and INFREG to an auxiliary set of 36 cases collated from two independent public data resources: the EPISURG dataset (a surgical cohort from the National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery, London, originally curated for post-resection cavity segmentation) and the IEEG Portal (an international intracranial EEG archive with matched structural MRI). Neither resource provides systematic histopathological confirmation of FCD type II, and the available lesion labels in these datasets are derived from neuroradiological reports or surgical records rather than the expert-consensus annotations that characterize ds004199. Consequently, these cases cannot support formal quantitative benchmarking. Nevertheless, they offer a useful qualitative test of algorithmic robustness across different scanners, acquisition protocols, and patient populations. Figures 6F and 6G presents nine representative examples from these supplementary datasets, showing that INFREG-based feature maps consistently highlighted suspicious regions that aligned with the clinically documented lesion locations, whereas MAP18 outputs were more frequently confounded by false-positive residuals in the same areas. Detailed information for all 36 cases, including dataset provenance and imaging parameters, is provided in Supplementary S13-16. While these auxiliary observations do not substitute for a prospective multi-center validation study, they provide encouraging preliminary evidence that the registration and detection advantages of INFREG are not confined to a single institutional cohort.

Further, a detailed quantitative assessment on a larger dataset was conducted. As shown in Figure 5A, voxel-level quantitative evaluation across 80 confirmed FCD cases revealed that INFREG demonstrates superior performance with CAT12 in Dice coefficients (extension-map: 0.078 ± 0.042 vs. 0.065 ± 0.055, p < 0.01; junction-map: 0.121 ± 0.137 vs. 0.042 ± 0.013, p < 0.001) and in the number of false positive clusters (extension-map: 10.5 ± 4.2 vs. 3.1 ± 0.8, 70.5% reduction, p < 0.001; junction-map: 22.4 ± 4.7 vs. 2.7 ± 0.6, 87.9% reduction, p < 0.001). Notably, the smaller overall Dice coefficients are since in most cases (partially visually negative), the lesion labels are delineated much larger than the T1 image anomalies. Furthermore, MAP18’s high sensitivity at the voxel level results from larger cluster coverage (partly due to a larger Gaussian kernel). In 80 cases, only 1 case was missed by INFREG and captured by MAP18. Excluding the top 30 challenging cases (almost negative on visual inspection), the superiority of INFREG becomes more evident in Dice coefficients (extension-map: 0.449 ± 0.176 vs. 0.231 ± 0.079, p < 0.001; junction-map: 0.487 ± 0.140 vs. 0.087 ± 0.023, p < 0.001). In horizontal comparison, the WGB-derived junction-map achieved significantly higher Dice coefficients and specificity than the extension-map, corroborating that FCD pathomorphology predominantly manifests at blurred white-gray junctions rather than through cortical thickening.

Spatio analysis of CAT12 registration errors (Figures 5D–5G) uncovered two high-error clusters: the middle frontal gyrus (MFG) and temporoparietal junction encompassing angular gyrus (AnG) and middle temporal gyrus (MTG). These regions, known for inter-individual sulcal variability, showed higher registration inaccuracies of CAT12 (SSIM <0.5) or low registration confidence as reported.36 Moreover, extension-map artifacts of MAP18 spatially overlapped (Dice = 0.31) with true FCD probability maps, making it difficult to identify true clusters of lesions. Conversely, junction-map artifacts predominantly aggregated at sulcal confluence zones, reflecting the anatomic propensity of FCD-II lesions.

In all, INFREG substantially improved spatial congruence in these challenging domains, decreasing false-positive clusters in morphometric analyses by 70%–88% (extension-map and junction-map). This refinement achieves unprecedented specificity in differentiating true dysplasia from registration artifacts.

Discussion

Registration-based template comparison methodologies, particularly morphometric analysis protocols (MAPs), significantly enhance diagnostic efficiency in neuroimaging for cerebral pathologies while enabling retrospective analysis of institutional imaging databases. Nevertheless, the clinical translation of these methods remains constrained by substantial false-positive outcomes arising from inter-regional neuroanatomical variability and algorithmic fitting limitations, necessitating resource-intensive manual verification by clinicians.

In this study, we therefore addressed this challenge by reducing registration-induced false positives through INFREG, which combines surface-based anatomical precision with volumetric clinical operability. Below, we contextualize our findings within the broader VBM/SBM methodological landscape.

Rethinking the clinical usability of different registration paradigms in cortical diseases

In neuroimaging research, VBM and SBM have long existed as distinct methodologies, often framed as a dichotomy between clinical pragmatism and anatomical fidelity. Comparative analysis of these approaches reveals fundamental methodological disparities with significant implications for both research and clinical translation.

The International League Against Epilepsy guidelines37 continue to prioritize voxel-level features (e.g., blurred white-gray junctions) as diagnostic criteria, reflecting clinical reliance on VBM frameworks. Yet, as our results demonstrate, this reliance comes at a cost: in high-curvature association cortices—where up to 60%–70% of the cortical surface resides—conventional volumetric registration systematically fails to align opposing sulcal banks, and the spatial smoothing applied to suppress the resulting misregistration residuals obscures the very gray-white junction features that define FCD type II. The MAP18 pipeline attempted to address this through dedicated junction and extension maps, but its persistently low specificity (6%–10%) highlights the fundamental nature of the problem: registration-induced false positives cannot be eliminated within a framework that constrains deformation fields to be continuous across sulcal boundaries. Below, we examine this constraint more formally through the lens of the cortical folding paradox and its implications for registration algorithm design.

Conversely, SBM better aligns sulcal patterns through cortical unfolding but faces clinical compatibility challenges:

1.High computational burden: surface reconstruction requires iterative mesh optimization and topological corrections, making it time-prohibitive for clinical workflows and unreliable for cases with post-surgical or traumatic topological disruptions.

High computational burden: surface reconstruction requires iterative mesh optimization and topological corrections, making it time-prohibitive for clinical workflows and unreliable for cases with post-surgical or traumatic topological disruptions.

2.Limited neuroanatomical coverage: surface-based metrics ignore subcortical and deep GM structures while losing fine-grained intensity information, complicating voxel-level correspondence with original DICOM images.

Limited neuroanatomical coverage: surface-based metrics ignore subcortical and deep GM structures while losing fine-grained intensity information, complicating voxel-level correspondence with original DICOM images.

3.Clinical interpretability barriers: surface parameters (e.g., local gyrification index) lack validated diagnostic thresholds and depend on specialized visualization tools incompatible with radiological orthodoxy (axial/coronal/sagittal slice review).

Emerging technologies, including deep learning registration frameworks like VoxelMorph,38 achieve over 0.7 Dice score with regional labels but still exhibit 2%–13% topological violations (negative Jacobian determinants). Similarly, hybrid voxel surface strategies39,40 attempting to map surface registration results to voxel space as initialization and constraint remain inadequate for modeling complex cross sulcal deformation fields.

Our work addresses clinical translation barriers through three key innovations:

1.Accelerated cortical reconstruction: leveraging the Vox2Cortex deep learning framework to achieve sub-minute surface generation while optimizing geometric fidelity to GM boundaries.

2.Deepening gray/white matter coverage: implementing bidirectional surface extension from initial cortical meshes to cover voxels around GM enabling voxel registration with anatomically informed deformation fields without constraint on the smoothness of transcranial sulcus deformation.

3.Radiology-compatible visualization: generating standardized voxel templates with low false-positive residual feature maps that maintain sulcal alignment and permit direct voxel-wise comparison with clinical DICOM series.

Radiology-compatible visualization: generating standardized voxel templates with low false-positive residual feature maps that maintain sulcal alignment and permit direct voxel-wise comparison with clinical DICOM series.

Future efforts should focus on end-to-end workflow integration, whole-brain voxel coverage, and comprehensive geometric feature mapping. The proposed geometric-voxel translation framework establishes a “bilingual translation protocol” between methodological paradigms, preserving SBM’s anatomical precision while inheriting VBM’s clinical operability. This dual-domain synergy opens new avenues for precision neuroimaging in epilepsy and developmental disorders.

Regional distribution of false-positive lesions of FCD

Human neuroanatomy exhibits spatiotemporal heterogeneity across brain regions, influenced by developmental trajectories and region-specific environmental factors. Template comparisons revealed fundamental differences between primary and secondary sulcal architecture. As reported in previous research, primary sulci (>15 mm depth), formed during 6–24 gestational weeks with over 90% inter-individual morphological consistency, exhibit minimal deformation requirements due to their conserved lobar-separating function. Secondary sulci (5–15 mm depth, gyral subdivision role) demonstrate substantial individual variability (∼60% similarity) and complex deformation patterns, correlating with expanded cortical surface area during gyrification. Intercontinental comparisons demonstrate greater temporal and cingulate regions coupled with smaller frontal and parietal lobes in Chinese populations compared to Western counterparts.41 Pathological alterations in diseased brains further compound these variations. Compared with international standardized templates (e.g., MNI), regionally and age-matched average templates constructed from local populations show potential to improve registration accuracy and reduce pseudo-abnormal findings.

The neuroanatomical complexity across brain regions imposes differential requirements for registration degrees of freedom (DOFs). Low-DOF affine registration (6–9 parameters) suffices for structurally stable regions like primary sensorimotor cortices. High-DOF nonlinear registration (>12 parameters, e.g., SyN algorithm) becomes essential for complex gyral regions.42 In our experiments, the results of the non-rigid registration algorithm with different DOF demonstrated hierarchical alignment capabilities: spline-based elastix achieved primary sulcal alignment (Sylvian, central, parieto-occipital, calcarine, cingulate sulci), while DARTEL or Geodesic Shooting-based CAT12 improved secondary sulcal registration, though residual variance persisted in high-curvature frontotemporal subregions.

However, excessive DOF introduces critical challenges: (1) noise overfitting from motion/vascular pulsation artifacts; (2) artificial blurring at gray-white matter borders; and (3) pathological feature erosion. Registration-induced artifacts predominantly manifest at gray-white matter junctions, periventricular zones, complex frontotemporal cortices, and deep nuclei. Subtle pathological signatures risk being obscured through conventional registration workflows. This means that the alignment of highly curled secondary and tertiary sulci could not be solved simply by stacking degrees of freedom.

The intrinsic conflict between registration precision and anatomical fidelity stems from continuity constraints in voxel-wise deformation fields across sulcal boundaries. Addressing this paradox, we propose a novel registration pathway operating on inflated (sulcal-unfolded) voxel domains. This approach achieved 32%–41% variance reduction in high-curvature regions (especially in MFG, AnG, and MTG). To mitigate deformation-induced feature distortion, our pipeline implements pre-registration intensity feature extraction to create a feature mean template and template-to-subject inverse mapping, preserving original contrast characteristics. Our methods decrease false-positive clusters in morphometric analyses by 70%–88% (extension-map and junction-map), particularly beneficial for detecting FCD type II lesions and epileptogenic zones. Such methodological advancements could reduce reliance on secondary clinical verification, potentially accelerating diagnostic workflows for cortical pathologies through more reliable automated analysis.

Conclusions

We have developed INFREG, a volume-inflation registration framework that reduces false-positive VBM findings in FCD type II by leveraging sulcal alignment in an inflated voxel domain. The method achieved superior registration accuracy, produced sharper population templates, and substantially decreased false-positive clusters compared with standard approaches. In a pilot reading experiment, INFREG-based feature maps halved manual verification time and resolved all VBM-indeterminate FCD type II cases. These results indicate that anatomically informed, sulcal-preserving registration can meaningfully improve the specificity of morphometric analysis for FCD type II. Future work should extend validation to FCD types I and III and to multicenter datasets.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be noted. First, our validation is restricted to FCD type II. FCDs are known to originate from somatic (postzygotic) variants43 in genes related to cell growth (e.g., in the mTOR pathway in type II),44 primarily occurring during prenatal brain development. The hallmark imaging feature in type II—blurring of the gray-white border—is directly captured by our junction map. FCD type I, which is commonly MRI negative, and type III, which occurs adjacent to other principal lesions, may not benefit equally from INFREG. Extension to these subtypes and to mild malformations of cortical development will require separate, histopathologically confirmed cohorts. Moreover, we did not perform stratified analysis of IIa and IIb subtypes because publicly available pathological labels were unavailable in the dataset; future studies with histological subtyping may reveal differential performance.

Second, this study used a single publicly available dataset (ds004199). Although it is the largest open FCD Type II cohort with expert manual labels, multi center replication on independent datasets and scanner platforms is necessary to establish generalizability. The present results should be interpreted as a proof of concept evaluation of registration fidelity and feature map false positive reduction. As a preliminary step toward addressing this limitation, we collated an additional 36 test cases from other publicly available epilepsy related imaging datasets (subsets of the EPISURG and IEEG datasets). While these data lack the rigorous histopathological confirmation and uniform imaging quality of ds004199, both MAP18 and INFREG detected characteristic abnormalities in the majority of cases, providing encouraging preliminary evidence of cross center applicability. Formal multi center validation using prospectively collected cohorts, such as the Multi-center Epilepsy Lesion Detection dataset,7 is planned.

Third, while we have demonstrated reduced false positive clusters and halved reading time in a pilot experiment, full clinical integration would require prospective validation, workflow integration with PACS systems, and assessment of impact on surgical decision making. The encouraging resolution of all 12 VBM indeterminate cases suggests potential value for subtle FCD type II, but larger multi reader studies are needed to confirm diagnostic accuracy and reader confidence.

Method details

Datasets and atlas

This study utilized a publicly available dataset (available at https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds004199/) comprising 85 patients with histologically or radiologically confirmed focal FCD II and 85 age-/sex-matched healthy controls, published on the OpenNeuro platform. The cohort included adult epilepsy patients treated at the University Hospital of Bonn (2006–2021), with MRI data anonymized through facial voxel removal and formatted in BIDS-compliant NIFTI files. Imaging protocols included 3D T1-weighted and 3D FLAIR sequences (partial FLAIR available). Two board-certified neuroradiologists performed manual lesion annotation on FLAIR images, ensuring ground truth reliability.45 To supplement this primary cohort with preliminary cross-center validation, we collated an additional 36 test cases from two other publicly available epilepsy-related data resources: (1) the EPISURG dataset (available at https://doi.org/10.5522/04/9996158), a clinical collection of T1-weighted MRI from 430 patients who underwent resective brain surgery for refractory epilepsy at the National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery (London, UK) between 1990 and 2018.46 This dataset was originally curated to develop and benchmark automatic segmentation algorithms for post-surgical resection cavities, and while it contains preoperative MRI with neuroradiologist-verified lesion labels, the accompanying annotations describe the surgically targeted region rather than providing histopathological FCD classification. (2) The IEEG Portal (ieeg.org), an open international archive of intracranial EEG recordings with matched structural MRI from over 1,200 epilepsy patients.47 We selected a subset of cases from this portal for which the provided clinical documentation included imaging-positive FCD-suspected lesions. Neither supplementary source offers the systematic histopathological confirmation of FCD Type II that characterises the ds004199 cohort; however, both contain preoperative MRI from patients who underwent epilepsy surgery, allowing us to compare MAP18 and INFREG feature-map outputs in a cross-center, multi-scanner setting. These cases serve as a preliminary qualitative assessment of algorithmic robustness outside the primary Bonn cohort and do not substitute for formal multi-center clinical validation.

Group Alignment assessment: “Mean closest distance” between corresponding curve points across brains. However, it might underestimate true alignment (overestimates distance) due to imperfect sulcal/gyral correspondence.

Assessment of feature maps for FCD lesions diagnosis and prediction

Two neurologists from Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital and Xuanwu Hospital participated in the manual scanning reading test. Utilizing MRIcroGL software to simultaneously display T1/FLAIR MRI and feature maps. An AutoHotkey script for workflow automation opens two instances of MRIcroGL side by side, presenting the original MRI and the feature maps MRI, respectively, with the “Yoke” option enabled for synchronized viewing. In each case, the timing was stopped from opening the image to determining the correct location of the lesion.

To quantitatively evaluate the effect of single-feature (jMap, eMap of MAP18 or INFREG) in predicting lesion areas, 4 metrics were calculated. For predicted lesion Lpred vs. ground truth LGT, the threshold value of binarization is selected to maximize the Dice score of random 10 subjects:

Assessment metrics include dice, sensitivity, and specificity coefficient at the voxel level, and cluster number (connected domain exceeding threshold strength and minimum volume) at the region level. Exponential weighting is used to increase the variance of sensitivity (α = 0.5) and specificity (α = 0.25) between the data for visual contrast.

For group comparisons, pairwise two-tailed t-tests were used, with FDR correction for multiple comparisons where applicable.
