Transcriptomic landscape reveals segment-specific molecular mechanisms underlying honeycomb-enhanced egg quality in laying hens
Authors: Dengxu Zhu, Shiji Zhu, Yukang Wu, Yuhao Zhang, Huiyu Wang, Yan Jiang, Wenju Liu, Shujuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHoneycomb is rich in bioactive compounds with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties. However, its effects on the poultry oviduct (magnum, isthmus, and uterus) remain poorly understood. This study investigated the effects of dietary honeycomb powder supplementation at 0, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 g/kg on egg quality, oviduct histomorphology, and transcriptomic profiles in 240 laying hens. Among them, 1.0 g/kg honeycomb powder produced the most pronounced improvements in egg weight, eggshell weight, and albumen weight. Histological analyses revealed segment-specific remodeling of the oviduct, including enhanced tubular gland development in the magnum, increased secretory cell abundance in the isthmus, and more developed mucosal folds with higher glandular and secretory cell densities in the uterus. Transcriptomic analysis further indicated that honeycomb supplementation was accompanied by segment-specific functional remodeling of the oviduct, involving mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) and Wingless/Integrated (Wnt) -related signaling pathways associated with epithelial secretion, extracellular matrix organization, and eggshell mineralization. Collectively, dietary honeycomb powder supplementation was associated with improved egg quality and accompanied by region-specific oviduct adaptations, including structural changes and transcriptomic alterations related to albumen secretion in the magnum, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization in the isthmus, and eggshell mineralization in the uterus. These findings provide new insights into the nutritional regulation of oviduct function and support the potential application of honeycomb powder as a functional feed additive in laying hens.Keywords: Egg formation, Egg quality, Oviduct, Honeycomb, Transcriptomic

Introduction

As a high-value nutritional food, the quality of eggs directly impacts human health and the economic viability of the egg-laying poultry industry (Zhou et al., 2023). The poultry oviduct serves as the core reproductive pathway for transforming eggs into commercial products, with its functional status directly determining both laying performance and egg quality. This complex process begins with the infundibulum capturing the ovulated ovum and facilitating fertilization, followed by the magnum synthesizing and secreting the majority of albumen proteins (Fernandez et al., 2003). Subsequently, the isthmus forms the eggshell membranes that shape the egg, while the uterus governs shell calcification and final hardening (Hincke et al., 2012). Consequently, these physiological processes within these specific oviduct segments fundamentally determine egg quality. The oviduct functions not only as a “biological factory” for egg production but also as a key physiological indicator that reflects the integrated effects of genetic, nutritional, environmental, and health status on the comprehensive quality of eggs (Zhang et al., 2025b).

In recent years, research on the poultry oviduct has increasingly shifted from macroscopic morphological observations to the exploration of underlying molecular mechanisms. With the widespread application of high-throughput sequencing, transcriptomic analysis has become a powerful tool to elucidate the complex, segment-specific gene expression networks that govern albumen synthesis and eggshell mineralization (Shimura et al., 2026). Current studies indicate that the oviduct is highly susceptible to oxidative stress, particularly during the late laying period (approximately 40–50 weeks), which can lead to irreversible structural and functional degeneration, thereby reducing egg quality (Liu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2025). Emerging evidence indicates that dietary supplementation with natural bioactive compounds (such as plant polyphenols) can effectively mitigate oviductal decline by modulating key signaling pathways related to epithelial repair (Li et al., 2025), ion transport (Wang et al., 2025a), and immune homeostasis (Wang et al., 2025b).

Honeycomb is the natural structural habitat for bees to raise brood and store their products. It consists of residual components such as beeswax, propolis, and honey, and is rich in bioactive compounds including flavonoids, carbohydrates, amino acids, and organic acids (Giusto et al., 2017). These nutritional and bioactive substances have been reported to confer diverse physiological benefits, and exhibit potent antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties (Tlak Gajger and Vlainić, 2026). Previous studies indicated that dietary supplementation with honeycomb powders addition to feed at 1,200 mg/kg enhances growth performance, slaughter performance, serum antioxidant capacity, and immune function in Cyan-Shank Partridge chickens (Zhang et al., 2025a). Previous research demonstrated reported that dietary supplementation with honeycomb extract improved growth performance, carcass traits, and serum antioxidant capacity in female dwarf yellow broilers, while simultaneously modulating immune function and promoting beneficial gut microbiota (Song et al., 2022). Furthermore, dietary supplementation with 1.5 g/kg honeycomb extract in laying hens improved egg crude protein content, optimizes fatty acid profiles, enhances total and flavor amino acid, and reduced egg cholesterol and trimethylamine contents in eggs. Meanwhile, it also improves serum antioxidant capacity and immune status (Chen et al., 2023). Nevertheless, variations in the biological effects of bee-derived products have been reported. Differences in chemical composition, processing methods, and supplementation strategies among honey, propolis, honeycomb extracts, and honeycomb powder may contribute to the observed differences in their biological activities and physiological effects (Silva et al., 2025). In addition, some studies have reported limited or inconsistent effects of bee product supplementation on growth performance or antioxidant-related parameters, suggesting that the beneficial effects of bee-derived products require further validation under specific experimental conditions (Al-Kahtani et al., 2022; Bölükbaşı Ş et al., 2023). Although previous studies have mainly focused on the systemic antioxidant, immune, and nutritional regulatory effects of bee-derived products, increasing evidence suggests that their bioactive compounds, such as flavonoids and phenolic compounds, exert broader biological functions. These compounds may regulate cellular antioxidant defense, inflammatory responses, epithelial homeostasis, and tissue remodeling processes (Cavallero et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2022). Given that the oviduct is a highly active secretory organ continuously exposed to metabolic stress during egg formation, these biological activities may potentially contribute to maintaining oviduct structural integrity and functional capacity. However, whether dietary honeycomb powder can directly regulate segment-specific responses of the chicken oviduct, including the magnum, isthmus, and uterus, remains unknown.

The transcriptome encompasses the entire array of ribonucleic acid (RNA) transcripts expressed within specific tissues under a defined physiological state. As a core component of functional genomics, transcriptomic analysis is increasingly utilized in poultry reproductive research to unravel complex biological mechanisms (Liu et al., 2025). Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of dietary honeycomb supplementation on egg quality, oviduct histomorphology (magnum, isthmus, and uterus), as well as segment-specific transcriptomic profiles of the oviduct during the late peak laying period. Collectively, these findings will provide molecular insights for the application of honeycomb as a functional feed additive in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The experiment complies with the Regulations on the Management of Experimental Animals and has been approved by the Animal Research Committee of Anhui Science and Technology University (license number 2025-014).

Poultry, feeding, and management

The honeycomb was purchased from Yinsong Beekeeping Farm in Chuzhou, Anhui Province, China. Before processing, visible honey residues and external impurities were manually removed from the honeycomb. The cleaned honeycomb was cut into cubes approximately 2-3 cm in size and dried at 50°C for 48 h, then cooled and frozen at − 80°C for 12 h. The frozen samples were subsequently crushed using a food-grade low-temperature grinder, passed through a 40-mesh sieve to obtain honeycomb powder, which was stored at –20°C. The preparation procedure was modified based on the method described by Lü Yaning (Chen et al., 2023; Lu, 2021). The moisture content, total flavonoids, total polyphenols, and crude polysaccharides were 7.01 %, 6.75 mg/g, 19.3 mg/g, and 4.84 mg/g, respectively, as previously determined (Zhu et al., 2026).

The Dawu Jinfeng laying hens used in this study were sourced from Kangyuan Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd. in Dingyuan County, Chuzhou City (Anhui Province, China). A total of 240 Dawu Jinfeng laying hens at 288 days of age (late peak laying period) with similar weight and laying performance were randomly divided into 4 groups, each with 6 replicates (10 hens per replicate). The experimental groups received basal diets supplemented with 0 g/kg (CON), 0.5 g/kg (H1), 1.0 g/kg (H2), and 2.0 g/kg (H3) of honeycomb powder, respectively. The basal diet was formulated according to the guidelines for laying hens (NY/T33-2004). The composition and nutritional levels of the basal diet are shown in Table 1. The hens were housed in an environmentally controlled facility maintained at 25°C under a 16 h photoperiod. Following a 7-day acclimation period, the feeding trial was conducted over 45 days. Birds were fed three times daily to ensure ad libitum access to feed, and fresh water was freely available throughout the study. Eggs were collected daily at 14:00. All husbandry practices strictly followed standard commercial protocols.

Sample collection

At 45 d of the experiment, five eggs were randomly collected from each replicate cage for egg quality analysis, resulting in 30 eggs per dietary treatment group (six replicates × five eggs). Egg quality parameters were measured individually, and the mean value of the five eggs within each replicate cage was calculated and used for statistical analysis. The 45-day feeding period was selected based on our previous study demonstrating that honeycomb supplementation for this duration effectively improved laying performance and regulated reproductive-related physiological functions in laying hens (Zhu et al., 2026). At the end of the feeding period, hens were euthanized by cervical dislocation at the same time of day to minimize potential variations associated with circadian rhythms and physiological status. Oviduct tissues, including the magnum, isthmus, and uterus, were collected immediately after euthanasia from consistent anatomical locations among individuals. The collected tissues were gently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for morphological analysis. Additional tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at - 80°C for subsequent RNA extraction. All sampling procedures were performed using the same protocol across dietary treatments to minimize variation unrelated to honeycomb supplementation.

Egg quality assessment

Egg quality parameters were systematically evaluated following standard procedural workflows. Eggshell color was determined using a CR-400 chroma meter (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). Egg height and width were recorded with a vernier caliper (14-648-17, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the shape index was calculated as the ratio of height to width. Eggshell strength was subsequently assessed using an RH-DQ200 egg force reader (Runhu Instrument Co., Guangzhou, China). Following the strength test, egg quality metrics including albumen height, Haugh unit, yolk color, and egg weight were quantified using an EMT-7300II multi-functional egg quality tester (Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). Finally, eggshell thickness was determined by averaging measurements taken from three distinct regions of the remaining shell (the blunt end/air cell, the equator, and the sharp end) using the aforementioned vernier caliper.

Oviduct histomorphology analysis

Oviduct tissues (magnum, isthmus and uterus) were collected from the same region cross sections and embedded along the longitudinal axis to ensure consistent orientation and standardized sectioning planes across individuals. The collected oviduct tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, trimmed into small pieces (approximately 2 × 3 × 0.5 mm), and rinsed under running tap water for 24 h before histological processing. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated using serial alcohol concentrations: 75 % overnight, 80 % for 2 h, 90 % for 2 h, 95 % I for 1 h, 95 % II for 1 h, 100 % I for 30 min, and 100 % II for 25 min. The samples were immersed in an alcohol–xylene mixture for 5 min, xylene I for 15 min, and xylene II for 15 min, and then immersed in wax I for 1 h, wax II for 1 h and wax III for 1 h and embedded. 3 sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm and 10 fields assessed per section. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Alcian Blue-Periodic acid–Schiff (AB-PAS) (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), and sealed with neutral resin before observation under a Motic B3 Microscope (Motic, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Total RNA isolation and library preparation

In our previous study, the 1.0 g/kg supplementation level showed the most favorable overall biological response, as indicated by the comprehensive evaluation of laying performance, egg production efficiency, ovarian function, and yolk precursor synthesis (Zhu et al., 2026). Therefore, H2 group (1.0 g/kg honeycomb powder supplementation) was considered the representative effective dose for further investigation of the molecular mechanisms underlying honeycomb-mediated oviduct functional remodeling. Total RNA was extracted from oviduct tissues using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme, R711-01) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7 were subjected to subsequent analysis. For RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), about 1 µg of total RNA was used for sequencing library construction by the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, NR606) and was sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq X Plus platform.

cDNA library construction and RNA-sequencing

The RNA-seq reads were trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.4) 38 and the clean reads were mapped to the reference genome (Gallus gallus, GRCg7b) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) 39. StringTie (v2.2.1) 40 was used to estimate gene expression levels based on fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (FPKM) values. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. An adjusted P-value < 0.05 and an |log₂FoldChange| > 1 was set as the threshold for significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). High-throughput sequencing was carried out by Yuanbao Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China).

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis were conducted by the R package clusterProfiler (v 3.2.0). Enrichment analysis was performed based on the hypergeometric distribution, and pathways with a P-value < 0.05 were considered enriched.

Construction of regulatory network in oviduct

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) information for the identified DEGs was retrieved from the STRING database, applying a stringent confidence threshold of > 0.9. The resulting interactome was imported into Cytoscape (v3.9.1) for visualization, where isolated nodes were excluded from the network. Simultaneously, hub genes were identified using the CytoHubba application by integrating five topological analysis algorithms, including MCC, MNC, Degree, EPC, and EcCentricity (Liu et al., 2018).

RT-qPCR verification

To validate the reliability of the RNA-seq data, 30 representative DEGs identified from the transcriptomic comparison between the H2 and control groups were selected for reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis. RT-qPCR was performed using the same RNA samples from the same biological groups (H2 and control) that were included in the transcriptomic analysis. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized utilizing the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Accurate Biology, Changsha, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The qPCR assays were performed on a Line Gene 9600 Plus Real-Time PCR System (Bioer Technology Line Gene 9600 Plus, Hangzhou, China) employing the SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Accurate Biology, Changsha, China). The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. All samples were analyzed in biological and technical triplicates. The relative mRNA expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous reference gene. Detailed primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The specificity of primer amplification was evaluated by melting curve analysis after qPCR amplification. A single specific melting peak was observed for each primer pair, indicating the absence of non-specific amplification.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For egg quality parameters, five eggs were randomly collected from each replicate cage, and the average value of the five eggs within each replicate was calculated. The replicate cage was considered the experimental unit for statistical analysis (n = 6 per treatment group). Prior to analysis, data normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. The significance of differences among the groups was assessed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's test for multiple comparisons. Data were visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Multiple egg quality indicators were employed to explore the effects of dietary honeycomb supplementation on the eggs of laying hens (Table 2). Dietary supplementation with honeycomb significantly increased egg weight, eggshell weight, and albumen weight compared to the control (P < 0.05). The H1 group exhibited significant increases in eggshell thickness (P < 0.05). However, there were no significant differences (P > 0.05) between the control group and the honeycomb treatment groups in egg shape index, eggshell color, yolk color, eggshell strength, Haugh unit, albumen height, yolk weight, and egg pH.

Effects of honeycomb on oviduct morphology

To investigate the effects of honeycomb supplementation on the histological morphology of the magnum, isthmus, and uterus in laying hens, the histological structures of these oviduct segments were observed and analyzed (Fig. 1). Histological examination revealed that the proliferation of tubular glands in the magnum mucosa was enhanced in all treatment groups compared with the control group. These glands were densely arranged, with markedly expanded and fully acinar lumina. In the uterine segment, mucosal folds in the treatment groups were more developed compared to those in the control. These folds presented higher branching complexity, greater numbers, and longer lengths. Additionally, the number of tubular glands within the uterine mucosa gradually increased in the honeycomb supplemented groups. No substantial morphological differences were observed in the isthmus among the groups. Quantitative analysis of H&E-stained sections further confirmed that dietary honeycomb supplementation induced segment-specific morphological adaptations in the oviduct (Fig. 1A-E). In the magnum, honeycomb supplementation significantly increased the length of primary mucosal folds compared with the control group (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed among the supplemented groups. In addition, the H3 group exhibited the greatest increase in secondary mucosal fold length, indicating enhanced development of the magnum mucosal structure. In the isthmus, honeycomb supplementation markedly increased both primary and secondary mucosal fold lengths. Notably, the H1 group showed the highest values for primary and secondary mucosal fold lengths among all groups (P < 0.05). In the uterus, honeycomb supplementation increased mucosal fold space compared with the control group, with the H2 and H3 groups showing the greatest numerical increases.

Effects of honeycomb on AB–PAS staining patterns in the oviduct

The avian oviductal epithelium consists of ciliated epithelial cells and secretory cells. AB-PAS staining revealed distinct segment-specific patterns of mucin distribution along the oviduct (Fig. 2). The magnum was predominantly positive for Alcian blue, indicating a predominance of acidic mucins or acidic mucopolysaccharides. In contrast, the isthmus mainly exhibited PAS-positive staining, suggesting a predominance of neutral mucins or neutral glycoprotein components. The uterus showed both Alcian blue- and PAS-positive reactions, with blue, magenta, and occasional blue-purple staining, indicating the coexistence of acidic and neutral mucin components. Histological observation showed no apparent difference in AB-PAS-positive secretory cells in the magnum among the experimental groups. In contrast, the isthmus exhibited an apparent increase in PAS-positive secretory cells in the honeycomb-supplemented groups, particularly at higher supplementation levels. In the uterus, stronger AB-PAS-positive staining and a visibly higher abundance of secretory cells were observed in the H2 and H3 groups compared with the control.

RNA sequencing quality assessment and transcriptome alignment

A total of 18 cDNA libraries were constructed for reference‑based RNA-seq, yielding 125.21 Gb of clean data. The effective data size each sample ranged from 6.9 to 7.07 Gb, and 93.31 % to 94.84 % of the bases had Phred quality scores ≥30. The average guanine–cytosine content was 47.49 %. After alignment to the reference genome, the mapping rates of each sample ranged from 91.88 % to 97.33 % (Supplementary table S2). These quality metrics confirm the reliability of the transcriptomic data for subsequent analyses.

Gene expression profiling and differential expression analysis

Gene expression levels were quantified using the FPKM method to normalize for sequencing depth and gene length, with the overall distribution of expression levels presented (Fig. 3a). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate the transcriptomic similarity among the 18 oviduct samples. These samples represented six experimental groups, including the magnum, isthmus, and uterus from H2 supplementation groups and the control groups (Fig. 3b). The PCA plot revealed a clear separation based on oviduct segments, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 54.83 % and 25.88 % of the total variance, respectively. Importantly, biological replicates within each specific segment and treatment group clustered closely together, demonstrating good intra-group consistency and biological reproducibility. This provided a robust foundation for subsequent differential gene expression analysis. Differential expression analysis revealed segment-specific transcriptional responses to honeycomb supplementation. Compared with the corresponding control groups, 365 DEGs were identified in the magnum (OMH2 vs. OMC), including 178 upregulated and 187 downregulated genes. In the isthmus (OIH2 vs. OIC), 269 DEGs were detected, comprising 115 upregulated and 154 downregulated genes. In the uterus (OUH2 vs. OUC), 266 DEGs were identified, including 93 upregulated and 173 downregulated genes. The complete lists of DEGs identified in the three oviduct segments are provided in Supplementary Table S3. The distribution and overlap of DEGs among the three oviduct segments are presented in the Venn diagrams (Fig. 3c), while volcano plots (Fig. 4a–c) and heatmaps (Fig. 4d–f) illustrate the differential expression patterns between H2 supplementation and control groups.

GO and KEGG analysis of DEGs in the magnum

To characterize the functions of the DEGs in the magnum between the OMH2 and OMC groups, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed (Fig. 5a-d). Upregulated GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of biological processes related to sodium ion transport, positive regulation of gene expression, regulation of transcription, ERK1/ERK2 cascade, and cellular response to TGF-β stimulus. The enriched cellular component terms included chromatin, plasma membrane, and SMAD protein complex, while the molecular function terms were mainly associated with transcriptional regulatory activity. In contrast, the downregulated GO were mainly enriched in structural- and stress-related terms. Significant enrichments were observed in connexin complexes, gap junctions, cytoplasmic microtubules, and endosome membranes. Furthermore, molecular functions highlighting thioredoxin-disulfide reductase activity, ATP-dependent protein-folding chaperone activity, and misfolded protein binding were suppressed.

KEGG pathway analysis showed significant enrichment of pathways including TGF-β, ErbB, mTOR, calcium signaling, focal adhesion, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction. Among these pathways, mTOR, TGF-β, ErbB, and calcium signaling pathways showed significant enrichment. These enriched pathways showed overlap with biological processes related to tissue development and cellular regulation. In contrast, the downregulated KEGG pathways in the magnum were primarily enriched in steroid hormone biosynthesis. In addition, gap junction, efferocytosis, FoxO signaling pathway, and pathways related to intercellular communication, stress adaptation, and tissue homeostasis were also significantly enriched.

GO and KEGG analysis of DEGs in the isthmus

The functional enrichment profiles of DEGs in the isthmus (OIH2 vs. OIC) were analyzed using GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (Fig. 6a-d). Among the upregulated GO, enriched biological processes were significantly enriched in immune and inflammatory responses, including Toll-like receptor and NF-κB signaling, as well as apoptosis related processes. The enriched cellular component terms included the ECM, tight junctions, and the MHC class I protein complex, while the molecular function terms were mainly associated with kinase activity, receptor binding, and cytoskeletal dynamics. In contrast, the downregulated GO were mainly enriched in biological processes associated with developmental regulation, cellular plasticity, Ras-related signaling, smooth muscle development, and intercellular communication. In the cellular component category, downregulated GO were mainly enriched in the connexin complex and related intercellular junction structures, while molecular function terms were associated with growth factor activity and ECM interactions.

GO and KEGG analysis of DEGs in the uterus

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses was conducted to clarify the biological functions of the DEGs in the uterus (OUH2 vs. OUC) (Fig. 7a-d). Upregulated GO enrichment analysis identified significant enrichment of biological processes involved in fibroblast proliferation, vascular branching morphogenesis, as well as immune regulation such as T cell differentiation and enhanced IL-1β synthesis. Molecular function terms were primarily associated with transcriptional regulatory activity and sequence-specific DNA binding. These enriched GO terms were associated with cellular growth-related signaling and vascular development-related processes. In terms of cellular component and molecular function categories, downregulated GO were mainly linked to basolateral plasma membrane, lateral plasma membrane, ECM, extracellular vesicle, connexin complex, monocarboxylic acid transmembrane transporter activity, and chemokine activity.

Integrated analysis of GO and KEGG enrichment profiles revealed both common and segment-specific transcriptomic responses among the magnum, isthmus, and uterus (Supplementary Table S4). The isthmus showed enrichment related to cell adhesion and ECM organization, whereas the uterus showed enrichment related to epithelial remodeling, transport, and metabolic regulation. Interaction network construction of DEGs

To identify the hub genes driving the functional and structural remodeling of the oviduct, DEGs across the magnum, isthmus, and uterus were used to construct protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks via Cytoscape. In the magnum, the PPI network comprised 102 nodes and 151 edges. Topological analysis identified MYC, JUN, FGF2, FGF19, SOX9, HSP90AA1, and CXCR4 as hub genes (Fig. 8a). These centrally located genes exhibited extensive connectivity across multiple functional modules, and were primarily involved in growth factor signaling, transcriptional regulation, epithelial differentiation, and cellular stress responses. This observation is consistent with the aforementioned enrichment of the TGF-β, ErbB, and mTOR signaling pathways. For the isthmus, the network contained 60 nodes and 70 edges, with H6PD, EGF, BDNF, GCH1, DHCR24, BCAT1, SPP1, FGFR3, and RHOB emerging as core regulatory hub genes (Fig. 8b). These genes collectively integrate proliferative signaling, metabolic support, redox balance, and tissue remodeling. Finally, the uterine PPI network consisted of 48 nodes and 54 edges, prominently featuring MYC, AICDA, RHOB, NFKBIA, and EGR1 as hub nodes (Fig. 8c). Collectively, the three oviduct segments exhibited distinct PPI network structures and hub gene profiles.

Discussion

The quality of eggs is closely related to the health and function of the oviduct (Nii, 2022; Yin et al., 2020). Therefore, maintaining oviduct health and regulating its function are crucial for the poultry industry. In recent years, bee products have attracted widespread attention in the poultry industry as natural supplements (Abd El-Aziz et al., 2023; He et al., 2021). In the present study, we investigated the influences of dietary honeycomb supplementation on egg quality and oviduct remodeling. Previous studies have reported variable responses to bee-derived products. While many studies have demonstrated improvements in laying performance and egg quality, others observed limited or no significant effects (Konanc and Ozturk, 2025), depending on the type of bee product, supplementation level, and experimental conditions (Pawłowska et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2025). Such inconsistencies may reflect differences in chemical composition, processing methods, and the physiological status of birds. Combined with egg phenotypic detection, histological observation and RNA-sequencing technology, we further systematically analyzed the transcriptomic expression patterns of the magnum, isthmus and uterus in hen oviducts. The results showed that significant improvements in egg quality, including egg weight, shell weight, and albumen weight. Interestingly, different supplementation levels exhibited distinct effects on individual egg quality traits. Although the H1 group showed the greatest increase in eggshell thickness, the H2 group demonstrated a more comprehensive improvement in egg quality parameters, particularly egg weight, albumen weight, and eggshell weight. The enhanced eggshell thickness observed in H1 may reflect a dose-specific response involving improved uterine mineralization efficiency or shell matrix deposition. Histological analysis further demonstrated that honeycomb supplementation induced segment-specific structural adaptations in the oviduct. Quantitative analysis of H&E-stained sections confirmed enhanced mucosal fold development in different oviduct regions, with increased fold length in the magnum and isthmus and expanded mucosal fold space in the uterus. These structural changes may provide greater functional capacity for albumen secretion, shell membrane formation, and eggshell mineralization, respectively. AB-PAS staining revealed segment-specific mucin profiles along the oviduct. These findings indicate that segment-specific functional adaptations of the oviduct. Our previous work identified 1.0 g/kg honeycomb supplementation as the optimal dose for improving laying performance through ovarian regulation and yolk precursor synthesis. Therefore, H2 was selected as a representative dose to further explore the downstream effects of honeycomb supplementation on oviduct function. We further performed transcriptome analysis on each segment of oviduct to clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying these phenotypic changes. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that honeycomb supplementation altered genes expression across different oviduct segments. These DEGs modulate multiple biological processes and signaling pathways, thereby coordinating oviduct morphological improvement and egg quality enhancement.

The magnum is the principal site of albumen synthesis and secretion in laying hens, and its functional activity directly determines albumen yield and internal egg quality (Chang et al., 2024). In the present study, the 1.0 g/kg group showed significantly increased albumen weight and improved glandular development in the magnum. These phenotypic changes suggest a potential enhancement of protein biosynthetic capacity in the magnum. In general, natural bioactive additives, especially those rich in flavonoids and phenolic acids, are well known for improving poultry health and egg production (Huang et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2024). For instance, phenolic compounds have been shown to enhance egg production, feed efficiency, and antioxidant status in laying hens (Xu et al., 2023). It is reported that adding flavonoid quercetin nanoparticles vitamin E, and their combination improve reproductive performance and promote reproductive organ development in age broiler breeder hens (Amevor et al., 2021). This is consistent with our current findings and may be attributable to the bioactive flavonoids and polyphenols present in dietary honeycomb. Transcriptomic analysis revealed segment-specific gene expression alterations in the magnum following honeycomb supplementation. DEGs in the magnum were enriched in pathways related to cellular growth, cytoskeletal organization, and secretory-associated regulation, including mTOR-, Wnt-, FoxO-, and focal adhesion-related pathways. These pathways have been reported to participate in cellular growth, transcriptional regulation, cytoskeletal organization, and secretory-related processes (Laplante and Sabatini, 2012). These DEGs potentially promote epithelial cell proliferation and glandular development in the magnum. They also exhibited extensive interactions across diverse functional modules, and were mainly involved in growth factor signaling, transcriptional regulation, epithelial differentiation, and cellular stress responses.

PPI analysis identified MYC, JUN, FGF2, FGF19, SOX9, HSP90AA1, and CXCR4 as the hub genes in the magnum. JUN acts as a key regulator of cell proliferation and apoptosis, and participates in the modulation of multiple cellular processes such as survival, differentiation, migration, and transformation (Kappelmann et al., 2014; Shaulian and Karin, 2001). It is potentially involved in regulating the proliferation and differentiation of cells within the magnum. Moreover, MYC serves as a well-established global regulator that modulates ribosome biogenesis, translation initiation, and the broader protein synthesis machinery (Jiao et al., 2023). The altered expression of MYC may contribute to the regulation of transcriptional programs related to ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis in the magnum. Fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling mediates cellular proliferation, migration, and differentiation, providing further molecular support for the observed tissue remodeling in the oviduct (Truong et al., 2023). FGF2 has been reported to regulate epithelial cell proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis in reproductive tissues (Filant et al., 2014; Jeong et al., 2017). Therefore, its altered expression may be associated with the structural remodeling of the magnum. BMP- and TGF-β-related signaling pathways showed enrichment among DEGs after honeycomb supplementation, which is consistent with the enhanced epithelial cell proliferation and gland development observed in the histological sections. BMP signaling induces the expression of ID2 and ID3 through the activation of SMAD1/5/8, which are critical for cell proliferation and tissue growth (García et al., 2017; Zinina et al., 2022). Together with the WNT9A driving epithelial development and CXCR4 mediating tissue remodeling, these findings suggest that multiple signaling networks may contribute to the transcriptional regulation of magnum remodeling following honeycomb supplementation. Overall, these results demonstrate that dietary supplementation with 1 g/kg honeycomb was associated with transcriptional alterations in mTOR-, Wnt-, and TGF-β/BMP-related signaling networks, accompanied by changes in hub genes including MYC, JUN, FGF2, and ID2. These transcriptional changes may contribute to the regulation of epithelial organization, glandular characteristics, and secretory-related functions in the magnum, ultimately enhancing albumen deposition and improving egg quality. These findings provide direct molecular evidence supporting the role of honeycomb in improving albumen quality in hens during the late peak laying period.

The isthmus plays a critical role in eggshell membrane formation, which mainly involves ECM protein secretion and structural protein deposition, together with epithelial cell differentiation. Transcriptome analysis revealed significant changes in genes expression and signaling pathways in the isthmus, suggesting that honeycomb supplementation may regulate oviduct isthmus function at the molecular level. KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the DEGs were enriched in signaling pathways related to epithelial development and cell structure regulation, including the Wnt signaling pathway, cell adhesion molecules, tight junction, focal adhesion, and regulation of actin cytoskeleton. Wnt signaling is essential for epithelial proliferation and stem cell maintenance (Clevers et al., 2014), whereas the focal adhesion and actin cytoskeletal pathways drive cell adhesion, cytoskeletal remodeling, and ECM deposition (Chai et al., 2024; Frydrych and Hrabia, 2025). The enrichment of these pathways suggests their potential involvement in epithelial organization and ECM-related processes in the isthmus. Several DEGs associated with epithelial development and ECM-related functions were identified, including EGF, WNT7A, FZD4, FZD5, LGR5, CLDN10, and SPP1, WNT7A, FZD4, FZD5, and LGR5 are key components of the Wnt signaling pathway and modulate epithelial cell proliferation, stem cell activation, and glandular development. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) governs cellular development, differentiation, and migration (Alan et al., 2023). It may effectively promote epithelial proliferation, thereby augmenting the secretory capacity of the oviduct. CLDN10 encodes claudin-10, a tight junction protein that contributes to epithelial barrier properties and paracellular ion transport (Günzel et al., 2009), thereby helping maintain epithelial homeostasis required for glandular secretion (He et al., 2024). Secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1), an acidic, glycosylated, and highly phosphorylated protein, is associated with calcium metabolism in birds and mammals. It also participates in matrix deposition and structural formation (Brionne et al., 2014) and may thereby contribute to eggshell membrane structure development. In addition, multiple genes involved in cytoskeletal organization and metabolic regulation were screened, including FGFR3, RHOB, TUBB2A, TUBB2B, DDIT4 and DHCR24. Specifically, Ras’s homolog family member B (RHOB) and tubulin genes (TUBB2A and TUBB2B) modulate cytoskeletal arrangement and cell morphology. Which may underlie cytoskeletal remodeling and epithelial structural reorganization during oviduct development. DDIT4 is a stress-responsive regulator that has been reported to suppress mTORC1 signaling under cellular stress conditions (Jiao and Xiang, 2025). DHCR24 is involved in cholesterol metabolism and cellular metabolism (Fu and Wang, 2024; Yuan et al., 2021). This may suggest metabolic reprogramming to meet the energy and biosynthetic demands requirements for tissue development (Cheng et al., 2025).

PPI network analysis identified EGF, SPP1, BDNF, FGFR3, RHOB, DHCR24 as hub genes in the isthmus. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) may promote epithelial cell activity by enhancing proliferation and migration (Zhang et al., 2020). Fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) is an important member of the FGFR family and plays pivotal roles in a wide range of biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, tissue repair, and metabolic homeostasis (Edirisinghe et al., 2024). RHOB belongs to the small GTPase family, and its core biological function is to regulate actin cytoskeleton dynamic changes, as well as cell migration and apoptosis (Graham et al., 2007; Sundgren et al., 2025). These findings indicate that dietary supplementation with honeycomb is associated with transcriptional alterations in signaling networks related to isthmus function, suggesting their potential involvement in epithelial organization and ECM-related remodeling. Although no significant improvement in eggshell strength was observed in the present study, eggshell membrane formation represents an important early step in eggshell development by providing the structural scaffold for subsequent calcification (Arias et al., 2020; Buss et al., 2023). Therefore, transcriptomic alterations associated with isthmus function may contribute to eggshell membrane-related processes.

The avian eggshell acts as an essential protective barrier for internal egg contents. Its mechanical strength and thickness are crucial to preventing microbial invasion and physical damage during commercial production and transportation (Fathi et al., 2019). Serving as the main region of eggshell mineralization, the uterus finely regulates calcium deposition, ion transport, and epithelial remodeling. These complex biological processes jointly govern eggshell quality (Sun et al., 2023). In the present study, the addition of 1.0 g/kg of honeycomb to the diet significantly enhanced eggshell weight and improved the development of uterine folds. Although eggshell weight increased significantly, eggshell strength did not differ among groups. This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that eggshell mechanical properties depend not only on shell thickness and mass but also on shell ultrastructure, crystal organization, mammillary layer characteristics, and matrix composition (Benavides-Reyes et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2025; Gautron et al., 2021). These phenotypic improvements indicate an enhanced mineralization capacity and structural maturation of the uterus. RNA-seq analysis identified 93 significantly upregulated and 173 downregulated DEGs in the hen uterus following honeycomb supplementation. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were mainly associated with epithelial organization, cellular signaling, extracellular regulation, and metabolism-related processes. Among these pathways, the Wnt signaling pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, tight junction formation, glycerolipid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction was enriched. WNT family member 4 (WNT4) belongs to the conserved WNT protein family and plays important roles in development and cell differentiation (Zhang et al., 2021). Whereas ACVR2B acts as a type II receptor in the TGF-β signaling pathway and exerts vital functions in regulating tissue growth and cellular differentiation (Olsen et al., 2015). Claudin proteins such as CLDN2 and CLDN8 are important components of tight junctions that regulate epithelial barrier integrity and paracellular ion transport (Ariyadi et al., 2013; Muto et al., 2010). Therefore, these genes may contribute to eggshell formation by regulating epithelial barrier function and ion transport. Vascular endothelial growth factor-D (VEGF-D) is a secreted protein that can promote the remodeling of blood vessels during development (Bokhari and Hamar, 2023). VEGFD may contribute to eggshell formation by improving blood supply and nutrient transport in the uterus to meet the substantial transport of calcium and other ions (Hunsaker and Sturkie, 1961; Sah and Mishra, 2018). Meanwhile, several genes including CCL20, IL6R, NGF, FN1, NFKBIA and ATP6V0A4, are related to immune response, metabolism, and cellular stress, which may favor mineral deposition and eggshell formation (Feng et al., 2023, 2020).

To further clarify the key regulatory networks underlying uterine function, a PPI network of the DEGs was constructed. MYC, EGR1, CNTF, RHOB, AICDA, and NFKBIA were identified as hub genes. MYC and EGR1 are important regulators of cell proliferation, differentiation, and tissue morphogenesis. The increased expression of these transcription factors may be associated with enhanced epithelial growth and uterine fold development. Ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) plays an important role in cell survival and tissue remodeling (Guo et al., 2022). In addition, NFKBIA is an important regulator of inflammatory signaling, and may indicate suppression of inflammatory pathways, creating a favorable environment for eggshell formation (Downton et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2017). RHOB is involved in cytoskeleton organization, cell adhesion, and epithelial structure maintenance (Gutierrez et al., 2019), indicating a possible role in regulating cellular architecture during uterine adaptation. Collectively, dietary honeycomb supplementation was associated with increased eggshell weight and segment-specific histological and transcriptional responses in the uterus. Altered expression of genes related to developmental signaling, epithelial regulation, cytoskeletal organization, and metabolism provides a potential molecular context for the observed differences in uterine histomorphology and eggshell-related traits. However, the functional relevance of these transcriptional changes to uterine function and eggshell formation requires further validation.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Although the integration of phenotypic, histological, and transcriptomic analyses provided insights into the potential mechanisms underlying honeycomb-mediated oviduct remodeling, the RNA-seq results represent transcriptional associations rather than direct evidence of pathway activation or causal regulation. Further studies involving protein-level validation, functional assays, and integrated analyses linking candidate gene expression with oviduct characteristics and egg quality traits are required to confirm the molecular mechanisms involved. In addition, the chemical characterization of honeycomb powder was limited to several major components, and future studies using comprehensive profiling approaches, such as liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry-based chemical profiling, are needed to identify the specific bioactive constituents responsible for its effects. Moreover, whether honeycomb supplementation can alleviate age-related deterioration of oviduct function and egg quality remains to be determined in aged laying hens. Future studies using aged laying hens are warranted to determine its potential role in delaying reproductive aging and maintaining egg quality during the late laying period.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary honeycomb supplementation effectively improves egg quality by promoting albumen deposition and eggshell formation in laying hens during the late peak laying period. Such beneficial improvements are attributed to coordinated and segment-specific regulation of oviduct physiological functions. Transcriptomic analysis identified alterations in signaling pathways related to Wnt, TGF-β, and cell adhesion, together with changes in candidate hub genes identified from the PPI networks. Collectively, this study reveals the coordinated molecular mechanism underlying oviduct functional specialization and provides novel insights into nutritional regulation strategies to enhance egg quality in laying hens.
