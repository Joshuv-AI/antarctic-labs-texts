Simultaneous estimation of connectivity and dimensionality in samples of networks
Wenlong Jiang, Chris McKennan, Jesús Arroyo, Joshua Cape

Abstract
An overarching objective in contemporary statistical network analysis is extracting salient information from datasets consisting of multiple networks. To date, considerable attention has been devoted to node and network clustering, while comparatively less attention has been devoted to downstream connectivity estimation and parsimonious embedding dimension selection. Given a sample of potentially heterogeneous networks, this paper proposes a method to simultaneously estimate a latent matrix of connectivity probabilities and its embedding dimensionality or rank after first pre-estimating the number of communities and the node community memberships. The method is formulated as a convex optimization problem and solved using an alternating direction method of multipliers algorithm. We establish estimation error bounds under the Frobenius norm and nuclear norm for settings in which observable networks have blockmodel structure, even when node memberships are imperfectly recovered. When perfect membership recovery is possible and dimensionality is much smaller than the number of communities, the proposed method outperforms conventional averaging-based methods for estimating connectivity and dimensionality. Numerical studies empirically demonstrate the accuracy of our method across various scenarios. Additionally, analysis of a primate brain dataset demonstrates that posited connectivity is not necessarily full rank in practice, illustrating the need for flexible methodology.



Keywords:
Dimension reduction; Low-rank matrix estimation; Multilayer network; Optimization; Stochastic blockmodel random graph.

1
Introduction

Networks describe interactions among entities, such as friendships among people in social networks
(
Yang et al. 2011
;
Leung
et al. 2014
;
Ameri
et al. 2023
)
, gene-gene associations in biological networks
(
Tong
et al. 2004
;
Xia
et al. 2015
;
Al-Aamri et al. 2019
)
, and region-region connectivity in brain networks
(
Sporns 2012
;
Zuo et al. 2012
;
Vecchio
et al. 2017
)
.
Network analysis has emerged as an active research area in the past several decades due in part to the abundance of high quality graph-structured and network-valued data.
Often, individual networks are viewed as instances of random graphs, where edges (interactions) reflect observable or inferred connectivity patterns among nodes (entities).
In practice, networks are structured, say, with nodes of a similar type sharing similar traits and connectivity patterns, in contrast to nodes of different types or with different traits.
These concepts can be codified using the formalism of latent node communities or blocks.

This paper focuses on the stochastic blockmodel (SBM)
(
Holland
et al. 1983
)
as the exemplar random graph model with community structure.
Stochastic blockmodel random graphs partition
n
nodes into
K
communities, typically with
K\ll n
, on the basis of latent block (community) memberships.
Conditional on the node memberships, edges between nodes are independently drawn from different Bernoulli distributions, where the Bernoulli edge connectivity probabilities are specified in a symmetric
K\times K
matrix.
As such, three key estimation tasks emerge from the study of SBMs as generative models for network data: to estimate the community memberships of nodes, to estimate the number of communities, and to estimate the matrix of connectivity probabilities.

The problem of estimating node memberships, also called
community detection
, has been well-studied in SBMs and is surveyed in
Abbe 2018
.
Popular techniques include modularity maximization
(
Newman and
Girvan 2004
;
Bickel and
Chen 2009
)
, likelihood-based approaches
(
Celisse
et al. 2012
;
Choi
et al. 2012
;
Zhao
et al. 2012
;
Amini
et al. 2013
)
, and spectral clustering
(
von
Luxburg 2007
;
Rohe
et al. 2011
;
Lei and
Rinaldo 2015
;
Paul and
Chen 2020
;
Lei and Lin 2023
)
.
Traditionally, community detection requires a pre-estimated or pre-specified (known) number of communities.
The problem of estimating the number of communities
K
has itself received significant attention in recent years, via the use of spectral methods
(
Le and
Levina 2022
)
, network cross-validation methods
(
Chen and
Lei 2018
;
Li
et al. 2020
)
, likelihood-based methods
(
Wang and
Bickel 2017
)
, model selection methods
(
Yang
et al. 2021
)
, and stepwise testing methods
(
Jin
et al. 2023
)
.

Comparatively fewer works focus on estimating the connectivity probability matrix and its embedding dimensionality or rank in SBMs.
This is due in part to the fact that connectivity probabilities are viewed as nuisance parameters when exclusively seeking to cluster nodes.
Further, stylized versions of SBMs, such as the homogeneous planted partition model with connectivity probability matrix
\mathbf{B}
satisfying
B_{kk}=p
and
B_{kl}=q
for
k\neq l
, are full rank and have historically received outsized attention in the literature.
In such settings, the most commonly used edge probability estimator, namely averaging over edges within or between communities
(
Choi
et al. 2012
;
Li
et al. 2020
)
, has competitive mean squared error
(
Tang
et al. 2022
)
and is almost always full rank itself.

In practice, though, the underlying or inferred probability matrix need not be full rank, particularly when
K
is large
(
Cai
et al. 2022
)
.
Namely, structural dependencies can exist between communities which can give rise to systematic relationships among connectivity probabilities, in which case the matrix rank (or an approximation thereof) may intrinsically determine connectivity estimation accuracy better than the number of communities.
Consequently, methods assuming the full-rankness of
\mathbf{B}
may not yield parsimonious dimensionality reduction for network embeddings, accurate estimates of connectivity probabilities, or appropriate conclusions for real data.
These observations suggest the potential usefulness of considering alternative strategies for estimating general
\mathbf{B}
matrices as investigated here.

For single network analysis, various network estimation methods have been proposed based on thresholding, smoothing, and mixing techniques
(
Airoldi
et al. 2013
;
Chatterjee 2015
;
Zhang
et al. 2017
;
Li and Le 2023
)
.
For SBMs specifically,
Tang
et al. 2022
studies competing methods for estimating the matrix
\mathbf{B}
and investigates their asymptotic efficiency properties.
The results therein require that the rank of
\mathbf{B}
, interpretable as its embedding dimensionality, be given or at least consistently estimable, and the network itself must be not too sparse.
For multiple, potentially quite sparse networks with shared latent structure, the challenge of estimating embedding dimensionality or rank is even more pronounced while remaining equally important for downstream tasks such as hypothesis testing and link prediction.
These observations motivate the present paper and its focus on datasets comprising multiple, potentially quite sparse networks.

1.1
Contributions

This paper proposes a method to simultaneously estimate the connectivity probability matrix and its dimensionality in samples of stochastic blockmodel random graphs (SBMs).
The method is formulated as a convex optimization problem and solved using an alternating direction method of multiplier (ADMM) algorithm
(
Eckstein and
Bertsekas 1992
;
Boyd
et al. 2011
)
.
To the best of our knowledge, this approach is new in the setting of statistical network analysis.
We provide theoretical support for the proposed method by establishing error bounds under the Frobenius norm and the nuclear norm for estimating the matrix of connectivity probabilities.
Our bounds quantify the influence of network sparsity and node misclustering rate on estimation accuracy without imposing stringent assumptions on either.
Numerical studies empirically demonstrate the accuracy of our method in various settings, even when individual graphs in the sample are themselves very sparse and hence contribute only limited usable information.
Further, when perfect membership recovery is possible and dimensionality is much smaller than the number of communities, the proposed method outperforms conventional estimation based on entrywise averaging.
Finally, a real data example shows that the proposed method is useful in practice when the posited connectivity probability matrix is plausibly rank degenerate.
Our contributions can be integrated with existing approaches for community detection
(
Paul and
Chen 2020
;
Jing
et al. 2021
;
Lei and Lin 2023
;
Xu
et al. 2023
)
and estimating
K

(
Lei 2016
;
Wang and
Bickel 2017
;
Chen and
Lei 2018
;
Yang
et al. 2021
;
Jin
et al. 2023
)
that do not require matrices of connectivity probabilities to be full rank.

2
Estimation using samples of networks

In this paper, we consider samples or collections of node-aligned networks,
\{\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{A}^{(L)}\}
, where
\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}
denotes the adjacency matrix for the
\ell
-th layer (network) in the collection.
In settings such as neuroimaging, samples of networks (e.g., derived from brain scans) are often collected on a fixed, shared set of nodes (e.g., brain regions) and are anticipated to exhibit similar connectivity patterns, for example, based on subjects’ characteristics such as disease status.
With this in mind, we specify the setting of multiple independent and identically distributed stochastic blockmodel graphs
(
Holland
et al. 1983
)
as follows, writing ‘mono’ to emphasize that all adjacency matrices (layers) are generated via a single, common matrix of connectivity probabilities.

Definition 1

(Monolayer Stochastic Blockmodel (MonoSBM))
.

Let
K
be the number of communities,
n
be the number of nodes, and
L
be the number of layers.
Across all layers, let
\mathbf{Z}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times K}
be the fixed membership matrix with
Z_{ik}=1
if and only if node
i
belongs to community
k
.
The matrix of connectivity probabilities is denoted by
\mathbf{B}^{*}\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
with
\operatorname{rk}(\mathbf{B}^{*})=d\leq K
.
For all
i\leq j
,
A_{ij}
is independently drawn from
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho B^{*}_{g_{i}g_{j}})
, yielding the symmetric adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
.
We call the collection
\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\operatorname{SBM}(\mathbf{B}^{*},\mathbf{Z})
for
\ell\in\llbracket L\rrbracket
a
monolayer stochastic blockmodel
.
Here,
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)})=\rho\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{B}^{*}\mathbf{Z}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
, where the sparsity term
\rho\equiv\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
is known.

Definition

1
specifies four population-level quantities of interest:
K
,
\mathbf{Z}
,
d
, and
\mathbf{B}^{*}
.
As mentioned in
Section

1
, numerous methods exist for estimating
K
and
\mathbf{Z}
, whereas this paper seeks to simultaneously estimate
d
and
\mathbf{B}^{*}
.
Here,
n
and
L
are conventionally given from context, while assuming that
\rho
is given avoids the ambiguity of individual finite-
n
graphs belonging to multiple asymptotic sparsity regimes wherein
n\rho_{n}\rightarrow\infty
.

Community memberships or block labels are identifiable only up to global relabelling, hence it is necessary to make estimation statements about
\mathbf{Z}
modulo orthogonal transformations, specifically column permutations.
This leads us to write

\overline{\mathbf{A}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A})+\mathbf{\Delta}_{1},\quad\rho\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{B}^{*}\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A})+\mathbf{\Delta}_{2},

(1)

where the permutation matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
satisfies
\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}=\underset{\bm{\Pi}}{\arg\min}\|\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\bm{\Pi}-\mathbf{Z}\|_{0}
given an estimated membership matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}
.
Here, each element of the former error matrix,
\mathbf{\Delta}_{1}
, converges to zero in probability as
L\rightarrow\infty
by the law of large numbers.

Equation

1
suggests
\overline{\mathbf{A}}
as a natural, interpretable estimator of
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A})
.
Depending on the choice of clustering method used (e.g., see
Lei and
Rinaldo 2015
;
Lei and Lin 2023
), each element in the latter error matrix,
\mathbf{\Delta}_{2}
, may concentrate near zero with high probability even for very sparse individual graphs.
If
\mathbf{Z}
can be perfectly or exactly recovered with high probability, then
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}=\mathbf{Z}
, hence
\mathbf{\Delta}_{2}=\mathbf{0}
holds with high probability.

Given
K
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}
, we wish to estimate the dimensionality and the connectivity per
\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{B}^{*}\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
, denoted simply by
\mathbf{B}^{*}
.
One approach for estimating
\mathbf{B}^{*}_{\rho}\equiv\rho\mathbf{B}^{*}
is to solve

\underset{\mathbf{B}_{\rho}:~\mathbf{B}_{\rho}=\mathbf{B}_{\rho}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}}{\mathrm{minimize}}~\|\overline{\mathbf{A}}-\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{B}_{\rho}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}

(2)

and then to set
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{\rho}/\rho
.
The solution to
Equation

2
is equivalent to blockwise averaging which yields full rank estimates in general.
To simultaneously estimate the rank, we shall introduce a penalty term into
Equation

2
to obtain the modified problem

\underset{\mathbf{B}_{\rho}:~\mathbf{B}_{\rho}=\mathbf{B}_{\rho}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}}{\mathrm{minimize}}~\|\overline{\mathbf{A}}-\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{B}_{\rho}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{B}_{\rho}\|_{*},

(3)

where
\lambda>0
is a tuning parameter and the nuclear norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
encourages low-rank solutions.

Equation

3
and the scaled, rewritten version in
Equation

6
are the focus of this paper.
To the best of our knowledge, this formulation together with
Equation

2
is novel for analyzing structured networks in the context of statistical network analysis.

Under MonoSBM, each layer shares the same probability matrix
\mathbf{B}^{*}
.
This is not always realistic in practice, though, such as for the primate brain data in
section

6
, originally collected in
Bakken et al. 2016
and investigated in
Lei and Lin 2023
, where layers correspond to time points.

Lei and Lin 2023
showed that adjacency matrices across layers have different connectivity patterns, suggesting that the underlying probability matrices differ.
This gives rise to the concept of the multilayer stochastic blockmodel
(
Han
et al. 2015
;
Paul and
Chen 2016
;
Lei and Lin 2023
)
which permits heterogeneous connectivity probability matrices in each layer but with the same node memberships across layers.

Definition 2

(Multilayer Stochastic Blockmodel (MultiSBM))
.

Let
K
be the number of communities,
n
be the number of nodes, and
L
be the number of layers.
The membership matrix
\mathbf{Z}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times K}
is fixed across all layers with
Z_{ik}=1
if and only if node
i
belongs to community
k
.
Let
\rho\equiv\rho_{n}\in(0,1]
denote the known sparsity term.
For
1\leq\ell\leq L
, suppose
\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}
are independent adjacency matrices from among
\widetilde{L}\leq L
different SBMs with connectivity probability matrices
\mathbf{B}^{*(\widetilde{\ell})}
and
\operatorname{rk}(\mathbf{B}^{*(\widetilde{\ell})})=d_{\widetilde{\ell}}
, for
\widetilde{\ell}\in\llbracket\widetilde{L}\rrbracket
, which we call a
multilayer stochastic blockmodel
.
Here,
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)})=\rho\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{B}^{*(\widetilde{\ell})}\mathbf{Z}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
if
\ell\in\mathbb{G}_{\widetilde{\ell}}
, where
\mathbb{G}_{\widetilde{\ell}}
is the index set of
\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}
associated to
\operatorname{SBM}(\mathbf{B}^{*(\widetilde{\ell})},\mathbf{Z})
.

Unlike MonoSBM, MultiSBM necessitates estimating the different matrices
\mathbf{B}^{*(\widetilde{\ell})}
.
Beforehand, to first estimate
\mathbf{Z}
in MultiSBM, we employ the bias-adjusted spectral clustering method proposed by
Lei and Lin 2023
, i.e., we cluster the rows of the leading eigenvectors of the matrix
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}[(\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)})^{2}-\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{1})]
.
We then adopt a two-stage method to estimate
\mathbf{B}^{*(\widetilde{\ell})}
in MultiSBM when
\widetilde{L}
is given.
Namely, we first estimate the probability matrix for each layer and cluster these matrices into
\widetilde{L}
groups based on their vectorized upper triangular elements, yielding
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_{\widetilde{\ell}}
.
The layers in each
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_{\widetilde{\ell}}
belong to MonoSBM with
\mathbf{B}^{*}=\mathbf{B}^{*(\widetilde{\ell})}
, so finally we solve
Equation

3
to estimate each
\mathbf{B}^{*(\widetilde{\ell})}
.

3
ADMM algorithm

Here, we derive a method to solve the optimization problem in
Equation

3
.
The problem is convex, so we employ the alternating direction method of multiplier (ADMM) algorithm
(
Eckstein and
Bertsekas 1992
;
Boyd
et al. 2011
)
.
In fact, we present an ADMM algorithm to solve a more general problem.
Given a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K}
, we seek a low-rank matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
so that
\mathbf{Y}\approx\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
, namely

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{W}}{\mathrm{minimize}}~\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{W}\|_{*},

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\ to}\ \mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}.

(4)

Equation

4
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{W,V}}{\mathrm{minimize}}~\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{V}\|_{*},

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\ to}\ \mathbf{V}=\mathbf{W}.

(5)

Consequently, the scaled augmented Lagrangian for
Equation

5
is given by

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{\Theta})=\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{V}\|_{*}+\frac{\rho_{1}}{2}\|\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Theta}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2},

where
\mathbf{\Theta}
is the dual variable and
\mathbf{W}
,
\mathbf{V}
are the primal variables.
Here,
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{V}
can be iteratively updated.
Solving the general problem in
Equation

5
is summarized in
Algorithm

1
, and the corresponding derivation is provided in the supplement.

Algorithm 1

ADMM algorithm for solving
Equation

5

1.

Initialize the parameters:

(a)

Primal variables,
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{0}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{0}
, each as the zero matrix.

(b)

Dual variable,
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{0}
, as the zero matrix.

(c)

Tuning parameter
\lambda>0
, Lagrangian parameter
\rho_{1}>0
, and tolerance
\epsilon>0
.

2.

Iterate until the stopping criterion
\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{t}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{t-1}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}/K^{2}\leq\epsilon
is met, where
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{t}
is the value of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
obtained at the
t
-th iteration:

(a)

Update
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{t}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{t}
:

(i)

Here,
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{t}
is the matrix form of
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{t}
, namely
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{t}=\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{t})
, where
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{t}\leftarrow(2\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\mathbf{C}+\rho_{1}\mathbf{I})^{-1}[2\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\mathbf{y}+\rho_{1}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{t-1}+\widehat{\bm{v}}^{t-1})]

,

,

, and

Assign

, where

is the singular value decomposition of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{t}-\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{t-1}
.

(b)

Update
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{t}
:

(i)

Assign
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{t}\leftarrow\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{t-1}+\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{t}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{t}
.

Remark 1

(Estimating matrix entries)
.

Using

1
to estimate

does not guarantee that

3.1
Tuning parameter selection

The convergence of
Algorithm

1
can be sped up with the help of a warm start.
To do so, begin with the smallest
\lambda
value among candidate initial values, and then use the output from the current trial as the initial value for the next choice of
\lambda
.
One may consider a range of values starting from a small positive number, as
Theorem

1
indicates a reasonable
\lambda
value should be away from zero.
An upper bound for
\lambda
is to consider the smallest value which ensures
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{t}
in
Algorithm

1
is the zero matrix.

When multiple adjacency matrices are available (corresponding to different layers), we propose to choose the tuning parameter
\lambda
via the cross-validation procedure in
Algorithm

2
.
If instead only a single observation of
\mathbf{A}
is available, then we consider an alternative approach detailed in the supplement.

Algorithm 2

M
-fold cross-validation for multiple networks

Input: Adjacency matrices
\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}
for
1\leq\ell\leq L
, estimated membership matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}
, tuning parameter value(s)
\lambda
, number of folds
M
.

1.

Sample splitting:
Randomly split
\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}
,
1\leq\ell\leq L
, into
M
equal-sized subsets. Let
\mathcal{I}^{(m)}
denote the matrix indices in the
m
-th fold.

2.

For each
1\leq m\leq M
and each value
\lambda
:

(a)

Set
\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(m)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}^{(m)}|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}^{(m)}}\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}
and
\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(-m)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}^{(-m)}|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}^{(-m)}}\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}
.

(b)

Apply
Algorithm

1
to
\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(-m)}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}
to obtain
\mathbf{B}_{\rho}^{\lambda}
.

(c)

Compute the validation loss
\operatorname{Loss}(\lambda)^{(m)}\coloneqq\|\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(m)}-\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{B}_{\rho}^{\lambda}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}
.

3.

Define
\operatorname{Loss}(\lambda)\coloneqq\sum_{m=1}^{M}\operatorname{Loss}(\lambda)^{(m)}
, and return
\widehat{\lambda}=\underset{\lambda}{\arg\min}\ \operatorname{Loss}(\lambda)
.

4
Theoretical guarantees

This section investigates the theoretical properties of our estimation method under the MonoSBM setting by leveraging the techniques in
Klopp 2014
;
Hamdi and
Bayati 2022
.
In particular, we establish expectation and high probability error bounds for the difference between
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
and
\mathbf{B}^{*}
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
is obtained by solving the optimization problem

\widehat{\mathbf{B}}=\underset{\mathbf{B}:~\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}}{\mathrm{minimize}}~\frac{1}{n}\|\overline{\mathbf{A}}-\rho\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{B}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}+\lambda\|\rho\mathbf{B}\|_{*}.

(6)

The first term in
Equation

6
differs from
Equation

3
by the scaling
n^{-1}
, thus leading to different selections of
\lambda
in the two formulations, but does not affect the resulting error bounds.

In preparation for
Theorem

1
, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1

(Commensurate ground-truth and estimated community sizes)
.

The true community sizes
\{n_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and the estimated community sizes
\{\widehat{n}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are both commensurate, namely, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 1
such that
n_{k}\in[n/(c_{1}K),c_{1}n/K]
and
\widehat{n}_{k}\in[n/(c_{2}K),c_{2}n/K]
for all
1\leq k\leq K
. Here,
K
can be fixed or grow with
n
.

The assumption of commensurate ground-truth community sizes is common in the literature
(
Fan
et al. 2022
;
Lei and Lin 2023
)
, and assuming commensurate estimated community sizes is reasonable due to existing clustering guarantees, as discussed in
Section

7
.
Nevertheless,
1
can indeed be relaxed, if desired, but at the expense of more tedious derivations and complicated resulting bounds.

In further preparation for the main result, we write the set of correctly clustered nodes as
\Omega\coloneqq\{i:\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}_{i\bullet}=\mathbf{Z}_{i\bullet}\}
, and hence, let
|\Omega^{c}|
denote the number of misclustered nodes.
Write
\bm{\Delta}_{3}\coloneqq\bm{\Delta}_{1}-\bm{\Delta}_{2}=\overline{\mathbf{A}}-\rho\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{B}^{*}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
for
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
and
\bm{\Delta}_{2}
in
Equation

1
.
Finally, we define the benchmark tuning parameter value

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\operatorname{val}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 16\sqrt{2}c_{1}\frac{1}{K}\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{L}}\left(\sqrt{K}+\sqrt{\log n}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}}{K}}+\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{\left|\Omega^{c}\right|}{n}}\right)^{2}\left(2\sqrt{2}\rho\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}n}{K}}\sqrt{|\Omega^{c}|}\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\rho|\Omega^{c}|\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{max}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(2\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}+2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}}{K}}\sqrt{\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}}\right)12\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{n\rho\log n}{L}},

(7)

which appears in
Theorem

1
, the general form of our main theoretical result.

Theorem 1

(Estimation of the connectivity probability matrix)
.

Consider a sample of observable networks per
Definition

1
.
Suppose
1
holds and
\operatorname{rk}(\mathbf{B}^{*})=d
.
Let
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
be the solution to
Equation

6
for tuning parameter
\lambda>0
satisfying

3\Lambda_{\operatorname{val}}\leq\lambda\leq 3C\Lambda_{\operatorname{val}},

(8)

where
C\geq 1
is any user-specified constant.
If
n\sqrt{L\rho}\geq C^{\prime}K(\sqrt{K}+\sqrt{\log n})
and
Ln\rho\geq C^{\prime\prime}\log n
for some sufficiently large positive constants
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{*}

(9)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{72d}\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}

\displaystyle\lesssim K^{2}d\Bigg[16\sqrt{2}\frac{c_{1}}{K}\sqrt{\frac{1}{Ln^{2}\rho}}\left(\sqrt{K}+\sqrt{\log n}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}}{K}}+\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}}\right)^{2}\left(2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}}{K}}\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\sqrt{\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}}+\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\max}\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}}{K}}\sqrt{\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}}+2\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}\right)12\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{Ln\rho}}\Bigg]

with probability at least
1-O(n^{-8})
.

In
Theorem

1
, for the special case
L=1
, sharper control of
n^{-1}\|\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\bm{\Delta}_{3}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
in the proof leads to an improved bound.
Namely, by replacing the term
12\sqrt{2}\sqrt{n\rho\log n/L}
in
Equation

7
with
C^{\prime\prime\prime}\sqrt{n\rho}
, where
C^{\prime\prime\prime}>0
denotes a sufficiently large constant, then an improvement of
Equation

9
is obtained by replacing the term
12\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\log n/(Ln\rho)}
with
C^{\prime\prime\prime}/\sqrt{n\rho}
.

Remark 2

(Deterministic versus stochastic estimated communities)
.

For simplicity,
1
and hence
Theorem

1
considers the estimated community sizes to be deterministic.
If a specific clustering method with quantifiable performance is employed, then the assumption can be restated as holding with high probability.
Namely, define the event

E\coloneqq\left\{\widehat{n}_{k}\in[n/(c_{2}K),c_{2}n/K]\text{ for some }c_{2}\geq 1\text{ for all }1\leq k\leq K\right\}.

Then, the upper bound in
Theorem

1
holds with probability at least
1-\mathbb{P}(E^{c})-O(n^{-8})
.

The proof of
Theorem

1
relies on the following key inequality implied by
1
.

Lemma 1

(Frobenius norm lower bound)
.

Under
1
, for any
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
,

\frac{1}{n}\|\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{B}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}\geq\frac{n}{(c_{2}K)^{2}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}.

In
Theorem

1
, the choice of tuning parameter
\lambda
stems from the following lemma.

Lemma 2

(Operator norm bound for tuning parameter selection)
.

Invoke
1
.
If
n\sqrt{L\rho}\geq C^{\prime}K(\sqrt{K}+\sqrt{\log n})
and
Ln\rho\geq C^{\prime\prime}\log n
hold for some sufficiently large positive constants
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\bm{\Delta}_{3}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda_{\operatorname{val}}

with probability at least
1-O(n^{-8})
.
If
L=1
, then the bound can be improved by replacing the term
12\sqrt{2}\sqrt{n\rho\log n/L}
in the definition of
\Lambda_{\operatorname{val}}
with
C^{\prime\prime\prime}\sqrt{n\rho}
for some constant
C^{\prime\prime\prime}>0
, per the earlier comment.

The following corollary presents special cases of
Theorem

1
and highlights the performance of the estimation method when node memberships are perfectly recovered.

Corollary 1

(Special case of
Theorem

1
)
.

Assume the hypotheses in
Theorem

1
.

1.

If
d,K=O(1)
, then

\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{Ln\rho}}+\sqrt{\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}}\left(\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\sqrt{\frac{|\Omega^{c}|}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{Ln\rho}}\right)

(10)

with probability at least
1-O(n^{-8})
.

2.

For general
d
and
K
, if
|\Omega^{c}|=0
, then

\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}\lesssim K\sqrt{d}\left(\sqrt{K}+\sqrt{\log n}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{Ln\rho}}

(11)

with probability at least
1-O(n^{-8})
.
Moreover, in expectation,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}\right]\lesssim K^{3/2}\sqrt{d}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{Ln\rho}}.

(12)

Remark 3

(Comparing estimators)
.

For comparison, consider the average-based estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\mathrm{Avg}}
when
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}=\mathbf{Z}
, i.e.,
|\Omega^{c}|=0
.
To understand the concentration properties of
\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\mathrm{Avg}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}
, it holds by direct computation that the expected squared Frobenius difference satisfies

\frac{K^{4}}{Ln^{2}\rho}~\lesssim~\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\mathrm{Avg}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}^{2}\right]~\lesssim~\frac{K^{4}}{Ln^{2}\rho}.

Hence, by Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\mathrm{Avg}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}\right]\lesssim K^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{Ln\rho}}.

Consequently,
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
per
Corollary

1
is preferable to
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{\mathrm{Avg}}
when
K
is large and
d\ll K
.
This is precisely the improvement one can hope to achieve using the proof techniques in
Klopp 2014
;
Hamdi and
Bayati 2022
.

Remark 4

(Properties of norms)
.

We pause here to comment on the significance of the perturbation analysis conducted to obtain
Theorem

1
and
Corollary

1
.
First, note that
\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{*}
and
\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}
denote the
\ell_{1}
norm and
\ell_{2}
norm of the vector of singular values of
\mathbf{B}^{*}
, respectively.
By elementary properties of norms,

\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}\leq\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{*}\leq\sqrt{K}\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}},\qquad\mathbf{B}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}.

Since
\operatorname{rk}(\mathbf{B}^{*})=d\leq K
, the stated results yield improved upper bounds on
\|\cdot\|_{*}
in terms of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle F}}
by replacing
\sqrt{K}
with
C\sqrt{\operatorname{rk}(\mathbf{B}^{*})}
for some dimension-free universal constant
C>0
.

Remark 5

(Avoiding suboptimal dependence on
K
)
.

The naïve bound
\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K
can be unnecessarily coarse.
Note that if
\mathbf{B}^{*}=\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
, then
\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\operatorname{op}}=\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Thus, if for example
\bm{v}=(p,p^{2},\dots,p^{K})^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
for some
p\in(0,1)
, then
\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p^{2k}=\frac{p^{2}(1-p^{2K})}{1-p^{2}}\leq\frac{p^{2}}{1-p^{2}}
irrespective of
K
.
Similarly,
\|\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
does not depend on
K
when
\mathbf{B}
is a diagonal matrix.

Remark 6

(Network sparsity)
.

Network sparsity is traditionally introduced and included to (at least partially) quantify problem difficulty (by reflecting the amount of available network information) and justify the applicability of analysis procedures to real-world non-dense graphs.
The typical sparsity condition for single-graph statistical analysis and clustering is of the form
n\rho\geq C\log n
, whereas the hypotheses in
Theorem

1
and
Corollary

1
show that the availability of multiple graphs (layers),
L
, compensates for having even sparser individual graphs; see also
Lei and Lin 2023
.
As expected, observe that the estimation error bounds degrade as the sparsity term
\rho
(in the denominator) gets smaller.
Further, having more sparsity can in turn lead to a larger misclustering rate,
n^{-1}|\Omega^{c}|
, but this aspect and its subsequent influence on estimation error bounds is implicit since the stated results are not confined to a specific choice of clustering algorithm or to specific clustering performance.

Section

7
provides details about the misclustering rate of several existing clustering approaches with explicit dependence on network sparsity.

Application to primate brain gene co-expression

We apply our method to a microarray dataset from
Bakken et al. 2016
obtained from the medial prefrontal cortex of developing rhesus monkeys originally containing
9173
genes with
10

The dataset consists of gene co-expression representing 10 different developmental time points, spanning from 40 days (E40) in the embryo to 48 months (48M) after birth.
Each time point corresponds to post-mortem tissue samples collected from multiple rhesus monkeys.
Previous studies demonstrated meaningful findings when applying stochastic blockmodel methodology to this dataset, with a focus on community detection rather than investigating the connectivity within and between communities
(
Lei
et al. 2020
;
Lei and Lin 2023
)
.
Here, our goal is instead to determine whether or not connectivity is plausibly full rank, with the aim of enabling more comprehensive analysis.

We use the original dataset together with the preprocessing

After preprocessing, each layer contains

Lei

7
Discussion

Our estimation error bounds under the Frobenius norm and the nuclear norm can be specialized to specific clustering algorithms by leveraging existing misclustering rate results in the literature.
For example,
Lei and Lin 2023
obtains a performance guarantee for bias-adjusted spectral clustering of the form

n^{-1}|\Omega^{c}|=O_{P}\left(K({n_{\min}n})^{-2}+K\log(L+n)(n_{\min}\sqrt{L}n\rho)^{-2}\right).

Elsewhere,
Xu
et al. 2023
develops a covariate-assisted method which in the absence of covariates achieves

n^{-1}|\Omega^{c}|=O_{P}\left(n_{\max}n^{4}n_{\min}^{-6}\log n(L\rho)^{-1}\right).

For
L=1
,
Zhang
et al. 2022
considers spectral clustering with random projection and random sampling; there, in the special case
B^{*}_{ii}=\rho a
and
B^{*}_{ij}=\rho b
with balanced community sizes
n/K
, it is shown that

n^{-1}|\Omega^{c}|=O_{P}(K^{3}(n\rho)^{-1})
,
as well as

\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{\max}=O_{P}\left((n\rho)^{-1}K^{3/2}\log n\left\{1+(1-K^{3}/\log n)^{-1}\right\}\right),

provided
n\rho\geq c_{0}\log n
for some suitable positive constant
c_{0}
.

Next, we briefly elaborate on applying our method to MultiSBM per
Definition

2
.
We cluster
L
layers into
\widetilde{L}
groups so that graphs in each group belong to MonoSBM.
Often,
\widetilde{L}
is given in multilayer network analysis due to domain-specific and problem-specific contextual knowledge
(
Pensky and
Wang 2024
;
Jing
et al. 2021
;
Fan
et al. 2022
;
Noroozi and
Pensky 2024
)
.
If
\widetilde{L}
is unknown, then we can use an elbow-based scree plot method applied to the matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times n(n+1)/2}
(
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times K(K+1)/2}
), where
\mathbf{G}_{\ell\bullet}
(
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}_{\ell\bullet}
) consists of the upper triangular elements of
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{(\ell)}
(
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{(\ell)}
).
For more general multilayer networks, we can either employ the elbow method on different matrices or utilize tensor decomposition techniques.
For MultiSBM, we use spectral clustering on
\mathbf{G}
(
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}
) to cluster
L
layers, which works well in our numerical studies and real data analysis.
For other types of multilayer networks, we can use alternative methods which enjoy guarantees for the between-layer clustering error rate,
R_{\mathrm{BL}}
, defined as

R_{\mathrm{BL}}\coloneqq\underset{\pi}{\min}~L^{-1}\sum_{\widetilde{\ell}=1}^{\widetilde{L}}|\mathbb{G}_{\widetilde{\ell}}\setminus\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_{\pi(\widetilde{\ell})}|,

where
\pi:\llbracket\widetilde{L}\rrbracket\to\llbracket\widetilde{L}\rrbracket
is a permutation.
In particular, the TWIST algorithm of
Jing
et al. 2021
exhibits

R_{\mathrm{BL}}=O_{P}\left(\log n/(Ln\rho)\right)

while the ALMA algorithm of
Fan
et al. 2022
exhibits

R_{\mathrm{BL}}=O_{P}\left(\log^{4}(n+L)/(n^{2}\rho)+\log^{4}(n+L)/(n\{\min(n,L)\rho\})^{2}\right),

both of which are iterative methods developed for multilayer SBMs.
In general, we note that caution is needed in method selection, as
\mathbf{B}^{*}
might be low rank, and some methods mentioned above only study
R_{\mathrm{BL}}
in full rank settings.

In the multilayer network literature, there is growing interest in the use of tensor techniques such as the Tucker decomposition.

Han
et al. 2022
develops a higher-order spectral clustering and higher-order Lloyd algorithm to achieve exact clustering in a tensor block model.
More closely related to our problem (where we do not assume all
\mathbf{B}^{*}
to be full rank), aside from the aforementioned method in
Xu
et al. 2023
for MultiSBM, is the TWIST method in
Jing
et al. 2021
which has the error rate for the
\widetilde{\ell}
-th layer group

n^{-1}|\Omega_{\widetilde{\ell}}^{c}|=O_{P}(\widetilde{L}{K_{\widetilde{\ell}}}^{2}\log n(Ln\rho)^{-1})
,
where
K_{\widetilde{\ell}}
is the number of node communities in the
\widetilde{\ell}
-th layer group.

In summary, the results in this paper complement and can be integrated with the aforementioned existing bounds, providing a point of departure for future work on multiple network analysis.
Collectively, the theoretical and empirical investigations presented here suggest the potential usefulness of the proposed approach, either in a stand-alone fashion or more realistically as one of several methods to be applied in tandem when analyzing networks.
While it is perhaps tempting to work with average-based estimators, given their conceptual and practical simplicity, doing so can be sub-optimal or inadvertently lead to overlooking structural properties of data-generating mechanisms.
As such, this paper provides one principled alternative for examining connectivity and dimensionality in samples of structured networks.
