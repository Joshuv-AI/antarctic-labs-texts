Unraveling the causal relationship between serum minerals and pancreatic cancer: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Cong Liu, Huajun Wu, Dongdong Cai, Xin Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer is among the most lethal malignancies, characterized by a poor prognosis and limited modifiable factors. Emerging evidence indicates that serum mineral levels may influence the likelihood of developing pancreatic cancer. However, the causal relationship between serum minerals and pancreatic cancer remains unclear and warrants further investigation.MethodsThis Mendelian randomization (MR) study was conducted to explore the causal effects of serum mineral levels on pancreatic cancer risk. Genetic variants associated with serum mineral levels, including calcium, iron, magnesium, zinc, selenium, and copper, were selected as instrumental variables (IVs) from large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data. Multiple methods, including inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, weight methods, were employed to perform MR analysis. The effect sizes from the MR analysis, using two independent GWAS summary datasets related to pancreatic cancer, were combined through meta-analysis. The Cochrane Q test, MR-Egger intercept test, MR-PRESSO global test, and leave-one-out test were conducted for sensitivity tests.ResultsOur MR analysis demonstrated a significant causal effect of genetically predicted serum calcium levels on increased pancreatic cancer risk [OR = 1.64, 95% CI 1.05–2.57, P = 0.029 (discovery cohort); OR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.07–2.15, P = 0.019 (validation cohort)], while no significant associations were found for other serum minerals (P > 0.05). Additional meta-analysis reinforces and substantiates this conclusion (pooled OR = 1.56, 95% CI 1.19–2.06, P = 0.001). No evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity was detected across multiple sensitivity tests (P > 0.05).ConclusionThis study provides new evidence supporting the causal role of certain serum minerals, particularly calcium, in the development of pancreatic cancer. These findings may help inform future research into preventive strategies or therapies aimed at modulating mineral levels in patients at high risk of pancreatic cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01695-6.Keywords: Mendelian randomization analysis, Minerals, Pancreatic neoplasms, Genome-wide association study, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is among the most lethal malignancies globally [1, 2], with a five-year survival rate of less than 10% [3, 4]. Despite advances in medical research, the prognosis for pancreatic cancer remains dismal due to its late-stage diagnosis and aggressive nature [5, 6]. Pancreatic cancer is one of the most lethal malignancies, with limited modifiable risk factors identified to date, posing significant challenges for early detection, prevention, and treatment [7, 8]. Established risk factors, such as smoking and chronic pancreatitis, provide some insights, yet they fail to fully account for disease variability. Recent evidence suggests that serum mineral levels may play a role in pancreatic cancer etiology, potentially offering new avenues for intervention [9, 10]. However, current studies primarily rely on observational designs, which are prone to confounding and reverse causation, leaving the causal relationship between serum mineral dysregulation and pancreatic cancer risk unclear.

Serum minerals are essential for numerous biological processes, including enzymatic reactions, oxidative stress regulation, and immune function [11, 12]. Dysregulation of these minerals has been linked to various chronic diseases [13–15], including cancer [16], through alterations at the cellular level. For instance, calcium, a key regulator of cellular signaling, plays a pivotal role in processes such as cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and proliferation [17]. Dysregulated calcium signaling can lead to uncontrolled cell proliferation and inhibit programmed cell death, both of which are hallmarks of tumorigenesis. Additionally, excessive calcium levels have been shown to activate matrix metalloproteinases and influence cytoskeletal remodeling, promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis [17]. Similarly, imbalances in zinc and selenium, potent antioxidants, can disrupt redox homeostasis [18, 19], leading to oxidative DNA damage and genomic instability—critical factors in cancer initiation. Magnesium deficiency, on the other hand, may impair DNA repair mechanisms and exacerbate genomic instability [20], further contributing to carcinogenesis. Likewise, the role of iron and copper metabolism abnormalities in cancer is receiving increasing attention [21–24]. Despite these insights, the specific roles of serum mineral dysregulation in pancreatic cancer remain poorly understood, particularly in the context of causal relationships.

Mendelian randomization (MR) offers a robust epidemiological approach to assess the causal effects of risk factors, such as serum mineral levels, on disease outcomes [25, 26]. By using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to proxy the exposure of interest, MR addresses confounding factors and reverse causality, which are common limitations in observational studies. This approach is particularly valuable for studying serum minerals, as large genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified strong genetic associations with mineral levels. These genetic variants can serve as reliable IVs to explore potential causal links between serum mineral levels and pancreatic cancer risk.

In this study, we employ MR analysis to determine whether genetically predicted serum mineral levels are causally associated with pancreatic cancer risk. By leveraging large GWAS datasets and applying various MR analysis methods, we aim to provide new insights into the role of mineral homeostasis in pancreatic cancer development and identify potential preventive strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design

MR is mainly classified into one-sample MR and two-sample MR (TSMR). One-sample MR analyzes SNPs, exposures, and outcomes within one dataset, allowing for the assessment of nonlinear causal relationships and subgroup heterogeneity. However, it often relies on individual-level data, such as from the UK Biobank, which limits accessibility and the selection of IVs. TSMR, the more widely used approach, utilizes summary GWAS data from independent cohorts. This method benefits from larger, more comprehensive datasets that are publicly accessible, enabling broader applications and more robust analyses. Based on the core assumptions of MR study [27] (Fig. 1), two-sample MR (TSMR) was employed to explore the causal relationship between serum minerals and pancreatic cancer using publicly available GWAS data. To ensure robust conclusions, a meta-analysis was conducted to combine the MR effect estimates derived from two independent GWAS datasets related to pancreatic cancer (Fig. 2).

Data of exposure and outcome

The summary GWAS data for serum minerals and pancreatic cancer was all sourced from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) open GWAS project database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), which is a large-scale repository that provides access to a wide range of genetic epidemiology resources including GWAS datasets. The corresponding IDs are “ebi-a-GCST90025990” (calcium levels), “ieu-a-1049” (iron levels), “ukb-b-7372” (magnesium levels), “ieu-a-1079” (zinc levels), “ieu-a-1077” (selenium levels), and “ieu-a-1073” (copper levels). Two independent GWAS on pancreatic cancer were used for mutual corroboration, with the corresponding IDs being “ieu-a-822” (discovery cohort, sample size = 3835) and “finn-b-C3_PANCREAS_EXALLC” (validation cohort, sample size = 174,611), respectively. Considering the genetic background differences across ethnicities, the populations used in this study were all of European descent. In addition, as the aforementioned GWAS data are publicly available, no additional ethical approval is required. Detailed information on all the GWAS used in this MR study is summarized in Table 1.

Identification of IVs

First, SNPs meeting the genome-wide significance threshold (P < 1 × 10⁻5) were selected from the GWAS on serum minerals, and the clumping process was conducted to remove SNPs in linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.001, kb = 10,000). Subsequently, to avoid bias from weak IVs, the F-statistic for each SNP was calculated, with F-statistics > 10 considered sufficient to counteract weak instrument bias. SNPs with F-values < 10 were excluded. The formula for calculation is as follows: F = β2/SE2 [28], where β is the effect size of the allele on exposure, and SE is the standard error of the exposure. Additionally, “LDTrait”, an online tool (https://ldlink.nih.gov/?tab=ldtrait), was employed to identify SNPs associated with potential confounders such as "mineral and other dietary supplements" and "vitamin D," which may influence serum mineral levels, thereby minimizing horizontal pleiotropy. Lastly, the Mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) test was conducted [29], and any detected outliers were eliminated before performing MR analysis on the remaining SNPs. The detailed information of the IVs used in this MR analysis is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis for MR

This study primarily employed inverse variance weighting (IVW) [30], MR-Egger [31], weighted median [32] and weighted mode. Since the IVW method has higher statistical power compared to other methods and could provide more robust results under conditions of heterogeneity, the IVW analysis was used as the main effect analysis. Cochran's Q statistic was used to assess heterogeneity, with P < 0.05 indicating the presence of heterogeneity. A leave-one-out analysis was conducted to evaluate whether bias existed due to the independent influence of a single SNP on the MR results. MR-Egger regression was used to evaluate pleiotropy by observing the intercept; P < 0.05 indicated the presence of pleiotropy. The MR-PRESSO outlier test was also performed to identify and remove outliers, correcting for horizontal pleiotropy [33]. Meta-analysis was further performed to combine the effect estimates by the IVW method from the discovery and validation cohorts. All statistical analyses were conducted using the "TwoSampleMR", “MRPRESSO”, and “meta” package in R software version 4.3.1. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant in this study.

Result

The causal effect of serum minerals on pancreatic cancer in the discovery cohort

The IVW results indicate that a per SD increase in genetically predicted serum calcium levels raises the risk of pancreatic cancer by 64% [odds ratio (OR) = 1.64, 95% confidence interval (95% CI) 1.05–2.57, P = 0.029]. MR-Egger also supports this potential association (OR = 2.27, 95% CI 1.18–4.36, P = 0.016), and although the other two methods did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05), the trend remained consistent (all OR > 1). There was no evidence to suggest an association between other serum minerals and the risk of pancreatic cancer (iron: OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.70–1.40, P = 0.958; magnesium: OR = 3.11, 95% CI 0.27–36.38, P = 0.367; zinc: OR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.73- 1.53, P = 0.778; selenium: OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.67–1.45, P = 0.956; copper: OR = 1.27, 95% CI 0.92–1.78, P = 0.151) (Fig. 3).

The causal effect of serum minerals on pancreatic cancer in the validation cohort

The validation cohort further confirmed the results of the discovery cohort. The IVW results showed that a per SD increase in genetically predicted serum calcium levels increased the risk of pancreatic cancer by 52% (OR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.07–2.15, P = 0.019). The weighted median also supported this association (OR = 2.07, 95% CI 1.12–3.82, P = 0.020), and although the other methods did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05), the trend remained consistent (all OR > 1). There was no evidence to suggest an association between other serum minerals and pancreatic cancer risk (iron: OR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.64–1.40, P = 0.782; magnesium: OR = 1.55, 95% CI 0.76–3.16, P = 0.225; zinc: OR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.74–1.27, P = 0.845; selenium: OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.81–1.18, P = 0.818; copper: OR = 1.07, 95% CI 0.92–1.25, P = 0.373) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to explore the causal relationship between serum mineral levels and pancreatic cancer risk using MR analysis. By leveraging genetic variants as instrumental variables, we aimed to overcome the limitations of observational studies, such as confounding and reverse causation, and provide robust evidence regarding the role of key serum minerals in pancreatic cancer etiology. Among the minerals analyzed, we identified a significant causal association between genetically predicted serum calcium levels and an increased risk of pancreatic cancer, while no significant associations were observed for iron, magnesium, zinc, selenium, and copper. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the potential role of calcium homeostasis in pancreatic cancer development but also offer new insights into risk assessment and future preventive strategies.

Existing research on serum minerals, particularly calcium, has increasingly suggested a link between mineral levels and pancreatic cancer risk [34]. A cross-sectional study involving more than 500 participants aimed at evaluating the complex association between dietary calcium intake and pancreatic cancer risk indicated that higher calcium consumption in the diet may increase the risk of developing pancreatic cancer [35]. Although epidemiological studies have identified associations between calcium levels and cancer, the causal nature of this relationship remains uncertain. Many of these studies are observational and may be influenced by confounding factors and reverse causation. Thus, applying MR analysis could provide valuable insights, utilizing genetic variants as IVs to explore the causal relationship between serum calcium and pancreatic cancer. This study employed MR methods to assess the impact of serum calcium and other minerals on pancreatic cancer risk. Our findings indicate that genetically predicted higher serum calcium levels are significantly associated with an increased risk of pancreatic cancer, with a 56% increase in risk per standard deviation rise in calcium levels. This association was consistent across various sensitivity analyses, supporting the hypothesis that calcium may serve as a potential risk factor for pancreatic cancer.

Elevated serum calcium levels may significantly contribute to the development and progression of pancreatic cancer by disrupting critical cellular processes. As a vital intracellular messenger, calcium regulates various cellular functions, including proliferation, apoptosis, motility, and differentiation. Dysregulated calcium signaling can promote tumorigenesis through multiple pathways. For instance, elevated intracellular calcium levels activate oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK, fostering uncontrolled cell proliferation and resistance to apoptosis [36]. Simultaneously, calcium dysregulation inhibits apoptosis by modulating key regulators like caspases and Bcl-2 family proteins and stabilizing mitochondrial membranes, thus preventing the activation of apoptotic cascades. Furthermore, calcium facilitates tumor invasion and metastasis by influencing cytoskeletal dynamics, regulating adhesion proteins like E-cadherin and integrins, and activating matrix metalloproteinases (e.g., MMP-2 and MMP-9) that degrade the extracellular matrix [37]. Chronic inflammation, a hallmark of pancreatic cancer, is exacerbated by high calcium levels through NF-κB signaling activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, creating a tumor-promoting microenvironment [38, 39]. Additionally, calcium overload induces oxidative stress by stimulating mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, leading to DNA damage, genomic instability, and the activation of oncogenic pathways. These mechanisms highlight the critical role of calcium homeostasis in pancreatic cancer biology. Our findings, demonstrating a causal relationship between elevated serum calcium levels and pancreatic cancer risk, suggest that calcium signaling pathways could be targeted therapeutically. Moreover, monitoring serum calcium levels in high-risk individuals may serve as a valuable biomarker for early detection and risk stratification, offering promising avenues for future research and clinical application.

MR has become a powerful tool in epidemiological research [40–42] offering distinct advantages in investigating causal relationships between risk factors and disease outcomes. One of the major strengths of MR, as demonstrated in this study, is its ability to reduce confounding bias. Since genetic variants are randomly assigned at conception and remain fixed throughout life, they are generally independent of environmental and lifestyle factors that can confound traditional observational studies. This random allocation mimics the design of a randomized controlled trial, allowing MR to provide more reliable estimates of causality. Additionally, the use of GWAS data, as in our study, enhances the statistical power of MR analyses, making it possible to detect even modest causal effects with greater precision.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, although MR minimizes confounding and reverse causation, the results are inherently dependent on the validity of the selected genetic instruments. While we carefully selected instrumental variables and conducted sensitivity analyses to address potential pleiotropy, residual pleiotropy cannot be entirely excluded. Second, our study relies on GWAS data predominantly derived from individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations with differing genetic and environmental backgrounds. Third, MR assumes a linear and unidirectional relationship between exposure and outcome, which may not fully capture the complex biological interactions involved in mineral homeostasis and pancreatic cancer. Finally, our findings were not validated using actual patient serum samples, an important step for confirming the clinical relevance of these results. Future research should address these limitations through experimental validation, diverse population studies, and comprehensive mechanistic investigations.

In conclusion, this study provides new evidence for the causal relationship between serum calcium levels and pancreatic cancer risk, suggesting that calcium may be a potential risk factor for this malignancy. Future large-scale prospective cohort studies and mechanistic investigations are needed to clarify this association and offer new perspectives for the prevention and intervention of pancreatic cancer.

Conclusion

This Mendelian randomization study provides robust evidence supporting a causal relationship between genetically predicted serum calcium levels and an increased risk of pancreatic cancer, with findings indicating a 56% heightened risk per standard deviation increase in serum calcium levels. This association was consistent across discovery and validation cohorts, reinforced by sensitivity analyses, while no significant causal links were identified for other serum minerals, including iron, magnesium, zinc, selenium, and copper. Mechanistically, dysregulated calcium signaling may contribute to tumorigenesis by promoting cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and enhancing metastatic potential, highlighting the importance of calcium homeostasis in pancreatic cancer development. Despite the study's strengths, such as the use of large-scale GWAS data and robust statistical methods, limitations exist, including potential residual pleiotropy and the focus on European populations, which may limit generalizability. Future research should explore the biological mechanisms underlying these associations through experimental studies and validate findings across diverse populations, while also considering prospective investigations into calcium modulation as a preventive strategy against pancreatic cancer. These findings emphasize the broader role of mineral homeostasis in cancer biology and its potential for informing precision interventions to mitigate pancreatic cancer risk.
