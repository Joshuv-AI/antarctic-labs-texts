Balancing sensitivity and specificity in diagnostic and prognostic scoring systems for acute-on-chronic liver failure
Authors: Kymentie Ferdinande, Alberto Zanetto, Marco Senzolo
Published: 2026

Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is a clinical syndrome characterised by acute decompensation of chronic liver disease accompanied by organ failures and high short-term mortality (1,2). Over the past decade, multiple consortia have proposed definitions of ACLF, including the European Association for the Study of the Liver-Chronic Liver Failure Consortium (EASL-CLIF) criteria in Europe, the Chinese Group on the Study of Severe Hepatitis B (COSSH) criteria in China, the North American Consortium for the Study of End-Stage Liver Disease (NACSELD) criteria in North America, and the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL) criteria in Asia (3-8). While each framework reflects regional epidemiology and underlying disease patterns, their coexistence has resulted in substantial heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria. This lack of uniformity complicates clinical decision-making, limits comparability across studies, and poses challenges for the design and interpretation of interventional trials. In this context, Luo and colleagues aimed to evaluate the applicability of the COSSH criteria across diverse aetiologies and geographic regions, with the broader goal of contributing to a more unified diagnostic and prognostic framework (9).

In this large, prospective multicentre study, 7,388 patients with acute deterioration of chronic liver disease were screened, of whom 5,288 were included in the final analysis after predefined exclusions (9). The cohort encompassed a broad spectrum of aetiologies, including hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related disease, non-HBV liver diseases, and non-cirrhotic chronic liver disease, making it one of the largest multi-aetiology ACLF cohorts reported to date (9). Luo et al. directly compared the diagnostic performance of the EASL-CLIF and COSSH criteria and assessed the prognostic accuracy of COSSH-derived scoring systems in both Asian and non-Asian populations, renaming the framework ‘China-CLIF’ to reflect its broader applicability (9).

Using the EASL-CLIF criteria, 844 of 5,288 patients were classified as having ACLF, with 28-day and 90-day transplant-free mortality rates of 40.7% and 57.0%, respectively (3,9,10). In contrast, COSSH criteria identified 2,038 patients, capturing an additional 22.6% of the cohort, albeit with lower mortality (27.3% and 41.0%, respectively) (5,9). Notably, COSSH classification resulted in a more balanced, pyramid-like distribution across severity grades (63.4%, 27.5%, and 9.1% for grades 1–3, respectively), compared with a more imbalanced distribution under CLIF criteria (25.8%, 56.3%, and 17.9%) (9). Among the prognostic models evaluated, the COSSH-ACLF II score demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy for short-term mortality across both cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic patients (9). Strengths of the study include its prospective design, inclusion of diverse aetiologies, and validation in independent non-Asian cohorts (9). These findings suggest that COSSH criteria identify patients earlier in the disease course and provide a more graduated stratification of severity.

However, several aspects of the China-CLIF framework require careful consideration. First, for conceptual clarity, it is important to delineate the terminology underlying the China-CLIF framework, which encompasses both diagnostic criteria and prognostic models (5,6,9). The COSSH consortium originally developed diagnostic criteria for HBV-related ACLF, placing hepatic dysfunction at the centre of the syndrome, defined by a total bilirubin ≥12 mg/dL in combination with an international normalized ratio (INR) ≥1.5 (5). These criteria allow the identification of ACLF in liver-dominant phenotypes without requiring clearly established extrahepatic organ failure, in contrast to the EASL-CLIF framework (5). Beyond diagnosis, COSSH investigators also developed prognostic scores to estimate mortality in patients already classified as having ACLF (6). The COSSH-ACLF II score incorporates age, total bilirubin, INR, serum creatinine, hepatic encephalopathy grade, white blood cell count, and blood urea nitrogen, whereas the COSSH-ACLFs score (renamed China-CLIFs) represents a simplified alternative that does not include urea (6). Only the China-CLIFs score underwent full external validation in non-Asian cohorts, whereas the COSSH-ACLF II score could not be comprehensively validated in these datasets due to the unavailability of urea measurements (9).

The central finding that COSSH criteria identify substantially more patients with ACLF than CLIF criteria warrants careful interpretation. In the present study, COSSH criteria were applied to the entire cohort, including both cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic patients, whereas CLIF criteria were restricted to the cirrhotic subset (9). This discrepancy in the underlying populations complicates direct comparisons and may partially account for the observed differences in prevalence and outcomes. More fundamentally, the inclusion of non-cirrhotic patients raises an important conceptual question regarding the underlying definition of ACLF. The EASL-CLIF framework deliberately restricts ACLF to patients with cirrhosis, reflecting the understanding that ACLF represents acute deterioration in a background of established cirrhosis (1-3,10). In contrast, COSSH criteria include patients with non-cirrhotic chronic liver disease, particularly those with HBV infection (5). While both approaches are biologically plausible, they likely capture distinct clinical syndromes with different pathophysiology, prognostic trajectories, and therapeutic implications. In addition, COSSH criteria permit ACLF diagnosis in liver-dominant phenotypes without requiring clearly established extrahepatic organ failure (5). Consequently, patients with severe hepatic dysfunction may be classified as ACLF under COSSH criteria, whereas they would be considered as having acute decompensation under CLIF definitions. The more balanced severity distribution observed with COSSH may therefore, at least in part, reflect the inclusion of patients with less advanced or differently structured disease trajectories (9). These considerations suggest that differences in diagnostic yield between frameworks may be more nuanced than a straightforward head-to-head comparison would imply. The EASL-CLIF and COSSH/China-CLIF criteria are based on the CLIF-C OF framework and define ACLF using fixed thresholds for individual organ failures, although COSSH/China-CLIF additionally includes patients with chronic liver disease without cirrhosis and allows liver-dominant phenotypes within grade 1 ACLF. Subscore 3 defines organ failure, while subscores 2 and 1 represent intermediate dysfunction and normal/mild impairment, respectively. The A-TANGO framework also introduces a fourth ACLF grade (≥4 organ failures) to address heterogeneity within the most severe disease category. The global consensus statement by Bajaj et al. provides standardized definitions for organ failure in cirrhosis, including revised thresholds for hepatic, renal, circulatory, and respiratory failure, and introduces additional domains such as immune and gastrointestinal failure. Placing these findings in the context of recent efforts to refine ACLF definitions highlights important differences in how organ failure is conceptualised and operationalised. Engelmann and colleagues recently introduced the A-TANGO organ failure score, derived from large multinational cohorts, which recalibrates organ failure thresholds based on outcome-driven inflection points, defining organ failure at the level corresponding to a ≥15% 28-day mortality risk (11). By anchoring thresholds to clinically meaningful prognostic transitions rather than fixed laboratory cut-offs, A-TANGO seeks to preserve the pathophysiological coherence of ACLF while improving calibration across diverse populations (11). This approach may enhance both sensitivity and specificity relative to existing CLIF-based definitions, particularly by more accurately identifying patients at a clinically relevant risk threshold (11). In addition, the introduction of a fourth ACLF grade addresses the marked heterogeneity within CLIF grade 3, thereby improving risk stratification among the most severely ill patients (11) (Table 1). In contrast, the global consensus statement by Bajaj and colleagues primarily focuses on standardising definitions of individual organ failures in cirrhosis, but also proposes a composite definition of ACLF based on the presence of liver failure in combination with extrahepatic organ dysfunction or failure (12) (Table 1). These consensus-based thresholds are generally lower than those used in existing frameworks and incorporate additional domains such as immune and gastrointestinal failure (12). While this broader conceptualisation may increase sensitivity and facilitate earlier recognition of organ dysfunction, it does so at the potential expense of specificity, raising important questions regarding the distinction between organ dysfunction and organ failure, a distinction that has historically been central to ACLF definitions (2,12). Considered alongside the findings of Luo et al., these approaches reflect differing strategies to balance sensitivity and specificity (9,11,12). The COSSH/China-CLIF framework identifies a substantially larger proportion of patients, in part through the inclusion of non-cirrhotic disease and liver-dominant phenotypes, thereby increasing sensitivity while broadening the clinical spectrum encompassed by ACLF (9). In contrast, A-TANGO refines existing definitions around clinically meaningful risk thresholds without substantially expanding this spectrum, whereas the consensus definitions move further towards inclusivity (11,12).

These differing approaches underscore that recent developments in ACLF definitions do not represent a linear progression towards either stricter or more permissive criteria, but rather reflect distinct conceptual strategies (11,12). More sensitive frameworks may enhance generalisability and facilitate earlier identification of patients across heterogeneous clinical settings, but risk diluting the specificity that underpins ACLF as a distinct and clinically actionable entity (12). The conceptual strength of ACLF lies in its ability to identify a well-defined pathophysiological state associated with a sharp inflection in short-term mortality, thereby delineating a narrow diagnostic window during which targeted interventions may alter the disease trajectory (2). Accordingly, the thresholds defining organ failure were not selected merely because they represent abnormal values, but because crossing them signals a fundamental shift in prognosis (3,4,10). Preserving this link between definition and outcome is essential if ACLF is to remain a meaningful construct in both clinical practice and research.

In this context, efforts towards harmonisation should prioritise the preservation of clinically meaningful thresholds that reflect true transitions in organ failure and prognosis. Expanding definitions to increase inclusivity may facilitate broader applicability, but risks undermining the biological coherence and discriminative precision required for effective patient stratification, rational trial design, and the development of targeted therapies. Maintaining an appropriate balance between sensitivity and specificity will be essential to preserve ACLF as a distinct and clinically actionable syndrome in both clinical practice and research.
