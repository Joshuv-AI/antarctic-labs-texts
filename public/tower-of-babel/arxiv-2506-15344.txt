Singular intersections on families of abelian varieties
Nicola Ottolini

Abstract
. Let S S be a smooth irreducible curve defined over ℚ ¯ \overline{\mathbb{Q}} , let 𝒜 \mathcal{A} be an abelian scheme over S S and 𝒞 \mathcal{C} a curve inside 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , both defined over ℚ ¯ \overline{\mathbb{Q}} . In this paper we prove that the set of points in which 𝒞 \mathcal{C} intersects proper flat subgroup schemes of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} tangentially is finite. This fits in the framework of the so-called problems of unlikely intersections, and can be seen as a variation of the relative Pink conjecture for abelian varieties.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
11G10, 11G05, 11G50, 14C17

1.
Introduction

Let
\lambda:\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
be the Legendre elliptic scheme,
where for every
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
the fiber
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{0}}
is an elliptic curve defined by

(1.1)

Y^{2}=X(X-1)(X-\lambda_{0}).

Masser and Zannier
[
MZ08
,
MZ10
]
showed that there are at most finitely
many complex numbers
\lambda_{0}\neq 0,1
such that the two points

\displaystyle(2,\sqrt{2(2-\lambda_{0})}),\quad

\displaystyle(3,\sqrt{6(3-\lambda_{0})})

both have finite order on the elliptic curve
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{0}}
.
Later, the same authors proved in
[
MZ12
]
that one can replace 2 and 3 by any two distinct complex numbers
(\neq 0,1)
or even choose
distinct
X
-coordinates
(\neq\lambda)
defined over an algebraic closure of
\mathbb{C}(\lambda)
, provided that the corresponding points
P_{1},P_{2}
are not
identically linearly related, i.e. there are no integers
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
not both zero such that
a_{1}P_{1}+a_{2}P_{2}=O
identically on
\mathcal{C}
.
This was further generalized by Barroero and Capuano
[
BC16
]
, who proved
that, taking an arbitrary number of points defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)}
such that they are not
identically linearly related,
the set of complex numbers
\lambda_{0}
such that they satisfy two independent linear condition with integer coefficient is again finite.

The same authors further generalized this result to
product of powers of two non-isogenous elliptic schemes
[
BC17
]
and then to
general abelian schemes
[
BC20
]
, also generalizing a result in
[
MZ20
]
, where the authors consider only the intersection with the torsion;
the main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
BC20
]
, Thm 1.1)
.

Let
S
be a smooth irreducible curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Consider
\mathcal{A}\to S
an abelian scheme and
\mathcal{C}
an
irreducible curve in
\mathcal{A}
not contained in a proper subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
, even after a finite base change.
Let

\mathcal{C}^{\{2\}}=\bigcup_{\overset{G\text{ flat}}{\text{codim }G\geq 2}}\mathcal{C}\cap G,

where
G
runs through all the
flat
subgroup schemes of codimension at least 2, that is, all the subgroup schemes of codimension at least 2 whose irreducible components dominate the base
S
;
then,
\mathcal{C}^{\{2\}}
is a finite set.

All these results fit in the framework of very general conjectures, formulated independently by Zilber
[
Zil02
]
and Bombieri, Masser and Zannier
[
BMZ99
]
in the toric case
and by Pink
[
Pin05
]
in the more general setting of mixed Shimura varieties, including classical conjectures such as Manin-Mumford, André-Oort, known as the Zilber–Pink conjectures.
Shimura varieties are a class of algebraic varieties
including abelian varieties, families of abelian varieties, modular curves and moduli spaces of abelian varieties.
Their structure allows us to define a certain (countable) subset of their subvarieties, called “special subvarieties”.
A general description is quite complicated in general, and can be found in
[
Del71
]
.
In the case of tori and abelian varieties, the special subvarieties are precisely the irreducible components of algebraic subgroups.
For families of abelian varieties, the situation is more complicated.
Flat subgroup schemes, that is, the subgroup schemes that are dominant on the base, are special subvarieties, and actually they are the only ones that are dominant on the base.
However, there are in general also special subvarieties that are not dominant.
For example, in the case of non-isotrivial elliptic schemes, they are the subgroups of the CM fibers.

Zilber-Pink conjectures involve the intersection of a fixed subvariety
Z
of a Shimura variety
\mathcal{X}
with the special subvarieties of
\mathcal{X}
of codimension bigger than the dimension of
Z
.
The prediction is that, under some natural geometric conditions, such intersection is not Zariski dense in
Z
.

The precise statement for curves in an abelian scheme is the following.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Let
S
be a smooth irreducible curve defined over
\mathbb{C}
.
Consider
\mathcal{A}\to S
an abelian scheme and
\mathcal{C}
an
irreducible curve in
\mathcal{A}
, defined over
\mathbb{C}
and not contained in a proper special subvariety of
\mathcal{A}
.
Consider the set

\mathcal{C}^{[2]}=\bigcup_{\overset{H\subseteq\mathcal{A}}{\text{codim }H\geq 2}}\mathcal{C}\cap H,

where
H
runs through all the special subvarieties of
\mathcal{A}
of codimension at least 2.
Then
\mathcal{C}^{[2]}
is a finite set.

This conjecture is known to hold when
\mathcal{A}
is a fibered power of an elliptic scheme
\mathcal{E}
, by work of Masser and Zannier
[
MZ12
]
, Barroero and Capuano
[
BC16
]
, Barroero
[
Bar17
]
and Barroero and Dill
[
BD25
]
, and in some cases of product of (fibered) powers of two non-isogenous elliptic schemes defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, due to Masser and Zannier
[
MZ14
]
, Barroero and Capuano
[
BC17
]
and Ferrigno
[
Fer24
]
. The general case, however, is still open.

More details on the Zilber-Pink conjectures on families of abelian varieties may be found in a survey by Capuano
[
Cap23
]
, and a more in-depth treatment of the topic can be found in books by Zannier
[
Zan12
]
and Pila
[
Pil22
]
.

The geometric idea behind these results is that the intersections we are considering are, for dimensional reasons, expected to be empty.
When this is not the case, we say that we have an “unlikely intersection”.
The statement of the conjecture can be interpreted as saying that this is indeed a rare phenomenon.

On the other hand, if in the same setting as above one intersects the curve
\mathcal{C}
with codimension 1 subgroups, then the union of such intersections will be infinite in general.
However, if we restrict ourselves to consider only the points of singular intersection,
\mathcal{C}\cap_{sing}G
, that is the points of
\mathcal{C}\cap G
where the multiplicity of intersection between
\mathcal{C}
and a codimension 1 subgroup
G
is strictly greater then 1, then we may hope to gain back finiteness.
This is because the intersection of a curve with another subvariety of codimension 1 is in general expected to be transverse, that is, not singular.
Geometrically, this corresponds to considering the points where for some
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
in the generic endomorphism ring, the section

a_{1}P_{1}+\dots+a_{n}P_{n}

intersects the zero section
tangentially
.

This kind of question has already been investigated, and answered positively, in some cases.
More specifically, the case of curves which are images of sections intersecting tangentially the torsion sections in a single copy of the Legendre elliptic scheme, was treated independently by works of Corvaja, Demejo, Masser and Zannier
[
CDMZ21
]
and Ulmer and Urzúa
[
UU20
,
UU21
]
.
In a different setting, namely for curves in
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
, the question was studied by Marché and Maurin
[
MM23
]
.

In this paper we extend such result to curves in more general one dimensional families
\mathcal{A}\to S
of abelian varieties.
To obtain a result analogue to the Zilber-Pink conjectures we want to prove the finiteness of

\mathcal{C}^{[1,sing]}=\bigcup_{\overset{H\text{ special}}{\text{codim }H\geq 1}}\mathcal{C}\cap_{sing}H,

where
H
runs through the special subvarieties of
\mathcal{A}
, for curves
\mathcal{C}
that are not contained in any proper special subvariety.
Since by assumption
S
is a curve, the special subvarieties of
\mathcal{A}
come in two types: they are either dominant on
S
, in which case they are irreducible components of flat subgroup schemes, or they are contained in some special fibers.
The finiteness of the singular intersections with special fibers is easier to prove, as it is trivially implied by the following statement.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
S
be a smooth irreducible curve defined over
\mathbb{C}
.
Consider
\mathcal{A}\to S
be an abelian scheme and let
\mathcal{C}
be an
irreducible curve in
\mathcal{A}
, not contained in any fiber.
Let

\mathcal{C}^{\{1,ram\}}=\bigcup_{s\in S}\mathcal{C}\cap_{sing}\mathcal{A}_{s}.

Then,
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,ram\}}
is a finite set.

The proof of this result is the content of Section
2
.
The rest of this paper is then dedicated to prove the finiteness of the singular intersections with flat subgroups, with some requirements on the abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
S
be a smooth irreducible curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Let us consider
\mathcal{A}\to S
an abelian scheme
that has no isotrivial elliptic component, that is we cannot write
\mathcal{A}=E\times\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
where
E
is a constant elliptic curve, even after base change.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
irreducible curve in
\mathcal{A}
, dominant over
S
and not contained in a proper subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
, even after a finite base change.
Let

\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}=\bigcup_{\overset{G\text{ flat}}{\text{codim }G\geq 1}}\mathcal{C}\cap_{sing}G,

where
G
runs through all the flat subgroup schemes of codimension at least 1.

Then,
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
is a finite set.

We remark that the statement is expected to be true also without the condition of
\mathcal{A}
not having isotrivial elliptic component, although our techniques require to exclude that case.
Work is in progress with Ballini and Capuano to prove the same finiteness result for tori and and product of (constant) elliptic curves. Such a result, combined with the rest of the proof of Theorem
1.4
, would allow us to drop such assumption.

To prove Theorem
1.4
,
similarly to
[
BC20
]
, we start by showing that we can substitute
\mathcal{A}
with an isogenous abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
Therefore we can assume, up to isogeny, that
\mathcal{A}
is the product
\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}^{n_{i}}
of fibered powers of pairwise non-isogenous simple abelian schemes.
The finiteness of the intersection of
\mathcal{C}
with the flat subgroups of codimension
\geq 2
is proven in
[
BC20
]
, so we can restrict ourselves to consider only codimension 1 subgroups.
Those will all be of the shape

H=\mathcal{A}_{1}^{n_{1}}\times_{S}\dots\times_{S}\mathcal{A}_{i-1}^{n_{i-1}}\times_{S}H_{i}\times_{S}\mathcal{A}_{i+1}^{n_{i+1}}\dots\times_{S}\mathcal{A}_{r}^{n_{r}},

where
H_{i}<\mathcal{A}_{i}
is a codimension 1 subgroup of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is a power of an elliptic scheme.
Therefore, it is enough to consider the case where
\mathcal{A}
is itself a power of an elliptic scheme.
By assumption,
\mathcal{A}
will be non-isotrivial, and adapting some work of Habegger
[
Hab13
]
we can reduce to the case where
\mathcal{A}
is a power of the Legendre scheme.
The full reduction is performed in the third section.

We are then left to prove the following statement.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\lambda:\mathcal{L}\to S=\mathbb{P}\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}
be the Legendre elliptic scheme with fibers
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
, and let
\mathcal{L}^{n}
be its
n
-th fibered power, with coordinates
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
.
For
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
let
\varphi_{\bm{a}}:\mathcal{L}^{n}\to\mathcal{L}
be the fiberwise linear map given by

\varphi_{\bm{a}}(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})=a_{1}P_{1}+\dots+a_{n}P_{n}.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}
be an irreducible curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and not contained in a fiber over
S
.
Assume that, for
every
j=1,\dots,n
,
there is no

\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that

(1.2)

\varphi_{\bm{a}}=O\circ\lambda,

identically on
\mathcal{C}
, where
O
is the zero section of
\mathcal{L}
.
Then, there are at most finitely many
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}
such that there exists
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
with

(1.3)

\varphi_{\bm{a}}(\bm{c})=O(\lambda(\bm{c}))

and such that
\mathcal{C}
and
\ker(\varphi_{\bm{a}})
intersect tangentially at
\bm{c}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
follows Pila-Zannier method, a strategy introduced by Pila and Zannier in

[
PZ08
]

to give an alternative proof of the Manin-Mumford conjecture (already proved by Raynaud in
[
Ray83
]
),
and used in several works in the field.
To prove finiteness, we prove that
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
is a set of bounded height and degree, and then conclude by Northcott property.
In particular, the height bound comes from Silverman Specialization Theorem
[
Sil83
]
, while for the degree we compare two estimates for the growth of
the size of the set of singular intersections of
\mathcal{C}
with subgroups defined by linear conditions with bounded coefficients.
To get an upper bound, we consider the
elliptic logarithms
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
of
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
restricted to the curve
\mathcal{C}
, their derivatives with respect to
\lambda
and the equations

(1.4)

\begin{cases}z_{j}=u_{j}f+v_{j}g,\\
\frac{\partial z_{j}}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial\lambda}f+u_{j}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}+\frac{\partial v_{j}}{\partial\lambda}g+v_{j}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda},\end{cases}

for
j=1,\dots,n
, where
f
and
g
are suitably chosen basis elements of the period
lattice of
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
. If we consider the coefficients
u_{j},v_{j},\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial\lambda},\frac{\partial v_{j}}{\partial\lambda}
as functions of
\lambda
and restrict
them to a compact set, we obtain a subanalytic surface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{4n}
.
The points where
\mathcal{C}
has singular intersection with a flat subgroup scheme give rise to two linear relations with integer coefficients, one for the
z_{j}
s and another one (actually the same) for the derivatives.
Consequently, the points will correspond to points
of
\Sigma
lying on linear varieties defined by equations whose coefficients are the same as those of the linear (on the elliptic curve) relations that the points satisfy.
Combining an o-minimality result by Habegger and Pila
[
HP16
]
with transcendence of the elliptic logarithms and their derivatives proven by Bertrand
[
Ber90
]
, we get that the number of points lying on
\Sigma
and these linear varieties with coefficients bounded by
T
grows slower than any power of
T
.

On the other side, if a point of degree
d
lies in
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
, it brings all of its Galois conjugates, and we can prove that all of them need to satisfy a linear equation with coefficients bounded by a constant times a certain power of
d
. Combining this with the upper bound forces the degree to be bounded, concluding the argument.

We conclude this introduction with a final remark on Theorem
1.4
.
In a similar way to
[
MM23
]
, the result can be reformulated in an unlikely intersections statement.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be the dual projectivized tangent bundle of
\mathcal{A}
, that is
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{P}^{*}(T\mathcal{A})
.
This comes with a natural map
\tau
towards
\mathcal{A}
whose fibers parametrize lines in the corresponding tangent space.
We will therefore denote by
(\bm{p},[\Delta])
the point of
\mathcal{X}_{\bm{p}}
corresponding to the line
\Delta\subseteq T_{\bm{p}}\mathcal{A}
.
The restriction to the smooth locus of the inclusion
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
lifts to
\tilde{\iota}:\mathcal{C}^{sm}\to\mathcal{X}
in the following way:
for
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{sm}
we have by definition that
T_{\bm{c}}\mathcal{C}\subseteq T_{\bm{c}}\mathcal{A}
is a line.
Therefore we define
\tilde{\iota}(\bm{c})=(\bm{c},[T_{\bm{c}}\mathcal{C}])
, and consider
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
as the closure of the image of
\mathcal{C}^{sm}
.
In a similar way, for
H<\mathcal{A}
a (flat) subgroup scheme we can define
\tilde{H}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
as

\tilde{H}=\{(\bm{p},[\Delta])\st\bm{p}\in H,\Delta\subseteq T_{\bm{p}}H\}.

By this construction, we get that
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
is again a curve, while if
H
has codimension 1 in
\mathcal{A}
, then
\tilde{H}
has codimension 2 in
\mathcal{X}
.
Theorem
1.4
can be restated in this language as an unlikely intersections statement.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\to S
be an abelian scheme with no isotrivial elliptic component, that is we cannot write
\mathcal{A}=E\times\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
where
E
is a constant elliptic curve, even after base change.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be an irreducible curve in
\mathcal{A}
, dominant over
S
and not contained in a proper subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
, even after a finite base change, and let
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
be as above.
Let

\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{[2]}=\bigcup_{\overset{H\text{ flat}}{\text{codim }H\geq 1}}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\cap\tilde{H},

where
H
runs through all the flat subgroup schemes of codimension at least 1, and
\tilde{H}
is constructed from
H
as above.
Then,
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{[2]}
is a finite set.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We recall the setting.
Let
S
be a smooth irreducible curve defined over
\mathbb{C}
;
consider
\pi:\mathcal{A}\to S
an abelian scheme of relative dimension
g
and
\mathcal{C}
an
irreducible curve in
\mathcal{A}
, not contained in any fiber.
Recall that we defined

\mathcal{C}^{\{1,ram\}}=\bigcup_{s\in S}\mathcal{C}\cap_{sing}\mathcal{A}_{s}.

We want to prove that
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,ram\}}
is a finite set.
Up to removing finitely many points on
S
and
\mathcal{C}
we may assume (and we will) that
\mathcal{C}
is a smooth curve.

We claim that
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,ram\}}
is
the ramification locus of
\pi\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$|$}_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{C}\to S
.
If this is the case, then the finiteness follows from the finiteness of the ramification locus,
[
Har77
, Prop. IV.2.2]
.
Notice that
\pi\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$|$}_{\mathcal{C}}
is a finite, separable and surjective map between algebraic curves, so by the same proposition the ramification locus is well defined, and it is the support of the relative differential sheaf
\Omega_{\mathcal{C}/S}
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be the sheaf of ideals associated with
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathcal{A}
.
The immersion
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
induces, by
[
Har77
, Prop. II.8.12]
, an exact sequence of sheaves

\frac{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{I}^{2}}\xrightarrow{d_{\mathcal{C}}}\left.\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}\right|_{\mathcal{C}}\to\Omega_{\mathcal{C}/S}\to 0.

By Nakayama’s Lemma, the support of
\Omega_{\mathcal{C}/S}
is given by the closed points
p
such that
\Omega_{\mathcal{C}/S}\otimes k(p)\neq\{0\}
, where
k(p)
is the residue field at the point
p
.
Hence, these are the points where the map

d_{\mathcal{C},p}:\frac{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{I}^{2}}\otimes k(p)\to\left.\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}\right|_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes k(p)=\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}\otimes k(p)

is not surjective.
Since
\mathcal{C}
is smooth, the dimension of
\frac{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{I}^{2}}\otimes k(p)
, the conormal space of
\mathcal{C}
, as a vector space over
k(p)
is exactly
\dim(\mathcal{A})-\dim(\mathcal{C})=g
, by
[
Har77
, Thm II.8.17]
.
Moreover, since the
\pi
is smooth, the dimension of the relative cotangent space
\left.\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}\right|_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes k(p)
is also
g
, so
d_{\mathcal{C},p}
is surjective if and only if it is injective.

The map
d_{\mathcal{C}}
is the map induced by
d_{\mathcal{A}/S}:\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{A}}\to\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}
, so it quotients via
d_{\mathfrak{m}_{p}}:\mathfrak{m}_{p}/\mathfrak{m}_{p}^{2}\to\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}
, where
\mathfrak{m}_{p}
is the (maximal) ideal associated with the closed subvariety
p
of
\mathcal{A}
.

Let
s=\pi(p)
. We have, by
[
Har77
, Prop. II.8.11]
, the exact sequence

\pi^{*}\Omega_{s/S}\to\Omega_{p/S}\to\Omega_{p/s}\to 0.

Since
s
is a closed subvariety of
S
, the sheaf
\Omega_{s/S}
is trivial. Moreover,
\Omega_{p/s}
is also trivial, since both
p
and
s
are
\mathbb{C}
-points.
Hence
\Omega_{p/S}
is trivial.
Since
p
is also a closed subvariety of
\mathcal{A}
, we also have the exact sequence

\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{p}}{\mathfrak{m}_{p}^{2}}\xrightarrow{d_{\mathfrak{m}_{p}}}\left.\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}\right|_{p}\to\Omega_{p/S}\to 0.

Since
\Omega_{p/s}
is trivial, tensoring with
k(p)
we have that

d_{\mathfrak{m}_{p},p}:\mathfrak{m}_{p}/\mathfrak{m}_{p}^{2}\otimes k(p)\to\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}\otimes k(p)
is surjective.
Moreover,
\dim_{k(p)}(\mathfrak{m}_{p}/\mathfrak{m}_{p}^{2}\otimes k(p))=\dim\mathcal{A}=g+1
, so the kernel of
d_{\mathfrak{m}_{p},p}
is one-dimensional.
Therefore,
p\in\mathcal{C}
is a ramification point for
\pi\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$|$}_{\mathcal{C}}
if and only if
\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^{2}\otimes k(p)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{p}/\mathfrak{m}_{p}^{2}\otimes k(p)
contains the kernel of
d_{\mathfrak{m}_{p},p}
.

Consider now the fiber
\mathcal{A}_{s}
of
\pi
over
s=\pi(p)
as a closed subvariety of
\mathcal{A}
and let
\mathcal{J}
be the associated sheaf of ideals. We have the exact sequence

\frac{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{J}^{2}}\xrightarrow{d_{s}}\left.\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}\right|_{\mathcal{A}_{s}}\to\Omega_{\mathcal{A}_{s}/S}\to 0.

Again we tensor by
k(p)
, getting

\frac{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{J}^{2}}\otimes k(p)\xrightarrow{d_{s,p}}\left.\Omega_{\mathcal{A}/S}\right|_{\mathcal{A}_{s}}\otimes k(p)\to\Omega_{\mathcal{A}_{s}/S}\otimes k(p)\to 0.

Since
\mathcal{A}_{s}
is a smooth fiber,
\Omega_{\mathcal{A}_{s}/S}\otimes k(p)
has dimension
g
, so by exactness
d_{s,p}
is the trivial map, sending everything to zero.
Moreover, we have again that
d_{s}
quotients through
\mathfrak{m}_{p}/\mathfrak{m}_{p}^{2}
.
We claim that the kernel of
d_{\mathfrak{m}_{p},p}
is exactly
\mathcal{J}/\mathcal{J}^{2}\otimes k(p)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{p}/\mathfrak{m}_{p}^{2}\otimes k(p)
.
This is the case, since
\mathcal{J}/\mathcal{J}^{2}\otimes k(p)
is positively dimensional and contained into the kernel of
d_{\mathfrak{m}_{p},p}
, and we have proven that this kernel is one-dimensional.

Now, recall that
\mathcal{C}
being tangent to
\mathcal{A}_{s}
at
p
is equivalent to the tangent space of
\mathcal{C}
being contained in the tangent space of
\mathcal{A}_{s}
, or, equivalently, that
\mathcal{J}/\mathcal{J}^{2}\otimes k(p)
, the conormal space of
\mathcal{A}_{s}
, is contained in
\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^{2}\otimes k(p)
, the conormal space of
\mathcal{C}
.
But, by the previous argument, this is exactly the condition of
d_{\mathcal{C},p}
not being injective, hence not surjective, and so
p
being a ramification point of
\pi\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$|$}_{\mathcal{C}}
.

∎

3.
Reduction to powers of the Legendre scheme

In this section we will reduce the proof of Theorem
1.4
to the case of powers of the Legendre scheme.
Similarly to
[
BC20
]
, we prove that our problem is invariant under finite base changes and isogenies.
Combining this with Poincaré Reducibility Theorem we reduce to two cases: the first one is the case of subgroups of codimension at least two, which is covered by a theorem of Habegger and Pila
[
HP16
]
in the case of isotrivial components and by the aforementioned result by Barroero and Capuano
[
BC20
]
for the non-isotrivial components.
The second case is that of codimension one subgroups in powers of an elliptic scheme, which is not isotrivial by assumption.

In the second part of this section we further reduce ourselves to consider powers of the Legendre scheme and flat subgroups defined by a single linear condition with integer coefficients.

This section is inspired by the second sections of
[
BC16
]
and
[
BC20
]
, and by
[
Hab13
]
.

For
V,W
subvarieties of a variety
X
, and
P\in V\cap W
we denote by
i(P;V,W;X)
the intersection multiplicity of
V
and
W
in
P
.
The main tool we will use in our reduction steps is the following.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
Now98
]
, p.173)
.

Let
f:X^{\prime}\to X
be a dominant morphism between smooth
varieties defined over an algebraically closed field
k
, let
V^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
be two subvarieties
of
X^{\prime}
that intersect properly at a closed point
P^{\prime}
, and let
V:=f(V^{\prime}),W:=f(W^{\prime})
and
P:=f(P^{\prime})
.
If the morphism
f
is étale at
P^{\prime}
, then

i(P^{\prime};V^{\prime},W^{\prime};X^{\prime})=i(P;V,W;X).

In other words, the intersection multiplicity is invariant under étale morphisms.

This will help us show that our problem is preserved by moving back and forward through étale morphisms.

We also state a corollary, that will be required in Section
9
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
f:X\to X
be an isomorphism between smooth varieties over an algebraically closed field
k
, and let
V
and
W
be two subvarieties of
X
invariant under
f
, that intersect properly at a closed point
P
. Then,

i(P;V,W;X)=i(f(P);V,W;X).

In particular, this holds when
k=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, the varieties
X
,
V
and
W
are defined over a number field and
f
is induced by a Galois action.

We also note that, since we are proving a finiteness result, we are always allowed to replace the base curve by a
non-empty open subset, and we will tacitly do so.
This allows us to pass from an abelian variety
defined over a function field of a curve to the corresponding abelian scheme over (a non-empty open subset of) the curve.

We recall our setting and some definitions. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian scheme over a smooth irreducible curve
S
,
where everything is defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. We call
\pi:\mathcal{A}\to S
the structural morphism.
A subgroup scheme
G
of
\mathcal{A}
is a closed subvariety, possibly reducible, which contains the
image of the zero section
O:S\to\mathcal{A}
, is mapped to itself by the inversion morphism and such that
the image of
G\times_{S}G
under the addition morphism is in
G
. A subgroup scheme
G
is called
flat

if
\pi\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$|$}_{G}:G\to S
is flat. By
[
Har77
, Proposition III.9.7]
, since
S
has dimension 1, this is equivalent to
require that all irreducible components of
G
dominate the base curve
S
.

3.1.
Reduction to powers of simple abelian schemes

We want to show that, in order to prove Theorem
1.4
, we can perform finite base
changes and isogenies.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be as in Theorem
1.4
. Let
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}\times_{S}S^{\prime}
for some finite cover
S^{\prime}\to S
and
let
f
be the projection
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{A}
. Then, if the conclusion of Theorem
1.4
holds for all irreducible
components of
f^{-1}(\mathcal{C})
, it holds for
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

First, we see that
f
is flat because it is a fibered product of two flat morphisms
\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}

and
S^{\prime}\to S
.
Moreover, since
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
have the same dimension, by
[
Har77
, Corollary III.9.6]
,
it follows that
f
is quasi-finite, and finite, since it is also proper.
Up to substituting
S^{\prime}
and
S
with an open dense subset, we can also assume that
S^{\prime}\to S
is unramified, and therefore
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{A}
as well, as it is a fibered product of unramified maps.
Hence, the projection
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{A}
is étale.
By
[
Har77
, Corollary III.9.6]
,
we have that if
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}
is an irreducible variety dominating
S
, as
f
is finite and flat, each
component of
f^{-1}(X)
is a variety of the same dimension dominating
S^{\prime}
.
It is clear now that, if
the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
hold for
\mathcal{C}
, then they must hold for all components of
f^{-1}(\mathcal{C})
.
Finally, the preimage of any point of
\mathcal{C}
lying in a flat subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
of codimension at
least 1 must lie in a flat subgroup scheme of the same codimension, namely the preimage. Moreover, by Proposition
3.1
, the tangency condition is also preserved.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
be abelian schemes over the same irreducible curve
S
, let
\eta
be the generic point of
S
, and let
f_{\eta}:\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\eta}\to\mathcal{A}_{\eta}

be an isogeny between the generic fibers defined over
k(S)
. Moreover, let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be a curve
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
. Then, if the claim of Theorem
1.4
holds for all
irreducible components of
f^{-1}(\mathcal{C})
, it holds for
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

By the proof of
[
BC20
, Lemma 2.2]
, we have that
f_{\eta}
extends to a map
f:\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{A}
that is finite, flat, and is an isogeny on every fiber.
So, to conclude in the same way as the previous lemma, we only need to show that
f
is also unramified, and thus étale.

Since
f
is finite and
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are locally noetherian, by
[
Liu02
, Chapter 4, Lemma 3.20]

f
is unramified if and only if, for every
p\in\mathcal{A}
, the fibers
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{p}
are finite.
By the same argument, for any
s\in S
, the map
f_{s}:\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{s}\to\mathcal{A}_{s}
is unramified if and only if for any
p\in\mathcal{A}_{s}
the fiber
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{s})_{p}
is finite.
By properties of the base change,
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{s})_{p}=\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{p}
, so it is enough to show that
f_{s}
is unramified for every
s\in S
.
This follows from
[
Mum88
, Section II.7, Corollary 1 of Thm. 4, p.74]
and the fact that
f
is fiberwise an isogeny.
∎

Now we can proceed as in
[
BC20
]
to show that it is enough to prove Theorem
1.4
for products of simple abelian schemes.
Let us consider the generic fiber
\mathcal{A}_{\eta}
of
\mathcal{A}
as an abelian variety defined over
k(S)
.
Since every abelian variety is isogenous to a product of simple abelian varieties (see
for instance
[
HS00
, Corollary A.5.1.8]
),
there are geometrically simple, pairwise non-isogenous
abelian varieties
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
, such that
\mathcal{A}_{\eta}
is isogenous to
A^{\prime}:=\prod_{i}B_{i}^{n_{i}}
.
Note that the abelian variety
A^{\prime}
and the isogeny might not be defined over
k(S)
, but over a finite extension of it, which is
k(S^{\prime})

for some irreducible, non-singular curve
S^{\prime}
covering
S
.
However, by Lemma
3.3
, we can (and do) assume
S^{\prime}=S
.
For the same reason, we can assume that the endomorphism ring of
A^{\prime}
is defined over
k(S)
.
Up to shrinking
S
, the abelian varieties
B_{i}
extend to abelian schemes
\mathcal{B}_{i}\to S
.
We define
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
as
\prod_{i}\mathcal{B}_{i}^{n_{i}}
, where the product and the powers are intended as fibered product and fibered powers over
S
.
Lemma
3.4
then allows us to reduce the proof of Theorem
1.4
to the case of products of simple abelian schemes.

We are now going to describe flat subgroup schemes of
\mathcal{A}
, which is a fibered product
\mathcal{A}_{1}\times_{S}\dots\times_{S}\mathcal{A}_{m}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is the
n_{i}
-th fibered power of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
and
\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{m}
are abelian schemes whose
generic fibers
B_{i}
are pairwise non-isogenous geometrically simple abelian varieties.
Moreover, we
let
R_{i}
be the endomorphism ring of
B_{i}
, which we can suppose to be defined over
k(S)
.

Fix
i_{0}
, with
1\leq i_{0}\leq m
.
For every
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n_{i_{0}}})\in R_{i_{0}}^{n_{i_{0}}}
, we have a morphism
\varphi_{\bm{a}}:\mathcal{A}_{i_{0}}\to\mathcal{B}_{i_{0}}
defined by

\varphi_{\bm{a}}(P_{1},\dots,P_{n_{i_{0}}})=a_{1}P_{1}+\dots+a_{n_{i_{0}}}P_{n_{i_{0}}}.

In a similar way, square matrices of size
n_{i_{0}}
with entries in
R_{i_{0}}
will encode endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}_{i_{0}}
,
and
m
-tuples in
\prod_{i}Mat_{n_{i}}(R_{i})
will encode endomorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
. These endomorphisms form a ring, which we call
R
.

If
\alpha\in R
, the kernel of
\alpha
is the fibered product of
\alpha:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
and the zero section
S\to\mathcal{A}
. We consider it as a closed subscheme of
\mathcal{A}
. It is quite easy to see that
\ker(\alpha)
is a subgroup scheme.

Let
g_{i}
be the relative dimension of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
over
S
.
If
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in R
, we define the rank
r(\alpha)
of
\alpha
to be

\sum_{i}\rk(\alpha_{i})g_{i}
, where
\rk(\alpha_{i})
is the matrix rank of
\alpha_{i}\in Mat_{n_{i}}(R_{i})
.
In practice,
\ker\alpha
is a closed subscheme of
\mathcal{A}
obtained by imposing
\rk(\alpha_{i})
independent equations
on each factor
\mathcal{A}_{i}
.

Lemma 3.5

(
[
BC20
]
, Lemma 2.3)
.

Let
G
be a flat subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
of codimension
d
with
1\leq d\leq n
.
Then, there exists an
\alpha\in R
of rank
d
such that
G\subseteq\ker(\alpha)
.
Moreover,
\ker(\alpha)
is a flat subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
of codimension
r(\alpha)
.

From this lemma, we can deduce that each flat subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
is contained in a flat
subgroup scheme of the same dimension and of the form

G=G_{1}\times_{S}\dots\times_{S}G_{m},

where, for every
i=1,\dots,m
,
G_{i}
is a flat subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
.

This implies that any flat subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
of
codimension at least 1 is contained in a
G=G_{1}\times_{S}\dots\times_{S}G_{m}
of codimension at least 1.
Moreover, up to enlarging
G
, and to possibly increasing its dimension, we can assume that all
G_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{i}
except for one index
i_{0}
, and that
G_{i_{0}}
is a maximal flat subgroup of
\mathcal{A}_{i_{0}}
,
In this way,
G_{i_{0}}
, and therefore
G
, has codimension exactly
g_{i_{0}}
.
By projecting on the factors, we only need to prove Theorem
1.4
in the case in which
m=1
, that is,
\mathcal{A}
is simple.

Since by assumption our abelian scheme does not contain any isotrivial elliptic factor, the case
\mathcal{A}
simple can be further divided in these three cases:

(1)

g_{1}\geq 2
and
\mathcal{A}
is isotrivial;

(2)

g_{1}\geq 2
and
\mathcal{A}
is not isotrivial;

(3)

g_{1}=1
and
\mathcal{A}
is not isotrivial.

In the first two cases,
G
needs to have codimension
\geq 2
, so the claim follows from
[
HP16
]
in the isotrivial case and from
[
BC20
]
in the non isotrivial one.
Therefore, we are only left to prove the third case.

We point out that, in the case that
\mathcal{A}
is an isotrivial family of elliptic curves, we still believe the conclusion to be true, although our method does not apply directly.
If we are intersecting with
G
of codimension
\geq 2
, this follows from
[
HP16
]
and from previous works of Rémond and Viada
[
RV03
]
, Viada
[
Via08
]
and Galateau
[
Gal10
]
.
The case of
G
of codimension 1, however, is still open, and is part of a work in progress, using different methods, with Ballini and Capuano.

3.2.
Reduction to the Legendre scheme

In the last subsection we showed that, to prove Theorem
1.4
, it is enough to prove the following special case:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
S
be an irreducible and smooth curve and let
\mathcal{E}\to S
be a non isotrivial elliptic scheme over
S
both defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{E}^{n}
be the
n
-th fibered power of
\mathcal{E}
over
S
,
and let
\mathcal{C}
be an irreducible curve in
\mathcal{A}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and not contained in any proper subgroup schemes of
\mathcal{A}
.
Let

\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}=\bigcup_{\overset{G<\mathcal{A}\text{ flat}}{\text{codim }G\geq 1}}\mathcal{C}\cap_{sing}G.

Then,
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
is a finite set.

We will show that this is implied by Theorem
1.5
.
To do so, we will need to use some results from a work of Habegger
[
Hab13
]
.

Consider the Legendre family defined by

Y^{2}=X(X-1)(X-\lambda).

This gives an example of a non isotrivial
elliptic scheme, which we call the Legendre elliptic scheme and denote by
\mathcal{L}
, over the modular curve
Y(2)=\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}
.
We write
\mathcal{A}_{L}
for the
n
-fold fibered power of
\mathcal{L}
and
\pi_{L}
for the structural morphism
\pi_{L}:\mathcal{A}_{L}\to Y(2)
.

Lemma 3.7

(
[
Hab13
, Lemma 5.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be as above. After possibly replacing
S
by a Zariski open, nonempty subset, there exists an irreducible, non-singular,
curve
S^{\prime}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
such that we have a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}_{L}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{Y(2)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces l}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{L}}

where

is finite,
\lambda
is quasi-finite,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is the abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}\times_{S}S^{\prime}
,

is finite and
flat and

is quasi-finite and flat. Moreover, the restriction of

and

to any fiber of

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
is an isomorphism of abelian varieties.

The following lemma, already stated in
[

]
, follows from the first part of Lemma 5.5 of
[

]
.

Lemma 3.8

(
[
BC16
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

If
G
is a flat subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
, then
e(f^{-1}(G))
is a flat subgroup
scheme of
\mathcal{A}_{L}
of the same dimension. Moreover, let
X
be a subvariety of
\mathcal{A}

dominating
S
and not contained in a proper flat subgroup scheme of
\mathcal{A}
, let
X^{\prime\prime}
be an
irreducible component of

and let
X^{\prime}
be the Zariski closure of

in
\mathcal{A}_{L}
.
Then,

has the same dimension as

, dominates

and is not contained in a

\mathcal{A}_{L}
.

Using these two lemmas we can now prove how Theorem
3.6
follows from Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
assuming Theorem
1.5
.

First, note that, since
\mathcal{C}
is not contained in any proper subgroup scheme, it is in particular not contained in any flat subgroup scheme. Moreover, it is also not contained in any fiber, as each fiber with the zero section forms a subgroup scheme.

To ease the notation, we also extend the definition of
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
to non-irreducible curves as follows:

\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}:=\bigcup_{\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\text{ irred}}\bigcup_{\overset{G\text{ flat}}{\text{codim }G\geq 1}}\mathcal{D}\cap_{sing}G

where
\mathcal{D}
runs through the irreducible components of
\mathcal{C}
.

Keeping the notation from Lemma
3.7
, since

is quasi-finite, if
e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}))
is finite, then
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
is finite.
By Lemma
3.8
and by Proposition
3.1
, we have that

\mathcal{Z}:=e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}))\setminus e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}))^{\{1,sing\}}

is contained in the fibers over the branch locus of
\lambda
and over the image by
\lambda
of the ramification locus of

.
Hence,
\mathcal{Z}
is contained in the intersection between the dominant curve
e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}))
and a finite number of fibers of
\pi_{L}
.
Then, it is a finite set, as by Lemma
3.8
no irreducible component of
e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}))
can be contained in a fiber of
\pi_{L}
.

It is then enough to prove that
e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}))^{\{1,sing\}}
is a finite set, since
e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}))\subseteq\mathcal{Z}\cup e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}))^{\{1,sing\}}
.

Let us consider the Zariski closure
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
of

for an irreducible component
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
of

.
Then,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is irreducible, and by Lemma
3.8
,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is a curve in
\mathcal{A}_{L}
dominating

and not contained in a proper flat subgroup scheme.
Moreover,
e(f^{-1}(\mathcal{C}))
is a finite union of such
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

Proving that
(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})^{\{1,sing\}}

is finite will make us conclude the proof.
By Lemma

, each flat subgroup scheme of codimension at least 1 of

is
contained in

for some

\bigcup\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap_{sing}\ker(\varphi_{\bm{a}})
is finite, where the union is taken over all
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. The claim now follows by applying Theorem

since
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is not contained in a proper flat subgroup scheme.
∎

4.
O-minimality and point counting

In the proof of Theorem
1.5
we will use some results on point counting due to Habegger and Pila
[
HP16
]
. In order to state the result, we need to first introduce some general properties of o-minimal structures.
For a more in-depth treatment we refer to
[
Dri98
]
and
[
DM96
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A structure is a sequence
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{S}_{N}),N\geq 1
, where each
\mathcal{S}_{N}
is a collection of
subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that, for each
N,M\geq 1
:

(1)

\mathcal{S}_{N}
is a boolean algebra (under the usual set-theoretic operations);

(2)

\mathcal{S}_{N}
contains every semi-algebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
;

(3)

if
A\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
and
B\in\mathcal{S}_{M}
, then
A\times B\in\mathcal{S}_{N+M}
;

(4)

if
A\in\mathcal{S}_{N+M}
, then
\pi(A)\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
, where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{N+M}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the projection onto the first
N
coordinates.

If
\mathcal{S}
is a structure and, in addition,

(5)

\mathcal{S}_{1}
consists of all finite union of open intervals and points,

then
\mathcal{S}
is called an
o-minimal structure
.

Given a structure
\mathcal{S}
, we say that
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a
definable set
if
S\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{M+N}
. For
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we set
U_{t_{0}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\st(t_{0},x)\in U\}
and call
U
a
family of subsets

of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, while
U_{t_{0}}
is called the
fiber
of
U
above
t_{0}
. If
U
is a definable set, then we call it a
definable
family
, and one can see that the fibers
U_{t_{0}}
are definable sets too. Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
f:S\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
be a
function. We call
f
a
definable function
if its graph
\{(x,y)\in S\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\st y=f(x)\}
is a definable
set. It is not hard to see that images and preimages of definable sets via definable functions are still
definable.
We recall some other properties of definable functions; for a reference see for example
[
DM96
, B.7]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a definable function in a structure
\mathcal{S}
. Then:

(1)

the set
\{x\in U\st f\text{ is continuous at }x\}
is definable in
\mathcal{S}
;

(2)

the set
U^{\prime}:=\{x\in U^{\circ}\st f\text{ is differentiable at }x\}
is definable in
\mathcal{S}
;

(3)

the derivative
Df:U^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is definable in
\mathcal{S}
;

(4)

the set of all
x\in U^{\prime}
such that
f
is
C^{1}
on an open neighborhood of
x
contained in
U
is definable in
\mathcal{S}
;

(5)

if
f:U\to R^{m}
is
C^{1}
, then the set
\{x\in U\st\rk\left(f(x)\right)=i\}
is definable in
\mathcal{S}
for every
i=1,\dots,m
.

There are many examples of o-minimal structures, see
[
Dri98
]
. In this article, we are mainly interested
in the structure of globally subanalytic sets, usually denoted by
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{an}}
, and the one obtained adjoining also the graph of the real exponential function, usually denoted by
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{an},\mathrm{exp}}
.
The o-minimality of this last structure was proved by van
den Dries and Miller
[
DM94
]
.

5.1.
A functional transcendence result

Keeping the same notation as the previous subsection, we prove the following transcendence result, that will be needed later.

Lemma 5.1
.

The functions
z_{1},\dots,z_{n},\frac{\partial z_{1}}{\partial\lambda},\dots,\frac{\partial z_{n}}{\partial\lambda}
are algebraically independent over

\mathbb{C}\left(\lambda,f,g,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda},\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}\right)
on
N_{\ast}
.

Proof.

The functions
z_{1},\dots,z_{n},f,g
are analytic functions of
\lambda
, linearly
independent over
\mathbb{Z}
. Indeed, a relation
a_{1}z_{1}+\dots+a_{n}z_{n}=a_{n+1}f+a_{n+2}g
, with integer
coefficients, would map via
\exp_{\lambda}
to a relation of the form (
1.3
) on
N_{\ast}
,
and therefore
on all of
\mathcal{C}
, which cannot hold by the hypothesis of the theorem.
Moreover, if
\wp_{\lambda}
is
the Weierstrass
\wp
-function associated with
\Pi_{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}}=f(\lambda)\mathbb{Z}+g(\lambda)\mathbb{Z}
, the
\wp_{\lambda}(z_{i})
are algebraic functions of

\lambda
because
\wp_{\lambda}(z_{j})=x_{j}-\frac{1}{3}(\lambda+1)
(see
[
MZ10
, (3.8), p. 1683]
).
Therefore, applying
[
Ber90
, Théorème 5, p. 136]
we have that
z_{1},\dots,z_{n},\frac{\partial z_{1}}{\partial\lambda},\dots,\frac{\partial z_{n}}{\partial\lambda}
are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}\left(\lambda,f,g,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}\right)
.

To conclude we will prove that
\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}\left(\lambda,f,g,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}\right)
, and so actually
\mathbb{C}\left(\lambda,f,g,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}\right)=\mathbb{C}\left(\lambda,f,g,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda},\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}\right)
. This part of the proof was suggested to us by Daniel Bertrand.

Since
f
and
g
are hypergeometric series,
they satisfy the hypergeometric differential equation (see
[
Hus87
, p. 182]
):

\lambda(1-\lambda)\frac{d^{2}\omega}{d\lambda^{2}}+(1-2\lambda)\frac{d\omega}{d\lambda}-\frac{1}{4}\omega=0.

Since they are linearly independent, they form a basis of the space of solution of such a differential equation.
We can therefore consider the Wronskian associated with them:

W(\lambda):=f\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}-g\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}.

By explicit computation, we get that

\frac{dW}{d\lambda}=-\frac{2\lambda-1}{\lambda(\lambda-1)}W;

solving this differential equation gives
W=k(\lambda^{2}-\lambda)^{-1}
for some
k\in\mathbb{C}
, hence
W\in\mathbb{C}(\lambda)
.
We conclude that

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}=\left(W+g\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}\right)\frac{1}{f}\in\mathbb{C}\left(\lambda,f,g,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}\right),

proving the claim.
∎

on

along the path.

To continue

from a point

to a

But we saw that

7.
Small generators of the relations lattices

In this section we recall general facts about linear relations on elliptic curves.

For a point
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{N}
, the absolute logarithmic Weil height
h(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})
is defined by

h(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})=\frac{1}{[\mathbb{Q}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}):\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v}\log\max\left\{1,\left\lvert\xi_{1}\right\rvert_{v},\dots,\left\lvert\xi_{N}\right\rvert_{v}\right\},

where
v
runs over a suitably normalized set of valuations of
\mathbb{Q}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})
.

We recall our running setting. We have an irreducible curve
\mathcal{C}
embedded in the
n
th fibered power of the Legendre elliptic scheme
\mathcal{L}^{n}\to Y(2)
, not contained in a fiber or proper flat subgroup of
\mathcal{L}^{n}
.
We considered the subset
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
, obtained by removing from
\mathcal{C}
finitely many points, and showed that for any point
\bm{c}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
, we can define generators of the period lattice

and

\bm{c}
.

We define, for

\displaystyle L(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\st a_{1}P_{1}(\bm{c})+\dots+a_{n}P_{n}(\bm{c})=O(\lambda(\bm{c}))\in\mathcal{L}_{\lambda(\bm{c})}\}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\st\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}z_{i}(\lambda(\bm{c}))=a_{n+1}f(\lambda(\bm{c}))+a_{n+2}g(\lambda(\bm{c}))\text{ for some }a_{n+1},a_{n+2}\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

This is a sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}

is contained in a proper subgroup of

We can also define the lattice of

relations:

\displaystyle L^{sing}(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\st a_{1}P_{1}(\bm{c})+\dots+a_{n}P_{n}(\bm{c})=O(\lambda(\bm{c}))\text{ tangentially}\}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\left\{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\;\middle|\;\exists a_{n+1},a_{n+2}\in\mathbb{Z}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}z_{i}(\lambda(\bm{c}))=a_{n+1}f(\lambda(\bm{c}))+a_{n+2}g(\lambda(\bm{c})),\right.\\
\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial z_{i}}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda(\bm{c}))=a_{n+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda(\bm{c}))+a_{n+2}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda(\bm{c}))\right\}.\end{multlined}

Suppose now that
\bm{c}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is one of the points considered in Theorem
1.5
, i.e. there exits
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
with

\varphi_{\bm{a}}(\bm{c})=O(\lambda(\bm{c}))

and
\mathcal{C}
and
\ker(\varphi_{\bm{a}})
intersect tangentially at
\bm{c}
.

In this situation we have that the ranks of
L^{sing}(\bm{c})
and
L(\bm{c})
are both positive, and
\lambda(\bm{c})
is an algebraic number, as
\mathcal{C}
is defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and not contained in any proper algebraic subgroup of
\mathcal{L}^{n}
.
Under this condition, we would like to show that
L^{sing}(\bm{c})
has a set of
generators with small norm
\left\lvert\bm{a}\right\rvert=\max\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
Unfortunately we are able to prove such a result only in the case when
\rk(L^{sing}(\bm{c}))=\rk(L(\bm{c}))
, but we will see that this will suffice.

By slight abuse of notation we will omit in the following the dependence on
\bm{c}
, writing
\lambda
for
\lambda(\bm{c})
and
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
for
P_{1}(\bm{c}),\dots,P_{n}(\bm{c})
.

Suppose that
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
are
defined over some finite extension
K
of
\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)
of degree
d=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
.
Furthermore, assume that
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
have
Néron–Tate height
\hat{h}
at most
q\geq 1
(for the definition of Néron–Tate height, see for example
p. 255 of
[
Mas88
]
).
In case the
P_{i}
are all torsion, we have
\hat{h}(P_{i})=0
, and we put
q=1
.

We recall the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1

(
[
BC16
]
, Lemma 6.1, based on
[
Mas88
]
)
.

Under the above hypotheses, there are generators
\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{r}
of
L
with

\left\lvert\bm{a}_{i}\right\rvert\leq\delta_{1}d^{\delta_{2}}(h(\lambda)+1)^{2n}q^{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)},

for some positive constants
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
depending only on
n
.

Before stating and proving the next result we notice that, by definition,
L^{sing}\subseteq L
. In general we do not expect them to be equal.

Lemma 7.2
.

One has that

\mathbb{Q}L^{sing}\cap L=L^{sing};

in particular, if
\rk(L^{sing})=\rk(L)
, then
L^{sing}=L
.

Proof.

Clearly we have
\mathbb{Q}L^{sing}\cap L\supseteq L^{sing}
, so we only have to prove the other inclusion.

Let
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Q}L^{sing}\cap L
. Since
\bm{a}\in L
, there exist some integers
a_{n+1},a_{n+2}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}z_{i}(\lambda)=a_{n+1}f(\lambda)+a_{n+2}g(\lambda)
, which are unique because
f
and
g
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{R}
.

Since
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Q}L^{sing}
, then for some integer
\mu
we have
\mu\bm{a}\in L^{sing}
, hence for some
b_{n+1},b_{n+2}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu a_{i}z_{i}(\lambda)-b_{n+1}f(\lambda)-b_{n+2}g(\lambda)=0\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu a_{i}\frac{\partial z_{i}}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)-b_{n+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)-b_{n+2}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)=0\end{aligned}\right.

We know, moreover, that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu a_{i}z_{i}(\lambda)-\mu a_{i+1}f(\lambda)-\mu a_{i+2}g(\lambda)=0
; since the
b_{i}
’s are unique again by the linear independence of
f
and
g
, we have that
\mu a_{i}=b_{i}
for
i=n+1,n+2
.
Hence we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu a_{i}\frac{\partial z_{i}}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)-\mu a_{n+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)-\mu a_{n+2}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial z_{i}}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)-a_{n+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)-a_{n+2}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)\right)

so

\sum a_{i}\frac{\partial z_{i}}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)-a_{n+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)-a_{n+2}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda)=0,

from which we deduce that actually
\bm{a}\in L^{sing}
, as desired.
∎

Combining the two lemmas we get the following.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
\bm{c},\lambda,q,d
be as in Lemma
7.1
;
assume
\rk(L^{sing}(\bm{c}))=\rk(L(\bm{c}))
.
Then, there are generators
\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{r}
of
L^{sing}(\bm{c})
with

\left\lvert\bm{a}_{i}\right\rvert\leq\delta_{1}d^{\delta_{2}}(h(\lambda)+1)^{2n}q^{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)},

for some positive constants
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
depending only on
n
.

8.
Bounded height

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the set of points
\bm{c}
of

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
considered by Theorem
1.5
, i.e. such that
P_{1}(\bm{c})
,
\dots
,
P_{n}(\bm{c})
satisfy
a linear relation with integer coefficients on the specialized elliptic curve
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda(\bm{c})}
, and fix
\bm{c}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
Since
\mathcal{C}
is defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, the

x_{j}(\bm{c}_{0})
and
\lambda(\bm{c}_{0})
must be algebraic, unless the
P_{j}
are identically linearly dependent on
\mathcal{C}
,
which we excluded by hypothesis.

Then, by Silverman’s specialization Theorem

[
Sil83
]
there exists
\gamma_{1}>0
such that

(8.1)

h(\lambda(\bm{c}_{0}))\leq\gamma_{1}.

This bound has some important consequences that we will see in the rest of this section.

First, we will show a “Large Galois Orbit” type of statement.
Let
k
be a number field on which
\mathcal{C}
is defined.
We can also assume that the finitely
many points we excluded from
\mathcal{C}
to get

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
, which are algebraic, are defined over
k
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.5
we want to get a bound on the degree of points in
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
, using Proposition
6.1
and the fact that every point in
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
has many Galois conjugates also in
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
.
However, Proposition
6.1
gives a bound only in a small neighborhood of a point.
The next lemma shows that a good proportion of Galois conjugates are actually in the same neighborhood.

Let
\delta>0
be a small real number,
and let

\mathcal{C}^{\delta}=\left\{\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}\st\lVert\bm{c}\rVert\leq\frac{1}{\delta},\lVert\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}\rVert\geq\delta\text{ for all }\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\right\},

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the standard norm of
\mathbb{C}^{2n+1}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

There is a positive
\delta
such that there are at least
\frac{1}{2}[k(\bm{c}_{0}):k]
different

k
-embeddings
\sigma
of
k(\bm{c}_{0})
in
\mathbb{C}
such that
\sigma(\bm{c}_{0})
lies in
\mathcal{C}^{\delta}
for all
\bm{c}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

See Lemma 8.2 of
[
MZ14
]
.
∎

Remark
8.2
.

We would like to point out that it would be possible to avoid the restriction to a smaller domain and the use of the previous lemma by either constructing the Betti coordinates
u_{j},v_{j}
in a more global way, following
[
Gao20
]
, or by using the results from Peterzil and Starchenko

]
and Jones and Schmidt

]
, who proved that the Weierstrass

\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{an,exp}}
.

Secondly, we show a bound for the Néron-Tate heights and the degree of the coordinates of the points in

, needed in order to apply Corollary

.

Lemma 8.3

(
[
BC16
, Lemma 7.2]
)
.

There exist positive constants
\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
such that, for every
\bm{c}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and every

\hat{h}(P_{i}(\bm{c}_{0}))\leq\gamma_{2},

Remark
8.4
.

In the assumptions of Lemma 7.2 of
[

]
the authors assume that

9.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

We want to show that there are at most finitely many
\bm{c}
on the curve such that

P_{1}(\bm{c}),\dots,P_{n}(\bm{c})
satisfy a linear relation with integer coefficients on
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda(\bm{c})}
with multiplicity
\geq 2
.
Let
\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}
be the set of such points.
Since
\mathcal{C}\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is finite, as observed in Section
5
, we only need to prove the finiteness of

\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}:=\mathcal{C}^{\{1,sing\}}\cap\widehat{\mathcal{C}}.

In the previous section we showed that for
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
the height of
\lambda(\bm{c})
is bounded by a constant
\gamma_{1}
.
By Northcott’s theorem
[
Nor49
]
we then only need to bound the degree
d
of
\lambda(\bm{c})
over
k
, a number field over which
\mathcal{C}
and the points of
\mathcal{C}\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
are defined.

Fix one
\bm{c}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
and
d_{0}:=[k(\bm{c}_{0}):k]
which we suppose large.
It is enough to prove that
d_{0}
is bounded, as
\lambda(\bm{c}_{0})\in k(\bm{c}_{0})
.
By Theorem 1.1 of
[
BC16
]
, up to removing a finite number of points from
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
we can assume that the
P_{i}(\bm{c}_{0})
’s do not satisfy two linearly independent linear relations, that is,
\rk(L(\bm{c}_{0}))=1
.
Since they satisfy a linear relation with tangency condition, we also have
\rk(L^{sing}(\bm{c}_{0}))=1
.

By Lemma
8.1
, we can
choose
\delta
, independently of
\bm{c}_{0}
, such that
\bm{c}_{0}
has at least
\frac{1}{2}d_{0}
conjugates in
\mathcal{C}^{\delta}
.
Now, since
\mathcal{C}^{\delta}
is compact, there are

\bm{c}_{1},\dots,\bm{c}_{\gamma_{4}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
with corresponding neighborhoods

N_{\bm{c}_{1}},\dots,N_{\bm{c}_{\gamma_{4}}}
and

D_{\bm{c}_{1}},\dots,D_{\bm{c}_{\gamma_{4}}}\subseteq\pi(\widehat{\mathcal{C}})
,
where
D_{\bm{c}_{i}}\subseteq\lambda(N_{\bm{c}_{i}})
contains
\lambda(\bm{c}_{i})
and is analytically isomorphic to a closed disc, and we have that
the
\lambda^{-1}(D_{\bm{c}_{i}})\cap N_{\bm{c}_{i}}
for
i=1,\dots,\gamma_{4}
cover
\mathcal{C}^{\delta}
.

We can assume that
D_{\bm{c}_{1}}
contains
t_{0}^{\sigma}=\lambda(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma})
for at least
\frac{1}{2\gamma_{4}}d_{0}
conjugates
\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma}
.
Since each
t\in\lambda(\mathcal{C})
has a uniformly bounded number of preimages
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}
,
we can suppose that there are at
least
\frac{1}{\gamma_{5}}d_{0}
distinct such
t_{0}^{\sigma}
in
D_{\bm{c}_{1}}
.

Now, the corresponding points
P_{1}(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma}),\dots,P_{n}(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma})
satisfy the same relations.
Moreover, by Corollary
3.2
, the tangency of these relations is also preserved,
so we have that
\rk(L^{sing}(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma}))=\rk(L(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma}))=1
.

By Lemma
8.3
,
\hat{h}(P_{i}(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma}))\leq\gamma_{2}
, and the
P_{i}(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma})
are defined over a field
K
of degree
\leq\gamma_{3}d_{0}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Hence, by applying Corollary
7.3
and recalling (
8.1
), there exist
\bm{a}\in L^{sing}(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma})
such that

(9.1)

\left\lvert\bm{a}\right\rvert\leq\gamma_{7}d_{0}^{\gamma_{8}}.

Recall that in Section
5
we defined on
D_{\bm{c}_{1}}
the functions
f,g
to be generators of the period lattice of
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
, and the
z_{i}
as the elliptic logarithms of the
P_{i}
.
Since
\bm{a}\in L^{sing}(\bm{c}_{0}^{\sigma})
, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} a_{1}z_{1}(t_{0}^{\sigma})+\dots+a_{n}z_{n}(t_{0}^{\sigma})&=a_{n+1}f(t_{0}^{\sigma})+a_{n+2}g(t_{0}^{\sigma}),\\
a_{1}\frac{\partial z_{1}}{\partial\lambda}(t_{0}^{\sigma})+\dots+a_{n}\frac{\partial z_{n}}{\partial\lambda}(t_{0}^{\sigma})&=a_{n+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}(t_{0}^{\sigma})+a_{n+2}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}(t_{0}^{\sigma}).\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Notice also that the same relationships are satisfied by all the other Galois conjugates of
t_{0}^{\sigma}
in
D_{\bm{c}_{1}}
, by Corollary
3.2
.

In Section
6
we defined
D_{\bm{c}_{1}}(\bm{a})
as the set of
t\in D_{\bm{c}_{1}}
such that relations of the form (
6.1
) (same as the above) hold.
In particular, we have that
t_{0}^{\sigma}\in D_{\bm{c}_{1}}(\bm{a})
for all the
t_{0}^{\sigma}
as above.
Therefore,
\left\lvert D_{\bm{c}_{1}}(\bm{a})\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{\gamma_{5}}d_{0}
.

On the other hand, by Proposition
6.1
we have
\left\lvert D_{\bm{c}_{1}}(\bm{a})\right\rvert\leq\gamma_{9}\left\lvert\bm{a}\right\rvert^{\varepsilon}
, so applying (

) we have that

\left\lvert D_{\bm{c}_{1}}(\bm{a})\right\rvert\leq\gamma_{10}d_{0}^{\gamma_{8}\varepsilon}
.
Therefore if we choose

we have a contradiction if

is large enough.

We just deduced that

is bounded and, by (

) and Northcott’s Theorem, we have the claim of Theorem

.

∎

10.
An application to elliptic divisibility sequences

In this section we discuss an application of Theorem
1.5
to geometric elliptic divisibility sequences.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an irreducible curve. A
divisibility sequence
on
\mathcal{C}
is a sequence of divisors
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
\forall m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
m\mid n
then
D_{m}\mid D_{n}
, that is,
D_{n}-D_{m}
is an effective divisor.
This notion is the function field analogue of an integral divisibility sequence.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{C}
be a group scheme with zero-section
O
.
As noted by Silverman in
[
Sil05
]
, every section
P:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{G}
that is not identically torsion, i.e.
nP\neq O
for all
n\geq 1
, has an associated divisibility sequence given by

(10.1)

D_{nP}:=O^{*}(nP),

i.e.,
D_{nP}
is the pull-back along the zero section of the divisor
nP
on
\mathcal{G}
; such divisibility sequences are called
geometric
.

A conjecture of Silverman, stated in
[
Sil05
]
, predicts that, if
\mathcal{G}
has relative dimension at least
2
, and the images of the multiples of
P
are dense in the generic fiber, then

D_{nP}=D_{P}
for infinitely many
n>0
.
This question has been studied by Silverman
[
Sil04
]
and Ghioca, Hsia and Tucker
[
GHT18
]
when
\mathcal{G}
is the product of two elliptic schemes, and by Barroero, Capuano and Turchet
[
BCT24
]
in the case of split semiabelian schemes.
The results proved in
[
GHT18
]
and
[
BCT24
]
are stronger than the one mentioned above: under the conditions predicted by Silverman’s conjecture, the set of
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
D_{nP}=D_{P}
is the complement of a finite union of arithmetic progressions.

Generalizing the setting above, if we have two sections
P
and
Q
of
\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{C}
, we can construct a sequence of divisors by replacing the zero section in (
10.1
) with
Q
, provided that
nP\neq Q
for all
n\geq 1
.
We then get

(10.2)

D_{nP,Q}:=Q^{*}(nP)=\pi_{*}(nP\cap Q),

where, by slight abuse of notation, we are identifying
nP
and
Q
with their image, and the intersection is intended to be the scheme-theoretic intersection as zero-cycles.

These sequences have also been studied by Ghioca, Hsia and Tucker
[
GHT18
]
and Barroero, Capuano and Turchet
[
BCT24
]
,
for
\mathcal{G}
the product of two elliptic schemes and a split semiabelian scheme respectively.
They proved that, under the same conditions on
P
as the above mentioned conjecture of Silverman’s,
D_{nP,Q}
is bounded by a divisor independent of
n
.

If the relative dimension of
\mathcal{G}
is 1, then there is no expectation that
D_{nP}=D_{P}
infinitely often.
However, in this case it is interesting to study when
D_{nP}
is reduced, that is, it is of the form
D_{nP}=\sum_{i}t_{i}
for
t_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
distinct points (equivalently, each non-zero coefficient of
D_{nP}
is equal to 1).
This condition is an analogue for an integer to be square-free.

In
[
UU21
]
, the authors prove that, for
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{E}
an elliptic scheme and
P
non identically torsion,
D_{nP}
is reduced for all
n
outside a finite union of arithmetic progressions.
Using Theorem
1.5
we prove that, under the further assumption that
\mathcal{E}
is not isotrivial, a similar statement holds for
D_{nP,Q}
.
We start by proving a consequence of Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem 10.1
.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{E}\to S
be a non-isotrivial elliptic scheme over a smooth curve
S
, and let
P,Q
be sections
S\to\mathcal{E}
.
If there exist infinitely many
s\in S(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
for which there exists
m_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
m_{s}P(s)=Q(s)
tangentially, then, for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have that
Q=mP
identically on
S
.

Proof.

Consider
(s_{n})_{n}
an infinite sequence of pairwise distinct
s_{n}\in S(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
for which there exists
m_{s_{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(10.3)

m_{s_{n}}P(s_{n})=Q(s_{n})\text{ tangentially}.

We may also assume that
m_{s_{n}}
has the smallest possible absolute value among the integers for which such a relation holds.

If the
m_{s_{n}}
are bounded, then, up to taking a subsequence, we have that for a fixed
m
there exist infinitely many
s\in S(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
where
mP(s)=Q(s)
, from which the claim follows.
We will therefore assume from now on that the
m_{s_{n}}
are unbounded.

From Theorem
1.5
it follows that, for some
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
not both zero,

(10.4)

aP=bQ

identically on
S
.
Combining this with (
10.3
), we find that
P(s_{n})
is torsion for infinitely many
n
, since we have

(10.5)

O(s_{n})=aP(s_{n})-bQ(s_{n})=aP(s_{n})-bm_{n}P(s_{n})=(a-bm_{n})P(s_{n})

and
(a-bm_{n})
is non-zero infinitely often, as
m_{n}
is unbounded.
Moreover, since (
10.3
) is true tangentially and (
10.4
) is true identically, we get that (
10.5
) also holds tangentially.
By
[
CDMZ21
, Proposition 2.4]
(see also
[
UU21
]
),
P
must be identically torsion, and so the same is true for
Q
, by (
10.4
).

∎

Theorem 10.2
.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{C}
be a non-isotrivial elliptic scheme, and let
P,Q:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{E}
be two sections such that no multiple of
P
is identically equal to
Q
.
Let
D_{nP,Q}
be as in (
10.2
).
Then the set

\Xi=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\st D_{nP,Q}\text{ is not reduced}\}

is the union of a finite set and a finite union of arithmetic progressions.

Proof.

For
s\in\mathcal{C}
let
\Xi_{s}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\st 2s\leq D_{nP,Q}\}
, that is, the set of
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
nP
and
Q
intersect tangentially in
s
.
Clearly,
\Xi=\bigcup_{s\in\mathcal{C}}\Xi_{s}
.
Moreover, by Theorem
10.1
, there are only finitely many
s\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\Xi_{s}
is nonempty. It is then enough to show that for each
s\in\mathcal{C}
we have that
\Xi_{s}
is either empty, a singleton, or a finite union of arithmetic progressions.
Suppose that
\Xi_{s}
is nonempty, and let
n_{0}
be its smallest element. For any other
n\in\Xi_{s}
we have that both
n_{0}P
and
nP
intersect
Q
tangentially over
s
. Hence,
(n-n_{0})P
intersects the zero section tangentially over
s
.
By
[
UU21
, Thm 1.4]
, we can deduce that
\Xi_{s}-n_{0}
is a finite union of arithmetic progressions starting from 0, so
\Xi_{s}
itself is a finite union of arithmetic progressions starting from
n_{0}
.
∎

Remark
10.3
.

In a similar way as Theorem
1.4
, we don’t expect the condition of
\mathcal{E}
being non-isotrivial to be necessary.
Indeed, having an isotrivial analogue of Theorem
1.5
would suffice, following the same proof as above.
Such a theorem is part of a work in progress with Ballini and Capuano.

Remark
10.4
.

The result of Theorem
10.2
is much weaker than what Ulmer and Urzúa get in the special case
Q=O
(
[
UU21
, Thm 1.4]
).
There, the authors prove that
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\st D_{nP}\text{ is not reduced}\}
is the finite union of arithmetic progressions
starting from 0
, implying that if
D_{P}
is reduced, then
D_{nP}
is reduced infinitely often.
In our case, the arithmetic progressions may not start from 0, so Theorem
10.2
does not provide an easy method to check wether
D_{nP,Q}
is reduced infinitely often.
