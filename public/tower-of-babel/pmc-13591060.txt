Anti‐Inflammatory Activity of Capparis cartilaginea Decne. Essential Oil and Its Main Constituents via Modulation of the NLRP3 Inflammasome
Authors: Bashaer Alsharif, Nadhim Hante, María Jose Santos‐Martínez, Fabio Boylan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Capparis cartilaginea Decne. (C. cartilaginea) is traditionally used for the management of inflammatory conditions; however, the contribution of its volatile constituents to these effects remains poorly understood. This study evaluated the anti‐inflammatory activity of the essential oil of C. cartilaginea and its major constituents, isopropyl isothiocyanate and 2‐methylbutanenitrile, using an in vitro model of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐induced inflammation in THP‐1 human macrophages. The essential oil and its main constituents significantly reduced cytokine release and nitric oxide (NO) production compared with LPS‐stimulated controls. These effects were associated with modulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and inhibition of caspase‐1 activation, partly mediated through MAPK‐dependent pathways. In addition, treatment significantly inhibited NF‐κB activation and phosphorylation. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the volatile fraction contributes to the anti‐inflammatory activity of C. cartilaginea, providing mechanistic support for its traditional use.

Introduction

Capparis cartilaginea Decne. (C. cartilaginea) belongs to the family Capparaceae, is a medicinal plant distributed across North Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, Western Asia and parts of India and is traditionally used for the treatment of inflammatory conditions, including arthritis and gout [1, 2]. While previous studies have demonstrated the anti‐inflammatory activity of its crude extracts [2, 3, 4], the specific contribution of its volatile constituents remains poorly understood.

The essential oil of C. cartilaginea is characterised by a distinctive chemical profile rich in glucosinolate‐derived compounds, particularly isothiocyanates and nitriles, which are formed through enzymatic hydrolysis of glucosinolates [5, 6]. Isothiocyanates are electrophilic compounds defined by a reactive ─N═C═S functional group, which enables covalent interaction with cellular nucleophiles, particularly cysteine residues in regulatory proteins [7]. This chemical reactivity underlies their ability to modulate redox‐sensitive signalling pathways involved in inflammation [8]. Consistent with this, isothiocyanates have been widely reported to exhibit anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant and chemopreventive activities, often through modulation of key inflammatory signalling pathways [9, 10]. Nitrile compounds, although less extensively studied, have also been identified as bioactive constituents and may contribute to the biological properties of glucosinolate‐containing plants [11]. The presence of these compounds suggests a potential role in the pharmacological activity of the essential oil [6].

One of the key mechanisms underlying inflammatory diseases such as gout and arthritis is the activation of the NLRP3 (nucleotide‐binding oligomerisation domain‐like receptor protein 3) inflammasome [12, 13, 14]. This intracellular multiprotein complex, triggered by stimuli including lipopolysaccharide (LPS), mediates the activation of caspase‐1 and the subsequent maturation and release of interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) and interleukin‐18 (IL‐18), thereby driving inflammatory responses. In parallel, nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) regulates the transcription of pro‐inflammatory genes, including tumour necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and pro‐interleukin‐1β (pro‐IL‐1β) and plays a critical role in inflammasome priming. In addition, mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling pathways, including JNK, ERK and p38, are key regulators of inflammatory responses, modulating NF‐κB activation and cytokine production and contributing to both the priming and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [14, 15, 16].

Given its distinct chemical composition, this study evaluated the anti‐inflammatory activity of C. cartilaginea essential oil and its major constituents, isopropyl isothiocyanate and 2‐methylbutanenitrile, in an LPS‐stimulated THP‐1 macrophage model, focusing on cytokine production, nitric oxide release and modulation of NLRP3 inflammasome and MAPK signalling pathways.

Experimental Section

Materials

LPS (Escherichia coli), phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO), paraformaldehyde, Triton X‐100, bovine serum albumin (BSA) and the Griess reagent were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (USA). Isopropyl isothiocyanate and 2‐methylbutanenitrile used in this study were commercially obtained from Sigma–Aldrich (USA). Cell culture reagents, including RPMI‐1640 medium and foetal bovine serum (FBS), were obtained from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay was used for cell viability assessment. Cytokine levels were quantified using ELISA kits (BioLegend, USA). Antibodies against NLRP3, ASC, caspase‐1, cleaved caspase‐1, IL‐1β and MAPK signalling proteins, as well as appropriate secondary antibodies, were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). PVDF membranes and chemiluminescence detection reagents were used for Western blot analysis. For immunofluorescence analysis, FITC‐conjugated secondary antibodies and 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) were used for nuclear staining.

Plant Material and Essential Oil Extraction

Leaves of C. cartilaginea were collected from the Al‐Taif region, Saudi Arabia, in June 2019. The plant material was authenticated by Prof. Ammar Bader, and a voucher specimen (SA‐UK 2019‐2) was deposited at the Pharmacognosy Laboratory, Umm Al‐Qura University. The collected leaves were air‐dried to obtain a stable weight and finely ground before processing.

The essential oil was obtained from air‐dried leaves of C. cartilaginea by hydrodistillation using a Clevenger‐type apparatus for approximately 3 h. The oil was separated from the aqueous phase, collected and stored in sealed vials at low temperature until use. The yield of the essential oil was approximately 0.2% (w/w). The chemical composition of this oil has been previously reported [6].

Sample Preparation

The essential oil and its major constituents, isopropyl isothiocyanate and 2‐methylbutanenitrile, were dissolved in DMSO and diluted in culture medium. The final concentration of DMSO did not exceed 0.01% (v/v). Based on cytotoxicity results, concentrations of 25 µg/mL (oil) and 25 µM (compounds) were used.

Cell Culture and Differentiation

THP‐1 human acute monocytic leukaemia cells (RRID: CVCL_0006; ATCC TIB‐202) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL and differentiated into macrophage‐like cells by treatment with PMA (60 ng/mL) for 48 h, followed by incubation in PMA‐free medium for 24 h prior to experimentation. For the cell viability, nitric oxide and cytokine secretion assays, a vehicle control group receiving ≤0.01% (v/v) DMSO alongside LPS stimulation was included. At this concentration, DMSO did not exhibit any independent cytotoxic or anti‐inflammatory effects compared with the LPS‐only group.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was evaluated using a colorimetric CCK‐8 assay. Differentiated THP‐1 macrophages were treated with the oil or compounds for 24 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and non‐cytotoxic concentrations were selected for subsequent experiments.

Induction of Inflammation and Cytokine Measurement

Inflammation was induced by stimulating differentiated THP‐1 macrophages with LPS (1 µg/mL). Cells were treated with C. cartilaginea oil or its constituents in the presence of LPS. After incubation for 24 h, culture supernatants were collected and analysed for pro‐inflammatory cytokines, using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of Nitric Oxide Production

Nitric oxide production was assessed by measuring nitrite accumulation in culture supernatants using the Griess reaction. Absorbance was recorded at 550 nm, and nitrite concentrations were calculated using a sodium nitrite standard curve.

NLRP3 Inflammasome Activation and Protein Extraction

THP‐1 macrophages were primed with LPS (1 µg/mL) followed by stimulation with ATP (5 µM) to induce NLRP3 activation. C. cartilaginea essential oil or its constituents were applied under the same conditions. Following stimulation, the cultures were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline and lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were collected by centrifugation and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis.

Immunoblotting (Western Blot Analysis)

Protein expression was analysed by SDS–PAGE and Western blotting. Briefly, equal amounts of protein lysates (20–30 µg) were separated on SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against NLRP3, ASC, caspase‐1, cleaved caspase‐1, IL‐1β, NF‐κB p65, phospho‐NF‐κB p65 and MAPK signalling proteins (JNK, ERK, p38 and their phosphorylated forms).

Following washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1–2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and visualised using an imaging system. Densitometric analysis was performed using Image Lab software (Bio‐Rad), and protein expression levels were normalised to β‐actin as a loading control. For NF‐κB analysis, nuclear protein fractions were prepared as described below.

Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction (NF‐κB Analysis)

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein fractions were isolated using a commercial nuclear extraction kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, treated THP‐1 macrophages were harvested, washed with ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline and lysed to separate cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions. The nuclear extracts were used for the analysis of NF‐κB p65 activation by Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence Analysis

Cells were seeded in appropriate plates and allowed to adhere prior to treatment. Following stimulation, samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, washed with phosphate‐buffered saline and permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 10 min. Non‐specific binding was blocked using 1% BSA for 30 min.

Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies against NLRP3 and ASC overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with FITC‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Leica Microsystems, Germany). Image analysis was performed to assess protein expression and localisation.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments (n = 3). For western blot analysis, protein expression levels were normalised to β‐actin prior to densitometric quantification. No outlier exclusion or additional data transformation was performed before statistical analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.1 (270) (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between groups were evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

The chemical composition of C. cartilaginea essential oil has been previously characterised by GC–MS analysis [6], resulting in the identification of 41 constituents representing 99.99% of the total oil composition. The oil is predominantly composed of glucosinolate‐derived compounds, particularly isothiocyanates (50.0%) and nitriles (47.4%), with only minor contributions from monoterpenes (1.9%) and other compounds (0.7%). The major constituents include isopropyl isothiocyanate (31.0%), 2‐methylbutanenitrile (21.4%), 2‐butyl isothiocyanate (18.1%), isobutyronitrile (15.4%) and 3‐methylbutanenitrile (8.2%). The complete profile of the volatile constituents and their relative abundances is detailed below in Table 1. Based on their abundance and chemical relevance, isopropyl isothiocyanate and 2‐methylbutanenitrile were selected for further biological evaluation, and their chemical structures are presented in Figure 1. While most identified constituents are consistent with previous reports on the genus Capparis, several nitrile compounds, including 2‐methylbutanenitrile, 3‐methylbutanenitrile, isobutyronitrile and 2‐methyl‐3‐butenenitrile, are reported here for the first time in this species.

Effect of C. cartilaginea Essential Oil on Cell Viability

C. cartilaginea essential oil showed a concentration‐dependent reduction in THP‐1 cell viability (Figure 2). Significant cytotoxicity was observed at higher concentrations (100–200 µg/mL), whereas cell viability remained above 80% at concentrations ≤25 µg/mL. A similar trend was observed for isopropyl isothiocyanate and 2‐methylbutanenitrile, which showed pronounced cytotoxic effects at ≥ 100 µM but acceptable viability at lower concentrations. Based on these findings, 25 µg/mL (oil) and 25 µM (compounds) were selected for subsequent experiments.

Statistical analysis was performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post‐test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001 vs. DMSO vehicle.

Effect of C. cartilaginea Essential Oil on Cytokines and NO Production

Macrophages play a central role in inflammation through the production of pro‐inflammatory mediators. Upon stimulation with LPS, THP‐1 macrophages release key cytokines, including IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐1β and IL‐18 and increase NO production, which collectively serve as indicators of inflammatory activation. In Figures 3 and 4, LPS stimulation significantly increased cytokine levels and NO production compared with the control group. Treatment with dexamethasone (50 µM) significantly reduced all measured parameters, confirming the responsiveness of the model. Both C. cartilaginea essential oil (25 µg/mL) and isopropyl isothiocyanate (25 µM) significantly reduced TNF‐α and decreased IL‐6, IL‐1β, IL‐18 and NO levels relative to LPS, indicating a broad inhibitory effect on macrophage activation. These findings are consistent with previous reports on isothiocyanates, which have been shown to suppress pro‐inflammatory cytokine production and nitric oxide release through inhibition of NF‐κB signalling and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression. In particular, compounds such as benzyl isothiocyanate and sulphoraphane have demonstrated similar cytokine‐reducing effects in macrophage models [7]. More recently, allyl isothiocyanate has also been reported to attenuate inflammatory responses and macrophage activation in experimental models, further supporting the anti‐inflammatory potential of naturally occurring isothiocyanates [17]. 2‐Methylbutanenitrile (25 µM) exhibited a more limited effect, with less pronounced reductions, particularly for IL‐1β, IL‐18 and NO. Overall, the essential oil demonstrated a consistent suppression of inflammatory mediators, suggesting that its activity may result from the combined effects of multiple components.

Effect of C. cartilaginea Essential Oil on NLRP3 Inflammasome Signalling Pathway

Building on the observed reduction in pro‐inflammatory cytokines and nitric oxide production, the underlying mechanisms were further investigated. The NLRP3 inflammasome regulates macrophage‐mediated inflammation through caspase‐1 activation and subsequent maturation of IL‐1β and IL‐18. Its activation occurs via a two‐step process: LPS‐mediated priming, which activates NF‐κB and induces NLRP3 and pro‐IL‐1β expression, followed by ATP‐induced activation, which promotes inflammasome assembly via potassium efflux, ASC recruitment and caspase‐1 activation [18, 19]. As shown in Figure 5, stimulation with LPS and ATP resulted in increased expression of NLRP3 and ASC, together with elevated levels of cleaved caspase‐1, confirming activation of the inflammasome complex. Treatment with C. cartilaginea essential oil significantly reduced the expression of NLRP3 and ASC, as well as cleaved caspase‐1, indicating inhibition of inflammasome activation at both the priming and activation stages. The isolated compounds showed similar trends, with isopropyl isothiocyanate producing a greater reduction, while 2‐methylbutanenitrile exhibited a comparatively weaker effect. The coordinated decrease in these proteins suggests that the essential oil interferes with inflammasome assembly and downstream signalling. Consistent with these findings, LPS/ATP stimulation increased both pro‐IL‐1β and mature IL‐1β levels (Figure 6). Treatment with the essential oil and its constituents significantly reduced both forms, indicating effects on cytokine expression and maturation. The reduction in mature IL‐1β is consistent with decreased caspase‐1 activation, supporting inhibition of inflammasome‐mediated processing.

The significant inhibitory effect of isopropyl isothiocyanate observed is consistent with previous reports on other isothiocyanates. For example, phenylethyl isothiocyanate (PEITC) has been shown to attenuate liver injury by suppressing the NLRP3–caspase‐1–GSDMD pyroptosis pathway [20], while benzyl isothiocyanate has been reported to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reduce IL‐1β maturation in macrophage models [21, 22]. These findings support the role of isothiocyanates as modulators of inflammasome signalling.

Effect of C. cartilaginea Essential Oil on the Phosphorylation of the MAPK Signalling Pathways

To investigate the upstream mechanisms involved in inflammasome modulation, the effect of C. cartilaginea essential oil on mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways was evaluated. The MAPK cascade, including JNK, ERK and p38, regulates inflammatory signalling by promoting NF‐κB‐dependent transcription of NLRP3 and pro‐inflammatory cytokines during the priming phase and contributing to inflammasome activation through mitochondrial stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [23, 24, 25]. LPS/ATP stimulation significantly increased the phosphorylation of JNK, ERK and p38 (Figure 7), indicating activation of MAPK pathways. Treatment with C. cartilaginea essential oil and its major constituents significantly reduced the phosphorylation of all three kinases without affecting total protein levels, indicating selective inhibition of MAPK activation.

These findings are consistent with the observed suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome signalling and downstream cytokine production. They also align with previous studies reporting that isothiocyanates inhibit MAPK signalling pathways [22, 26], thereby contributing to reduced inflammatory responses.

It is worth noting that while the current study establishes a clear link between MAPK inhibition and the downregulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, ROS are recognised as critical upstream mediators linking these pathways [27]. Intracellular ROS accumulation acts as a common trigger that bridges early cellular stress to both the priming phase (via NF‐κB activation) and the subsequent assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex [28]. Although direct measurement of oxidative stress parameters was beyond the scope of the present study, the observed simultaneous attenuation of the NF‐κB, MAPK and NLRP3 pathways suggests a potential upstream regulatory mechanism involving modulation of the cellular redox state by the electrophilic isothiocyanate constituents of the essential oil.

Effect of C. cartilaginea Essential Oil on NF‐κB Activation

NF‐κB is a key transcription factor regulating the expression of pro‐inflammatory mediators, including cytokines, nitric oxide and enzymes such as COX‐2 and 5‐LOX. Under basal conditions, NF‐κB remains retained in the cytoplasm by IκBα; upon stimulation with LPS, IκBα is degraded, allowing NF‐κB to translocate to the nucleus and initiate transcription of inflammatory genes, including IL‐1β and NLRP3. This pathway plays a central role in the priming phase of NLRP3 inflammasome activation by inducing the expression of inflammasome components [29]. LPS/ATP stimulation increased phosphorylation of NF‐κB p65 (Figure 8), indicating activation of NF‐κB signalling. Treatment with C. cartilaginea essential oil and its major constituents significantly reduced phosphorylated p65 levels without affecting total p65 expression, indicating inhibition of NF‐κB activation.

These findings are consistent with the observed reduction in pro‐inflammatory cytokines and support the role of NF‐κB inhibition in suppressing inflammasome priming. This effect may be associated with the known activity of isothiocyanates, which have been reported to modulate NF‐κB signalling through multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of upstream kinases and reduction of oxidative stress [22, 26]. Together with the suppression of MAPK signalling, these results suggest that C. cartilaginea essential oil modulates key upstream pathways involved in NF‐κB activation and NLRP3 inflammasome signalling, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response.

Immunofluorescence Analysis

To further validate these findings and visualise the cellular localisation of inflammasome components, immunofluorescence analysis was performed. As shown in Figure 9, LPS/ATP stimulation increased the expression and cellular accumulation of NLRP3 and ASC compared with control cells. Treatment with C. cartilaginea essential oil reduced the fluorescence intensity of both proteins, indicating suppression of inflammasome assembly. This observation is consistent with the Western blot results, confirming inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Conclusions

C. cartilaginea essential oil and its major constituents exhibited significant anti‐inflammatory activity in LPS‐stimulated THP‐1 macrophages, reducing pro‐inflammatory cytokines and nitric oxide production. These effects were associated with suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, as evidenced by decreased NLRP3, ASC, cleaved caspase‐1 and IL‐1β maturation. In addition, inhibition of MAPK phosphorylation suggests modulation of upstream signalling pathways. These findings provide mechanistic support for the anti‐inflammatory potential of C. cartilaginea.
