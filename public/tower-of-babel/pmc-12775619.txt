Do spine surgery patients research their surgeon ahead of outpatient visits?

Introduction

Background

The use of social media in healthcare, and specifically in spine surgery, is becoming increasingly prevalent as patients aim to navigate the complex system of spine surgeons, physician networks, and hospitals. In turn, the percentage of spine surgeons who have a physician page or website continues to grow. Over half of all active American members of the Cervical Spine Research Society have a LinkedIn page (1). The amount of time and effort that is invested by many spine surgeons and practices in maintaining an active online presence is significant. This raises the question of how often and to what extent these platforms are used by patients in choosing a specific spine surgeon.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Currently, much of the literature regarding this topic revolves around how social media presence of spine surgeons correlates to online reviews by patients. There is currently very limited literature on if and/or how the internet and/or social media are used by patients to search the surgeon prior to their initial meeting and how this influences which spine surgeon patients choose. Further, one would anticipate these metrics would vary by geographic area and by practice setting (academic versus private practice). Studies that quantify the use of social media and online search engines by patients in preparation for a consultation with a spine surgeon are needed. This information can inform physicians and practice groups on the extent to which online material is viewed prior to seeing a surgeon and thus how much energy and money should be spent cultivating an online presence.

Objective

The purpose of this study is to gather data via surveys administered to spine surgery clinic patients in order to assess (I) differences in how often patients who are self-referred versus those who are referred to a spine surgeon by a primary care physician (PCP) or other physician/department use social media to search the spine surgeon prior to their first clinic visit; and (II) how often these searches result in a change in behavior in terms which spine surgeon a patient chooses to see. We hypothesize that most of the patients will not have performed an online search on their spine surgeon prior to their clinic visit, and doing so would not impact their decision on which spine surgeon they decide to see. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-17/rc).

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was granted Institutional Review Board exemption by the University of Wisconsin Hospitals and Clinics. The study was performed at an academic tertiary referral center and its branch locations, specifically at the University of Wisconsin-University Hospital (UWH) and University of Wisconsin East Madison Hospital (EMH; a branch location) in outpatient orthopedic spine clinics. Surveys were administered to all willing new patients at their first clinic visit with a spine surgeon. Verbal informed consent was obtained prior to administering the survey, and it was clearly conveyed to patients that the survey was optional. It was also clearly communicated that the survey was anonymous and securely stored in a separate location. The surveys contained questions about if and how the patient searched the spine surgeon online prior to the visit (see Figure S1).

A variety of demographic information was also collected, such as age, gender, race, highest level of education, residential setting, and chief complaint.

The primary endpoint for this study was (I) if an online search engine or social media search was done prior to first spine clinic visit; (II) which social media platform was used most commonly for searches; and (III) patient preference of surgeon depending on social media search. Secondary endpoints included correlations between age, gender, and race of a patient and rates of social media searches prior to first spine clinic visit. The inclusion criteria for this study were patients over the age of 18 years who presented to orthopedic spine clinics as new patient visits beginning in March 2024. Exclusion criteria include missing/incomplete surveys, established patient visits, and patients <18 years.

Results

Demographics

A total of 181 surveys were collected, 11 of which had at least half of the survey incomplete, and one survey was filled out by patient age <18 years. Hence, these 12 surveys were excluded. One hundred and sixty-nine surveys were used for analysis in final analysis. One hundred and forty-three were referred by PCP or other doctor or department (i.e., emergency room). Nineteen were self-referred patients. The other seven did not answer who they were referred by. Average patient age was 59 years (range, 24–87 years), and 43.5% (74/169) were females. One hundred and forty-six of the 169 (86.3%) respondents were white, 3 (1.7%) were Black/African American, 2 (1.2%) were Hispanic, 2 were Asian, 2 were American Indian/Alaskan Native, and 7 (4.1%) did not disclose race. In terms of highest level of education among survey participants, 41/169 (24.2%) responded with having an Associate’s degree, 38 (22.4%) with a Bachelor’s degree, 34 (20.1%) with a high school or General Educational Development (GED) degree, 11 (6.5%) with a Master’s degree, 9 (5.3%) with trade/vocational school, 8 (4.7%) with a Doctorate degree, 3 (1.8%) with less than a high school diploma, and 5 (3.0%) preferred not to disclose. When looking at residential settings, 64 of the 169 participants (37.9%) of survey respondents lived in a rural setting, 57 (33.7%) lived in a suburban area, 33 (19.5%) lived in an urban setting, and 14 (8.3%) preferred not to disclose.

Of the 169 patients, 68 (40.2%) were referred by a PCP and 67 were referred by “other doctor”. Nineteen (11.2%) were self-referred, 1 patient was referred by the emergency department, and 1 patient was referred by a clinic scheduler. Seven patients did not disclose who they were referred by. Thirteen of the 19 (68%) of the self-referred patients were females. Of the 28 total patients who did an online search prior to their clinic visit, 15 (53.6%) were females and 13 (46.4%) were males.

All groups

One hundred and twenty-seven of the 169 (75.1%) surveyed patients responded they did not read online patient reviews about their spine surgeon prior to their clinic visit, and 33 (19.5%) stated they did (9 patients did not respond). One hundred and twelve (66.3%) patients stated they did not do an online search of their spine surgeon prior to their first clinic visit, and 28 (16.6%) said they did (29 did not answer the question). Of the total 28 patients that stated they did an online search on their spine surgeon across both the PCP-referred and self-referred groups, nearly half of them (13/28) also read online reviews about their surgeon. Self-referred patients

Of the 19 self-referred patients, 14 (73.6%) did not do any form of an online search prior to their first clinic visit with a spine surgeon. When asked how they found out about their spine surgeon, 4 (21.1%) found out about their spine surgeon via a family or friend, four responded with “other” (one patient worked at the hospital, two called the spine clinic, one was a returning patient), and 5 (26.3%) used internet or social media to look up their spine surgeon (specifically, used Google and hospital-sponsored physician profiles). One of the three self-referred patients who used internet search specifically had a spine surgeon in mind that she wanted to see and therefore requested that surgeon prior to her clinic visit. Eight of the 19 (42.1%) of self-referred patients read online patient reviews about their surgeon prior to the clinic visit. Only one self-referred patient requested a different spine surgeon based off of their online reading. This patient was one of two patients, out of the 169 total patients, who requested a different spine surgeon based off of online research (the other patient being one who was referred by PCP). Sixteen of the 19 (84.2%) self-referred patients responded that social media played no role in selecting their spine surgeon. There were a total of 5/169 (3.0%) patients who stated that social media played a very important role in selecting a spine surgeon, and three of the five were self-referred patients. Fourteen of the 19 (73.7%) self-referred patients responded that online search engines played no role or a neutral role in selecting their spine surgeon. There were a total of 9/169 (5.3%) patients who stated that online search engines played a very important role in selecting a spine surgeon, and four of those nine were self-referred patients.

Patients referred by outside department or PCP

Of the 143 patients referred either by a PCP or other doctor/department, 100 (69.9%) patients stated that they did not do an online search for their spine surgeon prior to their clinic visit, and 23 (16.1%) stated they did. 14.0% (20/143) elected not to respond to this question. From the 23 patients that responded they did an online search, 18 (78.3%) used Google as their main platform, one person used Facebook, and one person used LinkedIn, and three patients elected to not respond. When those 23 patients were asked if they used any webpages, 21 stated they used the hospital-sponsored physician profile page. The remaining two patients did not respond. Twenty-five out of 143 (17.5%) stated they read online reviews about their surgeon. Only one patient in the outside department/PCP-referred group requested to see a different spine surgeon based on online reading. When asked how much did social media platforms play a role in selecting a spine surgeon, 88.8% (123/143) responded “no role” or “not important”, 3.5% (5/143) responded “neutral”, 3.5% (5/143) stated “somewhat important”, 1.4% (2/143) stated “very important”, and eight did not respond. When asked how much did online search engines play a role in selecting a spine surgeon, 81.8% (117/143) stated “no role” or “not important”, 5.6% (8/143) said “somewhat important”, 3.5% (5/143) stated “neutral”, 3.5% (5/143) stated “very important”, and eight did not respond. Discussion

This study shows that a majority of our survey respondents were white males with an associate’s degree living in a rural setting who were referred to the spine clinic by a PCP or other physician. Very few of the patients in this study were self-referred. One of our objectives was to see differences in online search habits for patients who were referred to their spine surgeon by another doctor or department versus patients who were self-referred. Of the patients who were referred by a PCP or other physician, the majority of them stated they did not do an online search or read any online patient reviews for their spine surgeon prior to their clinic visit. Most patients in both other physician/PCP-referred and self-referred groups also stated that social media platforms and online search engines played an insignificant to no role in selecting their spine surgeon. In both groups, the patients who did do an online search mostly used Google and the hospital-sponsored physician profile page. One patient in the self-referred group and one patient in the other physician/PCP-referred group requested a different spine surgeon based on their online reading.

There is a dearth of literature that discusses social media use among spine surgeons. In a retrospective study of spine surgeons in Florida who were part of the North American Spine Society, Donnally et al. found that nearly half of the surgeons had a searchable Instagram, Facebook, or Twitter account and that surgeons with a presence on social media had a higher number of ratings on Google.com, Healthgrades.com, and Vitals.com (2). Other similar studies of spine surgeons in different states found relatively comparable results, stating that social media presence may positively impact patient feedback (3,4). However, these studies are more helpful in evaluating the correlation between social media presence of surgeons and patient satisfaction outcomes after the patient-physician interaction has already taken place. In contrast, our study focuses more on the online reading and research that takes place on behalf of the patient prior to the patient-physician interaction.

A study published in 2022 in Journal of Pediatric Orthopedics looked at 1,231 members of Pediatric Orthopedic Society of North America (POSNA) who had a social media profile. They found that the use of Instagram, Facebook, and LinkedIn as well as educational posts on Instagram and Facebook were positive, whereas the perceptions of TikTok and Twitter were negative (5). Another study published in Arthroscopy, Sports Medicine, and Rehabilitation in 2023 collected data from sports medicine orthopedic surgeons’ Instagram and Twitter profiles. A total of 1,193 Instagram posts and 1,284 Twitter posts were identified. The authors found that Instagram had significantly more social media engagement across all categories (team coverage, personal life, and pathway to becoming a physician) when compared to Twitter (6). JBJS published a systematic review in January 2024, which included looking at a total of 28 studies to analyze social media use by individual orthopedic surgeons as well as by orthopedic surgery residency and fellowship programs. The study found that Instagram, Twitter, and Facebook were the top platforms that patients and institutions used most frequently (7).

This study has several limitations. One of the main limitations is the lack of racial, age, and gender diversity in the survey participants, which is a direct result of the geographical location that the study was performed in: one isolated academic center in the Midwest. This lends itself to a very racially non-diverse cohort: rural white males around the age of 60 years, which could limit this study’s generalizability and potentially bias our study results in a way that shows that patients do not search their spine surgeons online prior to their clinic visit. It could be hypothesized that, generally speaking, older males from a rural area do not tend to be as inclined to use technology, social media, and/or online platforms for research prior to a clinic appointment, whereas perhaps younger patients living in urban areas may be more inclined to do so. This could be attributed to younger patients’ more familiarity with different online search engines and social media platforms as well as more readily available access to the internet when living in an urban area as opposed to a rural one. Spine clinics also tend to draw in more geriatric patients simply due to the chronicity of some of the most prevalent spine conditions/diseases, hence further adding to the lack of diversity in age in our survey patients and limiting external validity. Perhaps a study like this would yield different results if it were performed in a sports or pediatric orthopedic clinic, which could be direction for future studies.

Conclusions

The present study provides insight into the online search habits of spine clinic patients in the academic setting. Our study suggests that older patients are less likely to search the internet prior to an appointment. For patients who do seek information about their surgeon, Google and the hospital-sponsored physician profile are the most visited. This could help spine surgeons at this academic center gauge if social media and online presence are worth the time-investment depending on their practice, and if so, which platforms to focus on. Future studies should look to explore similarities and differences with our results across other orthopedic subspecialties, geographic regions, and practice types (academic versus private practice).
