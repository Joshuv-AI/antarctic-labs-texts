Major cardiovascular events in Japanese patients with acute and chronic coronary syndrome
Authors: Nobuhiro Osada, Feng Sheng, Chia-Hsien Chang, Mary Clouser, Satoru Tanaka, Maja Ludwikowska, Shun Kohsaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular (CV) disease remains the second leading cause of mortality in Japan, yet real-world estimates of recurrent CV risk across acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) phenotypes are limited. We quantified recurrent CV risk and characterised high-risk subgroups in Japanese patients with ACS and CCS.Methods and resultsThis retrospective cohort study used the Medical Data Vision claims database. Patients with ACS or CCS were followed from cohort entry to the first composite CV endpoint or end of data. The primary composite endpoint included all-cause death, myocardial infarction or stroke. The secondary composite endpoint included the primary endpoint plus hospitalisation for unstable angina, hospitalisation for heart failure or emergent or elective coronary revascularisation. Baseline clinical characteristics were evaluated as predictors. Time-to-event analyses were conducted.26 656 and 16 172 patients were included in the ACS and CCS cohorts, respectively. Median follow-up was 3.0 years in the ACS cohort and 3.2 years in the CCS cohort. The 1-year risk of the primary endpoint was 17.7% in first-time ACS, 8.0% in prior ACS and 5.3% in CCS; corresponding 5-year risks were 24.0%, 13.8% and 12.0%, respectively. In CCS, elderly men with prior ACS, heart failure, diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease had a 23.0% 1-year risk of the primary endpoint.ConclusionsRecurrent CV risk was highest after a first ACS event and was heterogenous in CCS, with substantially higher risk in comorbidity-burdened subgroups. These findings provide contemporary real-world estimates of recurrent CV risk and support further refinement and validation of risk-stratification approaches for secondary prevention.

Introduction

Cardiovascular (CV) diseases are the second leading cause of mortality in Japan, accounting for approximately 28% of deaths, underscoring the substantial burden placed on both individual health outcomes and the national healthcare system.1 While recent clinical guidelines delineate the spectrum of coronary artery disease (CAD) into acute coronary syndromes (ACS) and chronic coronary syndromes (CCS), CCS is increasingly conceptualised as a dynamic, lifelong condition in which periods of clinical stability can be interrupted by acute thrombotic events.2 In this context, long-term prognosis and recurrent event risk in routine Japanese practice remain insufficiently characterised.

To guide treatment intensification in these patients, numerous risk stratification frameworks have been introduced. Earlier paradigms, exemplified by the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III, defined ‘high’ and ‘very high’ risk categories largely based on the presence of established atherosclerotic CV disease (ASCVD), major risk factors or estimated 10-year CV risk.3 More recently, major outcomes trials have demonstrated that residual risk persists despite contemporary secondary prevention and that further risk reduction is achievable with additional therapies.4–6 Based on these findings, updated guidelines from organisations such as the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology/American College of Endocrinology, American College of Cardiology, American Heart Association and Asian Pacific Society of Cardiology have refined stratification by identifying ‘very high risk ASCVD’ patients, such as those with multiple major ASCVD events or one major event plus high-risk conditions.3 7 8

However, these refined risk stratification approaches have yet to be fully validated in Japanese or East Asian clinical practices. Japan faces unique challenges as a super-aged society, where patients often present with distinct phenotypic characteristics compared with Western cohorts, including a lower prevalence of obesity but a higher burden of age-related comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease (CKD) and stroke risk.9–11 Consequently, risk models developed in Western populations may not reliably predict outcomes in Japanese patients. Moreover, large-scale data remains limited that directly quantify and compare recurrent CV risk across clinically important strata (eg, first-time ACS, prior ACS and CCS) within a real-world dataset.

ACS and CCS represent different clinical phases along the spectrum of CAD, sharing an underlying atherosclerotic pathology but differing primarily in timing relative to an acute coronary event and the resulting clinical stability. Accordingly, risk profiles and subsequent event risk may vary across these phases, and clarifying predictors within each group can support more accurate risk stratification and tailored management. To address this gap, the present study used the nationwide claims database to estimate the incidence of recurrent CV events and to identify baseline predictors of adverse outcomes in Japanese patients with ACS and CCS, including stratification by first-time versus prior ACS history.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used the Medical Data Vision (MDV) claims database (MDV, Tokyo, Japan). The MDV database is one of the largest hospital-based datasets in Japan and includes integrated health insurance claim and clinical information from more than 500 institutions. As of April 2024, the database included anonymised records for approximately 47.39 million patients.12 Available data include demographics, diagnoses (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10] codes), procedures, medications (ATC codes), laboratory data (for approximately 12% of patients) and in-hospital outcomes. The data were de-identified and privacy-protected according to the Personal Information Protection Act of Japan.

Study population and study period

This retrospective cohort study included two partially overlapping subsets identified in the MDV database: an ACS cohort and a CCS cohort.

Eligible patients were at least 20 years of age at the index date (starting point of follow-up) and had at least 12 months of baseline data available before the index date. Patients were categorised into two cohorts:

ACS cohort: Patients hospitalised for ACS (defined as myocardial infarction (MI) or unstable angina (UA)) within the selection period. If a patient had multiple ACS hospitalisations, one eligible hospitalisation start date was randomly selected as the index date.

ACS cohort: Patients hospitalised for ACS (defined as myocardial infarction (MI) or unstable angina (UA)) within the selection period. If a patient had multiple ACS hospitalisations, one eligible hospitalisation start date was randomly selected as the index date.

Revascularised CCS cohort (hereafter referred to as the CCS cohort): Patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) after (or in the same month as) a CAD diagnosis within the selection period, and who had no hospitalisation for ACS in the 12 months before the index date. The index date was randomly selected from eligible PCI/CABG dates (if multiple eligible procedures occurred, one date was randomly selected as the index date).

Revascularised CCS cohort (hereafter referred to as the CCS cohort): Patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) after (or in the same month as) a CAD diagnosis within the selection period, and who had no hospitalisation for ACS in the 12 months before the index date. The index date was randomly selected from eligible PCI/CABG dates (if multiple eligible procedures occurred, one date was randomly selected as the index date).

The diagnosis and procedure codes used to identify ACS and CCS were based on the diagnostic algorithms described by Kanaoka et al13 and were reviewed and confirmed by medical experts.

The study period was from 1 April 2017 to 31 March 2024, reflecting contemporary clinical practice within the available data. The selection period for inclusion extended from 1 April 2018 to 31 March 2022 to allow for a 12-month preindex baseline period.

All eligible patients were followed from the index date until the earliest of the following: the end of the study period, the last available record in the database, or the first occurrence of a composite CV endpoint.

From the MDV database, a total of 333 145 patients were identified with at least one diagnosis of ACS or CAD during the selection period between 1 April 2018 and 31 March 2022. Two partially overlapping source populations were defined: 137 502 (41%) patients with a hospitalisation record for ACS and 1 78 302 patients (54%) who underwent a PCI or CABG after or in the same month as a CAD diagnosis and within the selection period (CCS source cohort). After the application of inclusion criteria, 26 656 patients were eligible for the ACS cohort (1751 patients with prior ACS and 24 905 patients with first-time ACS, defined as having no recorded history of ACS before the index date in the available MDV data, which covered the period from 1 April 2017 onward) and 16 172 patients met the eligibility criteria of the CCS cohort (figure 1).

Variables

The outcomes of this study were defined as primary and secondary composite CV endpoint. The primary composite CV endpoint was defined as the first occurrence of any of the following during the follow-up period: all-cause death, MI or stroke. The secondary composite CV endpoint included all-cause death, MI, stroke, hospitalisation for UA, hospitalisation for heart failure (HF) or emergent or elective PCI or CABG. MI, stroke, UA and HF were identified from hospitalisation records using ICD-10 diagnosis codes, whereas PCI and CABG were identified using procedure codes. Events that were part of the index ACS (MI or UA) were not counted as recurrent outcomes if they occurred during the index hospitalisation.

Baseline demographic and clinical variables were collected in the 12-month period preceding the index date. These variables included age, sex, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), laboratory values to include low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides and total cholesterol. Baseline comorbidities included obesity, HF, familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH), hyperlipidaemia, peripheral artery disease (PAD), hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM) and CKD.

A complete list of codes used to identify study population and study variables is provided in online supplemental file 1.

Clinical history variables were categorised based on timing relative to the index date: previous events were defined as those occurring at any time before the index date, while recent events were those occurring within the 12 months before the index date. This classification was applied to all clinical and procedural events. History of ASCVD was defined as the presence of previous ACS, ischaemic stroke (IS) or PAD. Other clinical history variables were recent or previous ACS and IS. Procedural history included recent or prior PCI and CABG, as well as prior carotid artery and PAD-related revascularisation procedures.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarised using standard descriptive statistics (mean±SD or median (IQR) for continuous variables, and counts with percentages for categorical variables), with between-group comparisons reported descriptively. Time-to-event analyses were conducted from the cohort entry date to the first occurrence of an endpoint, with censoring at the end of data availability or loss of follow-up. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was employed to estimate the cumulative incidence of both primary and secondary composite CV endpoint, and curves were compared descriptively across prespecified subgroups (ACS with prior ACS vs first-time ACS; CCS).

Cox proportional hazards regression models, adjusted for selected baseline variables to avoid collinearity, were used to identify risk factors associated with recurrent CV events. To mitigate multicollinearity, candidate covariates were prescreened using correlation diagnostics (eg, variance inflation factors and clinically redundant variable pairs), and parsimonious multivariable models were specified accordingly. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using standard diagnostics (including inspection of log–log survival plots and tests based on scaled Schoenfeld residuals). For model development, age and sex were retained a priori, and additional predictors were retained if they were statistically significant (two-sided p<0.05).

To predict the 1-year risk of primary and secondary composite CV endpoint in both ACS and CCS populations, parametric survival models were fitted using Weibull, Gompertz and Gamma distributions. Absolute 1-year risk was derived from the fitted survival function as 1–S (1 year), and covariate effects were incorporated through the linear predictor from the corresponding parametric regression model. In the ACS cohort, patients were stratified based on prior ACS history. Predictive modelling was limited to the subgroup without previous ACS, due to insufficient sample size in the group with ACS history. The performance of each model was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion and the model with the best fit was selected accordingly. The predictive model’s discriminative ability was evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (c-index), and model performance was summarised as poor-to-moderate, consistent with the observed C-statistics.

Missing data were reported as a separate category, but they were not included in the analyses. Additional information on data cleaning and the availability of supplementary study materials is provided in the online supplemental file 2.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 26 656 patients were included in the ACS cohort (1751 with prior ACS and 24 905 with first-time ACS), and 16 172 patients were included in the CCS cohort (table 1). In the ACS cohort, the mean age was 71.3±12.4 years, with 73.1% of patients being male (table 1). Comorbidities were generally more prevalent among patients with prior ACS than among those with first-time ACS. Hyperlipidaemia and hypertension were the most common comorbidities affecting over two-thirds of patients without a history of ACS and over 80% among those with previous ACS. Approximately 10% of patients in ACS cohort were diagnosed with CKD. PAD was observed in 5.0% of the total ACS cohort. FH affected only 0.4% of the ACS cohort (table 1). In the CCS cohort, the mean age was comparable to the ACS cohort (71.2±10.1 years) and 78.7% of patients were male. Similarly, hyperlipidaemia was most frequent (80.7%). PAD and CKD were each present in around 12% of patients and FH in 0.7% (table 1).

Baseline cardioprotective medication use differed by phenotype, with more frequent use in CCS and in ACS patients with prior ACS than in first-time ACS patients. For example, lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) was reported in 82% of ACS patients with a history of ACS and 15% of first-time ACS patients. In the CCS cohort, about 70% received LLT at baseline (table 1).

Risk of recurrent CV events

The median (quartile range) follow-up duration was 3.0 (2.1–4.0) years in the ACS cohort, and 3.2 (2.4–4.2) years in the CCS cohort (table 1). The 1-year risk of a recurrent primary composite CV endpoint in ACS patients was 17.1% and was higher in first-time ACS patients compared with those with a history of ACS (17.7% vs 8.0%). The 5-year risk of a recurrent primary CV endpoint in first-time ACS patients reached 24.0% and was almost twice as high as in those with a history of ACS (13.8%). In CCS patients, the 1-year risk of recurrent primary CV endpoint was 5.3% increasing to 12% over a 5-year period (table 2). Risks for the secondary composite endpoint were higher across cohorts: 1-year risks were 31.2% and 26.1% for ACS patients without and with history of ACS, respectively, while in CCS patients, the risk was 18.5%. Observed 5-year risks were approximately 41% in the ACS cohort and 33% in the CCS cohorts (table 2; Kaplan-Meier curves are presented in online supplemental figures 2,3; probabilities of the individual components of the primary and secondary CV events are presented in online supplemental table 5).

Factors associated with the risk of recurrent CV endpoint

In multivariable models for the primary endpoint, advanced age, prior IS and CKD were among the strongest predictors in ACS, while female sex was associated with lower risk (table 3). Among patients with prior ACS, prior CABG and CKD were prominent predictors (table 3). In CCS, prior ACS, HF and CKD were the dominant predictors, with DM also contributing; age was not a significant predictor in CCS (table 4). Predictors for the secondary endpoint were directionally similar, with CKD, HF, DM, PAD and prior IS consistently associated with higher hazard (online supplemental tables 1,2), and results were unchanged after additional adjustment for baseline treatments (online supplemental tables 3,4).

Predictive models

In the ACS population without a history of prior ACS, the predictive model estimated a broad range of 1-year risks for both primary and secondary composite CV endpoints. The highest estimated 1-year risk for the primary composite CV endpoint (38.2%) was found in male patients over 85 years old with previous diagnoses of IS and CKD. In contrast, the lowest risk (5.7%) was observed in females aged 55–64 years with a history of prior PCI, HF, hyperlipidaemia and hypertension. In the CCS population, the predicted 1-year risks also varied substantially for both outcomes. The highest estimated risk for the primary composite CV endpoint (23.0%) was observed in male patients over 85 years of age with a history of prior ACS, HF, DM and CKD. The lowest risk (2.1%) was estimated in females under 55 years of age without the specified comorbidities. Across models, discriminative ability was poor to moderate, with Harrell’s c-statistics ranging from 0.55 to 0.62.

Discussion

This study provided a contemporary overview of adverse CV event risk among hospitalised ACS and revascularised CCS patients in a nationwide Japanese administrative dataset. The risk of adverse CV event risk differs materially by clinical phenotype and prior event history; patients experiencing a first-time ACS event had the highest risks for both the primary and secondary endpoints. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence that patients remain at high risk of recurrent events after ACS, particularly early after discharge.14 Risk factors also differed across subgroups; in the first-time ACS group, prior stroke and CKD were associated with higher recurrence risk, whereas inverse associations were observed for prior PCI, HF, hypertension and hyperlipidaemia. These inverse associations may reflect treatment bias rather than true protective effects and should therefore be interpreted with caution. In CCS undergoing revascularisation, risk was heterogeneous and spanned a wide range; prior ACS, prior revascularisation and systemic comorbidities including diabetes, stroke, HF and CKD emerged as prominent predictors of both primary and secondary composite CV events, aligning with previous studies linking CKD and related comorbidity burden to worse CV outcomes.15 These findings are consistent with the 2023 Japanese Circulation Society Guideline on the Primary Prevention of Coronary Artery Disease, which highlights diabetes, CKD, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, smoking and obesity as major CV risk factors.16 Although our study addressed secondary prevention, diabetes and CKD remained strong predictors of recurrent CV events. Finally, an association between hospital size and recurrent CV outcomes was also observed. However, hospital size is likely a proxy for differences in referral patterns, case complexity, procedural volume and access to specialised CV care rather than an independent prognostic factor; therefore it was not considered in multivariate analyses. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that institutional characteristics, including hospital procedural volume, influence CV outcomes.17

In our study, the 1-year risk of adverse CV events among ACS patients was approximately threefold higher than that observed in patients with CCS undergoing revascularisation. Although direct comparisons across studies are limited by differences in endpoint definitions and data sources, our estimates are broadly concordant with prior reports demonstrating that patients remain at high risk of recurrent events after ACS, particularly early after discharge. For example, in a large US-based cohort, 3.8% experienced recurrent MI and 11.8% had CV rehospitalisations within 1 year post-MI.18 Accordingly, our findings likely reflect the clinical spectrum captured in MDV, including patients with potentially higher-risk profiles and inclusive outcome definitions, rather than indicating anomalously elevated risk. While this overall high risk in ACS was anticipated, we observed that first-time ACS patients had higher recurrence risk than those with prior ACS. We emphasise that this finding should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect both clinical and methodological factors rather than a true biological risk reversal. Specifically, patients with prior ACS events had more comorbidity burden, yet were more likely to have undergone revascularisation and to be receiving LLT and other intensive secondary prevention treatments at baseline, whereas first-time ACS patients may enter the system without documented CAD risk or without optimised preventive therapy.19 In addition, claims-based classification of ‘prior ACS’ may introduce selection and survivor bias, and prior events occurring outside the observable history window may be underascertained. These hypotheses cannot be directly evaluated using the present dataset and should therefore be considered exploratory. Nevertheless, irrespective of the underlying explanation, the finding reinforces a clinically important message: an index ACS event marks a high-risk state that warrants systematic early risk assessment and prompt implementation of guideline-directed secondary prevention, irrespective of documented event history.

In the CCS cohort, the 1-year recurrence risk was 5.3% for the primary endpoint and 18.5% for the secondary endpoint which was considerably lower than in the ACS cohort. However, while CCS patients were not uniformly low risk; for example, in our risk modelling elderly men with prior ACS, HF, DM and CKD had an estimated 1-year risk of 23.0% for the primary endpoint and 36.8% for the secondary endpoint, approaching the range observed in high-risk ACS patients. These findings highlight substantial heterogeneity in recurrent risk across CCS patients undergoing revascularisation. Although the predictive performance of the models was modest, readily available clinical characteristics such as age, sex and comorbidity profile may help support clinical risk assessment and should be interpreted alongside clinical judgement rather than as standalone prediction tools.2 Because lipid laboratory data were available for only a small proportion of patients, we could not evaluate LDL-C impact. Likewise, treatment variables, including intensive LLT, were not incorporated into the multivariable prediction models because the aim of these analyses was to identify baseline clinical predictors rather than estimate treatment effects. Moreover, LLT is closely related to baseline clinical characteristics, including prior ACS, previous coronary revascularisation and hyperlipidaemia, making it susceptible to multicollinearity and confounding by indication. In Japan, the JAS 2022 guideline already specifies LDL-C <100 mg/dL for secondary prevention and recommends considering a more stringent LDL-C target <70 mg/dL for higher-risk secondary-prevention conditions (including ACS, FH, DM and concomitant atherothrombotic stroke).20 21 Our real-world estimates may help identify CCS subgroups at particularly high recurrent CV risk, complementing current risk-stratified approaches to secondary prevention.

This study has several limitations. First, the MDV database lacks a universal patient identifier across institutions, limiting longitudinal tracking and potentially underestimating CV outcomes. However, event rates were comparable to the J-PCI registry, supporting the external validity of our findings, and our focus on 1-year outcomes helped mitigate this issue. Second, we included only CCS patients who underwent revascularisation (PCI or CABG) due to the heterogeneity of the broader CCS population and the limited coverage of stable outpatients in acute care hospital data. Consequently, the CCS cohort in this study represents patients with revascularised CCS rather than the overall CCS population, and the findings should not be generalised to patients managed conservatively or to the broader CCS population. Third, all-cause mortality was identified based on records available in the MDV database. As the MDV is a hospital-based claims database, deaths are captured only if they occur during hospitalisation at participating hospitals. Consequently, all-cause mortality may have been underestimated. Furthermore, the MDV database does not provide reliable information on the cause of death, precluding differentiation between CV and non-CV mortality. Therefore, mortality findings should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, residual confounding is possible given the absence of key variables such as lifestyle factors, medication adherence, blood pressure, socioeconomic status and lipid profiles—laboratory data were available for only ~12% of patients. In addition, the MDV database does not routinely capture several important anatomical and procedural characteristics, including the extent of CAD (eg, multivessel or left main disease) and completeness of revascularisation. Although ACS presentation (egST-segment elevation myocardial infarction [STEMI], non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction [NSTEMI], or UA) may be partially identified using diagnosis codes, claims data lack detailed clinical information and coding inaccuracies may occur. These limitations may have reduced our ability to fully account for differences in recurrent CV risk across patient groups, and residual confounding related to disease severity cannot be excluded. Lastly, the predictive models showed poor to moderate discrimination, with c-statistics ranging from approximately 0.55 to 0.62. This is in line with previous studies22 and likely reflects, at least in part, the strong impact of pharmacological treatment in secondary prevention. Models incorporating clinical laboratory tests or BMI achieved better discrimination but still had a Harrell’s Concordance Index below 0.7.23–25 Together, these findings suggest that adding conventional clinical variables alone has limited capacity to substantially improve prediction. Future improvements could involve integrating more detailed clinical information, such as advanced imaging findings (eg, coronary Computer Tomography angiography or echocardiographic parameters). In addition, using artificial intelligence and machine-learning approaches may help capture complex patterns among variables that traditional models cannot detect, potentially leading to more accurate and personalised risk prediction tools.

Despite these limitations, the study’s strengths include its large sample size and use of one of Japan’s largest hospital-based claims databases, providing robust, real-world evidence. Furthermore, the study fills an important evidence gap on long-term outcomes and risk stratification in ACS and CCS patients in Japan, and the differentiation between ACS patients with and without prior events offers valuable insights for optimising secondary prevention. The characterisation of high-risk CCS subgroups further provides clinically relevant information that may inform future guideline development and risk-based treatment strategies.

Conclusions

In Japan, patients with ACS, particularly those experiencing a first ACS event, have a high recurrent rate of adverse CV events, consistent with prior evidence that substantial residual risk persists after ACS despite contemporary care. While CCS patients undergoing revascularisation had lower overall risk, our results indicate marked heterogeneity, with comorbidity-burdened subgroups remaining vulnerable. These findings support refinement and validation of risk-assessment approaches in Japanese practice to better identify very high-risk patients who may benefit from intensified secondary-prevention strategies, in line with contemporary risk-based frameworks and Japanese lipid guideline recommendation for high-risk secondary prevention.
