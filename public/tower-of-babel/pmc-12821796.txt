Adaptation of the Non-Attachment Scale (NAS-7) and its associations with hope, social connectedness, and life satisfaction
Authors: Yusuf Akyıl, Azmi Çağlar, Beste Erdinç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Non-attachment, a construct rooted in Eastern and Buddhist philosophy, describes responding to experiences without clinging to transient thoughts or conditions. This research aimed to adapt the 7-item Non-Attachment Scale (NAS-7) into Turkish and evaluate its psychometric properties. The project consisted of two studies. Study I (N = 404) examined the scale’s factor structure using Confirmatory Factor Analysis, assessed item discrimination through Item Response Theory, and evaluated reliability using Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ϲ, and Guttmann’s λ6; measurement invariance across gender was also tested but not supported. Study II (N = 417) investigated the associations among non-attachment, hope, social connectedness, and life satisfaction using correlation analyses and Structural Equation Modeling. Results indicated that the NAS-7 demonstrated strong model fit, high internal consistency, and robust item discrimination. Additionally, hope and social connectedness simultaneously mediated the link between non-attachment and life satisfaction. Overall, the findings demonstrate that the Turkish NAS-7 is a valid and reliable instrument and that non-attachment contributes to life satisfaction both directly and indirectly, filling an important gap in the literature regarding the assessment of non-attachment in the Turkish context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03840-4.

Introduction

Psychological traditions grounded in Eastern and Buddhist philosophy emphasize the transient nature of life and suggest that clinging to impermanent experiences, people, and circumstances contributes to psychological suffering [45]. Within this framework, non-attachment refers to responding to experiences without excessive fixation or emotional investment. Importantly, non-attachment does not imply indifference; rather, individuals with higher non-attachment tend to be more receptive to unpleasant realities, less self-centered, more connected to others, and better able to adjust to changing conditions [41]. This orientation has been associated with reduced fear of loss and decreased preoccupation with the illusory self and mental fixations [23].

Given these theoretical foundations, non-attachment has been increasingly linked to psychological strengths such as hope, interpersonal functioning, and well-being. For instance, releasing mental fixation on desired outcomes may support greater openness and optimism, which aligns naturally with the concept of hope. Likewise, non-attachment appears to facilitate interpersonal well-being by reducing ego-centered concerns and enabling more authentic engagement with others [11, 48]. Social connectedness -defined as feeling close to, integrated with, and supported by others [33, 42]- may therefore be influenced by non-attachment, which encourages flexible, balanced responses rather than clinging or avoidance. A substantial body of research shows that non-attachment is positively associated with psychological well-being [26, 41] and may reduce the dependence of well-being on external circumstances [12]. These converging findings suggest that non-attachment can influence multiple domains of functioning, highlighting the need to examine its correlates in diverse contexts.

Building on this conceptual foundation, empirical work has distinguished non-attachment from patterns such as avoidant attachment, which reflects distance and discomfort with closeness [35]. Unlike avoidance, non-attachment involves flexible awareness rather than withdrawal, and it may therefore promote relational harmony, kindness, and emotional openness [11, 48]. This flexible stance toward inner experience can enhance subjective well-being by reducing emotional reactivity and supporting balanced psychological functioning. Consequently, non-attachment appears conceptually aligned with positive outcomes such as life satisfaction and social connectedness, and empirical testing of these associations is warranted.

Non-attached individuals may also experience a richer quality of life because they engage with their surroundings proactively and compassionately rather than indifferently [41]. Within broader frameworks of well-being -comprising happiness, life satisfaction, and positive psychological functioning [40]- non-attachment may serve as an important psychological resource. Psychological difficulties such as depression, anxiety, stress, and suicidal ideation show negative associations with non-attachment [22, 25, 27], while hope is positively linked to life satisfaction and well-being [30]. Since hope involves goal-directed motivation built on planning and willpower [44], and non-attachment supports resilience in the face of difficult experiences [12], examining the interplay among these constructs becomes increasingly relevant. Taken together, the literature suggests meaningful conceptual connections among non-attachment, hope, social connectedness, and life satisfaction, underscoring the importance of investigating these relationships empirically.

The present study

Different approaches to psychological structures help us evaluate and understand them culturally. At this point, while the Buddhism perspective strengthens the philosophical background of the research, the psychological infrastructure of the research is based on Attachment Theory [4]. Non-attachment is worth studying because it takes a novel approach to this issue, even though attachment theory has made significant contributions to the literature. In contrast to attachment, the concept of non-attachment relates to an individual’s attitude toward his or her experiences, thoughts, feelings, and desires, rather than interpersonal interactions. This notion implies that the individual does not mentally cling to their possessions or the situations they encounter but rather approaches them with a flexible, accepting, and transient awareness [41]. Non-attachment is described as the ability to accept the transient character of experiences and relate to them without becoming stuck in them. Furthermore, an individual’s approach to events, feelings, or thoughts with minimal attachment is regarded as a significant measure of psychological wellness [21]. As a result, non-attachment can benefit mental health by acknowledging the impermanent, lowering the urge for control, and building a more flexible relationship with inner experiences.

People’s lives contain social interactions, connection, and belonging. Individuals who form social attachments within the context of nonattachment have a poor experience. Because existence contains change, attachment becomes an experience that includes the threat of suffering inherent in life’s nature. Attachment is viewed as the result of a mistaken belief in the permanency of human experiences. As a result, some suggest that non-attachment is helpful to individual functioning. The concept of non-attachment, which has been present in Eastern philosophy for many years, has only recently entered the psychology literature, and a scale has been developed to measure non-attachment to observe it in individuals and investigate its relationships with other structures [12].

The non-attachment scale emphasizes a measurement tool for use in the field of mental health. The current study attempts to adapt an assessment tool for non-attachment in Turkish culture. However, because non-attachment may be expressed, interpreted, and experienced differently across cultural contexts, adapting the NAS-7 into Turkish requires more than direct linguistic translation. Cultural idioms, relational norms, and context-specific emotional expectations in Türkiye may shape how non-attachment is understood. Therefore, a culturally sensitive validation is essential to ensure conceptual equivalence and accurate measurement when applying the scale in a Turkish population. This need has become increasingly urgent as interest in non-attachment and related constructs has grown, yet no validated tool currently exists for assessing non-attachment in Türkiye. The cultural relevance of non-attachment to Turkey can be better understood in this adaptation study by looking at how it relates to the ideas of social connectedness, hope, and life satisfaction -all of which are thought to be related to the concept of non-attachment. Addressing this gap at this time is particularly important, as it allows researchers to examine psychological resources that may influence well-being in a rapidly changing social and cultural landscape. The results will allow us to recognize additional non-attachment concepts and perform research on understanding attachment from a new perspective. In this way, the present study offers a timely contribution by providing a culturally appropriate measurement tool and opening a path for future empirical work that has been unable to progress in the absence of such an instrument.

Study 1

Study 1 prioritizes translating the Nonattachment Scale (NAS-7; Elphinstone et al., [16] into Turkish. In the Turkish translation step, the original English version was first translated into Turkish by three experts in the field and language and then compared. These items were then translated back into English to ensure that they matched the original ones. CFA, IRT, and measurement invariance will be used in the preliminary research. To evaluate scale reliability, Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and Guttmann lambda coefficients will be calculated. The initial question to be answered within this theoretical framework is:

Method

Participants

The study involved a cohort of 404 adults, of which 146 were male (36.1%) and 258 were female (63.9%), representing 41 different cities in Turkey. In terms of education level, 67.3% of these participants were high school graduates, 29.2% were university graduates, 2.7% were master’s degree graduates and the rest were primary school graduates. When the distribution of these participants was examined, it was found that 87.1% were in the 18–24 age range, while the rest were older. The information was gathered via social media and online announcements. The study recruited only voluntary participants, who were also requested to carefully review the informed consent documents included in the scale sets. Participants were recruited through a convenience sampling approach, primarily via online dissemination. The information was gathered via social media and online announcements. The recruitment process involved sharing the survey link through widely used digital platforms (e.g., social media groups, university networks), allowing easy access for potential volunteers. To ensure methodological clarity, we also specified inclusion criteria: participants had to be adults (18+), fluent in Turkish, and willing to provide informed consent. The study recruited only voluntary participants, who were also requested to carefully review the informed consent documents included in the scale sets. Given that the NAS-7 consists of 7 items, this sample size of 404 participants exceeds widely cited factor-analytic recommendations requiring at least 5 participants per item, indicating that the sample is more than adequate for the planned analyses [49].

Yıldız Technical University Human Research Ethics Committee authorized the study I and study II’s protocol (Reference Number = 20240402864, Verify Code = 0f580). Furthermore, this study was carried out in compliance with the ethical guidelines established in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its modifications.

Non-Attachment Scale (NAS-7)

Elphinstone et al. [16] developed the NAS-7 to assess the level of non-attachment in adult individuals. In the present study, the scale was adapted into Turkish. The scale consists of seven items and is unidimensional. Each item is rated on a 6-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating lower attachment to one’s experiences. In the original scale development study, the authors reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.84, demonstrating adequate internal consistency. Model fit indices also indicated acceptable fit: CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.06, and SRMR = 0.05. These psychometric findings provided the foundation for adapting the instrument within the scope of the current research.

Process

After obtaining permissions for the measurement tools, the scales were administered via Google Form to volunteers who provided informed consent. The study followed the Helsinki Declaration. CFA was performed on the Turkish NAS-7 factor structure using AMOS. Acceptable fit was indicated by GFI, NFI, and CFI values ≥ 0.90, RMSEA, and SRMR values ≤ 0.08 [24, 34]. To determine equivalence, we examined measurement invariance factor structure by gender. This was done to compare male and female participants. Here, configurational, metric, and scalar analyses were tested. Literature suggests that ΔCFI < 0.010 indicates measurement invariance across groups [7, 8].

IRT assessed the scale’s discrimination, difficulty, and informativeness [6]. Stata 15.0 analyzed IRT. JASP was used to calculate Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and Guttmann lambda values to evaluate the Turkish NAS-7’s reliability.

Method

Participants

There were 417 adults from 40 Turkish cities, 268 of whom were female (64.3%) and 149 male (35.7%). Of these participants, 66.2% were high school graduates, 30.5% were university graduates, 2.6% were master’s degree graduates, and the rest were primary school graduates. A large majority of these participants (86.1%) are aged 18–24, while the remaining 13.9% are older. The data came from online and social media announcements. The study included only volunteers who carefully read the scale sets’ informed consent.

Measures

Non-attachment scale (NAS-7)

This instrument was created by Elphinstone et al. [16] to measure the degree of nonattachment in adults. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient in the current adaptation study was determined to be 0.75. The scale demonstrated acceptable levels of fit, according to the fit values (in the current study, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.06, SRMR = 0.04). The scale is composed of seven items and one dimension. A six-point Likert scale is used, with 1 denoting “strongly disagree” and 6 denoting “strongly agree.” Higher scores on the scale suggest that people are less emotionally invested in their experiences.

Persevering hope scale

The original hope scale was developed by Rueger et al. [39] to measure adult agency and goal achievement. The adaptation study by Ekşi and Başol [15] introduced hope into Turkish literature and measured it. The scale’s Cronbach alpha was 0.85. Fit indices (GFI = 0.999, CFI = 1.000, TLI = 1.007, SRMR = 0.0054, RMSEA = 0.000, χ²/df = 0.469, p <.01) indicate a well-fitted scale. Four items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = very little, 3 = occasionally, 4 = frequently, and 5 = very much). A high scale score indicates optimism in the face of adversity.

The satisfaction with life scale

Dağlı and Baysal [10] conducted a study to adapt, validate, and establish the reliability of the ‘Life Satisfaction Scale’ (LSS) from Diener et al. [13] into Turkish. This study examined adult life satisfaction. Cronbach’s alpha gave the scale an internal consistency coefficient of 0.88. Fit indices showed a good fit for the scale’s factor structure: RMS EA = 0.03; SRMR = 0.019; NFI = 0.99; GFI = 0.99; and AGFI = 0.97. Only one dimension makes up the five-item scale. One indicates strong disagreement, and five indicates strong agreement on the five-point Likert scale. People with high scores are very happy with their lives.

Social connectedness scale

The scale was originally developed by Lee and Robbins [32] and later translated into Turkish by Duru [14] to assess social connectedness in adults. The instrument consists of eight items and is unidimensional. Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale in which 1 = strongly agree, 2 = mostly agree, 3 = agree, 4 = disagree, 5 = mostly disagree, and 6 = strongly disagree. Total scores range from 8 to 48, with higher scores indicating lower levels of social connectedness. In the Turkish adaptation study, the scale demonstrated strong internal consistency, with an internal reliability coefficient of 0.90. These psychometric findings support the use of the scale in assessing perceived social connectedness among adult participants.

Process

Adults’ non-attachment, life satisfaction, hope, and social connectedness were examined using normality, descriptive, reliability, and correlation analysis. Then, SEM was performed. Multiple parameter choices make SEM a powerful quantitative analysis method [31]. The study followed Kline’s (2011) two-stage SEM recommendation. Hu and Bentler’s (1999) goodness of fit indices evaluated SEM results. In addition to chi-square (χ2) and degrees of freedom, GFI, RFI, CFI, NFI, IFI, TLI, SRMR, and RMSEA values were calculated. Important values include a χ2 to df ratio < 5, GFI, RFI, CFI, NFI, IFI, and TLI values above.90, and SRMR and RMSEA values below 0.08 [24, 46]. Instead, AIC, ECVI, and the chi-square difference test determined the best SEM model. The best model has a low AIC and ECVI [1, 5].

Non-attachment, life satisfaction, hope, and social connectedness are unidimentional, so SEM parcellates them. Parcellation reduces the number of observed variables, increases reliability, and allows scales to have a normal distribution of personality trait concepts [36]. Two dimensions each for non-attachment, life satisfaction, hope and social connectedness were created with the parceling technique.

This study used SEM and bootstrapping to strongly correlate and support mediation [38]. By bootstrapping, the sample size reached 10,000, and confidence intervals were generated. The mediation is significant if confidence intervals have no zeros.

Discussion

Non-attachment in fleeting life is the ability to be present-moment conscious without emotionally investing in anyone, anything, or oneself. By doing this, they can avoid the negative effects of fleeting experiences in a world without permanence. The non-attached person sees life as a continuous process without mental obsessions. This suggests non-attached people have a clear, objective view of life, not indifference. Avoidance, change, and conditional intervention are unnecessary for them. They’re open about feelings. Similar to Bowlby’s secure attachment, non-attachment supports work differently. Cross-cultural research has shown the importance of attachment for years. Therefore, understanding nonattachment and its connections helps. Culturally specific non-attachment scales may become more popular [17]; Wang, 2016). Unattachment to Turkish culture is unmeasurable, as is well known. Turks will adopt the non-attachment scale after this study. In cultures that value strong relationships and reciprocal emotional involvement, non-attachment may not resemble emotional distance. It could mean maintaining relationships without being overly dependent or having strict expectations. This suggests that non-attachment and relationship harmony can coexist.

This study found psychometric validity and reliability in the NAS-7. Finding answers to the initial research question. Cronbach’s alpha (α), McDonald’s omega (ϲ), and Guttmann lambda (λ6) are useful reliability measures, and the NAS-7’s single-factor structure is suitable for its intended use, according to the study. Karagöz [28] says these results are reliable from the literature. This study used ICC to examine participant-item response relationships. ICC results show scale items discriminate. Discrimination power measures the likelihood of discrimination between two random samples from the target population [43]. Its strong psychometric performance suggests that the construct resonates with culturally embedded ways of relating to emotions and experiences, showing that people in this cultural setting can meaningfully distinguish non-attachment.

In the second study, hope and social connectedness mediated non-attachment and non-life satisfaction. The analysis showed hope and social connectedness, mediated simultaneously. Non-attachment predicts life satisfaction through social connectedness and hope. Social connectedness and hope may increase non-attached people’s life satisfaction. This answer addresses the second research question. Non-attachment increases life satisfaction, according to Wang et al. [48]. Research shows a positive correlation between non-attachment and well-being, as life satisfaction and well-being are often linked. Lack of attachment and life satisfaction is linked to social connectedness and hope [29, 37. There is a conceptual link between hope, social connectedness, and non-attachment, despite the fact that no research has examined it. Research is indirectly supported. The results suggest that optimism, social connection, and a lack of attachment may affect life satisfaction. These pathways may reflect cultural trends that value emotional balance, interpersonal connection, and shared optimism as well-being. Non-attachment may help one interact with people without emotional strain rather than distance oneself from them.

Practical implications

By addressing a current issue, the NAS-7 that was developed in this research elucidated a significant void in the literature. In the Turkish sample, the psychometric properties of the NAS-7 have been discussed in greater depth. In the context of this research, the discriminatory capability of the scale items was assessed through the utilization of ITS in conjunction with CFA. Furthermore, a multitude of reliability analyses were performed. As a result, it is possible to conclude that this valid and dependable scale can be frequently utilized in future research.

The findings have practical applications beyond research. Interventions that promote non-attachment may benefit mental health, education, and counseling because they boost hope and social connectedness. Recently, digital tools, mentorship, and community-building have been shown to reduce isolation and increase engagement in educational settings [47]. These similarities suggest that non-attachment-based practices may support initiatives that empower learners through connection, emotional support, and participatory learning. Both researchers and field practitioners can benefit from non-attachment on this scale. Programs to help people detach can be created. The programs explain field effects.

Theoretical implications

These findings have several theoretical implications for understanding adult non-attachment. First, the validation of the NAS-7 in Turkey demonstrates the accurate assessment of non-attachment traits across cultures, such as decreased fixation, openness, and adaptability in internal experiences. This indicates that non-attachment is a psychological characteristic rather than a cultural phenomenon. Second, mediation shows how non-attachment affects well-being through hope and social connectedness. These pathways support positive psychological resources by promoting cognitive flexibility and decreasing ego-centeredness through non-attachment, according to theory. Finally, the results distinguish non-attachment from avoidance-based constructs by emphasizing its relationship with relational openness and life satisfaction. These contributions strengthen the theory of non-attachment as an adaptive psychological process linked to well-being.

Limitations and future research

Interpret this study’s findings with caution. Measurement approach, study design, and sampling frame may affect construct, internal, and external validity. Data from self-report scales limits research. Common-method variance can increase or decrease associations with single-source self-report. Despite voluntary participation, these scales may reveal social desirability errors. Thus, future research may prefer other data collection methods.

Second, cross-sectional research makes causality challenging to prove. Mediation is a statistical association, not a temporal mechanism, limiting internal validity. Future experiments and longitudinal studies may use this study’s findings. Multi-wave longitudinal designs, preregistered analyses, or experimental manipulations targeting proposed mechanisms can better test directional effects. These designs would allow researchers to examine whether non-attachment changes hope, social connectedness, or life satisfaction over time.

Test-retest reliability was not calculated despite multiple reliability analyses, the study’s third limitation. Lack of test–retest estimates limits score temporal stability. Calculate the scale’s temporal test-retest reliability later. Measurement invariance across subgroups (e.g., gender/age) and time, as well as item-level DIF, can improve measurement validity. Normal intervals are 2–4 weeks.

Finally, only Turkish data was collected. Culture restricts generalizability beyond the sampled context. As non-attachment is universal, research could use data from different cultures. We recommend cross-cultural replications with rigorous configural/metric/scalar invariance and translation/back-translation tests. Lack of marital and socioeconomic data limits generalizability. Future research should explicitly collect and model these covariates as moderators or controls for subgroup analyses and associations. Buddhists may see different results because non-attachment is Eastern. Religiosity, contemplative practice history, and affiliation may moderate relationships and require direct measurement. Understanding cultural and religious context as theoretically meaningful variability rather than background noise may improve future findings’ interpretability. Thus, these results should be interpreted and tested in more diverse populations, taking sample demographics into account. These constraints may increase or decrease effect sizes and should be considered when assessing findings’ magnitude and generality.

Conclusion

People live in relation to things, people, and events, so nonattachment is common. Two NAS-7 adaptation stages were performed on different samples in this study. The NAS-7 was proven accurate and reliable early on. We then validated this scale’s criterion correlation validity against reliable and valid scales. The NAS-7 is psychometrically consistent, and hope and social connectedness mediate the relationship between non-attachment and life satisfaction. This suggests that life satisfaction interventions can use non-attachment, hope, and social connectedness. Future research should test causal pathways with multi-wave longitudinal and experimental designs, assess measurement invariance and generalizability across demographic groups and cultures, use multi-method assessments, and examine boundary conditions.
