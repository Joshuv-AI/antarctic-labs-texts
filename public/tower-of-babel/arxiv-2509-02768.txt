Sequential Change Detection with Differential Privacy
Liyan Xie, Ruizhi Zhang

Abstract
Sequential change detection is a fundamental problem in statistics and signal processing, with the CUSUM procedure widely used to achieve minimax detection delay under a prescribed false-alarm rate when pre- and post-change distributions are fully known. However, releasing CUSUM statistics and the corresponding stopping time directly can compromise individual data privacy. We therefore introduce a differentially private (DP) variant, called DP-CUSUM, that injects calibrated Laplace noise into both the vanilla CUSUM statistics and the detection threshold, preserving the recursive simplicity of the classical CUSUM statistics while ensuring per-sample differential privacy. We derive closed-form bounds on the average run length to false alarm and on the worst-case average detection delay, explicitly characterizing the trade-off among privacy level, false-alarm rate, and detection efficiency. Our theoretical results imply that under a weak privacy constraint, our proposed DP-CUSUM procedure achieves the same first-order asymptotic optimality as the classical, non-private CUSUM procedure. Numerical simulations are conducted to demonstrate the detection efficiency of our proposed DP-CUSUM under different privacy constraints, and the results are consistent with our theoretical findings.



I
Introduction

Sequential change detection is a fundamental problem in statistics and signal processing, with applications across a wide range of practical domains. The canonical formulation considers a sequence of observations sampled independently, with an unknown changepoint at which the underlying distribution switches from one distribution to an alternative. The goal of sequential change detection is to detect the occurrence of the distributional change with a minimal delay while controlling the false alarm rate. This problem is of major importance in many applications, such as seismic event detection
[
1
]
, quality control
[
2
]
, dynamical systems
[
3
]
, healthcare
[
4
]
, social networks
[
5
]
, anomaly detection
[
6
]
, detection of attacks
[
7
]
, etc. However, in many of these applications, the data may contain sensitive personal information
such as financial or medical records
[
8
]
, while traditional sequential detection procedures typically release the decision directly without appropriate privacy protection. Thus, procedures with
good detection ability while preserving individual’s information are highly desirable.

Since Dwork
et al
.’s pioneering work
[
9
]
, differential privacy has garnered much attention. A wide variety of differentially private procedures with theoretical efficiency guarantees have been developed for many statistical problems such as point estimation
[
8
,
10
]
and hypothesis testing
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.
Informally, the field of differential privacy provides a systematic tool for constructing private algorithms or procedures by adding designed random noise, such that the output has a similar distribution whether or not the data is present for each individual participant, thereby helping to protect the information of individuals in the dataset.

In this article, we study the problem of differentially private sequential change detection when both pre-change and post-change distributions are known and specified.
We first extend the classical notion of
\epsilon
-differential privacy
[
9
]
for fixed databases to sequential change detection tasks over potentially infinite data streams, where the sample size is not fixed.
We then develop a differentially private (DP) variant of the CUSUM procedure, called
DP-CUSUM
, which satisfies the new
\epsilon
-DP constraint. Our proposed DP-CUSUM procedure involves computing the CUSUM statistics while adding an independent Laplace noise at each time step, as well as to the detection threshold. By doing this, we do not release the CUSUM statistics or the threshold directly during the detection procedure. Meanwhile, the DP-CUSUM retains the same computational efficiency as the classical CUSUM procedure, as its computational overhead is minimal when noise is added at each time step.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows. First, we introduce a new concept of
\epsilon
-DP for sequential detection procedures when the sample size is not fixed. Second, we construct DP-CUSUM procedure that satisfies the new
\epsilon
-DP constraint. Third, under the assumption that the log-likelihood ratio statistic is always bounded by a known constant, we prove a lower bound to the average run length (ARL) of DP-CUSUM which enables an analytical way of selecting the detection threshold, and an upper bound to Lorden’s worst-case detection delay (WADD)
[
14
]
, which thus proves its asymptotic optimality under weak privacy constraints. Fourth, we extend our results to scenarios with general unbounded log-likelihood ratios by relaxing to a slightly weaker differential-privacy definition with an added small constant
\delta
in the DP definition. Finally, we validate our theoretical findings through numerical simulations, illustrating how the choice of privacy parameter affects the delay-versus-false-alarm trade-off in practical settings.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
II
provides preliminaries about sequential change detection when both pre-change and post-change distributions are fully specified, and introduces the modified concept of
\epsilon
-DP for sequential detection procedures. Section
III
presents our proposed DP-CUSUM detection procedure and the theoretical analysis when the log-likelihood function is bounded. Section
IV
extends the method and theory to the general case with unbounded log-likelihood ratios. Finally, Section
V
presents simulation examples that demonstrate the performance of the proposed DP-CUSUM procedure under distributions with both bounded and unbounded log-likelihood ratios. All proofs are presented in the Appendix.

Related Work

The study of sequential change detection can be traced back to the early work of Page
[
15
]
and has been studied extensively for several decades. Most works address the detection problem under the assumption of independent observations, particularly when new methods and theories are first introduced, but significant progress has also been made in extending these methods and theories to more complicated data models; see
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
for thorough reviews in this field.

The first optimality result for sequential change detection appears in
[
23
]
, with a focus on detecting a change in the drift of a Brownian motion. It was studied under a Bayesian framework, where the changepoint was modeled as an independent, exponentially distributed random variable. In contrast, the non‑Bayesian (minimax) setting considers the changepoint to be deterministic but unknown. Under the minimax setting, the CUSUM procedure is arguably the most widely adopted change detection algorithm for the classical setup of detecting a change from a known distribution to a known alternative
[
15
]
. The CUSUM procedure was first proved to be asymptotically optimum in
[
14
]
when observations are i.i.d. before and after the change. Its exact optimality under the same data model was established in
[
24
]
. In addition to its strong optimality guarantees, the CUSUM statistic admits a recursive update, making it computationally efficient for sequential settings that require processing each new sample immediately. Although the problem of change detection when both pre-change and post-change distributions are known is fully studied in literature, these procedures do not consider the data privacy-preserving guarantees.

First introduced by Dwork et al.
[
9
]
, differential privacy has
garnered much attention. Many classical statistical procedures
are tailored to satisfy the privacy guarantees
[
8
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
. In particular, in the area of sequential change detection, an
\epsilon
-DP procedure was proposed in
[
25
]
to estimate the change point for sequential data when both pre-change and post-change are fully specified. This method relies on a fixed‑size sliding window and applies an offline change‑point estimator within each window, which may lead to relatively higher memory and computational overhead. Extensions to unknown post-change distributions have been considered in the univariate setting in
[
26
]
. More recent efforts have explored broader data modalities and definitions of privacy. For instance, in
[
27
]
, sequential change detection for multivariate nonparametric regression was studied under local differential privacy. In
[
28
]
, the authors extended the analysis of privatized networks from static to dynamic, underscoring the complexities and challenges of preserving privacy in the dynamic analysis of network data. However, none of these work provide the theoretical detection performance of their proposed private procedures in terms of average run lengh and the worst-case detection delay, which are two fundamental but important properties for sequential change detection problem.

II
Preliminaries and Problem Setup

In this section, we provide the necessary preliminaries and background on the sequential change detection problem and differentially private tools, then introduce the problem setup for differentially private sequential change detection.

II-A

Basics of Classical Sequential Change Detection

Suppose we observe data stream
\{X_{t},t\in\mathbb{N}\}
, where each
X_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}.
Initially, observations are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) following the probability density function (pdf)
f_{0}.
At some unknown time
\tau
, an event occurs and changes the distribution of the data after the time to a distinct pdf
f_{1}.
That is,

X_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{0},&t=1,2,\ldots,\tau,\\[2.0pt]
f_{1},&t=\tau+1,\tau+2,\ldots\end{array}\right.

(1)

In this article, we assume that
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are both
known
.
Here,
\tau\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
is a deterministic but unknown change time and the goal is to raise the
alarm as quickly as possible after the change has occurred, while properly controlling the false alarm rate
[
19
,
17
,
21
]
.

A sequential change detection procedure consists of a
stopping time

T
which denotes the time we stop and declare that a change has occurred before time
T
. Here
T
is an integer-valued random variable, and the decision
\{T=t\}
is based only on the observations in the first
t
time steps. That is,
\{T=t\}\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{t},
where we define the filtration
\mathscr{F}_{t}=\sigma\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{t}\}
and let
\mathscr{F}_{0}
denote the trivial sigma-algebra.
To evaluate the performance of
T,
we further denote by
\mathbf{P}_{\infty}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\infty}
the probability measure and corresponding expectation when all samples follow the pre-change distribution
f_{0}
(i.e., the change occurs at
\infty
). Similarly, we use
\mathbf{P}_{0}
and
\mathbf{E}_{0}
to represent the probability measure and expectation when all samples follow the post-change distribution
f_{1}
(i.e., the change occurs at 0). More generally, we denote by
\mathbf{P}_{\tau}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\tau}
the probability measure and expectation when the change happens at time
\tau
. Under the classical minimax formulation for the sequential change detection problem
[
14
,
24
]
, the optimal detection procedure is the one solving the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\inf_{T}\,\text{WADD}(T):=\sup_{\tau\geq 0}\,\essup\mathbf{E}_{\tau}[(T-\tau)^{+}|\mathscr{F}_{\tau}]

(2)

\displaystyle\text{subject~to:}~\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[T]\geq\gamma>1.

That is, among all stopping times that have an average false alarm period (also known as
average run length
, ARL) no smaller than a pre-specified constant
\gamma>1
, the optimal procedure should have the smallest
worst-case average detection delay
(WADD). Here we adopt Lorden’s definition of WADD, which takes the supremum, over all possible changepoints
\tau
, of the expected detection delay conditioned on the worst possible realizations before the change.

In the literature, it has been shown that the classical CUSUM procedure can solve the optimization problem (
2
)
[
14
,
24
]
. To be more concrete, the CUSUM statistic
\{S_{t},t\geq 1\}
corresponds to the maximum log-likelihood ratio over all possible changepoints
up to time
t
and can be calculated by a recursive form:

\displaystyle S_{t}=\max_{1\leq k\leq t}\sum_{j=k}^{t}\ell(X_{j})=\max\left(0,S_{t-1}\right)+\ell(X_{t}),~S_{0}=0.

(3)

where
\ell(X)=\log(f_{1}(X)/f_{0}(X))
is the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) function between
f_{1}
and
f_{0}
. The CUSUM procedure is then defined as the first time when the CUSUM statistic exceeds some pre-defined
threshold
b
. That is, the CUSUM procedure is given by

T(b)=\inf\{t>0:S_{t}\geq b\}.

(4)

For completeness, we provide in the following Lemma an asymptotic expression for the performance of the CUSUM procedure, which also serves as the information-theoretic lower bound for the WADD in problem (
2
). The proof of the following Lemma can be found in
[
29
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 1

(Performance of exact CUSUM
[
29
]
)
.

For threshold
b=b_{\gamma}=\log\gamma
, the CUSUM procedure in (
4
) satisfies

\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[T(b_{\gamma})]\geq\gamma,~~~~\mathbf{E}_{0}[T(b_{\gamma})]=\frac{\log\gamma}{I_{0}}(1+o(1)),

(5)

where
I_{0}=\mathbf{E}_{0}\left[\ell(X)\right]
is the Kullback-Leibler information number (divergence) of the post- and pre-change distributions.

From Lemma
1
we conclude that by applying the CUSUM procedure defined in (
4
) with threshold
b=\log\gamma
, the corresponding CUSUM stopping time
T
enjoys an asymptotic performance
captured by (
5
). By the optimality of CUSUM procedure
[
24
]
, no other stopping time
T^{\prime}
that satisfies the same false alarm constraint can have a limiting value for the ratio
\mathbb{E}_{0}[T^{\prime}]/\frac{\log\gamma}{I_{0}}
that is smaller than 1 as
\gamma\to\infty
.

II-B

Differentially Private Sequential Change Detection

While the classical CUSUM procedure achieves optimality in detecting distributional changes under the assumption of full data access, it may not be suitable in applications where individual-level data must remain private. In such scenarios, it is desirable to design change detection algorithms that also preserve the privacy of individuals contributing to the data stream. A principled way to achieve this is through the framework of differential privacy (DP)
[
9
,
30
]
.

Let us first review the classical definition of
\epsilon
-DP algorithms in the literature.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be the data domain (e.g.,
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
), and let a database
D=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
consist of
n
entries drawn from
\mathcal{X}
. Considering a random algorithm that maps from the database space
\mathcal{X}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R},
we say the algorithm is differentially
private if the outputs have similar distributions for neighboring datasets that we want to make it hard to distinguish. Here, two databases
D,D^{\prime}
are neighboring if they differ in at most
one
entry. We now introduce the formal definition of differential privacy
[
9
]
.

Definition 1

(
\epsilon
-DP)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\epsilon
-differentially private (DP) if for every pair of neighboring databases
D,D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n},
and for every subset of possible events
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R},

\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A}(D)\in\mathcal{S})\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}).

We should emphasize that in the definition of
\epsilon
-DP, the expectation is take over the randomness of the algorithm
\mathcal{A},
while the two databases
D
and
D^{\prime}
are fixed. One common technique to achieve
\epsilon
-differential privacy is
by adding a Laplace noise
[
30
]
. Specifically, for a real-valued deterministic function
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},
define its sensitivity as
\Delta(L)=\max_{D,D^{\prime}\text{are neighbors}}|L(D)-L(D^{\prime})|.
Then, a randomized
\epsilon
-DP algorithm can be obtained by adding an independent Laplace random noise
\text{Lap}(\Delta(L)/\epsilon)
to the realization of the
statistic
L(D).
That is,
\tilde{L}(D)=L(D)+\text{Lap}(\Delta(L)/\epsilon)
is a randomized algorithm satisfying the
\epsilon
-DP constraint. Here, we denote
\text{Lap}(\beta)
as the Laplace random variable with zero mean and scale parameter
\beta>0
, with probability density function
f_{\text{Lap}(\beta)}(x)=\exp(-|x|/\beta)/(2\beta).

In particular, for our problem of sequential change detection with
positive integer output in
\mathbb{Z}^{+},
we can modify the general definition of
\epsilon
-DP to obtain the following definition of
\epsilon
-DP sequential detection procedure. Let
X_{(1:t)}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t})
denote the sequence of observations up to time
t
, and let
X^{\prime}_{(1:t)}
be a neighboring dataset that differs from
X_{(1:t)}
in exactly one entry (i.e., they differ in at most one
X_{i}
for some
i\in[t]
). We now present the formal definition below.

Definition 2

(
\epsilon
-DP Sequential Detection Procedure)
.

A randomized sequential change detection procedure with stopping time
\mathcal{T}
is said to be
\epsilon
-differentially private, if for every pair of neighboring data streams
X_{(1:n)},X^{\prime}_{(1:n)}
(differing in at most one entry), the distribution over the randomized stopping time
\mathcal{T}
satisfies the differential privacy constraint,

\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{T}=n\mid X_{(1:n)})\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{T}=n\mid X^{\prime}_{(1:n)}),\quad\forall\,n\geq 1,

(6)

where the probability
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
is taken over the randomness in
\mathcal{T}.

Here, a larger value of
\epsilon
implies a weaker privacy constraint. Intuitively, the above definition means that altering any single observation in the data stream only slightly affects the distribution of the randomized stopping time
\mathcal{T}
. Therefore, one cannot easily infer individual data values from the detection output
\mathcal{T}
. Our goal is to devise a sequential detection procedure that satisfies the
\epsilon
-DP constraint in (
6
) while maintaining good detection performance with a small detection delay when the average run length is controlled. In the next section, we propose to first construct an
\epsilon
-DP sequential detection procedure based on the classical CUSUM procedure. Then, we will study the theoretical detection properties of our proposed procedure with the emphasis on how the privacy constraint parameter
\epsilon
affects its detection performance.

III
Proposed DP-CUSUM Procedure with Bounded Log-Likelihood Ratio

In this section, we design a differentially private variant of the CUSUM procedure that satisfies the sequential differential privacy constraint in (
6
) while retaining strong detection performance guarantees. We first define the sensitivity of the log-likelihood function
\ell(X)
as follows.

Definition 3

(Sensitivity of
\ell
)
.

The sensitivity of the log-likelihood ratio function
\ell(\cdot)
is defined as

\Delta=\Delta(\ell):=\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left|\ell(x)-\ell(y)\right|=\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}|\log\frac{f_{1}(x)}{f_{0}(x)}-\log\frac{f_{1}(y)}{f_{0}(y)}|.

In this section, we first consider the simpler case where
\Delta
is bounded. This assumption holds for a broad class of distributions, including any pair of pre‑ and post‑change distributions that are both discrete with the same support (e.g., Bernoulli, Binomial) or certain continuous distributions (e.g., Laplace). We will extend our method and analysis to more general distributions where
\Delta
is unbounded, under a relaxed differential privacy constraint in Section
IV
.

We define the randomized CUSUM statistic
\{\tilde{S}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
recursively as

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{t}

\displaystyle=S_{t}+Z_{t},

(7)

where
S_{t}
is the classical CUSUM statistics from (
3
), and
Z_{t}\sim\text{Lap}(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon})
are i.i.d Laplace noise at each time step
t
and they are also independent to all of data sequence
\{X_{t},t\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Then, our proposed private detection procedure is defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(b)

\displaystyle=\inf\{t>0:\tilde{S}_{t}\geq b+W\},

(8)

where
W\sim\text{Lap}(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon})
is an independent Laplace noise term added to the threshold, fixed upon generation, and
b
is a pre-specified deterministic threshold chosen by satisfying the false alarm rate constraint. The DP-CUSUM procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
. Note that the DP‑CUSUM procedure incurs only one additional Laplace‑noise sample per time step beyond the classical CUSUM updates. Hence, its overall time complexity remains
O(t)
as in the non-private case.

Algorithm 1

DP-CUSUM Procedure

0:

Data sequence
\{X_{t},t\in\mathbb{N}\}
, privacy parameter
\epsilon
, sensitivity
\Delta
of LLR
\ell
, threshold
b
.

0:

Stopping time
\tilde{T}(b)
.

1:

Sample
W\sim\text{Lap}\left(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon}\right)
.

2:

Initialize
t\leftarrow 0
,
S_{0}\leftarrow 0
,
\tilde{S}_{0}\leftarrow-\infty
.

3:

while

\tilde{S}_{t}<b+W

do

4:

t\leftarrow t+1
and observe a new data
X_{t}
.

5:

Update classical CUSUM statistic
S_{t}=\max(0,S_{t-1})+\ell(X_{t})
.

6:

Sample
Z_{t}\sim\text{Lap}\left(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon}\right)
.

7:

Compute privatized statistic
\tilde{S}_{t}=S_{t}+Z_{t}
.

8:

end

while

9:

Output stopping time
\tilde{T}(b)=t
; declare that a change has occurred before time
\tilde{T}(b)
.

We then present the following theorem, which guarantees that the proposed DP-CUSUM procedure, characterized by its stopping time
\tilde{T}(b)
, satisfies the
\epsilon
-differential privacy condition in (
6
). The proof follows the idea of AboveThreshold in
[
30
]
and can be found in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 1

(DP Guarantee)
.

Assume
\Delta
is bounded. The DP-CUSUM procedure
\tilde{T}(b)
is
\epsilon
-DP satisfying (
6
).

It is worthwhile mentioning that, compared to the existing private change detection procedure proposed in
[
25
]
, we require a smaller Laplace noise
Z_{t},W\sim\text{Lap}(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon})
added to both the CUSUM detection statistics and detection threshold, whereas the method in
[
25
]
added
Z_{t}\sim\text{Lap}(\frac{4\Delta}{\epsilon})
to the detection statistics and
W\sim\text{Lap}(\frac{8\Delta}{\epsilon})
to detection threshold. Such reduced noise can significantly improve the detection performance while still achieving
\epsilon
-DP, since smaller Laplace noise means less degradation in detection performance, as validated in our numerical comparisons in Section
V
.

Then, we analyze the theoretical detection performance of the proposed DP-CUSUM procedure
\tilde{T}(b)
. The following theorem provides a lower bound to the ARL of DP-CUSUM procedure.

Theorem 2

(ARL of DP-CUSUM)
.

Assume
\Delta
is bounded. For any
\epsilon>0
and threshold
b>0
, define the following quantity

h(\epsilon,\Delta)=\min\{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta},1\}.

(9)

Then we have

\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}(b)]\geq\frac{e^{h(\epsilon,\Delta)b-2}}{4(b+1)^{2}}.

(10)

The lower bound in (
10
) provides an analytical way to select the threshold without extensive simulation. That is, for any desired ARL
\gamma
, one can numerically solve for
b
such that the lower bound in (
10
) is no smaller than
\gamma
.
Furthermore, by Theorem
2
, we can see by setting the threshold
b
such that

\frac{e^{h(\epsilon,\Delta)b-2}}{4(b+1)^{2}}=\gamma\Rightarrow b=b_{\gamma}=\frac{\log\gamma+O(\log\log\gamma)}{h(\epsilon,\Delta)}=\frac{\log\gamma}{h(\epsilon,\Delta)}(1+o(1)),

(11)

our proposed procedure
\tilde{T}(b_{\gamma})
satisfies the ARL constraint
\mathbf{E}_{\infty}(\tilde{T}(b_{\gamma}))\geq\gamma.

We next analyze the worst-case average detection delay (WADD) of DP-CUSUM. In the following Lemma, we first show that the worst case is attained when the change occurs at time
\tau=0
, which is a useful property also enjoyed by the classical CUSUM procedure. Due to such property, it suffices to compute
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\tilde{T}(b)]
when evaluating the worst-case delay, without the need to enumerate all possible changepoint locations.

Lemma 2

(Worst-Case Average Detection Delay)
.

For any changepoint
\tau\geq 0
, we have that

\text{WADD}(\tilde{T}(b))\leq\mathbf{E}_{0}[\tilde{T}(b)].

(12)

We are now ready to analyze an upper bound for the WADD of our proposed DP-CUSUM procedure. By Lemma
2
, it suffices to establish an upper bound on
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\tilde{T}(b)]
. However, deriving such a bound is nontrivial due to the update rule, which includes both truncation at zero and the addition of Laplace noise at each time step. Since we only aim to find a suitable upper bound to the delay, we introduce an alternative stopping time in the proof that is guaranteed to incur a longer delay than
\tilde{T}(b)
but is easier to analyze. The details can be found in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 3

(WADD of DP-CUSUM)
.

Assume
\Delta
is bounded. We have for any
b>8I_{0},

\mathbf{E}_{0}[\tilde{T}(b)]\leq\frac{b}{I_{0}}+\frac{4\Delta}{I_{0}^{3/2}\epsilon}\sqrt{b}+C,

(13)

where
I_{0}=\mathbf{E}_{0}\left[\ell(X)\right]
is the Kullback-Leibler information number (divergence) of the post- and pre-change distributions, and
C
is a constant that does not depend on the threshold
b
but depends on
\Delta,\epsilon,I_{0}.

Setting the threshold
b=b_{\gamma}
as in (
11
), our proposed DP-CUSUM
\tilde{T}(b_{\gamma})
satisfies
\mathbf{E}_{\infty}(\tilde{T}(b_{\gamma}))\geq\gamma
and, by Lemma
2
and Theorem
3
, its WADD satisfies

\displaystyle\text{WADD}(\tilde{T}(b_{\gamma}))=\mathbf{E}_{0}[\tilde{T}(b_{\gamma})]\leq\frac{\log\gamma}{h(\epsilon,\Delta)I_{0}}(1+o(1)).

(14)

Note
h(\epsilon,\Delta)=\min\{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta},1\}
. When
\epsilon\geq 2\Delta
, our proposed DP-CUSUM yields the same order of detection delay as the CUSUM procedure in Lemma
1
. This result implies that, under the relatively weaker privacy requirement (
\epsilon\geq 2\Delta
), our proposed DP-CUSUM procedure achieves first-order
asymptotic optimality
while maintaining the privacy constraint, i.e., its detection delay asymptotically matches that of the classical CUSUM as ARL grows.

However, when
\epsilon<2\Delta
, the upper bound we established for the WADD of DP-CUSUM becomes
\frac{\log\gamma}{\epsilon/2\Delta\cdot I_{0}}(1+o(1))
, which is greater than the optimal detection delay
\frac{\log\gamma}{I_{0}}(1+o(1))
of the classical (non-private) CUSUM. This implies that our proposed DP-CUSUM will still keep the data privacy under the stronger privacy constraint at the price of losing some detection efficiency. Moreover, it remains an open question what the tight information-theoretic lower bound for the WADD is under the
\epsilon
-DP constraint (
6
) when
\epsilon<2\Delta
, and whether DP-CUSUM retains asymptotic optimality in this regime. We leave this as a direction for future work.

IV
Proposed
\delta
-DP-CUSUM Procedure with Unbounded Log-Likelihood Ratio

In the previous sections, we assumed that the LLR
\ell(x)
is bounded, which ensures a finite global sensitivity
\Delta<\infty
and allows the DP-CUSUM procedure proposed in (
7
) and (
8
) to achieve
\epsilon
-differential privacy. However, in many practical scenarios—particularly when dealing with continuous distributions such as Gaussian or exponential families—the LLR
\ell(x)
may be unbounded, and the global sensitivity
\Delta
becomes infinite.

To address this, in this section, we adopt a relaxed version of differential privacy by allowing the user to select a small probability
\delta
of failure, resulting in a relaxed
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy definition
[
25
]
. In this setting, we continue to use the DP-CUSUM procedure but modify the sensitivity parameter in Algorithm
1
to depend on the underlying data distributions and the user-defined privacy constraint
\delta
. This distribution-dependent choice ensures that the relaxed
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy condition is satisfied, as formalized below. To distinguish it from the vanilla DP‑CUSUM in the finite-
\Delta
case, we refer to this modified procedure as
\delta
-DP-CUSUM.

We first present the
\delta
-DP-CUSUM procedure when the sensitivity of
\ell(x)
is unbounded. For a given
\delta>0
, define

\displaystyle A_{\delta}=\inf\{t:\max_{i=0,1}\,\mathbf{P}_{X\sim f_{i}}\left(2|\ell(X)|\geq t\right)\leq\delta/2\}.

(15)

That is,
A_{\delta}
guarantees that the event
2|\ell(X)|\geq A_{\delta}
occurs with probability at most
\delta/2
under both the pre- and post-change distributions.
We then apply the DP-CUSUM procedure as described in the previous section or Algorithm
1
, with the sensitivity parameter
\Delta
replaced by the distribution-dependent quantity
A_{\delta}
. The following theorem establishes that this modified procedure satisfies a relaxed
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy guarantee.

Theorem 4

((
\epsilon,\delta
)-DP guarantee)
.

When the LLR is unbounded, the stopping time
\tilde{T}
in (
7
) and (
8
) with the choice of
\Delta=A_{\delta}
satisfies for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and every pair of neighboring data streams
X_{(1:t)},X^{\prime}_{(1:t)}
that differs in only one entry
X_{k}\neq X_{k}^{\prime}
, the distribution over the stopping time
\tilde{T}
satisfies the following weaker differential privacy constraint:

\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{T},X_{k},X^{\prime}_{k}}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X_{(1:t)\setminus k})\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{T},X_{k},X^{\prime}_{k}}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X^{\prime}_{(1:t)\setminus k})+\delta,

(16)

where the probability is taken over the randomness in
\tilde{T}
and the differing entry
X_{k},X_{k}^{\prime},
while the rest of data entries are fixed .

We would like to emphasize that this relaxed
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy guarantee in (
16
) is not the same as the classical definition of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP in literature
[
9
]
, which considers the probability taken over the randomness in the random algorithm while the database is fixed. Here in this slightly modified version (
16
), we use a small probability
\delta
to capture the failure case when the LLR at differing entries,
|\ell(X_{k})-\ell(X^{\prime}_{k})|
, is greater than
A_{\delta}
; we will still achieve
\epsilon
-DP conditional on this failure event not occurring, and
\delta
accounts for such failures. To avoid confusion, we named our proposed procedure as
\delta
-DP-CUSUM procedure instead of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP-CUSUM procedure.

We then extend our analysis in Section
III
to the current setting where the LLR
\ell(\cdot)
is unbounded. Following similar proof strategies as in Section
III
, we derive a lower bound for the ARL and an upper bound for the WADD of our
\delta
-DP-CUSUM procedure, which satisfies the relaxed
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy guarantee.

Corollary 1

(ARL of
\delta
-DP-CUSUM)
.

For the general case with unbounded LLR, for any
\epsilon>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and the corresponding
A_{\delta}
defined in (
15
),
we have

\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}(b)]\geq\frac{e^{h(\epsilon,A_{\delta})b-2}}{4(b+1)^{2}},

(17)

where
h(\epsilon,A_{\delta})=\min\{\frac{\epsilon}{2A_{\delta}},1\}
.

Proof.

Note that the proof of Theorem
2
does not require the log-likelihood ratio
\ell(\cdot)
to be bounded. Consequently, the same argument still applies by replacing
\Delta
with
A_{\delta}
.
∎

Corollary 2

(WADD of
\delta
-DP-CUSUM)
.

For the general case with unbounded LLR, assume the log-likelihood
\ell(X)
is
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian under both the pre- and post-change distributions. That is, for
i=0,1,
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

\mathbf{E}_{X\sim f_{i}}[e^{\lambda(\ell(X)-\mathbf{E}[\ell(X)])}]\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}}.

Then, for any
\epsilon>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and the corresponding
A_{\delta}
defined in (
15
), we have the worst-case average detection delay satisfies

\text{WADD}(\tilde{T}(b))\leq\frac{b}{I_{0}}+\frac{4A_{\delta}}{I_{0}^{3/2}\epsilon}\sqrt{b}+C,

(18)

where
C
is a constant that does not depend on the threshold
b
but depends on
\epsilon,\delta,I_{0},\sigma^{2}
.

Similar to Section
III
, the lower bound in (
17
) can be used to select the threshold
b
as

b=b_{\gamma}=\frac{\log\gamma+O(\log\log\gamma)}{h(\epsilon,A_{\delta})}=\frac{\log\gamma}{h(\epsilon,A_{\delta})}(1+o(1)),

(19)

to guarantee the ARL constraint
\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}]\geq\gamma
is met. Under this choice of threshold, the resulting WADD of the
\delta
-DP-CUSUM procedure is upper bounded by

\text{WADD}(\tilde{T}(b_{\gamma}))\leq\frac{\log\gamma}{h(\epsilon,A_{\delta})I_{0}}(1+o(1)),

(20)

which provides implications on how the privacy parameters
\epsilon
and
\delta
affect the detection performance of our proposed
\delta
-DP-CUSUM procedure. In particular, note
h(\epsilon,A_{\delta})\leq 1.

Our proposed procedure will achieve the first-order
asymptotical optimality
as the classical CUSUM procedure if
\epsilon\geq 2A_{\delta}.
On the other hand, if
\epsilon<2A_{\delta}

\frac{\log\gamma}{\epsilon/2A_{\delta}\cdot I_{0}}(1+o(1))
, which is greater than the optimal detection delay
\frac{\log\gamma}{I_{0}}(1+o(1))
of classical CUSUM. Therefore, a smaller value of

implies greater degradation in detection performance due to the privacy requirement. We further provide a Gaussian example below to illustrate how the privacy parameters
\epsilon
and

We emphasize that

\delta

VI
Conclusion

We introduce a theoretically grounded DP-CUSUM procedure for sequential change detection with differential privacy constraints. We derive the lower bound to the average run length and the upper bound to the worst-case average detection delay of DP-CUSUM. The theoretical results also yield the first-order asymptotic optimality of DP-CUSUM under weak privacy requirements. Experimental results on synthetic data demonstrate the good performance of our DP-CUSUM procedure compared with the baseline method and confirm our theoretical findings.
Our theoretical analysis provides analytical techniques for future extensions and the design of DP-CUSUM variants across diverse practical applications. Future work includes adapting this framework to more complex settings, such as unknown post-change distributions, multi-sensor detection with controlled sensing, network-structured data, and related applications.

Lemma 3

For a pair of neighboring sequence

\tilde{S}_{1}=\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{k-1}=\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{k-1};\tilde{S}_{k}>\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{k},\tilde{S}_{k+1}\geq\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{k+1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{t-1}\geq\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{t-1}
. Therefore, we have
\max_{1\leq j\leq t-1}\tilde{S^{\prime}_{j}}-\max_{1\leq j\leq t-1}\tilde{S_{j}}\leq 0
. Moreover, we have

|\max_{1\leq j\leq t-1}\tilde{S^{\prime}_{j}}-\max_{1\leq j\leq t-1}\tilde{S_{j}}|\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq t-1}|\tilde{S^{\prime}_{j}}-\tilde{S_{j}}|=\max_{1\leq j\leq t-1}|{S^{\prime}_{j}}-{S_{j}}|\leq\Delta,

by Lemma
3
in Appendix
A
; thus
|\hat{w}-w|\leq\Delta
. Meanwhile, we have
S_{t}-S_{t}^{\prime}\in[0,\Delta]
. Combining these together, we have
|\hat{z}-z|\leq\Delta
as well.

We can continue to Eq. (
B
) as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{Z_{t},W}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X^{1:t})

\displaystyle\overset{(iv)}{=}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{Z_{t}}(\hat{z}-\delta_{0})f_{W}(\hat{w}-\delta_{1})\mathbbm{1}[\hat{w}-\delta_{1}\in(\max_{1\leq j\leq t-1}{\tilde{S_{j}}}-b,{S}_{t}+\hat{z}-\delta_{0}-b]]\,d\hat{w}d\hat{z}}

\displaystyle\overset{(v)}{\leq}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\epsilon/2}f_{Z_{t}}(\hat{z})e^{\epsilon/2}f_{W}(\hat{w})\mathbbm{1}[\hat{w}\in(\max_{1\leq j\leq t-1}\tilde{S^{\prime}_{j}}-b,S_{t}^{\prime}+\hat{z}-b]]\,d\hat{w}d\hat{z}

\displaystyle\overset{(vi)}{=}e^{\epsilon}\mathbf{P}_{Z_{t},W}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X^{\prime}_{(1:t)}).

Here
(iv)
is by change of variables
\hat{z}=z+\delta_{0}
and
\hat{w}=w+\delta_{1}
,
(v)
is due to
|\delta_{0}|\leq\Delta
,
|\delta_{1}|\leq\Delta
, and if
Y\sim\text{Lap}(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon})
, then
\frac{f_{Y}(y)}{f_{Y}(y^{\prime})}=e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}(|y|-|y^{\prime}|)}\leq e^{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}|y-y^{\prime}|}
, and
(vi)
is due to the same derivation as in the derivation of Eq. (
B
).
This concludes the proof that
\tilde{T}
is
\epsilon
-DP.
∎

Proof of Theorm
2
.

We fix the threshold
b>0
and write
\tilde{T}=\tilde{T}(b)
for simplicity. Condition on
W=w
for some fixed
w\geq 0
, we first compute
\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]
and then apply the law of total expectation to compute
\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}]
. For any
x>0,
and any
\lambda\in(0,1),
by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]

\displaystyle\geq x\mathbf{P}_{\infty}(\tilde{T}\geq x|W=w)

\displaystyle=x(1-\mathbf{P}_{\infty}(\tilde{T}<x|W=w))

\displaystyle=x\left(1-\mathbf{P}_{\infty}(S_{n}+Z_{n}\geq b+w,\,\text{for some $1\leq n\leq x$})\right)

\displaystyle\geq x(1-\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor x\rfloor}\mathbf{P}_{\infty}(S_{n}+Z_{n}\geq b+w))

\displaystyle\geq x(1-\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor x\rfloor}e^{-\lambda(b+w)}\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[e^{\lambda Z_{n}}]\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[e^{\lambda S_{n}}])

\displaystyle\geq x(1-xe^{-\lambda(b+w)}\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[e^{\lambda Z}]\frac{1}{1-\lambda}),

where the last inequality due to
\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[e^{\lambda S_{n}}]\leq\frac{1}{1-\lambda}
(as shown in Corollary
3
) and
\lfloor x\rfloor\leq x
.

Note that for any
u>0,
the function
x(1-xu)
is maximized at
x=1/(2u)
with the maximum value
1/(4u)
. Thus, taking
x=1/(2e^{-\lambda(b+w)}\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[e^{\lambda Z}]\frac{1}{1-\lambda})
yields,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{e^{\lambda(b+w)}}{\mathbf{E}[e^{\lambda Z}]}.

Since
Z\sim\text{Lap}(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon})
, we have for any
0<\lambda<\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}
,
\mathbf{E}[e^{\lambda Z}]=\frac{1}{1-4\Delta^{2}\lambda^{2}/\epsilon^{2}}
. Substituting this into the expression above, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{\lambda(b+w)}}{4}(1-\lambda)(1-(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon}\lambda)^{2})\geq\frac{e^{\lambda(b+w)}}{4}(1-\lambda)(1-\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon}\lambda),

where the last inequality is due to
\lambda<\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}
.

We then consider the following two cases.

1.

If
\epsilon\leq 2\Delta
, we have
1-\lambda\geq 1-\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon}\lambda
and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{\lambda(b+w)}}{4}(1-\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon}\lambda)^{2}.

Taking the logarithm and differentiating with respect to
\lambda
, we find the maximizer of the right-hand side is
\lambda^{*}=\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}-\frac{2}{b+w}
and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]

\displaystyle\geq e^{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}(b+w)-2}(\frac{2\Delta}{(b+w)\epsilon})^{2}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]f_{W}(w)dw

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}(b+w)-2}(\frac{2\Delta}{(b+w)\epsilon})^{2}f_{W}(w)dw

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}b-2}(\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon})^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}w}\frac{1}{(b+w)^{2}}\frac{\epsilon}{4\Delta}e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}w}dw

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon b}\cdot e^{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}b-2}\geq\frac{e^{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}b-2}}{2b}.

2.

If
\epsilon>2\Delta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{\lambda(b+w)}}{4}(1-\lambda)^{2}.

Similarly, the maximizer of the right-hand side is
\lambda^{*}=1-2/(b+w)
and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]

\displaystyle\geq e^{b+w-2}\frac{1}{(b+w)^{2}}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}|W=w]f_{W}(w)dw\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{b+w-2}\frac{1}{(b+w)^{2}}f_{W}(w)dw

\displaystyle=e^{b-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{w}\frac{1}{(b+w)^{2}}\frac{\epsilon}{4\Delta}e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}w}dw=\frac{\epsilon}{4\Delta}e^{b-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(b+w)^{2}}e^{-(\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}-1)w}dw

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon}{4\Delta}e^{b-2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(b+w)^{2}}e^{-(\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}-1)w}dw\geq\frac{\epsilon}{4\Delta}e^{b-2}\frac{1}{(b+1)^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}w}dw

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{4\Delta}e^{b-2}\frac{1}{(b+1)^{2}}\frac{2\Delta}{\epsilon}(1-e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2\Delta}})\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{b-2}\frac{1}{(b+1)^{2}}(1-e^{-1})\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{e^{b-2}}{(b+1)^{2}}.

Combining these two cases together completes the proof.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let the changepoint be
\tau=0
, i.e., all samples are drawn from the post-change distribution. Condition on
W=w
for any
w\geq 0
, we denote
\tilde{b}:=b+w>0
, define an alternative process
U_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\ell(X_{i}),t=1,2,\ldots
, with

, and define another stopping time

by

\displaystyle\nu=\inf\{t\geq 1:U_{t}+Z_{t}\geq\tilde{b}\}.

(23)

Here

is the same Laplace noise as in (
7
).
Obviously we have
U_{t}\leq S_{t}
, thus
U_{t}+Z_{t}\leq S_{t}+Z_{t}=\tilde{S}_{t}
and
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\tilde{T}(b)|W=w]\leq\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]
. In the following, we derive an upper bound for
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]
. Note that for notational simplicity, we omit the conditioning on
W=w
in the following proofs unless explicitly stated otherwise.

Step 1: We first show
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]<\infty
for any fixed
b>0
. Note that for any integer
t\geq 1
and
\lambda\in(0,\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{2}\Delta}]
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(\nu\geq t+1)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}_{0}(U_{t}+Z_{t}<\tilde{b})\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda(U_{t}+Z_{t})}]}{e^{-\lambda\tilde{b}}}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\tilde{b}}\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda Z_{t}}]\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\ell(X_{i}))}]

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\tilde{b}}\frac{1}{1-4\Delta^{2}\lambda^{2}/\epsilon^{2}}(\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda\ell(X_{1})}])^{t}

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{\lambda\tilde{b}}(\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda\ell(X_{1})}])^{t},

where the last inequality is due to
1-4\Delta^{2}\lambda^{2}/\epsilon^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
when
\lambda\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{2}\Delta}
.
Recall that
\ell(x)
is bounded with range
\Delta
, and
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\ell(X_{1})]=I_{0}>0
, thus by Hoeffding’s inequality we have
\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda\ell(X_{1})}]\leq e^{-\lambda I_{0}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8}}
.
Substitute into the previous inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(\nu\geq t+1)

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{\lambda\tilde{b}}e^{-t(\lambda I_{0}-\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}.

Now take
\lambda=\min\{\frac{4I_{0}}{\Delta^{2}},\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{2}\Delta}\}
, we have
C_{1}:=\lambda I_{0}-\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8}>0
, and
\mathbf{P}_{0}(\nu\geq t+1)\leq 2e^{\lambda\tilde{b}}e^{-tC_{1}}
. Thus we have
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(\nu\geq t)<\infty
.

Step 2: Since
\ell(X_{1}),\ell(X_{2}),\ldots
are i.i.d. with
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\ell(X_{1})]=I_{0}
,
U_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\ell(X_{i})
, and
\nu
is a stopping time with
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]<\infty
, by Wald’s equation
[
32
, Theorem 4.8.6]
, we have
\mathbf{E}_{0}[U_{\nu}]=\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]\mathbf{E}_{0}[\ell(X_{1})]=\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]I_{0}
. We rewrite it as follows,

\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]=\frac{\mathbf{E}_{0}[U_{\nu}]}{I_{0}}=\frac{\mathbf{E}_{0}[U_{\nu}+Z_{\nu}-\tilde{b}]+\mathbf{E}_{0}[\tilde{b}-Z_{\nu}]}{I_{0}}=\frac{\tilde{b}+\mathbf{E}_{0}[U_{\nu}+Z_{\nu}-\tilde{b}]+\mathbf{E}_{0}[-Z_{\nu}]}{I_{0}},

(24)

where in the numerator, the first expectation is for the so-called overshoot
U_{\nu}+Z_{\nu}-\tilde{b}\geq 0
, and the second expectation is for the added Laplace noise
Z_{\nu}
at the stopping time.

We assume
Z_{0}=0
. Note that by definition of
\nu
,
U_{\nu-1}+Z_{\nu-1}<\tilde{b}
, thus the overshoot is upper-bounded as follows

\displaystyle U_{\nu}+Z_{\nu}-\tilde{b}

\displaystyle=U_{\nu-1}+\ell(X_{\nu})+Z_{\nu}-\tilde{b}+Z_{\nu-1}-Z_{\nu-1}

\displaystyle=U_{\nu-1}+Z_{\nu-1}-\tilde{b}+\ell(X_{\nu})+Z_{\nu}-Z_{\nu-1}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta+Z_{\nu}-Z_{\nu-1}.

Substitute into Eq. (
24
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]\leq\frac{\tilde{b}+\Delta+\mathbf{E}_{0}[Z_{\nu}-Z_{\nu-1}]+\mathbf{E}_{0}[-Z_{\nu}]}{I_{0}}=\frac{\tilde{b}+\Delta+\mathbf{E}_{0}[-Z_{\nu-1}]}{I_{0}}\leq\frac{\tilde{b}+\Delta+\mathbf{E}_{0}[|Z_{\nu-1}|]}{I_{0}}.

(25)

Step 3: We now derive an upper bound for
\mathbf{E}_{0}[|Z_{\nu-1}|]
.
Let
m_{0}=\lfloor\frac{\tilde{b}}{2I_{0}}\rfloor-1
and
m_{1}=\lfloor\frac{2\tilde{b}}{I_{0}}\rfloor+1
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[|Z_{\nu-1}|]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}_{0}|Z_{i}\mathbbm{1}(\nu=i+1)|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}\mathbf{E}_{0}\left(|Z_{i-1}|\mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(X_{j})+Z_{i}\geq\tilde{b})\right)}_{\text{Part I}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i={m_{0}}}^{m_{1}}\mathbf{E}_{0}|Z_{i}\mathbbm{1}(\nu=i+1)|}_{\text{Part II}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\underbrace{\sum_{i=m_{1}+1}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}_{0}\left(|Z_{i}|\mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(X_{j})+Z_{i}<\tilde{b})\right)}_{\text{Part III}}.

For Part I, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and for any
\lambda\in(0,\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{2}\Delta}]
, we have for any
i\leq m_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}\left(|Z_{i-1}|\mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(X_{j})+Z_{i}\geq\tilde{b})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbf{E}_{0}|Z_{i-1}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(X_{j})+Z_{i}\geq\tilde{b})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{E}_{0}e^{\lambda\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(X_{j})+Z_{i}\right)}}{e^{\lambda\tilde{b}}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})\sqrt{\frac{e^{(\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8}+I_{0}\lambda)i-\lambda\tilde{b}}}{1-4\Delta^{2}\lambda^{2}/\epsilon^{2}}}\leq 4(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}\tilde{b}}e^{\frac{1}{2}(I_{0}\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})i},

where the last inequality is due to
1-4\Delta^{2}\lambda^{2}/\epsilon^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
when
\lambda\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{2}\Delta}
.
Thus,

Part I

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\Delta}{\epsilon}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}\tilde{b}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}e^{\frac{1}{2}(I_{0}\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})i}\overset{(1)}{\leq}\frac{4\Delta}{\epsilon}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}\tilde{b}}\cdot\frac{e^{\frac{1}{2}(I_{0}\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})(m_{0}+1)}}{\frac{1}{2}(I_{0}\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}\overset{(2)}{\leq}\frac{8\Delta}{\epsilon(I_{0}\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}e^{-\frac{\tilde{b}}{4}\lambda(1-\frac{\lambda\Delta^{2}}{8I_{0}})},

where
(1)
is due to
\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}e^{Ci}=\frac{e^{C}(e^{Cm_{0}}-1)}{e^{C}-1}\leq\frac{e^{C(m_{0}+1)}}{C}
for any
C>0
, and
(2)
is due to
m_{0}+1\leq\tilde{b}/(2I_{0})
. Taking
\lambda=\bar{\lambda}:=\min\{4I_{0}/\Delta^{2},\epsilon/(2\sqrt{2}\Delta)\}
guarantees
(1-\frac{\bar{\lambda}\Delta^{2}}{8I_{0}})>0
and thus
\text{Part I}\leq\frac{8\Delta}{\epsilon(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}+\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}
.

Similarly for Part III, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and for any
\lambda\in(0,\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{2}\Delta}]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}\left(|Z_{i}|\mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(X_{j})+Z_{i}<\tilde{b})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbf{E}_{0}|Z_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(X_{j})+Z_{i}<\tilde{b})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{E}_{0}e^{-\lambda\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(X_{j})+Z_{i}\right)}}{e^{-\lambda\tilde{b}}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})\sqrt{\frac{e^{(\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8}-I_{0}\lambda)i+\lambda\tilde{b}}}{1-4\Delta^{2}\lambda^{2}/\epsilon^{2}}}\leq 4(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda\tilde{b}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(I_{0}\lambda-\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})i}.

Again taking
\lambda=\bar{\lambda}=\min\{4I_{0}/\Delta^{2},\epsilon/(2\sqrt{2}\Delta)\}
guarantees
(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}-\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})>0
and

Part III

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\Delta}{\epsilon}e^{\frac{1}{2}\bar{\lambda}\tilde{b}}\sum_{i=m_{1}+1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}-\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})i}\overset{(1)}{\leq}\frac{4\Delta}{\epsilon}e^{\frac{1}{2}\bar{\lambda}\tilde{b}}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}-\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}e^{-\frac{1}{2}m_{1}(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}-\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}

\displaystyle\overset{(2)}{\leq}\frac{8\Delta}{\epsilon(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}-\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{b}\bar{\lambda}(1-\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}\Delta^{2}}{4I_{0}})}\leq\frac{8\Delta}{\epsilon(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}-\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})},

where
(1)
is due to
\sum_{i=m_{1}+1}^{\infty}e^{-Ci}=\frac{e^{-C(m_{1}+1)}}{1-e^{-C}}\leq\frac{e^{-C(m_{1}+1)}}{C/(1+C)}\leq\frac{e^{-Cm_{1}}}{C}
for any
C>0
, and
(2)
is due to
m_{1}\geq 2\tilde{b}/I_{0}
.

For Part II, note

Part II

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=m_{0}}^{m_{1}}(\mathbf{E}_{0}|Z_{i}|^{2})^{1/2}\sqrt{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\nu=i+1)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})\left(\sum_{i=m_{0}}^{m_{1}}\sqrt{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\nu=i+1)}\right)\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})\sqrt{(\sum_{i=m_{0}}^{m_{1}}1)(\sum_{i=m_{0}}^{m_{1}}\mathbf{P}_{0}(\nu=i+1))}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})\sqrt{m_{1}-m_{0}+1}\leq 2\sqrt{2}(\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon})\sqrt{\frac{3\tilde{b}}{2I_{0}}+4}.

For
b
sufficiently large such that
b/(2I_{0})\geq 4
we can further simplify the above expression as Part II
\leq 4\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\tilde{b}/I_{0}}
.

Step 4: Substituting everything into Eq. (
25
) we have when
b/(2I_{0})\geq 4
,

\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]\leq\frac{\tilde{b}+4\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{I_{0}}\epsilon}\sqrt{\tilde{b}}+\Delta+\frac{8\Delta}{\epsilon(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}-\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}+\frac{8\Delta}{\epsilon(I_{0}\bar{\lambda}+\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{8})}}{I_{0}}=\frac{\tilde{b}+4\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{I_{0}}\epsilon}\sqrt{\tilde{b}}+C}{I_{0}},

where
C
is a constant that depends only on
\epsilon
,
\Delta
, and
I_{0}
, but is independent of
b
.

Above we have shown the upper bound for
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu|W=w]
when
w\geq 0
. Note that when
w<0
, we have
\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu|W=w]\leq\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu|W=|w|]
. Using
\sqrt{b+|w|}\leq\sqrt{b}+\sqrt{|w|}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{W}\mathbf{E}_{0}[\nu|W=w]\leq\mathbf{E}_{W}[\frac{b+|W|+4\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{I_{0}}\epsilon}(\sqrt{b}+\sqrt{|W|})+C}{I_{0}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{I_{0}}+\frac{\mathbf{E}_{W}|W|}{I_{0}}+\frac{4\Delta}{I_{0}^{3/2}\epsilon}(\sqrt{b}+\mathbf{E}_{W}[\sqrt{|W|}])+C^{\prime}=\frac{b}{I_{0}}+\frac{4\Delta}{I_{0}^{3/2}\epsilon}\sqrt{b}+C^{\prime\prime},

where
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
are both constants independent of
b
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Similar to the proof in Theorem
1
, for
t\in\mathbb{N}
and for any sequence
X_{(1:t)}
and
X^{\prime}_{(1:t)}
that differs only at time
k

S_{1}=S^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}=S^{\prime}_{k-1},
but
S_{k}\neq S^{\prime}_{k}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
S_{k}>S^{\prime}_{k}
. By the definition of CUSUM statistics (
3

S_{k+1}\geq S^{\prime}_{k+1}
,
\ldots
,
S_{t}\geq S^{\prime}_{t}
.
Since we fixed
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t-1}
, we have
\tilde{S}_{1}=\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{k-1}=\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{k-1};\tilde{S}_{k}>\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{k},\tilde{S}_{k+1}\geq\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{k+1},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{t-1}\geq\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{t-1}
.

Denote the event

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}:=\{|\ell(X_{k})|\leq\frac{A_{\delta}}{2}\text{ and }|\ell(X^{\prime}_{k})|\leq\frac{A_{\delta}}{2}\}.

Note that conditioning on event
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
, we have
|\tilde{S}_{k}-\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{k}|\leq A_{\delta}
and thus
|\tilde{S}_{j}-\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{j}|\leq A_{\delta}
for
j>k
. Therefore, we have
\max_{1\leq i\leq t-1}\tilde{S^{\prime}_{j}}-\max_{1\leq i\leq t-1}\tilde{S_{j}}\leq 0
and
|\max_{1\leq i\leq t-1}\tilde{S^{\prime}_{j}}-\max_{1\leq i\leq t-1}\tilde{S_{j}}|\leq A_{\delta}
. Meanwhile, we have
S_{t}-S_{t}^{\prime}\in[0,A_{\delta}]
. By proof of Theorem
1
, we have
\mathbf{P}_{Z_{t},W}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X_{(1:n)})\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbf{P}_{Z_{t},W}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X^{\prime}_{(1:t)})
still hold, conditioning on
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
.

By the definition of
A_{\delta}
, we have for any given
k
, under both pre- and post-change measure,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\delta})\geq 1-\delta,\text{ i.e., }\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\delta}^{c})\leq\delta.

Combining these, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X_{(1:t)\setminus k})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X_{(1:t)\setminus k},\mathcal{E}_{\delta})\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\delta})+\mathbf{P}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X^{\prime}_{(1:t)\setminus k},\mathcal{E}_{\delta}^{c})\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\delta}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbf{P}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X^{\prime}_{(1:t)\setminus k},\mathcal{E}_{\delta})\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\delta})+\delta

\displaystyle\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbf{P}(\tilde{T}=t\mid X^{\prime}_{(1:t)\setminus k})+\delta.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
4
.

∎

Proof of Corollary
2
.

We first note that the proof of Lemma
2
remains valid, and thus the worst-case detection delay of
\tilde{T}
is still attained when the changepoint occurs at
\tau=0
.
Furthermore, the proof of Theorem
3
does not explicitly rely on the boundedness of the log-likelihood ratio
\ell(\cdot)
; it only invokes the boundedness of
\ell(X)
through the use of Hoeffding’s inequality.
As a result, the same proof applies in the unbounded case provided that
\ell(X)
is sub-Gaussian under both the pre- and post-change distributions. Specifically, assume there exists a constant
\sigma^{2}>0
such that
\mathbf{E}[e^{\lambda(\ell(X)-\mathbf{E}[\ell(X)])}]\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Under this condition, the same argument in the proof of Theorem
3
continues to hold with Laplace noise
\text{Lap}(2\Delta/\epsilon)
replaced by
\text{Lap}(2A_{\delta}/\epsilon)
. The sub-Gaussian constant
\sigma^{2}
appears only in the additive constant term
C
in the resulting WADD bound. We omit the technical derivations, as they are essentially the same as the proof of Theorem
3
.

∎
