Safety evaluation of the food enzyme protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase from the non‐genetically modified Streptomyces mobaraensis strain AE‐BTG
Authors: EFSA Panel on Food Enzymes (FEZ), Holger Zorn, José Manuel Barat Baviera, Claudia Bolognesi, Francesco Catania, Gabriele Gadermaier, Ralf Greiner, Baltasar Mayo, Alicja Mortensen, Yrjö Henrik Roos, Marize L M Solano, Monika Sramkova, Henk Van Loveren, Laurence Vernis, Jaime Aguilera, Magdalena Andryszkiewicz, Natalia Kovalkovicova, Giulio di Piazza, Yi Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe food enzyme protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase (protein‐glutamine: amine γ‐glutamyltransferase; EC 2.3.2.13) is produced with the non‐genetically modified S. mobaraensis strain AE‐BTG by AJINOMOTO EUROPE SAS. The food enzyme was free from viable cells of the production organism. It is intended to be used in nine food manufacturing processes. Dietary exposure to the food enzyme–total organic solids (TOS) was estimated to be up to 0.398 mg TOS/kg body weight (bw) per day in European populations. Genotoxicity tests did not indicate a safety concern. The systemic toxicity was assessed by means of a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats. The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level of 538 mg TOS/kg bw per day which when compared with the estimated dietary exposure, resulted in a margin of exposure of at least 1351. A search for homology of the amino acid sequence of the food enzyme to known allergens was made and no match was found. Known sources of food allergens were used in the food enzyme manufacturing process, and the Panel considered that the risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to this food enzyme cannot be excluded. Based on the data provided, the Panel concluded that this food enzyme does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.Keywords: EC 2.3.2.13, EFSA‐2015‐00095, food enzyme, protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase, protein‐glutamine: Amine γ‐glutamyltransferase, Streptomyces mobaraensis, transglutaminase

INTRODUCTION

Article 3 of the Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 1 provides definition for ‘food enzyme’ and ‘food enzyme preparation’.

‘Food enzyme’ means a product obtained from plants, animals or microorganisms or products thereof including a product obtained by a fermentation process using microorganisms: (i) containing one or more enzymes capable of catalysing a specific biochemical reaction; and (ii) added to food for a technological purpose at any stage of the manufacturing, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of foods.

‘Food enzyme preparation’ means a formulation consisting of one or more food enzymes in which substances such as food additives and/or other food ingredients are incorporated to facilitate their storage, sale, standardisation, dilution or dissolution.

Before January 2009, food enzymes other than those used as food additives were not regulated or were regulated as processing aids under the legislation of the Member States. On 20 January 2009, Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes came into force. This Regulation applies to enzymes that are added to food to perform a technological function in the manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of such food, including enzymes used as processing aids. Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 2 established the European Union (EU) procedures for the safety assessment and the authorisation procedure of food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings. The use of a food enzyme shall be authorised only if it is demonstrated that:

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

there is a reasonable technological need;

there is a reasonable technological need;

its use does not mislead the consumer.

its use does not mislead the consumer.

All food enzymes currently on the EU market and intended to remain on that market, as well as all new food enzymes, shall be subjected to a safety evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and approval via an EU Community list.

The ‘Guidance on submission of a dossier on food enzymes for safety evaluation’ (EFSA, 2009a) lays down the administrative, technical and toxicological data required.

Protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferases catalyse the amide‐transfer reaction between the γ‐glutamyl group of a glutamine residue and the ε‐amino group of lysine in proteins, resulting in intra‐ and inter‐molecular cross‐linking of proteins. In the absence of amino substrates, transglutaminases catalyse the hydrolysis of the γ‐carboxyamide group of the glutaminyl residue, resulting in deamidation.

The food enzyme under assessment is intended to be used in nine food manufacturing processes: processing of dairy products for the production of (1) cheese, (2) fermented dairy products and (3) modified milk proteins; (4) processing of meat and fish products for the production of modified meat and fish; processing of cereals and other grains for the production of (5) baked products, (6) cereal‐based products other than baked and (7) brewed products; and processing of plant‐ and fungal‐derived products for the production of (8) plant‐based analogues of milk and milk products and (9) meat analogues.

Production of the food enzyme

The food enzyme is manufactured according to the Food Hygiene Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, 8 with food safety procedures based on Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points and in accordance with current Good Manufacturing Practice. 9

The production strain is grown as a pure culture using a typical industrial medium in a submerged, batch fermentation system with conventional process controls in place. After completion of the fermentation, the solid biomass is removed by filtration. The filtrate containing the food enzyme is then further purified and concentrated, including an ultrafiltration step in which enzyme protein is retained, while most of the low molecular mass material passes the filtration membrane and is discarded. Toxicological data

A battery of toxicological tests including a bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test), an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test and a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats has been provided.

Batch 4 (Table 1) used in these studies was one of the batches intended for commercialisation and was considered suitable as a test item.

Genotoxicity

Bacterial reverse mutation test

A bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test) was performed according to the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) Test Guideline 471 (OECD, 2020) and following Good Laboratory Practice (GLP). 27

Four strains of Salmonella Typhimurium (TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537) and E. coli WP2uvrA were used with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix), applying the pre‐incubation method.

A range‐finding test was conducted at concentrations of the food enzyme ranging from 19.5 to 5000 μg TOS/plate. Precipitates were observed at 1250 μg TOS/plate and above with or without metabolic activation.

The main experiment was carried out in triplicate, using five concentrations of the food enzyme of 78.1, 156, 313, 625 and 1250 μg TOS/plate. No growth inhibition was observed in any tester strain at any concentration level either with or without metabolic activation. Upon treatment with the food enzyme there was no biologically relevant increase in the number of revertant colonies above the control values, in any strain tested, with or without S9‐mix.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase did not induce gene mutations under the test conditions applied in this study.

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test

The in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test was carried out according to OECD Test Guideline 487 (OECD, 2016) and following GLP. 28 An experiment was performed with duplicate cultures of human lymphoblastoid TK6 cells.

In a range‐finding test no cytotoxicity above 50% was seen at any concentration tested up to 5000 μg TOS/mL with and without S9‐mix. Precipitates were observed at 1250 μg/mL and above. In the main experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for the frequency of cells with micronuclei at concentrations of 625, 1250 and 2500 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (4‐h exposure and 20‐h recovery period) with or without S9‐mix and in a long‐term treatment (24‐h exposure without recovery period) without S9‐mix.

No cytotoxicity was seen either in the short‐term with and without S9‐mix or in the long‐term treatment. The frequency of cells with micronuclei was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at all concentrations tested.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase did not induce an increase in the frequency of cells with micronuclei under the test conditions applied in this study.

Repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats

The repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study was performed under GLP and according to OECD Test Guideline 408 (OECD, 2018) with the following deviation: vaginal smears for determination of the oestrus cycle were not taken at necropsy. 29 The Panel considered that this deviation is minor and does not impact on the evaluation of the study.

Groups of 10 male and 10 female Sprague–Dawley (Crl:CD(SD)) rats received by gavage the food enzyme in doses of 500, 1000 or 2000 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day corresponding to 269, 538 or 1076 mg TOS/kg bw per day. Controls received the vehicle (water for injection).

No mortality was observed.

The feed consumption during the treatment period was statistically significantly decreased on day 7 in high‐dose males (−7%), on day 7 and 14 in mid‐dose males (−7% and −6%, respectively), on day 21 in high‐dose females (−10%), on day 7 and 14 in mid‐dose females (−6% and −11%, respectively) and on day 7 in low‐dose females (−11%). The feed consumption was increased on day 1 in low‐dose females (+13%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as they were only recorded sporadically, there was no dose–response relationship (males: days 7 and 14; females: days 1, 7, 14), there were no statistically significant changes in the body weight and in the total body weight gain.

In the functional observations, a statistically significant increase in the rearing count in week 1 and a decrease in the landing foot splay were observed in low‐dose females (+75% and −26%, respectively). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only recorded at a single time interval, they were only observed in one sex and there was no dose–response relationship.

Haematological investigations revealed statistically significant decreases in haemoglobin (HGB) in low‐ and mid‐dose males (−5% and −5%, respectively), in haematocrit (HCT) in low‐dose males (−5%), in basophil count (Baso) in low‐ and mid‐dose males (−50% and −50%, respectively) and in fibrinogen (FIB) in low‐ and high‐dose males (−18% and −24%, respectively). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only observed in one sex (all parameters), there was no dose–response relationship (HGB, HCT, Baso), the changes were small (HGB, HCT, Baso) and there were no changes in other relevant parameters (erythrocyte count for HGB and HCT; in leucocyte count for Baso; in other coagulation parameters for fibrinogen).

Clinical chemistry investigations revealed a statistically significant increase in low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐chol, +55%) and a decrease in albumin to globulin ratio (A/G ratio, −14%) in high‐dose females. The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as, they were only observed in one sex (both parameters) and there were no changes in other relevant parameters (for LDL‐chol in total cholesterol or in high density lipoprotein cholesterol; for A/G ratio in total protein or albumin levels).

A statistically significant change in hormone levels detected was a decrease in thyroxine in high‐dose females (−18%). The Panel considered the change as not toxicologically relevant as it was only observed in one sex, there were no changes in other thyroid hormones and there were no histopathological changes in the thyroid gland.

The urinalysis revealed that some high‐dose males had lower urine pH than the controls (pH ≤ 8 in 5/10 and pH ≥ 8.5 in 5/10 vs. pH ≥ 8.5 in 10/10 controls). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as they were only observed in one sex, there were no changes in other urinalysis parameters, in blood electrolytes and there were no histopathological changes in the kidneys.

Statistically significant changes in organ weights detected were an increase in the absolute pituitary weight in high‐dose males (+12%), a decrease in the absolute thyroid weight in mid‐dose males (−15%) and an increase in the relative spleen weight in high‐dose females (+12%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only observed in one sex (all parameters), the changes were small (spleen), there was no dose–response relationship (thyroid) and there were no histopathological changes in pituitary gland, thyroid and spleen.

The macroscopic examination revealed a dark red focus in the glandular stomach in 1/10 mid‐dose males and in 5/10 high‐dose males. The microscopic examination revealed in these males an erosion/ulcer in the glandular stomach, which was minimal in the mid‐dose male, mild in 4/10 and moderate in 1/10 high‐dose males. A minimal erosion/ulcer in the glandular stomach was also observed in 1/10 high‐dose females. Other histopathological changes reported were: (a) squamous cell hyperplasia in the limiting ridge of the stomach, which was minimal in 1/10 mid‐dose male and mild in 5/10 high‐dose males and minimal in 1/10 in high‐dose females, (b) minimal fibrosis of lamina propria in the glandular stomach in 2/10 high‐dose males and (c) hyalinisation of lamina propria in duodenum, which was minimal in 2/10 and mild in 5/10 high‐dose males. The Panel considered these changes as test item related and possibly due to irritation by the test item. The Panel noted that: (a) males appeared more sensitive to this effect of the test item, (b) the changes were not limited to the limiting ridge but affected the glandular stomach, (c) the changes at the mid‐dose were only recorded in two males and were of minimal severity, (d) the incidence and the severity of the changes increased in the high‐dose males and (e) the related changes were also observed in duodenum of the high‐dose males.

No other statistically significant or biologically relevant differences from controls were reported.

The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of 538 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the mid‐dose tested, based on the changes in the glandular stomach and the limiting ridge in high‐dose males.

Allergenicity

The allergenicity assessment considered only the food enzyme and not additives, preservatives, carriers or other excipients that may be used in the final formulation.

The potential allergenicity of the protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase produced with S. mobaraensis strain AE‐BTG was assessed by comparing its amino acid sequence with those of known allergens according to the ‘Scientific opinion on the assessment of allergenicity of GM plants and microorganisms and derived food and feed of the Scientific Panel on Genetically Modified Organisms’ (EFSA GMO Panel, 2010). Using higher than 35% identity in a sliding window of 80 amino acids as the criterion, no match was found. 30

No information was available on oral and respiratory sensitisation or elicitation reactions of the protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase under assessment.

A case report (Sander et al., 2020) has shown that protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase can cause occupational asthma. However, several studies have shown that adults with occupational asthma may be able to ingest respiratory allergens without acquiring clinical symptoms of food allergy (Brisman, 2002; Poulsen, 2004; Armentia et al., 2009).

Transglutaminases have been implicated in the pathogenesis of coeliac disease, as an autoantigen (Lerner & Matthias, 2019a, 2020). In addition, due to their functional similarities with endogenous transglutaminases, those from microbial origin may cross link with gluten proteins, enhancing their immunogenicity (Di Sabatino et al., 2012; Lerner et al., 2024; Lerner & Matthias, 2019b).

■■■■■ and ■■■■■, products which may cause allergies (listed in the Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 31 ) are used as raw materials. In addition, ■■■■■, a known source of allergens, is also present in the media fed to the microorganism. During the fermentation process, these products will mostly be degraded and utilised by the microorganisms for cell growth, cell maintenance and production of enzyme protein. The Panel considered that residual amounts of potentially allergenic proteins could still be present in the food enzyme. However, the likelihood of such reactions will not exceed the likelihood of allergic reactions to fish.

In conclusion, the Panel considered that the risk of allergic reactions, in particular individuals allergic to fish, cannot be excluded. Moreover, the risk of contribution to celiac disease cannot be excluded.

In all of the food manufacturing processes, the protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase catalyses the cross‐linking between glutamine and lysine residues, modifying the rheological properties of the foods and impacts their sensory attributes such as mouthfeel. 36

In the production of cheese, the food enzyme is usually added to milk together with the starter culture during coagulation. 37 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final foods.

In the production of fermented dairy products, the food enzyme may be added to milk during homogenisation or during the fermentation step. 38 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final foods.

In the production of modified milk proteins, the food enzyme is added to caseinate or whey proteins before the final drying. 39 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the modified milk proteins, which are used as ingredients in a variety of foods.

In the production of modified meat and fish products, the food enzyme is added to meat or fish during the mixing and the cutting step 40 in all the described processes except in the production of cooked ham, in which it is added in brine before the injection or during tumbling. 41 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final foods.

In the production of baked products and in the production of cereal‐based products other than baked, the food enzyme is added during dough making. 42 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final foods.

In the production of brewed products, the food enzyme could be added to cereals before the fermentation or before the final filtration steps 43 to reduce the gluten content of the brewed beverages. 44 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the brewed products.

In the production of plant‐based analogues of milk and milk products, the food enzyme is added to plant materials at the beginning of the manufacturing process, together with the coagulants. 45 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final foods.

In the production of meat analogues, the food enzyme may be added to plant proteins 46 or protein‐rich ingredients such as egg and yeast 47 or plant sources during the mixing step. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final foods.

Dietary exposure estimation

Chronic exposure to the food enzyme–TOS was calculated by combining the maximum recommended use level with individual consumption data (EFSA CEP Panel, 2021). The estimation involved selection of relevant food categories and application of technical conversion factors (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023) and input data provided in Appendix C. Exposure from all FoodEx categories was subsequently summed up, averaged over the total survey period (days) and normalised for body weight. This was done for all individuals across all surveys, resulting in distributions of individual average exposure. Based on these distributions, the mean and 95th percentile exposures were calculated per survey for the total population and per age class. Surveys with only 1 day per subject were excluded and high‐level exposure/intake was calculated for only those population groups in which the sample size was sufficiently large to allow calculation of the 95th percentile (EFSA, 2011).

The conservative approach applied to estimate the exposure to the food enzyme–TOS, in particular assumptions made on the occurrence and use levels of this specific food enzyme, is likely to have led to an overestimation of the exposure.

Margin of exposure

A comparison of the NOAEL (538 mg TOS/kg bw per day) identified from the 90‐day rat study with the derived exposure estimates of 0.018–0.201 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the mean and from 0.050 to 0.398 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the 95th percentile resulted in a margin of exposure of at least 1351.

CONCLUSION

Based on the data provided and the derived margin of exposure the Panel concluded that the food enzyme protein‐glutamine γ‐glutamyltransferase produced with the non‐genetically modified S. mobaraensis strain AE‐BTG does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.
