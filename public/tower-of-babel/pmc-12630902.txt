Deep learning model for assessing survival benefits in hepatocellular carcinoma patients undergoing intra-arterial therapies based on proliferative subtype
Authors: Fei Cao, Chao An, Shaolong Li, Xiaochun Hu, Da Li, Jiayu Pan, Zhijun Geng, Fei Gao, Mengxuan Zuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe proliferative hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) subtype is associated with aggressive disease and poor prognosis, yet it remains challenging to identify non-invasively. This retrospective multicenter study developed a multitask deep learning system for detecting proliferative HCC and predicting survival after intra-arterial therapy (IAT). Contrast-enhanced CT scans from two cohorts (surgical resection, n = 398; unresectable HCC receiving transarterial chemoembolization or hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy, n = 1749) were analyzed using an nnUNet-based segmentation pipeline for liver and tumor delineation. A novel Prototype Mamba Net (PMN) architecture was created to extract imaging features indicative of proliferative biology. The model achieved AUCs of 0.825 (95% CI: 0.781–0.884) and 0.792 (95% CI: 0.732–0.841) on training and testing sets, respectively, for detecting proliferative HCC. Prognostic nomograms combining radiomic and clinical variables outperformed traditional staging systems (time-dependent AUC: 0.83–0.87; integrated Brier score: 0.12 versus 0.18–0.23, all P < 0.001). Among low-risk patients, no significant difference in survival was observed. However, for high-risk patients, HAIC showed a significant survival benefit compared to TACE (training and testing, P < 0.001). This non-invasive deep learning method enables preoperative identification of proliferative HCC and supports personalized IAT treatment choices, potentially improving outcomes in unresectable HCC.Subject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) exhibits significant heterogeneity at both molecular and histological levels, and can be broadly categorized into proliferative and non-proliferative subgroups based on integrated transcriptomic and histopathological features1,2. The proliferative subgroup, accounting for approximately 30–50% of HCC cases, is associated with more aggressive clinical behavior and includes several distinct variants such as sarcomatoid, macrotrabecular-massive (MTM-HCC), neutrophil-enriched (e.g., G-CSF-producing HCC), and CK19-positive progenitor-type HCC. These tumors are characterized by chromosomal instability, TP53 mutations, activation of proliferative signaling pathways, and often show poor differentiation. Despite these well-defined molecular and histological correlates, clinical diagnosis of HCC still heavily relies on non-invasive imaging criteria rather than histopathological confirmation. Consequently, many proliferative HCCs are identified without prior tissue validation, potentially limiting the application of subtype-specific management strategies2,3. Recent studies have shown that proliferative HCC has distinct radiological features. Bao et al. identified CT-based predictors, including lobulated tumour margins, satellite nodules, mosaic internal architecture, and rim-type arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE)4. Kang et al. also found a significant link between rim APHE and large areas of hypo-enhancement on MRI with this phenotype. Recognising these imaging features may facilitate accurate, non-invasive identification before the initiation of treatment.

For patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC), treatment selection is primarily guided by disease stage and hepatic functional reserve. Locoregional intra-arterial therapies, notably transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), represent widely utilized treatment modalities in this patient population5–7. Phase III studies found that in patients with larger tumors, the objective response rate of HAIC was higher than that of TACE. Nonetheless, clinical results in patients with uHCC remain markedly uneven, primarily due to inherent differences in tumour types, genetic profiles, and molecular characteristics8. Prior studies indicate that the proliferative subtype is associated with reduced survival rates after TACE or surgical resection, in comparison to non-proliferative disease3,4. Still, it is not clear what the therapeutic value of HAIC is for this group of people.

Recent advances in deep learning (DL) have demonstrated impressive predictive capabilities, sometimes surpassing expert-level performance9–11. Vaswani et al. first introduced transformer-based designs that utilize self-attention mechanisms to uncover complex feature relationships, demonstrating potential in predicting cancer outcomes. Currently, there is no transformer-based framework developed explicitly for prognostic modeling in uHCC or for evaluating the preference between HAIC and TACE.

This study aimed to develop and assess a multitask transformer model capable of integrating CT-derived data for diagnosing proliferative HCC and optimizing intra-arterial therapy choices. This approach seeks to enhance personalized treatment options and improve patient outcomes by offering accurate, non-invasive phenotyping.

Proliferative HCC–related DL score

Segmentation masks covering the entire liver were used to exclude all image pixels outside the liver and tumor boundaries. For developing and validating the model in cohort 1, 7164 contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) images from both arterial and portal venous phases were analyzed. Multiple deep learning (DL) architectures were evaluated to identify the best-performing model, with hyperparameter settings summarized in Table S5. The model trained with combined arterial and portal phase images showed the highest accuracy (Fig. 2). This may be closely related to the dynamic information characteristics of blood supply in HCC tumors with biphasic phases compared to those with monophasic phases. Within the training set, this approach achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.825 (95% CI, 0.781–0.884), and in the testing set, the AUC was 0.792 (95% CI, 0.732–0.841) (Fig. 3). In both datasets, risk scores from the DL model were significantly lower for patients with proliferative HCC compared to those with non-proliferative tumors (training: mean 0.65 ± 0.07 vs. 0.43 ± 0.07, P < 0.001; testing: mean 0.59 ± 0.09 vs. 0.49 ± 0.08, P < 0.001).

Visualization interpretability

Feature maps produced by the Prototype Mamba Net (PMN) are shown in Fig. 4. For interpretability, heat maps were grouped by predicted proliferative or non-proliferative hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) status. Maps for predicted non-proliferative tumors displayed lower overall signal intensity compared to those from proliferative cases, aligning with established pathological distinctions in HCC. Additionally, higher-intensity signals were observed in patients classified within longer survival categories, suggesting that proliferative tumor–related features may significantly contribute to survival modeling.

Development of the deep learning–based nomogram for overall survival prediction

In the transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) cohort, multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis revealed that the amount of hepatic lesions (HR = 1.26; 95% CI, 0.99–1.60), the presence of underlying hepatitis infection (HR = 1.66; 95% CI, 1.08–1.56), and the maximum tumour size (HR = 1.07; 95% CI, 1.04–1.11) were independent prognostic factors for reduced overall survival (OS). The independently associated risk factors with DL risk score and predictive proliferative HCCs were used to develop the TACE-nomogram (Fig. 5a). The multivariable Cox model for the hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) cohort found that sex (HR = 0.76; 95% CI, 0.56–1.01), circulating aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels (HR = 1.00; 95% CI, 1.00–1.00), lesion count (HR = 1.28; 95% CI, 1.02–1.62), and the presence of portal vein tumour thrombus (PVTT) (HR = 1.57; 95% CI, 1.26–1.96) were all significant negative prognostic factors. The traits previously mentioned, in combination with DL-based risk assessment and the anticipated proliferative HCC phenotype, formed the foundation for the HAIC-focused nomogram (Fig. 5b). In all the models studied, an increase in the overall prognostic score and classification into a higher-risk group consistently corresponded with worse overall survival outcomes.

Evaluation of predictive accuracy: tumor staging versus deep learning models

We compared the predictive ability of the HAIC and TACE nomograms with well-known staging systems for liver cancer—such as BCLC, JSH, CNLC, EASL, and HKLC (Table 3). The time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (td-AUC) was 0.83 (95% CI, 0.64–1.00) in the training set, while it was 0.87 (95% CI, 0.75–0.98) in the testing set of the TACE group. For the HAIC group, the td-AUC values were 0.87 (95% CI, 0.68–1.00) for the development dataset and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.81–0.95) for the independent test dataset. In all treatment situations, our customized nomograms significantly outperformed standard staging methods in distinguishing different types of cancer (DeLong test, P < 0.001; Fig. 5c–f). We also used an integrated Brier score (IBS) with AUC analysis to evaluate prediction accuracy. The IBS for each nomogram was consistently lower than that of other staging frameworks, indicating better calibration. The DeLong test confirmed that all observed differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001; Fig. 5g–j).

Survival benefits of intra-arterial therapies in DL nomogram–defined risk groups

Survival curve analysis was employed to evaluate the prognostic value of intra-arterial treatments (IATs) using deep learning (DL)–derived nomograms. Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on predetermined cutoff values: –22.34 for the HAIC cohort and –26.25 for the TACE cohort. In the HAIC cohort, low-risk patients demonstrated significantly better cumulative overall survival (OS) at 1, 3, and 5 years compared to high-risk patients. In the training set, OS rates for low-risk patients were 87.8%, 62.1%, and 49.1%, respectively, while those for high-risk patients were 63.2%, 33.4%, and 25.4% (P < 0.001; Fig. 6a). These results were confirmed in the testing set, where low- and high-risk patients exhibited OS rates of 53.6%, 25.8%, 5.6% and 24.8%, 6.2%, 5.8%, respectively (P < 0.001; Fig. 6b). Similar survival differences were observed in the TACE cohort. In the training set, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates for low-risk patients were 62.7%, 42.3%, and 25.2%, respectively, compared to 51.2%, 4.4%, and 3.8% for high-risk patients (P < 0.001; Fig. 6c). The testing set showed consistent trends: low-risk patients had OS rates of 87.8%, 62.1%, and 49.1%, whereas high-risk patients had rates of 63.2%, 33.4%, and 25.4% (P < 0.001; Fig. 6d). After 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) in the training group, HAIC was associated with longer median OS than TACE in high-risk patients (12.2 vs. 7.8 months, P < 0.001; Fig. 6e). However, among low-risk patients, no significant difference in survival was observed between the two treatments (42.2 vs. 24.1 months, P = 0.085; Fig. 6c). Similarly, in the testing group, high-risk patients benefited more from HAIC than TACE (12.1 vs. 6.6 months, P < 0.001; Fig. 6g), while no significant advantage was seen in low-risk patients (57.6 vs. 25.2 months, P = 0.560; Fig. 6h).

Discussion

Intra-arterial therapeutic methods (IATs) are essential in managing unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC)12–16, with substantial evidence supporting their effectiveness in extending survival. However, the biological heterogeneity of UHCC leads to notable differences in clinical outcomes after TACE or HAIC. Although patients with the proliferative histological subtype often have poorer survival rates after curative therapy, there is a lack of direct comparative data between TACE and HAIC for this high-risk group. A prior Phase III randomized study showed that HAIC achieved a median overall survival (OS) of 23.1 months and an objective response rate (ORR) of 48%, surpassing TACE in patients with tumors larger than 7 cm in diameter. Despite these results, the impact of cancer histology, imaging phenotypes, and other tumor-specific biological factors on treatment efficacy remains underexplored7,8,17,18. To address this gap, we developed two multimodal deep learning (MDL) models optimized for imaging signals of proliferative hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). These models enable us to compare the survival outcomes of patients undergoing transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) or hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC).

We used an automated whole-liver segmentation approach and made manual adjustments with the help of skilled radiologists to maximize efficiency. This method enabled the creation of an online application to assist doctors in decision-making. Prior radiomics research has mainly focused on tumor-centric evaluations, sometimes including peritumoral areas, and these approaches have shown promise in predicting treatment responses19–22. In large HCCs with unclear edges, variability among and within observers remains an issue. Recent studies demonstrate that combining imaging features from both the lesion and the surrounding liver tissue yields more accurate predictions than using tumor data alone23. In our method, a nnU-Net architecture initially segmented the areas of interest (ROIs), which were then further refined by radiologists. This method employs three model configurations—2D U-Net, 3D full-resolution U-Net, and 3D low-resolution U-Net—to achieve robust volumetric segmentation and reduce the labor-intensive and subjective limitations of manual contouring.

Cohort 1 included 398 patients who underwent surgical resection (SR), with 187 (47.0%) confirmed as the proliferative subtype through pathology. Our deep learning-based classification model performed well, with an AUC of 0.825 in the training dataset and 0.792 in the testing dataset. The Prototype Mamba Net (PMN) design generated feature maps that facilitated studying the relationship between imaging patterns and histology features. A major limitation of deep learning in medical imaging is the challenge of interpretability, which can hinder adoption by interventional radiologists24. To address this, we used the Grad method to demonstrate the correlation between DL-extracted imaging features and pathological classifications25. The heatmaps showed that proliferative tumors exhibited increased signal in the portal venous phase, while non-proliferative tumors had less enhancement in the portal venous phase compared to the arterial phase. These phase-specific signal differences may reflect underlying metabolic reprogramming, potentially related to changes in the immune microenvironment or cancer cell activity26–29. Further molecular validation through transcriptome profiling or single-cell sequencing is needed to confirm these findings.

The proliferative subtype has been recognized as a prognostic marker in patients with uHCC receiving IATs4. This study employed the PMN architecture to extract both global and localized imaging descriptors from the hepatic parenchyma, enabling precise identification of proliferative subgroups and subsequent overall survival prediction in individuals treated with IAT. Our MDL framework incorporated pathology-derived variables relevant to proliferative biology, using a unified feature extraction approach for subtype classification and overall survival modeling. This dual-task approach identifies a high-risk subgroup in which HAIC confers a 4.4-month OS advantage over TACE, thereby improving the usefulness of categorization findings for making predictions. The inclusion of postoperative problematic variables significantly improved the predictive power of the preoperative model.

Using contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) images from 1749 patients in cohort 2, we developed MDL-based prediction nomograms specifically for TACE and HAIC. The time-dependent AUCs were 0.83 for TACE and 0.87 for HAIC, outperforming other cancer staging methods (DeLong test, P < 0.05). The prediction error analysis resulted in integrated Brier scores (IBS) of 0.146 for TACE and 0.106 for HAIC, both better than BCLC, JSH, CNLC, EASL, and HKLC frameworks. These nomograms accurately classified patients into high- and low-risk groups for each treatment cohort, demonstrating significant differences in overall survival between groups. This classification can inform therapeutic decisions, including the combination of systemic and locoregional treatments, potentially reducing toxicity and costs associated with overtreatment. To facilitate testing and application of the CECT-based MDL nomograms in clinical settings, all code and resources are publicly available at “https://github.com/Issory/-Multitask-PMN-for-HCC”.

Risk-stratified outcome studies showed that HAIC provides a survival benefit over TACE in the high-risk group, although overall survival rates are similar between the two treatments in low-risk patients. The findings suggest that MDL-driven risk classification can help identify patients most likely to benefit from HAIC, while also supporting either treatment in low-risk cases. The proposed nomogram framework offers a practical tool for personalized selection between TACE and HAIC.

There are a lot of limits that need to be acknowledged. This multicentre study was conducted across eight hospitals in China, each with unique procedural protocols and treatment preferences, potentially resulting in site-specific variability. Additionally, the cohort mainly included HBV-related HCC patients, reflecting the epidemiological context in China. Whether these findings are applicable to Western countries, where cirrhosis is the predominant cause, is uncertain. Ultimately, specific clinical and genetic markers known to influence overall survival were omitted from our models. Research involving broader patient subtypes and using multidimensional parameter models is necessary to validate whether the proposed model significantly enhances patient survival outcomes when applied in clinical decision-making.

In this study, we developed and validated two nomogram frameworks based on multimodal deep learning (MDL) to predict overall survival in patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC) after intra-arterial treatments (IATs). These comprehensive models help clearly distinguish between TACE and HAIC based on evidence and are available on an online platform. Further validation is needed to evaluate the use of our proposed MDL model as a decision-support tool in future clinical trials.

Methods

This retrospective analysis was conducted in compliance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) requirements. The institutional review board of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center granted ethical approval (Approval ID: B2023-511-01). All research endeavours complied with the ethical standards delineated in the Declaration of Helsinki (1975). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study patients

This retrospective multicenter study analyzed data from consecutive patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) treated at participating institutions. Between June 2018 and October 2022, 398 patients who underwent surgical resection at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center were included. Additionally, a separate cohort comprised 5,668 patients diagnosed with unresectable HCC (uHCC) who initiated first-line treatment with either hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) or transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) between January 2009 and May 2022. All participants underwent standardized amic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) using a 64-slice scanner within two weeks before surgery and TACE/HAIC. To mitigate intercenter variability, all imaging data were reviewed and processed using a standardized pipeline including quality control, harmonization of acquisition parameters, and centralized image analysis.

Eligibility criteria for both cohorts included: (a) age between 18 and 75 years; (b) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≤1; (c) Child–Pugh class A or B liver function; (d) receipt of either conventional TACE or FOLFOX-HAIC. Exclusion criteria were: (a) any prior anticancer treatment; (b) active secondary malignancies; (c) maximum tumor diameter <5 cm; (d) concurrent TACE and HAIC treatment; (e) lack of pretreatment CECT; or (f) loss to follow-up exceeding six months.

The proliferative HCC subtype was identified based on specific histological criteria: Macrotrabecular-massive (MTM-HCC): characterized by trabeculae >6 cells thick occupying >50% of the tumor area; Scirrhous HCC: defined by abundant dense fibrous stroma embedding neoplastic cell clusters; Sarcomatoid HCC: featuring a predominance of spindle-shaped cells; Neutrophil-rich HCC: marked by significant intratumoral neutrophil infiltration; CK19-positive HCC: defined by immunohistochemical expression of CK19 in >5% of tumor cells. Two experienced pathologists (L.L. and P.W., each with over 10 years of specialization in hepatic pathology) independently reviewed serial tumor sections. Clinical and imaging data were blinded during pathological assessment.

Intra-arterial therapy (IAT) procedure

IAT procedures have been outlined in earlier studies. All procedures were conducted using digital subtraction angiography (Philips, model FD 20, 1250 mA, Amsterdam, Netherlands). The artery sheath catheter was inserted into the femoral artery using the modified Seldinger technique. A 5-Fr Yashiro catheter (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced into the celiac trunk and superior mesenteric artery to evaluate the feeding hepatic artery. A 2.7-Fr micro-catheter (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted into the feeding artery.

During TACE, the feeding artery was super-selected whenever possible. Emulsion, containing 10–20 mL Lipiodol, 30–50 mg platinum drugs, and 20–40 mg epirubicin, was administered slowly until the target vessel was occluded. If needed, embolization using gel foam mixed with contrast medium was performed to reduce residual blood flow until no tumor staining was visible after repeat angiography. Treatment was repeated at intervals of 3–4 weeks based on an on-demand strategy, continuing until either unacceptable toxicity or disease progression was observed.

During HAIC, if the tumors receive blood supply from both the celiac trunk and superior mesenteric artery, the microcatheter was positioned into the largest tumor-feeding arteries. The portion of the micro-catheter outside the body was covered with sterile gauze and secured to the skin of the thigh with medical rubberized fabric and a bandage. A FOLFOX-based regimen, including oxaliplatin (85/130 mg/m² infused over 3 h on day 1), leucovorin (200 mg/m² over 3–5 h on day 1), and fluorouracil (400 mg/m² bolus, followed by 2,400 mg/m² continuous infusion over 23/46 h), were administered via hepatic arterial infusion through the micro-catheter. One cycle comprised this 3-week treatment period, and cycles were repeated until treatment was discontinued due to intrahepatic lesion progression or unacceptable toxicity.

Follow-up protocol

The follow-up for all patients who signed up was completed on October 30, 2023. After the designated multicycle intra-arterial treatment (IAT) protocol was performed, serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) tests and contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) scans were scheduled every three months during the first year. Subsequently, they were scheduled every six months. Radiological assessments were conducted 4–6 weeks after initiation of IAT by two independent radiologists (L.Z.L. and J.Z.), each with more than 10 years of professional experience. When reviewing the images, neither reviewer knew to which treatment group the patient belonged. Overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the start of IAT to death from any cause, was the primary endpoint of the study.

Multitasking Deep Learning Model for Proliferative HCC and Survival Prediction

Figure 1b shows that an automated segmentation of hepatic parenchyma and cancer lesions was performed in a single step using a pre-trained nnU-Net architecture. Among many deep learning-based segmentation methods, nnU-Net was utilized to segment the liver parenchyma and tumor areas due to its high accuracy and strong generalization properties. The nnU-Net can adapt itself and select the most suitable model parameters based on different datasets. In this study, we chose nnU-Net pretrained on the Liver and Liver Tumor Segmentation challenge (LiTS) dataset, as we had the same data modality and segmentation task. After preprocessing, nnU-Net designed three separate configurations: 2D U-Net, 3D full-resolution U-Net, and 3D low-resolution U-Net. All network configurations were trained using a 5-fold cross-validation approach. The nnU-Net evaluated each configuration by calculating the average Dice score across all foreground classes, resulting in a scalar metric per configuration. On the LiTS dataset, the scores were 0.7625 for the 2D U-Net, 0.8044 for the 3D full-resolution U-Net, 0.7796 for the 3D low-resolution U-Net, and 0.8017 for the full-resolution 3D U-Net in the cascade based on this evaluation scheme. In this work, the full-resolution 3D U-Net was used to segment the liver.

In this study, our MDL model was constructed using Prototype Mamba Net (PMN), which combines global features and local features from the liver (Fig. 1c). The detailed structure was shown in Fig. 7. The input of PMN was 3D CT images with a size (H×W×D). Then, the 3D CT images were projected to (H3×W3×D3) as a patch embedding. PMN was primarily constructed using a convolution branch for local features and an SSM branch for global features. These features would be combined as image features. Meanwhile, different prototypes (e.g., Proliferative prototypes, Non-Proliferative prototypes, HAIC ORR prototypes, TACE ORR prototypes, HAIC non-ORR prototypes, and TACE non-ORR prototypes) were built with the same size as the image features. The convolution branch was constructed with three convolution layers, and the SSM branch was mainly built using SS2D.

Deep learning score for overall survival after IATs in uHCC

The second patient cohort was used to develop and assess deep learning (DL)–based ratings for predicting overall survival (OS) in patients receiving hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC; n = 1070) or transarterial chemoembolization (TACE; n = 679). We employed the prototype Mamba Net (PMN) architecture to analyze contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) images from the arterial phase (AP) and venous phase (VP). The OS-predictive DL score incorporated imaging features from CT, the classification score for proliferative hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and relevant preoperative clinical variables. These elements were then integrated into a Cox proportional hazards model to generate accurate predictions of overall survival for patients treated with either HAIC or TACE.

Statistical analysis

We used the survival and rms packages in R software (version 3.6.3; http://www.r-project.org/) to perform all statistical analyses. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median values, along with interquartile range (IQR). The Mann–Whitney U test was adopted to compare continuous parameters across groups. Absolute frequencies and percentages summarized categorical data, and the chi-square test was used to determine differences between groups. We used the DeLong approach, which is a part of the MedCalc software (version 19.7), to look at the areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) for the multimodal deep learning (DL) model in predicting ASR in the test set. Overall survival (OS) was assessed by calculating the area under the time-dependent curve (td-AUC) values at a follow-up period of 12 to 60 months. The integrated Brier score (IBS) was used to measure how accurate the model predictions were. We used the Z test to compare the performance of the DL model with well-known clinical staging systems such as BCLC, EASL, CNLC, JSH, and HKLC. We used X-tile software (version 3.6.1; Yale University School of Medicine, New Haven, Connecticut) to determine the optimal method for dividing patients into high- and low-risk groups. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was used to indicate that the results were statistically significant.
