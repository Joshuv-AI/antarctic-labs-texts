Neighborhood environmental burden and the elderly’s complete edentulism prevalence in the US
Authors: Yang Liu, Xuewei Huang, Lijin Lin, Yuanyuan Cao, Jing Cheng, Fang Lei, Ke-Qiong Deng, Bo Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComplete edentulism affects the quality of life and overall health. Identifying environmental factors linked to teeth lost may help address the global public health burden of oral health. This study aimed to estimate the association between neighborhood-level cumulative environmental burden and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly across the US.MethodsThis cross-sectional study of 72 333 US neighborhoods using US Census tract-level data on environmental burden index (EBI) from the US CDC. Mixed-effects linear regression was applied to assess the relationship between neighborhood EBI and the prevalence of edentulism among the elderly, overall and by levels of neighborhood characteristics. The associations between 5 environmental burden domains and complete edentulism prevalence were further investigated. In addition, age proportion score-based matching and geospatially-based matching analyses were conducted to verify the robustness of the findings.ResultsAcross the US, compared to neighborhoods with the low-EBI, neighborhoods with the high-EBI were more likely to be located in the urban, to be more social vulnerable, have a lower percentage of males and the elderly (P < 0.001). After fully adjustment, the beta[95%CI] of the EBI was 1.759[1.620, 1.899] (P < 0.001). Among all 5 environmental burden domains, the built environment showed the strongest association (β = 2.735, 95% CI: 2.581–2.891). These associations were more pronounced in neighborhoods with male-percentage < = 50% and higher SVI scores, as well as in the urban and the south neighborhoods in (P < 0.001). Results were consistent after age proportion score-based matching and geospatially-based matching analyses among the high-EBI and low-EBI neighborhoods.ConclusionThis national study across US neighborhoods indicated a modest but statistically significant association between the cumulative environmental burden and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly. Interventions targeting adverse environmental conditions may hold potential for promoting oral health in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25069-x.

Introduction

With the extension of life expectancy and medical advancements worldwide, aging has become an increasingly important concern. Severe teeth lost, as one of the important signs of oral aging, requires joint management from various levels [1]. Teeth lost is mostly caused by periodontitis and untreated dental caries [2]. Teeth lost not only leads to poor chewing ability, malnutrition, and overall health damage, but also brings aesthetic and psychological challenges, such as decreased self-esteem and social isolation [3]. As one of the worst oral conditions, severe teeth lost increases the risk of over 50 systemic conditions, thereby imposing a significant socioeconomic burden [4–6]. Identifying novel risk factors for severe teeth lost in the elderly is crucial for reducing the disease burden in this population.

Emerging evidence suggests environmental factors may influence periodontal health. Specific pollutants (e.g., particulate matter, heavy metals) and built environment characteristics have shown associations with periodontitis risk [2, 7–10]. Investigation of individual environmental pollutants may inadequately reflect the real-world living environment, providing limited implications about the role of an optimal living environment in maintaining public oral health [11]. A cumulative environmental burden index (EBI)—integrating air pollution, water pollution, hazardous sites, transportation infrastructure, and built environment factors—may better represent holistic environmental impacts on oral health. Nevertheless, large-scale epidemiological evidence linking comprehensive environmental burdens to population-level tooth loss remains lacking.

Current evidence shows environmental burdens disproportionately affect socially vulnerable neighborhoods and vary by geographic region [7, 11]. However, it remains unclear whether environmental burdens interact significantly with neighborhood characteristics like social vulnerability or geographical location. Understanding how these factors collectively influence oral health could help develop targeted interventions. Such evidence would better guide efforts to reduce environmental hazards and improve oral health outcomes.

Therefore, we aimed to estimate the association between cumulative neighborhood-level environmental burden (collective and separate environmental burden domains) and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly based on a national dataset across the US. Moreover, we also sought to evaluate whether the association between environmental burden and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly varied across different levels of neighborhoods with different stratification factors (including social vulnerability, rurality, geographical region, and sex composition).

Materials and methods

Data source and study sample

This is a cross-sectional study of 72 333 US neighborhoods using US Census tract-level data on cumulative environmental burden from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The environmental burden index (EBI) was released by the US CDC and the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry in 2022. Data was collected together by the CDC, the US Environmental Protection Agency, the US Census Bureau, and the US Mine Safety and Health Administration. The indicators were determined through a review of the scope definition of cumulative environmental burden literature, and a series of evaluations were conducted on their accuracy, reliability, effectiveness, and scale availability. This is the first national, granular, and comprehensive measure of exposure to cumulative environmental burden factors affecting population health. Of those 72 333 US neighborhoods, neighborhoods without teeth lost data (n = 577) and without EBI data (n = 111) were first excluded. Neighborhoods with the extreme max 0.5% and the extreme min 0.5% sex composition were then excluded (n = 718). Finally, 70 927 US neighborhoods were included in our main analysis (Fig. 1). This research followed the guidelines for STROBE reporting for cross-sectional studies. Ethics committee approval was not required as all data are publicly available.Fig. 1Study flow chart diagram. EBI

Data on neighborhood-level EBI was derived from the US CDC and the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry. The EBI is calculated at the individual neighborhood level based on data from 2014 to 2021 encompasses 17 environmental burden indicators and spans 5 separate domains, including: domain 1: air pollution; domain 2: hazardous and toxic sites (eg, coal and lead mines); domain 3: the built environment (eg, a lack of recreational parks and a lack of neighborhood walkability); domain 4: transportation infrastructure (eg, high-volume roads and airports); domain 5: water pollution. The specific composition indicators of the 5 environmental burden domains are described in detail in STable 1. Each indicator is a percentile rank. The EBI is calculated by summing all the included indicators and converting the sum score into a total percentile score. The EBI score range for each neighborhood is from 0 to 1, where 0 represents the lowest cumulative environmental burden and 1 represents the highest cumulative environmental burden [11].

Covariates

Neighborhood-level age proportion (percentage of the elderly) score and sex composition data were obtained from the 2018 American Community Survey. Data on neighborhood-level routine check-ups last year was obtained from the CDC PLACES. According to the 2013 Rural–Urban Continuum Codes from the US Department of Agriculture, neighborhoods were designated as located in the urban (1–3) or in the rural (4–9).

The neighborhood-level social vulnerability index (SVI) was also derived from the CDC and the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry. The SVI encompasses 14 indicators based on 2015–2019 5-year data and spans 4 aspects, including (1) racial or ethnic minority status, (2) socioeconomic status, (3) household characteristics, and (4) housing type. Each indicator is a percentile rank. Similar to the EBI, the SVI is calculated by summing all the included indicators and converting the sum score into a total percentile score. The SVI score range for each neighborhood is from 0 to 1, where 0 represents the least social vulnerability and 1 represents the most social vulnerability [12].

Outcomes

We evaluated the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly (adults aged >= 65 years). The prevalence of all of their natural teeth lost within each Census tract (hereafter, neighborhood) among the elderly was extracted from the 2020 CDC PLACES data set, which is based on a small-area estimation approach that provided prevalence estimates across the US. Data on the self-reported prevalence of complete edentulism was collected through Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System surveys (BRFSS) via participants' responses to cellphone and telephone interviews. The BRFSS is a health-related system in the US that collects state data on health-related risk behaviors, chronic health conditions, and prevention service usage among US residents.

Statistical analysis

R-4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for all statistical analysis. All neighborhoods were divided into 3 EBI tertiles based on their EBI values. Neighborhoods in the first tertile were designated as the low environmentally burdened, while those in the third tertile were designated as the high environmentally burdened. Baseline characteristics were presented as the median ± interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables with skewed distributions and as the frequency (percentages) for categorical variables. Differences between the two groups were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous non-normal distributed variables, and the Fisher’s exact test or chi-square test for categorical variables. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The highest and the lowest missing rates of the included confounders were 0.013% and 0%, respectively (STable 7). Missing data filling utilized multiple imputation based on the mice package in R. There was no significant difference in the subsequent analysis results of the database before and after interpolation.

In the main analysis, using EBI as a continuous variable and a categorical variable respectively, we conducted mixed-effects linear regression models to evaluate the association between neighborhood EBI and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly. County federal information processing standards (FIPS) was included as a random effect term to account for the impact of the clustering of neighborhoods within counties. Model 1 was adjusted for neighborhood age proportion (percentage of the elderly) score and sex composition; Model 2 was adjusted for neighborhood age proportion (percentage of the elderly) score, sex composition, SVI quartile, rurality, and region (Midwest, Northeast, South, or West). In addition, we assessed the relationships between 5 separate environmental burden domains and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly, respectively.

We further incorporated an interaction term between the EBI and the potential effect modifier into the adjusted model to check the modification effects of the neighborhood characteristics. Sex composition (male-percentage < = 50% vs male-percentage > 50%), SVI quartile (first, second, third, fourth), rurality (urban vs. rural), and region (Midwest, Northeast, South, or West) were included as potential modifiers. Then we classified the neighborhoods according to the above-mentioned neighborhoods characteristics and conducted stratified analyses.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted four sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of the study results. In the first sensitivity analysis, we used age proportion-based matching of neighborhoods with extremely different levels of EBI (the high-EBI vs the low-EBI). We made the differences of the proportions of the elderly of the two neighborhood pairs < 0.01 and obtained the first set of neighborhood pairs. Then, we conducted multivariate mixed-effects linear regression models to verify associations between EBI and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly.

In the second sensitivity analysis, similarly, we used geospatially-based matching of neighborhoods with extremely different levels of EBI (the high-EBI vs the low-EBI). We calculated the geographic distance based on the longitude and latitude coordinates of the geometric centroid of neighborhoods obtained from the US Census Bureau, matched the two neighborhood pairs based on geospatial proximity within 50 miles, and obtained the second set of neighborhood pairs. Then, we conducted multivariate mixed-effects linear regression models to verify associations between EBI and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly.

In the third sensitivity analysis, to eliminate the potential impact of healthcare measures, we further adjusted for the rate of receiving a health check-up last year and previous confounders to determine associations between EBI (collective and 5 separate environmental burden domains) and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly.

In the last sensitivity analysis, we used neighborhood matching (the high EBI vs. the low EBI) based on the proportion of dental visits in the past year with significant differences in EBI levels to validate the robustness of the research results. We made the differences of the proportions of dental visits in the past year of the two neighborhood pairs < 0.01 and obtained a set of neighborhood pairs. Then, we conducted multivariate mixed-effects linear regression models to verify associations between EBI and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly.

Discussion

In this national cross-sectional study, we observed an association between cumulative environmental burden (encompassing air pollution, hazardous/toxic sites, built environment, transportation infrastructure, and water pollution) and complete edentulism prevalence among the elderly across US neighborhoods. Of all the environmental burden domains, the built environment (domain 3) exhibited the strongest association with edentulism prevalence. The association was more pronounced in neighborhoods with male-percentage < = 50% and higher SVI scores, and in neighborhoods in the urban and the south of the US. To verify the robustness of the main findings, we used age proportion (percentage of the elderly) score-based matching and geospatially-based matching analyses and obtained results consistent with the main findings. To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study to systematically examine the association between cumulative environmental burden and neighborhood-level edentulism prevalence, providing new evidence on the potential links between environmental factors and oral health in aging populations.

Our findings demonstrate a significant correlation between cumulative environmental burden and complete edentulism—the most severe consequence of periodontal disease. The strongest association was observed with built environment burden, for example, limited access to recreational parks and reduced walkability. This aligns with previous research showing independent benefits of walking for periodontal health [13]. The observed associations between air pollution (ozone and PM2.5) and edentulism risk are consistent with studies from China and Korea documenting links between air pollutants and periodontitis [7, 8]. Similar consistency is seen with NHANES data showing increased periodontitis risk from PAH exposure [9]. Our water pollution investigation results are parallel to the report of China's National Oral Health Survey, which links heavy metals in water with periodontitis [14]. Furthermore, we found an independent association between hazardous/toxic locations (including coal mines and lead mines) and an increased incidence of edentulism, which is consistent with NHANES analysis of cadmium and lead exposure [10]. These collective findings provide consistent evidence that multiple environmental factors contribute to tooth loss risk in elderly populations, with built environment characteristics showing particularly strong associations. The results underscore the importance of considering cumulative environmental exposures when addressing oral health disparities in aging populations.

Combined with the fact periodontitis is one of the major causes of severe tooth loss [2, 15], several possible mechanisms for the association between cumulative environmental burden and periodontitis have been proposed. According to the pathogenesis of periodontal disease, plaque bacteria trigger local and systemic inflammatory responses, upregulating levels of pro-inflammatory mediators and matrix metalloproteinases, ultimately leading to periodontal tissue destruction [15, 16]. Of the mechanisms of periodontitis, oxidative stress might play a key role in the establishment and progression of periodontitis by providing an environment conducive to the destructive processes observed in periodontitis [7, 15]. Prior studies on animal models and humans have shown that environmental pollutants from air, water, hazardous and toxic sites, and traffic (including PM2.5, UFPM, and NP, metals, and organic chemicals) can lead to chronic systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [17–19]. Furthermore, as multiple researches have documented in detail, air pollution, built environment, and green space will also lead to changes in gut microbiota and DNA methylation [17, 20–23]. It is evidenced that DNA methylation might alter periodontitis susceptibility and treatment outcomes [24, 25], while gut microbiota could interact with oral microbiota and participate in the pathogenesis of periodontitis [26]. Collectively, previous studies attempt to elucidate potential biological mechanisms of individual environmental risk factors on periodontitis, further research is needed to explore the impact of cumulative environmental burden on oral health.

Further analysis showed that cumulative environmental burden disproportionately influences neighborhoods with different characteristics. In neighborhoods with lower percentage of males and higher SVI, as well as neighborhoods in the urban and the south of the US, the association between EBI and complete edentulism among the elderly is more remarkable. Neighborhoods with higher percentage of females being more susceptible to the cumulative environmental burden might be due to the different anti-oxidative mechanisms by sex in response to pollution [27]. Previous studies have suggested in experimental animal models and humans, that females might be more vulnerable to traffic-related air pollution-induced oxidative stress compared with males on the one hand, and pollutants are more likely to deposit in females' lungs relative to males on the other hand. These two aspects collectively contributed to the more severe oxidative stress in pollution-exposed females than males, which might result in gender-differentiated progression of periodontitis [15, 27–29]. We also observed that the most socially vulnerable neighborhoods were more likely to bear higher levels of cumulative environmental burden, which is consistent with what has been documented in previous studies [11, 30]. Lower economic and educational levels, as well as higher proportions of race and ethnic minorities, may lead to lower political power and resources, thereby affecting the decision-making process of advocating policies that mitigate cumulative environmental burdens [11]. Moreover, we also observed that as the urban ratio increases, the EBI value also increases, and that neighborhoods located in the urban are more susceptible to the impact of the cumulative environmental burden on edentulous burden among the elderly. Considering the high proportion of neighborhoods located in urban and their continued growth in the coming decades, controlling the cumulative environmental burden is crucial in urban planning [31]. In addition, neighborhoods located in the South and Midwest of the US were observed to be more susceptible to the cumulative environmental burden-related edentulous burden among the elderly. The geographic difference suggested that policies and initiatives that mitigate the cumulative environmental burden should be formulated and implemented differentially by geographic region.

The study has several limitations. First, the inherent limitation of an observational study makes it challenging to establish a causal relationship between neighborhood-level EBI and the prevalence of all teeth lost; Second, although the cross-sectional analysis adjusted for demographic characteristics (age proportion and sex composition), social vulnerability, and geographic regions, there might still be other potential confounders that were not captured by the research dataset. However, the results were highly concordant even after performing age ration-based matching and geospatially-based matching analyses to address the possibility of unmeasured factors related to age composition and geography. Third, similar to the majority of previous studies, the calculation of EBI relied on the residential neighborhood addresses without considering incorporating physical activity [17]. Fourth, the prevalence of complete edentulism was obtained from self-reported diagnoses and like other self-reported sampling surveys, this data is also subject to social desirability and recall biases. Fifth, dental treatments for periodontitis and severe caries may alter the timing of teeth lost. Although we matched neighborhoods for the proportion of dental visits in the past year, residual confounding may still exist. Sixth, owing to the remote pathway between exposure to cumulative environmental burden and its potential pathological mechanisms, we observed a small but significant association. However, taking into account the universality of cumulative environmental burden on a global scale, small impacts translate to a substantial number of excess edentulous cases at a population level. Finally, our findings are crucial for understanding the association between cumulative environmental burden and oral aging health on the population level but caution should be exercised when generalizing exposure and outcomes to individual levels.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this national study across US neighborhoods indicated a modest but statistically significant association between the cumulative environmental burden and the prevalence of complete edentulism among the elderly. Our findings added support to the limited evidence of the negative impact of cumulative environmental burden on oral health among the elderly. Considering the worsening health burden of all teeth lost, it might be necessary for health systems, policymakers, and healthcare providers to intensify efforts to implement multifaceted strategies that both identify and address the cumulative environmental burden and its adverse effects on oral health.
