What is the best curriculum timing for an educational podcast aiming to improve medical students’ attitudes towards physician-pharmacist collaboration: a pre-post study
Authors: Magali L.A. Heijkoop, Salma Baddou, Jorie Versmissen, Andrea M. Woltman, Floor van Rosse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSafe and effective pharmacotherapeutic care requires collaboration between physicians and pharmacists. Such interprofessional collaboration demands mutual understanding of roles and responsibilities. Since interprofessional education can be logistically complex to implement, educational podcasts can provide a flexible and low cost alternative to facilitate unilateral education focusing on collaboration. To improve medical students’ understanding of the role and responsibilities of pharmacists, we created an educational podcast episode about community pharmacists.In this study, we explored the best timing in the curriculum to improve medical students' attitudes toward physician-pharmacist collaboration using an educational podcast. MethodsWe recorded a 48 minute audio-podcast episode in an interview format about the roles and responsibilities of community pharmacists. A prospective study using a pre-post design was conducted to evaluate the learning outcomes of the podcast in second year bachelor and final year master medical students. The effect on attitude towards physician-pharmacist collaboration was evaluated using the Scale of Attitudes towards Physician-Pharmacist Collaboration (SATP2C). SATP2C scores before and after listening to the podcast were evaluated using a paired t-test. Thematic analysis of the open ended questions was used to evaluate participants’ learning experiences and perceptions of the podcast.ResultsRespectively 161 bachelor and 105 master students agreed to take part in the study and completed the first questionnaire. Thirty-six participants in the bachelor group, and 32 participants in the master group filled out both questionnaires. Bachelor students showed a greater increase in attitude towards physician-pharmacist collaboration after listening to the podcast episode, with their mean SATP2C score improving significantly from 52.08 to 54.39 (p=0.001). Master students’ SATP2C score increased slightly, from 53.22 to 54.06. Analysis of the open-ended responses revealed that both groups gained new insights into the role and responsibilities of pharmacists.ConclusionsEducational podcasts can improve medical students’ attitude towards physician-pharmacist collaboration, particularly when introduced early in the curriculum. Podcasts offer a flexible and scalable format to enhance role understanding and promote interprofessional awareness among students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08439-2.

Background

Safe and effective pharmacotherapeutic care requires collaboration between physicians and pharmacists. Knowing about and valuing the skills and responsibilities of other team members can lead to more effective communication and collaboration in the context of medication safety, which is critical to safe medication prescribing [1]. The importance of medical students developing effective collaborative relationships with other healthcare professionals is empathised by multiple curricular frameworks for undergraduate medical education [2, 3]. Such interprofessional collaboration demands mutual understanding of roles and responsibilities [4]. In the United Kingdom, the medical training framework even explicitly describes that medical graduates have to be able to describe the role of pharmacists in making decisions about medications, and have to be able to collaborate with pharmacists in prescribing [5]. However, the responsibilities of pharmacists are often learned in clinical practice. While some medical students encounter pharmacists during clinical internships and learn about their responsibilities through this interaction, some might complete their training without any meaningful interaction with a pharmacist. This lack of knowledge about pharmacists’ roles and responsibilities can even persist after graduation, with physicians describing pharmacists as an ‘unknown group’ in a Norwegian study on the positioning of community pharmacists [6].

One way to improve medical students’ knowledge about the roles and responsibilities of pharmacists is interprofessional education [7]. However, due to logistical and time constraints and high costs, educators need to be critical of how and when to implement interprofessional education [8]. Interprofessional education is defined by the World Health Organization as education that ‘occurs when two or more professions learn about, from and with each other to enable effective collaboration and improve health outcome’ [9]. Alternatively, education for collaboration refers to broader educational forms that improve collaboration, including educational interventions where only one profession participates [8]. Paradis et al. argue that education for collaboration should provide developmentally appropriate, uniprofessional opportunities supplemented by workplace-based team training [8]. Such uniprofessional opportunities can be achieved using less costly and more flexible, and preferably open access, e-learning tools such as podcasts [10]. Podcasts can be accessed at any time and place, making them the ideal tool for just in time and self-directed learning [11]. Educational podcasts have previously been used to address topics that are not sufficiently addressed in the medical school curriculum, covering a wide variety of topics [12–14]. For instance, Aase et al. used a podcast to introduce students to the concept of the daily “huddle” at the clinical ward to teach about interprofessional interaction and communication. Since this topic is usually learned informally through experience and not directly addressed in the curriculum, the podcast was created to provide a more consistent and structured background. Podcasts have also been used in undergraduate medical education to introduce students to the perspectives of various individuals, ranging from people experiencing various conditions and their friends, family and caregivers to professionals working in jobs less visible to students [15–17].

The importance of improving the education of medical students about pharmacists’ contribution to the health-care team has been previously emphasised by Wolfe et al. [18]. When designing education for collaboration, the question of when it should be implemented arises. Early education about the roles and responsibilities of pharmacists has the potential to encourage medical students to seek out interaction with pharmacists during their clinical internships. However, students in the first years of their curriculum may still struggle to understand the scope of their own role and responsibilities [8]. In this study, we aimed to explore the best timing in the curriculum to improve medical students’ attitudes toward physician-pharmacist collaboration using an educational podcast.

Methods

Intervention

Our institution has a medical school, but no pharmacy school. We are currently piloting a small-scale interprofessional education activity with medical students, nursing students and pharmacy students interning at our inpatient pharmacy. In order to address the topic of physician-pharmacist collaboration for a larger group of students, we designed and recorded an educational podcast episode aimed to improve medical students understanding of the role and responsibilities of community pharmacists in the Netherlands. Community pharmacy was chosen because most medical students get their first prescribing experience during their general practice internship. In the podcast episode, a sixth year medical student and a pharmacist (MH) interviewed an experienced community pharmacist and a pharmacy student about the different responsibilities of community pharmacists in the Netherlands. The interview was based on questions submitted by medical students of different experience levels and junior doctors. Based on these questions, six learning objectives for medical students were drafted (Table 1). MH, the medical student and the pharmacy student made an outline with talking points based on these learning goals. The podcast was recorded in the hospitals’ podcast studio, and edited by their editor.

The podcast was hosted on various streaming platforms, and an information sheet with the learning objectives, key concepts, relevant sources and self-study questions was included in the show notes. The episode was part of a podcast series called ‘FarmacoLOGISCH’, a freely available educational podcast series about pharmacology and pharmacotherapy aimed at medical students [19].

Overview of study design

This prospective study was conducted from October 2024 until February 2025 at the ErasmusMC University Medical Centre in the Netherlands. We used a pre-post design to evaluate the impact of the podcast in second year bachelor and final year master students. The overview of the study design is displayed in Fig. 1.

Overview of study design

Setting and participants

In the Netherlands, medical school takes six years. It is divided into a three year bachelor program and a three year master program. In the ErasmusMC, students start their clinical internships in the master. Master students follow 63 weeks of clinical internships, with each internship period being interspersed with a teaching block. For this study we recruited second year bachelor students in the pre-clinical phase and final year master students, who had followed 40 to 46 weeks of clinical internships.

Data collection tool

We assessed the changes in medical students’ attitudes and perceptions towards pharmacists-physician collaboration using a Dutch translation of the Scale of Attitudes of towards Physician-Pharmacist Collaboration (SATP2C) [20]. This questionnaire consists of 16 item, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = agree, 4 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 16 to 64. Higher scores indicate a more positive attitude toward interprofessional collaboration.

The before-listening questionnaire consisted of demographical questions and the SATP2C [20]. The post-listening questionnaire consisted again of the SATP2C, and additional questions about the participants’ learning experience with the podcast. Both questionnaires are included in the supplementary materials.

SATP2C translation

To account for linguistic and cultural differences, as well as to ensure accuracy and relevance, we translated the SATP2C to Dutch using the guidelines for cross-cultural adaption of self-reported measures by Beaton et al. [21]. The questionnaire was translated to Dutch by SB and by MH and synthesized to a first Dutch version. This first version was back translated by a bilingual community pharmacist. SB revised the Dutch translation based on the back translation. This version was pretested to a group of six pharmacy and three medical students. Their feedback was used to finalise the Dutch translation. Retrospective permission to use the SATP2C in this study was obtained from Thomas Jefferson University [22].

Recruitment and data collection

SB and MH recruited students by pitching the study at the end of class. They were not involved in teaching the class. Students already received the participant information leaflet beforehand through their learning management system (Canvas). After the pitch, students who wished to participate in the study received an informed consent letter and the first questionnaire. The first questionnaire was conducted on paper. The answers were entered into Castor EDC by SB and MH, after which participants received an email with a link to listen to the podcast episode. The episode was available on multiple streaming platforms, to encourage participants to use the podcast in a way that was convenient to them. The email also contained a link to the second questionnaire in Castor EDC, to fill out after listening to the episode. Reminders were sent after one, two and four weeks to participants who had not yet completed the second questionnaire.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 28.0.1. The internal consistency of the SATP2C scale was assessed using the Cronbach’s alpha [23]. After confirming normal distribution, a paired t-test was conducted to compare the pre-podcast SATP2C score to the post-podcast score for both the bachelor and the master group separately [24]. Descriptive statistics were used for the demographic characteristics of participants, and for the data on participants’ usage of the episode.

Participants’ written responses to the two open questions were extracted to Microsoft Excel for analysis. SB and MH read the responses to familiarize themselves with the data. Initial coding was done by SB, who developed a preliminary code book. MH used this codebook to re-code the data, and redefine the codebook. FvR coded a sample of the data using this codebook. A high degree of intercoder agreement was observed between SB, MH and FvR. SB, MH and FvR discussed all codes together and developed themes from the codes.

Results

Participants

We approached 367 students in the bachelor phase and 240 students in the master phase to participate in our study. Respectively 161 (43.9%) and 105 (43.8%) students agreed to take part in the study and completed the first questionnaire. The second questionnaire was completed by 36 (22.4%) participants in the bachelor group, and 32 (30.5%) participants in the master group. Table 2 provides the demographics of the respondents. The majority of participants (82.9% in bachelor group and 68.8% in master group) reported that they filled out the second questionnaire within 24 h of listening to the podcast. The open questions were filled out by 65 participants (35 in the bachelor group and 30 in the master).

SATP2C score

The internal consistency of the translated SATP2C scale was deemed reliable with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.832.

Overall, the mean SATP2C score in the bachelor group improved significantly from 52.08 (4.75) pre-podcast to 54.39 (5.52) post-podcast (p = 0.001) (Table 3). This change in score was mainly attributed to an improvement of item 7 (Pharmacists have special expertise in counselling patients on drug treatment) and a decrease in score for item 9 (The primary function of the pharmacist is to fill the physician’s prescription without question). Item 7 improved significantly from 2.86 (0.64) to 3.5 (0.66), while item 9, a reverse scored item, decreased from 1.67 (0.76) to 1.19 (0.47). For the master group, the SATP2C score improved minimally from 53.2 (4.4) to 54.1 (5.5), however this increase was not significant (p = 0.252) (Table 3). In the master group, the change in score could not be attributed to one or multiple items, as some items increased minimally while other items decreased. Participants’ perceptions of the contents

Participants in the bachelor group rated the educational value of the episode with a mean of 3.8 (SD+- 0.58), while the master participants rated it 3.6 (SD +- 0.72). Four major themes were identified in the responses to the open questions regarding what participants’ liked and disliked about the content of the podcast episode (Fig. 2). These themes and the underlying codes are displayed in Fig. 2. The themes ‘Gaining insight into the medication and prescribing process’ and ‘Getting to know a new and different perspective’ were mainly supported by the answers to the question what participants liked about the content of the episode, while the themes ‘ Alignment of content relevance and depth’ and ‘Importance of collaboration’ were mainly supported by answers to the questions what participants disliked about the episode.Fig. 2Themes and underlying codes for the open questions about the contents of the podcast episode

Themes and underlying codes for the open questions about the contents of the podcast episode

The first theme was ‘Gaining insight into the medication and the prescribing process’. Participants indicated that they were unfamiliar with the role and the responsibilities of pharmacists, and that listening to the episode helped them understand the medication process. One participant reported: “I found it educational to learn that doctors can easily get in touch with pharmacists to discuss things like the dosage of a medication, which medications are needed, and possible side effects. This sounds very logical, as it allows for (physician and pharmacist) knowledge to be combined in order to ensure better patient care and patient safety. I hadn’t heard this before (I’m currently in my second year of medical school).” (M19, bachelor student). Multiple participants, mostly bachelor students, also reported not knowing that both physicians and patients could consult a pharmacist.

The second theme was ‘Getting to know a new and different perspective’. Participants reported that the usage of cases from clinical practice supported them in gaining insight into the perspective of pharmacists. Many respondents, both from the master and the bachelor, reported that this topic was not covered in their regular curriculum. With one participant reporting: ‘It’s very insightful to get a look into how a community pharmacy operates; it’s something we don’t actually hear anything about during the rest of the training, which is quite strange.’ (F24, master student). And another participant reporting: ‘In medical school, I had never learned about the role of pharmacists, so there was a lot I didn’t know. The fact that it was presented by several pharmacists (in training) from different perspectives made it even more educational’ (F19, bachelor student).

Both the themes ‘Gaining insight into the medication and the prescribing process’ and ‘Getting to know a new and different perspective’ were equally covered in the responses of participants in the bachelor and master phase. However, comments made by bachelor participants were more general, for example describing ‘I learned about the everyday work of a pharmacist’ (F19, bachelor student). Contrastingly, comments made by participants in the master phase were more detailed and related to clinical practice, such as comments on drug reimbursements, medication reviews and medication shortages. For example, one student mentioned ‘I now have a better understanding of the community pharmacy, reimbursements, and drug shortages’ (F, master student).

The third theme was ‘Alignment of content relevance and depth’. Some participants expressed a wish for more in depth content. What kind of content participants missed varied. Many participants reported wanting to hear more cases and examples from practice, while other participants expressed wanting more insight into how pharmacists and pharmacy technicians are educated, and what skills and competencies they have. One participant expressed wanting to hear ‘more biological information’. Participants also reported missing the perspective of the physician, with one participant expressing: “I think the role of the doctor could have been incorporated more, as it was now told solely from the perspective of an experienced pharmacist.” (F18, bachelor student). Contrastingly, while many participants reported on topics that they wanted to hear more about, some participants mentioned that some topics could have been skipped in the episode. The introduction and the segment on the history of pharmacists were identified as topics that were too long or unnecessary.

The last theme we identified was ‘Importance of collaboration’. Some participants expressed that they missed an explanation on how pharmacists and physicians can collaborate, and a vision on the importance of collaboration in general. One student provided an in depth reflection into this topic: “I found everything educational. However, there is something I would have liked to hear in the podcast: what are the solutions and ideas to improve collaboration between doctors and pharmacists? I’m very curious whether this is being addressed in education and if there are any plans to integrate it into the curriculum.” (M19, bachelor student).

Notably, 19 participants replied with ‘not applicable’ or ‘nothing’ to the question about what they disliked or liked less about the episode.

Using a podcast as a learning tool

How participants used the podcast as a learning tool varied. About half (49.3%) of the participants reported listening to the entire episode without pausing. 31.3% of the participants reported listening to the episode in two parts, and 19.4% listened to the episode in more than two parts. Almost all participants listened to the episode combined with another activity. Only two participants reported that they solely listened. In the analysis of the open questions, participants reported that listening to a podcast was an enjoyable way of studying, and that they would like more pharmacology and pharmacotherapy education delivered in this manner. However, many participants were critical of the length of the podcast and deemed it too long. Some participants related this to the interview format of the episode, and reported that they would have preferred a more concise way of information delivery.

Participants also reported that four speakers was too many, with one participant stating: ‘ Max 2–3 speakers; with more speakers, at some point you no longer know who is who and from which perspective a question is being asked.” (F, Bachelor student). Two participants reported that the audio quality was distracting.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the best timing in the curriculum to improve medical students’ attitudes toward physician-pharmacist collaboration using an educational podcast. Bachelor students showed a greater increase in attitude towards physician-pharmacist collaboration after listening to the podcast episode, with their mean SATP2C score improving significantly from 52.08 to 54.39. Master students’ SATP2C score only increased slightly, from 53.22 to 54.06. Analysis of the open-ended responses revealed that both groups gained new insights into the role and responsibilities of pharmacists.

Timing of teaching medical students about pharmacist-physician collaboration

The similar baseline SATP2C scores of bachelor students and master students (52.08 vs. 53.22) suggest that clinical exposure alone does not substantially improve attitudes towards collaboration. Similar results were seen in the two validation studies of the SATP2C, where fist year medical students only scored slightly lower than third year medical students (52.0 vs. 53.8) [20, 25]. Notably, in Brazil, first year medical students scored higher compared to last year medical students (52.3 v 49.5) [26]. Together, these findings suggest that as students progress, openness to collaboration with pharmacists may plateau or decline. While bachelor students’ attitude towards collaboration increased more after listening to the podcast, our qualitative data indicate that both bachelor and master students valued gaining an understanding of pharmacists’ responsibilities. Participants in both groups indicated that this topic was not covered in their regular curriculum, and that they would like to learn more about how to collaborate with pharmacists. While most participants in the master group indicated that the content of the episode was new to them, some indicated that the information was known to them. Because most participants reported that the tasks and responsibilities are not covered in the regular curriculum, this suggests that students have varied exposure to this topic during clinical internships. Early introduction of pharmacist roles in the curriculum could therefore prevent misconceptions and encourage students to seek out interaction with pharmacists during clinical internships.

Appropriateness of a podcast

Audio podcasts are an ideal teaching tool to provide ‘just-in-time’ learning, allowing students to access educational content at a time and pace convenient to them [11]. Our results show that participants combined listening to the episode with activities such as travelling by public transport or cycling, or doing household chores. Not all participants listened to the episode in one part, but spread out listening over multiple sessions. This aligns with the principles of self-directed learning theory, which emphasizes learner autonomy in choosing when and how to engage with educational material [27]. Furthermore, our study showed that a podcast was a feasible teaching tool to introduce medical students to the roles and responsibilities of pharmacists. Using an asynchronous format such as a podcast for such learning goals also reduces logistical challenges associated with implementing synchronous interprofessional education [10, 28].

When creating an educational podcast, aspects like the format, length and audio quality are essential for the acceptance of students [29]. This was highlighted by many participants mentioning the length as something they disliked about the episode. In the design of the podcast, multiple guides suggesting best practices for educators creating medical education podcasts were consulted [29, 30]. These guides suggest that learners prefer an episode length of under 20 min [30, 31]. However, length can be a trade-off between learner preferences and educator goals. Due to the decision to choose an expert interview as the episode format, a length of under twenty minutes covering all learning goals was not achievable. Although the podcast was recorded in a professional studio and edited by an experienced editor, some comments about the audio quality were made.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, it is one of the few studies to evaluate an intervention to improve medical students’ attitude towards physician-pharmacist collaboration. Furthermore, we explored the usage of audio podcasts for education for collaboration. While synchronous interprofessional education can be difficult to implement, our study showed that podcasts offer a flexible, scalable and low-cost teaching tool that can be easily integrated into existing curricula. A strength in the design of the podcast was that we co-designed the contents with a medical and a pharmacy student. This ensured that topics were covered that were relevant for clinical practice, but were not covered in the medical curriculum.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Our study was conducted within a single institution and with a relatively small sample size due to the low response rate of the post-podcast questionnaire. It is possible that there was a selection bias because the students who filled out the second questionnaire might have been more motivated to learn about pharmacists, or to use a podcast as an educational tool. Due to the design of our study, it was not possible to assess if participants listened to the podcast but did not fill out the post-podcast questionnaire. However, all but one participants filled out the questions about what they liked and disliked about the podcast. This suggests that all participants who filled out the post-intervention questionnaire listened to the episode.

While statistically significant, the improvement of 2.3 points in the bachelor group is small, making it difficult to assess the educational impact. However, with a maximum possible SATP2C score of 64, the baseline results found in our study (52.08 for bachelor students and 53.22 for master students) were already high, making it difficult to show an improvement in SATP2C score after intervention. The educational impact of the intervention is supported by the qualitative results, which show that participants gained new insights into the roles and responsibilities of pharmacists. For the bachelor participants, this seemed to be related to a better role understanding. For the participants in the master phase, the educational impact seemed to be related to a better understanding of specific topics such as reimbursement of medicines, which were not covered in the SATP2C questionnaire.

Another limitation of our study is that the podcast episode was not integrated into the curriculum. Our intervention only consisted of an asynchronous self-study activity, and we did not evaluate if participants applied the gained knowledge in a class setting or in clinical practice. We suspect that the absence of follow-up learning activities might have contributed to the low response rate, as participants were only reminded to fill out the post-podcast survey by email.

Future directions

Our findings suggest that podcasts are a promising way of delivering foundational knowledge about pharmacists’ roles, particularly early in the medical curriculum. In education for collaboration, podcasts can serve as an efficient and scalable alternative to synchronous interprofessional education for knowledge transfer, reserving limited in-class time for interactive and higher-level learning activities. While we studied the roles and responsibilities of community pharmacists, podcasts can be applied to teach healthcare students about the roles of other healthcare professions, even combining more than two professions. For example, an episode about the in-hospital medication process can be used to improve role understanding for students of medicine, nursing and pharmacy. Ideally, such podcasts are implemented as a flipped classroom activity and supplemented by workplace based learning. Future research should explore integrating podcasts into structured educational activities and assess the long-term effects on collaborative behaviour in clinical practice.

Conclusion

An educational podcast can positively influence medical students’ attitude towards physician-pharmacist collaboration, especially when taught early in the medical curriculum. Our study demonstrated that bachelor students’ attitudes towards physician-pharmacist collaboration increased significantly after listening to the podcast episode. Additionally, our podcast helped all participants gain insight into the medication and prescribing process, introduced them to a new and different perspective and encouraged them to think about the importance of collaboration. Alignment of content relevance and depth are essential to consider when designing an educational podcast. Due to its flexibility, educational podcasts are a suitable modality to deliver uniprofessional education for collaboration on topics such as role understanding, for students of various health professions.
