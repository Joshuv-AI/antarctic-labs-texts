AI-driven tongue image analysis for diagnosing and predicting coronary artery disease
Authors: Alireza Hekmat-Ardakani, Hamid R Rabiee, Armin Behnamnia, Amir Hooman Kazemi, Amir Sobh Rakhshankhah, Ramtin Mahmoudi Kashani, Alireza Yargholi, Bahar Morshed-Behbahani, Abolfazl Malekahmadi, Hamidreza Hekmat, Mehrdad Karimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronary artery disease is a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with current diagnostic methods presenting challenges of invasiveness and accuracy. This study investigates artificial intelligence-powered tongue image analysis as a novel, non-invasive diagnostic tool for coronary artery disease. Drawing on principles from Traditional Iranian Medicine, we analyzed tongue features, including color, fissures, coating, moisture, and red spots, in 906 patients undergoing coronary angiography. A dataset of 12,000 tongue images was used to train artificial intelligence models, which were created using high-resolution images captured with a Visioface device. The Segment-Anything Model facilitated tongue segmentation, while DeepLabv3+/DINOv2 architectures were employed for feature extraction and stenosis classification. Results indicated that artificial intelligence predictions achieved up to 80% accuracy for left circumflex artery stenosis, highlighting tongue color and fissures as significant predictors of this condition. This study presents artificial intelligence-driven tongue analysis as a rapid and cost-effective adjunct for screening coronary artery disease, particularly in resource-limited settings, and warrants further validation in diverse populations.Keywords: Tongue image analysis, Coronary artery disease, Traditional iranian medicine, Artificial intelligenceSubject terms: Cardiology, Image processing, Diagnosis

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases are the leading global cause of death, with coronary artery disease (CAD) being the primary contributor1. Notably, coronary artery disease can be asymptomatic in a significant proportion of individuals2. Early and precise diagnosis of these disorders can significantly reduce their mortality and morbidity. Simple and accessible methods for diagnosing heart disease can help practitioners make more convenient and faster diagnoses, ultimately improving patients’ health. While coronary angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosing CAD, its time-consuming nature and high costs, coupled with its invasive procedures, often deter physicians from ordering this test except in critical cases, and patients may be reluctant to undergo it3. Electrocardiography (ECG) is a widely used, noninvasive, and cost-effective diagnostic procedure that does not necessitate expensive equipment. While interpreting ECG results requires specialized training, this does not preclude many individuals from obtaining such training. Notably, the average accuracy of ECG in diagnosing CAD is approximately 57.8%, highlighting its limited diagnostic performance in assessing CAD4. Recently, a risk factor classifier has been used in conjunction with ECG to enhance its accuracy in diagnosing CAD. While this approach can improve ECG accuracy to as much as 94%, the classification and consideration of these risk factors necessitate a comprehensive understanding of the patient’s medical history and records, which is often unfeasible in emergencies5.

The exercise tolerance test enhances the diagnostic accuracy for CAD to 77%; however, its implementation necessitates specialized facilities and equipment, limiting its feasibility in all healthcare settings6. Additionally, echocardiography serves as another non-invasive diagnostic modality for CAD, demonstrating a sensitivity of 87% in identifying the condition7. However, echocardiography requires specialized personnel and appropriate equipment, which are not consistently available across all clinical centers8. Non-invasive cardiac imaging techniques that utilize radiation, such as CT scans, CT angiography, and myocardial perfusion scans, can enhance diagnostic sensitivity and accuracy, achieving rates of up to 94%9. In these instances, in addition to being costly and necessitating dedicated space, specialized personnel, and specific tools and equipment, these methods are further limited by their reliance on radiation. Therefore, the availability of more non-invasive techniques increases the likelihood of swiftly achieving an accurate diagnosis.

The application of artificial intelligence (AI) has recently emerged as a transformative force in cardiovascular diagnostics, offering promising solutions to overcome the limitations of traditional methods. Beyond conventional approaches, AI is being successfully applied to various non-invasive modalities. For instance, AI-enabled electrocardiography (AI-ECG) models have shown remarkable accuracy in identifying a wide range of cardiac conditions, from predicting long-term atrial fibrillation risk to detecting left ventricular hypertrophy. Similarly, AI algorithms are being integrated with echocardiography and cardiac MRI to automate image analysis, improve measurement accuracy, and facilitate early detection of structural heart diseases. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated the potential of AI in analyzing chest X-rays and retinal images to predict cardiovascular risk, proving that subtle visual biomarkers can be leveraged to screen for systemic diseases like CAD10,11.

Tongue assessment is a common practice in many holistic medicines, such as Traditional Iranian Medicine and Traditional Chinese Medicine, used by medical practitioners to evaluate a patient’s condition in various diseases, including heart disease. As an anatomical structure, the tongue serves as a reflecting indicator of the body’s physiology and pathology. The examination of the tongue is based on visual clues, with color, shape, movement, texture, and coating all playing important roles in the diagnostic process. Furthermore, the geometric arrangement of the tongue, which includes measurements such as thickness, size, fissures, and indentations, emerges as a contributing factor in evaluating an individual’s health12.

Recent studies suggest that the tongue can serve as a valuable diagnostic tool for detecting manifestations of internal and systemic diseases13. For example, iron deficiency anemia causes flattening of the tongue papillae. In the context of autoimmune diseases, the tongue manifestations are primarily due to the inflammation caused by the disease. For example, in Sjögren’s syndrome, the tongue is dry and appears bald, with a tombstone-like texture, exhibiting visible cracks and fissures. In Kawasaki, the tongue becomes swollen and red, and due to swelling of the papillae, it becomes strawberry-shaped. In scleroderma, the tongue becomes smooth, and its mobility decreases. In amyloidosis, a large and swollen tongue (macroglossia) is often present, and in lupus, granulomatous ulcers may appear on the tongue. Endocrine disorders also present with distinct manifestations on the tongue. These include tongue enlargement and macroglossia in hypothyroidism, as well as patchy brown macular pigmentation on the tongue in Addison’s disease. Tongue symptoms in diabetic patients include a dry tongue with large areas of thick, yellow coating13–15. Cardiovascular diseases may also present with a range of symptoms on the tongue, underscoring the notion that the tongue serves as a window to cardiac health16. The coloration of the tongue is a significant factor in predicting the severity of coronary artery stenosis17,18. In patients with stasis and coronary artery disease, the tongue often exhibits a bluish or reddish-purple coloration19.

Despite the ease and noninvasiveness of tongue examination, obtaining an objective and standardized evaluation proved to be a difficult task. Recent scholarly efforts have focused on addressing these issues. In recent years, artificial intelligence has become increasingly popular, enhancing various diagnostic capabilities through objective and standardized observations20.

Artificial intelligence and its associated technologies can analyze vast amounts of image data in medical science to more accurately determine the relationship between observed symptoms and signs in these images and the related diseases.

Within the medical field, several straightforward applications of artificial intelligence utilizing artificial neural networks can be highlighted. An exemplary application is assisting with medical diagnoses, such as those for melanoma. This is accomplished by using dermoscopic images of specific lesions obtained with a cell phone camera21.

Artificial intelligence algorithms have achieved precision comparable to that of professional practitioners using this approach22. As a result, the concept of computer-assisted tongue diagnosis offers great promise for providing more consistent and objective health evaluations, particularly in fields such as heart disease assessment23. This diagnostic technique can play a crucial role in cases where the rapid identification and implementation of therapeutic interventions are critical for limiting the progression of the patient’s condition, such as cardiovascular disorders.

Recognizing the high prevalence and critical importance of CAD, we have initiated a research project to investigate potential correlations between CAD and symptoms related to the tongue. This study aims to leverage advanced AI techniques to identify and analyze the relationship between tongue manifestations and CAD, thereby contributing to better diagnostic and preventive strategies in cardiovascular health.

The primary aim of this study is to establish a precise relationship between tongue signs and CAD through artificial intelligence (AI) and tongue image analysis. This could lead to the development of software capable of analyzing coronary artery status based on tongue images. The specific objectives of the study are as follows:

Within this rapidly expanding field, our study introduces a novel and non-invasive approach by leveraging traditional tongue diagnosis with advanced AI and computer vision techniques. While other AI-driven methods rely on complex and sometimes costly imaging, our method harnesses readily available visual information from the tongue. This makes it a unique and potentially scalable solution for mass screening, particularly in low-resource settings. By integrating the rich visual biomarkers of the tongue with a sophisticated deep learning framework, our research complements existing non-invasive diagnostic tools. It offers a new avenue for the early detection and risk stratification of CAD.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional study. From July 2021 to February 2022, tongue images were collected from 906 patients hospitalized in the angiography ward of Baharloo Hospital, affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences, due to cardiac symptoms. The methodology of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences under code IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.675. All procedures conducted in this study were carried out in accordance with the guidelines of this committee.

In parallel with the imaging process, a subset of our tongue images was manually annotated by a board-certified expert in Traditional Medicine. These expert annotations served as the ground truth for training our automated feature extraction models. It is crucial to note that while this expert evaluation provided the necessary foundation, our primary deep learning model was not trained on these subjective ratings. Instead, the model was designed to extract objective, quantitative features directly from the images. For coating (thickness and area) and tongue color, we don’t use expert annotations; instead, a fixed, automated image processing algorithm based on CIELAB color space is used, which doesn’t contain any assumptions other than the definition of the CIELAB space.

Artificial intelligence

We have a dataset comprising facial images of 906 subjects captured from three angles: front and two sides, along with their angiography results, which are labeled with the degree of stenosis for different regions of the LCX, RCA, and LAD. Additionally, we have more than 12,000 images from the normal population without specific labels. Our current focus is on the frontal images of the subjects.

To overcome the limitations of a relatively small, labeled clinical dataset, our methodology incorporates a two-stage training process. For feature extraction, our deep learning models were first pre-trained on a large, auxiliary dataset of 12,000 unannotated tongue images from the general population. This self-supervised pre-training was specifically critical for learning to identify and segment features with low prevalence, such as teeth marks and red spots, and for generalizing to highly variable features like moisture and fissures, which require a large number of images for robust training. This approach is crucial as it leverages a vast amount of available unlabeled data to establish a strong foundational knowledge for the model. Following this, the pre-trained models were fine-tuned on our primary, labeled dataset of 906 CAD patients for the specific task of disease prediction. This transfer learning approach significantly enhances the models’ performance and reduces the risk of overfitting to the limited clinical data.

Our proposed method consists of three main steps: (1) extraction of the tongue from the facial image, (2) extraction of tongue characteristics from the tongue image, and (3) estimation of the degree of coronary artery occlusion based on the extracted characteristics and tongue image. Tongue region extraction

The initial step involves processing the facial image for analysis. We employed the Segment-Anything Model (SAM)25, an image segmentation model based on visual transformers, to leverage its pre-trained capabilities in extracting the tongue region from the facial context. Using three fixed prompts, one for the foreground and two for the background, as shown in Fig. 5, we consistently segmented the tongues from the facial images. This method establishes a reliable foundation for the subsequent analysis.

Using SAM for facial image segmentation to extract the tongue region. This figure illustrates the function of the Segment Anything Model (SAM) in the initial stage of our image processing pipeline. Given an input prompt from the tongue and two different prompts from outside the tongue, the SAM model automatically and accurately identifies the “foreground” region, which is the tongue, and separates it from the “background” (the rest of the facial image). This method provides an efficient and accurate solution for the automated extraction of the tongue from the full image.

Tongue characteristics segmentation

In alignment with the principles of Iranian Traditional Medicine and other holistic medicines, such as TCM, we extract and note the suggested features that are critical for CAD. These include the coating, fissures, wetness, red spots, and tooth marks. Coatings are obtained differently from other features.

Detection of coating

To detect the coating, we process the image in the CIELAB color space. We focus on the ‘A’ channel, which represents the redness of the pixels (the intensity of red minus the intensity of green). This channel is denoised using a shifted truncation (ST) module defined as:

With parameters (a, b) = (145,119).

Otsu thresholding is then applied to isolate dominant areas. The inverse of this mask highlights regions with low red color intensity, which are then multiplied by the ‘B’ channel to identify regions exhibiting high yellow intensity and low red intensity. This result is denoised again using ST (; 120, 0). Finally, the output is filtered by the tongue mask to detect only the areas on the surface of the tongue, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Procedure for the quantitative analysis of tongue coating. This figure illustrates the computational process of extracting tongue coating. Regions of the tongue with low red color and high yellow tones, as measured in the CIELAB color space, are algorithmically identified as coating. The intensity and thickness of the coating are quantified based on the contrast of these pixels relative to the overall tongue color, providing an objective metric for this feature.

Detection of other tongue features

For the other characteristics, we utilize machine-learning techniques to develop models capable of performing pixel-wise segmentation of tongue images. To accomplish this, we require a training set of annotated tongue images. We organized mask annotation projects using the Label Studio library for each feature, with the annotations performed by a medical expert. A total of 450 tongue images were annotated and used to train our feature extraction models. Additionally, a dataset consisting of 12,000 tongue images from the general population was used to train the AI model to identify specific variables, including teeth marks, red spots, fissures, and moisture levels. These images were carefully reviewed by an Iranian medicine specialist, resulting in the selection and labeling of at least 200 representative images for each variable to ensure precision in the training process. Our ultimate goal is to develop a model that accurately detects the degree of occlusion in the LAD, LCX, and RCA arteries. The occlusion is categorized into three levels: ”Nothing/Mild” (low), ”Moderate” (medium), and ”Severe/Occluded” (high). For each artery, we train a separate model. We use the DINOv227 model architecture, a ViT-based deep neural network, which is used as a foundation model in Computer Vision. We replace the classification head of the model with a two-layer fully connected neural network, where the size of the hidden layer is 512, and fine-tune the entire model on our collected and annotated dataset. We conducted multiple experiments with different multichannel configurations, incorporating various combinations of extracted tongue characteristics and the RGB tongue image. Weighted cross-entropy loss is used to train the model. Weighted cross-entropy is used when the distribution of data samples in different classes is uneven, resulting in class imbalance in the dataset. The binary classification output of the model is designed to discern between two categories of heart vessel occlusions as observed in patient examinations: those indicative of severe occlusion and those without severe occlusion. DINOv2 is a Deep Neural Network trained based on self-supervised learning, in which models are trained on vast amounts of unsupervised image data. This enhances the representation power of the DINO model, making it a powerful tool for training on small image datasets. The patch-based global representations of the ViT architecture in DINOv2 make it a strong choice for complex structures and patterns in small-scale datasets. Moreover, DINOv2 demonstrates strong domain transfer capability and is well-suited for our scenario, where images of the human tongue are relatively rare in public image datasets. DeepLabV3 + is also especially good for fine-tuning on small datasets. The precise boundary recovery and strong capacity of this model make it suitable for complex segmentation tasks. DeepLabV3 + not only maintains spatial resolution but also utilizes a vast receptive field using atrous (dilated) convolutions, while controlling memory usage. The multi-scale feature fusion in this model enables it to recognize patterns and entities across different scales. SAM is a foundation model for image segmentation, typically much larger than CNN models used for training on most segmentation datasets. A model of such a large scale, efficiently trained on vast datasets (e.g., SA-1B with 1 billion segmentation masks and 11 billion images), is ideal for zero-shot inference. We utilize the pre-trained SAM model to directly and accurately identify the region of the tongue in the subject’s face, eliminating the need for training or additional datasets. The prompt-based structure of SAM enables us to specialize the task of segmentation for our own context and application. By using appropriate prompts, the accurate detection of the tongue’s region in the face was made possible due to SAM’s strong out-of-distribution segmentation capability. Also, SAM is object-agnostic and segments primarily based on visual coherence rather than semantical entities in the image.

In our analysis, tongue coating is treated as a distinct variable, separate from tongue color. The AI model did not require traditional machine learning methods to recognize this variable; instead, it was trained using advanced image processing techniques.

For the evaluation of tongue coating, three parameters were considered: area, color, and thickness. The final measurement of coating was calculated as a product of these three factors, thereby accounting for the influence of each aspect of the coating on the overall assessment.

Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the potential of tongue examination as a non-invasive and accessible diagnostic tool for assessing CVD. Utilizing artificial intelligence to analyze various tongue features such as size, color, fissures, coating, and moisture levels provides valuable insights into patients’ underlying health, particularly for individuals at risk of CAD. Our results suggest that specific tongue features may serve as reliable indicators of CVD, supporting prior research that has documented associations between tongue morphology and systemic diseases12–19. These findings support further investigation into the integration of tongue examination into routine clinical practice as a complementary, non-invasive approach for cardiovascular assessment.

This study also investigated the relationship between tongue symptoms and stenosis of the three main coronary arteries, LAD, LCX, and RCA. Leveraging AI, we developed a rapid, non-invasive method to accurately predict stenosis in these arteries, providing a novel quantitative assessment of stenosis severity. In a preliminary comparison, a single traditional medicine expert’s visual assessments of tongue features showed no statistically significant correlation with angiographic stenosis in any coronary artery or with the deep learning model’s predictions (p > 0.05). Traditional tongue diagnosis suffers from subjectivity and poor reproducibility, as expert-evaluated features showed no significant correlation with CAD. Our AI-driven approach overcomes this by extracting objective, quantifiable metrics such as CIELAB color values and fissure width measurements directly from images. This shift to computational analysis represents a significant advance in non-invasive diagnostics.

We conducted a severity-stratified evaluation of our AI‐driven tongue image model across the three main coronary arteries, using confusion matrices and sensitivity metrics. LCX predictions were most sensitive for mild (84.2%) and moderate (75.0%) stenosis, underscoring its value as an early screening tool. RCA sensitivity peaked at 81.0% for severe/occlusive lesions, highlighting its potential for urgent triage. LAD performance was highest for moderate stenosis (68.0%), aiding in risk stratification. This granular analysis—beyond overall accuracy—demonstrates the model’s differential strengths and supports tongue image analysis as a noninvasive method for both detecting CAD and assessing its severity.

A key finding of our study is the variation in the model’s predictive performance across the three major coronary artery branches. While the model showed strong accuracy for LCX stenosis, its performance for LAD and RCA was comparatively lower. We hypothesize that this discrepancy may be attributed to the anatomical and pathological variations that manifest on the tongue. The visual biomarkers associated with LCX stenosis, such as changes in the central tongue body and prominent fissures, may be more pronounced or consistent across the patient cohort, making them easier for the AI to learn. In contrast, the features associated with LAD and RCA may be more subtle or complex, requiring a larger and more diverse dataset to be fully captured. This observation underscores the need for further research to better understand the intricate relationships between the tongue and the arteries.

Model accuracy varied by artery, with LCX and LAD stenosis being predicted most accurately using fissures (F) and tongue color (RGB), highlighting these as key predictors. In contrast, RCA stenosis required a broader feature set—fissures, coating, red spots, and wetness—suggesting its association with a wider range of tongue abnormalities.

Our study uniquely mapped tongue regions to predict artery-specific stenosis. Features in the tongue’s right third most strongly predicted LCX and RCA stenosis, while the middle third was the primary predictor for LAD stenosis. This regional correlation provides a more localized clinical interpretation of diagnostic findings.

This study demonstrates a significant statistical association between specific tongue regions and coronary artery stenosis, resonating with mappings long asserted by Traditional Chinese and Iranian Medicine. Focusing on distinct thirds of the tongue enhances the accuracy of CAD diagnostic models compared to whole-tongue analysis. For future research, exploring the underlying physiological mechanisms through imaging and histological studies could establish causality. Integrating hyperspectral tongue imaging and validating the findings in larger, more diverse cohorts will enhance the clinical utility of these region-specific diagnostic markers.

Identifying the specific affected vessel and the extent of the occlusion is crucial for timely intervention. Notably, this is the first application of AI-enhanced tongue examination to predict patient outcomes, highlighting its potential to guide urgent clinical decisions. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine CAD diagnosis through a traditional diagnostic framework, for which no prior consensus scales or criteria exist. Accordingly, we report our AI model’s performance independently and emphasize the necessity for future work to develop standardized expert scoring guidelines and multi-center datasets for robust comparative validation.

A recent study has explored the use of tongue images as predictive indicators for CAD in individuals with hypertension. Their results indicated that tongue features could serve as valuable biological markers for CAD diagnosis, achieving a predictive accuracy of 76.0% among patients with hypertension-related risk factors. This aligns with the growing body of evidence supporting the potential of tongue analysis as a non-invasive diagnostic tool in cardiovascular risk assessment28. Nevertheless, our study not only predicted the extent and severity of stenosis in each of the three main coronary arteries but also forecasted patient outcomes, achieving higher accuracy compared to the referenced studies.

In our application of artificial intelligence, we incorporated advanced image processing techniques alongside deep learning methodologies. Image processing was employed to detect and quantify specific tongue features, including coating area, thickness, and color. Simultaneously, deep learning algorithms analyzed various characteristics, including color variations, fissures, moisture levels, teeth marks, and red spots, providing a comprehensive assessment of tongue morphology. Our findings indicate that tongue color and the presence of tongue fissures were the most significant predictors of coronary artery stenosis.

Several studies have confirmed the significance of tongue color in predicting the severity of coronary artery stenosis. These studies have demonstrated that tongue color can serve as a reliable indicator for assessing the degree and severity of vascular obstruction17. Additionally, the critical role of tongue color has been highlighted in diagnosing and evaluating the progression of cardiovascular disease29. These findings are consistent with the results of our own study and underscore the importance of observable tongue features in predicting and assessing vascular health.

Fissured tongue, predominantly observed in adults and rarely in children under 10, is linked to underlying inflammation and leukocyte infiltration beneath the epithelium. It serves as a potential indicator of inflammatory status30,31. In traditional Chinese medicine, this condition is associated with excessive heart activity and blood exhaustion32. The fissured tongue is closely connected to inflammatory processes and vascular abnormalities, which are significant factors in the development of coronary artery disease.

Our study’s strengths include the ability to predict the severity of stenosis in each of the three major coronary arteries and to forecast patient outcomes based on artificial intelligence analysis of tongue images. Additionally, our findings indicate that symptoms present on the right one-third of the tongue have a stronger correlation with coronary artery stenosis. To the best of our knowledge, this has not been previously reported.

One limitation of our study is that the mean age of participants was 57.9 years, and younger individuals were omitted. This may restrict the generalizability of the findings to a broader age group. Furthermore, the images used in this study were captured in a controlled environment without natural light, utilizing high-performance equipment. Future research should aim to analyze images taken with standard cameras in diverse settings and lighting conditions to establish acceptable imaging standards. Despite leveraging a large auxiliary dataset to alleviate data constraints, our primary clinical cohort (n = 906) remains limited. Future studies should validate model performance on larger, diverse, multi-center datasets to ensure generalizability. Additionally, although self-supervised pretraining addresses data scarcity, its dependence on general-population images may introduce biases that warrant further investigation.

While our model shows robust performance, particularly for LCX stenosis, we recognize the inherent limitations of a single-center study. Therefore, we emphasize that external validation on a large, independent, and geographically diverse patient cohort is the critical next step to confirm our findings and to pave the way for the clinical adoption of this technology.

While our study represents a novel application of AI to tongue imaging, its significance is best understood when contextualized within the broader landscape of non-traditional cardiovascular diagnostics. Recent advancements have demonstrated the remarkable potential of AI in analyzing various non-invasive data sources for assessing CAD risk. For example, AI algorithms have shown high accuracy in interpreting electrocardiograms (ECGs) to predict cardiac conditions. In contrast, others have successfully leveraged chest X-rays and retinal images to estimate cardiovascular risk. These approaches, by exploiting subtle patterns invisible to the human eye, are revolutionizing the field of early detection33,34.

Despite the promising nature of these methods, our tongue-based approach offers several unique advantages. Unlike AI-ECG and other digital health tools, our method relies on visual biomarkers that are not susceptible to electrical noise or patient movements. It is also more accessible and cost-effective than chest X-rays or retinal imaging, making it a highly scalable solution for mass screening, particularly in primary care and remote areas. The tongue’s holistic representation of systemic health provides a complementary set of biomarkers that are distinct from other modalities, offering a novel and promising avenue for early detection and risk stratification of CAD.

While this study investigates the potential benefits of tongue diagnosis for CAD, future research is necessary to validate and refine our diagnostic model in a larger, more diverse population. We anticipate that further research in this area will lead to the development of software capable of analyzing tongue images captured by digital cameras in various settings. This software aims to provide objective, image-based biomarkers for the early detection and monitoring of diseases affecting internal organs.
