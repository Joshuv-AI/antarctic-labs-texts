Introduction

We study the zeroth-order query complexity of sampling from an arbitrary
log-concave distribution
\pi
, assuming access to an evaluation
oracle for
-\log\pi
(up to an additive constant). This is a very
general setting for log-concave sampling—for example, it includes
uniform sampling from convex bodies, which serves as a running example
throughout this paper. Results in this general setting provide a theoretical
baseline, highlighting the benefits of additional assumptions often
made in other sampling work, such as log-smoothness (i.e.,
-\nabla\log\pi

is Lipschitz) or access to first-order oracles (i.e.,
-\nabla\log\pi
).
Indeed, much of our work is motivated by gaps between this general
case and more favorable settings where first-order oracles are available
for positive and log-smooth distributions. In practical terms, zeroth-order
samplers are natural default algorithms, since evaluation oracles
typically require only function value computations, making them the
easiest to implement.

We now make the problem setup more explicit, focusing for now on uniform
sampling. Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body
(i.e., a convex and compact set with non-empty interior) containing
the
d
-dimensional unit ball
B_{1}
. Given access to the membership
oracle for
\mathcal{K}
(which answers whether a queried point belongs
to
\mathcal{K}
)
[
GLS93
]
, we ask: how can we sample
uniformly from
\mathcal{K}
, and how many oracle queries are required?
More formally, given a target accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and a probability
divergence (or distance)
\mathsf{D}
—such as the total variation
(
\mathsf{TV}
) distance or
\chi^{2}
-divergence—what is the query complexity
of generating a sample whose law is
\varepsilon
-close to the uniform
distribution
\pi
over
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathsf{D}
?

In this work, we propose a simple uniform sampler that essentially
subsumes prior methods developed over the past three decades. Its
simplicity allows us to establish the first
balanced
complexity
guarantee in a general setting, meaning that the Rényi orders of
the initial warmness and the output metric are the same. This result
parallels guarantees found in recent sampling research in the log-smooth
setting. Next, we provide a simple annealing scheme that, for the
first time, generates a warm start in
q
-Rényi divergence. To
this end, we prove hypercontractivity under simultaneous heat flow,
given a logarithmic Sobolev inequality. Finally, we extend these results
to log-concave distributions under evaluation oracles. Alongside,
we present a streamlined analysis leveraging powerful tools from diverse
mathematical fields.

1.1
Backgrounds and results

In this section,
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
denotes the uniform distribution
over a convex body
\mathcal{K}
with diameter
D
, and
\pi
refers
to log-concave distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For clarity,
we recall the
q
-Rényi divergence, defined as
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\frac{q}{q-1}\log\,\lVert\nicefrac{{\mathrm{d}\mu}}{{\mathrm{d}\nu}}\rVert_{L^{q}(\nu)}

for
q>1
, and its monotonicity in
q
, and
2\,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}^{2}\leq\mathsf{KL}=\lim_{q\downarrow 1}\mathcal{R}_{q}\leq\mathcal{R}_{2}=\log(1+\chi^{2})\leq\chi^{2}\leq\mathcal{R}_{\infty}=\log\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\nu}\nicefrac{{\mathrm{d}\mu}}{{\mathrm{d}\nu}}
.
See §

for more details. When a sampler
is initialized from an initial distribution

toward a target

, we define the warmness parameter as
M_{q}=\lVert\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}/\mathrm{d}\nu\rVert_{L^{q}(\nu)}
.

1.1.1
Uniform sampling from a warm start

Classical algorithms: Ball walk and Hit-and-run.

The
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
and
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
are well-studied random walks (i.e., Markov
chain Monte Carlo methods) for uniform sampling. These algorithms
are analyzed through the conductance framework, where a lower bound
on conductance yields a mixing-time guarantee. Establishing such a
lower bound involves two ingredients: one-step coupling (i.e., the
closeness of transition kernels at two nearby points) and a Cheeger
isoperimetric inequality for the target distribution. The Cheeger
constant of a probability measure
\pi
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as

C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\pi):=\inf_{S:\pi(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\pi(\partial S)}{\pi(S)}\,,

where
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is any open set with smooth boundary,
S_{\eta}=\{x:d(x,S)\leq\eta\}
,
and
\pi(\partial S):=\liminf_{\eta\downarrow 0}\frac{\pi(S_{\eta})-\pi(S)}{\eta}
.

The
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
was introduced by Lovász
[
Lov90
]
; in
each step, it samples a random point uniformly from a ball of fixed
radius around the current point, accepting it if the point lies within

\mathcal{K}
. Later, Lovász and Simonovits established that the

\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
satisfies
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathsf{TV}
with complexity
\operatorname{poly}(d,D,M_{\infty}/\varepsilon)

[
LS93
, Remark on p. 398]
, meaning the initial warmness
should be given in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-divergence and the final output
is
\varepsilon
-close to
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
in
\mathsf{TV}
-distance.
They also analyzed an indirect approach that uses the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
(with
a Metropolis filter) to sample from a log-concave distribution and
then post-processes the sample to bring it closer to
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
.
The resulting algorithm, also of type
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathsf{TV}
, was
shown to have query complexity
\operatorname{poly}(d,D,\log\nicefrac{{M_{\infty}}}{{\varepsilon}})
.

This approach was further improved by Kannan, Lovász, and Simonovits

[
KLS97
]
, who introduced the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}:\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathsf{TV}
.

Similar to
[
LS93
]
, it begins by sampling from a biased
log-concave variant
\pi^{\prime}
(of
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
), which is invariant under
the following per-step transition: the next point
x_{i+1}
is sampled
uniformly from
\mathcal{K}\cap B_{\delta}(x_{i})
via rejection sampling,
using the uniform distribution over
B_{\delta}(x_{i})
as the proposal.
Then, a post-processing step is used to reduce the bias. They showed
that the mixing time of this chain is
O(d^{2}C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{-2}(\pi^{\prime})\log\nicefrac{{M_{\infty}}}{{\varepsilon}})

and that each rejection sampling step requires
O(M_{\infty})
queries,
leading to an overall complexity of
\widetilde{O}(M_{\infty}d^{2}D^{2}\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})

(due to
C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\pi)\gtrsim D^{-1}

[
LS93
]
). Hereafter,
by following convention, the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
refers to the
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
with the
post-processing step. Later, progress on the Kannan-Lovász-Simonovits
(KLS) conjecture
[
KLS95
]
improved this bound to
C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{2}(\pi)\gtrsim(\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log d)^{-1}

[
Kla23
]
. As a result, the complexity of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
is
now
\widetilde{O}(M_{\infty}d^{2}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})
.

The
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
algorithm
[
Smi84
]
was first analyzed by Lovász

[
Lov99
]
via
s
-conductance
[
LS90
]
, with
subsequent refinements by Lovász and Vempala
[
LV06b
]
. Each
step selects a random chord passing the current point and moves to
a random point on the chord. Here, uniform sampling from the chord
is implemented via rejection sampling with complexity
O(\log D)
,
where the proposal distribution is uniform over a slightly longer
chord (found by binary search). They established a novel Cheeger isoperimetric
inequality in terms of the cross-cut ratio (rather than Euclidean
distance) and showed
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}:\mathcal{R}_{2}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}
with complexity

\widetilde{O}(d^{2}D^{2}\log\nicefrac{{M_{2}}}{{\varepsilon}})
, making it the first
random walk with a balanced guarantee. Thus, from an
O(1)
-warm
start in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\geq\mathcal{R}_{2})
, the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
achieves faster
mixing (and better query complexity) than
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
.

However, despite these advances, a complete and clean understanding
of this fundamental problem is still lacking. First, the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

takes an
indirect
approach to uniform sampling: as noted earlier,
it samples from a biased distribution to obtain a mixing guarantee
with
\log\nicefrac{{M_{\infty}}}{{\varepsilon}}
-dependence, followed by a
post-processing step to reduce the bias. Second, conductance-based
analyses require an algorithmic modification making the chains
lazy

(i.e., the chain remains at its current point with some probability).
To achieve greater simplicity and elegance, it would be desirable
to develop a direct sampling algorithm that avoids these additional
processing steps. Finally, the highly unbalanced guarantee of the

\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathsf{TV}
) appears unnatural. As with

\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, we are led to ask whether we can obtain a balanced guarantee
using the improved Cheeger constant
C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{2}(\pi)\gtrsim(\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log d)^{-1}
.
This question becomes even more natural when considering recent developments
in sampling under log-smoothness.

Sampling under smoothness.

Modern research in sampling has made substantial progress by analyzing
discretizations of continuous-time processes. A central example is
the
Langevin diffusion

(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, described
by the stochastic differential equation

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\qquad X_{0}\sim\nu_{0}\,,

where
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a potential function satisfying mild regularity
conditions, and
B_{t}
denotes Brownian motion. The law
\nu_{t}

of
X_{t}
converges to the stationary distribution
\nu\propto e^{-V}

as
t\to\infty
. A spectral analysis of the infinitesimal generator
for this process yields a connection between the rate of convergence
and the
Poincaré constant of
\nu
(see
[
BGL14
, §4]
).
A probability measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to satisfy a Poincaré
inequality

with constant
C
if for any locally Lipschitz
function
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
,

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}f:=\int\Bigl(f-\int f\,\mathrm{d}\pi\Bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi\leq C\int|\nabla f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi\,,

(
\mathsf{PI}
)

and the smallest such
C
is called the Poincaré constant
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)
.
Furthermore, the seminal work of Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto
[
JKO98
]

interpreted Langevin diffusion as the
2
-Wasserstein gradient flow
of
\mathsf{KL}(\cdot\mathbin{\|}\nu)
, revealing a connection to the
logarithmic
Sobolev inequality
, which is strictly stronger than the Poincaré
inequality. A probability measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to satisfy
a logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant
C
if for any locally
Lipschitz function
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}(f^{2}):=\int f^{2}\log f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi-\int f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi\cdot\log\int f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi\leq 2C\int|\nabla f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi\,,

(
\mathsf{LSI}
)

and the smallest such
C
is referred to as the log-Sobolev constant

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)
. Under (

PI

) and (

LSI

) respectively,
the Langevin process satisfies the exponential convergences:

\chi^{2}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu)\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{2t}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu)}\bigr)\,\chi^{2}(\nu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{KL}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu)\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{2t}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)}\bigr)\,\mathsf{KL}(\nu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,,

(1.1)

which were later extended to
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergence in
[
CLL19
,
VW23
]
.

With bounded smoothness (i.e.,
\beta:=\lVert\nabla^{2}V\rVert<\infty
), a
short-time discretization of the Langevin process yields an algorithm
that preserves the balanced guarantees of the continuous process.
Specifically, it has been shown to converge in
\mathsf{KL}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{1}\to\mathcal{R}_{1}
)
with complexity
\operatorname{poly}(d/\varepsilon,\beta,C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu),\log\mathsf{KL}(\nu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\nu))

[
VW23
]
. This result was further extended to
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergence

[
CEL+24
]
achieving a query complexity of
\operatorname{poly}(qd/\varepsilon,\beta,C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu),\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\nu))

or
\operatorname{poly}(qd/\varepsilon,\beta,C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu),\log\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\nu))
.
We note that the dependence on
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}
can be made
polylogarithmic by incorporating a Metropolis filter (e.g.,
\mathsf{MALA}

[
CLA+21
,
WSC22
,
CG23a
]
) or by considering
\mathsf{MHMC}

[
CV22
,
CDWY20
,
CG23b
]
). For a comprehensive review,
we refer readers to the monograph
[
Che25
]
.

The complexity of the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
can be partially described as
\operatorname{poly}(qd/\varepsilon,\beta,C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu),\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\nu))
.
In fact, for log-concave probability measures
\pi
, it holds that

\frac{1}{4}\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{-2}(\pi)}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)}\leq 9\,,

where the first inequality is due to Cheeger
[
Che70
]
,
and the second follows from the Buser–Ledoux inequality
[
Bus82
,
Led04
]
.
Thus,
C_{\mathsf{PI}}
could be replaced with
C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{-2}
, although it remains
unclear how to interpret the smoothness parameter
\beta
in the
context of convex-body sampling.

Rise of the proximal sampler.

Following this line of work, the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
(
\mathsf{PS}
)
[
LST21
]

has brought substantial progress to sampling research. For a target
distribution
\pi^{X}\propto e^{-V}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathsf{PS}
considers
the augmented distribution
\pi(x,y)\propto\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma_{h}(y-x)

over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\gamma_{h}
denotes the centered Gaussian
with covariance
hI_{d}
. Each iteration consists of two steps: for
step size
h>0
, (i)
y_{k+1}\sim\pi^{Y|X=x_{k}}=\mathcal{N}(x_{k},hI_{d})
,
and (ii)
x_{k+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y=y_{k+1}}
. When
\pi^{X}
is
\beta
-(log-)smooth,
rejection sampling for the backward step
x_{i+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y=y_{i+1}}

can be performed using
O(1)
evaluation queries and one proximal
query

V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\arg\min\{V(\cdot)+\frac{1}{2h}\,\lVert\cdot-x\rVert^{2}\}

y_{i+1}

Even more surprising results were obtained by

,
which showed that one iteration of
\mathsf{PS}
can be interpreted as the

(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
from

to

, defined by
P_{t}f:=f*\gamma_{t}
, and the time-reversal
also admits a (non-homogeneous) semigroup
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
from

to
L^{2}(\nu*\gamma_{t})
, defined as
Q_{t}f:=P_{t}(f\nu)/P_{t}\nu

KP23
]
, this allowed

]

O(qd\beta C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu)\,\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\nu)\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})

-closeness in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
(i.e.,
\mathsf{PS}:\mathcal{R}_{q}\to\mathcal{R}_{q}

Proximal sampler for uniform sampling.

Kook, Vempala, and Zhang

adapted this idea to convex-body

\pi^{X|Y=y}\propto\gamma_{h}(\cdot-y)|_{\mathcal{K}}

the backward step can be completed using

Proximal sampler with restart for balanced guarantees (§
2
).

Our proposed sampler,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
—essentially
\mathsf{\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}}
with failure
replaced by restart—resolves this issue: starting with
x_{0}\sim\pi_{0}^{X}
,
step size
h
, and threshold
\tau
,

•

[Forward]
y_{k+1}\sim\pi^{Y|X=x_{k}}=\gamma_{h}(\cdot-x_{k})
.

•

[Backward]
x_{k+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y=y_{k+1}}\propto\gamma_{h}(\cdot-y_{k+1})\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot)
.
To this end, draw
x_{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(y_{k+1},hI_{d})
repeatedly until

x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{K}
. If this rejection loop exceeds
\tau
trials, discard

y_{k+1}
and return to the forward step, using the same
X=x_{k}
.

One
iteration
refers to the successful completion of both steps.
Note that each check

incurs a single query to the
membership oracle.

This algorithm allows
h
and
\tau
to depend solely on the dimension
and initial warmness, leading to the same Rényi orders of initial
warmness and final guarantee while leveraging the state-of-the-art
bound on Poincaré constants of log-concave probability measures
(i.e.,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}:\mathcal{R}_{q}\to\mathcal{R}_{q}

Warm-start generation in the well-conditioned setting.

The necessity of a warmness is not limited to this general setting;
its importance also arises in the well-conditioned setting (i.e.,

-\log\pi
is
\alpha

\beta

fact,
\mathsf{MALA}
from an

\widetilde{O}(\kappa d^{1/2}\operatorname{polylog}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}

in the well-conditioned setting. In contrast, from a feasible start,

\widetilde{O}(\kappa d\operatorname{polylog}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})

denote the condition number of

Recently,

]
showed that combining the Underdamped

\mathsf{PS}
can generate an
O(1)
-warm start
in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-divergence using
\widetilde{O}(\kappa d^{1/2})
first-order
queries, thus incurring no overhead for
\mathsf{MALA}
as a result.

Simulated annealing.

In our general setup, a classical strategy for warm-start generation
is an
annealing
scheme, which involves a sequence of probability
distributions
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\leq m}
that interpolate between a
tractable initial distribution
\mu_{0}
and the target distribution

\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
. The key idea is to ensure that each pair of consecutive
distributions
\mu_{i}
and
\mu_{i+1}
are
O(1)
-close in some
appropriate metric (e.g.,
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-divergence), thereby enabling
efficient transitions by a sampler along the sequence.

In the context of convex-body sampling, several annealing schemes
have been proposed: (i) uniform distributions over truncated convex
bodies intersected with balls of increasing radii
[
DFK91
,
LS90
,
LS93
,
KLS97
]
,
(ii) exponential distributions of the form
e^{-cx}
with decreasing

c
over a convex set, derived from the so-called “pencil construction”
[
LV06c
]
,
and (iii) Gaussian distributions with increasing variance, truncated
to the body
[
CV15
,
CV18
,
KZ25
,
KV25a
]
.

Among these, all approaches except
[
KZ25
]
provide a warm
start
\mu
satisfying
\lVert\mu-\pi_{\mathcal{K}}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\varepsilon
,
while
[
KZ25
]
guarantees
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness with
complexity
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}R^{2})
for
R^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}}[|\cdot|^{2}]
.
On the other hand,
\mathsf{TV}
-warmness can be achieved with better complexity

\widetilde{O}(d^{2}R^{3/2}\Lambda^{1/4}\operatorname{polylog}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})

[
KV25a
]
.
Unfortunately, no known result currently interpolates between warmness
results in
\mathsf{TV}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
(i.e., either very weak or
strong warmness, without known intermediate warmness).

Toward genuine warmness.

First of all, why do we even care about
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-warmness? Given
a theoretical gap in known complexities (for generating
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-warmness)
between the two settings discussed above, bridging this gap is theoretically
intriguing in its own right. In our general setup, it is not even
clear whether generating
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-warmness without significant
overhead is feasible, let alone whether one can tighten the complexities
of warm-start generation and sampling from a warm start.

Moreover,
\mathsf{TV}
-warmness serves only as a “fake” warm-start for
downstream sampling in some sense, since subsequent sampling guarantees
using this warmness are collapsed down to
\mathsf{TV}
-distance due to the
necessity of applying triangle inequalities—an issue referred to
as “
\mathsf{TV}
-collapse” by
[
KZ25
]
. In practice, this limitation
leads to cumulative sampling errors, resulting in somewhat cumbersome
post-processing steps, such as replacing the original target accuracy

\varepsilon
by a reduced accuracy
\varepsilon/m
, where
m
is the number
of times the triangle inequality is applied. Lastly, when combined
with
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
that achieves balanced guarantees, a
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-warmness
result would finally yield clean guarantees for uniform sampling in
terms of any
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergences.

\mathsf{TV}
-collapse.

A main difficulty in obtaining
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-warmness through annealing
is that we do not yet know how to relay
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-warmness without
compromise, leading nearly all previous work to encounter this
\mathsf{TV}
-collapse
problem. To illustrate this, consider annealing distributions
\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
,
where a sampler transitions from
\mu_{0}
toward
\mu_{1}
. MCMC
samplers are inherently approximate, producing a measure
\bar{\mu}_{1}

close to
\mu_{1}
, quantified via
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\bar{\mu}_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{1})\leq\varepsilon

for some
q>1
. In the next phase, the sampler moves toward
\mu_{2}

starting from the approximate measure
\bar{\mu}_{1}
instead of
the true
\mu_{1}
. Ideally, the complexity for this phase would
directly depend on
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\bar{\mu}_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{2})
. Unfortunately,
the lack of an exact triangle inequality for
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergence
prevents us from ensuring
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\bar{\mu}_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{2})=O(1)
,
even if
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{2})=O(1)
.

To circumvent this issue, prior studies have resorted to using the
triangle inequality (or a coupling argument) for
\mathsf{TV}
-distance.
Specifically, although
\bar{\mu}_{1}
differs from
\mu_{1}
, annealing
algorithms proceeds as if the initial distribution were
\mu_{1}
,
not
\bar{\mu}_{1}
, when running a sampler toward
\mu_{2}
. Denoting
the transition kernel of this sampler by
P
and the output distribution
of this sampler by
\bar{\mu}_{2}=\bar{\mu}_{1}P
, we can deduce
that

\lVert\bar{\mu}_{2}-\mu_{2}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\lVert\bar{\mu}_{2}-\mu_{1}P\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}+\lVert\mu_{1}P-\mu_{2}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\lVert\bar{\mu}_{1}-\mu_{1}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}+\lVert\mu_{1}P-\mu_{2}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\,,

where the second inequality follows from the data-processing inequality
(
2.1
). Since
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergence implies
\mathsf{TV}
-distance,
and each term on the RHS is bounded by
\varepsilon
, this leads to the
accumulation of error over
m
phases:
\lVert\bar{\mu}_{m}-\mu_{m}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq m\varepsilon
.
This necessitates accuracy adjustments like replacing
\varepsilon
with

\varepsilon/m
.

Boosting a Rényi-order.

The above discussion highlights the fundamental bottleneck arising
from the absence of a triangle inequality for Rényi divergences.
One promising direction to overcome this is to leverage a weak triangle
inequality (
1.3
), namely,
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\bar{\mu}_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{2})\lesssim\mathcal{R}_{2q-1}(\bar{\mu}_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{1})+\mathcal{R}_{2q}(\mu_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{2})
.
As
[
KV25a
]
has already designed annealing schemes that
can ensure
\mathcal{R}_{2q}(\mu_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{2})=O(1)
, one might consider
starting with a
\mathcal{R}_{2q-1}
-guarantee when sampling from
\mu_{1}
.
However, this effectively amounts to requiring a
\mathcal{R}_{\Theta(2^{m}q)}
-guarantee
for
\mu_{1}
-sampling when there are
m
many phases. Since
\mathsf{PS}

has linear dependence on
q
, and prior annealing approaches require

m\gtrsim d^{1/2}
, such a “backtracking” analysis would ultimately
incur an
\Omega(2^{\sqrt{d}}q)
-multiplicative overhead in the final
complexity.

Given the failure of this naïve approach, a more valid question
is whether one can ensure
\mathcal{R}_{2q-1}(\bar{\mu}_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{1})=O(1)

given
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\bar{\mu}_{1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{1})<\varepsilon
. Since
2q-1>q

for
q>1
, reversing the monotonic property of
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-divergence
at some overhead will be a main challenge. This reflection naturally
introduces an algorithmic question:
can we boost Rényi-orders
algorithmically
(i.e., can we transform
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)=O(1)

into a stronger bound
\mathcal{R}_{2q-1}(\mu^{\prime}\mathbin{\|}\pi)=O(1)
)? Solving
this question would enhance our theoretical toolkit, providing the
final piece toward comprehensive convex-body sampling and potentially
influencing applications in areas such as differential privacy.

Hypercontractivity of semigroups.

Perhaps the most relevant mathematical notion to tackle this question
is Gross’ hypercontractivity of semigroups
[
Gro75
]

(see Proposition
3.1
): for a semigroup

(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
reversible with respect to an invariant measure

\pi
, it is known that (

LSI

) for
\pi
is equivalent
to the property that
\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{q(t)}(\pi)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}

for any function
f\in L^{p}(\pi)
, where
q(t)
is an increasing
function with
q(0)=p
. For instance, the continuous-time Langevin
diffusion satisfies this property, but it is not immediately clear
whether such hypercontractivity is preserved upon discretization.
Recent advancements, such as
[
CEL+24
]
, showed that discretized
Langevin processes retain hypercontractivity under LSI when the target

\pi
is positive and log-smooth.

Nonetheless, extending hypercontractivity algorithmically beyond this
favorable setting remains nontrivial. In particular, circumstances
involving constraints—such as sampling from a convex body—or
more general smoothness assumptions (e.g.,
B_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
)
have resisted straightforward solutions. One might consider using
reflected Langevin diffusions, but discretizations like the projected
Langevin algorithm
[
BEL18
]
require access to a projection
oracle for
\mathcal{K}
, and existing analytical guarantees similarly
collapse to
\mathsf{TV}
-distance. Thus, achieving hypercontractivity in
broader contexts remains as an open and intriguing direction.

Hypercontractivity under simultaneous heat flow (§
3

To address the boosting question, we explore an alternative strategy
involving a different Markov semigroup that can be algorithmically
realized via
\mathsf{PS}
. Specifically, we aim to establish hypercontractivity
for the
L^{2}(\nu)
-adjoint
Q_{t}
of the heat semigroups
P_{t}

under (

LSI

) for the target measure
\nu

state the following hypercontractivity result for

Theorem 1.2

(Hypercontractivity under heat flow)
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure satisfying (

LSI

) with

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)<\infty

\nu_{t}=\nu*\gamma_{t}
for

\frac{q(t)-1}{p-1}=1+\frac{t}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)}\quad\text{for any }p\in(1,\infty)\,.

Then, the

\lVert Q_{t}f\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}\quad\text{for all }p\in(1,\infty),t\geq 0,\text{ and }f\in L^{p}(\nu)\,.

\mathcal{R}_{q(t)}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\leq\mathcal{R}_{p}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu).

This result has algorithmic implications for
\mathsf{PS}

Let

We streamline the design and analysis of a known Gaussian-annealing

\mathsf{PS}

1.2
Technical overview

Proximal sampler with restart.

Compared to the standard
\mathsf{PS}
, our approach has a key modification—restarting
the current iteration if the number of attempts in rejection sampling
for the backward step exceeds a fixed threshold. The analysis of
\mathsf{PS}

is split to two parts: the number of iterations required to achieve
desired sampling accuracy, and the per-iteration query complexity
arising from rejection sampling. Both components have been well-studied
in prior works. The convergence rate of
\mathsf{PS}
under (

PI

)
was established in
[
CCSW22
]
, along with a rigorous
justification for the general de Bruijn identity
[
KO25
]
.
Moreover,
[
KVZ24
]
proposed carefully tuned parameter choices
for
\mathsf{\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}}
that keep the per-step complexity under control.

Given these foundations, the main technical questions concern the
effect of the restarting mechanism. We must ensure that restarting
neither alters the transition kernel of
\mathsf{PS}
nor introduces excessive
overhead in query complexity. The first point is intuitively straightforward:
each iteration of
\mathsf{PS}
can be viewed as sampling from the true transition
kernel followed by an acceptance step. The distribution of the first
successful sample thus corresponds to the intended transition kernel
(Lemma
2.7
). The second point follows from the same
perspective. Suppose that each iteration succeeds with probability

p
, and the threshold for rejection retries is set to
\tau
. Then,
the expected number of retries until a successful iteration is bounded
by
\tau/p
. This bound remains moderate so long as the success probability
is bounded away from zero, allowing us to employ simpler choices of
step size
h
and threshold
\tau
than those used in
\mathsf{\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}}
.

Hypercontractivity under heat flow.

Overall, we follow a proof sketch in
[
BGL14
, Theorem 5.2.3]

along with technical tweaks. To begin, for any compactly supported,
smooth, positive function
f
, we define the probability measure

\mathrm{d}\mu\propto f\,\mathrm{d}\pi
. Denoting the convolved measures
\mu_{t}:=\mu*\gamma_{t}

and
\pi_{t}:=\pi*\gamma_{t}
, we introduce the functionals
\mathcal{F}(t,q):=\int(\nicefrac{{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}}{{\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}}})^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}

and
\mathcal{G}(t):=\frac{1}{q(t)}\,\log\mathcal{F}(t,q(t))
. Our goal is to
construct a suitable function
q(t)
such that
\partial_{t}\mathcal{G}\leq 0
.
Our proof proceeds by differentiating
\mathcal{G}
in
t
, which involves
computing
\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}
and
\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}
. The time derivative

\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}
follows from the general de Bruijn identity
[
KO25
]
,
while the exponent derivative
\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}
gives rise to
\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{t}}
,
where the log-Sobolev constant
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi_{t})
comes into picture.

Combining these ingredients, we derive a differential inequality for

\mathcal{G}(t)
, and
\partial_{t}\mathcal{G}\leq 0
holds provided that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi_{t})\leq(q(t)-1)/(q(t)-1)^{\prime}
.
Leveraging
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi_{t})\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)+t

[
Cha04
]
,
we solve
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)+t\leq(q(t)-1)/(q(t)-1)^{\prime}
, which holds when
q(t)-1=(1+\nicefrac{{t}}{{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)}})\,(p-1)
.
Altogether, these arguments establish the hypercontractivity of the
heat adjoint
Q_{t}
. Lastly, we extend this result beyond the space
of compactly supported, positive, smooth functions by employing standard
approximation techniques in analysis, such as the dominated convergence
theorem and Fatou’s lemma, thereby generalizing the statement to
L^{p}(\pi)
-space.

Streamlined analysis of Gaussian annealing.

In our analysis of Gaussian annealing (§
4.2
),
we present a more principled and streamlined approach to bounding
the closeness between successive annealing distributions. Whereas
prior works relied on ad-hoc arguments to establish such closeness,
we show that this property follows more cleanly from LSI, combined
with a mollification argument that allows for a limiting process.

To formalize this idea, we recall a Rényi version of LSI established
in
[
VW23
]
, which states that
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\nicefrac{{qC_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)}}{{2}}\,\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)

for any
q>1
and smooth
\mu/\pi
, where
\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)=q\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi})^{q}|\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}|^{2}]/\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi})^{q}]
.
When bounding
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}\mathbin{\|}\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}|_{\mathcal{K}})

for a convex body
\mathcal{K}
and some
\alpha>0
, a naïve calculation—ignoring
the truncation to
\mathcal{K}
—suggests that one can take
\alpha\asymp\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{qR}}

to make it
O(1)
. This heuristic choice recovers the scaling behavior
established in earlier results.

To rigorously account for the effects of truncation to
\mathcal{K}
, we employ
a mollification argument using a standard mollifier
\eta
supported
on the unit ball (see (
4.2
)). For
\varepsilon>0
,
we define
\eta_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-d}\,\eta(x/\varepsilon)
,
and convolve both truncated Gaussians with
\eta_{\varepsilon}
.
Since convolution with
\eta_{\varepsilon}
yields a smooth approximation

f_{\varepsilon}:=f*\eta_{\varepsilon}
that converges to
f
almost
everywhere as
\varepsilon\to 0
for any locally integrable function

f
, this mollification step allows us to apply the Rényi-LSI
to the regularized densities and pass to the limit, thereby recovering
the desired closeness bound.

An additional benefit of this approach is that it enables a clean
justification for early-stopping in the annealing schedule. Unlike
known annealing algorithms, we can terminate annealing when the variance

\sigma^{2}
reaches approximately
qR\Lambda^{1/2}
, at which point

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{\mathcal{K}})=O(1)
. This follows
directly from a Rényi version of PI (see (

⁢
R
-
PI

)),
combined with the mollification argument described above. Overall,
this approach simplifies the analysis and unifies the reasoning behind
closeness and early-stopping in Gaussian annealing.

1.3
Related work

We survey the literature on applications of zeroth-order log-concave
sampling and log-concave sampling in other settings (e.g., given access
to a different set of oracles).

More on uniform sampling.

Early work on this problem involved several algorithmic hacks aimed
at “making it work”. In
[
DFK91
]
,
\mathcal{K}
is first mollified
and then sampled using the
\mathsf{Grid}\text{ }\mathsf{walk}
, a random
walk on a suitably fine lattice over the body. This algorithm is then
analyzed through the conductance of the resulting Markov chain, the
well-known framework developed by Jerrum and Sinclair
[
JS88
]
.
To use this, they applied an isoperimetric inequality of Milman and
Schechtman
[
MS86
]
, showing that the
\mathsf{Grid}\text{ }\mathsf{walk}:\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathsf{TV}

requires
\operatorname{poly}(d,D,\nicefrac{{M_{\infty}}}{{\varepsilon}})
many queries to
achieve
\varepsilon
-
\mathsf{TV}
distance.

Lovász and Simonovits
[
LS90
]
generalized the conductance
framework to a continuous domain, introducing
s
-conductance
that lower-bounds the conductance of the chain for subsets of measure
at least
s>0
. To prove lower bounds on
s
-conductance, they developed
the well-known localization technique, proving a Cheeger isoperimetric
inequality for
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
, with
C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\pi_{\mathcal{K}})\gtrsim D^{-1}
(which
was originally established by Payne and Weinberger
[
PW60
]
).
These new tools improved the complexity of the
\mathsf{Grid}\text{ }\mathsf{walk}
,
although it still has dependence of
\operatorname{poly}(d,D,\nicefrac{{M_{\infty}}}{{\varepsilon}})
.

As mentioned earlier, Lovász and Simonovits
[
LS93
]

used the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
to develop an indirect approach with improved complexity

\operatorname{poly}(d,D,\log\nicefrac{{M_{\infty}}}{{\varepsilon}})
. It was achieved by
refining their earlier conductance framework and localization machinery;
they proved that any log-concave distribution
\pi
supported on
a convex body of diameter
D
also satisfies
C_{\mathsf{Ch}}(\pi)\gtrsim D^{-1}
.

Recently,
\mathsf{Coordinate\ Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
(
\mathsf{CHR}
), a special variant of
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, has
been shown to mix in polynomial time with respect to the dimension
and (
\ell_{2}
or
\ell_{\infty}
) diameter
[
NS22
,
LV23
,
FV23
]
,
and with a logarithmic dependence on the initial warmness
[
NRS25
]
.
This thus results in
\mathsf{CHR}:\mathcal{R}_{2}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}
.

KLS conjecture.

The Kannan-Lovász-Simonovits (KLS) conjecture
[
KLS95
]

posits that the Cheeger constant of any log-concave probability measure
is bounded below by
\Omega(\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{-1/2})
. Starting from

C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{2}\gtrsim\operatorname{tr}^{-1}(\operatorname{cov})
, subsequent work
[
Eld13
,
LV24
,
Che21
,
KL22
,
Kla23
]
—notably
through Eldan’s stochastic localization—has improved it to
C_{\mathsf{Ch}}^{2}\gtrsim(\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log d)^{-1}

[
Kla23
]
. However, the improved Cheeger constant in this
setting does not translate to a better Cheeger constant with respect
to the cross-cut ratio.

Rounding.

A notable application is the rounding of convex bodies, or more generally,
log-concave functions. It aims to make the geometry of the input object
more regular—typically by applying an affine transformation that
brings it into a nearly-isotropic position. This step is often a necessary
preprocessing phase for achieving a fully polynomial-time algorithm,
since worst-case shapes (e.g., highly skewed bodies) can cause sampling
algorithms to suffer from poor mixing.

Earlier approaches to rounding relied on deterministic methods based
on the ellipsoid algorithm
[
DFK91
,
LS90
]
, which guarantees

B_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{K}\subset B_{d^{3/2}}(0)
after a suitable affine
transformation. To improve the sandwiching ratio, randomized rounding
methods were pioneered in
[
LS93
]
and further developed
in
[
KLS97
,
LV06c
,
JLLV21
,
JLLV24
]
.
These methods use sampling to estimate the covariance matrix and then
apply affine transformations to round the body. The best known complexity
for isotropic rounding of a convex body is
\widetilde{O}(d^{3.5}\operatorname{polylog}R)

[
JLLV24
]
, with the analysis streamlined in
[
KZ24
]
.
These rounding techniques have also been extended to log-concave distributions
[
LV06a
,
KV25b
]
,
while preserving the query complexity.

Volume computation and integration.

One of the most well-known applications of uniform sampling is volume
computation, a problem with a long and rich history in theoretical
computer science and convex geometry; given
\varepsilon>0
, the goal is
to estimate the volume
V
of a convex body within a relative
\varepsilon
-error,

(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}\leq V\leq(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}
.

Computing the volume of a high-dimensional convex body is known to
be intractable via deterministic algorithms
[
Ele86
,
BF87
]
.
The breakthrough result by Dyer, Frieze, and Kannan
[
DFK91
]

showed that this task admits a randomized polynomial-time algorithm
using random walks. The standard technique introduced therein, referred
to as the
multi-phase Monte Carlo
method, constructs a sequence
of convex bodies such that each successive pair is close in some sense.
Rewriting the volume of
\mathcal{K}
as a telescoping product of volume ratios
between successive bodies, one can efficiently estimate each ratio
using samples obtained via uniform sampling over convex bodies.

This framework initiated a long line of research focused on improving
the mixing time, oracle complexity, and practical implementability
of such algorithms
[
Lov90
,
AK91
,
LS90
,
LS93
,
KLS95
,
KLS97
,
LV06b
,
CV15
,
CV18
,
JLLV21
]
.
The current fastest algorithm for isotropic convex bodies is due to

[
CV18
]
with complexity
\widetilde{O}(d^{3}/\varepsilon^{2})
.
A natural generalization of volume computation is the integration
of a log-concave function. This general problem was studied in
[
LV06a
,
KV25b
]
,
with the same complexity
\widetilde{O}(d^{3}/\varepsilon^{2})
. See
[
KV25b
, §5.2]

for a streamlined analysis.

Sampling with additional assumptions.

For constrained sampling problems—such as the convex-body sampling—there
exist approaches beyond general-purpose MCMC algorithms like the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
,

\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, and
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
. These alternatives often leverage additional
geometric structure of the constraint set
\mathcal{K}
, assume additional
regularities of target distributions (e.g., smoothness), or assume
access to stronger oracles, such as projection or first-order oracles.

(1) Sampling with local geometry.
This approach assumes the
existence of a tractable
self-concordant barrier

\phi
for
the constraint
\mathcal{K}
. At each step, a sampler draws from
an anisotropic Gaussian distribution whose covariance is pertinent
to the local geometry encoded by
\nabla^{2}\phi
. This leads to
an affine-invariant sampler that naturally adapts to the shape of

\mathcal{K}
, without requiring a separate rounding procedure. The most notable
example is the
\mathsf{Dikin\ walk}:\mathcal{R}_{2}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}

[
KN12
]
, which,
when used with a log-barrier for a polytope with
m
linear constraints,
achieves a mixing time of
O(md\log\nicefrac{{M_{2}}}{{\varepsilon}})
,
and its analysis was streamlined in
[
SV16
]
.

This barrier-based sampler has been generalized and improved through
more efficient barrier functions
[
Nar16
,
CDWY18
,
GN23
]
.
A unifying framework incorporating these developments—as well as
procedures for warm-start generation—was presented in
[
KV24
]
.
Moreover, complexity analysis beyond worst case was explored in
[
JC24
]
,
and alternative non-Euclidean schemes using log-Laplace transformations
was proposed in
[
GLL+23
]
.

(2) Sampling with first-order oracle.
When a first-order
oracle is available, faster non-Euclidean samplers become feasible.
These include Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
[
GC11
,
LV18
,
GKV24
]
,
which uses Hamiltonian dynamics on the Hessian manifold with metric

\nabla^{2}\phi
, and Riemannian Langevin Algorithm
[
GC11
,
LE23
]
,
which extends Langevin dynamics to a Riemannian manifold. Other approaches
include the Mirror Langevin Algorithm
[
ZPFP20
,
Jia21
,
AC21
,
LTVW22
,
SWW24
]
,
which use mirror maps to simulate Langevin-type updates in dual space,
and the Preconditioned Langevin Algorithm
[
SWW25
]
.

(3) Sampling with projection oracle.
In this setting, access
to a projection oracle—computing the projection of a point onto

\mathcal{K}
—enables other sampling methods: unconstrained steps (e.g.,
Euler–Maruyama discretization of the Langevin diffusion) are followed
by projections back into the feasible set
[
BEL18
,
Leh23
]
.
Alternatively, soft-constraint approaches have also been proposed,
in which the target distribution is modified with penalty terms that
discourage constraint violations while remaining close to the original
distribution. Such penalized schemes have been studied in
[
BDMP17
,
GHZ24
,
Yu24
]
.

(4) Zeroth-order sampling for well-conditioned distributions.

Those three general-purpose samplers—the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
,
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, and
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
—have
also been studied by
[
ALPW24
]
,
[
ALZ24
]
,
and
[
LST21
,
CCSW22
]
in the unconstrained well-conditioned
setting with access to an evaluation oracle. These samplers achieve
the mixing times of
\widetilde{O}(\kappa d\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})
under
various metrics (e.g.,
\mathcal{W}_{2}
,
\mathsf{KL}
,
\chi^{2}
).

Real-world applications and impacts.

Sampling research is not only of theoretical interest but also has
far-reaching implications for real-world applications. A prominent
application arises in systems biology, where uniform sampling from
a polytope can be used to analyze the efficiency of human metabolic
networks
[
T+13
,
HCT+17
]
. Advances in sampling
techniques have enabled more scalable biological inferences. For instance,
the development of non-Euclidean samplers has led to improved mixing
times and computational efficiency
[
YK17
,
CF21
,
CK21
,
KLSV22
,
KLSV23
,
SC24
]
.
Moreover, practical implementations of volume computation for convex
bodies have been developed in
[
CV16
]
, bridging the
gap between theoretical developments and real-world usability. Collectively,
these advancements demonstrate how sampling research continues to
yield practical tools with deep and lasting impact across science
and engineering disciplines.

Notation.

For
t>0
, we reserve
\gamma_{t}
for the centered Gaussian distribution
with covariance matrix
tI_{d}
. The indicator function of a set

S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
\mathds{1}_{S}(x):=[x\in S]
, and
\mu|_{S}

refers to a distribution
\mu
truncated to
S
(i.e.,
\mu|_{S}\propto\mu\cdot\mathds{1}_{S}
).
For two probability measures
\mu,\pi
, we use
\mu\pi
to denote
the new distribution with density proportional to
\mu\pi
.

Both
a\lesssim b
and
a=O(b)
mean
a\leq cb
for a universal
constant
c>0
.
a=\Omega(b)
means
a\gtrsim b
, and
a\asymp b

means
a=O(b)
and
a=\Omega(b)
. Lastly,
a=\widetilde{O}(b)
means

a=O(b\operatorname{polylog}b)
. For a positive semi-definite matrix
\Sigma
,

\lVert\Sigma\rVert
denote the operator norm of
\Sigma
.

Log-concavity.

We call a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)

log-concave
if
-\log f

is convex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a probability measure
\pi
(or distribution)
log-concave if it has a log-concave density function with respect
to the Lebesgue measure. We abuse notation by using the same symbol
for a distribution and density. We assume that any log-concave distributions
considered in this work are non-degenerate; otherwise, we could simply
work on an affine subspace on which degenerate distributions are supported.
For
t\geq 0
,
\pi
is called
t
-strongly log-concave
if

-\log\pi
is
t
-strongly convex (i.e.,
-\log\pi-\frac{t}{2}\,|\cdot|^{2}

is convex). Clearly, log-concavity is preserved under multiplication,
and a classical result by Prékopa and Leindler ensures that the
convolution also preserves log-concavity. A distribution is called

isotropic
if its barycenter is at the origin and has the identity
covariance matrix. Note that as the log-concave distributions decay
exponentially fast at infinity, they have finite moments of all orders.

Probability divergences and distances.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
denote probability measures over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function with
\varphi(1)=0
. The

\varphi
-divergence
of
\mu
toward
\nu
with
\mu\ll\nu

is defined as

D_{\varphi}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\int\varphi\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,.

The
total variation
(
\mathsf{TV}
) distance, the
\mathsf{KL}
-divergence,
and
\chi^{q}
-divergence
for
q\in(1,\infty)
can be recovered
through
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\,|x-1|
,
x\log x
, and
x^{q}-1
,
respectively. The
q
-Rényi divergence
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\frac{1}{q-1}\,\log\bigl(1+\chi^{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\bigr)=\frac{1}{q-1}\,\log\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\nu)}^{q}\,.

(1.2)

The
Rényi-infinity divergence
is defined as
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\log\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\nu}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
.
A weak triangle inequality holds for Rényi divergence: for any

q>1,\lambda\in(0,1)
, and probability measures
\mu,\nu,\pi
, it holds
that

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\frac{q-\lambda}{q-1}\,\mathcal{R}_{\frac{q}{\lambda}}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)+\mathcal{R}_{\frac{q-\lambda}{1-\lambda}}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\,.

(1.3)

For
\lambda=q/(2q-1)
, we have
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\frac{2q}{2q-1}\,\mathcal{R}_{2q-1}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)+\mathcal{R}_{2q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\pi)
.
We refer readers to
[
vEH14
,
Mir17
]
for more properties
of the Rényi divergence.

2
Uniform sampling from convex bodies

We begin by describing our approach for the general proximal sampler
(
\mathsf{PS}
) and then focus specifically on
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
in §
2.2
.

\mathsf{PS}
with step size
h
alternates between two steps: starting
with
k=0
and
x_{0}\sim\pi_{0}^{X}
, (i) sample
y_{k+1}\sim\pi^{Y|X=x_{k}}

and (ii) sample
x_{k+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y=y_{k+1}}
. Precisely, let
P_{h}

and
Q_{h}
be the transition kernel of the forward and backward
step respectively, defined as

P_{h}(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\pi^{Y|X=x}=\mathcal{N}(x,hI_{d})\,,\qquad Q_{h}(y,\mathrm{d}x)=\pi^{X|Y=y}\propto\pi^{X}(\cdot)\,\gamma_{h}(\cdot-y)\,.

Assume that there exists a suitable proposal distribution
\mu_{y}

such that
(\mu_{y}\,|\,\text{accepted})=Q_{h}(y,\cdot)
. In case
of uniform sampling, we use
\mu_{y}=\mathcal{N}(y,hI_{d})
and accept
the proposal if it lies inside of
\mathcal{K}
.

Given this setup, we implement the backward step using rejection sampling
with proposal
\mu_{y_{k+1}}
. If this rejection loop exceeds
\tau

trials, we discard
y_{k+1}
and return to the forward step using
the same
X=x_{k}
. We group each forward–backward pair (with rejection
sampling up to
\tau
retries) into a
stage
. For instance,
if all
\tau
retries fail in stage
1
, then we begin stage
2

by drawing a new
\bar{y}_{k+1}\sim\pi^{Y|X=x_{k}}
and sampling
from
x_{k+1}\sim\pi^{X|Y=\bar{y}_{k+1}}
. Stages are repeated until
one iteration completes successfully.

We now analyze the query complexity of
\mathsf{PS}
for obtaining a sample
whose law is
\varepsilon
-close to the target
\pi^{X}
in a chosen metric
(Theorem
1.1
). To this end, we decouple the analysis
into two components: (1) a mixing analysis and (2) a query complexity
of the backward step. The first part determines how many iterations

N
are required for the sample
X_{N}
to be
\varepsilon
-close to

\pi^{X}
. The second one determines how many queries
\mathsf{PS}
uses
in each iteration. Therefore, the total query complexity of the algorithm
is the product of these two components.

2.1
Mixing analysis

The data-processing inequality (DPI) is a handy tool in information
theory; for any probability measures
\mu,\nu
, Markov kernel
P
,

\varphi
-divergence
D_{\varphi}
, and
q\in(1,\infty]
, it holds
that

D_{\varphi}(\mu P\mathbin{\|}\nu P)\leq D_{\varphi}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,,\qquad\quad\text{and}\qquad\quad\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu P\mathbin{\|}\nu P)\leq\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,,

(2.1)

where
\mu P:=\int P(x,\cdot)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
denotes the distribution
obtained by applying one step of
P
starting from
\mu
. This implies
that for
D\in\{D_{\varphi},\mathcal{R}_{q}\}
and any
k\geq 0
,

D(\pi_{k+1}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})=D(\pi_{k+1}^{Y}Q_{h}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{Y}Q_{h})\leq D(\pi_{k+1}^{Y}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{Y})=D(\pi_{k}^{X}P_{h}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X}P_{h})\leq D(\pi_{k}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\,.

However, this is not sufficient to establish a mixing rate. It turns
out that adding an independent Gaussian random variable to
X_{k}

(i.e., convolving
\operatorname{law}X_{k}
with
\gamma_{h}
) makes
\operatorname{law}X_{k}

contract directly toward
\pi^{X}*\gamma_{h}=\pi^{Y}
, with the contraction
rate dependent on
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})
or
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi^{X})
.

This phenomenon, known as the
strong data-processing inequality

(SDPI) for the Gaussian channel
[
AG76
]
, has been extensively
studied in information theory
[
PW16
,
CPW18
]
.
Given a
\varphi
-divergence
D
, the contraction coefficient is defined
as

\eta_{D}(\mu,t):=\sup_{\nu:\,D(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\frac{D(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})}{D(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)}\,,

where
\nu_{t}:=\nu*\gamma_{t}
denotes the probability measure obtained
by convolving
\nu
with
\gamma_{t}
. Clearly,
0\leq\eta_{D}(\mu,t)\leq 1

by the DPI.

Bounding
\eta_{D}
relies on the well-known
de Bruijn identity
,

\partial_{t}\mathsf{KL}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathsf{FI}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})
,
where the relative Fisher information is defined as
\mathsf{FI}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[|\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}|^{2}]
;
see rigorous proofs by Barron
[
Bar84
]
and Klartag
and Ordentlich
[
KO25
]
(for its generalizations). Other
derivations can be found in
[
Guo09
,
VW23
,
CCSW22
]
,
assuming that probability measures involved in proofs are sufficiently
smooth to justify differentiation under the integral sign and vanishment
of boundary terms in integration by parts.

Combining this identity with, for example, (

LSI

) for
\mu

(i.e.,
\mathsf{KL}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})\leq\nicefrac{{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu_{t})}}{{2}}\,\mathsf{FI}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})
)
and
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu_{t})\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)+t

[
Cha04
]
, one
can obtain a differential inequality in terms of
\mathsf{KL}
, and solving
this leads to

\eta_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mu,t)\leq\bigl(1+\frac{t}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)}\bigr)^{-1}\,.

Under similar regularity assumptions, the SDPI for Rényi divergence
under (

PI

), (

LSI

), and Latała–Oleszkiewicz
inequality was studied in
[
CCSW22
]
and further extended
to constrained probability measures in
[
KVZ24
]
. These results
were rigorously unified and investigated in
[
KO25
]
under
a
\varphi
-Sobolev inequality
[
Cha04
]
(i.e., a generalization
of (

PI

) and (

LSI

)), with regularity assumptions
explicitly identified. We refer readers to
[
KO25
, Regularity conditions]

for a detailed account.

Specifically,
[
CCSW22
]
showed that under the regularity
assumptions, for any
q\geq 2
and
\nu\ll\mu
, the following bounds
hold:

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})\leq\begin{cases}\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)-\frac{\log(1+t/C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu))}{q}&\text{if }\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)\geq 1\,,\\
\frac{\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)}{(1+t/C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu))^{1/q}}&\text{if }\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)<1\,,\end{cases}

(2.2)

and
\chi^{2}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})\leq(1+t/C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu))^{-1}\,\chi^{2}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)
.
Applying this result to the forward step, along with the DPI for the
backward step, implies that
\mathsf{PS}
achieves
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi_{N}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\varepsilon

with

N\lesssim qh^{-1}C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi^{X})\log\frac{M_{q}}{\varepsilon}\,,\quad\text{where }M_{q}:=\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}^{X}}{\mathrm{d}\pi^{X}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\pi^{X})}\,.

We provide a version of this result in terms of
\chi^{q}
-divergence
(which generalizes their
\chi^{2}
-result), as working with
\chi^{q}
-divergence
leads to cleaner expressions and a bound on the contraction coefficient

\eta_{\chi^{q}}
under (

PI

). Our proof follows the argument
sketched above, combining the general de Bruijn identity in terms
of
\chi^{q}
-divergence under (

PI

). Below, note that
any log-concave probability measure satisfies (

PI

) with

C_{\mathsf{PI}}<\infty
, as established in
[
KLS95
,
Bob99
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any
q\geq 2
, it holds that

\eta_{\chi^{q}}(\mu,t)\leq\bigl(1+\frac{t}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu)}\bigr)^{-2/q}\quad\text{for }t>0\,.

To prove this result, we recall the general version of de Bruijn’s
identity from
[

Proposition 2.2

(General de Bruijn’s identity)
.

Let
\mu

and
\nu
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\chi^{q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)<\infty

and
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[|\cdot|^{4}]<\infty

and

\partial_{t}\chi^{q}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})=-\frac{q\,(q-1)}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\Bigl[\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}\bigr)^{q}\,\bigl|\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}\bigr|^{2}\Bigr]\underset{\text{if }q\geq 2}{=}-\frac{q-1}{2}\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q}(\mu_{t})}^{q}\,\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\nu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{t})\,,

where
\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)=q\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu})^{q}|\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}|^{2}]/\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu})^{q}]

denotes the
q

We also recall a Rényi version of (

) from
[

that for
q\geq 2
and probability measure
\nu
and
\mu
with

\nu\ll\mu
and smooth
\nu/\mu
,

1-\exp\bigl(-\mathcal{R}_{q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)\bigr)\leq\frac{qC_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu)}{4}\,\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\mu)\,.

(
\mathsf{R\text{-}PI}

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

We combine general de Bruijn’s
identity and the Rényi PI. For the former, we note that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[|\cdot|^{4}]<\infty

since (

PI

) ensures exponential integrability
[
BU83
,
GM83
]

(i.e.,
\int\exp(s\,|\cdot|)\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty
if
s^{2}<4/C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu)

BGL14

\nu_{t}
and

\chi^{q}+1=\lVert\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}\rVert_{L^{q}(\mu_{t})}^{q}:=L^{q}\geq 1

Correctness under algorithmic modifications.

It may not be immediately clear whether the transition kernel of
\mathsf{PS}

remains unchanged under two algorithmic modifications: (1) setting
a retry threshold
\tau
, and (2) restarting an iteration if all

\tau
retries fail.

For completeness, we prove that the transition kernel is indeed preserved
under these changes. We first show that imposing the threshold does
not alter the kernel of the backward step.

Lemma 2.6
.

Upon success of the rejection sampling
in the backward step, the accepted sample is distributed according
to
\pi^{X|Y=y}
.

Proof.

Consider the proposal
\mu_{y}
with success probability
p_{y}>0

such that
X_{i}\sim\mu_{y}
for each retry
i\in[\tau]
satisfies

\operatorname{law}(X_{i}\,|\,\text{accepted})=\pi^{X|Y=y}
. Let
X
be the random
sample given by the backward step. For any event
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we
have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\in A\,|\,\text{accepted})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(X\in A,\,\text{success})}{\mathbb{P}(\text{success})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\text{fail in the first }(i-1)\text{ trials},\,X_{i}\ \text{accepted},\,X_{i}\in A)}{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\text{success in the }i\text{-th trial})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}(1-p_{y})^{i-1}\,\mathbb{P}(X_{i}\in A,\,X_{i}\ \text{accepted})}{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}(1-p_{y})^{i-1}p_{y}}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A,\,X_{1}\ \text{accepted})}{p_{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A\,|\,\text{accepted})=\pi^{X|Y}(A)\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Restarting the forward step when
\tau
retries fail also does not
alter the transition kernel of
\mathsf{PS}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

The transition kernel of
\mathsf{PS}
is
P_{h}Q_{h}
.

Proof.

Let
X_{i}=x
be a current point, and let
f_{x}
be the failure
probability of a stage. For any event
A
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{i+1}\in A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{i+1}\in A\,|\,\text{success in the }j\text{-th stage})\,\mathbb{P}(\text{success in the }j\text{-th stage})

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int\pi^{X|Y=y}(A)\,\gamma_{h}(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}y\times f_{x}^{j-1}(1-f_{x})

\displaystyle=\int\pi^{X|Y=y}(A)\,\gamma_{h}(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int\pi^{X|Y=y}(A)\,(\delta_{x}P_{h})(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=(\delta_{x}P_{h}Q_{h})(A)\,,

where
(i)
follows from Lemma
2.6
.
Thus,
X_{i+1}
is distributed as
\pi_{i}^{X}P_{h}Q_{h}
when
X_{i}\sim\pi_{i}^{X}
.
∎

2.2
Query complexity per iteration

We now focus on the case of
\pi^{X}\propto\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}
, assuming
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{K}

by translation. Hereafter, we assume that the starting distribution

\pi_{0}^{X}
is
M_{q}
-warm with respect to
\pi^{X}
. By the
DPI, the same holds between
\pi_{1}^{Y}=\pi_{0}^{X}*\gamma_{h}

and
\pi^{Y}=\pi^{X}*\gamma_{h}
(i.e.,
\lVert\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}^{Y}/\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}\rVert_{L^{q}(\pi^{Y})}<\infty
),
and this property is preserved throughout subsequent iterations (i.e.,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi_{k}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})
).
For brevity, we will use the
L^{q}
-warmness,
\chi^{q}
-warmness,
or
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-warmness interchangeably, as justified by (
1.2
).

Recall from
[
KVZ24
]
a concentration phenomenon for
\pi^{Y}=\pi^{X}*\gamma_{h}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

For a convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
containing a unit ball,

\pi^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\mathcal{K}_{\delta})\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2h}+\delta d\bigr)\,,

where
\mathcal{K}_{\delta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:d(x,\mathcal{K})\leq\delta\}
.

Taking
h=(2cd^{2})^{-1}
and
\delta=1/d
for some
c>0
ensures

\pi^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\mathcal{K}_{\delta})\leq e^{-c+1}
. Note that the success
probability of the rejection sampling at
Y_{k}=y
is
\ell(y):=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{N}(y,hI_{d})}(\cdot\in\mathcal{K})
,
so the failure probability of a stage in the
k
-th iteration is

\int\bigl(1-\ell(y)\bigr)^{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{k}^{Y}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{\tau}]=:f_{k}\,.

Let
n_{k}
denote the number of trials required to complete the

k
-th iteration. Then, the total expected number of queries over

N
iterations is
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}n_{k}
. By the law of total
expectation,

\mathbb{E}n_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[n_{k}\,|\,\text{success in }i\text{-th stage}]\,\mathbb{P}(\text{success in }i\text{-th stage})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i\tau\times f_{k}^{i-1}(1-f_{k})=\frac{\tau}{1-f_{k}}\,.

Therefore, bounding the per-iteration complexity reduces to bounding

f_{k}
for each
k\in[N]
.

Let
p
be the conjugate of
q
(i.e.,
p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
for
q\geq 2
).
Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{\tau}]\leq M_{q}\,\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{\tau p}]\bigr)^{1/p}\,,

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-\ell)^{\tau p}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}\cdot+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell\geq\tau^{-1}\log(3M_{q})]}\cdot+\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<\tau^{-1}\log(3M_{q})]}\cdot=:\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{C}\,.

Using
\pi^{Y}=\ell/\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}
when bounding
\mathsf{C}
below,

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\leq\pi^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\mathcal{K}_{\delta})\leq e^{-c+1}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[\ell\geq\tau^{-1}\log(3M_{q})]}\exp(-\ell\tau p)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}\leq\bigl(\frac{1}{3M_{q}}\bigr)^{p},

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\cap[\ell<\tau^{-1}\log(3M_{q})]}\frac{\ell(y)}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{\log(3M_{q})}{\tau}\,\frac{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}{\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}}\leq\tau^{-1}e\log(3M_{q})\,,

where we used
\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\subset\operatorname{vol}((1+\delta)\,\mathcal{K})=(1+\delta)^{d}\,\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}\leq e\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}

(due to
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{K}
). Taking
c\asymp\log(3M_{q})^{p}
and

\tau\asymp M_{q}^{p}\log(3M_{q})
, we can ensure that
\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{C}\leq(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2M_{q}}})^{p}

and thus
f_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Putting these together, we bound the total query complexity during

N
iterations as

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}n_{k}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\tau}{1-f_{k}}\leq 2\tau N\,.

Using this, we derive the complexity of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{unif}}
with balanced guarantees.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For
M:=\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})
,
set
h^{-1}\asymp d^{2}M
and
\tau\asymp Me^{M}
. To achieve
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi_{N}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\varepsilon
,
by Lemma
2.5
, it suffices to iterate at most

N\lesssim q\,(1\vee h^{-1}\Lambda\log d)\log\frac{M}{\varepsilon}\lesssim q\Lambda d^{2}M\log d\log\frac{M}{\varepsilon}\quad\text{times}\,.

Therefore, the expected number of total queries during
N
iterations
is

2\tau N\lesssim q\Lambda d^{2}M^{2}e^{M}\log d\log\frac{M}{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{O}\bigl(q\Lambda d^{2}e^{\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

3
Hypercontractivity under the simultaneous heat flow

In this section, we establish hypercontractivity under simultaneous
heat flow, which will serve as a stepping stone for relaying a Rényi
warmness in annealing in the next section.

3.1
Adjoint of the heat semigroups and the proximal sampler

We first recap the Markov semigroup theory for a gentle introduction
to heat semigroup and its adjoint. Interested readers may want to
refer to
[
VH14
,
BGL14
,
Che25
]
for further
details on the semigroup theory. Meanwhile, we take detour to
\mathsf{PS}
,
since it sets up a context in which the introduction of heat semigroups
and its adjoint is natural.

Markov semigroup theory.

Given a (time-homogeneous) Markov process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on a
domain
E
, its associated
Markov semigroup

(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}

is defined as a family of functional operators such that

(P_{t}f)(x):=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t})|X_{0}=x]\quad\text{for every bounded measurable function }f:E\to\mathbb{R}\,.

One could readily check that
P_{t}
is a linear operator with
P_{0}=\mathrm{id}
,

P_{t+s}=P_{t}\circ P_{s}
for any
t,s\geq 0
(semigroup property),

P_{t}\mathds{1}=\mathds{1}
(mass conservation), and
P_{t}f\geq 0
for any

f\geq 0
(positivity preserving). Below, we omit
E
in the integral
sign.

A measure
\mu
is said to be
invariant
(or stationary) for

P_{t}
if
\int P_{t}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu
for every
t\geq 0
.
In addition, it is said to be
reversible
for
P_{t}
if
\int P_{t}f\,g\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int f\,P_{t}g\,\mathrm{d}\mu

for any
f,g\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
t\geq 0
. For any
p\geq 1
, it
follows from Jensen’s inequality that
P_{t}
is a contraction operator
on the bounded functions in
L^{p}(\mu)
(i.e.,
\lVert P_{t}f\rVert_{L^{p}(\mu)}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\mu)}
).
This furnishes the extension of
P_{t}
to
L^{p}(\mu)
by a density
argument.

The proximal sampler.

As examined in
[
CCSW22
]
(with further details in
[
Che25
]
),
the forward step (i.e.,
y_{k+1}\sim\pi^{Y|X=x_{k}}=\mathcal{N}(x_{k},hI_{d})
)
can be viewed as simulating Brownian motion for time
h
started
at
x_{k}
; when
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\mathrm{d}B_{t}
with
X_{0}=x_{k}
, we have

X_{h}\sim\operatorname{law}y_{k+1}
. The associated Markov semigroup of Brownian
motion is often called the
heat semigroup
, which admits the
formula of
P_{t}f=f*\gamma_{t}=:f_{t}
. Based on the classical heat
equation
\partial_{t}P_{t}f=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta P_{t}f
, rich theory for the
heat semigroup facilitates one to understand the forward step in a
better way (e.g., contraction under simultaneous heat flow).

This prompts a natural question whether one could also introduce a
semigroup structure to the backward step. It turns out that the backward
step can be interpreted as an SDE
(Z_{t}^{\leftarrow})_{t\geq 0}
,
given by

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{\leftarrow}=\nabla\log(\pi^{X}*\gamma_{h-t})\,(Z_{t}^{\leftarrow})\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\bar{B}_{t}\qquad\text{for another Brownian motion }\bar{B}_{t}\,,

so that if
Z_{0}^{\leftarrow}\sim\pi^{Y}
, then
Z_{h}^{\leftarrow}\sim\pi^{X}
.
Though it is non-trivial to justify, one could guess that
Z_{h}^{\leftarrow}\sim\pi^{X|Y=y}

if
Z_{0}^{\leftarrow}=y
. Hence, one may be able to introduce a
semigroup structure to the backward step via, for any suitable test
function
f
,

\overline{Q}_{h}f(y):=\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{h}^{\leftarrow})|Z_{0}^{\leftarrow}=y]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{X|Y=y}}f\,.

As seen shortly, Klartag and Putterman
[
KP23
]
defined
the same object as the
L^{2}(\pi)
-adjoint of the heat semigroups,
providing solid ground where similar rigorous theory can be developed
for the backward step.

L^{2}
-adjoint of heat semigroups.

Given a probability measure
\pi
,
[
KP23
]
viewed the
heat semigroup
P_{t}
as a contraction operator from
L^{2}(\pi_{t})

to
L^{2}(\pi)
:

\lVert P_{t}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}=\int(P_{t}f)^{2}\,\pi\leq\int P_{t}(f^{2})\,\pi=\int f^{2}\,P_{t}\pi=\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}\,,

where the equality in the middle follows simply from reversibility
of the heat semigroups. This perspective led them to define the
adjoint
operator

Q_{t}:L^{2}(\pi)\to L^{2}(\pi_{t})
as
Q_{0}=\mathrm{id}
and

Q_{t}f=\frac{P_{t}(f\pi)}{P_{t}\pi}=\frac{P_{t}(f\pi)}{\pi_{t}}\quad\text{for }t>0\,.

This heat adjoint could be viewed as the backward step of
\mathsf{PS}

with
\pi^{X}=\pi
and
t=h
, since

\overline{Q}_{t}f(y)=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{X|Y=y}=\frac{\int f(x)\,\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma_{t}(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int\pi^{X}(x)\,\gamma_{t}(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{(f\pi)*\gamma_{t}}{\pi*\gamma_{t}}=\frac{P_{t}(f\pi)}{\pi_{t}}=(Q_{t}f)(y)\,.

It follows from this formula that the adjoint operator satisfies mass
preservation (
Q_{t}\mathds{1}=\mathds{1}
) and positivity preserving (
Q_{t}f\geq 0

if
f\geq 0
). Moreover, it is a contraction operator; for
f\in L^{2}(\pi)\backslash\{0\}
,
by Cauchy-Schwarz

(Q_{t}f)^{2}\,\pi_{t}=\frac{\bigl(P_{t}(f\pi)\bigr)^{2}}{\pi_{t}}\leq\frac{P_{t}(f^{2}\pi)\,P_{t}\pi}{\pi_{t}}=P_{t}(f^{2}\pi)\,.

and integration of both sides yields
\lVert Q_{t}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi_{t})}^{2}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}
.

3.2
Hypercontractivity of an adjoint of the heat semigroups

We now show something stronger than mere contraction of
Q_{t}
from

L^{2}(\pi)
to
L^{2}(\pi_{t})
, called hypercontractivity. To
begin with, we recall hypercontractivity of Markov semigroups under
(

LSI

):

Proposition 3.1

(Gross)
.

For
C>0
and
p\in(1,\infty)
,
let
q(t)=1+(p-1)\,e^{2t/C}
for
t\geq 0
, and
P_{t}
be a reversible
Markov semigroup with stationary measure
\nu
. Then,
\nu
satisfies
(

LSI

) with parameter
C
if and only if

\lVert P_{t}f\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu)}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}\quad\text{for all }p\in(1,\infty),t\geq 0,\text{ and }f\in L^{p}(\nu)\,.

It means that when a stationary measure satisfies (

LSI

),
the semigroup
P_{t}
not only contracts in any
L^{p}
-spaces but
also improves integrability (recall
L^{q}\subset L^{p}
for
p<q

due to monotonicity of
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{p}}
in
p
). We now prove
Theorem
1.2
, hypercontractivity
under the simultaneous heat flow with different
q(t)=1+(p-1)\,(1+\nicefrac{{t}}{{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)}})
;

\lVert Q_{t}f\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}\quad\text{for all }p\in(1,\infty),t\geq 0,\text{ and }f\in L^{p}(\nu)\,.

Note that the reference measure
\nu_{t}

also
evolves over
time
t
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We first
prove the claim for
f\in C_{c,+}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{f+c:f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),f\geq 0,c>0\}
.
Let
\bar{f}
be the normalization of
f
with respect to
\nu

(i.e.,
\bar{f}=f/\int f\,\mathrm{d}\nu
).

Denoting a new probability measure
\mu
by
\mathrm{d}\mu=\bar{f}\,\mathrm{d}\nu
,
we define
\bar{f}_{t}:=Q_{t}\bar{f}=\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}/\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}
and

\mathcal{F}(t,q):=\int\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\bigr)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}=\int\bar{f}_{t}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\,,\qquad\mathcal{G}(t):=\frac{1}{q(t)}\,\log\mathcal{F}\bigl(t,q(t)\bigr)\,.

It then suffices to show that
\partial_{t}\mathcal{G}\leq 0
for a suitable
choice of
q(t)
. Note that

\partial_{t}\mathcal{G}=-\frac{q^{\prime}(t)}{q^{2}(t)}\,\log\mathcal{F}+\frac{\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}+q^{\prime}(t)\,\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}}{q(t)\,\mathcal{F}}\,.

With assumptions of
q(t),q^{\prime}(t)>0
for a moment,
\partial_{t}\mathcal{G}\leq 0

is equivalent to

\frac{1}{q^{\prime}(t)}\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}+\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}-\frac{1}{q(t)}\,\mathcal{F}\log\mathcal{F}\leq 0\,.

We now compute
\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}
and
\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}
. By general de
Bruijn’s identity in Proposition
2.2
(note
that
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[|\cdot|^{4}]<\infty
since (

LSI

) implies
(

PI

)),

\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}=-\frac{q\,(q-1)}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}\bigl[\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\bigr)^{q-2}\,\bigl|\nabla\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\bigr|^{2}\bigr]=-\frac{q\,(q-1)}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[\bar{f}_{t}^{q-2}\,|\nabla\bar{f}_{t}|^{2}]\,.

As for
\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\partial_{q}\int\bar{f}_{t}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\underset{(i)}{=}\int\bar{f}_{t}^{q}\log\bar{f}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}=\frac{1}{q}\,\Bigl(\operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_{t}}(\bar{f}_{t}^{q})+\int\bar{f}_{t}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\,\log\int\bar{f}_{t}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q}\,\Bigl(2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu_{t})\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[|\nabla(\bar{f}_{t}^{q/2})|^{2}]+\int\bar{f}_{t}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\,\log\int\bar{f}_{t}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\,\bigl(\frac{q^{2}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu_{t})}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[\bar{f}_{t}^{q-2}\,|\nabla\bar{f}_{t}|^{2}]+\mathcal{F}\log\mathcal{F}\bigr)\,.

Differentiation under the integral sign in
(i)
can be justified
as follows:
\partial_{q}\bar{f}_{t}^{q}=(Q_{t}\bar{f})^{q}\log Q_{t}\bar{f}

is continuous in
q
, and we can take a small
\delta>0
such that
for any
q^{\prime}\in(q-\delta,q+\delta)
with
q-\delta>1
,

\bar{f}_{t}^{q^{\prime}}\log\bar{f}_{t}=(\bar{f}_{t}\,\mathds{1}[\bar{f}_{t}\geq 1]+\bar{f}_{t}\,\mathds{1}[\bar{f}_{t}<1])^{q^{\prime}}\log\bar{f}_{t}\leq 2^{q+\delta-1}\,(\bar{f}_{t}^{q+\delta}+\bar{f}_{t}^{q-\delta})\log\bar{f}_{t}\,.

Hence, it amounts to showing integrability of the RHS. To this end,
we first show that
\bar{f}_{t}\in L^{r}(\nu_{t})
for any
r>1
.
By Jensen’s inequality,

\lVert\bar{f}_{t}\rVert_{L^{r}(\nu_{t})}^{r}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[(Q_{t}\bar{f})^{r}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[Q_{t}(\bar{f}^{r})]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\bar{f}^{r}]=\lVert\bar{f}\rVert_{L^{r}(\nu)}^{r}\,,

and
\bar{f}\in C_{c,+}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
ensures that
\lVert\bar{f}\rVert_{L^{r}(\nu)}<\infty
.
Thus, integrability of the RHS follows from
L^{s}\subset L^{r}\log L

for any
r<s
.

Combining the bounds on
\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}
and
\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\prime}(t)}\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}+\partial_{q}\mathcal{F}-\frac{1}{q(t)}\,\mathcal{F}\log\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{q\,(q-1)}{2\,q^{\prime}(t)}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[\bar{f}_{t}^{q-2}\,|\nabla\bar{f}_{t}|^{2}]+\frac{1}{q}\,\bigl(\frac{q^{2}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu_{t})}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[\bar{f}_{t}^{q-2}\,|\nabla\bar{f}_{t}|^{2}]+\mathcal{F}\log\mathcal{F}\bigr)-\frac{1}{q(t)}\,\mathcal{F}\log\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\bigl(C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu_{t})-\frac{q-1}{q^{\prime}(t)}\bigr)\times\frac{q}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[\bar{f}_{t}^{q-2}\,|\nabla\bar{f}_{t}|^{2}]\,.

Hence, it suffices to enforce

\frac{(q-1)^{\prime}}{q-1}\leq\frac{1}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu_{t})}\,.

As
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu_{t})\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)+t
, we can guarantee this by solving

\frac{(q-1)^{\prime}}{q-1}\leq\frac{1}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)+t}\,.

Integrating both sides over
[0,t]
, and setting
q(0)=p
,

\log\frac{q(t)-1}{p-1}\leq\log\frac{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)+t}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)}\,,

and thus we can simply take

\frac{q(t)-1}{p-1}=1+\frac{t}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)}\,,

which clearly satisfies
q,q^{\prime}\geq 0
. Thus,
\lVert\bar{f}_{t}\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\lVert\bar{f}\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}

(so
\lVert f_{t}\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}
),
and hypercontractivity holds on
C_{c,+}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Every
f\in C_{c,+}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be written as
f=f_{\delta}=g+\delta

for some non-negative
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\delta>0
.
We then apply the dominated convergence theorem to
\lVert Q_{t}f_{\delta}\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\lVert f_{\delta}\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}

as
\delta\to 0
, extending the main claim from
C_{c,+}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

to
C_{c,\geq 0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\{f\geq 0\}
.

Now, for any non-negative
f\in L^{p}(\nu)
, we can take a sequence

\{f_{n}\}
of functions in
C_{c,\geq 0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

f_{n}\to f
in
L^{p}
. Moreover, we can take a subsequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k}

such that
f_{n_{k}}\to f
almost everywhere. Since
Q_{t}f_{n}\geq 0

due to positivity-preserving, Fatou’s lemma implies that

\lVert Q_{t}f\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}^{q(t)}=\int\liminf_{k\to\infty}(Q_{t}f_{n_{k}})^{q(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int(Q_{t}f_{n_{k}})^{q(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\,,

and thus,

\lVert Q_{t}f\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\,\lVert Q_{t}f_{n_{k}}\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\,\lVert f_{n_{k}}\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}=\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}\,.

Lastly, for any
f\in L^{p}(\nu)
, note that
|f|=f_{+}+f_{-}

is also in
L^{p}(\nu)
. Thus,

\lVert Q_{t}f\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}=\lVert|Q_{t}f|\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\lVert Q_{t}(|f|)\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\lVert|f|\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}=\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}\,,

which finishes the proof.
∎

For
f:=\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\nu\in L^{p}(\nu)
(which is equivalent to
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)<\infty
),
hypercontractivity of
Q_{t}
implies that

\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}=\lVert Q_{t}f\rVert_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\nu)}=\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(\nu)}\,.

Then,
\mathcal{R}_{q(t)}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\leq\mathcal{R}_{p}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)

follows from

\mathcal{R}_{p}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)=\frac{p}{p-1}\log\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(\nu)}\geq\frac{p}{p-1}\,\log\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\geq\frac{q(t)}{q(t)-1}\,\log\,\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}\Bigr\|_{L^{q(t)}(\nu_{t})}\,.

This looks similar to the DPI for Rényi divergence, but we remark
that the Rényi order of the RHS is larger that that of the LHS.

Improved convergence rate of PS under LSI.

Just as in the SDPI under (

PI

), the SDPI under (

LSI

)
was established in

,
KO25
]
: for
q>1
and
two probability measures

with

,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)}{(1+t/C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu))^{1/q}}\,.

(3.1)

Combining this contraction with hypercontractivity, we can derive
a stronger mixing result of
\mathsf{PS}
: when
\pi_{k}
denoting the law
of the
k
-th sample returned by
\mathsf{PS}
with initial
\pi_{0}
and
target
\pi
, for any given

and

, we have
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi_{N}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\varepsilon

after
N\lesssim h^{-1}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\log\frac{q\mathcal{R}_{2\wedge p}(\pi_{0}\mathbin{\|}\pi)}{(p-1)\,\varepsilon}

iterations.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

We first iterate
\mathsf{PS}

N_{0}\asymp(2\wedge p)\,h^{-1}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\log\frac{\mathcal{R}_{(2\wedge p)}(\pi_{0}\mathbin{\|}\pi)}{\varepsilon}

times to bring
\pi_{N_{0}}
within
\varepsilon
-distance to
\pi
in

\mathcal{R}_{2\wedge p}
. Next, we iterate it
N_{1}\asymp h^{-1}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\log_{2}\frac{q-1}{1\wedge(p-1)}

more times, where we invoke DPI and hypercontractivity each iteration
as follows: for
i\leq N_{1}
and
\pi_{i}^{X}=\pi_{i}
,

\mathcal{R}_{q_{i+1}}(\pi_{N_{0}+i+1}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\mathcal{R}_{q_{i+1}}(\pi_{N_{0}+i+1}^{Y}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{Y})\leq\mathcal{R}_{q_{i}}(\pi_{N_{0}+i}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\,,

where
(q_{i+1}-1)/(q_{i}-1)=1+h/C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)
. Then,

\frac{q_{N_{1}}-1}{1\wedge(p-1)}=\frac{q_{N_{1}}-1}{q_{0}-1}=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\frac{q_{i}-1}{q_{i-1}-1}=\bigl(1+\frac{h}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)}\bigr)^{N_{1}}\geq\frac{q-1}{1\wedge(p-1)}\,.

Therefore,
\mathsf{PS}
suffices to iterate

times to attain the desired guarantee.
∎

As a simple corollary, this result leads to a mixing time of

in the well-conditioned setting, which improves the linear dependence
of
\mathsf{PS}
on

.

(Zeroth-order sampling in well-conditioned setting)
.

Let
\pi\propto e^{-V}
with
\alpha
-strongly convex and

-smooth

. Let

.

Proof.

One can find an initial distribution

Order-preserving annealing for simpler warm-start generation

In this section, we streamline prior warm-start generation algorithms
and their proofs. Above all, we interweave an overall analysis with

\mathcal{R}_{q}
-warmness.

Simpler warm-start generation.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a convex body with
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{K}
(by translation),

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|\cdot|^{2}]\leq R^{2}
, and
\Lambda=\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
.
We simplify prior annealing approaches in
[
KV25a
]
, moving
along a sequence
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}

\mu_{i}=\gamma_{\sigma_{i}^{2}}|_{\mathcal{K}}=\pi\gamma_{\sigma_{i}^{2}}
.

This prior algorithm consists of the following several steps: (i)

Preprocessing
: Truncate
\mathcal{K}
to the ball of radius
O(R)

such that
\pi(\bar{\mathcal{K}})\geq 0.99
where
\bar{\mathcal{K}}:=\mathcal{K}\cap B_{O(R)}(0)
,
(ii)
Initialization
: Sample from
\mu_{1}=\gamma_{d^{-1}}|_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}

by rejection sampling with proposal
\gamma_{d^{-1}}
, (iii)
Annealing
:
While
\sigma_{i}^{2}\lesssim R^{2}
, use
\mathsf{PS}
to sample from
\mu_{i+1}

with initial distribution
\mu_{i}
, where
\sigma_{i+1}^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\,(1+\frac{\sigma_{i}}{q^{1/2}R})

for

. In particular, there are two

: when

,
iterate
\mathsf{PS}

\widetilde{O}(d^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2})
times, (iii-2)
faster
mode
: when
\sigma_{i}^{2}\gtrsim qR\Lambda^{1/2}
, iterate
\mathsf{PS}

\widetilde{O}(d^{2}R\Lambda^{1/2})
times. (iv)
Termination
: Run

\mathsf{PS}
to sample from
\pi
with initial distribution
\gamma_{R^{2}}|_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}
.

We show that the preprocessing step is redundant and also that the
faster mode can be replaced by early-stopping: using
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
(§
4.1
)
below,

1.

Sample from
\mu_{1}=\pi\gamma_{d^{-1}}
by rejection sampling with
proposal
\gamma_{d^{-1}}
.

2.

While
\sigma_{i}^{2}\lesssim qR\Lambda^{1/2}\log^{1/2}d
, move from

\bar{\mu}_{i}
to
\bar{\mu}_{i+1}
by
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{3}(\bar{\mu}_{i}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{i})=O(1)
,
where

\sigma_{i+1}^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\,\bigl(1+\frac{\sigma_{i}}{R}\bigr)\,.

We need two ingredients to obtain a final guarantee: (1) the query
complexity of
\mathsf{PS}
for truncated Gaussians and (2) closeness of
two annealing distributions. The former was already studied in previous

as done for uniform sampling in an earlier section. The latter was

-divergence by a localization argument
[

]

and then was extended to

]
. We show

that this is in fact a simple

). Combining these together, we establish

-warmness.

4.1
Proximal sampler for truncated Gaussian

We study
\mathsf{PS}
for
\mu^{X}:=\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
, Gaussian distributions
truncated to a convex body
\mathcal{K}
containing
B_{1}(0)
. Its prototype
was already studied in
[
KZ25
,
KV25a
]
. The streamlined
algorithm, denoted by
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
, can be readily deduced from the
general framework of
\mathsf{PS}
.

Starting with
x_{0}\sim\mu_{0}^{X}
, step size
h>0
, threshold

\tau
, and
s:=\sigma^{2}/(h+\sigma^{2})\in(0,1)
,

•

[Forward]
y_{k+1}\sim\mu^{Y|X=x_{k}}=\gamma_{h}(\cdot-x_{k})
.

•

[Backward]
x_{k+1}\sim\mu^{X|Y=y_{k+1}}\propto\gamma_{sh}(\cdot-sy_{k+1})\,\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(\cdot)
.
To this end, draw
x_{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(sy_{k+1},shI_{d})
repeatedly
until
x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{K}
. If this rejection loop exceeds
\tau
trials,
discard
y_{k+1}
and return to the forward step using the same
X=x_{k}
.

As in the uniform-sampling case, its analysis is split into two parts:
a mixing analysis and per-iteration complexity. The per-iteration
analysis will be proceeded in a similar fashion, but the mixing analysis
can be improved due to the hypercontractivity of
\mathsf{PS}
.

Mixing analysis.

We already established the convergence rate of
\mathsf{PS}
under (

LSI

)
(see Corollary
1.3
). It is classical that
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu^{X})\leq\sigma^{2}

(e.g., use the Bakry–Émery criterion, and the convex boundary
term does not harm in satisfying this criterion; see
[
Kla09
,
BJ24
]
).
Thus, in order to be
\varepsilon
-close to
\mu^{X}
in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
,
for
q_{0}>1
,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
suffices to iterate

N\lesssim h^{-1}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{q\mathcal{R}_{2\wedge q_{0}}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\mu^{X})}{(2\wedge q_{0}-1)\,\varepsilon}\qquad\text{iterations}\,.

Per-step query complexity.

Denoting by
\ell(y)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{N}(sy,shI_{d})}(\cdot\in\mathcal{K})
the success
probability of the rejection sampling, we can readily check that

\mu^{Y}(y)=(\mu^{X}*\gamma_{h})(y)=\frac{s^{d/2}\ell(y)\exp(-\frac{1}{2s\sigma^{2}}\,|sy|^{2})}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,|x|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}\,,

and also recall a concentration phenomenon of
\mu^{Y}
from
[
KZ25
]
:
for
R=s^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
, if
\delta\geq hd
,

\mu^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash R)\leq\exp\bigl(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2h}+\delta d+hd^{2}\bigr)\,.

Taking
h=(cd^{2})^{-1}
and
\delta=d^{-1}
for some
c\geq 1
,
we have
\mu^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash R)\leq e^{-c/2+2}
. Just as we used
the bound of
\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}_{\delta}/\operatorname{vol}\mathcal{K}\leq e^{\delta d}
, we need the
following “expansion” lemma (see
[
KZ25
, Lemma 24]
):

s^{-d/2}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2s\sigma^{2}}\,|z|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z\leq 2e^{hd^{2}+\epsilon d}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,|z|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

This leads to the following bound: by change of variables via
y=s^{-1}v

below,

\frac{\int_{R}s^{d/2}\exp(-\frac{1}{2s\sigma^{2}}\,|sy|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,|x|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}s^{-d/2}\exp(-\frac{1}{2s\sigma^{2}}\,|v|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}v}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,|v|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}v}\leq 2e^{hd^{2}+\delta d}\leq e^{3}\,.

(4.1)

We show that the failure probability of one stage in the
k
-th iteration
is at most
1/2
under suitable choices of
h
and
\tau
. For
a conjugate pair
(p,q)
with
p,q>1
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{k}^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{\tau}]\leq M_{q}\,\{\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{\tau p}]\}^{1/p}

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-\ell)^{\tau p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash R}\cdot+\int_{R\cap[\ell\tau\geq\log(3M_{q})]}\cdot+\int_{R\cap[\ell\tau<\log(3M_{q})]}\cdot=:\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{C}\,.

Under
c\asymp\log(3M_{q})^{p}
and
\tau\asymp M_{q}^{p}\log(3M_{q})
,
this can be bounded by
(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2M_{q}}})^{p}
due to

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\leq\mu^{Y}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\mathcal{K}_{\delta})\leq e^{-c/2+2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R\cap[\ell\tau\geq\log(3M_{q})]}e^{-\ell\tau p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}\leq\bigl(\frac{1}{3M_{q}}\bigr)^{p}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R\cap[\ell\tau<\log(3M_{q})]}\mathrm{d}\mu^{Y}=\int_{R\cap[\ell\tau<\log(3M_{q})]}\frac{s^{d/2}\ell(y)\exp(-\frac{1}{2s\sigma^{2}}\,|sy|^{2})}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,|x|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(3M_{q})}{\tau}\,\frac{\int_{R}s^{d/2}\exp(-\frac{1}{2s\sigma^{2}}\,|sy|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,|x|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x}\underset{\eqref{eq:psgauss-handy-lemma}}{\leq}\frac{e^{3}\log(3M_{q})}{\tau}\,.

Thus,
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{k}^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{\tau}]\leq 1/2
, and the expected
number of trials during the
k
-th iteration is bounded by
2\tau

as in §
2.2
. Combining all these
together, we can prove Theorem
1.4
on the query
complexity of
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

For
M:=\mathcal{R}_{2\wedge q_{0}}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\mu^{X})
,
we set
h^{-1}\asymp d^{2}M
and
\tau\asymp Me^{M}
. As mentioned
in the mixing analysis,
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
returns a sample
X_{N}
such
that
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\operatorname{law}X_{N}\mathbin{\|}\mu^{X})\leq\varepsilon
by iterating

N\lesssim Md^{2}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{qM}{(2\wedge q_{0}-1)\,\varepsilon}\quad\text{times}\,.

Thus, the expected number of total queries during
N
iterations
is

2\tau N\lesssim M^{2}e^{M}d^{2}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{qM}{(2\wedge q_{0}-1)\,\varepsilon}=\widetilde{O}\bigl(d^{2}\sigma^{2}\,e^{\mathcal{R}_{2\wedge q_{0}}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\mu^{X})}\log\frac{q}{(2\wedge q_{0}-1)\,\varepsilon}\bigr)\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Functional inequalities to closeness and early-stopping

We will show that two consecutive Gaussians are
O(1)
-close in

\mathcal{R}_{q}
, finding a connection to (

LSI

). We then show
that annealing can be stopped around
\sigma_{i}^{2}\asymp qR\Lambda^{1/2}\log^{1/2}d
,
much earlier than
\sigma_{i}^{2}\asymp R^{2}
, finding a connection
to (

PI

). Moreover, the second result improves a mathematical
result established in
[
KV25a
]
as an immediate corollary.
We then prove Theorem
1.5
on the query
complexity of our annealing algorithm.

Mollification.

To avoid any regularity issues, we work with mollifications of log-concave
distributions. As detailed in
[
Eva10
, §C.4]
, the

standard mollifier

\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\eta(x):=\begin{cases}C\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\lVert x\rVert^{2}-1}\bigr)&\lVert x\rVert\leq 1\,,\\
0&\lVert x\rVert>1\,,\end{cases}

(4.2)

where the constant
C
is chosen to ensure
\int\eta=1
. For
\varepsilon>0
,
one can consider

\eta_{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\,\eta\bigl(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\,,

whose support is bounded by
B_{\varepsilon}(0)
. Then, for locally integrable

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, its mollification
f_{\varepsilon}:=f*\eta_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

f_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
f_{\varepsilon}\to f
almost everywhere
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Streamlined analysis.

Recall Scheffé’s lemma that if a sequence
\pi_{n}
of probability
densities converges to a probability measure
\pi
almost everywhere,
then
\pi_{n}
converges to
\pi
in
\mathsf{TV}
-distance (thus weakly).
Hence,
\pi_{t}:=\pi*\eta_{t}\to\pi
weakly as
t\to 0
due to the
property of the mollifier. Then, it is a property of weak convergence
that
\int\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}\to\int\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi

for any Gaussian density
\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
. Therefore, probability
measures
\pi_{t}\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
converge to
\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}

almost surely (and thus weakly).

A Rényi version of LSI was established in
[
VW23
, Lemma 5]

that for
q>1
and smooth
\mu/\nu
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\leq\frac{qC_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)}{2}\,\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,,

(
\mathsf{R\text{-}LSI}
)

where
(\nicefrac{{\mu}}{{\nu}})^{q}\log\nicefrac{{\mu}}{{\nu}}
is continuous,
locally uniform, and integrable.

Lemma 4.1

(Closeness)
.

Let
\pi
be a log-concave
probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|\cdot|^{2}]=R^{2}
.
For
\alpha,\sigma^{2}>0
and
q>1
, probability measures
\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}

and
\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}
satisfy

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\mathbin{\|}\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)})\leq\frac{q^{2}\alpha^{2}R^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\,.

In particular, they are
O(1)
-close in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
if
\alpha\leq\nicefrac{{\sigma}}{{qR}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu\propto\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
and
\nu\propto\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}
,
which clearly satisfies
\mu\ll\nu
. For
t>0
, consider mollified
probability measures
\mu_{t}\propto\pi_{t}\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
and

\nu_{t}\propto\pi_{t}\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}
. On
\operatorname{supp}\nu_{t}
,

\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}=\nabla\log\frac{\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}}{\gamma_{\sigma^{2}(1+\alpha)}}=\frac{\alpha x}{(1+\alpha)\,\sigma^{2}}\,,

which admits a smooth extension (as
\frac{\alpha x}{(1+\alpha)\sigma^{2}}
)
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for some
\delta>0
independent of
t
,

\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})=q\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}})^{q}\,|\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}}|^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}})^{q}]}=\frac{q\alpha^{2}}{\sigma^{4}\,(1+\alpha)^{2}}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}})^{q}\,|\cdot|^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}})^{q}]}=\frac{q\alpha^{2}}{\sigma^{4}\,(1+\alpha)^{2}}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[\gamma_{\delta}\,|\cdot|^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\gamma_{\delta}}\,.

(4.3)

Using (

⁢
R
-
LSI

) with
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu_{t})\leq\sigma^{2}\,(1+\alpha)
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\leq\frac{qC_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu_{t})}{2}\,\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})\leq\frac{q^{2}\alpha^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[\gamma_{\delta}\,|\cdot|^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\gamma_{\delta}}\,.

As noted earlier,
\mu_{t}\to\mu
and
\nu_{t}\to\nu
weakly. By

[
AFP00
, Theorem 2.34]
, the lower semi-continuity of

\mathcal{R}_{q}
implies that
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\leq\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\nu_{t})
.
Also,
\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[\gamma_{\delta}\,|\cdot|^{2}]\,/\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\gamma_{\delta}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\gamma_{\delta}\,|\cdot|^{2}]\,/\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\gamma_{\delta}

due to the weak convergence of
\pi_{t}\to\pi
. Thus,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\leq\frac{q^{2}\alpha^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\gamma_{\delta}\,|\cdot|^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\gamma_{\delta}}\,.

We now need a general fact that for any integrable function
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\frac{\int|\cdot|^{2}\,\gamma_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,}{\int\gamma_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}\rho}\leq\frac{\int|\cdot|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho}{\int\mathrm{d}\rho}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[|\cdot|^{2}]\,.

Observe that
\operatorname{cov}_{\rho}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\rho^{\otimes 2}}[\{f(X)-f(Y)\}\,\{g(X)-g(Y)\}]
.
As
f(\cdot)=|\cdot|^{2}
increases in
|\cdot|
while
g=\gamma_{\delta}

decreases,
0\geq\operatorname{cov}_{\rho}(f,g)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[fg]-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}f\,\mathbb{E}_{\rho}g
,
which proves the claim. Using this fact with
\rho=\pi
, we complete
the proof of the main claim.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(Early-stopping)
.

Let
\pi
be a log-concave
probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|\cdot|^{2}]=R^{2}
.
For
q\geq 2
, if
\sigma^{2}\gtrsim qR\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}\log^{1/2}d
,
then
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\mathbin{\|}\pi)=O(1)
.

Proof.

Let
\mu\propto\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}
be the probability measure,
and
\mu_{t}
and
\pi_{t}
the mollifications of
\mu
and
\pi
.
Recall from (

⁢
R
-
PI

) that

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{t})\leq\log\Bigl(1-\frac{qC_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi_{t})}{4}\,\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{t})\Bigr)^{-1}\,.

(4.4)

By
[
Mil09
, §5]
or
[
CG20
, Theorem 3.13]
,
if two log-concave probability measures
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}

satisfy
\lVert\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
then

C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu_{1})\lesssim\frac{1\vee\log\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}\,C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu_{2})\,.

Since
\pi_{t}\to\pi
in
\mathsf{TV}
-distance (by Scheffé), and
\pi_{t}

is log-concave (by Prékopa–Leindler), we have
\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi_{t})\lesssim C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)
.
Next, emulating the proof of Lemma
4.1

with
\alpha=\infty
, (
4.3
) yields

\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{t})=\frac{q}{\sigma^{4}}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\,|\cdot|^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}}\leq\frac{q\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|\cdot|^{2}]}{\sigma^{4}}=\frac{qR^{2}}{\sigma^{4}}\,.

Putting these together and using
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log d

[
Kla23
]
,

\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{qC_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi_{t})}{4}\,\mathsf{RFI}_{q}(\mu_{t}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{t})\lesssim\frac{q^{2}R^{2}\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\log d}{\sigma^{4}}\,.

Thus, if
\sigma^{2}\gtrsim qR\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}\log^{1/2}d
, then
the RHS can be made smaller than (say)
0.1
. Putting this back to
(
4.4
), it follows that
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)=O(1)
.
∎

Covariance of strongly log-concave distributions.

[
KV25a
]
showed that
\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}\rVert\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert

if
h\gtrsim R\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}\log^{2}d\log^{2}\nicefrac{{R^{2}}}{{\Lambda}}

for
R=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}|\cdot|
. This can be improved as an immediate
corollary of the early-stopping result. For the barycenter
b_{\pi}

of
\pi
, any unit vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mu=\pi\gamma_{\sigma^{2}}

with
\sigma^{2}\gtrsim R\,\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{1/2}\log^{1/2}d
,

\Bigl(\int\bigl(v^{\mathsf{T}}(x-b_{\pi})\bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\Bigr)^{2}\leq\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}\int\bigl(v^{\mathsf{T}}(x-b_{\pi})\bigr)^{4}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\lesssim\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert^{2}\,,

where we used the reverse Hölder in the last inequality. Thus,

\lVert\operatorname{cov}\mu\rVert\lesssim\lVert\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\,.

Since
\mathcal{R}_{2}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)=O(1)
, we have

\lVert\operatorname{cov}\mu\rVert\lesssim\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert\,.

Query complexity of Rényi-warmness generation.

Now that all pieces are ready, we show that an
O(1)
-warm start
in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
can be generated using
\widetilde{O}(qd^{2}R^{3/2}\Lambda^{1/4})

membership queries for
q\geq 2
, where
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{K}
,
\Lambda=\lVert\operatorname{cov}\pi\rVert
,
and
R^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|\cdot|^{2}]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

The initialization
by rejection sampling requires
O(1)
queries in expectation due
to

\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-d\,|x|^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}e^{-d\,|x|^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{d^{-1}}(\mathcal{K})}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{d^{-1}}(B_{1})}\lesssim 1\,.

In the annealing phase, suppose that we are given
\bar{\mu}_{i}

such that
\mathcal{R}_{3}(\bar{\mu}_{i}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{i})=O(1)
. By a weak
triangle inequality for Rényi divergence (with
\lambda=2/3
),

\mathcal{R}_{2}(\bar{\mu}_{i}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{i+1})\leq\mathcal{R}_{3}(\bar{\mu}_{i}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{i})+\mathcal{R}_{4}(\mu_{i}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{i+1})\leq O(1)+2=O(1)\,,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
4.1
.
By Theorem
1.4
, from an
O(1)
-warmness in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-divergence,

\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
needs
O(d^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2})
queries to sample
X_{i+1}^{*}\sim\bar{\mu}_{i+1}

such that
\mathcal{R}_{3}(\bar{\mu}_{i+1}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{i+1})=O(1)
. Also,
doubling of initial
\sigma_{i}^{2}
requires
O(R/\sigma_{i})

phases, so each doubling needs
O(d^{2}\sigma_{i}R)
queries in
total. Since there are at most
O(\log qdR\Lambda^{1/2})
doublings,
and
\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq\sigma_{\text{last}}^{2}:=O(qR\Lambda^{1/2}\log^{1/2}d)
,
we can obtain a sample
X^{*}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{3}(\bar{\mu}_{\text{last}}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{\text{last}})=O(1)
,
using

O\bigl(q^{1/2}d^{2}R^{3/2}\Lambda^{1/4}\log^{1/4}d\times\log qdR\Lambda^{1/2}\bigr)=\widetilde{O}(q^{1/2}d^{2}R^{3/2}\Lambda^{1/4})\quad\text{queries}\,.

By hypercontractivity of
\mathsf{PS}
(Corollary
1.3
),
iterating
\mathsf{PS}_{\textup{Gauss}}
started from
X^{*}
towards
\mu_{\text{last}}

O(d^{2}\sigma_{\text{last}}^{2}\log_{2}q)
times leads to a new
sample
X^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{2q}(\operatorname{law}X^{\prime}\mathbin{\|}\mu_{\text{last}})=O(1)
.
Hence, the entire annealing has used

\widetilde{O}\bigl(q^{1/2}d^{2}R^{3/2}\Lambda^{1/4}+qd^{2}R\Lambda^{1/2}\bigr)\leq\widetilde{O}\bigl(qd^{2}R^{3/2}\Lambda^{1/4}\bigr)\quad\text{queries (due to }R^{2}\geq\Lambda\text{)}\,.

Lastly, we conclude the proof by noting the early-stopping result
(Lemma
4.2
) and the weak triangle inequality,
which results in
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\operatorname{law}X^{\prime}\mathbin{\|}\pi)=O(1)
.
∎

5
Log-concave sampling under an evaluation oracle

To extend our approach to log-concave distributions, we generalize
what we did for uniform sampling, streamlining algorithms in
[
KV25a

We first recall a result on closeness of two log-concave distributions
from
[

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathrm{d}\nu\propto e^{-V}\,\mathrm{d}x

\widetilde{O}(d^{2.5}+qd^{2}(R^{3/2}\vee 1)(\Lambda^{1/4}\vee 1))

1.6

\mu_{0}

Each outer annealing is followed by

goes back to

,

6
Discussion

We conclude this work by highlighting interesting directions for future
research.

Further simplification of general log-concave sampling.

We now have the clean end-to-end routine for warm-start generation
and uniform sampling. However, we adopted a more convoluted approach
for generating a warm start for general log-concave distributions,
since our current approach to log-concave sampling incurs the additive

O(qd^{2}\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})
queries in the complexity. Nonetheless,
we believe that general log-concave sampling can be further streamlined,
and an intriguing open question is whether this additive factor can
be removed. Resolution of this question would lead to a simpler algorithm
and analysis.

Irreversible algorithms for uniform sampling.

Unlike the well-conditioned setting with access to a first-order oracle,
the complexity of
\mathcal{R}_{2}
