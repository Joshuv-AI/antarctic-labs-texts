Machine learning based model for the detection of multiple sclerosis from OCT-derived macular and optic disc retinal biomarkers
Authors: Muhmmad Aqib Anwar, Ghassan Mohamedsalih, Fatema Al Mannaei, Anant Pai, Tanvir Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic neurological disease characterized by inflammatory demyelination and progressive neuroaxonal damage. Retinal layer thickness in the macular and optic disc regions, measurable using optical coherence tomography (OCT), might be considered as a promising non-invasive biomarker for detecting MS-related neurodegeneration.MethodsIn this study, we present a novel AI framework leveraging macular ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GCL-IPL) and Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer (RNFL) thickness across sectors (covering \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{360}^{\circ}$$\end{document}) and peripapillary biomarkers from both macula and optic disc regions of retina. These biomarkers were collected from bilateral scans of 74 MS and 44 controls subjects. The retinal features were then fed into machine learning (ML) model for the detection of MS patients. Feature engineering and hyperparameter tuned patient level model was proposed as the final model for MS detection. SHAP and PCA biplot analysis improved the explainability of the ML models.ResultsThe proposed ML model developed from patient-level GCL sectors achieved highest F1-score of 94.29%. RNFL biomarkers extracted from the peripapillary region yielded slightly lower performance, with F1-score of 87.84%. After integrating both GCL and RNFL sector markers, the proposed ML model achieved the best performance with F1-score of 95.71% and precision of 97.10%, outperforming other existing benchmark results. Statistical analysis (one-way ANOVA) revealed significant enlargement of multiple peripapillary regions (i.e., vertical and average cup-disc-ratio) in MS patients. On the other hand disc area, cup area, and cup volume were thinned in MS patients, though the results were not statistically significant. Notably, all the sectors of GCL and RNFL layers were found thinning for MS patients compared to the controls (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe proposed AI-based framework shows promising results for the detection of MS using OCT-derived retinal biomarkers, particularly GCL-IPL sectors, RNFL quadrants thickness, and peripapillary regions. Moreover, the explainable nature of the proposed framework supports clinical adoption and serve as a proof-of-concept for AI-enabled diagnosis of MS using retinal biomarkers.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03598-8.

Background

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is characterized by pathological processes such as inflammation, demyelination, axonal degeneration, and neuronal loss in the central nervous system (CNS) [1]. These pathological changes manifest as thinning of the retina and optic nerve [2, 3]. With an estimated 2.8 million cases worldwide in 2020, MS ranks among the most prevalent neurological disorders globally [4]. The disease may lead to progressive physical disability and cognitive impairment, among other symptoms, significantly compromising patients’ quality of life. [5]. The uniqueness and variability of MS are reflected in its highly heterogeneous clinical presentation, as it is a chronic neurodegenerative disorder of the CNS [6]. Definitive MS diagnosis is currently performed according to the modified McDonald criteria [7], which are principally based on oligoclonal bands and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) biomarkers, a process that is quite lengthy and invasive [8]. Although MRI has played a key role in monitoring CNS damage in MS, it has inherent limitations. Abnormal findings are not pathognomonic for MS, as other conditions may produce similar brain changes. Conversely, a normal MRI cannot exclude MS, since some patients present without detectable brain lesions [9], possibly due to spinal cord injuries or occult lesions. Therefore, alternative biomarkers are needed to support MS diagnosis and improve clinical outcomes.

In recent years, Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) has become a sensitive and practical alternative to MRI for studying neurodegeneration in MS [10]. OCT is accessible, non-invasive, fast, and repeatable. It uses light to create three-dimensional images of eye structures [11]. These features make OCT a valuable tool for improving MS diagnosis. MS induces widespread structural changes in the retina and optic nerve, particularly within the macula and optic disc regions [12]. These changes can be assessed using OCT to derive clinically useful disease biomarker [13, 14]. OCT-derived imaging markers, including peripapillary RNFL thickness and the combined thickness of the macular ganglion cell layer (GCL) and inner plexiform layer (IPL), have been proposed as promising biomarkers for neurodegenerative diseases such as MS [15]. Beyond the GCL-IPL and RNFL, inflammatory disease activity may also lead to changes in inner nuclear layer (INL) thickness [16]. Such thinning can be quantified by subtracting retinal layer measurements from those of a healthy normative cohort. Collectively, these layer thickness biomarkers support both MS detection and prognosis [17, 18].

A key characteristic of OCT recordings is the large number of features that can be extracted from retinal scans. Depending on the acquisition device, it is possible to segment between 6 and 10 distinct retinal layers, with thickness measurements available at multiple spatial locations for each layer. For instance, Triton swept-source OCT can generate measurements from approximately 40 × 60 sampling points [19], whereas Spectral-domain OCT systems typically provide measurements from an 8 × 8 grid [20], among other configurations. While the literature has identified retinal structures and regions most affected by neurodegenerative diseases such as MS, systematic biomarker selection remains essential for optimizing the performance of automatic classification models [21, 22].

Multiple studies have applied artificial intelligence (AI) to OCT data for MS diagnosis, utilizing measurements of retinal layer thickness variations [12, 23, 24]. Recently, machine learning (ML) have been applied to these retinal biomarkers for the detection of neurological diseases. For instance, Martin et al. [24] utilized feedforward neural networks and SVM on a cohort of 48 MS patients and 48 healthy controls, achieving an AUC of 83% for early-stage MS detection, with the GCL++ layer identified as having the greatest discriminant capacity. The authors additionally employed an ANN on this small cohort, which may lead to overfitting. Dongil et al. [4] employed an SVM with an RBF kernel to a cohort of 79 MS patients and 69 controls, achieving 69% sensitivity and 85.5% specificity based on neuroretinal thickness features. AUROC analysis identified GCL and IPL thickness as the most discriminative features. {In a subsequent study [25], the same authors used Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) with a linear SVM on the same cohort, achieving improved results of 86% sensitivity and 90% specificity. Khodabandeh et al. [13] assessed the performance of SVM, RF, and ANN across two datasets comprising 151 MS patients and 467 controls, achieving 88% accuracy, 63% recall, and a 68% F1-score. The authors identified composite GCL-IPL and INL as the most relevant layers. However, the performance is skewed toward the control class, indicating that the model may struggle to correctly identify MS patients despite high overall accuracy. Recently, Ortiz et al. [18] applied SVM with RFE and SHAP analysis on a dataset of 79 MS patients and 69 controls. They obtained 97% sensitivity, and 86% specificity, identifying macula-GCL and macula-RNFL as the most influential structures with average thickness values proving more important than inter-eye differences. The reviewed studies employed conventional ML classifiers, primarily focusing on a single retinal region with limited layers. Detection performance still requires improvement. Summary of recent studies on OCT-based MS diagnosis using ML and DL models

Furthermore, deep learning studies have illustrated prominent progress in OCT-based MS detection. Montolío et al. [22] employed Fully Connected Neural Network (FCNN) and LASSO regression to a cohort of 72 MS patients and 30 controls. Additionally, their investigation showed that the macular-GCL had a high prognostic value, with an accuracy of 81.9% in predicting 8-year outcomes. According to Gende et al. [26], segmentation models (MGU-Net, CNN) that were exclusively trained on healthy controls function poorly on neurodegenerative patients, frequently oversegmenting the RNFL and GCL layers. Ortiz et al. [17] identified GCL thickness and IPL inter-eye variations as important biomarkers, achieving 87% accuracy with CNN. While using nnU-Net with multi-view techniques, Rodriguez et al. [5] achieved an F1-score of 80%, emphasizing RNFL and overall retinal thickness as potent MS markers. Varela et al. [12] achieved 89% accuracy using a MobileNet (v2) model trained on synthetic slices, further confirming the RNFL and macular-GCL as critical biomarkers. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the effectiveness of deep learning in retinal layer segmentation and direct MS classification. Moreover, they highlight the importance of diverse training datasets and advanced data augmentation strategies. Despite its promise, AI-based OCT analysis for MS suffers from several unresolved gaps in the literature: (1) subtle retinal thickness changes are not discernible on raw OCT images; (2) black box AI models lack interpretability for trustworthy clinical application; (3) single-center datasets yield poor generalizability; and (4) instance-wise cross-validation overestimates predictive accuracy.

Our study examines the detection of MS leveraging ML models to distinguish MS patients from normal controls. Retinal layer thickness measurements from both the right (OD) and left (OS) eyes, along with comprehensive optic disc structural parameters, were extracted and used for model development. The following retinal features were collected from each of the 118 subjects (74 MS patients and 44 healthy controls): average and quadrant-based RNFL thickness, RNFL symmetry, rim area, disc area, cup-to-disc ratios, and cup volume from the optic disc region, along with GCL-IPL thickness metrics from the macula region. These features are systematically arranged into feature vectors for each eye: a 23-feature vector for RNFL (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 23} $$\end{document}) and an 8-feature vector for GCL-IPL (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \mathbb{G}^{1 \times 8} $$\end{document}), which are then concatenated to form a comprehensive 31-feature vector (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \mathbb{X}^{1 \times 31} $$\end{document}) constituting each eye. After feature extraction, we performed explanatory data analysis by using PCA biplot, ANOVA test and SHAP to determine the biomarkers significance. The preprocessing is carried out, where feature values are normalized using StandardScaler to ensure consistent scaling across biomarkers at both the eye and patient levels. All preprocessing steps were performed exclusively within each training fold of the Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) procedure to prevent data leakage and overly optimistic performance estimates. Specifically, feature scaling, hyperparameter tuning, and patient level aggregation were carried out in this manner.

OCT dataset collection

This retrospective study included a cohort of 74 patients clinically diagnosed with MS and 44 demographically matched HC subjects. Bilateral retinal OCT scans (left and right eyes) were obtained for all participants, resulting in a total of 118 OCT scans used for feature extraction and analysis. The MS cohort comprised 44 female patients (mean age: 36 ± 10 years) and 30 male patients (mean age: 44 ± 15 years). The control group comprised of 44 healthy individuals with no history of neurological disorders, ocular disease, or any systemic conditions known to affect retinal structure or visual function. All MS patients received a diagnosis from a neurologist according to the 2010 revision of the McDonald criteria [27]. All control individuals underwent a detailed ophthalmological examination to confirm the absence of retinal pathology, including glaucoma, cataracts affecting imaging quality, optic nerve abnormalities, and other ocular conditions that could confound RNFL or GCL-IPL thickness measurements. Patients history of Optic Neuritis (ON) were not present in the dataset. For ML suite processing, features were initially extracted at the eye level from bilateral scans and subsequently aggregated to generate patient-level representations prior to model training and validation. Retinal OCT biomarkers extraction

We acquired retinal biomarkers from OCT scans obtained using standard clinical OCT devices. In particular, the methodology examined two types of OCT frames: macula-centered frames for assessing GCL-IPL thickness, and optic disc-centered frames for evaluating RNF parameters. The methodology incorporates comprehensive examination of the GCL combined with the IPL, collectively measured as GCL-IPL thickness. For each eye, we extracted six sector specific thickness measurements to provide a comprehensive regional assessment of the macular ganglion cell complex. In addition to these sectoral readings, the methodology records both the average GCL-IPL thickness and the minimum GCL-IPL thickness, which are critical parameters for detecting subtle neuronal loss. The addition of percentile indicators improves the framework’s diagnostic utility for detecting neurodegenerative changes. These indicators support comparison against normative databases and allow thickness measurements to be categorized as normal or significantly abnormal. Furthermore, retinal biomarkers derived from optic disc-centered OCT frames focused on the RNFL were added as a feature vector representing RNFL biomarkers. For acquiring these biomarkers, key structural metrics were recorded for each eye, including inter-eye RNFL symmetry and average peripapillary RNFL thickness, to evaluate global axonal loss. Rim area, disc area, average and vertical cup-to-disc ratios, and cup volume were used to further capture detailed optic disc morphometry, resulting in a multi-parametric evaluation of neuroretinal integrity. We also incorporated a comprehensive quadrant- and clock-hour–based analysis of peripapillary RNFL thickness to capture regional variations around the optic disc. For each eye, thickness measurements are systematically recorded across the four retinal quadrants: superior, nasal, temporal, and inferior, providing a broad assessment of sectoral RNFL integrity. To further enhance spatial resolution, additional gradient measurements and hour-specific readings across clock-hour positions are incorporated, enabling the detection of focal thinning patterns that may be obscured in global or quadrant-averaged analyses. Machine learning classifier development

Several conventional ML classifiers were used in this study; they were selected for their architectural diversity and proven effectiveness in analyzing medical biomarkers. The Random Forest classifier is renowned for its ensemble architecture and resilience to overfitting [28]. An ensemble of T decision trees aggregates predictions via majority voting: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y} = \mathrm{mode}\{h_t(\mathbf{x})\}_{t=1}^T$$\end{document}, where each tree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_t$$\end{document} is trained on a bootstrap sample of the data and a random subset of features. Multiple boosting classifiers (XGBoost, CatBoost, AdaBoost, and Gradient Boosting) were used to exploit their sequential learning, which progressively emphasizes challenging examples to enhance model accuracy [29]. Boosting iteratively builds an additive model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \eta_t h_t(\mathbf{x})$$\end{document} where each subsequent weak learner \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_t$$\end{document} focuses on the errors of the previous ensemble by minimizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell(y_i, F_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_i) + h_t(\mathbf{x}_i))$$\end{document} with instance-specific weights or gradients. The k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) predicts the label for a test point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{x}$$\end{document} as the majority class among its k nearest neighbors: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y} = \mathrm{mode}\left\{y_i : \mathbf{x}_i \in \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{x})\right\}$$\end{document}, where distance is measured by Minkowski metric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i) = \left(\sum_{j=1}^m |x_j - x_{ij}|^p\right)^{1/p}$$\end{document} (with p=2 for Euclidean distance) [30]. A decision tree recursively partitions the feature space using splits at node m that minimize an impurity measure, such as the Gini index: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_m = 1 - \sum_{c=1}^C p_{mc}^2$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{mc}$$\end{document} is the proportion of samples of class c in node m [31]. Ultimately, Naive Bayes, as a probabilistic generative classifier [32], was employed to simulate the distribution of retinal biomarkers and to estimate class-conditional probabilities for each subject. It applies Bayes’ theorem with the conditional independence assumption: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(y|\mathbf{x}) \propto P(y) \prod_{j=1}^m P(x_j|y)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(x_j|y)$$\end{document} is typically modeled as a Gaussian distribution for continuous features: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(x_j|y=c) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{jc}^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x_j - \mu_{jc})^2}{2\sigma_{jc}^2}\right)$$\end{document}.

Model evaluation

We employed LOOCV to evaluate model performance and ensure generalization. In this approach, N-1 subjects were used for training, while the remaining subject was used for testing in each iteration. This process was repeated until every subject had been used once as the test sample, enabling a comprehensive subject-level evaluation. Model performance was assessed using the following metrics formulas: 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Accuracy = \frac{(t_p+t_n)}{(t_p+t_n+f_p+f_n)}$$\end{document}

3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Sensitivity (recall) = \frac{t_p}{(t_p+f_n)}$$\end{document}

4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Specificity = \frac{t_n}{(t_n+f_p)}$$\end{document}

5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Precision = \frac{t_p}{(t_p+f_p)}$$\end{document}

6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F1 Score = 2* \frac{Precision*Recall}{Precision+Recall} $$\end{document}

7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AUC score = Area\:Under\:the\: ROC\:Curve$$\end{document}

Here, tp, fp, fn, and tn denote the number of true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives, respectively. Given the dataset’s imbalance, sensitivity (recall), specificity, and the AUC score are significant performance indicators given the dataset’s imbalance. The AUC (Area Under the Curve) reflects the model’s ability to distinguish between MS and control group; higher AUC value indicates better discrimination. Overall, the AUC metric provides a robust summary of the classification performance of all classifiers at both the eye and patient levels.

Discussion

Principal findings

According to our investigation, the combination of GCL-IPL and RNFL retinal biomarkers is highly significant for MS diagnosis and detection. These biomarkers capture richer biological information near the fovea, the central point of vision, where MS-related neuroaxonal damage most prominently disrupts retinal layer thickness. We obtained promising results for MS detection after an extensive experimentation. Eight ML classifiers were evaluated using retinal biomarker features, with the proposed framework established for both eye and patient-level analysis to provide baseline benchmark results for MS detection.

The macular biomarkers, consisting of GCL-IPL thickness measurements from both eyes, achieved strong performance with the Random Forest classifier. At the patient level, Naive Bayes attained the highest accuracy of 93.10% using combined bilateral features. Conversely, RNFL biomarkers performed less than GCL-IPL at the eye and patient analytical levels. Notably, CatBoost at the eye level and Random Forest at the patient level delivered the best results, with ROC-AUC scores of 93.32% and 92.19%, respectively. To achieve SOTA results, our primary focus was on combining features from both GCL-IPL and RNFL. In this configuration, the decision tree outperformed all other classifiers in both levels of analysis. At the eye level, it achieved the highest trade-off between sensitivity and specificity at 95.45% and 96.0%, respectively. At the patient level, we achieved sensitivity of 94.37% and specificity of 95.12%, further demonstrating that combined features serve as the most effective biomarkers for distinguishing between MS and control subjects. Manifestation of MS on retinal layer thickness biomarkers

The one-way ANOVA analysis revealed statistically significant differences in several retinal biomarkers between MS patients and healthy controls, indicating substantial structural alterations associated with neurodegeneration. In particular, macular GCL-IPL related parameters demonstrated the strongest discriminative power as shown in Table 6. Similarly, several macular sectors (Sector1–Sector6 regions) exhibited consistent thinning in the MS group, all showing highly significant p-values (p < 0.001) and large effect sizes, confirming widespread ganglion cell layer degeneration in MS. Biomarker explanation across macula and optic disc regions

Figure 7 illustrates the retinal regions from which biomarkers are extracted for MS detection. The 6-sector breakdown is designed to match the retinal anatomy and the distribution of ganglion cells around the fovea [37]. The standard six sectors include: Temporal Superior (TS), Superior (S), Nasal Superior (NS), Nasal Inferior (NI), Inferior (I), and Temporal Inferior (TI). Measurements of GCL-IPL thickness within these sectors provide important biomarkers for detecting neuroretinal damage related to diseases such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) [38], ischemic optic neuropathy (ION) [39], and MS [40]. In addition to macular biomarkers, peripapillary RNFL measurements are extracted from the optic disc region. RNFL thickness is analyzed across four primary quadrants: superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal to assess regional axonal integrity around the optic nerve head. Additional biomarkers including average RNFL thickness, RNFL symmetry, Rim area, Disc area and Cup volume are also collected from this region (see Fig. 2). Furthermore, RNFL clock-hour analysis divides the peripapillary region into twelve sectors, each representing a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{30}^{\circ}$$\end{document} segment around the optic disc [41]. These sectors are numbered analogously to a clock face, with 12 o’clock corresponding to the superior region and 6 o’clock corresponding to the inferior region. This comprehensive sectoral analysis facilitates the identification of localized axonal thinning that may remain undetected in quadrant-level assessments. All retrieved biomarkers from the GCL-IPL and peripapillary RNFL are documented as retinal features and combined to provide the input feature set for the proposed multiple sclerosis detection method.Fig. 7Biomarkers of GCL-IPL layer thickness across the macula region captured in 6 sectors, and biomarkers across the optic disc region for RNFL thickness in 4 quadrants. Comparison with other models

The effectiveness of our proposed framework was validated by comparing its performance against reported results from SOTA methods. For this reference comparison, we used similar experimental settings (e.g., retinal layer biomarkers, subject-level validation) as in previous studies, acknowledging that direct performance transfer is not guaranteed across different datasets. Existing studies primarily utilize retinal structural parameters such as GCL-IPL thickness, RNFL thickness, and inter-eye differences in the IPL as input features for classification (Table 1). According to a review of recent literature, the majority of benchmark research in this domain relies on private datasets, which restricts the ability to directly compare various models. Furthermore, there is a methodological gap in reproducibility and uniform evaluation, as many studies do not specify the precise techniques used to derive retinal thickness measurements from OCT scans.

Despite these limitations, we conducted an analysis using three benchmarks that reported comparable biomarkers (GCL-IPL and RNFL thickness) and experimental setups (LOOCV at subject level). The proposed framework achieves competitive results compared to existing methods at subject level (see Fig. 8), with sensitivity = 94.4% (95% CI: 88.3–98.7%, SD: 0.02), specificity = 95.1% (95% CI: 87.5–100%, SD: 0.03), and AUC = 94.7% (95% CI: 90.0–98.6%, SD: 0.02). In particular, our approach achieved an accuracy of 94% on our private dataset, which is higher than the range of 87–92% reported by Ortiz et al. [17, 18] on their respective datasets. Their study also reported sensitivity values between 82 and 97% and specificity values between 87 and 92%. Although the study by Ortiz et al. [18] reported a slightly higher sensitivity of 97%, our model achieved improved specificity, indicating better discrimination between MS patients and healthy controls. Furthermore, on our private dataset, our methodology achieved an accuracy approximately 8% higher, and sensitivity and specificity each approximately 17% higher, than the respective values reported by Dongil et al. [25] on their dataset. The improved performance confirms the value of integrating multiple retinal biomarkers within the proposed framework.Fig. 8Comparison of achieved performance metrics for the proposed methodology with existing literature

Limitations

It is important to take into account the limitations of this study when evaluating the findings. First, the analysis is based on a retrospective private dataset comprising 118 participants, including 74 patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) and 44 healthy controls. This is relatively a small cohort. Although, the cohort size is exceeding some of the previous studies (see Table 1), the use of a private dataset limits direct comparison with benchmark studies that rely on different data sources. Consequently, ensuring the reproducibility and external validation of the results remains challenging. Moreover, class imbalance between the MS and control may introduce bias into ML model, but as we obtained similar level of sensitivity (94%) and specificity (95%) at patient-level model performance (see Table 5), we believe our proposed model successfully avoided that bias.

Secondly, the collected dataset lacks information on consequential clinical comorbidities and ophthalmic conditions, such as optic neuritis (ON), which is a familiar manifestation of MS and can significantly influence retinal biomarkers [42]. The absence of additional clinical markers may limit the ability to fully interpret the relationship between retinal structural changes and disease pathology.

Third, our study is limited by a significant age difference between MS patients (41 ± 8 years) and controls (35 ± 13 years; p = 0.03). Since age influences retinal thickness [43], age-related changes may have confounded our results, and we did not adjust for age as a covariate in our primary analysis. Furthermore, all OCT scans were acquired using a single imaging system, which may restrict the robustness of the model across different OCT devices or imaging protocols. Variations between acquisition systems could potentially affect retinal thickness measurements and model performance in real-world clinical settings. Finally, detailed clinical information regarding disease duration and severity was not available for all patients. In particular, the duration of MS and baseline Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) scores were unknown. Variability in disease duration and disability status may introduce bias in retinal biomarker measurements and could affect the interpretation of the results.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that retinal biomarkers, particularly GCL-IPL and RNFL thickness measurements, serve as reliable indicators for detecting MS. Our proposed approach concatenates biomarkers extracted from both macular and optic disc regions, which are then used as input to conventional ML classifiers to differentiate MS patients from healthy controls. The proposed methodology emphasizes the diagnostic importance of GCL-IPL and RNFL biomarkers, demonstrating their significant contribution to classification performance. Among the several classifiers assessed using the combined GCL-IPL and RNFL features, the decision tree provided benchmark performance at the patient level with sensitivity of 94.3% and specificity of 95.1%, along with a PR-AUC of 95.2%. PCA biplot analysis identified ten key biomarkers with the highest discriminatory power, comprising nasal and temporal sectors along with average RNFL and GCL-IPL thickness, thereby validating the strategic selection of macular and optic disc regions. One-way ANOVA confirmed statistically significant differences (p < 0.001) in layer thickness across all sectors between MS patients and healthy controls, reinforcing the biological plausibility of these biomarkers. All GCL-IPL and RNFL biomarkers remained significant after FDR correction (q = 0.05), while rim area and disc area did not survive correction. The findings indicate that OCT-derived retinal biomarkers could represent an affordable, non-invasive method to aid in the diagnosis of multiple sclerosis in clinical settings. Future work will focus on external validation using public datasets to assess model generalizability of proposed model.
