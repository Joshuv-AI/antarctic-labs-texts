Representation theory of the twisted Yangians in complex rank
Arun S. Kannan, Shihan Kanungo

Abstract
. In 2016, Etingof defined the notion of a Yangian in a symmetric tensor category and posed the problem to study them in the context of Deligne categories. This problem was studied by Kalinov in 2020 for the Yangian Y ⁡ ( 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 t ) Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{t}) of the general linear Lie algebra 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 t \mathfrak{gl}_{t} in complex rank using the techniques of ultraproducts. In particular, Kalinov classified the simple finite-length modules over Y ⁡ ( 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 t ) Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{t}) . In this paper, we define the notion of a twisted Yangian in Deligne’s categories, and we extend these techniques to classify finite-length simple modules over the twisted Yangians Y ⁡ ( 𝔬 t ) Y(\mathfrak{o}_{t}) and Y ⁡ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔭 t ) Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{t}) of the orthogonal and symplectic Lie algebras 𝔬 t , 𝔰 ​ 𝔭 t \mathfrak{o}_{t},\mathfrak{sp}_{t} in complex rank.

1.
Introduction

A
symmetric tensor category
(STC) is an abstraction of the representation category of a group, or more generally, an affine supergroup scheme. However, not all symmetric tensor categories arise as such representation categories; in other words, some do not
fiber
over the category of complex super vector spaces
\sVec_{\mathbb{C}}
. Working with these exotic categories yields new forms of commutative algebra, algebraic geometry, and representation theory beyond the realm of (super) vector spaces. Unless stated otherwise, all symmetric tensor categories considered in this paper are defined over
\mathbb{C}
.

The most prominent examples of STCs that do not fiber over
\sVec_{\mathbb{C}}
are the
Deligne categories
, introduced in
[
DMOS82
,
Del90
,
Del02
,
Del07
]
. These categories generalize classical representation categories such as
\Rep(GL_{n})
,
\Rep(O_{n})
, and
\Rep(Sp_{n})
to the setting where the rank parameter
n
is replaced by a complex parameter
t\in\mathbb{C}
. An analogous construction exists for symmetric groups. In recent years, Deligne categories have been the subject of extensive study from multiple perspectives.

The present work concerns Yangians in symmetric tensor categories. The notion was introduced by Etingof in
[
Eti16
]
, who showed that to any quadratic Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
in a symmetric tensor category
\mathcal{C}
, one can associate an algebra
Y(\mathfrak{g})
, called its
Yangian
. Although this is a categorical rephrasing of Drinfeld’s original definition
[
Dri85
]
, Etingof provided a streamlined construction via
R
-matrices in the case of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{t})
, where
\mathfrak{gl}_{t}=V\otimes V^{*}
and
V
is the tautological object in
\Rep(GL_{t})
. Etingof further posed the problem of classifying the irreducible representations of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{t})
that have finite-length when viewed as objects in
\Rep(GL_{t})
. This question was solved by Kalinov
[
Kal20
]
, who used ultrafilter and ultraproduct techniques to bypass the lack of a triangular decomposition, following earlier applications of these methods by Harman
[
Har16
]
and Deligne
[
Del07
]
to Deligne categories. Kalinov showed that simple finite-length representations of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{t})
are parametrized by pairs of sequences of Drinfeld polynomials subject to a certain stabilization condition. Independently, Utiralova
[
Uti19
]
proved a version of Olshanski’s centralizer construction in complex rank for
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{t})
.

In this paper, we introduce the
twisted Yangians

Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{t})
and
Y(\mathfrak{o}_{t})
, corresponding to the categories
\Rep(Sp_{t})
and
\Rep(O_{t})
, respectively. Extending Kalinov’s methods, we classify their irreducible representations. As expected, irreducible modules are parametrized by Drinfeld polynomials, analogous to those appearing in the integer-rank theory of
Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{2n})
and
Y(\mathfrak{o}_{2n})
. Specifically, these are highest weight representations, where the highest weight
\mu(u)=(\mu_{1}(u),\mu_{2}(u),\ldots)
is an infinite sequence of
\mu_{i}(u)\in 1+\mathbb{C}\llbracket u^{-1}\rrbracket
satisfying

\frac{\mu_{i+1}(u)}{\mu_{i}(u)}=\frac{P_{i}(u+1)}{P_{i}(u)}

for monic polynomials
P_{i}(u)
, and the
\mu_{i}(u)
eventually stabilize and equal some even series
\mu^{m}(u)\in 1+u^{-2}\mathbb{C}\llbracket u^{-2}\rrbracket
. This implies that the
P_{i}(u)
stabilize and equal
1
. As in the integer-rank case, the polynomials
P_{i}(u)
are called
Drinfeld polynomials
. Interestingly, the classification has the exact same form for
Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{t})
and
Y(\mathfrak{o}_{t})
, even though
Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{2n})
and
Y(\mathfrak{o}_{2n})
have different classification theorems. This is perhaps a consequence of the fact that
\Rep(O_{t})=\Rep(Sp_{-t})
as tensor categories.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In
Section

2.1
, we introduce the representations of the groups
GL_{N},O_{N},Sp_{N}
for an even integer
N=2n
, discuss the simple objects and the representations of the Lie algebras. We also discuss the positive-characteristic analogs of these categories, as they will be important later on.

In
Section

3
, we first define the Yangians and twisted Yangians. We then prove positive-characteristic versions of the classification theorems of finite-dimensional irreducible representations of the twisted Yangians, similarly to how Kalinov in
[
Kal20
, Section 3]
proves a positive-characteristic analog of the classification theorem of finite-dimensional irreducible representations of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
. In
Section

4
, we define the Deligne categories
\Rep O_{t}
and
\Rep Sp_{t}
and give their construction using ultraproducts.
Finally, in
Section

5
, we assemble the results of Sections
3
and
4
together using ultraproducts to establish the final classification theorem,
Theorem

5.9
. Finally,
Appendix

A
proves the necessary results about the Sklyanin determinant used for proving
Theorem

3.4
, an important tensor product decomposition that relates representations of
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
to those of
SY(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
(where
\mathfrak{g}_{n}=\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}
or
\mathfrak{o}_{2n}
), the
special twisted Yangian
, analogous to the Yangian
Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{n})
.

Several natural directions remain open. First, one may seek to describe the simple objects of
\Rep(GL_{t})
,
\Rep(O_{t})
, and
\Rep(Sp_{t})
appearing in irreducible representations of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{t})
,
Y(\mathfrak{o}_{t})
, and
Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{t})
. Second, the categorical framework allows one to define a wide class of Yangians and twisted Yangians beyond the classical types, inviting further classification questions. Finally, it remains to be seen whether an analog of Olshanski’s centralizer construction exists for twisted Yangians in complex rank, paralleling
[
Uti19
]
.

2.
The representation categories
\bfRep(GL_{N})
,
\bfRep(SO_{N})
,
\bfRep(Sp_{N})

In this section, we review some basic properties about representation categories of classical groups and their Lie algebras. We assume all representations are finite-dimensional, and we work over an algebraically closed field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic
p\neq 2
. We freely reference
[
Jan03
]
.

2.1.
Representation categories of classical groups

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a reductive algebraic group with fixed choice of split maximal torus
H
and character lattice
X(H)
. Every module over
M
over
H
admits a weight-space decomposition
M=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in X(H)}M_{\lambda}
. In particular, the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
\mathcal{G}
admits a weight-space decomposition given by

\mathfrak{g}={\mathfrak{g}}_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in R}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha},

where
R
is the set of roots, which are the non-zero weights of
H
such that
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}\neq 0
. We will let
\mathfrak{h}
denote the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
corresponding to
\mathfrak{g}
. Each
f:H\rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m}\in X(H)
gives a Lie algebra map
\mathfrak{h}\mapsto\mathbb{K}\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
; in particular, if we fix a basis for
X(H)
, the image of the basis under this map gives a basis of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. We will use the same symbols to describe both bases, identified via this map. This will be convenient to pass from weights of
\mathcal{G}
to those of
\mathfrak{g}
. Now, let’s set up notation for the reductive algebraic groups we will consider.

Let
GL_{N}
denote the general linear group scheme, which is the linear algebraic group of
N\times N
invertible matrices, where
N=2n
is an even integer. The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
of
GL_{N}
is the space of all
N\times N
matrices, for which we will use the usual elementary matrices
E_{ij}
as basis. However,
rather than using the usual choice of
1,\ldots,2n
to index rows and columns, we will use
I_{N}\coloneqq\{-n,\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,n\}
. Consequently, all results we cite from
[
Kal20
]
must first be translated to the indices
-n,\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,n
.

Our choices of split maximal torus
H
in
GL_{N}
and Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
in
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
will be the subgroup and subalgebra of diagonal matrices, respectively. We let
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i\in I_{N}}
denote the basis of
X(H)
where
\varepsilon_{i}
is the character
diag(t_{-n},\dots,t_{n})\mapsto t_{i}
. Any weight
\lambda\in X(H)
is therefore of the form
\lambda=\sum_{i\in I_{N}}\lambda_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. It follows by our convention that the same set of symbols will denote the basis of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
dual to
\{E_{ii}\}_{i\in I_{N}}
so that any
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
-weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
can also be expressed
\lambda=\sum_{i\in I_{N}}\lambda_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
except now with
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
. Finally, we will use the conventional triangular decomposition on
\mathfrak{g}
where the simple roots are given by
\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i+1}
, corresponding to the root vector
E_{i,i+1}
for
i\in I_{N}\setminus\{-1\}
, and
\varepsilon_{-1}-\varepsilon_{1}
, corresponding to the root vector
E_{-1,1}
.

The main groups in this paper, however, will be subgroup schemes of
GL_{N}
preserving a form. In particuar let
G
be an invertible symmetric (resp. skew-symmetric) matrix valued in
\mathbb{K}
. The
orthogonal group

O_{N}(G)
(resp.
symplectic group

Sp_{N}(G)
) is defined to be subgroup scheme given by the relation

\{x\in GL_{N}\ |x^{t}Gx=G\}.

Because
O_{N}(G)
is not connected, at times it will be convenient to work instead with the special orthogonal group
SO_{N}(G)
of determinant
1
matrices in
O_{N}(G)
. They both have the same Lie algebra. For the symplectic case we point out that
N
has to be even for a skew-symmetric form to exist. In fact, from now on we will assume
N=2n
is even, as we will not need to work with orthogonal groups for odd
N
.

Because different choices of
G
yield isomorphic groups, we choose a particular form for
G
(defined below) and omit it from the notation. We define the
N\times N
matrix
G=[g_{ij}]
by

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\begin{cases}\delta_{i,-j}&\text{in the orthogonal case}\\
\delta_{i,-j}\cdot\sgn i&\text{in the symplectic case.}\end{cases}

The matrix
G
has the following forms in the orthogonal and symplectic cases, respectively:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&1\\
0&0&\cdots&1&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\iddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&1\\
0&0&\cdots&1&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\iddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&-1&\cdots&0&0\\
-1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

This is not the most conventional choice (for instance, see
[
Hum12
]
), but it is what Molev uses in
[
Mol07
]
, which will be useful for our purposes. We let the set of diagonal matrices
H
in
\mathcal{G}=SO_{N},Sp_{N}
be our maximal split torus in
\mathcal{G}
and is given by matrices of the form
diag(t_{n}^{-1},\dots,t_{1}^{-1},t_{1},\dots t_{n})
. We then define the character
\varepsilon_{i}\in X(H)
by sending such a matrix to
t_{i}
.

Let’s define the Lie algebras of these groups. Define
E
as the
N\times N
matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry is
E_{ij}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
(we are using
I_{N}
to index the rows and columns of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
as well). Next, define the matrix
F
by:

\displaystyle F\coloneqq E-G^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{t}G,

from which a straightforward calculation shows that

F_{ij}=E_{ij}-\theta_{ij}E_{-j,-i},

where

\displaystyle\theta_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{in the orthogonal case}\\
\sgn i\cdot\sgn j&\text{in the symplectic case.}\end{cases}

Then, the Lie algebras
\mathfrak{o}_{N}
and
\mathfrak{sp}
are the subalgebras of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
spanned the entries of
F
in the orthogonal case and symplectic case, respectively. The Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
corresponding to the choice of torus
H
has basis given by
F_{i,i}=E_{i,i}-\theta_{i,i}E_{-i,-i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
; the basis dual to this is precisely the basis induced
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
by the basis
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
X(H)
given above. Finally, if we let the span of the
F_{ij}
for
1\leq i<j\leq N
denote the span of the positive root spaces, then our choice of positive roots
\Phi^{+}
is given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{-\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j},\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}\}_{i<j}&\mathcal{G}=SO_{N}\\
\{-\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j},\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}\}_{i<j}\cup\{-2\varepsilon_{i}\}&\mathcal{G}=Sp_{N}\end{cases},

where
i,j
range over
1,2,\ldots,n
.

Now, let’s discuss a little about the representation theory. Let
\mathcal{G}
be any one of
GL_{N},SO_{N}
or
Sp_{N}
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Call any weight
\lambda\in X(H)

dominant integral
if it satisfies
\langle\lambda,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for all
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
, where
\alpha^{\vee}
is the coroot associated to
\alpha
, and let
X(H)^{+}
be the set of dominant integral weights. Explicitly, this is given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\lambda_{-n}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}&\mathcal{G}=GL_{N}\\
-|\lambda_{1}|\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n-1}\geq\lambda_{n}&\mathcal{G}=SO_{N}\\
0\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}&\mathcal{G}=Sp_{N}\end{cases}.

with each
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Impose a partial ordering
\preceq
on
X(H)
by
\mu\preceq\lambda
if
\lambda-\mu
is the nonnegative linear combination of positive roots. Then, it is well-known that the simple modules all have a unique maximal weight with respect to
\preceq
called the
highest weight
. Moreover, it must be dominant integral and for each dominant integral weight
\lambda
there is a simple module
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
with that highest weight. In the case of
\mathcal{G}=GL_{N}
, we will also denote the simple module of highest weight
\lambda
by
L(\lambda)
or
L(\lambda,p)
we if we want to emphasize the characteristic. For
\mathcal{G}=SO_{N},Sp_{N}
, we will also denote this simple module by
V(\lambda)
or
V(\lambda,p)
. This will be so that we are consistent with the notations of Molev in
[
Mol07
]
later on when discussing Yangians.

We will also consider another class of modules, called
Weyl modules
. For each dominant integral weight
\lambda\in X(H)^{+}
, we have a Weyl module by
\Delta(\lambda)
or
\Delta(\lambda,p)
defined by the universal property that it admits a surjection to any module generated by its highest-weight vector. In the case the characteristic
p=0
, we have
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\Delta(\lambda)
. For
p>2
, it can loosely be thought of as a mod
p
-reduction of the characteristic-zero module
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
(so its character is also given by the Weyl character formula). For a more precise definition, see §II.2 in
[
Jan03
]
.

Let’s also state classification simple
O_{N}
-modules. Let
\mathcal{G}=SO_{N}
and recall that
N
is even. Then, the simple
O_{N}
-modules are indexed by the following:

(1)

the collection
\{\lambda\ |\ \lambda\in X(H)^{+},\lambda_{1}<0\}
. In particular, the simple modules are given by the induced modules
\mathrm{ind}_{SO_{N}}^{O_{N}}\ V(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in X(H)^{+}
with
\lambda_{1}<0
. Moreover, replacing
\lambda_{1}
by
-\lambda_{1}
and then inducing gives the same module;

(2)

the collection
\{(\lambda,\varepsilon)\ |\ \lambda\in X(H)^{+},\lambda_{1}=0,\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}\}
. In this case, the
SO_{N}
-module structure on
V(\lambda)
lifts to the structure of an
O_{N}
-module where a distinguished order-
2
element acts by
\varepsilon
.

When it is clear we are working with
O_{N}
, we will let
X(H)^{+}
for
O_{N}
be the set of
\lambda\in X(H)
such that
0\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
and denote the simple module corresponding to
\lambda
with the notation
V(\lambda)
or
V(\lambda,p)
. As written, this is ambiguous when
\lambda_{1}=0
, but we deliberately ignore the sign
\varepsilon
for the case
\lambda_{1}=0
because it is not detectable at the level of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{o}_{N}
and basically has no bearing on what is to come. Moreover, it will turn out we won’t really be interested in the case where
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
due to stabilization with respect to a certain limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
, so in a loose sense we truly only care about the underlying
SO_{N}
-module structure.

We will not prove this classification here, but a proof is given in
[
GW00
]
(the proof therein assumes characteristic
p=0
but only needs
p\neq 2
).

Remark 2.1
.

For
\mathcal{G}=SO_{N}
with
N=2n+1
odd, the root system for
\mathcal{G}
is the same as the even case except with the additional short roots of
\varepsilon_{i}
and
-\varepsilon_{i}
. The criteria for dominant integrality for
\lambda\in X(H)
then becomes the same as that for
Sp_{N}
. Since we have
O_{N}=SO_{N}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, it is clear that
O_{N}
-representations are indexed by the set
\{(\lambda,\varepsilon)\ |\lambda\in X(H)^{+},\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}\}
.

2.2.
Representations of classical Lie algebras

Continue to let
\mathcal{G}
be one of
GL_{N},SO_{N}
or
Sp_{N}
, and let
\mathfrak{g}
denote the Lie algebra of
\mathcal{G}
. We are working towards describing the representation theory of the Yangian
Y(\mathfrak{g})
, so it will also be useful to state some basic facts about the representation theory of
\mathfrak{g}
. In characteristic zero, restricting a representation of
\mathcal{G}
to
\mathfrak{g}
is essentially the same, so we are mostly concerned with positive characteristic.

Let
C_{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the
fundamental alcove
of
\mathcal{G}
, which is given by

C_{\mathbb{Z}}=\{\lambda\in X(H)\ |\ 0\leq\langle\lambda+\rho,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle<p^{\prime},\forall\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\},

where
p^{\prime}=p
if
p>0
and
p^{\prime}=\infty
if
p=0
and where
\rho
is half the sum of the positive roots. For
O_{N}
, we define its fundamental alcove to be that for
SO_{N}
. Then, we have the following well-known fact (cf. §II.5.6 in
[
Jan03
]
):

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
\lambda,\mu\in C_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap X(H)^{+}
, we have
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\Delta(\lambda)
, and moreover there are no non-trivial extensions between
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{L}(\mu)
.

Corollary 2.3
.

For
\lambda\in C_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap X(H)^{+}
, the
\mathcal{G}
-module
\Delta(\lambda)
is irreducible upon restriction to
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

If
p=0
, this is well-known. So suppose
p>0
. First of all, by Proposition
2.2
,
\Delta(\lambda)
is simple. Then, because
\mathcal{G}
is reductive, the restriction of a simple
\mathcal{G}
-module to
\mathfrak{g}
can instead be thought of as module over the first Frobenius kernel over
\mathcal{G}
. But
\lambda
is a restricted dominant integral weight because it is both dominant integral and in the fundamental alcove, so the claim follows by the Steinberg tensor product theorem (see §II.3.17 in
[
Jan03
]
).
∎

The following lemma will also be useful (recalling
N=2n
) :

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
p>2
, and let
\lambda
be a dominant integral weight for
\mathcal{G}=SO_{N},Sp_{N}
. Then, the condition
-2\lambda_{n}+2n<p
implies
\lambda\in C_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

We want to show that
-2\lambda_{n}+2n<p
implies

0\leq\langle\lambda+\rho,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle<p

for every positive root
\alpha
. Let us first consider the case of
\mathcal{G}=SO_{N}
, whose positive roots are
\pm\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}
for
i<j
. In this case, we have

\rho=-\varepsilon_{2}-2\varepsilon_{3}-\cdots-(n-1)\varepsilon_{n}.

The condition that
\lambda
lies in the fundamental alcove is then equivalent to:

\displaystyle 0\leq\langle\lambda+\rho,(\pm\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j})^{\vee}\rangle=(\pm\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\mp(i-1)+(j-1)<p.

Since
\lambda
is dominant integral,
i<j
means
0\geq\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{j}
, so the first inequality is satisfied. Additionally,
\pm\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\leq-2\lambda_{n}
and
\mp(i-1)+(j-1)\leq 2n
, so if
-2\lambda_{n}+2n<p
, the second is satisfied, so
-2\lambda_{n}+2n<p
implies
\lambda
lies in the fundamental alcove. The same argument applies, with minor adjustments, to the case of
\mathcal{G}=Sp_{N}
, whose positive roots are
\pm\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}
for
i<j
and
-2\varepsilon_{i}
, and

\rho=-\varepsilon_{1}-2\varepsilon_{2}\cdots-n\varepsilon_{n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Representation categories

We would like to define some useful notation for later on. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a reductive algebraic group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. If
A
is some Lie algebra, affine group scheme, or associative algebra algebra (in the category of vector spaces), let
\bfRep A
denote the category of finite-dimensional
A
-modules. For instance, we let
\bfRep\mathcal{G}
denote the category of finite-dimensional representations of
\mathcal{G}
, which obviously is a symmetric tensor category. In characteristic
0
, it is semisimple.

Now, let
A
be an associative unital algebra equipped with an algebra homomorphism
U(\mathfrak{g})\rightarrow A
. Let us define
\bfRep_{\mathcal{G}}A
to be the category of
\mathcal{G}
-modules
M
that also admit the structure of an
A
-module such that the action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
M
through
\mathcal{G}
and through
A
coincide (we will treat the
\mathcal{G}
-module admitting multiple such structures as separate objects). The morphisms will be
\mathcal{G}
-module homomorphisms. For instance, if
A=U(\mathfrak{g})
and the equipped algebra homomorphism is the identity map, then this category is just the category
\bfRep\mathcal{G}
. We will particularly be interested in the case where
A
is one of the (twisted) Yangians
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
,
Y(\mathfrak{o}_{N})
, or
Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{N})
, defined in §
3
. We do so because we are interested only in representations that lift to the group, after which we will take ultraproducts to construct representations in complex rank.

3.
Twisted Yangians

Before we can develop the theory of twisted Yangians in Deligne’s categories, we would like to first discuss twisted Yangians in finite rank. We will review basic results in characteristic zero. It will also be necessary to prove some basic results about twisted Yangians in positive characteristic due to a limit construction later on that passes from fields of positive characteristic to the complex numbers using ultrafilters.
1
1

1

Discussion of twisted Yangians in positive characteristic appears to be virtually nonexistent (cf.
[
BT18
]
for the ordinary Yangian of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
), so we prove what we need here.
We closely follow the presentation in
[
Mol07
]
. Retain the notation from
Section

2
. In particular, we work over the algebraically closed field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic
p\neq 2
and have
N=2n
be some even positive integer.

3.1.
The (twisted) Yangians
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
,
Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{N})
,
Y(\mathfrak{o}_{N})

We recall the definitions of the Yangian of
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
and the twisted Yangians of
\mathfrak{o}_{N}
and
\mathfrak{sp}_{N}
, following Chapter 4 of
[
Mol07
]
, as well as some basic properties.

The Yangian
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
is the associative algebra generated by the generators generators
t_{ij}^{(r)}
for
r\geq 1
and
i,j\in I_{N}
subject to the well-known ternary relation (see
[
Mol07
]
or (
A.1
)). It will be useful to consolidate these and define the formal power series

t_{ij}(u)\coloneqq\delta_{ij}+t_{ij}^{(1)}u^{-1}+t_{ij}^{(2)}u^{-2}+\cdots

and also to further assemble these into the
N\times N
matrix
T(u)=[t_{ij}(u)]
, whose coefficients can be viewed as elements of

\Hom(V,V\otimes Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N}))\llbracket u^{-1}\rrbracket,

where
V=\mathbb{K}^{N}
. It can be thought of as a generalization of the universal enveloping algebra of
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The map

(3.1)

\displaystyle t_{ij}(u)\mapsto\delta_{ij}+E_{ij}u^{-1}

defines a surjective homomorphism

\pi_{N}:Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})\to U(\mathfrak{gl}_{N}).

Moreover, we have an embedding
i_{n}:U(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})\hookrightarrow Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle E_{ij}\mapsto t_{ij}^{(1)}.

Here
E_{ij}
denotes the elementary
N\times N
matrix in
\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
which has a
1
in the
(i,j)
position and zeros elsewhere. Moreover,
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
is a Hopf algebra and the maps above are compatible with the Hopf algebra structure on
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
. A useful power series is the
quantum determinant
, given by

(3.3)

\displaystyle\qdet T(u)\coloneqq\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot t_{1,\sigma(1)}(u-N+1)\cdots t_{n,\sigma(n)}(u).

In characteristic zero, the coefficients
d_{1},d_{2},\ldots
of the quantum determinant are algebraically independent and generate the center of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
. In positive characteristic, this is not true. However, as seen in
[
Kal20
]
, the coefficients are still central and algebraically independent; hence we can define
Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N}))
to be the subalgebra generated by the coefficients of
\qdet T(u)
.
It will also be useful to work with the Yangian
Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{N})
of
\mathfrak{sl}_{N}
. It is defined as follows. It can be checked that for any power series
f(u)\in 1+u^{-1}\mathbb{K}\llbracket u^{-1}\rrbracket
, the map

(3.4)

\displaystyle T(u)\mapsto f(u)T(u)

defines an automorphism of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
. The Yangian
Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{N})
of
\mathfrak{sl}_{N}
is defined as the subalgebra of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
invariant under all such automorphisms. It was shown in
[
BT18
]
that

(3.5)

\displaystyle Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})=Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N}))\otimes Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{N}).

Now, let us define the twisted Yangians
Y(\mathfrak{o}_{N})
and
Y(\mathfrak{sp}_{N})
. Since we will be dealing with both cases simultaneously, we let
\mathfrak{g}_{n}=\mathfrak{o}_{N}
or
\mathfrak{sp}_{N}
.
Recall our choice of the matrix
G
from
2.1
, and that we are using
I_{N}=\{-n,\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,n\}
to index the rows and columns. For an
N\times N
matrix
A
, we define the transpose operation
′
with respect to
G
by

(3.6)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}\coloneqq G^{-1}A^{t}G.

In contrast,
[
Mol07
]
defines
A^{\prime}
as
GA^{t}G^{-1}
. Our choice will make it easier to state definitions categorically later on, as it coincides with the transpose with respect to the bilinear form on
V
induced by
G
. However, since our choice of
G
satisfies
G=\pm G^{-1}
, the two definitions are equivalent. Consequently, all results we cite from
[
Mol07
]
remain valid in our setting. If
A=[a_{ij}]
and
A^{\prime}=[a^{\prime}_{ij}]
, we can compute:

\displaystyle a_{ij}^{\prime}=\theta_{ij}a_{-j,-i}.

Then
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{E}-\mathbf{E}^{\prime}
. We defined
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
to be the algebra spanned by the
F_{ij}
, but we can alternatively describe it as the set of all all
X\in\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
satisfying
X^{\prime}=-X
.

Next, define the
R
-matrix
R(u)
, an
N\times N
matrix, by:

R(u)\coloneqq 1-\frac{P_{n}}{u}\in R(u)\in(\End(V)\otimes\End(V))\llbracket u^{-1}\rrbracket,

where
P_{n}
is the
N\times N
matrix given by:

P_{n}=\sum_{i,j}E_{ij}\otimes E_{ji}.

Note that
P_{n}
is the flip map.
The transposed
R
-matrix is then defined as:

R^{\prime}(u)\coloneqq G_{1}^{-1}R^{t_{1}}(u)G_{1}=G_{2}^{-1}R^{t_{2}}(u)G_{2},

where
G_{i}
and
t_{i}
act on the
i
-th tensor component for
i=1
,
2
.

Similarly, we define the twisted Yangian by introducing a family of generators
\mathbf{s}_{ij}^{(r)}
and the formal series

\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u)\coloneqq\delta_{ij}+\mathbf{s}_{ij}^{(1)}u^{-1}+\mathbf{s}_{ij}^{(2)}u^{-2}+\cdots.

We then assemble these into the
N\times N
matrix
\mathcal{S}(u)
with entries
\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u)
. Writing

\mathcal{S}(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{i}u^{-i},

we note that
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is simply the identity matrix.

Definition 3.1
.

The twisted Yangian
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
is the associative unital algebra generated by the elements
\mathbf{s}_{ij}^{(r)}
, with defining relations given by

(3.7)

\displaystyle R(u-v)\mathcal{S}_{1}(u)R^{\prime}(-u-v)\mathcal{S}_{2}(v)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{2}(v)R^{\prime}(-u-v)\mathcal{S}_{1}(u)R(u-v)

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(-u)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(u)\pm\frac{\mathcal{S}(u)-\mathcal{S}(-u)}{2u}.

We call the first relation the
quaternary
relation and the second the
symmetry
relation.
The following standard results (again, proved in the same way as characteristic zero; see
[
Mol07
, Chapter 2]
) will be useful.

Proposition 3.2
.

We define the evaluation homomorphism
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})\to U(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
by

(3.9)

\displaystyle\varrho_{n}\colon\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u)\mapsto\delta_{ij}+F_{ij}\left(u\pm\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}.

Conversely, the embedding
U(\mathfrak{g}_{n})\hookrightarrow Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
is given by

(3.10)

\displaystyle i_{n}\colon F_{ij}\mapsto\mathbf{s}_{ij}^{(1)}.

Proposition 3.3
.

For any series

g(u)=1+g_{1}u^{-2}+g_{2}u^{-4}+\cdots\in 1+u^{-2}\mathbb{K}\llbracket u^{-2}\rrbracket,

the mapping

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(u)\mapsto g(u)\mathcal{S}(u)

defines an automorphism of
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
.

There is a natural inclusion
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})\hookrightarrow Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
that aligns with the embedding
\mathfrak{g}_{n}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{gl}_{N}
. This inclusion is given by

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(u)\mapsto T(u)T^{\prime}(-u).

We define the
special twisted Yangian

SY(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
as the subalgebra of
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
that remains invariant under all automorphisms of the form (
3.11
). Equivalently, it can be characterized as

SY(\mathfrak{g}_{n})=Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{N})\cap Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n}).

We define the scalar
\alpha_{q}(u)
as follows:

(3.13)

\displaystyle\alpha_{q}(u)=\begin{cases}1&\text{in the orthogonal case}\\
\frac{2u+1}{2u-q+1}&\text{in the symplectic case}.\end{cases}

Next, define the special map
\varphi_{N}:\mathfrak{S}_{N}\to\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, which acts on permutations as in
[
Mol07
, Section 2.7]
. Let
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
be a permutation of
I_{N}
.

Define

(3.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)&\coloneqq(-1)^{n}\alpha_{N}(u)\\
&\times\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn\sigma\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{-a_{\sigma(1)},a_{\sigma^{\prime}(1)}}(-u)\cdots\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{-a_{\sigma(n)},a_{\sigma^{\prime}(n)}}(-u+n-1)\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt\times\mathbf{s}_{-a_{\sigma(n+1)},a_{\sigma^{\prime}(n+1)}}(u-n)\cdots\mathbf{s}_{-a_{\sigma(N)},a_{\sigma^{\prime}(N)}}(u-N+1)\end{split}

to be the
Sklyanin determinant
. In characteristic zero, the coefficients of the Sklyanin determinant generate the center of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
. In positive characteristic, this is not true. However, as we will see below, they are still central, and hence we can define
Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N}))
as the algebra generated by the coefficients of
\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)
. Write

\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)=1+c_{1}u^{-1}+c_{2}u^{-2}+\cdots.

Theorem 3.4
.

There exists a series
\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)\in 1+u^{-1}Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})\llbracket u^{-1}\rrbracket
such that the coefficients of
\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)
are central, and the even coefficients
c_{2},c_{4},\ldots
are algebraically independent and generate
Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n}))
; so
Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n}))=\mathbb{K}[c_{2},c_{4},\ldots]
.
We have the following tensor product decomposition:

(3.15)

\displaystyle Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})=Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n}))\otimes SY(\mathfrak{g}_{n}).

See
Appendix

A
for the proof. These results are all well-known in characteristic zero; see
[
Mol07
]
. To prove the first statement in positive characteristic, we simply repeat the same arguments as in characteristic zero. To prove the tensor product decomposition, we use (
3.5
). We do not show that the series
\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)
in
Theorem

3.4
matches (
3.14
) as it is not important for our purposes.

3.2.
Representations of
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
denote the algebraic group
O_{N}
or
Sp_{N}
such that its Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
. Molev derives the classification finite-dimensional simple modules over
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
in
[
Mol07
]
in characteristic zero. The goal of this subsection is to extend the results therein to positive characteristic, imposing assumptions as needed. Due to the ultraproduct construction later on, we mainly only care about representations detectable at the level of
\mathcal{G}
, so we will primarily work with representations in the category
\bfRep_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
(recall the notation from §
2.3
).

Finally, we will need some facts about the representation theory of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
in positive characteristic. For the definition and theory of highest weight modules for
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
in positive characteristic, see
[
Kal20
]
and
[
BT18
]
.

Definition 3.2
.

A representation
V
of the twisted Yangian
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
is called a
highest weight representation
if there exists a nonzero vector
\xi\in V
such that
V
is generated by
\xi
and the following condition holds: for each
i
, there is a formal power series

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(u)=1+\mu_{i}^{(1)}u^{-1}+\mu_{i}^{(2)}u^{-2}+\cdots,\quad\mu_{i}^{(r)}\in\mathbb{K},

such that

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u)\xi

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for }i<j,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{ii}(u)\xi

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}(u)\xi\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq n.

The vector
\xi
is called the
highest weight vector
of
V
, and the tuple
\mu(u)=(\mu_{1}(u),\ldots,\mu_{n}(u))
is called the
highest weight
.

Notice that the symmetry relation gives

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{-i,-i}=\mathbf{s}_{ii}(-u)\pm\frac{\mathbf{s}_{ii}(u)-\mathbf{s}_{ii}(-u)}{2u}

for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Then (
3.19
) implies that
\xi
is an eigenvector for
\mathbf{s}_{-i,-i}
as well.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mu(u)=(\mu_{1}(u),\ldots,\mu_{n}(u))
. The
Verma module

M(\mu(u))
over
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
is the quotient of
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
by the left ideal generated by the coefficients of the series

\begin{cases}\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u)&i<j\\
\mathbf{s}_{ii}-\mu_{i}(u)&i=1,\ldots,n.\end{cases}

A standard argument shows that
M(\mu(u))
has a unique simple quotient, which we will denote by
V(\mu(u))
, and also has the universal property that it surjects onto any highest weight module with the same highest weight.

Recall the notation for simple
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and
GL_{N}
-modules from §
2.1
. We have the following proposition:

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
V\in\bfRep_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
be simple and such that
V=V(\lambda_{1},p)\oplus\cdots\oplus V(\lambda_{k},p)
for
\lambda_{i}\in X(H)^{+}\cap C_{\mathbb{Z}}
with
(\lambda_{i})_{1}=0
. Then, the module
V
has a unique singular vector (up to scaling), whose
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
weight is maximal.

Proof.

Let us first contextualize the assumptions. First of all, the condition
(\lambda_{i})_{1}=0
is only there to ensure that each
\mathcal{G}_{n}
-module appearing above is irreducible when restricting to
SO_{N}
and therefore to
\mathfrak{o}_{N}
, when
\mathcal{G}_{n}=O_{N}
. The condition that
\lambda_{i}\in X(H)^{+}\cap C_{\mathbb{Z}}
is so that the character of
V(\lambda_{i},p)
is given by the Weyl character formula, which loosely speaking is to ensure that for a given
\lambda_{i}
, the module
V(\lambda_{i},p)
“looks the same” as
p
varies (this will be relevant when working with ultraproducts).

Now, let’s prove the statement. The problem here is to rule out the possibility of
V
not having any singular vectors at all. Since we can regard
V
as a
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
-module, we can repeat verbatim the argument given in Theorem 4.2.6 of
[
Mol07
]
.
∎

Hence
V
must be a highest weight representation
V=V(\mu(u))
by the universal property of
M(\mu(u))
. We also get the following corollary (Corollary 4.2.7 in
[
Mol07
]
).

Corollary 3.6
.

If
V
satisfies the conditions of
Proposition

3.5
, and
\eta\in V
with
\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u)\eta=0
for all
i<j
, then
\eta
is a scalar multiple of the highest weight vector
\xi
of
V
.

Now, we will use what is known about
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
to say more about representations of
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
. As noted at the start of Section 3 in
[
Kal20
]
, all the statements in Proposition 2.1.2 of
[
Kal20
]

Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
is still a Hopf algebra. Moreover, recall that we can view
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
as a subalgebra of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
via the embedding

\mathcal{S}(u)=T(u)T^{\prime}(-u).

Letting
\Delta
denote the comultiplication on

[

(3.20)

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u))=\sum_{a,b}\theta_{bj}t_{ia}(u)t_{-j,-b}(-u)\otimes\mathbf{s}_{ab}(u)

holds in any characteristic. This gives us the following proposition:

Proposition 3.7
.

There is a functor
\bfRep_{GL_{N}}Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})\boxtimes\bfRep_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})\rightarrow\bfRep_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
induced by
\Delta
.

Proof.

Notice that
\Delta
is an algebra homomorphism that maps
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
to
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})\otimes Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
. Moreover it sends
U(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
to
U(\mathfrak{g}_{n})\otimes U(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
. It follows that if
M\in\bfRep_{GL_{N}}Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
and
M^{\prime}\in M
, then
M\otimes M^{\prime}
is a
\mathcal{G}_{n}
module where both
\mathfrak{g}_{n}
actions are compatible.
∎

Let
\zeta
be the highest weight vector of the
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
-module
L(\lambda(u))
(see
[
Kal20
]
for the definition of a
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
-highest weight module), where the highest weight is given by
\lambda(u)=(\lambda_{1}(u),\ldots,\lambda_{N}(u))
. Similarly, let

denote the highest weight vector of the
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
-module

.

Proposition 3.8
.

The submodule
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})(\zeta\otimes\xi)
of the

-module

over

is a highest weight representation with highest weight vector

. Moreover, the highest weight is
\lambda_{i}(u)\lambda_{-i}(-u)\mu_{i}(u)
, for

.

Proof.

The proof is the same as the characteristic zero case; see
[

.

The evaluation homomorphism (

) gives a functor
\bfRep{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\rightarrow\bfRep_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})

Let

, and let

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(u)=(1+\lambda_{i}^{(1)}u^{-1})\cdots(1+\lambda_{i}^{(k)}u^{-1}).

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(u)=\frac{1+(\mu_{i}\pm\tfrac{1}{2})u^{-1}}{1\pm\tfrac{1}{2}u^{-1}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n

The submodule

The following is a consequence of (

Proposition 3.12
.

Every finite-dimensional irreducible representation of the twisted Yangian

remains irreducible when restricted to the special twisted Yangian

We write

\frac{\nu(u)}{\mu(u)}=\frac{P(u+1)}{P(u)}

Proposition 3.13
.

If

is finite-dimensional and satisfies the conditions of
Proposition

3.5
, then

(3.25)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(u)\longrightarrow\mu_{2}(u)\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\mu_{n}(u).

Proof.

The proof closely parallels Proposition 4.2.8 in
[
Mol07
]
. Let
J
be the left ideal of
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
generated by the coefficients of
\mathbf{s}_{-i,j}(u)
, for

, and consider

\displaystyle V^{J}=\{\eta\in V(\mu(u))\mid\mathbf{s}_{-i,j}(u)\eta=0\text{ for all }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\}.

The highest weight vector
\xi\in V(\mu(u))
belongs to

. The defining relations give

[\mathbf{s}_{-i,j}(u),\mathbf{s}_{kl}(v)]=0\mod J,

for

, so the operators
\mathbf{s}_{kl}(u)
with

. Moreover for

(u-v)[\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u),\mathbf{s}_{kl}(v)]=\mathbf{s}_{kj}(u)\mathbf{s}_{il}(v)-\mathbf{s}_{kj}(v)\mathbf{s}_{il}(u)\mod J,

which is formally identical to that of

admits a

-action by

is thus a finite-dimensional highest weight module of

with weight

.

as a

-module, and recalling that the Weyl group stabilizes the weights, yields that for any

-weight

This verifies the condition of Proposition 3.2.1 in
[

]
. It follows from Theorem 3.2.5 in
[

]
that (

) holds.

3.2.1.
Finite-dimensional irreducible representations of

in positive characteristic

Proposition 3.14
.

Suppose

Theorem 3.20
.

Suppose

Consider the

\displaystyle R(u-v)T_{1}(u)T_{2}(u)=T_{2}(u)T_{1}(u)R(u-v),

\displaystyle R(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})=(R_{m-1,m})(R_{m-2,m}R_{m-2,m-1})\cdots(R_{1m}\cdots R_{12}),

where

denotes the

\displaystyle R(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})=(R_{12}\cdots R_{1m})\cdots(R_{m-2,m-1}R_{m-2,m})(R_{m-1,m}).

Define

T_{i}=T_{i}(u_{i}),\quad\mathcal{S}_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}(u_{i}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq m

R_{ij}^{\prime}=R_{ji}^{\prime}=R_{ij}^{\prime}(-u_{i}-u_{j}).

For an arbitrary permutation

\displaystyle\left<\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{p_{m}}\right>=\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}}(R_{p_{1}p_{2}}^{\prime}\cdots R_{p_{1}p_{m}}^{\prime})\mathcal{S}_{p_{2}}(R_{p_{2}p_{3}}^{\prime}\cdots R_{p_{2}p_{m}}^{\prime})\cdots\mathcal{S}_{p_{m}}.

Proposition A.1
.

We have the identity

\displaystyle R(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})T_{1}\cdots T_{m}=T_{m}\cdots T_{1}R(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}).

Proof.

First, by repeated application of the ternary relation (

) and the fact that
R_{ij}
and
T_{k}
are permutable for
i,j,k
distinct, we get

(R_{1m}\cdots R_{12})T_{1}(T_{2}\cdots T_{m})=(T_{2}\cdots T_{m})T_{1}(R_{1m}\cdots R_{12}).

Since

R(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})=R(u_{2},\ldots,u_{m})(R_{1m}\cdots R_{12}),

by induction on
m
we are done.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

We have the identity

(A.5)

\displaystyle R(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})\left<\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{m}\right>=\left<\mathcal{S}_{m},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{1}\right>R(u_{1},\ldots u_{m}).

Proof.

Let
i,j,k
be distinct indices in
\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Note that we have

(A.6)

\displaystyle R_{ij}\mathcal{S}_{i}R_{ij}^{\prime}\mathcal{S}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{j}R_{ji}^{\prime}\mathcal{S}_{i}R_{ij}

(A.7)

\displaystyle R_{ij}R_{ik}^{\prime}R_{jk}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=R_{jk}^{\prime}R_{ik}^{\prime}R_{ij}.

Indeed, (
A.6
) is simply the quaternary relations, and for (
A.7
), we start with the Yang-Baxter equation

(A.8)

\displaystyle R_{ij}R_{ik}R_{jk}=R_{jk}R_{ik}R_{ij}.

Then apply the transposition with respect to the matrix
G
on the
k
’th component to get

(A.9)

\displaystyle R_{ij}R_{jk}^{\prime}R_{ik}^{\prime}=R_{ik}^{\prime}R_{jk}^{\prime}R_{ij}.

Now we have
P_{ij}R_{jk}^{t}P_{ij}=R_{ik}^{t}
, where
P
is the permutation operator and

R_{jk}^{t}=R_{jk}^{t}(-u_{j}-u_{k}).

Then we get that

(G_{k}^{-1}P_{ij}G_{k})R_{jk}^{\prime}(G_{k}^{-1}P_{ij}G_{k})=(G_{k}^{-1}P_{ij}G_{k})(G_{k}^{-1}R_{jk}^{t}G_{k})(G_{k}^{-1}P_{ij}G_{k})=G_{k}^{-1}R_{ik}^{t}G_{k}

which is simply
R_{ik}^{\prime}
. So we conjugate both sides of (
A.9
) by
(G_{k}^{-1}P_{ij}G_{k})
, and since conjugating
R_{ij}
gives us
R_{ij}
again, since
R_{ij}
and
G_{k}
commute, and
P_{ij}R_{ij}P_{ij}=R_{ij}
, we get (
A.7
). From here, we can proceed in exactly the same way as the proof of Proposition 2.5.1 in
[
Mol07
]
, replacing “
A^{t}
” transpose symbols with the “
A^{\prime}
” transpose with respect to
G
.
∎

Consider the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
acting on
\{1,\ldots,m\}
. The anti-symmetrizer in the group algebra of
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
is given by

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot\sigma\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{m}].

Let
A_{m}
be the image of this anti-symmetrizer under the natural action of
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
on
(\mathbb{C}^{N})^{\otimes m}
. We use
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
to be the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, we get

(A.10)

\displaystyle A_{m}(e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{m}})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot e_{i_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{\sigma(m)}}.

Note that we have
A_{m}^{2}=m!A_{m}
. For
m\leq N
the right hand side is nonzero because we assume
N<p
, see
Proposition

3.5
.

Proposition A.3
.

For
m\leq N
, if
u_{i}-u_{i+1}=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,m-1
, then

R(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})=A_{m}.

Proof.

We can use the same proof as Proposition 1.6.2 in
[
Mol07
]
. There are no division by zero problems because all denominators are
\leq m\leq N<p
.
∎

Now set
m=N
and

u_{i}=u-i+1,\quad i=1,\dots,N.

By the previous propositions,

(A.11)

\displaystyle A_{N}T_{1}\cdots T_{m}=T_{m}\cdots T_{1}A_{N}

and

(A.12)

\displaystyle A_{N}\left<S_{1},\ldots,S_{N}\right>=\left<S_{N},\ldots,S_{1}\right>A_{N}.

The image of
A_{N}
on
(\mathbb{C}^{N})^{\otimes N}
is one-dimensional. Indeed, for any basis element
e_{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{N}}
, if
i_{k}=i_{\ell}
for some
k\neq\ell
, then by (
A.10
),

A_{N}(e_{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{N}})=0.

Thus,
A_{N}
is nonzero only on pure tensors indexed by permutations of
(1,\dots,N)
, where it evaluates to

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot e_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{\sigma(N)}.

It follows that (
A.11
) and (
A.12
) reduce to
A_{N}
multiplied by a scalar series with coefficients in
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
or
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
, respectively.

Definition A.1
.

The
quantum determinant
(resp.
Skylanin determinant
) of the matrix
T(u)
(resp.
\mathcal{S}(u)
) is the formal series with coefficients in
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
(resp.
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
) given by

\qdet T(u)=d_{0}+d_{1}u^{-1}+d_{2}u^{-2}+\cdots,

respectively

\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)=c_{0}+c_{1}u^{-1}+c_{2}u^{-2}+\cdots,

such that the element (
A.11
) (resp (
A.12
)) equals
A_{N}\qdet T(u)
(resp.
A_{N}\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)
).

Proposition A.4
.

We have for any permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}

\displaystyle\qdet T(u)

\displaystyle=\sgn(\pi)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot t_{\sigma(1),\pi(1)}(u)\otimes\cdots\otimes t_{\sigma(N),\pi(N)}(u-N+1)

\displaystyle=\sgn(\pi)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot t_{\pi(1),\sigma(1)}(u-N+1)\otimes\cdots\otimes t_{\pi(N),\sigma(N)}(u).

Proof.

The characteristic zero proof works just as well in positive characteristic, see the proof of Proposition 1.6.6 in
[
Mol07
]
.
∎

Due to this, we see that this definition of the quantum determinant matches the one in
[
Kal20
]
, so we know that its coefficients are central and algebraically independent.

Theorem A.5
.

Under the embedding (
3.12
),

\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)=\alpha_{N}(u)\qdet T(u)\qdet T(-u+N-1),

where
\alpha_{N}(u)
is given by (
3.13
).

Proof.

Consider the mappings

\displaystyle T(u)

\displaystyle\mapsto G^{-1}T(u)G,

\displaystyle T(u)

\displaystyle\mapsto T^{t}(u),

\displaystyle T(u)

\displaystyle\mapsto T(-u).

The first is an automorphism, while the second and third are anti-automorphisms by Propositions 1.3.1 and 1.3.3 in
[
Mol07
]
((1.22), (1.26), (1.25)). The proof follows from the characteristic-zero case. Their composition yields

(A.13)

\displaystyle T(u)\mapsto G^{-1}T^{t}(-u)G=T^{\prime}(-u),

an automorphism denoted
\tau_{N}
.

From (
3.12
), we obtain
S_{i}=T_{i}T^{\prime}_{i}
, where
T_{i}=T_{i}(u-i+1)
and
T_{i}^{\sigma}=T_{i}^{\prime}(-u+i-1)
. Thus, the left-hand side of (
A.12
) rewrites as

(A.14)

\displaystyle A_{N}T_{1}T_{1}^{\sigma}R_{12}^{\prime}\cdots R_{1N}^{\prime}T_{2}T_{2}^{\sigma}R_{23}^{\prime}\cdots R_{2N}^{\prime}\cdots T_{N-1}T_{N-1}^{\sigma}R_{N-1,N}^{\prime}T_{N}T_{N}^{\sigma},

where
R_{ij}^{\prime}=R_{ij}(-2u+i+j-2)
. Applying the ternary relation (
A.1
) and partial transpose with respect to
G
on the first component,

T_{1}^{\prime}(u)R^{\prime}(u-v)T_{2}(v)=T_{2}(v)R^{\prime}(u-v)T_{1}^{\prime}(u).

Thus,

(A.15)

\displaystyle T_{i}^{\sigma}R_{ij}^{\prime}T_{j}=T_{j}R_{ji}^{\prime}T_{i}^{\sigma}.

For
i\neq j,k
, both
T_{i}
and
T_{i}^{\sigma}
commute with
R_{jk}^{\prime}
, allowing us to rewrite (
A.14
) as

(A.16)

\displaystyle A_{N}T_{1}(T_{1}^{\sigma}R_{12}^{\prime}T_{2})R_{13}^{\prime}\cdots R_{1N}^{\prime}(T_{2}^{\sigma}R_{23}^{\prime}T_{3})\cdots(T_{N-1}^{\sigma}R_{N-1,N}^{\prime}T_{N})T_{N}^{\sigma}.

Applying (
A.15
) repeatedly, we obtain

\displaystyle A_{N}T_{1}\cdots T_{N}R_{12}^{\prime}\cdots R_{1N}^{\prime}R_{23}^{\prime}\cdots R_{2N}^{\prime}\cdots R_{N-1,N}^{\prime}T_{1}^{\sigma}\cdots T_{N}^{\sigma}.

This simplifies to

\displaystyle\qdet T(u)A_{N}\left<I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{N}\right>T_{1}^{\sigma}\cdots T_{N}^{\sigma},

where
I_{i}
is the identity operator. Since the mapping
\mathcal{S}(u)\mapsto I
defines a representation of
T(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
, we obtain from (
A.12
)

(A.17)

\displaystyle A_{N}\left<I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}\right>=\left<I_{N},\ldots,I_{1}\right>A_{N}=A_{N}\beta_{N}(u),

for some scalar function
\beta_{N}(u)
. Applying
\tau_{N}
to (
A.11
),

A_{N}T_{1}^{\sigma}\cdots T_{N}^{\sigma}=A_{N}\tau_{N}(\qdet T(u)).

Since

\qdet T(u)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot t_{\sigma(1),1}(u)\cdots t_{\sigma(N),N}(u-N+1),

and
\tau_{N}
maps
T(u)\mapsto T^{\prime}(-u)
, we find

\displaystyle\tau_{N}(\qdet T(u))=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot t_{N,N+1-\sigma(1)}(-u)\cdots t_{1,N+1-\sigma(N)}(-u+N-1).

Introducing
\pi(i)=N+1-i
, this rewrites as

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot t_{\pi(1),(\pi\circ\sigma)(1)}(-u)\cdots t_{\pi(N),(\pi\circ\sigma)(N)}(-u+N-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sgn(\pi)\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot t_{\pi(1),\sigma^{\prime}(1)}(-u)\cdots t_{\pi(N),\sigma^{\prime}(N)}(-u+N-1).

Thus,
\tau_{N}(\qdet T(u))=\qdet T(-u+N-1)
, leading to

A_{N}\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)=A_{N}\beta_{N}(u)\qdet T(u)\qdet T(-u+N-1).

Applying this to
e_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{N}
,

\displaystyle\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)e_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\sigma(N)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{N}(u)\qdet T(u)\qdet T(-u+N-1)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sgn(\sigma)e_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\sigma(N)}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)=\beta_{N}(u)\qdet T(u)\qdet T(-u+N-1).

To show
\beta_{N}(u)=\alpha_{N}(u)
, consider first the orthogonal case. With

R(u)=1-\frac{P}{u},\quad P=\sum_{i,j}e_{ij}\otimes e_{ji},

we obtain

R^{\prime}(u)=1-\frac{P^{\prime}}{u},\quad P^{\prime}=\sum_{i,j}e_{ij}\otimes e_{N+1-i,N+1-j}.

By direct computation,
PP^{\prime}=P^{\prime}
, so

(1-P)R^{\prime}(u)=(1-P).

For
i<j
, this implies

A_{N}R_{ij}^{\prime}=A_{N}.

Thus,
\alpha_{N}(u)=1
, agreeing with (
3.13
). In the symplectic case, applying
R^{\prime}_{jk}
to the basis vector

v=e_{1}\otimes e_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{n}\otimes e_{n+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{2n}

yields

\frac{2u-2n+3}{2u-2n+1}A_{2n}v.

Continuing this process leads to

\frac{2u+1}{2u-2n+1}A_{2n}v,

matching (
3.13
) and proving
\beta_{N}(u)=\alpha_{N}(u)
.
∎

As a consequence of this theorem, the coefficients of
\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)
are central, allowing us to define
Z_{HC}(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
as the algebra generated by these coefficients.

Applying
Theorem

A.5
, we immediately obtain

\alpha_{N}(-u+N-1)\cdot\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)=\alpha_{N}(u)\cdot\sdet\mathcal{S}(-u+N-1).

From this, it follows that all the odd coefficients can be expressed in terms of the even ones. Consequently,
Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}))
is generated by the even coefficients. Finally, the fact these coefficients are algebraically independent follows in the same way as the characteristic zero case; see the proof of Theorem 2.8.2 in
[
Mol07
]
.

Next, we construct a series
\widetilde{d}(u)\in 1+u^{-1}Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}))\llbracket u^{-1}\rrbracket
satisfying

\widetilde{d}(u)\widetilde{d}(u-1)\cdots\widetilde{d}(u-N+1)=\qdet T(u)=1+d_{1}u^{-1}+d_{2}u^{-2}+\cdots.

Writing

\widetilde{d}(u)=1+\widetilde{d}_{1}u^{-1}+\widetilde{d}_{2}u^{-2}+\cdots,

expanding the power series reveals that the
u^{-k}
coefficient of
\widetilde{d}(u-m)
takes the form
\widetilde{d}_{k}
plus terms depending only on
\widetilde{d}_{i}
for
i<k
. Thus, the
u^{-k}
coefficient of
\widetilde{d}(u)\widetilde{d}(u-1)\cdots\widetilde{d}(u-N+1)
is
N\widetilde{d}_{k}
plus terms involving only
\widetilde{d}_{i}
for
i<k
. Since
N<p
, we can recursively determine the
d_{i}
starting from
k=1
.

By
Proposition

A.4
, automorphisms of the form (
3.4
) map

\widetilde{d}(u)\mapsto f(u)\widetilde{d}(u).

Finally, we define

\widetilde{t}_{ij}(u)=\widetilde{d}(u)^{-1}t_{ij}(u).

It follows that
\widetilde{t}_{ij}(u)
remains invariant under automorphisms of the form (
3.4
), implying that
\widetilde{t}_{ij}(u)\in Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{N})
.

Now we can finish the proof of
Theorem

3.4
.

First, we establish the decomposition

(A.18)

\displaystyle Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})=Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n}))SY(\mathfrak{g}_{n}).

We regard
Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
as a subalgebra of
Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})
via the embedding (
3.12
). Under this embedding, we have

\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u)=\sum_{k}\theta_{kj}t_{ik}(u)t_{N+1-j,N+1-k}(-u).

Define the series

\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{ij}(u)=(\widetilde{d}(u)\widetilde{d}(-u))^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u).

Since
\widetilde{d}(u)
has central coefficients, it follows that

\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{ij}(u)=\sum_{k}\theta_{kj}\widetilde{t}_{ik}(u)\widetilde{t}_{N+1-j,N+1-k}(-u).

Now, we compute

\displaystyle\alpha_{N}(u)^{-1}\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)=\qdet T(u)\qdet T(-u+N-1)

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{d}(u)\widetilde{d}(-u))(\widetilde{d}(u-1)\widetilde{d}(-u+1))\cdots(\widetilde{d}(u-N+1)\widetilde{d}(-u+N-1)).

From this, it follows that all coefficients of
\widetilde{d}(u)\widetilde{d}(-u)
can be expressed as polynomials in the coefficients of
\sdet\mathcal{S}(u)
, implying that
\widetilde{d}(u)\widetilde{d}(-u)\in Z_{HC}(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
.

Similarly to how
\widetilde{t}_{ij}(u)
belongs to
Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{N})
, we deduce that
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{ij}(u)\in SY(\mathfrak{g}_{n})
. Thus, the decomposition

\mathbf{s}_{ij}(u)=\widetilde{d}(u)\widetilde{d}(-u)\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{ij}(u)

yields the desired result (
A.18
).

Finally, the tensor product decomposition follows from the relation

Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N})=Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N}))\otimes Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{N}),

along with the inclusions
Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{g}_{n}))\subset Z_{HC}(Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{N}))
(which follows from
Theorem

A.5
) and
SY(\mathfrak{g}_{n})\subset Y(\mathfrak{sl}_{N})
(by the same argument as in characteristic zero).

This completes the proof of (
3.4
).
