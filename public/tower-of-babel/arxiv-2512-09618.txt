Preprojective categories of type A A
J. D. Rock, H. Thomas

Abstract
. We introduce a continuous version of preprojective algebras of type A A . In particular, we are interested in the preprojective category over an open, bounded subinterval 𝕀 \mathbb{I} of ℝ \mathbb{R} , denoted Λ 𝕀 \Lambda_{\mathbb{I}} . We study the representable projective modules and define a useful type of sub- and quotient module called decorous modules. These are completely described by a function from the closure 𝕀 ¯ \overline{\mathbb{I}} of 𝕀 \mathbb{I} to ℝ \mathbb{R} whose ‘slopes’ are not too steep anywhere. We later use these to describe permuton ideals, a generalization of the support τ \tau -tilting ideals of preprojective algebras of type A n A_{n} , which we call permutation ideals. Once we have our generalization, we show that permutation ideals can be recovered from permuton ideals. Moreover, permutation ideals are τ \tau -rigid and we show an analogous property for our permuton ideals. Along the way, we classify all the brick Λ 𝕀 \Lambda_{\mathbb{I}} -modules.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Context and goal

There is a generalization of the representation theory of a linearly oriented
A_{n}
quiver which replaces the discrete quiver by an interval
\mathbb{I}
of the real line. (For simplicity, we will restrict our discussion here to the case of open, though not necessarily bounded, intervals.) A representation
M
of
\mathbb{I}
associates a finite-dimensional vector space
M(x)
to each point
x\in\mathbb{I}
. For any
x_{1}<x_{2}
in
\mathbb{I}
, there is an associated map from
M(x_{2})
to
M(x_{1})
; these maps satisfy certain natural compatibility relations. The representation theory of such intervals is now well-understood
[
BCB20
,
Roc19
,
IRT23
]
. Familiar features of the
A_{n}
case reappear in this setting. In particular, the finitely presentable indecomposable representations correspond to subintervals
(a,b]
of
\mathbb{I}
. The representation theory of intervals turns out to be relevant to applications in topological data analysis.
See, for example,
[
Oud15
]
.

In a similar spirit, one might ask about continuous versions of other finite-dimensional algebras. In general, it is not clear how this should be approached.
One approach to this, via thread quivers, is studied in
[
PRY25
]
.
In the present paper, we consider the problem of defining a continuous version of the preprojective algebras of type
A
. Similarly to the case of the continuous linearly oriented type
A
quiver, we start with an open interval
\mathbb{I}
of the real line. A representation
M
associates to each
x\in\mathbb{I}
a finite-dimensional vector space. For
x_{1}<x_{2}
, there is a map from
M(x_{1})
to
M(x_{2})
, and a map from
M(x_{2})
to
M(x_{1})
, subject to certain natural relations which we shall explain shortly.

Since the preprojective algebra of type
A_{n}
is wild for
n\geq 6

[
ES06
]
, it would be unreasonable to hope to give an explicit description of the full module category of the continuous preprojective algebra. Instead, we shall describe an interesting class of modules. Recall that the radical filtation of an indecomposable projective module of type
A_{n}
has the appearance of a (discrete) diamond. For our continuous preprojective algebra, the representable indecomposable projectives look like (continous) diamonds. We will primarily focus our attention their subquotients, which still admit a two-dimensional description. We call these
sheet modules
. We think of them as corresponding to a diamond with a top part and a bottom part removed.

The basic support
\tau
-tilting modules of a preprojective algebra of type
A_{n}
form a collection of two-sided ideals, each indexed by a permutation
w\in S_{n+1}
, the Weyl group of type
A_{n}

[
Miz14
]
. We refer to these as
permutation ideals
.
We consider an analogous construction for the continuous preprojective algebra. The suitable indexing objects turn out to be a generalization of permutations known as
permutons

[
HKM+13
,
GGKK15
]
; we call the corresponding objects
permuton ideals
.

In Section
6
we turn our attention to bricks.
As a consequence of Proposition

We define a

, denoted

(Definitions

Given a sawtooth function

, denoted

Theorem B

(Theorem

.

Let

In Section
7
, we begin generalizing the concept of permutation ideals. As mentioned above, the objects constituting our generalization of permutation ideals are indexed by a permuton. See Section
7
for the definition. There is an injection from permutations in
S_{n+1}
into permutons, for any
n
; we write
\gamma_{w}
for the permuton associated to the permutation
w
.

From a permuton
\mu
, we define a collection of induced functions
(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
, one for each
x\in(0,1)
.
Here we are using
\Lambda_{(0,1)}
as a stand in for the equivalence class of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
’s where
\mathbb{I}
is open and bounded.
In Lemma
7.1
, we show that the functions induced by permutons are precisely the
\partial
functions from Theorem
A
.
The immediate consequence is that each permuton
\mu
defines a decorous submodule
D_{\mu}^{a}
of
P_{a}
, for each
a\in(0,1)
.

Just as the permutation ideals can be described as submodules of
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}
, for a preprojective algebra of type
A_{n}
, our permuton ideals are submodules of
\bigoplus_{x\in(0,1)}P_{x}
in
\Rep(\Lambda_{(0,1)})
.
The permuton ideal is then
I_{\mu}=\bigoplus_{a\in\mathbb{I}}D_{\mu}^{a}
. Consdering
\Lambda_{(0,1)}
as a ring, we show that the ideal is two sided (Proposition
7.3
).

In Section
8
, we show how to recover the (picture of) the radical filtration
I^{i}_{w}
of a permutation ideal
I_{w}
as a summand of the permuton ideal corresponding to
\gamma_{w}
(Theorem
8.1
).
We show there is a clear condition on permutons that determines when one permuton ideal contains another (Proposition
9.1
). We give a natural definition of the
permuton Bruhat order
, and show that
\mu\geq\nu
if and only if
I_{\mu}\subseteq I_{\nu}
as modules.
We also show that, given permutons
\gamma_{u},\gamma_{v}
from permutations
u,v
, respectively, we have
\gamma_{u}\leq\gamma_{v}
in our permuton Bruhat order if and only if
u\leq v
in the Bruhat order on permutations (Theorem
9.2
).

In Section
10
we extend the
\tau
-rigidity of permutation ideals to an analogous property for permuton ideals.
For a permuton
\mu
and
a\in(0,1)
, we have the decorous submodule
D_{\mu}^{a}
of
P_{a}
.
From Proposition
4.4
we know that

, in the short exact sequence

We label this as

.
In the context of preprojective algebras of type

, if

is a submodule of

, we have a short exact sequence

, for each projective
P_{i}
.
Thus, in our context, it is natural to view

as playing the role of
\tau D_{\mu}^{a}
, although we do not actually have an interpretation of Auslander–Reiten translation in our setting.
Our analogue of
\tau
-rigidity for a permuton ideal

is the following.

Theorem C

(Theorem
10.1
)
.

Let
\mu
be a permuton and

.
Then we have
\Hom(D_{\mu}^{a},U_{\mu}^{b})=0

2.2.
Permutation ideals

There is a class of ideals of a preprojective algebra which is in bijection with the elements of the corresponding Weyl group. See
[
Miz14

for the simply-laced Dynkin case, which is the case relevant here. We will describe this correspondence in the case that

In Section

For

Theorem 2.3

(

2.3.
Auslander–Reiten translation

The Auslander–Reiten translation of a module
M
, generally denoted
\tau M
,
is calculated as follows. Take a minimal projective presentation of
M
, say

Q\xrightarrow{g}P\rightarrow M\rightarrow 0.

Then
\tau M
is by definition the kernel of
\nu g
, where
\nu
is the Nakayama functor sending
P_{i}
to the corresponding injective. See
[
ASS06
, Chapter IV]
for more details.

We will need the following lemma:

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
M
be a submodule of the indecomposable projective module
P_{i}
. Then
\tau M\simeq P_{i}/M
.

Proof.

If we draw the piecewise linear curve separating the composition factors in
M
from the composition factors not in
M
, and going from the left corner of the projective to the right corner of the projective (as we have already done to describe
(I_{w})^{i}
), then the terms in the minimal projective presentation can be described as follows. If the function has a local minimum at
x=i/n
, there is a summand
P_{i}
in
P
. If the function has a local maximum at
x=i/n
, then there is a summand
P_{i}
in
Q
.
When we apply
\nu
to
Q\xrightarrow{g}P
, it changes from a projective presentation of
M
to an injective copresentation of
P_{i}/M
, which proves the lemma. ∎

A module is called
\tau
-rigid if
\Hom(M,\tau M)=0
. Mizuno showed the following result (in greater generality):

Theorem 2.8

(
[
Miz14
, Proposition 2.10]
)
.

I_{w}
is
\tau
-rigid.

3.
A preprojective category

In this section we seek to define preprojective categories of type
A
over
\mathbb{R}
and over an interval
\mathbb{I}\subset\mathbb{R}
, and to describe the representable projectives in the case when
\mathbb{I}\subset\mathbb{R}
is of the form
(a,b)
, for
a<b\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.1.
Definition for
\mathbb{R}

In this section we define a preprojective category over the real line.

Let
\mathbb{R}
have the usual total order.
We construct a category
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
in the following way.
We set
\mathrm{Ob}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}})=\mathbb{R}
.
For each
x<y
we have the morphisms
\alpha_{yx}:x\to y
and
\alpha^{*}_{xy}:y\to x
.
(The
\alpha
’s go right and the
\alpha^{*}
’s go left.)
For notational purposes, we identify the morphisms
\alpha_{xx}=\alpha^{*}_{xx}=\boldsymbol{1}_{x}
.
The
\alpha
’s and
\alpha^{*}
’s are subject to the following relations:

(1)

\alpha_{zy}\alpha_{yx}=\alpha_{zx}
and
\alpha^{*}_{xy}\alpha^{*}_{yz}=\alpha^{*}_{xz}
, for
x\leq y\leq z
in
\mathbb{R}
, and

(2)

\alpha^{*}_{x+a-b,x+a}\alpha_{x+a,x}=\alpha_{x+a-b,x-b}\alpha^{*}_{x-b,x}
for
a,b
positive reals.

When we write
\alpha^{(*)}_{xy}
we mean whichever of
\alpha_{xy}
or
\alpha^{*}_{xy}
exists, depending on whether
y\leq x
or
x\leq y
, respectively.

As in the discrete case, we define the length of
\alpha^{(*)}_{xy}
to be
|x-y|
, and we define the length of a composition of these to be the sum of their individual lengths.

By
\Bbbk
-linearizing, for some algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
, we have
\Hom
spaces given by

\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}}(x,y)\cong\Bbbk^{\mathbb{R}_{\geq|x-y|}},

where the
(|x-y|)
th coordinate corresponds to
\alpha^{(*)}_{yx}
.
That is,
\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}}(x,y)
contains all finite sums
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}
where each
\lambda_{i}\in\Bbbk
and each
f_{i}:x\to y
is a composition of
\alpha
and
\alpha^{*}
’s.

Also as in the discrete case, we refer to a non-zero composition of
\alpha
’s and
\alpha^{*}
’s as path like.
(Over
\mathbb{R}
, any composition is non-zero, but later we will consider situations where some compositions are zero.)
We denote by
\len(f)
the length of a path like morphism in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Notice that, when
x=y
, we have
\End(x)\cong\Bbbk^{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
.
This acts like a graded ring because we can use the relations 1 and 2 to obtain

\alpha^{*}_{x,x+b}\alpha_{x+b,x}\circ\alpha^{*}_{x,x+a}\alpha_{x+a,x}=\alpha^{*}_{x,x+a+b}\alpha_{x+a+b,x}.

In fact every path like morphism in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
has a length, or grading, such that the length of
g\circ f
is equal to the sum of lengths of
f
and
g
, for path like
f
and
g
.
For convenience, we consider
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
to have a zero object (an object that is both initial and terminal in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
).

3.2.
Definition for other intervals

In this section we define and examine
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
for an interval
\mathbb{I}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{I}
be an arbitrary subinterval of
\mathbb{R}
.
We define
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
to be the unique
quotient
category of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
obtained by setting
x
isomorphic to the zero object for each
x\notin\mathbb{I}
.

If
\mathbb{I}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}
there is a consequence on
\Hom
spaces.
First note that this implies
\mathbb{I}
has a lower bound or upper bound (possibly both).
If
\mathbb{I}
has one bound, call it
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
, then we let
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x}=|x-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}|
.
If
\mathbb{I}
has both an upper bound and lower bound, say
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
respectively, then we let
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x}=\min\{|x-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}|,|x-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{1}|\}
.
We denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}
the closest endpoint to
x
, whether
\mathbb{I}
has one or two endpoints.
If
|x-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}|=|x-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{1}|
, and exactly one of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
is not in
\mathbb{I}
, we pick
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}
to be the one not in
\mathbb{I}
.
If
|x-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}|=|x-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{1}|
and either
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{1}\in\mathbb{I}
or
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{1}\notin\mathbb{I}
, we pick
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}
.

Set
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{xy}=\min\{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x},\boldsymbol{\ell}_{y}\}
. For a pair
x
and
y
in
\mathbb{I}
, let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}
if
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x}<\boldsymbol{\ell}_{y}
and let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{y}
if
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{y}<\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x}
.
If
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x}=\boldsymbol{\ell}_{y}
then we determine
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}
as follows:

•

If
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}\in\mathbb{I}
but
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{y}\notin\mathbb{I}
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{y}
.

•

If
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{y}\in\mathbb{I}
but
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}\notin\mathbb{I}
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}
.

•

If
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{y}\in\mathbb{I}
or
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{y}\notin\mathbb{I}
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}=\min\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{y}\}
.

Notice
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{yx}
,
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{xy}=\boldsymbol{\ell}_{yx}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xx}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}
, and
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{xx}=\ell_{x}
.

We can now describe
\Hom
spaces in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
:

\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(x,y)\cong\begin{cases}\Bbbk^{[\,|x-y|,\,|x-y|+\boldsymbol{\ell}_{xy}\,]}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}\in\mathbb{I}\\
\Bbbk^{[\,|x-y|,\,|x-y|+\boldsymbol{\ell}_{xy}\,)}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{xy}\notin\mathbb{I}.\end{cases}

This also gives us
\End_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(x)\cong\Bbbk^{[\,0,\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x}\,]}
or
\End_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(x)\cong\Bbbk^{[\,0,\boldsymbol{\ell}_{x}\,)}
, depending on if
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}\in\mathbb{I}
or
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}\notin\mathbb{I}
, respectively.
Since
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}}=0
, notice that, for
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0},y\in\mathbb{I}
, we have
\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0},y)\cong\Bbbk
.
This is similarly true for
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
, or
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
if
\mathbb{I}
has just one bound.

Intuitively, one should think of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}
being infinitesimally closer to a point
x\in\mathbb{I}
if
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{x}\notin\mathbb{I}
than if
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\in\mathbb{I}
, although doing this technically would only complicate things.

There is an interesting situation that can happen with
\mathbb{I}
that cannot happen with finite-dimensional preprojective algebras.
Typically, for a vertex
i
at one end of the
A_{n}
diagram, there is a unique path in the preprojective path algebra from
i
to any other vertex
j
.
In our context, we say that we have a one-dimensional
\Hom
space, like with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}
above.
However, it is possible that
\mathbb{I}
is a bounded open subinterval of
\mathbb{R}
.
This yields nontrivial graded endomorphism rings everywhere and always multi-dimensional
\Hom
spaces.

In our setting, we actually want
\mathbb{I}
to be open since the point(s) on its boundary do not significantly contribute to
\Hom
spaces or endomorphism rings.
Moreover, when we look at permutons in Section
7
, we will see that taking
\mathbb{I}
to be open removes some technical considerations.

Proposition 3.2
.

Up to equivalence, there are three preprojective categories
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
for an open
\mathbb{I}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
:

(1)

\mathbb{I}
is bounded. That is,
\mathbb{I}=(a,b)
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

\mathbb{I}
is bounded on one side but not the other. That is,
\mathbb{I}=(-\infty,b)
or
\mathbb{I}=(a,+\infty)
, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

(3)

\mathbb{I}=\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

First we show that for any open, bounded
\mathbb{I}
and
\mathbb{J}
, the categories
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
and
\Lambda_{\mathbb{J}}
are equivalent.
For any pair of finite intervals
\mathbb{I}
and
\mathbb{J}
there is a linear, order preserving homeomorphism
\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{J}
as intervals that extends to a functor between the corresponding categories.
Let
r
be the length of
\mathbb{J}
divided by the length of
\mathbb{I}
.
Then a path like morphism
f
in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
with length
a
is sent to a path like morphism in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{J}}
with length
ra
.
This gives us an equivalence of categories.

If
\mathbb{I}
and
\mathbb{J}
are both bounded above or both bounded below, say by
a
and
b
, respectively, then we may simply translate
\mathbb{I}
to
\mathbb{J}
as intervals.
In particular, we send
x
in
\mathbb{I}
to
(b-a)+x
in
\mathbb{J}
.
This gives us an equivalence of categories between
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
and
\Lambda_{\mathbb{J}}
.
If
\mathbb{I}
is bounded below, then
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
is equivalent to
\Lambda_{(0,+\infty)}
.
Then, multiplying by
-1
, we see
\Lambda_{(0,+\infty)}
is equivalent to
\Lambda_{(-\infty,0)}
.
Thus, for all half bounded
\mathbb{I}
and
\mathbb{J}
, the categories
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
and
\Lambda_{\mathbb{J}}
are equivalent.

Now we show the three cases are distinct.
We can immediately see that
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
is distinct from the bounded and half-bounded cases because there are no path like morphisms in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
that compose to 0.
To see the bounded and half bounded classes are mutually exclusive, let
0<x<z
in
\mathbb{I}=(0,+\infty)
.
Choose any
y\in\mathbb{I}
strictly between
x
and
z
.
Then
\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(x,z)\to\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(y,z)
, given by
f\mapsto f\circ\alpha^{*}_{xy}
, is always a strict inclusion.
However, if we have the bounded interval
\mathbb{J}=(0,1)
and choose
x=\frac{1}{2}
, there is no pair
z
and
y
such that
\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{J}}}(x,z)\to\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{J}}}(y,z)
is a strict inclusion by any
\alpha^{(*)}
.
One may use the grading to quickly check that this is true.
If there is no inclusion with an
\alpha^{(*)}
then there can be no inclusion by a path like morphism or a finite sum of path like morphisms.
∎

So, from now on, we assume
\mathbb{I}=(0,1)
when
\mathbb{I}
is open and bounded.

Remark 3.3
.

For any bounded open interval
\mathbb{I}=(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
, we see that
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
is canonically isomorphic to
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}^{\text{op}}
by sending each object
x\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
to the object
x\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}^{\text{op}}
.
On morphisms we have
\alpha_{yx}\mapsto(\alpha^{*}_{xy})^{\text{op}}
and
\alpha^{*}_{xy}\mapsto(\alpha_{yx})^{\text{op}}
, for
x<y
in
\mathbb{I}
.

3.3.
Representable projectives

This section begins the study of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
-modules, for some bounded and open
\mathbb{I}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}
, starting with representable projectives.
In particular, we consider our modules to be functors from
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
to
\Bbbk\text{-}\mathrm{vec}
.
In later sections we will consider right
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
-modules even though in earlier sections we consider functors written as left modules.
Because of the canonical isomorphism
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}\cong\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}^{\text{op}}
(Remark
3.3
) this discrepancy does not actually cause any issues.

We relate representable projective
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
-modules to objects in the bounded derived category
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A_{\mathbb{I}})
, where
A_{\mathbb{I}}
is a continuous quiver of type
A
, indexed over
\mathbb{I}
instead of
\mathbb{R}
, with finitely-many sinks and sources.
See
[
IRT23
]
for the definition and foundational theorems about representations of continuous quivers of type
A
.

A
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
M
is a submodule of
P_{x}=\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(x,-)
.
If
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}\in M(y)\subseteq\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(x,y)
, for each
\lambda_{i}\in\Bbbk
and each
f_{i}
path like, then
f_{i}\in M(y)
for each
1\leq i\leq m
.
Moreover, if
f\in\Hom_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(x,y)
is path like with length
\ell
and there is some
g\in M(y)
with length less than
\ell
, then
f\in M(y)
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction of
m
, the number of terms appearing in the sum, with
m=1
as the trivial base case. While, logically, the
m=2
case can be treated as a special case of the general
m
analysis, we write it out explicitly, as a warm-up.
Suppose, then, that
\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\lambda_{2}f_{2}\in M(y)
, where
f_{1},f_{2}
are path like and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\Bbbk
.

There must be a path like endomorphism
\lambda_{3}f_{3}
in
\End_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(y)
of length
\len(f_{2})-\len(f_{1})
, or else
f_{2}=0
.
Then
\lambda_{2}f_{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}f_{3}f_{2}\in M(y)
.
So, we must have

\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\lambda_{2}f_{2}-\lambda_{2}f_{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}f_{3}f_{2}=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}f_{3}f_{2}\in M(y).

Set
\lambda^{\prime}_{2}=-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}
,
f^{\prime}_{2}=f_{3}f_{2}
.

Then we start again with
\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\lambda^{\prime}_{2}f^{\prime}_{2}
except
\len(f^{\prime}_{2})-\len(f_{1})=2(\len(f_{2})-\len(f_{1}))
.
In finitely-many steps, we obtain
\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}_{2}f^{\prime\prime}_{2}
where
2(\len(f^{\prime\prime}_{2})-\len(f_{1}))>\boldsymbol{\ell}_{xy}-\ell_{1}
.
In this situation, we multiply by
\lambda^{\prime\prime}_{3}f^{\prime\prime}_{3}\in\End_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(y)
whose length is
\len(f^{\prime\prime}_{2})-\len(f_{1})
.
We obtain simply
\lambda^{\prime\prime}_{2}f^{\prime\prime}_{2}
.
Then
\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}_{2}f^{\prime\prime}_{2}-\lambda^{\prime\prime}_{2}f^{\prime\prime}_{2}=\lambda_{1}f_{1}\in M(y)
.
Therefore
f_{1}\in M(y)
and
f_{2}=\lambda_{2}^{-1}(\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\lambda_{2}f_{2}-\lambda_{1}f_{2})\in M(y)
.

We now give the general argument for
m\geq 2
. Let us write
F
for
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}
.
The strategy is the same: by subtracting from
F
a multiple of
F
by an endomorphism of
y
, we can cancel off the next-to-lowest length term in
F
, at the expense of introducing some higher-length terms.
We seek to do this repeatedly, until the length of the next-to-lowest length term would be greater than
\ell_{xy}
, which would allow us to conclude that there is no next-to-lowest length term, and
\lambda_{1}f_{1}\in M(y)
.
At this point, we know that
F-\lambda_{1}f_{1}\in M(y)
, and we can proceed by induction.

The inconvenience here, compared to the
m=2
case, is that we cannot initially determine how much we will increase the length of the next-to-lowest length term at each step, and in principle, there could be an infinite sequence of steps yielding smaller and smaller increases.
It is possible this sequence would never get us to the point where the next-to-lowest length would be greater than
\ell_{xy}
.
We therefore need a lower bound on the increase at each step.

Let
R
be the additive semigroup generated by
\{\len(f_{i})-\len(f_{1})\}_{2\leq i\leq m}
.
Clearly, all the lengths appearing in
F
lie in
\len(f_{1})+R
.
We claim that this remains true at each step of the process, because we always want to cancel off some length which appears (and which, by assumption, is of the form
\len(f_{1})+n
with
n\in R
).
We therefore subtract off
gF
, where
g\in\End_{\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}}(y)
is of length
n
.
Thus, a term in
gF
of length
\len(f_{1})+n^{\prime}
, with
n^{\prime}\in R
, contributes a term of length
\len(f_{1})+n^{\prime}+n
to the new sum.

Now observe that there are only finitely many elements of
R
which are less than
\ell_{xy}-\len(f_{1})
.
Therefore, there is some minimal difference between them, say
\delta
.
At each step in the process, we increase the length of the next-to-lowest length term of the sum by at least
\delta
.
Therefore, after a finite number of steps, we will achieve a sum equal to
\lambda_{1}f_{1}
, and we can proceed by induction as explained above.

Finally, since
M
is a submodule of
P_{x}
, if
f\in M(y)
is path like with length
\ell
then we must have all path like
f^{\prime}\in M(y)
where the length of
f^{\prime}
is greater than
\ell
and
f^{\prime}\in P_{x}(y)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

and
\lub\mathbb{I}

Now suppose
\partial
is some function as described in the lemma.
For each

In the following proposition, we use that

Proposition 4.6

Let

Proof.

First let

In particular,

and
\partial^{\prime}(1)=1-x

By Proposition

of

∎

means the choice for
\phi(f_{y^{\prime}})
is independent.
What’s happening is that
\mathsf{C}_{y,a}=\mathsf{C}_{z,a}
in the first case but
\mathsf{C}_{y,a^{\prime}}\cap\mathsf{C}_{z,a^{\prime}}=\emptyset
in the second case.

We introduce the following definition to check for situations similar to this.

Definition 5.9
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}
be sheets in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
and let
\mathsf{C}_{y,a}
be as before.
The
range of codependence of
\mathsf{C}_{y,a}
is the set of all
b\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq a}
such that, for all
z,w\in\mathsf{C}_{y,a}
, if
\mathsf{C}_{z,b}
and
\mathsf{C}_{w,b}
are nonempty then
\mathsf{C}_{z,b}=\mathsf{C}_{w,b}
.

Notice that the range of codependence of
\mathsf{C}_{y,a}
always includes
a
.

Using this definition we see that, in the situation from Figure
8
,
b
is outside the range of codependence of
\mathsf{C}_{y,a}
.

So, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 5.10
.

Assume the hypotheses from Lemma
5.8
.
Let
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}
be a sum of path like morphisms in
S^{\prime}(y)
such that, for each
1\leq i\leq m
, the length of
f_{i}
is
\partial_{\uparrow}(y)+b<\partial_{\downarrow}^{\prime}(y)
, where
b
is in the range of codependence of
\mathsf{C}_{y,a}
.
Then there is a morphism
\phi:S\to S^{\prime}
determined by
\phi(f_{y})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}
.

Proof.

For each
b
in the range of codependence of
\mathsf{C}_{y,a}
and each
z,w\in\mathsf{C}_{y,a}
, we know that
\mathsf{C}_{z,b}=\mathsf{C}_{w,b}
if they are both nonempty, by definition.

For each
f_{i}
, let
b_{i}=\len(f_{i})-\partial_{\uparrow}(y)
.
Thus, for each
b_{i}
and each
f_{i}
, we can use Lemma
5.8
to construct a morphism
\phi_{i}:S\to S^{\prime}
determined on
C_{b}(y)
by
\phi_{i}(f_{y})=f_{i}
.
Then the morphism
\phi
in the present proposition is given by
\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\phi_{i}
.
∎

Lemma
5.8
and Proposition
5.10
give rise to the following definitions.

Definition 5.11
.

We introduce two definitions together.

(1)

A morhism
\phi:S\to S^{\prime}
in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
, where
S,S^{\prime}
are sheets, is called
elementary
if it is of the form in Proposition
5.10
.

(2)

Let
\{(y_{i},a_{i})\}\subset\mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{R}
be a possibly infinite collection.
Assume that if
i\neq j
then
B_{a_{i}}(y_{i})\cap B_{a_{j}}(y_{j})=\emptyset
.
Choose an elementary morphism
\phi_{i}
for each
(y_{i},a_{i})
.
Then the morphism
\phi=\sum_{i}\phi_{i}
is called
multi-elementary
.

To the knowledge of the authors, it does not seem possible to construct a morphism between sheets in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
that is not a sum of multi-elementary morphisms.
This leads us to the following conjecture.

Conjecture
.

Any morphism
\phi:S\to S^{\prime}
in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
, where
S,S^{\prime}
are sheets, is a finite sum of multi-elementary morphisms.

6.
Bricks

In this section we classify the bricks in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
and relate them to a result in
[
Asa22
]
(see Theorem
6.9
in the present paper).
We continue to consider modules as functors
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}\to\Bbbk\text{-}\mathrm{Vec}
, which coincides with left modules, but these are equivalent to right modules since
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
is canonically isomorphic to
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}^{\text{op}}
(Remark
3.3
).

We first need to define deep modules.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
M
be a module in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
.
We say
M
is
deep
if there is a path like
g:x\to x
in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
, with
x\in\supp M
, such that
M(g)\neq 0
.

We also need a special type of endomorphism called a push up endomorphism.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
For any module
M
in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
, the
push up endomorphism
\psi_{\ell}\in\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
of length
\ell
is the endomorphism where
(\psi_{\ell})_{x}=M(g)
where
g:x\to x
is path like with length
\ell
if such a path like morphism exists in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
and
g=0
otherwise.

It is straightforward to check that
\psi_{\ell}
is a morphism in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
for any module
M
, but is possibly the
0
morphism.

Remark 6.3
.

We make the following remarks about push up morphisms.

•

There is a pushup morphism
\psi_{\ell}\in\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
with
\ell>0
if and only if
M
is deep.

•

Let
\psi_{\ell}
be a push up morphism in
\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
for some
M
in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
.
It follows form the definition that
(\psi_{\ell})^{n}=\psi_{n\ell}
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In particular, the endomorphism
\psi_{0}:M\to M
is the identity morphism.

Recall that a (left or right) module
M
over a ring
R
is called a
brick
if
\End(M)
, in the category of (left or right)
R
-modules, is a division ring.
Thus, we say a representation
M
of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
is a
brick
if
\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
is a division ring.

Proposition 6.4
.

If
M
in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
is deep then
M
is not a brick.

Proof.

Since
M
is deep there exists a nonzero
\psi_{\ell}\in\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
such that
\ell>0
.
Then there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
such that
n\ell>1
and so
(\psi_{\ell})^{n}=\psi_{n\ell}=0
.
Thus,
\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
is not a division ring and so
M
is not a brick.
∎

In particular, Proposition
6.4
says that none of the following modules can be bricks: submodules of indecomposable projectives, decorous quotient modules of indecomposable projectives, and sheet modules.

Now that we know what kinds of modules are not bricks, we introduce sawtooth functions.
These will help us classify bricks.

Definition 6.5
.

Let
\partial
be a function as in Corollary
4.7
and
[a,b]\subset[0,1]
a closed subinterval.
We say
\partial|_{[a,b]}
is a
sawtooth function
if there is some subinterval
I
of
\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
and set
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subset[0,1]
satisfying the following:

(1)

We have
\min I\neq\max I\in I
.

(2)

If
i<j\in I
then
x_{i}<x_{j}
in
[a,b]
.

(3)

We have
x_{\min I}=a
and
x_{\max I}=b
.

(4)

For each
2i,2i+1\in I
, we have
x_{2i+1}-x_{2i}=\partial(x_{2i})-\partial(x_{2i+1})
.

(5)

For each
2i,2i-1\in I
, we have
x_{2i}-x_{2i-1}=\partial(x_{2i})-\partial(x_{2i-1})
.

Notice the lack of absolute values in the last two items!

See examples of sawtooth function in Figure
9
.

Some examples of sawtooth functions from Definition
6.5
. The right most example has an accumulation of the sawtooth waves, which is allowed.

We can use sawtooth functions to define a nice class of modules.

Definition 6.6
.

Let
M
be in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
.
We say
M
is a
sawtooth module
if there exists a sawtooth function
\partial|_{[a,b]}
such that

•

the closure of
\supp M
in
[0,1]
is
[a,b]
,

•

a\in\supp M\Leftrightarrow a=x_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

•

b\in\supp M\Leftrightarrow b=x_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

•

if
M(f)\neq 0
for some path like
f:x\to y
in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
, then

f=\begin{cases}\alpha_{yx}&\text{if }x_{2i-1}\leq x\leq y\leq x_{2i}\\
\alpha^{*}_{yx}&\text{if }x_{2i}\leq y\leq x\leq x_{2i+1}.\end{cases}

We will show that the bricks in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
are certain kinds of sawtooth modules.
To do this, we first must prove the following lemma.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
M
be an indecomposable in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
such that
\supp M
is not a singleton and
M
is not deep.
Then
M
is a sawtooth module.

Proof.

Choose some
x\in\supp M
such that
x\notin\{\inf\supp M,\lub\supp M\}
.
Consider the following sets:

\text{(i)}\ \{y\in[x,1)\mid M(\alpha_{yx})\neq 0\}\qquad\qquad\text{(ii)}\ \{y\in[x,1)\mid M(\alpha^{*}_{xy})\neq 0\}.

By assumption on push up functions in
\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
, exactly one of these sets must be
\{x\}
and the other must contain more than just
x
.
The technique we will use is symmetric to either case.
So, for simplicity of writing, we assume the set (i) contains more than just
x
and (ii) is
\{x\}
.
Set
A_{x}
equal to set (i).

If
\lub A_{x}\notin A_{x}
then we see
\lub A_{x}=\lub\supp M\notin\supp M
, or else
M
is not indecomposable.
Let
y_{0}=\lub A_{x}
.
Suppose
x_{0}\in A_{x}
and
x_{0}\lneq\lub\supp M
.
Then we define
A_{x_{0}}=\{y\in[x_{0},1)\mid M(\alpha^{*}_{x_{0}y}\neq 0\}
, similar to set (ii) above.
Again, either
\lub A_{x_{0}}\in A_{x_{0}}
or
\lub A_{x_{0}}=\lub\supp M
.

We proceed inductively where the
A_{x_{2i}}
’s are defined in style (ii) and the
A_{x_{2i+1}}
’s are defined in style (i).
For each
x_{i}
, the other type of set must be a singleton, as before.
If there is a largest
i
in this process, then
\lub\supp M=x_{i}
for some
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
If, for each
i\geq 0
in
\mathbb{Z}
there is an
x_{i+1}
, then the
x_{i}
’s must accumulate to
\lub\supp M
, or else
M
is not indecomposable.
Moreover, in this case,
\lub\supp M\notin\supp M
for the same reason so we set
x_{+\infty}=\lub\supp M
.

We now construct the sets
A^{x},A^{x_{-1}},A^{x_{-2}},\ldots
where

\displaystyle A^{x}

\displaystyle=\{y\in(0,x]\mid M(\alpha_{xy})\neq 0\}

\displaystyle A^{x_{2i-1}}

\displaystyle=\{y\in(0,x_{2i-1}]\mid M(\alpha^{*}_{yx_{2i-1}})\neq 0\}

\displaystyle A^{x_{2i}}

\displaystyle=\{y\in(0,x_{-2i}]\mid M(\alpha_{x_{-2i}y})\neq 0\}.

Again, either this process terminates so
\inf\supp M=x_{i}
for some
i\in\mathbb{Z}
or
\lim_{i\to-\infty}x_{i}=\inf\supp M
and
\inf\supp M\notin\supp M
.
In the later case,
x_{-\infty}=\inf\supp M
.

We now have enough to define a sawtooth function.
Let
I\subset\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
be the set such that
i\in I
if
x_{i}
is defined above.
Define
\partial(x_{0})=0
.
Inductively, for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
i\neq 0
, define

\partial(x_{i})=\begin{cases}\partial(x_{i+1})+x_{i}-x_{i+1}&i<0,i\text{ even}\\
\partial(x_{i+1})+x_{i+1}-x_{i}&i<0,i\text{ odd}\\
\partial(x_{i-1})+x_{i-1}-x_{i}&i>0,i\text{ even}\\
\partial(x_{i-1})+x_{i}-x_{i-1}&i>0,i\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

If
x_{\pm}
has been defined, set

\partial(x_{\pm\infty})=\lim_{i\to\pm\infty}\partial(x_{i}),

where the
\pm
’s must match.
For points
x
such that
x_{i}<x<x_{i+1}
, we define
\partial(x)
to be the point such that
|x-x_{i}|=|\partial(x)-\partial(x_{i})|
and
|x-x_{i+1}|=|\partial(x)-\partial(x_{i+1})|
.
Denote by
\overline{\supp M}
the smallest closed subinterval of
[0,1]
containing
\supp M
.
For
x\in[0,1]
such that
x\notin\overline{\supp M}
, define

\partial(x)=\begin{cases}\partial(\inf\supp M)&x<\inf\supp M\\
\partial(\lub\supp M)&x>\lub\supp M.\end{cases}

It is straightforward to check that
\partial
is a function as in Corollary
4.7
and that
\partial|_{\overline{\supp M}}
is a sawtooth function.

We have shown

•

[a,b]
is the closure of
\supp M
in
[0,1]
,

•

for each
y<z
in
[x_{2i},x_{2i+1}]
,
M(\alpha_{zy})=0
, and

•

for each
y<z
in
[x_{2i-1},x_{2i}]
,
M(\alpha^{*}_{yz})=0
.

Therefore,
M
is a sawtooth module with sawtooth function
\partial|_{\overline{\supp M}}
.
∎

An immediate consequence of the lemma is that any brick must be a sawtooth module.
But we can do better.

Let
\partial|_{[a,b]}
be a sawtooth function.

For an interval
[a,b]\subset(0,1)
, define the map
\zeta_{M}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
by

\zeta(x)=\begin{cases}\tan((x-\frac{a+b}{2})\cdot\frac{\pi}{b-a})&x\leq\frac{a+b}{2}\text{ and }(a=x_{-\infty}\text{ or }a=0)\\
\tan((x-\frac{a+b}{2})\cdot\frac{\pi}{b-a})&x\geq\frac{a+b}{2}\text{ and }(b=x_{+\infty}\text{ or }b=1)\\
(x-\frac{a+b}{2})\cdot\frac{1}{b-a}&x\leq\frac{a+b}{2}\text{ and }0<a=x_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}\\
(x-\frac{a+b}{2})\cdot\frac{1}{b-a}&x\geq\frac{a+b}{2}\text{ and }1>b=x_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}\end{cases}

We extend
\zeta
so that we have a bijection in the following way.
First,

\displaystyle\hat{a}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a&a=x_{-\infty}\\
0&a=x_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\hat{b}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}b&b=x_{+\infty}\\
1&b=x_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}.\end{cases}

Then we define
\hat{\zeta}:(\hat{a},\hat{b})\to\mathbb{R}
by

\hat{\zeta}(x)=\begin{cases}\zeta(x)&x\in[a,b]\\
\tan((x-a)\cdot\frac{\pi}{a-\hat{a}})-1&\hat{a}<x<a\\
\tan((x-b)\cdot\frac{\pi}{\hat{b}-b})+1&b<x<\hat{b}.\end{cases}

If
\hat{a}=a
then the second line in the definition is not used.
Similarly, if
\hat{b}=b
then the third line in the definition is not used.
If
\hat{a}<a
then
\zeta(a)=-1
and
\lim_{x\to a^{-}}\hat{\zeta}(x)=-1
.
The similarly statement for
b<\hat{b}
is also true.
Thus,
\hat{\zeta}
is a continuous strictly order preserving bijection from
(\hat{a},\hat{b})
to
\mathbb{R}
.

A continuous quiver
Q
of type
A
is essentially a pair
(S,\preceq)
where
S\subset R
is a discrete set (possibly infinite) and
\preceq
is a new partial order on
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
s,s^{\prime}\in S
such that
\not\exists s^{\prime\prime}\in S
such that
s<s^{\prime\prime}<s^{\prime}
.
Then the order
\preceq
on the set
[s,s^{\prime}]\subset\mathbb{R}
must be either the same as
\leq_{\mathbb{R}}
or the opposite.
We think of
Q
as a category where the objects are
\mathbb{R}
and there is a unique morphism
g_{yx}:x\to y
in
Q
if
y\preceq x
.
A representation
M
of
Q
is a functor
Q\to\Bbbk\text{-}\mathrm{Vec}
.
It is pointwise finite-dimensional if
M(x)
is a finite-dimensional vector space for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
By
[
IRT23
, Theorem 2.3.2]
, and independently
[
BCB20
, Corollary 5.9]
, all of the indecomposable representations of
Q
are interval represenations in the same way the indecomposable representations of type
A_{n}
are interval representations.
By
[
IRT23
, Theorem 3.0.1]
these are also all bricks, just like the
A_{n}
case.
See
[
IRT23
]
for more details and the introduction to the study of all representations of continuous quivers of type
A
.

Let
Q
be a continuous quiver of type
\mathbb{A}
whose sinks are
\{\zeta(x_{2i})\}
and whose sources are
\{\zeta(x_{2i+1})\}
.

Then we define a functor
Z:Q\to\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
by

\displaystyle Z(x)

\displaystyle=\hat{\zeta}^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle Z(g_{yx}:x\to y)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{(*)}_{\hat{\zeta}^{-1}(y),\hat{\zeta}^{-1}(x)}.

It is quick to check that
Z
is a functor.
Then we have the induced functor
Z^{*}:\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})\to\mathrm{Rep}(Q)
on modules given by
M\mapsto M\circ Z
on objects.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
M
be a sawtooth module with function
\partial|_{[a,b]}
and induced functions
\zeta,\hat{\zeta}
.
Let
Q
be the corresponding continuous quiver of type
\mathbb{A}
with functors
Z,Z^{*}
.
Then
\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)\cong\End_{\Rep(Q)}(Z^{*}M)
.

Proof.

First, note that if
a=\inf\supp M\in\supp M
then
a=x_{i}
for some
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and so
\zeta(\inf\supp M)=-1
.
The similar statement is true if
b=\lub\supp M\in\supp M
.
Thus,
\supp M
is in the domain of
\zeta
.

Let
\phi\in\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
.
Suppose
Z^{*}\phi=0
.
Then, since
\supp Z^{*}M=\zeta(\supp M)
we see
\phi=0
.

Now suppose
\bar{\phi}\in\End_{\Rep(Q)}(Z^{*}M)
and
\bar{\phi}\neq 0
.
Then define
\phi:M\to M
by
\phi_{x}=\bar{\phi}_{\zeta(x)}
.
If
x_{2i}\leq y\leq x\leq x_{2i+1}
with
x,y\in\supp M
then
M(\alpha^{*}_{yx})\neq 0
and
Z^{*}M(g_{\zeta(y)\zeta(x)})=M(\alpha^{*}_{yx})
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\phi_{y}M(\alpha^{*}_{yx})

\displaystyle=\bar{\phi}_{\zeta(y)}M(g_{\zeta(y)\zeta(x)})

\displaystyle=M(g_{\zeta(y)\zeta(x)})\bar{\phi}_{\zeta(x)}

\displaystyle=M(\alpha^{*}_{yx})\phi_{x}.

Similarly, if
x_{2i-1}\leq x\leq y\leq x_{2i}
with
x,y\in\supp M
then
\phi_{y}M(\alpha_{yx})=M(\alpha_{yx})\phi_{x}
.

For all other path like
f
in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
, we know
M(f)=0
and so trivially
\phi_{y}M(f)=0=M(f)\phi_{x}
.
Thus,
\phi\in\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)
and, by definition,
Z^{*}\phi=\bar{\phi}
.
Therefore,
\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)\cong\End_{\Rep(Q)}(Z^{*}M)
.
∎

Notice that the bijection in Proposition
6.8
is induced by
Z^{*}
.

The relation of our brick modules to the result just after Theorem 0.4 in
[
Asa22
]
comes from the following theorem.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
M
in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
be a module.
Then
M
is a brick if and only
M
is simple or
M
is a sawtooth module and the module
Z^{*}M
is also a brick in
\Rep(Q)
, where
Q
and
Z^{*}
are induced from the sawtooth function
\partial|_{[a,b]}
given by
M
.

Proof.

The converse direction is trivial for simple modules and trivial for other modules by Proposition
6.8
.

Now suppose
M
is a brick.
If
M
is simple we are done, so suppose not.
Since
M
is a brick,
M
is indecomposable and
\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)\cong\Bbbk
.
By Proposition
6.4
we know
M
is not deep.
Then, by Lemma
6.7
we see
M
is a sawtooth module.
So we have the functor
Z^{*}:\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})\to\Rep(Q)
from before.
Then, by Proposition
6.8
we see that
\End_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(M)\cong\End_{\Rep(Q)}(Z^{*}M)\cong\Bbbk
.
∎

Finally, we relate bricks back to decorous submodules of representable projectives.

Proposition 6.10
.

Let
M
be a brick in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
with sawtooth function
\partial|_{[a,b]}
.
Assume either
x_{\min I}>0
or
\min I
is not odd and assume either
x_{\max I}<1
or
\max I
is not odd.
Then there is a representable projective
P_{z}=\Hom_{\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})}(z,-)
and decorous submodule
\widehat{M}\subset P_{z}
such that
M
is a quotient of
\widehat{M}
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{M}
be the decorous submodule of
P_{z}
corresponding to
\partial
, by Corollary
4.7
.
If
\min I
is odd, then
x_{\min I}>0
and so
x_{\min I}\in\supp\widehat{M}
if
x_{\min I}>0
.
Similarly,
x_{\max I}\in\supp\widehat{M}
if
x_{\max I}<1
.
Thus,
\supp M\subset\supp\widehat{M}
.

We now define
\phi:\widehat{M}\to M
.
For each
x\in(0,1)\setminus\supp M
, set
\phi_{x}=0
.
For
x\in\supp M
, define
\phi_{x}(f_{y})=1\in\Bbbk=M(x)
and define
\phi_{x}(f)=0
for all other path like elements in
M(x)
.
By the Definition
6.6
, we know that
M(f)=0
if
f
is not one of
\alpha_{yx}
for
x_{2i-1}\leq x\leq y\leq x_{2i}
or
\alpha^{*}_{yx}
for
x_{2i}\leq y\leq x\leq x_{2i+1}
.
In these cases,

\phi_{y}\widehat{M}(\alpha^{(*)}_{yx})(f_{x})=\phi_{y}(f_{y})=1=M(\alpha^{(*)}_{yx})(1)=M(\alpha^{(*)}_{yx})\phi_{x}(f_{x}).

For all other path like
h:x\to y
in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
, we know
\widehat{M}(h)(f_{x})\neq f_{y}
, by construction.
Thus,
\phi_{y}\widehat{M}(h)(f_{x})=0=M(h)\phi_{x}(f_{x})
.
Therefore,
\phi:\widehat{M}\to M
is a morphism in
\mathrm{Rep}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}})
and we see that it is also surjective.
∎

If
\min I
is odd and
x_{\min I}=0
then the proposition fails.
This is because
(x_{\min I},x_{\min I+1})\cap\supp\widehat{M}=\emptyset
but
(x_{\min I},x_{\min I+1})\subset\supp M
.
Similarly, the proposition fails if
x_{\max I}=1
and
\max I
is odd.

It is possible to “discretize” the methods in this section to preprojective algebras of type

and recover the result just after Theorem 0.4 in
[
Asa22
]
.

7.
Permutons and ideals in the continuous preprojective algebra

Given the result of Mizuno recalled earlier as Theorem
2.3
, it is natural to wonder if there is a kind of “continuous permutation” which defines an ideal in the continuous preprojective algebra. It turns out that the correct notion of continuous permutation is not the naive one (permutations of the points in
(0,1)
), but rather the notion of
permuton
, which was introduced in order to study the limit of a sequence of permutations of increasing size
[
HKM+13
]
.

We follow the slightly later
[
GGKK15
]
(which was also the first paper to use the term “permuton”) in defining a permuton as a measure
\mu
on the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets of
[0,1]\times[0,1]
such that

\mu([0,k]\times[0,1])=k=\mu([0,1]\times[0,k])
.

A permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
defines a permuton
\gamma_{w}
as follows. Divide the unit square up into
n
equal rows and
n
equal columns, and then put a uniform measure of weight
1/n
on each of the squares
(i,w(i))
. General permutons arise as limits in a suitable sense of those coming from permutations.

Here is the permuton for the permutation
w=25341
. (We maintain our convention that
y
increases downwards.) Each grey square has total measure
1/5
.

We will now show how to associate to an arbitrary permuton
\mu
an ideal in the continuous preprojective algebra on
(0,1)
.

A function
f
from
(0,1)
to
\mathbb{R}
satisfying that if
0<x<y<1
, then
|f(y)-f(x)|\leq y-x
is necessarily continuous, so it has well-defined values at 0 and 1, which we refer to as
f(0)
and
f(1)
. For
k\in(0,1)
, define a set of functions
\mathcal{B}_{k}
to be those functions satisfying the previous property, such that as well
f(0)=k
and
f(1)=1-k
.

We now consider right modules, which are equivalent to functors
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}\to\Bbbk\text{-}\mathrm{Vec}
(left modules) since
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}
is canonically isomorphic to
\Lambda_{\mathbb{I}}^{\text{op}}
(Remark
3.3
).
In particular, the results from Sections
3.3
and
4

Lemma 7.1
.

The functions defining a decorous submodule of
P_{k}
are exactly the functions in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

Proof.

Use Corollary
4.7
. ∎

For

, let

be the submodule of
P_{k}
defined by

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\mu
be a permuton. For fixed

belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{y}

Proof.

For
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
, we have

\mu([0,x_{1}]\times[0,y])-\mu([0,x_{2}]\times[0,y])=\mu((x_{1},x_{2}]\times[0,y)

0\leq\mu((x_{1},x_{2}]\times[0,y)\leq\mu((x_{1},x_{2}]\times[0,1])=x_{2}-x_{1}.

The remainder of the proof is completely straightforward. ∎

Thus, we can define the permuton ideal:

It is a right ideal of the continuous preprojective algebra, since it is a submodule of the continuous preprojective algebra viewed as a (right) module over itself. In fact, analogously to the discrete case, we have the following:

Proposition 7.3
.

For
\mu
a permuton,
I_{\mu}
is a two-sided ideal.

Proof.

We need to check that an element of
I_{\mu}

. It suffices to check for
a\in I_{\mu}^{q}
and

that
\alpha^{*}_{pq}(a)\in I_{\mu}^{p}
and
\alpha_{rq}(a)\in I_{\mu}^{r}
. We only consider the case of
\alpha^{*}_{pq}(a)
; the other is the same. The map induced by
\alpha^{*}_{pq}
from
P_{q}
to

sends the top of
P_{q}
to the top point of
P_{p}
in the

Thereby,
\alpha^{*}_{pq}

We must check

\displaystyle f_{\mu,q}(x)+(q-p)

\displaystyle=-2\mu([0,x]\times[0,q])+(q-x)+(q-p)

\displaystyle=-2\mu([0,x]\times[0,p]+(p-x)+2(q-p)-2\mu([0,x]\times(p,q])

8.
Permuton ideals recover permutation ideals

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 8.1
.

For
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, the summand of the permuton ideal
I_{\gamma_{w}}^{i/n}
coincides with the picture of the radical filtration of
I_{w}^{i}
defined in Section
2.2
.

Before we prove the theorem, we will require some notation and a lemma.

Write
\mathfrak{S}_{\langle i\rangle}
for the subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
generated by the adjacent transpositions other than
s_{i}
. It is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{S}_{i}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n-i}
. For
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, if we consider the coset
\mathfrak{S}_{\langle i\rangle}w
, it has a unique element of minimal length
[
BB05
, Corollary 2.4.5]
. Call it

. It follows that

can be factored as
w=w_{\langle i\rangle}w^{\langle i\rangle}
, with
w_{\langle i\rangle}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\langle i\rangle}
and
\ell(w)=\ell(w_{\langle i\rangle})+\ell(w^{\langle i\rangle})

[
BB05
, Proposition 2.4.4]
. (We note that if we were more closely following the notation of
[
BB05
]
we would write
{}^{\langle i\rangle}w
.)

Example 8.2
.

Consider
w=25341
. The factorization as

is obtained by starting from

and attempting to move adjacent transpositions in

If

, we obtain the factorization:

If

, we obtain the factorization:

The minimal-length coset representatives of

in fact admit a simple description:

(
[

, Exercise 2.4]
)
.

The minimal-length coset representative

of

is given by rearranging the elements of

so they are in increasing order (while occupying the same positions) and similarly for

.

Example 8.4
.

Continuing Example

\displaystyle w^{\langle 2\rangle}

\displaystyle=(23)(34)(45)=13452

\displaystyle w^{\langle 3\rangle}

\displaystyle=(34)(23)(45)=14253.

Lemma 8.5

(I_{w})^{i}=(I_{w^{\langle i\rangle}})^{i}
.

Proof.

Because

which consists of a reduced expression for

using this reduced expression for

has no effect on

of

and

are the same. The result follows. ∎

Example 8.6
.

and

.

The advantage of being able to replace

Lemma 8.7
.

If

is a minimal length coset representative with respect to

, then

has a reduced expression

u=(s_{i}s_{i+1}\dots s_{u^{-1}(i)-1})(s_{i-1}\dots s_{u^{-1}(i-1)-1})\dots(s_{1}\dots s_{u^{-1}(1)-1}).

Proof.

Note that, by Lemma

u^{-1}(1)<\dots<u^{-1}(i)

Now, if we let

be the permutation given by the reduced expression in the sstatement of the lemma, it is easy to check that

for

Further, since the number of pairs

Proof of Theorem
8.1
.

Clearly, it is sufficient to show that the piecewise-linear curve that defines the top of the picture of the radical filtration of

agrees with the piecewise-linear curve that defines

,
and applying Lemma

9.
Inclusion order on permuton ideals

In this section we consider the inclusion orders on permutation ideals and permuton ideals.

Let
u,v\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

[
ORT15
, Lemma 6.5]
says that
I_{u}\leq I_{v}
if and only if
u\geq v
in Bruhat order on permutations (one of whose equivalent definitions we shall shortly recall).

On the other hand, the following proposition is easy to see:

Proposition 9.1
.

I_{\mu}\subseteq I_{\nu}
if and only if
\mu([0,a]\times[0,b])\leq\nu([0,a]\times[0,b])
for all
0<a,b<1
.

By analogy, if the equivalent conditions of the proposition hold, we say that

\mu\geq\nu
in “permuton Bruhat order.”

The permuton Bruhat order in fact recovers Bruhat order on permutations. The main result of this section is the following theorem.

Theorem 9.2
.

For
u,v\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we have that
\gamma_{u}\leq\gamma_{v}
with respect to the permuton Bruhat order if and only if
u\leq v
with respect to Bruhat order.

One approach to proving Theorem
9.2
would be via Theorem
8.1
. For
u,v
two permutations, if
\gamma_{u}\geq\gamma_{v}
, then
D_{\gamma_{u}}\subseteq D_{\gamma_{v}}
, and from Theorem
8.1
, it follows that
I_{u}\subseteq I_{v}
, so
u\geq v
by
[
ORT15
, Lemma 6.5]
, immediately establishing one direction of the theorem. Nonetheless, we give a direct proof, because it is straightforward and seems potentially of independent interest.

There are several equivalent definitions of Bruhat order. Some of them refer to reduced expressions and/or covering relations, concepts which are not readily applicable to permutons.

To give the definition which we shall use, we need one piece of notation.
For
u\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, define
u[i,j]=\{a\in\{1,\dots,i\}\mid u(a)>j\}
.
Now,
[
BB05
, Theorem 2.1.5]
says that
u\leq v
in Bruhat order if and only if
u[i,j]\leq v[i,j]
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
.

We can now prove Theorem
9.2
.

Proof of Theorem
9.2
.

If
\gamma_{u}\leq\gamma_{v}
, then clearly
u\leq v
, since
u[i,j]=\gamma_{u}([0,i/n]\times[j/n,1])
.

For the converse direction, suppose
u\leq v
, and let
a,b\in(0,1)
. We need to check that

\gamma_{u}([0,a]\times[b,1])\leq\gamma_{v}([0,a]\times[b,1])
. Let
a=i/n+\delta
,
b=j/n-\epsilon
, with
\delta,\epsilon<1/n
. Then

\displaystyle\gamma_{u}([0,a]\times[b,1])=

\displaystyle(1-\delta)(1-\epsilon)u[i,j]+\delta(1-\epsilon)u[i+1,j]

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-\delta)\epsilon u[i,j-1]+\delta\epsilon u[i+1,j-1].

Since the same formula holds with
u
replaced by
v
, the desired inequality follows.
∎

10.
Permuton ideals satisfy an analogue of
\tau
-rigidity

Our goal in this section is to prove an analogue of Mizuno’s
\tau
-rigidity result (Theorem
2.8
) in the continuous setting. In the setting of modules over the continuous preprojective algebra, the Auslander–Reiten translation is not well-defined, so the definition of
\tau
-rigidity cannot be directly applied. By analogy with the discrete case, in which Lemma
2.7
says that if
M
is a submodule of
P_{i}
then
\tau M\simeq P_{i}/M
, in the continuous setting we will treat as a definition of
\tau
for a module contained in an indecomposable projective
P_{i}
, that
\tau M\simeq P_{i}/M
.

Let
\mu
be a permuton. Write
D_{\mu}^{x}
for the summand of the submodule of
P_{x}
in the ideal corresponding to
\mu
. Write
U_{\mu}^{x}=P_{x}/D_{\mu}^{x}
.
We will show the following analogue of Mizuno’s
\tau
-rigidity result:

Theorem 10.1
.

For
a,b\in(0,1)
, we have
\Hom(D_{\mu}^{a},U_{\mu}^{b})=0
.

This will follow from the following more general proposition.

Proposition 10.2
.

Let

. Suppose further that

is either weakly decreasing or weakly increasing. Then
\Hom(D_{f},U_{g})=0
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider only the weakly decreasing case; the weakly increasing case follows by swapping the roles of left and right. So suppose that

is weakly decreasing.

Suppose there is some non-zero morphism
\phi
from

to

be the largest of them.

\epsilon=f(x_{0})-g(x_{0})

x=0
(in which case we define

to

All the points on
\ell_{1}

(x_{0},y_{0})

\phi

x=0

(x_{2},y_{2})

\ell_{2}

(x_{1},y_{1})

Points in
D_{f}
strictly above

Since

Near

Let
