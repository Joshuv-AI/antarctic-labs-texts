Design of experiments and bioanalytical LC–MS/MS method for analysis of melatonin and tasimelteon in rat brain homogenate with whiteness assessment
Authors: Serkan Levent, Abeer Elriş, Hazal Avcı, Saniye Özcan, Nafiz Öncü Can
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sleep disorders include a range of common problems that affect the quality of sleep at night and, as a result, impact an individual’s daily functioning. Treatment protocols vary from over-the-counter products to regulated pharmaceuticals. Melatonin and Tasimelteon are two compounds utilized for severe to moderate sleeping disorders. This study developed and validated a sensitive, simple bioanalytical LC–MS/MS method for the measurement of Melatonin and Tasimelteon in spiked rat brain tissue. Chromatographic analyses were conducted in isocratic mode, with Citalopram selected as an appropriate internal standard. The Supelco Ascentis® Express Phenyl-Hexyl column was used for the stationary phase, and the mobile phase comprised 0.2% formic acid in a mixture of acetonitrile and water (65:35, v/v). A response surface methodology is applied. The Box-Behnken design was used to optimize the influence of three independent factors (acetonitrile%, formic acid%, and flow rate (mL/min)) on the response. The study focused on finding the most significant factors influencing chromatographic separation, namely the resolution between Tasimelteon and Melatonin, as well as the tailing factors of both. Statistical analysis of variance provided the optimal conditions for separating the substances as well as the most influential factors. Validation of the analytical method was conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization guideline M10 related to bioanalytical method validation. The method validated was precise and linear in 55.00–1650 (ng/mL) and 20–600 (ng/mL) for the Melatonin and Tasimelteon, respectively. The validated method’s lower limit of quantification values was 55 and 20 ng/mL for Melatonin and Tasimelteon, respectively. For Melatonin, intraday accuracy (recovery, %) ranged from 96.53% to 102.68%, and precision (expressed as relative standard deviation) ranged from 0.26% to 0.96%. And inter-day accuracy ranged from 96.58% to 103.08%, and inter-day precision ranged from 0.33% to 3.55%. Intraday accuracy results for Tasimelteon 99.61%-103. 75% precision results were in the range 0.23%–0.93%; additionally, inter-day accuracy was 99.37–103.87%, and the precision range was 1.04–2.11%. The total run time was 3 min, with retention time for Melatonin and Tasimelteon at 1.9 and 2.5 min, respectively, achieving effective chromatographic separation under optimum conditions. The Red Green Blue 12 score for whiteness was determined to be 79.2%.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01808-y.

Introduction

Sleep–wake states and other physiological processes in the body demonstrate circadian rhythms. Circadian rhythm sleep disorders result from discrepancies between the body’s intrinsic timing mechanism and external environmental factors. Symptoms involve diminished quality of life, excessive sleepiness, and sleeplessness [1]. Melatonin (MEL) is a hormone produced by the pineal gland that regulates the circadian rhythm. MEL receptors are mainly categorized into ML1 (high-affinity) and ML2 (low-affinity) receptors. ML1 receptors are crucial for the neurological system and comprise two subtypes, MT1 and MT2, which are known to be connected to the regulation of the circadian rhythm. Besides its impact on the circadian rhythm, MEL possesses sleep-inducing characteristics. MEL supplements are widely used in Europe and the United States to relieve sleep latency disorders in both children and adults [2, 3]. Tasimelteon (TAS) is a compound that received Food and Drug Administration approval in 2014 for the treatment of non-24-h sleep–wake disorder. It is a structurally unique chemical and a MEL receptor agonist with significant affinity for both MT1 and MT2 receptors in humans. TAS facilitates sleep by regulating the sleep–wake cycle via MT2 receptors, thereby reducing phase-shifting and circadian rhythm disturbances, and by decreasing wake-promoting effects through MT1 receptors [4, 5].

Recently, chemometric tools have been widely utilised for method optimisation. Chemometric methods develop mathematical equations that evaluate the statistical significance of the factors under investigation and their interaction effects. Particularly Box-Behnken Designs (BBD) are commonly preferred to optimise analytical methods efficiently by evaluating multiple variables and their interactions simultaneously. The determination of optimised conditions is carried out by calculating the desirability function (D), which usually gives values between 0 and 1 for each response affected by the factors with minimal experimental runs, overcoming the limitations of traditional one-variable-at-a-time (OVAT) approaches [6, 7].

A few chromatographic methods for TAS exist in the literature. One of them is a stability-indicating study utilizing LC–MS/MS and LC-DAD previously reported by our research team. This work proposed a method utilizing two different detectors for TAS, and a degradation product was identified using high-resolution mass spectrometry [8]. Another analytical method developed by Yadlapalli et al., documented in the literature, was established for the quantification of TAS in plasma utilizing the LC–MS/MS methodology [9]. There are more documented methods for MEL in the literature than for TAS. These include HPLC–PDA methods [10–13], mass spectrometry methods [14–17], capillary electrophoresis methods (CE) [18–20], and thin-layer chromatography (TLC) methods [21]. While these methods are useful for single analyte determinations in relatively simple matrices, these existing methods generally lack the ability for simultaneous quantification and matrix effect reduction required for complex samples. HPLC–PDA and TLC methods may be disadvantageous for trace quantification in lipid-rich tissues, while current LC–MS/MS and CE methods offer better analytical sensitivity, but are generally developed for single analyte determinations in relatively simpler matrices such as plasma, saliva, or pharmaceutical formulations. Furthermore, most methods are developed with univariate optimization that does not address the complex interactions between chromatographic parameters; this leads to relatively long analysis times, excessive organic solvent consumption, and inappropriate peak resolutions. Most importantly, none of the methods currently available in the literature address the simultaneous quantification of MEL and TAS in the target tissue of the molecules, the brain.

While MEL is marketed as an easily available dietary supplement in many countries, TAS is a prescription drug. Both components must absolutely meet quality standards. Individuals who need supplements often believe that since they do not obtain these products with a prescription under a doctor's supervision, they have no adverse effects or contain natural ingredients. However, these supplements can have physiological effects, interact with medications, and are often subject to mislabeling issues and incorrect dosages, leading to serious consequences [22]. These over-the-counter products were not subject to approval before being released to the market in terms of purity, labeling, safety, and efficacy, according to the Dietary Supplements Health and Education Act of 1994. Regulations introduced by authorities aim to eliminate these errors by tightening requirements for the supplement market; therefore, there is a demand for special and sensitive analytical methods to monitor and control the fate of active ingredients in both dietary supplement formulations and biological tissues [23, 24]. For this purpose, various analytical methods have been developed for commonly used components in the dietary supplement market [25–27]. The proposed LC–MS/MS technique is also critical for the precise quantification of MEL and TAS compounds in complex biological matrices, ensuring both therapeutic efficacy and compliance with regulatory authorities, as well as elucidating potential problems that may arise in biological systems from the possible combined use of the two agents. Since both MEL and TAS target the same receptors (MT1 and MT2) in the central nervous system (CNS) to regulate circadian rhythms. Neuropharmacological monitoring of both molecules and understanding their potential interactions is critical to creating treatment regimens. Simultaneous analysis is crucial for these two compounds due to clarifying receptor competition, synergistic interactions, or the effect of TAS use on endogenous MEL levels at the target tissue (brain) level. It is also necessary for interpreting clinical and pharmacokinetic studies.

TAS ((1R-trans)-N-[[2-[2,3-dihydro-4-benzofuran]cyclopropyl]methyl]propanamide), known by its chemical name, possesses two chiral centers. The chemical formula is C15H19NO2, the molecular weight is 245.32, and the logP value is roughly 2.2 [4, 28] MEL, also N-[2-(5-methoxy-1H-indol-3-yl)ethyl]acetamide, is a compound with the chemical formula C13H16N2O2, a molecular weight of 232.28 g/mol, and a logP value of roughly 0.8 [29]. Brain tissue is a complex matrix with an important amount of lipids and proteins. Lipophilic compounds (MEL and TAS) show a strong affinity for these endogenous structures, which leads to significant matrix effects and ion suppression in mass spectrometry detectors in the brain matrix. Moreover, the simultaneous and rapid separation of two analytes (MEL and TAS) presents severe difficulties. OVAT optimization techniques can produce poor results in complex matrices, concluding in inefficiency and non-reliable optimization of many analyte separations with numerous parameters. Design of Experiment (DoE) assesses the interactions between chromatographic parameters and their second-order relationships, facilitating the achievement of optimal separation conditions in a short period of time. In this regard, optimization of the method was conducted via a chemometric methodology, employing BBD to determine the optimum conditions. Despite that crucial pharmacological effect, a comprehensive literature review reveals a significant analytical gap regarding their simultaneous evaluation of cerebral tissue. To the best of our knowledge, no chemometrically optimized LC–MS/MS method has been reported to date for the concurrent determination of MEL and TAS in rat brain matrix. Thus, this study aims to fill this crucial gap by establishing a rapid, simple, and DoE optimized bioanalytical technique for the simultaneous quantification of TAS and MEL in rat brain tissue, utilizing citalopram (CT) as the internal standard (IS). Furthermore, this work presents as a detailed case study for the practical application of the current International Council for Harmonization guideline M10 (ICH M10).

Finally, since achieving the required high sensitivity in bioanalytical procedures often compromises their environmental friendliness and practicality, the developed method was comprehensively evaluated using the novel Red Green Blue 12 (RGB-12) whiteness metric. This allowed for a holistic assessment of the method's analytical performance, practical applicability, and environmental impact, proving its suitability for routine clinical and pharmacological applications [30].

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

TAS reference standard (99.9%) purity was purchased from TRC Company (Cat. No TRC-T007740, Canada). The standard of MEL (95%) are purchased from TRC Company (Cat. No TRC-M215000, Canada), CT (> 98%), TRC Company (Cat. No: TRC-C505000, Canada) and Rat (Sprague–Dawley) brain was purchased from Amerigo Scientific (Cat. No: ATAO517838LIF USA). Formic acid (FA) (ACS grade) was purchased from Scharlau (Cat. No: AC10852500 Barcelona, Spain), and acetonitrile (ACN) from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Ultrapure water, with a resistance of > 18.0 Ω/cm, was employed.

Instrumentation

The mass spectrometric analyses were conducted using LCMS-8040 tandem mass spectrometer instrument connected to a Nexera XR Series LC (Shimadzu, Japan), which comprised a DGU-20A3R degasser, LC-20AD gradient pump, SIL-20AC autosampler, CBM-20A communications bus module, CTO-10ASVP column oven.

The LC system is controlled by LCSolutions 1.11 SP1 software. The mass spectrometer utilized electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive mode for multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The optimal mass spectrometry settings were as follows: drying gas (nitrogen) flow rate, 15 L/min; collision gas: argon; nebulizing gas (nitrogen) flow rate, 3.0 L/min; heat block temperature, 450 °C; curved desolvation line temperature, 250 °C.

Chromatographic conditions

Analyses were performed in isocratic mode. The mobile phase consisted of 0.2% (v/v) FA in ACN and 0.2% (v/v) FA in water (65:35, v/v). The flow rate was 0.55 mL/min. The injection volume of samples was 0.5 μL. A Supelco Ascentis® Express Phenyl-Hexyl column (10 cm × 4.6 mm, 2.7 µm particles) was used as the stationary phase. In the acidic mobile phase (0.2% FA), the basic tertiary amine group of CT undergoes protonation in ESI positive mode, producing a stable response similar to MEL and TAS. Chromatographically, CT possesses a fluorophenyl aromatic group that effectively enables interactions with the phenyl-hexyl stationary phase similarly to analytes. Furthermore, CT elution time was shorter than analytes, and effective separation was achieved without the IS component prolonging total analysis time. Consequently CT was selected as the internal standard according to its ionisation and chromatographic properties.

Preparation of standard solutions and brain sample

Tissues previously stored at − 80 °C were thawed at room temperature and weighed precisely. Deionized water was added to each brain tissue sample (1:4, w/v), and the samples were homogenized [31]. The protein precipitation process was carried out using the protocol suggested by Polson et al. (2003) using Methanol (MeOH) at a 1:2 ratio of sample to solvent [32]. This ratio is chosen to reduce sample dilution. Briefly, brain homogenate (3 mL), and MeOH (6 mL) was added to the homogenate, the mixture was vortexed, and then centrifuged at 9000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered via 0.22 µm polyvinylidene fluoride filter and collected in a 10 mL volumetric flask, and the volume was completed to 10 mL with MeOH. The stock solutions of MEL (1000 μg/mL) and TAS (1000 μg/mL) were prepared by dissolving the MEL and TAS standards in ethanol and ACN, respectively. All solutions were vortexed in order to ensure complete dissolution. All further dilutions were prepared by using stock solutions of MEL, TAS, and CT. All solutions are kept at − 20 °C until required.

CT was kept at 50 ng/mL all working and calibration solution. The concentrations of the quality control (QC) solutions used in the validation (lower limit of quantification (LLOQ), low quality control (LQC), medium quaility control (MQC), high quality control (HQC) were 55, 165, 880, 1320 ng/mL for MEL and 20, 60, 320 and 480 ng/mL for TAS, respectively.

Initial experiments were performed to identify the critical parameters of the experimental design. This experiment examined the stationary phase (C8, C18, Phenyl-Hexyl), the organic modifier in the mobile phase (ACN and MeOH), the aqueous mobile components (ammonium formate, ammonium acetate, FA, and acetic acid), and the flow rate. The preliminary results indicate that three components of BBD at three levels were chosen to enhance the separation with optimal resolution. Method validation

Validation of the bioanalytical method in accordance with guidelines is essential for the measurement of an analyte in clinical trials. The analytical method has been thoroughly validated in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) M10 guideline for bioanalytical procedures. The studied parameters are as follows: selectivity, calibration range, LLOQ, accuracy, precision, matrix effect, carry-over, dilution integrity, reinjection reproducibility and stability. Validation and working solutions were developed by spiking standard solutions into a blank brain matrix. Selectivity was assessed by analyzing empty brain matrix using the developed method to determine whether there was interference corresponding to the MEL, TAS, and IS retention time. Calibration standards and quality controls were prepared from different stock solutions during the study period. Calibration sets were conducted over a period of three days. During the validation procedure, accuracy and precision were assessed by assessing five replicates of four QC concentration levels: LLOQ, LQC, MQC, and HQC, all of which were within the calibration curve range.The concentrations of the QC solutions were as follows: at the LLOQ, LQC within three times the LLOQ, MQC around 30–50% of the calibration curve range, and HQC at a minimum of 75% of the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ). Accuracy and precision were evaluated intraday and interday. The matrix effect was calculated by preparing of the solution at LQC and HQC levels in an empty brain medium, as well as by preparing LQC and HQC solutions in a solvent medium, and then comparing the areas. A carry over effect was conducted on the analytical instrument to assess whether residues from a previous analysis remained on the instrument. During validation, carryover was assessed by analyzing blank samples after the calibration standard at the ULOQ. In compliance with ICH M10, the carry over in the blank sample was expressed as a percentage of the analyte response at the LLOQ, and as a percentage of the IS response for the internal standard.

To verify dilution integrity, it was diluted using the blank matrix solution utilized for diluting quality controls. To guarantee sufficient accuracy and precision, six duplicates were conducted for dilution factor individual execution. To ensure sufficient precision, the dilution factor needed to result in ± 15% of the nominal concentration. The stability of analytes was evaluated on QC solutions, freeze–thaw short-term and long-term. Reinjection reproducibility was evaluated by determining whether the same samples could be reanalyzed with high accuracy and precision after storage of processed samples in case of interruptions in analysis for any reason (device failures, etc.). Reinjection reproducibility was conducted by evaluating 5 replicate analyses of QCs after storage conditions.

Results

Optimization

The preliminary studies aimed to identify the optimal column and internal standard. So initially the logP and pKa values were examined for TAS and MEL as reported in the literature. The logP and pKa values of MEL are 0.8 and 16.51 [33]. respectively, while TAS has a logP of 2.2. and pKa 15.74 value [34]. Both compounds demonstrate slight apolarity. CT’s pKa value 9.8 and logP value was 3.2, indicating a relatively more apolar structure and providing separation efficiency for the analytes [35]. Accordingly, C8 (10 cm × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm) and C18 (10 cm × 3.0 mm, 2.7 μm) and phenyl-hexyl (10 cm × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm) were tested. The C8 and C18 columns were found to be insufficient for analyte separation; also, the peak morphologies were excessively broad, indicating inadequate interaction among the analytes and column and the theoretical plate number (N) fell below the acceptable range. Due to the presence of π bonds in the chemical structures of TAS and CT molecules, including the aromatic benzene ring and MEL indole ring, the phenyl-hexyl column provided sharper peaks and enhanced separation efficiency by facilitating better interaction with the π electrons of the aromatic ring structures.

Mass-spectrometric conditions

The initial MS analysis of CT, MEL, and TAS was performed in full-scan mode to identify the precursor ions. The most prominent peaks in the chromatogram corresponded to the [M + H] + at 325.90 m/z [CT + H] + , the [M + H] + at 233.10 m/z [MEL + H] + , and the [M + H] + at 246.20 m/z [TAS + H] + . Thereafter, the product ions were selected by adjusting the collision energy to -25, -35, and -45 V in the MS2, resulting from the fragmentation of precursor ions at 325.90, 233.10, and 246.20 m/z. The optimal collision frequency occurred at 20 V. The most prominent product ions were 109.10 m/z for CT, 130.10 m/z for MEL, and 173.10 m/z for TAS; thus, these values were employed for qualification and quantification. The formation mechanisms of product ions were considered when determining the MRM transitions for TAS, CT, and MEL compounds. Proposed precursor ions and their product ions were represented in Fig. 1. For TAS, the protonated molecular ion observed at m/z 246 was cleaved by heterolytic cleavage of the bond between the amide nitrogen and the carbon adjacent to the cyclopropane-containing side chain, resulting in the formation of a stable product ion with m/z 173. In CT, the protonated precursor ion at m/z 325.9 primarily exhibits charge-directed cleavage in bonds adjacent to the tertiary amine center where protonation occurs. The cleavage of the C–O bond in the benzofuran structure, followed by heterolytic cleavage of the bond between the benzis carbon and the first carbon of the aliphatic side chain, results in the formation of a resonance-stabilized fluorophenyl-containing aromatic cation with m/z 109.05. In MEL, the protonated molecule observed at m/z 233 results from the breaking of the bond between the first and second carbons in the side chain and the simultaneous separation of the methoxy chain from the ring, forming an indole-based product ion at m/z 130. In all three compounds, the selected product ions were preferred in MRM analysis because they are structure-specific and analytically selective fragments that can be explained by heterolytic bond breaking, charge retention, and resonance stabilization provided by aromatic/conjugated systems. Box-Bhenken design

Three critical parameters, ACN (%), flow rate (mL/min), and FA (%), were chosen for optimization by BBD as the most important factors for separation. The BBD was established for 15 analyses utilizing diverse combinations across three levels (− 1, 0, + 1). The optimum chromatographic conditions were chosen based on the best separation of MEL and TAS and the shortest total analysis time. The maximum D value was anticipated to be close to 1. To clarify the effects of the factors on the chosen responses, which are the Rs and Tf of MEL and TAS, experimental results were analyzed with ANOVA.

A statistical approach was implemented for choosing a quadratic model, Rs and Tf MEL, and a linear model, Tf TAS. Following the collection of experimental data as suggested by DoE, model selection was performed using the “Fit Summary Assessment” obtained from the DoE software. At this point, the aim was to choose a mathematical equation that more adequately represents the experimental data. The equation that provides the ideal values for sequential p-value, lack of fit p-value, adjusted R2, and predicted R2 was preferred. These equations were established as a quadratic model Rs and Tf MEL and a linear model Tf TAS. ANOVA results also supported the selection of the model (Tables 4S–6S). For the Tf TAS, solely linear coefficients were statistically significant, and existing second-order coefficients could be seen for Rs and Tf MEL. Adding more terms to the linear model.

After selecting an adequate model, the significance of the models was assessed in detail using F-values and p-values. The selection of the most adequate mathematical models, a quadratic model for MEL's Rs and Tf and a linear model for TAS's Tf, was confirmed by the ANOVA in Tables 4S–6S and presented in Table 1. The established models demonstrated a suitable signal-to-noise ratio, high statistical significance (p < 0.05) with high and appropriate F-values, and sufficiently adequate sensitivity values (> 4). Moreover, lack of fit was absent for all models (p > 0.05), and the estimated R2values were in appropriate relation with the adjusted R2 (difference < 0.2). To exclude overfitting and improve model reliability, nonsignificant terms were systematically not included in the models. In addition, the competence of the established models rigorously met all statistical acceptance criteria of the Design-Expert software in terms of the standard errors and confidence intervals (CI) for the model coefficients.

Conclusion

This study outlined the development and full validation of a bioanalytical LC–MS/MS method for the simultaneous quantification of MEL and TAS in rat brain homogenate by taking into account a detailed evaluation of the proposed method to compare the literature via RGB12 tools. This work addresses a crucial analytical gap and also supports clinical and pharmacokinetic studies by offering the first method capable of evaluating both MT1/MT2 receptor-targeting compounds concurrently directly within their target CNS tissue. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently a lack of published analytical methods to support it. clinical studies of two compounds targeting the same receptor. Besides, as far as we know, the suggested method is the first that targets TAS (targeted analyte species) to quantify the CNS as its target region. By employing a BBD for multivariate optimisation, the second-order interactions between critical chromatographic parameters were thoroughly elucidated, facilitating the rapid and effective simultaneous separation of the analytes. The proposed method for the quantification of MEL and TAS utilises a MeOH-based protein precipitation technique, and it can be easily implemented in all laboratories without the requirement of labour-intensive workflows such as solid-phase extraction or liquid–liquid extraction and so can be applied to the reliable examination of the transport of analytes into brain tissue, the interactions between analytes, and the impact of TAS on MEL levels.

The method also demonstrated appropriate analytical performance in full compliance with ICH M10 bioanalytical guidelines, displaying good sensitivity with LLOQ values of 55 ng/mL and 20 ng/mL for MEL and TAS, respectively, alongside a highly efficient run time of 3.0 min. Furthermore, an overall evaluation using the RGB-12 metric presented a high whiteness score of 79.2%, proving that the method serves an optimal balance among analytical performance, practical applicability, and sustainability. Finally, this validated method establishes a robust and essential analytical framework to clarify and support future clinical, pharmacokinetic, and neuropharmacological studies investigating the interactions and receptor dynamics of MEL and TAS.
