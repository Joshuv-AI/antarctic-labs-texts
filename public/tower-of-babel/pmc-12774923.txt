Driving knowledge assessment in cognitively impaired older adults: evidence from geriatric day hospitals
Authors: Clémentine Rivière, Karl Götze, Sophie Lacaille, Claire Paquet, Julien Dumurgier, Capucine Diard, Marie Laure Seux, Charlotte Tomeo, Antoine Lynch, Sandrine Greffard, Agathe Raynaud Simon, Théodore Decaix, Matthieu Lilamand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence and appropriate tools are often lacking to support the decision of driving discontinuation in cognitively impaired older adults by Alzheimer’s disease and related disorders. Maintaining strong driving knowledge as individuals age is crucial for ensuring their fitness to drive. The objectives of this study were to describe the driving knowledge of older drivers > 65 referred for cognitive assessment compared to control subjects. We also analyzed the relationship between neuropsychological tests and driving knowledge assessment.MethodsCross-sectional, observational, multicenter study in geriatric day hospitals including older drivers with cognitive complaints who underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment. Their performance on a driving theory test (knowledge of driving laws and road rules) and self-evaluation of driving abilities were assessed via a computer-based exam and compared to those of healthy younger drivers. Regression models were used to examine the relationship between driving knowledge and the neuropsychological examination scores with adjustment for age, gender and cognitive performance.ResultsWe included 144 patients (mean age 79.6 ± 4.9) and 249 controls (mean age 28.1 ± 6.6). Performance in the driving theory test was significantly lower in patients than in controls (p < 0.001). We showed a significative association between driving knowledge, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) independent from age, gender and cognitive performance.ConclusionOur study has raised concerns regarding the overall poor theoretical driving skills in older drivers and their low self-evaluation ability. The MoCA and the DSTT may be useful for guiding driving discontinuation in cognitively impaired older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03223-0.Keywords: Driving skills, Older drivers, Cognitive disorders, Alzheimer’s disease

Introduction

Driving is crucial for maintaining the independence of older adults, supporting both daily commuting and long-distance travel needs [1]. Also driving cessation was strongly associated with an increased risk of functional limitations [2]. However, many age-related conditions increase the risk of driving impairment in this population. Studies show that crash rates increase with age due to older driver responsibility, and the oldest drivers, similar to the youngest, exhibit the highest rate of fatal crashes per kilometer driven when compared to middle-aged drivers [3, 4]. Although crash rates among elderly drivers are lower than those of younger drivers when expressed per inhabitant, they are significantly higher when expressed per kilometer driven [5].

Normal brain aging is associated with declines in working memory, processing speed, episodic memory, and executive functions, even in the absence of pathological processes [6]. However, the consequences of brain aging are heterogeneous: some older adults can continue to engage in complex activities, such as driving, under suitable conditions [7]. Age is also a well-known risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and related disorders such as vascular cognitive impairment, which can impair driving abilities and limit individuals’ awareness of their driving limitations [8, 9]. As a result, many patients with neurocognitive diseases would be unable to pass driving theory tests. However, various factors can mitigate the link between a neurocognitive condition and the ability to continue driving, including the type of pathology, disease stage, specific cognitive symptoms, multimorbidity, and self-awareness of the disorder. A recent debate in France highlighted the complexity of establishing a definitive link between AD and driving unfitness [10].

The challenge of assessing driving capacity in older adults with cognitive impairments is compounded by the lack of reliable clinical indicators. The French Memory Centers Federation has emphasized the limited evidence supporting specific assessment tools. While the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) test has garnered some consensus, determining a clear cut-off score at which driving discontinuation should be recommended remains unclear [11, 12].

Although passing the driving theory test is a legal prerequisite for obtaining a license, such tests are not routinely administered to older adults in clinical practice. In contrast, cognitive assessment should be systematically performed in geriatric evaluations. Identifying cognitive domains that are associated with theoretical driving performance may therefore help clinicians to detect older drivers who are more likely to experience driving difficulties, even before an incident occurs. This could guide targeted referrals for specific driving assessments (theory and on-road), rather than relying solely on reported accidents or subjective concerns, which often occur too late. While the scientific literature on the validity of the driving theory test is scarce, and one study in young adults reported that executive functions correlated with practical road test success but not with theory test success [13], the theory test remains a de facto gold standard, as it is a mandatory requirement for licensing. Exploring its associations with cognitive performance in older adults therefore has both conceptual and practical relevance.

The main objective of this study was to assess the driving theory test performance of drivers aged 65 and older with cognitive complaints and to compare their results with cognitively intact younger drivers. Additionally, we explored the associations between driving knowledge assessment, cognitive subtest scores, the stage of cognitive impairment, and self-assessment abilities.

Methods

Design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional, observational, multicenter study in six geriatric day hospitals (i.e., outpatient care facilities for older adults) in the Paris area, France. Geriatric day hospitals provide multidisciplinary assessment and care for older adults with complex medical, cognitive, or functional issues while allowing them to remain in their home environment. Patients referred to these facilities often present with age-related cognitive decline, mobility limitations, or chronic conditions requiring coordinated medical and rehabilitative care. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of assessing driving abilities in this population, as driving cessation can impact mobility, independence, and overall quality of life [14].

We prospectively enrolled patients aged over 65, considered active drivers, who underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment based on their geriatrician’s recommendation in the last 12 months and had a good understanding of French, between 2021 and 2023. Active drivers were defined as those who had driven at least once in the last two years and had no plans to stop driving permanently.

Exclusion criteria were patients under legal protection and inability to undergo a driving theory test, that is, (i) major neurocognitive disorder with a Mini Mental Status Examination (MMSE) score < 15/30 (ii), delirium, or (iii) major depressive disorder.

In addition to the patient group, a control group was constituted with active drivers who were under 65 years of age. These individuals were holding a valid driver’s license and driving independently for daily activities such as commuting, running errands, or social engagements. They were recruited as relatives or acquaintances of the hospital staff involved in the study and were sent an information note describing the aims of the study.

Procedure

Patients were not informed about the study protocol prior to their hospital visit to prevent any potential study preparation. All active drivers were consecutively recruited from the day hospitals: patients who agreed to participate were seated in front of a computer, with the help of a caregiver, in a quiet environment to undergo the driving knowledge assessment. The test duration was about 30 min. Participants were left alone to answer the questionnaires and could only ask for assistance in case of technical issues with the computer. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare patient outcomes based on the stage of cognitive impairment. The characteristics of patients and controls, as well as their driving theory test results, were compared using either Student’s t-test or the Chi-square test. The correlation between the estimated and actual driving knowledge assessment scores in both groups was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Multivariable linear regression models were used to examine the association between neuropsychological test results and driving knowledge assessment scores. The models included an intercept and the covariates age, sex, and MMSE. Predictors were included in their original scale (no centering), and no interaction terms were modeled. Covariates were included to adjust for potential confounding effects on the estimated scores. All models were fitted using available-case analysis. We evaluated potential clustering by geriatric day centers using a random-intercept linear mixed model with hop as the grouping factor. Statistical significance was set at p = 0.05 for all tests. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.

Ethical and regulatory aspects

Informed oral consent was obtained from all the participants after being provided with oral and written information concerning the terms of the study and the aims of the research. The protocol was approved by the ethics committee for non-interventional research involving humans (Comité de Protection des Personnes Ile-de-France III, 2022). Study database was declared to the French Commission Nationale Informatique et Libertés.

Patients across the seven centers were broadly comparable with respect to key characteristics, including age, education, comorbidities, cognitive scores (MoCA, MMSE), and functional stage of cognitive or physical impairment. The variance attributable to centers was effectively zero (variance = 0, ICC = 0). This suggests that the outcomes do not cluster within centers and that there is no significant center effect influencing the results.

Discussion

This study, conducted in geriatric day hospitals, aimed to describe the theoretical driving knowledge of older adults and compare their performance to younger cognitively unimpaired individuals. Our findings revealed that older drivers performed significantly worse on the theoretical driving test than the control subjects, with a substantial effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.27). On average, the control group missed 8 out of 30 questions. To contextualize, in 2022, only 56.6% of French candidates passed the official theoretical driving test [28]. In the control group, self-evaluation closely matched actual performance. In contrast, older adults reported no greater driving difficulties than controls, despite a fourfold increase in suggestions from relatives to stop driving. Surprisingly, the severity of neurocognitive disorder was not associated to performance on the driving knowledge test. This disconnect in self-assessment among older patients may stem from several factors, including anosognosia in conditions like Alzheimer’s disease, denial of limitations to preserve autonomy, or insufficient awareness of medical driving guidelines.

The inability of patients to accurately self-assess their driving abilities raises significant safety concerns. Paire-Ficout et al. [29] observed a trend in overestimating driving skills in older adults, likely due to the slow progression of cognitive decline, which may mask noticeable changes in driving habits [30]. Moreover, motor and sensory impairments are often more readily acknowledged as contraindications to driving than cognitive impairment in older patients [31]. Therefore, an objective external professional assessment is essential for evaluating the fitness to drive in older drivers.

The choice of appropriate tools for assessing driving-related risk in older adults is critical, yet there is no consensus on which method is most effective. French law [32] recently recommended that individuals with neurodegenerative diseases like AD should stop driving, with an irrevocable prohibition once they reach stage three on the Reisberg scale [33]. However, this scale is rarely used in clinical practice in France or other European countries, and the Federation of French Memory Centers has strongly opposed this recommendation [10]. Our driving theory test is an original driving knowledge assessment that has not been used in previous studies. Our findings thus provide important insights into theoretical driving ability—a crucial component in France where all active drivers must demonstrate fundamental driving knowledge to make safe decisions on the road. Ingley et al. [34] showed that a Highway Code Questionnaire was the only significant predictor of prospective road traffic accidents over a 12-month follow-up period in a population of drivers over 65.

Among the cognitive functions assessed in our study, we found that processing speed, selective attention, motor speed, and visuo-spatial functions were associated with driving knowledge assessment, regardless of age, sex, or overall cognitive performance. Driving theory knowledge typically involves recalling established rules and guidelines. This task likely taps into long-term declarative memory and the ability to integrate diverse cognitive processes—functions well captured by the MoCA and DSST. Hollis et al. [35] proposed a MoCA score below 19 as a cut-off for driving ability. Each point lost on the MoCA increased the likelihood of failing the driving theory test by 1.36 times. While the MoCA has shown promise, its accuracy in predicting performance on on-road driving tests remains limited. Our findings support the usefulness of the MoCA, as it was associated with driving performance irrespective of age and gender. A MoCA score above 27/30 has been suggested as indicative of safe driving in younger drivers [12], though the mean MoCA score of 25.0 (3.1) in our older population was within the normal range, despite their poor driving knowledge assessment. Notably, the MoCA demonstrated a stronger association with driving theory test performance than the MMSE. This difference likely reflects the MoCA’s higher sensitivity to subtle executive dysfunctions, which are critical for problem-solving and decision-making when answering driving-related questions. Compared to the MMSE, the MoCA places greater emphasis on cognitive domains such as executive function, divided attention, and visuo-spatial abilities—skills that are crucial for processing complex traffic scenarios, anticipating hazards, and adapting to changing driving conditions. Prior studies have also highlighted the MoCA’s superiority in detecting early cognitive decline, particularly in individuals with mild cognitive impairment or early-stage neurodegenerative diseases [36]. Given that safe driving relies on rapid information processing, multi-tasking, and cognitive flexibility, the MoCA’s stronger association with driving performance underscores its potential utility in assessing driving fitness in older adults.

Notably, the Digit Symbol Substitution Test emerged as the most highly associated with driving theory performance after adjusting for age, sex, and MMSE scores. The DSST requires intact motor speed, attention, and visuo-perceptual functions [37] and may also reflect associative learning abilities and executive functions such as planning, strategizing, and working memory [38]. Interestingly, prior research of Lafon [39] has highlighted the DSST as the best cognitive indicator of impaired driving risk in both Alzheimer’s patients and normally aging adults. Thus, the DSST could serve as a valuable global indicator of fitness to drive in older patients.

AD is frequently cited as a key reason older adults become unfit to drive [40]. However, the use of biomarkers has transformed our understanding of AD, allowing for earlier diagnoses [41]. Many patients diagnosed with early-stage AD exhibit mild symptoms and may be capable of driving for years [42, 43]. The variability in cognitive decline, influenced by genetics, environment, and life events, further complicates decision-making regarding driving cessation. Thus, a systematic approach to driving discontinuation could be viewed as a restriction of personal liberty, potentially impacting functional independence.

This study presents several strengths. First, it is based on real-world data from older drivers referred for cognitive assessment in a geriatric day hospital setting. This provides valuable insight into the practical issues encountered in clinical practice. The use of standardized and internationally validated neuropsychological tests enhances the reliability of our findings. Additionally, the theoretical driving test we employed mirrors the official French driving theory test, ensuring ecological validity. Another key strength is that we compared the performance of older adults with cognitive complaints to that of a younger, cognitively unimpaired control group, allowing for meaningful comparisons.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The patient population consisted of individuals referred for cognitive assessments, meaning those who were considered cognitively unimpaired may still be at risk of preclinical cognitive decline, potentially limiting their driving abilities more than the general older population. Only a quarter of our source population of geriatric day hospital were active drivers, and it would have been useful to analyze data on those who had already stopped driving. Our older patient’s population is mostly men (65%), unlike the usual geriatric day hospital population, which is predominantly women. Studies have found that women are much more likely than men to self-regulate by adjusting their driving habits or stopping altogether [44]. Besides, active drivers who did not require a comprehensive cognitive evaluation did not undergo detailed neuropsychological testing and were therefore not included in this analysis.

The comparison with younger control subjects, while helpful in providing a perspective on driving test scores, is not ideal, as driving knowledge and testing conditions may have changed over time. The more recent exposure to driving theory among younger adults may confer a recency bias, giving them an advantage in test performance.

Future studies should aim to establish the external validity of the theory test by linking it with on-road performance or driving simulator outcomes. Such approaches would help clarify whether the age-related decline we observed translates into measurable differences in real driving safety and behavior.

While the questionnaire was not designed for clinical research purpose, it was reviewed by experienced driving instructors to ensure content relevance.

Moreover, the control group did not undergo neuropsychological assessments, but it is unlikely that they had any significant cognitive conditions given their performance. Another limitation was that data were based on patient self-reports, without verification from relatives or caregivers, which might introduce recall bias. Analyses involving the estimated score were conducted on available cases, and we acknowledge that missing values impaired our statistical power and may limit generalizability of our findings. The computer-based driving theory test, though mandatory in obtaining a driving license, may not perfectly reflect real-life driving abilities, particularly given the lower familiarity older adults often have with information technology. We also acknowledge the broader limitations of using the driving theory test as a proxy of actual driving performance, including potential measurement error, cohort effects, and limited ecological validity.

Finally, factors beyond cognitive performance, such as motor skills and sensory abilities, which also significantly impact driving fitness, were not assessed in this study. A comparison with older adults from the general population who are still active drivers would have been ideal to refine our findings. However, this approach is challenging in practice due to recruitment and acceptability issues. Additionally, a more detailed characterization of our sample, including a thorough assessment of comorbidities and medications, would have allowed for a more precise analysis of the factors influencing driving test performance. While we accounted for certain factors such as visual impairments, mood disorders, and psychotropic medication use, these did not alter the significant association between MoCA, DSST, and driving knowledge performance (data not shown).

Conclusion

This original study highlighted the performance gap in driving theory knowledge between older drivers with cognitive complaints and younger, cognitively unimpaired drivers. Despite poorer performance, older adults tended to overestimate their driving abilities, raising concerns about their awareness of their limitations and their ability to make informed decisions about continuing to drive. While global cognitive efficiency was not significantly associated with driving test performance, psychomotor speed, selective attention, and visuo-spatial skills were important predictors. The DSST and MoCA emerged as promising tools for evaluating fitness to drive in older adults. However, further large-scale studies are necessary to validate these tools and refine the criteria for driving discontinuation in this population. Future research should also integrate more ecologically valid measures of driving competence, such as simulator-based evaluations and on-road performance tests, to better establish predictive validity. Longitudinal designs will be particularly valuable to determine whether specific cognitive domains predict subsequent declines in driving safety.
