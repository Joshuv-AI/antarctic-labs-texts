Scale-Dependent Suppression Functions and Functional Space Geometry in Renormalization
Daniel Ketels

Abstract
We analyze the effects of a scale-dependent suppression function Ω ⁡ ( k , Λ ) \Omega(k,\Lambda) on the functional space geometry in renormalization theory. By introducing a dynamical cutoff scale Λ \Lambda , the suppression function smoothly regulates high-momentum contributions without requiring a hard cutoff. We show that Ω ⁡ ( k , Λ ) \Omega(k,\Lambda) induces a modified metric on functional space, leading to a non-trivial Ricci curvature that becomes increasingly negative in the ultraviolet (UV) limit. This effect dynamically suppresses high-energy states, yielding a controlled deformation of the functional domain. Furthermore, we derive the renormalization group (RG) flow of Ω ⁡ ( k , Λ ) \Omega(k,\Lambda) and demonstrate its role in controlling the curvature flow of the functional space. The suppression function leads to spectral modifications that suggest an effective dimensional reduction at high energies, a feature relevant to functional space deformations and integral convergence in renormalization theory. Our findings provide a mathematical framework for studying regularization techniques and their role in the UV behavior of function spaces.

Introduction

By an
optimized suppression function
we mean a family of smooth maps

\Omega(\cdot,\Lambda)\,\colon\,D\,\to\,(0,1],\quad\text{with }\,\,\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,:=\,\frac{1}{\,1\,+\,\bigl(\tfrac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\bigr)^{\beta}\,}\,-\,\frac{\,\eta\,\exp\!\bigl[-\,k^{2}/\Lambda^{2}\bigr]}{\epsilon\!\bigl(k^{2}\bigr)},

where:

•

D=\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
(or more generally
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is the momentum‐space domain,

•

\Lambda>0
is a dynamical cutoff scale,

•

\beta,\,\eta>0
control power‐law vs. exponential suppression,

•

\epsilon\in C^{\infty}\bigl(D,\,(0,1]\bigr)
ensures a smooth, Lorentz‐invariant transition without hard cutoffs.

Hence
\Omega(k,\Lambda)\in(0,1]
, is smooth, and suppresses high‐momentum modes without imposing a sharp momentum cutoff.

Definition of
\beta
(Using the Gamma Function)

In
d
dimensions, a factor
k^{-2\beta}
converges at large
\|k\|
if
2\beta>\tfrac{d}{2}
.
Concretely,

\int_{\Lambda}^{\infty}k^{\tfrac{d}{2}-1}\,k^{-2\beta}\,dk\,\text{converges for}\,\tfrac{d}{2}-1-2\beta<-1\,\Longleftrightarrow\,2\beta>\tfrac{d}{2}.

Equivalently, inserting a factor
e^{-\Lambda\,k}
and evaluating via
\Gamma
-function yields the same condition. Thus:

\beta\,:=\,\inf\Bigl\{\gamma>0\,\Bigm|\,\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{d}{2}-2\gamma\bigr)\text{ finite}\Bigr\},\quad\text{ensuring }2\beta>\tfrac{d}{2}\text{ for UV damping}.

Definition of
\eta
(Additional Exponential Damping)

If
\eta>0
, then

\int_{0}^{\infty}k^{\tfrac{d}{2}-1}\,e^{-\eta\,k^{2}}\,dk\,=\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\eta^{-\tfrac{d}{2}}\,\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{d}{2}\bigr)\,<\,\infty,

so exponential decay can overcome strong divergences. We pick
\eta>0
as a positive parameter for extra damping strength in
\Omega
.

Definition of
\epsilon(k^{2})

The function
\epsilon:\,D\to(0,1]
manages the IR–UV transition smoothly. A canonical choice:

\epsilon\!\bigl(k^{2}\bigr)\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\bigl(\tfrac{k^{2}}{k_{c}^{2}}\bigr)^{\alpha}},\quad\alpha>0,

so that
\epsilon(k^{2})\approx 1
for
k^{2}\ll k_{c}^{2}
and
\approx 0
for
k^{2}\gg k_{c}^{2}
.
No sharp cutoff arises, ensuring Lorentz invariance and continuity across momentum scales.

Proposition 1.1

(Positivity and Boundedness of
\Omega
)
.

Assume there exist constants
\eta>0
and a smooth function

\epsilon:\,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to(0,1]
such that for all
\Lambda>0

and all
k\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\eta\,e^{-\,\tfrac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}}\,\leq\,\frac{\epsilon\!\bigl(k^{2}\bigr)}{1+\bigl(\tfrac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\bigr)^{\beta}}\quad.

(1)

Then the scale-dependent suppression function

\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,=\,\frac{1}{\,1+\bigl(\tfrac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\bigr)^{\beta}}\,-\,\frac{\,\eta\,e^{-\,\tfrac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}}}{\epsilon\!\bigl(k^{2}\bigr)}

satisfies

0\,\leq\,\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,\leq\,1\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\Lambda>0.

In particular,
\Omega(\cdot,\Lambda)
remains in the interval
(0,1]
and
never becomes negative.

Proof.

By definition
\Omega(k,\Lambda)\in(0,1]
, and by using the definition:

\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,=\,\frac{1}{\,1+(k^{2}/\Lambda^{2})^{\beta}}\,-\,\frac{\,\eta\,e^{-\,k^{2}/\Lambda^{2}}}{\epsilon(k^{2})}.

The condition rearranges to

0<\frac{\eta\,e^{-\,k^{2}/\Lambda^{2}}}{\epsilon(k^{2})}\,\leq\,\frac{1}{\,1+(k^{2}/\Lambda^{2})^{\beta}}.

Which follows dirrectly from
\Omega(k,\Lambda)>0
and both terms are strictly decreasing for increasing
\Lambda>0
. For
\|k\|\ll\Lambda
, we get

\tfrac{1}{\,1+(k^{2}/\Lambda^{2})^{\beta}}<1
and
\eta\,e^{-\tfrac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}}<\eta
, so as
\epsilon(\cdot)\leq 1
,
the expression does not exceed 1 (or can be bounded by a constant factor).
Hence
0<\Omega(k,\Lambda)\leq 1
.

∎

Remark 1.2

\Omega

is:

strictly decreasing in
\|k\|

, preserving IR modes (

•

2
Momentum-Space Integral Convergence

Before turning to functional integrals, let us see how
\Omega
regulates typical momentum‐space integrals in quantum field theory (QFT). A general loop integral looks like

I\,=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(k)\,d^{d}k,

which might diverge for large
\|k\|
. Replacing
d^{d}k
by
\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,d^{d}k
yields

I_{\Omega}\,=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,f(k)\,d^{d}k,

and we want to show
I_{\Omega}
is finite under certain conditions on
\beta
,
\eta
, etc.

Proof of Convergence in Different Regimes

Theorem 2.1

(Convergence of Suppressed Integrals)
.

Let
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
be the function in defined above and suppose
f(k)\sim k^{\alpha}
as
\|k\|\to\infty
. Then:

•

If
2\beta>d+\alpha,
the integral
\int\Omega\,f(k)\,d^{d}k
converges by power‐law suppression.

•

If
\eta>0
and
\epsilon
does not vanish too fast, exponential damping ensures convergence regardless of
\alpha
.

Proof.

By definition, for large
k
, we have

\Omega(k,\Lambda)~\approx~k^{-2\beta}

thus
\Omega\,f(k)\approx k^{-2\beta}\,k^{\alpha}=k^{\alpha-2\beta}
. Spherical coordinates show that
k^{\alpha+d-1-2\beta}
is integrable at
\infty
if
2\beta>d+\alpha
.

Additional exponential factor:

if
\eta>0
in

\tfrac{\eta\,e^{-k^{2}/\Lambda^{2}}}{\epsilon(k^{2})}
,
the decay beats any polynomial growth. Hence the integral converges for all
\alpha
.

∎

Implications for Renormalization.

In QFT, loop integrals typically behave like
\int k^{d-1-2n}\dots
, requiring
2\beta>d-2n
or exponential damping. Meanwhile IR physics is preserved since
\Omega\approx 1
at small
k
. Thus divergences are removed without imposing a hard cutoff.

2.1
Functional Integrability

Extending to functional integrals over fields
\phi(x)
, define

\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}\,\phi\,:=\,\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}e^{-S_{\Omega}[\phi,\Lambda]}\,\mathcal{D}\phi,

where

S_{\Omega}[\phi,\Lambda]\,=\,\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\|k\|\leq\Lambda}\bigl[1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)\bigr]~|\tilde{\phi}(k)|^{2}\,d^{d}k.

If
\Omega\approx 0
(large
k
), the UV portion of
\phi
is strongly damped, i.e. the measure is finite except for superexponential growth, which we assume is not present.

Theorem 2.2

(Well-Defined Functional Measure)
.

Assume that either

1.

the power-law decay parameter satisfies

2\beta>\frac{d}{2},

or

2.

an additional exponential damping is present (i.e.
\eta>0
and
\epsilon(\cdot)
does not grow too fast).

Then the regulated functional measure

\int_{F}D_{\Omega}\phi\,e^{-S[\phi]}

remains finite in the cutoff limit
\Lambda\to\infty
. In other words,
D_{\Omega}\phi
defines a proper Gaussian-type measure on the space of field configurations.

Proof.

We begin by expressing the field
\phi(x)
in momentum space:

\phi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{\phi}(k)\,e^{ik\cdot x}\,\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}.

The regulated quadratic action is defined as

S_{\Omega}[\phi,\Lambda]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\|k\|\leq\Lambda}\Bigl[1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)\Bigr]\,|\tilde{\phi}(k)|^{2}\,d^{d}k,

where the suppression function
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
satisfies

0<\Omega(k,\Lambda)\leq 1.

Step 1: Gaussian Integration Over Modes

For each momentum mode
k
, the contribution to the path integral is a one-dimensional Gaussian integral:

I(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\tilde{\phi}(k)\,\exp\!\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)\Bigr)|\tilde{\phi}(k)|^{2}\Bigr].

This integral evaluates to

I(k)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)}}.

Thus, the full partition function is expressed as an (infinite) product:

Z=\prod_{\|k\|\leq\Lambda}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)}}.

Taking the logarithm yields

\ln Z=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\|k\|\leq\Lambda}\Bigl[\ln(2\pi)-\ln\Bigl(1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)\Bigr)\Bigr]\,d^{d}k.

For the measure to be well-defined as
\Lambda\to\infty
, this integral must converge.

Step 2: Analysis in the Infrared (IR) and Ultraviolet (UV) Regimes

Infrared (IR) Region:
For small momenta (
\|k\|\ll\Lambda
), the suppression function is designed so that
\Omega(k,\Lambda)\approx 1
. Here, even though
1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)
is small and therefore
\ln\bigl(1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)\bigr)
is large in magnitude, the IR integration is known to converge.

Ultraviolet (UV) Region:
For large momenta, we distinguish two cases:

1.

Polynomial (Power-Law) Suppression:
If

\Omega(k,\Lambda)\sim\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\|k\|}\right)^{2\beta}\quad\text{for large }\|k\|,

then for
\|k\|\gg\Lambda
we have
1-\Omega(k,\Lambda)\approx 1
. However, the density of momentum states grows as
\sim k^{d-1}
. Convergence is then ensured if

2\beta>\frac{d}{2}.

2.

Exponential Damping:
If an additional exponential factor is present (i.e. terms behaving as
\sim e^{-k^{2}/\Lambda^{2}}
contribute), the high-momentum modes are suppressed much more strongly. Hence, exponential decay guarantees convergence of the integrals independent of any polynomial growth.

Step 3: Convergence of the Functional Integral

Since the measure in momentum space is constructed as an infinite product of standard Gaussian integrals, its overall convergence depends on the summability of the logarithms. Under the conditions (assuming an approbiate choice of
\epsilon
)

2\beta>\frac{d}{2}\quad\text{or}\quad\eta>0,

the high-momentum contributions are sufficiently suppressed, so that the sum (or integral) in the logarithm converges.
Thus, the regulated functional measure

D_{\Omega}\phi\,e^{-S[\phi]}

is finite as
\Lambda\to\infty
, meaning that
D_{\Omega}\phi
defines a proper Gaussian-type measure.

∎

.

\Omega(k,\Lambda)~:=~\frac{1}{1+\bigl(\tfrac{k^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\bigr)^{\beta}}~+~\frac{\eta\,e^{-\,k^{2}/\Lambda^{2}}}{\epsilon\bigl(k^{2}\bigr)}.

As

3.1
Metric and Induced Curvature in Functional Space

In momentum space, the usual metric on field configurations
\phi(k)
is

ds^{2}~=~\int d^{d}k~\bigl|\delta\phi(k)\bigr|^{2}.

Including
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
modifies this to

ds_{\Omega}^{2}~:=~\int d^{d}k~\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,\bigl|\delta\phi(k)\bigr|^{2}.

High-energy modes (
k\gg\Lambda
) get contracted by a factor
\Omega(k,\Lambda)\ll 1
, curving the geometry in the UV domain.
One can interpret the resulting Ricci curvature
R(\Lambda)
as depending on
\Lambda
, i.e. differentiating with respect to
\Lambda
shows how the geometry flows under RG.

Remark 3.1
.

For large
k\gg\Lambda
,
(k/\Lambda)^{2\beta}\to\infty
, so
\Omega\approx\Lambda^{2\beta}/k^{2\beta}
, vanishing at high

. The IR remains largely flat: for
k\ll\Lambda
,
\Omega\approx 1
.

4
Operator-Theoretic Properties

To analyze the mathematical role of the function
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
in functional spaces, we examine its effect on integral operators and spectral regularization
[
3
]
. In particular, we investigate the conditions under which the operator
T_{\Omega}
is compact and belongs to standard operator classes
[
1
]
.

4.1
Definitions and Preliminaries

Definition 4.1

(Hilbert-Schmidt Operator)
.

An operator
T:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is said to be
Hilbert-Schmidt
if there exists a measurable kernel function
K(k,k^{\prime})
such that

(Tf)(k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(k,k^{\prime})f(k^{\prime})\,dk^{\prime},

(3)

and the Hilbert-Schmidt norm satisfies

\|T\|^{2}_{\text{HS}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|K(k,k^{\prime})|^{2}\,dk\,dk^{\prime}<\infty.

(4)

Definition 4.2

(Trace-Class Operator)
.

A compact operator
T:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is said to be
trace-class
if its singular values
\{\sigma_{n}\}
satisfy the summability condition
[
2
]

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}<\infty.

(5)

The trace norm of
T
is then defined as

\|T\|_{\text{tr}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}.

(6)

4.2
Integral Operators and Compactness

Consider the integral operator
T_{\Omega}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by

(T_{\Omega}f)(k):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{\Omega}(k,k^{\prime})f(k^{\prime})\,dk^{\prime},

(7)

where the kernel
K_{\Omega}(k,k^{\prime})
is given by

K_{\Omega}(k,k^{\prime}):=\frac{\Omega(k,\Lambda)\Omega(k^{\prime},\Lambda)}{\|k-k^{\prime}\|^{\alpha}}.

(8)

We now establish sufficient conditions for
T_{\Omega}
to be a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
be a measurable function satisfying

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Omega(k,\Lambda)|\leq C<\infty.

(9)

If
\alpha>d/2
, then the operator
T_{\Omega}
is Hilbert-Schmidt.

Proof.

To establish that
T_{\Omega}
is Hilbert-Schmidt, we explicitly compute the Hilbert-Schmidt norm:

\|T_{\Omega}\|^{2}_{\text{HS}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{\Omega(k,\Lambda)\Omega(k^{\prime},\Lambda)}{|k-k^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\right|^{2}dk\,dk^{\prime}.

(10)

Using the bound
|\Omega(k,\Lambda)|\leq C
, we can the bound:

\|T_{\Omega}\|^{2}_{\text{HS}}\leq C^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{|k-k^{\prime}|^{2\alpha}}\,dk\,dk^{\prime}.

(11)

Since the inner integral corresponds to the well-known singular integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{dk^{\prime}}{\|k-k^{\prime}\|^{2\alpha}}\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{d-1}}{r^{2\alpha}}dr,

(12)

which converges for
2\alpha>d
, the double integral is finite under the condition
\alpha>d/2
. This proves that
T_{\Omega}
is Hilbert-Schmidt.

∎

4.3
Spectral Properties and Regularization

A key consequence of the suppression function is its influence on the spectral properties of integral operators. If
T_{\Omega}
is compact, then its eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{n}\}
satisfy the asymptotic summability condition:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{n}|^{p}<\infty,\quad\text{for some }p>0.

(13)

Theorem 4.4
.

If the suppression function satisfies the decay condition

\Omega(k,\Lambda)\leq Ce^{-\gamma|k|},\quad\gamma>0,

(14)

then
T_{\Omega}
belongs to the trace-class
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.

Proof.

Since
T_{\Omega}
is compact, it admits a spectral decomposition:

T_{\Omega}f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\langle f,\phi_{n}\rangle\phi_{n}.

(15)

By the trace-class condition, we require:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{n}|<\infty.

(16)

Applying standard trace-class criteria for integral operators with exponentially decaying kernels show, as
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
has exponential suppression, the eigenvalues decay sufficiently fast to satisfy the trace-class summability condition
[
3
]
.

∎

Thus, under sufficient decay conditions on
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
, the integral operator
T_{\Omega}
has well-controlled spectral properties, ensuring smooth suppression of high-energy contributions.

4.4
Suppression-Induced Spectral Gaps

We examine how the suppression function
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
modifies the spectral properties of differential operators in functional spaces. In particular, we analyze its effect on the Laplacian operator and the emergence of spectral gaps.
Consider the standard Laplacian operator
\Delta
acting on functions in momentum space:

(\Delta f)(k)=k^{2}f(k).

(17)

With the introduction of a scale-dependent suppression function
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
, we define a modified Laplacian:

(\Delta_{\Omega}f)(k):=\Omega(k,\Lambda)k^{2}f(k).

(18)

The eigenvalue equation for
\Delta_{\Omega}
is then given by:

\Delta_{\Omega}\phi_{k}=\lambda_{k}^{\Omega}\phi_{k}.

(19)

Substituting the definition of
\Delta_{\Omega}
, we obtain the eigenvalue relation:

\lambda_{k}^{\Omega}=\Omega(k,\Lambda)k^{2}.

(20)

To illustrate the spectral modification, consider an exponentially decaying suppression function:

\Omega(k,\Lambda)=e^{-\gamma k^{2}},\quad\gamma>0.

(21)

Substituting this into the eigenvalue equation, we obtain:

\lambda_{k}^{\Omega}=k^{2}e^{-\gamma k^{2}}.

(22)

The behavior of
\lambda_{k}^{\Omega}
as a function of
k
reveals a spectral suppression effect:

•

For small momenta (
k
close to
0
), we have
\lambda_{k}^{\Omega}\approx k^{2}
, implying minimal modification in the infrared (IR).

•

For large momenta (
k\to\infty
), the suppression term
e^{-\gamma k^{2}}
dominates, leading to exponential decay of eigenvalues.

This results in a spectral gap at high energies, since eigenvalues decrease significantly beyond a characteristic scale determined by
\gamma
.

5
Measure-Theoretic Interpretation

Beyond operator theory, the function
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
naturally defines a measure transformation, affecting function space topology and integral convergence. In this section, we formalize this transformation using measure theory and examine conditions under which integral transformations remain well-defined.

5.1
Weighted Measure Spaces and Absolute Continuity

We begin by defining a deformed measure space induced by
\Omega(k,\Lambda)

[
4
]
.

Definition 5.1

(Weighted Measure)
.

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B},\mu)
be a standard measure space with
\mu
being the Lebesgue measure. The
weighted measure

\mu_{\Omega}
associated with
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
is defined as:

d\mu_{\Omega}(k):=\Omega(k,\Lambda)d\mu(k).

(23)

The absolute continuity of
\mu_{\Omega}
with respect to
\mu
is central to ensuring that integral transformations remain well-behaved.

Definition 5.2

(Absolute Continuity)
.

A measure
\mu_{\Omega}
is
absolutely continuous
with respect to
\mu
, denoted
\mu_{\Omega}\ll\mu
, if there exists a non-negative function
h(k)
such that for all measurable sets
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mu_{\Omega}(E)=\int_{E}h(k)\,d\mu(k).

(24)

For our suppression function
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
, we have:

\frac{d\mu_{\Omega(\Lambda,k)}}{d\mu}=\Omega(k,\Lambda).

(25)

To ensure that
\mu_{\Omega}
defines a valid measure,
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
must satisfy an integrability condition.

Theorem 5.3

(Integrability Condition for Weighted Measures)
.

The given measure
\mu_{\Omega}
is well-defined if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk<\infty.

(26)

For
\mu_{\Omega}
to be finite, we require:

\mu_{\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk<\infty.

(27)

If this condition holds, then
\mu_{\Omega}
is a finite measure. If
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
does not satisfy this integrability condition, then
\mu_{\Omega}
may be infinite, thus not easily providing well defined norms.

5.2
Integral Convergence in Weighted Spaces

The measure
\mu_{\Omega}
induces a transformation on integrals of functions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We seek conditions under which the integral

I_{\Omega}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(k)\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk

(28)

remains finite for various classes of functions.

Theorem 5.4

(Weighted Integral Convergence)
.

Let
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu_{\Omega})
be a function in the weighted
L^{p}
space. Then
I_{\Omega}
converges if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(k)|^{p}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk<\infty.

(29)

Proof.

Since
I_{\Omega}
is an integral with respect to the measure
\mu_{\Omega}
, its convergence is determined by the standard
L^{p}
norm:

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mu_{\Omega})}^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(k)|^{p}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk.

(30)

By definition, if an integral is finite in
L^{p}(\mu_{\Omega})
, this norm is finite.

∎

5.3
Compactification of Functional Space via Weighted Metric Structure

To rigorously analyze how the suppression function
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
induces a compactification of functional space, we study the impact of the weighted metric on function norms and topological structure.
Given the weighted inner product:

\langle f,g\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(k)g(k)\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk,

(31)

the corresponding norm is:

\|f\|_{\Omega}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(k)|^{2}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk\right)^{1/2}.

(32)

with a naturally induced metric on the functional space:

d_{\Omega}(f,g)=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(k)-g(k)|^{2}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk\right)^{1/2}.

(33)

For a suppression function of the form:

\Omega(k,\Lambda)=e^{-\gamma k^{2}},

(34)

for some
\gamma>0
, this distance function explicitly becomes:

d_{\Omega}(f,g)=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(k)-g(k)|^{2}e^{-\gamma k^{2}}\,dk\right)^{1/2}.

(35)

effectively shrinking the function (contraction the space at large
k
).

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\Omega(k,\Lambda)
be a rapidly decaying function, such as:

\Omega(k,\Lambda)=e^{-\gamma k^{2}},\quad\text{or}\quad\Omega(k,\Lambda)=(1+k^{2})^{-\beta},\quad\beta>d/2.

(36)

Then the function space
L^{2}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by the norm:

\|f\|_{\Omega}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(k)|^{2}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk

(37)

is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the standard norm.

Proof.

To establish the compact embedding of
L^{2}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we follow these steps:

Step 1: Definition of Weighted Space and the Embedding

The space
L^{2}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by the norm:

\|f\|_{\Omega}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(k)|^{2}\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk.

(38)

which defines a Hilbert space with the inner product:

\langle f,g\rangle_{\Omega}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(k)g(k)\Omega(k,\Lambda)\,dk.

(39)

We seek to show that the natural embedding is compact:

L^{2}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(40)
