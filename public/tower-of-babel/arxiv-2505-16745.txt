Flipping and Forking
Wojciech Przybyszewski, Szymon Toruńczyk

Abstract
Monadic stability and the more general monadic dependence (or NIP) are tameness conditions for classes of logical structures, studied in the 80’s in Shelah’s classification program in model theory. They recently emerged in algorithmic and structural graph theory and finite model theory as central notions in relation with the model checking problem for first-order logic: the problem was shown to be fixed-parameter tractable for inputs which come from a fixed class of graphs which is monadically stable, and is conjectured to be tractable in all monadically dependent classes. Several combinatorial characterizations of such graph classes turned out to be essential in their algorithmic treatment; they are all based on the fundamental operation of “flipping” a graph. We introduce the notions of flips and flip independence in arbitrary relational structures. We lift prior combinatorial characterizations of monadically stable graph classes to monadically stable classes of relational structures. We show the equivalence of flip independence with forking independence (over models) – a logical notion of paramount importance in stability theory – in monadically stable structures, shedding new light on the relevance of flips, also characterizing forking independence (over models) combinatorially. We give more precise descriptions of forking independence in the case of monadically stable graphs, and relational structures with a nowhere dense Gaifman graph.

Over the past two decades, the following problem has attracted significant attention in the areas of finite and algorithmic model theory,

Contribution

In this paper, we make a step towards generalizing
\Cref
thm:ms-mc to classes of relational structures.
To this end, we extend two known combinatorial characterizations
of monadically stable graph classes, which have been pivotal in the establishment of
\Cref
thm:ms-mc, to monadically stable classes of relational structures. The prior characterizations rely on the graph-theoretic
flip
operation
– inverting the adjacency between two sets of vertices of a graph –
which has been fundamental in the analysis of dense graphs
[
20
,
15
,
16
]

Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose a notion of

for relational structures (see
\Cref
def:definable-flip-intro) and of
flip-independence
,

2.

We obtain the first combinatorial characterizations of monadically stable classes of relational structures, extending previous results concerning graph classes
(see
\Cref
thm:flip-flatness-rel),

3.

We provide the first combinatorial characterization of

(over models) – a quintessential notion in stability theory – in monadically stable relational structures, in terms of flips (see
\Cref

4.

We provide more refined characterizations of forking independence in monadically stable graph classes
(see
\Cref

In addition, we characterize the latter classes, extending the result of

]

(see

We now describe those contributions in greater detail. We start with discussing the role of flips in previous results in the area, concerning graphs.

2.1
What’s in a flip?

Flips are a fundamental tool in the recent combinatorial and algorithmic study of monadically stable and monadically dependent graph classes
[
15
,
20
,
16
,
35
,
4
]
.
For a graph
G
,
flipping
a pair
X,Y
of sets of vertices of
G
,
results in the graph
G^{\prime}
obtained from
G
by inverting the adjacency of pairs of different vertices in
X\times Y
.
If a graph
G^{\prime}
can be obtained from a graph

by applying this operation
k

G^{\prime}
is a
k
-flip
of
G
.
Flips emerge naturally in the study of dense graphs, such as graphs of bounded clique-width, as a means for replacing the graph
G
under consideration by another graph
G^{\prime}
, which
is somewhat “sparser” than
G
, yet is logically equivalent to it.

To illustrate the use of this notion, the following result has a Ramsey-type flavor, and generalizes a property
called
uniform quasi-wideness

[
12
]
of nowhere dense graph classes.

Theorem 4

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a monadically stable graph class. For all

For every graph

whose elements are pairwise at distance
{>}r

The following result can be viewed as a recursive process which locally simplifies a given graph; it is usually formulated in terms of a game between two players (c.f.
[

For a graph

\textup{flip-rk}_{r}(G)\coloneqq 1+\min_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G^{\prime}:}}{{\text{a $1$-flip of $G$}}}}\max_{v\in V(G^{\prime})}{\textup{flip-rk}_{r}(G^{\prime}[B_{r}^{G^{\prime}}(v)])},

Flips serve a similar role

For instance, a crucial notion in the analysis of planar graphs, or graphs of bounded treewidth, is that of a

Definable flips of structures

The central definition of this paper is the following notion of flips for arbitrary structures, which
is then justified by
\Cref
thm:forking-for-structures,thm:flip-flatness-rel extending

\Cref
thm:graphs-forking-flipping,thm:flip-flatness,thm:fg-ranks to monadically stable classes of relational structures.

Definition 12
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
be two structures with the same domain
A
,
and let
S\subseteq A
.
We say that
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
is an
S
-flip
of
\mathbf{A}
if
every relation of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
is definable in
\mathbf{A}
by a quantifier-free formula with parameters from
S
,
and vice-versa.

(A more concrete form of flips, also sufficient for obtaining the results in the paper, is described in
\Cref
def:syntactic-flip).

Note that in the case of graphs, the
S
-flips
in
\Cref
def:definable-flip-intro above differ from the
S
-definable flips
of graphs mentioned earlier – see
\Cref
ex:tree-flip below – although this distinction is not crucial.

Example 13
.

Coming back to the tree example discussed earlier,
if
T
is a tree and
T^{s}

is the graph obtained from
T
by isolating the vertex
s
,
then
T^{s}
is usually
not
an
S
-flip of
T
according to the above definition (although it is an
S
-definable flip in the sense of graph flips).
However, if

is additionally equipped with a unary predicate

marking all neighbors of

in

, then we have:

T\models E(u,v)\iff T^{s}\models(U(u)\land(v=s))\lor(U(v)\land(u=s))\lor E(u,v),

so the edge relation of
T
is definable in
T^{s}
(equipped with the relations
E
and
U
) by a quantifier-free formula with parameter
s
.
Conversely, both relations
U
and
E
of
T^{s}

are definable in
T
, using quantifier-free formulas with parameter
s
.
Thus, now

is an
\{s\}
-flip of

(and vice-versa), according to
\Cref
def:definable-flip-intro. Similarly,

is also an
\{s\}
-flip of

,
the edge-complement of

.∎

Example 14
.

This example uses a ternary relation.
Again, let

be a rooted tree,
and let

be the structure
with domain

, equipped with a ternary relation

,
consisting of all triples

such that

is the closest common ancestor of

and

in

Fix a vertex

of

, and
let

be the structure
with domain

,
equipped with the following three relations:

\displaystyle R_{0}

\displaystyle=\{(u,v,w)\mid(u,v,w)\in R,u,v,w\neq s\}

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(v,w)\mid(s,v,w)\in R\}

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(u,w)\mid(u,s,w)\in R\}.

Then

-flips of each other; moreover,

2.4
Applications: flip-flatness and separation game

As two applications of
\Cref
thm:forking-for-structures, we prove the following two combinatorial characterizations of monadically stable classes of relational structures,
extending
\Cref
thm:flip-flatness,thm:fg-ranks.

The following notion is
an analogue of the condition in
\Cref
thm:flip-flatness from
[
15
]
.

Definition 18
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a class of structures in a finite relational language.
The class
{\mathcal{C}}
is
flip-flat
if for every
r\in\mathbb{N}

there is some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and an unbounded function
U\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
with the following property.
For every
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathcal{C}}
and set
X\subseteq V(\mathbf{A})
, there is a
set
S\subseteq V(\mathbf{A})
with
|S|\leqslant k
, an
S
-flip
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
of
\mathbf{A}
,
and a set
Y\subseteq X
with
|Y|\geqslant U(|X|)
,
whose elements have pairwise distance greater than
r
in the Gaifman graph of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
and a structure
\mathbf{A}
.
For two sets
U,S\subseteq V(\mathbf{A})
,
define the
separation rank
of
U
over
S
, denoted

\textup{srk}_{r}^{\mathbf{A}}(U/S)
,
by
\textup{srk}_{r}^{\mathbf{A}}(U/S)\coloneqq 0

if
U\subseteq S
,
and otherwise,

\textup{srk}_{r}^{\mathbf{A}}(U/S)\coloneqq 1+\min_{s\in\mathbf{A}}\ \max_{v\in U}\ \textup{srk}_{r}^{\mathbf{A}}(U\cap B^{r}_{Ss}(v)/Ss),

where
Ss=S\cup\{s\}
and
B^{r}_{Ss}(v)=\{u\in\mathbf{A}\mid v\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 3.92064pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 2.00034pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{Ss}^{r}u\}
.
Note that for all
U,S\subseteq V(\mathbf{A})

we have that

\textup{srk}_{r}^{\mathbf{A}}(U/S)\leqslant\textup{srk}_{r}^{\mathbf{A}}(V(\mathbf{A})/\emptyset)\eqqcolon\textup{srk}_{r}(\mathbf{A}).

The parameter
\textup{srk}_{r}(\mathbf{A})

can be explained in terms of a two player game on
\mathbf{A}
,
see
\Cref
sec:apps.
In the case of graph classes, the condition that
\textup{srk}_{r}({\mathcal{C}})<\infty
holds for all
r\in\mathbb{N}

implies the condition in
\Cref
thm:fg-ranks (see
[
21
, Section 3]
).

Theorem 19
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a class of structures in a finite relational language. The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

{\mathcal{C}}
is monadically stable,

2.

{\mathcal{C}}
is flip-flat,

3.

for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
there is some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
{\textup{srk}_{r}(\mathbf{A})<k}
holds for all
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

As was the case with
\Cref
thm:fg-ranks, we expect that
the last condition in
\Cref

2.5
Relationship to other work

Baldwin and Shelah
[
3
]
introduced the notion of monadic stability, and studied this concept from a model-theoretic perspective. Some of our results resemble
(or reprove) some of their results in a more explicit, combinatorial setting; for instance, the last condition in
\Cref
thm:flip-flatness-rel is closely related (and a finitary analogue) of the decompositions of models from
[
3
]
, while
\Cref
thm:forking-for-structures implies that the forking relation on singletons defines an equivalence relation (c.f.
[
3
, Lemma 4.2.6]
).

Braunfeld and Laskowski
[
10
]
provide a more precise model-theoretic understanding of monadically stable and monadically dependent classes;
in particular, they study the existential theories of such classes.

Gajarský, Mählmann, McCarty, Ohlmann, Pilipczuk, Przybyszewski, Siebertz, Sokołowski, and Toruńczyk

[
20
]
introduced the notion of flip independence, and proved a fundamental property (called
r
-separability
) of monadically stable graph classes, from which they then derived combinatorial properties, related to the flipper game, flip-flatness, and a characterization via obstructions.
We follow the approach of
[
20
]
, and extend their results in two ways.
First, by a refined analysis of flip independence, we obtain
a combinatorial characterization of forking independence in monadically stable graphs. Second, we
extend their approach to monadically stable relational structures. To this end, we introduce a notion of flips in relational structures. Lifting the results from graphs to relational structures makes the analysis technically more challenging. While flips of graphs remain graphs, it seems essential that in order to study flips of relational structures,
more relations need to be introduced.
In the case of graphs, a rather explicit description of the obstructions to monadic stability follows from the constructions (see
[
21
, Section 6]
), which can then be used for proving algorithmic hardness results (see
[
13
]
). In the case of relational structures, we believe that a finer analysis of the proof from
\Cref
sec:flip-fork might lead to similar results; however, the analysis becomes much more cumbersome than in graphs, due to the extra added relations.

Braunfeld, Dawar, Eleftheriadis, and Papadopoulos

[
7
]

study monotone, monadically stable classes of relational structures, and characterizes them. We provide a straightforward proof of a more general result
(see
\Cref
thm:nowhere-dense-struct).
However, we do not obtain algorithmic hardness results; this would require additional Ramsey-based arguments.

Ivanov
[
26
]
characterizes forking independence in nowhere dense graphs. Our results generalize his result in two ways: by characterizing (in
\Cref
thm:discrepancy) forking independence in monadically stable graphs (although only over models), and by characterizing (in
\Cref
thm:forking-nowhere-dense-structures) forking independence (over models) in relational structures whose Gaifman graphs are nowhere dense.

Paper outline

In
\Cref
sec:prelims we introduce the required basic notions from logic, model theory, and Ramsey theory.
In
\Cref
sec:flip-ind-graphs,
we characterize forking independence in monadically stable graphs. Those results are later generalized to relational structures in
\Cref
sec:flip-fork. In the graph setting, we obtain a finer analysis,
while in
\Cref
sec:flip-fork, the proofs become more technical.
In
\Cref
sec:nd-struc we study relational structures whose Gaifman graphs are nowhere dense. In
\Cref
sec:flip-fork we prove our main result, characterizing forking independence in monadically stable structures. In
\Cref
sec:apps, we derive several characterizations of monadically stable classes, some of which are purely combinatorial.

Due to space constraints, some proofs are relegated to the appendix.

3.1
Structures, logic

In this paper, we only consider logical structures in a language

Graphs are undirected, without self-loops,

E(G)\subseteq{V(G)\choose 2}

A graph

is viewed as a relational structure equipped with one binary relation

For two structures
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
over the same relational language, and with

we say that
\mathbf{A}
is an
induced substructure
of
\mathbf{B}
if
for every
k
and
k
-ary relation symbol
R
, and for every
k
-tuple
\bar{a}\in A^{k}
,
we have

\bar{a}\in R_{\mathbf{A}}\quad\iff\quad\bar{a}\in R_{\mathbf{B}}.

A class
{\mathcal{C}}
of relational structures is
hereditary
if for every
\mathbf{B}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, if
\mathbf{A}
is an induced substructure of
\mathbf{B}
, then
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

A

of a structure
\mathbf{A}
is a structure
\mathbf{B}

obtained from
\mathbf{A}
by forgetting some relations and constants.
A
lift
of
\mathbf{A}
is a structure
\mathbf{A}^{+}
such that
\mathbf{A}
is a reduct of
\mathbf{A}^{+}
. If all the relations of
\mathbf{A}^{+}
which are forgotten in
\mathbf{A}
are unary, we say that
\mathbf{A}^{+}
is a
monadic lift
of
\mathbf{A}
.

In this paper, we only consider formulas of first-order logic.
An
atomic formula

is a formula of the form
R(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k})
,
where
R
is a
k
-ary relation symbol in
\mathcal{L}\cup\{=\}
,
and each
\tau_{i}
is either a variable, or a constant symbol in
\mathcal{L}
.
For a formula
\varphi
and set
\bar{x}
of variables, we sometimes write
\varphi(\bar{x})
to indicate that
\bar{x}
is the set of free variables of
\varphi
.
A
partitioned formula

\varphi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
is a formula
\varphi
whose set of free variables is partitioned into two distinguished sets
\bar{x}
and
\bar{y}
.

If
\mathbf{M}
is an
\mathcal{L}
-structure and
S\subseteq V(\mathbf{M})
,
then
a
formula with parameters from
S
is a formula
\varphi(\bar{x})

in the language
\mathcal{L}\cup S
,
where each element

is viewed as a constant symbol,
interpreted as
s
.

3.2

The

of a structure
\mathbf{A}

denoted

, is the graph
with vertices

and edges

such that
there is some relation

of
\mathbf{A}
such that

a
and
b
occur simultaneously in some tuple in

.
For a structure
\mathbf{A}
and elements

,
let

denote the distance between

and

in

For two sets or tuples

For an element

If the structure

The following result is a consequence of Gaifman’s locality theorem
[

]
.

Fact 20
.

For every first-order formula

of quantifier rank

there is some

and formulas

and

and tuples

, if

We only use the following.

For every first-order formula
\varphi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
of quantifier rank

,
structure

and tuple
\bar{b}\in M^{\bar{y}}
,
there is a first-order formula

with the following property.

with

Types

be a structure,

a set of elements.

over

is the set
of all formulas

with parameters

in

such that

\mathbf{M}\models\varphi(\bar{a};\bar{b}).

For

, we write

The

is the set of

The

of

3.4
Model theory and stability

For two structures
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
with
V(\mathbf{A})\subseteq V(\mathbf{B})
, we say that
\mathbf{A}
is an
elementary substructure
of
\mathbf{B}
, and write
\mathbf{A}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{B}
,
if for every first-order formula
\varphi(\bar{x})

and any tuple
\bar{a}\in A^{\bar{x}}
,
we have

\mathbf{A}\models\varphi(\bar{a})\quad\iff\quad\mathbf{B}\models\varphi(\bar{a}).

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a class of structures in some language
\mathcal{L}
.
A partitioned formula
\varphi(\bar{x};\bar{y})

is
stable
in
{\mathcal{C}}

if there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}

such that for every
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
there are no tuples

\bar{a}_{1},\ldots,\bar{a}_{n}\in A^{\bar{x}}

and
\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{n}\in A^{\bar{y}}

such that

\mathbf{A}\models\varphi(\bar{a}_{i};\bar{b}_{j})\quad\iff\quad i\leqslant j\qquad\text{ for all $i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$}.

A class
{\mathcal{C}}
is
stable
, resp.
atomic-stable
,
if every partitioned formula, resp. atomic formula, is stable in
{\mathcal{C}}
.
A class
{\mathcal{C}}
is
monadically stable

if for every class
{\mathcal{C}}^{+}
such that
every structure
\mathbf{A}^{+}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{+}
is a monadic lift of some
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, the class
{\mathcal{C}}^{+}
is stable.
For hereditary classes, monadic stability is equivalent to the definition from the introduction
[
10
]
, see
\Cref
thm:bl-ms below.
Examples of monadically stable classes
coming from graph theory include
all nowhere dense classes (see below),
as well as any class obtained from a nowhere dense graph class

{\mathcal{C}}
by means of a first-order formula
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

(namely, for each graph
G\in{\mathcal{C}}
construct a structure
\varphi(G)

with domain
V(G)
and relation
R
consisting of those
k
-tuples

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
with
G\models\varphi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
).

A class
{\mathcal{C}}
is
dependent
,
resp.
existentially dependent

if for every partitioned formula
\varphi(\bar{x};\bar{y})

(resp. existential partitioned formula)
there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}

such that
for every
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, there are no tuples

(\bar{a}_{i}:i\in[n])
and
(\bar{b}_{J}:J\subseteq[n])

such that

\mathbf{A}\models\varphi(\bar{a}_{i};\bar{b}_{J})\quad\iff\quad i\in J\qquad\text{ for all $i\in[n],J\subseteq[n]$}.

A class
{\mathcal{C}}
is
monadically dependent
,
resp.
existentially monadically dependent
,
if for every class
{\mathcal{C}}^{+}
such that
every structure
\mathbf{A}^{+}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{+}
is a monadic lift of some
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, the class
{\mathcal{C}}^{+}
is dependent, resp. existentially dependent.
Clearly, every stable class is dependent,
and every monadically stable class is monadically dependent.
Monadically dependent graph classes include
all monadically stable graph classes (such as nowhere dense graph classes),
every class of bounded clique-width, and more generally, every class of bounded twin-width
[
6
]
or of bounded flip-width
[
35
]
.
Note that the adjective
dependent
is sometimes replaced with
NIP
in model theory.
A combinatorial characterization of monadically dependent graph classes is given in
[
16
]
.

Theorem 22

(
[
10
]
)
.

The following are equivalent for a hereditary class of structures in a finite relational language:

1.

{\mathcal{C}}
is stable,

2.

{\mathcal{C}}
is monadically stable,

3.

{\mathcal{C}}
is monadically dependent and atomic-stable.

The following lemma is immediate.

Lemma 23
.

Every monadically stable class of relational structures
is atomic-stable and existentially monadically dependent.

For a class
{\mathcal{C}}
of structures, by
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}

we denote the
elementary closure
of
{\mathcal{C}}
,
which is the class of all structures
\mathbf{M}

which satisfy all the sentences
\varphi
that hold
in all structures in
{\mathcal{C}}
. Note that if
{\mathcal{C}}
contains
structures of arbitrarily large size, then
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
contains infinite structures (even of arbitrarily large cardinality).

It is not difficult to prove that
atomic-stability and existential monadic dependence (and similarly existential monadic stability) are preserved by taking the elementary closure.
In some of our proofs, this is why we consider those notions, rather than monadic stability, for which this is is unclear (but ultimately follows from the equivalences in
\Cref
sec:apps, or the results of
[
10
]
).

Lemma 24
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an atomic-stable class, resp. an existentially monadically dependent class of structures.
Then
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
is atomic-stable, resp. existentially monadically dependent.

We provide a proof of
\Cref
lem:closure in Appendix
A
.

3.5
Forking independence and definability of types

Forking independence is a fundamental tool in the study of stable classes.
Instead of recalling the original definition of forking independence, we use the following characterization of forking independence over models in stable theories in terms of so-called
finite satisfiability
(c.f. Definition 8.1.1 and Corollary 8.3.7 in
[
34
]
).

Definition 25

(Finite satisfiability)
.

Let
\mathbf{N}
be a structure,
A\subseteq B\subseteq N
, and
\bar{v}\in N^{k}
.
We say that
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{v}/B)
is finitely satisfiable in
A

if for every formula
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathrm{tp}(\bar{v}/B)
(with parameters from
B
),
there is some
\bar{w}\in A
such that
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathrm{tp}(\bar{w}/B)
.

Definition 26

(Forking over models)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}
be two stable structures and let
B\subseteq N
.
A set

A\subseteq N
is
forking independent
of
B
over
M
, denoted
A\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{M}B
,
if for every tuple
\bar{a}\in A^{k}
,
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{a}/M\cup B)
is finitely satisfiable in
M
.

Observe that if
\mathbf{M}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}

then
N\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{M}M
.
In other words,

is finitely satisfiable in

,
for all

.
Indeed, suppose

is a formula with parameters

with

.
Then
\mathbf{N}\models\exists\bar{x}.\varphi(\bar{x};\bar{m})
,
and so
\mathbf{M}\models\exists\bar{x}.\varphi(\bar{x};\bar{m})
since

.
Therefore, there is some
\bar{a}^{\prime}\in M^{k}
with
\mathbf{N}\models\varphi(\bar{a}^{\prime};\bar{m})
.

In the case of monadically stable structures Baldwin and Shelah proved that for any sets

the independence

The following result is a fundamental fact about stable formulas (see e.g.
[
30

Theorem 28

(Definability of types)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}
be two structures and
\varphi(\bar{x};\bar{y})
be a formula that is stable in
\mathbf{M}
.
For every
\bar{n}\in\mathbf{N}^{\bar{x}}
there is some formula
\psi_{\bar{n}}(\bar{y})
with parameters from
\mathbf{M}
, which is a positive boolean combination of formulas of the form
\varphi(\bar{m};\bar{y})
for
\bar{m}\in\mathbf{M}^{\bar{x}}
, such that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}\models\varphi(\bar{n};\bar{a})\ \iff\ \mathbf{M}\models\psi_{\bar{n}}(\bar{a})\qquad\text{ for all }\bar{a}\in\mathbf{M}^{\bar{y}}.

(1)

Any such formula
\psi_{\bar{n}}(\bar{y})
is called a
definition
of the
\varphi
-type of
\bar{n}
over
\mathbf{M}
.
Moreover, the size of the formula
\psi_{\bar{n}}(\bar{y})
can be bounded by some constant depending only on
\mathbf{N}
and
\varphi
.

Note that there is a unique first-order formula
\psi_{\bar{n}}(\bar{y})
satisfying (
1
), up to equivalence in
\mathbf{N}
:
If
\psi_{\bar{n}}(\bar{y})
and
\psi^{\prime}_{\bar{n}}(\bar{y})
are two such formulas,
then by (
1
), for all
\bar{a}\in\mathbf{M}^{\bar{y}}
we have

\mathbf{N}\models\psi_{\bar{n}}(\bar{a})\iff\psi_{\bar{n}}^{\prime}(\bar{a})
.
Since
\mathbf{M}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}
, this implies that
for all
\bar{a}\in\mathbf{N}^{\bar{y}}
we have

\mathbf{N}\models\psi_{\bar{n}}(\bar{a})\iff\psi_{\bar{n}}^{\prime}(\bar{a})
.

We also state a version of the Harrington’s Lemma (c.f.
[
34
, Lemma 8.3.4]
) about definitions of stable formulas.

Lemma 29

.

3.7
Sparsity

A subgraph of a graph

is a graph obtained from
G
by removing vertices and edges arbitrarily.
A graph class is
monotone
if it is closed under taking subgraphs.

A graph class
{\mathcal{C}}
is

if for every

there is some

such that
the
r
-subdivision of

(obtained by replacing each edge of

with a path of length

)
is not a subgraph of any graph
G\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

The following characterization of nowhere dense graph classes is known.
A graph class
{\mathcal{C}}

is
weakly sparse
if some biclique

is not a subgraph of any graph in
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Nowhere dense graph classes include: the class of planar graphs, any class which excludes a fixed graph as a minor, and any class of graphs of maximum degree bounded by a fixed constant. On the other hand, the
class of cliques, or the class of bicliques, is not nowhere dense, but is monadically stable.

(Consequence of

,
2
]

]

4.1

Towards a proof of
\Cref

enjoys some finitary analogues of the properties enjoyed by forking independence.

One of the standard properties of forking is called

In full generality, it says that for any three sets

Let

Our aim now is to get a more detailed description of the equivalence relation

We will show that it corresponds to the connected components in a certain graph which can be
combinatorially defined in terms of

Recall from
\Cref

that for
G\preccurlyeq H
and every

there is a (unique, up to equivalence in
H

with parameters from
G
such that
for all

we have

H\models E(u,v)\iff H\models\psi_{u}(v).

We first define an auxilliary binary relation
E^{\prime}
on
V(H)

so that

E^{\prime}(u,v)\iff\psi_{u}(v).

Intuitively,
E^{\prime}(u,v)
means that
it is “expected” that
H\models E(u,v)
, basing
only on the knowledge of
N(u)\cap G
and
N(v)\cap G
.
The relation
E^{\prime}
is symmetric, as by
\Cref
lem:harrington, we have that
H\models\psi_{u}(v)\iff H\models\psi_{v}(u)
for any two formulas
\psi_{u}
and
\psi_{v}
that define the atomic types of
u
and
v
over
G
respectively. However,

may have self-loops, e.g. if

are both complete graphs and

holds.
Note that
E^{\prime}
is definable with parameters from
G
.

Next, we define a binary relation
\leftrightsquigarrow_{G}
on
V(H)
as the symmetric difference of
E
and
E^{\prime}
, that is,

u\leftrightsquigarrow_{G}v\iff H\models E^{\prime}(u,v)\xor E(u,v)\iff H\models\psi_{u}(v)\xor E(u,v)

for
u,v\in V(H)
.
Observe that the relation
\leftrightsquigarrow_{G}
is symmetric,
since both
E
and
E^{\prime}
are.
Intuitively,
u\leftrightsquigarrow_{G}v
means that there is a discrepancy between the expected and actual relation between
u
and
v
.
Note also that
\neg(u\leftrightsquigarrow_{G}v)
holds whenever either
u
or
v
is in
G
, by the defining property of
\psi_{u}(v)
.
Let
\leftrightsquigarrow_{G}^{*}
denote the transitive, reflexive closure of

.

See
4

In the special case when

and
v

Indeed, for every vertex

[
10
, Lemma 4.4]
, we get that a class of structures
{\mathcal{C}}
is monadically stable if and only if its theory
\Th({\mathcal{C}})
is monadically stable.
To be more precise, in the statement of
[
10
, Lemma 4.4]
the authors assume that
{\mathcal{C}}
is a hereditary class of structures, but this assumption can be dropped, as both
properties (
{\mathcal{C}}
is monadically stable and
\Th({\mathcal{C}})
is monadically stable) are preserved by taking the hereditary closure of
{\mathcal{C}}
(the latter follows from
[
10
, Proposition 2.5]
).
We remark that the same holds for monadically dependent classes of structures and monadically dependent theories.

Now we state (a fragment of)
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
.
We say that a theory
T
has the
f.s. dichotomy
if the following holds: for all models
\mathbf{M}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}
of
T
and all finite tuples
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in N
such that
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{b}/M\bar{a})
is finitely satisfiable in
\mathbf{M}
, for any singleton
c\in N
we have that either
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{b}/M\bar{a}c)
or
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{b}c/M\bar{a})
is finitely satisfiable in
\mathbf{M}
.

[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
states in particular that a theory
T
is monadically dependent if and only if it has the f.s. dichotomy.

Proof of
\Cref
prop:forking-for-structures-converse.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a stable structure such that for every elementary extension
\mathbf{M}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}
, every elementary substructure
\mathbf{S}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}
, and all
a,b\in V(\mathbf{N})

a\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}b\qquad\iff\qquad a\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}^{r}b\quad\text{for all $r\in\mathbb{N}$}.

Take any model
\mathbf{S}
of
\Th(\mathbf{M})
and an elementary extension
\mathbf{S}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}^{\prime}
.
By a simple application of compactness, we can find a model
\mathbf{N}
of
\Th(\mathbf{M})
such that both
\mathbf{M}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}
and
\mathbf{N}^{\prime}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{N}
.
Take any finite tuples
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in N
such that
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{b}/S\bar{a})
is finitely satisfiable in
\mathbf{S}
.
Since
\mathbf{S}
is stable, this is equivalent to
\bar{a}\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}\bar{b}
.
By assumption, we have that
\bar{a}\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}^{r}\bar{b}
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

Recall that by
\Cref
lem:flip-dependence-transitivity, for any three elements
u,v,w\in N
, if
u\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 3.92064pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 2.00034pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}^{r}v
and
v\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 3.92064pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 2.00034pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}^{q}w
then
u\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 3.92064pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 2.00034pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}^{r+q}w
(this lemma was stated for graphs but the proof works in the same way for structures).

Take any element
c\in N
.
Observe, that it can’t happen that
c\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 3.92064pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 2.00034pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}^{r}a
and
c\mathop{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\textstyle\kern 5.71527pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 8.00134pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 3.87495pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 5.71527pt}{\scriptstyle\kern 2.80048pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 3.92064pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 1.89871pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 2.80048pt}{\scriptscriptstyle\kern 1.42882pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mathchar 12854\relax$\kern 2.00034pt\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\mid$\hss}\lower 0.96873pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\smile$\hss}\kern 1.42882pt}}_{S}^{q}b

and elements

as that would give us

\Th(\mathbf{M})
has the f.s. dichotomy, which in turns implies that
\mathbf{M}
is monadically stable.
