Early Prediction of Carbapenem Resistance in Gram-Negative Organisms at Culture Ordering from Routinely Collected Hospital Data: Temporal and External Validation of ORDER-CARB
Authors: Emmanuel Pio Pastore, Daniele Roberto Giacobbe, Antonio Vena, Matteo Bassetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmpirical therapy for severe suspected infection is selected before organism identification and antimicrobial susceptibility results are available. ORDER-CARB estimates, at culture ordering, the probability of subsequent recovery of a carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative organism using routinely collected structured data available before the index culture.MethodsORDER-CARB was developed using a large publicly available dataset, the Antibiotic Resistance Microbiology Dataset, with chronological model-fitting, calibration, and temporal-test splits. The frozen model was transported to the independent and also publicly available East Carolina University Health (ECUH) and University of Texas Southwestern (UTSW) cohorts, locally recalibrated using older data, and evaluated in future hold-outs. The primary analysis included microbiologically labeled urine, respiratory, and blood cultures that subsequently yielded at least one Gram-negative organism with interpretable carbapenem susceptibility. A secondary two-stage analysis evaluated all blood-culture episodes in the external cohorts.ResultsORDER-CARB identified subsequent carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative isolates with an AUROC of 0.858 in internal temporal testing and of 0.797 at ECUH and 0.876 at UTSW in future external cohorts. Using locally selected cutoffs intended to identify approximately 9 of 10 resistant isolates and thereby prompt early review of whether empirical coverage against carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative organisms was warranted while susceptibility results were pending, sensitivity in the future cohorts was 91.6% at ECUH and 85.7% at UTSW. This approach would have prompted review in 55.4% and 27.7% of cultures, respectively, with positive predictive values of 5.7% and 9.4%. In the secondary analysis including all blood-culture episodes, irrespective of the subsequently identified organism, the model also showed high discrimination for carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bloodstream infection, with AUROCs of 0.928 (95% CI 0.869–0.987) at ECUH and 0.967 (95% CI 0.909–1.000) at UTSW.ConclusionsORDER-CARB retained useful ranking after chronological external transport, but the marked prevalence shift materially affected positive predictive value and review burden, and operating thresholds remained site-specific. The score should not replace susceptibility testing, severity assessment, or clinical judgment. Prospective evaluation is needed to determine whether it can support empiric treatment and stewardship decisions after local recalibration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-026-01441-1.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Carbapenem resistance, Gram-negative bacteria, Prediction, Empirical therapy

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Empirical treatment for suspected Gram-negative infection is often selected before organism identification and susceptibility results are available, creating a risk of both inadequate initial therapy and unnecessary escalation to agents reserved for resistant organisms.Whether resistance risk can be estimated from routinely available information at the time of culture ordering, and whether such estimates remain informative when applied to different hospital populations, is clinically relevant for empiric treatment and antimicrobial stewardship. What was learned from the study? ORDER-CARB identified subsequent carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative isolates using information available at culture ordering and retained discrimination in future external cohorts from two independent healthcare systems.Clinically oriented operating points identified most resistant isolates while quantifying the corresponding stewardship review burden. In the secondary analysis of all blood-culture episodes, ORDER-CARB also showed high discrimination for carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bloodstream infection.The marked external prevalence shift, however, substantially changed positive predictive value and stewardship workload. Prospective real-world validation incorporating illness severity, infection syndrome, local epidemiology, and comparison with clinical assessment will be essential to establish whether ORDER-CARB could improve empiric treatment and stewardship decisions.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance continues to impose a large clinical and public-health burden, and carbapenem resistance in Gram-negative bacteria occupies a high-risk niche because carbapenems are often reserved for severe infections or pathogens with few remaining active options [1–3]. For patients undergoing evaluation for possible invasive bacterial infection, the interval between culture ordering and organism identification with final susceptibility testing, usually 36–48 h, remains a period of unavoidable uncertainty. During that interval, empiric therapy is chosen without complete microbiology data. A clinically credible order-entry estimate of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria risk could support stewardship review, prioritize microbiological and clinical attention, and inform early escalation or de-escalation discussions while complete results are pending.

Previous studies show that antimicrobial resistance is often predictable from prior microbiology and treatment history. For example, Yelin and colleagues showed that longitudinal clinical history, prior resistance, and antibiotic purchasing patterns can support personalized resistance prediction in urinary tract infections [4]. Yang and colleagues extended this logic to interpretable prediction of antibiotic resistance in complicated urinary tract infection [5]. McGuire and colleagues reported an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.846 for carbapenem-resistance prediction at culture collection in a single-center cohort of admitted adults [6]. The ICU-CARB study and its multicenter validation addressed carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria carriage on intensive-care-unit admission rather than culture-order prediction before organism identification [7, 8]. Yuan and colleagues addressed resistance prediction in Enterobacterales bloodstream infection (BSI) and showed improved performance after post-culture genus and species information was added, highlighting the distinction between order-entry prediction and later dynamic prediction once the microbiology workflow has begun [9].

Two issues are central for a deployable order-entry model. The first is temporal leakage. Retrospective studies can overstate performance if they use variables influenced by downstream workflow, current organism identity, or measurements recorded after culture collection [10–13]. A carbapenem-resistance model intended to support empiric therapy cannot assume that the causative organism is already known to be a specific Gram-negative genus or even species. The second issue is transport. A model may preserve discrimination after moving to a new hospital while losing calibration and operational usefulness because case mix, prevalence of Gram-negative etiology, and prevalence of carbapenem resistance differ by site. Predictive values and decision curves depend strongly on local prevalence, not only on ranking [14–16].

This study developed and evaluated ORDER-CARB, an order-entry carbapenem-resistance framework built around strict chronology, frozen transport, site-specific external recalibration, and prevalence-aware threshold selection. The model was trained internally on the Dryad Antibiotic Resistance Microbiology Dataset (ARMD) with temporal separation between fitting, calibration, and testing, and was transported to two independent external Dryad datasets from East Carolina University Health (ECUH) and University of Texas Southwestern (UTSW) Medical Center [17–19]. The intended role was not to recommend a specific antibiotic but to provide an early resistance estimate that could inform empiric therapy and stewardship review while susceptibility results were pending.

Methods

ORDER-CARB refers to the full workflow of chronological development, frozen transport, site-specific external recalibration, and prevalence-aware threshold selection. The workflow was designed around the information available when a culture was ordered, before organism identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing results were available.

Data Sources, Endpoints, and Definitions

Model development used ARMD, a deidentified electronic health record-derived resource built to support antimicrobial-resistance research [19]. External validation used two independent datasets modeled on the same structure: ARMD-ECUH, released by East Carolina University Health, and ARMD-UTSW, released by the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center [17, 18]. This study used publicly available, de-identified secondary data and was exempt from ethics committee approval.

The primary analysis was conducted in a microbiologically labeled culture-order cohort. Eligible primary analysis records were urine, respiratory, and blood culture orders with a deidentified patient identifier, a unique culture-order identifier, a non-missing order time, subsequent Gram-negative organism evidence, and an available and interpretable (i.e., categorized as susceptible, intermediate/increased exposure, or resistant, see below) source-reported carbapenem susceptibility result for imipenem, meropenem, doripenem, or ertapenem. The primary unit of analysis was an eligible culture order. Patients were not collapsed to a single record because the intended use case is repeated order-entry prediction. A patient could contribute more than one eligible culture order if orders occurred at different times. Patient identifiers were used only to order longitudinal history, define prior exposures before each index culture order, and cluster bootstrap resampling for uncertainty intervals. The primary endpoint was microbiological detection of a carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative organism. Accordingly, the primary model estimated carbapenem resistance among cultures that later yield a Gram-negative organism with interpretable susceptibility; it did not estimate the probability of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative infection among all patients for whom a culture is ordered. In polymicrobial cultures, the outcome was positive if at least one identified Gram-negative organism was carbapenem resistant.

A secondary, clinically oriented analysis employed the denominator observable at empirical decision time in the external datasets and was restricted to blood culture orders only: all blood culture orders, regardless of whether they later proved culture-negative or yielded Gram-positive bacteria, fungi, carbapenem-susceptible Gram-negative, and/or carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. The endpoint for this analysis was BSI episodes by carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. Within each patient, eligible blood-culture orders occurring within a 48-h window were grouped into a single BSI episode indexed by the first blood-culture order. Later culture orders from the same patient remained eligible as BSI episodes if occurring beyond the 48-h window. In polymicrobial BSI episodes, the outcome was positive if at least one identified Gram-negative organism was carbapenem-resistant. The clinical BSI analysis used a two-stage approach constructed entirely from data preceding the future hold-out at each external site. The transported ORDER-CARB resistance probability was combined with a locally fitted model estimating the probability that a blood-culture episode would yield a Gram-negative organism; the two probabilities and their product were entered into a logistic clinical model. The resulting clinical score was recalibrated on the older local recalibration block, thresholds were selected on older local data, and the score was then applied to the future hold-out without further fitting.

Carbapenem resistance was defined from the released source-reported categorical susceptibility interpretation. Any resistant interpretation for imipenem, meropenem, doripenem, or ertapenem classified the culture order as positive. Susceptible and intermediate/increased exposure interpretations were grouped as negative (i.e., susceptible) because the model targeted frank source-reported carbapenem resistance rather than broad carbapenem non-susceptibility. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values were not retrospectively reinterpreted under a uniform contemporary Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) or European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoint table across institutions and years [20, 21]. Source-reported susceptible, intermediate-/increased-exposure, and resistant distributions by site, year, and carbapenem are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Leakage Control, Preprocessing, and Model Development

Leakage control was defined from the clinical decision point. For each index culture order, the model was allowed to use only information that could have been known when empiric therapy was being chosen. Prior microbiology, prior medication exposure, demographics, care location, prior procedures, nursing-home exposure, and historical laboratory summaries were admissible only when they preceded the index culture order. Index-culture organism identity, culture description, susceptibility interpretations, MIC values, and any other microbiology information generated from the index event were excluded. Laboratory or medication information recorded after the index order was also excluded for that prediction.

Additional technical details are provided in the Supplementary Methods. The evaluated potential predictors and pre-index eligibility and preprocessing rules are available in Supplementary Table S2, while abbreviations, terminology, and operating metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Internal and external datasets were mapped into a shared schema covering demographics and care location, prior medication exposures, prior procedures, prior infecting organisms, nursing home exposure, and prior laboratory summaries. Absence-coded history variables, including prior antibiotic classes, prior procedures, prior organisms, and nursing home indicators, were filled with zero because missingness represented no recorded prior exposure or event. Non-absence-coded variables retained missingness and were imputed inside the training pipeline. Candidate predictors with more than 50% missingness in the model-fitting split after absence-coded zero filling were excluded. Numerical variables were median-imputed with missingness indicators; categorical variables were imputed with the most frequent category and one-hot encoded. Every preprocessing operator was fit on the internal model-fitting subset only.

Temporal splitting was used throughout. The internal cohort was first split chronologically into development and temporal test subsets. The development subset was then split chronologically into a model-fitting set and a temporally later internal calibration set. The base learner was a random forest chosen for mixed structured hospital data and nonlinear effects without prespecified interaction terms. A class-weighted random forest was selected by grid search in the model-fitting subset, and sigmoid calibration was fit on the temporally later internal calibration subset. Full implementation details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

External Recalibration, Threshold Selection, and Evaluation

For each external dataset, the frozen internal preprocessing pipeline and internally calibrated random forest were applied directly to generate imported probabilities. External culture orders with complete labels and non-missing order times were split chronologically into an older external subset and a future external hold-out. The older subset was further divided chronologically into an earlier 30% block for local recalibration and a later 70% block for threshold selection. The earlier block was used only to fit site-specific logistic recalibration on the logit scale. The threshold-selection block was used only to choose operating thresholds. The future external hold-out remained untouched until final evaluation. The complete implementation split and recalibrated discrimination results are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

The primary threshold-selection strategy was rule-out oriented. Thresholds were selected on the older local threshold-selection block by minimizing the fraction of cultures flagged for early review while requiring sensitivity of at least 0.85, 0.90, or 0.95. The selected threshold was then applied unchanged to the future external hold-out. A secondary threshold family maximized positive predictive value subject to negative predictive value floors of 0.93, 0.95, 0.97, 0.99, and 0.995. This secondary family was included to characterize how negative predictive values behave in rare-event external cohorts and was not the primary deployment rule.

Performance was summarized using AUROC, average precision, Brier score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, fraction flagged for early review, balanced accuracy, and Matthews correlation coefficient. The Brier score was used as the primary proper scoring rule for overall probabilistic accuracy [22]. Decision-curve analysis followed the net-benefit formulation introduced by Vickers and Elkin [23]. For the primary external rule-out operating points, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated with a patient-level cluster bootstrap. For the clinical BSI analysis, AUROC 95% CIs were estimated from 2000 patient-level bootstrap replicates; resampling details are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Operational burden was additionally expressed as false-positive reviews per true positive and number needed to review, calculated as the total number of flagged cultures divided by true positives.

Calibration, Decision Curves, and Interpretation Analyses

Calibration curves and decision curves were generated for the internal temporal test set and the future external hold-out subsets. Calibration plots assessed agreement between predicted and observed risk, with the internal reliability plot constructed using quantile bins and displaying the number of observations contributing to each occupied bin. Decision-curve analysis compared the model with treat-none and treat-all strategies. Because the intended clinical application was rule-out at low predicted probabilities, interpretation focused on the low-threshold operating region together with the corresponding numbers of alerts and missed outcome events per 1000 evaluated culture orders or blood-culture episodes.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses examined clinically relevant uncertainty in the microbiological endpoint and cohort composition. An alternative endpoint grouped intermediate/increased-exposure and resistant interpretations as positive and susceptible interpretations as negative (I+R vs. S), using a separately fitted model and calibration workflow. Future external performance was also estimated separately in monomicrobial and polymicrobial primary-analysis cultures and stratified by blood, urine, and respiratory specimen source. These specimen-source analyses retained the conditional primary microbiological denominator and were distinct from the all-blood-culture BSI analysis. Source-reported susceptible, intermediate/increased-exposure, and resistant distributions were summarized by site, year, and carbapenem.

Exploratory model-interpretation analyses assessed feature importance and the effect of removing individual predictors; interaction and effect-modification analyses are reported in the Supplementary Material. These analyses were descriptive and were not interpreted causally [24].

Results

Cohorts and Prevalence Shift

The internally modeled cohort contained 72,691 culture orders. After carbapenem-label harmonization and application of the pre-index eligibility rules, the first chronological split yielded 36,270 culture orders for development and 15,545 for internal temporal testing. The development portion was split again into 29,016 model-fitting culture orders and 7254 internal calibration culture orders. Carbapenem resistance prevalence shifted over time: the model-fitting split contained 9408 carbapenem-resistant culture orders (32.4%), whereas the internal temporal test set contained 1749 carbapenem-resistant culture orders (11.3%).

External transport was evaluated in two independent datasets. The ECUH dataset contributed 13,218 culture orders to the older external subset and 29,788 to the future external hold-out. The UTSW dataset contributed 8913 and 17,744 culture orders to the corresponding subsets. External resistant-event prevalence was substantially lower than in the internal temporal test set, ranging from 3.0% to 3.6% across external subsets (Table 1). The taxonomic composition of the microbiologically labeled cohorts, including genus- and species-level counts and percentages for each internal and external temporal split, is reported in Supplementary Table S5.

Primary Model Performance After Chronological Transport

The final internal model was a class-weighted random forest selected after grid search on the temporally earliest model-fitting data. The best cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve in model fitting was 0.934. After sigmoid calibration on the internal calibration split, the frozen model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.858, average precision of 0.583, and Brier score of 0.0678 on the internal temporal test set.

The transported model retained discrimination at both external sites after site-specific recalibration. In the future external hold-out, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.797 at ECUH and 0.876 at UTSW. Average precision was 0.112 at ECUH and 0.191 at UTSW, corresponding to approximately 3.3 and 6.3 times the local future hold-out event prevalence. Recalibration improved probabilistic accuracy without changing rank discrimination, with future hold-out Brier score 0.0331 at ECUH and 0.0268 at UTSW (Table 2).

Deployment-Style Rule-Out Thresholds

The primary external rule-out analysis selected thresholds on older local threshold-selection data by minimizing the fraction flagged for early review subject to sensitivity floors of 0.85, 0.90, and 0.95. At ECUH, these historical floors transferred to future sensitivities of 0.857, 0.916, and 0.970, with corresponding fractions flagged of 45.1%, 55.4%, and 69.0%, respectively. At UTSW, future sensitivities were 0.804, 0.857, and 0.930, with lower fractions flagged of 19.2%, 27.7%, and 46.1%, respectively (Table 3; Fig. 1). Thus, the same sensitivity targets translated into materially different stewardship workloads across sites: ECUH preserved the historical sensitivity targets more closely but required broader review, whereas UTSW achieved lower review burden with lower future sensitivity at the same historical floors.

Bloodstream Infection Episodes Analysis

The secondary, clinically oriented analysis evaluated the two-stage clinical score in the BSI denominator observable at empiric decision time. ECUH contributed 342,503 blood culture orders grouped into 183,477 BSI episodes, of which 68 contained carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative BSI. UTSW contributed 285,359 blood culture orders grouped into 153,197 BSI episodes, of which 78 contained carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative BSI. The future hold-out contained 55,226 ECUH BSI episodes with 35 events and 37,372 UTSW BSI episodes with 18 events. The taxonomic composition of BSI episode cohorts is reported in Supplementary Table S10. As shown in Table 4, future hold-out AUROC was 0.928 (95% CI 0.869–0.987) at ECUH and 0.967 (95% CI 0.909–1.000) at UTSW; the small number of resistant BSI events limits the precision of these estimates. Summary characteristics of BSI episode cohorts are reported in Supplementary Table S11.

Calibration, Decision Curves, and Model Interpretation

Calibration plots reflected the prevalence shift across settings. In the internal temporal test set, occupied quantile bins spanned a broad probability range and remained close to the diagonal through the region containing most observations. In the external future hold-outs, most occupied bins sat in the low-risk region because resistance-event prevalence was close to 3%. On ECUH future hold-out data, mean predicted risk was 0.0367 against an observed event rate of 0.0344, with calibration slope 1.002. On UTSW future hold-out data, mean predicted risk was 0.0291 against an observed event rate of 0.0304, with calibration slope 1.097 (Fig. 3). Decision-curve analysis provided the complementary clinical-utility view. The model retained positive net benefit across a broad threshold range in the internal temporal test set and remained above treat none through the low-threshold region relevant for a rare resistant outcome in the two external cohorts. At the 0.90 sensitivity-floor operating point, model net benefit was 0.0177 at ECUH and 0.0206 at UTSW, compared with 0.0090 and 0.0093 for treat all and 0 for treat none. Alternative Endpoint and Subgroup Analyses

With intermediate/increased-exposure and resistant results defined as positive, the separately fitted model yielded a future event prevalence of 4.77% at ECUH and 4.40% at UTSW, with AUROC 0.763 and 0.841, respectively (Supplementary Table S15). At the 0.90 sensitivity floor, future sensitivity was 0.926 and 0.881, while 68.6% and 46.1% of cultures were flagged (Supplementary Table S16). In monomicrobial cultures, AUROC was 0.804 (95% CI 0.791–0.819) at ECUH and 0.879 (95% CI 0.846–0.903) at UTSW; corresponding polymicrobial AUROC was 0.702 and 0.815. Discrimination also varied by specimen source, with full source-stratified results reported in Supplementary Table S17. These source analyses refer to the primary microbiologically labeled cohort and not to the all-blood-culture BSI denominator.

Model interpretation was dominated by prior microbiological history, particularly previous Pseudomonas spp. isolation, with additional contributions from prior antimicrobial exposure and care context. These analyses describe model behavior rather than causal effects; detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S18 and Supplementary Figures S1–S6.

Discussion

ORDER-CARB retained useful discrimination after transport to two external hospital systems under a protocol designed to resemble clinical adoption rather than a conventional retrospective hold-out. The internal temporal test AUROC was 0.858, and, after site-specific recalibration, the future external hold-out area under the curve was 0.797 at ECUH and 0.876 at UTSW. These results are most relevant to a pre-susceptibility-testing order-entry task. The primary model was evaluated in a microbiologically labeled cohort of eligible urine, respiratory, and blood-culture orders that subsequently yielded a Gram-negative organism with interpretable carbapenem susceptibility. The secondary BSI analysis used a different denominator, including all blood-culture episodes, and a two-stage score combining the transported resistance estimate with a local estimate of Gram-negative BSI before local recalibration. Future BSI episode AUROC was 0.928 (95% CI 0.869–0.987) at ECUH and 0.967 (95% CI 0.909–1.000) at UTSW, although only 35 and 18, respectively, future carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative BSI events were observed.

The transport results show why local recalibration and local operating points matter. ECUH required slope expansion on the logit scale, whereas UTSW mainly required intercept correction. Recalibration did not meaningfully change AUROC because it was monotone, but it changed the probability scale used for calibration displays, thresholding, and local interpretation. The approximately tenfold difference between model-fitting prevalence (32.4%) and external prevalence near 3% materially affected positive predictive value and review workload. At the 0.90 sensitivity floor, positive predictive value was 5.7% at ECUH and 9.4% at UTSW, corresponding to 17.6 and 10.6 cultures, respectively, reviewed per true positive. ECUH would require review of 55.4% of eligible cultures, which would be difficult to sustain as an indiscriminate stewardship alert, whereas the corresponding UTSW review burden was 27.7%. Decision-curve analysis showed positive net benefit relative to treat-all and treat-none at this operating point, but the absolute benefit was small and should be interpreted together with workload. The secondary negative-predictive-value-constrained analysis illustrated the same point from another angle: in low-prevalence cohorts, negative predictive value can remain high even when sensitivity is too low for safe resistant-culture capture, so negative predictive value is best reported as an outcome rather than used alone to define rule-out thresholds. These findings indicate that implementation would require both local recalibration and a locally acceptable operating threshold rather than automatic treatment escalation.

In clinical practice, resistance prediction becomes relevant after the treating team has already judged a Gram-negative etiology sufficiently plausible to warrant Gram-negative coverage. At that point, the additional question is whether empirical therapy should also cover carbapenem-resistant organisms while susceptibility results are pending. A low predicted resistance risk at a high-sensitivity threshold could support stewardship or infectious diseases review before escalation to agents specifically active against carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative organisms, particularly when the patient is clinically stable and the ecological or toxicity costs of unnecessary broader therapy are meaningful. Conversely, in septic shock or other situations in which the consequence of initially inactive therapy is high, tolerance for false-negative classification would be much lower, and the score could only complement bedside assessment. A high-risk estimate should prompt earlier clinical and microbiological review rather than dictate a specific drug. In settings already enriched for Gram-negative infection, the absolute prevalence of carbapenem resistance may also be higher than in an unselected culture-order population, potentially improving positive predictive value and review efficiency; this remains to be tested prospectively. The two-stage BSI analysis is clinically relevant because it combines the resistance estimate with the probability of Gram-negative BSI across all blood-culture episodes.

The interpretability results support this operational picture and provide a clinically recognizable risk structure. Prior Pseudomonas spp. isolation dominated global importance and ablation analyses, consistent with microbiological memory as the model’s main anchor. Prior Escherichia spp. isolation and nitrofurantoin exposure contributed in the opposite direction in several analyses, suggesting a lower-risk recurrent urinary-culture phenotype after other history variables were considered. Prior Staphylococcus spp. isolation, prior organism counts, medication timing, intensive-care context, nursing home exposure, and ordering mode acted as healthcare-exposure and care-setting modifiers. The score therefore combines prior ecology, therapeutic pressure, and care context rather than relying mainly on broad demographic surrogates or documentation artifacts.

Several limitations follow from the retrospective design and the structure of the source data. The primary culture-order analysis was restricted to cultures that subsequently yielded a Gram-negative organism with interpretable carbapenem susceptibility; its predictive values and review burden therefore do not apply directly to all culture orders. The all-blood-culture BSI analysis provides a complementary estimate for bloodstream infection, but an equivalent all-culture analysis was not evaluated for urine or respiratory specimens. Culture positivity was not clinically adjudicated as infection versus colonization or contamination, and quantitative or semiquantitative growth information was not available to apply urine or respiratory growth thresholds. The monomicrobial analysis should therefore be interpreted as a microbiological sensitivity analysis rather than a substitute for clinical infection adjudication. The pipeline was built to mimic deployment but has not yet been tested prospectively in a live workflow. Prospective silent deployment remains necessary to verify real-time feature availability, monitor drift, and track sensitivity, fraction flagged, false negatives per 1000 culture orders or BSI episodes, and recalibration parameters before clinical activation. The unit of analysis was an order or episode rather than a first-ever patient encounter. This matches repeated clinical decision points, and uncertainty intervals were clustered by patient, but the reported performance is per-order or per-episode rather than per-patient performance. The BSI episode definition was limited to blood cultures collected within a 48-h window. This was intended to reflect prospective order-entry risk in the hospital population, but cultures collected beyond the 48-h window may still represent the same BSI episode in clinical practice. The three datasets came from a common ARMD ecosystem, which made schema harmonization feasible, but may make transport easier than across institutions with different extraction conventions [17–19]. The source datasets provided categorical susceptibility interpretations rather than a fully harmonized organism-specific breakpoint history or site-year CLSI/EUCAST record. Minimum inhibitory concentrations were not retrospectively reinterpreted under a common standard, and changes in categorical interpretation across organisms, sites, and years may have reduced outcome comparability. The alternative I+R versus S endpoint retained discrimination but required broader review at comparable sensitivity targets. Illness severity and adjudicated infection syndrome were not available as harmonized treatment-decision variables across datasets, and specimen-source analyses showed heterogeneous discrimination, particularly for respiratory cultures. Because the consequences of missing resistance differ across acuity and syndromes, a single operating threshold should not be assumed appropriate for all clinical contexts. No contemporaneous clinician risk estimates were recorded, precluding retrospective assessment of incremental value over experienced clinical judgment. Finally, ORDER-CARB is not a treatment recommender. It produces an early risk estimate whose usefulness depends on illness severity, infection source, local prevalence, stewardship practice, available alternative drugs, and the clinical consequences of false negatives and false positives.

Overall, ORDER-CARB demonstrated encouraging transportability across two independent healthcare systems and retained discrimination for early carbapenem-resistance risk stratification. The marked external prevalence shift, however, substantially changed positive predictive value and stewardship workload. These findings support further evaluation of the score as an early aid to resistance prediction while susceptibility results are pending, not as a stand-alone treatment rule. Prospective real-world validation incorporating illness severity, infection syndrome, local epidemiology, and comparison with clinical assessment will be essential to establish whether ORDER-CARB could improve empiric treatment and stewardship decisions.
