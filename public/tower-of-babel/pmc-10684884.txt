Protocol for the isolation and culture of mouse dorsal root ganglion neurons for imaging applications
Authors: Patrick R Smith, Angela Meyer, Sarah Loerch, Zachary T Campbell
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummarySensory neurons play pervasive roles throughout biology. In vitro studies to probe their functions hinge on the successful application of primary cell culture. Here, we present a protocol for the isolation and culture of mouse dorsal root ganglion neurons for imaging applications. We describe steps for extracting dorsal root ganglia, preparing cultures, maintaining them for days in vitro, and performing immunocytochemical labeling. We also include special considerations with respect to additional downstream applications.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Smith et al. (2021).1Subject areas: Cell culture, Cell isolation, Cell-based Assays, Neuroscience

Before you begin

This protocol describes the specific steps for collecting and culturing primary dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons from mice. It is important to note that all neurons are post-mitotic. Thus, these cultures cannot be passaged or expanded – the number of neurons at the time of the experiment will never exceed the number initially recovered. It is therefore critical to plan experiments ahead of time with respect to scale and timing. Users of this protocol should ensure availability of animals of the appropriate age, sex, and genotype when planning experiments. The protocol below was optimized to maximize neuron yield and survival from DRG. However, following this protocol will not generate homogenous neuronal cultures as the DRG contains a heterogeneous population of cells (see limitations). Finally, this protocol was optimized for the preparation of primary DRG neuron cultures for immunocytochemistry, but has also been scaled to prepare primary cultures for a number of downstream applications. These include: immunoblots, RNA sequencing, ribosome profiling, and single-particle cryogenic electron microscopy.1,2,3,4,5,6,7 We note changes in scale required for these diverse applications in the protocol.

Despite strain differences, we find minimal changes in the yield of viable neurons between age-matched Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice.

CRITICAL: 5-Fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine is toxic if ingested and a skin and eye irritant. It is also a suspected mutagen. Always wear a lab coat, gloves, and eye protection when handling.

Alternatives: Cytosine arabinoside (AraC) is sometimes used instead of 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine. Due to the difference in mechanism of action, media supplemented with AraC does not require additional uridine. However, AraC is reportedly neurotoxic and must be used at low concentrations.10,11,12

Step-by-step method details

Dissection and harvesting of DRG

Timing: 1–2 h (depending on number of animals)

Cultures from 4 – 8 week old animals provide the best recovery of viable neurons. It is expected that each animal will yield approximately 50,000 cells, assuming cervical, thoracic, and lumbar DRG are collected. However, cell counting methods do not effectively distinguish between neurons and non-neuronal cells and thus are poor measures of sensory neuron yield. Details for estimating number of animals needed can be found in the "Before you begin” section. The yield of viable neurons is highly dependent on the accuracy and speed with which the experimenter can harvest DRG.

Note: The isolation of DRG from mice is a very precise and delicate procedure. If you do not have experience with fine dissections or surgeries, it is recommended that you perform a trial culture at a small scale before culturing cells for critical experiments. A similar protocol from Sleigh et al. contains excellent high resolution images and videos that can be a useful resource for experimenters unfamiliar with fine dissections or the relevant anatomy.13

Plating and culture

Timing: 45 min to 1 h

The plating method described here is optimized to concentrate DRG neurons in a localized area on the chosen culture dish, improving neuronal survival and efficiency of imaging.

22.Resuspend cells in enough media to plate as a single droplet per well. The volume will depend on the culture area and the number of cells plated. Note: To ensure the recovery of viable neurons, monitor the morphology of the cultures over time. This is easiest with a phase contrast microscope. After the first media change, neurons should be clearly visible and can be identified by their large circular soma. Non-neuronal cells will appear smaller, flatter, and have an elongated or triangular morphology. Neurites begin growing within hours, and should be visible on all neurons within a day, though they can be difficult to see with low magnification.

Note: A significant amount of debris will be present in the cell suspension upon plating. Much of this debris will initially be loosely attached to the plate, but should be almost entirely removed from the surface of the plate after the first media change. Some debris may be seen floating at the surface of the media. This will not affect the culture and will be removed with the next media change.

Note: The duration of the culture will depend on the experiment being performed. For example, to examine growth cones or axon regeneration, neurons may be cultured for 1–3 days. To examine a more mature state, neurons may be cultured for 6–9 days.

If stored at −20°C, slides can be imaged > 1 week after mounting. Many fluorophores are also stable for long periods (> 1 week) at 4°C. However, these recommendations should not supersede manufacturer’s recommended storage conditions for your reagents.

CRITICAL: Always perform all experimental conditions and controls for a replicate in parallel and always image them on the same day. Though fluorophores will be visible for > 1 week if properly stored, they will always exhibit some loss of fluorescence over time. If images from one experiment are acquired on different days, quantifications cannot be considered to be properly normalized.

CRITICAL: When collecting immunofluorescence data, it is necessary to use the chosen cell-type marker to set a region of interest (ROI) to define the area to be measured.

Expected outcomes

Following this protocol should yield an appropriate density of neurons for the purpose of imaging. Though glial cell proliferation is limited by the addition of 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine, some proliferation may be noticeable in older cultures (Figure 6B, DAPI). Morphology of soma will remain similar during the lifetime of the culture. However, neurite morphology will exhibit substantial changes as the culture matures. The neurites of DRG neurons cultured for 1–4 days before fixation will appear sparser and less ordered (Figure 6C, left panel). In cultures fixed after 5–7 days in vitro (d.i.v.), neurites are noticeably denser, exhibit brighter immunofluorescence for some markers (ex. Peripherin), and appear to project in straighter paths, often with groups of nearby neurites oriented similarly (Figure 6C, right panel).

Limitations

The addition of 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine or AraC will limit the proliferation of non-neuronal cells in culture, but they will not be eliminated. Additionally, DRGs harbor several different classes of neurons. Primary DRG cultures are therefore always heterogenous cell populations. This should be taken into account when planning downstream experiments and reporting data. A variety of protocols have been reported for DRG cultures for a range of applications including peptide release14 and viral infection.15 Our protocol is optimized for immunofluorescence experiments. We encourage investigators to modify this protocol as needed for their particular downstream application.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1: Fungal contamination

Fungal contamination is a common problem of primary DRG cultures. Contaminated cultures will exhibit fibrous growths if viewed under a microscope. Contamination is usually visible to the naked eye within 2 days and appears as “fuzzy” growth in the media, often floating at the surface. The major source of fungal contamination is the animals’ fur. The penicillin/streptomycin antibiotics added to the culture media do not prevent this form of contamination. Though fungal contamination is common, it can be prevented with simple precautions.

Potential solutions

•Be sure to keep the animals’ fur soaked in 70% ethanol throughout the dissection procedure (Steps 1–3).

Be sure to keep the animals’ fur soaked in 70% ethanol throughout the dissection procedure (Steps 1–3).

•Wash the vertebral column with ddH2O after removal to rid it of fur (after Step 4).

Wash the vertebral column with ddH2O after removal to rid it of fur (after Step 4).

•Move to a clean workspace and change gloves after the vertebral column is removed (after Step 4).

Move to a clean workspace and change gloves after the vertebral column is removed (after Step 4).

Problem 2: Poor neuron recovery

If a culture appears to have numerous non-neuronal cells but relatively few neurons, there is likely a problem with neuron recovery. This is typically noticed after the first media change. Low density at plating could be caused by poor DRG harvesting, or insufficient resuspension of digested tissue. For experimenters new to this protocol, the main causes of poor neuron recovery are failure to properly identify and harvest DRG, damaging the DRG during harvesting, and allowing the tissues to dry during harvesting. These issues generally improve with experience. The temperature at which the dissociation steps are performed is critical. Performing incubations at elevated temperatures can cause cell death.

Potential solutions

•When harvesting DRGs (Step 5), it may be helpful to use a stereomicroscope or magnifying goggles, particularly for beginners.

When harvesting DRGs (Step 5), it may be helpful to use a stereomicroscope or magnifying goggles, particularly for beginners.

•Be sure to fully resuspend the digested tissues in Solution T, and completely wash the cell strainer (Step 8, 9).

Be sure to fully resuspend the digested tissues in Solution T, and completely wash the cell strainer (Step 8, 9).

•Check that the water bath is filled and is stably maintaining 37°C the day before culturing. Do not add water or adjust the temperature within a few hours of tissue dissociation as many water baths will overshoot the set temperature before stabilizing at 37°C.

Check that the water bath is filled and is stably maintaining 37°C the day before culturing. Do not add water or adjust the temperature within a few hours of tissue dissociation as many water baths will overshoot the set temperature before stabilizing at 37°C.

Problem 3: Neuron detachment

During the culture period, neurons may detach while non-neuronal cells remain viable. Loss of neurons after plating may be caused by aggressive media changes, drying of cultures during media changes, or improper PDL coating (usually due to expired PDL).

Potential solutions

•When aspirating media (Step 26), vacuum from the top of the media and do not place the vacuum tip too close to the cells. It may help to tilt the plate at an angle towards the vacuum. Add media slowly from the side wall of the culture vessel so as to not wash neurons off of the plate.

•If changing media for several wells, only change media from a few wells at a time to avoid drying. A small amount of media may be left in each well to further prevent drying (Step 26).

•DRG fibers are lipid-rich, particularly the largest fibers associated with the lumbar DRG. If DRG fibers are not removed (Step 6e), this can add excess debris and glial cells to the cell suspension which may affect attachment of neurons at plating. It is best practice to always remove the fibers during harvesting.

•Be sure PDL has not expired (Step 2). Attachment efficiency is not affected by moderate changes in PDL concentration, but may be impacted if off by an order of magnitude or more. Always double check when calculating PDL amount for a given surface area.

Problem 4: Loss of viability during culture

After cells have attached, cultures can lose viability afterwards as evidenced by subsequent detachment. This is often due to the incubator conditions while culturing (Step 26), and will affect both neurons and most non-neuronal cells. This is easily prevented by having redundant measures of temperature and atmospheric conditions and keeping to a regular schedule of calibrating the CO2 and replacing the incubator water.

Problem 5: Difficulties with protein or RNA detection after culture

When processing cultures to collect protein or RNA, there can be signal loss that is typically the result of other forms of contamination. The potential solutions below are practices to be used generally and do not refer to specific steps in this protocol.

Potential solutions

•Use filtered tips and RNase/protease free solutions.

•Maintain a clean work environment, particularly when working with RNA.

•Routinely clean pipettes with strong detergents or cleaning detergents and rinse with distilled water afterward.
