Genome-wide analysis of the Amorphophallus konjac AkCSLA gene family and its functional characterization in drought tolerance of transgenic arabidopsis
Authors: Changxin Luo, Shicheng Luo, Zhe Chen, Rui Yang, Xingfen He, Honglong Chu, Zhumei Li, Wei Li, Yumei Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmorphophallus konjac (A. konjac), a perennial tuberous plant, is widely cultivated for its high konjac glucomannan (KGM) content, a heteropolysaccharide with diverse applications. The cellulose synthase-like (CSL) gene family is known to be a group of processive glycan synthases involved in the synthesis of cell-wall polysaccharides and plays an important role in the biological process of KGM. However, in A. konjac the classification, structure, and function of the AkCSLA superfamily have been studied very little.ResultsBioinformatics methods were used to identify the 11 AkCSLA genes from the whole genome of Amorphophallus konjac and to systematically analyze their characteristics, phylogenetic evolution, promoter cis-elements, expression patterns, and subcellular locations. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the AkCSLA gene family can be divided into three subfamilies (Groups I- III), which have close relationships with Arabidopsis. The promoters of most AkCSLA family members contain MBS elements and ABA response elements. Analysis of expression patterns in different tissues showed that most AkCSLAs are highly expressed in the corms. Notably, PEG6000 induced down-regulation of the expression of most AkCSLAs, including AkCSLA11. Subcellular localization results showed that AkCSLA11 was localized to the plasma membrane, Golgi apparatus and endoplasmic reticulum. Transgenic Arabidopsis experiments demonstrated that overexpression of AkCSLA11 reduced the plant’s drought tolerance. This overexpression also inhibited the expression of drought response genes and altered the sugar components of the cell wall. These findings provide new insights into the response mechanisms of A. konjac to drought stress and may offer potential genetic resources for improving crop drought resistance.ConclusionIn conclusion, the study reveals that the AkCSLA11 gene from A. konjac negatively impacts drought tolerance when overexpressed in Arabidopsis. This discovery provides valuable insights into the mechanisms of plant response to drought stress and may guide future research on crop improvement for enhanced resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05747-5.Keywords: Amorphophallus konjac, AkCSLA gene family, Drought tolerance, Gene expression

Background

Konjac, with the by name of Moyu in China and Juruo in Japan, is a perennial tuberous plant of the Araceae family [1]. Amorphophallus konjac (A. konjac) holds great significance as it is the most pivotal and commonly used species within this genus. The spathe of A. konjac has a vivid deep purple-red hue; however, the oval-shaped fruit chamber changes from green to orange as the fruit ripens. Because of its exceptional adaptability, A. konjac cultivation has expanded beyond China and Japan to Southeast Asian countries such as Thailand and Indonesia [2–5]. Konjac glucomannan (KGM) is a naturally occurring macromolecular heteropolysaccharide extracted from konjac corms. This heteropolysaccharide consists of D-glucose and D-mannose units, connected by β-1, 4-glycosidic linkages, offering potential applications across various domains [1, 6]. The primary hemicellulose component in A. konjac cell walls is KGM, with its concentration in corms ranging between 50% and 70% [7, 8]. Hydrogels derived from KGM have been predominantly utilized in the food industry, biomedical fields, and water purification processes. Due to its prebiotic effects and properties such as anti-obesity, antioxidant, and antibacterial, KGM provides numerous health benefits [9–12]. However, A. konjac cultivation has been considerably affected by increasing environmental pollution, which has contributed to extreme temperatures and the greenhouse effect-induced cold damage, water scarcity–triggered drought, and contamination of irrigation water. The main manifestations include reduced resistance of the plants to abiotic stress, slow growth and development, severe sunburn of leaves under high-temperature stress, increased susceptibility to white rot and soft rot diseases, decreased yield, and rotting of tubers during digging, harvesting, and storage [13–16].

Drought stress is a major environmental factor that restricts plant growth, development, and distribution. Throughout extended evolution periods, plant cells have withstood drought stress by sustaining regular metabolic functions through regulatory mechanisms, such as augmenting osmotic regulatory compounds and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity [17]. Abscisic acid (ABA), a vital phytohormone, significantly impacts signal transduction and regulates various processes essential for plant growth, development, and stress tolerance. These processes include seed dormancy, germination, stomatal closure, and responses to abiotic stress [18]. Various elements involved in ABA signal transduction are essential in numerous stages of the plant life processes [19]. Drought or salt stress generally triggers extensive transcriptional reprogramming in plants, which initiates mechanisms that protect plants against damage caused by these environmental stress [18]. Various transcription factors (e.g., CBF, MYB, WRKY, bZIP, bHLH, HSF2, NAC, AP2/ERF, and DREB) have been recognized as critical controllers of plant reactions to nonliving stress. These regulators play a crucial role in harmonizing the activation of multiple target genes, consequently enhancing the resilience of plants facing different ecological adversities like high temperatures, lack of water, high salinity, low temperatures, and oxidative stress [19–25].

Around 33% of the cell wall in plants is made up of hemicellulose, a diverse non-cellulosic polysaccharide that is complex in nature [15]. Members of the cellulose synthase (CESA) and cellulose synthase-like (CSL) families encode glycosyltransferases that synthesize the β-1, 4-linked glycan backbones of cellulose and most hemicellulosic polysaccharides that comprise plant cell walls [26, 27]. CSL proteins can be subdivided into nine subfamilies: CSLA, CSLB, CSLC, CSLD, CSLE, CSLF, CSLG, CSLH, and CSLJ. Differences have been reported between monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous CSL gene families [26, 28]. The Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis) CSL gene family can be classified into six groups: CSLA, CSLB, CSLC, CSLD, CSLE, and CSLG. Two additional groups (CSLF and CSLH) have been exclusively identified in grasses. A third group, CSLJ, has been identified in the genomes of both grasses and certain dicots [26, 29–31]. Plant CSL genes are essential for multiple development processes, such as maintaining root hair integrity, regulating organ size, controlling tiller numbers, and preserving adhesive mucilage structure [32–36]. CSLs are linked with plant resilience or adaptability to both biotic and abiotic stresses, including those caused by salt, boron, heavy metals, and pathogen infections [36, 37].

The CSLA gene family plays a crucial role in mannan biosynthesis across a variety of plant species. The specific type of mannan that is synthesized is determined by the substrate that is available [38–41]. Synthesis of recombinant CSLA proteins indicates that the enzymatic activity can be conferred to the foreign host through the expression of a single CSLA protein and that a single enzyme can facilitate the incorporation of mannose and glucose into the glucomannan chain [42, 43]. In Arabidopsis, the CSLA gene family has nine members. A study investigating CSLA mutants and the Arabidopsis plants overexpressing CSLA genes reported that CSLA proteins can synthesize glucomannan in vivo [44]. The β-linked mannan polymers were synthesized through the heterologous expression of recombinant Arabidopsis CSLA proteins in insect cell culture, suggesting that CSLAs are responsible for encoding mannan synthases [40]. The glucomannan content was found to be low in the Arabidopsis csla2 csla3 csla9 triple mutants, whereas CSLA9 overexpression was found to increase the glucomannan content [44]. Mutants of Arabidopsis CSLA7 showed hindered pollen tube development and embryogenesis. This suggests that CSLAs, which play a role in producing mannan hemicellulosic polysaccharides, are vital contributors in maintaining the cell wall’s role in these key developmental processes [44]. To summarize, AkCSLA plays a substantial role in the glucomannan synthesis pathway. However, detailed studies on the biological functions of A. konjac AkCSLA genes under abiotic stress conditions are currently lacking.

We here conducted a genome-wide analysis of the AkCSLA superfamily in A. konjac. The members of the AkCSLA superfamily in A. konjac were identified and characterized. Additionally, the phylogenetic relationship between AkCSLAs in A. konjac and Arabidopsis was studied. To explore the potential functions of the AkCSLA superfamily in A. konjac growth and development, we conducted cis-acting element prediction and subcellular localization analysis. In order to examine the patterns of AkCSLA gene expression across various tissues, real-time quantitative PCR trials were executed. Additionally, the evaluation of AkCSLA gene expression patterns was performed under conditions of drought stress. The phenotype and the expression levels of drought-related marker genes were analyzed under drought stress treatment using transgenic Arabidopsis plants that were engineered to overexpress AkCSLA11 (referred to as OE-AkCSLA11). The findings can be useful in understanding the AkCSLA11 functions and action mechanism under drought stress. This study provides a basis for understanding A. konjac growth and development, and its response to drought stress, thereby laying the foundation for future studies exploring the functional roles of the AkCSLA gene family in A. konjac.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growing conditions

The experiment involved the use of Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0, Nicotiana benthamiana, and Amorphophallus konjac. Seeds of Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (https://abrc.osu.edu/). Nicotiana benthamiana seeds were obtained from our laboratory. Since Fuyuan County (25°35′36″ N, 104°5′32″ E) in Yunnan Province is one of the largest growing areas for Amorphophallus konjac in China, we selected local varieties that are representative of the region to carry out the experiment. The Amorphophallus konjac seeds came from the Fuyuan Konjac Research Institute of Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Fuyuan County, Yunnan Province, China. The Arabidopsis seedlings were grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar medium (0.8% concentration) for approximately 5 to 7 days prior to being transferred to soil. In this setup, they were exposed to a 16-hour light and 8-hour dark cycle at 23℃, with 60% relative humidity, and received a light intensity of around 100 micromoles per square meter per second for a duration of 3 to 4 weeks. Similarly, N. benthamiana plants were planted in soil and subjected to the same 16-hour light and 8-hour dark cycle, at a temperature of 28 ℃, along with a relative humidity of 60% for about 3 to 4 weeks. The A. konjac seeds were disinfected before being placed on MS agar medium (0.8% concentration) for 3 to 4 months under conditions of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness at 23℃.

Genome-wide identification of A. konjac CSLA genes

The genome sequence of A. konjac was downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [5]. Amino acid sequences of the Arabidopsis CSLA family genes were obtained from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The ExPASy website (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was employed to predict the physicochemical properties of the AkCSLA genes, with the predictions being. Specifically, the protein size, molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point (theoretical pI), aliphatic index, and GRAVY index were evaluated.

Phylogenetic analysis and gene structure

In order to fully analyze the evolutionary connection between the CSLA family in A. konjac and Arabidopsis, a tree without roots was built by utilizing the amino acid sequences of CSLA proteins from both species [45]. Furthermore, the structure of the gene was displayed visually using the Gene Structure Display Server GSDS2.0. (http://gsds.gaolab.org/Gsd.sabout.Php).

Conserved motif and cis-acting element analysis

The AkCSLA protein sequences were analyzed for conserved motifs using the online tool Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation (MEME) Version 5.5.4 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), with the limit set to a maximum of 10 motifs. To predict cis-acting elements, the 2000-bp upstream genomic DNA sequences were referenced against the PlantCARE database (Table S1) (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). TBtools was then used to visualize the results [46].

Expression analysis of AkCSLAs in A. konjac

The AkCSLA transcript levels in the leaves, stems, corms, and fibrous roots of A. konjac plants, and variations in these levels in response to PEG6000-induced stress were analyzed through qRT-PCR. In order to induce stress, the roots of in vitro-grown plants that were 4 weeks old after germination were immersed in a 1/2 MS solution containing 20% PEG6000. The whole plants were sampled at 0, 1, 3, and 6 h after treatments, and their AkCSLA expression levels were subsequently analyzed.

To extract total RNA from plant samples stored at -80 °C, the Quick RNA isolation Kit (Huayueyang, Beijing, China) was utilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cDNA synthesis, the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) was used. The primer sequences can be found in Table S2. The eukaryotic initiation factor 4 A (AkEIF4A) served as the internal control gene [47]. Real-time PCR amplification was carried out on the Roche LightCycler® 96 Instrument using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The reaction mixture included 10 µL of SYBR Master Mix buffer, 0.8 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of template, and 6.4 µL of ddH2O. The following program was used: 30 s at 95 ℃, 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 15 s, 60 ℃ for 30 s, and a final step at 72 ℃ for 20 s. The expression levels of each gene were determined in three biological replicates and analyzed using the 2−∆∆Ct method [48].

Plasmid construction and subcellular localization analysis

The CDS containing a stop codon for AkCSLA11 was amplified and inserted into a pCAMBIA1300 vector for transient expression along with an eGFP fluorescent marker and UBQ10 promoter. Subsequently, the resulting plasmid was transferred to Agrobacterium GV3101 using the conventional freeze-thaw method. Following this, either empty pCAMBIA1300-UBQ10-eGFP or pCAMBIA1300-UBQ10-eGFP-AkCSLA11 GV3101 was promptly introduced into 4-week-old N. benthamiana leaves using an expression buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 100 µM acetosyringone). Using the same method, pCAMBIA1300-UBQ10-eGFP-AkCSLA11 was introduced into 4-week-old benthamiana leaves together with Golgi marker (G-rk) and ER marker (ER-rk) respectively. After the infiltrated N. benthamiana plants were incubated under low light conditions for 48 h, fluorescent signals from eGFP and RFP were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM600 confocal laser scanning microscope; SOPTOP).

Construction of transgenic Arabidopsis plants and drought treatments

The recombinant plasmid pCAMBIA1300-UBQ10-eGFP-AkCSLA11 used for the subcellular localization analysis was transformed into GV3101, and AkCSLA11-overexpressing A. thaliana lines were obtained through Agrobacterium-mediated flower impregnation. The T1 transgenic A. thaliana seeds were planted in a 1/2 MS plate containing 50 mg/µL hygromycin. Overexpressing lines from the T2 generation segregating in the 3:1 ratio were obtained using the same screening method. Through RT-PCR, we selected two homozygous lines with relatively moderate AkCSLA11 expression levels for further functional analysis. During the drought experiment, seedlings were cultivated in soil under optimal watering for a 14-day period, followed by a period of 10–15 days without water. The response of plant phenotypes to the stress of drought was documented and analyzed, as outlined in earlier studies [49].

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Arabidopsis seedlings were grown on MS agar medium (0.8%, w/v) for 7 days under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle at 22 °C. Following this, the 7-day-old seedlings were transplanted into soil and allowed to mature for an additional 2 weeks. Total RNA was isolated from the leaves of seedlings grown under normal conditions and subjected to drought stress using the MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The RNA was then converted to cDNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). qRT-PCR analysis was performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) using the internal control AtActin. The primers for stress-related gene analysis in this study were obtained from previous research [50].

Approximately 5 mg of sample was hydrolyzed with trifluoroacetic acid (2 M) at 121 °C for 2 h in a sealed tube. Dry the sample with nitrogen. Add methanol to wash, then blow dry, repeat methanol wash 2–3 times. The residue was re-dissolved in deionized water and filtered through 0.22 μm microporous filtering film for measurement. The sample extracts were analyzed by high-performance anion-exchange chromatography (HPAEC) on a CarboPac PA-20 anion-exchange column (3 by 150 mm; Dionex) using a pulsed amperometric detector (PAD; Dionex ICS 5000 + system). Data were acquired on the ICS5000+ (Thermo Scientific), and processed using chromeleon 7.2 CDS (Thermo Scientific). Quantified data were output into excel format. Results

Identification and classification of AkCSLA genes

Through the utilization of the BLASTp method, we successfully identified 11 AkCSLA genes in A. konjac. These genes are designated as AkCSLA 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, and 14 (Table S1). The research provided comprehensive details about the gene family, covering aspects such as their names, genome serial numbers, coding DNA sequence (CDS) lengths, amino acid numbers, molecular weights, isoelectric points, aliphatic indices, and Grand Average of Hydropathy (GRAVY) values (Table 1). The lengths of their corresponding proteins were in the range of 530–608 amino acids, with molecular weights spanning from 60.757 kDa (AkCSLA12) to 68.539 kDa (AkCSLA9). The predicted pI value ranged from 8.35 (AkCSLA9) to 9.20 (AkCSLA12). The aliphatic index ranged from 94.54 (AkCSLA9) to 102.71 (AkCSLA1). The predicted GRAVY was 0.116–0.248, and all the 11 AkCSLAs were classified as hydrophobic proteins.

Analysis of AkCSLA gene promoter cis-acting elements, conserved protein structure, and gene structure

Full-length AkCSLA sequences were aligned, and accordingly, an unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA11 software. Based on the amino acid sequences of 9 Arabidopsis AtCSLAs and 11 AkCSLAs, a phylogenetic tree of the CSLA family was constructed. According to the topological structure of the maximum likelihood tree and 1000 repeated bootstrap analyses, these proteins were grouped into four categories. In Arabidopsis, the AtCSLA proteins, except AtCSLA2 and AtCSLA9, were primarily clustered within group IV; AtCSLA2 and AtCSLA9 were assigned to group III. In A. konjac, AkCSLA11, AkCSLA12, and AkCSLA13 were classified into group III, and the remaining seven AkCSLAs were classified into groups I and II (Fig. S1). To ascertain the role of AkCSLAs in the abiotic stress response of plants, we first analyzed AkCSLA promoter regions by using PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). The cis-acting element analysis unveiled that AkCSLAs primarily participate in functions related to environmental stress, abiotic stress, hormone response, and light response, among others. Photosensitivity-related G-Box elements, abscisic acid responsiveness elements (ABRE), methyl jasmonate response-linked CGTCA-motif, auxin response-related TGA element, MBS (drought-inducible) cis-acting elements, and low-temperature response-associated elements were identified as the main cis-acting elements. Additionally, LTR elements and the cis-regulatory elements ABREs and TCAs are pivotal in plant resistance to biotic/abiotic stressors and other biological pathways (Fig. 1A). The MEME analysis was conducted to identify putative motifs in A. konjac AkCSLA genes. In total, 10 motifs were analyzed in 11 AkCSLA proteins, and their distribution, types, and numbers were generally consistent. An exon–intron structure map was graphically plotted to investigate the evolutionary relationship and structural diversity among A. konjac AkCSLA genes (Fig. 1B). The results showed that each gene typically consists of 8–10 exons. Specifically, AkCSLA2, 3, 5, 6, and 8 have 10 exons each; AkCSLA1, 9, 11, 12, and 14 contain nine exons, and AkCSLA13 comprises eight exons. Notably, we found that AkCSLA9 has a longer intron. Intron position and length in the remaining AkCSLA genes were relatively consistent (Fig. 1C).

Analysis of AkCSLA expression patterns in the A. konjac plant tissues

AkCSLA expression patterns in various A. konjac tissues, such as leaves, stems, corms, and fibrous roots, were analyzed using real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) (Fig. 2). AkCSLA6 and AkCSLA9 showed elevated levels of expression in the stems, while AkCSLA12 was predominantly found in the fibrous roots. The remaining AkCSLA genes expressed primarily in the corms. Overall, most AkCSLAs highly expressed in the corms, aligning with the fact that KGM predominantly accumulates in corms.

PEG6000 can induce the expression of AkCSLA genes

According to the promoter analysis, the AkCSLA promoter region contained drought stress-related cis-acting elements. To further understand the biological functions of AkCSLAs, their expression was analyzed under drought stress conditions, including treatment with 20% PEG6000 for 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. PEG6000 treatment significantly induced AkCSLA expression. Notably, AkCSLA1 and AkCSLA8 exhibited marked upregulation, with expression levels increasing by 4.9- and 3.3-fold, respectively, at the 6 h. AkCSLA14 displayed downregulation at 1 and 3 h, followed by restoration at 6 h. After PEG6000 treatment for 1,3, and 6 h, changes in down-regulated expression levels of the remaining AkCSLAs were observed. Most AkCSLA genes showed upregulation at 12 h (Fig. 3). Together, these findings highlight the distinct functions of the AkCSLA gene family and suggest their potential involvement in coping with and tolerating drought stress. The expression level of AkCSLAA11 was down-regulated by over three times after 1, 3, and 6 h of PEG6000 treatment compared to untreated samples. However, expression was up-regulated and reached a maximum at 12 h, and returned to normal levels at 24 h. AkCSLA11 will be further investigated for its potential in drought resistance research.

Subcellular localization analysis of AkCSLA11

To determine the location of AkCSLAs functions, a transient gene expression experiment was conducted. Initially, the complete CDS sequences of AkCSLA11, including the stop codon, were cloned (Fig. S2). Vectors for pCAMBIA1300-UBQ10-eGFP-AkCSLA11 fusion proteins were generated and introduced into the Nicotiana benthamiana leaves by using the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. According to the subcellular localization analysis, AkCSLA11 localized in the cell membrane and displayed many punctate localizations in the cells (Fig. S3). To further investigate the specific localization of AKCSLA11 within the endomembrane system, eGFP-AkCSLA11 was co-expressed with the Golgi marker G-rk and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker ER-rk in Nicotiana benthamiana leaf cells, respectively. Observations revealed that eGFP-AkCSLA11 exhibits partial colocalization with both G-rk and ER-rk (Fig. 4). These results indicate that AkCSLA11 is primarily localized in cell membranes, Golgi, and ER.

AkCSLA11-overexpressing Arabidopsis lines increases plant sensitivity to drought stress

To determine whether AkCSLA11 is involved in plant response to abiotic stress, we constructed the AkCSLA11-containing transformation vector pCAMBIA1300-UBQ10-eGFP-AkCSLA11 under the control of UBQ10-enhanced promoters and used Arabidopsis for transfection. In total, more than 10 homozygous lines of T3 generation were obtained after two rounds of screening. Two lines were randomly selected to extract RNA for the expression and phenotypic analyses. The RT-PCR assay unveiled that AkCSLA11 did not express in wild-type (WT) plants, but expressed to varying degrees in the overexpressing lines OE-AkCSLA11-1 and OE-AkCSLA11-2 (Fig. 5A, S4). To determine whether AkCSLA11 is actually involved in the response to abiotic stress, a WT, OE-AkCSLA11-1, and OE-AkCSLA11-2 lines were used to perform stress-related assays. Compared with the WT plants, the OE-AkCSLA11-1, and OE-AkCSLA11-2 had a higher rate of water loss during water stress (Fig. 5B, C). To assess the sensitivity of plants growing in soil to drought stress and confirm their drought resistance, WT, OE-AkCSLA11-1, and OE-AkCSLA11-2 plants were exposed to normal conditions. Subsequently, all plants were placed under dry conditions for an additional 14 days and then rewatered for 14 days. The study analyzed the survival rate of plants by assessing their capacity to generate new standard leaves and recover normal growth. The OE-AkCSLA11 plants exhibited notably lower survival rates compared to the wild-type plants (Fig. 5D, E). These results indicate that AkCSLA11 plays an important role in the regulation of water loss during the plant response to drought stress.

AkCSLA11 overexpression suppresses the expression of stress-responsive genes in Arabidopsis

Due to the observation that OE-AkCSLA11 Arabidopsis plants demonstrated reduced drought resilience, we aimed to ascertain if drought conditions influenced the expression of drought-responsive genes in these plants. The expression profiles of several drought-responsive marker genes, including AtCOR15A, AtRAB18, AtSnRK2.6, AtP5CS, AtP5CS1, and AtAREB1, were assessed in OE-AkCSLA11-1, OE-AkCSLA11-2, and wild-type plants using qRT-PCR. Under standard conditions, there was no observable difference in the expression of these genes between wild-type and overexpressing lines. Notably, the expression of these genes increased significantly in the wild type under drought stress. Nevertheless, the OE-AkCSLA11-1 and OE-AkCSLA11-2 lines exhibited significantly lower expression levels of these genes compared to wild-type plants under drought conditions (Fig. 6). These findings suggest that overexpression of AkCSLA11 in Arabidopsis under drought conditions leads to a decrease in the expression of certain stress-responsive genes.

Alteration of cell wall composition in AkCSALA11 overexpressing Arabidosis

The metabolism of the cell wall is a vital factor in how plants respond to environmental stresses, and this process is facilitated by a range of proteins that modify the cell wall. These proteins are essential in adjusting the structure and characteristics of the cell wall, leading to alterations in cell growth and expansion as plants acclimate to these challenges [51]. Polysaccharides in the cell wall play a role in promoting plant growth as well as in providing resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses. Changes in the availability of one or more nucleotide sugars required for sugar biosynthesis can lead to plastic changes in the cell wall [52]. We performed a quantitative analysis of nucleotide sugars (including fucose, rhamnose, arabinose, galactose, glucose, xylose, galacturonic acid, mannose) on 4-week-old Arabidopsis plants of WT, OE-AkCSLA11-1, and OE-AkCSLA11-2 (Fig. 7). The results showed glucose, galacturonic acid and mannose levels increased by approximately 10–40% in OE-AkCSLA11-1 and OE-AkCSLA11-2 plants compared to WT. However, no significant changes in the fucose, rhamnose, arabinose, galactose, and xylose levels. In conclusion, changes in nucleotide sugar levels in OE-AkCSLA11 may lead to alterations in cell wall plasticity, thereby increasing the plant’s sensitivity to drought stress.

Discussion

The growth and development of plants are significantly hindered by drought conditions. Fortunately, advancements in genetic engineering have made it possible to grow plants that are resistant to drought. This research project focused on the discovery of a specific gene, AkCSLA11, which is downregulated in response to drought stress. This gene exhibited varying expression levels among different A. konjac tissues. A. konjac seedlings subjected to drought stress treatment exhibited a lower expression level of AkCSLA11 than the seedlings grown under normal conditions (Fig. 2I), which indicates the potential negative role of AkCSLA11 in response to drought stress in A. konjac.

Rapid development has recently been made in various omics technologies, such as genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. These advancements have immensely facilitated precision genetic breeding techniques for crop plants [53]. Konjac corms are rich in KGM, which offers multiple health benefits, including weight reduction, blood lipid regulation, blood sugar control, intestinal health promotion, immune system enhancement, and potential anticancer properties [54]. Moreover, owing to its high viscosity and water absorption capacity, KGM can be used as an emulsifying, water-retaining, and thickening agent. It has been extensively used for various applications, including health care products, medicines, chemicals, petroleum, textiles, and food production [11, 55]. The identification of some KGM synthesis-associated genes, such as CSLA, MSR1, CSLD, GMPP, and PMM provide a direct and effective approach for enhancing the KGM yield and improving its quality in A. konjac [55, 56]. We observed that AkCSLA6 and AkCSLA9 expression levels were relatively high in the stems; however, other genes exhibited the highest expression levels in the corms (Fig. 2). This result corroborates the fact that AkCSLAs participate in the synthesis of KGM that primarily occurs in the A. konjac corms.

The AkCSLA gene family is a crucial player in plant growth and development [43]. CSLA genes have been identified in various plant species, including Arabidopsis, Orchidaceae, Dendrobium catenatum, rice, Populus Trichocarpa, and Physcomitrella patens [41, 57, 58]. CSLA family members can be found within the Golgi bodies and play a role in mannan polysaccharide synthesis [31, 44]. During transient AkCSLA11 expression in tobacco leaf epidermal cells, AkCSLA11 localized mainly in cell membranes, Golgi, and ER (Fig. 4).

This study successfully identified 11 AkCSLA genes in A. konjac. The phylogenetic analysis unveiled that the functions of AkCSLA proteins in A. konjac significantly diverged during evolution compared with those in Arabidopsis. Notably, the motif composition and exon–intron structures of AkCSLA genes have remained highly conserved. The A. konjac CSLA genes play a crucial role in various hormone regulatory pathways and in the defense against stress-induced challenges (Fig. 1). In this study, PEG6000 treatment induced the expression of most CSLA genes in A. konjac. These results indicated that AkCSLA genes are widely involved in biological and abiotic stress responses, thereby offering a robust basis for further analysis of their functions in drought tolerance. AkCSLA11-overexpressing Arabidopsis plants showed decreased drought resistance under drought stress (Fig. 5). In addition, an investigation was conducted to determine if variations were present in the expression of stress-response genes in the wild-type and AkCSLA11 overexpression lines when subjected to drought conditions. As expected, during drought stress, the levels of expression for AtCOR15A, AtRAB18, AtSnRK2.6, AtP5CS, AtP5CS1, and AtAREB1 were significantly decreased in the overexpressing lines compared to the wild-type plants. This suggests a reduction in the drought stress response of the AkCSLA11 overexpressing line. Members of the CSLA family are involved in the synthesis of mannan and glucomannan, which are important components of plant cell walls. Cell wall plasticity plays an important role in plant nutritional and reproductive development under drought conditions, and is a potential trait for crop yield increase under drought conditions [59, 60]. AkCSLA11-overexpressing Arabidopsis plants showed glucose, galacturonic acid and mannose levels increased by approximately 10–40% (Fig. 7). Therefore, CSLAs may regulate drought tolerance by altering the plasticity of cell walls. In conclusion, our findings suggest that AkCSLA11 acts as a negative regulator of drought response in transgenic Arabidopsis plants by controlling the expression of stress-related genes and the content of sugar components in cell walls, leading to a better understanding of the drought response mechanism in A. konjac (Fig. 8).

Simplified model of AkCSLA11 regulating drought stress response. Drought stress inhibited the expression of AkCSLA11, which affected the structure of cell wall and the expression of stress response genes, and promoted the resistance of negatively regulated plants to drought

Although this study has provided new insights into the role of AkCSLA11 in the response to drought stress, we also recognize the limitations that exist and believe that these limitations provide direction for our future research. First, the exact mechanism by which AkCSLA11 regulates the expression of drought-responsive genes is not yet clear. To gain a deeper understanding, future studies will need to employ molecular biology and genetic methods, such as transcriptome analysis, protein-interaction studies, and mutant analysis, to reveal how AkCSLA11 affects the expression of downstream genes and the biosynthesis of the cell wall. Secondly, our research focused on the singular role of AkCSLA11, neglecting the potential synergistic or antagonistic effects of other members of the CSLA gene family. Considering the complex interactions that may exist among family members, future research should consider the collective effects of these genes and how they collectively influence the plant’s response to drought stress. This may involve multi-gene engineering or systems biology approaches to comprehensively assess the role of the CSLA gene family in plant physiology. Furthermore, our study was conducted in the model plant Arabidopsis, and its results may not be entirely applicable to A. konjac or other crops. Therefore, future research should verify the function of AkCSLA11 in A. konjac and other related crops to determine its conservation and specificity across different plant systems. Lastly, although we have observed the negative impact of AkCSLA11 overexpression on the drought tolerance of Arabidopsis, we have not yet explored the physiological and biochemical basis of this effect. Future research should focus on how AkCSLA11 affects the plant’s water absorption, transportation, and retention, as well as how it regulates the mechanical properties of the cell wall and adaptability to environmental stress. In summary, while this study has uncovered the potential role of AkCSLA11 in the response to drought stress, it also points out areas that require further research. By addressing these limitations, we can gain a more comprehensive understanding of the role of the CSLA gene family in plant drought tolerance and provide a deeper molecular basis for crop improvement.

Conclusions

In this study, we delve into the effects of abnormal expression of the AkCSLA11 from A. konjac on drought tolerance in Arabidopsis. It was observed that the expression of AkCSLA11 is reduced under drought conditions in A. konjac, and introducing it in Arabidopsis led to decreased resistance to drought. Further investigation revealed that Arabidopsis plants with AkCSLA11 overexpression displayed lower levels of drought-responsive gene expression compared to normal plants. This indicates that AkCSLA11 acts as a dampener in the drought stress response by controlling the activity of genes related to stress. The study forms the basis for unraveling the mechanisms underlying growth, development, and response to drought stress in A. konjac, and sets the stage for future examinations on the functional aspects of the AkCSLA gene family in these species.
