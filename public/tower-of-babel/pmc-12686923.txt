Traffic sign dataset for connected and automated vehicle operations in rural areas
Authors: Mohammed Zakaria, Tesfamichael Getahun, Ali Karimoddini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate traffic sign recognition is essential for the safe operation of Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs). However, many existing datasets, such as the LISA Traffic Sign Dataset, are predominantly composed of signs found in urban environments, offering limited representation of those common in rural settings. To address this gap, this article introduces an augmented LISA Traffic Sign Dataset that includes rural-specific traffic signs such as deer crossing, cattle crossing, and farm machinery warning signs. The dataset was compiled through a combination of real-world data collection using frames extracted from rural driving videos, and screenshots taken from google map street view. The dataset increased the number of classes in the original LISA Traffic Sign Dataset from 47 to 55 classes. Experiments conducted using a YOLOv11 -based recognition model demonstrated that the augmented dataset improved recognition performance, achieving an overall mean Average Precision (mAP) of 98.7 %, compared to 97.0 % with the original dataset. Precision and recall for the augmented dataset also increased form 95.5 % and 95.1 % to 96.7 % and 97.5 %, respectively. This expanded dataset is intended to support the development and benchmarking of recognition systems for rural CAV deployments and is structured to enable reuse in related research areas, including infrastructure assessment, rural navigation systems, and intelligent transportation studies.Keywords: Intelligent transportation systems, Traffic sign detection, Autonomous driving, Deep learning, Object detection

Background

The original motivation for compiling this dataset stemmed from the need to support the safe and effective deployment of CAVs in rural areas. Most publicly available traffic sign datasets such as the LISA Traffic Sign Dataset are biased toward urban environments, limiting their utility in developing recognition systems that can generalize to rural contexts [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Additionally, recent advancements in traffic sign recognition have primarily focused on enhancing the quality and quantity of existing classes within the datasets, rather than expanding them to include additional classes for signs frequently encountered in rural environments [[6], [7], [8]]. Moreover, while datasets such as the Chinese traffic sign datasets are quite comprehensive, the signs they contain differ significantly from those commonly found in the United States [9]. This dataset was created to address that limitation by incorporating rural-specific traffic signs such as animal crossings and farm equipment warnings in the LISA Traffic Sign Dataset.

The methodological approach was grounded in computer vision and deep learning, particularly in the development of robust traffic sign recognition models. Data was collected using a combination of Google Maps Street View and publicly available rural driving videos, with frames processed using OpenCV and labeled manually.

This dataset was initially compiled in support of a research article focused on evaluating the physical infrastructure needed for CAV operations in rural settings. The data article provides added value by offering a curated and reusable dataset that supports further research in traffic sign recognition, rural roadway infrastructure assessment, and autonomous vehicle navigation.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The data was compiled to supplement the LISA Traffic Sign Dataset by focusing on signs commonly found in rural environments. The collection process involved multiple phases: sourcing, extraction, labelling, and augmentation. There are eight (8) new classes, 1098 images, and 1342 annotations in the dataset, with 80 % of the images containing a single traffic sign annotation and the remaining 20 % containing 2 to 5 traffic sign annotations. The dataset provides a valuable resource for training and evaluating deep learning models that can adapt to rural environments. Image collection and processing

Two different procedures were employed in the image collection. The first involved taking screenshots of images that contained traffic signs of interest from Google Maps Street View. This was done by virtually traversing rural roadways using Google Maps Street View. The second process of data collection involved the extraction of video frames that contained traffic signs of interest from rural environments. Image annotation process

The annotation of images was performed using Roboflow [10], a widely used platform for dataset preparation in computer vision tasks such as object detection. After collecting the raw images, they were uploaded to a dedicated traffic sign project on Roboflow. This project was based on the original LISA Traffic Sign Dataset, which initially contained 47 traffic sign classes. The annotation process was conducted manually by a single annotator to ensure consistency and accuracy. Each image was carefully reviewed, and bounding boxes were drawn around the traffic signs of interest. Every bounding box was assigned a corresponding class label that accurately represented the type of traffic sign. During this process, eight (8) new classes, specific to the scope of this project, were introduced in addition to the existing LISA classes. This resulted in a total of 55 traffic sign classes, significantly enhancing the dataset's comprehensiveness and improving model generalization for diverse traffic sign detection scenarios.

Frame extraction

To construct the dataset from video sources, individual frames were extracted using the OpenCV library in Python. To balance dataset size with meaningful scene variation, frames were sampled at a rate of one frame per second (1 FPS). This sampling strategy provided sufficient diversity across driving scenarios while minimizing redundancy and storage overhead. A pseudocode used for frame extraction is presented in Table 2.

Annotation

After extracting the frames, the Roboflow Annotate tool was used to manually label each image. The annotator carefully identified traffic signs within each frame and drew bounding boxes around them. Each bounding box was tagged with its corresponding class label from the 55-class arrangement developed during the initial annotation phase. Once labelling was complete, the annotated dataset was exported in YOLOv11 format, which included:

Data augmentation

To increase the robustness of the model and enhance generalization to real-world conditions, data augmentation techniques were applied the training dataset. These augmentations simulated variations commonly encountered in real traffic environments, such as lighting changes, camera angles, and image quality distortions. The applied augmentation techniques included:

•Rotation: 10° clockwise and counterclockwise rotation to simulate different sign orientations.

Rotation: 10° clockwise and counterclockwise rotation to simulate different sign orientations.

•±15° Hue, ±15 % Saturation, and ±15 % Brightness adjustments: to mimic changes in lighting and weather conditions.

•±10 % Exposure adjustments: for under/overexposure scenarios

•2.5px Blur: to represent motion blur or poor camera quality

These augmentations improved the diversity of the dataset, making the trained model more resilient to environmental variability.

Limitations

Rural traffics signs are underrepresented in publicly available sources such as Google Maps Street View and YouTube driving videos. This limited the variety and frequency of certain sign types, leading to imbalanced dataset. Also, the dataset is primarily sourced from rural areas in the United States, which may introduce geographic bias and limit its generalizability to rural settings in other countries with different signage standards. Despite these constraints, the dataset provides a valuable starting point for improving rural traffic sign recognition.
