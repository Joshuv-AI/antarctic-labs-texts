Abstract
In this work, we investigate an effective method for showing that functors between categories are left adjoints. The method applies to a large class of categories, namely locally finitely presentable categories, which are ubiquitous in practice and include standard examples like 𝐒𝐞𝐭 \mathbf{Set} , 𝐆𝐫𝐩 \mathbf{Grp} , etc. Our method relies on a known description of these categories as orthogonal sub-classes of presheaf categories. The functors on which our method applies are the ones that can be presented as particular profunctors, called Kan models in this context. The method for left-adjointness then relies on computing that a particular criterion is satisfied. From this method, we also derive another method for showing that a category is cartesian closed. As proofs of concept and effectivity, we give a concrete implementation of the structures and of the left-adjointness criterion in OCaml and apply it on several examples.



Introduction

Over the years, category theory has proven to be an efficient tool to formalise
arguments and constructions in a mathematical setting. First developed in the
context of algebra and topology by Eilenberg and MacLane in the 1940s, it
quickly expanded to other fields than mathematics. In computer science notably,
category theory found several useful applications in programming languages,
semantics, logic, and other topics. For example: the notion of
monad

allows modeling the effects of programming languages
[
18
]
, and is
at the heart of the
Haskell
programming language
[
10
]
;
cartesian closed categories provide models of simply-typed
lambda-calculus
[
13
]
; star-autonomous categories
provide models of linear logic
[
23
]
; etc.

The approach of category theory can be considered
abstract
, in the sense
that the emphasis is not put on the concrete definitions of the constructions
being considered but by the universal properties they satisfy. For example,
while the cartesian product of two sets
A
and
B
is often
concretely

defined in set theory as
the
set
A\times B\mathrel{\hat{=}}\{\{\{a\},\{a,b\}\}\mid a\in A,b\in B\}
, in category theory, it is
considered as
a
set
A\times B
, together with functions
l\colon A\times B\rightarrow A
and
r\colon A\times B\rightarrow B
, satisfying the following universal
property: for every set
X
and every pair of function
f\colon X\rightarrow A
and
g\colon X\rightarrow B
, there is a unique function
h\colon X\rightarrow A\times B
such that
f=l\circ h
and
g=r\circ h
. This categorical definition of the cartesian
product is just an instance of the definition of the more general notion of

product
in the category
\mathbf{Set}
of sets and functions, but can be
expressed in any category. Other standard categorical constructions include:
(co)limits, exponentials, left and right adjoints, (co)ends, left and right Kan
extensions, etc. Using this abstract approach, one can go a long way using the toolbox of
category theory to make abstract constructions or prove properties using generic
arguments, without actually considering concrete definitions of the objects
being considered.

The abstraction of category theory raises the question of computation: despite
most constructions being abstractly defined, is it possible to compute them or
reason about them computationally? More generally, to which extent category
theory can be made computable?

In order to computationally manipulate or reason about some mathematical
objects, one first need to be able to represent them, or
encode
them, in
a form that a program can understand, typically as instances of datatypes. Once
such an encoding is given, one can start to wonder what can be computed from it.
For example, one can easily encode finite sets and functions between them using
standard computational structures. With such an encoding, one can compute
several categorical constructions on finite sets, like (co)products, pullbacks,
pushouts, and more generally, any finite limit or colimit. But this encoding
only works for the category
\mathbf{Set}
and its objects that are finite sets, and
does not directly extend either for infinite sets or other categories, which can
seem limited.

In order to consider other kind of computations in a categorical setting, an
idea is to change the encoding for one similar to the ones used in the context
of algebraic structures. Indeed, computations on groups, rings, etc. often rely
on
presentations
of such structures, which specify a set of generators
and a set of equations satisfied by these generators. When such presentations
are
finite
, one can encode them and give them as input to a computer.
Despite the finiteness, a lot of structures of interest can be described through
finite presentations, and several computations on these structures can be
carried out through these presentations. In group theory for example, one can
consider the word problem on a group presented by a finite presentation; one can
also use the Todd–Coxeter algorithm to enumerate the cosets of a group described
by a finite presentation
[
25
]
; etc.

One can similarly consider categories described by finite presentations and
investigate the computations that can be done through this encoding. Several
earlier works have taken this approach with successful results. But the
categories described this way are very specific: they have a finite number of
objects (specified by the finite presentation) and a countable number of
morphisms, and are defined up to isomorphism. Standard examples of categories,
like
\mathbf{Set}
, the category of groups
\mathbf{Grp}
, etc., on the other hand, have an
infinite number of objects and morphisms, which do not even form a set, and are
morally defined up to equivalence of categories. So that these standard examples
cannot be described with finite presentations, contrary to the situation of
other algebraic structures, where most examples of interest are.

In order to encode these giant structures, one can use a more creative notion of
presentation. In the same way that finitely presentable categories form a
particular class of categories that can be presented by a finite presentation as
discussed above, there is a class of large well-behaved categories, called

locally finitely presentable (l.f.p.) categories
, that can be fully
described by a smaller data than its whole classes of objects and morphisms.
L.f.p. categories include a lot of very common categories, whose object can be
described as models of some sort of equational theories called
essentially
algebraic theories

[
9
,
1
]
: the categories
of groups, rings, graphs, small categories, etc. are classical examples. This
notion of l.f.p. categories relies on the former notion of finite presentation
that we discussed: it requires that every object of these categories can be
expressed as a particular colimits of finitely presented objects. This way,
every object is characterised by the sets of morphisms from finitely presented
objects to it. By some theoretical developments, it implies that objects of an
l.f.p. category
\mathcal{C}
can be modeled as particular presheaves over some category

C
, satisfying some
orthogonality conditions
. When
C
is finite, it can
be encoded and, under similar finiteness conditions, the orthogonality
conditions can be encoded as well, so that the category
\mathcal{C}
can be encoded, in
a sense that there is an inhabitant of a datatype that describes
\mathcal{C}
up to
equivalence of categories. Moreover, we are able to computationally represent
the objects of
\mathcal{C}
that are equivalent to finite presheaves.

Now it might appear that this new kind of encoding only allows one to generalise
the computations that were possible on finite sets, like finite limits and
colimits, to more general l.f.p. categories and their objects that can be
described by finite presheaves. While this is still valuable, it still feels
limited compared to more general constructions or problems of category theory.
Indeed, category theory is often concerned about
global
properties of
categories (does this category have limits and colimits of a particular shape?
Is it cartesian closed? etc.), or of functors between categories (is this
functor preserving limits or colimits of a particular shape? Does it have an
adjoint? etc.). While the above encoding is able to encode classical (large)
examples of categories, it still seems that we are not able to do much more than
computing finite limits and colimits, like for finite sets. Also, we still miss
a way to encode another important notion of category theory, namely the one of
functor between categories.

The goal of this paper is to push the limitations of this encoding, in order to
extend the scope of what can be considered computable in category theory. For
this purpose, based on the discussed encoding of l.f.p. categories, we show that
functors between such categories can be encoded and that the problem of left
adjointness, that is, whether a functor is a left adjoint or not, can be given a
computational treatment.

Outline and contributions

In
Section

I
, we recall some basic definitions
about l.f.p. categories and in particular discuss how they can be described as
full sub-categories of orthogonal objects of presheaf categories, through a
notion that we call
presheaf model
. In
Section

II
, we
show how one can use these presheaf models to represent functors between l.f.p. categories, using another structure called
Kan model
. In

Section

III
, we consider the problem of left adjointness of
functors between two l.f.p. categories and give a sufficient criterion based on
presheaf and Kan models. Checking this criterion will rely on a construction
called
reflection
, that we will study in detail to investigate its
computability. While reflection is not always fully computable, finite
approximations are often enough to decide whether the criterion for left
adjointness is satisfied. The computations of these approximations is organised
into some sort of game called
game of reflection
, that one needs to play
to show the criterion. The exploration of the moves that can be played to reach
a winning configuration can be delegated to a computer, resulting in a
computational method to show left adjointness.

As a proof of concept, we give a first implementation in
OCaml
of the method,
discussed in
Section

IV
, and apply it to several examples.

I
L.f.p. categories, expressed with presheaves

In this section, we recall the notion of
locally finitely presentable

(abbreviated
l.f.p.
) categories, which defines a large class of
well-behaved categories, suited for the development of methods that are amenable
to computations. These categories are often described as categories of models of

essentially algebraic theories
, making them ubiquitous in practice. The
methods we will develop will rely on a particular characterisation of l.f.p.
categories as orthogonal sub-classes of presheaf categories that we will recall.
We moreover illustrate this characterisation on several examples. We defer the
computational treatment of this description of categories to

Section

IV
, focusing here on the mathematical modeling in greater
generality.

I-A

Finite presentability

The theory of l.f.p. categories is centered on the notion of finitely
presentability, itself derived from the notion of directed colimit. See

[
1
]
for details.

Definition 1
.

A partial order
(D,\leq)
is
directed
when
D\neq\emptyset
and for all
x,y\in D
, there exists
z\in D
such that
{x\leq z}
and
{y\leq z}
. A small category
I
is called
directed
when it
is isomorphic to a directed partial order
(D,\leq)
. A
directed colimit

in a category
\mathcal{C}
is a colimit taken on a diagram
d\colon I\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
where

I
is directed.

Definition 2
.

An object
P
of a category
\mathcal{C}
is
finitely presentable
(abbreviated
f.p.) when its hom-functor
\operatorname{Hom}(P,-)\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
commutes with directed
colimits.

Concretely, it means that for every directed colimit
(p_{i}\colon d(i)\rightarrow L)_{i\in\Ob(I)}
on a diagram
d\colon I\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
and every morphism
f\colon P\rightarrow L
, there is
i\in\Ob(I)
and
u\colon P\rightarrow d(i)
such that
f=p_{i}\circ u
,
and this pair
(i,u)
is
essentially unique
: given another such pair

(i^{\prime},u^{\prime})
, there is
j\in\Ob(I)
with
i,i^{\prime}\leq j
such that
d(i\leq j)\circ u=d(i^{\prime}\leq j)\circ u^{\prime}
.

Example 1
.

When
\mathcal{C}=\mathbf{Set}
, the f.p. objects are the finite sets. In the case of
\mathcal{C}=\mathbf{Grp}
, the category of groups, the f.p. objects are the groups which admits a
presentation through a finite number of generators and a finite number of
equations. More generally, the f.p. objects in other categories are often the
objects which can be presented using a notion of finite presentation.

I-B

L.f.p. categories

Definition 3

(
[
1
, Definition 1.9]
)
.

A locally small category
\mathcal{C}
is
locally finitely presentable
(or
l.f.p.) when

1.

it has all small colimits;

2.

every object
X\in\mathcal{C}
is a directed colimit of finitely presentable objects;

3.

the full subcategory over the finitely presentable objects of
\mathcal{C}
is

essentially small
, that is, equivalent to a small category.

Example 2
.

We can already observe that
\mathbf{Set}
is an l.f.p. category: indeed, every set is a
directed union (in particular, a directed colimit) of its finite subsets, and
the full sub-category of finite subsets is essentially small.

Definition

3
is a theoretical but rather abstract definition of
l.f.p. categories, that hides their relevance in practical situations. One usually
prefer to recognise l.f.p. categories as models of
essentially algebraic
theories

[
9
,
1
]
. The latter are an
extension of standard algebraic theories for algebraic structures that allows
for operations with restricted domains. See
Section

-F
for details.

Example 3
.

The categories of
\mathbf{Mon}
of monoids,
\mathbf{Grp}
of groups,
\mathbf{Grph}
of graphs,

\mathbf{Cat}
of small categories, etc. are all categories of models of essentially
algebraic theories. For example,
\mathbf{Mon}
is the category of models of a theory
with one sort
M
, a constant
e
and an operation
m\colon M\times M\rightarrow M

satisfying the equations
m(e,x)=x
,
m(x,e)=x
and
m(m(x,y),z)=m(x,m(y,z))
for any
x,y,z
of
M
. Similarly for
\mathbf{Grp}
,
\mathbf{Grph}
and
\mathbf{Cat}
.
We deduce that they are all l.f.p. categories by a well-known characterisation
(see
Theorem

1
).

I-C

Presheaves on a category

The methods we develop in this article rely on a description of l.f.p. categories
as particular sub-classes of presheaf categories. Presheaves are particularly
relevant for the computational methods we investigate in this article, since
they are rather easy to encode. We recall here some basic definitions and facts
about presheaves and presheaf categories. Let
C
be a small category.

Definition 4
.

A
presheaf
on
C
is a functor
X\colon C^{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
. A

morphism
between two presheaves
X
and
Y
is a natural transformation

\alpha\colon X\Rightarrow Y
. We write
\hat{C}
for the
presheaf category
of
presheaves on
C
and presheaf morphisms.

A lot of naturally occurring structures can be described as presheaves on an
adequate category:

Example 4
.

Considering the category
G
with two objects
N
and
A
and two non-trivial
morphisms
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\colon N\rightarrow A
, we can consider the presheaf category

\hat{G}
, which can be equivalently described as the category of directed
graphs and their morphisms.

Presheaf categories have a lot of good properties, notably:

Proposition 1
.

Given a small category
C
,
\hat{C}
is l.f.p., complete, cocomplete and
cartesian closed. Moreover, the colimits and limits are pointwisely computed
as in
\mathbf{Set}
: given a diagram of presheaves
X_{(-)}\colon I\rightarrow\hat{C}
and
c\in C
, we have
(\colim_{i\in I}X_{i})(c)\cong\colim_{i\in I}(X_{i}(c))

(and similarly for limits).

The category
C
can be mapped into the category
\hat{C}
through the Yoneda
functor, which happens to be fully faithful:

Definition 5
.

The
Yoneda functor
is the functor
\mathrm{y}_{C}\colon C\rightarrow\hat{C}
defined
by mapping
c\in\Ob(C)
to the presheaf
C(-,c)\colon{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
.

Given
c\in\Ob(C)
and
X\in\Ob(\hat{C})
, a morphism
f\colon\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\rightarrow X
can be mapped to an element of
X(c)
, by looking at the image of
\operatorname{id}_{c}

by
f_{c}
. The family of these mappings for
c\in\Ob(C)
defines a natural
isomorphism, as stated the Yoneda lemma:

Proposition 2

(Yoneda lemma)
.

The mapping just described induces a bijection

\hat{C}(\mathrm{y}_{C}(c),X)\quad\cong\quad X(c)\qquad\in\qquad\mathbf{Set}

which is natural in
c\in\Ob(C)
.

I-D

L.f.p. categories as orthogonal sub-categories

While every presheaf category is an l.f.p. category, the converse is not true.
However, by a known characterisation
[
1
]
, every l.f.p. category can be identified as a full sub-category of a presheaf category
\hat{C}
. The presheaves that belong to this sub-category are the ones that are

orthogonal
to a set of
orthogonality conditions
, the latter being
morphisms of
\hat{C}
. These conditions will enable additional structures and
equations on these orthogonal presheaves, making them expressive enough to model
objects of other l.f.p. categories. Since this notion of orthogonality is not at
all specific to presheaf categories, we introduce in a more general setting.

Definition 6

(
[
1
, Definition 1.32]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category. Let
X
be an object of
\mathcal{C}
and
g\colon A\rightarrow B
be
a morphism of
\mathcal{C}
. We say that
X
is
orthogonal
to
g
, denoted
X\perp g
, when we have the following unique lifting property: for all
f\colon A\rightarrow X
, there exists a unique
h\colon B\rightarrow X
such that
f=h\circ g
.
Graphically,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\forall f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists!h}
.

(1)

Given a set
O
of morphisms of
\mathcal{C}
, we write
(\mathcal{C},O)^{\perp}
, or even

O^{\perp}
, for the full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
whose objects are the
X\in\mathcal{C}
which are orthogonal to every
g\in O
.

We can already mention the adjunction between
\mathcal{C}
and its orthogonal sub-category,
and whose construction will play an important role for showing the correctness
of the criterion of
Section

III
:

Theorem 1

(
[
1
, Construction 1.37]
)
.

Given a cocomplete category
\mathcal{C}
and a set
O
of morphisms
g\colon A\rightarrow B

of
\mathcal{C}
such that
A
and
B
are finitely presentable, the category

O^{\perp}
is a reflective subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
, that is, the embedding
functor
J^{(\mathcal{C},O)}\colon O^{\perp}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
(abbreviated

J
) is part of an adjunction

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces O^{\perp}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\perp}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}

for some
reflection functor

L^{(\mathcal{C},O)}
, for some unit

\eta^{(\mathcal{C},O)}\colon\operatorname{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\Rightarrow JL
and some counit

\epsilon^{(\mathcal{C},O)}\colon LJ\Rightarrow\operatorname{1}_{O^{\perp}}
(often
abbreviated
L
,
\eta
and
\epsilon
as well).

Remark 1
.

In particular, since
J
is fully faithful (
O^{\perp}
is a
full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
), the counit
\epsilon\colon LJ\Rightarrow\operatorname{1}_{O^{\perp}}
is an isomorphism.

L.f.p. categories are then characterised the following way using orthogonality:

Theorem 2

(
[
1
, Theorem 1.46]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category.
\mathcal{C}
is l.f.p. iff
\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to a category

(\hat{C},O)^{\perp}
for some small category
C
and some set
O
of
morphisms
g\colon A\rightarrow B\in\hat{C}
such that both
A
and
B
are finitely
presentable.

The above property justifies the following definitions:

Definition 7
.

A
presheaf model
is a pair
(C,O)
where
C
is a small category and

O
is a set of morphisms
g\colon A\rightarrow B
of
\hat{C}
such that both
A
and

B
are finitely presentable presheaves.
A (necessarily l.f.p.) category
\mathcal{C}
is
modeled
by the presheaf model

(C,O)
when
\mathcal{C}\simeq(C,O)^{\perp}
.

II
Modelling functors between categories

Now that we have a convenient description for an interesting class of
categories, we now introduce a similar description of the functors between such
categories.
For this purpose, we propose another modeling device, called a
Kan model
,
which is able to model a decent class of such functors and which can be encoded
as well under reasonable hypotheses. The modeled functors are then recovered
from these devices using
left Kan extensions
, the definition of which
relies on the notions of
tensors
and
coends
that we recall below.

II-A

Tensors

The notion of tensor discussed here is an instance of a more general notion of
tensors for enriched categories
[
12
,
2
]
. In
our unenriched setting, tensors will be quite easy to define, but will still be
useful in our developments.

Definition 8
.

Given a category with coproducts
\mathcal{C}
, an object
X\in\mathcal{C}
and
S\in\mathbf{Set}
, we write
X\otimes S
for the
tensor
of
X
by
S
, defined as

X\otimes S\quad=\quad\coprod_{s\in S}X\makebox[0.0pt][l]{.}

This operation naturally extends to a functor

(-)_{1}\otimes(-)_{2}\colon\quad\mathcal{C}\times\mathbf{Set}\quad\rightarrow\quad\mathcal{C}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{.}

Tensors satisfy the following universal property, which is normally expressed in
the broader setting of enriched categories:

Proposition 3
.

Given a category
\mathcal{C}
as in
Definition

8
, and
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
and
S\in\mathbf{Set}
, the copairing operations on morphisms induces a natural isomorphism

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(S,\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y))\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X\otimes S,Y)\makebox[0.0pt][l]{.}

As a direct consequence, we have:

Proposition 4
.

Given
X\in\mathcal{C}
and
S\in\mathbf{Set}
, the functors
X\otimes(-)\colon\mathbf{Set}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
and
(-)\otimes S\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
preserve colimits.

II-B

Coends

Another tool that will be useful to us is the notion of coends that we recall
briefly below in a restricted case. We refer the reader to the existing
literature, like
[
16
, Sec. IX.6]
or
[
14
]
for
more details:

Definition 9
.

Given two categories
C
and
\mathcal{D}
and a functor
H\colon C\times C^{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
, a
cowedge
for
H
is the data of an object
W
of
\mathcal{D}
and
morphisms
w_{c}\colon H(c,c)\rightarrow W
for each
c\in C
such that the
squares

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(c,c^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(c^{\prime},c^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(c,c)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(f,c^{\prime})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(c,f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces w_{c^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces w_{c}}

(2)

commutes. A
coend
, denoted
\int^{c\in C}H(c,c)
is a cowedge which
is initial among cowedges, that is, such that any other cowedge
W
on
H

induces a unique morphism of cowedges
\int^{c\in C}H(c,c)\rightarrow W
.

Remark 2
.

In the case where
C
is small and
\mathcal{D}
is cocomplete,
\int^{c\in C}H(c,c)
can be alternatively characterised (see
[
16
, Proposition
IX.5.1]
) as the quotient

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coprod\limits_{\mathmakebox[18pt][c]{f\colon c\rightarrow c^{\prime}\in C}}(H(c,c^{\prime}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coprod\limits_{c\in C}H(c,c)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\int^{c\in C}H(c,c)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[\iota_{c^{\prime}}\circ(H(f,c^{\prime}))]_{f:c\rightarrow c^{\prime}\in C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[\iota_{c}\circ(H(c,f))]_{f:c\rightarrow c^{\prime}\in C}}

of the coproduct
\coprod_{c\in C}H(c,c)
under the conditions given by the
squares (
2
), where we wrote
\iota_{c}
for the
coprojections and
[-]_{f\in C}
for the copairing operation (we will use
again these notations in the following).

The notion of coends allows us to express an important proposition for
presheaves. Given a small category
C
and
X\in\hat{C}
, by the Yoneda lemma,
we have a canonical morphism
\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\otimes X(c)\rightarrow X
for every
c\in\Ob(C)
. These morphisms even factor through the coend as a morphism
\int^{c\in C}\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\otimes X(c)\rightarrow X
which is actually an isomorphism:

Proposition 5

(Density formula/co-Yoneda lemma)
.

Given a small category
C
and
X\in\hat{C}
, the canonical morphism

\int^{c\in C}\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\otimes X(c)\rightarrow X

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

See for example the proof of
[
14
, Proposition 2.2.1]
.
∎

II-C

Kan extensions

Here we recall a working definition of a Kan
extension
[
16
,
2
]
in the restricted case
that will interest us, that is, when the functor along which a Kan extension is
taken is the Yoneda embedding. Kan extensions will be the key construction to
present functors using Kan models in the next section.

Definition 10
.

Given a small category
C
, a cocomplete category
\mathcal{D}
and a functor
F\colon C\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
as in the above definition, the
left Kan extension
of
F
(along

\mathrm{y}_{C}
) is a functor
\Lan F\colon\hat{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
, defined for
X\in\Ob(\hat{C})
by

\Lan F(X)=\int^{c\in C}F(c)\otimes X(c)

and naturally extended to morphisms of presheaves
f\colon X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
.

As can be intuited from unfolding the definitions, given
X\in\Ob(\hat{C})
,

\Lan F(X)
is an object constructed with one copy of
F(c)
for every
x\in X(c)
for each
c\in\Ob(C)
, adequately glued together by the coend
operator: in other words, an
X
-weighted colimit
of
F
(see
[
2
, Section
6.6]
).

Remark 3
.

The left Kan extension

\Lan(-)\colon\mathbf{CAT}(C,\mathcal{D})\rightarrow\mathbf{CAT}(\hat{C},\mathcal{D})

provides a left adjoint to the precomposition functor

(-)\circ\mathrm{y}_{C}\colon\mathbf{CAT}(\hat{C},\mathcal{D})\rightarrow\mathbf{CAT}(C,\mathcal{D})\makebox[0.0pt][l]{.}

The density formula (
Proposition

5
) then states that the counit
of this adjunction is an isomorphism, as a consequence of the
density

(see
[
16
, Section X.6]
) of
\mathrm{y}_{C}
.

III
Left adjointness through modeling

We now apply our modeling of categories and functors to tackle the problem of
showing that a functor is a left adjoint. For this purpose, we introduce an
effective criterion that relies on this modeling. As we will show in a later
section, under reasonable finiteness hypotheses, this criterion can be
computationally checked, either fully automatically in good cases, or by
providing proof assistance to the user.

III-A

The reflection construction

Our criterion will be derived from a careful study of the proof of the
adjunction between an orthogonal subcategory and its containing category, as is
stated by
Theorem

1
. Although we will not recall the whole proof of
that theorem here, we will at least describe the construction of the functor

L
on objects. For that, it is useful to analyse how the object
X

can fail to be in
O^{\perp}
and what can be done to fix that. Consider
f\colon X\rightarrow A
and

such that (
1
) commutes. In this case, we can
“change”

X
for such a factorisation to exist: we take the pushout

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}

III-B

A criterion for left adjointness

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be two categories modeled by presheaf models
(C,O)
and

(D,P)
, and
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be a functor modeled by a Kan model
F\colon C\rightarrow\hat{D}
. In this section, we give a criterion based on

to determine
whether
\mathcal{F}
is a left adjoint.

First, since
\mathcal{F}\cong\bar{F}

for
\bar{F}
, or even
\tilde{F}^{\prime}
, since
\mathcal{C}\simeq O^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{D}\simeq P^{\perp}
. We have the following folklore result for l.f.p. categories:

Proposition 7
.

A functor between two l.f.p. categories is a left adjoint if and only if it
preserves all colimits.

Proof.

In order to prove that a functor
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
between two categories
is a left adjoint, it is enough, by a dualised version of the Special Adjoint
Functor Theorem (see
[
3
, Theorem 3.3.4]
), to show that:

1.

\mathcal{C}
is cocomplete;

2.

\mathcal{F}
preserves small colimits;

3.

\mathcal{C}
is co-well-powered;

4.

\mathcal{C}
has a generating family.

But, in the case where
\mathcal{C}
is l.f.p., we have that
\mathcal{C}
is cocomplete (by
definition),
\mathcal{C}
is co-well-powered (see
[
1
, Theorem
1.58]
) and
\mathcal{C}
has a generating family (see
[
1
, Theorem
1.20]
). Thus, the property holds.
∎

The functor

III-C

Computing reflections, exhaustively

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category and
O
be a finite set of morphisms of
\mathcal{C}
. By what
we have exposed, we are interested in knowing when a morphism
m\colon X\rightarrow Y\in\mathcal{C}
is sent to an isomorphism by the reflection functor
L\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow(\mathcal{C},O)^{\perp}
. We have different ways, or strategies, through which this
can be done, and that will determine the kind of computer aid we can get.

The first one might be called the
exhaustive strategy
, which actually
computes
L(m)
completely: we first compute the reflection
LY
, together with the morphism
\eta_{Y}\colon Y\rightarrow JLY
. We
then compute
m^{\prime}=\eta_{Y}\circ Y\colon X\rightarrow JLY
and
finally compute the liftings of
m^{\prime}
against the chain

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces JLY}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m^{\prime}}

so that we get
JL(m)\colon JLX\rightarrow JLY
since
JLX
is the colimit of
the
X_{i}
’s. While this method is quite straight-forward, it has some problems.
First, it is infinite as it is. Indeed, to compute both
JLX
and
JLY
, we need to do an infinite number of steps,
which are impossible to simulate on a computer with a finite time of execution.
But, this might not always be a problem. Indeed, when
\operatorname{e}_{i_{e}}\colon X_{i_{e}}\rightarrow X_{i_{e}+1}
is an isomorphism for some
i_{e}\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\operatorname{e}_{i}\colon X_{i}\rightarrow X_{i+1}
is an isomorphism for every
i\geq i_{e}
,
so that
JLX\cong X_{i_{e}}
and we can stop the computation
of
JLX
at the step
i_{e}
. Same for the computation of

JLY
. However, even when the computation of the
reflections of
X
and
Y
can be done in a finite time, the exhaustive method
is very costly: at each step
i
, we must compute the sets
J^{i,e}
and

J^{i,u}
, which is computationally expensive.

III-D

The game of reflection

Better strategies can be found, which basically give up on completely computing
the reflection of morphisms. Instead, they try to win the following game, in as
few moves as possible. This
“game”
consists of a class of configurations,
among which some of them are winning, and some moves that can be played to go
from a configuration to another.

\mathcal{C}=\mathbf{Cat}
,
(C,O)=(\mathrm{C}^{\mathrm{c}},\mathrm{O}^{\mathrm{c}})
and
B=\mathbf{2}
, the
category with two objects and only one non-trivial
“walking arrow”
between
them, and
Y
is an object of
\widehat{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{c}}
corresponding to

\mathbf{2}
. In order to prove that
-\times\mathbf{2}
is a left adjoint,
we just need to show that
\tilde{\mathrm{F}^{\mathrm{times}}}
sends each of
g^{\mathbf{p}}
,

g^{\mathbf{lu}}
,
g^{\mathbf{ru}}
,
g^{\mathbf{ass}}
to isomorphisms. Concerning
g^{\mathbf{lu}}
,
since
{\mathrm{F}^{\mathrm{times}}}^{\prime}(g^{\mathbf{lu}})
is essentially
g^{\mathbf{lu}}\times Y\colon A^{\mathbf{lu}}\times Y\rightarrow B^{\mathbf{lu}}\times Y\in\widehat{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{c}}
, we can observe
(and compute!) that
{\mathrm{F}^{\mathrm{times}}}^{\prime}(g^{\mathbf{lu}})
fails to be an isomorphism because
three elements of
B^{\mathbf{lu}}(\mathbf{m})
have two antecedents each: this is simply
three copies (because
Y(\mathbf{m})
has three elements) of the situation observed
on
g^{\mathbf{lu}}
alone, where
u
and
w
in
A^{\mathbf{lu}}
are both sent to
u^{\prime}\in B^{\mathbf{lu}}
. In order, to make
{\mathrm{F}^{\mathrm{times}}}^{\prime}(g^{\mathbf{lu}})
into an isomorphism, we just
need to apply three Dom-E moves with
g^{\mathbf{lu}}
itself to unify each copy of
the pair
u,w
in
A^{\mathbf{lu}}\times Y
. The cases of
g^{\mathbf{ru}}
,
g^{\mathbf{ass}}
and

g^{\mathbf{p}}
are similar.

III-E

A criterion for cartesian closure

From our criterion for left-adjointness, a criterion for cartesian closure can
be derived. Recall that a category with finite products
\mathcal{C}
is
cartesian
closed
when for all objects
A,B\in\mathcal{C}
, there is an object
B^{A}
and a
morphism
\operatorname{ev}_{A,B}\colon A\times B^{A}\rightarrow B
satisfying the following universal
property: given an object
X\in\mathcal{C}
and a morphism
f\colon A\times X\rightarrow B
,
there is a unique morphism
\bar{f}\colon X\rightarrow B^{A}
such that
f=\operatorname{ev}_{A,B}\circ(A\times\bar{f})
. A standard characterisation of cartesian closure, which is
essentially a reformulation of the above definition, is

Proposition 14
.

A category with finite products
\mathcal{C}
is cartesian closed iff for every
A\in\mathcal{C}
, the functor
A\times(-)\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is a left adjoint.

In the case of l.f.p. categories modeled by presheaf models, we can already use
the criterion of
Theorem

3
to prove that the functors
A\times(-)\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
are left adjoints. But this gives us an infinite number of
instances to check, which cannot be done by a computer. However, a presheaf
model
(C,O)
for
\mathcal{C}
allows us to reduce the number of instances to check to
one instance per object of
C
:

Theorem 4
.

Given an l.f.p. category
\mathcal{C}
modeled by a presheaf model
(C,O)
,
\mathcal{C}
is
cartesian closed if for every
c\in\Ob(C)
, the functor
L(\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\times J(-))\colon(\hat{C},O)^{\perp}\rightarrow(\hat{C},O)^{\perp}

is a left adjoint.

Proof.

By
Proposition

14
, we just need to show that, given
A\in O^{\perp}\simeq\mathcal{C}
, the functor
A\times(-)\colon O^{\perp}\rightarrow O^{\perp}
preserves colimits. By
Proposition

6
, and the
fact that the functors
(-)\otimes(JA)(c)
and the coend
construction adequately preserves colimits, this is a consequence of the
hypothesis.
∎

Then our criterion for left adjointness applies, since:

Proposition 15
.

Given a presheaf model
(C,O)
and
c\in C
, the functor

L(\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\times J(-))\colon(\hat{C},O)^{\perp}\rightarrow(\hat{C},O)^{\perp}
can be modeled by a Kan model.

Proof.

Given
B\in O^{\perp}
, by the density formula
(
Proposition

5
), we have

\displaystyle L(\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\times JB)

\displaystyle\cong L(\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\times\int^{d\in C}\mathrm{y}_{C}(d)\otimes JB(d))

\displaystyle\cong L(\int^{d\in C}(\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\times\mathrm{y}_{C}(d))\otimes JB(d))

since the functor
\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\times(-)
commutes with colimits by the
cartesian closure of
\hat{C}
. Thus, an adequate Kan model for the functor of the
statement is given by
F\colon C\rightarrow\hat{C}
defined by
F(-)=\mathrm{y}_{C}(c)\times\mathrm{y}_{C}(-)
.
∎

IV
An implementation

We now discuss how one can implement the methods we introduced, which rely on
computations on presheaves and presheaf morphisms. One of the main concern for
such an implementation, in addition to the actual used algorithms, is the one of

encoding
, that is, of the representation the instances of structures we
consider as finite elements of some datatypes. Under adequate finiteness
conditions, both encodings and computations for our structures can be derived
from the ones on finite sets and finite functions, which can be easily
represented.

Since our prototype implementation is written in
OCaml
, our explanations will
be illustrated by syntax and types close to the ones of this language.

IV-A

Encoding finite sets

A crucial point to note about our methods we introduced is that all
constructions we need (left Kan extension, pushouts, coequalisers, etc.) are
defined up to isomorphism. Similarly, the arguments of these constructions can
be given up to isomorphism.

Thus, in order to build an implementation of our methods on computations on
finite sets, it is enough to represent sets up to isomorphism. But a finite set

S
is, up to isomorphism, a finite subset of
S^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{N}
or, more
generally, any finite subset of inhabitants of some infinite datatype. In this
regard, we define the type
sgen = SGen of int
to encode the elements of
the sets we consider (we gloss over the fact that
int
is technically a
datatype with finite number of inhabitants since it is
“big enough”
in
practice; the concerned reader can replace
int
by a type for
big
integers
). The
OCaml
standard library then provides a module
Set
to
represent sets of element of some type that need to be specified in advance. We
instantiate this module for the type
sgen
to obtain a module

SSet
. The finite sets will then be encoded by inhabitants of the type

set = SSet.t
.

Then, one needs to represent the functions
f
between two sets
A
and
B

encoded by two terms
a
and
b
of type

set
. One natural idea is to represent them as
OCaml
functions

f : sgen -> sgen
which would only be defined for the elements of
type
sgen
that belongs to
a
, and would return an
element of type
sgen
that belongs to
b
. However,
we want to incrementally construct such functions and to be able to inspect
them, which would be inefficient using
OCaml
functions. Instead, we use the

Map
module which provides an efficient datastructure to
represent a set of (key,value) bindings, that will use to represent the graph

\{(a,f(a))\mid a\in A\}
of the functions
f
we consider. We instantiate
this module for the keys of type
sgen
to obtain a module
SMap
.
The functions between two finite sets are then encoded by inhabitants of the
type
sfun = sgen SMap.t
.

IV-B

Encoding finite categories

We can now encode finite categories, that is, categories with a finite number of
objects and morphisms. Such finite categories can be encoded by the record

type cat = {
objs : set; morphs : set;
src : sgen -> sgen; tgt : sgen -> sgen;
id : sgen -> sgen; comp : sgen -> sgen -> sgen}

Here, we prefer to use the
OCaml
function type to represent functions, since we
will not need to inspect or change the operations of a category
C
once it is
introduced. The operation
id
will only be defined for those

sgen
that are members of
objs
, the operation
src
will
only defined for those
sgen
that are members of
morphs
,
etc. From these encodings, one can easily write a procedure to check whether
the unitality and associativity axioms of categories are satisfied.

IV-C

Encoding presheaves

Now that we have encodings for finite sets and finite categories, one can
define an encoding for the finite presheaves over a finite category
C
. It will
be a record

type ps = { ps_sets : set SMap.t ;
ps_maps : sfun SMap.t }

where
ps_sets
encodes the map
c\in\Ob(C)\mapsto X(c)
of a finite
presheaf
X
(the keys of the
SMap.t
are encoding the objects of
C
),
and
ps_maps
encodes the map
(f\colon c\rightarrow c^{\prime}\in C)\mapsto(X(f)\colon X(c^{\prime})\rightarrow X(c))
(the keys of the
SMap.t
are encoding the morphisms of

C
).

We can now encode morphisms between two presheaves
X
and
Y
using the type

type ps_morph = sfun SMap.t

where the keys of the
SMap.t
are encoding the objects of
C
, and the
value of this
SMap.t
are the encoding of the functions
X(c)\rightarrow Y(c)

for
c\in\Ob(C)
. We can easily write some procedure to check that an
inhabitant of
ps_morph
is indeed a correct encoding of a morphism
between
X
and
Y
.

Using this encoding, we will be able to encode the
finite
presheaf
models:

Definition 14
.

A presheaf model
(C,O)
is
finite
when
C
is a finite category and

O
is a finite set of morphisms
f\colon A\rightarrow B
where both
A
and
B
are
finite.

Indeed, we can use for example the record:

type ps_model = {
psm_cat : cat ;
psm_omorphs : (ps * ps * ps_morph) list }

where
psm_cat
encodes a category
C
and
psm_omorphs
encodes a
list of triples
(A\in\hat{C},B\in\hat{C},f\colon A\rightarrow B\in\hat{C})
, that
encodes the set
O
of orthogonality conditions of a presheaf model
(C,O)
.

Finite Kan models (for an adequate notion of
finiteness
) can be encoded
using similar ideas.

IV-D

Computing the operations

With the above encodings, one can write the code for the different operations we
need. First we can derive a procedure for computing colimits of finite
presheaves from a procedure to compute the colimits of finite sets. Indeed, one
can easily compute a finite coproduct of finite sets, and coequalisers of finite
sets, which is all that is required to compute more general finite colimits of
finite sets. Then, we can implement a procedure to compute the action of the
functor
F^{\prime}\colon\hat{C}\rightarrow\hat{D}
from an encoded Kan model
F\colon C\rightarrow\hat{D}
.
Indeed, for a given finite presheaf
X
,
F^{\prime}(X)
is just an adequate finite
colimit of finite presheaves which can be computed. Finally, we can write the
procedures for the operations needed to find and play the moves of the game of
reflection. See
Section

-G
for details.

IV-E

Code for the examples

We can now consider the code for the examples of this paper. For technical and
efficiency reasons, the actual datatypes used that we use are different from the
ones that we just discussed. One of the main difference is that we prefer to
encode finite categories using finite presentations, since it felt easier
describe examples this way, than to specify their whole set of morphisms. The
files
setCat.ml
,
categoryCat.ml
,
pairCat.ml
encodes
the presheaf models for
\mathbf{Set}
as in
Example

5
,
\mathbf{Set}\times\mathbf{Set}
as in

Example

6
, and
\mathbf{Cat}
as in
Example

7
respectively.

Then, the files
truncation_functor.ml
,
pair_functor.ml
and

product_w_C1_functor.ml
encode the Kan models of the functors of

Examples

9
,
10
and
14
and compute the
criterion for left adjointness using the exhaustive search for the reflection
procedure. While the two first examples terminate, and are able to give us
information discussed in

Examples

12
and
13
about the computed
reflections, the computation does not terminate in reasonable time for the last
file. Instead, we can manually play the game of reflection and show that we can
win the game in the cases of
g^{\mathbf{lu}}
,
g^{\mathbf{ru}}
and
g^{\mathbf{ass}}
in few
steps. The case of
g^{\mathbf{p}}
cannot be handled by our current implementation,
since it does not allow us to choose which specific instance of a move to play.
Instead, we can only instruct our program to execute all the moves of a certain
type, which results in a looping behavior in the case of
g^{\mathbf{p}}
. This
alternative computation is carried out in

product_w_C1_functor_unitl_assoc_only.ml
.

-F

Essentially algebraic theories

Here, we provide some details about the notion of
essentially algebraic
theories
, which provides a characterisation of l.f.p. categories. It is closely
related to the notion of generalised algebraic theories, which are basically an
extension of the notion of essentially algebraic theories which allows for
dependent typing in the definition of operations. We refer the reader to
standard references like
[
1
, Section 3.D]
for a longer
presentation.

Definition 15
.

Given a set
S
, an
S
-sorted signature
is the data of a set
\Sigma

of
symbols

\sigma\colon s_{1}\times\cdots\times s_{n}\rightarrow s

where
s_{1}\times\cdots\times s_{n}
is the
input arity
, with each

s_{i}
in
S
, and where
s\in S
is the
output sort
. From such a
signature and a
context

\Gamma=(x_{1}:s_{1},\ldots,x_{k}:s_{k})
of
distinct variables typed by sorts
s_{i}\in S
, one can build
terms
the
expected way, as trees whose inner nodes are made of symbols of
\Sigma
, and
whose leaves are variables
x_{i}:s_{i}\in\Gamma
, all respecting arities and
sorts. The
output sort
of such a term is the output sort of the root
node, or, if there are no nodes, the sort of the only variable. We write

\Sigma^{\ast}(\Gamma)
for this set of terms.

Definition 16
.

An
essentially algebraic theory
is a tuple

\mathbf{T}=(S,\Sigma,E,\Sigma_{t},\mathrm{Def})

where

S
is a set,

\Sigma
is an
S
-sorted signature,

E
is a set of triples
(\Gamma,t_{1},t_{2})
where
t_{1},t_{2}
are terms of

\Sigma^{\ast}(\Gamma)
with the same output sort,

\Sigma_{t}
is a subset of
\Sigma
,

\mathrm{Def}
is a function which maps
\sigma\colon s_{1}\times\cdots\times s_{n}\rightarrow s\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{t}
to a set of equations
t_{1}=t_{2}

where
t_{1},t_{2}
are terms of
\Sigma^{\ast}_{t}(x_{1}:s_{1},\ldots,x_{k}:s_{k})
.

In the above definition, the set
S
represents the different
sorts
of
the theory, the set
\Sigma
the different operations that appear in the theory,
the set
E
the global equations satisfied by the theory, the set
\Sigma_{t}
the
operations whose domains are total, and the function
\mathrm{Def}
the equations that
define the domains of the partial operations.

Definition 17
.

Given such an essentially algebraic theory
\mathbf{T}
, a
premodel
of
\mathbf{T}
, or
\mathbf{T}
-premodel
, is the data of

–

for all
s\in S
, a set
M_{s}
,

–

for all
\sigma\colon s_{1}\times\cdots\times s_{n}\rightarrow s\in\Sigma_{t}
, a function

M_{\sigma}\colon M_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times M_{s_{n}}\rightarrow M_{s}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{,}

–

for all
\sigma\colon s_{1}\times\cdots\times s_{n}\rightarrow s\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{t}
, a partial function

M_{\sigma}\colon M_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times M_{s_{n}}\rightarrow M_{s}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{.}

Given such a premodel, a context
\Gamma=(x_{1}:s_{1},\ldots,x_{k}:s_{k})
, a term

t\in\Sigma^{\ast}(\Gamma)
of output sort
s
, and
\bar{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in M_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times M_{s_{n}}
, the

interpretation

\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}
is either not defined or an
element of
M_{s}
according to the following rules:

–

if
t=x_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, then
\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}
is defined and

\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}=y_{i}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{,}

–

if
t=\sigma(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
where
n
is the arity of
\sigma
, and
terms
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\Sigma^{\ast}(\Gamma)
, then
\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}
is
defined if and only if
\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}},\ldots,\llbracket t_{n}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}
are defined and
M_{\sigma}
is defined at
\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}},\ldots,\llbracket t_{n}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}
and, in this case,

\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}=M_{\sigma}(\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}},\ldots,\llbracket t_{n}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}})\makebox[0.0pt][l]{.}

A premodel of
\mathbf{T}
is then a
model of
\mathbf{T}
, or

\mathbf{T}
-model
, when moreover

–

for all
\sigma\colon s_{1}\times\cdots\times s_{n}\rightarrow s\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{t}
,
M_{\sigma}
is defined at
\bar{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
in
M_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times M_{s_{n}}
if and only if,
for all
(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathrm{Def}(\sigma)
, we have
\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}=\llbracket t_{2}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}
,

–

for every triple
(\Gamma,t_{1},t_{2})\in E
where
\Gamma=(x_{1}:s_{1},\ldots,x_{n}:s_{n})
, given a tuple
\bar{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in M_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times M_{s_{n}}
, if both
\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}

and
\llbracket t_{2}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}
are defined, then
\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}=\llbracket t_{2}\rrbracket_{\bar{y}}
.

A
morphism
between two models
M
and
M^{\prime}
is a family of functions

F=(F_{s}\colon M_{s}\rightarrow M^{\prime}_{s})_{s\in S}

such that, for every
\sigma\colon s_{1}\times\cdots\times s_{n}\rightarrow s\in\Sigma
, for every
\bar{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
in
M_{s_{1}}\times\cdots\times M_{s_{n}}
such that
M_{\sigma}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is defined, we have that
the element
M^{\prime}_{\sigma}(F_{s_{1}}(y_{1}),\ldots,F_{s_{n}}(y_{n}))
is defined and

F_{s}(M_{\sigma}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}))=M^{\prime}_{\sigma}(F_{s_{1}}(y_{1}),\ldots,F_{s_{n}}(y_{n}))\makebox[0.0pt][l]{.}

We write
\operatorname{Mod}(\mathbf{T})
for the category of
\mathbf{T}
-models and
morphisms.

Essentially algebraic theories allows one to easily recognise a lot of
categories as l.f.p. using the following property:

Theorem 1

(
[
1
, Theorem 3.36]
)
.

A category
\mathcal{C}
is l.f.p. if and only if it is equivalent to the category

\operatorname{Mod}(\mathbf{T})
for some essentially algebraic theory
\mathbf{T}
.

Example 15
.

The category
\mathbf{Set}
is essentially algebraic since it is the category of
models of the essentially algebraic
theory
(\{\mathbf{s}\},\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset)
.

Example 16
.

The category
\mathbf{Mon}
of monoids and monoid morphisms is the category of models
of the essentially algebraic theory

\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{mon}}=(\{\mathbf{s}\},\{\mathbf{e}\colon\mathrm{1}\rightarrow{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{m}\colon{{\mathbf{s}}}\times{{\mathbf{s}}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{s}}\},E,\{\mathbf{e},\mathbf{m}\},\emptyset)

where
E
consists of three equations

–

\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{e},x_{1})=x_{1}
in the context
(x_{1}\colon{\mathbf{s}})
,

–

\mathbf{m}(x_{1},\mathbf{e})=x_{1}
in the context
(x_{1}\colon{\mathbf{s}})
,

–

\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{m}(x_{1},x_{2}),x_{3})=\mathbf{m}(x_{1},\mathbf{m}(x_{2},x_{3}))
in the context
(x_{1}\colon{{\mathbf{s}}},x_{2}\colon{{\mathbf{s}}},x_{3}\colon{{\mathbf{s}}})
.

In particular,
\mathbf{Mon}
is locally finitely presentable.

Example 17
.

The category
\mathbf{Cat}
of small categories is the category of models of the
essentially algebraic theory
\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{cat}}=(S,\Sigma,E,\Sigma_{t},\mathrm{Def})
defined as follows. The set
S
consists of two
sorts
\mathbf{o}
and
\mathbf{m}
corresponding to objects and morphisms, and

\Sigma=\{\mathbf{s}\colon\mathbf{m}\rightarrow\mathbf{o},\;\mathbf{t}\colon\mathbf{m}\rightarrow\mathbf{o},\;\mathbf{id}\colon\mathbf{o}\rightarrow\mathbf{m},\;\mathbf{c}\colon\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{m}\rightarrow\mathbf{m}\}\makebox[0.0pt][l]{.}

Moreover,
E
consists of the equations

–

\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{id}(x_{1}))=x_{1}
and
\mathbf{t}(\mathbf{id}(x_{1}))=x_{1}
in the context
(x_{1}\colon\mathbf{o})
,

–

\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{c}(x_{1},x_{2}))=\mathbf{s}(x_{1})
and
\mathbf{t}(\mathbf{c}(x_{1},x_{2}))=\mathbf{t}(x_{2})
in the context
(x_{1}\colon\mathbf{m},x_{2}\colon\mathbf{m})
,

–

\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{id}(\mathbf{s}(x_{1})),x_{1})=x_{1}

and
\mathbf{c}(x_{1},\mathbf{id}(\mathbf{t}(x_{1})))=x_{1}
in the context
(x_{1}\colon\mathbf{m})
,

–

\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{c}(x_{1},x_{2}),x_{3})=\mathbf{c}(x_{1},\mathbf{c}(x_{2},x_{3}))
in the
context
(x_{1}\colon\mathbf{m},x_{2}\colon\mathbf{m},x_{3}\colon\mathbf{m})
.

Finally,
\Sigma_{t}=\{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t},\mathbf{id}\}
, and
\mathrm{Def}(\mathbf{c})
is
the singleton set containing the equation
\mathbf{t}(x_{1})=\mathbf{s}(x_{2})
. This
shows that
\mathbf{Cat}
is a locally finitely presentable category.

Details about the implementation of the operations

Here, we give additional details about the implementation of the operations that
were briefly discussed in
Section

IV-D
, like the
computation of finite colimits of finite presheaves, of left Kan extensions and
of the operations of the game of reflection; the code for the latter operations
can also be used to compute the reflection using the exhaustive strategy.

-G
1
Computing colimits

One of the main operations that we will need to compute in our methods is the
one of colimit of presheaves. Indeed, the Kan extension we need to build the
functor
F^{\prime}
from a Kan model
F\colon C\rightarrow\hat{D}
can be expressed as a
particular colimit, and the moves made in the game of reflection are computed
using pushouts and coequalisers.

We will only consider the more elementary computation of colimits of finite
sets, since a method for computing colimits of sets readily extend to
presheaves, because colimits of presheaves are pointwisely computed.

First, remember that any colimit in a cocomplete category can be computed as a
coequaliser:

Proposition 16
.

Given a category
\mathcal{C}
with coproducts, a small category
I
and a diagram
d\colon I\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, the colimit
\colim_{i}d(i)\in\mathcal{C}
, when it exists, can be
computed as the coequaliser

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coprod_{f\colon i\rightarrow i^{\prime}\in I}d(i)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coprod_{i\in I}d(i)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[\iota_{i}\circ\operatorname{id}_{d(i)}]_{f\colon i\rightarrow i^{\prime}\in I}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[\iota_{i}\circ f]_{f\colon i\rightarrow i^{\prime}\in I}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}

where the
\iota_{i}\colon d(i)\rightarrow\coprod_{i\in I}d(i)
’s are the coprojections
and
[-]_{f\colon i\rightarrow i^{\prime}\in I}
is the copairing operation for the coproduct

\coprod_{f\colon i\rightarrow i^{\prime}\in I}d(i)
. The coprojections
p_{i}\colon d(i)\rightarrow\colim_{i}d(i)
are then, up to the isomorphism
\colim_{i}d(i)\cong L
, the
morphisms
q\circ\iota_{i}
.

Computing the coproduct
S
of a finite list

Computing reflection

Given a finite presheaf model
(C,O)
, we must now consider the computation of
the moves of the game of reflection.

First, we might want to know what are the available moves of a certain type,
starting from a morphism
m\colon X\rightarrow Y\in\hat{C}
seen as a configuration of
the game. Consider the Dom-E moves relative to some orthogonality condition
g\colon A\rightarrow B\in\hat{C}
, for example. In order to know all the Dom-E moves
involving
g
, we must:

1.

compute all the morphisms
f\colon A\rightarrow X

2.

for each such
f\colon A\rightarrow X
, compute all the
h\colon B\rightarrow Y
such that

h\circ g=m\circ f

3.

return the pairs
(f,h)

Note that one can compute all the functions between two finite sets
S
and

S^{\prime}
. By extension, one can compute all the morphisms between two given finite
presheaves
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
of
\hat{C}
: one just need to compute all the possible
families of functions
(k_{c}\colon Z(c)\rightarrow Z(c))_{c\in\Ob(C)}
and then filter
out the ones that do not satisfy the naturality condition of natural
transformations
Z\Rightarrow Z^{\prime}
, which is a computable condition on such family of
morphisms, based on the encodings of
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
. Thus, the above procedure can
be computed. Similarly, one can compute all the instances of the Dom-U,
Cod-E and Cod-U moves.

Once one knows all the possible moves of a certain type relative to a certain
g\colon A\rightarrow B\in O
, one might want to compute the result of applying the move.
We will again consider for example an instance
(f,h)
of a Dom-E move, from
the list computed above. One must first compute the pushout
(
11
), which can be done, since every finite colimit of
finite presheaves can be computed as we explained earlier.

Once that such a pushout is computed, one must compute the factorisation
m^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\rightarrow Y
of the cocone
(m,h)
. Since every element of
X^{\prime}
is the image of
either one element of
X
or one element of
B
, the encoding of
m^{\prime}
can be
computed by iterating over the elements of
X
and
B
, computing their images
in
X^{\prime}
and
Y
, and then adding the computed bindings to the definition of

m^{\prime}
. This way, we were able to compute the result

of a particular instance

(f,h)
of a Dom-E move. Computing the results of the other moves can be
similarly achieved.

These methods allow one to compute the result of moves one by one, but one can
adapt the procedure to compute the result of all the available moves at once.
This can be done by computing a colimit on a diagram similar
to (
