Consensus recommendations on age-related sarcopenia in South Asia: A nutrition-focused approach
Authors: Bhakti Samant, Anita Jatana, Shivshankar Timmanpyati, Archana Sinha, Amreen Sheikh, Daphnee Lovesley, Datta Patel, Kiran Dalal, Ritika Samaddar, Saima Faruqui, Sheetal Ghali, Shifali Mehraa, Simmi Shah, Amal Firouse, Atul Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAge-related sarcopenia, defined as the progressive decline in muscle function, strength, and mass with age, is a growing health concern in South Asian countries (Bangladesh, India, and Sri Lanka) due to their rapidly aging populations. It tends to appear earlier, is frequently overlooked, and worsens after age 60. Despite its prevalence, standardized tools for screening and assessing sarcopenia and dietary status are lacking. Since diet quality is a modifiable risk factor, nutritional strategies play a key role in prevention and management. However, international dietary guidelines are often unsuitable for South Asians due to regional dietary differences and variations within countries. As sarcopenia onset occurs earlier in this population, there is an urgent need to implement standardized screening, diagnosis, and nutrition assessments during dietetic consultations. To address this gap, a panel of experienced dietitians from Bangladesh, India, and Sri Lanka developed this South Asian consensus to provide evidence-based recommendations for identifying and managing age-related sarcopenia. The panel emphasized the use of locally available, affordable, and nutritionally adequate foods to support practical, region-specific interventions.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Geriatric, Primary sarcopenia, Age-related sarcopenia, South Asia

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a syndrome characterized by the loss of any 2 of the following muscle-related parameters: function, strength, and mass [1,2]. Age-related sarcopenia (or primary sarcopenia) is a global physiological problem. The prevalence of sarcopenia in various Asian populations ≥60 years varied from 5.5 to 25.7% [3]. In India, 36.1% of individuals over 60 years were diagnosed with sarcopenia [4]. The prevalence of sarcopenia varies with the screening method, with an Indian study reporting 47.69% with SARC-F scoring and 63.08% by Ishii scoring in individuals ≥60 years [5].

Primary sarcopenia is caused by the age-related decline in lean muscle mass at a rate of 3–8% loss/decade after 30 years of age [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. The decline is even higher in healthy individuals over age 50, as they experience an annual loss of muscle mass by 1% to 2% and muscle strength by 1.5% [6,10,11]. The problem intensifies after 60 years of age [6,7,12]. A study from Sri Lanka noted that total lean mass increased by 15% from the second (20–29 years) to the fourth decade (40–49 years), then decreased by 13% between the fifth decade and ≥60 years, with 9% loss occurring after 59 years [12]. Fig. 1 shows the age-related decline in muscle mass, strength, and function with advancing age, which typically begins in the third decade of life.

Age-related decline in muscle mass, strength, and function [9,13]. The dominant handgrip strength (in kg) (A), the appendicular skeletal muscle index (in kg/m2) (B), and usual gait speed (in m/s) (C) in Indian males and females decline progressively from the second decade of life; the appendicular skeletal muscle index peaks in the third decade and then progressively declines [13].

Along with a decline in muscle mass, there is a progressive decline in bone density and joint integrity with advancing age in individuals aged 50–80 years [9,12]. Therefore, primary sarcopenia is a complex multifactorial syndrome in which psychosocial, endocrine, dietary, and physical activity-related factors contribute to the pathogenesis of age-related sarcopenia (Fig. 2) [2,[8], [9], [10],14]. These changes result in a decreased intake of diet and nutrients [5,10,15,16] and an increased risk of disability, falls, and fractures [2,9,10,12,13] in older adults.

Mechanisms implicated in the pathogenesis of age-related sarcopenia [2,[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

Early dietary intervention coupled with adequate physical exercise is crucial to mitigating the impact of sarcopenia and maintaining the functional independence of older adults [5,9]. There is a dearth of comprehensive data on the diagnosis and management of sarcopenia in the South Asian countries of Bangladesh, India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka [2]. Most of the available literature is based on Caucasian cut-offs [13].

Diet quality is a potentially modifiable risk factor for sarcopenia [17]. Many international guidelines provide recommendations on various nutrients for the prevention and management of sarcopenia [[18], [19], [20]]. However, the South Asian Working Action Group on Sarcopenia (SWAG-SARCO) guidelines do not address the daily nutrient intake of individuals diagnosed with sarcopenia. Although the Indian Council of Medical Research-National Institute of Nutrition (ICMR–NIN) guidelines provide sample diets for preventing sarcopenia in older men and women [21], comprehensive guidelines from South Asian countries such as Bangladesh, India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka generally lack guidance on the nutritional management of age-related sarcopenia. Older adults in South Asian countries tend to reside with their families, and assisted living, old age homes, and long-term care facilities is less common in these regions. Accordingly, guidelines specifically designed for the nutritional management of age-related sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults in South Asia are needed.

Consensus process

An advisory board meeting was convened to develop consensus on nutrition-focused approaches to sarcopenia. Before the meeting, detailed Google Scholar and Medline searches were conducted to retrieve sarcopenia-related literature from the 4 South Asian countries (Bangladesh, India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka), as well as national, South Asian, and international guidelines. This data was shared with all the experts before the meeting.

Fourteen nutrition experts from South Asian countries (Bangladesh, India, and Sri Lanka) participated in the meeting in India, held on November 23–24, 2024. During the meeting, the discussions were structured into 3 panel groups addressing the following: (1) age-related sarcopenia, (2) disease-related sarcopenia, and (3) sarcopenia associated with catabolic states.

The objective of the first panel was to formulate region-specific consensus recommendations for the prevention, screening, diagnosis, and management of age-related sarcopenia in South Asia, with a primary emphasis on dietary interventions. The representatives from India and Sri Lanka comprised the panel addressing age-related sarcopenia. The experts discussed the literature and their experience in managing the condition. An experienced Registered Dietitian (RD)/trained Clinical Dietitian (CD) moderated the discussion and presented the main points on which the majority (>80%) of the experts agreed on. Consensus statements were formulated, and supporting literature was added as commentary.

Clinical tools to assess muscle strength

Consensus statement: Hand grip strength (HGS) and lower extremity muscle strength (LEMS) should be used to clinically assess muscle strength of the upper and lower limbs using the hand-held dynamometer (HHD). The same HHD or HHDs of the same make should be used throughout. The highest of 3 readings should be recorded as the HGS.

Commentary: HGS for muscle strength of the upper limb and LEMS can be easily measured in primary care and community settings using a hand-held dynamometer (HHD) (Fig. 3) [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. Although isokinetic dynamometers are more precise for measuring HGS, they are bulky and costly, thus limiting their use in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [23]. HGS and LEMS are assessed thrice with a resting interval, and the highest of the 3 readings per limb is taken as muscle strength [24,26,27]. However, the mean of 3 readings is the accepted protocol followed by some practitioners [27].

Hand grip strength (HGS) and lower extremity muscle strength (LEMS) measured using hand-held dynamometer (HHD) [22,23,28].

The HHD readings vary with the type of dynamometer and encouragement level provided during the test. SWAG-SARCO recommended that the same HHD or the same-make HHD and the same encouragement level be provided at all time points when testing muscle strength [2].

Manual muscle testing (MMT) can be used if HHD is unavailable, but it is unreliable as it can cause assessment variability [2]. HGS is measured using the single-arm military press (SAMP) test (30 s considered normal) [29]. The one‐repetition‐maximum knee extension (1-RMKE) method can be used to measure complete knee joint extension (LEMS) (Fig. 4) [30].

One‐repetition‐maximum knee extension (1-RMKE): patient sitting comfortably on a standard quadriceps chair, back straight against the backrest, and legs hanging freely from the chair (A) in resting position and (B) raising increasing weight against gravity in extended knee position [30]. (C) Single-arm military press (SAMP): Hand grip strength (HGS) measured by the patient raising a 1-kg weight by 1 arm over the shoulder, elbow extended, for ≥30 s [29,31].

Western cut-offs for HGS, as suggested by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP), cannot be used in South Asians because they have lower muscle mass than the Western population, and using EWGSOP can result in an overestimation of sarcopenia [32,33]. Pooled data from various Indian studies show that the average HGS in young men (20–35 years) was 36.5 ± 6.8 kg, which reduced to 26.6 ± 7.1 kg in men >45 years. The corresponding results for women were 20.5 ± 4.9 kg (20–35 years) and 17.6 ± 4.6 kg (>45 years) [33].

The Sarcopenia-Chandigarh Urban Bone Epidemiological Study (Sarco-CUBES; average age 44.4 years) and SWAG-SARCO guidelines defined low HGS as <27.5 kg in males and <18.0 kg in females [2,13]. Based on these cut-offs, the study noted that 14.6% had “probable sarcopenia,” 3.2% had “sarcopenia,” and 2.3% had “severe sarcopenia” [13]. The Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 used <28 kg in males and <18 kg in females to diagnose age-related sarcopenia [3]. The experts stressed that RDs and trained clinical dieticians should receive hands-on training using standard protocols for measuring muscle strength. This will help them diagnose sarcopenia effectively.

Clinical tools to assess muscle function

Consensus statement: A 6-m gait speed test should be used to assess muscle function and performance.

Commentary: Muscle function and performance can be reliably assessed using the 6-m gait speed test (the time taken to cover 6 m on a flat surface) [26]. A normal person usually takes <5 s, so a gait speed of <0.8 m/s is considered normal [13,26]. The <0.8 m/s gait speed was validated as the diagnostic cut-off in Indian studies and recommended by the SWAG-SARCO guidelines [34]. A study from Sri Lanka suggested that ≤0.96 m/s should be used as a cut-off to diagnose sarcopenia [35]. The AWGS 2019 recommended <1.0 m/s as the cut-off [3].

Muscle function can also be assessed using the five-times-sit-to-stand test (5TSTS) and 30-s chair stand test (30CST) [36,37]. A Colombian study showed that a <1 standard deviation in STS values of 2.2 ± 0.7 W kg−1 in males and 1.6 ± 0.5 W kg−1 in females was diagnostic of sarcopenia [37]. Each 1-s increment in performing the 30CST increases the probability of sarcopenia by 8% [38]. The AWGS 2019 recommended ≥12 s as a cut-off for 5TSTS [3].

Other less popular tests include the timed up-and-go test (TUGT), short physical performance battery (SPPB) score, 400-m walk time, 6-min walk time, and climbing up the stairs [2,26]. The AWGS 2019 recommended a score of ≤9 for SPPB [3]. The SWAG-SARCO guideline recommended <10.2 s for TUGT based on data from a study from Singapore [2,39].

Clinical tools to assess muscle mass

Consensus statement: Mid-arm circumference (MAC), mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), and calf circumference (CC) should be used to assess skeletal muscle mass (SMM), except in patients with gross edema.

Commentary: MAC or MUAC, and CC are clinically valid, easy to implement, and economical tools that effectively assess SMM and nutritional status in South Asians, even in patients having sarcopenia due to chronic energy deficiency [26,[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Measuring the maximum circumference with a non-elastic tape is sufficiently sensitive and specific for predicting sarcopenia (Fig. 5) [3].

Measuring mid-arm circumference (MAC) or mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) and calf circumference (CC) using a non-elastic tape.

The Longitudinal Aging Study in India-Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia (LASI-DAD) (>60 years) defined the optimal lower and upper cut-offs for CC as 28.1 cm and >31.5 cm, respectively, for males; and 26 cm and >29 cm, respectively, for females [43]. Another Indian study reported that the average CC in the elderly (≥60 years) was 27.6 ± 3.7 cm [42]. The SWAG-SARCO [2] and AWGS 2019 [3] recommended CC <34 cm in men and <33 cm for women as low muscle mass to diagnose sarcopenia, as these values have been validated for Southeast Asians.

To avoid including fat mass in the MAC/MUAC, triceps skinfold (TSF) thickness is often used as an alternative test (MAMC (cm) = MUAC (cm) – [TSF (mm) x 0.314]) [44]. The LASI-DAD study noted that optimal lower and upper cut-offs for MAC were 23.9 cm and >26.9 cm in males; and 22.5 cm and >25 cm in females [43]. The study by Selvaraj reported a mean MUAC of 25.6 ± 3.7 cm [42]. The experts suggested that these cut-offs may be used to diagnose low muscle mass. They also stressed that RDs and trained clinical dieticians should receive hands-on training using standard protocols for measuring muscle mass. This will help them diagnose sarcopenia effectively.

Consensus statement: Imaging and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) should be used to assess muscle mass in people suspected of sarcopenia only if clinical measures cannot guide diagnostic or treatment decisions. The choice of imaging and BIA should depend on availability, accessibility, and affordability.

Commentary: BIA can be as reliable as an axial computed tomography (CT) scan, CC, or HGS for diagnosing sarcopenia [45]. BIA is less expensive than dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and portable enough to be easily used in a community setting. However, BIA is less reliable for BMIs ≥35 kg/m2 [23]. DXA is a reliable tool, and though costly, its use for diagnosing sarcopenia has been suggested if available and affordable [2,46,47].

CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can accurately measure muscle mass and density, and differentiate between lean mass and fatty infiltration [23,48,49]. However, CT and MRI have limited use in diagnosing sarcopenia in LMICs as they are relatively costly and not available across centers [2]. On the other hand, skeletal muscle ultrasound (USG-M) is a relatively cheaper and easily available imaging tool. Although not sensitive enough for diagnosing sarcopenia, its use has been suggested in select cases where imaging is required to drive diagnostic and treatment decisions [2,50].

Appendicular lean mass (ALM) adjusted for body mass index (BMI) or height, known as appendicular skeletal muscle index (ASMI) or appendicular lean mass index (ALMI), can help differentiate between age-related sarcopenia and secondary or pathological sarcopenia, as ALM decreases progressively with age [33,46,47]. Pooled data from various Indian studies show that the average ALM in young men (20–35 years) was 20.5 ± 2.5 kg, which reduced to 18.7 ± 3.3 kg in men >45 years. The corresponding results for women were 13.5 ± 1.8 kg (20–35 years) and 13.3 ± 2.3 kg (>45 years) [33]. The pooled ALMI data showed that the average ALMI in young men (20–35 years) was 7.4 ± 0.7 kg/m2, which reduced to 7.0 ± 1.0 kg/m2 in men >45 years. The corresponding results for women were 5.7 ± 0.6 kg/m2 (20–35 years) and 5.8 ± 0.8 kg/m2 (>45 years) [33].

Asian or South Asian cut-offs should be used to assess low muscle mass as they are significantly lower than those of the Western population (Table 1) [2]. The Sarco-CUBES study defined ASMI <6.11 kg/m2 in males and <4.61 kg/m2 in females as low muscle mass [13]. The SWAG-SARCO proposed ASMI (Lee's equation for Asians [34]) with values <7.0 kg/m2 in males and <5.7 kg/m2 in females as diagnostic of low muscle mass [2].

Algorithm for screening and diagnosing sarcopenia

Using questionnaires to screen for sarcopenia

Consensus statement: The SARC-F (score ≥4) or SARC-CalF (score ≥11) questionnaire should be used to screen for sarcopenia in all patients suspected of having it based on symptoms and signs.

Commentary: The SARC-F and SARC-CalF are user-friendly, simple questionnaires for screening patients suspected of sarcopenia based on their signs and symptoms of muscle weakness (Box 1) [11,53,54]. All healthcare professionals (HCPs) in primary and community settings should be aware of these signs and symptoms and trained to identify risk factors [25].

Signs and symptoms suggestive of sarcopenia [11,53].

Alt-text: Box 1

International guidelines (The International Conference of Frailty and Sarcopenia Research (ICFSR) [55] and EWGSOP2 [56]), Asian guidelines (AWGS 2019 Consensus Update on Sarcopenia Diagnosis and Treatment [3]), and South Asian guidelines (SWAG-SARCO [2]) recommend the SARC-F questionnaire as a tool to screen for sarcopenia.

The SARC-F questionnaire (Box 2) screens 5 components of sarcopenia on a scale of 0 to 10: strength, assistance with walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls; a score ≥4 is predictive of sarcopenia [57]. A study from Korea found that a SARC-F score ≥4 had 95.35% sensitivity and 56.94% specificity for identifying sarcopenia among patients with hip fractures. The score had a positive predictive value (PPV) of 56.94% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 71.3% [58].

The AWGS 2019 and SWAG-SARCO guidelines also recommended clinical suspicion combined with CC or the SARC-CalF questionnaire to screen sarcopenia [2,3]. This questionnaire added calf and thigh circumference to SARC-F, and a score of ≥11 is considered predictive of sarcopenia [3,[59], [60], [61]]. An Indonesian study in elderly outpatients found that SARC-CalF had a sensitivity of 15.79%, specificity of 99.01%, PPV of 75.00% and NPV of 86.21% [62]. Thus, a SARC-CalF score of ≥11 is more likely to detect positive sarcopenia cases and rule out negative cases than a SARC-F score of ≥4 [59,61].

Ideally, in South Asia, since primary care physicians see most patients, screening for sarcopenia should be initiated at the primary care level. This will prevent delays in diagnosis and management [25].

Consensus statement: Sarcopenia should be screened only in suspected cases, and clinical tools should be used to diagnose and confirm only those screened positive for sarcopenia, as this is an economical pathway for LMICs.

Consensus statement: The same clinical tool should be used at all follow-ups as was used for the initial assessment.

Consensus statement: Sarcopenia should be suspected based on signs and symptoms in all individuals aged 30 years or above.

Commentary: The EWGSOP2 guidelines [56] and SWAG-SARCO [2] propose an economical algorithm for age-related sarcopenia (Fig. 6) that involves 4 basic steps: Screening for sarcopenia in suspected cases, assessing for sarcopenia in those screened positive (using grip strength or a chair stand test), confirming sarcopenia (through clinical assessment; imaging such as MRI, CT, DXA, BIA should be performed only in select cases and if patients can afford) and finally, assessing severity only in confirmed cases (using gait speed, SPPB, TUGT, and 6-m walk tests). This approach is economical compared to various other approaches, such as sarcopenia scoring assessment models (SarSA-Mod) and the mini sarcopenia risk assessment (MSRA) [63]. This method should be used to screen and diagnose age-related sarcopenia in South Asian LMICs like Bangladesh, India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka. The experts agreed that clinical measures should be prioritized over imaging, as DXA and BIA are expensive tools for LMICs and are unavailable in most primary care settings [25]. The experts stressed that there is no single measure for diagnosing sarcopenia, and different measures have different sensitivity and specificity [66,67]. Therefore, the same measure should be used at all follow-ups as was used during the initial assessment.

The SO-CUBES study from India reported sarcopenia in 3.2% of apparently healthy adults aged ≥20 years (mean age: 44.4 years). Peak HGS, ASMI, and gait speed were observed in the third and fourth decades [13,68]. Muscle loss may initiate by 30 years of age in some individuals and progress at a rate of 3–8% per decade, aggravating to 1-2% per year after 50 years of age [10]. Another study from India reported a sarcopenia prevalence of 10% with 4.2% having severe sarcopenia in adults over 40 years of age (mean age 53 years) [69].

Preventing age-related sarcopenia

Consensus statement: Age-related sarcopenia can be prevented at 6 levels: primordial, primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary, and quinary, and each level of prevention is important.

Consensus statement: Physical activity should be encouraged as a habit and practiced as part of the daily routine to prevent sarcopenia.

Consensus statement: Dietary diversity and balance should be encouraged for all age groups. Daily diets should include at least 5 food groups and the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) of protein and other nutrients.

Commentary: Since muscles do not work in isolation, optimal functioning of the entire osteo-arthro-muscular (OAM) triad and the involved nerve (neuropathy) is important in preventing sarcopenia [2,70]. Adequate diet (protein and energy intake) and exercise (resistance/strength and aerobic) form the backbone of the prevention and management of sarcopenia [20]. Primordial prevention encourages exercise and physical activity as a daily routine habit [2,54]. It encourages South Asian physical activity practices such as squatting (to use floor-level toilets or to mop floors), sitting cross-legged on the floor, washing clothes by hand, bathing with a bucket and mug, and using stone grinders [2,74].

Primary prevention includes nutritional and physical lifestyle adjustments in patients with risk factors, early diagnosis, and timely management. Secondary prevention aims to prevent complications by focusing on diet and strength training to improve muscle mass and gait and prevent falls [2]. This approach builds on primary prevention for age-related sarcopenia, with a greater focus on comorbid conditions.

Tertiary prevention includes providing assistive devices and home-care equipment to ease activities of daily living (ADLs) and prevent falls. Mobile applications can be encouraged to help select and order nutritious food, learn easy recipes, or set reminders to eat [75]. Patients may need nutritional rehabilitation and may need to be provided with adapted cutlery, plates, glasses, and cups that are easy to hold and prevent spills [75].

Quaternary prevention aims to prevent over-management (diagnosis, investigations, and treatment) and spread the right message [2]. Lastly, quinary prevention aims at spreading the right message and preventing the spread of wrong information [2].

Management of sarcopenia

Since sarcopenia is a multifactorial syndrome, no single treatment modality is sufficient enough to achieve the desired improvement in the OAM triad [2,26]. Pharmacological therapies have a limited role in preventing sarcopenia, halting its progression, and preventing its negative health outcomes [76]. Scientific evidence shows that a combination of physical activity, exercise, diet, and nutrition (especially protein and vitamin D), customized to an individual's needs, is currently the most effective strategy to prevent and manage sarcopenia [2,[76], [77], [78]]. The combination of exercise and nutrition optimization works better than individual components [54,78].

Exercise and physical activity

Consensus statement: A combination of exercises at the intensity and duration prescribed by trained HCPs should be performed under their guidance to prevent and manage sarcopenia.

Commentary: The Society for Sarcopenia, Cachexia, and Wasting Disease (SCWD) and several literary sources recommend resistance and aerobic exercises as a cost-effective measure to enhance muscle strength and gait speed and improve quality of life years (QALY) [14,20,54,79]. The type and duration of exercises should be prescribed by a trained HCP and performed under their guidance [2,72].

Different exercises have distinct effects on the OAM triad. A combination of exercises (resistance, aerobic, stretching, and balance) yields better outcomes than a single exercise type [54,[79], [80], [81]]. They improve knee extension strength, gait speed, TUGT, leg muscle mass, and appendicular mass [14,20,54,79,82].

Programs such as Reablement Strategies targeting Sarcopenia (ReStart-S), which include a combination of exercises and vary intensity according to need, can be effective [79]. A less intense ReStart-S is devised for individuals with possible sarcopenia than those with severe sarcopenia (Table 3) [79]. Furthermore, since patients with sarcopenia may be unable to perform certain exercises, the exercise combination must be individualized to accommodate their physical limitations.

Nutritional management

Physiological anorexia associated with aging results in decreased protein and energy intake and a decline in muscle mass and weight [19,20]. Older individuals need a higher protein intake than younger individuals as they have lower metabolic efficiency and a higher incidence of catabolic conditions [19,20]. Diet quality is positively associated with muscle strength and, to some extent, with muscle mass and function (Table 4) [17].

Dietary assessment

Consensus statement: All older adults should be routinely screened for age-related sarcopenia using a validated tool to identify those at risk of malnutrition.

Commentary: Various nutrition guidelines recommend that dietary assessment include screening for the risk of malnutrition by asking about dietary intake, appetite, and involuntary weight loss, or by using a validated tool such as the malnutrition screening tool (MST) (Fig. 9) [18,19,83]. However, the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines recommend the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) tool for screening [3,19]. The experts stressed that older adults who screen negative for sarcopenia should be re-assessed after 1 year to identify those at risk of malnutrition.

Algorithm for nutrition screening, assessment, and intervention in age-related sarcopenia [18].

Consensus statement: A positive malnutrition screening shall be followed by a systematic assessment.

Commentary: Nutrition guidelines recommend using a validated tool such as MNA to assess those at risk of malnutrition by RDs or trained CDs or registered dietary nurses (RDNs) [18,19]. The MNA is easy to complete it takes 5 min. It assesses malnutrition, immobility, and neuropsychological problems [19]. If MNA is not available, Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) can be used. MNA and SGA can be used at the community level and in assisted living care [18].

Dietary habits should be assessed by taking a nutrition history and asking questions about actual intake of different nutrients, food intolerances, refusal to eat, changes in dietary habits, number of meals per day, and religious and cultural preferences [83]. The ESPEN guidelines suggest using the plate method to assess the amount of food and fluid intake [19].

Diet quality can be assessed using scoring indices to cover adequacy (food serving size), variety (dietary diversity with a focus on protein), moderation (total and type of fat consumed), and overbalance (macronutrient and fatty acid ratio) [17]. Thus, diet quality can be scored as poor, needing improvement, or good using any of the diet quality assessment measures available to RDs and trained CDs [17].

The dietary assessment should include physical impairments affecting diet, such as dysphagia and alterations in taste and smell [83]. These dietary assessments, along with anthropometric measures such as BMI and BIA (if possible, affordable, and required to manage sarcopenia) and clinical tools for assessing muscle strength, function, and mass, can help develop a holistic diet plan for the patient [83]. The SCWD recommends measuring 25-hydroxy vitamin D (25(OH)D) levels in all patients with sarcopenia [20].

Dietary interventions

South Asians usually eat a diet high in carbohydrates and low in protein, with protein intake below the RDA [72,84]. Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) is common in South Asian adults, necessitating increased energy and protein supplementation in these patients [84,85].

The ESPEN guidelines categorize nutritional interventions into 4 main types (or 4 pillars of dietary interventions): supportive interventions (enhancing meal ambiance, providing nursing interventions); dietary/nutritional counseling; dietary modifications (adding snacks and finger food, fortification, modifying texture); and oral nutritional supplements (ONS) [19].

Consensus statement: Nutrition and hydration should be individualized yet comprehensive enough to ensure adequate nutritional intake, maintain or improve nutritional status, and improve clinical outcomes and quality of life.

Commentary: Older adults from South Asia have poor dietary intake, and most cannot meet their daily requirements for protein, energy, vitamin D, calcium, vitamin B6, folate, vitamin E, and dietary fiber (Table 5) [15,[86], [87], [88], [89], [90], [91], [92]]. The daily intake of protein, energy, vitamins, and other micronutrients recommended by various nutrition guidelines for age-related sarcopenia is outlined in (Table 6). The ESPEN and evidence-based nursing practice (EBNP) guidelines recommend that total energy and protein intake be individualized according to nutritional status, physical activity level, and tolerance [18,19]. These recommendations can be considered while prescribing medical nutrition therapy (MNT), or ONS, or food fortification to boost protein and energy intake [18].

Hydration is often overlooked during meal planning and is critical for older adults due to reduced thirst sensation, changes in renal function, and medications such as diuretics that cause water loss [76]. Dehydration can influence cognition and physical function in the elderly [76].

Consensus statement: Good quality adequate protein intake should be ensured to improve muscle mass, strength, and function.

Commentary: Inadequate intake of protein, energy, and various macro- and micronutrients has been implicated in the pathogenesis of age-related sarcopenia (Fig. 10) [10], while adequate protein intake has been linked to improved muscle mass, strength, and function [14,17]. An RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day is considered inadequate to maintain muscle mass in older adults [14]. Many studies and guidelines recommend a higher protein intake for older adults with sarcopenia or at risk of sarcopenia [6,18,19,78]. It is also suggested that the protein RDA should be increased in older individuals with higher activity levels (Table 6) [18,78].

Impact of poor dietary intake in the pathogenesis of age-related sarcopenia [10].

Apart from quantity, the quality, timing, and distribution of protein intake throughout the day may also affect muscle mass, strength, and function [11,14,100]. Both animal (dairy, egg, meat, poultry, and fish) and plant proteins (millet, soy, and pulses) are good sources of high-quality protein [11,100,103]. High-quality proteins such as whey protein, which are rich in leucine and have high essential amino acid (EAA) content, are recommended for the prevention and management of age-related sarcopenia due to their positive anabolic effects [2,21,78,100].

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) concluded that protein source did not affect absolute lean body mass and strength, but animal protein intake resulted in a higher percentage of lean body mass than plant protein [11,104]. A study from China showed that the amount of protein consumed affected SMM in community-dwelling older adults more than the source of protein [103]. Evidence indicates that distributing protein intake evenly between meals (breakfast, lunch, and dinner) shows better protein synthesis than consuming the total daily intake in 1 meal [100].

Consensus statement: Vitamin D supplementation should be initiated in all individuals diagnosed with sarcopenia.

Commentary: Vitamin D has been associated with muscle recovery and, along with leucine, stimulates protein anabolism [78]. A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs showed that vitamin D supplementation significantly increased muscle strength, particularly in those with baseline 25 (OH) D levels <30 nmol/L [105]. Vitamin D was supplemented in a dose of 700–1000 IU/day as vitamin D (3) (cholecalciferol) or vitamin D (2) (ergocalciferol); or 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (3) (1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol) or as 1α-hydroxyvitamin D (3) (1α-hydroxycalciferol) [106]. The supplement intake reduced the risk of falls in older adults by 19%. Other systematic reviews (38 RCTs involving 61 350 participants; ≥700 IU vitamin/day [106] and 35 RCTs involving 58 937 participants; 800–1000 IU/day [107]) have also reported the beneficial effects of vitamin D supplementation on reducing the risk of falls.

Calcium is important for muscle function [100]. Evidence shows that combining vitamin D with calcium increases calcium absorption [100]. This approach reduces the risk of falls more effectively than supplementing only calcium [107].

Consensus statement: Food fortifications, modifications, and medical nutrition therapy should be considered for individuals who are not meeting their daily protein and energy requirements.

Commentary: Food fortification is a food-first approach and an accepted strategy to enhance protein and energy intake [18]. MNT prescribed by an RD/trained CD or HCP considers food fortification and modification to individualize and meet the dietary goals of an individual with sarcopenia.

The prescribed MNT and food fortification should adhere to the 9 A's depicted in Box 3, be easy to prepare, and provide enough alternatives so that individuals adhere to the meal program designed for them [18,72]. A study noted that older adults and even younger individuals requiring protein fortification preferred to consume the fortified food at breakfast or as a between-meal snack [108]. In addition, engaging family members and caregivers and coordinating with other HCPs in nutrition planning improved adherence to therapy [18,72,109]. Generally, RDs and trained CDs consider food fortification an affordable and easily accessible approach [109].

Millets are affordable protein sources that can be mixed with cereals, pulses, and lentils to enhance the quality of protein in South Asian dishes such as chapatti, porridge, and daliya or rice preparations such as khichadi, pulao, poha, dosa, and idli. Millets can also be used as a substitute for or combined with animal protein sources [84,110,111].

Consensus statement: ONS should be prescribed to individuals whose dietary intake is inadequate to meet or improve the nutritional requirements.

Commentary: A randomized controlled trial from Sri Lanka reported that 200 mL of ONS providing 247 kcal per serving for 12 weeks significantly improved HGS and knee extension strength compared to placebo (water) (P < 0.001 for both) [112]. At the end of 12 weeks, patients on ONS had an HGS of 43.96 ± 18.61 kg vs 32.81 ± 17.92 kg for those on placebo. Similarly, the knee extension strength was 23.45 ± 2.29 kg in ONS and 16.41 ± 2.09 kg in the placebo group [112]. Patients on ONS showed a significant improvement in gait speed vs those on placebo (1.31 ± 0.52 m/s vs 0.87 ± 0.26 m/s).

The SCWD suggested a balanced protein-energy supplement may help prevent or reverse sarcopenia [20]. The EBNP guidelines recommended that RDs, trained CDs, and other HCPS recommend ONS 1 to 2 times per day to increase calorie and protein intake, especially for patients who need to improve their nutritional status and weight [18]. ONS should be considered an integral part of nutritional intervention in community-dwelling older adults, especially those with limited food availability or the ability to cook meals [18]. The EBNP guidelines note that ONS could be a low-preparation and convenient protein and energy source for these individuals [18]. The ESPEN guidelines also consider ONS as one of the 4 pillars of nutrition intervention [19].

Evidence shows that ONS with specific foods for special medical purposes (FSMP), such as whey protein-rich FSMP, increases post-prandial plasma amino acids more than other protein ONS, thus encouraging better protein synthesis [78]. The positive effect of Whey-FSMP is seen irrespective of exercise or additional leucine or energy supplementation [78].

The SWAG-SARCO guidelines recommended protein supplements, including whey, combined with resistance exercise to improve muscle strength, muscle mass, and walking capacity [2]. EAA supplementation was encouraged [2]. Evidence from a systematic review showed that branched-chain amino acids (BCAA), in combination with vitamin D, boost mitochondrial bioenergetic and redox activity and therefore could help in sarcopenia management [113]. As discussed above, leucine intake should be encouraged through meals and supplementation [78,100]. A metabolite of leucine, β-hydroxy β-methylbutyric acid (HMB), is also reported to prevent the loss of muscle mass, strength, and function [78]. However, the SWAG-SARCO found no consistent benefit of HMB supplementation [2].

Omega-3 fatty acids, particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), positively modulate muscle protein metabolism to improve muscle mass and function in older adults. Their anti-inflammatory properties mitigate the chronic inflammation in sarcopenia [100]. The SWAG-SARCO guideline encouraged fatty acid supplementation (omega-3 fatty acids) in combination with exercise and/or protein intake [2].

Dietary monitoring

Consensus statement: A dietary intervention should be monitored and modified according to the changing needs and goals of individuals with sarcopenia.

Commentary: A dietary intervention is designed based on specific nutritional goals. The effectiveness of a dietary intervention can be assessed by monitoring these goals using the SMART criteria: specific, measurable, achievable, related, and time-bound. The periodicity of monitoring can be decided based on the individual's needs and adherence to the nutrition therapy. The intervention and the goal may need to be modified based on the individual's needs. The entire care team and caregivers should be involved in setting goals to achieve the desired results [19]. Repeated counseling of the patients and caregivers should be scheduled to monitor and maintain the nutritional level.

Conclusions

This is the first dietary guideline for age-related sarcopenia from South Asian countries (India, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka). The experts formulated these guidelines based on available literature and their clinical experience. They recognized the need for more literature from these countries to define precise cut-offs for diagnosing sarcopenia. The group also stressed the need for collaborative, multidisciplinary care that integrates sarcopenia screening and care into routine clinical practice and raises awareness of this rapidly aging population.
