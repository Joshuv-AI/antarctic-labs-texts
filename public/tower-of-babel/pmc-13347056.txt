An integrated machine learning framework for EV charging management
Authors: Nandith Sreekumar, Rahul Satheesh, G S Asha Rani, Sheik Mohammed Sulthan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the shift to electric mobility intensifies, unpredictable EV charging challenges grid stability. This study proposes a multi-layered machine learning framework balancing grid optimization and user service. First, session-level prediction models estimated energy and cost; XGBoost achieved the highest energy accuracy (), while Random Forest best predicted cost (). Second, a station-level forecasting model using XGBoost demonstrated exceptional precision for daily demand (, MAE=0.90 kWh). Finally, K-Means clustering segmented drivers, revealing a user base dominated by Heavy Energy Users (43.5%) and Occasional Visitors (38.8%). This segmentation enables Charge Point Operators to design personalized services and demand response strategies. Overall, the framework integrates prediction, forecasting, and behavioral segmentation to support scalable, data-driven decisions. Ultimately, these insights equip utility providers and operators with the necessary tools to proactively manage load congestion and optimize capital expenditure planning.Keywords: Charging behavior, Customer segmentation, Demand forecasting, Electric vehicles, Machine learning, Smart gridSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

EVs are at the forefront of a massive, worldwide shift in the transport system, which is moving consumption away from internal combustion engines (ICE) towards environmentally friendly, battery powered alternatives1,2. In fact, the idea of using electric propulsion goes back to the 19th century, however, the recent vibrant growth is mostly attributed to the significant improvements in battery technology and the escalation of environmental concerns3. This quick, global acceptance of the technology is accelerating rapidly, and it is being credited to the government subsidies that support it, the extension of the charging infrastructure and the increase in the consumer’s desire for a cleaner transport4. The current EV market is defined by longer driving ranges, lower battery prices, and more models to choose from5. If we consider the next mobility innovations, they are expected to be dominated by the market forces, which will be influenced by the breakthrough technologies such as ultra, fast charging, vehicle to grid (V2G) integration, and self driving capabilities6–8.

The massive adoption of EVs is putting a lot of pressure on the current power grids which in turn require the introduction of intelligent management systems. At the beginning, researchers mainly concentrated on the real time control aspect. For example, Yang et al. proposed a method for energy management in parking areas where the process was done in real, time, the main goal was to increase EV penetration to the maximum level without the grid having to be changed9. In contrast, several authors dealt only with the economic issues by implementing offline and online scheduling algorithms that would facilitate energy transfer between vehicles and thus lower the costs of charging and make the share of renewable energy used higher at the same time10. Kakkar et al. took a step further to address the issues of operational efficiency and security, they researched into how the fusion of blockchain and IoT technology can be used to create a consensus mechanism that is not only efficient in scheduling EVs at charging stations (CHS) realizes but also security aspects like eliminating the possibility of conflicts11. The studies from12 can be considered as an introduction to these changes by giving a detailed review of the topics that cover the components, technologies, and future of EVs.

The expansion in the infrastructure and technology to keep up with the EV electrification is very crucial. The major blackout that happened to a large area of the charging network was just one of the very visible consequences of the infrastructural challenge that the sector is facing. The necessity of this development has been guided by the comprehensive reviews that have been made in13gives a very detailed overview of management technologies and it covers charging methods, international standards, station architectures, and power converter configurations in detail. Similarly14, presents a pictorial view of the evolutions in the charging sector by outlining various techniques such as fast charging, wireless charging, and battery swapping. In order to successfully handle the load as well as enhance the user’s experience, a data driven strategy for modelling EV charging habits has been the subject of interest in a very short time. Researchers have shown the implementation of such a strategy by creating a machine learning ensemble algorithm whose main purpose is the accurate prediction of EV user’s behavior with regard to charging duration and energy consumed15. In their review of the methodologies, Shahriar et al. emphasized on the various machine learning techniques used in the analysis and prediction of EV charging behavior taking into account the supervised, unsupervised, and deep learning methods16. In accordance with these principles, the method involving off grid electric vehicle buses used a neural network to estimate power demand for the next day, therefore, the charging was done on the basis of the minimization of total operational cost and battery health retention17. To address the problem of large scale, high precision predictions, the authors of18 have developed a systematic approach that leads to better precision by clearly distinguishing the linear and nonlinear variables that influence charging energy.

Moving towards advanced control and operational optimization, effective EV charging management remains a multifaceted challenge. In this domain19, provides a comprehensive review of the latest technical and economic trends, categorizing coordinated scheduling by stakeholder levels. This management relies increasingly on data driven insights, as demonstrated by Ramoliya et al.20, who analyzed real world datasets to understand consumption patterns based on location, time, and user to inform efficient grid energy distribution. Specific model implementations include a LSTM-DNN (Long Short-Term Memory - Deep Learning Network) mixture model that takes the future route and traffic condition as input to predict the EV range more accurately, reducing driver instability21. Furthermore, along the line from prediction to real-time control, the authors of22used deep reinforcement learning to design an energy management system that locates cost effective charging schedules that also work well with integrated renewables. Addressing critical issues of security and privacy in AI driven energy prediction23, proposed a secure enhanced federated learning (FL) framework, which assesses the correctness of models sent by CHS to maintain the global prediction model’s accuracy and integrity while penalizing low quality or malicious contributions.

The work done in24 utilizes an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) based model to perform day ahead forecasting of weather parameters, which are then used to calculate solar power generation. The study25combined statistical and ML models to predict charging hub power demand through user behavior and EV features analysis. Focusing on model interpretability26, forecasted the length of charging sessions using ensemble ML and applied Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP). On the consumer side27, employed algorithms for predicting domestic energy consumption, a successful method that28also recognized as beneficial for guiding EVs to the least used CHS. Beyond forecasting and control29, proposed the idea of a nested ML framework to control the storage of hybrid energy in vehicles, managing fuel consumption and battery life. The research done in30 anticipates a “Smart Community Grid” where distributed ledger technology and smart agents facilitate a peer-to-peer energy trade involving EVs. The research31 targets maximizing the economic and environmental performance of industrial parks through a mixed integer linear programming model that is done concurrently with carbon trading, hydrogen energy, and controlled electric vehicle charging loads. Finally, the study32 investigates the problem of reducing total time costs in heterogeneous traffic networks by a two stage alternating minimization algorithm, which coordinates charging station allocation and velocity optimization for both manned vehicles and electric vehicle platoons.

Problem statement and research contribution

Most of the existing prediction models tend to look at the total load while ignoring the extreme fluctuations and the complex interdependencies of the individual sessions. In addition, there is often a lack of clarity in the correlation between the energy consumption and the final cost paid by the user because of time based tariffs and fixed fees. The randomness of individual EV charging sessions, which are influenced by users and vehicle conditions, makes it difficult to carry out accurate, detailed energy consumption forecasting. At the same time, CPOs do not have access to highly accurate models that can also predict the user’s final cost, thus creating a barrier to the introduction of transparent, dynamic pricing systems that are needed for changing user behavior and increasing revenue. In many cases, the deployment of charging infrastructure is a reaction to current situations or is simply proportional to certain static models, which results in the grid capacity not being in harmony with the peak demand. To forecast accurately, it is necessary to have strong models that are able to deal with complex time-series features (lags, seasonality) for local load management. The locally unpredictable, aggregated load fluctuations of individually charged stations are a major cause of anxiety for the local grid and also result in expensive underutilized infrastructures. There is a need for highly accurate, feature rich time-series forecasting models capable of providing short to medium term station-level energy demand prediction to enable proactive load scheduling, congestion mitigation, and efficient capital expenditure planning. The machine learning algorithms used in this research study are typical but represent the first time an integrated, three tier framework and domain specific feature engineering pipeline has been implemented. This proposed pipeline is unique to this work and incorporates strict chronological ordering of historical features. Previous works typically fail to meet these standards due to temporal data leakage when aggregating historical features33,34. The resulting lack of indicator leakage links the stochastic behaviour of individual session energy consumption with forward looking macro grid forecasts, providing CPOs with a fully integrated operational toolkit. Methodology

The methodological framework of this study is a multifaceted design to thoroughly investigate, understand, and delineate the behavior of EV charging. The proposed workflow is shown in Figure 1. In essence, this framework introduces the integration of three separate yet interconnected analyses: first, the session level prediction of energy and cost. Here session-wise prediction implies that the predictions are done for each specific charging session beforehand, so they are not made on an aggregated or averaged basis (such as daily or monthly totals). Second is the station level time-series forecasting of aggregate energy demand. A station-level forecast, conversely, would estimate the total energy required per day or the mean utilization rate of that CHS, that is, by combining or simulating the general trends of all sessions taking place at that location. User behavior clustering is the last stage in this process. The process of user segmentation changes the unprocessed data of charging into what is called practical intelligence. This allows the creation of services tailored to the user’s needs, the efficient management of the grid, and making infrastructure and policy decisions based on data in the EV ecosystem. The end to end workflow of this study entails quite intensive data preprocessing and feature engineering and, subsequently, employing suitable ML models for each task. After that, the models are evaluated, followed by interpretability analysis.

Data preprocessing

The original data with which the analysis was to be performed, was a set that had numerous irregularities and formatting issues in the time, related aspects of the data, thus it had to be carefully corrected before any analysis could be done. Both the created and the concluded timestamps which indicate the start and the end of the individual charging sessions, had their year prefixes recorded wrongly, as can be seen in 0014 instead of the correct 2014. To fix this problem, an automated string correction algorithm was run through all the date fields by replacing any leading 00 that occurs with 20, thus it was ensured that the timestamps were giving the correct chronological years they were representing. After this correction, the fields were changed to standard datetime objects using pandas to_datetime(), and records of the sessions with invalid or unprocessable timestamps were removed in order to avoid distortions in the subsequent time, dependent modeling.

After timestamp fields verification, a number of temporal features were derived from them so that the risk of data leakage could be avoided. Hour of day, month, and year were all directly obtained from the rectified created timestamp and thus the basic temporal descriptors for the following modeling activities were made. These derived variables allow time, aware prediction models to operate as they capture charging behavior diurnal and seasonal variations. The dataset also had categorical day of week labels; however, these labels were inconsistent across the entries (e.g., Tue, Tues, Thu, Thurs). Moreover, a standardized mapping table for weekdays was used to reconcile these discrepancies into full weekday names which thereby reduced the unnecessary categorical fragmentation.

Besides that, the data collection unveiled that there were some missing values in non temporal variables as well. It was discovered that the distance field had several missing entries, as it is the distance traveled prior to charging that is indicated by this field. Since distance is generally skewed in EV usage datasets, the median imputation method was applied in order to avoid the impact of outlier values and at the same time ensure the stability of the dataset. Missing values for the reportedZip attribute were replaced with the class “Unknown”, thus maintaining categorical interpretability without the creation of artificial numeric representations. These imputation techniques were used to make sure that the dataset was still complete and ML pipelines could work with it without compromising its statistical integrity.

In conclusion, preliminary quality assessments validated that each session retained its critical identifiers, including sessionId, userId, and stationId, while all non essential anomalies were eliminated. The final preprocessed dataset yielded a clean, temporally coherent, and structurally dependable foundation upon which more advanced feature engineering and modeling procedures could be systematically implemented.

To ensure robust model selection and prevent temporal data leakage, the work use RandomizedSearchCV to tune hyperparameters with time series split cross-validation using three-fold (3) cross validation (CV). The strict use of chronological (or time-based) CV method for cross-validating training and testing of folds ensures there is no future data generated through predictive modelling at the fold level and that the training fold only uses past data to forecast future instances like in a real world scenario. The features included complex historical features (lagged user averages, station congestion proxies) as well as temporal features (cyclic hour/month encoding), which gave the models essential contextual insights. The model performance, measured by and MAE, showed that tree, based ensemble methods (XGBoost and Random Forest) were the most accurate predictors for both goals, with Random Forest in particular being very good at cost prediction (showing strong alignment with tariff structures) and XGBoost being very good at energy prediction (handling non, linear associations effectively). After that, SHAP analysis was used to interpret the best models, supporting the idea that features related to previous user behavior were the most important predictors.

The secondary objective shifted the focus to the holistic control of energy resources with the help of a time series forecasting framework to predict the daily energy needs of the most visited charging station. This approach required the conversion of the session, wise data into a continuous, univariate time series of daily total energy consumption. The forecasting models i.e. XGBoost, Random Forest, and MLP Neural Network were created based on a large number of time, series specific features. These features included, among others, very significant auto, regressive lags (e.g., lag, 7, lag, 30), rolling window statistics (mean and standard deviation), and also seasonal components (day, of, week, day, of, year sine/cosine transformations) to be able to fully explain the cyclical patterns of weekly and yearly demand. To achieve a robust validation, time ordered partitioning and time series split cross, validation were employed, in a similar way to the first objective. The XGBoost model was the one that stood out as the most powerful predictor, reaching high performance metrics (>0.95) by effectively mapping the non, linear relationship between the large number of historical and contextual features and the next aggregate demand. Such a highly accurate forecast at the level of individual stations is indispensable for the deliberate moving of electric utility loads and the scheduling of future capacity of the infrastructure. The ultimate goal of this research was the implementation of unsupervised machine learning methods to categorize users according to their charging habits over time. User specific profiles were developed through the use of three essential descriptive features: average energy consumption per session, average session duration, and frequency of sessions (total number of sessions). K-Means clustering algorithm was utilized due to its ease of understanding and computational speed. The determination of the best number of clusters was made by combining results from the Elbow Method (which minimizes the Within, Cluster Sum of Squares, WCSS) and Silhouette Analysis (which maximizes the separation between clusters), thus leading to the recognition of four different segments. After the clustering stage, a detailed cluster profiling analysis was performed which associated each group with a certain behavioral pattern like “Rush Hour Commuters” (characterized by high frequency and specific peak periods) or “Fast Charging Users” (characterized by extremely high average energy consumption over a few sessions). This segmentation provides essential implications for CPOs to tailor the services, set up the targeted pricing schemes, and improve the user experience.

Evaluation and interpretability

Time-ordered split and validation

The main evaluation plan mainly depended on an 80/20 time split of the dataset. The last 20% of charging sessions were in fact used for a completely hold, out test set in this. This approach is very similar to the deployment in the real world, where a model would be trained on past data to make predictions of future events which have not been seen yet. Hyperparameter tuning was done with the help of the Time series split cross validation technique, and only the training set was used for that. This CV strategy thus ensures that each validation fold keeps the temporal ordering, hence, it prevents future data from being leaked into the training process and thus confirms the model’s real capability of generalizing to new data.

Interpretability with SHAP analysis

To move beyond a simple prediction accuracy and extract actionable insights from the dataset, SHAP was carried out for the best performance ensemble models: XGBoost and Random Forest. SHAP values quantify the contribution of each feature to the final prediction for every single instance, allowing model-agnostic and instance-specific interpretation.

Cluster interpretation and actionability

The four customer segments include Daily Commuters, Heavy Energy Users, Opportunity Chargers, and Occasional Visitors. These represent the distinct behavioral segments of EV drivers that were uncovered through the clustering analysis. Each one reflects a different combination of energy consumed, session duration, and session frequency, all of which is vital information for CPO and grid planners alike. Daily Commuters are those that have high frequency, or a lot of sessions. Their charging times are predictable and coincide with morning and evening peak traffic hours, suggesting daily workplace or commuter hub charging, placing high stress on the grid at predictable times. Heavy Energy Users are those whose average energy consumed per session is the highest, often accompanied by long session durations.

This suggests that heavy energy users use charging to replenish significant amounts of battery capacity. Long distance driving or larger battery vehicles may be reasons for this, making them key candidates for scheduled, off peak charging programs. Opportunity Chargers engage in low energy, very short duration sessions, indicating that they prefer quick top offs whenever an available charging spot presents itself. Their behavior necessitates charging infrastructure that is easy to access and quick turnaround. Lastly, Occasional Visitors have the lowest session frequency and show their sporadic reliance on the public charging network. This, in effect, makes their behavior highly variable, while posing the least direct challenge to daily demand management.

Results and discussion

Session-wise prediction

The function of XGBoost Regressor was identified as the best model for energy consumption prediction (kWhTotal) with the coefficient of determination ( = 0.5855). This denotes that the ensemble gradient boosting framework has proficiently illustrated 58.55% of the variability in session energy demand. The difference in performance between XGBoost and the second best model, i.e. Random Forest ( = 0.5806), is very close. Random Forest was able to attain a marginally lower MAE (1.1582 kWh) implying that, while XGBoost may be able to better capture overall variance, the Random Forest model is making smaller average magnitude errors which is generally preferable for practical forecasting. The MLP Network and the SVR Model were chosen to establish the baseline for creating benchmarks for loading forecasts36. The results show a distinct difference in the effectiveness of the models when they are used on micro-level data of electric vehicle (EV) charging. It has been proven by many researches that SVR is one of the most effective models for forecasting the electricity load on the main grid at the level of very large aggregates. However, in this study, SVR failed to model the complex non-linear patterns of individual charging sessions resulting in a negative (−0.052) for cost prediction. On the other hand, XGBoost and Random Forest models performed much better than SVR. It is obvious that the sequential boosting and decision tree structures are far more capable of dealing with the highly fragmented and behavior dependent variableslike time-efficiency proxies and lagged user averages that have a major impact on EV charging. The MLP Network ( = 0.5171) and SVR Model ( = 0.4155) serve as the major standards in the current literature on load forecasting with numerous authors using these two models as reference points in their load forecasting studies. The performance of the tree-based ensemble models developed in the newly engineered feature space significantly exceeded the performance of the MLP Network and SVR Model on the initial training/validation dataset and thus provided a clear demonstration of how well non-linear ensemble methods work when dealing with complex, fragmented EV charging datasets.

The SHAP analysis of the energy prediction offered essential model-agnostic interpretability that tracks the average absolute influence of each factor to the output of the model made locally for all data points to the energy consumption (kwhTotal). Comparing all four models (XGBoost, Random Forest, MLP, and SVR), the main interpretation of the analysis stays the same i.e., features from history and the user dominate the prediction, by far, these features lead the model, temporal features of day or hour barely make a noise.

Here, the feature num__user__energy__trend (rolling 3-session mean energy), num__time__efficiency__proxy (session duration relative to average station duration), and num__time__since__last__charge (time gap between sessions) are always at the top, especially for the two best-performing models, XGBoost and Random Forest, where they are most important. This discovery confirms the central methodology: the prediction of the energy to be used in a session is less dependent on the exact time of day or month (features like num__hour__cos and num__month__sin are ranked lowest) and relies more on individual user consumption habits and the immediate context of the station. The reason is that EV energy consumption is mostly driven by user behavior and vehicle state of charge (from prior trends and time between charges) rather than simple load patterns, thus providing an actionable insight that model development should focus on these lagged behavioral features first.

The SHAP analysis for the session-wise Cost Prediction problem depicts a very similar trans model (Random Forest, XGBoost, SVR and MLP) feature hierarchy to a great extent. The most important feature by far is num_time_efficiency_proxy (the ratio of the session duration to the lagged average station duration). The overwhelming dominance of this feature strongly implies that the actual charging cost is the primary driver for the duration of the session, in connection with which tariffs that incorporate connection fees, idle fees, or time-based penalties after a certain period are very likely.

Other top-ranked features in the ensemble models are num_station_avg_charge_time and num_average_station_duration, which confirm that station-level norms or average charging times are at the core of the pricing calculation. This is affirmation of the inference that the charging cost is the one that is not figured out only by the energy (kWh) consumed, but rather, by a time based component and the context of the charging location.

Most of the user specific historical features and general time variables are, however, only slight contributors to the predicted cost, which means that the pricing rules are in effect evenly irrespective of an individual’s past behavior. This SHAP insight is essential in proving that CPOs create tariffs in a way that the cost goes up exponentially with the session duration, not just energy.

Customer segmentation

The 3D customer segment graph shown in Figure 6 displays the clusters of users in the space of three main features: Average Energy (kWh), Average Duration (hrs), and Session Frequency. From the graph, it can be seen that the four clusters, physically separated and structurally intact, have been confirmed by the data. Cluster separation, the individual data points do not show any signs of being evenly distributed throughout the axis but rather they form closed groups. The clusters are mainly distinguished along the Session Frequency (Z-axis) and Average Energy (X-axis) directions. High frequency users shown in darker points are points with a high Session Frequency values (top section of the plot, higher Z-axis values) are distinctly separated from the main body of the data, thus, indicating a dedicated group (probably the Daily Commuters) of users. Low frequency users shown in blue/green points are most of the users are found to be clustered at the lower end of the Session Frequency axis (Z-axis below 50). In this group of users, the difference is determined based on Average Energy and Average Duration, thereby, distinguishing the ones who rarely use low-energy and those who consume moderate energy within similar session duration. Heavy users seen in yellow points along the mid-range are a handful of points that especially feature moderate frequency alongside higher energy and duration values, hence, represent the Heavy Energy Users or Occasional Visitors with unusual consumption patterns, thereby, confirming that the clustering has successfully recognized these different profiles in the multivariate space.

The allocation indicates that CPOs should put most of their energy into dealing with the Heavy Energy (high-volume) and Daily Commuter (high-impact) segments.

Computational complexity and time cost

To evaluate the practical deployability of the proposed framework, computational time costs were recorded. The models were trained and evaluated on a standard local workstation. The system configuration is 8gb ram, Processor is Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-9300H CPU @ 2.40GHz (2.40 GHz) with NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1650 (4 GB) and Intel(R) UHD Graphics 630 (128 MB) graphics card. The execution times for each pipeline stage are 40 sec for data preprocessing and feature engineering, 35 minutes for model training and hyperparameter tuning across all pipelines, and 22 ms for a single session/station forecast. The very low inference latency demonstrates that these optimized tree-based ensemble models can be used for real-time/edge level management of EV Charging, and provide a significant computational advantage over larger deep learning model architectures.

Conclusion

This study has been able to create and prove the effectiveness of a ML framework that deals with the core issues that come from managing the charging infrastructure of EV and the comprehension of user behavior in a comprehensive manner. The three main objectives of the analysis have been met with the provision of predictive and strategic insights of a very high confidence level. Ensemble methods were the best performers in session-wise prediction, with the Random Forest Regressor leading to an extremely accurate () prediction of charging cost a result that is very important for dynamic pricing transparency while XGBoost was the most reliable session energy predictor (). SHAP interpretability analysis suggested that these predictions are support mainly by historical user trends and time-based factors, thus giving a rationale for the complex feature engineering strategy used.

At the same time, the forecasting at the station level turned out to be very efficient, the XGBoost Regressor, in particular, was able to demonstrate exceptional performance () in the prediction of daily aggregate demand. Such an extent of precision is extremely important to load management on the side of the utility that is proactive, thereby enabling the utility to maintain grid stability and CPOs to make the best decisions regarding the capacity to be expanded. The most important part of customer segmentation in this research, which was a strategic element, utilized k, means clustering to understand complex behavioral patterns and come up with four simple user profiles which are Daily Commuters, Heavy Energy Users, Opportunity Chargers, and Occasional Visitors. Such a difference is necessary for the shift from generalized management to personalized service design. In this way, by identifying the segments such as Daily Commuters (who drive a predictable peak, hour load) and Heavy Energy Users (who consume the highest volume) CPOs can effectively tailor demand response incentives as well as facility deployment to achieve the greatest return on investment. Together, the perfect integration of high precision load forecasting at both granular and aggregate levels with a detailed behavioral segmentation model provides a complete, data driven toolkit for dealing with the planning, operating, and managing challenges that the ever, changing EV charging ecosystem poses.
