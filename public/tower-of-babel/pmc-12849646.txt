Analysis of related factors of spontaneous premature birth in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus and construction of its nomogram risk prediction model
Authors: Qiuhong Huang, Fengdan Lai, Liubing Lan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the related factors of spontaneous premature birth (SPB) in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and to construct its nomogram risk prediction model.MethodsA retrospective collection of clinical data was conducted on 410 GDM patients admitted to our hospital from October 2020 to October 2023 as the training set. In addition, clinical data of 144 GDM patients admitted to our hospital from November 2023 to September 2024 retrospectively were collected as the validation cohort set for external validation. Both groups were separated into SPB group and non-SPB group based on whether SPB occurred.ResultsLogistic analysis of training set showed that age, pre-pregnancy BMI, history of spontaneous abortion, history of infection during pregnancy, family history of diabetes, hypertension, and premature rupture of membranes were the risk factors for SPB in GDM pregnant women (P < 0.05). The AUC of the ROC curve for the discrimination of the training set was 0.850, the optimism-corrected C-index was 0.753,and the H-L test showed χ2 = 6.987 (P = 0.699). DCA curve showed that when the threshold probability was between 0.13 and 0.99, the model had high clinical application value. The external validation results showed that the AUC of the ROC curve was 0.891, the optimism-corrected C-index was 0.771, and the H-L test showed χ2 = 7.016 (P = 0.699), and the threshold probability of the DCA curve results was between 0.11 and 0.87, indicating that the model had high clinical application value.ConclusionAge, pre-pregnancy BMI, history of spontaneous abortion, history of infection during pregnancy, family history of diabetes, hypertension, and premature rupture of membranes are the risk factors for SPB in GDM pregnant women. The nomogram prediction model constructed based on these factors has good calibration and discrimination.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Spontaneous premature birth, Influencing factors, Nomogram

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common metabolic complication during pregnancy, characterized by abnormal glucose tolerance and varying degrees of hyperglycemia that first appear during gestation. Its incidence has been increasing annually in recent years. If blood glucose is not controlled in a timely manner, it can increase the risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes for both the mother and offspring, and may also increase the occurrence of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth [1, 2]. Preterm birth is one of the leading causes of neonatal death and is also a common complication of GDM, with spontaneous preterm birth (SPB) accounting for about 70% of all preterm births. The risk of SPB in pregnant women with GDM is approximately twice that of healthy pregnant women, and SPB can adversely affect perinatal outcomes and seriously impact maternal and infant health [3, 4]. Currently, clinical management primarily focuses on diet, exercise, and medication to lower blood glucose levels in pregnant women with GDM, thereby reducing the incidence of complications. However, some patients still experience SPB, leading to underdeveloped organs in preterm infants and affecting neonatal growth [5, 6]. Therefore, early screening for high-risk populations for SPB among pregnant women with GDM, along with the implementation of preventive and therapeutic measures, is beneficial for controlling the risk factors for SPB and improving pregnancy outcomes. Currently, limited clinical methods are available to predict the occurrence of SPB in pregnant women with GDM, making early detection and risk assessment challenging. A nomogram model is a tool commonly used in clinical practice to evaluate prognosis. It can predict the risk value of a certain event by analyzing differential influencing factors, meeting the needs of clinical integrated models and promoting personalized medicine, and can assist clinicians in developing corresponding measures to reduce the risk of morbidity based on influencing factors [7, 8]. At present, there are few reports on the use of nomograms for SPB in pregnant women with GDM. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the related factors of SPB in pregnant women with GDM and to construct a nomogram risk prediction model.

Materials and methods

General information

A retrospective collection was made of 410 GDM patients admitted to our hospital from October 2020 to October 2023 as the training set. The sample size was calculated using PASS 15 software (two-sided test, α = 0.05, power = 70%, d = 0.35), yielding a required total of 394 cases; considering a 5% dropout rate, 410 patients were finally included.Additionally, 144 GDM patients admitted to our hospital from November 2023 to September 2024 were collected as the validation set for external validation. The sample size for this cohort was calculated using PASS 15 software (two-sided test, α = 0.05, power = 70%, d = 0.55), yielding a required total of 131 cases; considering a 10% dropout rate, 140 patients were finally included. Both sets were divided into the SPB group and the non-SPB group based on whether SPB occurred. The case collection flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Inclusion criteria: (1) met the diagnostic criteria for GDM [9], confirmed by an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), in which the normal values were fasting plasma glucose < 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour glucose < 10.0 mmol/L, and 2-hour glucose < 8.5 mmol/L; exceeding any of these thresholds was diagnostic; (2) met the criteria for SPB [10], with gestation from 28 to < 37 weeks, regular uterine contractions, cervical dilation, accompanied by cervical canal effacement ≥ 80%(for patients with delayed delivery, the tocolytic agent nifedipine may be administered); (3) age 22–38 years; (4) complete clinical data available. Exclusion criteria: (1) pre-gestational diabetes mellitus; (2) severe metabolic syndrome; (3) failure of important organs; (4) cervical incompetence; (5) history of cervical surgery; (6) infectious diseases; (7) immune dysfunction; (8) reproductive tract infection; (9) preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM). The ethics committee of our hospital approved this study.

Case flow collection diagram

Clinical data collection

The data of pregnant women were collected through our hospital’s inpatient electronic system, mainly including age, pre-pregnancy BMI(grouped according to the median for statistical convenience), education level, parity type, number of pregnancies, age at menarche, smoking history, place of residence, history of spontaneous abortion, menstrual cycle disorders, history of infection during pregnancy, history of cesarean section, family history of diabetes, hypertension(gestational hypertension occurring after 28 weeks of gestation), premature rupture of membranes(rupture of the fetal membranes before 37 weeks of gestation and prior to the onset of labor), fasting blood glucose in the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), OGTT 1-hour blood glucose, OGTT 2-hour blood glucose, and insulin use.

Statistical processing

The data were processed using SPSS 25.0 software. Measurement data were analyzed using the t-test and expressed as (), while count data were analyzed using the χ2 test and expressed as n (%). Logistic analysis was used to identify the influencing factors of SPB in pregnant women with GDM in the training set (forward stepwise method, inclusion criterion 0.05, exclusion criterion 0.1). R software (R3.6.3 software and the rms package) was used to construct the nomogram prediction model. The ROC and calibration curves were used to evaluate the discrimination and consistency of the nomogram prediction model; bootstrap was used for internal validation; DCA was used to evaluate the clinical application value of the model. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Construction of the nomogram model for SPB in pregnant women with GDM

The risk factors obtained from the Logistic analysis were entered into the R software to construct a nomogram model. P = eˣ / (1 + eˣ),where x = − 4.225 + 1.764 × age + 1.200 × pre-pregnancy BMI + 1.179 × history of spontaneous abortion + 1.278 × history of gestational infection + 1.386 × family history of diabetes + 1.115 × hypertension + 1.843 × premature rupture of membranes. The results showed that the most important influencing factor in this model was premature rupture of membranes, followed by family history of diabetes, history of infection during pregnancy, pre-pregnancy BMI, hypertension, history of spontaneous abortion, and age. For example, for a pregnant woman aged < 35 years (0 points), with a pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2 (66.5 points), a history of spontaneous abortion (57.5 points), a history of infection during pregnancy (69.5 points), a family history of diabetes (74.0 points), hypertension (61.5 points), and no premature rupture of membranes (0 points), the total score would be 328.5 points. By drawing a vertical line from this total score, the predicted probability is 85%. See Fig. 2.

Internal validation of the nomogram model for SPB in pregnant women with GDM

The ROC curve was used to verify the discrimination, with an AUC of 0.850 (95% CI = 0.792 ~ 0.909). The C-index was 0.828, and the average optimism obtained from 1,000 bootstrap resamples was 0.071; after optimism correction, the C-index was 0.753, indicating good concordance. The calibration curve showed a slope and intercept of 0.754 and 0.314, respectively.The H-L test was used to verify the goodness of fit, with χ2 = 7.016 (P = 0.699),suggesting that the predicted SPB risk in GDM pregnant women was highly consistent with the actual outcomes. Discussion

During pregnancy, women experience significant changes in their body’s metabolic systems (e.g., glucose and lipid metabolism) and in hormone levels (e.g., estrogen). This enhances the body’s insulin resistance and affects the blood glucose feedback regulation system, thereby increasing the risk of developing GDM. GDM mostly occurs in the middle and late stages of pregnancy. As the fetus grows, it can cause metabolic and endocrine abnormalities in the pregnant woman. With the increase in obesity and advanced maternal age, the incidence of GDM is gradually rising [11, 12]. SPB can lead to diseases such as sepsis in preterm infants. The shorter the gestational age at delivery for patients with GDM, the higher the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes and the greater the medical burden on the pregnant woman [13]. The results of this study found that among the 410 patients in the training set, 76 experienced SPB, with an incidence of 18.54%, which is similar to previous research [7]. This indicates a high incidence, and therefore, analyzing the risk factors for SPB in pregnant women with GDM can be beneficial for the clinical management of SPB.

This study, based on logistic regression analysis, identified age, pre-pregnancy BMI, history of spontaneous abortion, history of infection during pregnancy, family history of diabetes, hypertension, and premature rupture of membranes as risk factors for SPB in pregnant women with GDM. The reasons are analyzed as follows: (1) Studies have found that as maternal age increases, the quality and quantity of follicles decrease, which affects fertility and pregnancy outcomes. Genetically, the accumulation of age-related genetic damage can impair embryonic growth, thereby increasing the risk of preterm birth [14]. Furthermore, advanced maternal age can alter the metabolic and endocrine systems, affecting placental function and impairing the nutrient and oxygen supply to the placenta, thus increasing the risk of SPB [15]. Therefore, in clinical practice, older pregnant women require close attention and regular prenatal testing for timely problem detection and prevention. (2) A higher pre-pregnancy BMI enhances insulin resistance, leading to an imbalance in the body’s glucose metabolism, which affects placental development and fetal growth. The chronic low-grade inflammation associated with obesity can impair placentation. The mechanical load associated with a high BMI increases pressure on the uterus and pelvis affecting the remodeling of uterine spiral arteries and placental blood supply, thereby increasing the risk of SPB [16, 17]. Therefore, pregnant women with a high pre-pregnancy BMI require health management and a healthy lifestyle, strengthened prenatal care services, and early intervention to control BMI within a reasonable range. (3) Pregnant women with a history of spontaneous abortion may experience changes in the uterine endometrial environment that affect embryo implantation and placental development. A history of abortion is associated with vascular endothelial dysfunction, which can affect uterine blood flow. Moreover, GDM increases the risk of vascular complications, causing insufficient placental blood flow and thereby increasing the risk of SPB [18]. Therefore, it is necessary in clinical practice to strengthen the dissemination of relevant knowledge, promote a correct understanding of the impact of abortion on the uterus, and guide women to undergo systematic examinations before pregnancy to prevent SPB. (4) A history of infection during pregnancy indicates a disorder in the maternal-fetal immune microenvironment. Leukocytes may chemotax to placental tissues, and a pro-inflammatory response can occur at the maternal-fetal interface. Inflammatory factors can activate the myometrium, and the inflammatory response can accelerate the expulsion of the fetus from the mother’s body. Unstable blood glucose control in GDM can also cause placental hypoxia, promoting the production of pro-inflammatory factors, further disrupting the maternal immune system and increasing the risk of intrauterine infection, thus increasing the risk of SPB [19, 20]. Therefore, regular prenatal check-ups are needed in clinical practice to assess for infection and intervene in a timely manner. (5) Patients with a family history of diabetes have an increased risk of developing diabetes themselves. A hyperglycemic state in the patient can cause vaginal flora imbalance. Furthermore, when the elevated maternal glucose can cross the placenta, causing high osmotic diuresis, which may inhibit fetal growth and development; growth restriction thus increases the risk of SPB [21, 22]. In clinical practice, it is necessary to understand the pregnant woman’s family medical history as early as possible, control diet, and reduce adverse fetal effects. (6) Pregnant women with hypertension experience increased atherosclerosis and reduced blood perfusion to the body’s organs, affecting organ blood circulation. It also increases the risk of microarterial spasms, promoting separation of the uterine wall and placenta, causing placental abruption and leading to SPB [23]. It is recommended that medical staff popularize relevant knowledge, enhance preventive awareness, achieve early detection and treatment, and prevent the condition from worsening. (7) Premature rupture of membranes affects the integrity and function of the fetal membranes and may be associated with cervical incompetence. Loss of amniotic fluid also reduces the protective intrauterine environment, making fetal malposition and intrauterine infection more likely, thereby increasing the risk of SPB [24, 25]. Therefore, in clinical practice, pregnant women should be advised to have regular prenatal check-ups, maintain a healthy diet, and enhance prenatal care and monitoring to reduce the risk of premature rupture of membranes.

By constructing the nomogram model, the AUCs for the training set and external validation were 0.850 and 0.891, respectively. The Hosmer–Lemeshow (H–L) test showed good calibration. The DCA curve for the training set showed a threshold probability of 0.13–0.99, and for the external validation, it was 0.11–0.87. Within these threshold ranges, the nomogram model demonstrated high accuracy and a favorable net benefit. Therefore, for SPB in pregnant women with GDM, the above factors should be closely monitored, and individualized surgical plans should be actively developed for patients with a higher predicted risk of SPB to further reduce its occurrence.This indicates that the nomogram model has high clinical application value and can assist medical staff in predicting the risk of SPB and implementing timely prevention, thereby improving pregnancy outcomes. Compared with previous predictive tools, this model is simple to use and can assist clinicians in early assessment. Furthermore, external validation of the nomogram model in this study demonstrated its high practical applicability.

In summary, age, pre-pregnancy BMI, history of spontaneous abortion, history of infection during pregnancy, family history of diabetes, hypertension, and premature rupture of membranes are risk factors for SPB in pregnant women with GDM. The nomogram prediction model constructed based on these factors has good calibration and discrimination. However, this study was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, and all participants were recruited from the same hospital, which may not capture variables from different regions (such as a history of SPB), potentially limiting the generalizability of the results. In addition, the external validation cohort included a relatively small number of cases. In future studies, multicenter large-sample cases from different regions will be collected to identify more potential factors influencing SPB and to further validate the clinical applicability of the nomogram model.
