Tropical cyclone activity predicts autumn vagrancy of Nearctic birds on a remote North Atlantic island
Authors: David Monticelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWind drift has long been invoked as a primary driver of the seasonal appearance in Europe of long-distance migrants from North America (‘Nearctic vagrants’), presumed to be displaced beyond their normal range by westerly crosswinds and tropical storms encountered during migration. Yet few studies have tested how tropical cyclone (TC) activity shapes long-term patterns in offshore vagrancy. I assembled a 20-year autumn time series (2005–2024) from the small oceanic island of Corvo (Azores, Portugal) and used generalized linear models (GLMs) to relate fluctuations in Nearctic vagrancy to meteorological indices of TC activity. Across ~900 days of sampling, 126 TC events coincided with field effort, during which 1,660 individuals representing 111 Nearctic taxa were recorded. Both TC frequency and vagrant counts increased over the study period, and autumns with more sustained, basin-wide North Atlantic cyclonic activity tended to yield more species and individuals on Corvo. To assess short-term timing, I fitted GLMs to a focused subset of October landbirds from 2017 onward (8 years), comparing candidate temporal lags; the best-supported model incorporated a 5-day lagged response to TC activity. Overall, these results indicate that vagrancy on this remote island is, at least in part, cyclone-driven. Given projections of greater variability and more active TC seasons under climate change, Nearctic vagrancy in the North Atlantic may correspondingly increase in the years ahead.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40850-025-00252-0.Keywords: Accumulated Cyclone Energy index, Community science, Neotropical migrants, North Atlantic hurricane season, North Atlantic Oscillation index, Transatlantic vagrancy

Background

Spatial shifts in the distributional ranges of many migratory bird species are increasingly linked to anthropogenic drivers such as climate change and land use change at breeding and wintering areas, along migration routes, and at stopover sites [1]. The use of new migratory pathways and colonization of novel areas, documented in a number of species, is thought to have been initiated by a fraction of individuals—also referred to as vagrants—that have strayed over unusually long distances during migration (e.g., [2–5]). Both endogenous and exogenous factors like weather conditions (strong winds, storms), navigational errors, and inexperience in young migrants, have been invoked to explain the accidental appearance of some individuals outside a species’ normal distribution range [6]. Vagrancy can positively contribute to population dynamics, for instance by facilitating range expansion, as recently demonstrated in growing populations of some North American bird species [7–9]. As a result of accelerating climate change and shifts in distribution ranges, vagrancy may also be increasing [10].

Weather, including extreme events, is an important proximate driver of vagrancy [11]. In particular, offshore vagrants from North America cross the North Atlantic to reach Europe annually, presumably aided by fast-moving low-pressure systems (westerlies) and tropical storms [12]. Transatlantic vagrancy has been documented in multiple groups, including ducks [13], waders [14, 15], gulls [16], terns [5], and near-passerines and passerines [17–21]. Although single New World vagrants to Europe are unlikely to establish populations, several non-landbird taxa show nascent colonization, including breeding Elegant Terns (Thalasseus elegans; [5, 22]), Spotted Sandpipers (Actitis macularius; [23]) and Killdeer (Charadrius vociferus; [24]). Among ducks, hybridization between Eurasian species and their North American counterparts has also been reported in Europe, involving American Black Duck (Anas rubripes; [25, 26]), American Green-winged Teal (A. crecca carolinensis) and American Wigeon (Mareca americana; [13, 27, 28]). While no Nearctic landbird has yet been recorded breeding in Europe, New World landbird species frequently recorded as vagrants could potentially establish themselves on the Eurasian continent [29].

A peak in Nearctic vagrants in Europe from August – November coincides with the migration period of many North American species along the eastern U.S. seaboard [30] and across a broad range of latitudes where westerlies usually prevail in autumn [31]. As a result, Nearctic vagrants reaching Europe originate from multiple regions in North America [32], though the numbers recorded in Europe vary considerably from year to year [11, 12, 20]. Such interannual fluctuations have been studied in relation to basin-wide climate phenomena such as the North Atlantic Oscillation [13, 14, 20], while comparatively few studies have tested the effect of seasonal tropical cyclone (TC) activity [33, 34]. Nonetheless, observers have noted large influxes of American vagrant species in the Azores and other North Atlantic oceanic islands [35], often describing arrivals over the next few days after storm-related conditions. Using synoptic pressure charts, authors have also related above-normal ‘landfalls’ of Nearctic vagrants in Europe to periods of intense TC activity in the North Atlantic (e.g., [18, 20, 36]). Radar studies have recently highlighted that tropical storms, particularly in September-October, are important carriers of migratory birds across the North Atlantic [33], and the Accumulated Cyclone Energy (ACE) index proposed by NOAA [37]—which captures the strength and duration of all tropical storms in a season—has been linked to variations in breeding and migration strategies of Nearctic species (e.g., [38]). To my knowledge, however, long-term trends and changes in Nearctic vagrants displaced across the North Atlantic have not been evaluated in relation to TC activity, likely due to methodological difficulties in assembling time series of vagrant records, which are typically sparse in space and time. Addressing this gap is nevertheless relevant as the North Atlantic basin has already experienced an increase in TC activity over the past 50 years, with a further rise in the frequency and intensification rate of tropical storms projected [39, 40].

Here, I leverage a 20-year dataset of Nearctic vagrant records on the small oceanic island of Corvo (Azores, Portugal), compiled from citizen-science observations and encompassing over 100 taxa of landbirds and waterbirds, to test whether ACE and other parameters (i.e., TC frequency and duration) used to characterize seasonal TC activity [37] predict interannual variation in transatlantic vagrancy. Because landfalls of Nearctic species on Corvo and in the British Isles have been documented in the absence of tropical storms, during periods of sustained, albeit lighter, westerlies (e.g., [18, 20]), I additionally examine whether the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) explains part of the interannual variation in transatlantic vagrancy. The NAO is defined by the surface sea-level pressure difference between the subtropical high (the ‘Azores High’) and the polar low (the ‘Icelandic Low’); positive NAO phases strengthen westerlies, whereas negative phases weaken them [41, 42].

While many vagrant landbirds and waterbirds can sustain long overwater flights, especially when compelled by storm winds, landbirds are a particularly valuable group for testing hypotheses about transoceanic vagrancy. Unlike waterbirds, they cannot rest on the ocean surface and must therefore maintain continuous flight until making landfall. This physiological constraint makes them well suited for studying short-term links between large-scale weather disturbances—such as tropical storms—and subsequent vagrancy, because the connection between storm events and arrivals may be more direct or predictable [43]. Geolocator and tracking-radar studies suggest that landbirds may fly at groundspeeds of ~30–50 km/h with tailwinds [44, 45]. Corvo Island lies about 1900 km from the nearest point in Newfoundland (Cape Spear/Cape Race), roughly 2700–2900 km from Nova Scotia, around 3300 km from Cape Cod, and about 3900 km from Cape Hatteras. Long-distance migrants blown off course from these western Atlantic coasts therefore have to cross on the order of 2000–4000 km of open ocean to reach the Azores, a journey that some passerine species appear able to complete in roughly 2–3 days under favourable winds (e.g., [46]). Thus, my second aim in the present study was to test for a temporal lag between cyclonic activity and vagrant arrivals on Corvo Island.

Overall, elucidating the mechanisms that bring vagrants to North Atlantic oceanic islands can inform our broader understanding of vagrancy, including avian responses to changing global weather systems and extreme events [47].

Materials and methods

Study area

Fieldwork was conducted from 2005 onwards on Corvo Island (39°42’N, 31°06’W; Fig. 1), a small (17 km2) UNESCO Biosphere Reserve in the Azores archipelago (36°–39°N, 25°–31° W), whose climate and natural habitat structure are characteristic of oceanic subtropical environments [48]. Owing to its relatively close proximity to North America, Corvo has long been recognized for hosting unusually high numbers of Nearctic vagrants, particularly during September – October [17, 18, 49], which fall within the core period of the North Atlantic hurricane season.

Vagrant dataset preparation

Citizen-science volunteers (hereafter ‘observers’) regularly visit Corvo Island to search for vagrant birds and report their records online, including species and/or subspecies, precise locations and supporting photographs. Most records are collated and reviewed by the Portuguese Rarities Committee, which publishes annual reports freely available online (https://spea.pt/publicacoes/publicacao/anuario-ornitologico/). Drawing on these sources and my own observations from multiple visits, I compiled seasonal histories of vagrant records for each year from late August through November over 2005–2024. Individuals seen on multiple occasions (e.g., over several consecutive days) were counted only once (i.e., on the date of initial discovery). In some years, certain Nearctic species occurred in larger numbers (e.g., White-rumped Sandpipers Calidris fuscicollis, Ring-necked Ducks Aythya collaris, Red-eyed Vireos Vireo olivaceus, Indigo Buntings Passerina cyanea), hence to minimize double counting, these observations were critically reviewed and tallied conservatively. In total, the 2005–2024 dataset comprised 1,735 individuals within 118 Nearctic taxa (108 species and 10 subspecies; Table S1). Seven taxa were omitted from further analyses: one because breeding has been confirmed in the Azores during at least one study year (American Black Duck Anas rubripes; [25]) and six because some records could not be unequivocally assigned to a Nearctic origin, due to limited documentation and/or identification challenges vis-à-vis closely similar Palearctic taxa.

Sampling effort

Owing to the limited geographic area and the focused, high-quality observer effort it receives in autumn, the likelihood of detecting offshore vagrants making landfall on Corvo Island is relatively high. For example, following Hurricane Wilma in October 2005, a single experienced observer documented over 70 Nearctic individuals representing 26 species [50], suggesting that even minimal observer presence can yield comprehensive vagrant detection. Individual observer visit dates were not available; however, observer activity typically peaks in October, with lower numbers in September and November. I therefore used the number of days with observer presence on the island—measured as the span from the first to the last vagrant report in a given autumn (Fig. 2)—as a proxy for sampling effort. This metric was assumed to approximate the temporal window during which the detection of vagrants was possible, consistent with accounting for variation in the observation process [51]. However, it assumes that variation in observer’s presence does not systematically bias detection and does not capture potential day-to-day variation in effort, or days when observers were present but conditions were poor (poor weather and visibility). Noteworthy, many vagrants remain for multiple days when making landfall on Corvo Island, so individuals missed on a poorly covered day may still be detected subsequently. Because observer presence varied annually, the dates of the first and last recorded vagrant sightings differed among years (Table S2). In all 20 study years, both the first and last vagrant reports were Nearctic species, except of 2008 and 2015, when the first autumn reports were a Paddyfield Warbler (Acrocephalus paludicola) of Palearctic origin and a Cattle Egret (Bubulcus ibis) of unknown origin (this latter species occurs on both sides of the Atlantic; Table S2).

Weather covariates

Tropical cyclone (TC) activity in the North Atlantic basin is forecast and monitored by NOAA with post-season analyses and verification reports available online. The Atlantic hurricane season (including the Caribbean Sea) runs from 1 June to 30 November, and is fully covered therein, with data on the frequency, track, lifetime, and strength of all TC events, from subtropical storms to hurricanes (Fig. S1). Parameters such as frequency (no. of TC events), lifetime (duration of TC events) and Accumulated Cyclone Energy (ACE), which incorporates both duration and strength by summing 6-hourly wind energy across all TC systems [37], have been used to document strong interannual variability in North Atlantic seasonal TC activity [52]. Accordingly, data series of cyclone activity matching the reporting periods were extracted from NOAA (https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/index.php), including the number of TC events and the number of TC days (Fig. 2, Table S3). Notably, during the study, some major landfalls on Corvo Island (cf. [18]) were associated with tropical storms, including hurricanes, that developed over a broad range of latitudes; therefore all named storms (TC events) reported by NOAA for a given season in the North Atlantic basin were considered in the analyses. Annual ACE index values were retrieved from Colorado State University (https://tropical.atmos.colostate.edu/Realtime/index.php?arch%26loc=northatlantic; Table S3). To test for the influence of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), monthly NAO index values for each autumn (September – November) during the 2005–2024 study period were obtained from NOAA (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/monitoring/nao/).

Statistical analyses

Modeling interannual variation in autumn records

I used generalized linear models (GLMs) to examine, over a 20-year period (2005–2024), the effects of potential predictors (see below) on two response variables: (i) the accumulated number of Nearctic taxa each autumn (‘vagrant richness’), and (ii) the accumulated number of Nearctic individuals each autumn (‘vagrant abundance’). Tests for normality and Poisson distributions (Tables S4 and S5) suggested that vagrant abundance was approximately log-normally distributed, as can occur with aggregated count data [53]. I therefore developed candidate models in a GLM framework with normal errors and with a log-normal–type distribution approximated via a Gamma distribution with a log link [54]. Predictors included (i) the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) index for October only (October NAOI) and (ii) the NAO index averaged across the reporting period (September – November NAOI). To characterize TC activity, I used 3 predictors (i) number of TC events per reporting period (TC frequency), (ii) number of TC days per reporting period, and (iii) annual ACE index values. To evaluate collinearity among these five environmental predictors, I first examined Pearson correlation coefficients, and subsequently developed the candidate model set using combinations of predictors that were not significantly correlated. Additional covariates fitted to the data included Year (to test for a temporal trend) and sampling effort [55]. Sampling effort in GLMs can be incorporated as an offset or as a covariate [56]; I opted, however, to model effort as a covariate to permit direct estimation of its effect. I also log-transformed sampling effort to relax the assumption of a strictly linear or proportional relationship with the response variables.

Modeling interannual variation in October landbird records

To facilitate standardized comparison of vagrant records, the original 20-year dataset was truncated to a 8-year (2017–2024) subset encompassing only landbirds recorded during the same fixed 4 weeks (a 28-day period) in October, thereby minimizing temporal variability in observer effort and improving comparability. To assess whether tropical cyclone (TC) activity influences vagrant bird occurrences in subsequent days, and to identify the most likely time lag, I calculated the number of TC days for each 28-day period using temporal lags from 0 to 10 days. I then performed a cross-correlation analysis to examine lagged relationships between tropical cyclone (TC) activity (number of days) and Nearctic landbird abundance, using Pearson correlation coefficients. Based on this scoping analysis, a 5-day lag was the most promising candidate (Pearson’s r ≥ 0.7; Fig. 3) but lags of 3 and 10 days were also retained for subsequent modeling. Accordingly, a second set of GLMs was developed using October vagrant abundance as the response and the following predictors: (i) number of TC days per 28-day period (date-matched and lagged values), (ii) the October NAO index (October NAOI), and (iii) Year (temporal trend).

Model selection and parameter estimates

Akaike’s Information Criterion corrected for small sample size (AICc) was used to compare competing models, and select the models that best fitted the data with the fewest number of parameters (i.e., the most parsimonious models; [57]). The model with the lowest AICc value corresponded to the best model, and models differing from the best AICc-ranked models by more than 2 units (∆AICc > 2) were regarded as having little support. I also report AICc weights (wi) as they provide a relative measure of how well a model supports the data compared to other models [57]. Parameter estimates from AICc-ranked models were interpreted using Wald χ2 tests and their 95 and 85% confidence intervals (CIs), noting that AIC-based selection is functionally equivalent to adopting a more liberal significance threshold of p < 0.157 [58]. To assess goodness-of-fit, I compared models including covariates against a null (intercept-only) model [16]. Data processing and analyses were conducted in SPSS 30 and R [59].

Results

Dataset, effort, and summary statistics

The dataset comprised 1,660 Nearctic vagrant individuals within 111 taxa (Table S6), including 38% of waterbirds (42 out of 111 taxa) and 62% of landbirds (Table S1), across 906 effort-days (autumns 2005–2024; Fig. 2). Interannual variability was highest for the accumulated number of Nearctic individuals (CV = 54%) and lowest for sampling effort (CV = 28%; Table S6). About 126 TC events overlapped the 906 sampling days, spanning 504 days in total (Table S6). TC frequency varied widely among years (CV = 56%; Table S6) and increased over time (Table S7). The two response variables—vagrant richness and vagrant abundance—were strongly positively correlated (r = 0.877, p < 0.001) and both showed positive temporal trends (Table S7). No temporal trend was apparent in log-transformed sampling effort or in the other environmental predictors (Table S7).

Interannual variation in autumn records

The collinearity analysis among five environmental variables using Pearson correlation coefficients (Table S8) identified two correlated pairs: the two NAO index series (r = 0.87) and the TC frequency/TC days series (r = 0.84). Accordingly, the set of candidate models for each response variables was developed using combinations of uncorrelated predictors. Models testing NAO and ACE effects were poorly supported (i.e., lowest AICc values for models 6–10 and 16–17; Table 1). Models including TC frequency were also less favored by AICc ranking than models including TC days as a covariate (Table 1). For both response variables, the best-supported models consistently included TC days (models 1–2 and 11–12; Table 1). Models that additionally included sampling effort (models 3 and 13; Table 1) received weak support (Δi > 2units from the top models), while adding a temporal trend (Year) improved the maximized log-likelihood and yielded the lowest AICc (models 1 and 11; Table 1). For each response variable, models with Δi < 2 AICc units had combined wi of 0.77–0.78 (Table 1). Comparisons of models with covariates (full models) versus intercept-only models (wi = 0.78–0.91; Table 2) indicated that parameterized models were well supported by the data. Predictors in models within 2 AICc units of the top model had 95% CIs that did not overlap zero (all p < 0.05), except model 11 (p = 0.062; Table 2); however, for the latter, 85% CIs did not overlap zero (0.003–0.023; Table 2), suggesting some support.

Interannual variation in October landbird records

The landbird subset comprised 452 Nearctic individuals, representing 27% of the full dataset. The number of TC days recorded during eight 28-day October sampling periods (2017–2024) varied markedly among years, ranging from a low of 5 in October 2021 to a high of 23 in October 2023 (Fig. 2). Models testing for NAO effects and a temporal trend (Year) were not supported by the data (i.e., lowest ranked models were 9 and 10; Table 3). Models using the number of TC days as a predictor ranked higher overall, although models using date-matched or 10-day lagged values received no support (Tables 3 and 4). The best-ranked models were those including 3-day and 5-day lags (models 1–5; combined Akaike weight wi = 0.84; Table 3). Parameter estimates for TC days in the top-ranked model (5-day lag) and in models within 2 AICc units of it had 95% CIs that did not overlap zero (models 1–5; Table 4).

Discussion

I synthesised two decades of community-science records from Corvo Island (Azores, Portugal) to examine interannual variation in Nearctic vagrancy in relation to meteorological and oceanographic conditions. Across >1,650 individuals spanning waterbird and landbird taxa, models indicate that both vagrant richness (species count) and abundance (individuals) increase in autumns with more sustained tropical cyclone (TC) activity in the North Atlantic. Re-analysing a subset of landbirds detected during the most intensively surveyed October period yielded the same qualitative result: TC activity (in no. of TC days) remained the dominant forcing, with the strongest association at a 5-day lag between this environmental driver and the response. Together, these convergent patterns are consistent with an association between North Atlantic TC activity and transatlantic vagrancy.

Altogether, 126 tropical storm events did overlap with about 900 days of sampling effort spanned by the present dataset, with an average of 6 TC events (range 1–14 named storms) recorded during each sampling autumnal season, with one or more hurricanes on records in all years but 2013. Indeed, arrival waves (or landfalls) of transatlantic vagrants to Europe, including the Azores, Iceland and the British Isles have been documented on multiple occasions following days of fierce and fast-moving weather systems in the North Atlantic (e.g., [12, 18, 20, 36, 60]); some of the most striking examples being those of hurricanes Wilma in 2005 [61], Ophelia in 2017 [18], and Lee in 2023 [60]. The exact mechanisms by which vagrants are deflected offshore from their southward migratory path along the eastern U.S. seaboard are complex, but typically, a peak of cyclonic activity is recorded in the North Atlantic in August – October, coinciding with the migration season of many trans-oceanic Nearctic migratory species [11, 12]. During these months, tropical weather systems moving west off the coast of Africa between 10° – 20°N and 60° – 20°W [39] and gaining in strength while approaching North America, either make landfall in the southeast U.S. and Caribbean and/or move polewards from the Caribbean Sea. Recent polarimetric radar observations revealed that when such fast-moving low-pressure systems go on to develop into storms and hurricanes around Florida and Puerto Rico, hurricane-driven winds and thunderstorms become important carriers of birds over long distances, from seabirds to landbirds, with more intense tropical cyclones transporting more birds [33]. In addition, many tropical cyclones that move north along the eastern U.S. seaboard, recurve (i.e. turn northeast), get swept into the mid-latitude westerlies, and often complete extratropical transition while crossing the Atlantic toward Europe [39, 62], thereby facilitating accidental transatlantic crossings [11]. Waders, which frequently interact with tropical storms [34], were the most numerous non-landbird group recorded during the study. In particular, White-rumped Sandpiper (Calidris fuscicollis) (181 ind. in total, including a peak flock of 55 in 2012) and Pectoral Sandpiper (C. melanotos) (51 ind. in total) were the most prominent species, both being Arctic breeders whose migration route runs offshore along the Atlantic coast from eastern Canada to South America [15]. Similarly, many common landbird vagrants, including Red-eyed Vireos Vireo olivaceus (238 ind. in total, including a peak of 29 in 2023), migrate over the Atlantic and the Gulf of Mexico toward the Caribbean and South America, leaving them susceptible to offshore drift and to being caught up in hurricanes and the westerlies associated with these events, ultimately facilitating transatlantic vagrancy to Europe [63].

Given that the shortest transatlantic distance to Corvo is from Atlantic Canada—Nova Scotia and Newfoundland are only c. 2,000 km away—one might expect most vagrants on Corvo to originate largely from these northern latitudes. However, recent deuterium-isotope work shows that some vagrants on Corvo originated much farther south, including southeastern U.S. [32]. This pattern has been linked to reversed movements along the Atlantic seaboard [12], supporting the idea that long-distance migrants can be displaced offshore across a wide latitudinal band, especially by tropical storms and hurricanes that span vast areas. Presumably, longer-lived storms traverse greater distances and expand over larger areas, thereby increasing the likelihood that Nearctic vagrants are displaced thousands of kilometers across the northeast Atlantic and boosting overall numbers of displaced individuals; for example, major Hurricane Wilma (15–25 October 2005) persisted for >10 days while tracking from 15°N to 40°N, precipitating unprecedented Nearctic vagrancy first in the Caribbean [35] and later in the Azores [50]. Notably, major landfalls on Corvo Island have also followed storms that never interacted with the U.S. coast (e.g., Ophelia in 2017; [18]). These dynamics are complex and likely reflect sequences of weather conditions operating over large spatial scales, justifying the use of basin-wide indices (NAO, ACE) of North Atlantic TC activity in the present analyses. Although the ACE index, which measures the total wind energy of all TCs in a season, has been a useful surrogate of TC activity for long-distance migrants such as the Veery (Catharus fuscescens) [38], ACE was not a significant predictor here. A likely reason is a timing mismatch: vagrant detections on Corvo usually peak during the first half of October, whereas the Atlantic hurricane season typically peaks in mid-September, so annual ACE values include many TC events occurring outside the sampling window studied here. Of the three TC metrics evaluated, the number of TC days was the best predictor of vagrancy levels, thereby reflecting that sustained storminess within the sampling period appears to increase vagrant numbers. For example, 2010 had many short-lived storms (14 TCs, 4–5 days each) yet produced far fewer vagrants than 2017 and 2023, when fewer but longer-lived storms (5–9 TCs, 10–15 days each) led to outstanding years on Corvo and elsewhere in Europe (e.g., [60]).

No effect of large-scale climate indices, as expressed by the NAO, was evident in the present analyses. The two NAO series (‘October only’ vs. ‘September–November’) were strongly correlated, a pattern presumably explained by the fact that both positive and negative NAO phases often persist for several months. Prior work has examined seasonal and interannual NAO variability in relation to vagrant abundance [64] and references therein). Protracted positive NAO phases strengthen prevailing westerlies over the North Atlantic [31], which has been linked to increased transatlantic vagrancy in North American waders [14] and landbirds [20]. Conversely, strong negative NAO events are frequently associated with a higher probability of tropical storms in the southeastern U.S. [38]. Taken together, these patterns suggest that NAO effects are not unidirectional—strong events of either phase could promote vagrancy—which may help explain the lack of a significant linear NAO effect in this study. Similarly, previous analyses of North American vagrant ducks reaching Europe also found no significant NAO–vagrancy relationship [13].

A notable feature of these analyses is the large volume of vagrant records, especially landbirds, and the tendency for numbers to rise through time. Barcelos et al. [65] suggested that the upward trend in Nearctic species recorded in the Azores reflects increased observer presence in recent years. Indeed, Corvo Island has become a popular autumn destination for experienced observers over the last decade, and intensified seasonal coverage could have plausibly contributed to the temporal increase in reports. In addition, compared with the British Isles, Iceland, or continental Europe, Corvo lies closer to the North American Atlantic seaboard, so displaced migrants have a shorter distance to cover to make landfall, which likely increases vagrant survival rates (see also [49]) and subsequent detections. Event-based studies linking tropical storm tracks with migrant flows along the U.S. coast consistently document pulses of Nearctic vagrants making landfall within several days of a cyclone’s transit (e.g., [20, 50, 60]). For example, Hurricane Lee (5–16 September 2023) produced mass arrivals concentrated on 20–24 September, thereby implying a multi-day lag between atmospheric forcing and vagrant detections on North Atlantic islands and in the British Isles [60]. This is in line with the present study’s finding that failure to account for a temporal lag resulted in no significant effect of TC activity (Table 4), whereas incorporating a temporal lag markedly improved fit. Given geolocator evidence that small landbirds such as Blackpoll (Setophaga striata) and Connecticut (Oporornis agilis) Warbler fly non-stop for 2–3 days over 2,270–2,770 km [44, 46, 66], a 2–3-day crossing to Corvo (2,000–3,000 km from the U.S. East Coast) is physically feasible with tailwinds. My best-supported model had, however, a 5-day lag, which may perhaps be explained by a combination of: (i) detection delays relative to actual arrival (e.g., reduced observer activity and/or visibility during poor weather), (ii) storm evolution, whereby 1–2 days elapse after the TC ends before the extratropical system reaches latitudes intersecting major migrant streams, then followed by 2–3 days of flight to Corvo, and (iii) broad capture zones, with migrants captured across wide swaths and released along the TC track, producing serial landfalls over 2–5 days as the TC event progresses.

Nearctic vagrants, particularly landbirds, have been long known to complete transatlantic crossing aided by ships [36, 67], primarily in autumn, although some reports of American warblers on ship exists in spring as well (e.g., [68]). If ship-assistance was the primary source of vagrants to Corvo Island, I would, however, probably not expect the relationship between TC activity and level of vagrancy obtained here.

Conclusion

Overall these results add to the narrative that TC activity in the North Atlantic coinciding with the autumn migration of New World species is an important driver of transatlantic vagrancy. Future research should assess whether fluctuations in breeding population abundance in northern U.S. regions influence the occurrence of Nearctic vagrants on Corvo Island, and model their stopover duration to better understand the ecological impacts of vagrancy, including potential competition for food resources with native species and the possible introduction of parasites or pathogens.
