Risk of periprosthetic joint infection within 1 year following robotic-assisted versus conventional primary total knee arthroplasty: a propensity-score-matched cohort study
Authors: Shuaifei Tian, Yicheng Li, Wenbo Mu, Baochao Ji, Xiaogang Zhang, Li Cao, Jin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty (RA-TKA) is increasingly being adopted for its ability to enhance bone-resection accuracy and component alignment. However, whether these technical gains influence the risk of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) remains uncertain, especially in the context of prolonged operative duration. This study aimed to compare the 1-year rate of PJI following conventional total knee arthroplasty (cTKA) and RA-TKA in a propensity-score-matched cohort.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 1284 consecutive patients who underwent primary TKAs at a single centre between 2021 and 2023. The patients were stratified according to surgical technique (cTKA versus RA-TKA) and subsequently matched 1:1 using propensity score analysis (age, sex, body mass index [BMI], American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] score, Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI] score, CCI components and smoking), resulting in 522 pairs (1044 patients) for the final comparative analysis. Operative time and 1-year PJI were assessed using multivariable logistic regression. Infections were stratified according to timing: ≤ 90 days and from 90 days to 1 year after surgery.ResultsThe 1-year rate of PJI was 0.77% (4/522) after RA-TKA and 0.96% (5/522) after cTKA (P = 1.000). All PJIs in patients who underwent RA-TKA occurred within 90 days, whereas PJIs in patients who underwent cTKA occurred in both time windows. Multivariable logistic regression analysis did not identify surgical modality as an independent predictor of PJI (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 0.75, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.22–2.90; P = 0.57). The median operative time was longer in the RA-TKA group than in the cTKA group (115 (range, 90–145) versus 85 (range, 60–105) min; P < 0.001).ConclusionsRA-TKA was associated with a longer operative time, while no statistically significant difference in 1-year PJI rates was detected compared with cTKA. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of infection events.Level of Evidence: Level 3, non-randomised observational study.Keywords: Robotic-assisted, Total knee arthroplasty, Periprosthetic joint infection, Propensity score matching, Operative time

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is widely regarded as the definitive treatment for end-stage knee osteoarthritis [1–4]. Robotic-assisted TKA (RA-TKA) has rapidly gained popularity because it allows sub-millimetre bone resections and supports a functional-alignment philosophy. However, whether these technical refinements translate into measurable clinical benefit remains controversial [5–14].

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a rare but catastrophic complication after TKA [15–17]. Proponents argue that the greater precision and potentially smaller soft-tissue envelope of RA-TKA could mitigate the risk of PJI. Conversely, RA-TKA entails longer operative times, additional instrumentation and pin-site portals—factors that may increase infection risk [18–25]. However, the net effect of RA-TKA on PJI remains unclear.

While meta-analyses have included studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up, most individual investigations remain limited by the use of PJI as a secondary endpoint and inadequate adjustment for confounding variables [26–28]. Although one prior study used PJI as a primary endpoint, its observation period was limited to 90 days postoperatively [29]. Given that a significant proportion of PJIs present beyond this early window, longer follow-up is necessary to provide robust evidence. Therefore, this propensity-score-matched cohort study aims to compare the 1-year rate of PJI after primary RA-TKA with that of conventional TKA (cTKA).

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This single-centre retrospective cohort analysis was approved by the appropriate institutional review board (IRB) (approval number: K202201-02). The study included patients who underwent primary TKA for primary osteoarthritis between 1 June 2021 and 31 December 2023 at a single facility; owing to the retrospective nature of the investigation, the sample size was governed by the consecutive enrolment of all eligible patients during the study interval rather than by a prospective power calculation. The exclusion criteria comprised the following: (1) individuals aged < 18 years or > 90 years; (2) preoperative diagnoses of post-traumatic arthritis or inflammatory arthritis (including rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, etc.); (3) previous history of osteomyelitis, septic arthritis or internal fixation at the surgical site; (4) concomitant bilateral TKA; and (5) instances of incomplete clinical documentation or follow-up durations < 1 year. Overall, 1284 patients were included in the analysis and were stratified into a traditional manual TKA cohort (n = 756) and a robotic-assisted TKA cohort (n = 528) on the basis of the surgical approach. Data collection and surgical techniques

This study incorporated patients who met stringent inclusion criteria encompassing age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores, alongside various demographic and clinical parameters, including surgical duration. The assignment to either surgical technique was based solely on patient choice and was not influenced by surgeon preference, conventional practice or case-specific technical considerations. All surgical procedures were performed under uniform conditions: a tourniquet was used to mitigate intraoperative haemorrhage, with a standard anteromedial incision made to facilitate the implantation of a cemented prosthesis via a medial parapatellar approach; all components were fixed using antibiotic-loaded acrylic bone cement (1 × 40 g; Biomet France, Valence, France; batch no. AU23DF2703). The RA-TKA group received the Triathlon Total Knee System (Stryker, Mahwah, NJ, USA), whereas the cTKA group received the b-ONE Total Knee System (b-ONE Ortho Corp, Suzhou, China).

For the RA-TKA group, procedures were performed using the Stryker Mako system (software version 1.0). In these cases, the femoral tracking pins were inserted through the main surgical incision and therefore did not require additional skin incisions. On the tibial side, two separate small skin incisions were made approximately three fingerbreadths distal to the tibial tubercle for placement of the tracking pins. At the conclusion of the procedure, all tracking pins were removed; the tibial pin-site incisions were closed with sutures and covered with sterile gauze dressings. Postoperatively, these pin sites were managed according to the routine wound-care protocol and were assessed during follow-up for wound-healing or pin-site-related complications.The cTKA group employed standard extramedullary guides for proximal tibial osteotomy and standard intramedullary guides for distal femoral osteotomy. All TKA procedures were performed by two senior surgeons (L.C. and X.G.Z.) in our institution. Each surgeon had participated in over 30 robotic-assisted procedures during their learning curves, and these cases were excluded from the study.

Perioperative management adhered to a standardised protocol: all patients routinely underwent preoperative blood glucose assessments and evaluations for active infections at other anatomical sites (e.g. urinary tract infections). Furthermore, patients received prophylactic antibiotic treatment, with antibiotics (cefuroxime 1.5 g) administered 30 min prior to the procedure. A unified cocktail injection formulation (betamethasone 3 mL, ropivacaine hydrochloride 10 mL, morphine 1 mL, flurbiprofen 5 mL and 0.9% saline 50 mL) was employed for peri-articular analgesia. Tranexamic acid was administered via both intravenous injection and local infusion, and no drains were left in situ postoperatively. All patients participated in a standardised postoperative rehabilitation program, encompassing early mobilisation, active and passive ranges of motion (ROM) exercises and immediate weight-bearing as tolerated.

Outcome definition and assessment

All patients were followed up at intervals of 1, 3, 6 and 12 months, followed by subsequent annual assessments. The primary outcome measure was the rate of PJI occurring within 1 year following surgery. All suspected PJI events were assessed in a standardised manner by an orthopaedic surgeon who was not involved in the index procedure, was blinded to group assignment and had substantial experience in the diagnosis of PJI. PJI was defined in accordance with the 2016 modified Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria [30]. PJI was confirmed when at least one of the following three criteria was present: (1) two positive periprosthetic (tissue or fluid) cultures with matching organisms; (2) presence of a sinus tract communicating with the implant; and (3) three of the following five minor criteria: (a) clinical signs of infection, such as calor, rubor or wound drainage with an elevated serum erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR; not applicable to acute PJI; > 30 mm/h in chronic PJI) and serum C-reactive protein (CRP) level (> 100 mg/L in acute PJI; > 10 mg/L in chronic PJI); (b) increased synovial fluid white blood cell count (> 10,000 cells/μL in acute PJI; > 3000 cells/μL in chronic PJI) or “+ +” (or greater) change on leukocyte esterase test strip of synovial fluid; (c) increased synovial fluid polymorphonuclear neutrophil percentage (PMN%; > 90% in acute PJI; > 80% in chronic PJI); (d) positive inflammation detected during histopathological examination of periprosthetic tissue (> 5 neutrophils per high power field [HPF]); and (e) a single positive periprosthetic (tissue or fluid) culture.

Propensity score matching

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed between the two cohorts using 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching with a greedy algorithm. Propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression based on age, sex, BMI, smoking history, ASA classification and baseline comorbidity burden. A caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score was applied using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A total of 522 matched pairs were derived from the initial cohort of 1284 patients (756 cTKA and 528 RA-TKA). After matching, all covariates demonstrated adequate balance, with standardised mean differences < 0.1; covariate balance was additionally assessed using a Love plot (Fig. 2). All 522 matched patients had complete 1-year follow-up data and were included in the outcome analysis.

Data analyses

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were reported as median [interquartile range (IQR]). Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression with Firth correction was used to evaluate the association between surgical modality and PJI while accounting for residual confounding, with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.5.0, and a two-tailed P < 0.05P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and prosthetic joint infection outcomes

Baseline characteristics were generally comparable between the propensity-score-matched cTKA and RA-TKA cohorts. The overall 1-year PJI rate did not significantly differ between the groups (0.77% [4/522] versus 0.96% [5/522], P = 1.000). Subgroup analysis showed that the PJI rate within 90 days postoperatively was 0.77% (4/522) in the RA-TKA group and 0.58% (3/522) in the cTKA group (P = 1.000). During the period from 90 days to 1 year postoperatively, two cases of PJI occurred in the cTKA group, whereas none were observed in the RA-TKA group; however, because of the extremely low number of events, no intergroup statistical comparison was performed (Table 1). Among the 522 patients in the RA-TKA cohort, no tracker pin-site-related wound complication, delayed healing or pin-site infection was observed during the 1-year follow-up.

Operative time analysis

The median operative time was significantly longer in the RA-TKA group than in the cTKA group 115 (90–145) versus 85 (60–105) min, P = 0.001). In the cTKA group, the median operative times for patients with and without infection were 99 (90–120) and 85 (70–115) min, respectively, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (Mann–Whitney U test, W = 876.5, P = 0.215). In the RA-TKA group, the corresponding median operative times were 155 (134–169) and 115 (110–130) min, respectively, also without a statistically significant difference (Mann–Whitney U test, W = 465.5, P = 0.057) (Table 3).

Given that the final sample size after propensity matching (n = 1044) depended on the matching effectiveness, a compromise power analysis was used to assess design sensitivity: under the conditions of a baseline infection rate of 0.5% in the conventional group, α = 0.05 and β = 0.20, the current sample size could detect an increased risk of OR > 2.5.

Discussion

The principal finding of the present study was that no statistically significant difference in 1-year PJI risk was detected between RA-TKA and cTKA after primary surgery, despite a significantly longer median operative time in the robotic-assisted group. These findings are broadly consistent with those of previously published studies with short-term follow-up. Similarly, studies by Bendich et al. [31] (n = 1,307,411) and Wang et al. [25] (n > 300,000) found no significant association between robotic assistance and PJI within 90 days postoperatively. LaValva et al. [29] likewise reported a nonsignificant trend towards a lower 90-day PJI rate in the RA-TKA group (0.3% versus 0.5%; OR 0.61; 95% CI 0.33–1.15; P = 0.128). Taken together, current evidence has not demonstrated a clear increase in early postoperative infection with RA-TKA, although the available data remain largely observational and should be interpreted cautiously.

Evidence regarding longer-term outcomes, however, remains limited and inconclusive. Although previous studies have explored the clinical differences between robotic-assisted TKA and conventional manual TKA, to our knowledge, only one study has evaluated PJI as the primary outcome, with follow-up limited to 90 days postoperatively. Prior evidence has shown that a substantial proportion of arthroplasty-related infections are not identified within 90 days after surgery; in primary TKA, approximately 42.33% of infections are diagnosed between 91 days and 1 year postoperatively, suggesting that a 90-day observation window may underestimate the overall incidence of PJI [32]. In addition, the occurrence and recognition of PJI are temporally heterogeneous, and some infections caused by low-virulence pathogens may present in a delayed manner because of gradual biofilm formation and subtle clinical manifestations [33, 34]. Accordingly, the longer follow-up used in the present study may complement prior 90-day analyses by providing additional information on later-presenting infections.

In our cohort, no PJI occurred in the RA-TKA group between 90 days and 1 year postoperatively, whereas the incidence in the cTKA group was approximately 0.38% (2/522). However, because of the low number of events, formal hypothesis testing was not feasible, and this observation should not be overinterpreted. A similar uncertainty exists in the literature. In a large propensity-score-matched analysis, Wainwright et al. [28] found a significantly lower 1-year PJI rate in the RA-TKA group (OR 0.402; P < 0.001), whereas the meta-analysis by Raj et al. [35] calculated a pooled 1-year incidence of only 0.154% for RA-TKA. In contrast, DeSouza et al. [26] found no significant difference (OR 1.27; P = 0.13), and Lee et al. [36] reported comparable long-term infection rates between robotic-assisted and conventional TKA. This lack of consensus may be attributable to heterogeneity in follow-up definitions, robotic platforms, surgeon experience and institutional protocols.

Although previous studies have compared RA-TKA with computer-navigated TKA, few have adequately investigated the risk of PJI, and most have had limited control of potentially relevant confounding factors. LaValva et al. [29] provided the only analysis primarily focused on PJI and found no significant difference in the 90-day incidence between the matched computer-navigated total knee arthroplasty (CN-TKA; n = 5174) and RA-TKA (n = 743) cohorts. The present study adds at least 1 year of follow-up to evaluate later-occurring infections. The sample size of the RA-TKA cohort in our study was more limited than that of LaValva et al. [29], which is a recognised challenge in studies using a 1-year endpoint, as it is difficult to include a sufficient number of patients to detect differences in such a rare complication as PJI, often resulting in inadequate power to detect clinically meaningful effect sizes. In this context, the present findings should be viewed as complementary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of equivalence or absence of increased risk. No late PJI events occurred in the RA-TKA group; however, this may be attributable to chance or to residual bias introduced by unmeasured confounders not accounted for in the model.

The present study also demonstrated that the RA-TKA group had a significantly longer median operative time than the conventional TKA group (115 versus 85 min, P < 0.001). However, despite this overall increase in operative time, no corresponding significant increase in observed PJI events was detected in the RA-TKA group. This prompted exploratory consideration of the relationship between operative time and infection risk. In stratified within-group analyses, a tendency towards longer operative time was observed among patients with infection only in the RA-TKA group, and descriptive analyses suggested a numerical increase in PJI frequency at the longest operative durations (e.g. 160 min, corresponding to the 95th percentile in the present study). Given the low number of events, these observations are exploratory only, and no threshold inference can be made. Nevertheless, prolonged wound exposure remains a plausible factor to consider when operative duration becomes excessive [37–39].

Several hypotheses may be considered regarding why RA-TKA did not show a clearly higher observed infection rate despite longer operative time. For example, this technique avoids violation of the femoral intramedullary canal, may reduce perioperative blood loss and may allow more precise bone resection and soft-tissue handling [40]. In addition, some of the extra time required for RA-TKA is related to procedural steps such as registration, mapping and balancing, rather than necessarily reflecting greater surgical trauma [8]. However, these potential explanations remain speculative, and the present data do not support any inference of a protective effect.

Notably, LaValva et al. [29] reported shorter operative times among patients who developed PJI, in contrast to the findings of the present study. This discrepancy underscores the uncertainty surrounding the relationship between different components of operative workflow and infection risk in robotic-assisted procedures. Whether unusually short or prolonged operative times reflect procedural factors relevant to complications remains unclear and requires dedicated study. Future studies are needed to explore these hypotheses and to better characterise whether specific procedural intervals are associated with complications.

This study has several limitations. First, despite the use of propensity score matching, the retrospective, non-randomised design remains susceptible to selection bias and residual confounding, as treatment allocation was based on patient choice rather than random assignment. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as an adjusted observational comparison rather than a causal estimate. Second, the very low number of PJI events limited statistical power and estimate precision. However, such retrospective power calculations should be interpreted cautiously. Although no statistically significant difference was observed between groups, small but clinically meaningful differences cannot be excluded. Third, this was a single-centre study performed by two experienced surgeons using a single robotic platform after the learning curve had been overcome, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other institutions, surgeons, robotic systems or early implementation phases. Fourth, the implant designs differed between the two groups, and the potential influence of implant-related factors on the observed outcomes cannot be fully excluded. Finally, no microbiological subgroup analysis was performed; therefore, possible differences in pathogen profiles or infection characteristics between groups could not be assessed.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides complementary evidence suggesting that, in primary total knee arthroplasty, RA-TKA, despite a longer operative time, does not appear to be associated with a higher short-term risk of periprosthetic joint infection. Nevertheless, given the low event rate, potential residual confounding and the single-centre design, larger multicentre studies are warranted to confirm these findings.
