Prognostic Value of Ultrasonographic Optic Nerve Sheath Diameter in Patients with Sepsis in the Intensive Care Unit: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Kamuran Uluç, Ahmet Oğuzhan Küçük
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction and may involve the central nervous system through blood–brain barrier disruption and increased intracranial pressure. Ultrasonographic optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) is a non-invasive surrogate marker of intracranial pressure. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of ONSD for mortality prediction and its association with disease severity in patients with sepsis admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).Materials and MethodsThis prospective observational study included 110 adult patients diagnosed with sepsis according to Sepsis-3 criteria and admitted to a tertiary ICU between June 2024 and March 2025. ONSD was measured at ICU admission using standardized ultrasonographic techniques. Demographic data, clinical scores (SOFA, APACHE II, GCS, and CCI), laboratory parameters, and outcomes were recorded. Patients were followed until ICU discharge or death, and survival time was defined as the interval from ICU admission to death or censoring at discharge. Cox proportional hazards regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed.ResultsThirty-five patients (32%) were non-survivors and 75 (68%) were survivors. ONSD was significantly higher in non-survivors than survivors [5.33 (4.95–5.76) mm vs 4.81 (4.67–4.93) mm; p=0.001]. Non-survivors also had higher SOFA and APACHE II scores, lactate, CRP, and procalcitonin levels, and lower albumin levels and PaO2/FiO2 ratios (all p=0.001). In univariate analysis, ONSD was associated with mortality; however, multivariate Cox regression identified SOFA (HR: 1.165; p=0.044), albumin (HR: 0.470; p=0.012), and lactate (HR: 3.324; p=0.006) as independent predictors. ONSD showed good discriminatory performance (AUC: 0.855; cut-off: 5.0 mm), correlated with severity and inflammatory parameters, and ONSD ≥5 mm was associated with shorter survival (p=0.001).ConclusionONSD may serve as a rapid, bedside, non-invasive complementary marker for early risk stratification when interpreted with established clinical scores and laboratory parameters.Keywords: sepsis, optic nerve sheath diameter, ultrasonography, prognosis, intensive care unit

Introduction

Sepsis is a clinical syndrome characterized by life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.1 Beyond its effects on peripheral organs such as the lungs and kidneys, sepsis may also involve the central nervous system (CNS) through disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), neuroinflammation, cerebral edema, and alterations in intracranial pressure (ICP). Excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, increases BBB permeability and may contribute to vasogenic cerebral edema and secondary neurological dysfunction.2,3

Increased ICP results from disruption of the volumetric balance among brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid within the intracranial compartment. In sepsis, inflammatory BBB injury, cerebral edema, hypoxia, hypercapnia, and impaired venous return may contribute to increased cerebral blood volume and ICP elevation.4–6 However, invasive ICP monitoring is not feasible or routinely indicated in most septic ICU patients. Therefore, the detection of possible ICP elevation using rapid and non-invasive bedside methods may be of clinical importance.7,8

The optic nerve is surrounded by dura mater and is continuous with the subarachnoid space. Due to this anatomical continuity, increases in ICP are rapidly reflected by expansion of the optic nerve sheath, which can be assessed by ocular ultrasonography. Optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) has therefore been proposed as a practical, non-invasive bedside parameter for estimating increased ICP and monitoring neurological involvement in critically ill patients.7,9

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the relationship between bedside ultrasonographic ONSD measurements and mortality and clinical severity indicators in adult patients diagnosed with sepsis, and to investigate the potential utility of ONSD as a non-invasive biomarker for early prognostic prediction.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This study was designed as a prospective observational study and was conducted in the tertiary intensive care unit of Muş State Hospital between June 2024 and March 2025. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kafkas University Faculty of Medicine (Ethics approval number: 2024/06/464/03 Date: 26.06.2024). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the study or their legal representatives.

Patient Selection and Criteria

During the study period, all consecutive eligible adult patients admitted to the ICU with sepsis according to the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis (Sepsis-3) were prospectively screened. Patients aged over 18 years who met the inclusion criteria and had no exclusion criteria were included in the study.1 Patients with a history of intraocular surgery, glaucoma, orbital trauma or anatomical deformity, intracranial mass, hydrocephalus, cerebral hemorrhage or other pathologies that could affect intracranial pressure (ICP), known optic neuropathy, or clinically advanced sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) were excluded from the study. Patients were followed until ICU discharge or death, and survival time was defined as the interval from ICU admission to death or censoring at discharge.

Data Collection and Parameters

Study data were prospectively recorded at the time of admission to the intensive care unit. The following parameters were evaluated: patients’ age and sex, primary sources of infection, length of ICU stay, and outcomes (death/discharge).

Comorbid conditions including diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HT), ischemic heart disease (IHD), cerebrovascular disease (CVD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and malignancy were documented based on medical history.

Clinical scores including Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) were recorded.

Arterial blood gas parameters including pH, PaCO2, HCO3−, and lactate levels were recorded. Laboratory parameters including albumin, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin, and the arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) [PaO2/FiO2] ratio were also documented.

Optic Nerve Sheath Diameter (ONSD) Measurement

Ultrasonographic ONSD measurements were performed by a clinician experienced in bedside ultrasonography, using a high-resolution (5–12 MHz) linear probe with B-mode ultrasonography (Model: Samsung HM70 EVO). During the measurement, patients were positioned in the supine position with the head in the midline and elevated at 30°, and the eyelids were kept closed. To minimize mechanical pressure on the globe, an adequate amount of ultrasound gel was applied over the closed eyelid, and utmost care was taken to avoid compression of ocular structures. The measurement and data calculation process was carried out in accordance with established technical standards.10 The optic nerve sheath was evaluated separately for each eye in both vertical (sagittal) and horizontal (transverse) planes. Two measurements were obtained in each plane, resulting in a total of four measurements taken at 3 mm posterior to the globe. The arithmetic mean of these four measurements was calculated to determine the ONSD value for each eye (right and left). Subsequently, the mean values obtained from both eyes were averaged again to obtain the final ONSD value used in the analyses, which was recorded in millimeters (mm). All measurements were performed by the same experienced specialist to ensure technical consistency and to avoid interobserver variability during measurement; however, interobserver reliability could not be assessed. Device settings (depth 4–5 cm, focus, and gain settings) were optimized for each patient.

Power Analysis

G*Power 3.1.9 software was used to determine the sample size of the study. Cohen’s d was used as the measure of effect size. Group means, standard deviations, and group sizes were used for the calculation. Based on reference studies, the effect size was calculated as d = 0.38. Accordingly, with a 95% confidence level (1−α), 95% statistical power (1−β), and an effect size of d = 0.38, it was determined that a minimum of 97 patients would be sufficient for the study.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 27. Descriptive statistics were calculated as number, percentage, mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Considering the distribution characteristics of the data and the fact that most continuous variables were not normally distributed, continuous variables were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with mortality. Variables found to be significant were included in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate diagnostic performance; for each parameter, cut-off values, area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. The relationships between ONSD and clinical scores and ICU length of stay were analyzed using Spearman correlation analysis. In survival analysis, mortality was accepted as the endpoint. Overall survival (OS) curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and mean survival times and survival rates at predefined time points (days 7, 15, 30, and 45) were calculated. Differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all comparisons.

Discussion

In this study, the relationship between ultrasonographic optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD), mortality, and clinical severity scores was evaluated in patients with sepsis admitted to the intensive care unit. The findings demonstrated that ONSD was higher in non-survivors, correlated with clinical severity parameters, and showed good discriminatory performance in ROC analysis. However, ONSD was not retained as an independent predictor in the multivariate Cox regression model. Therefore, ONSD should be interpreted as a complementary prognostic marker rather than an independent predictor of mortality. The integrity of the blood–brain barrier plays a critical role in maintaining central nervous system homeostasis. The inflammatory response developing in sepsis may increase blood–brain barrier permeability, leading to cerebral edema and alterations in intracranial pressure. In addition, impairment of cerebral blood flow autoregulation, hypoxia, hypercapnia, and venous congestion may contribute to this process. These mechanisms may explain the association between increased ONSD and poor clinical outcome in patients with sepsis.11–13

Our findings are consistent with the prospective study by Gültekin, which reported that ONSD measured at the time of admission to the intensive care unit in critically ill COVID-19 patients is a strong predictor of mortality.14 In both studies, the demonstration of high discriminative performance of ONSD with similar cut-off values in critically ill patient groups with different etiologies suggests that the increase in ONSD may represent a common pathophysiological process reflecting increased intracranial pressure. The fact that cerebral edema developing due to systemic inflammation and blood–brain barrier dysfunction in sepsis and the mechanisms related to hypoxia and hypercapnia in COVID-19 reach a similar endpoint may explain this situation.

In the prospective study conducted by Syed et al in intensive care patients, ONSD was shown to be significantly associated with mortality, particularly in septic cases, and was higher in non-survivors.15 In that study, it is noteworthy that ONSD increased not only at ICU admission but also during follow-up, and this serial increase was associated with poor prognosis. In the present study, although ONSD was measured only once at ICU admission, it showed good discriminatory performance for mortality and was associated with disease severity parameters. However, the 5.0 mm threshold identified in this cohort should not be interpreted as a universal or definitive cut-off. Rather, it represents an ROC-derived, cohort-specific threshold for mortality discrimination in this study population. Since ONSD is a dynamic parameter, serial ONSD trends may provide more robust prognostic information than a single measurement. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as supporting the potential complementary prognostic value of admission ONSD, while future studies should evaluate serial ONSD measurements and changes over time.

In the prospective study by Mowafy et al examining sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE), ONSD was shown to be closely associated with both the development of SAE and mortality, and increasing ONSD values, especially during follow-up, were reported to reflect poor prognosis. In their study, the positive correlation of ONSD with SOFA score and intensive care mortality suggests that ONSD enlargement may parallel increasing disease severity and brain dysfunction in sepsis. The present study differs from that of Mowafy et al in terms of patient population and measurement strategy. While Mowafy et al specifically focused on patients with SAE and serial ONSD changes, our study evaluated a general sepsis population and assessed ONSD at ICU admission.7 Although patients with clinically advanced SAE were excluded from our study, mild or subclinical neurological involvement could not be completely ruled out. Therefore, increased ONSD in our cohort may reflect not only systemic disease severity but also possible sepsis-related neurological involvement, early cerebral edema, or intracranial pressure alterations.

In the prospective study by Yang et al, ONSD was reported to be useful in distinguishing the presence of SAE in patients with sepsis and was found to be markedly higher in the SAE group.16 Although ONSD showed a negative correlation with GCS and albumin level, its role in predicting mortality was limited. In the present study, ONSD was significantly associated with mortality and correlated with clinical severity parameters in patients with sepsis without a specific SAE-based grouping. However, because ONSD was not retained as an independent predictor in the multivariate Cox regression model, these findings should be interpreted as indicating complementary prognostic value rather than independent predictive superiority.

Accordingly, high ONSD should not be interpreted as a direct marker of more severe sepsis in all cases. ONSD enlargement may result from multiple overlapping mechanisms, including systemic inflammation, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, cerebral edema, oxygenation impairment, hypercapnia, impaired venous return, and possible sepsis-related neurological involvement.11–13,16 Therefore, ONSD should be evaluated together with clinical severity scores, laboratory parameters, and neurological findings rather than interpreted as an isolated marker of sepsis severity.

In the prospective study conducted by Özdemir et al in medical intensive care patients, admission ONSD values were not associated with mortality, whereas the increase in ONSD during follow-up (ΔONSD) was identified as an important prognostic parameter.17 This difference may be explained by differences in study populations and measurement strategies. Özdemir et al evaluated a mixed medical ICU population and focused on serial ONSD changes, whereas the present study included patients with sepsis and evaluated a single ONSD measurement at ICU admission. Therefore, our ROC-derived 5.0 mm threshold should be interpreted as a cohort-specific finding rather than a definitive cut-off. Serial ONSD trends may provide additional prognostic information and should be evaluated in future studies.

In the review by Bhide et al evaluating the use of ONSD in non-traumatic brain injury, it was emphasized that data regarding the prognostic value of ONSD in heterogeneous patient groups such as sepsis and SAE are limited.18 Although there are studies regarding the prognostic role of ONSD in sepsis, there is still a need for studies with larger sample sizes evaluating the consistency of findings with standardized measurement protocols and different outcomes in different centers.

In the prospective cohort study by Luo et al, ONSD was reported to be strongly associated with the development of sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE), although mortality was not directly evaluated as an outcome.19 In the present study, ONSD was associated with mortality and clinical severity parameters in a general sepsis population. However, because ONSD was not retained as an independent predictor in multivariate analysis, it should be considered a complementary marker that may reflect both systemic disease severity and possible neurological involvement.

In the study conducted by Czempik et al in patients with septic shock, ONSD was suggested as a potential screening tool for sepsis-associated brain dysfunction, although its prognostic value was reported to be limited.20 In the present study, ONSD was associated with mortality and showed good discriminatory performance; however, it should not be considered a replacement for established severity scores or a standalone prognostic tool. Rather, ONSD may provide complementary bedside information when interpreted together with clinical scores and laboratory parameters.

The effect of methodological differences in optic nerve sheath diameter measurement on results was particularly emphasized in the international consensus statement published by Hirzallah et al.10 In this consensus, the importance of standardizing the measurement technique for comparability in clinical studies was stated. In our study, the fact that ONSD measurements were performed in accordance with a defined and standardized protocol and that a significant relationship with mortality was shown supports that standardized ONSD measurement may be a reliable prognostic tool in patients with sepsis.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The single-center nature of the study and the limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of the results. ONSD measurements were performed at a single time point at ICU admission, and the prognostic contribution of serial measurements and ONSD trends could not be evaluated. In addition, intracranial pressure was not directly measured with invasive methods. Another limitation is that ONSD measurements were performed by a single experienced specialist. Although this approach may have ensured technical consistency and avoided interobserver variability during measurement, interobserver reliability could not be assessed. Moreover, the operator was not blinded to the patients’ clinical status and severity scores, which may have introduced potential observer bias. Finally, although patients with clinically advanced sepsis-associated encephalopathy were excluded, mild or subclinical neurological involvement could not be completely ruled out.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ultrasonographic ONSD was significantly associated with mortality and disease severity in patients with sepsis admitted to the ICU. Although ONSD was not identified as an independent predictor in multivariate Cox regression analysis, its good discriminatory performance and correlation with clinical severity parameters suggest that it may provide complementary prognostic information. As a rapid, bedside, and non-invasive method, ONSD may support early risk stratification when interpreted together with established clinical scores and laboratory parameters. Larger multicenter studies including serial measurements and interobserver reliability assessments are needed to confirm these findings and clarify the role of ONSD in clinical algorithms.
