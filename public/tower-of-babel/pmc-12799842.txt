CTLA4 genetic variants influence immune regulation and susceptibility of HPV associated cervical cancer
Authors: Maylla Cardoso de Oliveira, Giulia Mariane Fortunato, Pamella Rodrigues da Silva, Bianca Lisley Barboza Pacheco, Mariane Ricciardi da Silva, Karen Brajão de Oliveira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer remains a significant global health burden, representing one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among women. While persistent infection with oncogenic types of Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is the primary risk factor for cervical carcinogenesis, only a subset of infected women progress to malignancy, suggesting that host genetic factors play a critical role in disease susceptibility. This narrative review explores the influence of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) in the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA4) on the risk of HPV-associated cervical cancer, with a focus on the modulation of this immune checkpoint pathway. CTLA-4 is expressed on the surface of T cells and functions as a negative regulator of immune activation. Its genetic variants may influence gene transcription efficiency, protein processing and transport, and/or binding affinity to its B7 ligand, thereby affecting viral clearance and immune surveillance against HPV-transformed cells. Current evidence supports associations between specific CTLA4 SNVs, such as rs5742909 (− 318 C > T), rs231775 (+ 49 A > G) and rs3087243 (+ 6230 G > A), and increased susceptibility to cervical cancer, supporting their potential as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer immunotherapy. These findings highlight the relevance of host genetics in the personalized management of cervical cancer.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer and is the fourth most common cause of cancer-related death in women [1]. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) is a well-established prerequisite for cervical carcinogenesis, with HPV DNA found in 99.7% of cervical cancer cases [2]. Nonetheless, HPV infection alone is not sufficient to induce malignancy, as most individuals (70–90%) eliminate the virus spontaneously within 12 to 24 months. The transition from infection to neoplasia depends on multiple cofactors, including hormonal, environmental, and, notably, immunogenetic factors [3].

Among the mechanisms that determine immune surveillance and tolerance, immune checkpoint molecules play a critical role. CTLA-4 (Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte Antigen 4) is a co-inhibitory receptor expressed by activated T cells, which competes with CD28 for CD80/CD86 binding, leading to downregulation of immune responses. Genetic variants in the CTLA4 gene have been associated with altered T-cell regulation and susceptibility to several cancers, including cervical cancer development [4–12]. Furthermore, studies suggest that genetic variants in CTLA4 may play an important role in modulating the immune response against the tumor and also to anticancer immunotherapy [13, 14]. Therefore, understanding how CTLA4 genetic variants modulate the immune response against HPV-infected cells is essential to better characterize interindividual differences in disease susceptibility and therapeutic response.

This narrative review aims to synthesize and critically analyze the available evidence on the role of CTLA4 genetic variants in HPV-associated cervical cancer, exploring how these polymorphisms may influence immune modulation and their potential as biomarkers in the context of immunotherapy. We opted for a narrative approach to allow a broader and more flexible analysis of the literature, highlighting key findings, knowledge gaps, and future perspectives. The studies included were selected based on their relevance to the topic, originality, and scientific quality, as judged by the authors.

Human papillomavirus (HPV) and cervical cancer

HPV belongs to the Papillomaviridae family and is a non-enveloped virus consisting of an icosahedral protein capsid with a diameter of 50 to 55 nm containing its circular double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) of approximately 8 kb [15]. HPV exhibits tropism for epithelial cells and causes infections of the skin and mucous membranes [16]. The HPV types are divided into five main genera: Alphapapillomavirus, Betapapillomavirus, Gammapapillomavirus, Mupapillomavirus and Nupapillomavirus [17]. The Alphapapillomavirus are mainly divided into low-risk HPV (LR-HPV) and high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) based on their ability to cause cancer [18]. LR-HPV types that are precursors of benign lesions, such as benign genital warts or condyloma acuminatum. The most frequently reported and investigated LR-HPV types are HPV 6 and 11 [19]. While HR types, especially HPV 16 and 18, are strongly linked to malignant transformations. A group of 14 HR-HPV types (HPV 16, 18, 31, 45, 33, 35, 39, 51, 52, 56, 58, 66, 68 and 70) has been recognized for its role in cervical carcinogenesis [20, 21].

HR-HPV types can induce cell transformation leading to squamous intraepithelial lesions, which can be classified as low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) according to the Bethesda system - a standardized reporting model for cervical cytology. Over time, these lesions can develop into cervical cancer [22].

The molecular basis for the progression to cervical cancer lies in the activity of viral oncoproteins that disrupt tumor suppressor functions and subvert host immune surveillance. HR-HPV integrates into the host genome and expresses oncoproteins (primarily E6, E7, and, to a lesser extent, E5) that cooperate in cellular transformation and immune evasion. E6 promotes p53 degradation through the E6/E6AP complex and activates Human Telomerase Reverse Transcriptase (hTERT), enhancing cell survival and genomic instability [23, 24]. E7 disrupts the pRb/E2F pathway, inducing uncontrolled proliferation and cell cycle progression [23, 25]. E5 contributes to reduced antigen presentation and resistance to apoptosis by downregulating Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I and II molecules and modulating Fas-mediated signaling [26–28].

Collectively, these actions not only favor immortalization and proliferation of infected keratinocytes but also attenuate antiviral responses, such as STING/IFN pathway interference and cytokine suppression, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that enables viral persistence [29, 30]. Within this context, genetic variations in immune checkpoint regulators such as CTLA−4 may modulate the degree of local immune tolerance, influencing HPV clearance capacity and, consequently, susceptibility to cervical carcinogenesis.

Host immune response to HPV-driven cervical cancer

The host immune response to HPV involves the coordinated activation of innate and adaptive mechanisms. Pattern-recognition receptors, particularly Toll-like receptors (TLRs) expressed on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as macrophages and dendritic cells, detect viral components and initiate inflammatory and antiviral signaling cascades. This recognition promotes antigen presentation and the activation of HPV-specific T cells, triggering a predominantly Th1-type adaptive response that is essential for viral clearance [31, 32].

However, HPV has evolved multiple mechanisms to evade immune detection. The virus downregulates MHC-I expression and interferes with the STING/IFN-I pathway, reducing antigen presentation and impairing type I interferon signaling [29]. As infection persists, a tumor-promoting microenvironment emerges, characterized by M2 macrophage polarization and secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines such as Interleukin-10 (IL−10), Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), and Transforming Growth Factor (TGF-β), which inhibit cytotoxic responses and promote angiogenesis [30, 33]. Persistent antigenic stimulation also drives functional exhaustion of effector T cells, evidenced by upregulation of inhibitory receptors such as PD−1, TIM−3, and CTLA−4 [34].

In this context, CTLA-4 plays a crucial role in sustaining immune tolerance to HPV-infected cells. Regulatory T cells (Tregs), which constitutively express high levels of CTLA-4, suppress effector T-cell activity by sequestering CD80/CD86 on dendritic cells through trans-endocytosis and by inducing the expression of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), a potent immunosuppressive enzyme [35, 36]. Increased infiltration of FOXP3⁺CTLA-4⁺ Tregs and reduced cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell density have been reported in cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and invasive HPV-associated carcinomas [37, 38]. Moreover, overexpression of CTLA-4 in exhausted CD8⁺ T cells further dampens antiviral and antitumor responses, favoring HPV persistence and disease progression [39]. Together, these findings highlight how CTLA-4-mediated immunoregulation shapes the local immune landscape, providing a mechanistic link between HPV immune evasion and host genetic factors that influence CTLA-4 expression and function.

Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4)

CTLA−4, also known as CD152, is a pivotal immune checkpoint receptor that regulates the amplitude and duration of T-cell activation. It is expressed primarily on activated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells and constitutively on Tregs, functioning as a critical brake on immune responses [40] The CTLA−4 protein consists of three domains and a leader peptide [41] (Fig. 1). Structurally, CTLA−4 shares homology with CD28 and competes for the same ligands, CD80 and CD86, on APCs. Upon ligand engagement, CTLA−4 transmits inhibitory signals that downregulate Interleukin-2 (IL−2) production and arrest T-cell proliferation [42] (Fig. 2).

Other inhibitory receptors such as PD−1, TIM−3, and ICOS also contribute to the maintenance of immune homeostasis, but they act at distinct stages of the immune response. PD−1 primarily regulates effector T cells within peripheral tissues and tumors, limiting cytotoxic function during chronic antigen exposure. TIM−3 is frequently co-expressed with PD−1 on exhausted T cells and further reinforces functional impairment through interaction with galectin−9 and other ligands. ICOS, although belonging to the same CD28 family, can exert dual effects, costimulatory in effector T cells or inhibitory in Tregs, depending on the microenvironmental context [34, 38]. In contrast, CTLA−4 acts earlier in the activation process, mainly in lymphoid organs, serving as the first checkpoint that prevents excessive T-cell priming and autoimmunity [40]. This hierarchical regulation highlights the complementary but nonredundant roles of CTLA−4 and other inhibitory pathways in immune modulation.

Functionally, CTLA−4 mediates immune suppression both intrinsically, through inhibitory signaling in effector T cells, and extrinsically, via Treg-dependent mechanisms. Tregs use CTLA−4 to remove CD80/CD86 from dendritic cells by trans-endocytosis and to induce indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which depletes tryptophan and inhibits effector T-cell proliferation [35, 36]. Genetic variants in the CTLA4 gene, such as rs5742909 (−318 C >T), rs231775 (+ 49 A >G), and rs3087243 (+ 6230 G >A), have been reported to alter gene expression, ligand binding, or isoform balance, ultimately influencing immune tolerance and susceptibility to HPV-associated cervical carcinogenesis [43].

Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) of CTLA4

The genome undergoes variations from one individual to another, known as genetic variants, which can affect immune regulation and alter susceptibility to disease development [38, 44, 45]. The most common form of genetic variation is called single nucleotide variant (SNV) and is characterized by the exchange of one nucleotide for another in a specific region of the genome [46].

CTLA4 has more than a hundred genetic variants, for example, rs5742909 (− 318 C >T), rs231775 (+ 49 A >G), rs3087243 (+ 6230 G >A), rs4553808 (− 1661 A >G), and rs733618 (−1722 A >G) genetic variants, which are key contributors in certain autoimmune diseases and cancers [47–49]. Studies report that these genetic variants are associated with susceptibility to different types of malignancies such as colorectal cancer [50–53], hepatocellular carcinoma [9, 54], cervical cancer [4, 9, 55], melanoma [14], head and neck cancer [56], renal cell carcinoma [57], bladder cancer [58] and others [48, 59–63] (Fig. 3).

Some case-control studies have investigated the association between CTLA4 genetic variants and susceptibility to cervical cancer in different populations (Table 1). Among the SNVs analyzed, the T allele of the variant rs5742909 (−318 C >T) was associated with an increased risk of cervical cancer in the populations studied by Oliveira et al. (2025), Su et al. (2007), Pawlak et al. (2010), Jiang et al. (2011) and Xiong et al. (2014) [4, 5, 7, 11, 12]. In contrast, Rahimifar et al. (2010) found an association between the C allele and disease susceptibility in their analysis of the Iranian population. C allele linked to lower promoter activity and reduced CTLA-4 expression; insufficient inhibitory signaling may cause chronic T-cell activation and exhaustion, weakening antiviral control [10]. Gokhale et al. (2013), who studied populations from India and Sweden, found no statistically significant association between this variant and the development of malignant cervical neoplasms [6] (Table 1). The genetic variant rs5742909 consists of a transition from cytosine (C) to thymine (T) at position − 318 of the CTLA4 promoter sequence. The promoter region of the CTLA4 gene is essential for transcriptional regulation, as specific transcription factors bind to this sequence and recruit protein complexes critical for the initiation of transcription. Alterations in this region can create or eliminate transcription factor binding sites, thereby modifying the efficiency of gene expression [64]. The study by Angulo-Aguado et al. (2023) demonstrated, through luciferase reporter assays and EMSA, that the T allele of the rs5742909 genetic variant increases CTLA4 promoter activity in T cells, potentially due to higher affinity for transcription factors of the TCF7/LEF1 family. This alteration in promoter activity may result in increased expression of the CTLA-4 protein, which in turn contributes to the negative regulation of T cell activation and may promote immune evasion mechanisms, especially in tumor contexts [52].

Furthermore, a study conducted functional analyses using peripheral blood mononuclear cells from individuals with different genotypes in relation to the CaSki cell line (HPV 16-positive cervical cancer). Cells from individuals carrying the TT genotype exhibited significantly lower cytotoxicity against tumor cells compared to those with the CT or CC genotypes. This suggests that the T allele promotes a stronger negative regulation of T-cell proliferation and function, thereby increasing susceptibility to cervical cancer [65].

Regarding the SNV rs231775 (+ 49 A >G), Hu et al. and Li et al. reported a significant association between the AA genotype and the development of cervical cancer in their case-control studies [9, 66]. Gokhale et al. also found a significant association between the AA genotype and the development of this malignancy in the Indian population and additionally observed a significant association when they analyzed only the A allele [6]. However, Oliveira et al., Pawlak et al., Jiang et al. and Xiong et al. found no significant association between the SNV rs231775 (+ 49 A >G) and the development of cervical cancer [4, 5, 7, 11] (Table 1).

The genetic variant rs231775 consists of a transition from adenine (A) to guanine (G) in exon 1 at position + 49 of CTLA4, which leads to a substitution of threonine by alanine at codon 17 [67]. Evidence indicates that the amino acid substitution caused by the rs231775 (+ 49 A >G) SNV may alter the interaction between the CTLA-4 protein and its B7 ligands, thereby affecting its regulatory function in the immune response [9]. Studies have shown that this variant interferes with the glycosylation of the protein, compromising its membrane localization and altering its binding capacity to B7 ligands, as demonstrated by in vitro expression assays and glycosylation analyses [53, 68–70]. On the other hand, the studies by Hu et al. (2010) and Mäurer et al. (2002) reported that the GG genotype is associated with a greater inhibitory immune capacity, with reduced lymphocyte proliferation compared to individuals with the AA genotype. These findings suggest that the rs231775 variant, despite its potential structural effects, may favor a more efficient functional interaction between CTLA-4 and B7 ligands, enhancing inhibitory signaling and promoting suppression of T cell activation and proliferation [9, 71]. Functional studies conducted by Wagner et al. showed that the AA genotype (Thr17) is associated with lower levels of T-cell activation and proliferation compared to the GG genotype (Ala17). In addition, the Thr17 variant exhibits higher binding affinity to CD80 and a stronger inhibitory effect, whereas Ala17 is linked to a significant reduction in CTLA-4 expression on the cell surface, affecting receptor trafficking and function [65].

Regarding the SNV rs3087243 (+ 6230 G >A), Xiong et al. found that the AA genotype and the A allele were associated with protection against the development of cervical cancer, while the presence of the G allele was associated with susceptibility to this type of cancer [5]. However, Oliveira et al., Pawlak et al. and Jiang et al. found no significant association between the SNV rs3087243 (+ 6230 G >A) and the development of cervical cancer [4, 7, 11] (Table 1).

The genetic variant rs3087243 consists of a transition from guanine (G) to adenine (A) in the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) at + 6230 of CTLA4. The 3’ untranslated regions of mRNAs are known to regulate various processes such as mRNA localization, mRNA stability and translation [72]. This genetic variant modulates alternative splicing events, altering the balance between the membrane-bound and soluble isoforms of CTLA-4 [73]. In their studies, Ueda et al. (2003) demonstrated that the rs3087243 (+ 6230 G >A) variant is associated with reduced expression of the soluble CTLA-4 isoform (sCTLA-4) in CD4⁺ T cells, particularly in individuals with the GG genotype, who exhibit approximately 50% less sCTLA-4 compared to those with the AA genotype. This reduction compromises the negative regulation of T cell activation, promoting pro-activation signaling via the CD28/CD80–CD86 pathway, which may result in enhanced T cell activation and breakdown of immune tolerance [43].

It is worth noting that the cited studies were conducted in different regions of the world, which may directly influence the results. In this context, most of the studies referenced were carried out in Asia, particularly in China, with only two conducted in other continents: one in Poland and another in Brazil. The Brazilian population is highly underrepresented in genetic association studies, and its known admixture complicates both the interpretation and comparison of results. Even within Brazil, extrapolating genetic data between regions is challenging due to varying ancestry patterns, which include different proportions of Native American, European, African, and Asian ancestries [74]. Similarly, the Chinese population comprises 56 officially recognized ethnic groups, distributed across the country [75]. The Iranian and Indian populations, which also contributed to CTLA4 SNV analyses, present their own specific characteristics, with Iranians being predominantly of Persian ancestry, while Indians represent a more genetically diverse population [76, 77]. Among the cited cohorts, the Polish population might be considered the most genetically homogeneous [78].

Using genome-wide studies, population differences in SNV distribution have been categorized into two main types of region-specific alleles: abundant region-specific alleles (ARSA), which are SNVs with a frequency higher than 18% in a specific geographic region and nearly absent elsewhere; and regional absence of common alleles (RACA), which are SNVs with high global frequency (>20%) but completely absent in one continent [79]. ARSAs are particularly relevant in explaining why genetic association studies may yield conflicting results across geographically distant populations. Given these allele distribution discrepancies, analyzing genetic variants in a broad range of ethnic groups is essential, as findings from one group cannot be reliably extrapolated to others.

Although the influence of genetic variants on CTLA-4 expression is not yet fully understood, cancer immunotherapy based on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including CTLA-4, has shown promising results in the treatment of various solid tumors in recent years [80, 81].

CTLA-4 as a therapeutic target

CTLA-4 is an essential immune checkpoint exploited by tumor cells to promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment and thus represents a potential target for immunotherapy [82–86].

CTLA-4 has been targeted in cancer immunotherapy, which aims to overcome the suppression of the immune system by blocking immune checkpoints to enhance the antitumor activity of immune cells. Monoclonal antibodies targeting and blocking CTLA-4 have been developed, such as ipilimumab and tremelimumab, which can be used in combination with other therapies or other checkpoint inhibitors [87].

Ipilimumab (MDX-010; Medarex/Bristol-Myers Squibb, Princeton, NJ, USA), the first fully human monoclonal antibody of the IgG1 class targeting CTLA-4, was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2011 for the treatment of patients with advanced melanoma [88, 89]. Since then, clinical trials have demonstrated its significant antitumor activity and improved prognosis in various cancers, including advanced melanoma [90–92] renal cell carcinoma [93], B-cell lymphoma [94] and metastatic prostate cancer [95].

Ipilimumab can also be combined with other immunotherapies. Its combination with nivolumab, an anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody, has led to better clinical outcomes and significantly improved survival of patients with advanced melanoma compared to monotherapy [96–100]. These benefits also apply to other malignant diseases such as metastatic osteosarcoma [101], colorectal cancer [102], recurrent glioblastoma [103] and renal cell carcinoma [104].

Another promising CTLA-4 inhibitor is tremelimumab (CP675,206; Pfizer, New York, NY, USA), a fully human monoclonal antibody of the IgG2 class. Its action is based on blocking CTLA-4 and promoting immune activation through the expansion of T cells, leading to a potential antitumor effect [105]. Clinical trials with tremelimumab have shown acceptable tolerability and significant clinical activity in various malignancies, including melanoma [106–108], metastatic colorectal cancer [109], hepatocellular carcinoma [110] and malignant mesothelioma [111].

The IgG2 isotype was intentionally selected because it is known to cause less complement activation and less induction of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and also minimizes the risk of cytokine release syndrome [105, 112]. These properties also make tremelimumab an attractive agent for combination therapies, including with PD-L1 inhibitors. The combination of anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-L1 antibodies has shown additive antitumor effects based on complementary immunomodulatory mechanisms [113, 114]. One example is the Single Tremelimumab Regular Interval Durvalumab (STRIDE) regimen, in which a single high dose of tremelimumab is combined with durvalumab, an anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody, at regular intervals. This strategy has shown relevant clinical activity and a manageable safety profile in patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma, regardless of whether or not they were previously treated with sorafenib [115].

In addition to the combination with anti-PD-L1 agents, CTLA-4 inhibition has also been combined with other therapeutic modalities such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy. These treatment regimens have shown promising results in various malignancies, including lung cancer [116–118], advanced melanoma [119]– [120] and metastatic prostate cancer [121].

Although immunotherapy has shown promising therapeutic results in various advanced cancers [122], only 15 to 60% of patients show a positive response [123]. The actual clinical utility of these novel therapies may largely depend on the identification of predictive biomarkers that guide the treatment of patients likely to respond, considering that differences in response to immunotherapy may be at least partly due to individual genetic variations such as SNVs. Studies suggest that genetic variants in CTLA4 may serve as promising biomarkers for modulating this response [13, 14]. These SNVs are functionally relevant as they can influence the expression and activity of CTLA-4 in T lymphocytes by altering gene transcription efficiency [43, 124], protein processing and transport [125] and/or interaction with its B7 ligand [68]. Therefore, a better understanding of CTLA4 genetic variants may be crucial for the development of personalized immunotherapy strategies to ultimately improve treatment outcomes and optimize patient selection.

It is also important to note that the clinical relevance of CTLA4 SNVs remains under investigation, with conflicting findings across studies. Some associations have failed to reach significance in replication cohorts, and the functional consequences of certain variants remain unclear. Additionally, variability in allele frequencies between ethnic groups and differences in study design (e.g., genotyping methods, cancer staging, or HPV typing) contribute to inconsistent results. These discrepancies highlight the need for cautious interpretation and underscore the importance of large-scale, multicenter studies to validate the prognostic or predictive value of CTLA4 polymorphisms.

Beyond the lack of robust validation as a biomarker, several practical and clinical challenges also limit the widespread integration of CTLA‑4 inhibitors into personalized cancer treatment strategies. Although these agents can de-inhibit T cells and enhance anti-tumor responses, this widespread immune activation frequently leads to a range of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). These irAEs, which can be mild to severe and even fatal, affect multiple organ systems such as the gastrointestinal tract, skin, liver, and endocrine glands, posing a considerable clinical challenge [126].

Furthermore, the therapeutic efficacy of CTLA-4 inhibitors as monotherapy can be suboptimal in certain cancer types, particularly in tumors with low T-cell infiltration. Many cancer patients exhibit primary or acquired resistance to immunotherapy, leading to short-lived responses or disease recurrence. Dobosz et al. (2022) emphasize that many cancer patients do not respond to immunotherapy, or their response remains short-lived, and the recurrence of the disease seems to be inevitable mainly due to the rapidly evolving resistance [127]. This resistance is multifactorial, involving genomic factors, tumor heterogeneity, and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, as further discussed by Vatsavai et al. (2025) [126]. Such limitations underscore the ongoing need for detailed research into resistance mechanisms and the development of combination therapies to improve clinical outcomes and expand benefits to a larger patient population.

Conclusions

In summary, this narrative review highlights the potential impact of CTLA4 genetic variants on the susceptibility to HPV-associated cervical cancer, emphasizing their role in modulating immune checkpoint pathways. The evidence suggests that such genetic variants may influence host immune responses to HPV infection, thereby contributing to interindividual differences in the risk of cervical carcinogenesis. Understanding these immunogenetic interactions contributes not only to a deeper comprehension of host-virus interactions but also to the identification of promising biomarkers for early diagnosis, prognosis, and the development of personalized immunotherapeutic strategies. We also acknowledge the methodological scope of our narrative approach, which, while not designed for quantitative synthesis, allowed for a broad and critical exploration of the available literature. Importantly, despite promising associations, the functional consequences of CTLA4 SNVs in cervical cancer development remain to be elucidated. Future studies employing experimental models and functional assays are warranted to explore the causal mechanisms linking CTLA4 genetic variants to immune evasion and tumor progression in HPV-driven cervical carcinogenesis.
