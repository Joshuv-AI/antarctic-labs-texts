Factors affecting voriconazole pharmacokinetic variability in critically ill patients: a systematic review
Authors: Anne Coste, Cécile Aubron, Séverine Ansart, Jason Roberts, Florent Morio, Matthieu Grégoire
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficacy and toxicity of voriconazole, a broad-spectrum antifungal agent, are correlated with its trough concentration (Ctrough). Patients hospitalised in the intensive care unit (ICU) frequently experience inadequate voriconazole exposure. We sought to identify factors associated with variability of voriconazole Ctrough in ICU patients.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, Web of Science and Cochrane Library databases from inception through July 2025.ResultsThirty-one studies describing nine factors of variability were included. Liver dysfunction, high body mass index, high SOFA score and high inflammation status were associated with voriconazole overexposure, whereas the oral route of administration, renal replacement therapy and low body mass index were associated with voriconazole underexposure. The effects of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and hypoalbuminemia on voriconazole exposure were unclear.ConclusionsA systematic dose adaptation is required for patients with liver dysfunction, and voriconazole dose calculation should not be based on total body weight, with an adjusted body weight a preferred alternative. Intravenous administration should be favoured in patients without severe renal dysfunction. Further research to define the benefit of an individualised dosing approach for voriconazole in ICU patients that combines pharmacokinetic modelling and machine learning, is warranted. Meanwhile, close therapeutic drug monitoring is needed for ICU patients, especially those with identified risk factors for under- or overexposure.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05697-w.

Background

Voriconazole is a broad-spectrum triazole antifungal agent approved for the treatment of invasive fungal infections [1, 2]. Voriconazole is metabolised by the liver via the cytochrome P450 isoenzymes (CYPs) 2C19, 3A4 and 2C9. According to the manufacturer, voriconazole has a high oral bioavailability of 96%, a nonlinear and saturable elimination, a medium protein-binding ability of 58%, and a lipophilic character (logP = 1.8) [3, 4]. Since voriconazole has low solubility, the intravenous formulation of voriconazole contains the solubilising agent sulfobutylether-β-cyclodextrin (SBECD), which is renally excreted. A voriconazole dose of 6 mg/kg/12h on day 1 and then 4 mg/kg/12h is recommended for intravenous administration [1, 2].

Voriconazole is the treatment of choice for invasive aspergillosis, which is associated with a mortality rate of up to 79% [5–7] in ICU patients. Voriconazole efficacy and toxicity have been correlated with its trough concentration (Ctrough) [8, 9], and therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is highly recommended [1, 10]. A Ctrough between 2 and 6 mg/L is recommended for patients treated for severe Aspergillus infections [1]. However, both hepatotoxicity and neurotoxicity have been associated with Ctrough as low as 3 mg/L, with higher odds being observed when Ctrough exceeds 4 mg/L [9, 11, 12]. The recommended range includes toxic concentrations, creating situations where clinicians may accept some degree of toxicity to favour efficacy.

ICU patients frequently experience inadequate voriconazole exposure [13–16]. Subtherapeutic concentrations are observed in 12 to 53% of patients, whereas supratherapeutic concentrations are observed in 19 to 27% of patients. A recent multicenter retrospective study reported that voriconazole Ctrough-related hepatotoxicity, defined by the Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse Events, was present in 28% of ICU patients [17].

Many factors associated with voriconazole metabolism, such as CYP2C19 and CYP3A4 polymorphisms [18, 19], inflammation and C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations [20, 21], concomitant medication use (i.e., proton‒pump inhibitors (PPIs), ritonavir, and rifampicin) [22, 23], liver cirrhosis [24], and body weight, have been highlighted. Although a weight-adjusted dose of voriconazole is recommended for all patients, obese patients more frequently display supratherapeutic Ctrough [25].

ICU patients develop many pharmacokinetic changes [26], including increased distribution volume secondary to systemic inflammation response syndrome, renal clearance changes from augmented renal clearance to kidney failure, and/or altered protein binding balance secondary to hypoproteinemia. In addition, organ support treatments, such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and renal replacement therapy (RRT), might also impact drug pharmacokinetics [27, 28]. ICU patients are at high risk of subtherapeutic drug exposure or drug toxicity, making it crucial to well identify parameters associated with those pharmacokinetic changes.

This systematic review aims to identify patient-related and treatment-related factors associated with voriconazole exposure variability in ICU patients and describe their impact on the voriconazole Ctrough.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023483375). This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines [29].

Search strategy

The MEDLINE via PubMed (including Epub ahead of print), Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library databases were systematically searched from 1946 through July 2025 in English and French. Pfizer was also contacted to identify potentially unpublished data on voriconazole concentrations in ICU patients. We conducted further searches using the “related article” feature and the reference lists of the included articles, including conference abstracts. We searched for studies reporting voriconazole concentrations in ICU patients. Search terms included voriconazole, critically, ICU, ECMO, extracorporeal, renal replacement, filtration, dialysis, sepsis-related organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, feeding tube, and nasogastric tube. The search strategy is available in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Selection criteria

Article screening was first performed by one reviewer on the basis of titles and abstracts. Two reviewers (AC and MG) subsequently assessed the full texts of studies that appeared potentially relevant to determine their eligibility for inclusion. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. We included studies meeting the following criteria: (1) enrolled adult patients; (2) were conducted in an ICU setting; and (3) reported voriconazole concentrations in at least two subgroups of patients according to a distinctive factor, with a statistical comparison, or reported the impact of a distinctive factor via a pharmacokinetic model.

Data extraction

Two investigators used a predesigned data extraction form to collect the following variables: author information, publication year, study design, eligibility criteria, number of patients, number of dosages, and voriconazole concentrations among the patient subgroups. Further details were collected for ECMO and RRT.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias of the included studies via the adapted Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) quality checklists for cohort studies [30] (Suplementary Appendix 2). The checklist provides measures for assessing internal validity (selection of subjects, assessment of exposure, confounding, and statistical analysis) and overall study quality. Three items were withdrawn from the original SIGN quality checklist because of the specifics of pharmacokinetic research. One point was given for each checklist item fulfilled. A total of 12 items were retained, including 11 items quoted 0 points (if not fulfilled, not applicable, or sufficient information for assessment was not available) or 1 point (if fulfilled). The last item evaluating the risk of bias was assessed on a scale of 0–2, with a maximum of 1 point given to retrospective studies, as recommended by the SIGN. A maximum score of 13 points was given. Studies were considered to have a low risk of bias when the score was ≥ 12 points, a moderate risk of bias when the score was 8–11 points, and a high risk of bias when the score was ≤ 7 points.

Results

Search results and study characteristics

The literature search recovered 1728 citations, with 523 duplicates identified. Pfizer provided no additional relevant unpublished studies. Seventy-five studies were selected for full-text assessment (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow-chart showing the number of included studies. Abbreviation: ICU, intensive care unit

Flow-chart showing the number of included studies. Abbreviation: ICU, intensive care unit

Finally, thirty-one studies met the inclusion criteria [14, 15, 17, 31–58]. All studies were in English. All the studies were observational, but one, which was a crossover study [50]. Thirteen studies were prospective, and one study combined prospective and retrospective data. Seven studies were case reports [31, 34, 38, 42–44, 52]. The risk of bias was assessed to be moderate for 9 studies [35, 36, 40, 45, 49, 51, 55, 56, 58] and high for the remaining 22 studies.

Nine different factors of interest were identified. Patient-related factors included the SOFA score at the time of voriconazole administration, hepatic function, albumin concentration, body weight or body mass index, inflammation on the basis of the CRP or procalcitonin (PCT) concentration and genotype. Treatment-related factors included RRT, ECMO, the administration route (oral or IV), and comedication with PPIs or steroids.

The potential association between the Child–Pugh score and voriconazole concentration was investigated via two population pharmacokinetics models. The total clearance of the drug was associated with the Child‒Pugh score in both models [45, 49]. The first study included 17 patients with liver dysfunction [45]. The total clearance rate was approximately 45% lower in patients with Child‒Pugh class C score than in those with Child‒Pugh class A score. The second study, conducted by the same authors, included 26 patients with liver dysfunction [49]. Monte Carlo simulations were performed, assuming an MIC up to 0.5 mg/L. The authors recommended a reduced intravenous loading dose of 5 mg/kg/12h and a maintenance dose regimen of 100 mg/12h for Child‒Pugh class A/B patients and 50 mg/12h for Child‒Pugh class C patients.

In a study comparing 6 patients with acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) with 9 patients without liver failure (all of whom were under continuous RRT), a numerically lower mean body clearance in the ACLF group was observed (2.95 vs 4.83 L/h), although this difference was not significant [36]. The authors explained the absence of a significant impact on liver function by increased clearance via RRT in patients with liver failure. This observation could also be an artefact from the small sample size. In the study by Ruiz et al., which included 33 patients, cirrhosis was not associated with increased voriconazole concentrations according to univariate or multivariate analysis [14].

Four studies reported an impact of bilirubin concentrations on voriconazole exposure [14, 45–47] whereas 7 studies investigated this relationship but found no significant association [15, 39, 40, 49, 55, 56, 58]. Three studies found an impact of aspartate transaminase concentrations on voriconazole exposure [45, 55, 58], while 8 did not [15, 39, 40, 46, 47, 49, 56, 57]. Other liver function parameters (alanine transaminase, alkaline phosphatase, lactate dehydrogenase, prothrombin time, international normalized ratio and platelets) did not affect voriconazole exposure, except in four studies [45, 55, 56, 58].

Sequential organ failure assessment (sofa) score

Six studies evaluated the associations between the SOFA score, a surrogate of patient severity, and voriconazole exposure [15, 39, 45, 48, 56, 57] (Table 2). The multicenter retrospective study by Bienvenu et al. including 70 ICU patients found that a one-point increase in SOFA score on the day of TDM was associated with a 0.19 mg/L increase in voriconazole concentration in multivariate analyses (p = 0.025) [15]. However, the multivariate analysis included only sex, age and the use of cytochrome P450 inhibitors. The daily dose of voriconazole, which was also significantly associated with voriconazole concentration in the univariate analysis, was not included in the multivariate analysis because of collinearity. In a study including 132 patients, Ye et al. also found, through multiple linear regression analysis, that an increased SOFA score was associated with an increased Ctrough (p = 0.027) [39]. In the study by Li et al., including 125 ICU patients with TDM, the SOFA score was not associated with a supratherapeutic Ctrough according to univariate and multivariate logistic regression [48]. The factors included in the multivariate analysis were not components of the SOFA score. In those two previous studies, the authors did not indicate when the SOFA score was calculated [39, 48]. In the pharmacokinetic study by Wang et al. including 408 patients, the SOFA score on the day of TDM was not retained as a covariate of pharmacokinetic parameters in the final model. However, individual components of the SOFA score were included as covariates. Vanstraelen et al. investigated the impact of hypoalbuminemia on the unbound voriconazole fraction in patients treated with voriconazole (20 samples in 13 patients) and in plasma samples from ICU patients spiked with voriconazole (66 samples from 22 patients) [47]. Unbound and bound voriconazole fractions were separated via equilibrium dialysis. The median serum albumin concentration was 26.7 [13.8–38.7] mg/L. Voriconazole plasma protein binding (PPB) was significantly correlated with albumin concentration according to univariate analysis and a multiple linear mixed model. Voriconazole PPB decreases by 0.67% (therefore, increasing unbound and pharmacologically active voriconazole concentrations) if the plasma albumin concentration decreases by 1 g/L.

The remaining studies investigated the impact of hypoalbuminemia on total voriconazole concentrations. An increased albumin concentration was associated with a decreased Ctrough in univariate and multivariate analyses in the studies by Li et al. (OR = 1.12, 95% CI [1.02–1.23]) and Ronda et al. (β coefficient = −0.023, 95% CI [−0.046-(−0.001)]) [41, 48]. An increased albumin concentration was also associated with a decreased Ctrough according to univariate but not multivariate logistic regression analysis in the study by Ye et al. [39]. On the other hand, four studies did not find an association between albumin concentration and voriconazole concentration via univariable linear regression analysis [14, 15, 57] or multivariate analysis [58]. None of the five pharmacokinetics studies found a correlation between albumin concentration and pharmacokinetic parameters [45, 46, 49, 55, 56].

Increased TBW was associated with increased volume of distribution pharmacokinetic parameters (including peripheral and central compartment volumes and apparent volume of distribution) in two out of four pharmacokinetic studies [45, 49]. However, Wang et al. did not found an impact of TBW on pharmacokinetic parameters in a two-compartment model with first-order elimination including multiple covariates, based on 746 voriconazole concentration from 408 patients [56]. Similarly, Chen et al. failed to find an impact of TBW on pharmacokinetic parameters in their population pharmacokinetic model [46], although their model was limited to one compartment and included only one covariate. Both studies featured a narrow weight distribution, with a median weight of 65 kg and an interquartile range of 10 kg [56], and a mean of 60 kg and a standard deviation of 10 kg [46].

When studies without pharmacokinetic modelling were considered, there was no significant association between TBW and voriconazole exposure in five studies [14, 39, 40, 48, 57]; a significant impact was found according to univariate analysis but not multivariate analysis in two studies [15, 51]. However, the voriconazole dose calculation was based on TBW in most studies [14, 15, 39, 40, 51], which could compensate for the potential effect of body weight on voriconazole exposure. Moreover, in the study by Ruiz et al., the variable dose/kg was included in the multivariate model [14].

Four studies evaluated the impact of BMI on voriconazole exposure [41, 51, 56, 57]. In the study by Hoenigl et al., which included only 21 ICU patients, patients with Ctrough < 1.5 mg/L had a significantly lower BMI (p = 0.006) according to univariate analysis but not multivariate analysis [51]. Patients with a potentially toxic Ctrough > 5.5 mg/L had a significantly greater BMI (p < 0.001) according to univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis (OR = 1.14, p = 0.024). The dose calculation was based on weight, and the multivariate model did not include the voriconazole dose. In the study by Ronda et al. including 25 patients, patients received a voriconazole dose on the basis of adjusted body weight in obese patients [41]. A high BMI was associated with a low Ctrough normalised per dose and body weight in the univariate analysis but not in the multivariate analysis. The pharmacokinetic model by Wang et al. did not include BMI as a covariate of pharmacokinetic parameters [56]. The BMI distribution was narrow, with an interquartile range of 5 kg/m2. The study by Roberts et al. was limited by its small sample size [57].

Characteristics of studies evaluating the association between inflammation and the voriconazole Ctrough

CRP: C-reactive protein; PCT: procalcitonin

Ruiz et al. reported an association between a CRP concentration > 100 mg/L and supratherapeutic voriconazole concentrations via both univariate and multivariate analyses in a prospective study including 33 patients [14]. The median CRP concentrations were not provided. In a retrospective study including 50 COVID-19 patients, Gatti et al. reported a significant association between voriconazole concentrations and both CRP and procalcitonin (PCT) concentrations via receiver operating characteristic curve analysis [54]. In this study, the sensitivities for predicting a voriconazole Ctrough > 3 mg/L were 52% for CRP values > 115 mg/L and 59% for PCT values > 0.3 ng/mL. Two pharmacokinetic studies, including one conducted exclusively in patients with COVID-19, found a negative association between CRP concentrations and total clearance of the drug, indicating that increased CRP was associated with increased voriconazole concentrations [55, 56]. The strength of these studies was reinforced by the inclusion of multiple covariates in the estimation of total clearance.

Six other retrospective studies, including two pharmacokinetic studies, did not find any association between CRP (n = 4) or PCT (n = 3) concentrations and voriconazole exposure [15, 39, 41, 47, 49, 58]. However, these studies may be limited by missing data inherent to their retrospective design, with CRP and PCT concentrations absent in over half of the patients in the study by Bienvenu et al. [15], while the extent of missing data was not reported in other studies. Additionally, the timing of CRP measurements was not specified.

Genotype

Five studies assessed the impact of the CYP2C19 genotype on voriconazole exposure in ICU patients [45, 49, 53, 55]. Weigel et al. retrospectively classified 17 patients into poor metabolisers (CYP2C19*2/*2), extensive metabolisers (CYP2C19*1/*1), and ultrarapid metabolisers (CYP2C19*1/17 or *17/*17) [45]. The first three Ctrough values were < 1.7 mg/L in 15%, 33% and 77% of the patients in the poor, extensive and ultrarapid metaboliser groups, respectively. Tanaka et al. reported values of voriconazole clearance determined by noncompartmental analysis > 0.5 L/h/kg in two extensive metabolisers, whereas voriconazole clearance was < 0.31 L/h/kg in three intermediate metabolisers (CYP2C19*1/*2 or *1/*3).

Two pharmacokinetic studies did not find an impact of CYP3A4 genotype on the PK parameters of patients with liver dysfunction [45, 49]. The interpretation of the results of these studies is limited by the small sample size [45, 53], while liver dysfunction itself may reduce the influence of CYP3A4 on voriconazole metabolism.

Similarly, Wang et al. found no impact of CYP2C19 or CYP3A4 genotype on voriconazole exposure in their pharmacokinetic study including 72 patients with COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis [55]. The authors attributed this lack of association to the limited number of patients classified as poor metabolizers (n = 4) and ultra-rapid metabolizers (n = 1), and to the presumed greater influence of CRP compared to genotype on voriconazole metabolism.

A dialysis pharmacokinetic model using blood samples collected from the line upstream from the predilution and effluent from the output line to evaluate CRRT clearance (CLCRRT) in patients receiving CRRT was available in 6 studies, including 42 patients [31–36]. The median [Q1-Q3] voriconazole CLCRRT was 1.29 [1.12–1.40] mL/h, which represented approximately 15% of the total clearance of the drug. The total clearance of the drug ranged between 4 and 20 L/h. The mean area under the concentration–time curve from 0–12 h after intake (AUC12h) ranged between 17 and 65 mg.h/L. These results are similar to those reported in healthy volunteers [46] and non-ICU patients [47].

In the study by Wang et al. including 379 patients, 122 of whom were receiving CRRT, CRRT was identified as a covariate of drug clearance in the population pharmacokinetic model [56]. Clearance increased by 1.46-fold in patients receiving CRRT in the final model. When estimating pharmacokinetic parameters grouped by CRRT status, clearance in CRRT patients was approximately 17% higher than in those not receiving CRRT. The difference in the magnitude of the association is attributable to the inclusion of multiple covariates in the final model, including creatinine clearance rate. In another pharmacokinetic study involving 72 patients, including 25 on CRRT, CRRT was also retained as a covariate of drug clearance in a model that included multiple covariates[55]. Clearance increased by 1.62-fold in patients receiving CRRT in the final model.

In two studies using standard regression analyses, including a total of 278 patients of whom one third received RRT, RRT was no associated with voriconazole exposure in either univariate or multivariate analysis [48, 58].

One study and one case report including a total of 5 patients evaluated the impact of prolonged intermittent RRT, defined as 8 h on RRT, on voriconazole exposure [37, 52] (Table 6). Burkhardt et al. did not estimate CLRRT but reported pharmacokinetic parameters similar to those of healthy volunteers [37]. A high CLRRT was reported in a case report [52]. The pharmacokinetic model was based on 5 samplings performed on the same day and during the same RRT episode; CLRRT represented 94% of the total clearance of the drug.

Three studies described SBECD (intravenous voriconazole solubilization vehicle) concentrations in a total of 15 ICU patients under RRT over time [33, 37, 38]. The first study revealed an accumulation of SBECD between day 1 and day 5 in all 4 patients under extended daily dialysis [37]. A second study reported SBECD concentrations > 400 mg/L after day 7 in 3 of 4 patients under intermittent hemodialysis, with an average frequency of dialysis sessions of 24–48 h [38]. SBECD concentrations reached 600 mg/L in one patient on day 13. Another study including 10 patients under CVVH did not observe any accumulation of SBECD over time [33]. SBECD exposure was higher than that reported in previous studies of healthy volunteers, with a mean Cmax of 687 mg/L vs 458 mg/L, but SBECD clearance was similar to that reported in patients with an estimated creatinine clearance of 50 mL/min.

Characteristics of studies evaluating the association between ECMO and the voriconazole Ctrough

Two studies found no association between voriconazole exposure and ECMO status. The retrospective multicenter study by Van Daele et al. compared voriconazole Ctrough on and off ECMO in 69 patients, of whom 74% received venovenous ECMO [40]. Neither the presence of ECMO nor the squared ECMO day (to study the presence of circuit saturation) was significantly associated with voriconazole exposure (considered a categorical or continuous variable) in the univariate or multivariate analysis. The squared day of ECMO was used instead of the day of ECMO, since the relationship between the day of ECMO and voriconazole Ctrough was expected to follow a squared function with a decrease in voriconazole exposure just after ECMO initiation, and an increase after a few days of ECMO due to saturation of the circuit. Similarly, the pharmacokinetic study by Wang et al., including 393 patients of whom 108 received ECMO, found no statistical difference in pharmacokinetic parameters estimates between ECMO patients (n = 108) and those without ECMO (n = 285) [56]. ECMO status was not retained as a covariate in the final model, because of a stronger effect of other covariates. In those two studies, parameters of liver function and inflammation were included in the analysis.

Conversely, three other studies reported measurable impacts of ECMO status on voriconazole exposure. The retrospective study by Hinze et al. including 153 patients of which 52 received ECMO (the type was unspecified), ECMO use was associated with higher rate of below target Ctrough in univariate and multivariate analysis (adjusted OR = 4.6, 95% CI [1.7–12.2]). The multivariate analysis included parameters of liver function only. The retrospective monocenter study by Ye et al. compared the voriconazole Ctrough in 66 ECMO patients, of which 94% received venovenous ECMO, with that of other patients who had never received ECMO support or with that of the same patients before or after ECMO treatment (n = 66) [39]. The use of ECMO was associated with subtherapeutic Ctrough in univariate and multivariate analysis (adjusted OR = 7.8, 95% CI [1.6–35.0]). The multivariate analysis included several covariates but did not incorporate any liver function or inflammation parameter. The author found no statistically significant association between voriconazole Ctrough and ECMO blood flow rate nor days of ECMO therapy on sampling time. In the study by Ronda et al., ECMO support was also associated with a lower Ctrough in both univariate and multivariate analyses. The results of this study and others are limited by the small number of patients and the presence of confounding factors.

In the two-compartment pharmacokinetic model of Wang et al., the bioavailability (F) of voriconazole estimated at 84%. This study included 408 patients, with 68% receiving voriconazole intravenously, 11% orally, and 21% via a nasogastric tube [56]. Nonintravenous (including oral and via a feeding tube) administration was associated with a lower Ctrough in multivariate logistic regression analyses (OR = 4.8, 95% CI [1.6–14.4]) in the study by Li et al. including 125 patients [48]. Wang et al. also described significantly different Ctrough values between intravenous and oral therapy (4.1 ± 3.1 mg/L and 2.7 ± 1.8 mg/L, p < 0.05) in their study including 363 ICU patients [17]. A multivariate analysis was not performed. In the study by Hinze et al., a significant difference in voriconazole Ctrough (analysed as a three-category variable) was observed in univariate analysis (p = 0.04), also only 5% of the 153 included patients received oral voriconazole. Oral application was not included in the multivariate analysis. In all of these studies, the type of feeding was not described.

In contrast, a crossover study by Karoubi et al. did not find any impact of the route of administration on voriconazole exposure. Eight ventilated patients received intravenous voriconazole for 4 days, followed by an enteral dose increased by 1 mg/kg administered via nasogastric tube along with continuous enteral feeding. The AUC/dose ratio were not different between the two routes, and bioavailability was estimated at 106% [50]. However, the crossover design was not ideal, as it was conducted in unstable ICU patients, which may have introduced confounding factors. Two other studies, including one large study of 70 patients but only 24 receiving oral voriconazole, did not find any association between the voriconazole route of administration and subtherapeutic or potentially toxic Ctrough [15, 51]. However, the study by Hoenigl et al. included only 21 ICU patients out of 61 patients. In the study by Tanaka et al., powdered and suspended voriconazole adsorption to feeding tubes was evaluated in vitro and was estimated to be negligible; the pharmacokinetics of feeding tube-administered voriconazole determined by noncompartmental pharmacokinetic modelling in 5 patients were similar to those of usual oral administration [53].

In the study by Hoenigl et al., which included 40 patients with hematological malignancies and 21 ICU patients, a significant association between concomitant PPI use and subtherapeutic Ctrough was found via univariate and multivariate logistic regression (OR = 5.31, 95% CI [2.00–14.09]) [51]. The authors justified this surprising result by the wider use of PPIs in ICU patients than in non-ICU patients as a confounding factor. Two larger studies analysing 125 and 132 ICU patients also reported an association between concomitant PPI use and subtherapeutic Ctrough in univariate analysis but not in multivariate analysis [39, 48]. No associations were found in all five population pharmacokinetic models including a total of 585 patients [45, 46, 49, 55, 56].

No impact of concomitant steroid administration on voriconazole exposure was found in six studies including a total of 874 patients [14, 46, 48, 51, 55, 56, 58]. There was an independent association between concomitant steroid therapy and a low Ctrough in one other large study including 66 patients [39].

Discussion

This systematic review summarises current available knowledge about patient-related and treatment-related factors associated with voriconazole exposure variability in ICU patients. These data suggest that patients with hepatic dysfunction, high BMI, high SOFA score and severe inflammation are at high risk of overexposure, whereas patients with oral administration, low BMI, and ultrarapid metabolism are at high risk of underexposure (Table 10, Fig. 2). Nonetheless, the overall quality of the included studies was suboptimal, with studies that were mainly single center or retrospective, leading to important biases. Notably, the retrospective design of the included studies results in missing data and a lack of precision concerning the exact timing of Ctrough measurements. Risk factors for voriconazole pharmacokinetic variability in critically ill patients

Factors associated with overexposure

Settings associated with voriconazole overexposure and an increased risk of side effects in ICU patients include hepatic dysfunction, high BMI, severe illness with high SOFA score, or severe inflammation (Table 10).

Voriconazole is metabolised by the liver. Unsurprisingly, ICU patients with hepatic dysfunction are at increased risk of having high Ctrough values and experiencing voriconazole toxicity. Although multiple biological parameters have been associated with voriconazole concentrations, the Child–Pugh score remains a more accurate reflection of overall liver function compared ot individual markers. Importantly, it has been used in both pharmacokinetic and preclinical studies to guide dosing decisions, and should therefore guide dosing adjustements. According to the manufacturer, the FDA and the EMA, the maintenance dose should be reduced to 50% in Child‒Pugh class A and B patients (no guidance is available for Child‒Pugh class C patients). The most robust pharmacokinetic study performed in ICU patients supports a maintenance dose reduction to 100 mg/12h in Child‒Pugh class A and B patients and to 50 mg/12h in Child‒Pugh class C patients [49]. However, voriconazole may also be the cause of hepatic dysfunction with drug-induced liver injury [17]. Therefore, some authors recommend the use of alternative antifungal agents in this setting [62].

Body weight is well correlated with voriconazole exposure in ICU patients, as observed in non-ICU patients, but the impact remains important enough to be observed in ICU patients, who experience dramatic pharmacokinetic changes. According to the manufacturer, the FDA and the EMA, the voriconazole dose should be determined on the basis of a linear relationship with the total body weight (a maintenance dose of 4 mg/kg/12h) when administered intravenously. However, this dose calculation does not seem adequate, as it leads to a risk of overexposure in obese patients while potentially causing underexposure in patients with a low BMI – a risk that has not yet been clearly demonstrated [51]. The prevalence of obesity is increasing and is approximately 20% in ICU patients, and even higher in some countries; obesity may play a significant role in voriconazole overexposure [63]. Instead, some authors recommend a dose calculation of the maintenance dose on the basis of ideal body weight or adjusted body weight [64, 65], together with TDM.

The negative impact of inflammation on cytochrome P450 expression (particularly CYP2C19 and CYP3A4) and voriconazole exposure has been well described during the last decade [20]. Overexposure is frequently observed in ICU patients experiencing inflammation [14, 54]. However, the importance of this relationship has yet to be determined. Dose adaptation cannot be recommended according to the CRP or PCT concentration, especially as the inflammation status may vary significantly within a few days. Close monitoring of voriconazole concentrations is needed in patients with a previous satisfying Ctrough who develop sepsis or, on the contrary, who are recovering from sepsis.

Finally, high SOFA scores are also associated with voriconazole overexposure. Indeed, high SOFA scores are associated with both hepatic dysfunction and intense systemic inflammation, which in turn contribute to the downregulation of cytochrome P450 enzyme expression. Patients with SOFA scores ≥ 7 require close monitoring of voriconazole concentrations.

Factors associated with underexposure

The following situations are associated with a risk of voriconazole underexposure and, therefore, at risk of treatment failure: oral administration (including administration via a nasogastric tube) and low BMI (Table 10).

Oral administration is sometimes chosen for ICU patients because of the increased risk of SBECD accumulation in patients with kidney failure. However, several studies have demonstrated underexposure in ICU patients receiving nonintravenous voriconazole. ICU patients receiving voriconazole via nasogastric tube are often simultaneously on enteral nutrition, which may reduce the drug’s absorption. Given the current state of knowledge, dose adaptation cannot be recommended for ICU patients receiving oral voriconazole. Consequently, intravenous administration should be preferred whenever possible, especially the loading dose. Of note, the manufacturer recommend administrating oral vorcionazole to patients with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 50 mL/min to avoid SBECD accumulation [66]; this systematic review did not adress this specific situation, as eGFR does not appear to be a factor of voriconazole pharmacokinetic variability.

However, studies evaluating the accumulation of the intravenous formulation vehicle SBECD in patients under RRT were not conclusive, and no toxicity was observed. When accumulation was observed, SBECD concentrations corresponded to an SBECD dose administered per kg that was at least ten times lower than the lowest dose administered per kg that was associated with toxicity in dogs. Accumulation of SBECD in animals has been associated with liver and kidney toxicity as a consequence of vacuolation in rats [67]. Interestingly, SBECD toxicity has yet to be observed in humans. Given the current stage of knowledge, the risk‒benefit balance may favour intravenous administration in ICU patients undergoing RRT, especially those receiving CVVH.

RRT (both hemodiafiltration and hemofiltration) appears to have a small but not clinically significant effect on voriconazole exposure. Based on several yet well-conducted cohort studies and case reports employing dialysis pharmacokinetic modeling, voriconazole clearance by RRT represents approximately 15% of the total clearance of the drug. All the authors except one recommended no dose adjustment in patients with acute renal failure and RRT. The limited effect of RRT is consistent with voriconazole’s non-linear pharmacokinetics and predominant hepatic elimination.

Other factors of variability

The impact of ECMO status on voriconazole exposure remain unclear. In the eventuality of such an effect, the effect size would probably be small considering the extremely high variability of the voriconazole Ctrough in ICU patients. Multivariate analysis of four well-conducted retrospective studies evaluating the effect of ECMO on voriconazole Ctrough have yielded contradictory results, and none of the authors advised a systematic empirical dose adjustment upon ECMO initiation. Moreover, the studies reporting no impact of ECMO on voriconazole exposure conducted more robust analysis by including the key known factors influencing voriconazole variability.

Hypoalbuminemia occurs in approximately 40% of ICU patients, but its impact on voriconazole exposure is unclear [68]. A single study revealed that patients with hypoalbuminemia may present lower Ctrough values [48]. On the other hand, patients with hypoalbuminemia display decreased voriconazole protein binding and higher unbound voriconazole concentrations, potentially leading to toxicity [47]. Thus, hypoalbuminemia may lead to the underestimation of the voriconazole pharmacologically active fraction. The authors recommended adjusting the measured total Ctrough with albumin concentration and total bilirubin, especially in patients with severe hypoalbuminemia.

The impact of concomitant use of PPIs or steroids remains unclear. While steroids are known inducers of CYP3A4 and PPI are known inhibitors of CYP2C19, the effect of these medications on voriconazole exposure has not yet been demonstrated. Of note, the results of the study by Hoenigl et al. contradict those of multiple other studies performed outside the ICU, as well as the expected effects of CYP2C19 inhibitors.

Implications of the review findings

Five days after treatment initiation, when steady-state is presumed to be reached, a measure of voriconazole Ctrough is currently recommended for adjusting the dose. The current literature suggests that dose adjustment is necessary in patients with liver failure and extreme BMIs and that repeated dosages in high-risk patients, including patients with high SOFA scores and severe inflammation, should be performed until those factors are resolved (Fig. 2). However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the high risk of bias in the included studies. Although our review did not report any change in voriconazole exposure in patients undergoing ECMO or RRT, these patients are at high risk of encountering other risk factors and should also be monitored for voriconazole dose adjustment. The magnitude of dose adjustment should be discussed with the pharmacologist/pharmacist, as voriconazole pharmacokinetics are not linear. In the case of difficulties in achieving PK/PD targets, other azole antifungal agents, such as isavuconazole, are promising alternatives and could be considered even if more data regarding their use in the ICU are warranted [14].

Future research

Our findings support the need for larger well-designed multicenter studies on voriconazole pharmacokinetics in ICU patients. The apparent complexity of the relationship between factors of variability and voriconazole exposure necessitates the use of more complex statistical methods, such as pharmacokinetic modelling, to determine the optimal dose. However, in the present state of knowledge, no pharmacokinetic model has demonstrated high predictive performance meaning that application in a clinical setting should be done cautiously [70] due to high interindividual and intraindividual pharmacokinetic variability. Combining TDM with a pharmacokinetic model in a model-informed precision dosing framework could be helpful, with machine learning potentially providing additional support [71]. Optimal exposure may then be promoted through antifungal stewardship programs [72]. More advanced approaches, such as those discussed above, may ultimately enable voriconazole dose adjustment earlier than day 5, or even a priori, in order to increase the likelihood of achieving the pharmacokinetic target defined at day 5, beyond what is possible with standard TDM.

Conclusions

There is a need to better handle voriconazole therapy in ICU patients given the high pharmacokinetic variability of voriconazole, the associations between exposure and both efficacy and toxicity, and the poor outcomes in this high mortality rate population. Dose adaptation is required in patients with liver dysfunction and a high BMI, whereas close voriconazole Ctrough monitoring should be performed in other subgroups of patients displaying risk factors for pharmacokinetic variability. Combining pharmacokinetic modelling and TDM with a model-informed precision dosing framework may help decrease the variability of voriconazole Ctrough.
