Moser’s twist theorem revisited
Yi Liu, Lin Wang

Abstract
. Inspired by the work of Katznelson and Ornstein [ 11 ] , we present a short way to achieve the almost optimal regularity in Moser’s twist theorem. Specifically, for an integrable area-preserving twist map, the invariant circle with a given constant type frequency α \alpha persists under a small perturbation (dependent on α \alpha ) of class C 3 + ε C^{3+\varepsilon} . This result was initially established independently by Herman [ 8 ] and Rüssmann [ 18 ] in 1983. Our method differs essentially from their approaches.

1.
Introduction

The existence of invariant circles (i.e., homotopically non-trivial invariant curves) for area-preserving twist maps was first established by Moser in his seminal work
[
14
]
, commonly referred to as
Moser’s twist theorem
. This groundbreaking result is also recognized as one of the foundational contributions to the KAM (Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser) theory, where the map was initially required to be of class
C^{333}
. The regularity condition was significantly relaxed to
C^{4+\varepsilon}
by Rüssmann
[
17
]
, where
\varepsilon
denotes, once and for all, an arbitrarily small positive constant. Subsequently, Moser
[
15
, Page 53]
asserted that
C^{3+\varepsilon}
regularity suffices for the existence of invariant circles by employing more refined estimates within the KAM framework. The full proof of this assertion was independently provided by Rüssmann
[
18
]
and Herman
[
8
]
, each utilizing distinct methodologies. In
[
18
]
, Rüssmann introduced a novel iteration process that yields invariant circles which are merely continuous. In contrast, Herman’s approach in
[
8
]
leverages the Schauder fixed point theorem, building upon his celebrated work on circle diffeomorphisms (
[
7
]
). Although Herman’s method is restricted to invariant circles with constant type frequency, it achieves superior smoothness for these circles, specifically
C^{2+\varepsilon}
.

Herman
[
8
]
also constructed an example demonstrating that each invariant circle can be destroyed by a small
C^{3-\varepsilon}
perturbation. This implies that
C^{3+\varepsilon}
regularity is almost optimal. Furthermore, Herman
[
9
]
, using a result by Meyer
[
13
]
, achieved the optimal regularity result. Specifically, he showed that an invariant circle with a constant type frequency persists under small perturbations in the
C^{3}
topology.

Inspired by the work of Katznelson and Ornstein
[
11
]
, we propose a novel approach to determine the almost optimal regularity in Moser’s twist theorem. This method diverges from both Rüssmann’s and Herman’s techniques. Similar to Herman
[
8
]
, our goal is to achieve the smoothness (
C^{2}
plus the Hölder condition) for invariant circles with constant type frequency. However, this approach alone appears insufficient to reduce the regularity requirement from
C^{3+\varepsilon}
to the optimal
C^{3}
. We plan to address this limitation in future research.

Let
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
denote the flat circle. Let
|\cdot|
represent the Euclidean metric in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The flat metric on
\mathbb{T}
is defined as

(1)

\|\cdot\|:=\inf_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}|\cdot+p|.

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number, expressed in its continued fraction form as
\alpha=[a_{0},a_{1},\ldots]
. The convergents
\left\{\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\alpha
are given by
\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}=[a_{0},\ldots,a_{i}]
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
q_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. An irrational number
\alpha
is said to be of constant type if all coefficients
a_{i}
in its continued fraction are bounded. We denote this bound by
A_{\alpha}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
represent the set of constant type irrational numbers in
(0,1)
. It is well-known that
\mathcal{C}
has zero Lebesgue measure but full Hausdorff dimension.

Given
k\in[0,+\infty)\cup\{+\infty\}
, let
C^{k}(\mathbb{T})
denote the space of
C^{k}
functions on the circle
\mathbb{T}
. Given any
u\in C^{k}(\mathbb{T})
. Let us recall the
C^{k}
-norm:

\|u\|_{C^{k}}:=\max_{|r|\leq k}\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}|D^{r}u(x)|,

where
r:=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
,
|r|:=r_{1}+\cdots+r_{n}
.

We consider a special class of generating functions
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

(2)

G_{n}(x,x^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{2}(x-x^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{q_{n}^{4+\varepsilon}}V(q_{n}x^{\prime}),

where
V\in C^{4+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})
and
q_{n}
denotes the denominator of the
n
-th convergent of
\alpha
. This means that the construction of
G_{n}
depends on
\alpha
; for notational simplicity, we suppress the subscript
\alpha
in our notation. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
F_{n}:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
be the exact area-preserving twist map generated by
G_{n}
.

Theorem 1
.

For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists a sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
F_{n}
admits an invariant circle with frequency
\alpha
and the preserved invariant circle can be represented as a graph of the function of class
C^{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}}
for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
.

It is straightforward to verify that Theorem
1
remains valid if the perturbation term in
G_{n}
is replaced by
U_{n}\in C^{4+\varepsilon}(q_{n}\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
, provided
\|U_{n}\|_{C^{4+\varepsilon}}
is bounded independently of
n
. It is also noticed that
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon
is still true (
[
8
]
).

Let
T(x,y)=(x+y,y)
. By definition, for each
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
, we have

\|F_{n}-T\|_{C^{3+\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

According to
[
8
]
, any invariant circle can be destroyed by a
C^{3-\varepsilon}
perturbation. Thus, Theorem
1
is nearly optimal (up to
\varepsilon
).
The proof of Theorem
1
combines the circle diffeomorphism technique developed by Katznelson and Ornstein
[
11
]
with the action-minimizing method introduced by Mather
[
6
]
.

Moser
[
16
]
observed that every
C^{\infty}
twist diffeomorphism corresponds to the time-one map of a suitable
C^{\infty}
periodic Hamiltonian system, although the converse does not hold. To ensure the validity of the converse, the condition that there are no conjugate points must also be imposed. Consequently, a result analogous to Theorem
1
can be directly derived for Hamiltonian systems with two degrees of freedom. More precisely, let
\mathbb{T}^{2}
be the 2-dimensional flat torus. We denote by
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the set of frequency vectors
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying the constant-type Diophantine condition: there exists a constant
D>0
such that for all non-zero integer vectors
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
,

(3)

|\langle\omega,k\rangle|\geq\frac{D}{|k|}.

Consider the integrable Hamiltonian
H_{0}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

H_{0}(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2},\quad p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Corollary 1.1
.

For each
\omega\in\mathcal{D}
, there exists a sequence of perturbed Hamiltonians
\{H_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of class
C^{4+\varepsilon}
such that for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
:

(1)

\|H_{n}-H_{0}\|_{C^{4+\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
;

(2)

each
H_{n}
admits an invariant torus with frequency
\omega
;

(3)

the preserved invariant torus is a graph of class
C^{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}}
.

While Corollary
1.1
is not a new result, it has appeared in many literatures, either explicitly or implicitly (see
[
1
,
19
,
20
]
for instance). We include it here as a corollary of Theorem
1
merely to illustrate that this result can be obtained through a novel approach distinct from existing literatures. Under the consideration that an individual KAM torus with constant frequency persists, the current findings on the minimal smoothness requirement (
C^{2d}
plus the Dini condition) for the Hamiltonian function are presented in
[
1
,
20
]
. By
[
3
,
12
]
, it is also possible, through a modified KAM approach, to identify nearly integrable Hamiltonian systems that admit
d
-dimensional invariant tori with positive Lebesgue measure under
C^{2d+\varepsilon}
perturbations. Conversely, as shown in
[
4
,
21
]
, any
d
-dimensional Lagrangian invariant torus can be destroyed by a small
C^{2d-\varepsilon}
perturbation.

Strategy of the proof of Theorem
1
.
Katznelson and Ornstein
[
11
]
introduced a criterion for the existence of invariant circles (referred to as Criterion 1 in this paper), expressed in terms of certain vectors with endpoints on orbit segments (called Type-I chords here; see Definition
3.3
). However, working directly with such chords makes it difficult to achieve almost optimal result on the regularity of the perturbation for twist maps. We therefore introduce a more refined class of chords (called Type-II chords; see Definition
3.4
), whose definition imposes an additional constraint on the “difference of positions” of their endpoints on the minimal configuration.

Based on these Type-II chords, we formulate a new criterion for invariant circles (Criterion 2) and prove its equivalence to Criterion 1 (Lemma
3.6
). To connect the construction with circle diffeomorphism theory, we further reduce Criterion 2 to Criterion 3, which is a boundedness condition on
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{0}
(defined in (
17
)) related to a certain multiplicative cocycle. This reduction is carried out in Section 3.

In light of the idea to prove the Denjoy inequality for circle diffeomorphisms, Katznelson and Ornstein
[
11
]
established a corresponding result for twist maps (Proposition
4.1
), which implies the quantity
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{0}
can be controlled by a certain distortion
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{1}
(defined in (
22
)). Introducing the second and third-order difference quotients
\nabla^{1}
and
\nabla^{2}
of the minimal configuration (see definitions (
23
) and (
24
)), and under suitable hypotheses on
\nabla^{1},\nabla^{2}
(conditions (
27
) and (
28
)), one obtains exponential decay of
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{1}
(Lemma
4.3
). Boundedness of
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{0}
then follows from Proposition
4.1
.

The proof of Lemma
4.3
is most delicate part in this paper. Establishing the exponential decay of
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{1}
requires several steps. We first prove a monotonicity property for
\nabla^{1}
(Lemma
4.4
). When the orbit segment is short relative to the scale of the chord-pair involved in
\nabla^{1}
, we show exponential decay of
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{1}
(Lemma
4.6
). Using the assumption on
\nabla^{2}
, we then extend this decay to the case where the length of the orbit segment matches the scale involved in
\nabla^{1}
(Lemma
4.8
). Finally we obtain, a posteriori, a sharpened exponential decay estimate for
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{1}
(Proposition
4.9
). These arguments are contained in Section 4.

The last step consists of an induction argument showing that there exists a scale
\kappa_{0}
such that, on all scales finer than
\kappa_{0}
, the conditions on
\nabla^{1}
and
\nabla^{2}
((
27
) and (
28
)) are automatically satisfied (Lemma
5.1
). The initial scale
\kappa_{0}
depends in a precise way on the size of the perturbation of the system (Lemma
3.7
). This is presented in Section 5, together with a basic estimate for the vertical-coordinate difference between consecutive points of the orbit (Lemma
2.2
) given in Section 2. To deduce the final result from the induction hypothesis, we need to control the growth of
\nabla^{1}
in relation to
\nabla^{2}
(Lemma
5.4
) as the scale of the chord increases, and then combine this with the refined estimate for
\nabla^{2}
(see (
63
)) obtained by Katznelson and Ornstein (
[
11
, Corollary 3.1]
) to complete the proof of Theorem
1
.

Roughly speaking, the main idea to prove Theorem
1
is to prove that an Aubry-Mather set which “looks like” an invariant circle must indeed be an invariant circle. The precise meaning of “looks like” is encoded by the second and third-order difference quotients
\nabla^{1}
and
\nabla^{2}
of the minimal configuration. Through a bootstrap-type procedure, such an Aubry-Mather set cannot contain any “gaps” and therefore necessarily satisfies Criterion 1.

2.
The minimal configuration with frequency
\alpha

Let us recall some basic notions as preparations. Let
\tilde{F}
be a diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
denoted by
\tilde{F}(x,y)=(X(x,y),Y(x,y))
. Let
\tilde{F}
satisfy:

•

Lift condition:

\tilde{F}
is isotopic to the identity;

•

Twist condition:
the map
\psi:(x,y)\mapsto(x,X(x,y))
is a diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
;

•

Exact symplectic:
there exists a real valued function
H
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
H(x+1,X+1)=H(x,X)
such that

YdX-ydx=dH.

Then
\tilde{F}
induces a map on the cylinder denoted by
f
:
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
.
f
is called an exact
area-preserving twist map. The function
H
:
\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
a generating function of
\tilde{F}
, namely
\tilde{F}

is generated by the following equations

\begin{cases}y=-\partial_{1}H(x,X),\\
Y=\partial_{2}H(x,X),\end{cases}

where
\tilde{F}(x,y)=(X,Y)
.

The function
\tilde{F}
gives rise to a dynamical
system whose orbits are given by the images of points of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

under the successive iterates of
\tilde{F}
. The orbit of the point

(x_{0},y_{0})
is the bi-infinite sequence

\{...,(x_{-k},y_{-k}),...,(x_{-1},y_{-1}),(x_{0},y_{0}),(x_{1},y_{1}),...,(x_{k},y_{k}),...\},

where
(x_{k},y_{k})=\tilde{F}(x_{k-1},y_{k-1})
. The sequence

(...,x_{-k},...,x_{-1},x_{0},x_{1},...,x_{k},...)

denoted by
{\bf{x}}:=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is called a
stationary configuration associated to
\tilde{F}
if it satisfies the discrete Euler-Lagrange equation

\partial_{1}H(x_{i},x_{i+1})+\partial_{2}H(x_{i-1},x_{i})=0,\ \text{for\ every\ }i\in\mathbb{Z}.

Given a sequence of points
(z_{i},...,z_{j})
, we can associate its
action

W(z_{i},...,z_{j})=\sum_{i\leq s<j}H(z_{s},z_{s+1}).

A configuration
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}

is called minimal if for any
i<j\in\mathbb{Z}
, the segment

(x_{i},...,x_{j})
minimizes
W(z_{i},...,z_{j})
among all segments

(z_{i},...,z_{j})
of the configuration satisfying
z_{i}=x_{i}
and

z_{j}=x_{j}
. Then every minimal configuration is a
stationary configuration. For every minimal configuration
\mathbb{x}=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, the limit

\rho(\mathbb{x})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{x_{i+n}-x_{i}}{n}

exists and it is independent of
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
\rho(\mathbb{x})
is called
the frequency of
\mathbb{x}
. We recall the orientation-preserving property as follows (see
[
10
, Proposition 11.2.4]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

If
\rho(\mathbb{x})
is irrational, then for
n_{1},n_{2},m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
,

n_{1}\rho(\mathbb{x})+m_{1}<n_{2}\rho(\mathbb{x})+m_{2}

if and only if

x_{n_{1}}+m_{1}<x_{n_{2}}+m_{2}.

For simplicity, we use, once and for all,
u\lesssim v
(resp.

u\gtrsim v
) to denote
u\leq Cv
(resp.
u\geq Cv
), and
u\sim v
to denote
\frac{1}{C}v\leq u\leq Cv
for some
positive constant
C
.

Based on the construction of
G_{n}
, for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

x_{i+1}-x_{i}=y_{i},\quad y_{i+1}-y_{i}=\frac{1}{q_{n}^{3+\varepsilon}}V^{\prime}(q_{n}(x_{i}+y_{i})).

It is clear to see

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a minimal configuration with rotation number
\alpha
.
Then

|y_{i+1}-y_{i}|\lesssim\frac{1}{q_{n}^{3+\varepsilon}},\quad\forall\ i\in\mathbb{Z}.

3.
Criterions on the existence of the invariant circle

Let
F:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
be an exact area-preserving twist map defined on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
. Take an irrational number
\alpha
. It is known (see
[
2
,
6
]
) that there exists an Aubry–Mather set
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}\subset\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{T}
be the standard projection. Briefly, the projected set
\pi\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
onto
\mathbb{T}
consists of all minimal configurations with rotation number
\alpha
. For simplicity, we do not distinguish here between a minimal configuration on
\mathbb{T}
and its lift to
\mathbb{R}
. Denote by
H:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
the generating function of the lift
\tilde{F}
.
More precisely, let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a minimal configuration generated by
H
. Define

y_{0}:=-\partial_{1}H(x_{0},x_{1}).

Let

\mathcal{O}:=\{F^{i}(x_{0},y_{0})\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}},

where
\mathcal{O}
is a complete orbit passing through
(x_{0},y_{0})
.
If the rotation number
\rho(\mathbf{x})
is irrational, and we set
\alpha=\rho(\mathbf{x})
, then
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}
can be expressed as

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}=\overline{\mathcal{O}},

where
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
denotes the closure of
\mathcal{O}
in the topology induced by the Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
.

The mapping
x_{i}\mapsto i\alpha
is orientation-preserving from
\mathcal{O}
to the circle
\mathbb{T}
. A vector
v
is called a chord if its endpoints lie on some orbit segment of
\mathcal{O}
. Denote by
\lambda_{1}(v)
the length of the arc between the corresponding multiples of
\alpha
on
\mathbb{T}
. Concretely, if the chord
v
connects
z_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
and
z_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j})
, then

\lambda_{1}(v)=\|(j-i)\alpha\|,

where
\|\cdot\|
is defined by (
1
). It is clear that
\lambda
is invariant under
F
. For clarity,
\lambda_{1}(v)
is called the
\lambda
-length of
v
on
\mathcal{O}
.
Under the same assumption that the chord
v
connects
z_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
and
z_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j})
, we also write

\theta(v):=x_{j}-x_{i},\qquad\Theta_{1}(v):=\|\theta(v)\|.

The quantity
\Theta_{1}(v)
is called the
\vartheta
-length of
v
on
\mathcal{O}
.

Remark 3.1
.

By the definitions of the
\lambda
-length and the
\vartheta
-length of
v
on
\mathcal{O}
, for two chords
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
on
\mathcal{O}
, even if their
\lambda
-distances coincide, their
\vartheta
-lengths may still differ.
In
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}
we fix, once and for all, an arbitrary complete orbit
\mathcal{O}
.
All subsequent discussions about a chord
v
refer to the vectors that arise from some orbit segment whose endpoints belong to this chosen complete orbit
\mathcal{O}
.

Based on the orientation-preserving property (see Proposition
2.1
),
\lambda_{1}(v)
and
\Theta_{1}(v)
can be related with each other in the sense of average. Recall
q_{n}
denotes the denominator of the
n
-th convergent of
\alpha
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
v
be a chord. If
\lambda_{1}(v)\geq\frac{2}{q_{N}}
for
N
large enough, then

\left|\frac{1}{q_{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{q_{N}-1}\Theta_{1}(F^{j}(v))-\lambda_{1}(v)\right|\leq\frac{1}{q_{N}}.

3.1.
\kappa
-chords

Since
\alpha
is irrational, then
\{\|m\alpha\|\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
is dense in
\mathbb{T}
. It means that for each
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
m:=m(\kappa)
such that
\|m\alpha\|\in[\frac{1}{2^{\kappa+4}},\frac{1}{2^{\kappa}}]
.
Given
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, one can find
\kappa_{0}:=\kappa_{0}(n_{0})
such that

(4)

\|q_{n_{0}}\alpha\|\in\left[\frac{1}{2^{\kappa_{0}+4}},\frac{1}{2^{\kappa_{0}}}\right].

In fact, by Dirichlet’s approximation,

(5)

\frac{1}{(a_{n+1}+2)q_{n}}<\|q_{n}\alpha\|<\frac{1}{a_{n+1}q_{n}}.

We choose

(6)

\kappa_{0}=\mathrm{log}_{2}a_{n_{0}+1}+\mathrm{log}_{2}q_{n_{0}}.

Then we get

\frac{1}{2^{\kappa_{0}+4}}\leq\frac{1}{(a_{n_{0}+1}+2)q_{n_{0}}}<\frac{1}{a_{n_{0}+1}q_{n_{0}}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{\kappa_{0}}}.

Define
\varphi(m):=\mathrm{log}_{2}a_{m+1}+\mathrm{log}_{2}q_{m}
. Note that
q_{m+1}=a_{m+1}q_{m}+q_{m-1}
. A direct calculation shows

(7)

0<\varphi(m+1)-\varphi(m)\leq\mathrm{log}_{2}A_{\alpha}+1.

We also define

(8)

\phi(m):=[\varphi(m)].

We use, once and for all,
[c]
to denote the largest integer not greater than
c
for
c\in\mathbb{R}
. It is clear to see that
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is nondecreasing. In general,
\phi
may not be surjective. Meanwhile,
\phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
may not be strict increasing. Consequently,
\phi^{-1}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
may not be well-defined. Denote
S:=\phi(\mathbb{N})
. We introduce
\psi
as a substitution of
\phi^{-1}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
. For each
\kappa\notin S
, there exists a maximal
\hat{\kappa}\in S
satisfying
\hat{\kappa}<\kappa
. Note that

(9)

\phi(m+3)>\phi(m).

We have

\kappa-2\leq\hat{\kappa}<\kappa.

Denote

\check{\kappa}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip-3.99994pt\kappa,\quad\kappa\in S,\\
\hskip-3.99994pt\hat{\kappa},\quad\kappa\notin S.\end{array}\right.

The function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined as

(10)

\psi(\kappa):=\min\{m\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ \phi(m)=\check{\kappa}\}.

It follows that
\kappa\to+\infty
if and only if
\psi(\kappa)\to+\infty
. In the following, we define two types of
\kappa
-chords. The first one was introduced by
[
11
]
.

Definition 3.3

(Type-I)
.

We call
v
a Type-I
\kappa
-chord, denoted by
v\in(\kappa)_{\text{I}}
, if
\lambda_{1}(v)\in[\frac{1}{2^{\kappa+4}},\frac{1}{2^{\kappa}}]
.

To achieve the almost optimal regularity in Theorem
1
, we need another kind of
\kappa
-chord.

Definition 3.4

(Type-II)
.

Assume
v
connects
(x_{i},y_{i})
and
(x_{j},y_{j})
. We call
v
a Type-II
\kappa
-chord, denoted by
v\in(\kappa)_{II}
, if
v\in(\kappa)_{I}
and

{\frac{1}{(A_{\alpha}+1)^{6}}q_{n_{\kappa}}\leq|i-j|\leq(A_{\alpha}+1)^{6}q_{n_{\kappa}},\quad\lambda_{1}(v)\in[\|q_{n_{\kappa}}\alpha\|,2^{4}\|q_{n_{\kappa}}\alpha\|]}

where
n_{\kappa}:=\psi(\kappa)
.

For the convergents
\{\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\alpha
, there holds for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(11)

q_{n+2}>2q_{n},\quad(A_{\alpha}+1)^{n}>q_{n}>(\sqrt{2})^{n}.

Given

3.2.
Criteria

We consider the following quantities

\Lambda_{\iota}(\kappa):=\max\left\{\sup_{v\in(\kappa)_{\iota}}\frac{\lambda_{1}(v)}{\Theta_{1}(v)},\sup_{v\in(\kappa)_{\iota}}\frac{\Theta_{1}(v)}{\lambda_{1}(v)}\right\},\quad\iota\in\{I,II\}.

Roughly speaking,
\Lambda_{I}(\kappa)
can be viewed as an analog of the derivative of the conjugate function in the theory of circle diffeomorphisms. Based on this analogy, we derive a criterion for the existence of an invariant circle with a prescribed frequency. As demonstrated by Katznelson and Ornstein

, for a given

generated by

admits an invariant circle with rotation number

•

Criterion 1:
The sequence
\{\Lambda_{I}(\kappa)\}_{\kappa\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.

Obviously, we only need to consider the case with
\kappa
large enough. For
\kappa
large enough, if

, then we have

and

\frac{\lambda_{1}(v)}{\Theta_{1}(v)}
(resp.
\frac{\Theta_{1}(v)}{\lambda_{1}(v)}

\frac{\lambda_{1}(v^{\prime})}{\Theta_{1}(v^{\prime})}

\frac{\lambda_{1}(v^{\prime\prime})}{\Theta_{1}(v^{\prime\prime})}

\frac{\Theta_{1}(v^{\prime})}{\lambda_{1}(v^{\prime})}
and

is nondecreasing (up to a subsequence). It suffices to verify the boundedness of

. Moreover, one can show that

.

For each
v\in(\kappa)_{I}
with
\kappa\in S
, there exist

Fix
