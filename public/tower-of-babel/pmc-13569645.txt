Utility of Combining FIESTA‐C and MENSA for Facial Nerve Identification in Parotid Gland Surgery
Authors: Kento Yumita, Takuya Mikoshiba, Takeyuki Kono, Mariko Sekimizu, Ryoto Nagai, Hirokazu Fujiwara, Hiroyuki Ozawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveFacial nerve palsy is a major complication of parotid surgery. Several reports have demonstrated that the intraparotid facial nerve can be directly visualized using MRI. This study aimed to evaluate the role of fast imaging employing steady‐state acquisition cycled phases (FIESTA‐C) and multi‐echo steady‐state acquisition (MENSA) in facial nerve identification and tumor localization.MethodsThirty‐six patients with parotid tumors were analyzed. We evaluated whether the facial nerve could be identified and assessed tumor localization. The diagnostic accuracies of FIESTA‐C, MENSA, and their combinations were compared.ResultsThe main trunk of the facial nerve was identified in all patients, and its bifurcation into the temporofacial and cervicofacial divisions was visualized in 81% using FIESTA‐C, 81% using MENSA, and 94% using their combinations. Tumor localization was correctly identified in 75%, 67%, and 83% using FIESTA‐C, MENSA, and their combinations, respectively.ConclusionCombination of FIESTA‐C and MENSA could improve the accuracy of facial nerve identification and tumor localization compared to either sequence alone.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Postoperative facial nerve palsy is one of the most significant complications of parotid gland surgery. Large‐scale studies on parotid gland surgery indicate that the incidence of postoperative facial nerve palsy in benign parotid gland tumors ranges from 9.8% to 19.8% [1, 2, 3, 4], with tumor localization being a significant risk factor. The rate of postoperative facial nerve palsy is 10.5% for superficial lobe tumors, which are exterior to the facial nerve, compared to 22.8 to 38.5% for deep lobe tumors [1, 2, 4], with a significantly higher risk associated with the deep lobe [1, 2, 4]. Therefore, it is essential to predict the spatial relationship between the tumor and facial nerve to assess the risk of postoperative facial nerve palsy.

Various landmarks have been used for tumor localization, such as the retromandibular vein (RV) and facial nerve line (FNL). The diagnostic accuracies using these methods ranged from 62.5% to 91.2% for the RV [5, 6, 7, 8, 9], and 64% to 90% for the FNL [5, 6, 7, 9]. However, there is wide variability in diagnostic accuracy because these methods indirectly estimate the facial nerve route, and the indicators are affected by anatomical variations [8, 9].

Recently, several reports have demonstrated that the intraparotid facial nerve could be directly visualized using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14], including fast imaging employing steady‐state acquisition (FIESTA) [10], constructive interference in steady‐state sequence (CISS) [11, 12, 13], balanced fast‐field‐echo [14], multi‐echo in steady‐state acquisition (MENSA) [15], and three‐dimensional (3D) double‐echo steady‐state (DESS) with water‐excitation [6, 9, 12, 13]. However, few studies have directly compared these sequences [12], and their respective strengths and limitations in detecting the facial nerves have not been sufficiently assessed.

In this study, we used both FIESTA cycled phases (FIESTA‐C) and MENSA to visualize tumors and facial nerves in patients who underwent parotid gland surgery. Using image analysis software, we generated 3D models for comparison with the surgical findings, thereby assessing the diagnostic utility of each sequence individually and in combination.

Materials and Methods

We retrospectively reviewed 44 consecutive patients who underwent parotidectomy for parotid gland tumors at the Department of Otolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery, Keio University School of Medicine between November 2021 and September 2025, and underwent MRI before surgery, including FIESTA‐C and MENSA sequences. Four patients in whom the main trunk of the facial nerve was not identified during surgery, two patients in whom the facial nerve was resected due to malignant tumors, and two patients who were not evaluable because of motion artifacts were excluded, leaving 36 patients for the analysis. No patient had preoperative facial nerve palsy, neoplastic salivary disease, or chronic inflammation. All tumors were resected under general anesthesia to confirm the actual tumor location, whether superficial or deep. No patients experienced postoperative facial nerve palsy.

All patients underwent a 3 T MRI scans (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The parameters of FIESTA‐C were as follows: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 6.1/2.9 ms, flip angle (FA) = 45°, matrix = 240 × 340, field of view (FOV) = 180–200 × 199–221 mm, slice thickness = 1 mm, and acquisition time = 230–250 s. The parameters of MENSA were as follows: TR/TE1/TE2 = 16.3/6.1/10.6 ms, FA = 45°, matrix = 260 × 260, FOV = 180 × 180 mm, slice thickness = 1.4 mm. Both FIESTA‐C and MENSA sequences were obtained in the coronal plane.

The facial nerve was visualized as a linear structure with low signal intensity relative to the parotid parenchyma on FIESTA‐C (Figure 1A) and high signal intensity on MENSA (Figure 1B). First, the facial nerve was detected at the stylomastoid foramen, proceeded anteriorly and inferiorly into the parotid gland, and traced distally. Only the segments that could be continuously and clearly followed were assessed. DICOM datasets were exported from medical records and processed using the open‐source software Slicer (version 5.6.2; https://www.slicer.org). Axial, coronal, sagittal, and 3D views were displayed simultaneously, and the tumors and facial nerves were manually marked slice‐by‐slice using different colors. Each structure was then reconstructed, and the models were superimposed on a semitransparent MRI of the head and neck to approximate the surgical field (Figure 2). The time required for 3D reconstruction was initially ~60 min per case but decreased to 20–30 min as experience accumulated.

After these procedures, we evaluated whether the main trunk, temporofacial division, cervicofacial division, and peripheral branches of the facial nerves could be visualized. Peripheral branches were defined as the temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, and cervical branches. If at least one of these branches was identifiable, we defined the peripheral branch as visualized. We then assessed whether the tumor was located in the superficial or deep lobes based on its relationship with the facial nerve. When tumors extended into both lobes, the predominant site was recorded as the localization site. If localization could not be determined, it was classified as “indeterminate.” Furthermore, the diagnostic accuracies of the FIESTA‐C and MENSA sequences in predicting tumor localization were evaluated by comparing MRI‐based and surgical findings. Similar to the MRI‐based evaluation, tumor localization was classified based on the surgical findings: tumors located superficial to the facial nerve were categorized as superficial lobe, those located deep to the nerve as deep lobe. Tumors extending across both lobes were categorized by predominant localization. The MRI‐based evaluation was considered correct when it was concordant with the surgical findings; cases with discordant findings or those deemed indeterminate on MRI were considered incorrect. For cases in which the localization was incorrect, we examined the reasons for our inability to evaluate. We also assessed the diagnostic accuracy of a combination of the two imaging sequences. Specifically, if tumor localization was correctly identified in one sequence, while the other sequence yielded an indeterminate result, the case was considered correct for the combined assessment. Two authors (K.Y. and T.M.) independently evaluated the MRI images using this method while blinded to the surgical findings. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus after discussion. Interobserver agreement for tumor localization was assessed using Cohen's kappa.

For comparison, tumor localization was assessed using the RV and FNL methods. According to the RV method, tumors located lateral to the RV were classified as superficial, whereas those located medially were classified as deep. The FNL was defined as a line connecting the lateral margin of the posterior belly of the digastric muscle to the lateral surface of the mandibular ramus [5, 7]. The center of the tumor lateral to these landmarks was classified as superficial, and the center medial to the landmarks was classified as deep [5, 7]. T1‐ and T2‐weighted images were used for these evaluations.

Results

The patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the patients was 55 ± 12 years. About 16 patients were male and 20 were female. Histology revealed pleomorphic adenoma in 27 patients, Warthin tumor in four, basal cell adenoma in two, oncocytoma in one, basal cell adenocarcinoma in one, and mucoepidermoid carcinoma in one. Tumors were located in the superficial lobe in 26 patients and in the deep lobe in 10 patients. The main trunk of the facial nerve was successfully visualized in all patients using both the FIESTA‐C and MENSA. Bifurcations into the temporofacial and cervicofacial divisions were identified in 29 patients (81%) with the FIESTA‐C and in 29 patients (81%) with the MENSA. Peripheral branches were depicted in nine patients (25%) with the FIESTA‐C and in nine patients (25%) with the MENSA (Table 2).

When the two sequences were combined, the main trunk was visualized in all patients, the temporofacial division and cervicofacial division of the facial nerve were visualized in 34 patients (94%), and the tumor localization was correct in 30 patients (83%) (Tables 2 and 3).

On the other hand, using the RV method, correct localization was achieved in 23 patients (64%). Among the incorrect cases, seven patients (19%) were misclassified as being in the opposite lobe, and in six patients (16%), the localization was indeterminate. Using the FNL method, correct localization was achieved in 25 patients (69%). Among the incorrect cases, eight patients (22%) were misclassified as being in the opposite lobe, and in three patients (8%), the localization was indeterminate.

When analyzing the causes of indeterminate tumor localization using the FIESTA‐C and MENSA, the following results were obtained: With FIESTA‐C, two of the nine indeterminate patients, the RV coursed directly beneath the facial nerve. As both the facial nerve and vessels appeared as low‐signal structures on FIESTA‐C (Figure 3A), differentiation between the two was impossible, thereby precluding accurate nerve tracing and tumor localization. The other seven patients had peripherally located tumors. In these cases, the facial nerve gradually became thinner during distal tracing, which made visualization difficult and prevented assessment of the spatial relationship between the tumor and the facial nerve. In a subset of these patients, the nerve was difficult to distinguish from adjacent small vessels during tracing, precluding clear evaluation of nerve continuity. With MENSA, it was difficult to distinguish the facial nerve from the parotid parenchyma in six of the 12 indeterminate cases. On the MENSA images, the signal intensity of the parotid parenchyma resembled that of the facial nerve (Figure 3B). Consequently, the facial nerve could not be clearly differentiated from the surrounding parenchyma during distal tracing. The remaining six indeterminate cases involved a peripherally located tumor. Meanwhile, on FIESTA‐C images, the facial nerve became thinner during distal tracing, hindering visualization and preventing the determination of its relationship with the tumor.

Discussion

This study assessed the diagnostic utility of the FIESTA‐C and MENSA sequences for the direct visualization of the intraparotid facial nerve and preoperative localization of parotid gland tumors. Our results demonstrated that combining FIESTA‐C and MENSA in a complementary manner provided more precise identification of the facial nerve and more accurate tumor localization than either sequence alone.

In systematic reviews, the identification rates on MRI were 99.8% for the main trunk, 90.4% for the temporofacial division, and 96.3% for the cervicofacial division of the facial nerve [16]. The accuracy of tumor localization in previous studies ranged from 83.9% to 100% [5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 12, 17]. In the present study, the identification rates of the facial nerve and the accuracy of tumor localization were lower than previously reported. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, we applied stricter criteria for nerve identification, requiring continuous and clear visualization across all slices [6, 9, 12]. Contrastingly, some previous studies have accepted partial discontinuity in facial nerve depictions [6, 9]. In addition, accurate localization of peripherally located tumors was challenging in the present study. Our study included a wide variety of parotid gland tumors, and the distribution of tumor locations may have differed from that in previous reports, potentially contributing to the observed discrepancy.

Our results suggested that direct visualization of the facial nerve using MRI was more useful for tumor localization than indirect methods, including those that use the RV or FNL. A previous meta‐analysis reported that the diagnostic accuracy of tumor localization using direct visualization of the facial nerve was higher than that using indirect methods [18]. Our results using the FIESTA‐C were consistent with those of a previous study. Additionally, misclassification of superficial lobe tumors as deep lobe tumors or vice versa occurred in 19% and 22% of patients when the RV method and the FNL method were used, respectively. However, no such misclassification was observed with direct visualization. Thus, these results suggested that direct visualization of the facial nerve provided greater accuracy and clinical utility than indirect visualization.

Combining the FIESTA‐C and MENSA sequences in a complementary manner can improve the accuracy of facial nerve identification and tumor localization. Direct visualization of the facial nerve is useful; however, the use of either sequence alone has inherent limitations. On FIESTA‐C, differentiation can be difficult when the nerve runs adjacent to the vessels, as both appear as low‐signal linear structures [19]. Contrastingly, MENSA suppresses fat and vascular signals and depicts facial nerves as a high‐signal structure [15, 20]. Distinguishing the facial nerve from the parotid parenchyma becomes challenging when the parotid parenchyma exhibits heterogeneous hyperintensity. In this study, since no patients were misclassified as having superficial lobe tumors being deep or vice versa with either sequence, combining both allowed visualization of the main trunk and the temporofacial and cervicofacial divisions of the facial nerve in 94% of patients, resulting in a correct localization rate of 83%. These findings indicate that both FIESTA‐C and MENSA should be performed for optimal preoperative evaluation of tumor localization.

Visualization of the spatial relationship between the tumor and the facial nerve using a 3D model could be valuable for surgeons in preoperative simulations. Parotid gland surgery requires delicate maneuvers, including dissection around the tumor and the facial nerve, which makes obtaining an accurate understanding of their spatial relationships essential. In this study, we created a 3D model by superimposing images of the tumor, facial nerve, and entire head and neck region to simulate the actual surgical position. Slicer is a free software that allows the easy construction of 3D models; therefore, it can be implemented without additional software costs. Moreover, sequences comparable to FIESTA‐C and MENSA are available across different MRI platforms, such as CISS and 3D DESS [16]. FIESTA‐C and comparable sequences are widely established in the field of neuroimaging for the assessment of cranial nerves [11]. In contrast, MENSA and comparable sequences are commonly used in orthopedics for articular cartilage evaluation [21]. Therefore, these sequences are widely available in clinical practice. Regarding field strength, although many previous reports have been performed at 3 T, feasibility at 1.5 T has also been demonstrated [17]. Furthermore, the MRI acquisition can be performed using the imaging parameters consistent with previous studies and our protocol. Accordingly, incorporating FIESTA‐C and MENSA into the routine MRI protocol before parotid gland surgery is feasible in daily practice and may be broadly applicable to other centers. Previous reports have suggested that preoperative simulation using 3D models may reduce the prevalence of postoperative facial nerve palsy and shorten operative time [13]. In our study, none developed postoperative facial nerve palsy. On the other hand, the sample size was insufficient to evaluate clinical outcomes, and the study was not powered to determine whether this approach contributes to reducing postoperative facial nerve palsy. The accumulation of additional patients in future studies will enable further evaluation of the clinical utility of this method.

This study has some limitations. First, the number of patients was small. Second, image analysis and 3D reconstruction were performed by only two authors (K.Y. and T.M.), limiting the assessment of inter‐observer reproducibility. Future multicenter studies and the incorporation of artificial intelligence‐based segmentation may help validate and generalize the utility of these methods.

Conclusion

We visualized the anatomical relationship between the parotid tumor and the facial nerve before surgery and compared it with the surgical findings using FIESTA‐C and MENSA. When the two sequences were combined, the main trunk and temporofacial and cervicofacial divisions of the facial nerve were observed in 94% of patients. The diagnostic accuracy of the tumor location was 81%, which was superior to that of either FIESTA‐C or MENSA. Our results suggest that preoperative evaluation using both sequences can improve the accuracy of tumor localization.
