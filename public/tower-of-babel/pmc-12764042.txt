Necessity of daily IGRT? Dosimetric study of beam position offsets at central double-beam junction in pediatric craniospinal irradiation
Authors: Bo Gao, Zaojie Yao, Lizhen Wu, Kongfeng Shao, Xijin Lin, Qiandong Liang, Xizhen Huang, Haiyan Chen, Zhanquan Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCraniospinal irradiation (CSI) is an essential treatment modality for pediatric patients with tumors at high risk of leptomeningeal spread. However, accurate reproduction of the original simulation posture is difficult in subsequent CSI sessions as expansion of the target volume cannot fully compensate for variations, potentially resulting in dose uncertainties caused by the beam junction.PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of offsets in beam positions on dose uncertainty at the central double-beam junction during CSI radiotherapy.MethodsTo compute the dose distribution in different positional offsets, the relative positions of the beams and CT images of the brain and spine beam centers were adjusted according to the original treatment plan of individual patients. Four parameters were calculated to assess the quality of the radiotherapy plan: the conformity index (CI), homogeneity index (HI), percentage of 1-V95%, and percentage of V110% at the planning target volume (PTV) at the beam junction.ResultsThe quality parameters (CI, HI, 1-V95%, V110%) were observed to deteriorate with increasing positional offsets. Overall, higher absolute values of beam center offset were associated with poorer dose distributions at the beam junction. Movement along the craniospinal axis toward the brain was defined as positive, while movement toward the spine was defined as negative. Shifting of the brain beams by + 3 mm and the spine beams by -3 mm resulted in an average CI value of 0.50, compared to 0.76 in the non-offset scenario, while the average HI was 0.33 versus 0.11 and the average 1-V95% value was 41.05% compared to 0.52% in the reference plan. When the brain beams were shifted by -3 mm and the spine beams by + 3 mm, the average V110% was 62.75%, compared to 2.01% without offset. Considering all beam offset scenarios, the worst-case doses to organs at risk were a maximum dose 7.78 Gy to the lens and average doses of 10.01, 8.58, 8.46, and 5.57 Gy to the parotid gland, thyroid gland, heart, and kidneys, respectively, while the lung V5 was 47%.ConclusionGreater discrepancies in beam positioning during CSI radiotherapy result in poorer dose distributions at the beam junction. Therefore, opposing shifts of the brain and spinal fields can lead to significant underdosing or overdosing at the junction. Therefore, daily image-guided radiation therapy is recommended for CSI.Keywords: Craniospinal irradiation, Beam junction, Double-beams center radiotherapy

Introduction

Craniospinal irradiation (CSI) is an essential treatment modality for pediatric patients with tumors with high risk of leptomeningeal spread [1–3]. The field size of linear accelerators is generally shorter than the length of the CSI target volume, necessitating seamless integration of multiple beams to cover the entire treatment area. This study used a TrueBeam accelerator, with a maximum field size of 40 cm [4–6]. CSI radiotherapy involves the piecing together of multiple beams to achieve the treatment goal [7]. In terms of double-beam or triple-beam centers, the double-beam approach is more commonly used. During multi-beam stitching, both centers contribute to the dose in the beam junction area, thereby effectively delivering the prescribed dose [7, 8].

In radiotherapy, challenges such as patients’ difficulty in maintaining the exact simulation position and differences in therapists’ expertise can lead to positional deviations [9, 10]. To address the problem of underdosing caused by setup deviations, conventional radiotherapy typically includes expansion of the margin around the target volume (e.g., 5 mm) [11–16]. This expansion mitigates underdosing at the periphery of the target in standard single-center treatments; however, for double or multiple-field center treatments, where overlapping fields are situated within the target volume, simple expansion of the target not sufficient to address dose discrepancies in these internal regions.

Deviations between the position of the patient during treatment and that of the initial CT can have adverse effects on the dose distribution at the beam junction; these are particularly evident along the cranial-caudal axis, which can cause underdosing or overdosing at these junctions [17]. While 3D conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) [18] is traditionally used for CSI, it is highly dependent on precise positioning due to its susceptibility to dose non-uniformity at the beam junction. Therefore, many institutions have started using intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) for CSI [19, 20], which provide enhanced stability against beam positioning offsets compared to 3D-CRT. Despite these advances, current techniques are limited in addressing positional uncertainty. Thus, recent research is now focusing on optimizing the planning of dose gradients at the beam junction along the cranial-caudal axis. This involves ensuring that the doses from the beam junction create a linear ramp, with the beams from the two centers complementing each other [21, 22].

The introduction of the feathering algorithm in version 15 + of the Eclipse (VARIAN, Siemens Healthineers) treatment planning system has simplified achievement of a smooth linear dose gradient [23], thereby improving dose robustness at the beam junction. However, few studies have addressed the issue of whether the feathering algorithm can effectively compensate for dose instabilities at the beam junction caused by actual variations in patient positioning during treatment. Moreover, there have been few studies on dose delivery at the beam junction using the feathering algorithm. This study is based on real patient treatment plans incorporating the feathering algorithm. Beam positioning offsets were intentionally introduced to evaluate the effects on dose variations at the beam junction. The findings provide empirical support for future research and practical guidance for clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Patient characteristics

This study included six patients who underwent CSI using dual-beam center radiotherapy techniques at the radiotherapy department of our institution between September 2023 and September 2024 (Table 1). The inclusion criteria were patients who underwent treatment in a supine position, use of the feathering algorithm in all treatment plans, patients who completed the entire radiotherapy course and for whom full treatment data were available, and the use of dual-beam centers in the treatment plans.

With the adoption of inverse modulation techniques, more patients are undergoing CSI in the supine position, making this approach more typical and representative. The feathering algorithm helps mitigate dose inconsistencies at the beam junction, a challenge that has not received extensive attention to date. The requirement that patients must have completed their treatment ensured the completeness and relevance of the included data. The focus on dual-field centers aligns with common practices used in pediatric CSI, addressing the specific needs of this demographic. These 24 cases, with 10 employing IMRT and 14 utilizing VMAT, provide a balanced representation for analysis.

Treatment planning

ll radiotherapy plans were designed and validated by two radiation physicists using the Eclipse treatment planning system (version 15.6). All radiotherapy plans were calculated using the conventional AAA algorithm, with 6 MV photon beams from a TrueBeam linear accelerator. For each patient, both IMRT and VMAT techniques were employed in the development of treatment plans. The final plan was selected based on its effectiveness in covering the target volume and minimizing the dose to critical organs.

The dual-field center approach divides the plan into cranial and spinal centers, with dose superposition occurring at a specific point in the cervical spine. Adjustments were made by shifting the field center point by a predetermined distance in one direction, while keeping other field parameters constant. This adjustment enabled direct calculation of dose variations within the treatment planning system, simplifying the evaluation of dose discrepancies caused by beam positioning offsets. The study focused on changes in the dose at the beam junction in response to variations in positioning offset. The positioning offset involved six variables: lateral (lat), longitudinal (lng), vertical (vrt), pitch, yaw, and roll. However, among these variables, the effect of the lng positioning offset predominates in causing changes in dose at the beam junction. Consequently, this study focused on the lng positioning offset to investigate its impact. For each positioning offset, radiotherapy doses were computed at 1 mm intervals, ranging from − 3 mm to 3 mm. The ± 3-mm systematic offset was derived from the daily IGRT offset values of the 24 patients. As shown in Fig. 2, the maximum positional deviation across all treatment fractions did not exceed 3 mm, with an average deviation within 1 mm. For each patient, based on the original treatment plan, artificial offsets were introduced to the center points of both the brain and spine beam fields along the craniospinal axis. To simplify the computation and facilitate result interpretation, only two types of offset scenarios were considered: simultaneous displacement of both beam centers in the same direction, and relative displacement between the two centers (including both convergent and divergent movements). The offset step was set to 1 mm, with a total displacement distance of 3 mm, and the absolute values of the offsets were equal for both beam centers. Displacement along the craniospinal axis toward the brain was defined as positive, while displacement toward the spine was defined as negative. For example, (−1, + 1) indicates that the brain beam center was shifted − 1 mm (caudally), while the spine beam center was shifted + 1 mm (cranially). The tested offset combinations included: (−3, −3), (−2, −2), (−1, −1), (+ 3, + 3), (+ 2, + 2), (+ 1, + 1), (−3, + 3), (−2, + 2), (−1, + 1), (+ 3, −3), (+ 2, −2), (+ 1, −1), as well as the original non-offset scenario (0, 0). Each patient thus had 13 distinct radiotherapy plans, resulting in a total of 312 plans for the 24 patients.

Plan evaluation

All patients receiving CSI underwent a radiotherapy regimen with localized dose escalation. For instance, the entire brain and spinal cord received a standard dose of 36 Gy, while the targeted intracranial region was given an escalated dose of 54 Gy. During analysis of radiotherapy plans, parameters such as the homogeneity index (HI) and hotspot were determined relative to the prescribed dose. However, the use of either 36–54 Gy as a reference did not yield objective results, primarily due to degradation of the dose at the beam junction. Thus, all parameters used for evaluation were focused specifically on the dose at the beam junction (Fig. 3), indicating the contributions from the spinal and brain fields and their cumulative dose at the beam junction.

The dose distribution at the center of the bi-directional field. From left to right, the images indicate the dose contributed by the spine section beams, the dose contributed by the brain section beams, and the total dose resulting from the overlap of the two, respectively

The evaluation of dose deviation caused by positioning offsets included analysis of the dose cold and hot spots, CI, and HI. The dose cold spot is defined as the area of the target receiving < 95% of the prescribed dose, while the dose hot spot represents the area receiving > 110% of the prescribed dose, with both expressed as percentages. The CI index represents the alignment of the three-dimensional shape of the prescribed dose with the shape of the target area, aiming for a value as close to 1 as possible, with 1 indicating perfect conformity. The HI was calculated using the method recommended in ICRU Report 83. This index quantifies the dose homogeneity within the target volume, with values closer to 0 indicating greater uniformity. An HI value of 0 represents a perfectly uniform dose distribution.

Linear dose fall-off at beam junction

All radiotherapy plans utilized the feathering algorithm for dose calculations. The dose from the central beams at the beam junction indicated the presence of a linear gradient, with beams from each center complementing one another (Fig. 4A1-A3). As can be observed from the three Fig. 4B1-B3, in the absence of feathering algorithms, the total dose in the junction region fluctuated more dramatically with changes in the relative positions of the brain and spinal beams. This was because the conventional algorithm has no control over the proportion of dose contributed by each beam field at the beam junction, resulting in a higher degree of randomness. Consequently, the total dose becomes more sensitive to positional offsets.

Results

Target volume CI in the beam junction

Figure 5 illustrates the variations in conformity index (CI) values at the beam junctions with displacement of the beam centers in the 24 patients. A CI value closer to 1 indicates higher conformity to the dose, indicating greater alignment between the three-dimensional 95% prescribed dose distribution and the target volume. The scenarios associated with beam displacement included simultaneous cranial displacement of both the brain and spine beam centers along the craniospinal axis (positive offset values), simultaneous caudal displacement (negative offset values), convergent displacement (negative offset of the brain beam center and positive offset of the spine beam center), and divergent displacement (as opposed to convergent displacement in offset signs). The CI values ranged from 0.9 to 0.35. Overall, greater absolute displacement of the beam center was associated with lower CI values. CI reduction was less pronounced when both beam centers were shifted in the same direction, compared that observed in shifts in opposite directions. Notably, under divergent displacement, where beam centers move away from one another, the CI values declined most rapidly, leading to a significant deterioration in dose conformity. The original plan had a mean CI of 0.76, with a range from 0.67 to 0.90. When the beam centers were offset at (+ 3, − 3), the average CI declined to approximately 0.50, varying between 0.37 and 0.61. For same-direction shifts, the lowest average CI occurred at the coordinate (–3, − 3), with a mean value of approximately 0.68 and a range of 0.58 to 0.80.

HI in the beam junction

Figure 6 shows variations in homogeneity index (HI) values at the beam junctions of 24 patients in response to beam center displacement. A lower HI value, approaching zero, indicates better dose uniformity, reflecting a closer match between the actual dose distribution and the prescribed dose across the beam junction target area. The HI values of the patients ranged from 0.09 to 0.45. Overall, HI increased to a greater extent when the beam centers were shifted in opposite directions compared to their movement in the same direction. Notably, under divergent displacement, where the brain beam center moved cranially and the spine beam center moved caudally, the HI was observed to increase significantly, resulting in a rapid deterioration in dose uniformity. The original treatment plan had a mean HI of 0.11, with a range of 0.09 to 0.16. In Fig. 6B, at positional offsets of (+ 3, − 3), the average HI value was 0.33, varying between 0.26 and 0.45. In contrast, Fig. 6A shows that the worst HI values occurred with the coordinate (+ 3, + 3) when shifts were in the same direction, with a mean HI of 0.24 and a range of 0.17 to 0.34.

1-V95% in the overlapping beam junction

Figure 7 shows variations in 1-V95% values at the beam junction in response to beam displacement among the 24 patients. This metric reflects the percentage of the target volume receiving less than 95% of the prescribed dose at the beam junction, with higher values indicating more extensive underdosed regions. The 1-V95% values of the patients ranged from 0.1% to 54.6%. Overall, greater beam center displacement was correlated with a more pronounced increase in 1-V95%, indicating an expansion of dose-deficient areas. Notably, increases in 1-V95% were more marked when the brain and spine beam centers shifted in different directions. In Fig. 7, when the positional offsets were (+ 3, − 3), the average 1-V95% value was 41.05% with a range from 32% to 54.6%. In contrast, under other beam center offset scenarios, the maximum 1-V95% value remained below 11.7%.

V110% in the overlapping beam junction

Figure 8 shows variations in V110% values at the beam junction in response to beam center displacement among the 24 patients. This parameter indicates the percentage of the target volume that receives more than 110% of the prescribed dose at the beam junction, with higher values reflecting greater overdosage. The V110% values of the patients ranged from 0% to 76%. Notably, when the brain and spine beam centers shifted in different directions, the V110% values decreased. In contrast, when the beam centers moved toward one another, the V110% values increased significantly. In Fig. 8, under the positional offset condition of (–3, + 3), the average V110% value was 62.75%, ranging from 47.6% to 76%. In comparison, under other beam center offset scenarios, the maximum V110% value remained below 21%.

The Dmax to the lens varied between 4.82 Gy and 7.78 Gy. The baseline average Dmean to the parotid glands was 8.8 Gy. Discussion

For CSI, as the length of the PTV exceeds the coverage of a single field, it is necessary to set two or more field centers. This inevitably leads to an issue of dose robustness at the beam junction. Specifically, in the case of field center shifts, the dose at the beam junction will be reduced, potentially leading to adverse clinical effects as insufficient CSI doses are associated with an increased risk of tumor recurrence [24]. Conversely, excessive doses may induce radiation myelopathy and elevate the risk of hematological toxicity [25].

To enhance the dose robustness of CSI radiotherapy, particularly at the beam junction, Matthew et al. [19] investigated the advantages of replacing 3D-CRT with IMRT/VMAT. It was found that IMRT significantly outperformed traditional 3D-CRT in terms of both target dose coverage and dose homogeneity. B Sarkar et al. [26] also compared the dose stability at the beam junction between VMAT and 3D-CRT, associated with longitudinal shifts in the brain and spine beams, showing that VMAT was more stable. The development of IMRT/VMAT in CSI radiotherapy has led to a focus on dose optimization at the beam junction. Qilin Li et al. [20] investigated the impact of different field arrangement angles on the dose at the beam junction. To improve dose robustness at the beam junction when using IMRT/VMAT, especially in situations where the patient shifts in position between treatments, both Strojnik et al. [21] and Manthala Padannayil Noufal et al. [27] proposed a controlled dose gradient-optimization method. In this method, at the beam junction, the dose of the brain and spine beams change as linearly as possible along the longitudinal axis of the body. Advances in CSI radiotherapy are also associated with the gradual maturation of dose feathering at the beam junction, and treatment-planning systems after VARIAN’s Eclipse version 15 + have introduced feathering algorithms.

In this study, the setup errors in CSI radiotherapy, based on daily image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) for the 24 enrolled patients showed that the maximum error did not exceed 3 mm, and the mean and median errors were both within 1 mm. Similarly, Becksfort et al. [28] investigated 165 pediatric patients, finding that under cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) guidance, the beam center shifts were within 2 mm. When using planar X-rays for 3D-2D image registration, the errors were within 3 mm. Additionally, the 3-mm systematic error used in this study already demonstrated significant dose deterioration at the beam junction. Particularly when the brain and spine beams shifted in opposite directions, the dose distribution became worse, potentially even falling below clinically acceptable levels. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that larger offset values will lead to greater dose deterioration.

In Fig. 5, the CI index values of the patient indicated greater deterioration in the radiotherapy dose when the centers of the brain and spine beams moved in opposite directions along the craniospinal axis, compared to situations when both centers shifted in the same direction. This can be explained by the production of larger underdosed or overdosed regions following opposite-direction beam shifts, thereby compromising the conformity and coverage of the dose distribution within the target volume. This phenomenon is supported by Figs. 7 and 8. Specifically, Fig. 7 shows that when the brain and spine beam centers moved away from one another along the craniospinal axis, the 1-V95% volume (indicating underdose) at the beam junction increased rapidly, reflecting a sharp decline in the total dose received in that region. Conversely, as shown in Fig. 8, when the beam centers shifted toward one another, the total dose at the beam junction increased significantly, resulting in a rapid rise in the V110% volume (indicating overdosage). These observations corroborate those illustrated in Fig. 6, indicating that the uniformity of the target dose deteriorates more rapidly in response to opposite-direction beam shifts. Therefore, it is strongly recommended that, in the absence of daily image-guided corrections to the setup during CSI treatment, care should be taken to avoid positioning errors that result in opposite-direction beam shifts along the craniospinal axis.

Figure 10 presents variations in the doses received by different at-risk organs commonly monitored in pediatric CSI. The institutional dose constraints for these organs are as follows: maximum lens dose (Dmax) < 9 Gy, mean parotid dose (Dmean) < 26 Gy, mean thyroid dose (Dmean) < 10 Gy, mean heart dose (Dmean) < 10 Gy, mean kidney dose (Dmean) < 10.5 Gy, lung V5 < 68%, and lung V20 < 28%. According to the simulation results of this study, the maximum lens dose was 7.78 Gy, while the maximum average doses to the parotid gland, thyroid, heart, and kidneys were 10.01, 8.58, 8.46, and 5.57 Gy, respectively. All these values fall within clinically acceptable limits. In this study, only lung V5 was analyzed, as pediatric CSI typically results in lung V20 values that are well below the constraint. The maximum lung V5 value observed in this study was 47%, which is considered safe. Moreover, analysis of the doses received by organs at risk focused only on scenarios where the brain and spine beam centers moved in opposite directions along the craniospinal axis, as these represent the most critical and clinically relevant worst-case scenarios.

In clinical practice, offsets in beam positioning may occur randomly across treatment fractions. For instance, one fraction may involve beam shifts toward the cranial and caudal directions, leading to underdosage at the beam junction, while the subsequent fraction may involve opposite shifts, causing overdosage in the same region. Consequently, in terms of cumulative dose distributions, the actual dose at the beam junction may not be as severe as depicted in this study. However, this scenario was not simulated here, as the study was designed to represent worst-case conditions and had already fulfilled its research objectives. Further investigations may be warranted in the future. Positioning errors involve six degrees of freedom: lateral, longitudinal, vertical, pitch, yaw, and roll. A limitation of this study is that rotational errors (pitch, yaw, and roll) were not analyzed, primarily due to current technical limitations in the accurate rotation of CT images to specified angular positions.

The protocol for CSI radiotherapy includes the acquisition of CBCT images during each of the first five treatment sessions and weekly thereafter, a protocol that is widely used by numerous institutions [29, 30]. However, this study is the first to systematically demonstrate the instability of the dose at the beam junction in CSI radiotherapy. Clinical institutions should consider whether to incorporate CSI into daily IGRT based on their own patients’ daily IGRT offset values.

Conclusion

In CSI, multi-field center treatment plans can result in significant dose degradation at the beam junction due to offsets in patient position during actual treatment. These offsets can lead to excessive dose delivery or failure to achieve the prescribed dose, both of which are detrimental to patient outcomes. When the brain beam center shifts in a positive direction (i.e., toward the brain along the craniospinal axis) and the spine beam center shifts in a negative direction (i.e., toward the spine), significant underdosing can occur at the beam junction. This can increase the risk of tumor recurrence. In contrast, when the brain beam center shifts in a negative direction and the spine beam center shifts in a positive direction, overdosing can occur at the beam junction, which can also be harmful to the patient. Therefore, it is recommended to use three-field or multicenter plans only when necessary due to the increased risk of dose inaccuracies.

In summary, multi-field center treatment plans in CSI can result in significant dose degradation at the beam junction due to offsets in patient position during actual treatment. These offsets can lead to either excessive dosage or failure to achieve the prescribed dose, both of which are detrimental to patient outcomes. It is recommended that daily IGRT be performed when using a conventional linear accelerator for CSI. This practice would minimize the likelihood of dose variations at the beam junction caused by positioning offsets.
