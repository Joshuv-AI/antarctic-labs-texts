Findings of a patent omphalomesenteric duct in children with umbilical cord hernia showing different prognosis
Authors: Alvin Santoso Kalim, Aditya Rifqi Fauzi, Raffi Gani, Ekawaty Lutfia Haksari, Akhmad Makhmudi, Gunadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUmbilical cord hernia (UCH) is a rare midline abdominal defect, with the incidence of 1:5,000 live birth. It might be accompanied by other congenital anomalies, including patent omphalomesenteric duct (OMD). Here, we reported two pediatric cases of UCH with the findings of patent OMD and different prognosis.Case presentationWe described two cases UCH with patent OMD: the first case, UCH with the incidental findings of patent OMD during the defect closure; while the second case, UCH with the prolapse of patent OMD. Both cases underwent exploratory laparotomy, patent OMD resection, and primary ileal anastomosis. The first patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged on postoperative day 21. The second patient developed respiratory acidosis and subsequently died on postoperative day 7.ConclusionsThe patent OMD might present in children with UCH with different prognosis. Our findings suggest the attending pediatric surgeon should look for the presence of other congenital malformation associated with abdominal wall defect. These might affect the prognosis of patients with UCH.

Introduction

Umbilical cord hernia (UCH) is a rare midline abdominal defect. It is not completely understood, and often misdiagnosed as small omphalocele. UCH is a rare midline abdominal defect, with the incidence of 1:5,000 live birth. It might be accompanied by other congenital anomalies, including patent omphalomesenteric duct (OMD) [1, 2].

Physiological herniation of a larger section of the intestines into the proximal part of the umbilical cord, known as the extracoelomic cavity, occurs during early fetal development. The intestines migrate into the abdominal cavity at 10–12 weeks of pregnancy, the umbilical ring generally closes, and the extracoelomic cavity vanishes, leaving Wharton’s jelly and umbilical vessels in the cord. The umbilical ring can fail to close in some cases, allowing portions of the intestines to remain in the extracoelomic cavity. UCH is the initial symptom of this disease [3]. Here, we reported two pediatric cases of UCH with the findings of patent OMD and different prognosis. While both patients underwent similar surgical management, they demonstrated markedly different postoperative outcomes. These cases highlight the variable clinical presentation and prognosis of this rare anomaly and emphasize the importance of careful perioperative evaluation and management.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 24 h-old male neonate weighing 3650 g was admitted to our emergency unit with chief complaint of abdominal organs protrusion through a defect in the umbilicus. This baby was delivered via caesarean section at term. Physical examination revealed prolapsing abdominal organs viscera (small bowels) covered with amniotic membrane with a diameter of 2 cm and height of 6 cm (Fig. 1). There was no evidence of clinical bowel passage disturbances and no bilious vomiting or reflux before surgery.

From the laboratory examination, remarkable results include elevated haemoglobin of 22.2 g/dl, elevated white blood cell 22,750/mm3 (neutrophil 73.8%, lymphocytes 16.3%), and elevated serum potassium 5.66 mmol/litre. Babygram was performed and showed small intestine dilatation and omphalocele (Fig. 2). Then, we decided to perform defect closure laparotomy and found a patent omphalomesenteric duct, 15 cm from ileocecal junction with a length of 10 cm connecting ileum to the umbilical cord. Ileo-ileal anastomosis was performed after the resection of patent omphalomesenteric duct, umbilical reconstruction was attempted when closing the operation wound (Fig. 3). Post operatively, third generation cephalosporin antibiotics were administered with analgesics of Paracetamol. The patient was managed in the neonatal intensive care unit for routine monitoring and supportive care. Mechanical ventilatory support was required for the duration of 10 days. No complications nor leakage was found. The patient was discharged at post-operative day (POD) 21 uneventfully.

Case 2

A 24-hour-old male neonate was born from a 37-year-old mother, with a gestational age of 35 weeks and 2410-gram birthweight, delivered via Section caesarian due to fetal distress. It was known that the first meconium was excreted before 24 h. At birth, an abdominal wall anomaly showed a protrusion of the intra-abdominal organs (small intestines with parts of the colon) in the Wharton jelly-covered accompanied by prolapse of the proximal and distal parts of the intestinal mucosa. The meconium was excreted from the prolapsed intestine (Fig. 4). There was no evidence of bowel passage disturbance and no bilious vomiting or feeding intolerance was found.

The size of the defect was 5.5 cm high and 2.8 cm in diameter. The laboratory examination showed elevated leucocyte of 17.6 × 103/µL (neutrophils 70%, lymphocytes 21%), slightly lower albumin 3.17 g/dL, and slightly lower serum sodium 133 mmol/L. When admitted, the baby’s glucose level was 40 mg/dL; after receiving dextrose 10% 2 cc/Kg, the glucose level reached normal values. We planned to close the defect by laparotomy and found the prolapsed organ was a patent omphalomesenteric duct (POMD). The prolapsed POMD was 10 cm proximal from the ileocaecal junction in the distance. It was found that the distal part of the prolapsed POMD looked collapsed, and the appendix was attached to the Wharton jelly. The POMD was then resected end-to-end ileal anastomosis with the appendix preserved. Furthermore, no intestinal rotation disorder was observed, and the intestine was normally fixed, afterward umbilical reconstruction was attempted. Histopathological evaluation of the resected tissue was not available; therefore, the presence of heterotopic mucosa could not be assessed. Preoperatively and postoperatively, broad spectrum antibiotics and Metronidazole were administered along with analgesia of Paracetamol. The patient was managed postoperatively in the neonatal intensive care unit and required mechanical ventilatory support due to respiratory compromise. The patient unfortunately died at POD 7 due to a respiratory acidosis considered to be related to sepsis and neonatal respiratory distress.

Discussion

UCH is the most uncommon form of ventral abdominal wall anomaly and can be misdiagnosed as a small omphalocele because both defects are covered by a membrane and the umbilical cord appears to arise from the sac. However, UCH is considered a distinct entity with different embryologic basis and clinical associations, and accurate recognition is important for counseling and early management [4].

Embryologically, the anterior abdominal wall is formed by the fusion of the cranial, caudal, and paired lateral folds during the early weeks of gestation. The OMD, which connects the midgut to the yolk sac, normally involutes by the end of the seventh week; meanwhile, physiologic midgut herniation occurs at approximately 5–6 weeks and returns to the abdominal cavity by 10–12 weeks of gestation. Failure of complete return of the midgut into the abdomen with persistence of herniation at the base of the umbilical cord results in UCH [5].

From a practical standpoint, differentiating UCH from omphalocele is critical because it influences expectations for associated anomalies, delivery-room handling, and operative strategy. UCH typically presents with a small fascial defect at an intact umbilical ring with herniated bowel contained within Wharton’s jelly at the base of a normally inserted cord, whereas omphalocele generally involves a larger abdominal wall defect and broader syndromic associations. Careful inspection of cord insertion, membrane characteristics, and the relationship between the umbilical vessels and the defect—supported by targeted imaging when needed—may reduce misclassification and guide appropriate surgical planning [6, 7].

Patent OMD may coexist with UCH across a spectrum ranging from incidental intraoperative findings to overt mucosal prolapse with meconium discharge through the defect. The latter presentation may accelerate fluid and heat loss, increase bacterial contamination risk, and predispose to ischemia or necrosis of the prolapsed segment—factors that can contribute to heterogeneous postoperative trajectories reported in the literature [3, 8, 9]. In our cases, both patients underwent resection of the patent OMD and primary ileal anastomosis; nevertheless, the markedly different outcomes suggest that overall physiologic reserve and perioperative stability (e.g., prematurity and respiratory compromise) may be decisive contributors to prognosis.

Previous reports have described variable presentations of patent omphalomesenteric duct associated with umbilical cord hernia, ranging from incidental intraoperative findings to bowel prolapse with meconium discharge. Similar to the cases reported by Mirza et al. and Raicevic et al., our second patient demonstrated prolapsed intestinal mucosa through the umbilical defect. However, unlike most previously reported patients who survived after surgical correction, our second patient experienced postoperative respiratory deterioration and mortality, possibly reflecting the contribution of prematurity and reduced physiological reserve to postoperative outcome. These findings support previous observations that prognosis may vary considerably depending on associated clinical conditions and perioperative stability [3, 8].

In addition to patent OMD, other associated intestinal anomalies have been reported in UCH, including cloacal anomaly, Meckel’s diverticulum, and intestinal atresia. Therefore, a systematic intraoperative survey of the gastrointestinal tract is warranted once the defect is explored, particularly when the clinical presentation suggests additional pathology [10]. Finally, prenatal diagnosis and delivery-room vigilance remain essential to prevent iatrogenic bowel injury from inadvertent umbilical cord clamping in unrecognized cases, and to facilitate timely referral to pediatric surgical care [11].

Conclusions

Patent omphalomesenteric duct may be associated with umbilical cord hernia and may present with variable clinical severity and postoperative outcomes. Careful evaluation of associated intestinal anomalies and perioperative clinical status is important in the management of these patients.
