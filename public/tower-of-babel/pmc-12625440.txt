Postoperative wound microbiota in diabetic patients with perianal abscess: a 16s rRNA gene sequencing study
Authors: Fanyu Meng, Ting Yu, Minning Xie, Ming Chen, Xinghua Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerianal abscesses are common in colorectal surgery, requiring surgical drainage and wound care. Diabetic patients often experience delayed wound healing and increased infection risk due to systemic factors and altered microbiota. This study investigated the postoperative wound microbiota in diabetic patients with perianal abscesses using 16s rRNA sequencing, aiming to identify potential microbial contributions to wound healing outcomes.ResultsThe study compared wound microbiota between 22 diabetic patients and 22 non-diabetic patients undergoing perianal abscess surgery. Diabetic patients exhibited significantly lower microbial diversity and richness compared to non-diabetic patients. Specific bacterial taxa were enriched in the diabetic group, including Enterobacteriaceae, Streptococcus, and Lactobacillales, while beneficial bacteria like Bacteroides and Ruminococcaceae were reduced. Functional prediction analysis revealed significant differences in metabolic pathways, with diabetic wounds showing increased carbohydrate metabolism and chitin biosynthesis, and decreased energy metabolism and endocrine system function.ConclusionsThis study highlights the distinct wound microbiota characteristics of diabetic patients with perianal abscesses, suggesting potential microbial contributions to delayed wound healing. The findings underscore the importance of considering the wound microbiome in managing postoperative wound care for diabetic patients. Strategies to modulate the wound microbiota, such as probiotics or antibiotics, may be beneficial in improving healing outcomes. Further research is needed to understand the specific mechanisms driving the observed microbial dysbiosis and its impact on wound healing in diabetic patients.Keywords: Perianal abscess, Diabetes, 16 s rRNA sequencing, Microbiota, Wound healing

Background

Perianal abscesses (PA) are a common occurrence in colorectal surgery, characterized by infections in the tissues surrounding the anus that lead to pus accumulation. Surgical drainage is typically the primary treatment for PA, followed by meticulous wound care and dressing changes to facilitate healing and prevent recurrence. The composition of the microbial community within these surgical wounds plays a critical role in determining healing outcomes, influencing both the speed of recovery and the likelihood of complications [1, 2].

Diabetes mellitus, a chronic metabolic disease, profoundly affects wound healing through multiple molecular and cellular mechanisms. Hyperglycemia impairs neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytosis, reduces macrophage-mediated clearance of pathogens, and disrupts the balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines [3–5]. In addition, microvascular complications of diabetes lead to tissue hypoxia and impaired angiogenesis, limiting nutrient and oxygen delivery essential for wound repair. Fibroblast dysfunction and impaired collagen synthesis further compromise extracellular matrix remodeling [6]. These systemic effects create a pro-inflammatory, hypoxic, and metabolically imbalanced wound environment that delays healing.

Importantly, these pathophysiological changes also influence the wound microbiome. Chronic hyperglycemia and immune dysregulation can favor colonization by opportunistic pathogens while reducing colonization by beneficial commensals [7]. For instance, studies have shown that diabetic wounds exhibit reduced microbial diversity and increased abundance of pathogenic taxa such as Enterobacteriaceae and Streptococcus, whereas beneficial bacteria such as Bacteroides and Ruminococcaceae are often depleted [8, 9]. Dysbiosis in the wound microbiota can exacerbate local inflammation, impair epithelialization, and increase the risk of chronic infection, thereby linking host metabolic dysfunction with microbial imbalance [10].

Individuals with diabetes also experience higher incidence rates of PA, recurrence, and fistula formation compared to the general population, potentially leading to chronic non-healing wounds [11, 12]. Therefore, understanding the interplay between diabetes-associated host factors and the wound microbiota is essential for optimizing postoperative wound management in this patient population. Recent advancements in molecular techniques, particularly 16 S rRNA sequencing, have enabled comprehensive analyses of microbial communities in surgical wounds. This study utilizes 16 S rRNA gene sequencing to compare the postoperative wound microbiota between a diabetic perianal abscess group (DPAG) and a non-diabetic perianal abscess group (NDAG), with the aim of elucidating the microbial and host-related mechanisms contributing to impaired wound healing in diabetic patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a study conducted at the Department of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Jinshan Hospital, Fudan University, from October 2022 to October 2023. A total of 44 patients diagnosed with perianal abscess were enrolled and divided into two groups: the diabetic perianal abscess group (DPAG, n = 22) and the non-diabetic perianal abscess group (NDAG, n = 22). All patients underwent radical resection of perianal abscess, with fistulectomy performed simultaneously if a fistula was identified intraoperatively. None of the patients received antibiotics, immunosuppressive agents, or topical antiseptics perioperatively. Wound exudate samples were collected on postoperative day 3, as secretion volumes on the first two days were insufficient for reliable analysis. Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, smoking status, and wound healing time, were recorded (Table 1).

Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis of diabetes according to World Health Organization standards and PA diagnosis according to the “Chinese Expert Consensus on Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment of Perianal Abscess” [13]. (2) Patients aged between 18 and 75 years. (3) Written informed consent obtained from all participants.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Presence of other serious conditions, such as malignant tumors, inflammatory bowel disease, or hematological disorders. (2) Presence of perianal skin diseases. (3) Postpartum women.

Surgical and postoperative management

All patients underwent radical resection of perianal abscess in a single stage under spinal anesthesia. If an anal fistula was identified intraoperatively, a fistulectomy was performed simultaneously. Hemostasis was achieved with standard techniques, and wounds were left open for secondary healing.

Postoperatively, patients received routine wound care consisting of daily dressing changes with sterile saline irrigation and application of sterile gauze. No antibiotics, immunosuppressive agents, or topical antiseptics were administered perioperatively. Pain management was provided according to institutional guidelines.

Wound tissue fluid was collected on the third postoperative day. On day 3, wound secretion was consistently adequate for sampling across all patients. After rinsing the wound with sterile saline, a sterile cotton swab was rotated for 5 s within a 1 cm² area at the wound center, with moderate pressure applied to collect sufficient fluid. Samples were immediately frozen at − 80 °C until analysis.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from the wound tissue fluid samples using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide/sodium dodecyl sulfate (CTAB/SDS) method, as previously described by Wilson and Doyle & Doyle with minor modifications [14, 15]. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the DNA was diluted to a working concentration of 1 ng/µL with sterile water.

For amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene, the V4–V5 hypervariable region was targeted using primers 515 F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 907R (5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3′), consistent with protocols reported in Caporaso et al. [16]. The PCR mixture and cycling conditions followed the manufacturer’s instructions for Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) with the following settings: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

Minor modifications included adjusting the annealing temperature to 50 °C (instead of the commonly used 55 °C) to optimize amplification efficiency for wound samples with low microbial biomass. PCR products were visualized on a 2% agarose gel, purified with the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific), and pooled in equimolar concentrations for sequencing. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (paired-end 250/300 bp).

To ensure data reliability, negative extraction and PCR controls were included during DNA extraction and amplification to monitor potential contamination. All procedures were performed in a sterile environment to minimize exogenous DNA introduction. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform, yielding an average of 58,000 raw paired-end reads per sample. After quality filtering (removal of ambiguous bases, reads with average Phred score < 20, and chimeric sequences), an average of 52,300 high-quality reads per sample were retained, corresponding to an average effective rate of 90.2%. The average sequence length after merging was 420 bp, and the proportion of bases with Q30 scores exceeded 94% across all samples.

To account for differences in sequencing depth between samples, all datasets were rarefied to 50,000 reads per sample prior to alpha and beta diversity analyses. This normalization ensured comparability of microbial community metrics across samples.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw paired-end reads were first quality filtered and merged using FLASH v1.2.7 [17]. Reads containing ambiguous bases, adapter contamination, or an average Phred quality score < 20 over a 50 bp sliding window were removed using QIIME v1.9.1 [18]. After quality control, chimeric sequences were identified and eliminated using UCHIME in de novo mode [19].

High-quality sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity using the UPARSE algorithm implemented in USEARCH v7.0 [20]. A representative sequence from each OTU was selected and assigned taxonomy with the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) Classifier v2.2 [21] against the SILVA 16 S rRNA gene reference database (release 138) [22]. Sequences with confidence scores < 0.7 were classified as “unassigned.”

Alpha-diversity indices (ACE, Chao1, Shannon, Simpson) and beta-diversity metrics (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity) were calculated. Ordination analyses, including principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), were conducted with the R package vegan v2.5–7. To assess the differences in microbial community structure, PERMANOVA (Adonis test) with 999 permutations was performed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices.

The “in-house Perl scripts” were designed as wrappers to automate several preprocessing tasks, including: (1) standardizing sequence formats, (2) rarefying samples to the same sequencing depth, (3) calculating alpha-diversity metrics, and (4) generating OTU tables and Bray–Curtis distance matrices for downstream R-based analyses. These scripts primarily ensured consistent reproducibility across samples and reduced manual errors in handling large datasets.

Functional prediction of microbial communities was performed using both Tax4Fun [23] and PICRUSt2 v2.3.0-b [24]. For Tax4Fun, normalized OTU tables were mapped to KEGG Orthology groups (KEGG release 99.1), and for PICRUSt2, the default hidden-state prediction model was applied to predict metagenome content. Functional pathways were annotated at KEGG Level 2, and relative abundance profiles were compared between groups.

Statistical analysis

Differences in taxonomic abundance between the DPAG and NDAG groups were analyzed using Metastats [25]. Metastats was selected because it performs nonparametric t-tests with permutation-based correction, making it suitable for small-sample microbiome datasets with high variability and sparsity. Although newer approaches such as metagenomeSeq [26] are available, Metastats was chosen for its robustness in handling uneven sequencing depth and its widespread use in microbial ecology.

LEfSe [27] was employed to identify bacterial taxa with significantly different relative abundances between groups. LEfSe is particularly appropriate when the number of features (species or OTUs) greatly exceeds the number of samples, as it integrates nonparametric testing with linear discriminant analysis to provide both statistical significance and effect size estimation.

To assess the differences in microbial community structure, we applied PERMANOVA (Adonis test) implemented in the R package vegan v2.5–7, using 999 permutations and Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices.

Results

Sequencing data quality

A total of approximately 2.55 million raw reads were generated from 44 wound samples. After stringent quality control, including trimming, filtering, and chimera removal, 2.30 million high-quality reads were retained, with an average of 52,300 reads per sample (range: 48,500–55,600). The mean read length after merging was 420 bp, and more than 94% of bases achieved a Q30 score, indicating high sequencing accuracy. Negative extraction and PCR controls yielded negligible reads, suggesting minimal contamination. To account for differences in sequencing depth between samples, all datasets were rarefied to 50,000 reads per sample before alpha and beta diversity analyses.

Patient demographics

Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 40.1 ± 13.0 years in the diabetic perianal abscess group (DPAG) and 43.5 ± 12.5 years in the non-diabetic perianal abscess group (NDAG), with no statistically significant difference between groups (P > 0.05). The sex distribution was comparable (19 males/3 females in DPAG vs. 20 males/2 females in NDAG, P > 0.05). Smoking prevalence was 22.7% (5/22) in DPAG and 36.4% (8/22) in NDAG, with no significant difference observed (P > 0.05).

The mean wound healing time was 29.4 ± 3.7 days in DPAG and 26.1 ± 2.9 days in NDAG, indicating that diabetic patients required a significantly longer healing period than non-diabetic patients (P < 0.05).

Diversity analysis

Alpha diversity analysis

To evaluate within-sample microbial diversity (alpha diversity), four indices were calculated: ACE, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson (Fig. 1). Alpha diversity indices demonstrated significant differences between groups. The Shannon index was significantly lower in the DPAG compared with the NDAG (2.12 ± 0.65 vs. 3.59 ± 0.37, P < 0.001), indicating reduced microbial diversity. Similarly, the Simpson index was lower in DPAG patients (0.68 ± 0.21 vs. 0.94 ± 0.03, P < 0.001). Measures of community richness, including Chao1 (230.71 ± 85.24 vs. 374.43 ± 199.43, P = 0.008) and ACE (229.62 ± 95.52 vs. 370.13 ± 200.20, P = 0.010), were also significantly decreased in the DPAG group.

Beta diversity analysis

Beta diversity, which quantifies between-sample differences in microbial community composition, was assessed using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity metric. Pairwise distances between samples were calculated and visualized using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). The first two principal components explained 13.5% and 12.01% of the total variance in microbial community structure, respectively (Fig. 2A). The PCoA plot revealed a clear separation between the DPAG and NDAG groups, indicating distinct microbial communities. This difference in community structure and composition was further confirmed by a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) using the Adonis function (Adonis R² = 0.11; P = 0.001). While the overall composition of dominant phyla was similar across samples, we observed notable differences in the relative abundance of specific taxa among individuals (Fig. 2B). These findings demonstrated robust statistical support for compositional shifts in the wound microbiome of diabetic patients.

Composition and differential analysis of species

Taxonomic composition

Analysis of the microbial community composition at the phylum level revealed that the dominant phyla were largely consistent between the DPAG and NDAG groups (Fig. 3A). The four most abundant phyla in both groups were Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Actinobacteria. However, the DPAG group exhibited a lower relative abundance of Bacteroidota (20.12% vs. 28.27%) and Actinobacteria (1.20% vs. 4.23%) compared to the NDAG group.

At the genus level (Fig. 3B), we observed notable differences in the relative abundance of several taxa. Specifically, the DPAG group had a lower abundance of Acinetobacter (5.30% vs. 15.06%) and Prevotella (1.42% vs. 4.81%), while the abundance of Streptococcus (15.74% vs. 2.78%) and Escherichia (7.68% vs. 4.61%) was higher in the DPAG group compared to the NDAG group.

LEfSe analysis

Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis was conducted to identify bacterial taxa that differed significantly in abundance between the DPAG and NDAG groups. LEfSe identified several taxa with significantly different abundances (LDA score > 4.0) between the two groups (Fig. 4). In the DPAG group, the most differentially abundant taxa included Lactobacillales, Enterobacteriaceae, Streptococcus, and Mycoplasma. Conversely, the NDAG group exhibited a higher abundance of Clostridia, Bacteroides, Ruminococcaceae, Prevotella, and Actinobacteriota, among other taxa.

Functional prediction analysis using PICRUSt2

To investigate potential functional differences in the wound microbiota between the two groups, we performed functional prediction analysis using PICRUSt2 software. This analysis infered the functional potential of a microbial community based on its taxonomic composition and known gene content in reference databases. Our analysis focused on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, specifically at level 2 (L2), which provides a broader functional categorization.

We identified 11 KEGG L2 pathways that differed significantly between the DPAG and NDAG groups (Fig. 5). These pathways were primarily involved in amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, cofactor and vitamin metabolism, endocrine system functions, and chitin biosynthesis and metabolism.

Comparison of KEGG pathway enrichment in DPAG and NDAG groups. Left panel: Bar plot showing the mean proportion of KEGG pathways in DPAG (blue) and NDAG (orange) groups. Right panel: Difference in mean proportion of KEGG pathways between DPAG and NDAG groups with 95% confidence intervals

Compared to the NDAG group, the DPAG group exhibited increased enrichment in pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism, chitin biosynthesis and metabolism, and infectious diseases. Conversely, the DPAG group displayed decreased enrichment in pathways associated with energy metabolism, endocrine system functions, cellular motility, cofactor and vitamin metabolism, and amino acid metabolism.

Discussion

This study used 16 S rRNA gene sequencing to compare the postoperative wound microbiota between diabetic (DPAG) and non-diabetic (NDAG) patients with perianal abscesses, revealing a significant microbial dysbiosis in the diabetic group that likely contributes to impaired healing [28–30]. A primary finding was the significantly lower microbial diversity and richness in the wounds of diabetic patients, as shown by lower ACE (P = 2.4e-05) and Chao1 (P = 2.9e − 05) indices. This reduced diversity is a known hallmark of diabetic wounds, compromising the ecosystem’s stability and making it more vulnerable to pathogen colonization [31, 32]. The distinct microbial community structures between the groups, confirmed by PCoA analysis (Adonis R2 = 0.11; P = 0.001), suggest that diabetes creates a selective wound environment—likely driven by hyperglycemia, hypoxia, and inflammation—that favors specific bacteria [33–35].

Specifically, the DPAG group showed an enrichment of potential pathogens like Enterobacteriaceae and Streptococcus, whose pro-inflammatory components and virulence factors can perpetuate tissue damage and delay healing [36–38].Intriguingly, Lactobacillales, an order often considered beneficial, was also more abundant in the diabetic group. Although typically linked to short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production and immune modulation, their enrichment in this context may represent a paradoxical or compensatory response [39, 40]. Certain Lactobacillus species may flourish in diabetic patients due to altered glucose metabolism and the anatomical proximity to the gut, yet under hyperglycemic and inflammatory conditions, their protective effects might be diminished or even maladaptive [41–43]. Conversely, there was a depletion of beneficial bacteria such as Bacteroides, Prevotella and Ruminococcaceae, which produce anti-inflammatory short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) essential for promoting epithelial repair [44–46]. This imbalance points to a dysregulated “gut-skin axis,” where the perianal wound’s proximity to the gut may allow for microbial translocation, especially in diabetic patients with compromised gut barrier function [46, 47].

Delving deeper, the observed dysbiosis likely impairs wound healing through several interconnected mechanisms involving host-microbiome interactions, immune dysregulation, and metabolic reprogramming. The loss of beneficial, SCFA-producing bacteria weakens the epithelial barrier, while enriched pathogens directly damage tissue, creating a cycle of injury and inflammation. This is compounded by immune dysregulation, where pathogen-associated molecules like lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Enterobacteriaceae persistently activate immune cells via pathways like TLR4, leading to a chronic, non-resolving inflammatory state that prevents the transition to the proliferative phase of healing [48, 49]. Our functional prediction analysis supports this by showing an altered metabolic landscape in diabetic wounds. The enrichment of pathways for carbohydrate metabolism suggests pathogens are thriving on excess glucose, while the depletion of pathways for energy and amino acid metabolism indicates the microbial community is less able to support the high energetic demands of cell proliferation and matrix deposition required for wound closure [50, 51].

This study advances the field by providing a detailed microbial snapshot of diabetic perianal abscess wounds, linking dysbiosis to impaired healing and underscoring the potential for microbiome-targeted therapies. Strategies such as probiotics to restore diversity, bacteriophage therapy to eliminate specific pathogens, or even topical microbiome transplants could reprogram the wound environment to promote healing [51–53]. However, This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest and the cross-sectional design captured only a single timepoint, limiting the ability to establish causality. Second, the use of 16 S rRNA sequencing provided predictive functional insights but lacked the resolution of metagenomic or metabolomic approaches. Third, as a single-center study, unadjusted confounders such as diet, vascular disease, and obesity may have introduced bias, despite efforts to exclude patients on antibiotics or immunosuppressants and to ensure comparable demographics and smoking status between groups. Future studies should therefore adopt larger, multicenter, longitudinal designs with multi-omics integration and multivariable adjustments to validate and expand these findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that diabetic patients with perianal abscesses exhibit reduced microbial diversity, enrichment of potential pathogens, and depletion of beneficial commensals in postoperative wounds, indicating a dysbiotic state that may impair healing. Functional prediction further suggests altered metabolic pathways in diabetic wounds, reinforcing the link between microbiome imbalance and delayed repair. These findings highlight the importance of considering the wound microbiome in postoperative management of diabetic patients and provide a basis for future studies exploring microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies.
