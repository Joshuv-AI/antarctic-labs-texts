Silicosis predicts drug resistance and retreatment among tuberculosis patients in India: a secondary data analysis from Khambhat, Gujarat (2006–2022)
Authors: Mihir P. Rupani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndia, with the highest global burden of tuberculosis (TB) and drug-resistant TB, aims to eliminate TB by 2025. Yet, limited evidence exists on drug resistance patterns and retreatment among patients with silico-tuberculosis. This study explores these patterns and assesses the impact of silicosis on TB retreatment in India.MethodsThis secondary data analysis stems from a larger retrospective cohort study conducted in Khambhat, Gujarat, between January 2006 and February 2022. It included 138 patients with silico-tuberculosis and 2,610 TB patients without silicosis. Data from the Nikshay TB information portal were linked with silicosis diagnosis reports from the Pneumoconiosis Board using the unique Nikshay ID as the linking variable. Drug-resistant TB was defined as resistance to any anti-TB drug recorded in Nikshay. Retreatment refers to TB patients who have previously undergone anti-TB treatment for one month or more and need further treatment. Recurrent TB denotes patients who were previously declared cured or had completed treatment but later tested positive for microbiologically confirmed TB. Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine the impact of co-prevalent silicosis on drug resistance and retreatment.ResultsPatients with silico-tuberculosis showed a higher proportion of retreatment compared to those without silicosis (55% vs. 23%, p < 0.001). Notably, 28% of patients with silico-tuberculosis were recurrent TB cases, compared to 11% among those without silicosis. Regarding drug resistance, the silico-tuberculosis group exhibited a higher rate (6% vs. 3%), largely due to rifampicin resistance (5% vs. 2%, p = 0.022). Co-prevalent silicosis was associated with a 2.5 times greater risk of drug-resistant TB (adjusted OR 2.5, 95% CI, 1.1–5.3; p = 0.021). Additionally, patients with silico-tuberculosis had a fourfold increased risk of retreatment for TB (adjusted OR 4, 95% CI, 3–6; p < 0.001).ConclusionsCo-prevalent silicosis significantly elevates the risk of drug resistance, recurrence, and retreatment among TB patients in India. This study indicates a need for improved treatment protocols and suggests that future research should focus on randomized controlled trials to evaluate appropriate anti-TB regimen and duration of therapy for this high-risk group. Given India’s goal to eliminate TB by 2025, addressing the challenges posed by silico-tuberculosis is critical.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03338-6.

Introduction

In 2022, there were 10.6 million new cases of tuberculosis (TB) reported worldwide, with approximately 0.4 million exhibiting resistance to rifampicin and isoniazid, the two primary drugs used in TB treatment [1]. India, which has the highest burden of TB globally, reported 2.9 million new cases in the same year, with 0.11 million showing resistance to rifampicin and isoniazid [1, 2]. This number represents 27% of the total global cases of both new and drug-resistant TB [1, 2]. According to the first anti-tuberculosis drug resistance survey in India, 28% of TB patients showed resistance to any anti-TB drugs, while 6% were multi-drug resistant [3]. India aims to eliminate TB by 2025 [4], coinciding with an expected increase in the number of silica-dust-exposed workers, which could reach a staggering 52 million [5].

Research on drug resistance patterns has largely focused on South African gold miners, with some studies indicating higher levels of drug resistance and others examining the impact of HIV on drug resistance [6–8]. While there have been studies addressing drug resistance among patients with silico-tuberculosis, there is limited recent evidence [9–13]. In India, services for drug-resistant TB began in 2007, significantly later than the last available studies on silico-tuberculosis drug resistance, which were published in 1989–1990 [9, 12]. Since then, the only reported study on drug resistance in silico-tuberculosis, conducted in China and published in a foreign language, appeared in 2002, indicating that over 50% of patients with silico-tuberculosis showed drug resistance [14].

A meta-analysis has identified several factors that contribute to TB relapse, including low compliance with treatment, drug resistance, and the presence of cavitations [15]. However, it noted that the role of comorbidities, such as silicosis, in TB relapse remains controversial [15]. Despite this, other reviews and case reports have highlighted the potential role of silicosis in increasing the risk of TB relapse, suggesting that patients with silico-tuberculosis might require longer treatment regimens [16–20]. Most of the existing evidence linking silicosis to TB relapse is outdated, with many studies recommending extended chemotherapy for these patients [9–11, 21–24]. Additionally, a study on TB recurrence among mine workers found that silicosis was not a significant risk factor for relapse, though the study's focus was primarily on the impact of HIV [25].

Recent studies in India have acknowledged the issue of drug resistance and relapse among patients with silico-tuberculosis, but often without extensive detail [26, 27]. Several potential mechanisms contributing to these outcomes have been proposed, including exposure to crystalline silica dust, which can induce oxidative and nitrosative stress, compromise immune function, impair macrophage activity, and hinder drug penetration [26, 28]. These factors may allow the TB bacilli to persist in the alveoli of the lungs, suggesting a higher risk of drug resistance and relapse among these patients.

Silicosis has been identified as a risk factor for the development of TB, with an estimated fourfold increase in the likelihood of contracting the disease [29]. Additionally, it has been associated with a twofold increase in the risk of unfavorable TB treatment outcomes and a threefold increase in the risk of TB treatment failure [26, 27]. Given these elevated risks and the limited evidence on drug resistance patterns among patients with silico-tuberculosis in India, it is crucial to investigate whether silicosis significantly predicts drug resistance and contributes to the likelihood of TB retreatment. This study aims to address this gap by examining drug resistance patterns among patients with silico-tuberculosis and determining the impact of silicosis on TB retreatment in the Indian context.

Methods

Study design, setting, and duration

This study is a secondary analysis derived from a larger retrospective cohort study conducted in Khambhat, Gujarat, India, involving 138 patients with silico-tuberculosis and 2,610 patients with tuberculosis (TB) who did not have silicosis [26]. The primary objective of the original cohort study was to investigate the impact of silicosis on TB treatment outcomes, including unadjusted estimates for drug resistance and relapse [26]. The current study builds on this data to assess whether silicosis predicts drug resistance and TB recurrence through adjusted analysis estimates. The patients were enrolled in the analysis between January 2006 to February 2022 from the TB treatment and notification registers of the national TB elimination program (NTEP) [26]. The registers were accessed from the Khambhat block of Gujarat, India, known for its agate-stone items and consequent exposure to silica dust exposure, at a time when Khambhat was housing around 50,000 workers in the industry [30–32]. The reported prevalence of silicosis among agate-stone workers has ranged from 18 to 69%, with the prevalence of silico-tuberculosis reported as 5% [5].

Study population

This study included two groups of patients: those with confirmed silico-tuberculosis (TB with silicosis) and those with TB without silicosis. All patients aged ≥ 19 years diagnosed with silico-tuberculosis and registered for TB treatment in the Nikshay portal between January 2006 and February 2022 in Khambhat block, were included in the silico-tuberculosis group [26]. Silico-tuberculosis was diagnosed by the Pneumoconiosis Board at Civil Hospital Ahmedabad, which comprises a pulmonologist, a radiologist, an official from the Directorate of Industrial Safety & Health (DISH), and a medical officer from the State TB Training and Demonstration Center (STDC) [26]. The diagnosis was based on the evaluation of chest X-rays of agate workers, which were referred by the Khambhat TB unit for the assessment of silicosis and silico-tuberculosis [26]. In cases where the chest X-ray results were inconclusive for TB, the Pneumoconiosis Board recommended follow-up testing for agate workers using the sputum nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT). The diagnosis of TB was then retrospectively confirmed based on the NAAT results.

The TB without silicosis group included all TB patients aged ≥ 19 years, notified for treatment under the TB program in the Nikshay portal between January 2017 and February 2022 in Khambhat block, who did not have a diagnosis of silicosis [26]. The age criterion was based on the Indian TB program’s definition of adulthood (≥ 19 years) [33, 34]. Patients with potential exposure to silica dust, such as those working in the agate industry or mine workers, were excluded from this group to ensure they had no risk of silicosis [26]. Exclusion was done using lists of agate workers and cross-referencing with records from the Pneumoconiosis Board and the 'key population' field in the Nikshay portal [26].

No specific codes or automated algorithms were used to identify subjects from the database. Instead, patient records were manually reviewed, and relevant patients were included in the study. Although formal linkage between two separate databases was not required, the identification of silico-tuberculosis patients was achieved by matching the Pneumoconiosis Board’s reports with Nikshay IDs in the Nikshay portal. This ensured accurate integration of TB treatment information and silicosis diagnosis for analysis.

Program for drug-resistant TB (PMDT) in India

India has a comprehensive approach to managing drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) through its Programmatic Management of Drug-Resistant TB (PMDT) [35]. The process begins with universal drug susceptibility testing (DST) for all TB patients, using nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) to detect rifampicin resistance and confirm the diagnosis [35]. The goal of universal drug susceptibility testing (UDST) is to quickly identify rifampicin resistance (RR) and fluoroquinolone (FQ) resistance among those with rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB). The diagnostic algorithm allows upfront NAAT testing for specific groups of presumptive TB cases, such as people living with HIV (PLHIV), suspected extrapulmonary TB cases, children, TB contacts, smear-negative but X-ray-suggestive TB cases, and other vulnerable populations [35].

Patients diagnosed with DR-TB are managed through a nationwide network of DR-TB centres, supported by the National TB Elimination Program (NTEP) staff, general healthcare personnel, community volunteers, and private healthcare providers [35]. Before treatment begins, patients undergo a comprehensive pre-treatment evaluation to identify any risk factors. The choice of treatment regimen is influenced by the DST results, the patient's previous TB treatment history, and any known adverse reactions to TB drugs [35]. Depending on clinical conditions and patient preferences, treatment can be initiated in a hospital setting or on an outpatient basis. This flexibility ensures that patients receive the care they need, tailored to their specific circumstances, and are supported throughout their treatment journey.

Definitions

Drug-resistant TB

According to the national TB program, drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) is defined as resistance to any of the anti-TB drugs recorded in the TB treatment registers [35, 36]. This definition encompasses various patterns of drug resistance, indicating that TB bacilli are resistant to one or more anti-TB drugs. Drug resistance is categorized into four main groups: multi-drug resistant or rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB), pre-extensively drug-resistant TB (pre-XDR TB), extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB), and isoniazid-resistant TB [2, 35, 36]. MDR/RR-TB refers to TB with resistance to rifampicin with or without resistance to isoniazid, the key anti-TB drugs [2, 35, 36]. Pre-XDR TB is a condition where resistance to both rifampicin and/or isoniazid (MDR/RR-TB) and any fluoroquinolone (a class of second-line anti-TB drug) is detected [2, 35, 36]. XDR-TB involves resistance to rifampicin with/without isoniazid, any fluoroquinolone, and at least one Group A drug (like bedaquiline or linezolid) [2, 35, 36]. Isoniazid-resistant TB indicates resistance to only isoniazid, another primary anti-TB drug [2, 35, 36].

Retreatment for TB

Retreatment refers to TB patients who have previously been treated with anti-TB drugs for one month or more from any source and require treatment again for various reasons [36]. Recurrent TB denotes patients who were previously declared successfully treated (cured or completed treatment) but later tested positive for microbiologically confirmed TB [36]. Treatment after failure refers to patients whose prior treatment failed at the end of the most recent course [36]. Treatment after lost to follow-up (LFU) describes patients who were previously treated for at least one month and were declared LFU in their most recent treatment but were later found to have microbiologically confirmed TB [36]. Other previously treated patients are those who have received TB treatment in the past but do not fit into any of the above categories [36].

Study variables

Outcome variable

The study focused on two primary outcome variables: drug-resistant TB and retreatment for TB. Both of these variables were defined as dichotomous, indicating whether or not a patient exhibited drug resistance and whether or not they required retreatment for TB. The codes used to define TB drug resistance and retreatment follow standard definitions employed by the National Tuberculosis Elimination Program (NTEP) and the Nikshay portal, which classifies cases into “new,” “retreatment: recurrent,” “retreatment: treatment after loss to follow up,” “retreatment: treatment after failure,” “retreatment: others” and “PMDT” (programmatic management of drug-resistant TB). Due to the nature of secondary data and its integration from routinely collected health records, the specific codes or algorithms used in the classification of these variables are part of the standardized system used in the Nikshay portal, and detailed algorithms cannot be explicitly listed in this manuscript.

Primary predictor variable

The primary predictor variable was the presence of co-existing silicosis and TB, a condition termed "silico-tuberculosis." This variable was also dichotomous, allowing comparison between TB patients with silicosis and those without.

Other predictor variables

Age, gender, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status, diabetes status, microbiologically positive TB (sputum positive on microscopy or NAAT or culture), and site of TB (pulmonary vs. extrapulmonary) were considered as additional predictor variables. Age was treated as a continuous variable, while the rest were dichotomous variables.

Data collection and study data sets

To create the primary predictor variable for silico-tuberculosis, data were sourced from the TB treatment and notification registers provided by the TB unit in the Khambhat block [26]. The data were supplied in an Excel sheet, allowing the identification of patients with co-existing silicosis and TB [26]. In these registers, basic demographic information such as age, gender, and site of disease was readily available and used as predictor variables [26].

Additional variables were derived from the Nikshay portal, the national TB information system, which is password-protected and accessible only to authorized personnel such as TB health workers and medical officers. While the investigator was not directly able to log into the system, the TB health visitor facilitated data extraction by logging into the portal to retrieve detailed information. The variable for microbiologically confirmed TB was extracted from the 'basis of final diagnosis' in Nikshay [26]. Similarly, the variable for drug-resistant TB was generated from the 'type of case' variable in Nikshay, which categorizes TB cases into new cases, PMDT cases (drug-resistant TB), or retreated cases [26]. Due to the restricted access to the portal, several visits to the TB unit were required to ensure complete data collection, and the health visitor's assistance was critical in accessing drug resistance test results.

All patient information, including drug resistance patterns and treatment history, was primarily obtained from the Nikshay portal, which serves as an online entry system for the TB treatment registers under the National TB Elimination Program (NTEP). For patients with silicosis, diagnoses from the Pneumoconiosis Board were linked to the TB treatment data through the use of Nikshay IDs. No formal linkage between separate databases was required for this study; instead, Nikshay served as the central data source, supplemented by information manually added from the Pneumoconiosis Board and TB treatment registers.

Efforts to minimize bias

This study addressed several potential sources of bias. Selection bias was mitigated by defining clear inclusion criteria, including all patients aged ≥ 19 years diagnosed with TB in Khambhat block. Exclusion of agate workers and cross-referencing with the Pneumoconiosis Board reports ensured accurate classification of silicosis status. Information bias was reduced by linking records through unique Nikshay IDs and by manual verification of data consistency across sources. Potential confounding variables such as age, gender, HIV status, and diabetes were adjusted for using multivariable logistic regression. Finally, recall bias was minimized by relying on comprehensive medical records from the Nikshay portal and Pneumoconiosis Board reports rather than patient self-reports.

Data cleaning methods

The data were sourced from the Nikshay online portal and TB treatment registers, then compiled into an Excel sheet. Silicosis and silico-tuberculosis cases were confirmed using Pneumoconiosis Board reports and manually added to the dataset alongside TB treatment data. We carefully reviewed all demographic and clinical variables, including age, gender, disease site, and drug resistance. Any discrepancies were cross-checked with the original records, ensuring accurate representation of silico-tuberculosis and TB cases without silicosis. Missing or inconsistent data were corrected or excluded based on eligibility criteria, resulting in a clean, high-quality dataset for analysis.

Data linkage

The study did not require person-level or institutional-level data linkage between different databases. Instead, all data were housed within the Nikshay portal, which contains detailed records of TB treatment and drug resistance. Additional data, such as the presence of silicosis, were manually entered using the Nikshay ID as the linking variable. The quality of the linkage was ensured by verifying each patient’s ID and corresponding information across the Pneumoconiosis Board reports and the TB treatment registers.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as counts with percentages in parentheses. We analyzed continuous variables using the median test and categorical variables using Pearson’s chi-square test. For univariable logistic regression, each predictor variable was tested individually against the outcome variable to assess its impact. Variables with a p-value below 0.2 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable analysis.

However, certain variables were excluded from the multivariable analysis due to specific reasons. The site of disease (pulmonary vs. extrapulmonary TB) was removed because there were few drug-resistant cases among patients with extrapulmonary TB, which could lead to skewed results. Additionally, the variable representing retreated TB was excluded because drug resistance (which can be primary or secondary) is inherently a part of the retreated TB variable. Including both in the same analysis would create collinearity and potentially obscure the distinct effects of each on the outcome variables. Thus, drug resistance and retreated TB were not mutually included as predictors in the analysis to avoid redundancy and misinterpretation of their individual effects. Additionally, microbiologically confirmed TB (sputum-positive TB) was not included because there were no cases of sputum-negative TB in the drug-resistant group, making it impossible to derive meaningful conclusions from this data.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the independent impact of silicosis on drug-resistant TB and retreatment for TB in separate analyses. This approach allowed us to control for potential confounding factors and isolate the effect of silicosis on these outcomes. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 23. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Selection of study participants

A total of 165 cases of silico-tuberculosis were identified from the notification register between January 2006 and February 2022 [26]. Manual cleaning involved removing 22 duplicate entries and excluding 5 cases that were not started on TB treatment [26]. This process resulted in a final inclusion of 138 silico-tuberculosis patients for analysis [26]. For TB cases without silicosis, 3,787 cases were identified from the notification register between January 2017 and February 2022 [26]. A multi-step cleaning process was followed, where 506 cases with “outcome not assigned,” 64 with “outcome not evaluated,” 88 cases with “regimen changed,” 231 patients under 19 years of age, 271 agate workers, 15 cases of pure silicosis, and 2 mine workers were excluded from the dataset [26]. Thus, 2,610 TB patients without silicosis were included in the study [26]. The demographic and clinical characteristics among patients with silico-tuberculosis (n = 138) compared to those with TB without silicosis (n = 2610) in the Khambhat block were reported in a previous study [26]. Of the 45 patients with HIV, 19 were receiving anti-retroviral therapy; however, specific details regarding the medications were not available from the Nikshay portal.

TB retreatment and drug resistance pattern

Overall, the proportion of patients who were retreated for TB was significantly higher in the silico-tuberculosis group (55%, 76/138) compared to the group without silicosis (23%, 601/2610), with a p-value of < 0.001 (Table 1). Notably, 28% (38/138) of patients with silico-tuberculosis were classified as recurrent TB cases, compared to only 11% (282/2610) among those without silicosis. Regarding drug resistance, the silico-tuberculosis group had a higher rate, with 6% (8/138) exhibiting drug resistance compared to 3% (85/2610) in the group without silicosis (p = 0.108). Rifampicin resistance was significantly higher in the silico-tuberculosis group compared to the TB without silicosis group [(5%, 7/138) vs. 2%, 55/2610, p = 0.022)]. The primary drug resistance rate—drug resistance among newly diagnosed TB patients—was observed to be 2% in both the silico-tuberculosis and TB without silicosis groups. In contrast, the secondary drug resistance rate—drug resistance among recurrent TB cases—was higher in the silico-tuberculosis group at 4%, compared to 1% in the TB without silicosis group. Predictors of drug-resistant TB

In the uni-variable analysis, co-prevalent silicosis, age, and diabetes demonstrated p-values below 0.2, suggesting a possible link with drug-resistant TB (Table 2). However, after adjusting for confounding factors, only silicosis and diabetes remained significant predictors of drug resistance. The multivariable analysis revealed that patients with co-prevalent silicosis had a 2.5 times greater risk of developing drug-resistant TB, with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 2.5 (95% CI, 1.1–5.3; p = 0.021). Similarly, patients with diabetes had three times greater odds of drug-resistant TB compared to those without diabetes, with an adjusted OR of 3 (95% CI, 2–5; p < 0.001). Predictors of TB retreatment

In the uni-variable analysis, co-prevalent silicosis, gender, diabetes, microbiologically confirmed TB, and pulmonary TB showed p-values below 0.2, suggesting possible associations with retreated TB (Table 3). However, after adjusting for confounding variables, the significant predictors of retreated TB were co-prevalent silicosis, microbiologically confirmed TB, and pulmonary TB. The multivariable analysis indicated that patients with co-prevalent silicosis had a fourfold increased risk of being retreated for TB, with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 4 (95% CI, 3–6; p < 0.001). Similarly, patients with microbiologically confirmed TB had 2.5 times greater odds of retreatment, with an adjusted OR of 2.5 (95% CI, 2–3; p < 0.001). Additionally, patients with pulmonary TB had a 1.7 times greater risk of retreatment compared to those with extrapulmonary TB, with an adjusted OR of 1.7 (95% CI, 1.2–2.5; p = 0.007). Discussion

In this study, we found that co-prevalent silicosis significantly raised the odds of drug resistance and retreatment among patients with TB in India. Of particular concern was the higher incidence of resistance to rifampicin, a critical first-line anti-TB drug. Additionally, comorbid diabetes was associated with an increased risk of drug resistance, while sputum positivity and pulmonary TB correlated with a higher likelihood of retreatment. These findings emphasize the need for improved treatment protocols for patients with silico-tuberculosis in India, given their unique risk profile for both drug resistance and retreatment.

In this study, we found that patients with silico-tuberculosis had a 2.5 times higher chance of drug resistance compared to those with TB but without silicosis, with a notable increase in multi-drug or rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB). Specifically, 6% of patients with silico-tuberculosis exhibited drug resistance, with 5% showing resistance to MDR/RR and 1% showing resistance to isoniazid alone. Comparing these results with other studies, we observe varying rates of drug resistance. A recent study conducted among glass factory workers in Uttar Pradesh, India, reported detection rates of 16% for MDR/RR TB and 5% for resistance to isoniazid alone [37]. Among South African gold miners exposed to silica dust, newly diagnosed patients showed a drug resistance rate of 12%, with 6% resistant to isoniazid and 1% resistant to MDR/RR [9]. Among those previously treated for TB, drug resistance was higher, with 21% for any drug, 14% for isoniazid, and 7% for MDR/RR [12]. In Hong Kong, one study reported an overall drug resistance rate of 15% among patients with silico-tuberculosis, with 3% exhibiting MDR/RR [11]. Another study in Hong Kong, by the same research group, found 17% overall drug resistance, with 9% showing resistance to isoniazid and 2% to rifampicin [10]. Older studies have reported significantly higher drug resistance rates among patients with silico-tuberculosis, with some studies showing as much as 58% resistance to isoniazid or rifampicin [13, 14]. A more recent publication, though published in a lower-quality journal, indicated a lower drug resistance rate of just 5% [38]. These findings highlight the variability in drug resistance rates among patients with silico-tuberculosis, suggesting that occupational exposures, treatment protocols, and population demographics may significantly influence resistance patterns.

The present study found that patients with silico-tuberculosis had a fourfold higher chance of retreatment for TB compared to patients with TB but without silicosis, with 55% of those with silico-tuberculosis undergoing retreatment, and 28% being recurrent TB cases. This contrasts with studies among South African gold miners, where researchers did not find a significant link between pre-existing silicosis or silica dust exposure and relapse, nor did they observe notable differences in relapse rates between the silico-tuberculosis and control groups [9, 24]. However, a later study reported a slight increase in relapse risk among silico-tuberculosis patients, but the difference was statistically insignificant [22]. In Hong Kong, research on patients with silico-tuberculosis indicated a relapse rate of 22% with a 6-month treatment regimen, whereas the relapse rate dropped to 7% with an 8-month regimen [11], closely aligning with the results from our study. Despite silicosis experts suggesting that silicosis or silica dust exposure are not significant risk factors for recurrence, relapse, or reinfection [39], many researchers agree on the necessity of longer treatment durations for patients with silico-tuberculosis to reduce the risk of relapses [11, 21]. This observation underscores the need for tailored treatment approaches and closer monitoring of these patients to prevent retreatment and recurrent TB.

In this study, we found that diabetes was associated with an increased risk of drug resistance to TB drugs. Although some recent studies have shown mixed results regarding this relationship [40, 41], two meta-analyses support the link between diabetes and anti-TB drug resistance, aligning with our study findings, [42, 43]. The resistance is particularly notable with isoniazid [43], suggesting that more research is needed to understand the relationship between diabetes and TB drug resistance [44]. Additionally, our study revealed that microbiologically confirmed TB and pulmonary TB were significant predictors of retreatment, a finding that aligns with at least one other study [45]. The limited evidence underscores the need for additional research to better understand these relationships and to develop more targeted strategies for managing TB in patients with comorbid conditions.

The focus of this study was on retreatment, which can encompass recurrence, treatment after failure, or treatment after loss to follow up. Our previous analysis identified a higher rate of treatment failure and greater percentages of loss to follow up among patients with silico-tuberculosis [26, 27]. The reasons for recurrence in this group require further investigation, as it could be due to reinfection or the reactivation of latent TB infection, both plausible among silica-dust-exposed workers [5, 28, 46]. Research has shown that these populations exhibit high rates of latent TB infection, ranging from 36 to 89% [47–52], making them more susceptible to reactivation. Moreover, drug resistance has been identified as a factor that increases the risk of relapse and treatment failure among silica-dust-exposed groups [12, 21, 27, 53]. Patients who do not respond to standard treatment may later be found resistant to certain drugs, leading to retreatment. To avoid overtreatment among silico-tuberculosis patients, reported in case studies [20], it is essential to conduct more randomized clinical trials in this group, incorporating newly approved drugs like bedaquiline and delamanid alongside standard drugs. Our study highlights the critical role of improved treatment protocols for silico-tuberculosis due to the significantly higher rates of drug resistance and retreatment observed among this group. The increased risk of multi-drug resistance and retreatment necessitates a more focused approach, emphasizing personalized treatment and close monitoring. While silicosis control measures must be integrated into India's broader TB elimination strategy, the complex factors influencing treatment outcomes, such as reactivation of latent TB, reinfection, and drug resistance, require a multidisciplinary approach. Collaboration between pulmonologists, infectious disease specialists, occupational health experts, and public health authorities will be essential in addressing these challenges. Additionally, patient education and community outreach can help reduce silica dust exposure, potentially lowering the incidence of silicosis and its associated TB complications. Our study underscores the importance of further research to explore the mechanisms behind drug resistance and retreatment, aiming to develop effective strategies to achieve TB elimination in India by 2025.

This study adheres to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional analysis and the RECORD (Reporting of studies conducted using observational routinely-collected health data) statement for secondary data (see Additional file 1) [56, 57]. It is one of the few recent studies to explore drug resistance patterns among patients with silico-tuberculosis. This focus is especially important given India's goal to eliminate TB by 2025 [4], with silicosis posing a significant challenge due to its association with drug resistance and retreatment. Integrating silicosis control measures into the national TB elimination strategy is crucial, particularly for cases not responding to primary anti-TB drugs [19, 26, 58].

This study has limitations due to the use of secondary data not specifically collected for the research question. It lacked detailed information on the severity of silicosis and levels of silica dust exposure, which could be significant confounding factors [26, 27]. Additionally, other potential confounders, such as socioeconomic status, nutritional status, smoking habits, and comorbidities, were not adequately accounted for, as these data were unavailable from the records [26, 27]. Misclassification bias is also a concern, as HIV status was recorded as reactive or non-reactive without anti-retroviral therapy (ART) details. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, follow-up data on newly diagnosed TB cases for the development of recurrence or secondary drug resistance could not be collected, limiting the assessment of long-term outcomes. Additionally, the small sample size of patients with silico-tuberculosis could affect the study’s power; however, this reflects the condition’s localized occurrence in populations exposed to silica dust. These limitations underscore the need for future prospective studies to control for a broader range of confounding factors and to follow up on newly diagnosed cases to better track recurrence and the development of drug resistance. While this study was based on data from Khambhat, Gujarat, its findings may be generalizable to other silica-exposed populations in India, highlighting the importance of tailored treatment strategies in similar occupational settings.

Conclusions

Co-prevalent silicosis significantly elevates the risk of drug resistance and retreatment among TB patients in India. Furthermore, the presence of microbiologically-confirmed pulmonary TB and diabetes compounds this risk. Future research should focus on randomized controlled trials to evaluate appropriate anti-TB regimen and duration of therapy for this high-risk group. Addressing these unique challenges will be essential in supporting India’s goal of eliminating TB by 2025.
