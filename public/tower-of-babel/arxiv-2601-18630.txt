Assessing the Quality of Mental Health Support in LLM Responses through Multi-Attribute Human Evaluation
Abeer Badawi, Md Tahmid Rahman Laskar, Elahe Rahimi, Sheri Grach, Lindsay Bertrand, Lames Danok, Frank Rudzicz, Jimmy Huang, Elham Dolatabadi

Abstract
The escalating global mental health crisis, marked by persistent treatment gaps, availability, and a shortage of qualified therapists, positions Large Language Models (LLMs) as a promising avenue for scalable support. While LLMs offer potential for accessible emotional assistance, their reliability, therapeutic relevance, and alignment with human standards remain challenging to address. This paper introduces a human-grounded evaluation methodology designed to assess LLM-generated responses in therapeutic dialogue. Our approach involved curating a dataset of 500 mental health conversations from datasets with real-world scenario questions and evaluating the responses generated by nine diverse LLMs, including closed-source and open-source models. More specifically, these responses were evaluated by two psychiatric-trained experts, who independently rated each on a 5-point Likert scale across a comprehensive 6-attribute rubric. This rubric systematically captures Cognitive Support (Guidance, Informativeness, Safety) and Affective Resonance (Empathy, Helpfulness, Interpretation), providing a multi-dimensional perspective on therapeutic quality. Our analysis reveals that LLMs provide strong cognitive reliability by producing safe, coherent, and clinically appropriate information, but they demonstrate unstable affective alignment, particularly in empathy and helping. Although closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4o) offer balanced therapeutic responses, open-source models show greater variability and emotional flatness. We reveal a persistent cognitive–affective gap and highlight the need for failure-aware, clinically grounded evaluation frameworks that prioritize relational sensitivity alongside informational accuracy in mental-health-oriented LLMs. We advocate for balanced evaluation protocols with human-in-the-loop that center on therapeutic sensitivity and provide a framework to guide the responsible design and clinical oversight of mental-health-oriented conversational AI.

Methodology

We adopt an established data curation and human evaluation protocol for assessing LLMs in mental health support settings
(
6
)
. Following this protocol, we define a set of clinically grounded assessment attributes and apply expert human evaluation on 500 representative counseling-style conversations. In this section, we first describe the data sources and filtering process used to construct the evaluation set, the LLM responses generation process, and then define the evaluation attributes and rating scale, and finally outline the expert rating procedure and analysis.

Dataset Overview

To ensure clinically grounded and contextually diverse evaluation, we draw from three publicly available mental health counseling datasets containing real user-authored messages and therapist-style responses: MentalChat16K
(
36
)
, EmoCare
(
37
;
27
)
, and CounselChat
(
7
)
. MentalChat16K contains de-identified transcripts from clinician–youth sessions collected during the PISCES clinical trial, and has been converted to question-and-answer style using AI. EmoCare (Psych8K) includes human-therapist sessions that were standardized through AI-assisted rephrasing while retaining the original therapeutic intent and structure. CounselChat provides a community-sourced collection of user-submitted mental health questions and licensed-therapist responses, covering a wide range of topics and counseling styles. From these sources, we selected a representative subset of 500 conversations, ensuring diversity across emotional intensity and thematic domains such as anxiety, trauma, grief, and interpersonal stress. Each conversation includes an anonymized user prompt and a therapist-style response. To prevent overfitting to any specific conversational pattern, sampling was stratified across the three datasets, balancing emotional complexity and topical coverage. This design supports controlled yet realistic testing of LLM behavior under conditions resembling real-world digital counseling interactions (e.g., helplines, chatbots, or therapy apps).

Performance Evaluation

Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation follows the structured framework introduced in
(
6
)
, developed to assess therapeutic quality in mental health dialogue systems. This framework extends established psychological assessment principles and incorporates dimensions commonly used in large-scale LLM evaluations
(
19
)
, providing a clinically informed and context-sensitive lens for performance measurement. The objective is to evaluate both the cognitive and affective dimensions of model behavior—capturing not only the factual clarity and problem-solving adequacy of responses but also their emotional sensitivity and interpersonal appropriateness. To achieve this, we adopt a dual-dimensional schema. The Cognitive Support Score (CSS) represents the model’s capacity to deliver structured, problem-oriented, and clinically sound guidance. It encompasses three attributes—Guidance, Informativeness, and Safety—which collectively assess how effectively a model provides actionable feedback, maintains accuracy, and avoids harmful or misleading content. Complementing this, the Affective Resonance Score (ARS) evaluates the model’s emotional attunement and supportive tone across attributes of Empathy, Helpfulness, and Interpretation. These measures capture a model’s ability to recognize user distress, convey understanding, and maintain alignment with the relational goals of therapeutic dialogue. All responses were rated using a 5-point Likert scale
(
25
)
, where higher scores indicate greater adherence to clinical and communicative quality standards.

Human Evaluation

To ensure clinical reliability and interpretive consistency, human evaluation was conducted on a subset of 500 conversations. Each conversation contained the original user message and nine anonymized model-generated responses. Two independent evaluators with psychiatric training and a degree assessed every response based on six therapeutic attributes, encompassing both cognitive support (guidance, informativeness, safety) and affective resonance (empathy, helpfulness, interpretation). Evaluators were blinded to model identity to prevent source-related bias and instructed to rate each response on a 5-point Likert scale. The resulting ratings were aggregated across experts to produce final per-response scores. This setup provided a clinically grounded reference point for comparing the behavioral reliability of different LLMs in mental health counseling contexts
(
38
)
.

Evaluation Process

Following data collection, all human evaluations were systematically processed and analyzed through a multi-stage normalization and scoring pipeline. The primary objective was to derive comparable, interpretable performance statistics for each model and attribute while preserving the strict six-attribute evaluation framework. First, all attribute dictionaries were parsed and standardized to ensure consistent labeling across evaluators and models. Only conversations with all six valid attribute scores were retained for analysis, ensuring uniform completeness and comparability across models. From these filtered records, we computed the mean and standard deviation for each attribute per model, as well as the overall mean and aggregate variability across all attributes. These statistics capture both central performance trends and the dispersion of therapeutic quality across models. To further quantify reliability and risk, we measured the frequency distribution of high-scoring (score
\geq
4), which includes 4/5 and 5/5, and low-scoring (score
\leq
3), which includes 1, 2, and 3 out of 5 responses per attribute. These proportions served as indicators of a model’s stability and failure likelihood under varying conversational conditions. In addition, we recorded the lowest single attribute score observed for each model as a marker of extreme failure cases.

Overall Performance and Attribute Strengths

Our human-grounded evaluation across 500 counseling conversations provides a comprehensive overview of LLMs performance in mental health support. Each of the nine evaluated LLMs generated a response to every user prompt, subsequently rated by two psychiatric-trained domain experts on a 5-point Likert scale across six therapeutic attributes. Table
1
summarizes the mean ± standard deviation for each model, while Figure
1
visualizes the comparative distribution of these mean scores. Across all models and attributes, the overall average score was 4.29 ± 0.47, indicating that LLMs provided moderate-to-strong support quality in counseling contexts. Models performed particularly well on Safety (4.89 ± 0.41), Informativeness (4.11 ± 0.74), and Interpretation (4.85 ± 0.45), demonstrating high factual reliability and clarity in conveying therapeutic meaning. In contrast, affective dimensions such as Empathy (4.03 ± 0.84) and Helpfulness (4.06 ± 0.75) trailed behind cognitive ones, suggesting limitations in emotional attunement and relational sensitivity. Guidance (3.80 ± 0.87) also displayed greater variability, reflecting diverse model capacities for offering actionable, contextually appropriate support. We also note that the Safety and Interpretation both achieved above 4.5 out of 5 for all nine models—highlighting the LLMs’ proficiency in delivering coherent, directive, and content-rich responses.

LLM-level averages reveal distinct capability tiers across the nine evaluated systems. GPT-4o achieved the highest overall mean of 4.70, followed closely by Gemini 2.0-Flash (4.56) and GPT-4o-mini (4.57)—all closed-source models that demonstrated both cognitive reliability and balanced affective tone. Llama-3.1 (4.48) maintained strong, consistent performance across attributes, outperforming most open-source peers. In contrast, DeepSeek-LLaMA (4.23), DeepSeek-Qwen (4.20), Qwen-2.5 (4.16), and Claude-3.5-Haiku (4.12) exhibited moderate capability but higher variance, particularly in empathy and helpfulness. Qwen-3 (3.60) ranked lowest overall, indicating limited robustness in both cognitive and affective dimensions. These per-model averages highlight a clear hierarchy of performance: top-tier models provide both factual precision and communicative coherence, whereas mid-tier and smaller models show uneven skill profiles, excelling in safety and interpretation but lagging in affective understanding. This reinforces that progress in cognitive reliability has outpaced emotional alignment—an essential prerequisite for genuinely therapeutic conversational AI. To further investigate these disparities, the next section examines how specific attribute-level patterns and inter-model variability shape these aggregate outcomes, providing a more granular understanding of where each model excels or falls short.

Conclusion and Future Work

This study presents a human-grounded evaluation of nine large language models (LLMs) across 500 counseling conversations. Most LLMs demonstrate strong cognitive reliability—especially in
Safety
,
Interpretation
, and
Informativeness
—but show inconsistent affective skills in
Empathy
and
Helpfulness
. This persistent asymmetry between factual precision and emotional attunement limits their therapeutic validity. Closed-source models such as GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0-Flash consistently outperform open-source models, achieving over 90% high-confidence ratings with the lowest incidence of low-scoring responses. Yet even top-tier systems exhibit subtle failures in emotional resonance, indicating that advances in conversational safety have not been matched by equivalent progress in affective alignment.

Our findings highlight that the core limitation of current mental-health LLMs is not overt harm but a lack of consistent emotional calibration—responses that are accurate and safe yet fail to convey warmth or relational understanding. This “cognitive-affective gap” underscores the need for multi-dimensional fine-tuning strategies that jointly optimize informational accuracy and interpersonal sensitivity. The multi-attribute human evaluation framework introduced here provides a transparent foundation for benchmarking therapeutic behaviors and advancing standardized assessment in digital mental health research. It also supports reframing LLMs from mere assistants to potential
co-creators
in care, provided their design and deployment remain ethically grounded and clinically supervised. Future work will extend this evaluation to multi-turn dialogues, diverse cultural contexts, and larger pools of expert raters. We also aim to identify specific failure modes driving affective inconsistency and to develop automated metrics that align with expert human judgment, enabling scalable yet clinically valid assessment of mental-health-oriented AI systems.
