Convergence Rates for Gradient Descent on the Edge of Stability for Overparametrised Least Squares
Lachlan Ewen MacDonald, Hancheng Min, Leandro Palma, Salma Tarmoun, René Vidal

Abstract
Classical optimisation theory guarantees monotonic objective decrease for gradient descent (GD) when employed in a small step size, or “stable", regime. In contrast, gradient descent on neural networks is frequently performed in a large step size regime called the “edge of stability", in which the objective decreases non-monotonically with an observed implicit bias towards flat minima. In this paper, we take a step toward quantifying this phenomenon by providing convergence rates for gradient descent with large learning rates in an overparametrised least squares setting. The key insight behind our analysis is that, as a consequence of overparametrisation, the set of global minimisers forms a Riemannian manifold M M , which enables the decomposition of the GD dynamics into components parallel and orthogonal to M M . The parallel component corresponds to Riemannian gradient descent on the objective sharpness, while the orthogonal component is a bifurcating dynamical system. This insight allows us to derive convergence rates in three regimes characterised by the learning rate size: (a) the subcritical regime, in which transient instability is overcome in finite time before linear convergence to a suboptimally flat global minimum; (b) the critical regime, in which instability persists for all time with a power-law convergence toward the optimally flat global minimum; and (c) the supercritical regime, in which instability persists for all time with linear convergence to an orbit of period two centred on the optimally flat global minimum.

Introduction

The well-known gradient descent (GD) iteration

\theta\mapsto\mathrm{GD}^{\ell}(\eta,\theta):=\theta-\eta\nabla\ell(\theta),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

(1)

for minimisation of an objective function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, with learning rate
\eta>0
, is the foundation of most practical algorithms for training deep neural networks (DNNs). Despite the apparent simplicity of the algorithm and
practical
ease with which DNNs can be trained using GD,
theoretically
GD on DNNs remains poorly understood. In addition to the well-known non-convexity of DNN loss functions
\ell
, our theoretical understanding is hampered by the fact that in practice DNNs tend to be trained with larger learning rates than permissible according to standard optimisation theory.

Specifically, classical optimisation theory requires that the learning rate
\eta
be less than twice the reciprocal of the largest eigenvalue
\lambda
(the
sharpness
) of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\ell
. The reason for this is easily seen by considering the simplest quadratic objective
\ell(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\theta^{2}
of a single variable
\theta
: if
\eta>2/\lambda
, then the iterates
\theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_{t}
of
\mathrm{GD}^{\ell}(\eta,\cdot)
grow in magnitude like
|1-\eta\lambda|^{t}
leading to rapid divergence. In contrast, DNNs are typically trained in a regime called the
edge of stability
(EOS)
coheneos
, in which
\eta
is often (pointwise) strictly larger than
2/\lambda
. Miraculously, despite this large learning rate, GD is capable of converging
non-monotonically
to a global minimum of the loss, exhibiting an apparent bias toward less sharp (
flatter
) minima.

Despite significant theoretical progress in the past few years, GD at the edge of stability is still poorly understood when compared with its small learning rate counterpart. In particular, while convergence rates for GD in the small learning rate regime can be obtained for DNNs using standard methods
pl
, convergence rates for GD at the edge of stability have so far only been obtained in settings wherein monotonic loss decrease can be eventually guaranteed
wu2023eos
;
wu2024eos
;
cai2024eos
;
liueos
.

Our purpose in this paper is to provide convergence rates in a
least squares
setting, wherein sustained monotonic decrease of the loss
may never occur
. To our knowledge, these are the first rates of this kind to be provided in the literature.

Our analysis synthesises
Riemannian geometry
and
dynamical systems theory
to formalise an intuition that has been folklore in the literature for some time

;
chen2023eos
, namely that GD with a large step size implicitly favours flatter global minima. This formalisation is achieved for
codimension 1, overparametrised least squares problems
, including deep scalar factorisation, in which the solutions (global minima) are guaranteed to form a
p-1
-dimensional Riemannian submanifold
M
of the
p
-dimensional Euclidean parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Inspired by the “self-stabilisation” idea proposed in
damianeos
, we introduce new coordinates
(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})
for a neighbourhood of
M
, where
\theta^{\parallel}
is a point in
M
and
\theta^{\perp}
coordinatises the direction orthogonal to
M
, with respect to which the GD map
(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\mapsto(\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp}),\mathrm{GD}^{\perp}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp}))
has approximately the following form:

\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}:(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\mapsto\mathrm{GD}_{M}^{\lambda}\bigg(\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})},\theta^{\parallel}\bigg)\qquad\mathrm{GD}^{\perp}:(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\mapsto(1-\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}))\theta^{\perp}+(\theta^{\perp})^{3}.

(2)

The left map
\mathrm{GD}_{M}^{\lambda}
is Riemannian gradient descent on
\lambda
along
M
(see (
4
)), with step size
\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}/(2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}))
for some strictly positive number
c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})
; the right is the normal form for a period-doubling bifurcation
kuznetsov
. Our analysis thereby clarifies the implicit bias of GD toward flat minima:

GD with a large learning rate
implicitly performs Riemannian GD on the sharpness
along the solution manifold, and oscillates as a
bifurcating dynamical system
in the direction orthogonal to the solution manifold.

Paper contributions.
In the context of codimension 1 least squares problems, we:

1.

Make the implicit bias of GD toward flat minima explicit: overparametrisation enables the decomposition of GD into an explicitly sharpness-minimising component along the solution manifold, controlled by an oscillating component orthogonal to the solution manifold.

2.

Prove that a class of non-convex scalar factorisation problems fit into our framework. In particular, we prove that despite the non-convexity of the landscape itself, the sharpness in such examples is
geodesically strongly convex
along a geodesic ball in the solution manifold. To our knowledge, this observation has not previously been made.

3.

Identify and prove convergence and implicit bias results for large step size GD in three regimes:

(a)

the
subcritical
regime, in which the iterates can be guaranteed to (eventually) decrease monotonically at a linear rate until convergence to a suboptimally flat minimum;

(b)

the
critical
regime, in which the iterates converge non-monotonically with a power law rate to the sharpness-minimising global minimum;

(c)

the
supercritical
regime, in which the iterates converge linearly to a stable period-two orbit orthogonal to the solution manifold at the point of minimum sharpness.

Although the former and the latter have already been observed in some examples, to our knowledge the middle regime has not yet been identified. Moreover, our work is the first to be able to theoretically quantify the rate of convergence and implicit bias in all three regimes.

4.

Verify our theory against numerical experiments.

Theoretical setting

3.1
Notation

Given a smooth (i.e., infinitely differentiable,
C^{\infty}
) manifold
M
without boundary, and a point
\theta\in M
, we will use
T_{\theta}M
to denote the tangent space to
M
at
\theta
, and
TM:=\bigsqcup_{\theta\in M}T_{\theta}M
to denote its tangent bundle. Given a smooth map
g:M\rightarrow N
of smooth manifolds, we will denote by
D_{M}g:TM\rightarrow TN
its derivative, acting at each point
\theta\in M
as a linear map
D_{M}g(\theta):T_{\theta}M\rightarrow T_{g(\theta)}N
between tangent spaces.

A
Riemannian metric
on
M
is a smoothly-varying inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\theta}
on each
T_{\theta}M
; we will usually drop the subscript and denote the metric by simply
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Given Riemannian metrics on
M
and
N
, any higher derivative
D^{k}_{M}g:TM^{\otimes k}\rightarrow TN
is also defined, and at each point
\theta\in M
acts a symmetric, multilinear map
D^{k}_{M}g(\theta):T_{\theta}M^{\otimes k}\rightarrow T_{g(\theta)}N
whose value on vectors

we denote
D^{k}_{M}g(\theta)[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
(respectively,
D^{k}_{M}g(\theta)[v^{\otimes k}]
if
v_{i}=v\,\,\forall i
). This
D^{k}_{M}g
may also act on vector fields
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}:M\rightarrow TM
to give a map
D^{k}_{M}g[V_{1},\dots,V_{k}]:M\rightarrow TN
defined by the formula
D^{k}_{M}g[V_{1},\dots,V_{k}](\theta):=D^{k}_{M}g(\theta)[V_{1}(\theta),\dots,V_{k}(\theta)]
(resp.
D^{k}_{M}g[V^{\otimes k}](\theta):=D^{k}_{M}g(\theta)[V(\theta)^{\otimes k}]
if
V_{i}=V\,\,\forall i
) for

.

If

is scalar-valued, then

using the Riemannian metric. Specifically, given any
\theta\in M
and tangent vectors

is the unique element of

satisfying

\displaystyle\langle\nabla^{k}_{M}g(\theta)[v_{2},\dots,v_{k}],v_{1}\rangle=D^{k}_{M}g(\theta)[v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{k}],\qquad\forall v_{1}\in T_{\theta}M,

(3)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Riemannian metric in
T_{\theta}M
. The objects
\nabla_{M}g
and
\nabla^{2}_{M}g
are the
Riemannian gradient
and
Riemannian Hessian
respectively. In particular,
D^{k}_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}g
and
\nabla^{k}_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}g
will be denoted simply
D^{k}g
and
\nabla^{k}g
;
\nabla g
and
\nabla^{2}g
then are the ordinary Euclidean gradient and Hessian respectively.

A

is a locally length-minimising curve in
M
(for instance, geodesics in Euclidean space are just straight lines). We will use
d_{M}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
to denote the geodesic distance between
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in M
, which is the length of the shortest geodesic connecting

to

;

. See Appendix
A

is associated to a

Recall that a

6
Limitations, discussion and conclusion

Our work has a number of limitations, all of which point toward avenues for further exploration along the lines we have developed.

Higher order orthogonal dynamics.
Prior work has indicated that beyond the 2-periodic behaviour we studied in this work, higher-order periodicity and chaos emerge as the learning rate increases
kalraeos
. Our Theorem
4.1
provides new insight into this behaviour as a manifestation of a bifurcating system oscillating orthogonally to the solution manifold. Our analysis only handles the simplest (namely 2-periodic) case of this supercritical behaviour. We expect our framework to be able to admit convergence proofs of higher oscillatory and chaotic behaviour also, using ideas from dynamical systems theory, however we do not expect this to be easy and have not attempted it in this paper.

Higher codimension least squares.
Our theory only allows us to treat codimension 1 problems. This is clearly a severe limitation, ruling out for instance overparametrised regression on multiple datapoints or multiple outputs. It is not difficult to extend our definitions to higher codimension, however proving anything in this setting seems very challenging, as it would require an understanding of higher-dimensional bifurcating dynamical systems. We leave this question to future work.

Geodesic convexity of sharpness for more general solution manifolds.
One of the most surprising results of our work is that the sharpness of the loss is geodesically strongly convex over a geodesic ball in the solution manifold for a number of overparametrised non-convex problems. Should this prove to be a more general fact, which we suspect may be the case, it would go a long way to explaining the still-mysterious ease of optimisation and implicit bias of GD on DNNs. We leave a more thorough investigation of this question to future work.

Finding the tubular neighbourhood.
Our theory is premised on the
assumption
that GD is initialised in a tubular neighbourhood of the solution manifold. While there is no reason to think this is the case with standard initialisation schemes used in practice, we conjecture that GD does converge rapidly to a tubular neighbourhood from a standard initialisation during the phase known as progressive sharpening
coheneos
, after which the familiar dynamics described here would apply. We leave exploration of this question to future work.

matrix-valued function

P_{TM}(\nabla^{2}\lambda-\langle n,\nabla\lambda\rangle Dn)P_{TM},

(30)

on
M
.

Proof.

When
M
is an embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the action of the Levi-Civita connection on a function
\lambda
(i.e., the Riemannian gradient operator) is given by
P_{TM}\nabla\lambda
, where
\nabla
is the ordinary Euclidean gradient. The action of the Levi-Civita connection on a vector field
X:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
is given by
P_{TM}\,DX\,P_{TM}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda

\displaystyle=P_{TM}D\big(P_{TM}\nabla\lambda)P_{TM}=P_{TM}(\nabla^{2}\lambda-Dn\langle n,\nabla\lambda\rangle-n((Dn)^{T}\nabla\lambda)^{T}-nn^{T}\nabla^{2}\lambda)P_{TM}

(31)

\displaystyle=P_{TM}(P_{TM}\nabla^{2}\lambda-\langle n,\nabla\lambda\rangle Dn-n((Dn)^{T}\nabla\lambda)^{T})P_{TM}

(32)

\displaystyle=P_{TM}(\nabla^{2}\lambda-\langle n,\nabla\lambda\rangle Dn)P_{TM},

(33)

where the final line follows from

P_{TM}(nv^{T})=(I-nn^{T})(nv^{T})=nv^{T}-nv^{T}=0

(34)

for any vector
v
.
∎

We will be particularly interested in the case of a hypersurface
M=f^{-1}\{y\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, where
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth submersion, with metric
g
inherited from the ambient Euclidean space. Specifically, when
n
is the unit normal vector field defined by

n(\theta):=\frac{\nabla f(\theta)}{\|\nabla f(\theta)\|},\qquad\theta\in M,

(35)

the Riemannian metric
g
on
M
is defined by

g(\theta):=P_{TM}(\theta):=I-n(\theta)n(\theta)^{T},\qquad\theta\in M

(36)

where we use
P_{TM}(\theta)
to denote the projection onto
T_{\theta}M
. We will be particularly interested in computing the Riemannian Hessian of the function
\lambda:=\|\nabla f\|^{2}
, which admits the following formula entirely in terms of
f
and its derivatives.

Lemma A.2
.

For a hypersurface
M:=f^{-1}\{y\}
of Euclidean space defined by a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the Riemannian Hessian of
\lambda:=\|\nabla f\|^{2}
is given by

\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda=2P_{TM}\big((\nabla^{3}f\nabla f+(\nabla^{2}f)^{2})-\langle n,\nabla^{2}fn\rangle\nabla^{2}f\big)P_{TM}

(37)

where
\nabla^{3}f
is regarded as a map
\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, or as the map sending a direction vector
v
to the directional derivative of the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}f
in the direction
v
.

Proof.

By Lemma
A.1
, we have that

\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda=P_{TM}\big(\nabla^{2}\lambda-\langle n,\nabla\lambda\rangle Dn\big)P_{TM},

(38)

so it remains only to compute the various quantities. The following identities are elementary to verify:

\nabla\lambda=2\nabla^{2}f\nabla f,

(39)

\nabla^{2}\lambda=2\big(\nabla^{3}f\nabla f+(\nabla^{2}f)^{2}\big),

(40)

and

Dn=P_{TM}\frac{\nabla^{2}f}{\|\nabla f\|}.

(41)

The result now follows.
∎

Our next lemma allows us to lower-bound the
convexity radius

\text{conv}(\theta)
at
\theta\in M
which is the largest
r>0
such that the geodesic ball
B_{M}(\theta,r)
is geodesically convex. This will be used in verifying Assumption
3.4
for multilayer scalar factorisation. If
M
is a hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with normal vector field
n
, then the
second fundamental form
of
M
is the map
h:TM\otimes TM\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle h(\theta)[v,w]:=-\langle Dn(\theta)[v],w\rangle

(42)

for all
\theta\in M
and
v,w\in T_{\theta}M
.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
M
be a smooth hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with normal vector field
n
, and fix
\theta_{*}\in M
. Assume that for all
R>0
, there exists
C_{R}>0
such that

\displaystyle|h(\theta)[u,v]|\leq C_{R}

(43)

for all
\theta\in B_{M}(\theta_{*},2R)
and all orthonormal vectors
u,v\in T_{\theta}M
. Then for all
R>0
, one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{conv}(\theta_{*})\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\bigg\{\frac{\pi}{C_{R}},R\bigg\}.

(44)

Proof.

The bound follows from a well-known relationship between the convexity radius and the
injectivity radius
, which, at a point
\theta\in M
, is the largest
r>0
such that
\exp_{\theta}:B_{T_{\theta}M}(0,r)\rightarrow M
is injective. We thus first bound the injectivity radius. Fix
R>0
. By the Gauss equation and the hypothesis, at any point
\theta\in B(\theta_{*},2R)
, the sectional curvature
K(\theta)
of
M
satisfies

\displaystyle|K(\theta)[u,v]|=\big|h(\theta)[u,u]\,h(\theta)[v,v]-h(\theta)[u,v]^{2}\big|\leq C_{R}^{2}

(45)

for all unit, orthogonal pairs
u,v\in T_{\theta}M
. Consequently, by Klingenberg’s formula
[
34
, Lemma 6.4.7]
, the injectivity radius
\text{inj}(\theta)
at
\theta
, namely the largest value of
r
such that
\exp_{\theta}:B_{T_{\theta}M}(0,r)\rightarrow M
is injective, satisfies

\displaystyle\text{inj}(\theta)\geq\min\bigg\{\frac{\pi}{C_{R}},\frac{1}{2}L(\theta)\bigg\},

(46)

where
L(\theta)
is the length of the shortest geodesic loop based at
\theta
. If
L(\theta)\geq 2R
, then this simply yields
\text{inj}\geq\min\{\pi/C_{R},R\}
. If
L(\theta)<2R
, then the shortest geodesic loop
\gamma:[0,L(\gamma)]\rightarrow M
at
\theta
is contained entirely in
B(\theta_{*},2R)
. Fenchel’s theorem then combines with the hypothesis to give

\displaystyle C_{R}\geq\int_{0}^{L(\theta)}\big|h(\gamma(t))[\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t)]\big|dt\geq\frac{2\pi}{L(\gamma)},

(47)

so that
(1/)L(\theta)\geq\pi/C_{R}
. Consequently, one has the bound

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in B(\theta_{*},R)}\text{inj}(\theta)\geq\bigg\{\frac{\pi}{C_{R}},R\bigg\}

(48)

in general.

Now, setting

\displaystyle r_{R}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\bigg\{\frac{\pi}{C_{R}},R\bigg\},

(49)

one sees that the hypotheses of
[
34
, Theorem 6.4.8]
hold, implying that
B(\theta_{*},r_{R})
is geodesically convex.
∎

Appendix B
Case study: deep scalar factorisation

In this section, we verify that all of our assumptions are satisfied for deep scalar factorisation, where
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle f(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{p}):=\theta_{1}\cdots\theta_{p},\qquad\theta:=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(50)

For
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
having all entries nonzero, it will be convenient to denote

\displaystyle v(\theta):=(\theta_{1}^{-1},\dots,\theta_{p}^{-1}).

(51)

We first demonstrate that Assumption
3.1
is satisfied.

Proposition B.1
.

Any
y\neq 0
is a regular value of
f
. Consequently, Assumption
3.1
is satisfied.

Proof.

Since
y\neq 0
, any point
\theta^{\parallel}\in f^{-1}\{y\}
has all coordinates being nonzero. Hence
Df(\theta^{\parallel})=y\,v(\theta^{\parallel})\neq 0
making
y
a regular value of
f
.
∎

The pre-image manifold
f^{-1}\{y\}
of any
y\neq 0
has several connected components. Without loss of generality, we will assume from hereon that
y>0
and denote by
M
the component of
f^{-1}\{y\}
contained in the positive orthant. We next demonstrate that Assumption
3.2
is satisfied.

Proposition B.2
.

Set
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}:=y^{1/p}1_{p}
, where
1_{p}
is the vector of 1s. Then the line spanned by the normal vector
n(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
is invariant under gradient descent on
\ell=(1/2)(f-y)^{2}
for any
\eta
, so that Assumption
3.2
is satisfied.

Proof.

At
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}:=y^{1/p}1_{p}
, one has
n(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})=p^{-1/2}1_{p}
. For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, one sees that

\displaystyle\nabla\ell(\alpha\,n(\theta^{\parallel}_{*}))

\displaystyle=\big(f(\alpha p^{-1/2}1_{p})-y\big)\nabla f(\alpha p^{-1/2}1_{p})

(52)

\displaystyle=\big(f(\alpha p^{-1/2}1_{p})-y\big)\,\alpha^{p-1}\,p^{-(p-1)/2}\,1_{p}

(53)

is a multiple of
n(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
, implying that the span of
n(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
is invariant under gradient descent on
\ell
.
∎

We will continue to denote
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}:=y^{1/p}1_{p}
, which is often referred to in the literature as the “balanced" solution. We next come to Assumption
3.3
.

Proposition B.3
.

Assumption
3.3
holds along
M
. In particular, at
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
,

\displaystyle c(2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*}),\theta^{\parallel}_{*})=\bigg(\frac{32}{p^{3}}\binom{p}{2}^{2}-\frac{8}{p^{2}}\binom{p}{3}\bigg)y^{-2/p},

(54)

where we use the convention
\binom{p}{3}=0
if
p<3
.

Proof.

Given symmetric, multilinear functionals
A,B
taking
k
and
l
inputs respectively, let
A\odot B
denote their symmetric product:

\displaystyle(A\odot B)[v_{1},\dots,v_{k+l}]=\frac{1}{(k+l)!}\sum_{\sigma\in\text{Perm}(k+l)}A[v_{\sigma(1)},\dots,v_{\sigma(k)}]B[v_{\sigma(k+1)},\dots,v_{\sigma(k+l)}],

(55)

where
\text{Perm}(k+l)
is the group of permutations of
k+l
elements. Since
\ell\equiv(1/2)(f-y)^{2}
, one has

\displaystyle D\ell=(f-y)Df\Rightarrow D\ell|_{M}=0,

(56)

\displaystyle D^{2}\ell=Df\odot Df+(f-y)D^{2}f\Rightarrow D^{2}\ell|_{M}=Df\odot Df

(57)

\displaystyle D^{3}\ell=3D^{2}f\odot Df+(f-y)D^{3}f\Rightarrow D^{3}\ell|_{M}=3D^{2}f\odot Df

(58)

\displaystyle D^{4}\ell=4D^{3}f\odot Df+3D^{2}f\odot D^{2}f+(f-y)D^{4}f\Rightarrow D^{4}\ell|_{M}=4D^{3}f\odot Df+3D^{2}f\odot D^{2}f.

(59)

One then sees that

\displaystyle c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\eta}{2}D^{3}\ell[n^{\otimes 3}](\theta^{\parallel})\bigg)^{2}-\frac{\eta}{6}D^{4}\ell[n^{\otimes 4}](\theta^{\parallel})

(60)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{3\eta}{2}D^{2}f[n^{\otimes 2}]Df[n]\bigg)^{2}(\theta^{\parallel})-\frac{\eta}{6}\bigg(4D^{3}f[n^{\otimes 3}]Df[n]+3(D^{2}f[n^{\otimes 2}])^{2}\bigg)(\theta^{\parallel})

(61)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{9\eta^{2}}{4}\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\bigg)\big(D^{2}f(\theta^{\parallel})[n(\theta^{\parallel})^{\otimes 2}]\big)^{2}-\frac{4\eta}{6}\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|\,D^{3}f(\theta^{\parallel})[n(\theta^{\parallel})^{\otimes 3}].

(62)

By continuity, it suffices to prove the claim at
\eta=2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})=2/\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}
; substituting this into the above yields

\displaystyle c(2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}),\theta^{\parallel})=\frac{8}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}}D^{2}f(\theta^{\parallel})[n(\theta^{\parallel})^{\otimes 2}]^{2}-\frac{8}{6\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|}D^{3}f(\theta^{\parallel})[n(\theta^{\parallel})^{\otimes 3}].

(63)

One computes

\displaystyle Df_{l}(\theta^{\parallel})=\frac{y}{\theta_{l}},

(64)

\displaystyle D^{2}f_{l_{1}l_{2}}(\theta^{\parallel})=(1-\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}})\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{1}}\theta_{l_{2}}},

(65)

\displaystyle D^{3}f_{l_{1}l_{2}l_{3}}(\theta^{\parallel})=\begin{cases}(1-\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}})(1-\delta_{l_{1}l_{3}})(1-\delta_{l_{2}l_{3}})\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{1}}\theta_{l_{2}}\theta_{l_{3}}}&\text{ if $L\geq 3$}\\
0\text{ if $L=2$}\end{cases}.

(66)

Consequently,

\displaystyle D^{2}f(\theta^{\parallel})[n(\theta^{\parallel})^{\otimes 2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}}D^{2}f(\theta^{\parallel})[Df(\theta^{\parallel})^{\otimes 2}]

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}}(1-\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}})\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{1}}\theta_{l_{2}}}\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{1}}}\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{2}}}

(68)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}}\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}}\frac{y^{3}}{\theta_{l_{1}}^{2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{2}}\neq 0.

(69)

If
p=2
, then, since
D^{3}f\equiv 0
, Assumption
3.3
trivially holds for all
\theta^{\parallel}\in M
and all
\eta
close to
2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})
. Let us assume then that
p\geq 3
. One has:

\displaystyle D^{3}f(\theta^{\parallel})[n(\theta^{\parallel})^{\otimes 3}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{3}}D^{3}f(\theta^{\parallel})[Df(\theta^{\parallel})^{\otimes 3}]

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{3}}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},l_{3}}(1-\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}})(1-\delta_{l_{1}l_{3}})(1-\delta_{l_{2}l_{3}})\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{1}}\theta_{l_{2}}\theta_{l_{3}}}\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{1}}}\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{2}}}\frac{y}{\theta_{l_{3}}}

(71)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{3}}\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}<l_{3}}\frac{y^{4}}{\theta_{l_{1}}^{2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{2}\theta_{l_{3}}^{2}}.

(72)

Thus one has

\displaystyle c(2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}),\theta^{\parallel})=\frac{32y^{6}}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{6}}\bigg(\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}}\frac{1}{\theta_{l_{1}}^{2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{2}}\bigg)^{2}-\frac{8y^{4}}{\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{4}}\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}<l_{3}}\frac{1}{\theta_{l_{1}}^{2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{2}\theta_{l_{3}}^{2}}.

(73)

Substituting

\displaystyle\|Df(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{p}\frac{y^{2}}{\theta_{l}^{2}}

(74)

then yields

\displaystyle c(2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}),\theta^{\parallel})

\displaystyle=32\bigg(\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}}\theta_{l_{1}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{-2}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\sum_{l}\theta_{l}^{-2}\bigg)^{-3}-8\bigg(\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}<l_{3}}\theta_{l_{1}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{3}}^{-2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{l}\theta_{l}^{-2}\bigg)^{-2}

(75)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{l}\theta_{l}^{-2}\bigg)^{-3}\bigg(32\bigg(\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}}\theta_{l_{1}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{-2}\bigg)^{2}-8\bigg(\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}<l_{3}}\theta_{l_{1}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{3}}^{-2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{l}\theta_{l}^{-2}\bigg)\bigg)

(76)

\displaystyle=(pS_{1}(\theta^{\parallel}))^{-3}\bigg(32\binom{p}{2}^{2}S_{2}(\theta^{\parallel})^{2}-8p\binom{p}{3}S_{1}(\theta^{\parallel})S_{3}(\theta^{\parallel})\bigg),

(77)

where
S_{i}(\theta^{\parallel})
are the elementary symmetric means in the variables
\theta_{l}^{-2}
. Newton’s inequality for the elementary symmetric means gives
S_{2}(\theta^{\parallel})^{2}\geq S_{1}(\theta^{\parallel})S_{3}(\theta^{\parallel})
, so that
c(2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}),\theta^{\parallel})>0
since
\binom{p}{2}^{2}=p^{2}(p-1)^{2}/4>p^{2}(p-1)(p-2)/6=p\binom{p}{3}
.

To complete the proof, we evaluate at
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}=y^{1/p}1_{p}
. One has:

\displaystyle\sum_{l}\theta_{l}^{-2}=py^{-2/p},\qquad\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}}\theta_{l_{1}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{-2}=\binom{p}{2}y^{-4/p},\qquad\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}<l_{3}}\theta_{l_{1}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{2}}^{-2}\theta_{l_{3}}^{-3}=\binom{p}{3}y^{-6/p},

(78)

from which it follows that

\displaystyle c(2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*}),\theta^{\parallel}_{*})=\frac{32}{p^{3}}\binom{p}{2}^{2}y^{-2/p}-\frac{8}{p^{2}}\binom{p}{3}y^{-2/p},

(79)

thus yielding the claimed formula.
∎

We finally come to verifying Assumption
3.4
. This requires us both to demonstrate the existence of a geodesically convex ball containing
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
, and to demonstrate that
\lambda
is geodesically strongly convex in this ball.

We first give an explicit ball about
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
which is geodesically convex using Lemma
A.3
.

Lemma B.4
.

The geodesic ball of radius

\displaystyle r:=\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if $p=2$}\\
y^{1/p}\bigg(\frac{4\pi+1-\sqrt{16\pi+1}}{16\pi-8}\bigg)&\text{if $p\geq 3$}\end{cases}

(80)

centred on
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
is geodesically convex.

Proof.

That
r
can be taken to be
\infty
if
p=2
follows from the fact that
M
in this case is a copy of the real line.

Suppose that
p\geq 3
. Observe that for any
\theta^{\parallel}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{p})\in M
and any
u,v\in T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M
, one has

\displaystyle h(\theta^{\parallel})[u,v]

\displaystyle=-\langle Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[u],v\rangle

(81)

\displaystyle=-\|\nabla f(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{-1}\,\langle u,\nabla^{2}f(\theta^{\parallel})\rangle

(82)

\displaystyle=-\bigg(\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{-2}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\langle u,\big(n(\theta^{\parallel})n(\theta^{\parallel})^{T}-\text{diag}((\theta_{i}^{-2})_{i=1}^{p})\big)v\rangle

(83)

\displaystyle=-\bigg(\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{-2}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\sum_{i}u_{i}v_{i}\theta_{i}^{-2}\bigg),

(84)

since
n(\theta^{\parallel})^{T}w=0
for any vector
w\in T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M
. Consequently, for any
R\in[0,(1/2)y^{1/p})
, since
B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},2R)\subset B_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},2R)
by the distance minimising property of geodesics, if
\theta^{\parallel}\in B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},2R)
then
\theta_{i}\in[y^{1/p}-2R,y^{1/p}+2R]
for all

, so that

\displaystyle|h(\theta^{\parallel})[u,v]|\leq(y^{1/p}-2R)^{-2}(y^{1/p}+2R)=:C_{R}.

(85)

By Lemma
A.3
, for all
R\in[0,(1/2)y^{1/p})
the ball of radius

\displaystyle r_{R}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\bigg\{\frac{\pi}{C_{R}},R\bigg\}

(86)

is geodesically convex; it thus remains only to optimise this
r_{R}
. Since
R\mapsto R
is monotonically increasing and
R\mapsto\pi/C_{R}
is monotonically decreasing, it suffices to find the first smallest value of
R
for which
\pi/C_{R}=R
. This equality gives a quadratic whose solution is

\displaystyle R=y^{1/p}\bigg(\frac{4\pi+1-\sqrt{16\pi+1}}{8\pi-4}\bigg),

(87)

from which the result follows.
∎

We next use Lemma
A.2
to compute the Riemannian Hessian of
\lambda
along
M
.

Lemma B.5
.

For
\theta^{\parallel}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{p})\in M
, define
s_{1}(\theta^{\parallel}):=\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{-2}
and
s_{2}(\theta^{\parallel}):=\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{-4}
. Then the function
\lambda
has Riemannian Hessian

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda=\begin{cases}2y^{2}P_{TM}\bigg(3\,\text{diag}\big(v^{\odot 4}\big)-\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}}\text{diag}\big(v^{\odot 2}\big)\bigg)P_{TM}&\text{ if $p>2$}\\
2y^{2}P_{TM}\bigg(\text{diag}(v^{\odot 4})+\bigg(s_{1}-\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}}\bigg)\text{diag}(v^{\odot 2})\bigg)P_{TM}&\text{ if $p=2$}\end{cases}.

(88)

In particular, at
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}\in U
,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})=4y^{2-4/p}\,I_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*}).

(89)

Moreover, if
p=2
, then
\lambda
is 2-geodesically strongly convex over all of
M
; if
p>2
, then for any
\delta\in[0,2-\sqrt{3})
,
\lambda
is
2y^{2-4/p}(1+\delta)^{-4}(1-\delta)^{-2}\big(3(1-\delta)^{2}-(1+\delta)^{2}\big)

\delta y^{1/p}
about
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
in
M
.

Proof.

For notational convenience, set
V_{1}:=\text{diag}(v^{\odot 2})
and
V_{2}:=\text{diag}(v^{\odot 4})
, where

denotes the Hadamard (componentwise) product and
v
is the vector field defined in (

). From the identities (
64
), (
65
) and (
66
), one sees that along
M
one has

\displaystyle\nabla f\equiv y\,v,\qquad\nabla^{2}f\equiv y(vv^{T}-V_{1}),\qquad n\equiv s_{1}^{-1/2}\,v.

(90)

The pointwise-bilinear map
\nabla^{3}f
is zero if

, and if

\displaystyle(\nabla^{3}f[z])_{ij}=

\displaystyle y(1-\delta_{ij})\sum_{k}(1-\delta_{jk})(1-\delta_{ik})v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}z_{k}

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y(1-\delta_{ij})\big(\sum_{k}(v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}z_{k}-\delta_{jk}v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}z_{k}-\delta_{ik}v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}z_{k}+\delta_{jk}\delta_{ik}v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}z_{k})\big)

(92)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y(1-\delta_{ij})\big((v^{T}z)vv^{T}-v(V_{1}z)^{T}-(V_{1}z)v^{T}+\text{diag}(v^{\odot 3}\odot z)\big)_{ij}

(93)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y\big((v^{T}z)vv^{T}-v(V_{1}z)^{T}-(V_{1}z)v^{T}+\text{diag}(v^{\odot 3}\odot z)+

(94)

\displaystyle-(v^{T}z)V_{1}+\text{diag}(v^{\odot 3}\odot z)+\text{diag}(v^{\odot 3}\odot z)-\text{diag}(v^{\odot 3}\odot z)\big)_{ij}

(95)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y\big((v^{T}z)(vv^{T}-V_{1})-v(V_{1}z)^{T}-(V_{1}z)v^{T}+2\text{diag}(v^{\odot 3}\odot z)\big)_{ij},

(96)

where the fourth line follows from the fact that
(1-\delta_{ij})A_{ij}=(A-\text{diag}(A))_{ij}
for any matrix

and the fact that
\text{diag}(v(V_{1}z)^{T})=\text{diag}((V_{1}z)v^{T})=\text{diag}(v^{\odot 3}\odot z)
. Substituting

one sees that

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}f\nabla f=\begin{cases}y^{2}\big(s_{1}(vv^{T}-V_{1})-vv^{T}V_{1}-V_{1}vv^{T}+2V_{2}\big)&\text{ if $p>2$}\\
0&\text{ if $p=2$}\end{cases}.

(97)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}f)^{2}=y^{2}(s_{1}vv^{T}-vv^{T}V_{1}-V_{1}vv^{T}+V_{2})

(98)

and

\displaystyle\langle n,\nabla^{2}f\,n\rangle\nabla

{}^{2}f=\frac{y^{2}}{s_{1}}\langle v,vv^{T}v-V_{1}v\rangle(vv^{T}-V_{1})=y^{2}(s_{1}-s_{2}/s_{1})(vv^{T}-V_{1}),

(99)

so that, by Lemma

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda

\displaystyle=2P_{TM}\big(\nabla^{3}f\nabla f+(\nabla^{2}f)^{2}-\langle n,\nabla^{2}f\rangle\nabla^{2}f\big)P_{TM}

(100)

\displaystyle=2y^{2}P_{TM}\big(3V_{2}-(s_{2}/s_{1})V_{1}+(s_{1}+(s_{2}/s_{1}))vv^{T}-2vv^{T}V_{1}-2V_{1}vv^{T}\big)P_{TM}

(101)

if
p>2
and

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda

\displaystyle=2y^{2}P_{TM}\big(V_{2}+(s_{1}-(s_{2}/s_{1}))V_{1}-vv^{T}V_{1}-V_{1}vv^{T}+(s_{2}/s_{1})vv^{T}\big)P_{TM}

(102)

if
p=2
. Now, let

be any tangent vector field to
M
. Since

, so that

\displaystyle\big((s_{1}+(s_{2}/s_{1}))vv^{T}-2vv^{T}V_{1}-2V_{1}vv^{T}\big)\tau=-2vv^{T}V_{1}\tau

(103)

and

\displaystyle\big(-vv^{T}V_{1}-V_{1}vv^{T}+(s_{2}/s_{1})vv^{T}\big)\tau=-vv^{T}V_{1}\tau

(104)

are both multiples of the normal vector

; it follows that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda=\begin{cases}2y^{2}P_{TM}\big(3V_{2}-(s_{2}/s_{1})V_{1}\big)P_{TM}&\text{ if $p>2$}\\
2y^{2}P_{TM}\big(V_{2}+(s_{1}-(s_{2}/s_{1}))V_{1}\big)P_{TM}&\text{ if $p=2$}\end{cases}

(105)

as claimed. In either case, evaluating this matrix at
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}=y^{1/p}1_{p}
gives the claimed identity

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})=4y^{2-4/p}I_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*}).

(106)

In particular, if
p=2

\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda
on the unit tangent vector field
\hat{\tau}:(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\mapsto(-\theta_{1},\theta_{2})/\sqrt{\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}}
yields

\displaystyle\big(\hat{\tau}^{T}\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda\,\hat{\tau}\big)(\theta^{\parallel})

\displaystyle=\frac{2y^{2}}{(\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2})}\bigg(\theta_{1}^{-2}+\theta_{2}^{-2}+\frac{4\theta_{1}^{-2}\theta_{2}^{-2}}{\theta_{1}^{-2}+\theta_{2}^{-2}}\bigg)

(107)

\displaystyle=\frac{2y^{2}}{\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}}{\theta_{1}^{2}\theta_{2}^{2}}+\frac{4}{\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}}\bigg)=2+\frac{8y^{2}}{(\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2})^{2}}\geq 2

(108)

for any

as claimed.

We now assume that

and come to determining a neighbourhood of
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
on which

be positive; we will show that this sufficient condition can be guaranteed over a geodesic ball in
M
centred at
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
. Since the geodesic ball of radius
r
at a point in
M
is contained in the Euclidean ball of radius
r
at the same point by the distance-minimising property of geodesics, it suffices to show that this sufficient condition can be guaranteed over a Euclidean ball centred at
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
.

Fix
\delta\in[0,1)
: for any
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{p})
in the Euclidean ball
B_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},\delta y^{1/p})
, one has
\theta_{i}\in[(1-\delta)y^{1/p},(1+\delta)y^{1/p}]
. Consequently, for any
\theta\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},\delta y^{1/p})
one has

\displaystyle\min_{i}\theta_{i}^{-2}\geq(1+\delta)^{-2}y^{-2/p},\qquad\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}}(\theta)\leq\max_{i}\theta_{i}^{-2}\frac{s_{1}}{s_{1}}(\theta)\leq(1-\delta)^{-2}y^{-2/p}.

(109)

It follows that for all
\theta\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},\delta y^{1/p})
one has

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(3V_{2}-(s_{2}/s_{1})V_{1})(\theta)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{i}\theta_{i}^{-2}\big(3\min_{i}\theta_{i}^{-2}-(s_{2}/s_{1})(\theta)\big)

(110)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\delta)^{-2}y^{-2/p}\big(3(1+\delta)^{-2}y^{-2/p}-(1-\delta)^{-2}y^{-2/p}\big)

(111)

\displaystyle=(1+\delta)^{-4}(1-\delta)^{-2}y^{-4/p}\big(3(1-\delta)^{2}-(1+\delta)^{2}\big),

(112)

which is strictly greater than zero provided that
3(1-\delta)^{2}-(1+\delta)^{2}>0
, which holds provided that
0\leq\delta<2-\sqrt{3}
. This proves the result.
∎

Appendix C
The normal form of gradient descent

To prove Theorem
4.1
rigorously requires a number of steps.
First
, one must give leading order formulae for the expression of gradient descent in tubular neighbourhood coordinates. It is essential here that the errors in these leading order formulae are appropriately sharpened from their naive estimates using the structure of the tubular neighbourhood map (Lemma
C.2
) and the orthogonal stability assumption (Lemma
C.4
); without this sharpening our convergence theorems are impossible. These considerations culminate in an intermediate coordinate expression for gradient descent in Proposition
C.5
.
Second
, one must invoke Assumption
3.3
to transform the
\perp
-dynamics of Proposition
C.5
into the easily-analysed normal form quoted in Theorem
4.1
. This is formalised in Theorem
C.6
and concludes this section.

Throughout this section, given functions
g,h_{1},\dots,h_{s}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{l}
we will use the notation

\displaystyle g=O(h_{1},\dots,h_{s})

(119)

to mean that there exist constants
C_{1},\dots,C_{s}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|g(w)\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{s}C_{i}\|h_{i}(w)\|

(120)

for all
w
sufficiently close to zero.

In addition to this asymptotic notation, we will continue the notation of the main body of the paper, denoting by
M:=f^{-1}\{y\}
the submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
obtained as the preimage of a regular value
y\in\mathbb{R}
of an analytic map
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We will also denote by
n:=\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|
the unit normal along
M
. Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
are assumed in all of what follows. Assumption
3.4
is
not
needed for this section.

Consider the map
E:M\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
defined by

E(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp}):=\theta^{\parallel}+\theta^{\perp}n(\theta^{\parallel}).

(121)

Observe that

DE(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})=(I_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel})+\theta^{\perp}\,Dn(\theta^{\parallel}),n(\theta^{\parallel})),

(122)

where
I_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel}):T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M\rightarrow T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M
is the identity map. The derivative
DE(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})
acts as the linear map

T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M\oplus T_{\theta^{\perp}}\mathbb{R}\ni(v^{\parallel},v^{\perp})\mapsto v^{\parallel}+\theta^{\perp}\,Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel}]+v^{\perp}n(\theta^{\parallel})\in T_{E(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})}\mathbb{R}^{p}

(123)

between tangent spaces, and can equivalently be written in terms of the
shape operator

S(\theta^{\parallel}):T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M\ni v\mapsto-Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[v]\in T_{\theta}M
as

DE(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})=(I_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel})-\theta^{\perp}S(\theta^{\parallel}),n(\theta^{\parallel})).

(124)

Observe that since
n(\theta^{\parallel})
is orthogonal to
T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M
,
DE(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})
is invertible whenever
|\theta^{\perp}|<\|S(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{-1}
, where
\|S(\theta^{\parallel})\|
denotes the operator norm of
S(\theta^{\parallel}):T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M\rightarrow T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M
with respect to the Riemannian metric. Applying the inverse function theorem proves the following.

Proposition C.1
.

The map
E:M\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
is invertible on an open neighbourhood
U
of
M\times\{0\}
.
The image of
U
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a tubular neighbourhood
N
of
M
, and we denote the inverse of
E
thereon by
E^{-1}
.

The coordinates
E:(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\mapsto E(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})
are called
tubular neighbourhood coordinates
. The next step is to derive a formula for
E^{-1}
and thereafter derive a formula for the conjugate of gradient descent in the coordinates
(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})
induced by
E
. The following technical lemma will be necessary to get the correct asymptotics for the error terms in our formula for
E^{-1}
.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
be an open set and let
g:U\ni(x,y)\mapsto g(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a
C^{3}
function. Assume that
D^{k}_{y}g\equiv 0
on
U
for
k=2,3
and that
g
is invertible on
U
. Then for any
(x,y)\in U
and all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
sufficiently small, setting
(v_{x},v_{y})=Dg(x,y)^{-1}[v]
and
z:=g(x,y)
, the point
z+v
is contained in the domain of
g^{-1}
and one has

\displaystyle g^{-1}(z+v)=

\displaystyle g^{-1}(z)+Dg^{-1}(z)[v]+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}g^{-1}(z)[v,v]+O(\|v_{x}\|^{3},\|v_{x}\|^{2}\|v_{y}\|,\|v_{x}\|\|v_{y}\|^{2}).

(125)

Proof.

Since
g
is invertible on
U
and
U
is open,
g(U)=\text{domain}(g^{-1})
is also open. Fix
(x,y)\in U
and set
z:=g(x,y)
. Then since
g(U)
is open,
z+v\in g(U)
for all sufficiently small
v
so that
g^{-1}(z+v)
makes sense. Moreover, for all such
v
, there exists
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
such that
g((x,y)+u)=z+v
so that
g^{-1}(z+v)=(x,y)+u=g^{-1}(z)+u
. Hence one has the following formula for the remainder
R(v)
of the Taylor expansion of
g^{-1}(z+v)
about
z
:

\displaystyle R(v):=

\displaystyle g^{-1}(z+v)-g^{-1}(z)-Dg^{-1}(z)[v]-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}g^{-1}(z)[v,v]

(126)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u-Dg^{-1}(z)[v]-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}g^{-1}(z)[v,v].

(127)

To bound
R(v)
, therefore, we seek a formula
u(v)
for
u
in terms of
v
.

From
g((x,y)+u)=z+v
, one obtains

\displaystyle v=G(u):=Dg(x,y)[u]+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}g(x,y)[u,u]+S(u),

(128)

where the remainder
\tilde{u}\mapsto S(\tilde{u})
satisfies
S(\tilde{u})=O\big(\|\tilde{u}_{x}\|^{3},\|\tilde{u}_{x}\|^{2}\|\tilde{u}_{y}\|\big)
since
D^{k}_{y}g\equiv 0
for
k=2,3
by hypothesis. Since
Dg(x,y)
is invertible by the invertibility of
g
on
U
,
G
is smoothly invertible on a neighbourhood
V
of
0
by the inverse function theorem. Consider now the second-order approximation

\displaystyle u^{\prime}:=Dg(x,y)^{-1}\bigg(v-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}g(x,y)\big[Dg(x,y)^{-1}[v]^{\otimes 2}\big]\bigg)

(129)

to
u
. Since
u^{\prime}=O(\|v\|)
, taking
v
smaller if necessary we are guaranteed that
u^{\prime}\in V
. Thus there is
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime}-u\|\leq C\|G(u^{\prime})-G(u)\|=C\|G(u^{\prime})-v\|.

(130)

We will show that the right side of (
130
) admits a sufficiently tight bound
b(v)
that
u(v)=u^{\prime}(v)+O(b(v))
suffices to give the desired decay in
R(v)
.

For notational convenience, set
w:=(1/2)\,Dg(x,y)^{-1}D^{2}g(x,y)\big[Dg(x,y)^{-1}[v]^{\otimes 2}\big]
. Then
u^{\prime}=Dg(x,y)^{-1}[v]-w
, and substituting this expression into (
128
) yields

\displaystyle G(u^{\prime})-v=-D^{2}g(x,y)\big[Dg(x,y)^{-1}[v],w\big]+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}g(x,y)[w^{\otimes 2}]+S\bigg(Dg(x,y)^{-1}[v]-\frac{1}{2}w\bigg).

(131)

Since
D^{2}_{y}g\equiv 0
by hypothesis, one has
w=O(\|v_{x}\|^{2},\|v_{x}\|\|v_{y}\|)
as well as

\displaystyle D^{2}g(x,y)\big[Dg(x,y)^{-1}[v],w\big]=O(\|v_{x}\|^{3},\|v_{x}\|^{2}\|v_{y}\|,\|v_{x}\|\|v_{y}\|^{2}),

(132)

\displaystyle D^{2}g(x,y)[w^{\otimes 2}]=O(\|w\|^{2})=O(\|v_{x}\|^{4},\|v_{x}\|^{3}\|v_{y}\|,\|v_{x}\|^{2}\|v_{y}\|^{2})

(133)

and

\displaystyle S\bigg(Dg(x,y)^{-1}[v]-\frac{1}{2}w\bigg)=O(\|v_{x}\|^{3},\|v_{x}\|^{2}\|v_{y}\|).

(134)

Thus, by (
130
),

\displaystyle u=

\displaystyle u^{\prime}+O\big(\|v_{x}\|^{3},\|v_{x}\|^{2}\|v_{y}\|,\|v_{x}\|\|v_{y}\|^{2}\big)

(135)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Df(x,y)^{-1}[v]-\frac{1}{2}Df(x,y)^{-1}D^{2}f(x,y)\big[Df(x,y)^{-1}[v]^{\otimes 2}\big]+

(136)

\displaystyle+O\big(\|v_{x}\|^{3},\|v_{x}\|^{2}\|v_{y}\|,\|v_{x}\|\|v_{y}\|^{2}\big).

(137)

Finally, applying the chain rule to the equation
g^{-1}\circ g=\text{Identity}
shows that
Dg^{-1}(z)=Dg(x,y)^{-1}
and
D^{2}g^{-1}(z)=-Dg(x,y)^{-1}D^{2}g(x,y)[Dg(x,y)^{-1},Dg(x,y)^{-1}]
. It thus follows from substituting (
137
) into (
127
) that

\displaystyle R(v)

\displaystyle=u^{\prime}-Df^{-1}(z)[v]-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}f^{-1}(z)[v,v]+O\big(\|v_{x}\|^{3},\|v_{x}\|^{2}\|v_{y}\|,\|v_{x}\|\|v_{y}\|^{2}\big)

(138)

\displaystyle=O\big(\|v_{x}\|^{3},\|v_{x}\|^{2}\|v_{y}\|,\|v_{x}\|\|v_{y}\|^{2}\big)

(139)

as claimed.
∎

A formula can now be given for

using a Taylor expansion as follows.

Proposition C.3
.

For all
\theta^{\parallel}\in M
and all

\displaystyle E^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel}+v)=\begin{pmatrix}\theta^{\parallel}+v^{\parallel}+Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel}]v^{\perp}\\

The result follows from a second-order Taylor expansion with remainder:

\displaystyle E^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel}+v)=E^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel})+DE^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel})[v]+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}E^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel})[v,v]+\text{error}(\theta^{\parallel},v).

(141)

\theta^{\perp}

(142)

\displaystyle DE(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\bigg[\begin{pmatrix}v^{\parallel}\\

\displaystyle D^{2}E(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\bigg[\begin{pmatrix}v^{\parallel}\\

\displaystyle D\bigg(DF(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\bigg[\begin{pmatrix}v^{\parallel}\\
v^{\perp}\end{pmatrix}\bigg]\bigg)\bigg[\begin{pmatrix}v^{\parallel}\\
v^{\perp}\end{pmatrix}\bigg]

(144)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{\theta^{\parallel}}\big(v^{\parallel}+\theta^{\perp}Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel}]+v^{\perp}n(\theta^{\parallel})\big)[v^{\parallel}]+

(145)

\displaystyle+D_{\theta^{\perp}}\big(v^{\parallel}+\theta^{\perp}Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel}]+v^{\perp}n(\theta^{\parallel})\big)[v^{\perp}]

(146)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta^{\perp}D^{2}n(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel},v^{\parallel}]+v^{\perp}Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel}]+v^{\perp}Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel}]

(147)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta^{\perp}D^{2}n(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel},v^{\parallel}]+2v^{\perp}Dn(\theta^{\parallel})[v^{\parallel}],

(148)

for any
(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\in M\times\mathbb{R}
and
(v^{\parallel},v^{\perp})\in T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M\times\mathbb{R}
. Recalling that
E

\displaystyle DE^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel})=DE^{-1}\circ E(\theta^{\parallel},0)=(DE(\theta^{\parallel},0))^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel})\\

\displaystyle DE^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel})[v]=\begin{pmatrix}v^{\parallel}\\

yields

\displaystyle D^{2}E^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel})

\displaystyle=D^{2}E^{-1}\circ E(\theta^{\parallel},0)=-DE(\theta^{\parallel},0)^{-1}\,D^{2}E(\theta^{\parallel},0)[DE(\theta^{\parallel},0)^{-1},DE(\theta^{\parallel},0)^{-1}],

\displaystyle D^{2}E^{-1}(\theta^{\parallel})[v,v]=\begin{pmatrix}P_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel})\\

Lemma C.4

We are now able to give the tubular neighbourhood coordinate expression for gradient descent.

Proposition C.5
.

In the tubular neighbourhood coordinates
E:(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\mapsto E(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})

\mathrm{GD}_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}^{\ell}(\eta,\cdot):\theta\mapsto\theta-\eta\nabla\ell(\theta)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\theta^{\parallel}\\
\theta^{\perp}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\theta^{\parallel}-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})+O\big((\theta^{\perp})^{3}\min\{1,d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\}\big)\\
(1-\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}))\theta^{\perp}-\frac{\eta}{2}D^{3}\ell[n^{\otimes 3}](\theta^{\parallel})(\theta^{\perp})^{2}-\frac{\eta}{6}D^{4}\ell[n^{\otimes 4}](\theta^{\parallel})(\theta^{\perp})^{3}+O((\theta^{\perp})^{4})\end{pmatrix}

(154)

Proof.

Since

\mathrm{GD}^{\ell}_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}(\eta,\cdot)

\displaystyle=P_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel})v=-\eta P_{TM}(\theta^{\parallel})\nabla\ell(E(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp}))

Appendix D
Convergence theorems

The purpose of this section is to provide proofs of the main convergence theorems presented in the paper. In addition to assuming Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
as in the previous section, in this section we will also assume Assumption
3.4
, which states that there is a geodesically convex ball
B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
centred on
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
over which
\lambda
is geodesically smooth, geodesically strongly convex, and has unique global minimum at
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
with Riemannian Hessian at
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
being a multiple of the identity.

D.1
Subcritical regime

Our convergence theorem in the subcritical regime has two components. First, it is proved that gradient descent implicitly descends
\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})
from being initially
>2/\eta
to eventually being
<2/\eta
. For this, it is necessary to have a descent lemma for the perturbed Riemannian gradient descent component of Theorem
4.1
(Lemma
D.1
) as well as upper and lower bounds on
|\theta^{\perp}_{t}|
during this phase (Lemma
D.3
). Following this transient phase, convergence is easily proved using the fact that
|\theta^{\perp}_{t}|
contracts exponentially to zero.

Lemma D.1

(Descent lemma for perturbed Riemannian gradient descent)
.

For the
\mu
-geodesically strongly convex,
L
-geodesically smooth function
\lambda
on the geodesically convex ball
B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
centred on
\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
in
M
, consider the map

\displaystyle\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})=\exp_{\theta^{\parallel}}\bigg(\frac{-\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\bigg)+O\big((\theta^{\perp})^{3}\min\{1,d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\}\big)

(175)

of Theorem
C.6
. Then for any
\eta>0
and all
\theta^{\perp}
sufficiently close to zero,
\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\cdot,\theta^{\perp})
maps
B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
into
B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
. Moreover, for any
\eta>0
, any
\theta^{\parallel}\in B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
and all
\theta^{\perp}
sufficiently close to zero one has

\displaystyle\lambda\big(\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\big)-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\leq\bigg(1-\frac{\mu\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{4c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\bigg)(\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})).

(176)

Proof.

For
\theta^{\perp}
sufficiently small, there is unique
\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})=O\big((\theta^{\perp})^{3}\min\{1,d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\}\big)
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})=\exp_{\theta^{\parallel}}\bigg(-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})+\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\bigg).

(177)

To see that
\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\cdot,\theta^{\perp})
preserves the ball
B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
, consider the function
\rho(\theta^{\parallel}):=(1/2)d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{2}
. By the Gauss lemma
[
27
, Corollary 6.10]
,
\nabla_{M}\rho(\theta^{\parallel})=-\log_{\theta^{\parallel}}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
. Fixing
\theta^{\parallel}\in B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
, for any vector
w\in T_{\theta^{\parallel}}M
one therefore has

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\rho\bigg(\exp_{\theta^{\parallel}}\big(-t\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})+t^{3/2}w\big),\theta^{\parallel}_{*}\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla_{M}\rho(\theta^{\parallel}),\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\rangle

(178)

\displaystyle=\langle\log_{\theta^{\parallel}}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*}),\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\rangle

(179)

\displaystyle\leq-2\mu\rho(\theta^{\parallel})<0,

(180)

where the third line follows from Lemma
E.2
. Thus, for all
\theta^{\perp}
sufficiently small, we are assured that
\rho\big(\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\big)<\rho(\theta^{\parallel})
, implying that
\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\in B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
.

We now come to the descent lemma. Since
B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
is geodesically convex, we may perform a covariant Taylor expansion and invoke the geodesic smoothness of
\lambda
to obtain

\displaystyle\lambda\big(\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\big)\leq

\displaystyle\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})+\bigg\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}),-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})+\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\bigg\rangle+

(181)

\displaystyle+\frac{L}{2}\bigg\|\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\bigg\|^{2}

(182)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\bigg(1-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}L}{4c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\bigg)\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}+

(183)

\displaystyle+\bigg(1-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}L}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\bigg)\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}),\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\|^{2}.

(184)

From Cauchy-Schwarz and the estimate
0\leq(\epsilon^{1/2}a-\epsilon^{-1/2}b)^{2}
(implying that
ab\leq(1/2)(\epsilon a^{2}+\epsilon^{-1}b^{2})
) with
\epsilon=\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}/(4c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}))
, one obtains

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}),\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|\|\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\|

(185)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{8c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}+\frac{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}\|\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\|^{2}.

(186)

Taking
|\theta^{\perp}|
sufficiently small that
\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}L/(4c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}))\leq 1/4
, therefore, one obtains

\displaystyle\lambda\big(\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{4c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}+\bigg(\frac{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}+\frac{L}{2}\bigg)\|\delta(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\|^{2}

(187)

\displaystyle=\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{4c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}+O\big((\theta^{\perp})^{4}\min\{1,d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{2}\big).

(188)

Subtracting
\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
from both sides and applying geodesic strong convexity (
\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|^{2}\geq 2\mu(\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*}))
by Lemma
E.3
) yields

\displaystyle\lambda\big(\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})\big)-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\leq\bigg(1-\frac{\mu\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\bigg)(\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*}))+O\big((\theta^{\perp})^{4}\min\{1,d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{2}\}\big).

(189)

Finally, invoking geodesic strong convexity once more to estimate
d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{2}\leq(2/\mu)\big(\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\big)
as in Lemma
E.1
and taking
\theta^{\perp}
sufficiently small allows one to absorb the remainder term on the left side into the
\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
factor and yields the result..
∎

The next result will be used in giving uniform bounds on the magnitudes of the
\theta^{\perp}
iterates.

Lemma D.2
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, define
f_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(x):=-(1+\alpha)x+x^{3}+O(x^{4}).

(190)

For
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{R}

f_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}}:=f_{\alpha_{1}}\circ f_{\alpha_{0}}

\gamma>0

1.

[-2\sqrt{\gamma},2\sqrt{\gamma}]

2.

admits the sole fixed points
0
,

\displaystyle\xi_{-}=-\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}}{2}}+O(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}),\qquad\xi_{+}=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}}{2}}+O(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1})

(191)

in the interval
[-2\sqrt{\gamma},2\sqrt{\gamma}]

Proof.

For
\gamma>0
small and all
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\in[0,\gamma]

\displaystyle f_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}}(x)=(1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0})x-2x^{3}+O(\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}x,\alpha_{1}x^{3},\alpha_{0}x^{3},x^{4}),

(192)

\displaystyle Df_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}}(x)=1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0}-6x^{2}+O(\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}x^{2},\alpha_{0}x^{2},x^{3}).

(193)

In particular, for all
|x|\leq 2\sqrt{\gamma}

\displaystyle Df_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}}(x)\geq 1+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0}-24\gamma+O(\gamma^{3/2})>0

(194)

for all
\gamma
sufficiently small, so that
f_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}}|_{[-2\sqrt{\gamma},2\sqrt{\gamma}]}
is monotonically increasing. Set

\displaystyle\Phi(x):=f_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}}(x)-x=2\beta x-2x^{3}+O(\beta^{2}x,\beta x^{3},x^{4}).

(195)

Fixed points of
f_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}}
are precisely roots of
\Phi
, one of which is clearly
x=0
. Moreover, if
\beta=0
, then
\Phi(x)=-2x^{3}+O(x^{4})
so that in this case

is the only root of
\Phi
on
[-2\sqrt{\gamma},2\sqrt{\gamma}]
for all
\gamma
sufficiently small. Suppose now that
\beta>0
. We will demonstrate the existence of a unique root
\xi_{+}
of
\Phi

(0,2\sqrt{\gamma}]

\gamma
sufficiently small,
\Phi(x)
is strictly positive on
(0,2^{-1/2}\sqrt{\beta}]
and so cannot admit any roots therein. However, observe that for all
\gamma
sufficiently small one has

\displaystyle D\Phi(x)=2\beta-6x^{2}+O(\beta^{2},\beta x^{2},x^{3})<0

(196)

for all

\Phi
is monotonically decreasing on
[2^{-1/2}\sqrt{\beta},2\sqrt{\gamma}]
, and thus admits

one root therein. Since moreover
\Phi(2^{-1/2}\sqrt{\beta})>0

one root
\xi_{+}
in

\sqrt{\beta}

\displaystyle 0=\Phi(\xi_{+})=\Phi(\sqrt{\beta})+D\Phi(z)(\xi_{+}-\sqrt{\beta}),

Lemma D.3

(0,2\sqrt{\alpha_{0}}]
, either

\displaystyle\theta^{\perp}_{2t}\leq\xi_{2t+1,2t,+}\Rightarrow\theta^{\perp}_{2t}\leq f_{2t+1,2t}(\theta^{\perp}_{2t})=\theta^{\perp}_{2t+2}\leq\xi_{2t+1,2t,+}\leq 2\sqrt{\alpha_{0}},

(204)

or

\displaystyle\theta^{\perp}_{2t}\geq\xi_{2t+1,2t,+}\Rightarrow\xi_{2t+1,2t,+}\leq f_{2t+1,2t}(\theta^{\perp}_{2t})=\theta^{\perp}_{2t+2}\leq\theta^{\perp}_{2t}\leq 2\sqrt{\alpha_{0}}

(205)

by the inductive hypothesis; in either case
\theta^{\perp}_{2t+2}\leq 2\sqrt{\alpha_{0}}
, thus proving the claim. A symmetric argument shows that the odd iterates satisfy
|\theta^{\perp}_{2t+1}|\leq 2\sqrt{\alpha_{0}}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha_{s}\geq 0
for all
s\leq 2t
, thus proving the upper-bound in (
200
).

We now prove the lower bound. Invoking the upper bound,
\alpha_{0}
can be taken sufficiently small such that the recursive estimate

\displaystyle|\theta^{\perp}_{t+1}|

\displaystyle=|\theta^{\perp}_{t}|(1+\alpha_{t}-(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}+O((\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{3}))

(206)

\displaystyle\geq|\theta^{\perp}_{t}|(1-2(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2})

(207)

holds as long as
\alpha_{t}\geq 0
. By taking
\alpha_{0}
even smaller if necessary, squaring, taking the reciprocal of both sides and applying
1/(1-x)^{2}\leq 1+3x
for
x
sufficiently small one deduces that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\theta^{\perp}_{t+1})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}}(1+6(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2})\Rightarrow\frac{1}{(\theta^{\perp}_{t+1})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}}\leq 6,

(208)

from which it follows by a telescoping sum that

\displaystyle|\theta^{\perp}_{t}|\geq\frac{|\theta^{\perp}_{0}|}{\sqrt{1+6(\theta^{\perp}_{0})^{2}t}}

(209)

for all
t
such that
\alpha_{t}\geq 0
. This proves the claim.
∎

Theorem D.4
.

Assume that
\eta<2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
, and let
0<c,C<\infty
satisfy
c\leq c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})\leq C
for all
\theta^{\parallel}\in B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
. Then for all
\theta^{\perp}_{0}
sufficiently close to zero and all
\theta^{\parallel}_{0}\neq\theta^{\parallel}_{*}\in B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
, if
\eta>2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{0})
is sufficiently small, then there is
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
, with

\displaystyle\tau\leq\tau(\theta^{\parallel}_{0},\theta^{\perp}_{0}):=\bigg\lceil\frac{1}{6(\theta^{\perp}_{0})^{2}}\bigg(\frac{2/\eta-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})}{\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{0})-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})}\bigg)^{-\frac{24C}{\mu\eta}}\bigg\rceil,

(210)

such that
\eta<2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})
for all
t\geq\tau
and
\eta\geq 2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})
for all
t<\tau
. Consequently, setting
\beta:=1-(2-\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{\tau}))<1
, the iterates
(\theta^{\parallel}_{t},\theta^{\perp}_{t})
converge to a global minimum
(\theta^{\parallel}_{\infty},0)
of
\ell
in
M
with suboptimal sharpness

\displaystyle\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{\infty})-\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\geq\exp\bigg(-\frac{4\eta L^{2}}{c\mu}\frac{(\theta^{\perp}_{\tau})^{2}}{1-\beta^{2}}\bigg)(\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{\tau})-\lambda_{*}).

(211)

at a rate
O(\beta^{t})
.

Proof.

For any
\theta^{\parallel}_{0}\neq\theta^{\parallel}_{*}
, take
\theta^{\perp}_{0}\neq 0
sufficiently close to zero that the hypotheses of Lemma
D.1
and Lemma
D.3
are satisfied, with
\lambda_{0}
in Lemma
D.3
taken to be equal to
\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{0})
, and assume that
\eta\lambda_{0}>0
is sufficiently small as in Lemma
D.3
. Then we may assume that
\lambda_{t}:=\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})
is monotonically decreasing and the bounds of Lemma
D.3
apply to
\theta^{\perp}_{t}
. Since
\{\lambda_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotonically decreasing, any
\tau
satisfying
\eta<2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})
for all
t\geq\tau
and
\eta\geq 2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})
for all
t<\tau
is necessarily unique. We claim that this
\tau
satisfies the upper bound
\tau\leq\tau(\theta^{\parallel}_{0},\theta^{\perp}_{0})
. Indeed, suppose for a contradiction that
\tau>\tau(\theta^{\perp}_{0},\theta^{\parallel}_{0})
. Then for all
t<\tau
, denoting
\lambda_{*}:=\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
, one would have

\displaystyle\lambda_{t+1}-\lambda_{*}\leq\bigg(1-\frac{\mu\eta}{4c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})}{\frac{(\theta^{\perp}_{0})^{2}}{(1+6(\theta^{\perp}_{0})^{2}t)}}\bigg)(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{*})

(212)

by Lemmas
D.1
and
D.3
. Invoking
c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})\leq C
, taking logarithms, applying the upper-bound
\ln(1-x)\leq-x
and applying a telescoping sum then yield

\displaystyle\ln(\lambda_{t})-\ln(\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\mu\eta}{24C}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{6(\theta^{\perp}_{0})^{2}}+s}

(213)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\mu\eta}{24C}\ln(1+6(\theta^{\perp}_{0})^{2}t)

(214)

by Lemma
E.4
, so that

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}\leq(\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{*})(1+6(\theta^{\perp}_{0})^{2}t)^{-\frac{\mu\eta}{24C}}+\lambda_{*}.

(215)

However, plugging in
t=\tau(\theta^{\perp}_{0},\theta^{\parallel}_{0})
then reveals that
\lambda_{t}<2/\eta
, thus contradicting the assumption that
\tau>\tau(\theta^{\perp}_{0},\theta^{\parallel}_{0})
and implying that
\tau\leq\tau(\theta^{\perp}_{0},\theta^{\parallel}_{0})
as claimed.

We now come to the final convergence and sharpness suboptimality claim. For all
t\geq\tau
, Lemma
D.1
continues to imply that
\lambda_{t}<2/\eta
decreases monotonically, while
\theta^{\perp}_{t}
contracts exponentially according to the estimate

\displaystyle|\theta^{\perp}_{t+1}|=|\theta^{\perp}_{t}||1+(\eta\lambda_{t}-2)-(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}+O(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{3}|\leq|\theta^{\perp}_{t}||1-(2-\eta\lambda_{t})|\leq|\theta^{\perp}_{t}||1-(2-\eta\lambda_{\tau})|.

(216)

This proves the claim that the iterates
(\theta^{\parallel}_{t},\theta^{\perp}_{t})
converge to a global minimum
\theta^{\parallel}_{\infty}
of
\ell
at rate
O\big((1-(2-\eta\lambda_{\tau}))^{t}\big)
. We now turn to proving the suboptimality of the sharpness at
\theta^{\parallel}_{\infty}
. The geodesic strong convexity of
\lambda
in the form of Lemma
E.2
implies that

\displaystyle\mu d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{2}\leq\langle\Pi_{\theta^{\parallel}\rightarrow\theta^{\parallel}_{*}}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}),\log_{\theta^{\parallel}_{*}}(\theta^{\parallel})\rangle\leq\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|\,d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})

(217)

for any
\theta^{\parallel}\in B_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},r)
, so that the
\parallel
-update can be written

\displaystyle\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})

\displaystyle=\exp_{\theta^{\parallel}}\bigg(-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})+O\big((\theta^{\perp})^{3}\min\{1,d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\}\big)\bigg)

(218)

\displaystyle=\exp_{\theta^{\parallel}}\bigg(-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel})}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})+O\big((\theta^{\perp})^{3}\min\{1,\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|\}\big)\bigg).

(219)

Consequently, invoking the geodesic strong convexity of
\lambda
once again in the form of Lemma
E.1
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{t+1}\geq

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}+\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t}),\log_{\theta^{\parallel}_{t}}(\theta^{\parallel}_{t+1})\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\log_{\theta^{\parallel}_{t}}(\theta^{\parallel}_{t+1})\|^{2}

(220)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}-\bigg\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t}),\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})+O\big((\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{3}\min\{1,\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})\|\}\big)\bigg\rangle

(221)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu}{2}\bigg\|\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})}\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})+O\big((\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{3}\min\{1,\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})\|\}\big)\bigg\|^{2}

(222)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})}\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})\|^{2}(1+O(\theta^{\perp}_{t}))

(223)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}}{c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})}\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{t})\|^{2}

(224)

for sufficiently small
2\sqrt{\eta\lambda_{0}-2}>|\theta^{\perp}_{t}|
. Subtracting
\lambda_{*}
from both sides and invoking the Lipschitz gradients (
\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})\|\leq Ld_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
) and strong convexity (
(\mu/2)d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{2}\leq\lambda(\theta^{\parallel})-\lambda_{*}
from Lemma
E.1
) properties of
\lambda
then yield

\displaystyle\lambda_{t+1}-\lambda_{*}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{*}-\frac{\eta(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}}{c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})}L^{2}d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{t},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{2}

(225)

\displaystyle\geq(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{*})\bigg(1-\frac{2\eta(\theta^{\perp}_{t})^{2}L^{2}}{c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})\mu}\bigg)

(226)

\displaystyle\geq(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{*})\bigg(1-\frac{2\eta L^{2}(\theta^{\perp}_{\tau})^{2}}{c\mu}\beta^{2t}\bigg),

(227)

where in the final line we have invoked
c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{t})\geq c
. It follows that

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{*}\geq\prod_{s=\tau}^{t-1}\bigg(1-\frac{2\eta L^{2}(\theta^{\perp}_{\tau})^{2}}{c\mu}\beta^{2s}\bigg)(\lambda_{\tau}-\lambda_{*}).

(228)

Taking
2\sqrt{\eta\lambda_{0}-2}>|\theta^{\perp}_{\tau}|
smaller if necessary and using the bound
\ln(1-x)\geq-2x
valid for small
x
one has

\displaystyle\ln\bigg(\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}\bigg(1-\frac{2\eta L^{2}(\theta^{\perp}_{\tau})^{2}}{c\mu}\beta^{2s}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{4\eta L^{2}(\theta^{\perp}_{\tau})^{2}}{c\mu}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\beta^{2s}

(229)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{4\eta L^{2}(\theta^{\perp}_{\tau})^{2}}{c\mu}\frac{1}{1-\beta^{2}}

(230)

from which the result follows.
∎

D.2
Critical regime

The convergence theorem in the critical regime is the most difficult to prove. This is because in this regime, the fixed point to which the iterates converge is merely
parabolic
rather than
hyperbolic
as is (at least asymptotically) true in the subcritical and supercritical regimes. The convergence theorem in the critical regime will follow from a careful analysis of the following parabolic system.

Fix
0<a<c
and
b>0
. Let
A(x,y)=O(x,y)
,
B(x)=O(x)
and
C(y)=O(y)
be analytic functions, and consider the analytic functions

\displaystyle T_{x}(x,y)=\big(1-(a+A(x,y))y^{2}\big)x,\qquad T_{y}(x,y)=\big(1+(b+B(x))x^{2}-(c+C(y))y^{2}\big)y.

(231)

Denote by
T=(T_{x},T_{y}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
the corresponding analytic map. Clearly
0
is a fixed point of
T
; we will be concerned with proving convergence of the iterates of
T
to this fixed point. The following result guarantees that
T
admits an invariant manifold approaching the origin. This result is essential, since our final convergence proof is a two-stage proof; first, convergence to a neighbourhood of the invariant manifold is proved, following which, second, convergence to the origin is easy.

Lemma D.5
.

There exists
r>0
and a function
u:\{x>0:|x^{2}-r|<r\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
whose graph
\Gamma_{u}:=\{(x,u(x)):x\in\text{dom}(u)\}
is invariant under
T
, and for which
u(x)=\sqrt{\frac{b}{c-a}}x+O(x^{2})
.

Proof.

The analytic map
T:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
extends uniquely to a holomorphic map
\tilde{T}
defined on a neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Like
T
, at the origin the map
\tilde{T}
is the identity to first order, vanishes at order two, and has third order Taylor coefficients given by

\displaystyle\partial_{xyy}^{3}T_{x}(0,0)=-2a,\quad\partial^{3}_{yxx}T_{y}(0,0)=2b,\quad\partial^{3}_{yyy}T_{y}(0,0)=-6c,

(232)

with all other third order coefficients being zero. Observe that on the vector
v
given by

\displaystyle v=\bigg(1,\sqrt{\frac{b}{c-a}}\bigg)

(233)

one has

\displaystyle\partial^{3}T(0,0)[v,v,v]

\displaystyle=\bigg(-6a\frac{b}{c-a},6b\sqrt{\frac{b}{c-a}}-6c\sqrt{\frac{b}{c-a}}^{3}\bigg)

(234)

\displaystyle=-6a\sqrt{\frac{b}{c-a}}^{3}\,v,

(235)

so that
v
is a non-degenerate characteristic direction of
\tilde{T}

[
6
, Definition 4.1]
, which is moreover attracting since
-6a\sqrt{b/(c-a)}<0
. By
[
6
, Section 6]
, therefore, there exists
r>0
and a holomorphic function
u:\{x\in\mathbb{C}:|x^{2}-r|<r\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that
u(x)=\sqrt{\frac{b}{c-a}}x+O(x^{2})
whose graph is invariant under
T
. Restricting this
u
to the intersection of its domain with the positive real axis yields the result.
∎

The next lemma will be key in establishing convergence to the invariant manifold.

Lemma D.6
.

Let
u(x)=\sqrt{\frac{b}{c-a}}x+O(x^{2})
be the function from Lemma
D.5
, and define
\Delta(x,y):=y-u(x)
. Then
\widetilde{\Delta}(x,y):=\Delta(x,y)/x
satisfies

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\Delta}\circ T(x,y)|\leq|\widetilde{\Delta}(x,y)|\bigg(1-\frac{c-a}{2}y^{2}\bigg)

(236)

for all sufficiently small
x>0
,
y\geq 0
.

Proof.

We estimate
\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}\circ T}{\widetilde{\Delta}}
. Define

\displaystyle D(x,y):=\frac{T_{x}(x,y)}{x},\qquad N(x,y):=\frac{\Delta\circ T(x,y)}{\Delta(x,y)},\qquad(x,y)\in\text{dom}(u)\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

(237)

Observe that both
D
and
N
are analytic on the domain of
u
. Observe moreover that

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}\circ T(x,y)}{\widetilde{\Delta}(x,y)}=\frac{\Delta\circ T(x,y)}{T_{x}(x,y)}\cdot\frac{x}{\Delta(x,y)}=\frac{N(x,y)}{D(x,y)}=1-\frac{D(x,y)-N(x,y)}{D(x,y)},

(238)

so that estimation of
\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}\circ T}{\widetilde{\Delta}}
is reduced to estimating
\frac{D-N}{D}
. We first estimate
D-N
. For this, observe that since
T(x,0)=(x,0)
, one has
D(x,0)-N(x,0)=0
for all
x\in\text{dom}(u)
. Thus, by analyticity of
D
and
N
, there is a unique analytic function
H
on
\text{dom}(u)\times\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle D(x,y)-N(x,y)=yH(x,y).

(239)

We compute
H(x,y)
to second order. For notational convenience, set

\displaystyle\kappa:=\sqrt{\frac{b}{c-a}}

(240)

so that
u(x)=\kappa x+O(x^{2})
. Differentiate (
239
) with respect to
y
and evaluate at
y=0
to obtain

\displaystyle H(x,0)=\partial_{y}D(x,0)-\partial_{y}N(x,0),

(241)

Observe that

\displaystyle\partial_{y}D(x,0)=\partial_{y}(1-ay^{2}+A(x,y)y^{2})|_{y=0}=0

(242)

while, from
N=(\Delta\circ T)/\Delta
, one has

\displaystyle\partial_{y}N(x,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta\,\partial_{y}(\Delta\circ T)-(\Delta\circ T)\,\partial_{y}\Delta}{\Delta^{2}}(x,0).

(243)

From
\Delta(x,y)=y-u(x)
, is easily seen that

\displaystyle\Delta(x,0)=-u(x),\qquad\partial_{y}\Delta(x,0)=1,\qquad\Delta\circ T(x,0)=-u(T_{x}(x,0))=-u(x)

(244)

while

\displaystyle\partial_{y}(\Delta\circ T)(x,0)=\partial_{y}T_{y}(x,0)-u^{\prime}(x)\partial_{y}T_{x}(x,0)=1+bx^{2}+O(x^{3})

(245)

so that (
243
) yields

\displaystyle H(x,0)=-\partial_{y}N(x,0)=\frac{bx^{2}+O(x^{3})}{u(x)}=(c-a)\kappa x+O(x^{2}).

(246)

To compute the second order component of
H
, differentiate (
239
) twice with respect to
y
and evaluate at
y=0
to obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{y}H(x,0)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\partial^{2}_{y}D(x,0)-\partial_{y}^{2}N(x,0)\big).

(247)

One sees that

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{y}D(x,0)=\partial^{2}_{y}(1-ay^{2}+A(x,y)y^{2})|_{y=0}=-2a+O(x),

(248)

while

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{y}N(x,0)=\bigg(\frac{\partial^{2}_{y}(\Delta\circ T)}{\Delta}-\frac{(\Delta\circ T)\,\partial^{2}_{y}\Delta}{\Delta^{2}}-2\frac{\partial_{y}(\Delta\circ T)\,\partial_{y}\Delta}{\Delta^{2}}+2\frac{(\Delta\circ T)\,(\partial_{y}\Delta)^{2}}{\Delta^{3}}\bigg)(x,0).

(249)

Since

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{y}(\Delta\circ T)(x,0)=\partial^{2}_{y}T_{y}(x,0)-u^{\prime\prime}(x)(\partial_{y}T_{x}(x,0))^{2}-u^{\prime}(x)\partial^{2}_{y}T_{x}(x,0)=2a\kappa x+O(x^{2})

(250)

one obtains

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{y}N(x,0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2a\kappa x+O(x^{2})}{\kappa x+O(x^{2})}-2\frac{1+bx^{2}+O(x^{3})}{\kappa^{2}x^{2}+O(x^{3})}+2\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}x^{2}+O(x^{3})}

(251)

\displaystyle=-2a-2b/\kappa^{2}+O(x)=-2c+O(x).

(252)

Thus

\displaystyle\partial_{y}H(x,0)=\frac{1}{2}(-2a+2c+O(x))=(c-a)+O(x).

(253)

We deduce from (
246
), (
253
) and (
239
) that

\displaystyle D(x,y)-N(x,y)=(c-a)(y^{2}+\kappa yx)+O(yx^{2},y^{2}x,y^{3}).

(254)

It follows that for all sufficiently small
x>0,y\geq 0
, one has
D(x,y)-N(x,y)\geq\frac{(c-a)}{2}y^{2}
and
1\geq D(x,y)>0
so that

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}\circ T(x,y)}{\widetilde{\Delta}(x,y)}=1-\frac{D(x,y)-N(x,y)}{D(x,y)}\leq 1-\frac{c-a}{2}y^{2}

(255)

as claimed.
∎

Lemma
D.7
below gives uniform upper-bounds on the magnitudes of iterates, which are used in enabling certain estimates in the convergence theorem.

Lemma D.7
.

Define
W(x,y):=bx^{2}+\frac{a}{2}y^{2}
. Then for all
(x_{0},y_{0})
in a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
0
, with
y_{0}>0
, the sequence
x_{t}
is monotonically non-increasing and the sequence
y_{t}
satisfies the uniform bound
y_{t}^{2}\leq 2W(x_{0},y_{0})/a
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

In any sufficiently small neighbourhood
U
of
0\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
, one has

\displaystyle W(T(x,y))

\displaystyle=bx^{2}\big(1-ay^{2}+O(xy^{2},y^{3}))\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}ay^{2}\big(1+bx^{2}-cy^{2}+O(x^{3},y^{3})\big)^{2}

(256)

\displaystyle=bx^{2}(1-2ay^{2})+O(x^{3}y^{2},x^{2}y^{3})+\frac{1}{2}ay^{2}(1+2bx^{2}-2cy^{2})+O(x^{3}y^{2},y^{5})

(257)

\displaystyle=W(x,y)-abx^{2}y^{2}-acy^{4}+O(x^{3}y^{2},x^{2}y^{3},y^{5})

(258)

\displaystyle\leq W(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}(abx^{2}+acy^{2})

(259)

for all
(x,y)\in U
,
so that
W
is non-increasing as a function of the iterates of the map close to zero.

Now fix
(x_{0},y_{0})
sufficiently small that, with
W_{0}:=W(x_{0},y_{0})
one has
(x_{0},\sqrt{2W_{0}/a})
contained in
U
. Then in particular
(x_{0},y_{0})
is contained in
U
since
y_{0}\leq\sqrt{2W_{0}/a}
. Suppose as an inductive hypothesis that
y_{t}\leq\sqrt{2W_{0}/a}
,
x_{t}\leq x_{0}
and
W_{t}\leq W_{0}
. Shrinking
U
yet further if necessary, one sees that

\displaystyle 1>1-y_{t}^{2}+O(x_{t}y_{t}^{2},y_{t}^{3})>0

(260)

thus yielding
x_{t+1}<x_{t}\leq x_{0}
. Moreover, the inductive hypothesis implies that
(x_{t},y_{t})\in U
; thus, denoting
W_{t}:=W(x_{t},y_{t})
and
W_{t+1}:=W(x_{t+1},y_{t+1})
, one sees that

\displaystyle W_{t+1}\leq W_{t}-\frac{1}{2}ay_{t}^{2}(bx_{t}^{2}+cy_{t}^{2})\leq W_{0},

(261)

so that
y_{t+1}\leq\sqrt{2W_{t+1}/a}\leq\sqrt{2W_{0}/a}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Finally we can prove convergence of the iterates of
T
toward zero.

Theorem D.8
.

Set
\kappa:=\sqrt{b/(c-a)}
. For all sufficiently small
x_{0}\geq 0
and
y_{0}>0
and all
0<\epsilon<\kappa
, there is

\displaystyle\tau\equiv\tau(y_{0},\epsilon)=O(y_{0}^{-2}\epsilon^{-3c/(c-a)})

(262)

so that for all
t\geq\tau
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{x_{\tau}}{\sqrt{1+3a(\kappa+\epsilon)^{2}x_{\tau}^{2}(t-\tau)}}\leq x_{t}\leq\frac{x_{\tau}}{\sqrt{1+2a(\kappa-\epsilon)^{2}x_{\tau}^{2}(t-\tau)}}

(263)

and

\displaystyle(\kappa-\epsilon/2)x_{t}\leq y_{t}\leq(\kappa+\epsilon/2)x_{t}.

(264)

In particular,
x_{t},y_{t}=\Theta(t^{-1/2})
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
D.7
, we may take
(x_{0},y_{0})
sufficiently small that the quantity
\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}:=\widetilde{\Delta}(x_{t},y_{t})
of Lemma
D.6
satisfies the recursion

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t+1}|\leq|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}|\bigg(1-\frac{c-a}{2}y_{t}^{2}\bigg)

(265)

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. By taking
(x_{0},y_{0})
yet smaller if necessary we may also assume that
y_{t}
satisfies the lower-bound

\displaystyle y_{t+1}\geq y_{t}(1-2cy_{t}^{2}).

(266)

Squaring and taking the reciprocal yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{y_{t+1}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{y_{t}^{2}}\frac{1}{1-2cy_{t}^{2}},

(267)

and, taking
(x_{0},y_{0})
yet smaller if necessary so that
1/(1-2cy_{t}^{2})\leq 1+6y_{t}^{2}
for all
t
, one obtains the recursive estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{y_{t+1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{y_{t}^{2}}\leq 6c,

(268)

from which one obtains the bound

\displaystyle y_{t}^{2}\geq\frac{y_{0}^{2}}{1+6cy_{0}^{2}t}

(269)

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
by telescoping. Plugging this into the recursion for
\widetilde{\Delta}
and taking logarithms, one has

\displaystyle\ln|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t+1}|-\ln|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}|\leq-\frac{c-a}{2}\frac{y_{0}^{2}}{1+6cy_{0}^{2}t}.

(270)

Taking a telescoping sum yields

\displaystyle\ln|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}|-\ln|\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}|\leq-\frac{c-a}{12c}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{6cy_{0}^{2}}+s}\leq-\frac{c-a}{12c}\ln(1+6cy_{0}^{2}t)

(271)

by Lemma
E.4

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}|\leq|\widetilde{\Delta}_{0}|(1+6cy_{0}^{2}t)^{-(c-a)/(12c)},

(272)

and for any

one sees that for any

greater than or equal to

\displaystyle\tau(y_{0},\epsilon):=\bigg\lceil\frac{1}{6cy_{0}^{2}}\bigg(\bigg(\frac{\epsilon}{4|\Delta_{0}|}\bigg)^{-12c/(c-a)}-1\bigg)\bigg\rceil

(273)

one has
|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}|<\epsilon/4

\widetilde{\Delta}(x,y):=(y-\kappa x+O(x^{2}))/x
that

implies

\displaystyle y_{t}\in[\kappa x-(\epsilon/4)x+O(x^{2}),\kappa x+(\epsilon/4)x+O(x^{2})]\subset[(\kappa-\epsilon/2)x,(\kappa+\epsilon/2)x],

(274)

where, shrinking
x_{0},y_{0}
if necessary, the final containment is obtained using Lemma
D.7

and the bounds
(\kappa+\epsilon/2)x_{t}\geq y_{t}\geq(\kappa-\epsilon/2)x_{t}
in hand, one obtains

\displaystyle(1-a(\kappa+\epsilon)^{2}x_{t}^{2})x_{t}\leq x_{t+1}\leq(1-a(\kappa-\epsilon)^{2}x_{t}^{2})x_{t},

(275)

again shrinking
x_{0},y_{0}
and invoking Lemma
D.7
if necessary to deal with the higher-order terms. Inverting and squaring yield the bounds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{t+1}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{x_{t}^{2}}\frac{1}{(1-a(\kappa+\epsilon)^{2}x_{t}^{2})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{x_{t}^{2}}(1+3a(\kappa+\epsilon)^{2}x_{t}^{2})

(276)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{t+1}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{x_{t}^{2}}\frac{1}{(1-a(\kappa-\epsilon)^{2}x_{t}^{2})^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{x_{t}^{2}}(1+2a(\kappa-\epsilon)^{2}x_{t}^{2}),

(277)

again shrinking
x_{0},y_{0}
and invoking Lemma
D.7
if necessary to obtain the final upper bound in (

\displaystyle 3a(\kappa+\epsilon)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{x_{t+1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{x_{t}^{2}}\geq 2a(\kappa-\epsilon)^{2},

(278)

from which telescoping summation yields the bounds (
263
). The corresponding bounds for

now follow from the limits of (

∎

Assume that

D.3
Supercritical regime

Our convergence theorem in the supercritical regime is the easiest of the three. The orbit to which the iterates are attracted is hyperbolic unlike in the critical case, and the transient phenomena are relatively simple compared with those of the subcritical case.

Theorem D.10
.

Assume that
\eta>2/\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})
is sufficiently small. Then there are positive constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
and a stable orbit of period two of the form
\big(\theta^{\parallel}_{*},\pm(\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})-2)^{1/2}+O(\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})-2)\big)
to which the iterates
(\theta^{\parallel}_{t},\theta^{\perp}_{t})
starting from any
(\theta^{\parallel}_{0},\theta^{\perp}_{0})
satisfying
0<|\theta^{\perp}_{0}|\leq C_{1}(\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})-2)^{1/2}
and
d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel}_{0},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\leq C_{2}(\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})-2)^{1/2}
converge with rate
O\big((1-C_{3}(\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})-2))^{t}\big)
.

Proof.

Arguing using covariant Taylor expansions as in the proof of Theorem
D.9
, one sees that

\displaystyle d_{M}\big(\mathrm{GD}^{\parallel}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp}),\theta^{\parallel}_{*}\big)=\bigg(1-\frac{\eta\nu}{2c(\eta,\theta^{\parallel}_{*})}(\theta^{\perp})^{2}+O\big((\theta^{\perp})^{3},(\theta^{\perp})^{2}d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})\big)\bigg)d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})

(294)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{GD}^{\perp}(\eta,\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\perp})=-\bigg(1+(\eta\lambda(\theta^{\parallel}_{*})-2)+\frac{\eta\nu}{2}d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{2}-(\theta^{\perp})^{2}+O\big(d_{M}(\theta^{\parallel},\theta^{\parallel}_{*})^{3},(\theta^{\perp})^{3}\big)\bigg)\theta^{\perp}.

(295)

Consequently, the result follows from Theorem
D.11
below.
∎

Theorem D.11
.

Given
\alpha>0
and constants
a>0
and
b>0

\displaystyle F_{x}(x,y):=(1-ay^{2}+O(y^{3},xy^{2}))x,\qquad F_{y}(x,y)=-(1+\alpha+bx^{2}-y^{2}+O(x^{3},y^{3}))y.

(296)

Then, there are constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
such that for all
\alpha
sufficiently small, the map

admits a stable, period-2 orbit
\{(0,\xi_{+}),(0,\xi_{-})\}
with
\xi_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\alpha}+O(\alpha)
, and the iterates
(x_{t+1},y_{t+1}):=F(x_{t},y_{t})
starting from any

with

Proof.

The line
x=0
is preserved by
F
, and thereon one sees that

takes the form

\displaystyle F_{y}(0,\cdot):y\mapsto-(1+\alpha)y+y^{3}+O(y^{4})

(297)

, admits a period-two orbit
\{\xi_{+},\xi_{-}\}
of the form
\xi_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\alpha}+O(\alpha)

Via a routine calculation one sees that

\displaystyle F^{2}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}x\big(1-a(1+(1+\alpha)^{2})y^{2}+O(xy^{2},y^{3})\big)\\

(298)

The derivative of

\displaystyle DF^{2}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1-a(1+(1+\alpha)^{2})y^{2}&-2a(1+(1+\alpha)^{2})xy\\

(299)

\displaystyle+O(x^{3},x^{2}y,xy^{2},y^{3}).

(300)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{(1+\alpha)(1+(1+\alpha)^{2})}\leq y^{2}\leq 2\alpha,,

(301)

the matrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1-\frac{a\alpha}{1+\alpha}&O(x\sqrt{\alpha})\\
O(x\sqrt{\alpha})&1-(1/2)\alpha+O(x^{2})\end{pmatrix}+O(x^{3},\sqrt{\alpha}x^{2},\alpha x,\alpha^{3/2})

(302)

for all
\alpha
sufficiently small. Using the bound

C_{2},C_{3}>0
such that for all
\alpha
sufficiently small, all

satisfying

\displaystyle|x|\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}.

(303)

and all

satisfying (

.

Appendix E
Elementary lemmas

In this section, for ease of reference, we collect several well-known facts and their elementary proofs. Recall that a subset
M^{\prime}
of a Riemannian manifold
M
is
geodesically convex
if any two points of
M^{\prime}
can be connected by a unique geodesic contained entirely in
M^{\prime}
.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
\lambda:M^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
function on a geodesically convex subset
M^{\prime}
of a Riemannian manifold
M
. If
\lambda
is
\mu
-geodesically strongly convex in the sense that
\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda\succeq\mu I_{TM}
at all points on
M^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\lambda(y)\geq\lambda(x)+\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{x}(y)\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}d_{M}(x,y)^{2}

(309)

for all
x,y\in M^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Fix
x,y\in M^{\prime}
. Since
M^{\prime}
is geodesically convex, there exists a unique geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M^{\prime}
such that
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(1)=y
. This
\gamma
satisfies

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}(t)=\Pi(\gamma)_{t}\dot{\gamma}(0)=\Pi(\gamma)_{t}\log_{x}(y),\qquad\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|=d_{M}(x,y)

(310)

for all
t\in[0,1]
, where
\Pi(\gamma)_{t}:T_{\gamma(0)}M\rightarrow T_{\gamma(t)}M
is parallel transport. Define
\varphi(t):=\lambda(\gamma(t))
. Then using (
23
) and the chain rule,

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi}(t)=\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(\gamma(t)),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle,\qquad\ddot{\varphi}(t)=\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda(\gamma(t))\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle

(311)

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Thus, by Taylor’s theorem, there is
s\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\lambda(y)=\varphi(1)

\displaystyle=\varphi(0)+\dot{\varphi}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\ddot{\varphi}(s)

(312)

\displaystyle=\lambda(x)+\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{x}(y)\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\dot{\gamma}(s),\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda(\gamma(s))\dot{\gamma}(s)\rangle

(313)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda(x)+\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{x}(y)\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\dot{\gamma}(s)\|^{2}

(314)

\displaystyle=\lambda(x)+\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{x}(y)\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}d_{M}(x,y)^{2}

(315)

as claimed.
∎

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\lambda:M^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
function on a geodesically convex subset
M^{\prime}
of a Riemannian manifold
M
. If
\lambda
is
\mu
-geodesically strongly convex in the sense that
\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda\succeq\mu I_{TM}
at all points on
M^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{x\rightarrow y}\nabla_{M}\lambda(x)-\nabla_{M}\lambda(y),\log_{y}(x)\rangle\geq\mu\,d_{M}(x,y)^{2}

(316)

for all
x,y\in M^{\prime}
, where
\Pi_{x\rightarrow y}:T_{x}M\rightarrow T_{y}M
is parallel transport.

Proof.

Fix
x,y\in M^{\prime}
. From Lemma
E.1
, one has the estimates

\displaystyle\lambda(y)\geq\lambda(x)+\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{x}(y)\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}d_{M}(x,y)^{2},

(317)

\displaystyle\lambda(x)\geq\lambda(y)+\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(y),\log_{y}(x)\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}d_{M}(x,y)^{2}.

(318)

Since parallel transport is an orthogonal transformation, one has

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{x}(y)\rangle=-\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\Pi_{y\rightarrow x}\log_{y}(x)\rangle=-\langle\Pi_{x\rightarrow y}\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{y}(x)\rangle.

(319)

Substituting this into (
317
) and rearranging yields

\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{x\rightarrow y}\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{y}(x)\rangle\geq\frac{\mu}{2}d_{M}(x,y)^{2}+\lambda(x)-\lambda(y)

(320)

while (
318
) may be rearranged to give

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(y),\log_{y}(x)\rangle\geq\frac{\mu}{2}d_{M}(x,y)^{2}+\lambda(y)-\lambda(x).

(321)

Adding these estimates then yields the result.
∎

Lemma E.3
.

Let
\lambda:M^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
function on a geodesically convex subset
M^{\prime}
of a Riemannian manifold
M
. If
\lambda
is
\mu
-geodesically strongly convex in the sense that
\nabla^{2}_{M}\lambda\succeq\mu I_{TM}
at all points on
M^{\prime}
and admits a critical point
x_{*}
in
M^{\prime}
, then
x_{*}
is the unique global minimum of
\lambda
in
M^{\prime}
and

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(x)\|^{2}\geq 2\mu\big(\lambda(x)-\lambda(x_{*})\big)

(322)

for all
x\in M^{\prime}
.

Proof.

That the global minimum
x_{*}
is unique follows from Lemma
E.1
: for any
x\neq x_{*}
in
M
, one has

\displaystyle\lambda(x)\geq\lambda(x_{*})+\frac{\mu}{2}\,d_{M}(x,x_{*})^{2}>\lambda(x_{*}),

(323)

thus proving the first claim. To prove the second claim, fix
x\in M^{\prime}
. Lemma
E.1
then applies to yield

\displaystyle\lambda(x)-\lambda(x_{*})

\displaystyle\leq-\langle\nabla_{M}\lambda(x),\log_{x}(x_{*})\rangle-\frac{\mu}{2}\|\log_{x}(x_{*})\|^{2}

(324)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu}{2}\bigg\|\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla_{M}\lambda(x)+\log_{x}(x_{*})\bigg\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(x)\|^{2}

(325)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\nabla_{M}\lambda(x)\|^{2},

(326)

from which the result follows.
∎

Lemma E.4
.

For any
c>0
, and any integers
0\leq s<t
, one has

\ln\bigg(\frac{c+t+1}{c+s}\bigg)\leq\sum_{k=s}^{t}\frac{1}{c+k}\leq\frac{1}{c+s}+\ln\bigg(\frac{c+t}{c+s}\bigg).

(327)

Proof.

Since
x\mapsto\frac{1}{(c+x)}
is monotonically decreasing along the positive reals, one has

\int_{n}^{n+1}f(x)dx\leq\int_{n}^{n+1}f(n)dx=f(n)=\int_{n-1}^{n}f(n)dx\leq\int_{n-1}^{n}f(x)dx

(328)

for any natural number
n
, from which it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t+1}\frac{1}{(c+x)}dx\leq\sum_{k=s}^{t}\frac{1}{(c+k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(c+s)}+\sum_{k=s+1}^{t}\frac{1}{(c+k)}

(329)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(c+s)}+\int_{s}^{t}\frac{1}{(c+x)}dx.

(330)

Integrating both sides now yields the result.
∎

The KKT conditions for the constrained optimisation problem

\displaystyle\min_{\theta^{\parallel}\geq 0}\frac{1}{2}\|\theta^{\parallel}-\theta\|^{2}\quad\text{such that}\quad\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta^{\parallel}_{i}=y

(332)

are given by

\displaystyle\theta^{\parallel}_{i}-\theta^{\parallel}_{i}\theta_{i}+\alpha\frac{y}{\theta^{\parallel}_{i}}=0,\qquad i=1,\dots,p,

(333)

where
\alpha
is the constraint parameter. These equations admit the quadratic solutions

\displaystyle\theta^{\parallel}_{i}(\alpha)=\frac{\theta_{i}\pm\sqrt{\theta_{i}^{2}-4\alpha y}}{2},\qquad i=1,\dots,p.

(334)

It is easily verified
\alpha\mapsto\theta^{\parallel}_{i}(\alpha)
, is monotonically decreasing (when (
334
) is taken with the
+
sign) or monotonically increasing (when (
334
) is taken with the
-
sign); consequently, the map
\phi:\alpha\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta^{\parallel}_{i}(\alpha)
is also monotonic provided the signs are consistent among the
\theta^{\parallel}_{i}(\alpha)
.

The sign conventions we use are as follows. If
\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}<y
, then we must take the
+
sign for all
\theta^{\parallel}_{i}(\alpha)
. If
\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}\geq y
, then, noting that
2^{-p}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}
is the minimum value possible for
\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta^{\parallel}_{i}(\alpha)
when all
\theta^{\parallel}_{i}(\alpha)
are taken with the positive sign in (
334
), either:

1.

y\geq 2^{-p}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}
, in which case we take the positive sign in (
334
) for all
i
.

2.

y<2^{-p}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}
, in which case we take the negative sign in (
334
) for all
i
.

Having determined the sign to use uniformly over all
i=1,\dots,p
in (
334
), we are assured that
\alpha\mapsto\phi(\alpha)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta^{\parallel}_{i}(\alpha)
is monotonic, hence that
\phi(\alpha)=y
admits a unique solution which we obtain using the bisection method.
