Evolving extreme mass-ratio inspirals in a perturbed Schwarzschild spacetime
Michael LaHaye, Colin Weller, Dongjun Li, Patrick Bourg, Yanbei Chen, Huan Yang

Abstract
In this work, we develop the modified Teukolsky formalism that describes the GW radiation from a point mass orbiting around a perturbed Schwarzschild BH. This perturbation of the background spacetime induces a secular change in the orbital phase of the point mass. In turn, this causes a modification in the GW flux, which can be used to probe the background spacetime. We explicitly apply this formalism to a bumpy Schwarzschild spacetime as a proof of principle. The results pave the way for the description of EMRIs in generic perturbed Kerr spacetime in future developments.

Spherically and axially symmetric BHs in vacuum GR are well described by the Schwarzschild
Schwarzschild 1916
and Kerr
Kerr 1963
metrics, respectively. Measuring possible deviations from Schwarzschild or Kerr spacetimes via EMRIs requires accurate modeling of the EMRI orbital evolution. Within GR, the long-term evolution of EMRIs can be formulated in a two-timescale framework using gravitational self-force theory
Mino et al. 1997
;
Hughes 2000
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
;
Hughes 2017
;
Van De Meent and Warburton 2018
;
Barack and Pound 2019
;
Miller and Pound 2021
;
Pound and Wardell 2021

the Newman-Penrose (NP) formalism

\Psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{4}

Teukolsky equations

;

;

\Psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{4}

Our goal is to use BH perturbation theory to model the EMRI evolution in a deformed Kerr background, arising from bGR corrections, environmental effects, or BH mimickers. One challenge of extending the EMRI modeling procedures for a Kerr BH to a deformed Kerr background is that the Teukolsky formalism requires the background spacetime to be Ricci flat and Petrov type D, which are conditions not necessarily satisfied by deformed BHs or BH mimickers

;
Yunes and Stein 2011

Yagi et al. 2012

Herdeiro and Radu 2014

\epsilon

\epsilon

\zeta

\Psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{4}

;
Hussain and Zimmerman 2022

Despite all the progress, none of the previous literature has explicitly computed the source terms within the modified Teukolsky equations for an EMRI system, which is crucial for calculating modified gravitational flux, although some of the procedures for studying ringdown in
Cano et al. 2023a
;
Wagle et al. 2024
might still apply here.

To resolve this issue, we are applying the MTF to study EMRIs around deformed supermassive BHs by explicitly calculating the source terms in the modified Teukolsky equations for a generic deformed background spacetime. As a proof of principle, this work focuses on nonrotating BHs with generic axisymmetric deformations, but the procedures developed here can be directly generalized to deformed rotating BHs. Since the flux balance law for the Carter constant in vacuum GR may not necessarily hold in more general background spacetimes, we focus on circular and equatorial orbits to avoid complications arising from inclined or eccentric trajectories. Such orbits are typical of wet EMRIs formed through disk-assisted capture and migration in active galactic nuclei, as interactions with the disk largely reduce the eccentricity and inclination of the secondary before it enters the LISA frequency band
Pan et al. 2021
;
Pan and Yang 2021
.

Moreover, deviations of the background metric often arise from corrections to the Einstein-Hilbert action or backreactions of matter and other fields, leading to additional gravitational self-interactions (e.g., in higher-derivative gravity) or interactions with other nonmetric fields (e.g., for ultralight scalar clouds). These additional interactions are usually captured in some effective stress-energy tensors, but the details of which rely on the underlying bGR theory or matter model. For this reason, we ignore these model-dependent contributions to gravitational flux and focus on the more universal contribution due to background geometry corrections in this work. This work serves as a necessary middle step in using the MTF to model the EMRI evolution in physically motivated scenarios, such as ultralight scalar clouds considered in
Li et al. 2025b

g_{\mu\nu}=g^{(0,0)}_{\mu\nu}+\epsilon q^{(0,1)}_{\mu\nu}+\zeta h^{(1,0)}_{\mu\nu}+\zeta\epsilon Q^{(1,1)}_{\mu\nu}+\cdots,

(11)

where
g_{\mu\nu}^{(0,0)}
is the Schwarzschild or Kerr background metric, and
h_{\mu\nu}^{(1,0)}
is the deviation of the background spacetime. The GW driven by the secondary in vacuum GR is encoded in
q_{\mu\nu}^{(0,1)}
, and the additional GW sourced by the background spacetime deviation is represented by
Q_{\mu\nu}^{(1,1)}
. In this work, we have used different symbols for metric perturbations of different orders, so we will drop their superscripts
(n,m)
denoting the terms at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{n},\epsilon^{m})
if possible for simplicity. In
Li et al. 2023
, the authors further show that one can always construct a background tetrad such that all the NP quantities follow the same expansion in Eq. (
11
), e.g.,

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}^{(0)}+\epsilon\Psi_{0}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}^{(0,0)}+\zeta\Psi_{0}^{(1,0)}+\epsilon\Psi_{0}^{(0,1)}+\zeta\epsilon\Psi_{0}^{(1,1)}\,,

(12)

where we take the Weyl scalar
\Psi_{0}
as an example. In the cases where we can hide the expansion in
\zeta
, we only use the superscript for an expansion in
\epsilon
, as shown in the first line of Eq. (
II.1
).

II.2
Derivation of the modified Teukolsky equation

To derive the modified Teukolsky equation for an EMRI system, let us first define several operators:

\displaystyle D_{[a,b,c,d]}

\displaystyle=D+a\varepsilon+b\bar{\varepsilon}+c\rho+d\bar{\rho}\,,

(13a)

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}_{[a,b,c,d]}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Delta}+a\mu+b\bar{\mu}+c\gamma+d\bar{\gamma}\,,

(13b)

\displaystyle\delta_{[a,b,c,d]}

\displaystyle=\delta+a\bar{\alpha}+b\beta+c\bar{\pi}+d\tau\,,

(13c)

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}_{[a,b,c,d]}

\displaystyle=\bar{\delta}+a\alpha+b\bar{\beta}+c\pi+d\bar{\tau}\,,

(13d)

where
\{D,\bm{\Delta},\delta,\bar{\delta}\}
are the directional derivatives associated with the NP tetrad basis vectors
e_{a}^{\mu}
, and the remaining quantities are spin coefficients in the NP formalism. The definitions of all NP quantities are provided in
Li et al. 2023
, or refer to
Newman and Penrose 1962
;
Chandrasekhar 1983
;
Pound and Wardell 2021
for more comprehensive reviews of the NP formalism. As shown in the following, most parts of NP equations can be conveniently expressed in terms of the operators in Eq. (
13
). Moreover, these operators can be easily translated into the GHP operators
Geroch et al. 1973
;
Pound and Wardell 2021
or the Chandrasekhar operators
Chandrasekhar 1983
;
Dolan et al. 2024
;
Ma and Yang 2024
, which can help us simplify the source terms.

Similar to the original derivation of the Teukolsky equation in
Teukolsky 1973
, we will work with the following two Ricci identities and one Bianchi identity,

\displaystyle F_{1}\Psi_{0}-J_{1}\Psi_{1}-3\kappa\Psi_{2}=S_{1}\,,

(14a)

\displaystyle F_{2}\Psi_{0}-J_{2}\Psi_{1}-3\sigma\Psi_{2}=S_{2}\,,

(14b)

\displaystyle E_{2}\sigma-E_{1}\kappa-\Psi_{0}=0\,,

(14c)

where
\kappa
and
\sigma
are spin coefficients. The operators
F_{1,2}
,
J_{1,2}
, and
E_{1,2}
are defined as

\displaystyle F_{1}\equiv\bar{\delta}_{[-4,0,1,0]}\,,\quad

\displaystyle F_{2}\equiv\bm{\Delta}_{[1,0,-4,0]}\,,

\displaystyle J_{1}\equiv D_{[-2,0,-4,0]}\,,\quad

\displaystyle J_{2}\equiv\delta_{[0,-2,0,-4]}\,,

\displaystyle E_{1}\equiv\delta_{[-1,-3,1,-1]}\,,\quad

\displaystyle E_{2}\equiv D_{[-3,1,-1,-1]}\,.

(15)

The source terms
S_{1,2}
are defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{1}\equiv&\;\delta_{[-2,-2,1,0]}\Phi_{00}-D_{[-2,0,0,-2]}\Phi_{01}\\
&\;+2\sigma\Phi_{10}-2\kappa\Phi_{11}-\bar{\kappa}\Phi_{02}\,,\end{split}

(16a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{2}\equiv&\;\delta_{[0,-2,2,0]}\Phi_{01}-D_{[-2,2,0,-1]}\Phi_{02}\\
&\;-\bar{\lambda}\Phi_{00}+2\sigma\Phi_{11}-2\kappa\Phi_{12}\,,\end{split}

(16b)

where
\Phi_{ij}
are projections of the Ricci tensor
R_{\mu\nu}
onto the NP tetrad basis
e^{\mu}_{a}
, that is,

\Phi_{ij}\sim R_{\mu\nu}e^{\mu}_{a}e^{\mu}_{b}\,,

(17)

with complete expressions provided in
Newman and Penrose 1962
;
Chandrasekhar 1983
;
Pound and Wardell 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
. In this work, we assume a vacuum BH spacetime in GR, so
\Phi_{ij}^{(0,0)}=0
.

For terms of order larger than
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{0})
in
\Phi_{ij}
, there are two origins.
First, the effective stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}
in Eq. (
II.1
) due to bGR corrections or environmental effects will result in nonzero
\Phi_{ij}^{(1,0)}
and
\Phi_{ij}^{(1,1)}
. For corrections only involving metric fields, such as higher-derivative gravity
Cano and Ruipérez 2019
;
Cano et al. 2022
;
Cano et al. 2023a
;
Cano et al. 2023b
;
Cano et al. 2024
;
Maenaut et al. 2024
,

is always proportional to
\alpha_{{\mbox{\tiny bGR}}}
and
\zeta\propto\alpha_{{\mbox{\tiny bGR}}}
, so after the linearization in Eq. (
11
),

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}\sim\zeta[g_{\mu\nu}^{(0,0)}]\,,

(18a)

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}\sim\zeta[q_{\mu\nu}^{(0,1)}]\,,

(18b)

where
[f]
represents combinations of the field
f
and its derivatives. In the presence of nonmetric fields, these extra fields usually enter at fractional powers of
\zeta
due to hierarchical equations of motion: GWs in vacuum GR first drive these extra fields, which then source additional GWs
Yunes and Pretorius 2009
. For example, in dCS gravity
Yunes and Pretorius 2009
;
Cardoso and Gualtieri 2009

Molina et al. 2010

Pani et al. 2011
;

;

Wagle et al. 2022
;
Srivastava et al. 2021
;
Wagle et al. 2024
;
Chung et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025a
, the pseudoscalar

satisfies

\square\vartheta=-\frac{\alpha_{{\mbox{\tiny dCS}}}}{4}R_{\nu\mu\rho\sigma}{}^{*}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,,

(19)

which, with Eq. (
II.1
), implies that
\vartheta
enters at
\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{{\mbox{\tiny dCS}}})

\alpha_{{\mbox{\tiny dCS}}}^{2}

. The stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}
in Eq. (
II.1
) after the expansion in Eq. (
11

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}\sim

\displaystyle\;\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}}[g_{\mu\nu}^{(0,0)}][\vartheta^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}]+[g_{\mu\nu}^{(0,0)}][\vartheta^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}]^{2}\,,

(20a)

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}\sim

\displaystyle\;\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}}[q_{\mu\nu}^{(0,1)}][\vartheta^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}]+[q_{\mu\nu}^{(0,1)}][\vartheta^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}]^{2}

\displaystyle\;+[g_{\mu\nu}^{(0,0)}][\vartheta^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}][\vartheta^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}]\,.

(20b)

We can observe that both the metric and nonmetric fields in Eqs. (
18
) and (
20
) enter at an order below
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
. Similar arguments can also be made for such a perturbative treatment of environmental effects
Brito and Shah 2023
;
Rahman and Takahashi 2025
;
Dyson et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025b
;
Datta and Maselli 2025

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}\sim T^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}_{\mu\nu}e_{a}^{\mu}e_{b}^{\nu}\,,

(21a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}\sim T^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}_{\mu\nu}e_{a}^{\mu}e_{b}^{\nu}+T^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}_{\mu\nu}e_{a}^{\mu(0,1)}e_{b}^{\nu}\,,

(21b)

where we have dropped the superscript
(0,0)
of all
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{0})
quantities for simplicity.
Thus,
\Phi_{ij}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}
and
\Phi_{ij}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}
only depends on fields or order below
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
, which is a crucial feature enabling the decoupling of
\Psi_{0,4}^{(1,1)}
from other geometrical quantities, as shown later.

Second, the point-particle stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}^{p}
associated with the secondary in Eq. (
8
) can contribute to
\Phi^{(0,1)}_{ij}
and
\Phi^{(1,1)}_{ij}
. Since

is proportional to the particle’s mass

, it always enter at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{1})
. Since the particle’s four-velocity can be modified on a deformed background spacetime, one generally gets the following after the expansion in Eq. (
11
):

T_{\mu\nu}^{p(1,1)}\sim T_{\mu\alpha}^{p(0,1)}h_{\nu}^{\alpha(1,0)}\,,

(22)

where
T_{\mu\nu}^{p(0,1)}
is Eq. (
8
) with
m_{p}
factored out and evaluated on a Kerr background. Since the perturbed NP Ricci scalars driven by
T_{\mu\nu}^{p}
take the form

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}^{p(0,1)}\sim T^{p(0,1)}_{\mu\nu}e_{a}^{\mu}e_{b}^{\nu}\,,

(23a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}^{p(1,1)}\sim T^{p(1,1)}_{\mu\nu}e_{a}^{\mu}e_{b}^{\nu}+T^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(0,1)}_{\mu\nu}e_{a}^{\mu(1,0)}e_{b}^{\nu}\,,

(23b)

both
\Phi_{ij}^{p(0,1)}
and
\Phi_{ij}^{p(1,1)}
do not contain any fields at or above
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
given Eq. (
22
).

To derive the modified Teukolsky equation,
Li et al. 2023
made some convenient gauge choices for both the background spacetime and dynamical perturbations following Chandrasekhar
Chandrasekhar 1983
. Since we care about BH spacetimes perturbatively deviating from Petrov type D spacetimes in GR, we can set

\Psi_{0,1,3,4}^{(0,0)}=0\,.

(24)

For dynamical perturbations,
Li et al. 2023
showed that one can rotate the
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{1})
and
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
parts of the tetrad such that

\Psi_{1,3}^{(0,1)}=\Psi_{1,3}^{(1,1)}=0\,.

(25)

Other than these gauge choices, we make no further assumptions about the Weyl scalars.

Under the gauge above, one can then easily decouple
\Psi_{0}^{(1,1)}
from other NP quantities and derive a single equation for
\Psi_{0}^{(1,1)}
(and similarly for
\Psi_{4}^{(1,1)}
) following
Li et al. 2023
. The set of NP equations in Eq. (
14
) for EMRIs does not fundamentally differ from the one in
Li et al. 2023
for ringdown, except for the additional contribution of the secondary’s stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}^{p}
to the NP Ricci scalars
\Phi_{ij}
. Therefore, similar procedures in
Li et al. 2023
can be carried out here by first solving
\kappa
and
\sigma
in terms of
\Psi_{0,1}
and
S_{1,2}
from Eqs. (
14a
) and (
14b
) and then plugging
\kappa
and
\sigma
back to Eq. (
14c
). Performing the two-parameter expansion in Eq. (
II.1
) on the resulting equation and applying the gauge choices in Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
), we get
Li et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2025b

\displaystyle H_{0}^{(0,0)}\Psi_{0}^{(1,1)}=\mathcal{S}_{{\mbox{\tiny geo}}}^{(1,1)}+\mathcal{S}_{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}^{(1,1)}+\mathcal{S}_{p}^{(1,1)}\,,

(26)

where the operator
H_{0}
is defined as

\displaystyle H_{0}=\mathcal{E}_{2}F_{2}-\mathcal{E}_{1}F_{1}-3\Psi_{2}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}=E_{1}-\Psi_{2}^{-1}\delta\Psi_{2}\,,\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}=E_{2}-\Psi_{2}^{-1}D\Psi_{2}\,,

and the operators
E_{1,2}
and
F_{1,2}
are defined in Eq. (
II.2
). At
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{0})
,
H_{0}^{(0,0)}
reduces to the Teukolsky operator acting on the perturbed Weyl scalar
\Psi_{0}^{(1)}
in GR
Teukolsky 1973
. As we show below, the source terms in Eq. (
26

\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
, so Eq. (
26

The source terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (
26
) are divided into three groups. The first source term
\mathcal{S}_{{\mbox{\tiny geo}}}^{(1,1)}
takes the form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{{\mbox{\tiny geo}}}^{(1,1)}=-H_{0}^{(1,0)}\Psi_{0}^{(0,1)}-H_{0}^{(0,1)}\Psi_{0}^{(1,0)}+H_{1}^{(0,1)}\Psi_{1}^{(1,0)}\,,

\displaystyle H_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{2}J_{2}-\mathcal{E}_{1}J_{1}\,.

(28)

This source term is regarded as purely “geometrical” since it only depends on the corrections to the central BH’s geometry [i.e., the terms at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{0})
] and GWs in vacuum GR [i.e., the terms at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{1})

h_{\mu\nu}
generic in this work, so we will express all the terms at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{0})
in terms of a generic

in the final equation. For the latter contribution, we choose to directly solve the RW and ZM equations associated with a secondary in circular equatorial orbits in Sec.
IV
and express

in terms of the RW and ZM functions.

The second source term
\mathcal{S}_{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}^{(1,1)}
is directly driven by the effective stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}
due to bGR corrections or external matter fields, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}^{(1,1)}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(0,0)}S_{2}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}+\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(0,1)}S_{2}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}-\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(0,0)}S_{1}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}

(29)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(0,1)}S_{1}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}\,.

The terms
S_{i}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}
(
i=1,2
) are defined analogously to
S_{i}
in Eq. (
16
), using the
\Phi_{ij}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}
associated with
T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}

T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}
begins at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1})
,
S_{i}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}
likewise start at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1})
. As discussed above,
\Phi_{ij}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}
will only involve fields at orders below
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
, so
S_{i}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}
do not depend on any geometrical quantities at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
. In this work, we do not consider
T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}
of any specific bGR theory or matter model, so we will be generic about the contribution from
T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,1)}

T_{\mu\nu}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}
can be calculated from the background metric corrections
h_{\mu\nu}
.

In comparison, the last source term
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{(1,1)}
is directly driven by the stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}^{p(0,1)}
associated with the secondary,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{p}^{(1,1)}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(0,0)}S_{2}^{p(1,1)}+\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(1,0)}S_{2}^{p(0,1)}-\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(0,0)}S_{1}^{p(1,1)}

(30)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(1,0)}S_{1}^{p(0,1)}\,,

where
S_{i}^{p}
(
i=1,2
) are defined analogously to
S_{i}
in Eq. (
16
) with
\Phi_{ij}
replaced by those associated with
T_{\mu\nu}^{p}
, respectively. Since
T_{\mu\nu}^{p}
begins at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1})
,
S_{i}^{p}
start at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1})
. Although
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(1,0)}
and

depend on corrections to the background BH spacetime [i.e., terms at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{0})
], they do not directly depend on the effective stress-energy tensor

that generates these corrections. For the same reason as above,

do not depend on any geometrical quantities at or above
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
.

The equation of
\Psi_{4}^{(1,1)}
can be obtained from Eq. (
26

\displaystyle H_{4}^{(0,0)}\Psi_{4}^{(1,1)}=\mathcal{T}_{{\mbox{\tiny geo}}}^{(1,1)}+\mathcal{T}_{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}}^{(1,1)}+\mathcal{T}_{p}^{(1,1)}\,,

(31)

where

is the Teukolsky operator in GR for
\Psi_{4}

\displaystyle\;-\mathcal{E}_{3}^{(0,1)}S_{3}^{{\mbox{\tiny eff}}(1,0)}\,,

(32b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{p}^{(1,1)}=

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{E}_{4}^{(0,0)}S_{4}^{p(1,1)}+\mathcal{E}_{4}^{(1,0)}S_{4}^{p(0,1)}-\mathcal{E}_{3}^{(0,0)}S_{3}^{p(1,1)}

S_{4,p}

(33a)

\displaystyle S_{4}\equiv

\displaystyle\;-\mathbf{\Delta}_{[0,1,2,-2]}\Phi_{20}+\bar{\delta}_{[2,0,0,-2]}\Phi_{21}

III
Background NP Quantities in Vacuum GR

In this section, we briefly review the coordinate and tetrad choices for the background spacetime in vacuum GR [i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{0})
], while a more comprehensive review can be found in
Pound and Wardell 2021
. For simplicity, we drop the order-counting superscript
(0,0)
of quantities at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{0})
. We are interested in EMRI evolutions around deformed Schwarzschild and Kerr BHs, the latter of which has the line element in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates
Boyer and Lindquist 1967

(t,r,\theta,\phi)
as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=

\displaystyle\;-\left(1-\frac{2Mr}{\Sigma}\right)dt^{2}-\frac{4aMr\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}dtd\phi+\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}dr^{2}

\displaystyle\;+\Sigma d\theta^{2}+\left[\Delta+\frac{2Mr(r^{2}+a^{2})}{\Sigma}\right]\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2}\,,

(36)

where
\Delta=r^{2}-2Mr+a^{2}
,
\Sigma=r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}{\theta}
,
M
is the BH mass, and
a
is the BH spin. In the nonrotating limit, the Kerr metric becomes the Schwarzschild metric, i.e.,

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2}\right)\,,

(37)

where
f(r)=1-2M/r
.

When studying Kerr BHs using the NP formalism, the two most common choices of tetrad are the Kinnersley tetrad
Kinnersley 1969
and the Carter tetrad
Carter and Hartle 1987
. The original Teukolsky equation in
Teukolsky 1973
was derived using the Kinnersley tetrad such that the Teukolsky equation naturally separates into second-order ordinary differential equations for the radial and angular parts of
\Psi_{0,4}
, respectively. However, certain symmetries of the Kerr spacetime are not preserved by the Kinnersley tetrad but are respected by the Carter tetrad
Pound and Wardell 2021
. For instance, the Carter tetrad
\{l^{\mu},n^{\mu},m^{\mu},\bar{m}^{\mu}\}
transforms as
l^{\mu}\to-n^{\mu}
and
m^{\mu}\to\bar{m}^{\mu}
under the map
\{t,\phi\}\to\{-t,-\phi\}
. Although all results in this work could be obtained in the Kinnersley tetrad, we adopt the Carter tetrad for the remainder of this work, as its symmetry facilitates sanity checks of the source terms. In Boyer–Lindquist coordinates, the Carter tetrad is given by

\displaystyle l^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Delta\Sigma}}\left[r^{2}+a^{2},\Delta,0,a\right]\,,

(38a)

\displaystyle n^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Delta\Sigma}}\left[r^{2}+a^{2},-\Delta,0,a\right]\,,

(38b)

\displaystyle m^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Sigma}}\left[ia\sin\theta,0,1,i\csc\theta\right]\,,

(38c)

\displaystyle\bar{m}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Sigma}}\left[-ia\sin\theta,0,1,-i\csc\theta\right]\,,

(38d)

and is related to the Kinnersley tetrad
\{l_{K}^{\mu},n_{K}^{\mu},m_{K}^{\mu},\bar{m}_{K}^{\mu}\}
by

\displaystyle l_{K}^{\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{2\Sigma}{\Delta}}l^{\mu}\,,\;\;\quad n_{K}^{\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{2\Sigma}}n^{\mu}\,,

\displaystyle m_{K}^{\mu}=\frac{\sqrt{\Sigma}}{\Gamma}m^{\mu}\,,\quad\bar{m}_{K}^{\mu}=\frac{\sqrt{\Sigma}}{\bar{\Gamma}}\bar{m}^{\mu}\,,

(39)

where
\Gamma=r+ia\cos{\theta}
. In the Schwarzschild limit, Eq. (
38
) reduces to

\displaystyle l^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2f(r)}}\left(1,f(r),0,0\right)\,,

(40a)

\displaystyle n^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2f(r)}}\left(1,-f(r),0,0\right)\,,

(40b)

\displaystyle m^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}r}\left(0,0,1,i\csc{\theta}\right)\,,

(40c)

One convenient formulation of the NP formalism we will use in this work is the GHP formalism

Geroch et al. 1973
;
Pound and Wardell 2021

\{p,q\}
, from which its spin-weight
s=(p-q)/2
, associated with rotations of the tetrad vectors
\{m^{\mu},\bar{m}^{\mu}\}
, and boost-weight

, associated with boosts of the tetrad vectors
\{l^{\mu},n^{\mu}\}
, are determined. By performing rotations and boosts, one can determine that the tetrad basis vectors have the following GHP type:

l^{\mu}:\{1,1\}\,,\;n^{\mu}:\{-1,-1\}\,,\;m^{\mu}:\{1,-1\}\,,\;\bar{m}^{\mu}:\{-1,1\}\,.

(43)

Similarly, one can find the GHP type of the following spin coefficients to be

\kappa:\{3,1\}\,,\;\sigma:\{3,-1\}\,,\;\rho:\{1,1\}\,,\;\tau:\{1,-1\}\,.

(44)

The GHP type of other spin coefficients is given by the prime transformation, under which
\{l^{\mu},m^{\mu}\}\leftrightarrow\{n^{\mu},\bar{m}^{\mu}\}
, so the GHP type
\{p,q\}
of an object is transformed to
\{-p,-q\}
. For example, the spin coefficient
\kappa=-m^{\mu}Dl_{\mu}
becomes

under the prime transformation. The other spin coefficients are related by the prime transformation as follows:

\{\kappa^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\}=-\{\nu,\lambda,\mu,\pi,\gamma,\alpha\}\,,

(45)

which tells us the GHP type of
\{\nu,\lambda,\mu,\pi\}
. Other useful GHP transformations can be found in
Geroch et al. 1973
;
Pound and Wardell 2021
.

The remaining spin coefficients
\{\varepsilon,\gamma,\alpha,\beta\}
do not possess definite GHP type
Geroch et al. 1973

Pound and Wardell 2021

\displaystyle\text{\TH}=D-p\varepsilon-q\bar{\varepsilon}\,,\quad\text{\TH}^{\prime}=\bm{\Delta}-p\gamma-q\bar{\gamma}\,,

\displaystyle\cpartial=\delta-p\beta-q\bar{\alpha}\,,\quad\cpartial^{\prime}=\bar{\delta}-p\alpha-q\bar{\beta}\,,

(46)

where
p
and
q
are determined by the GHP type
\{p,q\}
of the object acted on by these operators.
Þ
and
\text{\TH}^{\prime}
are boost-weight raising and lowering operators, as they shift the GHP type of the target by

\{-1,-1\}

are spin-weight raising and lowering operators, as they shift the GHP type of the target by

For the metric perturbation

at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{1})

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}^{1}[q_{\alpha\beta}]=8\pi T_{\mu\nu}^{p}\,,

where
G_{\mu\nu}^{1}
is the linearized Einstein operator, and

is the stress-energy tensor of a point particle. For Schwarzschild BHs, Regge and Wheeler

, Zerilli

, and Vishveshwara

developed the procedures to solve Eq. (
47
) in the RW gauge, where the even-parity metric perturbation
q_{\mu\nu}^{e}
and odd-parity metric perturbation
q_{\mu\nu}^{o}
decouple, i.e.,

q_{\mu\nu}=q_{\mu\nu}^{o}+q_{\mu\nu}^{e}\,.

(48)

After conducting a harmonic expansion of

, i.e.,

q^{e/o}_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{\ell m}\int e^{-i\omega t+im\phi}q^{e/o,\ell m}_{\mu\nu}(r,\theta)\,d\omega.

and decomposing

C
), one can then find a master function to completely characterize the radial parts of each parity: the ZM function

and the RW function

for even and odd parity, respectively. The master functions
Q^{e/o}_{\ell m}(r)
are related to the radial parts of

by

\displaystyle Q^{o}_{\ell m}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(r)}{r}h^{\ell m}_{1}(r)\,,

(50)

\displaystyle Q^{e}_{\ell m}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{\lambda r+3M}K^{\ell m}(r)+\frac{rf(r)}{i\omega(\lambda r+3M)}H_{1}^{\ell m}(r)\,,

(51)

where
\lambda=(\ell+2)(\ell-1)/2
. The functions

,
K^{\ell m}(r)
, and

are radial parts of

in Eqs. (
104
) and (
103
). For

\displaystyle{\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dr_{*}^{2}}+\omega^{2}-V^{e/o}_{\ell m}(r)\right]Q^{e/o}_{\ell m}(r)}=S^{e/o}_{\ell m}(r)\,,

where

\displaystyle V^{o}_{\ell m}(r)

\displaystyle=f(r)\left(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}-\frac{6M}{r^{3}}\right)\,,

(53a)

\displaystyle V^{e}_{\ell m}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(r)}{r^{2}\Lambda^{2}}\left[2\lambda^{2}\left(\lambda+1+\frac{3M}{r}\right)+\frac{18M^{2}}{r^{2}}\left(\lambda+\frac{M}{r}\right)\right]\,,

(54a)

\displaystyle S^{e}_{\ell m}(r)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{c}_{3}\delta(r-r_{p})+\mathfrak{c}_{4}\frac{d}{dr}\delta(r-r_{p})\,,

(54b)

For each

\displaystyle Q_{\ell m}^{e/o,\mathrm{in}}(r_{*}\to-\infty)=e^{-i\omega r_{*}}\,,

(55a)

(55b)

(56)

where

\displaystyle c_{\ell m}^{e/o,\mathrm{in}}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W_{e/o}}\int_{r}^{\infty}\,dr^{\prime}\frac{Q_{\ell m}^{e/o,\mathrm{up}}(r^{\prime})S^{e/o}_{\ell m}(r^{\prime})}{f(r^{\prime})}\,,

(57)

\displaystyle c_{\ell m}^{e/o,\mathrm{up}}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W_{e/o}}\int_{2M}^{r}\,dr^{\prime}\frac{Q_{\ell m}^{e/o,\mathrm{in}}(r^{\prime})S^{e/o}_{\ell m}(r^{\prime})}{f(r^{\prime})}\,,

(59)

The above approach was later formulated by Moncrief in a gauge-invariant way using a Hamiltonian description

For more generic orbits, one can use the method of extended homogeneous solutions to include eccentricity
Hopper and Evans 2010
;
Barack et al. 2008

Mathisson 1937
for a spinning secondary
Mathews et al. 2022
.

Calculating metric perturbations of Kerr BHs is more challenging. Unlike the case for Schwarzschild spacetime, one cannot directly decouple the equations of different metric components and separate their radial and angular parts. The most widely used alternative is to solve the Teukolsky equation for the curvature perturbations characterized by the Weyl scalars
\Psi^{(1)}_{0}
or
\Psi^{(1)}_{4}
and reconstruct the corresponding metric perturbations. For vacuum perturbations, one main metric reconstruction method is the one developed by Chrzanowski, Cohen, and Kegeles (CCK)
Chrzanowski 1975
;
Kegeles and Cohen 1979
;
Ori 2003
. Nonetheless, this approach is only valid in a radiation gauge
Price et al. 2007
, which restricts the structure of source terms that can appear for sourced perturbations. In particular, a radiation gauge is not generically allowed for the stress-energy tensor of a point particle
Toomani et al. 2022
. Some progress has been made by matching the vacuum perturbations inside and outside the particle’s orbit for circular equatorial orbits
Keidl et al. 2010
;
Shah et al. 2011
;
Pound et al. 2014
;
Merlin et al. 2016
and precessing inclined orbits
van de Meent 2016
;
van De Meent 2017
;
Nasipak 2025
. However, the solution generated by this approach is usually singular along null rays extending from the particle’s worldline to the horizon or infinity
Green et al. 2020
. Motivated to construct a more regular solution necessary for studying nonlinear contributions in self-force theory, several reconstruction techniques have been developed over the past few years, such as the Green, Hollands, and Zimmerman’s extension of the CCK method to sourced perturbations via a corrector tensor
Green et al. 2020
;
Toomani et al. 2022
;
Bourg et al. 2024
, the Lorenz gauge reconstruction method developed by
Dolan et al. 2022
;
Dolan et al. 2024
for point particles, and the extension of the latter to generic sources
Wardell et al. 2024
based on the formalism by Aksteiner, Anderson, and Backdähl
Aksteiner et al. 2019
;
Nasipak 2025
. In our follow-up work, we will apply one of these techniques to study the EMRI evolution of a deformed Kerr BH.

VI
Computing the Modified Flux

In this section, we derive the leading-order correction to the GW flux due to a generic background metric deformation in terms of perturbations of the Weyl scalars
\Psi_{0,4}
. We first review how the effective stress-energy tensor for linear gravitational radiation in GR at null infinity is related to the Weyl scalar
\Psi_{4}
, and then discuss how this relation can be extended to calculate the gravitational flux of an EMRI system in a deformed Kerr geometry. We briefly outline the limitations of this derivation and specify the subclass of theories in which it remains valid. In addition, we highlight the difficulties involved in computing the horizon flux in bGR theories or, more generally, beyond-Kerr geometries.

Within GR, the effective stress-energy tensor due to linear gravitational radiation was first computed by Issacson
Isaacson 1968a
;
Isaacson 1968b

\displaystyle T^{\text{I}}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{32\pi}\left\langle\left(\nabla_{\mu}q^{\alpha\beta}\right)\left(\nabla_{\nu}q_{\alpha\beta}\right)\right\rangle\,,

(82)

where the brackets
\langle\rangle
denote a Brill-Hartle type averaging procedure
Brill and Hartle 1964
, and
q_{\alpha\beta}
corresponds to GW in vacuum GR. This expression is only valid at future null infinity and in the Lorenz gauge. In GR, it has been shown that the transverse traceless gauge, equivalently the Lorenz gauge plus the trace-free condition, exists for any vacuum perturbation of a globally hyperbolic, vacuum solution
Wald 1984
. This allows the GW energy flux at future null infinity in GR to be related to the perturbed Weyl scalar
\Psi^{(1)}_{4}

Teukolsky 1973

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}E^{\infty}}{dtd\Omega}

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}r^{2}T^{r}{}_{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{r^{2}\omega^{2}}{16\pi}\left[\left(A_{+}\right)^{2}+\left(A_{\times}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{r^{2}}{4\pi\omega^{2}}\left|\Psi_{4}^{(1)}\right|^{2}\,,

(83)

where we have set
T_{\mu\nu}
to be the Issacson stress-energy tensor and assume a decomposition of
q_{\mu\nu}
in the form

\displaystyle q_{\mu\nu}=\Re\left\{\left(A_{+}e^{+}_{\mu\nu}+A_{\times}e^{\times}_{\mu\nu}\right)e^{-i\omega(t-r)}\right\},

(84)

where
\Re
takes the real component, while
e^{+}_{\mu\nu}
and
e^{\times}_{\mu\nu}
are the usual plus and cross polarization tensors, respectively. Using the tetrad in Eq. (
38
) to compute the corresponding
\Psi_{4}^{(1)}
of
q_{\mu\nu}
, we get the last line of Eq. (
VI
).

For a subset of bGR theories with dynamical scalar fields coupled to higher-order curvature terms, such as dCS and EdGB gravity, the effective stress–energy tensor of linear gravitational radiation has been shown to reduce to the Isaacson stress-energy tensor in Eq. (
82
)
Stein and Yunes 2011
. In particular, these additional higher-derivative terms do not modify the form of Eq. (
82
). For this class of bGR theories, only the transverse, tensor degrees of freedom contribute to the infinity flux. Hence, using the expansion scheme in Eq. (
11
), we can compute the leading-order correction to the infinity energy flux by expanding Eq. (
VI
) up to
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}E^{\infty}}{dtd\Omega}=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{r^{2}}{4\pi\omega^{2}}\left(\Psi^{(0,1)}_{4}\bar{\Psi}^{(1,1)}_{4}+\bar{\Psi}^{(0,1)}_{4}\Psi^{(1,1)}_{4}\right)\,,

(85)

where
\Psi_{4}^{(0,1)}
is the solution to the Teukolsky equation in vacuum GR driven by a point particle, while
\Psi_{4}^{(1,1)}
is the solution to the modified Teukolsky equation we developed in this work and corresponds to additional GWs propagating to null infinity due to bGR or environmental effects.

However, in general, there are six propagating polarizations (two tensors, two scalars, and two vector types) in linear metric perturbations that can modify the stress-energy tensor in Eq. (
82
). Some bGR theories can have additional propagating degrees of freedom, including de Rham–Gabadadze–Tolley (dRGT) massive gravity
Hinterbichler 2012
;
Hassan and Rosen 2012
;
Rham 2015
;
Barack et al. 2019
, Einstein-æther gravity
Jacobson 2007
, pure metric
f(R,R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu})
gravity
Sotiriou 2006
;
Sotiriou and Faraoni 2010
;
Vilhena et al. 2021
, and other scalar-vector-tensor theories
Bekenstein 2004
;
Bekenstein and Sanders 2006
. For example, in dRGT massive gravity, the effective GW stress-energy tensor is given by
Cardoso et al. 2018

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{32\pi}\left\langle q_{\alpha\beta,\mu}q_{,\nu}^{\alpha\beta}-q_{,\mu}q_{,\nu}\right\rangle\,,

(86)

where the trace
q=g^{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha\beta}
encodes a scalar breathing mode and contributes to the total energy flux. Nevertheless, a modified effective GW stress-energy tensor does not always change the form of Eq. (
85
). Specifically, if modified equations of motion allow extra polarizations to decouple from the plus and cross polarizations, behaving as non-minimally coupled matter fields, Eq. (
85
) is still accurate up to order
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
. In this case, the total energy flux at future null infinity should include not only gravitational radiation, but also contributions from dynamical matter fields (e.g., scalar or vector fields)
Wald and Zoupas 2000
;
Ould El Hadj 2023
;
Ashtekar and Speziale 2024
;
Li et al. 2025b
.

In addition, bGR theories or environmental effects could also modify the relation between the horizon flux and the Weyl scalar
\Psi_{0}
, which is more difficult to calculate and has not been systematically studied to the best of the authors’ knowledge. Within GR, the energy flux to the horizon can be related to the infinitesimal change in the horizon’s surface area via the first law of BH thermodynamics. The surface area
\Sigma
of a Kerr BH can be written in terms of its mass
M
and angular momentum
J
,

\displaystyle\Sigma=4\pi\left(r_{+}^{2}+a^{2}\right)=8\pi\left[M^{2}+\sqrt{\left(M^{4}-J^{2}\right)}\right]\,,

(87)

where
a=J/M
and
r_{\pm}=M\pm\sqrt{M^{2}-a^{2}}
. We can use Eq. (
87
) to express the change in the horizon’s area
d\Sigma
in terms of the change in mass
dM
and the change in angular momentum
dJ
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Sigma=\frac{8\pi}{M\sqrt{\left(M^{2}-a^{2}\right)}}\left(2M^{2}r_{+}\,dM-J\,dJ\right)\,.

(88)

Since a Kerr BH is stationary and axisymmetric, the perturbed quantities have a harmonic
t
- and
\phi

e^{-i\omega t+im\phi}
, where
m
is an azimuthal quantum number and
\omega
is a frequency. This permits a simple relationship between
dJ
and
dM

\displaystyle dJ=\frac{m}{\omega}dM\,.

(89)

Then, inserting Eq. (
89

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\Sigma}{dtd\Omega}=\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon_{0}}\left(1-m\omega_{+}/\omega\right)\frac{d^{2}M}{dtd\Omega}\,,

(90)

where
\varepsilon_{0}=\sqrt{M^{2}-a^{2}}/(4Mr_{+})
, and
\omega_{+}=a/(2Mr_{+})
is the horizon frequency. On the other hand, the horizon area change
d\Sigma
can be computed from the perturbed spin coefficient
\sigma^{(1)}
in GR
Hawking and Hartle 1972
;
Teukolsky and Press 1974
;
Chandrasekhar 1983
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\Sigma}{dtd\Omega}=\frac{2Mr_{+}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\left|\sigma^{\text{HH}(1)}(r_{+})\right|^{2}\,,

(91)

where
\sigma^{\text{HH}(1)}
is
\sigma^{(1)}
in the Hartle–Hawking (HH) frame
Hartle 1974
. Since
\sigma^{\text{HH}(1)}
can be reconstructed from the perturbed Weyl scalar
\Psi^{\text{HH}(1)}_{0}
on the horizon via
Teukolsky and Press 1974
;
Chandrasekhar 1983

\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{HH}(1)}(r_{+})=-\frac{\Psi_{0}^{\text{HH}(1)}(r_{+})}{i\left(\omega-m\omega_{+}\right)+2\varepsilon_{0}}\,,

(92)

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\text{HH}(1)}(r_{+})=\frac{\Delta^{2}\Psi_{0}^{(1)}(r_{+})}{16M^{2}r_{+}^{2}}\,,

(93)

where
\Psi_{0}^{\text{HH}(1)}

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}E^{H}}{dtd\Omega}=

\displaystyle\;\lim_{r\rightarrow r_{+}}\bigg[\frac{1}{64\pi}\frac{\omega}{\omega-m\omega_{+}}\frac{1}{(\omega-m\omega_{+})^{2}+4\varepsilon^{2}_{0}}

\displaystyle\;\frac{\Delta^{4}}{(2Mr_{+})^{3}}\left|\Psi^{(1)}_{0}\right|^{2}\bigg]\,.

(94)

In bGR theories or non-vacuum environments, several parts of this procedure to derive Eq. (
VI
) could fail and must be reestablished on a case-by-case basis. For instance, Eq. (

\Phi_{00}

\mathcal{O}({\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1}})

Vigeland et al. 2011
contain such source terms and must be taken into account when relating
\Psi^{(1,1)}_{0}
and
\sigma^{(1,1)}

VII
Discussion

In this work, we have extended the MTF to compute the gravitational radiation from EMRI systems around nonrotating BHs with generic, axisymmetric, non-vacuum deformations. While previous work has focused on non-relativistic approximation schemes for matter environments
Yunes et al. 2012
;
Baumann et al. 2019
;
Baumann et al. 2020
;
Baumann et al. 2022a
;
Baumann et al. 2022b
;
Takahashi et al. 2023
and relativistic calculations for spherically symmetric metric deformations
Brito and Shah 2023
;
Duque et al. 2024
;
Speeney et al. 2024
, we have employed the Teukolsky formalism to establish essential foundations for modeling general, fully relativistic EMRI systems in non-vacuum environments and bGR theories.

We have reviewed the MTF and utilized its two-parameter expansion scheme to separate contributions from the theory-agnostic background metric correction at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{0})
and the usual GW radiation produced by an EMRI system with a fixed trajectory in GR at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{1})
. Given the background metric correction
h_{\mu\nu}
and the GR GW
q_{\mu\nu}
obtained from the RW and ZM equations, we constructed the corresponding tetrads
e^{\mu(1,0)}_{a}
and
e^{\mu(0,1)}_{a}
. We then outline the procedure to compute the perturbed spin coefficients, directional derivatives, and Weyl scalars at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{0})
and
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{1})
, following the prescription in
Chandrasekhar 1983
;
Campanelli and Lousto 1999
;
Wagle et al. 2024
. These quantities are required to compute the geometrical [Eqs. (
II.2
) and (
32a
)], particle stress-energy [Eqs. (
30
) and (
32c
)] and effective stress-energy [Eqs. (
29
) and (
32b
)] source terms. Although the form of the final source terms is complex, we have organized them using the operator notation of Sec.
IV
and tabulated them in Appendix
D
. Having obtained a solution to the master equation for the Weyl scalar
\Psi^{(1,1)}_{4}
[i.e., Eq. (
31
)], the modified flux at future null infinity can be computed using Eq. (
85
). This assumes that the transverse tensor polarizations in GR decouple from any additional polarizations in a bGR theory or non-vacuum environments. To account for the additional propagating degrees of freedom, one may have to compute: i. the modification to the effective Issacson stress; ii. reconstruct (or partially reconstruct) the metric perturbation
Q_{\mu\nu}^{(1,1)}
. Obtaining
Q_{\mu\nu}^{(1,1)}
would also aid the computation of the modified horizon flux. We leave a full analysis of these cases for future work.

One avenue for future research is to adopt a specific bGR theory or a prescribed matter configuration within GR, which would allow the computation of the Ricci tensor up to
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{1})
. At this order, the Ricci tensor contributes directly to the source term within the master equations of
\Psi^{(1,1)}_{0,4}
and must be included for a complete calculation. In particular, this contribution captures both how GWs are evaluated with the modified gravitational equations of motion and how the
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{0},\epsilon^{1})
stress-energy tensor couples to the modified geometry at
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{1},\epsilon^{0})
.
The latter is particularly important since geodesic motion for the Kerr metric is integrable. This feature is generically lost for BHs in bGR theories
Deich et al. 2022
;
Das et al. 2024
and other parametrized beyond-Kerr BHs
Weller et al. 2025
;
Vigeland et al. 2011
;
Glampedakis and Babak 2006
;
Cardoso et al. 2014
