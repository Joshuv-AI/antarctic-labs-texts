The impact and implications of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health experiences of medical students in Hong Kong
Authors: Olga Zayts-Spence, Pauline Luk, David Matthew Edmonds
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis qualitative study examines the question of how the COVID-19 pandemic impacted the mental health experiences of medical students in Hong Kong. Students’ poor mental health is a serious public health concern because it impacts the quality of patient care they may eventually provide. It may also impact healthcare organizations through staff retention. Research in Hong Kong on this topic remains scarce, and most studies are quantitative and fail to capture medical students’ actual mental health experiences during the pandemic. Understanding their experiences during the pandemic remains relevant given how radically it reshaped healthcare and education.Methods18 interviews with ‘COVID-19 cohorts’ of students (2020–2023) were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analysed qualitatively using the principles of reflexive thematic analysis. Themes related to mental health stressors and coping strategies were identified.ResultsThe main mental health stressors were physical, socio-relational and educational. The coping strategies focused on support from others and more drastic measures such as leaving medicine altogether.ConclusionsOur findings underscore the importance of consolidated efforts and close collaboration between universities and healthcare organizations (workplaces) to ensure that policies, procedures, and infrastructure are aligned with the goal of supporting students’ mental health.Keywords: Medical students, COVID-19 pandemic, Mental health, Stressors, Coping

Background

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic impacted nearly every facet of our social lives for several years. Indeed, the pandemic led to overwhelming financial and economic instability [1]. One sector of society that was negatively impacted was healthcare. The pandemic strained many countries’ medical systems, with many medical staff and patients endangered by the disease [2–5]. Medical teaching was also altered at universities, with many implementing social distancing policies, with in-person teaching shifting to an online mode of learning as a result [6]. In-person clinical training and ‘on-the-job’ attachment programs were also either cancelled and modified [7, 8].

Owing to these changes and upheaval, those medical students1 who studied or graduated during the pandemic form a unique group. Their education and work were radically transformed. Indeed, for some of those in their final year of education, they had what one might call an ‘accelerated transition’ into the workforce. Responding to the need for more healthcare staff on the frontlines, medical students in some countries around the world graduated early to join the workforce [9–11]. Other medical students also experienced a ‘taste’ of moving into the workforce as they took on other supporting roles, including assisting in vaccination drives [12, 13]. In all, the pandemic had interdependent impacts on the higher educational and healthcare sectors. The social distancing requirements in the education system were done under the presumed auspices of preserving the healthcare system. In addition, medical students also had to enter the workforce to help ‘plug the gaps’ in the healthcare sector.

These disruptions and changes led to negative outcomes for medical students, including a fear of becoming infected [14], an isolation from others because of their place of work [15], and mental health problems [16]. This paper centres on this group of medical students in Hong Kong to consider the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on their mental health. It is imperative to consider the mental health of medical students as it impacts not just themselves, but also patients and healthcare organizations [17]. Furthermore, medical students’ mental health can impact staff retention [18] and the quality of patient care that students and graduates provide [19].

In focusing on the mental health of this population, our study draws upon Bronfenbrenner’s “ecology of human development” perspective [20, p.514]. This perspective views people as nested within, and influenced by, different levels of systems—namely, the microsystem, mesosystem, and macrosystem [20, p.514–515]. Other scholars have adapted this theory and referred to the systems as the individual and interpersonal (microsystem), institutional and community (mesosystem), and public policy (macrosystem) levels [21, p.237, 22, p.11, 23, p.355]. Bronfenbrenner’s perspective argues that a person’s development is influenced by “the changing immediate environments in which it lives” and “by relations within and between these immediate settings, as well as the larger social contexts” [20, p.514]. In recent years, this perspective has been adapted into a socio-ecological model of mental health [24]. Such a model posits that a person’s mental health is influenced by multiple stressors at different levels (systems). This model has been applied to understanding the mental wellbeing of different populations in the pandemic, such as healthcare workers [21, 22] and the elderly [25]. We draw upon the socio-ecological model to conceptualize medical students’ experiences of different stressors from multiple spheres of their lives. Specifically, medical students’ mental health is impacted by their social and personal relationships (microsystem), education (mesosystem), the healthcare sector (mesosystem), as well as the broader government pandemic policies (macrosystem) [20–25].

There is a wealth of research examining the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health of medical students. The pandemic and associated disruptions to education and work led to problems such as burnout [26, 27], stress [16], anxiety [14, 16], and insomnia [28] amongst this demographic group. There is evidence suggesting that the mental health of medical students during the pandemic was worse than that of the general public [29]. Research also reveals a range of drivers of mental health problems. One variable associated with poor mental wellbeing was the fear of COVID-19 infection, which is perhaps unsurprising given that this group often had contact with COVID-19 patients [14, 15, 27]. Social isolation from others and the severing of bonds has also been identified as a stressor [15]. This stressor is important because support from other people was both a protective factor against, and a means to cope with, mental health problems [30, 31]. Finally, in some cultural contexts, religiosity and prayer were also important coping strategies for dealing with mental health stressors [15, 31].

Our study is situated within a setting in which the pandemic was uniquely experienced. Hong Kong introduced arguably some of the longest and the strictest COVID restrictions in the world [32]. A compulsory mask-wearing order was in place for 945 days [33], as just one part of a suite of pandemic control measures implemented by the government including compulsory quarantine for all infected cases and a three-week hotel quarantine for all in-bound travellers [32]. Government restrictions also impacted the regulations that were put in place by medical schools. For example, clinical training that would have been done in face-to-face settings was instead moved online [34]. In addition, clinical attachments occurring in the final year of study were cancelled [35].

Given this contextual backdrop, it is understandable that there was poor mental health amongst the city’s medical students during the pandemic, with people reporting concerning levels on indices of stress, depression, and anxiety [36, 37]. Existing studies have shown that, as in other geographic contexts, the fear of potential COVID-19 infection was a source of great stress for medical students [37]. Support was also as a crucial factor, with those living alone reporting higher rates of mental health issues [36]. Yet, these studies are all based on survey data, providing us with only general trends and reported rates of mental health problems. Qualitative examinations of medical students’ mental health in Hong Kong during the pandemic were rare. One directly relevant study to ours is that by Yip and colleagues [38], which is a qualitative investigation of early career nurses’ general experiences during the pandemic. This demographic group partly overlaps with the sample examined in this study, as we also included participants who were already in the work force (recent graduates). A key difference to our study it that we have a broader sample in demographic terms, because we included medical students, as well as nursing graduates, those who already graduated, and those who were yet to graduate. Finally, Yip and colleagues’ study explicitly focused on nurses who cared for COVID-19 patients, whereas our sample had not necessarily treated infected patients. Yip and colleagues found that nurses reported poor mental health during the pandemic and that they drew upon self-care and support from others as coping strategies [38]. Our study fills a gap by providing a comprehensive qualitative examination of the research question, how did the COVID-19 pandemic impact the mental health experiences of both medical students and recent medical graduates in Hong Kong? Our study identifies the various sources of adverse mental health and further explicates the coping strategies that medical students used to deal with such stress. It remains imperative to understand these experiences given that the pandemic instilled lasting changes in both the healthcare and education sectors.

Methods

This article reports on the results of a nested study from within a larger project on the impacts of COVID-19 on university students transitioning to the workforce in Hong Kong [39, 40]. In the larger project, we conducted audio-recorded semi-structured interviews with 68 students about their experiences during these transitions. From this larger corpus, we selected a smaller subsample of interviews to focus on (a nested study). Namely, we focused on interviews with medical students2. We selected medical students as the participants for this nested study for several reasons. First, the second author had experiences with their educational needs as she is a teacher of these students. Second, we were particularly interested in understanding medical students’ experiences as they had distinctly different pandemic learning circumstances compared to other students. For example, their hands-on clinical training was moved online or replaced by simulation-based technologies [34]. In addition, some medical students were also involved in frontline healthcare provision to patients, such as through vaccination drives [12]. Third, focusing solely on a subsample of medical students allowed us to examine a broadly comparable group of participants. Although the findings from the larger project have been published elsewhere [39], this is the first time that we have presented findings on this topic in relation to the nested study. The sampling for this nested study included various criteria. Students must have attended university in Hong Kong. In addition, they needed to be over 18 years of age. Participants must have studied or graduated from university between 2020 and 2023. The reasoning for this criterion is that we wanted to interview those whose education had been most directly impacted by COVID-19. Prospective participants must also have lived in Hong Kong for at least six months since the start of the pandemic. We included this criterion to ensure that we were interviewing participants who had been similarly impacted by Hong Kong’s unique restrictions. Finally, participants must have studied medicine, surgery, nursing, or dentistry at university This final criterion ensured that our subsample participants had all been involved in healthcare-related disciplines. Only 18 interviews were conducted because we had reached the required number of interviews for the larger project and because of time constraints. For the latter point, all the data needed to be collected within a particular timeframe in the larger project.

Participants were recruited into the larger project in several ways. First, potential interviewees were identified through a survey that was undertaken as part of the larger study. The survey respondents were invited to leave their contact details if they wished to be interviewed. Second, we recruited participants through the personal networks of colleagues and members of the wider research team. Third, we sent e-mails to all students in one university to promote the study. Fourth, we attempted to utilize snowball sampling, whereby the interviewees were asked to refer the study to other eligible people. Due to ethical reasons, we did not keep a record of which participants were referred by specific previous interviewees. During the recruitment, the participants were provided with an information sheet about the study and an informed consent form by email. All participants gave written consent to participate in the study before data was collected. Participants were informed of the fact that their participation was voluntary, that they could withdraw from the study at any point, and that all identifying information would be anonymized. Ethical approval was provided by the University of Hong Kong’s Human Research Ethics Committee (EA200104).

The semi-structured interviews occurred face-to-face or online via Zoom. The interviews were audio-recorded. The interviews were conducted in either English or Cantonese, and two participants chose to be interviewed in the latter language. The findings were broadly comparable regardless of the mode of the interview, or the language used. The interview guide focused on the impact of the pandemic on participants’ mental health, mental health stressors, changes to study and career plans, and coping strategies. It was developed in a multi-pronged way as the questions were informed by the survey results from the larger project and an existing scoping review and engagement with the literature [40]. Questions were developed and refined by the research team and those team members who spoke both English and Cantonese translated the interview guide. The length of the interviews ranged from 15 to 32 min. Interviews were conducted in October 2022 and between January and June 2023.

The audio-recorded interviews data were transcribed verbatim. The interviews in Cantonese were translated into English by research team members who spoke both languages. All identifying information in the transcripts was anonymized. We analysed the data using reflexive thematic analysis [RTA], which is a methodological approach to analysing qualitative data [41, 42]. RTA involves researchers extensively coding a qualitative dataset to generate and identify themes, whereby “a theme captur[es] a pattern of meaning across the dataset.” [41, p.20] In accordance with RTA, we neither utilized codebooks, nor calculated interrater reliability [42]. We acknowledge that such an approach means our analysis was to some extent subjective. However, our focus was on exploring participants’ meaning-making in their interviews [41]. Codebooks can be too restrictive in approaching qualitative data. In addition, we dealt with issues of interrater reliability by the first and second authors meeting to discuss codes and resolve differences in interpretation of the data. It is important to be reflexive and consider how our positionality affected our understanding of the data. The first and second authors are both university educators and dealt with students extensively during the pandemic. In particular, the second author teaches medical students—the demographic group of interest in the nested study. These positionings were valuable as they sensitized us to the importance of mental health amongst such a sample and aided in identifying the stressors in the data. In addition, the authors’ experiences helped in determining (to some extent) the reliability and validity of medical students’ narratives, as we were able to determine whether they aligned with what we had observed amongst the very students that we teach. Finally, our values as researchers shaped our engagement with the data. We take a positive view of mental health, and this fact led us to see medical students as resilient figures in this period despite the numerous stressors they faced [40, 43].

The analysis began with the first author repeatedly reading the entire subset of 18 interviews, alongside making notes. The coding process was top-down in focus because it was motivated by a pre-existing interest in students’ mental health. It was followed by the second author verifying the coding process. As a point of clarification, we conceived of mental health broadly, as a continuum anchored by positive psychological wellbeing on one side, and (negative) psychological distress on the other side [40]. Our conceptualization of mental health also incorporated both positive and negative emotions, because of the well-established connections between affect and mental wellbeing [40, 43–45]. It should be noted that the two authors engaged in the coding process separately. The first and second authors coded all utterances by individual interviewees. The codes aimed to capture “the manifest (explicit) and latent (underlying) meaning” of utterances [46, p.298]. The coding process was recursive, with the authors revisiting earlier interviews considering the coding of later ones. A large set of initial codes were generated across the dataset of interviews. These codes were then organized into preliminary categories—where each category attempted to capture a singular theme [47]. Following this, both authors met multiple times to review and compare both of their codes and thematic categories. After these collaborative discussions, both authors combined and adjusted the thematic groupings. These discussions ensured that the analytic process was transparent and resolved any issues with discrepant codes and themes. There were no substantive disagreements in the process of coding and allocating names to themes. All themes were agreed upon during these discussions between authors. The only differences in opinion between authors were in the naming of the themes. To be sure, these differences were minor and only resulted in slight changes to the names of themes. It should also be noted that throughout the coding process, the authors focused only on categories, themes, and meanings that were in common across the interviews. In other words, we did not seek out deviant cases. Ultimately, these discussions led to the refinement of the analytic argument. Through this process, two superordinate themes were generated. Superordinate themes can be thought of as “overarching themes” [47, 48, p.465]. The two superordinate themes were mental health stressors and coping strategies. Our analysis is structured around these two superordinate themes and is accompanied by the presentation of short extracts of transcripts as evidence of these themes3࿼.

Results

Mental health stressors

Multiple demographic groups reported increased rates of psychological distress during the pandemic [49, 50], and medical students were no different [29]. The medical students in our study reported three broad kinds of mental health stressors. First, they talked about physical stressors. Second, medical students talked about the socio-relational stressors of infecting others and social isolation. Third, the medical students reported experiencing adverse mental health due to the educational stressor of insufficient preparedness. These stressors interacted with each other and had a compounded negative effect on students’ mental health These factors are depicted graphically in Fig. 1 below.

Thematic diagram of mental health stressors

Physical stressors

In Hong Kong, medical students were deployed to the frontline as part of the healthcare workforce during the pandemic, working with COVID-19 patients and in other-pandemic related work, such as vaccination drives [12]. Their roles meant that they were amongst the people in Hong Kong with likely the highest risk of exposure to the virus. One reason for this was that under Hong Kong’s zero-COVID approach, positive cases in the community were immediately isolated, quarantined, and treated by medical professionals [51]. One risk that was cited as a mental health stressor was the physical proximity in the workplace to potential carriers of the virus. The interviewee below cites the mental toll it took on being close to people who could have the virus. The physical proximity was also overlaid with a degree of uncertainty of both the nature of the virus and their jobs.

“The biggest challenge to me, mmm, I think it is the mental stress. Because every day you have to be apprehensive as you don’t know whether or not you will contract the virus. If you contract the virus- because the workplace environment makes us susceptible to contracting the virus” (Interviewee 58).

The physical risk also had social consequences, as the interviewee below stated that the potential for infection altered how they interacted with colleagues and consequently affected their wellbeing.

“If you ask me, the biggest impact had on my interactions with my colleagues at the workplace is that there are some negative emotions. Maybe because for those working in the frontlines, we are most exposed to the dangers of the pandemic when a colleague becomes sick” (Interviewee 58).

Another stressor for medical students was the physical environment that they encountered in their hospital workplaces. The two interviewees in the excerpts below detailed that using personal protective equipment (PPE) for extended periods of time, as well as physical constraints such as separating COVID patients with plastic, shower-like curtains (“barricades”) contributed to their poor mental health.

“Another thing is, we have to wear masks for an extended amount of time in our working environment […] We also feel very hot wearing all the gear which is why my mood would often be more negative” (Interviewee 58).

“I think people are more stressed, and more tired, more frustrated with you know the long hours of working […] the plastic barricade, the gown up, gown down, and then the difficulty of wearing the N95 mask for very long hours […] all these things might not add up to a great job satisfaction score” (Interviewee 57).

Ultimately, these excerpts reveal the tightly interconnected nature of physical and psychological health. Usually, one would expect them to be in equilibrium. Yet, problems with physical health (discomfort due to PPE), led to problems with psychological health (negative emotions and psychological distress).

Socio-relational stressors

An often-reported stressor by medical students was the threat of COVID-19 infection. Earlier, we saw how this threat was linked to physical proximity and risk. Yet, infection was also a socio-relational stressor (see dashed connecting line in Fig. 1). That is, the main vector of infection was through contact with other people and these worries also related to one’s relationships with other people.

“I am worried about infecting the people I live with, especially if there are elderly” (Interviewee 58).

Interviewer: “So, did the pandemic affect your mental health and your emotions in any ways?”

Interviewee: “If simply talking about the disease. It was like simply maybe throughout all the time you would be afraid to get it yourself or you are afraid that someone’s going to around you get that because it’s actually pretty troublesome. If you have some relatives or someone who (you) live together, get COVID at the close contact ((inaudible)) eh it’s very troublesome” (Interviewee 11).

The interviewees above refer to dual concerns—namely infecting and being infected by others. This fear was not unique to Hong Kong medical students. Indeed, Magruder and colleagues found that social workers in the USA also reported anxiety being caused by the potential of infecting others, such as their clients [21]. However, infections gained an important status—namely, as something to avoid—in Hong Kong because positive cases and their close contacts were forcibly isolated in government-run quarantine facilities for up to 21 days [52]. Thus, infection could cause a domino effect of sorts for others, as they would also be quarantined. Therefore, infection was relational as it explicitly involved those to whom you had social ties.

The excerpts above also reflect the inherently moral character of COVID-19 infection as a stressor. Interviewees expressed their “worry” and fears of harming others through infection. In addition, infection was cast as something that should be avoided. These statements evoke obligations for not infecting others and potentially subjecting them to extensive quarantine periods. Finally, one interviewee expressed concern about infecting those who may be at higher risk of death, such as the “elderly” and immunocompromised. In all, one’s pre-existing social bonds lend themselves to an expressed norm of avoiding risk of infection for oneself and others.

Another socio-relational stressor was in fact characterized by a lack of contact with other people. Isolation from others was reported as taking a toll on medical students’ psychological wellbeing by causing adverse mental health experiences and negative emotions. Social isolation was due to government-imposed social distancing requirements and the obligations to not infect other people (see dashed connecting line in Fig. 1).

“Being stuck in a place that I can’t go out or do any activities and being alone is very stressful to me. I really can’t hold it” (Interviewee 62).

“So, mental health, because I had to stay home basically most of the time, because of like, there’s even if I wish to go out of my house, there is nothing I can do, because like shops are closed, restaurants are- well, I try to avoid eating at restaurants as well and I can’t go to the hospital to like meet cases or anything. So mental health wise, I think I am confined to my house, even my room. So, it’s kind of depressing” (Interviewee 66).

“I think the most mentally challenging part was that- I think for me, I enjoy going out, I enjoy going to the lessons, I enjoy having physical interaction with people” (Interviewee 64).

From the extracts above, social isolation reshaped medical students’ daily lives, and indeed, the physical world around them. The students remarked about being largely confined to their homes. Notably, this isolation was also likely exacerbated by the fact that Hong Kong homes are amongst the smallest in the world [53]. In fact, there is an established link between poor mental wellbeing and living in a small housing space in Hong Kong [54, 55]. The students reported being unable to visit places such as shops and restaurants, leading them to be unable to undertake many of their usual activities and hobbies. Thus, we see that mental health was linked to a semblance of ‘normality’ and ‘routine’ that was disrupted by the pandemic.

Isolation from classmates was also identified as a mental health stressor for medical students.

“I think the most- the biggest impact (of) the isolation because I cannot know the progress of my classmates and where I should be, at like which level I should be at. So, this makes me, like I have (not)- like I would say, I lost my, like I don’t know where I am in the study, so I lack the sense of progress during study in the time of COVID outbreak. I think that makes me feel lonely and frustrated at the time” (Interviewee 71).

This kind of isolation was problematic because it meant that students lost a key form of emotional and educational support. It was particularly difficult to lose that source of support—or at least have it heavily curtailed—because their classmates were often the only ones who knew the difficulties that they were facing as fellow medical students. In all, the mental health impact of COVID-19 was multifaceted. COVID-19 caused psychological distress and negative emotions due to disrupted social relationships and upheaval to educational attainment and progress.

Educational stressors

The final stressor was a reported feeling of insufficient preparedness for clinical practice. Feeling unprepared for the workforce and having anxiety when entering one’s first job is neither unique to medical students, nor to crisis periods [40, 56]. However, the pandemic did fundamentally alter medical education. In Hong Kong, there were numerous changes to medical education, including clinical training rotations being cancelled, lectures and tutorials being moved online, and the introduction of simulation and video-based replacements for training [34]. The flow on effect of this educational change was a change in the self-reported abilities and preparedness of medical students, which was not conducive to mental wellbeing.

“So that definitely made me feel more like uncomfortable and sad. And because of the lack of clinical exposure at school, again, I felt really like unsure about whether or not I was a competent person to be able to graduate. And I was really like lacking confidence in a lot of my clinical skills, mainly that yeah, and like- the lack of like confidence part definitely affected the mental health” (Interviewee 70).

“I can’t speak on behalf of all my classmates, but I do feel like there’s a sense of imposter syndrome […] It’s almost like the curriculum has been swept under your feet. It’s been kind of altered to the point of not necessarily like beyond recognition, but it’s very different experience” (Interviewee 69).

“I feel a little bit nervous because I’ll be a doctor since a few weeks ahead but my learning may not be as good as my precedent’s colleagues before COVID. So I feel a little bit nervous but I guess it is still okay because I will work under supervision anyway” (Interviewee 71).

As visible in the final excerpt, some interviewees drew explicit comparisons between themselves and the ‘pre-COVID’ cohorts. These latter cohorts did not have their learning and curricula altered by the pandemic. Furthermore, these students’ disrupted education led to self-doubt, questioning their own abilities as doctors, and other negative emotions, as they moved into their first clinical roles. It remains an open question—and beyond the scope of this paper—whether these comparisons to others, impaired education, and negative emotions led to material differences in these students’ clinical abilities once they entered the healthcare workforce.

Coping strategies

Students reported adverse mental health experiences because of the COVID-19 pandemic. However, they usually did not just accept these problems. Instead, participants reported utilizing different coping strategies to deal with the mental health stressors of working and learning during the pandemic (see Fig. 2).

Thematic diagram of coping strategies

Support as a coping strategy

Medical students reported seeking professional mental health support in both educational and work settings. This finding is perhaps unsurprising given that there is existing evidence that medical students are more likely to seek mental health support than students in other disciplines [57]. Seeking out support can be conceptualized as a reactive coping strategy in our data because students needed assistance (support) to deal with the past and ongoing mental distress caused by the pandemic [58, 59]. These forms of support were both professional and accredited because they involved students utilizing university health services where they could schedule appointments with trained counsellors and psychiatrists. Students were appreciative of these services, even if they were not well advertised. The interviewees cited advantages of these services, such as easy accessibility and low cost, as opposed to the prohibitive cost of using private providers.

“I used some of those, like counsellors […] So, actually, the university has provided quite a lot of support, which is good” (Interviewee 62).

A distinguishing feature between medical students and those in other disciplines is that the former had within-faculty staff, such as counsellors, that were only accessible to them. The interviewee below describes the presence of a formal scheme instituted by their faculty that is also conveniently located, “just [go] down the stairs”.

“Mental health, there is the, like the faculty provided a team called Keep us Healthy! [pseudonym]. So basically, they are equipped with, like psychologist or even psychiatrist for you if you have any problems. […] So basically, if you have any questions, just go down the stairs and you can ask for like any help” (Interviewee 67).

Such schemes raise the question of inequalities existing in access to university mental health support services for students. Specifically, these resources are limited and tailored to one small group of students (those in the medical departments). In contrast, those students in other faculties can only access the generic services provided to all students at the university, for which demand is much higher. It is beyond the scope of the current paper to consider these inequalities and whether students feel disadvantaged or not by such differential access to resources.

Professional support is also available to those individuals who are in the workforce. The Hong Kong health department and hospitals have set-up various schemes in the workplace, as detailed in the extract below.

“They have something I’m also not sure of the full name, but it’s called Splendor. [pseudonym] ((inaudible)) It is an organization that actually regularly they will try to address you via like email. That maybe help you do a self-assessment or session where you do talk so you could actually join them. And yeah, it’s still going on under the pandemic, but I’m not sure how effective that might be” (Interviewee 11).

The interviewee describes the most prominent of these schemes, Splendor (pseudonym), which provides psychological assessments and sessions where staff get together to share their experiences of working and mental health issues. The interviewee also alludes to the fact that it is appreciated that these support mechanisms are available, whilst also questioning their efficacy.

Medical students also drew upon socio-relational support from their immediate social networks, such as friends, family, and colleagues.

“So having kind of someone to talk with, to ventilate was really important” (Interviewee 59).

“Being together with colleagues does make things a little better. So working alongside each other while chatting and sharing laughs makes things a little better” (Interviewee 58).

In both excerpts, the interviewees describe the support gained from interacting with other people, “ventilate”, “chatting”, and “sharing laughs”. In these ways they draw upon commonsense sociocultural discourses of verbal disclosure of one’s problems to others as a means of support and as a way of lessening the severity of mental health problems [43, 60].

The support provided by medical students’ social networks was largely emotional in nature. In contrast, some medical students received more instrumental forms of support from their classmates. Importantly, the classmates provided instrumental support in the form of updating them on their progress, issues with coursework, as well as how to adapt to the new modes of learning.

“We see less of my classmates. And I think that also can be stressful because you don’t really know what other people are doing. And so, you can’t really support each other and also- and also kind of gauge where your revision level is with other people and you can’t really talk to other people about like sharing information and sharing tips with each other” (Interviewee 56).

COVID-19 fractured social relationships through enforced distancing from others. Thus, a major way through which medical students obtained social support was by technologically mediated means.

“So I will Zoom or FaceTime with my friends. Although meeting in like meeting in person is definitely different from meeting on the computer, but at least I can still talk to people and keep my relationships and also had a great, a much greater time at home” (Interviewee 66).

“For mental health, I also would zoom a lot of my friends or call a lot of my friends as well” (Interviewee 70).

The interviewees above detailed how support was obtained through mediated forms of communication, such as Zoom and FaceTime. Interviewees noted their preference for in-person support, yet still valued the support provided through technologically mediated means.

Drastic measures

Medical students sought out multiple ways of remedying their mental health problems during the pandemic. These coping strategies were largely adaptive and took the form of professional and informal relational support networks. However, interviewees also took what we term “drastic actions” to cope with mental stress. These actions were termed drastic as they were the most ‘extreme’ coping strategies utilized by participants in our data. These drastic measures were taken largely by those already working in the healthcare sector. Some interviewees reported that they themselves and colleagues had left medicine altogether or temporarily. Indeed, other studies have also shown that the pandemic has had an impact on healthcare professionals and medical students’ intentions to leave practice or drop out of their programs respectively [30, 61, 62]. Leaving medicine can be considered a reactive coping strategy because it involved students dealing with an ongoing problem (mental health and distress) and responding to it in an extreme way—leaving the profession [58, 59].

Interviewer: “So you’ve talked about switching while taking a sabbatical and going into the US. So why did you take this step and do you mind elaborating on it?”

Interviewee: “Sure. 100%. Just pure COVID burnout. […] I think the combination of both COVID and the hours kind of led to burnout. And the moment I finished the required training as a houseman, I think it was just the best choice to do for my mental health and physical health to leave and kind of get out of a place that had such strict COVID rules where because other countries it was much more open already” (Interviewee 63).

The interviewee above detailed that he took a temporary sabbatical from medicine due to the stress experienced while working. The excessive workload during the pandemic caused burnout for this recent medical graduate. The pause in his career was just that, a temporary one. Nevertheless, such drastic measures were viewed as necessary steps to either ameliorate their mental health problems before resuming their careers.

Discussion

Our study investigated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health of medical students in Hong Kong. While the pandemic is over, the impacts on medical curriculum and work are likely to continue for at least some years. Our study has shed light on the main mental health stressors and coping strategies that our interviewees drew on during the pandemic. We also suggest ways to support medical students, as an important part of the medical workforce, should another health crisis happen in the future.

At a general level, an implication of our analysis is that there were numerous mental health stressors across multiple domains of one’s life. At an interpersonal level [21–23], we found that medical students routinely expressed worrying about infecting other people. While primarily a socio-relational stressor, we also documented how this stressor was bound up with another stressor at a more institutional level [21–23]. Namely, the physical proximity and risk of being in a workplace close to infected patients. Furthermore, the fear of infecting other people was driven by a public policy level concern [21–23]. Specifically, the governmental policy of forced quarantine and the desire to avoid submitting close social relations to such a fate. Similarly, an implication of our findings is that stressors and coping strategies were also bound up with each other. Social isolation negatively affected medical students’ mental health. However, it was also precisely this isolation which impaired—to some extent—social support. That is, being isolated and distanced from others made it difficult to access face-to-face support and medical students instead had to rely on technologically mediated connections. The implication of this discussion is to demonstrate the complex and interconnected nature of mental health stressors on medical students during COVID-19. These findings support a socio-ecological framework insofar as we cannot consider medical students and their mental health in isolation, instead, it is affected by different facets and areas of their lives [20–23]. The ultimate implication of all this is to point to the fact that interventions and support mechanisms for medical students and their mental health need to be multidimensional and address the interrelated stressors and coping strategies [21–23].

In terms of other stressors, we found that, for those already working in some capacity, physical stressors such as excessive time spent in PPE was associated with psychological distress. These findings contrast other research which has shown that medical students were worried by a lack of PPE [16, 63, 64]. In contrast, we found that the presence was a stressor. One implication of this finding is for medical schools to introduce use of PPE in students’ clinical training, for them to be physically and psychologically accustomed to wearing it for sustained periods of time. Our analysis also corroborated the widely reported finding that medical students were psychologically distressed by the risk of infection [14, 15, 27, 37]. We further extend this finding by showing the moral and relational nature of this stressor. It is not just about being infected, but it was framed as a need to avoid infecting others because of how it could affect those social relationships. It is important to note that this was potentially a restricted stressor in contextual and time-limited terms. That is, it was likely limited to the time in which the pandemic occurred. The stress was likely caused by COVID-19 being a novel and largely unknown disease with uncertain clinical outcomes. However, COVID-19 is now largely endemic [65]. One implication is that future research should investigate whether potential COVID-19 infection still causes psychological distress to medical students and healthcare professionals. Importantly, for future pandemics, it will be crucial to understand that a key driver of distress might be the fear around infection and its impact on social relationships.

Educational stressors were also reported by the students in our sample. Namely, insufficient preparedness for the workforce was related to disrupted clinical training. This finding is concordant with other studies which have shown the relationship between an upheaval in medical education caused by the pandemic and a correspondent self-reported lack of confidence in their own skills on behalf of medical students [66, 67]. Additionally, our participants negatively compared themselves to other cohorts that had graduated and were not impacted by the pandemic. This finding might reflect a more general trend that some groups of students—particularly those in the preclinical stage—might have been more adversely impacted than others [27, 68]. An implication of these findings is that future research should consider whether anxiety, distress, and the associated disruption to skills persist post-pandemic. It will be necessary for workplaces to provide sustained support to those ‘COVID cohorts’ as they manage and navigate their early careers. In future health crises, when education is disrupted, universities should actively seek ways to compensate for limited available clinical training through other alternative teaching and learning modes and methods.

Our study identified support from others as a coping strategy, and thus, we replicated the existing finding that the presence of social support was associated with better mental health for medical students during the pandemic [30, 34, 36]. Our qualitative investigation sheds light on the means through which support was beneficial for medical students’ mental health. Namely, it was effective through the presence of formalized support schemes, simple emotional disclosure, and the instrumental support provided by classmates. The implication of this finding is simple, to provide medical students with support systems, beyond those already in place. Efforts should be made to provide medical students with services such as group support networks, peer learning support systems (for instrumental support), drop-in services, and even technologically mediated networks. The goal is to provide students with sufficient support to facilitate communication with others during crisis times.

One strength of our study is that we focused on the unique context of Hong Kong. The city formed a perspicuous setting in which to examine the impact of the pandemic on mental health [69]. It was a perspicuous setting precisely because of the extensive and heightened infection control mechanisms put in place, compared to other places around the world. While there were existing quantitative studies on the mental health of Hong Kong university students during COVID-19, these studies provided superficial and broad findings [36, 37]. In contrast, our qualitative lens provided investigated the in-depth mental health experiences of medical students to reveal the interconnected nature of stressors and coping strategies used. Another strength of this study was our somewhat broad participant inclusion criteria. We recruited participants that were both in university and in the workforce during the pandemic. Thus, our subsample in the nested study utilized a more expansive range of participants than previous qualitative studies [38]. In doing so, we were able to arrive at a more holistic understanding of mental health experiences of medical students during the pandemic.

A limitation of our study is that data was collected during very specific phases of the pandemic—namely, October 2022 and from January to June 2023. These latter dates were towards the end of the pandemic, after the worst had passed. Furthermore, they occurred toward the end of confinement measures in Hong Kong. Quarantine requirements were lifted in January 2023 [70] and the mask mandate was abolished in March 2023 [33]. Thus, there would likely have been a change in medical students4’ mental health and coping at that point in time. It is possible that their mental health was largely back to normal toward this tail end of the pandemic, and that this may have coloured their recall of their earlier psychological states. Another limitation relates to the potential for selection bias in this study. Both the larger corpus in the project and the subsample were self-selecting. Some students may have been driven to participate by aspects of the topic, such as university-to-work transitions, the impact of COVID-19, and mental wellbeing. As such, we may have interviewed participants who were motivated and interested in the topic, which may have altered the narratives and findings we gathered. Finally, the relatively small sub-sample of medical students may not be generalizable and representative of the wider population in Hong Kong.

Conclusions

To conclude, this study identified the main mental health stressors that medical students experienced during the pandemic. While some of these stressors (for example, risk of infection) are difficult to control, other stressors related to workplace and studying practices, as well as existing infrastructures, could and should, be addressed to ensure that both universities and workplaces are prepared for any future health crisis. Consolidated efforts and close collaboration between universities and workplaces could ensure that issues raised by the medical students, such as clinical exposure and preparedness for the workforce are dealt with effectively. The health of medical students is not only a serious public health concern but is also a serious societal issue, as these medical students are the core of the future medical workforce.
