Abstract
. Frankl and Pach [ 8 ] and Erdős [ 5 ] conjectured that any ( d + 1 ) (d+1) -uniform set family ℱ ⊆ ( [ n ] d + 1 ) \mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1} with VC-dimension at most d d has size at most ( n − 1 d ) \binom{n-1}{d} when n n is sufficiently large. Ahlswede and Khachatrian [ 1 ] showed that the conjecture is false by giving a counterexample of size ( n − 1 d ) + ( n − 4 d − 2 ) \binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2} . For a set family ℱ ⊆ ( [ n ] d + 1 ) \mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1} , the condition that its VC-dimension is at most d d can be reformulated as follows: for any F ∈ ℱ F\in\mathcal{F} , there exists a set B F ⊆ F B_{F}\subseteq F such that F ∩ F ′ ≠ B F F\cap F^{\prime}\neq B_{F} for all F ′ ∈ ℱ F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F} . In this direction, the first author, Xu, Yip, and Zhang [ 4 ] conjectured that the bound ( n − 1 d ) \binom{n-1}{d} holds if we further assume that | B F | = s |B_{F}|=s for every F ∈ ℱ F\in\mathcal{F} and for some fixed 0 ⩽ s ⩽ d 0\leqslant s\leqslant d . The case s = 0 s=0 is exactly the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, and the cases s ∈ { 1 , d } s\in\{1,d\} were proved in the paper by the first author, Xu, Yip, and Zhang [ 4 ] . In this short note, we show that the conjecture holds when s ⩽ d / 2 s\leqslant d/2 , and the maximal constructions are stars. Moreover, we construct non-star set families of size ( n − 1 d ) \binom{n-1}{d} satisfying the condition for d / 2 < s ⩽ d − 1 d/2<s\leqslant d-1 , which suggests that the problem is substantially different in these cases.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
be a
(d+1)
-uniform set system over the ground set
[n]\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{1,\dots,n\}
. The
Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension (VC-dimension)
of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted as
\textup{VC}(\mathcal{F})
is defined to be the size of the largest set
S\subseteq[n]
such that every subset of
S
is realized as an intersection
S\cap F
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}
, i.e. for all
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
, there exists
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
S^{\prime}=S\cap F
.
Since every set family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
has VC-dimension at most
d+1
, it is natural to ask for the maximum size of
\mathcal{F}
with VC-dimension at most
d
. Frankl and Pach
[
8
]
proved that any such set family has size at most
\binom{n}{d}
. Then Frankl and Pach
[
8
]
and Erdős
[
5
]
conjectured that the upper bound can be improved to
\binom{n-1}{d}
when
n
is large enough. However, Ahlswede and Khachatrian constructed a counterexample of size
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
, and this was later generalized by Mubayi and Zhao
[
10
]
where they constructed a class of constructions attaining the same size. The upper bound was recently improved to
\binom{n-1}{d}+O_{d}(n^{d-2})
by Yang and Yu
[
12
]
, matching the second order term of the family constructed in
[
10
]
up to a multiplicative constant. For more works along this line, see
[
4
,
9
,
11
]
.

Notice that in the case when
\mathcal{F}
is a
(d+1)
-uniform family, we can equivalently formulate the condition that
\textup{VC}(\mathcal{F})\leqslant d
as follows: for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a subset
B_{F}\subseteq F
such that for any
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq B_{F}
. We call
B_{F}
a
witness
of
F
in the sense that it cannot be realized as an intersection of
F
with any set
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. In this note, we are interested in the case where all witness sets
B_{F}
have the same size.

Definition 1.1

(
s
-witness family)
.

Let
n\geqslant d+1
and
0\leqslant s\leqslant d
. A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is an
s
-witness family
if it satisfies the following condition: for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists
B_{F}\subseteq F
of size
s
such that
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq B_{F}
for every
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.

A
0
-witness family
\mathcal{F}
is exactly an intersecting family since we have
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
for all
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. The well-known Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem states the following upper bound for intersecting families.

Theorem 1.2

(Erdős–Ko–Rado
[
6
]
)
.

Let
d\geqslant 0
and
n\geqslant 2(d+1)
. If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is an intersecting family, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant\binom{n-1}{d}
. Moreover, when
n>2(d+1)
, equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is a star.

As a generalization of the Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem, the first author, Xu, Yip, and Zhang made the following conjecture in
[
4
]
.

Conjecture 1.3

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2(d+1)
and
0\leqslant s\leqslant d
. Suppose
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is an
s
-witness family. Then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant\binom{n-1}{d}
.

It is clear that when
s=0
,
1.3
follows from
Theorem

1.2
. In
[
4
]
, the authors confirmed
1.3
in the case where
s\in\{1,d\}
and
n
is sufficiently large. Moreover, they showed that equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is a star when
s\in\{1,d\}
and remarked that it might be plausible to conjecture that the maximum set families achieving the bound in
1.3
must be stars for all
0\leqslant s\leqslant d
.

In this note, we prove
1.3
in the case when
s\leqslant d/2
and
n
is sufficiently large.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
1\leqslant s\leqslant d/2
and let
n
be sufficiently large. Suppose
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is an
s
-witness family. Then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant\binom{n-1}{d}
. Moreover, if
|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{n-1}{d}
, then
\mathcal{F}
is a star.

Together with
Theorem

1.2
and the results in
[
4
]
, the remaining open cases of
1.3
are when
d/2<s\leqslant d-1
. In these remaining open cases, we give a construction that is not a single star achieving the size
\binom{n-1}{d}
, illustrating the barriers to adapting current methods as all the proofs of the known cases show that
\mathcal{F}
must be a single star if equality is achieved.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any
n\geqslant s+3
and
d/2<s\leqslant d-1
, there exists an
s
-witness family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
of size
\binom{n-1}{d}
that is not a star.

Since our proof of
Theorem

1.4
proceeds by approximating
\mathcal{F}
by a single star, it does not generalize easily to the cases where
s>d/2
. Thus, we believe new ideas are required to prove the remaining cases of
1.3
.

Paper Organization

In
Section

2
we present the proof of
Theorem

1.4
. Then in
Section

3
we give the construction that proves
Theorem

1.5
. Finally, in
Section

4
we make some concluding remarks.

Notations.
For sets
A,B\subseteq[n]
, we denote their symmetric difference as
A\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
. We always consider
s
and
d
as fixed and
n\to\infty
. We use
O_{d}(\cdot)
and

to suppress leading constants depending on
d
.

2.
Proof of
Theorem

1.4

Our proof consists of three steps split into the three subsequent subsections. In the first step presented in
Section

2.1
, we analyze the structure of an
s
-witness set family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
for
1\leqslant s\leqslant d/2
. In particular, we show in
Lemma

2.2
that we can capture the intersection properties of
\mathcal{F}
using set families of constant size. Then in
Lemma

2.4
, we define a certain injection of a subset of
\mathcal{F}
into
\binom{[n]}{d}
that will be the main object that we analyze later on. Next, in
Section

2.2
, we show that any family
\mathcal{F}
that is close to achieving the maximum size can be approximated by a star family. Finally, in
Section

2.3
, we perform a stability analysis, showing that in fact any set family achieving size
\binom{n-1}{d}
must be a single star, concluding the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

From now on, we fix
1\leqslant s\leqslant d/2
and let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
be an
s
-witness family. In particular, for
F\in\mathcal{F}
, we always fix a choice of its witness
B_{F}\subseteq F
of size
s
so that
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq B_{F}
for any
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Let us recall the Erdős-Rado sunflower lemma that will be used in the proof of
Lemma

2.2
. For
r\geqslant 2
, a family of
r
sets
H_{1},\dots,H_{r}\subseteq[n]
is called a
k
-sunflower
if there exists
S\subseteq[n]
such that
H_{i}\cap H_{j}=S
for all
i\neq j\in[r]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Erdős-Rado sunflower lemma
[
7
]
)
.

Let
k
and
r\geqslant 2
be positive integers. If
\mathcal{F}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
has size
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant k!(r-1)^{k}
, then
\mathcal{F}
contains an
r
-sunflower.

The bound in
Lemma

2.1
was later improved by Alweiss-Lovette-Wu-Zhang
[
2
]
and Bell–Chueluecha–Warnke
[
3
]
, but the bound stated in
Lemma

2.1
suffices for our purposes. Now we begin the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

2.1.
Structural Analysis

In this section, we prove that one can capture the intersection properties in
\mathcal{F}
using set families of constant size. First, observe that we can interpret the uniform witness condition as follows. For any
B\in\binom{[n]}{s}
and
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
B_{F}=B
and
B\subseteq F^{\prime}
, we must have
(F\setminus B)\cap(F^{\prime}\setminus B)\neq\varnothing
. In other words, the two families
\{F\setminus B\mid F\in\mathcal{F},\,B_{F}=B\}
and
\{F^{\prime}\setminus B\mid F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F},\,B\subset F^{\prime}\}
are cross-intersecting.

Define
\mathcal{B}=\{B\in\binom{[n]}{s}\mid\exists F\in\mathcal{F}\text{ such that }B_{F}=B\}
to be the set of all witnesses for
\mathcal{F}
. We have the following modeling lemma that captures for each
B\in\mathcal{B}
the structure of the sets
F\in\mathcal{F}
containing
B
.

Lemma 2.2

(Modeling Lemma)
.

For every
B\in\mathcal{B}
, there exists a set family
\mathcal{A}_{B}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus B}{\leqslant d+1-s}
consisting of sets of size at most
d+1-s
satisfying the following:

(1)

\mathcal{A}_{B}
is an intersecting family, i.e.
A\cap A^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
for all
A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
.

(2)

For every
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
B_{F}=B
, there exists
A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
such that
A\subseteq F
.

(3)

For every
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
B\subseteq F
(possibly
B_{F}\neq B
), we have
A\cap F\neq\varnothing
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
.

(4)

|\mathcal{A}_{B}|=O_{d}(1)
.

Proof.

We construct
\mathcal{A}_{B}
iteratively. Initialize
\mathcal{A}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{F\setminus B\mid F\in\mathcal{F},B_{F}=B\}
. If
\mathcal{A}
contains a
(d+2)
-sunflower
A_{1},\dots,A_{d+2}\in\mathcal{A}
with a core
S\subseteq[n]\setminus B
, then we remove the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{d+2}
from
\mathcal{A}
and add the set
S
to
\mathcal{A}
. We repeat this operation until
\mathcal{A}
is
(d+2)
-sunflower-free and set
\mathcal{A}_{B}
as the final (
d+2
)-sunflower-free family. We show that
\mathcal{A}_{B}
satisfies all the properties in
Lemma

2.2
.

First, note that the initial family
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{F\setminus B\mid F\in\mathcal{F},B_{F}=B\}
satisfies properties (1)-(3). Indeed,
\mathcal{A}_{0}
must be an intersecting family, because if there exists
A\cap A^{\prime}=\varnothing
, then the sets
(A\cup B),(A^{\prime}\cup B)\in\mathcal{F}
violate the property that
B
is the witness of
A\cup B
and
A^{\prime}\cup B
. Property (2) is trivially satisfied by definition. Property (3) also follows from the fact that
B
is the witness for sets of the form
A\cup B
where
A\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
. For a set
F\supseteq B
, we must have
F\cap(A\cup B)\neq B
and thus
F\cap A\neq\varnothing
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
.

Now we show that the operation of replacing each sunflower in
\mathcal{A}
by its core preserves properties (1)-(3). Suppose
\mathcal{A}
satisfies properties (1)-(3). We first show that
\mathcal{A}
is intersecting after replacing a
(d+2)
-sunflower
A_{1},\dots,A_{d+2}\in\mathcal{A}
with the core
S\subseteq[n]\setminus B
. Let
T\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{A_{1},\dots,A_{d+2}\}
and write
A_{i}=S\sqcup D_{i}
for all
i\in[d+2]
. We want to check that
T\cap S\neq\varnothing
.
Suppose for contradiction that
T\cap S=\varnothing
, then we must have
T\cap D_{i}\neq\varnothing
for all
i\in[d+2]
. Since
D_{1},\dots,D_{d+2}
are pairwise disjoint, we must have that
T\cap D_{i}
are pairwise disjoint for all
i\in[d+2]
. If follows
|T|\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{d+2}|T\cap D_{i}|\geqslant d+2
contradicting the fact that
|T|\leqslant d+1-s
. Therefore we must have
T\cap S\neq\varnothing
and thus the family
\mathcal{A}
after the replacement operation remains intersecting. Similarly, Property (3) holds because given
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
B\subseteq F
, we have
F\cap A_{i}\neq\varnothing
for all
i\in[d+2]
and thus we must have
F\cap S\neq\varnothing
by the same argument. Property (2) is clearly preserved since
S\subseteq A_{i}
for all
i\in[d+2]
.

Finally, we upper bound the size of
\mathcal{A}_{B}
. The family
\mathcal{A}_{B}
is (
d+2
)-sunflower-free and consists of sets of size at most
d+1-s
. We must have
\mathcal{A}_{B}
is (
d+2
)-sunflower-free at every uniformity. Thus by
Lemma

2.1
we have

|\mathcal{A}_{B}|\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{d+1-s}k!(d+1)^{k}=O_{d}(1).

This completes the proof.
∎

Define

\mathcal{F}_{B}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}\mid\exists A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}\text{ such that }A\cup B\subset F\}.

In particular, we have
F\in\mathcal{F}_{B}
if
B_{F}=B
, but
\mathcal{F}_{B}
may also include other sets. Note that at this point we no longer need to keep track of the particular choice of
B_{F}
for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
, instead we record that

\mathcal{F}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{F}_{B}.

For
B\in\mathcal{B}
, define
\alpha_{B}=\min_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}}|A|
.

Claim 2.3
.

For any
B\in\mathcal{B}
, we have

|\mathcal{F}_{B}|=O_{d}\left(n^{d+1-s-\alpha_{B}}\right).

Proof.

Since each
F\in\mathcal{F}_{B}
contains an element of
\mathcal{A}_{B}
as a subset, we get by double counting and the definition of
\alpha_{B}
:

|\mathcal{F}_{B}|\leqslant|\mathcal{A}_{B}|\cdot{n-s-\alpha_{B}\choose d+1-s-|A|}=|\mathcal{A}_{B}|\cdot O_{d}\left(n^{d+1-s-\alpha_{B}}\right).

By
Item

4
in
Lemma

2.2
, we have
|\mathcal{A}_{B}|=O_{d}(1)
and thus we have the desired bound.
∎

Define
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\{B\in\mathcal{B}\mid\alpha_{B}=1\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\geqslant 2}=\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{1}
. For
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
let
x_{B}
be the unique element so that
\{x_{B}\}\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
(
x_{B}
is unique since
\mathcal{A}_{B}
is an intersecting family). Since
\mathcal{A}_{B}
is intersecting, if
\{x_{B}\}\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
, then we can redefine
\mathcal{A}_{B}=\{\{x_{B}\}\}
and the properties in
Lemma

2.2
are still satisfied. Now we define an injection of a subset of
\mathcal{F}
into
\binom{[n]}{d}
which will be a crucial part of our proof.

Lemma 2.4

(Injective mapping
F\rightarrow E
)
.

For
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
, define
\mathcal{E}_{B}=\{F\setminus\{x_{B}\}\mid F\in\mathcal{F}_{B}\}
and let
\mathcal{E}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\mathcal{E}_{B}
. Then we have the following properties:

(1)

If
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
E\in\mathcal{E}_{B}
are such that
E\subset F
, then we have
F=E\cup\{x_{B}\}
.

(2)

For
B\neq B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
with
x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}}
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{B}\cap\mathcal{E}_{B^{\prime}}=\varnothing
.

(3)

For
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
, let

\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime})=\left\{E\in{[n]\choose d}\,\middle|\,B\cup B^{\prime}\subset E,~x_{B},x_{B^{\prime}}\not\in E\right\}.

Then we have
\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{E}=\varnothing
for any
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
with
x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}}
.

The idea of constructing an injection from
\mathcal{F}
into
{[n]\choose d}
appears in earlier works as well (see
[
4
, Claim 3.1]
and
[
12
]
). However, our implementation of this idea is different and, in particular,
Item

3
in
Lemma

2.4
is new.

Proof.

We first prove
Item

1
. From the assumption, we have
B\subseteq E\subseteq F
. By
Item

3
in
Lemma

2.2
, we know that
F\cap A\neq\varnothing
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
. Since
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{B}=\{\{x_{B}\}\}
. Thus,
x_{B}\in F
and we must have
F=E\cup\{x_{B}\}
.

To prove
Item

2
, suppose for contradiction that we have
E\in\mathcal{E}_{B}\cap\mathcal{E}_{B^{\prime}}
for
B
and
B^{\prime}
with
x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}}
. Then we have
E=F\setminus\{x_{B}\}=F^{\prime}\setminus\{x_{B^{\prime}}\}
for some
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. By
Item

1
we must have
F=E\cup\{x_{B}\}=E\cup\{x_{B^{\prime}}\}
and thus
x_{B}=x_{B^{\prime}}
, contradicting the assumption.

Finally, to prove
Item

3
, consider
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
with
x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}}
. Assume for contradiction that there exists
E\in\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{E}
, then we must have
E=F^{\prime\prime}\setminus\{x_{B^{\prime\prime}}\}
for some
B^{\prime\prime}\subset F^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. On the other hand, since
B\subseteq F^{\prime\prime}
, by
Item

1
we must have
x_{B^{\prime\prime}}=x_{B}
. Similarly, since
B^{\prime}\subseteq F^{\prime\prime}
, we have
x_{B^{\prime}}=x_{B}
. Therefore we have
x_{B}=x_{B^{\prime\prime}}
contradicting the assumption that
x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}}
.
∎

Define
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{B}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\mathcal{F}_{B}
. Note that by definition
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant|\mathcal{F}_{1}|+|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|
. Then
Lemma

2.4
implies that the map
\varphi:\mathcal{F}_{1}\to\binom{[n]}{d}
defined by
F\mapsto F\setminus\{x_{B}\}
is an injection. In particular,
\mathcal{E}
is the image
\varphi(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d}
, so we have
|\mathcal{F}_{1}|=|\mathcal{E}|
, and it suffices to upper bound
\mathcal{E}
. From
Item

3
in
Lemma

2.4
, the set of
\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime})
for
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
with
x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}}
is contained in the complement of
\mathcal{E}
.

In order to have
\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
, we need
x_{B}\notin B^{\prime}
and
x_{B^{\prime}}\notin B
. Thus, we define

\mathcal{P}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}},x_{B}\notin B^{\prime},x_{B^{\prime}}\notin B\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{U}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\bigcup_{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime}).

Then we have the following useful inequality for bounding
|\mathcal{F}_{1}|
as a direct corollary of
Lemma

2.4
.

Corollary 2.5
.

We have

(1)

|\mathcal{F}_{1}|+|\mathcal{U}|\leqslant{n\choose d}.

2.2.
Star approximation

In this section, we prove that any
s
-witness family with
1\leqslant s\leqslant d/2
and size close to maximal can be approximated by a star family up to a bounded symmetric difference.

Recall that
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{B}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{\geqslant 2}}\mathcal{F}_{B}
, and
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant|\mathcal{F}_{1}|+|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|
. We have the following estimate.

Claim 2.6
.

We have
|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|=O_{d}(n^{d-1})
.

Proof.

Note that we have

|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|\leqslant\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{\geqslant 2}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|.

For each
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\geqslant 2}
, we have
\alpha_{B}\geqslant 2
and thus by
2.3
we have
|\mathcal{F}_{B}|=O_{d}(n^{d-s-1})
. Therefore, we obtain

|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|\leqslant|\mathcal{B}_{\geqslant 2}|\cdot O_{d}(n^{d-s-1})\leqslant\binom{n}{s}\cdot O_{d}(n^{d-s-1})=O_{d}(n^{d-1}).\qed

Now we state our result on star approximation.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose
d\geqslant 2s
and
n\geqslant 2(d+1)
. For any
K\geqslant 0
, if
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is an
s
-witness family with size

|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant{n-1\choose d}-Kn^{d-1},

then there exists a star family
\mathcal{F}^{*}
with
|\mathcal{F}\triangle\mathcal{F}^{*}|\leqslant O_{d}((K+1)n^{d-1})
.

Proof.

Note that by adjusting the implied constant in
O_{d}
, we may assume that
K\leqslant cn
for an arbitrarily small fixed constant
c>0
depending on
d
.

First note that by
2.6
, we have
|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|=O_{d}(n^{d-1})
, so we may focus on
\mathcal{F}_{1}
. We first prove a lower bound on
|\mathcal{B}_{1}|
in the following claim.

Claim 2.8
.

We have
|\mathcal{B}_{1}|=\Omega_{d}(n^{s})
.

Proof.

From
2.3
, we have

|\mathcal{F}_{1}|\leqslant\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|\leqslant|\mathcal{B}_{1}|\cdot O_{d}(n^{d-s}).

By rearranging, we get
|\mathcal{B}_{1}|=\Omega_{d}\left(|\mathcal{F}_{1}|n^{s-d}\right)
. Recall that we have
|\mathcal{F}_{1}|\geqslant|\mathcal{F}|-|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|
,
|\mathcal{F}_{2}|=O_{d}(n^{d-1})
, and
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\binom{n-1}{d}-Kn^{d-1}=\Omega_{d}(n^{d})
, where we use the assumption
K\leqslant cn
. It follows that
|\mathcal{F}_{1}|=\Omega_{d}(n^{d})
, and hence
|\mathcal{B}_{1}|=\Omega_{d}(n^{s})
.
∎

For
x\in[n]
, let
\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B}=x\}
. Define
x_{0}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\arg\max_{x\in[n]}|\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)|
and let
M\geqslant 0
be such that
|\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})|=|\mathcal{B}_{1}|-M
. Define the star family

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\left\{F^{*}\in\binom{[n]}{d+1}\,\middle|\,x_{0}\in F^{*}\right\}.

Note that we have

|\mathcal{F}\triangle\mathcal{F}^{*}|=|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}|+|\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}|.

By assumption, we have

|\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{F}^{*}|-|\mathcal{F}^{*}\cap\mathcal{F}|\leqslant(|\mathcal{F}^{*}|-|\mathcal{F}|)-|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}|\leqslant Kn^{d-1}+|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}|.

Thus it suffices to bound
|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}|
. By definition and
2.3
, we have

|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}|\leqslant|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|+\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|\leqslant O_{d}(n^{d-1})+M\cdot O(n^{d-s}).

So now our goal is to upper bound
M
. We first bound it in terms of
\left\lvert\mathcal{U}\right\rvert
.

Claim 2.9
.

We have

\left\lvert\mathcal{U}\right\rvert=\Omega_{d}\left(n^{d-2s}\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}\right\rvert\left(M-2\binom{n-1}{s-1}\right)\right).

Proof.

For any
(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}
, since
s\leqslant d/2
, we have
|\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime})|\geqslant\binom{n-2s-2}{d-2s}
. Note that every
E\in{[n]\choose d}
can contain
B\cup B^{\prime}
for at most
\binom{d}{s}\cdot\binom{d-s}{s}
pairs
(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}
. Thus, we have

\left|\mathcal{U}\right|\geqslant\binom{d}{s}^{-1}\binom{d-s}{s}^{-1}\sum_{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}}|\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime})|\geqslant\binom{d}{s}^{-1}\binom{d-s}{s}^{-1}\binom{n-2s-2}{d-2s}|\mathcal{P}|=\Omega_{d}(n^{d-2s}\left\lvert\mathcal{P}\right\rvert).

Now, we lower bound
\left\lvert\mathcal{P}\right\rvert
.
By the inclusion-exclusion principle, we know that
|\mathcal{P}|
is at least

\displaystyle|\left\{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}}\right\}|-|\left\{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B}\in B^{\prime}\right\}|-|\left\{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B^{\prime}}\in B\right\}|.

Note that
|\left\{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B}\neq x_{B^{\prime}}\right\}|\geqslant|\mathcal{B}_{1}|M
since there are at least
M
choices for
B^{\prime}
once
B
is chosen (see the definition of
M
). On the other hand, we know that
|\left\{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B}\in B^{\prime}\right\}|\leqslant\penalty|\mathcal{B}_{1}|\binom{n-1}{s-1}
since there are at most
\binom{n-1}{s-1}
choices for
B^{\prime}
. Similarly, we have
|\left\{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B^{\prime}}\in B\right\}|\leqslant|\mathcal{B}_{1}|\binom{n-1}{s-1}
. Therefore, we can conclude that

|\mathcal{P}|\geqslant|\mathcal{B}_{1}|M-2|\mathcal{B}_{1}|{n-1\choose s-1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we are ready to upper bound
M
.

Claim 2.10
.

We have
M=O_{d}((K+1)n^{s-1})
.

Proof.

If
M\leqslant 2{n-1\choose s-1}
, then the claim is trivially true. Otherwise, combining
2.8
and
2.9
, we get

\left|\mathcal{U}\right|=\Omega_{d}\left(n^{d-s}\left(M-2{n-1\choose s-1}\right)\right)=\Omega_{d}(n^{d-s}M)-O_{d}(n^{d-1}).

Now by
Corollary

2.5
, we have

\left|\mathcal{U}\right|\leqslant\binom{n}{d}-|\mathcal{F}_{1}|\leqslant\binom{n}{d}-|\mathcal{F}|+|\mathcal{F}_{\geqslant 2}|=\binom{n-1}{d}-|\mathcal{F}|+O_{d}(n^{d-1}).

Combining with the assumption that
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant{n-1\choose d}-Kn^{d-1}
, we can conclude that

Kn^{d-1}=\Omega_{d}(n^{d-s}M)-O_{d}(n^{d-1}).

After rearranging, we get
M=O_{d}((K+1)n^{s-1})
.
∎

Thus by
2.10
, we can conclude that

|\mathcal{F}\triangle\mathcal{F}^{*}|\leqslant Kn^{d-1}+2|\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}|\leqslant Kn^{d-1}+O_{d}(n^{d-1})+O_{d}((K+1)n^{d-1})=O_{d}((K+1)n^{d-1}),

and
2.7
follows.
∎

2.3.
Stability analysis

We are now ready to perform the stability analysis and finish the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
be an
s
-witness family with
1\leqslant s\leqslant d/2
and suppose
|\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\binom{n-1}{d}
. Then by
2.7
, there exists
x_{0}\in[n]
such that the star family centered at
x_{0}
defined as

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\left\{F^{*}\in\binom{[n]}{d+1}\,\middle|\,x_{0}\in F^{*}\right\}

satisfying
|\mathcal{F}\triangle\mathcal{F}^{*}|=O_{d}(n^{d-1})
. Let
L=|\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}|
and note that
L=O_{d}(n^{d-1})
. Our goal is to show that
L=0
.

We will give estimates regarding the following sets:

(1)

\mathcal{S}_{1}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{B\in\mathcal{B}\mid x_{0}\in B\}
;

(2)

\mathcal{S}_{2}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{B\in\mathcal{B}_{\geqslant 2}\mid x_{0}\not\in B\}
;

(3)

\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B}=x_{0}\}
;

(4)

\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\mid x_{B}\neq x_{0},\,x_{0}\notin B\}
.

Claim 2.11
.

We have

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|=O_{d}(L/n).

Proof.

Fix
B\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. We first lower bound the number of sets
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}
with
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
.
For any
A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
, we know that the number of sets
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
that contain
A\cup B
is at most
\binom{n-s-\left\lvert A\right\rvert}{d+1-s-\left\lvert A\right\rvert}=O_{d}(n^{d-s})
. On the other hand, there are
\binom{n-s}{d+1-s}
sets in
\mathcal{F}^{*}
that contain
B
. Since any set
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}\cap\mathcal{F}
with
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
must contain
A\cup B
for some
A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
, we know that the number of
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}
with
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is at least
\binom{n-s}{d-s+1}-O_{d}(n^{d-s})\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{B}\right\rvert=\Omega_{d}(n^{d-s+1})
.

Since every
F^{*}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}
contains at most
{d\choose s-1}
sets
B\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, we obtain

L=|\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\binom{d}{s-1}^{-1}\cdot\Omega_{d}(n^{d-s+1})\cdot|\mathcal{S}_{1}|.

Thus, we have
|\mathcal{S}_{1}|=O_{d}(Ln^{s-d-1})
. On the other hand, by
2.3
, for any
B\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, we know that
|\mathcal{F}_{B}|=O_{d}(n^{d+1-s-\alpha_{B}})=O_{d}(n^{d-s})
, so

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|=O_{d}(n^{d-s})\cdot|\mathcal{S}_{1}|=O_{d}(L/n).\qed

Claim 2.12
.

We have

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|=O_{d}(L/n).

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of
2.11
. Fix
B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
. We first lower bound the number of sets
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}
with
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
.
For any
A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
, we know that
\left\lvert A\right\rvert\geqslant 2
since
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\geqslant 2}
. By a direct calculation, the number of sets
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
that contains
A\cup B
is at most
\binom{n-s-1-\left\lvert A\right\rvert}{d+1-s-\left\lvert A\right\rvert}=O_{d}(n^{d-s-1})
(equality is achieved when
x_{0}\in A
and
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=2
). On the other hand, there are
\binom{n-s-1}{d-s}
sets in
\mathcal{F}^{*}
that contains
B
. Since any set
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}\cap\mathcal{F}
with
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
must contain
A\cup B
for some
A\in\mathcal{A}_{B}
, we know that the number of
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}
with
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is at least
\binom{n-s-1}{d-s}-O_{d}(n^{d-s-1})\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{B}\right\rvert=\Omega_{d}(n^{d-s})
.

Since every
F^{*}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}
contains at most
{d+1\choose s}
sets
B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
, we obtain

L=|\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}|\geqslant\binom{d+1}{s}^{-1}\cdot\Omega_{d}(n^{d-s})\cdot|\mathcal{S}_{2}|.

Thus, we have
|\mathcal{S}_{2}|=O_{d}(Ln^{s-d})
. On the other hand, by
2.3
, for any
B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
, we know that
|\mathcal{F}_{B}|=O_{d}(n^{d+1-s-\alpha_{B}})=O_{d}(n^{d-s-1})
, so

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|=O_{d}(n^{d-s-1})\cdot|\mathcal{S}_{2}|=O_{d}(L/n).\qed

Since we are focusing on the sets
F\in\mathcal{F}
that are not captured by
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, we need restrict our injection to the sets in
[n]\setminus\{x_{0}\}
. Thus, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{0})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})}\mathcal{F}_{B},\quad\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}

\displaystyle\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}_{B},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\mathcal{P}\cap(\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}

\displaystyle\bigcup_{(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime}).

Note that
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus\{x_{0}\}}{d}
since any set
E\in\mathcal{U}(B,B^{\prime})
does not contain
x_{0}=x_{B}
if
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})
. Thus, the injection
\varphi
we defined right after
Lemma

2.4
gives the following corollary.

Corollary 2.13
.

We have

\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{0})\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\right\rvert\leqslant\binom{n-1}{d}.

Proof.

Note that
\varphi(\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{0}))
,
\varphi(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*})
, and
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
are set families in
\binom{[n]\setminus\{x_{0}\}}{d}
and they are pairwise disjoint by
Lemma

2.4
.
∎

We also need the following variant of
2.9
.

Lemma 2.14
.

We have
\left\lvert\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\right\rvert=\Omega_{d}(n^{d-s}\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert)
.

Proof.

Similar to
2.9
, we know that
\left\lvert\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\right\rvert=\Omega_{d}(n^{d-2s}\left\lvert\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\right\rvert)
. Thus, we need to lower bound
\left\lvert\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\right\rvert
.

Note that for each fixed
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}
, there are at most
\binom{n-2}{s-1}
possible
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})
such that
x_{B^{\prime}}\in B
. Thus, there are at least
\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})\right\rvert-\binom{n-2}{s-1}
pairs of the form
(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
for each
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}
. From
2.8
and
2.10
with
K=0
, we have

\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})\right\rvert-\binom{n-2}{s-1}=\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}\right\rvert-M-\binom{n-2}{s-1}=\Omega_{d}(n^{s}).

Therefore,
\left\lvert\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\right\rvert\geqslant\Omega_{d}(n^{s}\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert)
, and the lemma follows.
∎

Now we are ready to finish the proof of
Theorem

1.4
. Note that
\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{0})\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathcal{B}
, and hence

\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert\leqslant\sum_{B\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|+\sum_{B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{0})\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert.

From
2.11
and
2.12
, we know that the first two terms in the right hand side are
O_{d}(L/n)
. Furthermore, we can write
\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert
. Together with
Corollary

2.13
, we get

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant O_{d}(L/n)+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}(x_{0})\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert

(2)

\displaystyle\leqslant O_{d}(L/n)+\binom{n-1}{d}-\left\lvert\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert.

For any fixed
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}
, we know that
|\mathcal{F}_{B}\cap\mathcal{F}^{*}|\leqslant\binom{n-s-2}{d-s-1}
since any set
F\in\mathcal{F}_{B}\cap\mathcal{F}^{*}
must contain
B\cup\{x_{0},x_{B}\}
. Therefore, we have

(3)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert=O_{d}(n^{d-s-1}\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert).

Combining
Lemmas

2.14
,
2
and
3
, we have

\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert\leqslant\binom{n-1}{d}+O_{d}(L/n)-\Omega_{d}(n^{d-s}\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert)

Recall that we assumed
\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert\geqslant\binom{n-1}{d}
. It follows that
\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert=O_{d}(n^{s-d-1}L)

Now, we bound
\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert
.
Note that if
F\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}
, then
F\in\mathcal{F}_{B}
for some
B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert\leqslant\sum_{B\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}|\mathcal{F}_{B}|+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert\leqslant O_{d}(L/n)+O_{d}(n^{d-s}\left\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}\right\rvert)=O_{d}(L/n),

where the second inequality follows from
2.12
and
2.3
.

However, we have

\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert-\left\lvert\mathcal{F}^{*}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}\right\rvert\geqslant\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-1}{d}+L=L.

Therefore,
O_{d}(L/n)\geqslant L
, and hence
L=\left\lvert\mathcal{F}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{F}\right\rvert=0
when
n
is sufficiently large. This implies that
\mathcal{F}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, and it is clear that
\mathcal{F}^{*}
is a maximal
s
-witness family. This completes the proof.
∎

3.
A Non-star construction for
d/2<s\leqslant d-1

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.5
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Fix any integer
m\in[s+2,n-1]
. Consider the set family
\mathcal{F}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}\cup\mathcal{A}_{12}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}

\displaystyle\left\{\{1\}\cup A\,\middle|\,A\in\binom{[3,n]}{d},\,\left\lvert A\cap[3,m]\right\rvert\geqslant s\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}

\displaystyle\left\{\{2\}\cup A\,\middle|\,A\in\binom{[3,n]}{d},\,\left\lvert A\cap[3,m]\right\rvert<s\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{12}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}

\displaystyle\left\{\{1,2\}\cup A\,\middle|\,A\in\binom{[3,n]}{d-1}\right\},

It is clear that
\mathcal{F}
is not a star. Moreover, we have

|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{n-2}{d}+\binom{n-2}{d-1}=\binom{n-1}{d}.

We choose the witness
B_{F}
for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
as follows.

•

F\in\mathcal{A}_{12}
:
F=\{1,2\}\cup A
for some
A\in\binom{[3,n]}{d-1}
. We set
B_{F}\subseteq A
to be any arbitrary subset of size
s
.

•

F\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
:
F=\{1\}\cup A
for some
A\in\binom{[3,n]}{d}
such that
|A\cap[3,m]|\geqslant s
. We set
B_{F}\subseteq A\cap[3,m]
to be any arbitrary subset of size
s
.

•

F\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
:
F=\{2\}\cup A
for some
A\in\binom{[3,n]}{d}
such that
|A\cap[3,m]|<s
. We set
B_{F}\subset A
to be any subset of size
s
containing
A\cap[m+1,n]
. This is well-defined by the assumption that
|A\cap[m+1,n]|\geqslant d-|A\cap[3,m]|\geqslant d-s+1
and
s\geqslant d-s+1
.

Now we justify that the above constructions are indeed valid witnesses.

•

F\in\mathcal{A}_{12}
: Since
\{1,2\}\subseteq F
and
\{1,2\}\cap F^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
for all
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, so we have
\{1,2\}\cap(F\cap F^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
. Since
B_{F}\cap\{1,2\}=\varnothing
, we have
B_{F}
is a valid witness for
F
.

•

F\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
: Since
1\in F
and
1\not\in B_{F}
, any
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
F\cap F^{\prime}=B_{F}
must be in
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. However, since
|F^{\prime}\cap[3,m]|<s
and
B_{F}\subseteq A\cap[3,m]
, we cannot have
F^{\prime}\cap F=B_{F}
.

•

F\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
: Similarly, since
2\in F
and
2\not\in B_{F}
, any
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
F\cap F^{\prime}=B_{F}
must be in
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. Then we must have
\left\lvert F^{\prime}\cap[m+1,n]\right\rvert\leqslant d-s
. Since we have
B_{F}\supseteq A\cap[m+1,n]
and
|A\cap[m+1,n]\geqslant d-s+1
, there must exist an element in
B_{F}
that is not in
F^{\prime}
, so
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq B_{F}
.

This shows that
\mathcal{F}
is an
s
-witness family.
∎

4.
Concluding remarks

In
Section

3
, the set family we constructed is contained in the union of two stars, namely
F\cap\{1,2\}\neq\varnothing
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
defined in the proof of
Theorem

1.5
. We were unable to find a construction beyond ones being contained in a union of two stars. This leads us to the following question.

Question 4.1
.

Let
d/2<s\leqslant d-2
and
n\geqslant 2(d+1)
. Does there exist an
s
-witness family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
with witnesses
B_{F}\subseteq F
of size
s
for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that

•

\left\lvert\mathcal{F}\right\rvert=\binom{n-1}{d}
