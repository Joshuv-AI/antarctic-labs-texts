Real‐Time AI‐Assisted Detection of Colonic Lesions Using the iIDEAS Intelligent‐C Module: A Prospective Single‐Center Validation Study
Authors: Hardik Rughwani, Rajat Garg, Sana Fathima Memon, Chung Kwong Yeung, Biji Sreedhar, Kwun Ping Lai, Rajendra Patel, Nitin Jagtap, Syed Jameel Hussain, Mohammed Sanny, Rakesh Kalapala, D. Nageshwar Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major cause of cancer mortality, with up to 26% of adenomas missed on conventional colonoscopy. Artificial intelligence (AI)–based computer‐aided detection (CADe) systems may reduce these miss rates. We aimed to validate the iIDEAS‐C CADe module in a prospective cohort, with co‐primary endpoints of adenomas per colonoscopy (APC) and lesion‐level miss rate.Methods and ResultsIn this prospective, nonrandomized, single‐center study (NCT05784935), consecutive adults (18–75 years) undergoing screening, surveillance, or diagnostic colonoscopy were enrolled between February and May 2023. Each colon segment was inspected twice: an initial blinded withdrawal without CADe, followed by a second inspection with iIDEAS‐C. Missed lesions were those identified by CADe during the second inspection and confirmed endoscopically. Secondary outcomes included PDR, ADR, PPC, lesion characteristics, and procedural times.Two hundred patients (mean age 44.1 ± 12.7 years; 64% male) were included. Eighty‐two polyps were detected across 42 patients (PDR = 21%). Endoscopists detected 61/82 polyps (74.4%), yielding a per‐polyp miss rate of 25.6%. The iIDEAS‐C module detected all 82 lesions, achieving 0% per‐polyp and per‐adenoma miss rate. Missed lesions were mostly diminutive and proximal. PPC increased from 0.305 to 0.410 (p < 0.0001), PDR from 17.5% to 21.0% (p = 0.016), and APC from 0.115 to 0.140 (p = 0.029). No adverse events occurred.ConclusionThe iIDEAS‐C module demonstrated a 0% lesion‐level miss rate and significantly improved key detection metrics, especially for small proximal lesions. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these findings and assess long‐term outcomes. ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05784935. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05784935.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, being the second leading cause of cancer death in the United States and the fourth globally, with incidence rising in many Asian countries [1]. Approximately 85% of CRCs arise from adenomas through genetic and epigenetic alterations, and removal of polyps or adenomas during colonoscopy reduces CRC incidence [2].

Colonoscopy remains the gold standard for CRC prevention and early detection [3]; however, the detection of missed lesions is an important limitation. Previously published meta‐analysis [4] indicates that up to 26% of adenomas, 9% of advanced adenomas, and 27% of serrated lesions are missed, particularly diminutive (< 5 mm), flat, and proximal colonic lesions. These contribute substantially to the burden of interval CRC. Although the adenoma detection rate (ADR) is an established quality metric, a higher ADR is inversely associated with interval CRC risk [5], recent data from the New Hampshire Colonoscopy Registry [6] demonstrate that higher adenomas per colonoscopy (APC) are strongly associated with a lower risk of post‐colonoscopy colorectal cancer (PCCRC); specifically, compared to endoscopists with APC < 0.25, hazard ratios for PCCRC decline progressively across higher APC categories, even reaching as low as HR = 0.20 for APC 0.50–< 0.70 and HR = 0.19 for APC ≥ 0.70. Importantly, among endoscopists who met the standard ADR benchmark of ≥ 25%, those with an APC < 0.50 had a 65% higher hazard of PCCRC than those with an APC ≥ 0.50 (HR = 1.65; 95% CI, 1.06–2.56). This finding highlights that APC adds granularity and predictive power beyond ADR alone, underscoring its value as a more robust quality metric, particularly for identifying endoscopists whose performance may meet ADR thresholds but still falls short in reducing PCCRC risk.

Artificial intelligence (AI)‐based computer‐aided detection (CADe) systems utilize deep learning to highlight suspected polyps in real time and have been shown in randomized trials and meta‐analyses to increase both ADR and APC [7, 8]. The iIDEAS Intelligent‐C (iIDEAS‐C) module is a novel CADe system designed to enhance real‐time polyp detection in both white‐light imaging (WLI) and narrow‐band imaging (NBI) modes. This study aimed to clinically validate iIDEAS‐C in a prospective cohort, with a primary focus on the APC and lesion‐level miss rate.

Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This was a prospective, nonrandomized, single‐center clinical validation study designed to evaluate the real‐time performance of the iIDEAS‐C module for detecting colorectal polyps during routine colonoscopies. The study was conducted at the Asian Institute of Gastroenterology (AIG) Hospitals in Hyderabad, India, a high‐volume tertiary care referral center for endoscopy.

The primary objectives of this prospective tandem study were to compare APC between CADe‐assisted and standard colonoscopy and to evaluate the lesion‐level miss rate of the CADe module. Secondary objectives included assessment of polyp per colonoscopy (PPC), ADR, Polyp Detection Rate (PDR), and descriptive analyses of lesion characteristics (size, location, histology, and distribution of missed lesions) and procedural metrics. Endpoints were analyzed at both the lesion and patient level, with prespecified subgroup analyses by lesion size and patient age.

The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of AIG Hospitals (AIG/IEC‐CT 68/08.2022‐01) and registered prospectively on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05784935). The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were consecutive adult patients aged 18–75 years who were referred for screening colonoscopy, postpolypectomy surveillance, or diagnostic evaluation for lower gastrointestinal symptoms, including altered bowel habits, rectal bleeding, anemia, or abdominal pain. Additional inclusion requirements were: adequate bowel preparation, defined as a Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) score ≥ 6 with no individual segment score < 2, and the ability to understand the study and provide written informed consent.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients were excluded if they had any of the following: known inflammatory bowel disease; a history of colorectal cancer; prior colorectal resection or other surgical alteration of colonic anatomy that prevented a standard colonoscopic examination; known hereditary polyposis syndromes (e.g., familial adenomatous polyposis, Peutz–Jeghers syndrome); colonoscopy within the preceding 12 months; contraindication to biopsy or polypectomy that could not be safely managed; pregnancy; inability or unwillingness to provide informed consent.

Postprocedure exclusions

Patients were excluded from the analysis if cecal intubation was not achieved or if bowel preparation was inadequate for the proximal colon (BBPS for the proximal colon < 2).

Outcome Measures

Primary outcome

1. APC is defined as the mean number of histologically confirmed adenomas detected per procedure. 2. Lesion‐level miss rate is defined as (number of lesions missed) ÷ (total lesions identified by the tandem reference standard).

Secondary outcomes

(1) PPC is the mean number of histologically confirmed polyps detected per procedure. (2) ADR is the proportion of procedures with ≥ 1 histologically confirmed adenoma. (3) PDR is the proportion of procedures with ≥ 1 polyp. (4) Withdrawal time differences between blinded and AI‐assisted phases.

Colonoscopy Protocol and Lesion Assessment

Bowel Preparation

All patients received standard split‐dose bowel preparation with four l polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution, administered as per institutional protocol. Patients were advised on dietary modifications the day before the procedure and instructed to complete the final liter of preparation on the morning of the colonoscopy.

Colonoscopy Procedure

Endoscopists

Procedures were performed by experienced gastroenterologists, each with > 2000 lifetime colonoscopies and a documented ADR ≥ 40%. All operators underwent structured training on the iIDEAS‐C module, including interpreting its real‐time visual and auditory outputs prior to patient recruitment.

Equipment and withdrawal protocol

High‐definition colonoscopes (Olympus EVIS EXERA III series, Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan, or Fujifilm 7000 series, Fujifilm Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) were used. The iIDEAS‐C module was connected in parallel to the endoscopic video feed.

The colonoscope was advanced to the cecum in the standard manner, with cecal intubation confirmed by identification of the ileocecal valve and appendiceal orifice, or by terminal ileum intubation. No lesion assessment was performed during insertion.

First Withdrawal: Blinded Phase

During the initial withdrawal, the CADe output (bounding boxes and auditory alerts) was displayed only on a secondary monitor visible to an independent observer, an experienced endoscopist not involved in the procedure. The primary endoscopist remained blinded to AI findings. The withdrawal time was standardized to ≥ 6 min, excluding time spent on therapeutic intervention.

AI‐Assisted Second Inspection

After the blinded withdrawal was complete, segmental unblinding was performed. If the independent observer identified lesions flagged by AI that were not detected during the blinded phase, the colonoscope was re‐inserted to the start of that colonic segment for targeted re‐examination under real‐time CADe assistance. Lesions identified by AI and confirmed endoscopically and histologically were defined as “missed lesions.”

Lesion Assessment and Histopathology

All polyps were measured in vivo using open biopsy forceps (7 mm span) or graduated snares and classified morphologically according to the Paris classification. The locations recorded were the cecum, ascending colon, transverse colon, descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum. Lesions were resected by cold snare, hot snare, or biopsy forceps as per standard practice.

Specimens were fixed in 10% buffered formalin, processed, and examined by gastrointestinal pathologists who were blinded to the detection modality. Lesions were classified as adenomatous (tubular, tubulovillous, villous), serrated (sessile serrated lesion, traditional serrated adenoma, hyperplastic polyp) or non‐neoplastic. Advanced adenomas were defined as ≥ 10 mm in size, with villous histology, or high‐grade dysplasia.

AI Module Description

The iIDEAS‐C module (iEMIS, Hong Kong) employs a region‐based fully convolutional network (R‐FCN) with a ResNet‐101 backbone [9, 10]. Detection occurs in two stages: lesion region proposal and bounding box refinement with probability scoring. The model was trained on over 500 000 annotated colonoscopy images (WLI, NBI, and tone enhancement) from multiple centers, with expert‐validated annotations and data augmentation to enhance generalizability. Images (600–1024 px) were processed using transfer learning from the Common Objects in Context (COCO) dataset and trained in TensorFlow on an NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU, achieving a frame rate of ≥ 25 fps and a latency of < 50 ms. The interface provides real‐time visual and auditory alerts, without using patient metadata. Figure 1 shows the study flow.

Tandem colonoscopy protocol. After cecal intubation, each colonic segment was examined from the cecum to the rectum by the endoscopist during the blinded first pass. Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was estimated based on the primary efficacy endpoint: the mean incremental adenoma per colonoscopy (APC) detected during the AI‐assisted second inspection. Although prior studies suggest an AI‐assisted incremental yield of approximately 0.3 adenomas per procedure, we adopted a conservative estimate of 0.2 additional adenomas per colonoscopy for this calculation. The standard deviation of the differences was estimated at 0.9 (three times the prior estimated increment) to account for potential overdispersion and variability in polyp distribution. Based on a paired t‐test for superiority, a sample size of 175 participants provides 90% power to detect this difference at a one‐sided significance level of 0.05. To account for a potential withdrawal or exclusion rate of 10% (e.g., due to poor bowel preparation or incomplete procedures), the calculated requirement increases to 195. Therefore, the final recruitment target was set at 200 participants. The sample size was driven by the APC co‐primary endpoint, which required the larger sample; the lesion‐level miss‐rate endpoint, evaluated against a pre‐specified 10% performance goal, was adequately powered given the 82 lesions identified.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) based on distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. The primary analysis of lesion‐level miss rate used an exact one‐sided binomial test, and exact Clopper–Pearson methods were used to derive 95% confidence bounds. Paired binary outcomes (PDR and ADR) were compared with McNemar's test. Paired continuous endpoints reflecting lesion yield (PPC and APC) were compared using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. All hypothesis tests were one‐sided at α = 0.05; exact p‐values are reported. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Adenomas per colonoscopy (APC) and lesion‐level miss rate were pre‐specified, trial‐registered co‐primary endpoints evaluated under a conjunctive success rule that required both to be met; because a positive study demands statistical significance on both endpoints, this strategy controls the family‐wise Type I error at ≤ 5% and no further adjustment for multiplicity was applied. Analyses of the adenoma subset, including the per‐adenoma miss rate, were prespecified as secondary and exploratory.

Results

Between February 2023 and May 2023, 200 patients were enrolled in the study, with a mean age of 44.1 ± 12.7 years; 128 participants (64%) were male (Table 1). The indications for colonoscopy included screening in 92 patients (46%), postpolypectomy surveillance in 54 patients (27%), and diagnostic evaluation in another 54 patients (27%). Cecal intubation was achieved in all procedures, and the quality of bowel preparation was adequate in every case, with a mean BBPS score of 7.0 ± 0.762 (range 6–9) (Table 1).

During the study, 82 lesions were identified in 42 patients (21%); 81 lesions (98.8%) were resected. Histopathology was available for 76 lesions (76/82, 92.7%). One lesion (1.2%) was not resected. Of the resected specimens, three (3.7% of all lesions) were not retrieved after resection, and two (2.4% of all lesions) lacked a histopathology report. Therefore, six lesions (6/82, 7.3%) lacked pathological confirmation. At the patient level, of the 42 patients with detected lesions, 39 had a complete pathology report, and 3 had no report (1 declined polypectomy and 2 reports were lost) (Figure 2). Histological examination of the 76 available specimens showed that 48 lesions (58.5% of all lesions) were non‐neoplastic, 27 (32.9% of all lesions) were tubular adenomas, and 1 (1.2% of all lesions) was an advanced adenoma. Analysis of missed lesions revealed that the majority were diminutive and located in the proximal colon. Specifically, 17 of the 40 (42.5%) diminutive lesions (≤ 5 mm) were missed by the endoscopists, compared with four of the 38 (10.5%) small lesions (6–10 mm), and none of the four large lesions (> 10 mm) were missed. In this trial, the iIDEAS‐C module detected 82 of 82 polyps (100%). For the co‐primary lesion‐level miss rate, an exact one‐sided binomial test against the prespecified 10% performance goal produced p < 0.001 (Clopper–Pearson 95% CI, 0% to 4.4%), confirming a per‐polyp miss rate below 10%. In a pre‐specified secondary analysis restricted to adenomas, iIDEAS‐C also achieved a perfect detection rate, identifying all 28 of 28 lesions (100%); the exact one‐sided binomial test against the 10% performance goal yielded p = 0.052 (95% Clopper–Pearson CI, 0% to 12.3%), narrowly above the 0.05 threshold. This reflects the smaller adenoma denominator and correspondingly lower power rather than any missed lesion and does not affect the co‐primary lesion‐level result.

As shown in Table 4, the use of the CADe module resulted in statistically significant improvements in both the PDR (p = 0.016) and PPC (p < 0.0001). This indicates that the module not only helps find polyps in more procedures but also increases the overall yield of polyps per procedure. For adenomas, the more clinically significant lesions, the module did not change the per‐procedure ADR. However, it did lead to a statistically significant increase in the APC (p = 0.029). This suggests that while CADe did not help find adenomas in new patients, it did help find additional adenomas in patients who already had at least one detected.

Discussion

In this prospective single‐center study, the iIDEAS‐C module demonstrated excellent performance in enhancing polyp and adenoma detection during colonoscopy. In 200 consecutive patients, the module identified all lesions detected during the procedure, including 21 polyps (25.6%) and five adenomas (17.9%) that were missed by experienced endoscopists during initial inspection. Most of these missed lesions were diminutive (≤ 5 mm).

The proximal colon accounted for a disproportionately high proportion of missed lesions (33.3% vs. 21.2% in the distal colon), with the transverse colon demonstrating the highest miss rate (38.5%) [4]. By eliminating these overlooked lesions, iIDEAS‐C achieved a per‐polyp miss rate of less than 10% (p < 0.001; 95% CI, 0% –4.4%), which led to a statistically significant increase in APC (p = 0.029), reinforcing the concept of AI as an effective “second observer” that mitigates human perceptual error.

Our findings are consistent with an expanding body of evidence supporting the use of AI‐driven CADe to improve colonoscopic performance. Multiple RCTs and meta‐analyses have reported significant increases in APC and reductions in AMR with CADe assistance [7, 11]. The benefit is most pronounced for diminutive adenomas and subtle lesions, precisely the categories where our endoscopists' misses clustered. Consistent with previous reports [12], our study did not observe missed large lesions (> 10 mm), indicating that the incremental yield of AI is greatest for small polyps that may otherwise be overlooked.

Although the absolute increase in APC was small (from 0.115 to 0.140), despite being statistically significant, and ADR remained unchanged (at 7% for both endoscopist‐only and CADe‐assisted procedures) in this study, several factors likely explain these findings. First, our cohort was relatively young (mean age 44 years) with a low adenoma prevalence [13], which reduces the expected adenoma yield per procedure. Second, the low observed ADR (7%) reflects the cohort rather than examiner performance: participants were young (mean age 44 years) and enrolled predominantly for surveillance or symptom evaluation rather than average‐risk screening, and the ≥ 25% ADR benchmark is derived from average‐risk screening populations aged ≥ 45–50 years and is not directly applicable here. Because baseline adenoma prevalence was low, the achievable increment in ADR was inherently limited even though APC rose significantly. Finally, CADe may preferentially increase detection of additional diminutive adenomas, thereby raising APC without materially affecting the proportion of procedures with any adenoma (ADR). Nonetheless, the complete elimination of missed lesions in this controlled setting is noteworthy, highlighting the potential of next‐generation CADe algorithms to achieve near‐perfect detection in real‐time.

The preferential detection of diminutive and proximal lesions by iIDEAS‐C is of particular clinical interest. Interval colorectal cancers (CRCs) are disproportionately located in the proximal colon [14] and often arise from missed lesions, including sessile serrated adenomas [15]. By improving detection in these segments, CADe may help reduce interval CRC incidence, although such an effect remains to be proven in long‐term outcome studies [7]. At the same time, the increased detection of non‐neoplastic lesions raises the possibility of overdiagnosis and unnecessary surveillance. This phenomenon has been observed in meta‐analyses, which estimate that CADe use leads to the resection of two additional non‐neoplastic polyps per 10 colonoscopies [11].

The predominance of non‐neoplastic lesions (58.5%) differs from typical average‐risk screening cohorts and is largely explained by our study population. Participants were relatively young (mean age 44 years) and were enrolled predominantly for surveillance or symptom‐driven indications rather than average‐risk screening; such populations have a lower adenoma prevalence and a higher relative frequency of diminutive distal hyperplastic polyps. In addition, CADe systems preferentially flag small and subtle lesions, a category enriched for non‐neoplastic histology, which further shifts the observed distribution. Given the modest absolute lesion counts, these proportions should be interpreted with caution.

One limitation of our study is the absence of false‐positive analysis. While all AI‐flagged lesions in our series corresponded to true polyps, CADe systems can generate false alerts from mucosal folds, stool residue, or bubbles [16, 17]. Although prior studies suggest such alerts are typically resolved quickly without prolonging withdrawal time [7], a high false‐positive rate could increase cognitive load, procedural interruptions, and result in resection of benign tissue. Without this data, we cannot fully assess the specificity or positive predictive value of iIDEAS‐C. A distinguishing feature of the iIDEAS‐C module is its capability to support real‐time lesion detection under both WLI and NBI modes. This contrasts with most currently available CADe systems and those evaluated in recent major meta‐analyses [7, 11], which typically function exclusively in WLI or automatically deactivate detection overlays when the endoscopist switches to image‐enhanced modes for characterization. However, it is important to note that our current trial was not designed to isolate and quantify the system's detection metrics specifically within NBI mode. Consequently, while the technical capability represents a potential workflow improvement, rigorous validation of the system's sensitivity and specificity under narrow‐band illumination remains a subject for future research. Additional limitations include the non‐randomized, single‐center design, modest sample size, mixed indications for colonoscopy, and the lack of a contemporaneous control group. Furthermore, the absence of long‐term follow‐up precludes conclusions about the impact of iIDEAS‐C on interval CRC incidence or mortality.

Our results should be interpreted within the evolving framework of international guidance. The 2025 American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) guideline [18] made no recommendation for or against routine CADe use, citing very low certainty of evidence for improvements in CRC incidence or mortality. Similarly, the British Medical Journal (BMJ) Rapid Recommendation 2025 [19] issued a weak recommendation against routine CADe in average‐risk adults, emphasizing uncertain clinical benefit, modest absolute ADR gains, and increased detection of non‐neoplastic lesions. In contrast, the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) 2025 [20] issued a weak recommendation in favor of CADe, reasoning that most informed patients may prefer a tool that marginally increases lesion detection. Our findings, demonstrating complete elimination of missed lesions in a controlled environment, are compatible with all three position statements, underscoring the balance between technical performance and demonstrable patient‐centered benefit.

In the broader evidence base, a recent meta‐analysis of 44 RCTs [21] confirmed that CADe increases ADR (RR 1.21) and APC (+0.22) but has no proven effect on advanced neoplasia detection, CRC incidence, or mortality. The evidence quality for hard outcomes was graded as very low. This reinforces the view that CADe should currently be considered an adjunct to quality improvement rather than a proven cancer‐prevention strategy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the iIDEAS‐C module demonstrated near‐perfect detection (0% lesion‐level miss rate) in this trial and significantly improved key detection metrics; however, larger multicenter trials with long‐term validation are warranted before widespread clinical adoption.
