Detection accuracy of an AI platform for dental treatment features on panoramic radiographs – tooth- and patient-level analyses
Authors: Natalia Kazimierczak, Nora Sultani, Natalia Chwarścianek, Szymon Krzykowski, Joanna Janiszewska-Olszowska, Zbigniew Serafin, Wojciech Kazimierczak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) has shown promise in dental imaging, yet its reliability for comprehensive diagnostic charting from panoramic radiographs (PAN) remains uncertain. We evaluated the diagnostic performance of a commercial AI platform (Diagnocat™, San Francisco, USA) in detecting common dental treatment features on PAN images. In this retrospective study, 147 patients (4,148 teeth) were analyzed against the consensus of two experienced readers. Tooth-level performance was assessed for missing teeth, fillings, crowns, pontics, endodontic treatments, orthodontic appliances, and implants, using patient-clustered nonparametric bootstrapping used to account for within-patient correlations. At the tooth level, the AI achieved high diagnostic accuracy across features (94.9–99.9%), with nearly perfect results for missing teeth, crowns, pontics, and implants. However, under a stringent patient-level “perfect match” criterion requiring error-free full-mouth reports, the AI succeeded in only 56.5% of cases (95% CI, 48.4–64.2%). Errors were most often related to fillings (57.3%) and endodontic treatments (19.5%). These findings highlight a critical gap between high per-tooth accuracy and clinically meaningful patient-level performance. These findings underscore that while the AI performs strongly at the tooth level, its patient-level performance is insufficient for autonomous diagnostic charting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32226-0.

Introduction

The digital transformation of dental imaging—marked by the routine use of panoramic radiography (PAN) and the rapid adoption of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)—has enabled precise diagnostics and tailored treatment plans. However, this level of precision also requires clinicians to possess high diagnostic skills. Computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) is a rapidly developing field in medical imaging that facilitates the detection of diverse conditions. It has gained significant momentum in the past few years, following the broad introduction of artificial intelligence (AI). Deep learning (DL), a subdivision of AI, has been used for more than 10 years to match or surpass human performance in fundamental image analysis tasks such as classification, object detection, and segmentation1. The application of such systems in dental imaging has allowed the creation of commercially available chairside software, generating automated dental charts and structured reports2.

PAN enables detailed visualization of the entire dentomaxillofacial region at a low radiation dose with a single exposition. However, their inherent two-dimensional (2D) projection, superimposition and geometric distortion can obscure subtle lesions and treatment-related findings3–5. In addition, research has indicated that examiners with greater experience achieve greater diagnostic accuracy than do their less experienced counterparts6. Despite this issue, owing to its wide availability and low cost, PAN remains the most frequently prescribed extraoral examination in dentistry7.

Accurate identification of previous dental interventions, such as root canal fillings, posts and cores, crowns, implants, orthodontic braces and other restorative materials, is essential for documentation, treatment planning, follow-up and forensic identification. Bringing AI support to this task promises greater diagnostic consistency and workflow efficiency across imaging techniques8. Previous research has demonstrated the potential of DL algorithms to automatically detect and classify various dental restorations on PAN images with high accuracy9,10. While challenges remain, particularly with restorations having radiodensities similar to tooth structure or appearing as fine details, the potential for AI to enhance diagnostic consistency and efficiency is evident10.

Diagnocat™ (Diagnocat Inc., San Francisco, USA) is a commercially available, widely used, cloud-based AI solution that provides automated detection and annotation of dental findings on CBCT images, periapical radiographs, and PAN images. Diagnocat™ is one of the most widely adopted AI platforms in Europe for panoramic, periapical, and CBCT imaging, and is currently used in several clinical centers and tele-radiology workflows. In Poland, Diagnocat is among the most frequently implemented CE-marked commercial systems, which makes its real-world reliability highly clinically relevant compared to other available systems (e.g., Algin X-ray Insights, WeDiagnostiX).

In addition to simple feature detection, the automation of the diagnostic process significantly reduces the time required for radiographic interpretation, thereby enhancing clinical workflow efficiency11. Despite the promising findings related to the use of this AI platform in CBCT analysis12–16, the reliability of the program in evaluating PAN images remains markedly lower17–20. Moreover, the platform’s reliability has not been comprehensively established against an expert consensus on a large cohort of patients exceeding 100 subjects17–20. While previous work has assessed specific features, a comprehensive and rigorous evaluation of the platform’s ability to identify a wide array of common dental treatments simultaneously, which is crucial for automated charting, has not been fully established against an expert consensus. Furthermore, while previous work has assessed specific features at the tooth level, a critical gap exists in evaluating the platform’s ability to generate a completely accurate, full-mouth diagnostic report—a prerequisite for trustworthy automated charting. High tooth-level accuracy does not necessarily translate to perfect patient-level performance, as even a small error rate can lead to a high probability of at least one error on a full-mouth scan.

Therefore, the aim of this study is twofold: first, to evaluate the tooth-level accuracy of the Diagnocat AI platform in detecting common dental treatment signs on PAN images, and second, to introduce a stringent patient-level analysis to assess its reliability for producing a completely error-free, full-mouth diagnosis.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 147 patients with 4148 teeth were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the participants was 34.46 years (SD = 13.29), ranging from 11 to 70 years. The median age was 33 years (quartile range 24.5–43.5). Most of the patients were female (65.99%; n = 97), whereas 34.01% (n = 50) were male. Interreader agreement

The initial agreement was found to be substantial to nearly perfect across most categories, including missing teeth (κ = 0.98), crowns (κ = 0.95), pontics (κ = 0.99), orthodontic appliances, implants (κ = 1.00), fillings (κ = 0.91), and endodontic treatments (κ = 0.89). Most discrepancies among the readers stemmed from minor errors in tooth numbering rather than from any disagreement regarding the presence of dental treatment features.

Anatomical distribution of AI errors

Error distribution demonstrated a clear spatial pattern across the dentition. Posterior teeth—particularly maxillary molars (teeth 15, 16, 24, 26, 27) and mandibular molars (35, 36, 37, 47, 48)—showed the highest discrepancy counts (7–11 errors per tooth position), whereas anterior teeth exhibited near-zero error frequency (0–3 discrepancies). This pattern was visualized using a per-tooth heatmap (Fig. 5), which confirmed distinct “hot zones” of diagnostic difficulty in posterior regions.

These findings are consistent with known PAN imaging limitations, including:

To quantify the impact of tooth location on diagnostic performance, an exploratory logistic regression model was fitted with error presence (yes/no) as the outcome and molar status as the main predictor. Molars were associated with significantly higher odds of AI error (OR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.51–2.97; p < 0.001), supporting the anatomical findings above. This association likely reflects the combined effect of structural overlap, increased anatomical complexity, and projection-related artifacts.

Discussion

This study provides a dual-faceted evaluation of a commercial AI platform, revealing a significant disparity between its performance at the tooth level and the patient level. At the tooth level, the AI achieved a high overall accuracy of over 94.9% across all six categories, with nearly flawless performance in detecting missing teeth, crowns, and pontics. These findings are consistent with a rapidly expanding body of literature demonstrating that AI systems can rival or exceed human performance in discrete radiological tasks21–27. However, the central finding of this study is the AI’s substantially lower accuracy of 56.5% when evaluated under a stringent patient-level criterion, where a diagnosis is considered correct only if it is entirely free of errors across the full dentition.

This discrepancy highlights a critical gap in the current validation of diagnostic AI tools in dentistry. While high-tooth-level metrics suggest reliability, they do not capture the cumulative probability of error across an entire patient case. A single missed finding or false positive can alter a treatment plan, making 100% accuracy the ultimate goal for autonomous charting. Our patient-level analysis demonstrated that even with high per-tooth accuracy, the likelihood of achieving a perfect full-mouth report is low. This suggests that most studies, which focus solely on tooth-level metrics, may present an overly optimistic view of the clinical readiness of these tools for fully autonomous use. The single-tooth assessments most commonly used to date should be supplanted by more holistic, patient-level evaluations to provide a more realistic measure of clinical utility.

Our findings at the granular tooth level align with the rapidly expanding body of literature on the application of AI in various areas of dentistry, including periodontology, orthodontics, conservative dentistry, and dental radiology21–27. Owing to its high level of digitization and the nature of radiological assessments, dental radiology is the field most predisposed to the implementation of AI systems. This observation is consistent with the broader trend in traditional radiology, where medical imaging accounts for approximately 85% of FDA-approved AI programs (data from 2023)28. In the field of dentomaxillofacial radiology, several AI-based systems have been developed to analyze two-dimensional images—examples include Denti. Ai, Overjet, and Pearl. Several platforms, such as Diagnocat and Relu, are also capable of evaluating three-dimensional CBCT images. However, given the growing number of users, it is imperative to rigorously and scientifically verify the diagnostic reliability of these systems to safeguard the well-being of patients whose examinations are analyzed by these platforms worldwide. Although multiple commercial platforms exist, published diagnostic accuracy data remain limited, and none include dual-level accuracy assessment; therefore, our results offer unique comparative value.

Two-dimensional (2D) radiographic imaging presents inherent limitations, including superimposition of anatomical structures, geometric distortion, anatomical noise, and restricted dimensionality29. Additional challenges, such as low contrast and poorly defined tooth margins, further impede diagnostic accuracy30. These limitations contribute to interobserver variability and necessitate cautious interpretation. These constraints equally affect PAN imaging and periapical radiography (PR), which often lack the resolution necessary for detailed assessments. Consequently, clinicians frequently prefer PRs or CBCT for detailed evaluations31,32. Nonetheless, PAN remains a widely used initial screening tool in dental practice because of its versatility, low cost and high availability.

Error patterns indicated that anatomical noise in posterior regions, radiopaque restorative materials, and structural overlap were major contributors to inaccuracies of AI platform. These findings align with known limitations of panoramic imaging and highlight areas where targeted algorithmic retraining may significantly improve performance. Given the systematic nature of certain errors, retraining or fine-tuning algorithms using misclassified cases—particularly posterior teeth and complex restorative situations—could meaningfully improve accuracy. Future collaboration with industry partners may facilitate such refinements.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that AI systems—especially those based on DL—achieve high accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity in identifying dental fillings, often rivaling or surpassing the performance of human clinicians19,33–38. For example, recent research reported F1 scores and accuracy rates above 90% for the detection of fillings and other restorations, with some models achieving near-perfect agreement with expert annotations34,37,38. However, some authors have shown that the diagnostic performance can vary depending on the type of filling (e.g., metal-based vs. resin-based) and the patient population (e.g., adults vs. pediatric patients)33,38,39. Although our study group included patients across a wide age range (11–70 years) and included both composite and amalgam restorations, the AI platform demonstrated excellent performance in detecting fillings, with a sensitivity of 91.2%.

Although PANs provide a broad overview of the maxillofacial region, their ability to detect fine details is limited40. Nonetheless, they are reliable for evaluating parameters such as the number of remaining and root-filled teeth (19). In the present study, our results demonstrated almost perfect alignment between the readers’ consensus and the AI platform in detecting missing teeth (sensitivity = 99.1%). However, the AI platform showed lower detection accuracy in identifying endodontically treated teeth (sensitivity = 88.6%). This reduction in accuracy may be attributed to the inherent subtlety of radiographic indicators of root canal filling, which can be obscured by overlapping structures or variations in material density. Conversely, superimposition of bony structures can also mimic signs of root canal therapy (Fig. 3). These challenges affect both human observers and AI algorithms, making endodontic assessment particularly demanding.

The slightly lower, although still clinically excellent, sensitivity for detecting endodontic treatments (88.6%) is also a recurring theme in similar studies on PAN images17,41. Kazimierczak et al.. reported a sensitivity of 90.7% for endodontically treated teeth17. However, the authors evaluated only 55 PAN images. In a 2022 study, Zadrożny et al.41. explored multiple correlations between the assessments of experienced readers and the Diagnocat AI platform with respect to various features in PAN images. They identified a high level of correlation for endodontic treatment (ICC = 0.948), missing teeth (ICC = 0.959), fillings (ICC = 0.92), and prosthetic materials such as crowns and posts (ICC = 0.968). The authors of both manuscripts concluded that the tested AI system can be beneficial for preliminary evaluation of PAN images, providing reliable results and recommending further diagnostic methods when more precise assessment is needed.

In a recent study, Turosz et al.. investigated the diagnostic accuracy of AI software (CS imaging software, Carestream Dental LLC, Atlanta, GA, USA) in the analysis of PAN images42. The authors assessed the program’s accuracy in detecting the location of missing teeth, root canal fillings, endodontic lesions, dental crowns, pontics, implants, and implant abutment crowns. Their findings revealed high performance—exceeding 90% accuracy—in identifying missing teeth, root canal fillings, and implant abutment crowns. In contrast, the software demonstrated moderate sensitivity (94.57%) for detecting endodontic lesions and the lowest sensitivity (80.92%) for identifying pontics. Consistent with previous work by Zadrożny, the authors concluded that AI software can be a valuable tool in clinical practice for diagnosis and treatment planning, although additional verification by clinicians is advisable, particularly for tasks such as the identification of dental crowns.

A comparable study conducted in 2022 evaluated the diagnostic reliability of the web-based AI platform Denti.AI using 300 PAN images33. The authors reported substantially lower accuracy in detecting crowns and fillings (89.53% and 56.95%, respectively) than did the previously cited methods and our own findings. The software exhibited particularly low sensitivity in identifying resin-based restorations (41.11%). Nevertheless, its performance in detecting implants and root canal treatments was similar to our results, with reported sensitivities of 100% and 91.9%, respectively. Despite the lower accuracy in detecting fillings, the authors concluded that the examined AI platform can improve overall diagnostic and treatment decisions by minimizing subjectivity and minimizing potential human error.

A significant strength of the evaluated AI is its potential to reduce the diagnostic workload and minimize human error, especially for less experienced clinicians37,42,43. The incorporation of AI systems such as the tested platform into routine clinical workflows can streamline radiographic interpretation, particularly in high-volume practices. By automating the detection of previously performed treatments, AI tools reduce the time clinicians spend on repetitive charting tasks, enabling a more focused assessment of pathologies. Furthermore, cloud-based deployment, as exemplified by Diagnocat, facilitates integration with telehealth platforms and remote diagnostics. It enables clinicians to remotely access patients’ images and thus facilitate treatment planning. These advantages underscore the potential of AI to support more efficient, accessible, and patient-centered care in dentistry. However, successful implementation requires institutional commitment, appropriate clinician training, and clear guidelines for interpreting AI outputs within existing diagnostic protocols. Nevertheless, clinician oversight remains essential, particularly for ambiguous or complex cases, and further research is needed to improve AI performance for challenging cases and patient populations33,42,44.

As AI becomes increasingly integrated into dental diagnostics, ethical and legal concerns must be addressed. These include ensuring data privacy, managing diagnostic accountability, and avoiding overreliance on automated outputs. Transparent reporting of algorithm limitations and continuous performance monitoring are essential to maintain patient safety and clinical trust. In cases where AI misses a clinically relevant finding, liability remains with the clinician, underscoring the need for transparent communication about AI involvement. Clear documentation in patient records—indicating whether AI assisted in diagnostic interpretation—may become necessary in future regulatory frameworks. Furthemore the frameworks must clearly define clinician responsibility when AI tools are used in diagnostic decision-making45,46.

The results of our study position the AI platform as a powerful assistive tool or “second reader” rather than a replacement for a clinician. From a clinical workflow perspective, the platform is best positioned as a pre-screening or parallel ‘second reader’ tool. Clinicians should pay particular attention to features with higher error rates—especially fillings and endodontic treatments—where human verification remains essential. A practical implementation strategy may include AI-generated charting followed by clinician validation of flagged regions or discrepancies, which can improve efficiency without compromising diagnostic safety. The platform can effectively automate routine diagnostic tasks, enhance the efficiency of radiological interpretation, and reduce the risk of oversight for individual findings. However, its inability to consistently generate error-free, full-mouth reports means that clinician oversight remains essential. This nuanced role is critical for managing expectations and ensuring safe and effective integration into clinical workflows. As emphasized by Uribe et al.., continuous performance monitoring and real-world validation are essential for the sustainable implementation of any AI tool47.

Despite these promising results, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. One of the primary limitations of this study is the limited number of some of the dental conditions examined, such as implants and pontics, which limits our detection accuracy analyses. The perfect detection of implants and pontics must be interpreted cautiously because of the very small number of positive cases (n = 14 and n = 28, respectively). These estimates inherently carry wide confidence intervals, limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions about real-world performance. The study was conducted at a single center using a single panoramic unit (Hyperion X9 Pro). The homogeneity of imaging parameters and the specific referral structure (primarily orthodontic and surgical patients) may introduce spectrum bias. Consequently, the AI’s performance may differ in general dentistry populations, mixed-device environments, or images of lower quality. Future studies should aim for a multicenter, prospective design with a consensus panel of multiple experts forming the gold standard to further validate these findings. Additionally, this study focused on treatment features; the AI’s efficacy in detecting carious lesions, periapical pathology, or periodontal disease was not assessed and warrants separate investigation. Another limitation is that intra-observer reliability was not assessed. Although both readers demonstrated substantial to near-perfect inter-observer agreement, repeat readings were not performed. The absence of intra-observer data may reduce the precision of the expert consensus used as the reference standard.

This study demonstrated that the evaluated AI software achieves high reliability and accuracy for identifying individual dental treatments on PAN images at the tooth level, with performance comparable to that of experienced readers for discrete tasks. However, the novel and stringent patient-level analysis reveals a “perfect match” accuracy of only 56.5% for producing a completely error-free, full-mouth diagnosis. This critical finding underscores the distinction between a feature-detection tool and a fully autonomous diagnostic system. While the platform holds significant promise for optimizing clinical workflows and improving diagnostic consistency as an assistive tool or “second reader,” it is not yet reliable enough to replace the holistic, integrative assessment of a clinician for final diagnostic charting. Therefore, we conclude that the AI platform is a valuable supplement in dental diagnostics, but meticulous clinical oversight remains indispensable to ensure patient safety and guarantee comprehensive diagnostic accuracy.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics approval

This is a retrospective, single-center detection accuracy study that evaluates the performance of a commercial AI platform in identifying dental treatment features on PAN images. The study was written according to the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) 201548. The study protocol is available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Patients

This retrospective detection accuracy study initially included 161 consecutive patients referred for PAN imaging at a single private dental center. Patients were referred for PAN imaging by orthodontists or dental surgeons between 01.2024 and 31.03.2025. The PAN serves as the initial imaging modality in surgical and orthodontic patients.

The main exclusion criteria were overall poor image quality and the presence of mixed dentition (primary and permanent teeth).

After the initial selection of patients, 2 patients were excluded because of poor image quality, and 12 patients were excluded because of the presence of primary teeth. The final analysis included 147 patients.

Sample size and precision of estimates

This study was designed as an estimation study. Our primary objective was to report point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for diagnostic accuracy measures at both the tooth and patient levels. Tooth-level CIs were constructed via patient-clustered nonparametric bootstrapping (3,000 replicates). Patient-level proportions were summarized with nonparametric bootstrap CIs over patients (3,000 replicates). Interreader agreement was summarized with Cohen’s κ and its 95% CI.

The achieved CI half-widths for common features (fillings, crowns, endodontic treatments, missing teeth) were within ± 5–10% points, indicating adequate precision for descriptive purposes. Owing to the small number of positive cases, rare features (e.g., pontics and implants) necessarily yield wider CIs. For future studies, ~ 400–450 patients would be required to achieve ≤ 10% point precision for rare features on the basis of observed prevalence and clustering.

Image acquisition

All the PAN images were acquired via a Hyperion X9 PRO (60–75 kVp, 5–10 mA; MyRay, Imola, Italy) machine. Patient identifiers were removed from all images, and images were coded to ensure blinded analysis.

Reader calibration was performed on 5 images (patients with multiple restorations and mixed endodontic status). Agreement was not assessed. Calibration images were independent from the final study dataset.

Statistical evaluation

The diagnostic performance of the Diagnocat AI platform was evaluated by comparing its findings to the consensus of the two human readers, which served as the reference standard. Performance metrics (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy) were computed for six tooth-level categories (missing teeth, fillings, crowns, pontics, endodontic treatment, orthodontic appliances). Because teeth within a patient are not independent, 95% confidence intervals were obtained via a patient-clustered nonparametric bootstrap: patients were resampled with replacement (n = 3,000 replicates), and all teeth belonging to the sampled patients were included within each replicate; percentile intervals were reported. Cells can contain multiple labels (e.g., ‘O, F’), which are parsed so that features can cooccur on a tooth.

In a patient-level analysis, presence/absence per feature was defined as positive if the feature occurred on any tooth of the patient; 95% CIs used a nonparametric bootstrap over patients (n = 3,000 replicates). Additionally, an exact match analysis revealed a patient as correct only when the AI exactly matched the reference across all teeth for all six features; CIs for the proportion were obtained via 5,000 patient bootstraps. The interreader reliability was calculated for each feature via Cohen’s kappa coefficient. All the analyses were conducted in R software, version 4.5.1.
