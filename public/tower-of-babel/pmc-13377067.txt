Virome diversity, evolution, transmission networks, and zoonotic potential of wildlife on the Qinghai-Tibet plateau
Authors: Hao Zhou, Ji Pu, Jinlv Liu, Yaqi Wang, Qiang Hou, Jiajia Ma, Yuqing Liu, Wei Wang, Shan Lu, Jianfeng Ba, Teng Qi, Jing Yang, Xuelian Luo, Jianguo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWildlife serves as a natural reservoir for emerging viruses. Although virome studies of bats and rodents have advanced our understanding of viral diversity, the virome characteristics and zoonotic risks of wildlife inhabiting the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau-a global biodiversity hotspot-remain poorly understood. Here, we conducted a virome survey of 22 wild animal species from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau using meta-transcriptomic sequencing. We characterized the diversity and distribution of wildlife-associated viruses and found that birds tended to harbor higher viral diversity and abundance than mammals. In addition, greylag goose (Anser anser) and Himalayan vulture (Gyps himalayensis) harbored a high proportion of cross-species viruses and occupied central positions in the transmission network. In total, we identified 36 viral genera with pathogenic potential to humans or animals. Among them, several viruses, including rotavirus A (RVA) and parainfluenza virus 5 (PIV-5), pose a high risk of zoonotic transmission. Notably, the plateau vole (Neodon fuscus) carried the highest number of high-risk viruses, and the RVA strains it harbors represent a distinct early evolutionary lineage. These findings highlight the public health relevance of wildlife on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and emphasize the need for continued surveillance of high-risk hosts and viruses.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Microbiology

Introduction

In recent years, RNA viruses such as SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, H7N9, and SARS-CoV-2 have caused multiple major infectious disease outbreaks, underscoring the critical role of wildlife as natural or intermediate hosts of emerging viruses1–4. It is estimated that ~75% of emerging infectious diseases are of animal origin5. Among wildlife, wild birds are the primary natural hosts of influenza A virus and West Nile virus6,7; bats are recognized as major reservoirs for Ebola virus and coronaviruses8,9; and rodents serve as important reservoir hosts for pathogens such as hantaviruses and rabies virus10,11. As human activities continue to expand into the margins of natural ecosystems, the frequency of human-wildlife interactions has increased markedly, intensifying the risk of cross-species viral transmission. Emerging infectious diseases have consequently become a persistent global public health concern12. In this context, prospective virome surveillance in wildlife and the identification of pathogens with zoonotic potential have become key strategies for the prevention of future infectious disease outbreaks.

Although previous studies have shown that bats, primates, and rodents harbor a disproportionately high proportion of known zoonotic viruses-earning them the designation of “high-risk hosts”-current virome research has predominantly focused on these taxa13,14, leading to the discovery of numerous viruses of public health significance, such as Wenzhou virus and Jingmen tick virus15,16. However, compared with the estimated 1.67 million undiscovered viruses in nature of which 630,000 to 827,000 may possess the potential for cross-species transmission and human infection-the current scope of investigation remains exceedingly limited17. As the world’s highest and largest plateau by average elevation, the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau features unique topographical and climatic conditions and is recognized as one of the most important global biodiversity hotspots18,19. It harbors a wealth of plateau-endemic species, including goa (Procapra picticaudata), plateau pika (Ochotona curzoniae), kiang (Equus kiang), Thorold’s deer (Cervus albirostris), and Himalayan vulture (Gyps himalayensis), reflecting remarkable species diversity. In recent years, several potential zoonotic pathogens-including tick-borne encephalitis virus, arenaviruses, and coronaviruses-have been detected in Himalayan marmot (Marmota himalayana), plateau pika, and other wild animals in this region20–23. However, these studies have largely focused on single host species or specific viral taxa. A systematic, cross-species virome investigation is still lacking, precluding a comprehensive assessment of the region’s viral diversity and its associated public health risks.

The threat posed by different viruses to human and animal health varies substantially24. Traditional viral risk assessment methods primarily rely on virus isolation, cell culture, and animal infection models. Although these approaches offer high accuracy, they are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and low-throughput, making them unsuitable in the context of the rapidly expanding discovery of novel viral sequences17,25. As a result, alternative genome-based evaluation strategies have been widely employed, such as inferring pathogenic potential based on genomic similarity to known pathogenic viruses, or estimating cross-species transmission capacity from the host range of phylogenetically related sequences26–29. However, the predictive accuracy of these methods remains insufficiently validated, as homologous viruses may differ significantly in both pathogenicity and host range13,24,30. Recently, computational models based on machine learning-such as ZoonoticRank and RNAVirHost-have provided new avenues for the rapid risk assessment of large-scale virome datasets31,32. These approaches help to overcome some limitations of the aforementioned methods and enhance our ability to evaluate the zoonotic and cross-species transmission potential of viruses.

To address the current knowledge gap, we conducted a systematic virome study of 22 wild animal species sampled from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. This study aimed to comprehensively characterize the viral diversity, distribution patterns, and evolutionary dynamics of viruses harbored by these wildlife hosts. Moreover, by integrating machine learning with traditional methods, we systematically assessed the zoonotic risk and cross-species transmission potential of the identified viruses, providing essential baseline data and scientific insights to support the prediction and prevention of emerging infectious diseases.

Results

Sampling overview

A total of 375 wild animal individuals were sampled in this study, encompassing 22 species (Supplementary Table 1) of mammals and birds across nine taxonomic orders: Rodentia, Carnivora, Artiodactyla, Perissodactyla, Lagomorpha, Anseriformes, Accipitriformes, Galliformes, and Passeriformes. None of the animals exhibited overt signs of disease at the time of sampling. In total, 430 biological specimens were collected, including 215 fecal samples, 160 intestinal content samples, and 55 internal organ samples (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from 11 individuals of bharal (Pseudois nayaur) (Fig. 1a). Among the five sampling sites, Dulan County exhibited the highest species diversity, with 12 species sampled, including species such as bharal (n = 38) and argali (Ovis ammon) (n = 18). Shiqu County yielded the largest number of specimens (n = 144), comprising four species-plateau vole (n = 100), plateau pika (n = 34), white-rumped snowfinch (Montifringilla taczanowskii) (n = 10), and Kam dwarf hamster (Cricetulus kamensis) (n = 4)-reflecting a typical ecological pattern of rodent-bird cohabitation. Samples from Golmud, Delingha, and Wulan were predominantly avian, with representative species including greylag goose and ruddy shelduck (Tadorna ferruginea) (Fig. 1b). Based on species composition, sample type, and sampling locality, a total of 430 samples were grouped and pooled into 33 meta-transcriptomic libraries, each comprising between 1 and 55 individual samples. Library sequencing output, adjusted according to RNA quality and sample input, ranged from 6.96 to 52.16 gigabases (Gb) per library, yielding a total of 554 Gb of raw data (Supplementary Table 1), which served as the basis for subsequent viromic analyses.

Viral diversity and distribution

This study identified 40,913 viral contigs (length: 300–41,151 bp; mean: 654 bp; N50: 670 bp). Among them, 104 contigs were nearly complete genomes (completeness > 90%). Of these, 36,172 (88.41%) could not be assigned to any known viral family (Supplementary Table 2), while the remaining 4741 contigs were annotated to 100 distinct viral families, highlighting substantial viral diversity (Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, 1730 contigs (4.23%) were classified as vertebrate-associated, 187 (0.46%) as invertebrate-associated, 1112 (2.72%) as plant- or fungal-associated, and 1712 (4.18%) as bacteria- or archaea-associated (Fig. 2a, c). To further resolve the unclassified sequences, higher-level taxonomic annotation revealed that the majority (n = 31,134; 86.07%) were affiliated with the class Caudoviricetes, with the highest abundance observed in kiang (n = 11,464; 36.82%). Notably, 4764 contigs (15.30%) remained unassigned to any known viral lineage (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 2).

After clustering the heatmap based on the taxonomic hierarchy of sampled species (class, order, species), avian species (class Aves) appeared to harbor a higher viral diversity compared with mammalian species (class Mammalia) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Among birds, greylag goose, ruddy shelduck, and white-rumped snowfinch exhibited the greatest richness, with 53, 30, and 29 viral families detected, respectively. In mammals, plateau vole carried the highest number of viral families (n = 32) (Supplementary Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 4). Among the 100 annotated viral families, 25 were classified as vertebrate-associated. Picobirnaviridae, Picornaviridae, Nodaviridae, and Astroviridae were identified in 18, 17, 12, and 11 libraries, respectively, indicating relatively high prevalence and abundance (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 3). Although Coronaviridae was detected in three libraries, no sequences related to SARS-CoV or SARS-CoV-2 were identified. At the species level, both white-rumped snowfinch and plateau vole harbored 11 vertebrate-associated viral families, suggesting elevated vertebrate viral diversity (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Among the 15 invertebrate-associated viral families, Iflaviridae was the most frequently detected, appearing in eight libraries and predominantly associated with passerine birds (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Both white-rumped snowfinch and red-billed chough (Pyrrhocorax pyrrhocorax) carried seven such families (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Among the 21 plant- and fungal-associated viral families, Tombusviridae was the most frequently detected (n = 23), with greylag goose, bharal, and chukar partridge (Alectoris chukar) harboring 11, 11, and 10 such families, respectively. Of the 39 bacteria- and archaea-associated viral families, Herelleviridae and Microviridae were the most frequently observed, each present in 18 libraries. Greylag goose, kiang and ruddy shelduck carried the highest numbers of these families, with 28, 18, and 17, respectively (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 1d).

Substantial virome variation was also observed among sympatric species inhabiting rodent-bird cohabitation environments. Despite lower sample numbers and comparable sequencing depths, Kam dwarf hamster and white-rumped snowfinch exhibited markedly higher viral richness and abundance than plateau pika (Supplementary Figs. 1c and 2a). Furthermore, plateau vole samples from nearby locations (<10 km; T1 and P2) displayed distinct differences in viromic composition and abundance, despite similar sample sizes and sequencing depths (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In tissue samples of bharal, Parvoviridae was detected exclusively in the liver, while unclassified viral sequences were identified in the lung, and no viral sequences were detected in other organs (Supplementary Fig. 2b). In contrast, the faecal (A1, K2) and intestinal content (C) samples from the same species revealed greater diversity of plant- and fungal-associated viruses.

Evolutionary diversity of vertebrate-associated viruses

We selected 264 representative viral sequences containing RdRp or replicase domains from 15 vertebrate-associated viral families. Excluding Picobirnaviridae, the remaining 127 sequences from 14 families represented 101 viral species, including 53 novel and 48 known viruses (Supplementary Table 5). Phylogenetic analyses revealed several viral lineages forming distinct clades that exhibited significant evolutionary divergence from known genera (Fig. 3). For instance, within Picornaviridae, sequences derived from greylag goose, red-billed chough, and Himalayan vulture formed divergent branches; in Hepeviridae, viruses detected in white-rumped snowfinch clustered separately; and Rhabdoviridae and Phenuiviridae sequences from red fox (Vulpes vulpes) could not be assigned to any recognized genus. Some viruses exhibited species-specific clustering, such as astrovirus in greylag goose and picobirnavirus in both Himalayan vulture and plateau vole. Several viruses expanded known host ranges or geographic distributions, including the first detection of Deltacoronavirus in Himalayan vulture, Circovirus in Thorold’s deer, Megrivirus and nodavirus in chukar partridge, picornavirus in desert hare (Lepus tibetanus), and Ambidensovirus in white-rumped snowfinch. Notably, a rhabdovirus identified in red fox exhibited closest phylogenetic affinity to a conspecific strain detected in Spain, despite an approximately 8,000-km geographic distance.

Among the 53 novel viruses, most were assigned to Picornaviridae (n = 23) and Nodaviridae (n = 12). Greylag goose and Himalayan vulture harbored 14 and 18 novel viruses, respectively, exhibiting significantly higher novel viral diversity than other sampled species. The Deltacoronavirus detected in Himalayan vulture shared only 74.10% amino acid identity in the ORF1b region with its closest known relative, representing a previously uncharacterized virus. An RVA identified in plateau vole occupied a basal position in the phylogenetic clade, indicative of an early-diverging lineage. Mobuvirus, previously considered insect-specific, was detected in white-rumped snowfinch, sharing only 50.53% amino acid identity with its closest known relative, leaving its host infectivity unresolved. Among known viruses, a Hepeviridae strain detected in white-rumped snowfinch was 100% identical at the amino acid level to a virus previously reported in plateau vole by an independent study. Furthermore, the same Rotavirus species was detected in both plateau vole and plateau pika, sharing 81.16% amino acid identity and exhibiting 88.70% identity with a bat-derived strain. Intriguingly, these host species coexist within a “rodent-bird cohabitation” micro-ecosystem.

Characteristics of Viruses with High Emergence Potential

A total of 36 viral genera with potential pathogenicity to humans or animals were identified, spanning 17 viral families. Among these, Picornaviridae, Caliciviridae, and Parvoviridae were predominant. Several genera exhibited broad host distributions, including Circovirus (n = 6), Mamastrovirus (n = 5), Sapelovirus (n = 4), Norovirus (n = 3), and Rotavirus (n = 3). Notably, some of these potentially pathogenic viruses also exhibit a high risk of human infection (Fig. 4a,b). Among avian samples, Himalayan vulture and greylag goose harbored 13 and 11 high-risk viral genera, respectively, mainly associated with animal pathogens, with Hepatovirus showing relatively high abundance in Himalayan vulture. In mammals, plateau vole carried nine pathogenic viral genera, eight of which are zoonotic, with RVA showing significant abundance. Kam dwarf hamster harbored five zoonotic genera, with Norovirus most abundant (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 7). Although some viral genera were detected with low frequency or abundance, their sequences exhibited high zoonotic risk. For example, Kobuvirus in plateau pika, Alphavirus in Himalayan vulture, Enterovirus in both Kam dwarf hamster and Himalayan vulture, and Bocaparvovirus in Thorold’s deer (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 9).

Phylogenetic analyses combined with ZoonoticRank risk prediction revealed that some viral sequences clustered with human strains or were assessed as having high or very high human infection risk. For example, in plateau vole, we detected hantavirus, RVA, and Norovirus, with hantavirus exhibiting 85.26% amino acid identity to human strains. Cardiovirus and Norovirus were detected in Kam dwarf hamster; Hepatovirus A and Cardiovirus in Himalayan marmot; Norovirus and Hepatovirus in Himalayan vulture. Notably, the PIV-5 hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN) protein sequences detected in desert hare and elk (Cervus canadensis) were identical and shared over 99% identity with human strains, with ZoonoticRank predicting a relatively high risk of human infection (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). These results indicate that these high-risk viruses possess potential zoonotic capabilities and a significant risk for human spillover.

Although the pathogenicity of some viruses remains unclear, such as Hunnivirus and Mosavirus in plateau vole and Pegivirus in white-rumped snowfinch, risk assessments indicate a high potential for human infection (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 9). Some potentially pathogenic viruses, while not clustering with human strains, showed high genetic similarity to viruses from other species, indicating cross-species transmission potential. For instance, H9 subtype avian influenza virus detected in greylag goose belonged to the Y439-like lineage and showed high homology with poultry and wild bird strains from multiple regions. Phlebovirus identified in red fox clustered with bat- and bird-derived strains, while Hepacivirus in plateau vole was closely related to rodent-derived strains (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 8 and 9).

Cross-species transmission and recombination

By constructing a host-virus association network, we identified 15 viruses with potential for cross-species transmission. Among them, Himalayan vulture, greylag goose, and Thorold’s deer harbored the greatest number of such viruses, 10, 9, and 8, respectively, occupying central positions within the transmission network. The viruses shared between greylag goose and Thorold’s deer were primarily from the genus Circovirus and the family Parvoviridae, whereas those detected in Himalayan vulture exhibited greater diversity, including Hunnivirus and Mamastrovirus in addition to the aforementioned viruses. Furthermore, Thorold’s deer shared Parvoviridae viruses and multiple Circovirus species with both Himalayan vulture and greylag goose; desert hare and elk shared PIV-5; and cross-species transmission of RVA was observed between plateau vole and plateau pika (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 10).

Host prediction for viral sequences from high-risk genera produced assignments at the kingdom and phylum levels (Layer 1) for 354 out of 441 sequences. Of these, 289 were predicted to infect members of Chordata, while the remainder were assigned to invertebrates (n = 33), Viridiplantae (n = 29), and fungi (n = 3). At the class and order levels (Layer 2), the most frequently predicted hosts included Artiodactyla (n = 72), Aves (n = 46), Primates (n = 39), and Reptilia (n = 39). A comparison between predicted and observed host taxa revealed substantial discrepancies. For example, viruses identified in plateau vole were predominantly predicted to infect Primates, Artiodactyla, and birds; those from greylag goose were mainly associated with reptiles, Artiodactyla, and invertebrates; and those from Himalayan vulture were primarily predicted to infect invertebrates and plants (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 11).

The Gammacoronavirus identified in greylag goose exhibited high amino acid identity (97.85–100%) with known strains for ORF1b, E, M, and N proteins, but substantially lower identity (78.98–85.40%) for ORF1a and the spike (S) protein (Fig. 5c). Recombination analysis revealed strong signals in both the ORF1a and S regions, each supported by at least five independent algorithms. A genome-wide similarity plot identified a major recombination breakpoint spanning over 3 kb within the S gene, nearly encompassing its entire length (Fig. 5d). Phylogenetic trees constructed using ORF1a, ORF1b, and S sequences displayed markedly discordant topologies: ORF1a clustered with strains from Charadriiformes, ORF1b with viruses from Anseriformes, and the S protein formed a distinct lineage, suggesting that this virus has undergone multiple recombination events (Fig. 5e). In Himalayan vulture, the Deltacoronavirus formed independent branches for both ORF1a and ORF1b, clustering with strains derived from Pelecaniformes, and showing consistent tree topologies (Supplementary Fig. 4a). However, the amino acid identity of these two regions with known coronaviruses was relatively low-49.12% for ORF1a and 74.61% for ORF1b (Supplementary Fig. 4b)-indicating that a substantial diversity of Deltacoronavirus in avian hosts remains undocumented. Notably, the systematic phylogenetic branching structure of its S protein differs significantly from that of ORF1a and ORF1b, possibly originating from a recombination event.

Evolutionary origins and host shifts of rotavirus A

A total of 229 RVA sequences covering 15 host orders were included to construct a maximum clade credibility (MCC) phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Table 12). The results showed that the virus formed two main evolutionary lineages. Notably, RVA strains identified from plateau vole (Rodentia) formed a completely independent and earliest diverging lineage. In contrast, the common ancestor of viruses from other hosts could be traced back to Chiroptera, from which a transmission lineage primarily associated with Artiodactyla evolved. This lineage also included some strains from Carnivora, Perissodactyla, and Primates, indicating frequent cross-host-order transmission ability and broad host adaptability of RVA. Furthermore, RVA strains identified from plateau pika derived from an ancestor in Chiroptera, and this lineage mainly included strains from birds belonging to Galliformes, Passeriformes, and Columbiformes (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 5).

Bayesian factor (BF) analysis identified 14 host-switching events with strong statistical support (BF > 10). Among them, Artiodactyla experienced five cross-order transmissions, including four with decisive support (BF > 100), involving transmission to Carnivora, Primates, Lagomorpha, and Perissodactyla. Additionally, one transmission route to Ostreida was detected with BF greater than 30, indicating the virus’s potential to spread to marine organisms. These results suggest that Artiodactyla play an important “donor” role in RVA cross-species transmission. Notably, despite relatively few Carnivora samples, this order participated in six host-switching events (three as donors, three as recipients), with transmissions to Chiroptera and Perissodactyla both strongly supported (BF > 100) (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table 13). Further statistics of all cross-order host-switching events with statistical support (BF > 3) showed that Carnivora accounted for the most events (10), with seven as donors, indicating a key “transmission hub” role. Rodentia mainly acted as recipient hosts, involved in five virus acquisition events (Fig. 6c).

Discussion

Unlike previous viromic studies that primarily focused on high-risk species or densely populated areas27,33–35, this study centers on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau-the world’s highest and largest plateau-characterized by diverse ecosystems and unique geographic isolation36,37. These conditions have fostered rich wildlife biodiversity and created an ideal setting for viral accumulation and independent evolution. However, due to harsh environmental conditions and logistical difficulties, studies of viral diversity in this region have long remained sporadic and systematically lacking18,20–23,38. Here, we systematically collected and analyzed samples from 22 wild animal species, providing the first comprehensive viromic profile of multiple plateau wildlife species. Notably, several viruses, including RVA, PIV-5, and Norovirus, exhibited both zoonotic potential and multi-host distribution, indicating a strong capacity for cross-species transmission. These findings underscore the urgency of implementing routine surveillance for high-risk viruses and key host species, particularly the plateau vole and the Himalayan vulture.

Among the viral sequences identified, Caudoviricetes accounted for 76.10%, aligning with their dominant presence across global ecosystems39. An additional 11.59% of sequences were annotated to 100 viral families, underscoring the high complexity and diversity of the regional virome. These viruses included not only zoonotic agents but also those infecting insects, plants, fungi, and microorganisms, which may impact agriculture and ecosystem stability. Avian species exhibited greater overall viral diversity than mammals, with Anseriformes (e.g., greylag goose) and Accipitriformes (e.g., Himalayan vulture) identified as diversity “hotspots.” The broad viral carriage observed in birds suggests a potential role as viral disseminators within the plateau’s ecological network, warranting heightened attention in future surveillance. Species such as white-rumped snowfinch, plateau pika, and plateau vole, which share nesting habitats and similar diets, showed evidence of cross-host viral sharing-e.g., identical RVA was detected in plateau pika and plateau vole, and a Hepeviridae virus highly similar to that in plateau vole was also found in white-rumped snowfinch. These observations suggest that ecological niche overlap may facilitate interspecies viral transmission to some extent. However, significant differences in virome composition among these species still exist, indicating that viral distribution in wildlife is jointly influenced by multiple factors, including host characteristics, home range, habitat, and feeding behavior. Among these, the biological attributes of the host remain the core driving factor determining viral distribution27,40–42.

We observed extensive evolutionary diversity within the viromes of Qinghai-Tibet Plateau wildlife. Multiple viral families (e.g., Picornaviridae, Hepeviridae, Phenuiviridae) harbored putative novel lineages highly divergent from known genera, suggesting the presence of previously unrecognized viral clades. In terms of host distribution, we identified eight zoonosis-related viral genera in plateau vole and five in Kam dwarf hamster, with plateau vole exhibiting the highest such diversity among sampled mammals. Remarkably, despite a sample size of only four, Kam dwarf hamster harbored multiple such viruses, supporting the view of rodents as high-risk reservoirs13. Some viral sequences exhibited high genetic similarity to known human pathogens and were predicted to pose a high risk of human infection. These included hantaviruses and RVA detected in plateau vole, as well as PIV-5 found in desert hare and elk. Notably, PIV-5 is capable of aerosol transmission and could have implications for wider spread43.

Cross-species transmission not only drives the emergence of infectious diseases but also facilitates genetic exchange between viruses44,45. We identified 15 viruses with cross-species transmission potential, with circoviruses being the most prevalent and exhibiting the broadest host range. Similar patterns have been reported in bats and shrews in Africa28. Their highly divergent viral entry proteins may enhance adaptability to diverse hosts, supporting the persistence of genetically diverse viral populations. Himalayan vulture, greylag goose, and Thorold’s deer are at the center of the interspecies viral transmission network. It should be noted that network centrality may be influenced by differences in sample size or sequencing depth among host species. Although sampling for cervids was limited, they were found to share parvovirus, circoviruses, and parainfluenza viruses with other hosts, indicating high susceptibility and potential for viral transmission. In several cases, the predicted hosts of the viruses did not match the detected hosts31, suggesting possible host adaptability across taxonomic orders.

The coronavirus spike (S) gene is a known recombination hotspot46. In this study, we detected recombination signals in the S genes of a Gammacoronavirus from greylag goose and a Deltacoronavirus from Himalayan vulture. Notably, the Deltacoronavirus in Himalayan vulture represents a previously undescribed lineage, suggesting that coronavirus diversity in this region may be underestimated. Although several Alphacoronavirus and Betacoronavirus have been reported in Qinghai-Tibet Plateau wildlife21, none were detected here, potentially due to limited sampling or strong spatiotemporal heterogeneity. Expanded sampling is warranted to better elucidate their ecological distribution.

RVA is the leading cause of severe diarrhea in children worldwide, accounting for over 90% of Rotavirus infections47,48. In plateau vole, we identified a novel RVA lineage occupying the most basal phylogenetic position; using Bayesian ancestral state reconstruction, we traced the global evolutionary history of animal-derived RVA and confirmed that this lineage represents an early-diverging and completely independent descendant branch. Previously, we also discovered an ancient arenavirus in plateau pika22, supporting the hypothesis that the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, due to its long-term geographic isolation and ecological barriers, may serve as a reservoir for ancient and evolutionarily significant viral genetic resources. Beyond evolutionary antiquity, RVA also exhibits asymmetric transmission patterns in certain host species. While Artiodactyla constitutes the primary backbone of the global evolutionary network, Carnivora plays a pivotal role as ‘superspreaders.’ This core donor status is likely facilitated by their extensive home ranges and frequent cross-niche predatory behaviors, which enhance connectivity with diverse host taxa and promote cross-species spillover. In contrast, Rodentia was identified as a predominant recipient host with no significant donor effect. This disparity may stem from the high population density and lower trophic status of rodents, rendering them highly susceptible to viral influx while their relatively confined ecological niches and the specialized, conservative evolutionary trajectory of their resident RVA lineages potentially restrict outward viral exportation.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of opportunistic sampling without statistical design led to uneven representation across host species and geographic locations, limiting systematic comparisons of viral distributions. Second, pooled-sample sequencing may dilute low-abundance viral signals, reducing detection sensitivity. Differences in sample size and sequencing depth across libraries may also affect the accuracy of diversity and abundance estimates. Lastly, while machine learning models can predict zoonotic risk and host range from fragmented sequences, their accuracy remains limited and cannot substitute for experimental validation. Therefore, such predictions may not fully capture true infectivity or transmission potential. Future studies should aim to address these limitations.

Methods

Sample collection and processing

Between March and September 2023, wild animal sampling was carried out at five sites in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, including Golmud, Delingha, Dulan, and Wulan within Haixi Mongolian and Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture of Qinghai Province, and Shiqu in the Ganzi Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture of Sichuan Province. A total of 22 wild animal species were sampled, several of which are endemic to the plateau, such as bharal, goa, kiang, plateau pika, and Himalayan marmot. Due to the extreme natural environment and limited transportation infrastructure in the plateau, sample collection was highly challenging; consequently, no statistical design was employed to predetermine sample sizes or spatial distribution. Organ tissues were collected exclusively from a single species obtained through legal wildlife disease surveillance programs; for the majority of protected taxa, invasive organ sampling was prohibited under national wildlife conservation regulations. Consequently, non-invasive fecal sampling was prioritized to minimize ecological impact. Intestinal content samples were primarily retrieved from small non-protected mammals or individuals deceased from natural causes. Sample collection of all protected animals involved in this research was performed in accordance with Chinese laws and regulations. Given that some wild animals, including the bharal, are nationally protected species (Class II) in China, an administrative license (Approval No. SH202303070090) was obtained in advance from the Forestry and Grassland Bureau of Qinghai Province. All sampling and handling procedures followed the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the China CDC and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the National Institute for Communicable Disease Control and Prevention (Approval No. 2023-001).

Identification of host animal species was initially performed by experienced wildlife biologists based on morphological characteristics and later confirmed via molecular methods by amplifying and sequencing the mitochondrial cytochrome b (cytb) gene. Three types of samples were involved, namely, feces, intestinal contents, and internal organs. Fecal samples were obtained non-invasively by observing defecation events and collecting fresh feces immediately after the animal’s departure to minimize disturbance. Small rodents such as plateau pika, plateau vole, and Kam dwarf hamster were live trapped, anaesthetized with ether, and intestinal contents were collected. For bharal, eleven individuals were captured using professional drop-net traps, anaesthetised, and dissected to obtain heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and intestinal contents. All samples were placed in 5 mL cryotubes prefilled with 2 mL RNAlater, immediately stored in portable freezers at −20 °C during fieldwork, and transferred to our lab in Beijing in dry ice and stored in the refrigerator at −80 °C prior to RNA extraction.

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted and libraries were constructed from pooled samples based on host species, sample type, and geographic origin. Fecal and intestinal content samples were homogenized in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), centrifuged, and the supernatant was subjected to nucleic acid extraction using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). Tissue samples were processed using the RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit (QIAGEN), following the manufacturers’ instructions. Prior to library construction, nucleic acid concentrations were measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher), and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Samples that passed quality control were depleted of ribosomal RNA using the Ribo-MagOff rRNA Depletion Kit (Vazyme), and sequencing libraries were prepared with the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme). Libraries passing final quality assessment were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads. Detailed library information is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Data processing and viral identification

Raw sequencing reads were first subjected to quality assessment and filtering using FastQC (v0.11.8)49 and Fastp (v0.23.4)50 with default parameters to obtain high-quality clean reads. De novo assembly was performed on clean reads using Megahit (v1.2.9)51 with default settings, and contigs shorter than 300 bp were discarded. The resulting contigs were queried against the non-redundant (nr) protein database (downloaded in December 2023) using Diamond (v2.1.9)52 in Blastx mode with parameters --outfmt 6 --evalue 1e-5 --max-target-seqs 1, and the best-hit annotations were retained. Contigs annotated as viral were considered candidate viral sequences. To reduce false positives, these candidate viral contigs were further aligned to the non-redundant nucleotide (nt) database (downloaded in December 2023) using blastn (v2.7.1), and non-viral sequences were removed based on the alignment results. The final set of viral sequences was subsequently assessed for genome completeness using CheckV (v1.0.3).

Taxonomic annotation and host association of viruses

The taxonomic affiliation of each viral contig was determined based on the top BLASTX hit, and all identified viral sequences were annotated at the family level. Given that retroviral sequences may originate from host genomic integrations, sequences affiliated with the family Retroviridae were excluded from downstream analyses. Considering that certain viral families (e.g., Peribunyaviridae, Phenuiviridae) exhibit broad host ranges, host assignment for some viral families remains challenging. To address this, we categorized the identified viral families into four host-associated groups based on information from ViralZone (https://viralzone.expasy.org/) and the Virus-Host Database regarding their primary natural hosts: vertebrate-associated, invertebrate-associated, plant- and fungal-associated and bacterial- and archaeal-associated. For viral sequences annotated as unassigned at the family level, their associated hosts were also designated as unassigned. A detailed mapping of viral families to host categories is provided in Supplementary Table S4. This classification framework facilitates the elucidation of viral ecological distribution patterns among different species and provides a foundation for evaluating their potential zoonotic risk.

Viral abundance quantification

To quantify viral abundance, clean reads that had passed quality control were first mapped to the SILVA database53 using Bowtie2 (v2.5.4)54 in end-to-end mode. Reads aligning to ribosomal RNA were removed with Samtools (v1.2.0). The remaining non-rRNA reads were then quantified by mapping to viral reference sequences using the pileup.sh script from the BBMap suite (v39.19)55. Viral abundance within each library was measured in transcripts per million (TPM)56, a length- and depth-normalized metric that corrects for sequencing biases. For each library, TPM values belonging to the same viral family were aggregated and log-transformed [log(TPM + 1)] to generate viral abundance heatmaps (Supplementary Table S3).

Phylogenetic analysis

To clarify the taxonomic placement and evolutionary relationships of vertebrate-associated viruses, phylogenetic trees were constructed at the family level based on conserved replication-associated proteins. RNA viruses were represented by RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), while DNA viruses were represented by replicase proteins. To obtain high-quality coronavirus sequences, we selected one coronavirus-positive sample from each of three coronavirus-positive libraries for deep sequencing and incorporated the resulting data into subsequent analyses (Supplementary Table S1). Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using Prodigal (v2.6.3)57, and the corresponding protein sequences were generated. RNA viral sequences were then compared against the RdRp HMM database58 using HMMER (v3.4)59 to extract sequences containing the RdRp domain, while DNA viral sequences were compared against the Pfam database to identify those harboring the replicase domain. Sequences lacking key domains or exhibiting insufficient length were discarded, with representative sequences retained for phylogenetic analysis. For each viral family, representative sequences were further subjected to blastx searches against the NCBI non-redundant (NR) database to retrieve homologous viral sequences. These were then combined with reference sequences from the corresponding viral families in the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) or GenBank to construct the phylogenetic dataset. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT (v7.525)60 with default parameters, followed by trimming of ambiguous regions using trimAl (v1.4.1)61 with the -automated1 option. Phylogenetic trees were inferred with IQ-TREE (v2.3.4)62 using the maximum likelihood (ML) method, with parameters -m MFP -bb 1000 -nt AUTO, where MFP denotes ModelFinder Plus for automatic model selection, and 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates were used to assess node support. Resulting trees were visualized using FigTree (v1.4.3.0) and the Chiplot web platform (https://www.chiplot.online/)63. Based on the classification criteria established by the ICTV, a novel virus species is typically defined by having less than 80% amino acid identity in the RdRp or replicase protein compared to any known reference. In this study, we applied this threshold uniformly across all viral taxa to delineate novel species64. Given the high genetic diversity and unclear phylogenetic classification of Picobirnaviridae65, novel viral species within this family were not designated at this time.

Pathogenicity and zoonotic risk assessment

To systematically assess the pathogenic potential of the vertebrate-associated viruses identified in this study and their likelihood of infecting humans, we compiled a catalogue of high-risk viral families and genera (Supplementary Table 6) based on taxonomic annotation results and information from the ICTV and ViralZone databases. A genus was considered potentially pathogenic to animals or humans if it included any known members with confirmed pathogenicity. Viral families containing one or more pathogenic genera were subsequently classified as high-risk. Based on the pathogenic host spectrum, these genera were further divided into animal-pathogenic or zoonotic types. All viral sequences from these high-risk families were compared against the NCBI non-redundant (NR) protein database using BLASTx, and the top three hits were retained to facilitate accurate and comprehensive genus-level annotation. Sequences matching the high-risk genera were then used to quantify their abundance across sampled host species. Representative sequences from these genera were selected for phylogenetic analysis to assess their clustering patterns and genetic similarity to known human-infecting strains. To further quantify the likelihood of these viruses infecting humans, we employed ZoonoticRank (https://github.com/Nardus/zoonotic_rank)32, a machine-learning model that predicts zoonotic potential based on viral genomic features.

Cross-species transmission potential and host range of viruses

To assess the potential cross-species transmission patterns of high-risk viral family sequences among different wild animals, all viral sequences were subjected to open reading frame (ORF) prediction using Prodigal (v2.6.3)57, and predicted proteins shorter than 50 amino acids were discarded. Subsequently, protein sequences were clustered using CD-HIT (v4.8.1)66 with a threshold of 80% amino acid identity (AAI) to identify highly similar viral proteins. Viral sequences derived from different host species that shared >80% AAI were considered to exhibit potential for cross-host transmission28. Based on these results, a virus-host sharing network was constructed using Gephi (v0.10.1)67 with the Fruchterman-Reingold layout algorithm to visualize virus sharing across host species. Given that some detected viruses, particularly those found in intestinal or fecal samples, may originate from dietary sources, environmental exposure, or symbiotic microbes, the true host identity of these viruses may be uncertain. To further infer viral host range, we employed the machine learning-based tool RNAVirHost (https://github.com/GreyGuoweiChen/VirHost)31 to predict the hosts of sequences assigned to high-risk viral genera, thereby assessing their possible origins and potential transmission routes.

Recombination analysis

A full-genome dataset was constructed for the greylag goose coronavirus and its homologous sequences, followed by multiple sequence alignment using MAFFT (v7.525)60. Recombination detection was performed with RDP4 software68, applying seven integrated methods (RDP, GENECONV, Chimaera, MaxChi, BootScan, SiScan, and 3Seq) under default settings. Only recombination events identified by at least three methods were retained64. These signals were further validated using the BootScan module of SimPlot (v3.5.1)69 with a window size of 600 bp and step size of 60 bp, generating similarity plots for detailed analysis. For the Himalayan vulture coronavirus, owing to the absence of homologous full-genome sequences in the NCBI database, protein sequences from its major coding regions were extracted for maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis (see “Phylogenetic analysis” section). Potential recombination events were inferred by assessing topological incongruence among phylogenetic trees derived from different genomic regions. The greylag goose coronavirus was analyzed using the same approach.

Ancestral state reconstruction and cross-species transmission analysis of rotavirus A

To investigate the evolutionary origin and cross-host transmission patterns of rotavirus A (RVA), we performed Bayesian discrete phylogeographic analyses in BEAST (v1.10.4)70, using host taxonomic orders as discrete trait states to infer the ancestral state of each node on the phylogenetic tree. Full-length VP1 gene sequences of animal-associated RVA were retrieved from the NCBI nucleotide database as of 18 June 2025. To ensure data quality, we excluded sequences that (i) lacked sampling time, host information, or geographic origin; (ii) contained ≥50 consecutive ambiguous bases (Ns); (iii) corresponded to vaccine strains; or (iv) were synthetically constructed71,72.

To reduce computational complexity and ensure representativeness, the dataset was stratified by combinations of host order and sampling continent, and sequences in each subset were dereplicated based on 95% nucleotide identity. Additionally, two newly identified sequences in this study from plateau vole and plateau pika were incorporated into the final dataset. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT (v7.525)60, and ambiguous regions were trimmed with trimAl (v1.4.1)61 using the “-automated1” parameter. The optimal nucleotide substitution model was selected using IQ-TREE (v2.3.4)62 based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)73, resulting in the selection of the GTR + F + R7 model. Temporal signal was assessed using TreeTime (https://treetime.biozentrum.unibas.ch/)74, which indicated insufficient temporal structure to reliably estimate substitution rates or the time to the most recent common ancestor (tMRCA). Therefore, a fixed substitution rate of 1.0 was applied in subsequent BEAST analyses. Phylogenetic reconstruction was conducted under a strict molecular clock and a constant population size coalescent prior, using an asymmetric substitution model and Bayesian stochastic search variable selection (BSSVS). Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations were run for 50 million generations, sampling every 5000 steps. Convergence was assessed using Tracer (v1.7.1)75, with a burn-in of 10% of the total chain length, and effective sample sizes (ESS) for all parameters were ≥200. The maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree was generated using TreeAnnotator (v1.10.4) and visualized in FigTree (v1.4.3.0)46,76.

To evaluate the statistical support for state transitions between host orders, Bayes factors (BF) were calculated in SpreaD3 (v0.9.6)77. Transitions with BF > 3 were considered statistically supported, with BF > 10, >30, and >100 indicating strong, very strong, and decisive support, respectively76. In this study, a stringent threshold of BF > 10 was applied to identify significant host-switch events, ensuring the robustness of our core evolutionary conclusions. Concurrently, for the analysis of overall cross-species transmission frequency, a threshold of BF > 3 was utilized to more comprehensively reveal transmission patterns and avoid overlooking links with substantial biological support.
