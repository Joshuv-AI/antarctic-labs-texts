Nurse-led visual triage against histopathology in a real-world low-resource setting cervical cancer prevention program
Authors: Simon M. Manga, Ivree Randolph, Lynda Ugwu, Grace Lee, Kathleen L. Nulah, Florence Manjuh, Comfort Ketu, Rebecca C. Arend
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•There is substantial burden of CIN2+ in a high‑risk population.•The nurse‑led visual triage, has potential for cervical cancer prevention in the absent of highly skilled personnel.•Nurse-led visual triage has good reliability in identifying ICC but poor for CIN2.

Introduction

Though cervical cancer is a highly preventable disease, it has remained the leading cause of death among women in resource limited settings (World Health Organization, 2014). From global perspective, cervical cancer ranks the fourth most frequently diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women (Ferlay et al., 2015). Cervical cancer incidence and mortality is disproportionally spread with at least 85% of new cases and 87% of deaths occurring in low- to middle- income countries (LMICs) (Ferlay et al., 2015) where age standardized cervical cancer mortality is sixfold higher compared to high income countries (Simms et al., 2019). Cervical cancer remains among the leading causes of cancer morbidity and mortality among women in Cameroon and the Sub-Saharan Africa (Ngwa et al., 2022).

Detection and treatment of precancerous lesions or cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) through screening is paramount to the prevention of cervical cancer (Wright and Cox, 2014). In pathological terms, cervical precancer categorizes levels of dysplasia by the proportion of epithelium exhibiting dysplastic cells, with grades ranging from CIN 1–3 (Cox et al., 2014). Among these, CIN3 also known as carcinoma in-situ is the main precursor to invasive cervical cancer (ICC) (Muruka et al., 2013). The progression from CIN to ICC takes about 10 years (Wright and Cox, 2014). Treatment of CIN or cervical precancer is the core of secondary prevention of cervical cancer, simply because, it prevents the progression of CIN to ICC.

Treatment of CIN can be done by either ablative techniques or excision techniques (Cox et al., 2014). Ablative technique involves destruction of the lesion including techniques such as cryotherapy and thermal ablation (TA). Excisional technique involves surgical removal of the affected portion of the cervix and constitutes Large Loop Excision of the Transformation Zone (LLETZ) also known as Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure (LEEP) or Cone Biopsy (Cox et al., 2014). One advantage of excisional treatment over ablative treatment is that it provides specimen for histopathology. Therefore, it serves as treatment and diagnosis at the same time. For lesions suspicious of ICC which cannot benefit from excision or ablative treatment, a biopsy is done for histopathological confirmation (Cox et al., 2014).

The histopathological reports from specimens from excision treatment and biopsy usually reveal a host of benign or non-benign/pathological conditions of the cervix. The common pathological conditions include ICC and its precursors; CIN 1, CIN2, CIN3 and adenocarcinoma insitu. The benign conditions range from infections such as cervicitis, condylomata, and genital tuberculosis to growths like polyps. The purpose of this study was to assess the histopathological reports from excision treatment and biopsy to identify the prevalence of clinically significant lesions (CIN2 +) including CIN2, CIN3, micro-invasion, and ICC among a cohort of women who had abnormal cervical cancer screening in Cameroon and to identify potential risk factors for CIN2 + in this population. In addition, we assessed the inter-rater agreement between presumptive diagnosis of clinic staff to that of the final pathological diagnosis.

Materials and methods

The study constitutes human subject research and received Institutional Review Board approval from the Cameroon Baptist Convention Health Services (CBCHS) − IRB2022-82 and the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) − IRB-300010928–002. Data collected complied with relevant patient data protection rules and guidelines. This research was conducted in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Setting and procedures

This study was a cross-sectional study using secondary data from women who underwent cervical cancer screening and provided tissue through excision treatment or biopsy between 2007–2023 in a large cervical cancer screening program called Women’s Health Program (WHP) run by the CBCHS. The CBCHS is a large faith-based healthcare organization that has a network of 95 health facilities in eight of the 10 regions of Cameroon. WHP is a nurse-led program within CBCHS that screens an average of 8000 women yearly for cervical cancer. The CBCHS implemented the WHP in 2007 to fight against cancer of the cervix and WHP has become the largest cervical cancer prevention program in Cameroon and it operates in 15 of the CBCHS facilities (Cholli et al., 2015, DeGregorio et al., 2016).

In 2007, the CBCHS partnered with Center for Infectious Disease Research of Zambia (CIDRZ) to implement a comprehensive nurse-led cervical cancer prevention program, the WHP. CIDRZ runs the largest cervical cancer prevention program in Africa and WHP was modelled after the Zambia program. In this program, cervical cancer screening is done by Digital Cervicography (DC) which is an adjunct to Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) and Visual Inspection with Lugol’s Iodine (VILI). This process has been described in detail in our previous work (Manga et al., 2015, Manga et al., 2020). Women with acetowhite lesions meeting ablative trearment criteria are provided cryotherapy or thermal ablation (TA). Large Loop Excision of the Transformation Zone (LLETZ) using an Electro-Surgical Unit (ESU) is used to treat acetowhite lesions that could not be adequately treated by cryotherapy or TA (Manga et al., 2020). Exocervical punch biopsy is performed on lesions suspicious for invasive cervical cancer (ICC) and lesions that are difficult to interpret. All procedures including biopsy and LEEP/LLETZ were performed by trained program nurses.

Beginning 2020, WHP uses human papillomavirus (HPV) testing with AmpFire as primary screening test for women 30 years and above. AmpFire, Atlia Biosystems, is a relatively new HPV detection technology that uses multiplex isothermal real time florescence assay to diagnosed 14 high risk HPV types while genotyping types 16 and 18 simultaneously (Zhang et al., 2020). Those positive for HPV are triaged to VIA-VILI-DC. The AmpFire technology has been recently upgraded to ScreenFire and the results are provided in four channels including HPV 16, HPV 18/45, HPV 31/33/35/52/58 and HPV 39/51/56/59/68. There is also an internal control to determine if the run is valid or not.

Histopathology

The LLETZ and biopsy specimens were analyzed at Yaoundé Gyneco-Obstetrics and Pediatric Hospital, Buea Regional Hospital, and Mbingo Baptist Hospital. The specimens were placed in 10% formalin solution in a specimen container, labeled, and shipped to the pathology laboratory for analysis. The pathologic diagnoses were classified as negative for dysplasia or malignancy, CIN 1, CIN 2, CIN 3, micro-invasion, or ICC. The program nurses provided a presumptive pathologic diagnosis which was compared with the final pathologic diagnosis.

Data management and analysis

Information that was not available in the WHP database was abstracted from the paper registries at the WHP clinics. All study patients were assigned a unique study ID and de-identified data were entered into a stand-alone Redcap database (Vanderbilt University, TN). We described baseline characteristics by estimating means, standard deviations (SD), counts, and percentages for continuous and categorical variables, as appropriate. Means and SDs were estimated for age and years of schooling. Counts and proportions were summarized for categorical variables including marital status, occupation, religion, HIV status, and VIA-DC results. T-tests and chi square tests were used to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively, between women with CIN2 + and women without CIN2 + .

Logistic regression models were used to identify potential risk factors for CIN2 + in this population. Models included potential factors such as age, marital status, occupation, education, HIV status, and VIA-DC screening results. These variables were selected a priori based on their relevance to the research objectives. To assess the inter-rater agreement between presumptive diagnosis of clinic staff to the final pathologic diagnosis, we measured weighted Cohen’s Kappa statistic. Statistical significance was evaluated at a 0.05 alpha level, and all statistical analyses were performed by study statisticians at UAB School of Medicine. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

More specifically, those with suspicious for cancer results had approximately 3-fold higher odds of CIN2 + compared to those with negative VIA-VILI-DC results. Marital status, occupation, and education were not significant predictors of CIN2 +.

Comparisons between concluding CIN1, CIN3, and Other between nurses’ presumptive pathologic diagnosis and final pathologic diagnosis produced kappa values of 0.216 (0.118, 0.313), 0.261, (0.196, 0.327) and 0.277 (0.199, 0.356), respectively, which suggests fair strength of agreement between the nurses and the pathologists (Table 5). When comparing CIN2 and negative results, we found that the kappa values were 0.180 (0.094, 0.266) and −0.002 (−0.028, 0.025), respectively, which suggests poor strength of agreement. While CIN2 was significantly different from zero (κ = 0.180, p < 0.0001), negative results were not (κ = -0.002, p = 0.916). When comparing ICC results, we found that there was a kappa value of 0.610 (0.568, 0.652) which suggests good strength of agreement and it was significantly different from zero (κ = 0.610, p < 0.0001).

Discussion

The results of this study highlight a very high burden of advanced cervical disease among women with abnormal screening reports in Cameroon. With 71.9% of participants having confirmed CIN2 + lesions and a high prevalence of Invasive Cervical Cancer (ICC), these findings emphasize the urgent need for robust secondary prevention strategies in this setting.

The relatively older average age of participants (mean age 48) and the finding that those with CIN2 + were significantly older (mean age 50) suggest that many women in this cohort are presenting for care late in the natural history of the disease. This aligns with the progression timeline from CIN to ICC, which typically spans a decade (World Health Organization, 2014). Our finding that age is a significant predictor of CIN2 + confirms that older women who have lacked access to regular screening throughout their reproductive years are at the highest risk. It has been established that advanced cervical lesions are more common in older women within Africa (Friebel-Klingner et al., 2021, Zewdie et al., 2023, Tekalign and Teshome, 2022). Furthermore, while the increased odds of CIN2 + among HIV-positive women in our study were not statistically significant, the 2-fold to 5-fold increase in odds warrants clinical attention, as HIV is a well-established driver of rapid cervical dysplasia progression (Mapanga et al., 2018, Sigfrid et al., 2017).

Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid and Lugol’s Iodine enhanced by Digital Cervicography (VIA-VILI-DC) remains a cornerstone of screening in Cameroon (Manga et al., 2015, Manga et al., 2020). Our model showed that women with suspicious ICC results had three times the odds of having CIN2 + compared to those with negative results. However, the uncertain VIA-VILI-DC results which were associated with 61% lower odds of CIN2 + demonstrate the subjective nature of visual screening (Cremer et al., 2011). Uncertain results represents those that where difficult to interpret and the nurses could not arrive at a conclusion such as atypical acetowhite appearing lesions which could be related to cervicitis. This variability underscores the importance of the digitalize component of DC, which allows for peer review of images and improved diagnostic accuracy in rural clinics compared to simple VIA-VILI (Manga et al., 2015). We therefore strongly recommend that all VIA/VILI programs should include the DC component. Though WHO has recommended Human Papillomavirus (HPV) screening as primary cervical cancer screening test in all settings, (WHO 2021) the visual screening method will remain a cornerstone in low resource settings like Cameroon in the foreseeable future due to logistic barriers and cost associated in running large HPV testing programs.

A critical finding of this study is the fair level of agreement (kappa = 0.356) between nurse-led presumptive diagnoses and final pathologic diagnosis as well as the level of agreement for ICC (kappa = 0.610). Nurses correctly identified ICC in 75.3% of cases they suspected were cancerous. This suggests that nurse-led visual triage is highly effective at identifying advanced disease that requires immediate referral or palliative care. This signifies that with adequate training, nurses can play a major frontline role in cervical cancer screening and prevention (Manga et al., 2015). However, there was a lower level of agreement for CIN2 (kappa = 0.180). This is particularly concerning because CIN2 is the clinical threshold for treatment. Surprisingly, the diagnosis of CIN2 is also a major challenge among pathologists. In an inter-rater and intra-rater evaluation of cervical tissues among seven pathologists, there was persistent poor agreement for CIN1 and CIN2, mediocre agreement for CIN 3 and excellent agreement for ICC (Ismail et al., 1990) and this is similar to what we observed with our nurses. In the LEEP/LLETZ-eligible group, 8.3% of women actually had ICC, while 8.8% had negative pathology. These results indicate that while nurses are excellent at identifying gross malignancy, distinguishing between grades of dysplasia (CIN1 vs. CIN2 vs. CIN3) remains a major challenge which is also similar with what the pathologists experience. This highlights the necessity of histopathological and clinico-pathological correlation whenever possible to prevent both under-treatment and over-treatment.

The high rate of ICC (62%) in the presumptive pathologic results reflects the mortality-to-incidence ratio in this population. The proportion of women deemed “LEEP/LLETZ-eligible” who had ICC (8.3%) demonstrates that visual triage alone may underestimate the severity of the disease, especially in very early-stage ICC. This supports the argument for excisional treatment over ablative treatment in this population because of the availability of tissue for additional pathological diagnosis (Bateman et al., 2015).

Strengths of this study include that it is a large-scale analysis (n = 1,294) that directly compares clinical suspicion with pathological reality, providing a clear metric for the reliability of nurse-led screening in Cameroon. In addition, some key variables like HIV status were self-reported and participants could have reported inappropriate status creating a potential bias towards the null. Limitations include potential referral bias given high prevalence of disease in this cohort as these women self-referred themselves for screening. Consequently, these results may not represent the general asymptomatic population. A prospective study using unselected women from a population-based sample to evaluate the same phenomenon is highly recommended especially when it is combined with HPV genotyping.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study confirms a high burden of CIN2 + and ICC among Cameroonian women with abnormal cervical cancer visual screening report. In our cohort, age and VIA-VILI-DC results were strong clinical predictors for CIN2 + . While nurse-led triage shows good reliability in identifying ICC, the fair-to-poor agreement in grading precancerous lesions (CIN2/3) reinforces the need for integrated clinico-pathological correlation. To eliminate cervical cancer in Cameroon by 2030 as outlined by WHO, screening programs must bridge the gap between visual suspicion and pathological certainty, ensuring that every woman receives the appropriate level of clinical intervention.
