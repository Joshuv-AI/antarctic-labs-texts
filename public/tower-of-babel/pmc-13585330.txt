Reverse Total Shoulder Arthroplasty After Proximal Humerus Fracture Using a Novel FiberTape Cerclage Tuberosity Fixation Technique
Authors: Thomas Hays, Christian Zapf, Nicholas Andriani, Mason Vialonga, Ian Hong, Asia Xaysana, Brendan Page, Richard Yoon, Bryan Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An 82-year-old woman presenting with an acute, 4-part proximal humerus fracture-dislocation after a ground-level fall was treated with reverse total shoulder arthroplasty using a novel FiberTape cerclage tuberosity fixation technique. Surgery was done within 24 hours of injury using a deltopectoral approach. The reconstruction technique incorporated a standard metaphyseal press-fit stem, autologous bone graft, and a modified Six-Tec–based suture tuberosity repair combined with a tension-band construct derived from a prophylactic humeral shaft FiberTape cerclage (Arthrex) anchored distal to fracture. The patient progressed from shoulder pendulums and active-assisted forward elevation in the scapular plane immediately postoperative to active range of motion at 6 weeks and removal of all restriction at 12 weeks. Simple Shoulder Test and QuickDASH scores were 10 and 9.09, respectively, at 96 weeks postoperatively with the patient reporting the ability to touch the back of her head and reach her buttock for self-hygiene. Active forward elevation was 140° at 1 year and 140° at 2.4 years with 30 degrees of external rotation. Tuberosity union was achieved by 3 months with minimal displacement. The patient did not experience any postoperative complications such as dislocation, implant failure, periprosthetic fracture, infection, humeral stem lucency, heterotopic ossification, or revision surgery.

Proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) account for 5.7% of all fractures and are the third most common fracture in patients older than 65 years, with a prevalence expected to triple by 2030.1-4 Surgical management is reported in 20% to 33% of cases.1,3,5

Historically, surgical management consisted of open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) or hemiarthroplasty (HA). HA outcomes are highly dependent on anatomic tuberosity healing, which is inconsistent, and long-term results are often limited by instability and reduced implant longevity.6,7 ORIF in the elderly carries high complication rates, including osteonecrosis, nonunion, and varus collapse.8,9 Consequently, reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) has gained favor for PHFs, offering superior range of motion (ROM), pain relief, and functional outcome scores in the elderly.5,10,11

Since its US introduction in 2004 for irreparable rotator cuff tears, RSA indications have expanded to include PHF-dislocations.5 Compared with ORIF, RSA avoids screw penetration risk, is less dependent on bone quality, and allows delayed surgery without compromising results.5 Although not as critical as in HA, anatomic greater tuberosity (GT) healing in RSA has consistently demonstrated improved patient-reported outcomes (PROMS), implant stability, and infection rates.12-15 Debate continues over the preferred technique for RSA when indicated for fracture.

This is a case of an acute PHF-dislocation managed with RSA using a novel FiberTape (Arthrex, Naples, FL) cerclage tuberosity fixation technique designed to enhance union, maintain reduction, minimize complications, and achieve durable functional results.

Case Report

Preoperative

An 82-year-old right-hand–dominant female patient presented with a closed, 4-part left PHF-dislocation after a ground-level fall (Figure 1, A and B). She was neurovascularly intact, reduced under sedation, and placed into a sling. Medical history included atrial fibrillation on Eliquis and hypothyroidism. She denied any history of smoking, diabetes, or vascular disease. An active, independent community ambulator, she engaged in low-impact activities. Given her age, fracture pattern, and activity level, RSA with novel tuberosity fixation was selected after informed consent.

Surgical Technique

The patient underwent general anesthesia and was placed in a semibeach chair position. A deltopectoral approach was done. The long head of the biceps tendon underwent tenodesis to the superior portion of the pectoralis major tendon. #2 FiberWire sutures (Arthrex) were placed into the subscapularis tendon and the posterior rotator cuff to control the tuberosities. The humeral head was excised. The Arthrex reverse total shoulder arthroplasty system was used. A +2 lateralized baseplate and +4 glenosphere were implanted per standard technique.

The humeral shaft was delivered, and a FiberTape cerclage (Arthrex) was placed around the shaft just distal to the fracture site (Figure 2) to prevent fracture propagation during reaming and broaching. The humeral metaphysis and shaft were then sequentially reamed/broached until rotational control was achieved with the implant at the appropriate height for the tuberosities to be slightly less than 6 cm above the pectoralis major tendon.16 The trial stem was removed, and a standard metaphyseal stem implant was press-fit with addition of autologous bone graft from the humeral head.

Photograph showing a FiberTape cerclage (Arthrex) is secured around the humeral shaft distal to the fracture site. The free tails ends of the cerclage are later used as a tension-band construct to unite the greater and lesser tuberosities to the humeral shaft. #2 FiberWire suture (Arthrex) connected to a hemostat can be seen tagging the subscapularis to control the lesser tuberosity.

Four looped #5 sutures (NiceLoop; Tornier, Memphis, TN) were passed through the posterior cuff at the bone-tendon junction of the greater tuberosity (two superior, two inferior; Figures 3 and 4) and wrapped around the metaphysis distal to the humeral tray implant. After reduction, humeral head bone graft was packed around the humeral tray and metaphysis, and the GT was then anatomically aligned and secured with two racking hitch Nice knots using one of the superior and one of the inferior #5 sutures. The remaining two posterior cuff sutures were shuttled through the subscapularis at the bone-tendon interface (Figure 5). The lesser tuberosity was reduced and cinched down in the anatomic position over bone graft using two more Nice knots that wrapped around the GT and lesser tuberosity. The initial anterior and posterior cuff tagging sutures were tied together for reinforcement. The remaining FiberTape cerclage suture tails were passed through the superior posterior cuff and subscapularis to form a tension band over the tuberosity repair (Figures 6 and 7) and were tied down with the arm in 90° abduction.

After copious irrigation, 1g vancomycin powder was placed in the wound, which was then closed with vicryl and monocryl followed by benzoin/steri-strips, and Aquacel dressing.

Postoperative

The patient was placed in an abduction sling for 6 weeks with immediate gentle hand/elbow ROM, shoulder pendulums, and active-assisted forward elevation (FE) within the scapular plane. Physical therapy began at 2 weeks.

At 6 weeks, she progressed from active-assisted to active ROM in all planes, achieving 80° of FE with a healed incision, intact neurovascular status, and tuberosities in the stable position. At 12 weeks, she advanced to full–weight bearing with strengthening exercises. Active FE was 90°, and she could touch the back of her head. Radiographs demonstrated tuberosity union (Figures 8 and 9) with minimal displacement. By 6 months, active FE was 130° and internal rotation was to the lumbar spine. The patient also reported being able to wipe her buttock for self-hygiene. At 1 year, active FE reached 140°, and at 2.4 years (125 weeks), active FE was 140° with 30° external rotation (ER). No complications occurred, including infection, dislocation, heterotopic ossification, radiographic stem lucency, periprosthetic fracture, or revision surgery.

At 96 weeks, PROMS showed a Simple Shoulder Test score of 10 of 12 and a Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) score of 9.09 (0 = asymptomatic, 100 = complete disability).

Discussion

This RSA technique for PHF combines a standard metaphyseal press-fit stem, autologous bone grafting, and a Six-Tec–based tuberosity repair that has been modified by substituting drill holes distal to the fracture site for a FiberTape cerclage to act as the anchor for the tension-band construct that reinforces the repair.

Cementation has been associated with stress shielding as evidenced by bone resorption and radiographic stem lucency.17,18 Lopiz et al17 identified stress-shielding in 30% of cemented versus 0% of noncemented stems and a higher anatomic tuberosity healing rate with noncemented stems. Press-fit stems allow for biologic fixation including tuberosity healing, as well as shorter surgical times, and lower risk of complications.5,19

The presented tension-band construct parallels the technique described by Boileau13 and the “turned stem tension band” of Takayama et al,20 which achieved a 100% tuberosity union rate. Unlike methods that anchor the construct through distal drill holes—creating potential stress-risers—this approach uses a FiberTape cerclage (Arthrex) around the humeral shaft to minimize fracture propagation and uses the tails to form the tension band. Given that PHFs are fragility fractures, this modification provides added protection for compromised bone.1 The tuberosity suture repair further incorporates the Nice knot, reported as the second strongest and stiffest mechanical suture configuration.21

Anatomic tuberosity union is consistently linked to superior functional and radiographic outcomes. Ohl et al15 found that anatomic GT healing after RSA for PHF produced higher functional outcome scores, greater FE, and improved stability compared with GT resorption/malunion/nonunion or excision. Boileau et al13 reported lower Simple Shoulder Value scores (65% vs. 83%) and active FE (115° vs. 141°) with nonunion. Sabah et al14 showed anatomic GT healing yielded markedly better Constant and Simple Shoulder Value scores, active FE (128° vs. 107°), and active ER (23° vs. 14°) with low dislocation and revision rates. Augmentation with autologous bone graft improves tuberosity union rates—78% with grafting versus 40% without,22 with an 84% union rate reported at more than 2 years in another series.13 Accordingly, this technique prioritized tuberosity union and achieved union by 3 months.

Our fixation method minimized tuberosity displacement. Platzer et al23 found increased GT displacement worsened PROMS, with 0 to 5 mm progressive displacement lowering Constant, Vienna, and UCLA Shoulder scores. Bono et al24 documented higher biomechanical demand required for shoulder abduction after GT osteotomy and Unnithan et al25 determined less than 5 mm GT displacement after fixation yielded markedly better Oxford, UCLA Shoulder, and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) scores.

At 1 year, our patient achieved 140° of active FE. Various RSA techniques for PHFs report active FE ranging from 97° to 141°.12-14,26-29 Our results were similar to Gallinet et al12 with 120° ± 30° of active FE reported in PHFs at 5 years treated with RSA and anatomic tuberosity repair and Grubhoffer et al29 with 118° of active FE at 22 months after fracture stem RSA with/without cement fixation and tuberosity suture repair. Our technique restored functional ROM required for key activities of daily living such as toileting and hair care, supporting sustained independence in elderly patients.

Functional outcome scores were also comparable with the literature. Bonnevialle et al30 reported a QuickDASH score of 28 ± 14 after noncemented RSA with tuberosity repair and Barbosa et al27 reported 6.8 after cemented RSA with repair. Our patient's QuickDASH score of 9.09 suggests similar favorable postoperative function.

Conclusion

This patient with PHF achieved favorable outcomes from a novel approach to RSA that incorporates a standard metaphyseal press-fit stem, autologous bone graft, and a modified Six-Tec–based tuberosity suture repair combined with a tension-band construct that unites the tuberosities to the shaft using a prophylactic FiberTape cerclage (Arthrex) anchored around the humeral shaft to prevent fracture propagation. This technique demonstrated promising results with tuberosity union and sustained reduction, low complications, functional ROM, and excellent patient-reported outcome scores beyond 1 year.
