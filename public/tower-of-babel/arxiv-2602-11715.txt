DICE : Diffusion Large Language Models Excel at Generating CUDA Kernels
Haolei Bai, Lingcheng Kong, Xueyi Chen, Jianmian Wang, Zhiqiang Tao, Huan Wang

Abstract
Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) have emerged as a compelling alternative to autoregressive (AR) LLMs, owing to their capacity for parallel token generation. This paradigm is particularly well-suited for code generation, where holistic structural planning and non-sequential refinement are critical. Despite this potential, tailoring dLLMs for CUDA kernel generation remains challenging, obstructed not only by the high specialization but also by the severe lack of high-quality training data. To address these challenges, we construct CuKe , an augmented supervised fine-tuning dataset optimized for high-performance CU DA ke rnels. On top of it, we propose a bi -phase c urated r einforcement l earning ( BiC-RL ) framework consisting of a CUDA kernel infilling stage and an end-to-end CUDA kernel generation stage. Leveraging this training framework, we introduce DICE , a series of di ffusion large language models designed for C UDA k e rnel generation, spanning three parameter scales, 1.7B, 4B, and 8B. Extensive experiments on KernelBench demonstrate that DICE significantly outperforms both autoregressive and diffusion LLMs of comparable scale, establishing a new state-of-the-art for CUDA kernel generation.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have shown powerful ability in a range of language tasks, and the dominant paradigm for LLMs is based on autoregressive modeling
(
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Grattafiori et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Comanici et al. 2025
)
for a long time. Recently, diffusion large language models (dLLMs)
(
Nie et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2025b
;
Peng et al. 2025
)
have emerged as a compelling alternative, achieving comparable or even better performance than similarly sized AR LLMs and faster inference speed. Different from the token-by-token generation mechanism introduced by AR LLMs, dLLMs mask the generated sequence and then refine the sequence through iterative denoising steps, which can account for global information and provide remarkably fast inference speed
(
DeepMind 2025
;
Labs et al. 2025
;
Song et al. 2025
)
.

Although dLLMs are rarely designed explicitly for code generation, their native bidirectional attention and iterative refinement paradigm align well with the inherently non-sequential nature of code generation
(
Gong et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025d
;
Ni et al. 2025b
)
, which often demands repeated back-and-forth refinement
(
Xie et al. 2025a
)
.
Commercial dLLMs, such as Gemini Diffusion
(
DeepMind 2025
)
, Mercury Coder
(
Labs et al. 2025
)
, and Seed Diffusion
(
Song et al. 2025
)
, have demonstrated that diffusion-based code models not only match AR LLMs in accuracy across a wide range of standard code evaluation benchmarks, but also deliver substantially faster inference speed.

Despite the remarkable abilities shown by open-source dLLMs designed for code generation
(
Gong et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2025b
;
Peng et al. 2025
)
, the specialized and computationally intensive task of
kernel generation
remains underexplored in the dLLM field, in stark contrast to the fruitful explorations on AR LLMs
(
Ouyang et al. 2025
;
Baronio et al. 2025
;
Fisches et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025c
)
. Recent studies
(
Ni et al. 2025a
;
Prabhudesai et al. 2025
)
demonstrate the superior data efficiency of dLLMs, especially under data-bound conditions. Given the scarcity of high-quality kernel data
(
Kong et al. 2025
)
, dLLMs are uniquely positioned to leverage their architectural advantages for this task. Aiming to bridge the gap, this paper introduces
DICE
, a series of dLLMs tailored for CUDA kernel generation, spanning parameter sizes of 1.7B, 4B, and 8B. Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

•

Preliminary

2.1
Autoregressive Language Models

Autoregressive LLMs estimate the probability of a sequence
\mathbf{x}=[x^{1},x^{2},\dots,x^{L}]
by factorizing the joint distribution into conditional probabilities:

p(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}p(x^{\ell}\mid x^{<\ell};\theta),

(1)

where
\theta
denotes the model parameters and
x^{<\ell}
represents the prefix of preceding tokens. During inference, the model predicts
x^{\ell}
conditioned on the previous context in a strictly left-to-right manner. The inherent sequential dependency introduces significant inference latency, particularly for long sequences. Furthermore, this rigid generation process diverges from iterative human programming workflows
(
Li et al. 2025a
)
, which often involve non-linear refinement. These limitations necessitate the exploration of alternative generation paradigms that align more effectively with the generation requirements of kernel programming.

Figure 2
:
The inference paradigm of diffusion large language models.
Left Part
: The sequence is divided into several blocks, where the block length equals four in this figure. The block diffusion mechanism enables models to generate autoregressively between blocks, while parallel discrete decoding within blocks. All the KV cache from previous blocks will be reused.

Right Part
: An actual step-by-step generation trajectory for an example CUDA kernel. While the overall trend remains autoregressive, we can clearly observe lots of non-autoregressive behavior during the generation process.

2.2
Block Diffusion Language Models

The block diffusion mechanism, also named semi-autoregressive diffusion decoding, has been widely adopted by several works
(
Arriola et al. 2025
;
Fathi et al. 2025
;
Han et al. 2023
)
, as it enables variable-length generation and the KV cache mechanism. This hybrid approach decomposes a sequence
\mathbf{x}
into
B
contiguous, non-overlapping blocks, denoted as
[x^{1},x^{2},\dots,x^{B}]
, where each block contains
L^{\prime}
tokens. The model follows an intra-block autoregressive while maintaining inter-block non-autoregressivity
(
Cheng et al. 2025
)
. Consequently, the training objective is formulated as a weighted cross-entropy loss over these blocks:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}},b\sim U[1,B],t}

(2)

\displaystyle\biggl[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}[{x}_{t}^{b,\ell}=\text{[M]}]\log p_{\theta}({x}_{0}^{b,\ell}\mid{x}_{t}^{b},x^{<b})\biggr],

where
L^{\prime}
is the block size,
[M]
denotes the special
[MASK]
token,
{x}_{t}^{b}
represents the corrupted sequence for block
b
, and
{x}_{0}^{b,\ell}
signifies the
\ell
-th original token within that block. Thus, the model is trained to reconstruct the original block
x^{b}
from its noised version
{x}_{t}^{b}
, conditioned on the preceding uncorrupted blocks
x^{<b}
.

An illustration of the dLLM generation paradigm is shown in Figure
2
. The left part shows the decoding mechanism, which hybridizes inter-block autoregression with intra-block diffusion to facilitate multi-token parallel decoding. The right part displays an actual generation order of a CUDA kernel, colored according to the temporal generation sequence. Notably, the presence of non-contiguous color blocks throughout the code suggests a non-autoregressive generation process. This observation indicates that kernel generation transcends strict left-to-right sequential dependencies, instead exhibiting significant non-autoregressive properties that align with human programming practices.

3
CuKe Dataset Construction

To address the scarcity of high-performance data in the field of CUDA kernel generation, we introduce CuKe, an augmented dataset that is substantially improved on top of the ConCuR dataset
(
Kong et al. 2025
)
. We enhance the original dataset from the following two dimensions:

High-Performance Sample.

While the SFT dataset of cudaLLM
2
provides numerous PyTorch-CUDA pairs, we notice that only 86% exhibit a speedup over their PyTorch equivalents. Furthermore, speedups near
1.0\times
are often statistically unreliable in GPU environments, as they can be confounded by pseudo-speedup factors such as kernel launch overhead, cache states, or measurement jitter. To ensure that our model learns CUDA kernels with the actual speedup effect, we apply a strict
2.0\times
speedup threshold as a robust filter. This filtration process guarantees that every data pair represents a substantial and verifiable performance improvement over standard PyTorch implementations. The application of this acceleration threshold yields a refined set of 1,425 high-quality PyTorch-CUDA pairs, ensuring that the model is aligned with truly optimized kernels rather than marginal or statistically noisy improvements.

Structural Diversity.

Beyond isolated operations, we argue that kernel generation should target the core components of AI systems. To this end, we incorporate complex structures from mainstream LLMs, such as Attention submodules and MLP blocks. To enhance the adaptability of models, we vary the input dimensions for these structures, as the underlying CUDA optimization logic often shifts significantly with tensor shapes. We curate 291 such structural samples and utilize Mercury Coder
(
Labs et al. 2025
)
to generate optimized CUDA kernels. Each candidate is executed up to five times, and only those demonstrating a confirmed speedup are retained. This validation yields 36 functionally verified instances that achieved demonstrable acceleration. Combining the samples from these two diverse dimensions results in a final, high-quality training set of 6,303 samples.

4.1.2
BiC-RL

To further extend the capabilities of DICE in CUDA kernel generation and mitigate the deceptive behavior, we propose a bi-phase curated reinforcement learning framework built upon TraceRL
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
. This framework operates in two dimensions. In the data dimension, we leverage Mercury Coder
(
Labs et al. 2025
)
to categorize the training data into hierarchical difficulty levels, progressing from fundamental operations to complex model structures. In the training dimension, the reinforcement learning process is split into two sequential stages: an initial CUDA kernel infilling stage, followed by an end-to-end CUDA kernel generation stage. This progressive paradigm ensures that the model masters the core implementation of the kernel before transitioning to end-to-end kernel generation.

Kernel In-filling Stage.

Recent works
(
Woo et al. 2025
)
and community blog
1
1

1

https://scalingintelligence.stanford.edu/blogs/kernelbenchv01/
have highlighted the presence of hacking shortcuts in generated kernels. We characterize this phenomenon as
deceptive behavior
, a prevalent issue in generated kernels that remains nontrivial for automated evaluation scripts to detect. Specifically, we identify three primary types: ① the model adheres to prompt-provided example kernel while defaulting to high-level PyTorch functions for the forward pass, effectively bypassing the kernel; ② the model generates a valid custom CUDA kernel but fails to implement the necessary invocation logic; and ③ the custom kernel is omitted from the forward function, precluding its execution during compilation.

To address these challenges, we decompose the optimized CUDA kernel into a tripartite structure: the prefix, the core implementation, and the suffix. As illustrated in Figure
3
, the prefix establishes the execution environment and global dependencies, primarily consisting of library imports (e.g.,
torch
,
cpp_extension
) that define the necessary namespace. The suffix acts as the structural wrapper; it encompasses the low-level CUDA source strings, the
load_inline
compilation logic, and the
nn.Module
class that integrates the kernel into a neural network architecture. And the core implementation part is the main logic of the CUDA kernel (
elementwise_add_source
function in this example) that the model needs to generate. By providing this scaffold, we ensure the model is grounded in the software stack and architectural constraints, allowing it to focus exclusively on the core logic while precluding the possibility of deceptive behavior.

Kernel Generation Stage.

Once the model has mastered the optimization of core logic for CUDA kernels during the infilling stage, we transition to a full-scale generation objective. In this subsequent phase, we train the model to perform end-to-end generation; given a high-level PyTorch reference, the model must generate the complete implementation of the corresponding CUDA kernel, including all necessary invocation logic. This progression ensures that the foundational knowledge acquired during infilling is successfully scaled to the complete CUDA kernel generation.

Evaluation Benchmark and Metrics.

We evaluate DICE and other models on KernelBench
(
Ouyang et al. 2025
)
, which is divided into three levels, covering a total of 250 tasks. Level 1 contains 100 single-kernel operators (e.g.,
Convolution
), Level 2 contains 100 fusion patterns (e.g.,
Matmul + Sigmoid
), and Level 3 includes 50 full model architectures (e.g.,
Mamba
). The model will be given a one-shot prompt, as presented in Appendix
A
, to guide it to generate a CUDA kernel based on the given PyTorch reference. The primary evaluation metrics are functional correctness, which assesses whether the generated kernels compile and execute equivalently to the PyTorch implementations, and
\text{fast}_{p}
, which measures the speedup performance over the PyTorch implementation, defined as follows:

\text{fast}_{p}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}(\text{correct}_{i}\land\{\text{SpeedUp}_{i}>p\}),

(3)

where
N
is the number of tasks in each level,
p
denotes the speedup factor, for example,
\text{fast}_{2}
indicates that the generated kernels are both correct and at least twice as fast as the PyTorch implementation.

Evaluation Details.

We evaluate all models using their default inference configurations, just varying the maximum output token length. Specifically, for AR LLMs, we set a maximum generation length of 32,768 for reasoning models and 4,096 for general and code models to fully utilize their capabilities. Regarding dLLMs, those subject to fixed-length generation sequence constraints (LLaDA, Dream, and DiffuCoder) are evaluated with a maximum of 1,024 output tokens. This threshold is empirically sufficient for complete CUDA kernel generation. Conversely, for dLLMs featuring adaptive stopping mechanisms, such as SDAR, TraDo, WeDLM, Stable-DiffCoder, and our DICE series, we set a maximum generation length of 4,096 tokens, as these models can terminate generation upon completion. More experimental details are provided in Appendix

6
Related Work

Diffusion Large Language Models.

The paradigm of large language models has been dominated by autoregressive
(
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2025
)
for a long time, but the emergence of diffusion large language models
(
Nie et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
DeepMind 2025
;
Labs et al. 2025
;
Ye et al. 2025
)
has broken this, offering a new choice for language models. Unlike autoregressive methods, which use a strict left-to-right, token-by-token decoding process, dLLMs first generate multiple masks, then gradually denoise the masked sequence during decoding, which allows for simultaneous sampling and decoding of multiple tokens. Moreover, dLLMs employ a bidirectional attention mechanism to more comprehensively understand the context. Advancing models, such as LLaDA
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
, an 8B-parameter diffusion language model trained from scratch, and Dream
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
, a 7B-parameter model initialized from pre-trained AR-based model weights. SDAR
(
Cheng et al. 2025
)
synergistically combines the advantages of diffusion and autoregressive. TraDo
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
proposes TraceRL, deriving the first long-CoT dLLM, which achieves strong performance over models of comparable parameter size. More recently, WeDLM
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
represents the first dLLM to surpass industrial autoregressive engines while maintaining high performance on reasoning tasks.

dLLMs for Code Generation.
Code generation is a crucial and relatively challenging task for dLLMs, while it is still under exploration. CodeFusion
(
Singh et al. 2023
)
is the first to propose a diffusion-based language model for code generation, but it is limited to small-scale models and simple tasks. Recently, DiffuCoder
(
Gong et al. 2025
)
demonstrates remarkable capabilities in code generation. Dream-Coder
(
Xie et al. 2025b
)
further improves the performance, and Stable-DiffCoder
(
Fan et al. 2026
)
achieves the current best performance. Open-dCoder
(
Peng et al. 2025
)
competes with much larger dLLMs, despite being only 0.5B parameters. Moreover, commercial dLLMs, like Gemini Diffusion
(
DeepMind 2025
)
, Mercury Coder
(
Labs et al. 2025
)
, and Seed Diffusion
(
Song et al. 2025
)
, have demonstrated that dLLMs can achieve performance comparable to leading autoregressive code models while offering significantly faster generation speeds. However, when it comes to more challenging domains such as kernel generation, the potential of dLLMs remains largely unexplored.

LLM Kernel Generation.
The advancement of LLMs has shifted the focus from general programming to hardware-specific task, kernel generation. Central to this progress is the development of specialized benchmarks, such as KernelBench
(
Ouyang et al. 2025
)
, MultiKernelBench
(
Wen et al. 2025
)
, TritonBench
(
Li et al. 2025b
)
, and FlashInfer-Bench
(
Xing et al. 2026
)
, which provide standardized environments for evaluating functional correctness and execution efficiency. Beyond evaluation, recent research emphasizes improving generation quality through sophisticated training paradigms and reasoning alignment. Specifically, Kevin
(
Baronio et al. 2025
)
, TritonRL
(
Woo et al. 2025
)
, and AutoTriton
(
Li et al. 2025c
)
employ reinforcement learning to navigate models to effectively generate high-performance GPU kernels. More recently, agentic systems such as Astra
(
Wei et al. 2025
)
, CudaForge
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
, and KernelEvolve
(
Liao et al. 2025
)
have introduced iterative refinement workflows to achieve near-expert performance. On the other hand,
Kong et al. 2025
notices the data scarcity issue of high-quality kernels and proposes ConCuR, an SFT dataset especially for CUDA kernel generation. Despite this progress toward kernel generation, how dLLMs can be effectively leveraged to address this challenging task remains an underexplored domain.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we curate the
CuKe
dataset to provide a robust foundation for supervised fine-tuning, mitigating the scarcity of high-quality training data in this domain. Then we introduce
BiC-RL
, which is specifically designed for CUDA kernel generation. Combining kernel infilling and end-to-end kernel generation stages can effectively improve training efficiency and enhance convergence. Our extensive experiments demonstrate that each training stage contributes significantly to stable convergence, and their combination yields substantial performance improvements. By integrating these components, we develop
DICE
, a family of diffusion large language models spanning three parameter scales, opening up a new perspective for this specialized area.

Impact Statement

The goal of this work is to advance the field of CUDA kernel generation. Specifically, we curate the
CuKe
dataset, a new reinforcement learning framework,
BiC-RL
, and a series of models called
DICE
, aiming to automate and CUDA kernel generation. By significantly lowering the technical barrier for high-performance computing (HPC) optimization, our research has the potential to accelerate the development of efficient AI systems and scientific computing tools. From a sustainability perspective, the improved kernel efficiency directly contributes to reduced energy consumption in large-scale data centers. We believe that by releasing our whole pipeline, we foster an open-source ecosystem that promotes the development of high-performance AI infrastructure.
