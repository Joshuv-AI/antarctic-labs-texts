Anton Björkman, Filip Elvander

Abstract
In this work, we consider the problem of multi-pitch estimation, i.e., identifying super-imposed truncated harmonic series from noisy measurements. We phrase this as recovering a harmonically-structured measure on the unit circle, where the structure is enforced using regularizers based on optimal transport theory. In the resulting framework, a signal’s spectral content is simultaneously inferred and assigned, or transported, to a small set of harmonic series defined by their corresponding fundamental frequencies. In contrast to existing methods from the compressed sensing paradigm, the proposed framework decouples regularization and dictionary design and mitigates coherency problems. As a direct consequence, this also introduces robustness to the phenomenon of inharmonicity. From this framework, we derive two estimation methods, one for stochastic and one for deterministic signals, and propose efficient numerical algorithms implementing them. In numerical studies on both synthetic and real data, the proposed methods are shown to achieve better estimation performance as compared to other methods from statistical signal processing literature. Furthermore, they perform comparably or better than network-based methods, except when the latter are specially trained on the data-type considered and are given access to considerably more data during inference.

Introduction

Fundamental frequency, or pitch, estimation is a common task in a variety of applications within signal processing, including speech processing
[
1
]
, music audio signal processing
[
2
,
3
]
, and biomedical modeling
[
4

. The task can be divided into two classes: single-pitch estimation, and multi-pitch estimation, with the latter being significantly more challenging, as well as less studied. Single-pitch estimation aims to identifying the fundamental frequency of one signal that consists of a single harmonic series, as seen in cases like a single instrument or for human speech with only one active speaker. In contrast, multi-pitch estimation arises naturally when there are multiple instruments playing or when there are several speakers. For the single-pitch problem, there are several methods available. Examples of these include YIN
[
5
]
, RAPT
[
6
]
and SWIPE
[
7
]
, where both YIN and RAPT are based on the autocorrelation of the signal. SWIPE instead attempts to find the best match for the fundamental frequency of a sawtooth waveform that best describes the spectrum of the input signal.
For the multi-pitch problem (for a general overview, see
[
8
]
), some recent methods are parametric methods based on dictionaries whose atoms correspond to harmonic series, such as PEBS
[
9
]
, and the signal components are identified by minimizing sparsity-promoting optimization criteria, often expressed as a regularized least-squares problem. An extension of PEBS was introduced in
[
10
]
as PEBS
2
TV, adding a total variation term to deal with subharmonic errors in the model. An additional method, PEBSI-lite, was introduced in
[
11
]
, improving upon PEBS
2
TV by better approximating one of the penalty terms as well as introducing self-regularization and an adaptive dictionary construction. Another method based on the same type of dictionary is PE-BSBL-Cluster (herein denoted PE-BSBL-C)
[
12
]
, which instead uses Bayesian learning with a block sparse prior, as well as intra-block clustering to deal with subharmonic errors. Here, the estimation is based on assumptions of the harmonic structure of the signal, such as an added penalty if the first harmonic is missing, or if nearby harmonics are missing. One of the drawbacks of these methods is that the dictionary atoms become highly coherent because of the overlap of the harmonics of different candidate pitches. Furthermore, these methods assume perfect harmonicity among each pitch, making them susceptible to errors in the face of inharmonicity, i.e., when individual sinusoids deviate from the harmonic model, as is the case, for example, with string instruments
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
and to some extent in human speech
[
16
]
.

Recently, pitch estimators based on deep learning have been proposed, showing promising results both for the single-pitch and multi-pitch problems. Estimators for the single-pitch problem include CREPE
[
17
]
, Harmof0
[
18
]
and SPICE
[
19
]
, while examples for the multi-pitch problem are DeepSalience
[
20
]
as well as three models from
[
21
]
, building upon the former. While these methods have proven effective for pitch estimation, they inherently impose certain limitations on the input. For example, if a model is trained on a specific type of data, such as speech, it may not be effectively detect pitches in other signal types like music. Moreover, parameters such as sampling rate, frame length, frequency range, and hop size are often enforced by the structure of the model itself, or required to match that of the training data. As a result, if one would want to change these parameters, the model would either need to be retrained, or a pre-processing step, such as re-sampling the signal, would need to be applied to the signal, as is commonly done for these estimators.

In similarity to the previously mentioned dictionary-based multi-pitch estimators, our proposed work uses a similar dictionary in order to form our estimates. However, in contrast to the other dictionary-based methods, we in this work propose to both alleviate the need of pitch-based dictionaries by formulating the multi-pitch estimation through a dictionary, but instead of having the atoms correspond to complete harmonic series, the atoms in our dictionaries correspond to Fourier basis functions, thus removing the harmonic overlap between atoms. In particular, the aim is to infer the spectral content of the observed signal, i.e., the power (or amplitudes) and frequencies of individual sinusoids, while simultaneously assigning these into a small number of groups, corresponding to pitches. This grouping is performed on the basis of the Monge-Kantorovich problem of optimal transport (OT)
[
22
]
. OT-based problems have previously been used for spectral estimation (see, e.g.,
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
), as well as in the context of pitch estimation
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, our previous work
[
31
]
was the first work to propose an OT-based estimator for solving the multi-pitch estimation as an
inverse
problem, where only noisy observations of the signal waveform are known, of which the work proposed herein is expanded upon.

In this paper, we aim to build upon our prior work in
[
31
]
. This work differs from our previous work in the following aspects: firstly, we define the problem in a more general form, which not only allows us to more precisely articulate the specific problem we aim to solve, but also allows us to return to it later: we first solve the pitch estimation problem, return to the general form, and then re-estimate the frequency structure of the signal. Secondly, we introduce a new estimation formulation, based on the auto-covariance of the measurements. Thirdly, we conduct more thorough experiments than in our previous work, here including real data as well as a Monte Carlo type study. We also provide a comparison with deep network-based methods, which was not included in our previous work, wherever applicable.

II-A

Prior art and motivation

In the perfectly harmonic case, i.e., when the relation (
4

\displaystyle\underset{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}{\mathrm{minimize}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{A}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),

represents samples from (perfectly harmonic) signals on the form (

. Although the method presented in

does not follow the structure of (

II-B

Stochastic model

Assume that the complex amplitudes
\alpha_{\ell}^{(k)}

\displaystyle r(\tau)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left(x_{t}\overline{x_{t-\tau}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{(k)}}\ \sigma_{\ell}^{2}e^{i\omega_{\ell}^{(k)}\tau},\;\tau\in\mathbb{Z}.

\displaystyle r(\tau)=\langle{\nu},e^{i(\cdot)\tau}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{i\omega\tau}d{\nu}(\omega),\;\tau\in\mathbb{Z}.

That is,

II-C

Deterministic model

Herein, we take the alternative view that the complex amplitudes
\alpha_{l}^{(k)}
are deterministic constants. Although we lose the concepts of covariance function and power spectrum, we can still associate the signal

with an element of
{\mathbb{C}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{T})

\displaystyle x_{t}=\langle{\mu},e^{i(\cdot)t}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{i\omega t}d{\mu}(\omega),\;t\in\mathbb{N},

by acting on the functions

, indexed by the time

\displaystyle{\mu}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{(k)}}\alpha_{\ell}^{(k)}\delta_{\omega_{\ell}^{(k)}},

\displaystyle{\mu}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mu}^{(k)},\text{ with }{\mu}^{(k)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{(k)}}\alpha_{\ell}^{(k)}\delta_{\omega_{\ell}^{(k)}}.

If we have access to

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}={\cal A}({\mu})

. It may here be noted that the problem of inverting the relation (

is discretized, this simply corresponds to

to a mass distribution. It may be noted that although
{\mu}\in{\mathbb{C}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{T})
, it is not an element of
{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})
. However, we may construct such an element by the variation
\left|{\mu}\right|\penalty\ \in\penalty\ {\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})
. Then, if the supports of the Dirac measures in (

\displaystyle\left|{\mu}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{(k)}}\left|\alpha_{\ell}^{(k)}\right|\delta_{\omega_{\ell}^{(k)}}.

If this is the case, then

\displaystyle\left|{\mu}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{K}|{\mu}^{(k)}|

III
Optimal transport

Consider two general, non-negative measures, or mass distributions,
{\mu}_{1},{\mu}_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(X)
defined on some space
X
, with same total mass, i.e.,
{\mu}_{1}(X)={\mu}_{2}(X)
. Then, the Monge-Kantorovich problem of OT considers the most cost-efficient way of transporting, or morphing,
{\mu}_{1}
as to be identical to
{\mu}_{2}
. The cost of moving one unit-mass from a support-point
\omega_{1}
of
{\mu}_{1}
to a corresponding support-point
\omega_{2}
of
{\mu}_{2}
is given by
c(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
, where
c:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is a so-called ground-cost function. For example, if
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, then
c
is typically picked as the squared Euclidean distance. The problem of minimizing the total cost of rearrangement is then stated as

\displaystyle\underset{m\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(X\times X)}{\mathrm{minimize}}

\displaystyle\left\{\langle m,c\rangle\triangleq\int_{X\times X}c(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})dm(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\right\}

(15)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}\times X}dm(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}d\mu_{1}(\omega),

\displaystyle\int_{X\times\mathcal{E}_{2}}dm(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}d\mu_{2}(\omega),

\displaystyle\text{for all sets }\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\subset X.

Here, the transport plan
m
describes how mass is moved from
{\mu}_{1}
to
{\mu}_{2}
, with the constraints ensuring that
m
indeed describes transport between the two distributions. This can be phrased as
m
having margins
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, and we use the short-hand
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(m)={\mu}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(m)={\mu}_{2}
, where the projection operators
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are uniquely defined by the constraints in (
15
).

The minimal objective in (
15
) serves as a notion of distance or dissimilarity on
{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(X)
, which has been exploited in problem machine learning and signal processing (see
[
42
]
for a review). Most closely related to the multi-pitch estimation problem considered in this work, OT has been earlier used for solving inverse problems of spectral estimation
[
25
,
25
]
. In contrast to the problem (
15
), we are in this work interested in the setting where we can only observe one of the marginals indirectly according to (
8
) or (
11
) and where the
second margin is free or unconstrained
in the sense that it is not related to observations or measurements. Instead, we will extend the transport problem (
15
) as to allow us to ultimately recover decompositions on the form (
9
) or (
13
).

IV
Optimal transport for harmonic spectra

Consider any
{\mu}\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})
. Building on
[
30
]
, we may quantify how close
{\mu}
is to being the power spectrum of a harmonic signal with a certain fundamental frequency
\omega_{0}^{\prime}
by means of an OT problem. In particular, let
c:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
be a ground-cost function, where
c(\omega,\omega_{0})
is the cost of associating the frequency
\omega
with a fundamental frequency
\omega_{0}
. Then, we may formally solve

\displaystyle\underset{m\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}{\mathrm{minimize}}\quad\langle m,c\rangle,\text{ s.t. }{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(m)={\mu},\;{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(m)=\mu(\mathbb{T})\delta_{\omega_{0}^{\prime}}.

(16)

Note here that the first margin of the transport plan
m
co-incides with
{\mu}
, whereas the second margin has all its mass located at the single point
\omega_{0}^{\prime}
. Then, if
m^{\star}
is a solution to (
16
),
S_{\mathrm{h}}^{c}(\omega_{0}^{\prime},{\mu})\triangleq\langle m^{\star},c\rangle
serves as a notion of distance between
{\mu}
and (any) harmonic spectrum with fundamental frequency
\omega_{0}^{\prime}
. It may be noted that this minimal objective can be computed in closed form. In particular,

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{h}}^{c}(\omega_{0}^{\prime},{\mu})=\langle m^{\star},c\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}c(\omega,\omega_{0}^{\prime})d{\mu}(\omega).

It should here be stressed that
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(m^{\star})
is not the spectrum of a harmonic signal. However, such a spectrum can be recovered from the pair
(m^{\star},c)
, or from
({\mu},c)
, and depends on the specific construction of the ground-cost
c
. In particular, in
[
30
]
, the squared distance to the closest harmonic was used, i.e.,
c=\hat{c}
, where

\displaystyle\hat{c}(\omega,\omega_{0})=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}(\omega-k\omega_{0})^{2},

(17)

where
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is the set of positive integers. The implied harmonic spectrum is then given by

\displaystyle{\mu}_{\text{h}}=\sum_{k}\sigma^{2}_{k}\delta_{k\omega_{0}},\text{ with }\sigma^{2}_{k}=\int_{(k-1/2)\omega_{0}}^{(k+1/2)\omega_{0}}d{\mu}(\omega).

It may here be noted that if
{\mu}
contains point-masses on the interval edges, then
{\mu}_{\text{h}}
is not unique. The same ground-cost was used in
[
27
]
in the context of music transcription. Although the choice
\hat{c}
is intuitively reasonable, it leads to a strong preference for low fundamental frequencies
\omega_{0}
if one is to use
S_{\mathrm{h}}^{\hat{c}}
to choose between different
\omega_{0}
fitting
{\mu}
. In particular, it may be readily verified that for any
{\mu}\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})
and
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
,

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{h}}^{\hat{c}}(\omega_{0}/2,{\mu})\leq S_{\mathrm{h}}^{\hat{c}}(\omega_{0},{\mu}),

which is a direct consequence of
\hat{c}(\omega_{0}/2,\omega)\leq\hat{c}(\omega_{0},\omega)
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{T}
. This inevitably leads to so-called sub-octave errors and is typically countered using heuristic rules for limiting the maximal harmonic order or imposing additional structure on the implied spectrum
{\mu}_{\text{h}}
. In this work, we propose to remedy this by a simple normalization and define the ground-cost as

\displaystyle c(\omega,\omega_{0})=\frac{1}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\hat{c}(\omega,\omega_{0})=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}-k\right)^{2}.

(18)

Note that, just as for
\hat{c}
,
c
induces transport plans
m
where mass is transported to the closest harmonic of a given
\omega_{0}
. Thus,
c
and
\hat{c}
imply the same harmonic spectrum
{\mu}_{\text{h}}
. However, the corresponding
S_{\mathrm{h}}^{{c}}
shows preference for
\omega_{0}
over
\omega_{0}/2
when applied to inharmonic signals. To see this, consider an inharmonic component located at
\omega_{k}=k\omega_{0}+\Delta
for some
k
, where
\Delta
, with
\left|\Delta\right|<\omega_{0}/2
, is the inharmonic deviation. Then, we have that
c(\omega_{k},\omega_{0})<c(\omega_{k},\omega_{0}/2)
if and only if
\left|\Delta\right|<\omega_{0}/3
. That is, in order to prefer
\omega_{0}/2
over
\omega_{0}
, the inharmonic deviations have to be substantial. An illustration of this is presented in Figure
2
, where both functions are plotted for the pitch candidates
\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{0}/2
.

Thus, for the proposed ground-cost function, the optimal solution with small harmonic deviations is obtained by the model with the
highest possible fundamental frequency
that can, in close proximity, describe all harmonics.

V
Harmonic clustering

In this section, we derive the proposed multi-pitch estimators, one for the stochastic and one for the deterministic case. For clarity of exposition, we focus the discussion on the simpler stochastic setting and then show the necessary modifications for the deterministic one.

V-A

Ideal problem and convex relaxation

For the model in (
2
), assume that the number
K
of pitch components is known. Letting
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{0}=\{\omega_{0}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
be the set of (unknown) fundamental frequencies, we would like to solve

\displaystyle\underset{{\nu}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}),\;{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{0}}{\mathrm{minimize}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{r}-{\cal A}({\nu}))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{\mathrm{h}}^{c}(\omega_{0}^{(k)},{\nu}^{(k)}),

(19)

where
{\nu}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\nu}^{(k)}
and where the first data-fit term takes into account that
\mathbf{r}
will in practice be a noisy estimate of the finite-length covariance sequence. Note that each term of the regularizing function quantifies how well a component measure, or spectrum,
{\nu}^{(k)}
fits a harmonic spectrum with fundamental frequency
\omega_{0}^{(k)}
. It may be verified that although this problem is convex in the set of
{\nu}^{(k)}
, it is not jointly convex in
(\{{\nu}^{(k)}\}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{0})
. In fact, it is not even marginally convex in
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{0}
due to the structure of the ground-cost
c
in (
18
). Although, one could envision addressing this problem with the iterative tools from
[
43
]
, this would require a good initial point for
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{0}
. To work around this, consider the case when
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{0}
is fixed and (
19
) is minimized with respect to the
{\nu}^{(k)}
. This can equivalently be formulated as

\displaystyle\underset{m\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}\times\Omega)}{\mathrm{minimize}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{r}-{\cal A}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}(m))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\langle m,c\rangle

(20)

where the transport plan
m\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}\times\Omega)
describes transport between
\mathbb{T}
and the discrete grid
\Omega=\{\omega_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\omega_{0}^{(K)}\}
. This is a direct extension of the single-pitch problem in (
16
); if
\Omega
is a singleton set then the problems are the same. It may be noted that the individual
{\nu}^{(k)}
can be retrieved from
m
as

\displaystyle{\nu}^{(k)}={\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)

where the projection
{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}:{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}\times\Omega)\to{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})
is defined by the relation

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)(\mathcal{E})=m(\mathcal{E}\times\{\omega_{0}^{(k)}\}),\;\forall\mathcal{E}\subset\mathbb{T}.

Intuitively, the projected, or
restricted
, measure
{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)
can be thought of as fixing the second argument of the transport plan
m
to
\omega_{0}^{(k)}
.

With these constructions, we may extend
\Omega
to a large grid
\Omega_{G}
consisting of
G\gg K
fundamental frequencies. We would want the support over
\Omega_{G}
to be sparse, i.e.,
m^{(g)}(\mathbb{T})=0
for most
g\in[\![G]\!]
, an illustrative example of which can be seen in Figure
3
. As to promote transport plans
m
that are sparse in this sense, while avoiding the combinatorial problem arising for counting the number of non-zero
{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)
, we propose to use the convex proxy

\displaystyle\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{\infty,1}=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\left\lVert{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)\right\rVert_{\infty},

where we with some slight abuse of notation over-load the mixed-norm notation often used for matrices. For elements
{\nu}\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}
containing only point-masses, we by
\left\lVert{\nu}\right\rVert_{\infty}
mean

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\nu}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{\omega\in\mathbb{T}}\>{\nu}(\omega),

corresponding to the coefficient of the largest point-mass. It may be noted that this can be directly extended to the case when
{\nu}
also contains a component with a density.

With this, we are ready to state our proposed multi-pitch estimator for the stochastic setting. We define it as the solution to the optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{m\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}\times\Omega_{G})}{\mathrm{minimize}}\>\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\!-\!{\cal A}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}(m))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\zeta\!\left(\langle m,c\rangle+\eta\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{\infty,1}\right),

(21)

where
\zeta
and
\eta
are positive regularization parameters controlling the trade-off between data-fit and regularization, and sparsity of the transport plan. Note here that increasing
\eta
has the effect of promoting transport plans
m
concentrated to a small number of points of
\Omega_{G}
. Assume that an optimal
m^{\star}
have been obtained from solving (
21
). Then, by inspecting the restricted measures
{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)
we can identify the pitches present in the signal, and their corresponding fundamental frequencies
\omega_{0}^{(g)}
. Furthermore, we can then restrict the grid
\Omega_{G}
to contain only these active pitches and solve (
20
) as to obtain a debiased solution, countering the shrinkage towards zero caused by the
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty,1}
-term. When designing an algorithm for solving (
21
), we will work with the equivalent formulation

\displaystyle\underset{{\nu}\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})}{\mathrm{minimize}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{r}-{\cal A}({\nu})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\zeta\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{stoch}}({\nu}),

(22)

where
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{stoch}}({\nu})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{stoch}}({\nu})=\underset{m\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}\times\Omega_{G})}{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle\langle m,c\rangle+\eta\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{\infty,1}

(23)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{P}}_{1}(m)={\nu}.

When solving (
22
) in practice, we will discretize the operator
{\cal A}
. This highlights a key difference between the method proposed herein and other multi-pitch estimators: the measurement operator is independent of the set of pitch candidates, corresponding to the set
\Omega_{G}
. For methods such as
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, the operator
{\cal A}
is constructed based on the set of candidate pitches, causing coherence problems as mentioned earlier.

V-B

Modification for deterministic model

Recall that for the deterministic model, the object of interest is
{\mu}\in{\mathbb{C}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{T})
. In order to arrive at an estimator analog to the stochastic one, we have to extend the formulation (
22
) rather than (
21
). The reason for this is that the projection of the transport plan
m
in (
21
) necessarily is an element of
{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})
. However, the extending formulation (
22
) allows us to use the variation construction introduced earlier. Recall that if
{\mu}\in{\mathbb{C}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{T})
can be decomposed into as set of components
{\mu}^{(k)}
, then their corresponding variations
\left|{\mu}\right|\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})
and
|{\mu}^{(k)}|\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T})
satisfy the inequality (
14
), where equality holds if the supports of the components
{\mu}^{(k)}
do not overlap, or have the same phase on any overlaping subset of
\mathbb{T}
. Now, consider a transport plan
m\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}\times\Omega_{G})
. In analog to the interpretation of the restricted measure
{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)
as representing a component measure
{\nu}^{(g)}
in the stochastic case, we can in the deterministic case view
{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)
as representing the
variation

|{\mu}^{(k)}|
of a component
{\mu}^{(k)}\in{\mathbb{C}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{T})
. In view of this and the inequality (
14
), it becomes natural to replace the equality constraint in (
23
) with the inequality

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(m)\geq\left|{\mu}\right|,

(24)

which should be understood as a point-by-point inequality on
\mathbb{T}
. We can then define the regularizer
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{det}}:{\mathbb{C}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{det}}({\mu})=\underset{m\in{\mathcal{M}_{+}}(\mathbb{T}\times\Omega_{G})}{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle\langle m,c\rangle+\eta\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{\infty,1}

(25)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{P}}_{1}(m)\geq\left|{\mu}\right|.

It may be verified that
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{det}}
is a convex function on
{\mathbb{C}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{T})
. Note also that convexity is lost if the inequality is replaced by equality in the constraint. With this, we can formulate the multi-pitch estimator for the deterministic model as the solution to

\displaystyle\underset{{\mu}\in{\mathbb{C}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{T})}{\mathrm{minimize}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{y}}-{\cal A}({\mu})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\zeta\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{det}}({\mu}).

(26)

As in the stochastic case, this is a convex optimization problem. Furthermore, we may retrieve the frequency content of the component pitches by inspecting the restricted measures
{\mathcal{P}}^{(g)}(m)
. It should however be noted that we in general can only hope to retrieve
|{\mu}^{(k)}|
rather than
{\mu}^{(k)}
as we lose phase-information at points in
\mathbb{T}
where the inequality in (
24
) is strict. However, at points where equality holds, which for example is the case when the support of the components do not overlap, the phase can also be retrieved.

VI
Discretization and entropy regularization

Expanding upon the problems in (
22
) and (
26
), we here aim to discretize both problems, allowing for efficient implementations. To this end, we introduce the grid
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{F}\}\subset[0,\pi)
consisting of
F
frequencies, corresponding to a discretization of the frequency space,
\mathbb{T}
. Then, the problem in (
22
) can be written in vector form as

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{F}_{+}}{\mathrm{minimize}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{r}}-{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\nu}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\beta(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{F})+\zeta{\mathbf{S}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}),

(27)

where
{\mathbf{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{a}}(\omega_{1})&\dots&{\mathbf{a}}(\omega_{F})\end{bmatrix}
is a dictionary matrix which is a matrix whose columns
{\mathbf{a}}(\omega_{f})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
consists of Fourier vectors corresponding to frequencies
\omega_{f}
,
f\in[\![F]\!]
, over the sample time. The matrix multiplication
{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\nu}
here corresponds to the operation
{\cal A}({\nu})
in (
22
), where
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F}
is a vectorized version of
{\nu}
.

Additionally,
\mathbf{\hat{r}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{T}}
denotes the (partial) sample-auto-covariance vector, formed based on noisy measurements.
3
3

3

In practice when solving (
27
), we consider the equivalent purely-real problem where we stack the real- and imaginary parts according to
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{R}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{Real}(\hat{\mathbf{r}})&\mathfrak{Imag}(\hat{\mathbf{r}})\end{bmatrix}^{T}
and correspondingly for
{\mathbf{A}}
.
To combat the addition of noise, we propose an
\ell_{1}
-norm regularizer on
\boldsymbol{\nu}
. The
\ell_{1}
-term promotes sparsity in the frequency estimates, pushing the components driven only by noise down to
0
. Since
\boldsymbol{\nu}
contains only non-zero elements, the resulting
\ell_{1}
-norm term becomes
\beta(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{F})
. We also introduce a normalizing factor of
2/\mathcal{T}
for the first term. Lastly, the last regularizing term is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\min_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times G}}

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{C},\mathbf{M}\rangle+\epsilon D(\mathbf{M})+\eta\left\lVert\mathbf{M}\right\rVert_{\infty,1},

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{G}=\boldsymbol{\nu},

corresponding to a discretization of
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{stoch}}({\nu})
, where
\mathbf{M}
is the discretized transport plan,
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times G}
is the cost
matrix
, with
\mathbf{C}_{f,g}=c(\omega_{f},\omega_{0}^{(g)})
, and where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty,1}
now denotes the regular mixed norm, i.e.,
\left\lVert\mathbf{M}\right\rVert_{\infty,1}=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\max_{f\in[\![F]\!]}\left|[\mathbf{M}]_{f,g}\right|
. Furthermore,
\epsilon D(\mathbf{M})=\epsilon\sum_{f,g}[\mathbf{M}]_{f,g}\log[\mathbf{M}]_{f,g}-[\mathbf{M}]_{f,g}+1
, with
\epsilon>0
, is an entropic regularization term
[
44
]
which will allow us to derive efficient numerical solvers. It may be noted that the addition of
\epsilon D(\mathbf{M})
renders (
27
) strictly convex.

Similarly, we discretize the problem in (
26
), yielding

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}^{F}}{\mathrm{minimize}}\quad\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\beta\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\rVert_{1}+\zeta\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\mu}),

(28)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\triangleq\underset{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times G}}{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{C},\mathbf{M}\rangle+\epsilon D(\mathbf{M})+\eta\left\lVert\mathbf{M}\right\rVert_{\infty,1},

(29)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{G}\geq\left|\boldsymbol{\mu}\right|.

The matrix multiplication
{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mu}
here corresponds to the operation
{\cal A}({\mu})
in (
26
), where
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F}
is a vectorized version of
{\mu}
. As we again work with noisy measurements, the
\ell_{1}
-norm term is used here as well. However, as the elements of
{\mu}
are complex valued, the
\ell_{1}
-norm term is not simplified, as was the case for the stochastic problem. In the following section, we introduce efficient solvers for the estimator presented herein.

VII
Numerical algorithms

In order to compute the solutions to (
27
) and (
28
), we use iterative algorithms. In particular, we in this work propose to use proximal gradient-type methods. As a brief general outline, consider the minimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{x\in\mathbb{C}^{F}}{\mathrm{minimize}}\quad E(x)=e(x)+d(x),

where
e
is a smooth convex function, and
d
is a (possibly non-differentiable) convex function. Under the assumption that
e
has a Lipschitz continuous gradient
L
, the problem can be solved through alternating between taking gradient steps with respect to
e
and computing the proximal mapping of the regularizer
d
, given by

\displaystyle x^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{prox}_{\gamma d}(x^{(j)}-\gamma\nabla e(x^{(j)}))

(30)

\displaystyle\triangleq\underset{x}{\mathrm{argmin}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\|x-u\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma d(x).

with
1/\gamma\geq L
being the step size,
u=(x^{(j)}-\gamma\nabla e(x^{(j)}))
, and
\mathrm{prox_{d}}
is the proximal operator of
d
. A generalization of proximal gradient descent has been proposed (see, e.g.,
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
), where an approximation of the function
e
depends on a Bregman distance associated with a function
\varphi
. The iterations for this are then given by

\displaystyle x^{(j+1)}=\underset{x\in\mathbb{C}^{F}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\left\{\gamma\langle\nabla e(x^{(j)}),x\rangle+\gamma d(x)+\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}(x,x^{(j)})\right\},

(31)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}(x,x^{(j)})=\varphi(x)-\varphi(x^{(j)})-\nabla\varphi(x^{(j)})(x-x^{(j)}).

It should be noted that with
\varphi(x)=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, the regular proximal gradient scheme is obtained. Furthermore, both descent methods have so-called
accelerated
versions, which speed up the convergence; see
[
48
,
47
]
for more information. Below, we first present the solver for the stochastic model, based on the Bregman proximal gradient schemes presented in this section.

VII-A

Stochastic model

We herein propose to solve (
27
) using the Bregman proximal gradient scheme in (
31
), with
e(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\left\lVert\mathbf{\hat{r}}-{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\nu}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\beta(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{F})
, and
d(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\zeta{\mathbf{S}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu})
. The function associated with the Bregman divergence we here use is
\varphi(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\sum_{f=1}^{F}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{f}\log\boldsymbol{\nu}_{f}
, yielding the Kullback-Leibler relative entropy, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{KL}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j)})=\sum_{f=1}^{F}[\boldsymbol{\nu}]_{f}\log([\boldsymbol{\nu}]_{f}/[\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j)}]_{f})+[\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j)}]_{f}-[\boldsymbol{\nu}]_{f}.

We here choose the step size to be
\gamma=\frac{1}{L}
, where
L\penalty\ =\penalty\ 2\left\lVert{\mathbf{A}}\right\rVert^{2}/\mathcal{T}
, and where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is the operator norm, as is also the case for the other method presented later. For this method, this does not guarantee convergence, but was shown by empirical testing to be a sufficiently small step size. Inspired by the work in
[
49

, the iterates for the solution are, nonetheless, given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j+1)}=\underset{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\quad

\displaystyle\gamma\left\langle\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\nu}\right\rangle+\mathcal{D}_{KL}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j)})+\gamma\zeta{\mathbf{S}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\nu}),

(32)

where
\mathbf{u}=\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\|\mathbf{\hat{r}}-{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\beta((\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j)})^{T}\mathbf{1}_{F}\right)
. For this minimization problem, the following proposition holds.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\odot
denote elementwise multiplication. Then, the update

(33)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}

is the dual norm of
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,1}

Proposition 2

Consider an iterative scheme with update in step

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{1+\gamma\zeta\epsilon}\left(\log(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})-\log(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k})\right),

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbf{\Psi}:\|\mathbf{\Psi}\|_{1,\infty}\leq\eta}{\mathrm{argmin}}\left\langle(\mathbf{v}^{k+1}\mathbf{1}_{G}^{T})\odot\mathbf{K},\exp\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mathbf{\Psi}\right)\right\rangle,

IX
Conclusion

This work addresses the problem of estimating pitch in multi-pitch signals. We show that the proposed methods achieves state-of-the-art performance on both simulated and real data in a frame-based setting setting, while, unlike existing non-network-based methods, it does not require prior knowledge of the harmonic order of the signal. The performance is surpassed only by network-based estimators. It should, however, be noted that those rely on access to a larger portion of the signal, while our approach operates in a setting where only a short portion of a signal is measured, not utilizing any previous information of the signal, and is better viewed as a building block for a recursive estimator, utilizing all or some of the prior measurements that are available.
