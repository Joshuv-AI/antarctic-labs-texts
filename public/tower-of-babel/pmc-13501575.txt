Young adults’ perceptions and experiences in using mobile apps and wearables for health: a qualitative study with public involvement
Authors: Gaia Leuzzi, Benedetto Giardulli, Mirko Job, Stella Leuzzi, Aldo Scafoglieri, Marco Testa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding how young people interact with health apps and wearables might help develop innovative strategies to support health management and improvement adopting a technology they are already familiar with. This study aimed to investigating the user experience of young adults who use apps or wearables for health purposes.MethodsA qualitative study using focus groups was conducted. Participants were eligible if they were between 18 and 26 years old and used an app or wearable to track at least one health variable (e.g., physical activity, diet, mental health). Transcripts were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) to identify themes grounded in the data.ResultsSixteen people participated (mean age: 24.3 ± 1.9 years; men/women: 9/7) and five themes were generated: I) My health easily all in one place: one click away; II) Exploring and learning health through catchy, personalised and user-friendly graphics; III) From monitoring my variables to empowerment: road to healthy habits; IV) Collecting achievements and connecting with others to improve my health; and V) Be careful in managing and giving too much weight to health variables!.ConclusionsYoung adults viewed apps and wearables as useful and powerful tools for managing their health, helping them gain a clear overview of different health aspects and how they interact. Features such as user-friendliness, personalisation, and achievement-based feedback were widely appreciated. In contrast, educational and community features were mentioned less often. Preventive and emergency functions were also valued, even among this young population. Despite these benefits, participants raised concerns about data accuracy and the potential negative impact of overly detailed information on people who are sensitive to tracking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27734-1.

Introduction

In recent years, interest in the adoption of mobile apps and wearables for health purposes has increased due to their growing use in this field [1]. These technologies offer numerous advantages such as continuous data collection [2], personalised interventions tailored to individual needs [3,4], support in decision-making for health behavioural changes [5], and monitoring of chronic conditions [6,7] as well as tracking different health variables (e.g., daily steps, water intake, sleep) in healthy people [8,9]. Consequently, mobile apps and wearables can be employed for both personalised health management and the improvement of many health variables across different life stages. Promoting health throughout the life course, often referred to as active ageing [10], is essential for supporting the aging population by fostering autonomy and well-being. Active ageing typically applies to older populations, but there is growing recognition that interventions designed to promote healthy behaviours should begin earlier in life to ensure long-term sustainability [10]. Therefore, active ageing should address young adults as well, as adopting early healthy behaviours can contribute to a healthier ageing process over time. Considering the complexity of these interventions and the potential of apps and wearables, the role of these technologies in promoting active ageing should be further explored to develop early, multidisciplinary, innovative, and more effective interventions [11], with a major focus on younger populations, as they are also very familiar with these technologies.

In this regard, a systematic and quantitative review has highlighted the importance of examining the role of health apps specifically for adolescents and young adults [12], as they represent the most engaged population in using mobile apps [7], making these technologies promising tools for early active ageing strategies sustained over time. In line with this, young adults’ policies to foster healthy lifestyles by adopting technologies are a public health priority, as they are also one of the unhealthier populations13.

However, current literature on young adults’ use of health apps tends to focus on a single domain of health variables, such as psychological aspects [14,15], physical activity [16,17] or diet [18,19]. Despite this approach being effective in assessing the effectiveness of apps and wearables in one specific domain (e.g. physical activity) and results promising, it might have left an important gap in considering the effectiveness of these technologies on different health domains simultaneously [20,21]. Addressing this gap is crucial, as health is a multifaceted concept that arises from the interaction of various domains [22]. Recent studies conducted in different countries [23,24] showed that young populations recognize potentials for health technologies, including apps and wearables, and consider them useful to improve health and wellbeing, learn about these topics, act to improve their daily habits and also prevent or treat pathologies [25,26]. Despite having potentials, the great heterogeneity in this field of research shows the need for standardised protocols and research design to further strengthen evidence and compare results in different populations, scenarios and for different mHealth tools [27]. Moreover, these health tools are not standardised, further promoting variability in research and making it difficult to directly compare them, other than underlining the need for standardised mHealth tools [28]. Furthermore, the characteristics and features that make apps and wearables effective in one domain (e.g., physical activity or diet) may not be as effective when combined to address multiple domains simultaneously (e.g., an app for both physical activity and diet). Therefore, investigating young adults’ use of health apps and wearables to address multiple domains simultaneously could enhance early, innovative and sustained active ageing strategies, offering a more holistic approach to health management and improvement.

To gain a deeper understanding of the topic investigated through people’s opinions and experiences, qualitative analysis can provide valuable and more comprehensive insight [29,30] into individual perspectives, offering a more comprehensive view of users’ motivations, preferences, expectations, and challenges. Gathering and analysing this information is essential for not only shaping interventions delivered via apps and wearables but also for improving the technologies themselves to better support young adults’ health, well-being and early active ageing strategies.

Hence, this qualitative research aimed to investigate young adults’ experiences with health variables monitoring apps and wearables and the factors influencing their use. More specifically, it investigated the experience of young adults using a mobile app or a wearable to monitor at least one health variable (e.g., steps, weight, sleep, emotions), while also exploring their interaction with the technology, including barriers, facilitators, expectations, motivation, and ideal device features.

Materials and methods

Study design

A qualitative study design based on online focus groups [31] was adopted to comprehensively explore the experience of young adults who use at least one mobile app or wearable for health, addressing physical activity, diet, or mental health. This study involving human participants and their data obtained ethical approval from the ethics committee of the University of Genoa (CERA2023.24, date of approval April 27, 2023). Study development and conduction followed the Declaration of Helsinki [32] and the International Conference on Harmonisation and Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines [33] for ethical conduct. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [34] were followed for the reporting of this manuscript (Supplementary materials).

Participants

Participants could take part in the study if they were young adults (18 to 26 years old included [13]) who used at least one mobile app or wearable for physical activity, diet, or mental health variables (e.g., sleep, mood, stress). Their eligibility was evaluated by GL. A purposeful sampling [35] was adopted, maintaining heterogeneity between the characteristics and backgrounds of the participants included to ensure different perspectives. People were invited to participate in the study via social media, emails, digital and printed materials, or personal invitations from the researchers. Participants were informed about the study aims and modalities and were further asked to read and sign the informed consent before participating in a focus group session. Recruitment of participants was carried out alongside the organisation of focus group sessions from the second to the last one, to ensure better organisation of sessions and quality of data collected. Recruitment was interrupted when researchers noted that no new patterns of meaning for data analysis were found in data provided by participants, therefore information redundancy was considered reached.

Public involvement

A female student (SL – age range: 20–30 years old) attending the Master of Science in Digital Humanities volunteered to participate in all study phases to enhance the study quality and the generalisability of findings. The student’s involvement has been reported following the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and Public (Supplementary Material). As a regular user of a mobile app and wearable for physical activity, with experience using a diet app, she helped refine the research question, focus group questions, and data analysis and provided insights on the results. Her collaboration was integral to the study and the final draft of this work, and she was finally offered the authorship at the end of this work, in compliance with the Contributor Roles Taxonomy (CRediT) standards [36]. Community engagement in this study was limited to the involvement of this one student contributor; no additional feedback was obtained from other people such as marginalised youth or community-based organisations.

Settings

Five focus group sessions were conducted online via Microsoft Teams, chosen for its convenience and compliance with the General Data Protection Regulations (GDPR). The platform enabled audio-video recording and verbatim transcription. Participants joined from any location, and two researchers (GL and BG) attended and moderated each session, with no other auditors present. The moderators maintained neutrality in each focus group, ensuring all participants felt free to express their thoughts and opinions without undue influence.

Data collection method

Before conducting the focus groups, a team consisting of a sports scientist (GL), a physiotherapist (GB), a bioengineer (MJ), and a psychologist developed a semi-structured protocol of questions (Table 1). Already existing questionnaires were consulted, but no questions were retrieved. New ones were developed to be more suitable for the research question and to cover a broader vision of user experience and expectations. The sports scientist and the physiotherapist were both users of apps and wearables for health. In particular, the sports scientist had previous expertise in using different types of apps and wearables for health and had been using them for many years. The bioengineer held expertise in wearable sensors of different natures. The public representative (SL) was also consulted for suggestions. All questions were not detailed for a specific health app or wearable but tried to remain as general and neutral as possible. Just one question was positive on purpose (i.e., number 6) to further invite the participants to agree or not with it, also providing an explanation. Based on the feedback received from the research team members and the public representative, modifications were made and the protocol was tested in the first focus group. After the initial session, minor adjustments were made. The interview guide adopted is reported in Table 1.

Each focus group was audio-video recorded and a verbatim transcription of the meeting was made. Transcriptions were manually checked for integrity, grammar, and spelling after each session. Researchers were free to write personal notes to aid in the interpretation of results.

At the start of each focus group, researchers conducted a brief introduction, familiarising themselves with the participants, who were also encouraged to introduce themselves. This introductory exchange aimed to establish a comfortable and inclusive environment, facilitating open discussion, and acquiring general data about each participant. Every participant was assigned a code based on their interview order (e.g., ID1). In this study, a few participants were previously acquainted with one of the researchers (GL), while most were strangers. Importantly, the prior relationships did not influence the results, as the research focused solely on capturing the honest user experiences of the participants, not their positive or negative assumptions about the mHealth tools investigated. The results further confirmed this assumption.

Data preparation and analysis

Sociodemographic data were collected for each participant, including age, level of education, profession and/or educational path, type of mobile app and/or wearable used, and health variables monitored. Transcriptions were manually checked for integrity and accuracy by one of the researchers (GL) and compared with the audio recordings to ensure that no spelling errors were present and that the concepts were reported exactly as participants expressed them. No content was removed from the transcripts, as the research team preferred to retain participants’ original wording and explanations, even when these were redundant.

Data analysis was conducted after the conclusion of all focus groups. Interviews were analysed by adopting a constructionist epistemology as we aimed to extensively explore the user experience of health apps and wearables, assuming that the language used and the experience relationship are bidirectional, therefore influencing each other rather than considering language a simple reflection of the experience itself. To analyse the dataset and generate meaningful patterns we adopted the “Reflexive Thematic Analysis” (RTA) by Braun and Clarke [37]. Reflexivity, intended as personal reflections relevant to the production of knowledge, is an additional value to the study due to the fact that each researcher had a different professional and cultural background (see Supplementary Material 2) that ensured an intersection of the data through the researchers’ positioning, skill and interpretative lens. RTA belongs to the ‘Big Q’ paradigm, not adhering to minimising bias, coding accuracy and the use of techniques to increase data trustworthiness but viewing the researchers’ subjectivity as an advantage. The approach adopted was predominantly inductive, not imposing a predetermined framework, and staying on the surface meaning of data (semantic coding). An experiential qualitative approach allowed us to look for reflexed shared meaning among the datasets. Data analysis followed the six steps of RTA [38,39] (Supplementary Material 2). First, two researchers (GL and BG) independently read each transcription multiple times to immerse themselves into the dataset. Researchers also shared the personal notes they collected during each focus group and considered them during the coding and theme development process. These notes included thoughts, ideas, impressions, potential biases, and key points raised by participants. Each researcher was free to record any information they considered potentially useful for the subsequent stages of data analysis. Following the initial step of the individual transcripts reading, researchers blindly started to create initial codes and then organised them to obtain broader patterns of meaning, thus creating initial themes supporting the research question. The two researchers joined to discuss codes and themes and obtained the final ones reflective of the dataset. Afterwards, quotations were extracted from the original Italian transcriptions, and they were translated blindly into English by GL and BG and further discussed to obtain a consensus. Finally, the translation most explicative of the participant’s meaning was chosen for supporting codes and the explanation of themes. Both GL and BG are proficient in English and Italian. Because they participated directly in the focus group sessions and share the same socio-cultural context as the participants, hence, they were well positioned to select the most accurate and contextually appropriate translation for each quotation. A third researcher (AS) was consulted in case of disagreement to reach a consensus. To maintain rigour in the analysis, reflexivity was ensured and incorporated using audit trial of code generation, peer debriefing, and records of all data field notes.

Results

Five focus groups were conducted between December 2023 and March 2024, for a total of 16 participants (mean age: 24.3 ± 1.9 years; men/women: 9/7) from Northern and Central Italy. The number of participants varied according to their schedules, and each focus group lasted between 45 and 75 min. The characteristics of the sample are detailed in Table 2. Half of the participants were students at various educational levels, while the other half were workers. Most participants used at least one app or wearable for physical activity, and half used at least one for diet.

From the data analysis, five themes, reflecting the comprehensive experience of participants, were developed: (I) My health easily all in one place: one click away; (II) Exploring and learning health through catchy, personalised and user-friendly graphics; (III) From monitoring my variables to empowerment: road to healthy habits; (IV) Collecting achievements and connecting with others to improve my health; (V) Be careful in managing and giving too much weight to health variables!. Young adults’ suggestions are also reported and summarised in Table 3. Codes and quotations for each theme are reported in Supplementary Materials 3 (Tables 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8).

Theme 1: “My health easily all in one place: one click away”

This theme was generated from participants’ shared desire to manage their overall health comprehensively, spanning from leisure time to potential medical emergencies, through the adoption of practical apps or wearables capable of integrating various health aspects. Participants stressed the importance of having a single tool where health data could be collected, stored, organized, and analysed, seeing it as a crucial first step towards improved health management as health is deemed a multidimensional aspect where different spheres converge and interact, such as physical activity, sleep, nutrition, social and psychological aspects. Data collected could be physiological (e.g., heart rate, stress, oxygen saturation, blood sugar levels, menstrual cycle, sleep), performative (e.g., sports and physical activity metrics, Global Positioning System (GPS) tracks), diet-related (e.g., calorie intake, water intake, macronutrients), psychological (e.g., emotional well-being, mood), or related to other different areas such as medical prevention (e.g., falls detection and fatigue) and leisure time (e.g., hobbies).

“For me, a single device where I monitor everything, without then having to compare things” – ID13.

“The integration with other types of devices. If I measure my blood pressure, if my blood pressure monitor could integrate with my phone via Bluetooth and was not only compatible with my application that I usually use to keep track of everything, it would be an incredible convenience.” – ID14.

Moreover, to be able to easily manage health, participants expressed a preference for data presentation that offered a quick and useful overview of personal health data and their potential correlations with different variables. They appreciated the ability to check for possible relationships between various health variables, facilitating a deeper understanding of their overall well-being. Participants also reported the need to integrate options to track and monitor hobbies and leisure time as part of overall health management. They perceived that these elements could influence other health variables and health itself. Furthermore, tracking leisure time could prove valuable for comparing it to the total time spent on work activities. Monitoring work hours, conditions, and associated mood should be considered as these factors could significantly impact an individual’s health.

Participants also highlighted the handiness of wearables, noting their ease of wear and interaction, as well as the automatic features that minimize interactions. Conversely, manual data entry was identified as a barrier, while the introduction of vocal assistance with artificial intelligence (AI) was seen as a potential facilitator. In this sense, scanning Quick Response (QR) codes to input diet items, and automatic data collection and integration, between different apps and wearables, were seen as strengths by participants. As an additional barrier, participants identified the costs associated with purchasing this technology as a significant one. They suggested that these devices should be more affordable to encourage broader adoption and ensure initial tracking of essential health variables for everyone, without requiring additional premium options to monitor other metrics.

“Yes, I agree too about the cost, which I see as a barrier sometimes. Another important aspect generated from the focus group was the medical dimension of health. Participants believed that doctors should recommend the autonomous use of apps and wearables to help manage risky conditions (e.g., chronic conditions, monitoring of variables after acute problems), prevent emergencies, and seek assistance when needed. Notifications capable of alerting users of dangerous situations or medical concerns were deemed essential features for all devices, regardless of the user’s health status.

“In my opinion, health professionals themselves should suggest certain people to use these devices” - ID14.

Finally, precision, accuracy, and reliability of the collected data were emphasized as fundamental elements for users, as they depend on these apps and wearables to provide accurate information about their health variables.

Theme 2: “Exploring and learning health through catchy, personalised and user-friendly graphics”

This theme was created by exploring the participants’ catchy, educative and personalised experience provided by apps and wearables. Daily use of these apps/wearables helped participants grasp the significance behind their collected data, and the contextual references provided by the apps improved data interpretation and reliability. In this scenario, curiosity played a crucial role in encouraging users to pay closer attention to their health variables. Additionally, educational opportunities, such as detailed information about specific health variables, encouraged users to delve deeper into health-related topics.

“It also provides you with some extra info on why you need to get a certain number of hours of sleep, what the values are, why they are like that, with also being supplied with this, which obviously gives them a sense of trust” – ID1.

“It also provides you with some extra info on why you need to get a certain number of hours of sleep, what the values are, why they are like that, with also being supplied with this, which obviously gives them a sense of trust” – ID1.

The aesthetic appeal of apps and wearables was a significant facilitator in navigating and interpreting the wealth of information. User-friendly interfaces and colourful graphics were deemed highly beneficial, offering better comprehension compared to numerical data alone. For instance, incorporating images related to food or providing vocal explanations in diet apps could assist individuals with vision impairment. Another innovative aspect could be creating apps and wearables specifically tailored for people with disabilities.

Furthermore, ease of use extends to understanding the various factors influencing an individual’s health, which is crucial in mHealth. Data presentation should not be limited to numbers and graphs but should include a general health indicator, as health is a multifaceted concept that should be seen as a goal rather than a specific number to achieve. Personalised advice based on individual data should be provided by apps and wearables, considering the objectives and characteristics of each person. Additionally, to help personalisation based on an individual’s characteristics and needs, AI could also help manage this task, overcoming difficulties in accessibility for people with different types of disabilities.

Theme 3: “From monitoring my variables to empowerment: road to healthy habits”

This theme was created by participants highlighting the role of apps and wearables in fostering empowerment toward a healthier lifestyle while enjoying the interaction with them. Therefore, apps and wearables should facilitate a transition for young adults from merely monitoring their health to actively improving it.

“How many steps do you take per day and maybe if you see that you’re close to reaching it, I have the standard goal of 10,000 steps, and if I see that I’m close to reaching it, then I try to reach the goal even if I see that I walk little, I try to move a bit more.” – ID16.

“How many steps do you take per day and maybe if you see that you’re close to reaching it, I have the standard goal of 10,000 steps, and if I see that I’m close to reaching it, then I try to reach the goal even if I see that I walk little, I try to move a bit more.” – ID16.

Apps and wearables helped participants plan personalised activities, including dietary habits and sports and motivated them through automatic reminders to drink water, take breaks, or go for walks.

The possibility of having personalised activities and thus reminders connected to them was crucial for tailoring participants’ habits to their lifestyles. Reminders throughout the day were deemed essential to adopt and maintain healthy habits, such as drinking more water or going for little walks interrupting prolonged sedentary time.

Finally, gamification and personalised dashboards were seen as important for enhancing the use of apps and wearables, as well as monitoring and improving health variables, thus ensuring higher adherence to mHealth usage. Gamification, involving game-like elements, along with curiosity, helped participants increase their interest in registering their data, checking them and understanding the benefits of improving health aspects.

Theme 4: “Collecting achievements and connecting with others to improve my health”

This theme was created to highlight the role of apps and wearables in connecting participants’ health journey through social and rewarding behaviours. Participants noted that using these tools helped them improve and empower themselves, and this motivation was a key factor in starting their use. Various features played a crucial role in fostering personal growth, such as engaging with a community where users could compare their progress with peers, friends, or family. The ability to challenge themselves and others with similar characteristics and conditions was seen as very helpful for motivating individuals to achieve specific goals and improve health variables. Challenges with personal self might include meeting a daily goal consistently, improving performance from previous days, or overcoming personal records. Additionally, being able to compare results with past performances and with others can be motivating, but this feature should be optional rather than mandatory.

“Sharing with people who started around the same time at you did, who are more or less at your level, and thus having the comparison with someone who is more or less at your level and seeing that when you reach a certain step that others have also reached after maybe the same amount of time of activity, could also help, right? Theme 5: “Be careful in managing and giving too much weight to health variables!”

This final theme was generated from the negative experience of using apps and wearables from the participants’ perspectives. The main concern was that these tools could be double-edged weapons, especially for more sensitive people. They might become obsessed with monitoring their health data and worry about having abnormal conditions due to unexpected results from the apps or wearables. This is particularly risky with medical data that are constantly tracked, such as heart rate, which can affect mental well-being and further lead to hypochondria. Participants suggested that certain medical data should be entered into the apps/wearables by health professionals (HPs) to prevent obsession and incorrect data entry. They also proposed that HPs should be able to control which variables patients can see to avoid negative reactions, while still having access to all data to monitor overall health conditions effectively.

“It brings attention to things that you would have initially ignored, maybe if someone also has a tendency to be stressed about these things, the stress itself then starts to bring other problems and creates a vicious circle. One participant mentioned getting a notification about poor sleep, which caused stress for the entire day. Discussions

This qualitative study explored young adults’ experiences with health apps and wearables, considering their motivations, usage preferences, and desired features to better support health management. Participants emphasised the need for an easy, centralised access to their health via digital tools (My health easily all in one place: one click away), with a personalised and catchy approach that supports the learning process of health management (Exploring and learning health through catchy, personalised and user-friendly graphics). Moreover, they expressed the need for moving towards health self-management (From monitoring my variables to empowerment: road to healthy habits) supporting this journey with achievements and community aspects (Collecting achievements and connecting with others to improve my health). Finally, young adults also expressed negative aspects about using this technology (Be careful in managing and giving too much weight to health variables! ). The following sections first discuss individual-level experiences with health apps and wearables, and then address broader community- and system-level implications relevant to public health practice and policy.

Theme 1 - My health easily all in one place: one click away

At an individual level, a clear preference emerged integrating all health-related data in one place to better manage the complex aspects of health. This would simplify the management of different variables, providing a quick and useful health overview and showing patterns of influence or improvement in an accessible way, as reported elsewhere in the literature [40]. Centralizing health data supports empowerment and self-management, allowing users to track progress and make informed decisions independently. In support of this concept, participants highlighted the multidimensionality of health and the challenges of using and comparing data from different tools to comprehensively monitor all its aspects. Health was described as multidimensional, encompassing medical, non-medical, hobbies and leisure-related aspects. Notably, this age group perceived hobbies and leisure time as integral parts of their health and expressed a desire for features to monitor these aspects and assess their potential influence on other health variables [41]. This sensitivity might be innovative and interesting for young adults [42], as it could be adapted to better address health behavioural interventions. Following the literature, participants reported that hobbies might positively influence a day [43], which could be crucial for maintaining high motivation and adherence during health interventions. This aspect should be carefully considered when designing health interventions for young adults as hobbies and leisure time should also be included. They also highlighted the importance of comparing work time with leisure time, as they reported a high priority for free time and maintaining work-life balance in their lives to better address their overall health.

From a broader public health perspective, participants, despite their young age, also focussed on the role of apps and wearables in the prevention and detection of emergencies [44, 45]. They valued features such as fall detection and alerts from mHealth apps that notify users of abnormal results in their health variables, prompting them to check their health condition [46]. Additionally, they emphasized that health professionals should advise patients to use apps and wearables to monitor their conditions and receive alerts when necessary. These points highlight the important role that apps and wearables should play in enhancing health and safety for young adults, but also in monitoring a long-term condition [47]. Moreover, young adults reported that these functions could be even more helpful for older people or people remaining alone, as this would be a continuous monitoring system that could call for help in case of emergency. These technologies were considered important tools in their health management, but their adoption might also be influenced by socioeconomic factors, such as income, cost of these technologies, lack of internet access, language [48], work shifts [49], and living in rural areas [50], hindering their use. A recent scoping review also found that socioeconomic factors influencing individual differences in using mental health apps should be deeply investigated because they seem to influence both their adoption and effectiveness [51]. On a broader level, mHealth should be developed specifically for the target population and their socio-economic and cultural background to try to ensure their adoption and effectiveness, as physical and mental health might be seen and approached differently [52]. On the other hand, recent evidence suggests that facilitators for mHealth adoption in people with low socioeconomic positions could be HPs interactions, teaching the users how to correctly adopt the device, stakeholders’ collaboration, and having good digital infrastructures [53].

Theme 2 - Exploring and learning health through catchy, personalised and user-friendly graphics

Considering the complexity of integrating all the aforementioned aspects into a single tool and that essential characteristics such as ease of use, automatic data collection and handiness should be guaranteed, participants suggested introducing AI and assistive technologies (e.g., vocal assistants) to help perform simple tasks [54]. These suggestions could be applied to ease the scaling up of technologies at community levels. AI could also assist in correcting and analysing data, early diagnosing and predicting health conditions [55], adapting the correct drug dose, and helping in decision-making for both clinicians and patients [56]. Young adults could benefit from apps and wearables with AI to be informed of potentially harmful health conditions or to be supported in improving their lifestyle with help in decision-making towards healthier habits. Despite the abovementioned potentialities in adopting AI for health purposes, issues with privacy, data security, ethics [57] and knowledge about AI functioning, its potential and possible use cases still persist. In line with this, studies conducted on medical students [58,59] reported that they were aware of the existence of AI but not completely aware of possible use cases for medical purposes, despite being favourable to adopt it. Moreover, assistive technologies could foster accessibility, also considering health and awareness on different topics [60]. Furthermore, AI and assistive technologies could be more beneficial for apps and wearables to further reduce user interactions and could lead to more accessible and inclusive mHealth. Interestingly, participants expressed interest in mHealth specifically designed for people with different types of disabilities [61]. Moreover, young adults expressed a clear preference for wearables over apps due to their immediate accessibility and handiness.

Other types of accessibility were highlighted, both concerning prices and software features. The cost of purchasing a wearable or accessing premium features was identified as a major barrier [30] for young adults. They expressed interest in monitoring more health variables but noted that this would involve higher costs that many could not afford. Another significant barrier identified was the poor battery capacity of wearables [30], which prevents effective monitoring of sleep as the device needs to be charged overnight to be usable during the day. Improving the battery capacity would foster the possibility for continuous, automatic monitoring of health variables over days.

Based on individuals’ feedback, curiosity was one of the most prevalent reasons for using apps and wearables. Colours, attractive graphics, presenting results in simple and understandable ways, non-numeric data visualisation, and gamification features were well received by participants and deemed essential for keeping users engaged and motivated to use the app or wearable [16, 17]. Additionally, the possibility to visualise data beyond just numbers was considered important, as health is a complex topic that cannot be easily reduced to numerical values [62]. Participants expressed this preference in addition to numerical data, as they also questioned the reliability and accuracy of the data collected and presented. Visualising health goals beyond just numbers could also help address accuracy concerns. These features also enhance digital health literacy, helping users understand complex health information and guiding them toward healthier behaviours. Moreover, some participants emphasised that data provided by mHealth should be viewed more as guidelines rather than unquestionable truths due to possible incorrect data collection or lack of individual personalisation.

Theme 3 - From monitoring my variables to empowerment: road to healthy habits

The educational value of apps and wearables [63] was considered important, but participants saw it as an optional feature rather than a requirement. While they believed including scientific references in the technology was crucial [64], they also felt that the ability to access and gain more knowledge about health and its variables should be optional or customisable. This approach prevents an overload of information, which could lead to confusion and further decrease the use of these tools.

Personalisation was a key aspect for young adults, as they expressed interest in selecting what data and information to see in their apps and wearables and having the ability to personalise it [65], even starting from standard templates. They believed that personalisation could make the technology more appealing and user-friendly. Participants were willing to invest more time initially to adapt the technology to their needs and expectations, considering it a worthwhile compromise to have access to information that truly interests them. Another interesting aspect was the ability to personalise not only the interface but also the exterior appearance of the wearable, such as using different straps, to adapt it to various events or occasions. As wearables were seen as the ideal tool for continuous health monitoring, it is important that their appearance can suit different situations [66]. This flexibility could encourage users to wear them on every occasion, ensuring better data collection and continuous monitoring.

Lastly, participants reported that they felt empowered using apps and wearables for health as they were more self-aware of their health and progress, also according to literature [67,68].

Theme 4 - Collecting achievements and connecting with others to improve my health

Another personalised element identified was setting personal goals, which participants found useful in maintaining motivation through the achievement of badges, virtual compliments, and notifications indicating progress towards completion [30]. In line with this, in a similar population motivation emerged from curiosity, individual needs and suggestions [69], thus supporting the hypothesis that personalisation is a key element in motivating young adults to use health apps and wearables. Moreover, these technologies are widely adopted for habit formation (e.g., improving physical activity) because they reduce the need for selecting personalised goals adopting rule-based adaptive goals aims to automatically provide gradual tasks, based on performances, time, and progress [70]. Moreover, a recent study reported that notifications might be more effective in increasing short-term engagement rather than a long-term one [71]. A recent study reported that young adults might perceive notifications as negative, discouraging or generating anxiety when they were prompted to take actions, while positive when reminded of the existence of the app or motivated to engage with the app [72]. Thus, possible future solutions could address with more personalised options supported by AI or the possibility of disabling notifications.

Some participants, particularly considering their age, were more sensitive to receiving compliments and viewed this feature as very positive. They found amusement in receiving compliments, even though they were aware that they were not coming from real individuals as real human interactions sometimes are perceived as judgemental [73]. Interactions with other people were often viewed positively [30], although not deemed essential by all participants. For some, sharing results and observing others with similar characteristics and goals reaching certain milestones served as an additional external motivator. However, for more self-directed individuals, community opportunities were seen as optional. This variability in preferences could also be considered as another aspect of personalisation within mHealth. Moreover, social influences and in particular recommendations from others are reported to be important for young adults’ adoption and trust in mHealth, especially when provided by people with great social influence [74]. Another study evidenced that young adults’ perceived risks, trust and performance expectations in mHealth are shaped by social influence [75], thus external recommendations might be useful in improving health apps and wearables engagement. Hence, goal-setting, achievements, and social interactions might promote behaviour change by reinforcing motivation and supporting sustained engagement with healthy routines.

Theme 5 - Be careful in managing and giving too much weight to health variables!

Despite apps and wearables being powerful and useful tools for health purposes, young adults also felt them as double-edged weapons [73]. As also previously reported in the literature, participants felt stressed when seeing incorrect data or parameters [76,77], not within the normal range or by notifications prompting further investigation of variables that could be of medical interest [78]. Moreover, inaccurate data could lead to generating false alerts [48] or wrong drug prescriptions [79], making young adults lose trust and interest in using the devices. While self-monitoring empowers users, it can also generate stress if data are misinterpreted, highlighting the importance of guidance and educational support to maximize health benefits. Additionally, inaccurate data could slow down or fasten the achievement of aims, respectively creating distress or a false sense of accomplishment not based on real improvements. Additionally, an overload of variable-related details was seen as a source of stress, potentially leading individuals to consider elements previously overlooked and of which they had little knowledge yet could have significant impacts on their health. Participants suggested solutions to these issues, emphasising the importance of addressing them to enhance the effectiveness of mHealth tools for long-term health management. They proposed the option to indicate how they felt about a variable before being presented with numerical results, enabling better control over their emotional responses and increased awareness of the variable’s impact on their health. Furthermore, participants recommended that HPs should insert patients’ precise medical data into apps and wearables. Thus, patients would be relieved of this task, avoiding the stress caused by poor understanding of medical data and their implications for health conditions, especially when data or results are negative. HPs might also play a crucial role when suggesting the adoption of mHealth to young adults as they spread a sense of trust in these technologies, empowering them through teaching their correct use and thus improving the possibility of reaching positive outcomes [53]. This suggestion highlights participants’ willingness to receive support from HPs when using this technology, particularly in cases of medical needs. Moreover, it also emphasises participants’ acceptability of these tools in supporting their medical treatments [80]. Taken together, these suggestions emphasise the need for coordinated public health and educational policy responses to ensure equitable and effective use of mHealth technologies in young adults. Findings underline that while apps and wearables empower users, professional oversight from HPs remains essential: clinicians can help interpret data, check and contextualise them on the patient, provide context and individualised indications, and ensure that self-monitoring via digital tools complements with professional care without replacing it, enhancing both safety and effectiveness. Moreover, HPs could set digital tools to remotely remind patients of drug prescriptions, check if they are adhering to the therapeutic indications provided, sharing important medical information with other HPs of family members, and allowing patients to ask questions and reach for help remotely.

Hence, health apps and wearables could be progressively integrated into public healthcare systems to support large-scale, coordinated prevention strategies. By allowing relevant health data to be shared with HPs when necessary, these tools could help build a continuous and comprehensive health record across a person’s life. This integration would also enable the use of AI and machine learning to support early detection and timely interventions for various conditions, benefiting both individuals and HPs. On a broader level, aggregated data from diverse populations considering regional, demographic, socioeconomic, cultural, and clinical factors, could inform more tailored preventive and therapeutic strategies across different layers of the health system.

Furthermore, to prevent negative outcomes from the use of apps and wearables, there should be improved education on both the technology itself and health aspects [81]. Education on these aspects should also be provided by school-based programs or community clinics. They could support early prevention by monitoring physical activity, nutrition, sleep, or mental health, while providing tailored feedback and guidance from healthcare or educational professionals. Young adults, especially those attending educational paths, often trust their teachers, so this approach not only promotes equitable access to digital health tools but also offers opportunities for learning directly from trusted experts, empowering young adults to make informed decisions and adopt healthier behaviours.

Notifications might also have negative impacts on health outcomes and device adoption when being too frequent, misleading or not customizable based on daily routines or individuals’ readiness in using mHealth [82]. Moreover, their content should align with the app purposes [82], also fostering motivation towards healthier behaviours, increase frequency of device use and be personalised based on individual needs and characteristics. Personalisation options and AI could be integrated in apps and wearables to further improve notification effectiveness, shaping them based on individual’s behaviours.

Hence, all the aforementioned elements (e.g., user-friendliness, motivational and educational aspects, catchy graphic) should be integrated into the ideal app or wearable for young adults’ health purposes. Consideration of all aspects that could impact long-term adherence to using these tools is crucial, as they play a major role in ensuring sustained monitoring and improvement of health variables over time with minimal effort. Finally, a major focus on possible public health risks associated with using digital tools for health, such as anxiety for inaccurate data or over-monitoring of variables, should be deeper investigated to prevent them, especially in already fragile or vulnerable people. HPs could assist people in this, suggesting or not the mHealth adoption in specific situations. Policy makers should also consider a wide range of benefits and potential drawbacks when using apps and wearables for health in populations with specific characteristics such as anxiety, possible designing ad hoc devices and their use.

Community-level implications for public health

Considering the potential for integrating health apps and wearables into young adults’ real-life scenarios, it is essential to acknowledge the broader social determinants that may limit their reach. Despite the worldwide diffusion of these technologies, many populations still face significant barriers to their access. Cost remains a major obstacle, especially for young people that might not have an income or for people with low socioeconomic positions, as devices and app subscriptions can be expensive, and many tools require stable internet connections or specific software requirements. Digital literacy is another key aspect: while many young adults are familiar with digital tools, this is not universal, and navigating multiple apps or interpreting health metrics may be challenging for those with lower digital or health literacy.

These inequities risk excluding individuals with limited financial resources, lower education, or unstable internet access. Moreover, commercial mHealth tools often rely on automated feedback that may not be culturally responsive or available in multiple languages, further disadvantaging users from marginalised communities. Effective monitoring of different health domains (e.g., physical activity, diet, sleep) often requires multiple devices or premium app features, which can widen the gap between those who can afford comprehensive monitoring and those who cannot. As a result, commercial health apps and wearables may unintentionally reinforce existing health inequities by primarily serving young adults with adequate economic means, higher literacy levels, and reliable digital access. Hence, to promote more equitable use of mHealth tools, public health practitioners could support initiatives such as: subsidized devices, simplified or low-data app designs, policies to support low-cost access to digital health tools especially for promoting long-term prevention starting from youth, promotion of strategies for digital or health literacy training for young adults, strengthen the collaboration with youth-communities and organisations and involve them in the creation and development of large-scale strategies. Involving community organisations and co-designing features with marginalised youth may also help ensure that these technologies are culturally and socially responsive, accessible, and truly meaningful and useful for diverse populations.

Our findings highlighted that cost, accessibility, and disability are not merely usability issues but represent important structural barriers to the adoption and effective use of health apps and wearables. Socioeconomic position, digital literacy, and device availability can limit engagement and worsen existing health inequities. Addressing these digital inequities is crucial for public health policy and practice, as interventions must consider affordability, inclusivity, and accessibility to ensure that digital health tools benefit diverse populations equitably.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this investigation lies in its balanced consideration of both the advantages and drawbacks associated with the use of health apps and wearable devices among young adults. This approach provides valuable insights for improving mHealth tools, particularly in identifying features should be implemented or removed to enhance user adoption and effectiveness. Furthermore, the inclusion of participants with diverse backgrounds and usage habits (including one with diabetes) contributed to a broader and more nuanced understanding of the topic. Exploring a range of apps and wearables also increased the generalisability of the findings and allowed the study to better reflect the real-life needs of this population.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the public representative consisted of a single student within the target age group, which may have limited the diversity of perspective compared to other potential representatives with different characteristics (e.g., a full-time employment, lower socioeconomic status, limited educational background, or lower digital literacy). As a result, the findings may represent a slightly more optimistic view of the ease of adoption and use of mHealth technologies. Additionally, participants had a relatively high average level of education, which may have influenced their responses, despite their otherwise varied backgrounds. All participants were either employed or student at the time of the research, which may have further shaped the results.

Moreover, most participants were familiar with technological devices and basic health-related topics, originated from the same geographical context (i.e., country - Italy), had similar socioeconomic positions, and did not represent a multiethnic sample. Their level of health-related knowledge may have influenced the emphasis placed on certain topics compared to the general population [83]. Nevertheless, the sample showed similarities in health literacy with comparable populations in different countries [26]. Participants’ demographic indicators such as income levels or digital access were not objectively assessed. However, digital access can be reasonably inferred from participants’ reported experiences using the European DigComp 3.0 framework [84], suggesting an overall intermediate level (e.g., the scale includes basic, intermediate, advanced, highly specialised). Lastly, physical activity-related apps and wearables were the most commonly used among participants, resulting in more limited insights into diet- and mental health-related tools. This imbalance may reflect wider diffusion of physical activity technologies, which often collect data automatically, unlike diet and mental health devices that typically require manual user input. This difference may contribute to lower usage and adherence rates for the latter [26]. In addition, physical activity tends to attract more interest in the general population, partly driven by the strong presence of fitness-related content on social media and its influence on people and the widespread attention to sports performance [85].

Diet and mental health apps may also be less widely adopted because they involve tracking subjective and potentially sensitive information, which some users may perceive as burdensome. These tools can require frequent self-reflection and self-assessment, and their feedback may sometimes be experienced as judgmental or intrusive [26]. Finally, diet and mental health are complex and less easily quantifiable than physical activity, which may reduce users’ perceptions of accuracy and immediate usefulness ultimately limiting their broader adoption.

Future directions

To address the issues and drawbacks emerged from young adults’ experience in using apps and wearables for health, elements such as user-friendly interfaces, personalised feedback, and enhanced educational resources are recommended. Professional involvement of health professionals in integrating medical data and providing practical support is crucial. Prioritizing user-centred design and considering diverse user needs will maximize the impact of these technologies, with minimal effort, on long-term health outcomes improvement towards more efficient active ageing strategies. Future research should expand this area of study across different age groups or countries to further compare results, also considering young adults with low socioeconomic positions, limited education, different chronic health issues and non-European citizens to expand results obtained and enrich and compare them with the ones already available in literature. Public involvement in future research should also consider individuals with low socioeconomic positions, poor digital literacy, limited educational background, and diverse cultural perspectives to expand results obtained and strengthen the discussion around the topic. Moreover, the hypotheses of creating specific health apps or wearables for young adults and for people with special needs or disabilities should be deeper investigated. Accessibility should be prioritized through inclusive designs, multilingual options and sensitivity to cultural differences in health perceptions. Future research may also benefit from participatory approaches, such as co-design workshops and community advisory boards involving young adults, HPs and policy makers. These strategies can improve alignment with public health research standards and enhance the relevance of outcomes. Moreover, adopting longitudinal and mixed-methods research designs would allow for a deeper exploration of habit formation and the mental health impacts associated with the use of health technologies. Finally, greater attention should be given to diet- and mental health-related digital tools, particularly from the perspective of young adults, to better understand both their benefits and limitations.

Conclusions

Young adults considered apps and wearables powerful tools for promoting health management and improvement. Participants valued personalised features and comprehensive data integration of different health aspects (such as hobbies and leisure time), but they also expressed concerns about potential stressors such as inaccurate data and information overload. Summarising, young adults’ preferences on health apps and wearables include:
