Nanoencapsulation of Anise oil and evaluation of its antimicrobial and antioxidant effects to propose for food preservation
Authors: Tina Ebrahimi Qazvini, Ehsan Zamani, Mehdi Evazalipour, Mehran Nemattalab, Pasha Ebrahimi Qazvini, Zahra Hesari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFood spoilage is a complex process. Despite modern preservation methods use of alternative antimicrobial agents such as essential oils, as natural preservatives can be beneficial. This study aims to comparatively evaluate Anise essential oil (AEO) encapsulated in Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLN) versus chitosan nanocarriers against E. coli and S. typhimurium and oxidative stress in HU02 cells. The probable improvement of antioxidant, antibacterial, and antibiofilm effects of AEO relevant to food preservation, using two different nanocarriers, was not investigated previously. Nanoparticles were synthesized using natural based excipients and the probe-sonication method and their physico-chemical, antioxidant and antimicrobial properties were characterized. The mean size and zeta potential of AEO-SLN 292.66 nm and − 21.9 mV and for AEO-CH 188.41 nm and 29.7 mV, revealing the smaller size and positive surface charge for chitosan nano-carrier. Both systems showed sustained release up to 72 h with slightly higher cumulative release from AEO-CH (87.06%) than AEO-SLN (84.07%). The MIC against E. coli and S. typhimurium were AEO-CH (45–50 µg mL−1) < AEO-SLN (200–220 µg mL−1) < free AEO (540–560 µg mL−1). Additionally, AEO-NPs reduced H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage by increasing cell viability and normalizing MDA and GSH levels. Therefore, these nano-formulations, particularly AEO-CH, are promising natural nanocarriers for controlling MDR pathogens and biofilms while decreasing oxidative damage in food preservation systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04638-y.Keywords: Anise oil, Food preservation, Chitosan, Solid lipid nanoparticle, Antibacterial, Antioxidant

Introduction

The food industry is highly valued globally, but faces a major concern of food spoilage [1]. Food manufacturing facilities may still harbor persistent microorganisms [2]. In addition, a rise in green consumption can be observed in the food industry with demands for natural food additives and antimicrobial agents with a minimal environmental impact, versus synthetic antimicrobials with the concerns of resistance, teratogenicity and toxicity [3]. Recently, essential oils have gained attention as natural compounds for use in the food industry, either alone or in combination with other technologies [4, 5].

Foodborne illness outbreaks are mainly caused by a few pathogens, including Salmonella and Escherichia coli (E. coli) [6]. Foodborne pathogens commonly develop biofilms on food-contact surfaces, facilitating bacterial spread and recurrent cross-contamination of food products [7].

Anise, also known as Pimpinella anisum`1 L., has been used in traditional medical practices for centuries [8]. Anise has been used for many years to treat gastrointestinal disorders, as well as for its antioxidant, antidiabetic, antimicrobial, antipyretic, and antifungal properties [9, 10]. Anise essential oil (AEO) is utilized as flavoring, antioxidant and preservative in industries, such as food, beverages, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals [11]. Although AEO exhibits antibacterial and antioxidant properties, its low water solubility and instability limit its antimicrobial activity and direct use in food systems [12]. Hence, the encapsulation of AEOs is a promising solution to overcome their limitations [13]. Several studies have encapsulated the AEO in chitosan-based delivery systems including film, microparticle, nanoparticle, etc. for food preservation or other applications [14–16]. Also, some researches have synthesized lipid-based delivery systems, mainly nano-emulsion of anise oil to enhance its physicochemical or antibacterial properties [17–21]. But, to our knowledge, there is no previous comparative study of AEO loaded in SLN versus chitosan nanoparticles assessing antibacterial, antibiofilm (especially against food born bacteria), and antioxidant effects in a unified design. This comparative evaluation fills a critical gap in selecting the most efficient nanocarrier for food preservation.

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) are a type of colloidal delivery system, used extensively in the food industries and pharmaceutical as they have become popular alternatives to emulsions, liposomes, polymeric structures, and microparticles [22]. SLNs represent a promising approach for drug delivery systems, allowing for controlled release and providing effective protection for bioactive substances against external factors, all while demonstrating high biodegradability and low toxicity [23, 24]. In addition, chitosan (CH) certified as GRAS by FDA, is a prevalent nanocarrier that its unique properties have made it an attractive material for food packaging [25]. For example, essential oil of clove buds (CEO) encapsulated in chitosan nanoparticles showed better antioxidant activity than the pure oil. Similarly, chitosan nanoparticles loaded with (CEO) had high antimicrobial activity against S. aureus and L. monocytogenes [26]. Consequently, it is widely used in multiple industries, including agriculture, food, and biopharmaceuticals because of its extensive antibacterial properties [27].

Oxidative stress arises when the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) overwhelms the system’s antioxidant defenses [28]. This can lead to DNA damage and mutation [29]. External antioxidants, such as catalase, superoxide reductase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione peroxidase, catalytically remove oxidants [30]. In addition, researchers have shown that malondialdehyde (MDA) is an indicator of lipid peroxidation, and lipid peroxidation is recognized as a prevalent process through which numerous drugs exert their toxic effects [31].CH and SLN are two compounds that have been researched and identified as being resilient to environmental stressors [32, 33].

We hypothesize that encapsulation of Pimpinella anisum EO into chitosan nanoparticles and solid lipid nanoparticles will (i) increase physical stability and control release, (ii) enhance antimicrobial and antibiofilm efficacy compared to free oil (chitosan, due to its antimicrobial activity, may outperform SLN in antibacterial/antibiofilm effects), and (iii) reduce oxidative damage in mammalian fibroblasts, thereby supporting potential food-preservation applications. Therefore, this study aimed to formulate AEO-SLN and AEO-CH; in vitro release; antioxidant effects in HU02 under H₂O₂; MIC/MBC and antibiofilm activity against standard and MDR E. coli and S. typhimurium.

Materials and methods

Materials

Soya lecithin (DUKSAN reagents, South Korea), dichloromethane, acetic acid and tween 80, all of the analytical grade, were purchased from Merck, Germany. Cholesterol (Sigma, Germany), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) with a molecular weight of 800000 Da, chitosan (9012-76-4- low molecular weight- Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) (7758-29-4, Sigma-Aldrich), dialysis bags (MWCO 12–14 kDa, Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany), crystal violet (Sigma, Steinheim, Germany), as well as Mueller Hinton Agar/Broth (MHA/B) (Merck, Germany) were purchased. Anise essential oil was purchased from Lavandor co. (Tehran, Iran). DMEM (Modified Eagle’s Medium) and FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany. MTT ((3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) and DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany.

Preparation of AEO -SLN

The emulsification ultrasonic homogenization method was employed to create AEO-loaded SLNs. Precisely, 100 mg of lecithin and 100 mg of cholesterol as a lipid phase were mixed with 800 µL of Tween 80 as a surfactant, and this mixture was dissolved in 10 mL of dichloromethane at room temperature. Next, 1 g AEO and 10 mL of Polyvinyl alcohol (4% w/v in water) were gradually added to the previous mixture and homogenized using a high-speed probe sonicator (ultra-probe sonicator, Hielscher UP400s, Germany) for 10 min at 1500 Hz. Ultimately, the produced milky emulsion was subjected to a rotary evaporator (Heidolph Hei-VAP Value, Germany) at 37 °C for 25 min for evaporation of dichloromethane to remain AEO-SLN dispersed in water [34].

Preparation of AEO-loaded chitosan nanoparticles

A 1% w/v solution of chitosan in acetic acid (1% v/v) was prepared using a magnetic stirrer at 40 °C for a period of 24 h. The resulting solution was then filtered through a filter with a pore size of 1 μm, after which 1 mL of Tween 80 was added as a surfactant to the 100 mL chitosan solution. Next, to adjust the acidity level, the pH of the solution was adjusted to 4.2, and the mixture was stirred at 50 °C for 90 min to ensure homogeneity. Following this, 1 mL of AEO was incorporated into 2 mL of the chitosan solution using a homogenizer at 13,000 rpm for 10 min in an ice bath to create an oil-in-water (o/w) emulsion. To facilitate ionic gelation of the chitosan, 6 mL of a 1% w/v TPP solution was added while continuously agitating for 30 min. Lastly, the mixture was centrifuged centrifuged (Froilabo, FW14R; France) at 9000×g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting particles were rinsed three times with deionized water [35].

Characterization of AEO-SLN

Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI) measurement, and Zeta Potential (ZP)

The particle size, ZP and size distribution of NPs were analyzed using a Zetasizer Nano S (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK). For the analysis, the NPs were diluted with purified water in a 1:10 ratio and measured at a temperature of 25 °C. The Ver.6.01 software was used to calculate the mean particle size. Additionally, the PDI was calculated to determine whether the produced nanoparticles were polydisperse or monodisperse.

TEM analysis

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) (ZEISS EM10C-100 KV, Oberkochen, Germany) was used to determine the morphology, shape, and particle size of the nanoparticles. Nanoparticle suspensions were placed on formvar-coated 200–300 mesh grids. The samples were dehydrated using serially increased ethanol concentration and analyzed by TEM.

FTIR spectrometry

FT-IR spectroscopy was conducted to investigate the molecular vibrations, surface chemistry, and possible incompatibilities between the oil and the excipients contained in the nanoparticles. The synthesized AEO-NPs and the oil were analyzed using a Spectrum Two FT-IR spectrometer with a UATR accessory (PerkinElmer ES Version 10.5.3, USA) at a resolution of 4 cm− 1, spanning a range of 400–4000 cm− 1 [36].

% Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL)

To assess the encapsulation efficiency (%EE) and drug loading (%DL), 500 mg of each nanoparticle was separately dispersed in 10 mL of medium (distilled water with 2% (v/v) of tween 80). The resulting dispersion was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 20 min at 25 °C (Sigma 4-16KHS heated refrigerated centrifuge, Germany). The supernatant was collected, and the concentration of free, unencapsulated AEO was analyzed by UV–Vis spectrophotometry (Agilent Technologies, Cary 60, Santa Clara, CA, USA) at 225 nm wavelength (n = 3) [37]. The release and release kinetic study of AEO from NPs

A study was conducted to examine the in vitro release of AEO from SLN and chitosan nanoparticles using a dialysis bag with a 12–14 kDa cutoff. The release medium was distilled water with 2% Tween 80. A total of 1200 mg of AEO-loaded nanoparticles was placed in the dialysis bag, which was stirred at 100 rpm and maintained at 37 °C in 100 mL of receptor medium. At intervals (0, 1, 3, 6, 24, 48, and 72 h), 3 mL samples were drawn and replaced with fresh medium to remain the sink condition, and samples were analyzed using UV spectrophotometry at 225 nm [38] (n = 3). The release kinetics of AEO from nanoparticles were examined by evaluating the in vitro release rates through different mathematical models, including zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Hixson-Crowell, and Korsmeyer-Peppas, using Microsoft Office 2016 Excel [39].

Cell culture and treatment

The HU02 fibroblast cell line, derived from healthy skin, was obtained from the Iranian Biological Resource Center (Tehran, Iran). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin) and were kept in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After the third passage, the cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in a 96-well culture plate for the MTT test and a 6-well culture plate for lipid peroxidation and glutathione content analysis. Non-adherent cells were removed, and adherent cells were exposed to different concentrations of the samples (the AEO loading in nanoparticles, based on DL% and EE% results, for both AEO-SLN and AEO-CH, 1 mg of NPs approximately equals to 0.3 mg of AEO). The experimental samples were categorized into specific groups as described below:

i.Control group: HU02 cells cultured in DMEM medium and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Control group: HU02 cells cultured in DMEM medium and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

ii.Positive control group: HU02 cells exposed to H2O2 (200 µM).

Positive control group: HU02 cells exposed to H2O2 (200 µM).

iii.NPs group: Various concentrations of AEO-loaded SLN and chitosan (1, 6, and 12 µg mL−1- containing 0.3, 1.8 and 3.6 µg mL−1 AEO).

NPs group: Various concentrations of AEO-loaded SLN and chitosan (1, 6, and 12 µg mL−1- containing 0.3, 1.8 and 3.6 µg mL−1 AEO).

iv.NPs group + H2O2: Various concentrations of AEO-loaded SLN and chitosan (1, 6, 12 µg mL−1) in combination with H2O2 (200 µM).

NPs group + H2O2: Various concentrations of AEO-loaded SLN and chitosan (1, 6, 12 µg mL−1) in combination with H2O2 (200 µM).

The cells were pretreated with various concentrations of SLN and CH (1, 6 and 12 µg mL−1) for 24 h. Cell treatment with H2O2 was performed for 4 h.

Various concentrations of NPs were utilized in the MTT assay to identify the most effective concentration. Subsequently, only the most effective NP concentration (6 µg mL−1) was employed to assess lipid peroxidation and the levels of glutathione. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cytotoxicity assay

NPs were tested for their ability to inhibit cell growth in the HU02 cell line using the MTT assay. After exposure, MTT solution (20 µL, 5 mg mL−1 in PBS) and DMEM were added to the cells and incubated for 4 h. MTT solution is highly sensitive to light and therefore was wrapped around the container and placed in the refrigerator. The supernatants were then discarded, and DMSO (150 µL) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals and shaken for 10 min. The optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm using an absorbance microplate reader (BioTek) (Epoch™ Microplate Spectrophotometer, BioTek) [36].

Measurement of lipid peroxidation

The level of MDA was assessed using a commercial colorimetric assay kit from ZellBio GmbH, located in Ulm, Germany (CAT No. ZB-MDA-48 A). This assay kit adheres to the manufacturer’s guidelines for evaluating the rate of lipid peroxidation by measuring the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), which is the end product of lipid peroxidation. In this study, thiobarbituric acid (TBA) functioned as the indicator, and the absorbance of the formed complex was recorded at 532 nm with an ELISA reader [40].

Measurement of glutathione content

The intracellular glutathione (GSH) of HU02 cells was measured using the glutathione assay kit (ZellBio GmbH, Ulm, Germany, CAT No. ZB-GSH-48 A). This kit employs a colorimetric method to analyze either total GSH or the reduced form GSH alone using a microtiter plate reader. Absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a microplate reader. Subsequently, the GSH level was calculated based on the GSH standard curve and equation [41].

Antibacterial assay

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

This research examined the antimicrobial effects of AEO and AEO-NPs against standard strains of S. typhimurium (ATCC 14028) and E. coli (ATCC 25922), in addition to ten isolates of multidrug-resistant (MDR) E. coli and S. typhimurium. To assess the susceptibility of the ten isolates of E. coli and S. typhimurium, collected from Shahrivar Children’s Hospital in Rasht, Iran, the disk-diffusion technique was employed according to the standards established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). The following antibiotics were tested (PadtanTeb Co, Iran): gentamicin (GM, 10 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), meropenem (MEN, 10 µg), ampicillin (AM, 10 µg), ciprofloxacin (CP, 5 µg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 1.25/23.75 µg), amikacin (AN, 30 µg), piperacillin-tazobactam (PTZ, 100/10 µg), levofloxacin (LEV, 5 µg), cefepime (FEP, 30 µg), ceftriaxone (CRO, 30 µg), cefazolin (CZ, 30 µg), and nitrofurantoin (FM, 300 µg). The CLSI standards were followed to interpret the results of susceptibility or resistance to each antibiotic, and the strains that showed resistance to at least three antibiotic classes were considered MDR [42].

Minimum inhibitory/ bactericidal concentration determination

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the AEO and AEO-NPs was examined using the broth microdilution method. In this study, various concentrations of AEO and AEO-NPs were tested on a sterile 96-well plate. Each sample was serially diluted from a concentration of 5 mg mL−1 to 0.015 mg mL−1 in Mueller-Hinton Broth (MHB). The concentration of microbes in each well was set to 106 CFU mL− 1, adjusting to a 0.5 McFarland standard, then diluting in MHB. Negative controls were MHB and the antimicrobial solutions and for positive control the bacterial inoculum was used. After 24 h of incubation at a temperature of 37 °C, the plates were visually examined to determine the level of turbidity. The precision of bacterial growth inhibition was verified by measuring the absorbance of each well using an ELISA reader set at a wavelength of 640 nm. Then, the MIC value was determined as the minimum concentration of the antimicrobial substance that prevents bacterial growth after 24 h. To determine the MBC, 0.1 mL of the solution from each well with no visible growth was cultured on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates. The plates were then incubated aerobically at 37 °C for next 24 h. The MBC is the minimum concentration of sample required to eliminate the 99.9% of bacterial population [43].

Anti-biofilm assay

The biofilm-forming potential of E. coli and S. typhimurium isolates with the highest resistance was evaluated using biofilm inhibition test and the microtiter plate spectrophotometric assay [44]. The bacterial strains (E2 and S3) were inoculated in MHB containing AEO-NPs and AEO with the concentrations of 1/2, 1/4, and 1/8 MIC and incubated at 37 °C for 24 and 48 h. Bacterial cells with MHB were used as a positive control (to form mature biofilm), and MHB without bacteria containing AEO-NPs as a negative control. Following the incubation period, each well was rinsed three times with sterile PBS, after which methanol was added to each well to preserve the biofilms. Subsequently, the methanol was discarded and the plates were left to dry at room temperature. In each well, 150 µL of a 1% crystal violet stain was applied to color the adhered cells. After allowing it to sit for 10 min, the contents of the wells were discarded, and then 200 µL of 95% ethanol was introduced, with the optical density (OD) of the stained bacterial films being assessed at 570 nm using an ELISA reader (n = 3). Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPadInStat software and SPSS 22.0 software. Statistical analyses, such as One-way ANOVA and independent t-tests, were performed to determine any significant differences among groups. The results were reported as mean ± SD. Differences were considered statistically significant when the P value was less than 0.05.

Results

Physicochemical characterization of nanoparticles

Particle size and zeta potential

The nanoparticles size and zeta potential were measured by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). The results demonstrated that the unfiltered AEO-SLN exhibited an average particle size of approximately 292.66 nm, with a negative charge of -21.9 mV and a PDI of 0.32 (Fig. 1a, b). In contrast, the average size of AEO-CH was around 188.41 nm with a PDI of 0.158. The zeta potential for AEO-CH was + 29.7 mV (Fig. 1c, d). The relatively high zeta potential suggests that the particles repel each other, leading to a stable colloidal dispersion. Also, the positive zeta potential of AEO-CH (+ 29.7 mV) is characteristic of chitosan and suggests strong electrostatic stabilization, which may also enhance interaction with negatively charged bacterial membranes.

Encapsulation efficiency and drug loading

The results of EE and DL are presented in Table 1. According to calculations, the AEO-CH had EE and DL values of 72.63 ± 0.31% and 30.21 ± 0.13% respectively, while the AEO-SLN showed EE and DL values of 67.7 ± 0.01% and 28.16 ± 0.003% respectively. The EE values > 67% for both formulations confirm the efficiency of the probe-sonication and ionic gelation methods in encapsulating the hydrophobic AEO.

ATR-FTIR spectrometry

Based on FTIR spectra (Fig. 3), strong absorption peaks at 3021 cm− 1 and 3010 cm− 1 were identified, representing the C-H stretching vibration peaks of the benzene ring and the C=C, respectively. The sharp absorption peaks between 1608 cm− 1 to 1446 cm− 1 are describing the stretching vibration peaks of the benzene ring. The peaks at 1248, 1175 and 1038 cm− 1 are presenting the stretching vibration peaks of C–O–C. The peaks at 842 cm− 1 and 790 cm− 1 are defining C–H bending vibration peaks of the substituent aromatics. Aforementioned characteristic peaks of AEO are mainly observable in AEO-SLN and AEO-CH spectra, confirming the stability of AEO in utilized nano-carriers. The FTIR spectra for incorporated excipients was previously studied [45, 46].

In vitro release and release kinetic

Figure 4 displays the AEO release profile from NPS. The drug release from both nanoparticles was nearly identical for the first 6 h; however, after that period, chitosan exhibited a slight increase in drug release. After 72 h, the release profile indicated 87.06 ± 3.59% for AEO-CH and 84.07 ± 4.11% for AEO-SLN. Both AEO-CH and AEO-SLN showed an increasing release trend over time. These results demonstrate that the cumulative drug release from nanoparticles is controlled and sustained over 72 h. Additionally, the release rate of AEO from chitosan was relatively higher than that of AEO-SLN during this period which may be attributed to the more hydrophilic and swellable nature of the chitosan matrix compared to the solid lipid core of the SLNs.

Determination of MIC and MBC

The results for MIC and MBC of AEO, AEO-SLN, and AEO-CH were determined against standard microorganisms as well as multidrug-resistant E. coli and S. typhimurium using the broth microdilution method, as shown in Table 4. The findings from the experiment demonstrated clear antimicrobial activity against the tested microorganisms, with S. typhimurium displaying greater susceptibility to Anise essential oil compared to E. coli. For E. coli, the determined MICs for AEO, AEO-SLN, and AEO-CH ranged from 560 to 570, 220 to 225, and 50 µg mL− 1, while the MBC values were between 885 and 895, 475 to 480, and 80 µg mL− 1, respectively (Table 4a). In addition, against S. typhimurium, the MICs were recorded as 540 to 550, 200 to 205, and 45 to 50 µg mL− 1, with MBC concentrations of 880 to 890, 465 to 475, and 80 µg mL− 1, respectively (Table 5). Conversely, solid lipid nanoparticles without the essential oil exhibited no antibacterial activity against either strain, and chitosan nanoparticles lacking Anise oil showed only a minimal effect (Tables 4 and 5). The results indicated that AEO had the least antibacterial efficacy against both microorganisms when compared to the nanoparticles. Although a comparison of the MIC and MBC ranges indicated that SLN enhances the antimicrobial properties of the pure oil against the tested microorganisms, AEO-CH demonstrated a significant improvement in antimicrobial effect as a nano-carrier system.

Anti-biofilm activity

The ability of clinical isolates to form biofilms was assessed through crystal violet staining. Following initial antibacterial evaluations, isolates E3 and S5 were identified as the most resistant and were chosen to test the anti-biofilm effectiveness of nanoparticles. At the concentrations of 1/2 MIC, 1/4 MIC, and 1/8 MIC AEO-CH showed 52.12%, 45.11%, 25.56% inhibition on E. coli and 55.87%, 48.07%, 28.24% inhibition on S. typhimurium biofilm after 24 h, respectively (Fig. 9 a, b). Results also demonstrated that after 48 h, the biofilm inhibitory effect of AEO-CH decreased slightly to 50.61%, 43.22%, 24.04% on E3 and to 52.84%, 46.1%, and 27.05% on S5. At the concentration of 1/2 MIC and 1/4 MIC, the inhibitory rates of AEO-SLN in 24 h were 41.82% 32.32% on E3 and 44.16%, and 33.88% on S5, respectively. These values significantly decreased to 32.24%, 18.22% (E3), and 34.61%, 18.94% (S5) after 48 h. Also, evaluation of pure oil showed inhibition of biofilm formation in 24 h in concentrations of 1/2 MIC and 1/4 MIC were 28.53%, 16.52%, and 30.81%, 17.62% in E3 and S5 respectively. The findings indicated that after 48 h of incubation, AEO-CH exhibited a notably greater inhibitory effect compared to both AEO-SLN and the pure oil. Discussion

Chemical additives are frequently utilized in the processing and preservation of food to inhibit microbial growth and oxidation and extend the shelf life. In this regard, essential oils derived from different plant parts have been thoroughly researched for their possible applications in the food sector [47]. They are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) substances for human consumption by the Food and Drug Administration [48].Hence, Anise essential oil’s effectiveness in inhibiting the development and proliferation of E. coli and S. typhimurium is thoroughly established due to its antimicrobial and antioxidant characteristics [49, 50]. However, the antibacterial activity of AEO-SLN compared to AE-CH has not yet been evaluated, yet. In this paper, we evaluated nanoparticles as a delivery system for AEO to inhibit the growth of pathogens responsible for food spoilage and foodborne illness for controlling post-processing contamination and extending shelf life in real food systems.

Comparing the MIC and MBC of AEO and AEO-SLN in this study revealed that AEO-SLN significantly decreased the MIC and MBC to approximately 200 to 225 µg mL− 1 and 465 to 480 µg mL− 1 (about twofold decrease) for both microorganisms (P value < 0.001). However, to our knowledge, there is no study on AEO loaded SLN and its antimicrobial effects, several studies have confirmed the potentiating influence of SLN as nanocarrier for antimicrobial agents. For example, in antibiotics, the MIC of Ofloxacin against P. aeruginosa showed a nearly two-fold decrease, when loading in SLN [51]. Furthermore, SLN was observed to increase the fungicidal activity of tea tree oil against C. guilliermondii and other species [52]. Likewise, encapsulation of lavender oil into SLN showed a threefold decrease in the MIC of lavender oil-loaded SLN against S. aureus compared to pure oil [43]. On the other hand, the antioxidant activity of AEO was previously reported and the strengthening effect of nano-formulations of AEO was studied [17, 53, 54] which suggests Sustained release and protection of AEO by nano-formulations to improve antioxidant protection in HU02 cells. Considering the lipid structure of AEO and nano-formulations, the underlying mechanism is not exactly discovered, it is suggested that solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) can prevent the evaporation of the essential oil, thus increasing its stability and duration of action [55, 56]. Also, solid lipid nanoparticles may enhance contact with bacterial surfaces and sustain release, potentially improving antibiofilm effect [57]. SLNs can enhance the antimicrobial activity of drugs by increasing the drug’s ability to penetrate the bacterial cell wall. The use of this nanoparticle reduces drug release by bacterial pumps and protects antimicrobial agents from enzymes [58–60].

Also, current results indicated that AEO-CH markedly reduced the MIC and MBC to 45–50 µg mL− 1 and 80 µg mL− 1, respectively compared to AEO-SLN and pure AEO. The functional enhancement of the antimicrobial agents by chitosan has been vastly reported, specifically regarding AEO, Pimpinella anisum essential oil in chitosan nano biopolymer CS-PAEO-Nm exhibited improved antifungal activity and anti-aflatoxigenic activity. The CS-PAEO-Nm treatment significantly inhibited ergosterol, enhanced ion leakage, and induced impairment in defense enzymes [61]. Moreover, chitosan nano-carriers markedly enhanced the antibacterial efficacy of several antibiotic medicines, such as cefotaxime incorporated into chitosan as a nano-antibiotic and cefazolin loaded into chitosan nanoparticles, corroborating our data [62, 63].

The precise mechanism behind the antifungal activity of chitosan is not fully understood. Nonetheless, it appears to depend on both the specific characteristics of the target organism, such as its cell wall or plasma membrane composition and its ability to break down chitosan and the degree of polycationic strength of the chitosan itself, which is influenced by factors like the degree of acetylation and the pH of the environment [64, 65]. These findings support the idea that chitosan may interact externally with negatively charged surfaces of the cell wall and membrane, leading to cell leakage and potentially cell death, as well as the possibility of internal interactions where chitosan binds to proteins or nucleic acids and disrupts normal metabolic processes [66, 67]. Both mechanisms may be at play, as chitosan polymers and oligomers likely have different modes of action. Larger chitosan polymers probably do not penetrate the cell, instead acting externally to compromise cell wall or membrane integrity. This disruption might then allow smaller molecules to enter the cell and exert intracellular effects, which could explain the synergistic antimicrobial activity observed when both forms are used together [68]. It is also suggested that the observed synergy results from different modes of action, such as chitosan interacting with the cell surface to facilitate the entry of other antifungal agents into the cell [69].

Conclusion

In order to introduce a novel effective natural preservative against foodborne pathogens, AEO was encapsulated in two different nano-carriers. The results indicated that nanoencapsulation of AEO into solid lipid and chitosan nanoparticles, significantly increased its antibacterial and antioxidant potencies. Among the nano-carriers, chitosan was the leading candidate due to its markedly superior antibacterial efficacy compared to SLN. However, However, this study faces some limitations, only performing in vitro assays, and future work should test the formulations in real food matrices and sensory contexts.
