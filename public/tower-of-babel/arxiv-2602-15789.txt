Displacement general solutions in strain gradient elasticity: review and analysis
Y. Solyaev, E. Hamouda, S. Sherbakov

Abstract
In this work, we provide an overview of general solutions for displacement fields in static problems of isotropic strain gradient elasticity (SGE). We not only review existing solutions but also derive new representations, showing that all classical elasticity solutions – including those of Boussinesq-Galerkin, Papkovich-Neuber, Naghdi, Lamé, Love and Boussinesq – can be simply generalized to SGE framework. In general, it is shown that SGE enables the use of any classical general solution representation combined with a Helmholtz decomposition for the gradient part of the displacement field. Consistency is also established between the presented Papkovich-Neuber representation and the general solutions of SGE proposed previously by Mindlin (1964), Lurie et al. (2006) and Charalambopoulos et al. (2020). Furthermore, we establish the relationships between the stress functions of different general solutions and show their completeness.

2
Preliminaries

When constructing solutions for gradient theories of grade two (in any area of continuum mechanics and physics
[
41
,
54
,
55
]
), one frequently encounters equations of the following form:

\left(1-l^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{B},

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{B}
are the scalar (or vector) fields (
\mathcal{F}
is the field variable to be determined, while
\mathcal{B}
is the prescribed right hand side); and the length scale parameter is real (
l\in\mathbb{R}
) so that the modified Helmholtz equation is represented by the operator
\left(1-l^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)
.

General solution of equation (
2
) can be presented in the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{c}+\mathcal{F}_{g},\qquad\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{c}=\mathcal{B},\qquad\left(1-l^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathcal{F}_{g}=l^{2}\mathcal{B}

(3)

in which
\mathcal{F}_{c}
and
\mathcal{F}_{g}
are the general solutions of
the Poisson equation and inhomogeneous modified Helmholtz equation,
respectively.

Representation (
3
) is useful since the general solutions and many kinds of particular solutions of obtained classical second-order equations are well known
[
56
,
57
]
.
Representation (
3
) is also complete in the sense that for any solution
\mathcal{F}
of equation (
2
) there exist the corresponding solutions
\mathcal{F}_{c}
and
\mathcal{F}_{g}
that can be found in the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}=\left(1-l^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathcal{F},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{g}=l^{2}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}

(4)

The proof of representations (
3
) and (
4
) is straightforward and can be found, e.g., in
[
41
,
54
]
.

In the following derivations we will involve the Green’s functions for determination of particular solutions of the Poisson equation and inhomogeneous modified Helmholtz equation that are given by the following convolutions (in three-dimensional space):

\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{D}\frac{\mathcal{B}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)}{\left|\mathbf{r}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\right|}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

(5)

\mathcal{H}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{D}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\left|\mathbf{r}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\right|/l}\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\xi})}{\left|\mathbf{r}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\right|}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

(6)

Finally, we will also refer to Helmholtz theorem that states that every sufficiently smooth vector field
\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)
(with appropriate behaviour at infinity
[
13
,
58
]
) can be written as follows:

\mathbf{u}=\nabla\varphi+\nabla\times\textbf{A}

(7)

where
\varphi
is the scalar potential,
A
is the vector
potential that is assumed to be solenoidal (
\nabla\cdot\textbf{A}=0
) without loss of generality, and it is valid that

\nabla^{2}\varphi=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u},\qquad\varphi=\mathcal{N}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}),\qquad\nabla^{2}\textbf{A}=-\nabla\times\mathbf{u}

(8)

in which the integral convolution operator
\mathcal{N}
is defined according to (
5
).

In classical elasticity, the fields
\varphi
and
A
should satisfy the Poisson equations. This result follows from classical equilibrium equations that can be obtained from (
1
) assuming
l_{1}=l_{2}=0
. Thus, using (
7
), (
8
) and similar Helmholtz decomposition for the body force (
\textbf{b}=\nabla\varphi_{b}+\nabla\times\textbf{A}_{b}
) in classical elasticity we have
[
8
]
:

\alpha\nabla\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}-\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{u}=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu}\quad\implies\quad\alpha\mu\nabla^{2}\varphi=-\varphi_{b},\quad\mu\nabla^{2}\textbf{A}=-\textbf{A}_{b}

(9)

The direct application of the Helmholtz theorem to static problems of classical elasticity is limited to incompressible materials
[
53
,
8
]
. This limitation arises because, according to (
8
) and (
9
), the displacement field must be divergence-free when
\mathbf{b}=0
(or at least when
\varphi_{b}=0
).
The Helmholtz theorem’s utility increases in the context of SGE. Here, it offers a direct representation of the general solution for the gradient part of the displacement field. Charalambopoulos et al.
[
38
]
first highlighted this application for a simplified SGE (with
l_{1}=l_{2}
). Section 3.8 of this paper will address the formulation for the general theory. An example of direct application of decomposition of the type (
7
) within plane problems of SGE was considered in Ref.
[
59
]
.

Notations
.
In the following sections we employ direct notation, with
boldface
for vectors and
italics
for scalars. Subscripts ”
c
” and ”
g
” denote the classical and gradient parts, respectively, of field variables (e.g., displacement, stress functions). An asterisk superscript (*) indicates particular solutions and related fields.

3
General solution representations in SGE

3.1
Mindlin solution

Mindlin
[
18
]
derived the following form of general solution for equation (
1
):

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{B}-l_{2}^{2}\nabla\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\kappa-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\left(\mathbf{r}\cdot\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{B}+B_{0}\right),

(10)

where
\kappa=\frac{\lambda+\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2(1-\nu)}
and the stress functions
\mathbf{B}
and
B_{0}
have to
satisfy the equations

\displaystyle\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu},

(11)

\displaystyle\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}B_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\mathbf{r}\cdot\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{b}-4l_{1}^{2}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}\right)

When
l_{1}=l_{2}=0
representation (
10
) reduces to classical Papkovich-Neuber solution. However, within SGE this representation proves to be quite complex. First, the governing equations for the potentials (
11
) are of the fourth order. While this issue is easily resolvable, and the representation can be simplified by using (
2
) and (
3
), a more significant difficulty lies in expressing the displacement solution through high-order derivatives of the potentials. In (
10
), it is necessary to compute derivatives up to the fifth order, which is rather inconvenient from a computational standpoint. Consequently, subsequent work in this field has aimed at simplifying representation (
10
).

3.2
Lurie-Belov-Volkov-Bogorodskiy (LBVB) solution

The possibility of representing the general solution to an equation (
1
) as a sum of classical and gradient parts appears to have been first established in Refs.
[
29
,
30
]
. This representation was initially proposed for a simplified gradient theory with a single length scale parameter. It was later generalized to equilibrium equations of Mindlin Form II (
1
) as follows
[
36
,
33
]
:

\displaystyle\textbf{u}

\displaystyle=\textbf{u}_{c}+\textbf{u}_{g},

(12)

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{c}

\displaystyle=\textbf{F}_{0}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\nabla(\textbf{r}\cdot\textbf{F}_{0}+f),

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{g}

\displaystyle=\textbf{F}_{2}-\nabla\nabla\cdot(l_{1}^{2}\textbf{F}_{1}+l_{2}^{2}\textbf{F}_{2})

where
\textbf{u}_{c}
is the classical part of the solution, which is defined through the classical Papkovich-Neuber harmonic vector potential
\textbf{F}_{0}
and scalar potential
f
;
\textbf{u}_{g}
is the gradient part of the solution that is defined through the two additional vector potentials
\textbf{F}_{1}
,
\textbf{F}_{2}
.

In absence of body forces, classical potentials should satisfy the Laplace equation:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\textbf{F}_{0}=0,\qquad\nabla^{2}f=0

(13)

while the gradient potentials should obey the modified Helmholtz equations:

\displaystyle\textbf{F}_{1}-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\textbf{F}_{1}=0,\qquad\textbf{F}_{2}-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\textbf{F}_{2}=0

(14)

A representation for the case of nonzero body forces can be found in a recent publication
[
33
]
. Since it employs different notation for the length scale parameters, we omit it here. An additional reason for this omission is that the representation to be introduced in Section 3.5 is very similar and is already formulated for nonzero body forces. For comparing these two solutions, the representation presented in Eq. (
12
)-(
14
) will be sufficient.

We can also note the redundancy in the number of independent functions used to represent the gradient part of the displacement field in (
12
)-(
14
). Clearly, the vector function
\textbf{F}_{1}
can be replaced by a scalar, since only its divergence is involved in the representation (
12
).
Thus, this representation involves four functions to determine the classical part of the displacement field (
\textbf{F}_{0}
,
f
) and an additional four independent quantities (
\nabla\cdot\textbf{F}_{1}
,
\textbf{F}_{2}
) for its gradient part.

As is known, for the classical Papkovich-Neuber representation (and consequently, for the classical part of the displacement field
\textbf{u}_{c}
in (
12
)), the use of four independent functions is necessary only for the case
\nu=0.25

[
8
]
. No such constraint applies to the gradient part of the displacement field
\textbf{u}_{g}
. Therefore, to represent its three components, it is sufficient to use only three independent functions.
This fact prompted the subsequent modification of the representation (
12
), which eliminated the redundant number of potentials for the gradient part of the displacement field (see Section 3.5).

3.3
Charalambopoulos-Gortsas-Polyzos (CGP) solution

In the paper
[
39
]
, the authors proposed the simplification of Mindlin’s general solution for SGE with single length scale parameter (
l_{1}=l_{2}=l
). It was shown that the displacement representation (
10
) can be replaced by the following:

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{B}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\nabla\left(\mathbf{r}\cdot\left(1-l^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{B}+B_{0}\right),

(15)

where the stress functions
\mathbf{B}
and
B_{0}
are the same as those in the Mindlin solution (
10
) and satisfy governing equations (
11
) under condition
l_{1}=l_{2}=l
.

This representation of the general solution (
15
) involves third-order derivatives of the potentials, therefore it is simpler than the Mindlin solution (
10
). Furthermore, considering representation (
3
), (
4
) for (
11
), we find that both the classical and gradient parts of the displacement field can be expressed through four independent functions each. Thus, the total number of independent functions that should be used to define CGP general solution is 8 that is similar to LBVB solution.

3.4
Generalized Boussinesq-Galerkin (BG) solution

Choosing appropriate definitions of Helmholtz potentials
\varphi
and
\mathbf{A}
(
7
) in terms of a single vector function
\mathbf{G}
,
an
extended form of the Boussinesq-Galerkin (BG) general solution of the
equilibrium equations (
1
) was derived in the following form

[
41
]
:

\mathbf{u}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\nabla\cdot\mathbf{G}-\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{G}

(16)

or equivalently

\mathbf{u}=\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathbf{G}-\kappa\left(1-l_{3}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\nabla\cdot\mathbf{G}

(17)

where
l_{3}^{2}=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}l_{2}^{2}-l_{1}^{2}\right)

and
\mathbf{G}
is the Galerkin vector stress function
that satisfies the following governing bi-Helmholtz/bi-Laplace equation:

\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\nabla^{2}\mathbf{G}=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu}

(18)

The BG representation is rather complex for solving particular boundary-value problems, as it requires the computation of high-order derivatives of
G
. However, it is convenient for proving the completeness of general solutions in SGE, by analogy with the classical approach
[
13
,
41
]
. It appears that a similar representation was first introduced in the work
[
60
]
within the third gradient elasticity theory. This work is rarely cited, and we only recently came across it, even though it also presents a generalization of Mindlin’s solution (
10
) to the third-order theory.

3.5
Generalized Papkovich-Neuber (PN) solution

Based on the analysis of the structure of LBVB general solution (
12
)-(
14
), it was shown in Ref.
[
40
]
that the number of potentials determining the gradient part of the displacement field can be reduced, and the representation (
12
) can be simplified. Subsequently, the resulting form of the general solution was derived in Ref.
[
41
]
based on deductive reasoning generalizing the classical Papkovich approach
[
3
]
. As a result, the simplest form of the Papkovich-Neuber (PN) general solution within SGE took the following form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{c}+\mathbf{u}_{g},

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{c}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\nabla\left(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{c}+\phi_{c}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{g}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{g}+l_{1}^{2}\nabla\phi_{g},

in which the stress functions
\mathbf{B}_{c},\mathbf{B}_{g},\phi_{c},\phi_{g}
have to obey the following governing
equations:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\mathbf{B}_{c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu},

(20)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mu}+2l_{1}^{2}\left(1-2\upsilon\right)\frac{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mu},

\displaystyle\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{B}_{g}

\displaystyle=-l_{2}^{2}\frac{\hat{\mathbf{b}}}{\mu},\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}_{g}=0,

\displaystyle\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\phi_{g}

\displaystyle=-l_{1}^{2}\frac{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\alpha\mu}

where
\hat{\mathbf{b}}
is the rotational part of the body load (related, e.g., to Coriolis or ponderomotive forces)
1
1

1

The final solution representation in Ref.
[
41
]
contains
\mathbf{b}
instead of
\hat{\mathbf{b}}
. This is a typo, as the derivation clearly requires
\hat{\mathbf{b}}
to maintain consistency with the right-hand side of the governing equation for
\textbf{B}_{g}
(see Eqs. (41), (42) in
[
41
]
).
.

An advantage of this solution is that, similar to the LBVB representation, it explicitly separates the classical and gradient parts of the displacement field. The classical part
\textbf{u}_{c}
coincides with the standard PN representation that is defined by
\mathbf{B}_{c}
and
\phi_{c}
. The gradient part is expressed through the minimal necessary number of additional potentials: a scalar potential
\phi_{g}
for the irrotational part of
\textbf{u}_{g}
, and a solenoidal vector function
\mathbf{B}_{g}
defining the rotational part of
\textbf{u}_{g}
.
By using standard representations from the vector analysis
[
56
]
, solenoidal field
\mathbf{B}_{g}
can be represented via two scalar potentials satisfying the Helmholtz equations (see, e.g.
[
40
,
43
]
). Therefore, in total we have only three independent functions that are used to define three components of
\textbf{u}_{g}
.
Moreover, the displacement representation in Eqs. (
19
), (
20
) involves only first-order derivatives of the potentials. Owing to its simplicity, we refer to this specific form as the generalized PN representation within SGE throughout this work.

Note that the representation for the gradient part of the displacement field,
\textbf{u}_{g}
in (
19
), (
20
), can be viewed as a Helmholtz decomposition. This decomposition yields irrotational and solenoidal parts,
\nabla\phi_{g}
and
\textbf{B}_{g}
, each satisfying a Helmholtz equation with a distinct length scale parameter (
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
, respectively). Additional discussion of this decomposition is provided in Section 3.8.

Reduction of LBVB solution (
12
)-(
14
) to the presented form of PN solution (
19
), (
20
) was discussed in Ref.
[
40
]
. Namely, it was shown that the relations between stress functions are the following:

\displaystyle\textbf{F}_{0}=\textbf{B}_{c},\quad f=\phi_{c},\quad\textbf{F}_{2}=\textbf{B}_{g},\quad\nabla\cdot\textbf{F}_{1}=-\phi_{g}

(21)

The reduced form of PN representation (
19
), (
20
) with
l_{2}=0
and
\textbf{B}_{g}=0
for the so-called dilatation gradient elasticity theory was considered in Ref.
[

]
. The representation for the couple stress theory
[

]
can be obtained if we put

and
\phi_{g}=0
in (

), (

3.6
Generalized Ter-Mkrtychan-Naghdi-Hsu (TNH) solution

In classical elasticity, TNH solution employs an integral representation of the displacement field through a single vector function

6

. An advantage of this representation is the fact that
\mathbf{P}
is unique
[
63
]
. In classical elasticity, TNH representation can be generalized to a nonlinear formulation
[
64
]
and to media with spatially varying properties
[
62
]
.

At first, we present the derivation of the general TNH representation that extends an approach originally introduced within classical elasticity theory
[
6
]
. Following this approach in SGE it becomes possible to derive two distinct TNH-type representations. They differ in the definition of the vector potential, which is expressed via the Green’s function for the Helmholtz equation (
6
) with either parameter
l_{1}
or
l_{2}
, responsible for gradient effects in the potential or solenoidal part of the displacement field, respectively.

To obtain the first variant of generalized TNH solution within SGE, we represent the solution of equilibrium equations (
1
) according to the Heomholtz theorem (
7
) and representation (
8
):

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{1}+\mathbf{u}^{2},\qquad\mathbf{u}^{1}=\nabla\mathcal{N}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\right),\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^{2}=0

(22)

where we use notation
\mathbf{u}^{1}
and
\textbf{u}^{2}
for the potential and solenodial parts of
u
, respectively.

Substituting (
22
) into (
1
), we obtain

\nabla^{2}\left(\alpha\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{u}^{1}+\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{u}^{2}\right)=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu},

(23)

which can be written in the form

\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\left(\alpha\mathcal{H}_{2}\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{u}^{1}+\mathbf{u}^{2}\right)=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu},

(24)

where
\mathcal{H}_{2}
is the integral convolution operator (
6
) that corresponds to the Green’s function of modified Helmholtz equation with the length scale parameter
l_{2}
, i.e. it is valid that
(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2})\mathcal{H}_{2}\textbf{u}^{1}=\textbf{u}^{1}
.

We next define the vector function within the brackets in (
24
) by

\mathbf{P}=\alpha\mathcal{H}_{2}\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{u}^{1}+\mathbf{u}^{2}

(25)

This vector function
P
should be treated as TNH potential within SGE. Combining (
24
) and (
25
), we get governing equation for this potential:

\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathbf{P}=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu}

(26)

Thus, according to (
2
), (
3
), within the first variant of TNH solution the representation for
P
will contain the classical harmonic part that obeys the Poisson equation and the additional part that obeys the modified Helmholtz equation with the length scale parameter
l_{2}
.

Applying the Helmholtz theorem to
\mathbf{P}
, we can write

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}^{1}+\mathbf{P}^{2}

(27)

where
\mathbf{P}^{2}
is its solenoidal part (
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}^{2}=0
) and for the potential part
\mathbf{P}^{1}
we propose the following definition:

\mathbf{P}^{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}\nabla\mathcal{N}\left(\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)

(28)

This definition extends the classical variant
\mathbf{P}^{1}=\nabla\mathcal{N}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)

[
6
]
. We have selected this specific generalized form of
\textbf{P}^{1}
, which, upon substitution of (
25
) into (
28
), yields the following useful result (see Appendix A):

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{1}=\alpha\mathcal{H}_{2}\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{u}^{1}

(29)

Then, considering (
25
), (
27
) and (
29
) we find that:

\mathbf{P}^{2}=\mathbf{u}^{2}

(30)

It should be noted that the classical derivation of TNH solution leads to an analogous result (
30
) (see
[
6
]
). From (
29
), we also find that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{H}_{1}\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{P}^{1}

(31)

where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is the integral convolution operator (
6
) that corresponds to the Green’s function of the modified Helmholtz equation with the length scale parameter
l_{1}
.

Finally, using Eqs. (
22
), (
28
), (
30
) and (
31
), one can obtain the following form of generalized TNH solution within SGE (for derivations see Appendix A):

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{P}-\kappa\nabla\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)-\tfrac{l_{2}^{2}-l_{1}^{2}}{1-\alpha}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)\right)

(32)

where as stated above the TNH potential should obey the fourth-order governing equation (
26
).

An alternative form of TNH solution in SGE can be obtained if, in equation (
24
), we factor out the modified Helmholtz operator with the length scale parameter
l_{1}
. In this case, one can obtain the following representation (see Appendix A):

\mathbf{u}=\left(1-\left(l_{1}^{2}-l_{2}^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)\mathbf{P}-\nabla\left(\kappa\,\mathcal{N}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)-(l_{1}^{2}-l_{2}^{2})\mathcal{H}_{2}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)\right)

(33)

in which the governing equation for
P
is the following:

\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathbf{P}=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu}.

(34)

Note that classical TNH representation
[
6
]
follows from (
26
), (
32
) as well as from (
33
), (
34
) under the standard assumption
l_{1}=l_{2}=0

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{P}-\kappa\nabla\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)\right),\qquad\nabla^{2}\mathbf{P}=-\tfrac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu}

(35)

Taking into account (
2
), (
3
), it can be easily shown that TNH solution in SGE (
26
), (
32
) or (
33
), (
34
) also admits an additive decomposition into a classical part of the displacement field defined by (
35

l_{1}=l_{2}=l
the displacement representation formally reduces to classical relation, while remaining the higher-order governing equation for

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{P}-\kappa\nabla\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)\right),\qquad\left(1-l^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathbf{P}=-\tfrac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu}

(36)

Presented forms of TNH displacement general solutions within SGE (

[

3.7
Solution representation for specific boundary value problems

In classical elasticity, there exist several general solutions for specialized formulations, such as those for problems without a rotation field (Lamé strain potential) and for axisymmetric problems (Love’s solution and Boussinesq potentials). In this section, we demonstrate how these simplified representations can be derived from the general solutions of SGE presented above.

3.7.1
Generalized Lamé strain potential

Let us apply the Helmholtz decomposition (
7
) to the displacement field in (
1
) under the assumption of a zero vector potential
\textbf{A}=0
. This corresponds to a solution that contains no rotational part. The resulting representation for the displacement field and the governing equation for the scalar potential
\varphi
– referred to as the Lamé strain potential – are then as follows:

\mathbf{u}=\nabla\varphi,\qquad\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\nabla\varphi=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\alpha\mu}

(37)

where the body load
b
should also be a potential field.

The analysis and application of this representation for solving boundary value problems were discussed in detail in Ref.
[
52
]
within the simplified formulation with
l_{1}=l_{2}
. Note that in the general formulation of SGE, the influence of gradient effects may be determined not only by the value of the length scale parameter
l_{1}
but also by other additional constants of SGE, due to influence of boundary conditions.

This representation (
37
) can be also obtained as the particular case of the other general solutions. For example, we can use
\textbf{B}=0
in CGP solution (
15
) or
\textbf{B}_{c}=\textbf{B}_{g}=0
in PN solution (
19
), etc.

3.7.2
Generalized Love strain function

Considering axisymmetric problems, we can assume that the Galerkin vector in (
17
) has a non-zero projection only along the symmetry axis, e.g.
\textbf{G}=G(r,z)\textbf{e}_{z}
, where
G(r,z)
is the Love strain function defined in cylindrical coordinates
(r,\theta,z)
. In this case, we obtain the following form of the Love general solution within SGE:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathbf{G}-\kappa\left(1-l_{3}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{G}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathbf{G}-\kappa\left(1-l_{3}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial z}\right)

which in components becomes:

\displaystyle u_{r}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\left(1-l_{3}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial r\partial z},\qquad u_{\theta}=0

(39)

\displaystyle u_{z}

\displaystyle=\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}G-\kappa\left(1-l_{3}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial z^{2}}

The form of the governing equation for Galerkin vector (
18
) provides limitations for the cases, when the body load is acted along the axis of symmetry only. For the Love strain function it takes the form:

\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\nabla^{2}G=-\frac{b_{z}}{\mu}

(40)

This representation reduces to classical form, when
l_{1}=l_{2}=0
. Its possible generalization for the problems with radial body forces can be obtained following approach suggested in Ref.
[
67
]
, although the appropriate and more simple form of axisymmetric solution is provided by Boussinesq potentials introduced in the next subsection.

3.7.3
Generalized Boussinesq potentials

For axisymmetric problems, Boussinesq proposed a simplified representation of the general solution, in which the order of derivatives of the potentials is reduced compared to Love’s solution. Boussinesq axisymmetric solution can most easily be obtained as a special case of PN solution (
19
), (
20
), in which for classical part of displacement we should assume
\textbf{B}_{c}=\psi_{c}(r,z)\textbf{e}_{z}
,
\phi_{c}=\phi_{c}(r,z)
and for gradient part we can use
\textbf{B}_{g}=\nabla\times(\psi_{g}(r,z)\textbf{e}_{\theta})
,
\phi_{g}=\phi_{g}(r,z)
. Also we assume that the body load is the potential field
\textbf{b}=\nabla\phi_{b}(r,z)
. Then, from (
19
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{c}+\mathbf{u}_{g},

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{c}

\displaystyle=\psi_{c}\textbf{e}_{z}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\nabla\varphi_{c},

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{g}

\displaystyle=\nabla\times(\psi_{g}\textbf{e}_{\theta})+l_{1}^{2}\nabla\phi_{g},

where we also introduce classical Boussinesq potential
\varphi_{c}=z\psi_{c}+\phi_{c}
that is directly related to the scalar potential in standard Helmholtz decomposition (
7
) applied to
\mathbf{u}_{c}
.

The components of the displacement field (
41
) is given by:

\displaystyle u_{r}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\tfrac{\partial\varphi_{c}}{\partial r}-\tfrac{\partial\psi_{g}}{\partial z}+l_{1}^{2}\tfrac{\partial\phi_{g}}{\partial r}

(42)

\displaystyle u_{r}

\displaystyle=\psi_{c}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\tfrac{\partial\varphi_{c}}{\partial z}+\tfrac{\partial\psi_{g}}{\partial r}+\tfrac{\psi_{g}}{r}+l_{1}^{2}\tfrac{\partial\phi_{g}}{\partial z}

From (
20
) it follows that the stress functions
\psi_{c},\psi_{g},\varphi_{c},\phi_{g}
have to obey the following governing
equations:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\psi_{c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu}\tfrac{\partial\phi_{b}}{\partial z},

(43)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\varphi_{c}

\displaystyle=2\tfrac{\partial\psi_{c}}{\partial z}-\tfrac{z}{\mu}\tfrac{\partial\phi_{b}}{\partial z}+\tfrac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla\phi_{b}}{\mu}+2l_{1}^{2}\left(1-2\upsilon\right)\tfrac{\nabla^{2}\phi_{b}}{\mu}

\displaystyle\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\psi_{g}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\phi_{g}

\displaystyle=-l_{1}^{2}\tfrac{\nabla^{2}\phi_{b}}{\alpha\mu}

where the equation for
\varphi_{c}
is obtained by combining the equations for the classical PN potentials in (
20
).

3.8
General decomposition of displacement field into classical and gradient parts in SGE

Let us consider the following modified form of generalized PN solution (
19
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{c}+\mathbf{u}^{*}_{c}+\mathbf{u}_{g},

(44)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{c}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\nabla\left(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{c}+\hat{\phi}_{c}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{*}_{c}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\nabla\phi^{*}_{c}

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{g}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{g}+l_{1}^{2}\nabla\phi_{g}

where the stress functions
\mathbf{B}_{c},\mathbf{B}_{g},\phi_{g}
have to obey the governing equations that are given in (
20
); while the classical scalar stress function is decomposed into two parts
\phi_{c}=\hat{\phi}_{c}+\phi_{c}^{*}
that produce corresponding decomposition of classical part of displacement field
\textbf{u}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{c}+\mathbf{u}^{*}_{c}
. The introduced stress functions
\hat{\phi}_{c},\phi_{c}^{*}
are used to separate the part of classical solution, which definition totally coincides with classical PN representation, including the right hand side in the governing equations for its potentials that take the form:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\hat{\phi}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mu},\qquad\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}_{c}

\displaystyle=2l_{1}^{2}\left(1-2\upsilon\right)\frac{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mu}

(45)

Thus, from (
20
), (
44
), (
45
)
1
we see that definition for
\hat{\textbf{u}}_{c}
is the classical PN representation:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{c}=\mathbf{B}_{c}-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\nabla\left(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{c}+\hat{\phi}_{c}\right)

(46)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\hat{\phi}_{c}=\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mu},\quad\nabla^{2}\mathbf{B}_{c}=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu}

Since the completeness of classical PN representation is proven
[
16
]
, then we can state that
\hat{\textbf{u}}_{c}
is any kind of solution of equilibrium equations of classical elasticity, i.e. it is a solution of equation:

\displaystyle\alpha\nabla\nabla\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{c}-\nabla\times\nabla\times\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{c}=-\frac{\mathbf{b}}{\mu}

(47)

Therefore, to define
\hat{\textbf{u}}_{c}
we can use any kind of classical general solution that is known up to date (see, e.g.
[
16
,
68
]
) and that is appropriate for the considered problem. At the same time, for the remaining introduced part of the solution
\textbf{u}^{*}_{c}
we can just find appropriate particular solution that can be defined by using (
45
)
2
, (
8
) as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{u}^{*}_{c}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{l_{1}^{2}}{\alpha\mu}\nabla\varphi_{b}

(48)

where
\varphi_{b}
is the scalar potential that defines the irrotational part of the body load so that
\nabla^{2}\varphi_{b}=\nabla\cdot\textbf{b}
(see (
8
), (
9
)).

Finally, we find that the general solution of equilibrium equations of SGE (
1
) can be presented as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{c}+\mathbf{u}_{g}-\tfrac{l_{1}^{2}}{\alpha\mu}\nabla\varphi_{b}

(49)

where
\mathbf{u}_{c}
is any kind of general solution of classical linear isotropic elasticity including PN, TNH, BG or other (we avoid using hat symbol for this part of solution),
\varphi_{b}
is the potential of body load, and
\textbf{u}_{g}
is the gradient part of displacement field defined by Helmholtz decomposition according to (
19
):

\mathbf{u}_{g}=\mathbf{B}_{g}+\nabla\phi_{g},\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}_{g}=0

(50)

and its potentials should obey the following equations according to (
20
):

\displaystyle\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{B}_{g}=-l_{2}^{2}\tfrac{\hat{\mathbf{b}}}{\mu},\qquad\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\phi_{g}=-l_{1}^{4}\tfrac{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\alpha\mu}

(51)

Representation (
49
)-(
51
) can be considered as the extension of the results presented by Charalambopoulos et al.
[
38
]
for simplified SGE (with
l_{1}=l_{2}
) to the general Toupin-Mindlin SGE (with
l_{1}\neq l_{2}
) in the presence of arbitrary body load. Related discussion on the decomposition of SGE general solution into classical and gradient parts was also given in Refs.
[
29
,
30
,
32
,
33
,
69
,
31
]
.
We can also note the so-called Ru-Aifantis theorem in simplified SGE
[
70
,
46
]
, which provides a decomposition of the displacement field into interrelated gradient and classical parts. However, this theorem can be applied only in unbounded homogeneous domains
[
71
,
72
,
73
]
.

In some simple cases the representation (
49
)-(
51
) can be directly used for derivation of analytical solutions within SGE based on the known closed-form solutions of classical elasticity
\mathbf{u}_{c}
, although there exist many examples, when the coefficients of classical part of general solution are modified within SGE in comparison to classical solutions
[
26
,
28
,
36
]
. Moreover, there exist examples of boundary value problems, for which the closed-form classical solution cannot be reproduced in SGE and infinite series containing coupled classical and gradient terms arise (see, e.g.
[
74
]
).
Nevertheless, the representation given by Eqs. (
49
)-(
51
) can be useful in applications, as it enables the use of any classical general solution representation and provides a simple definition for the gradient part of the displacement field. It is also useful from a theoretical standpoint, for example, for testing the structure and correctness of analytical solutions within SGE.

4
Relations between stress functions

In order to derive the completeness theorem for any of the general solutions to SGE equilibrium equations (
1

Note that the equivalence of the potentials in Mindlin solution (Section 3.1) and in CGP solution (section 3.3) was shown in Ref.
[
39
]
. The variants of general solutions for special problems discussed in Section 3.7 are shown to be the particular cases of BG, PN and other solutions, so we also do not need to consider these special solutions separaterly. Relation between PN solution and LBVB solution was discussed in details in Ref.

]
and relations between their potentials are given by (

Therefore, we need to consider only the relations between BG and PN solutions, BG and TNH solutions, and PN and TNH solutions within SGE. \displaystyle\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla^{2}\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle-2(1-l_{3}^{2}\nabla^{2})\nabla\cdot\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H}_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathbf{B}_{c})))\Big]\Big]

Note that given relations (

4.2
BG and TNH potentials

The relation between BG and TNH potentials can be obtained by extension of the approach that was suggested within the classical elasticity in Ref.
[
63
]
, although this extension is not trivial.

Let us introduce the following auxiliary vector field that is related to TNH potential
P
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\mathcal{G}(\textbf{P}),\qquad(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2})\nabla^{2}\mathbf{M}=\textbf{P}

where
\mathcal{G}(\textbf{P})
defines the integral convolution of TNH potential

(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2})\nabla^{2}

\mathcal{G}(\textbf{P})
in terms of corresponding integral operators of Laplace equation (

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\textbf{P})=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{P})+l_{1}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\textbf{P})

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\mathcal{N}(\textbf{P})=\textbf{P},\qquad(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2})\mathcal{H}_{1}(\textbf{P})=\textbf{P}

Next, we observe, that the following identity is fulfilled:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\nabla\cdot\textbf{P})=\nabla\cdot\mathcal{G}(\textbf{P})+M_{0}\implies\nabla\cdot\textbf{M}=\mathcal{G}(\nabla\cdot\textbf{P})-M_{0}

Note that in classical elasticity

\displaystyle(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2})\nabla^{2}M_{0}=0

Let us introduce then the definition for Galerkin stress function

\displaystyle\textbf{G}=\textbf{M}-c_{1}(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2})\nabla\mathcal{G}(M_{0})+c_{2}\nabla M_{0}

The suggested definition (

\textbf{G}=\textbf{M}-c_{1}\nabla\mathcal{N}(M_{0})

)
[

In this expression (

5
Completeness of general solutions

The completeness of the general solution means it can represent any displacement field governed by equilibrium equations
[
53
,
10
]
.
Following classical approach (see, e.g.
[
53
,
16
]
), the completeness of general solutions in SGE can be proven considering relations between their potentials. The proof based on BG and PN solutions was presented in
[
41
]
. Let us extend this result to the all other kinds of SGE general solutions considered above.

We start with BG representation of general solution (
16
):

\mathbf{u}=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(1-l_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\nabla\cdot\mathbf{G}-\left(1-l_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{G}

(92)

that can be equivalently rewritten as:

\bar{\alpha}\left(1-\bar{l}_{1}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\nabla\cdot\mathbf{G}-\left(1-\bar{l}_{2}^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{G}=-\frac{\bar{\textbf{b}}}{\mu}

(93)

where we introduce
\bar{\alpha}=1/\alpha
,
\bar{\textbf{b}}=-\mu\textbf{u}
,
\bar{l}_{1}=l_{2}
and
\bar{l}_{2}=l_{1}
.

Suppose further that displacement field
u
(that persists in the right-hand side of equation (
93
)) is an arbitrary solution of the equilibrium equations (
1
).
Then we observe that equation (
93
) formally coincides with the form of equilibrium equations of SGE (
1
) up to notation. Consequently, its solution can be represented by using any kind of general solution discussed above. In Ref.
[
41
]
, the generalized PN solution was used to define
G
in terms of
u
. In the present case, we can employ the generalized TNH solution (
26
), (
32
), which is more compact:

\mathbf{G}=\bar{\textbf{P}}-\bar{\kappa}\nabla\left(\mathcal{N}(\nabla\cdot\bar{\textbf{P}})-\tfrac{\bar{l}_{2}^{2}-\bar{l}_{1}^{2}}{1-\bar{\alpha}}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\nabla\cdot\bar{\textbf{P}})\right)

(94)

where
\bar{\kappa}=(\bar{\alpha}-1)/\bar{\alpha}
and the stress function
\bar{\mathbf{P}}
is defined by:

\displaystyle\bar{\textbf{P}}=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{u})+\bar{l}_{2}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{2}(\textbf{u})

(95)

in which we take into account representation (
2
)-(
6
) to define the particular solution of governing equation (
32
).

Thus, we can derive an expression for the Galerkin vector function
G
based on any predefined displacement field
u
satisfying (
1
). This means that BG representation (
16
) is complete within SGE. Given all the previously established relationships between the potentials of various general solutions in Section 4, we can assert that all these solutions are also complete. This includes the completeness of Mindlin’s representation, which, to our knowledge, has not been previously discussed in the literature.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented an overview and analysis of displacement general solutions for the equilibrium equations of SGE. We have shown that, in fact, all classical solutions can be generalized to SGE by extending the classical approaches used to construct these solutions. Furthermore, we demonstrated that essentially all classical solutions can be simply generalized to SGE by means of the universal representation (
49
)-(
51
), which considers the simple sum of any classical general solution, the Helmholtz decomposition for gradient part of the displacement field, and additional term related to the potential of body forces.

In the introduction to this article, we provided a brief overview of known analytical and semi-analytical solutions that have employed displacement general solutions in SGE. We note that there are far fewer such examples in SGE compared to classical elasticity, which is due to the fact that their construction requires significantly more complex approaches, as well as the fact that many modern problems are solved by numerical methods. Nevertheless, the application of analytical general solutions can be extremely useful, such as in validating numerical methods based on closed-form solutions
[
76
]
, deriving expressions convenient for processing experimental data
[
28
]
, and for constructing and analyzing complicated asymptotic solutions
[
40
,
43
]
.

We note that this paper has yielded several new results, including the derivation of a general solution in the TNH form for SGE, the development of a general representation incorporating an arbitrary classical displacement field, and the proof of relationships between various general solutions. Namely, presented relationship between the BG and TNH solutions is non-trivial within SGE and has been obtained here for the first time. Of particular interest, in our view, is also the established equivalence between solutions of the Mindlin type and those of the generalized PN type (including LBVB solution). These two forms of the general solution have existed in parallel for two decades, but the connection between them has not been shown previously.

Currently, there is significant growth and development in the fields where SGE models find application – in micromechanics
[
66
,
77
]
, in problems of modeling metamaterials and composites
[
78
,
79
,
80
]
, in dislocation theory
[
71
,
72
]
, in fracture mechanics
[
81
,
82
]
, in the mechanics of nanosized objects
[
83
,
84
]
etc. Both fundamental theoretical aspects, related to proving existence theorems and the correct formulation of boundary value problems
[
85
,
73
,
86
]
, and applied problems, related to the identification of materials constants
[
87
,
88
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
, are being considered.
The displacement general solutions presented in this work could be one of the convenient tools for addressing these challenges.
