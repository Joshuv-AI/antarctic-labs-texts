New isolates of Pseudomonas and Streptomyces as bioremediation tools for aclonifen removal
Authors: Paulina Książek-Trela, Ewa Szpyrka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aclonifen is a persistent diphenyl-ether herbicide whose accumulation in agricultural soils poses an increasing environmental challenge. Identifying efficient microbial degraders is therefore essential for developing sustainable remediation strategies. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time the ability of four previously characterized diflufenican-degrading bacterial isolates: Pseudomonas sp. 10Kp8 – A1, Pseudomonas chlororaphis subsp. aureofaciens B19 – A2, Pseudomonas baetica JZY4-9 – C1, and Streptomyces atratus ROA017 – D1, and their four-strain consortium to degrade aclonifen in liquid medium and soil. All strains were able to remove aclonifen as the sole carbon source, achieving degradation efficiencies of 50–60% in mineral salt medium (MSM) after 21 days. Strains A2 and D1 were the most effective and were therefore selected for soil experiments, where the maximum degradation reached 69% for strain D1 by day 28. In both environments, individual strains consistently outperformed the mixed consortium, indicating potential antagonistic interactions within the mixture. By demonstrating that bacteria originally selected for diflufenican removal can successfully target another herbicide, this work introduces a novel and promising biological strategy for the remediation of aclonifen-contaminated environments. These findings broaden the range of microbial candidates for sustainable pesticide remediation and establish a basis for the future development of biological strategies for contaminated agricultural soils.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52005-9.

Introduction

Pesticides are widely applied in agriculture to control pests and diseases, enhance crop productivity, and meet global food demands1. However, their extensive use has raised significant environmental and health concerns, including those associated with contamination of soil, water, air, and food, as well as potential risks to human health2,3. Among these, herbicides represent the most commonly used class of pesticides in crop production. Their frequent and excessive application can result in the accumulation of persistent residues in the environment, leading to long-term ecological damage4. Microbial degradation is one of the primary mechanisms by which herbicides are broken down in soil and groundwater. Biodegradation, particularly by bacteria, plays a central role in the mineralization and detoxification of such xenobiotic compounds through enzymatic activity2,5. The major enzymes involved in pesticide degradation are hydrolases, oxidoreductases, esterases, phosphotriesterases, and glutathione S-transferases6,7. Organisms with the highest pesticide degradation activity are bacteria belonging to Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Rhodococcus, Micrococcus, Acinetobacter, Arthrobacter, Aerobacter, Azotobacter, Acetobacter, Alcaligenes, Burkholderia, and Sphingomonas, Clostridium, Flavobacterium, and Actinomycetes genera, and fungi from Fusarium, Aspergillus, Absidia, Penicillium, Lentinula edodes, Lecanicillium, and Oxysporum genera7–11.

This study focuses on aclonifen (IUPAC name 2-chloro-6-nitro-3-phenoxyaniline), a hydrophobic diphenyl ether herbicide, used in the pre-emergence control of weeds in agricultural crops12. Aclonifen is considered to act on weeds by inhibiting enzymes involved in the synthesis of chlorophyll and carotenoids13.

Aclonifen is approved for use in the European Union2,14,15. It is persistent in soil systems and can be very persistent in aquatic systems. In soil, this herbicide’s half-life ranges from 29.5 to 195 days under laboratory conditions, and from 13 to 195 days in field studies16. Aclonifen is characterized by low water solubility (this reduces its mobility in soil, making it less prone to leaching), strong adsorption (it binds tightly to soil organic matter and clay particles, and this further limiting its movement through soil profile), and low volatility (this minimizes losses to the atmosphere, making soil and runoff the main pathways of environmental transport)16. Due to its low leaching potential, the risk of aclonifen contaminating groundwater is relatively low. However, surface runoff and erosion can transport bound residues to nearby water bodies, potentially affecting aquatic organisms, especially algae and aquatic plants. In 2013, aclonifen was included by the European Parliament and the Council in the list of priority substances in the field of water policy, which contain substances posing a significant risk to the aquatic environment17. Aclonifen is known to have a moderate effect (lethal concentration 50% (LC50) = 0.67 mg/L) on fish (Oncorhynchus mykiss), and on aquatic invertebrates (Daphnia magna, half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) = 1.2 mg/L), and a strong negative effect on aquatic plants (Lemna gibba, EC50 = 0.006 mg/L). Its effect on earthworms in soil is moderate, with LC50 = 150 mg/kg for Eisenia foetida on day 1416.

Aclonifen is degraded mainly by microbial activity, rather than by hydrolysis or photolysis. Therefore, identifying efficient bacterial degraders is crucial for bioremediation (Fig. 1). It is generally stable in water across a range of pH levels which implies that its chemical breakdown through hydrolysis is not a significant pathway18.

Diflufenican and aclonifen exhibit structural similarity, as both compounds are nitro- or halogen-substituted aromatics linked by ether or amide bonds. These shared structural features suggest that enzymes induced during diflufenican degradation, such as nitroreductases and hydroxylases, may also act on aclonifen. For example, diflufenican degradation by soil bacteria can yield 2,4-difluoroaniline, and aclonifen degradation is reported to produce aminophenolic intermediates such as 3-amino-2-chloro-4-nitrophenol2,16. This provides a mechanistic basis for cross-adaptation and supports the hypothesis that pre-exposed strains can exhibit broadened substrate specificity toward structurally related herbicides.

The aim of this study, and its novel aspect, was to examine the effect of four diflufenican-degrading soil isolates and their consortium on aclonifen degradation in liquid medium and in soil. This study introduces the concept of cross-adaptation, defined as the ability of microbial strains previously exposed to and specialized in degrading one xenobiotic compound, diflufenican, to metabolize a structurally related herbicide (aclonifen). This approach represents a novel strategy in bioremediation, expanding the applicability of pre-adapted microbial strains to a broader range of contaminants. The innovation lies not only in the discovery that these strains can use aclonifen as their sole carbon source, but also in the comparative analysis between single strains and bacterial consortia. Remarkably, individual strains were shown to outperform mixed cultures, challenging the conventional assumption that microbial consortia generally enhance degradation rates. Furthermore, the successful application of these strains in both liquid media and soil environments advances the practical application potential of biological remediation strategies in contaminated agricultural settings.

Materials and methods

The experimental design was based on previously reported biodegradation studies, where both individual strains and microbial consortia are commonly evaluated to compare their efficiency and potential interactions. The selected incubation times and sampling intervals were chosen to capture both the initial adaptation phase and the later stages of degradation. The use of both liquid medium and soil systems allowed for assessment of bacterial performance under controlled conditions and in a more complex, environmentally relevant matrix.

Experimental materials and sources

Chromatographic grade hexane and acetone were purchased from Chempur, Poland. The analytical standard of aclonifen and the internal standard Triphenyl Phosphate (TPP) were purchased from Dr. Ehrenstorfer GmbH, Germany. All other chemicals and reagents used during the study were purchased from standard sources. The commercial preparation Bandur 600 SC (600 g/L; Bayer AG, Germany) containing aclonifen as the active substance, was used. Bacterial inoculum preparation, consortium formation, and titer measurement

Starter cultures of the bacterial strains were cultivated in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 20 mL of nutrient broth. The flasks were incubated at 30 °C with agitation at 100 rpm for 24 h, using a laboratory shaker. Prior to inoculation, the optical density of each culture was adjusted to approximately 0.1 at 600 nm, measured with a TECAN Infinite M200 spectrophotometer (USA), corresponding to an estimated cell density of approximately 10⁶ colony-forming units (CFU) per mL. The bacterial consortium was prepared by combining equal volumes of four individual bacterial suspensions. For each experiment, bacterial suspensions were added to the medium or soil to achieve a final concentration of approximately 10⁶ CFU/mL (liquid medium) or 10⁹ CFU/g (soil), consistent with commonly applied inoculation levels in biodegradation studies.

Removal of aclonifen from liquid and soil environments

Cultures of each bacterial isolate and the consortium were incubated in flasks containing MSM with aclonifen at 100 mg/L as the sole carbon source. The selected aclonifen concentration was chosen to represent environmentally relevant levels reported in contaminated agricultural soils and to ensure comparability with previous biodegradation studies. Using a single concentration enabled a controlled assessment of the intrinsic degradation capacity of individual strains and the consortium under standardized conditions. The cultures were maintained at 22 °C and 100 rpm in a rotary shaker (GFL 3006, Poland) for three weeks. Samples for aclonifen residue analyses were collected at the start of the experiment, and on days 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21. Residue concentrations in MSM were determined following the method of Podbielska et al. (2020)19. On the basis of this degradation studies, strains A2 and D1 were identified as the most effective aclonifen degraders.

Consequently, the soil experiment evaluated the degradation potential of strains A2, D1, and the four-strain bacterial consortium. Bacterial strains individually and as a consortium were inoculated into sterilized soil, followed by the addition of an aqueous aclonifen solution (50 mg/L) 24 h later. The soil used in the experiment was a commercial peat-based universal substrate composed of peat, pine bark, sand, perlite, and dolomite (pH 6.0–7.3). The substrate was characterized by a high organic matter content typical of peat-based media (approximately 60–80%), with a loose, well-aerated structure corresponding to a sandy-organic texture. Prior to the experiment, the soil moisture content was adjusted to approximately 70% of water holding capacity to ensure optimal conditions for microbial activity. The soil was sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min to eliminate indigenous microorganisms and thus allow for the direct assessment of the degradation capacity of the introduced bacterial strains without interference from native microbial communities. This approach enables a controlled evaluation of strain-specific activity under defined conditions. Non-inoculated soil containing aclonifen was used as the abiotic control to account for non-biological dissipation processes such as adsorption and chemical degradation. Soil samples were collected on days 1, 4, 7, 14, 21, and 28, and aclonifen residues were quantified using the modified QuEChERS method20.

Herbicide analysis

Aclonifen residues were analyzed using an Agilent Technologies 7890 A gas chromatograph (USA) coupled with a mass detector equipped with three quadrupoles, model 7000 (GC-MS/MS QqQ). The dynamic multiple reaction monitoring (dMRM) mode was employed for the analyses, with three mass transitions for studied herbicide.

The following fragmentation reactions were monitored for aclonifen: 264 m/z → 194 m/z; 264 m/z → 77.1 m/z for qualification analysis; and 212.1 m/z → 182 m/z for quantification18.

The validation study was performed according to the guidelines for the analytical quality control and method validation procedures for the pesticide residues analysis21. The limit of quantification for aclonifen was 0.01 mg/kg in soil and in liquid medium. The average recoveries were 104.2% for the soil and 103.2% for liquid medium. The method was validated according to the SANTE 11,312/2021 recommendation and Słowik-Borowiec et al. (2022)20.

Statistical analysis of results

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to evaluate differences between treatments and sampling times. The use of Tukey’s post hoc test allowed for pairwise comparison between all treatments, ensuring robust identification of differences in degradation efficiency. Statistically significant differences were set at p < 0.05 using GraphPad Prism 10. Exact p-values are reported where appropriate, while significance levels in figures are indicated by letter annotations.

Results and discussion

On each day of sampling, the bacterial titer was analyzed and expressed as log₁₀ CFU/mL or log₁₀ CFU/g for aclonifen residue analysis in liquid medium and soil. The bacterial population dynamics observed in this study follow patterns commonly reported in biodegradation experiments involving xenobiotic-degrading microorganisms. In MSM, all strains showed a gradual increase in cell density, reaching the maximum CFU on day 14, followed by a decline by day 21 (Fig. 3). Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed for all tested samples on all sampling days. This growth pattern is consistent with earlier observations, indicating that microorganisms typically proliferate during the initial phase of xenobiotic availability, when the compound can be efficiently used as a carbon and energy source, and subsequently their numbers decline once substrate levels decrease or inhibitory metabolites accumulate23. The initial inoculum of approximately 10⁶ CFU/mL in MSM corresponds well to densities commonly used in similar studies, where effective biodegradation was also associated with early population growth followed by a stabilization or reduction phase24,25.

In soil, the initial bacterial titer was higher, of approximately 10⁹ CFU/g (Fig. 3), which reflects typical inoculation levels applied in soil bioremediation experiments to compensate for stronger sorption of xenobiotics, reduced mobility, and the competition from indigenous microorganisms26. The peak in CFU observed on day 1 for all strains and the consortium indicates rapid initial adaptation to soil environment, followed by a decline in viable bacterial counts due to environmental stress, competition, nutrient depletion, declining bioavailability of the target compound, or nutrient limitation27. Despite this decline, detectable populations persisted throughout the experiment, demonstrating that the introduced strains remained viable and metabolically active under soil conditions. This finding is aligned with studies showing that even declining bacterial populations may sustain xenobiotic degradation in soils, provided that sufficient bioavailable substrate remains28.

However, it should be noted that the present study was conducted under controlled (sterile soil) conditions and over a relatively short experimental period. Therefore, the long-term colonization ability and ecological stability of the introduced strains in natural soil environments cannot be fully assessed based on the current data. In non-sterile soils, the survival and activity of introduced microorganisms are strongly influenced by interactions with indigenous microbial communities, including competition for nutrients, niche exclusion, and predation. These factors may significantly affect both the persistence of the introduced strains and the sustainability of their biodegradation performance over time27,29. Previous studies have shown that even highly efficient degraders may exhibit reduced activity or fail to establish stable populations under field conditions due to these ecological pressures28.

The results obtained in this experiment clearly demonstrate that the tested bacterial isolates are capable of using aclonifen as the sole carbon source, both in liquid medium and soil. In MSM, the highest aclonifen degradation (60%), was recorded after the application of A2 and D1 strains separately (Fig. 4). These values were approximately 18% higher than those of the control samples, and this aligns with previous reports on the strong degradative potential of Pseudomonas spp. and Streptomyces spp. toward xenobiotic compounds30–33. The statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) confirm that the observed increase in degradation efficiency is associated with the metabolic activity of the tested strains rather than random variation. The observed ability of tested strains to utilize aclonifen as the sole carbon source provides strong evidence of cross-adaptation between it and diflufenican, two structurally related herbicides.

On the basis of these findings, the present study investigated the aclonifen degradation potential of the A2 and D1 strains in soil. The degradation efficiencies were generally lower than in MSM, except for strain D1, which reached 69% by day 28. Statistically significant differences in degradation (p < 0.05) were observed for all tested samples on all sampling days, with the exception of day 4, where no statistical significance was detected. This pattern likely results from the strong sorption of aclonifen to soil components, reducing its bioavailability and slowing microbial turnover. It should be noted that the use of sterilized soil limits the environmental realism of the experiment, as it does not account for interactions with native microbial communities, competition, or natural soil dynamics. Therefore, the observed degradation efficiencies may differ under field conditions, where complex biotic interactions can influence microbial activity. Future studies should include experiments in non-sterile soils to evaluate the performance and survival of the introduced strains under more environmentally relevant conditions. Despite this limitation, the use of sterilized soil provides essential baseline information on the intrinsic biodegradation potential of the tested strains.

A more detailed analysis of the kinetic parameters revealed clear differences in degradation efficiency between tested strains and the consortium (Tables 2 and 3). In MSM (Table 2), the highest degradation rate was observed for strain D1, characterized by the highest rate constant (k = 0.041) and the shortest half-life (t₁/₂ = 16.9 days). Similarly, strains A2 (k = 0.036; t₁/₂ = 19.3 days) and C1 (k = 0.034; t₁/₂ = 20.4 days) also demonstrated relatively high degradation efficiency compared to the control. In contrast, the consortium exhibited a lower degradation rate (k = 0.029; t₁/₂ = 23.9 days), which was only slightly higher than the control (k = 0.026; t₁/₂ = 26.7 days). This confirms that the combined system did not enhance degradation efficiency and may have been affected by interspecies interactions limiting overall performance. Time-dependent dissipation curves of aclonifen in MSM are shown in the Supplementary Materials Fig. S1. A similar trend was observed in soil samples (Table 3), although degradation proceeded generally slower due to the more complex environmental matrix. Strain D1 again showed the highest degradation efficiency (k = 0.033; t₁/₂ = 21 days), followed by strain A2 (k = 0.031; t₁/₂ = 22.4 days). The consortium (k = 0.029; t₁/₂ = 23.9 days) did not outperform individual strains, indicating that its effectiveness was limited also under soil conditions. Overall, the shorter half-lives and higher rate constants observed for individual strains confirm their superior biodegradation potential compared to the consortium in both liquid and soil systems. Notably, the half-life values in the control were identical in both MSM and soil systems (t₁/₂ = 26.7 days), indicating consistent abiotic or natural dissipation rates of aclonifen under the tested conditions. Time-dependent dissipation curves of aclonifen in soil are shown in the Supplementary Materials Fig. S2.

Sharipov et al. (2021) confirm that aclonifen is highly adsorbed and only slowly degraded in agricultural soil34. Strong sorption typically reduces the accessibility of hydrophobic herbicides to degrading microorganisms. This is consistent with the reduced degradation observed for most strains in soil, and confirms the results obtained in the present article35. Higher degradation values were obtained by Erguven et al. (2016), who isolated 5 bacterial (Bacillus simplex, B. muralis, Micrococcus luteus, M. yunnannesis, and Clostridium tetani) and 6 fungal (Penicillium thrichoderma, P. simplicissimum, P. talaromyces, Metacordyceps chlamydosporia, Stachybotrys chartarum, and Alternaria alternata) strains from the soil samples from the Thrace Region (Turkey), where aclonifen is used to protect sunflower crops. The highest bioremediation was observed in soil sample to which 10 mL of mixed culture of microorganisms were added. The aclonifen remediation was observed at the level of 93.2%. The authors continued their research based in these findings. Isolated fungi and bacteria strains showed the highest aclonifen removal efficiency, at the level of 99% over 5 days, whereas the mixture of bacteria and the mixture of fungi achieved the level of 71% and 91%. The most efficient degradation was found in soil studies with mixed cultures of bacteria and fungi, at the level of 97% after 5 weeks5. Complementary findings were reported by Perez-Lucas et al. (2020), who explored the dissipation and leaching behavior of aclonifen in non-sterile soils, both unamended and amended with three types of organic waste materials: agroforestry residues, agro-industrial by-products (e.g., spent malt grains, cereal dust, and yeast), and mixed livestock manure (from sheep and goats). Their laboratory-based experiment, conducted under controlled conditions, revealed prolonged persistence of aclonifen in all treatments. The estimated half-life (DT₅₀) was the highest in unamended soils (990 days), while soils amended with agro-industrial and agroforestry wastes showed the reduced persistence, with DT₅₀ values of 433 and 770 days, respectively. Additionally, aclonifen was characterized as an immobile compound of a minimal leaching potential, reinforcing its classification as a non-leacher under the tested conditions36.

A key finding of this work is that single strains consistently outperformed the four-strain consortium in both environments. In the last days of the experiments, aclonifen degradation by bacterial mixture reached 51% for the MSM (day 21) and 68% for the soil (day 28) (Fig. 4). Although microbial consortia are often assumed to enhance biodegradation by providing complementary metabolic pathways, recent reports emphasize that mixed communities may also experience competition for substrates, or production of inhibitory secondary metabolites reducing their overall efficiency29,37–39. The reduced degradation by the consortium observed here is consistent with these observations, indicating that antagonistic interactions may have limited the collective metabolic activity40. Our research results are confirmed by previous literature reports. Fuentes et al. (2013) demonstrated that a pure culture of Streptomyces sp. A5 had the highest pentachlorophenol removal percentages (10.6%), while Streptomyces sp. M7 achieved the best chlorpyrifos removal (99.2%). A mixed culture of Streptomyces spp. showed removal percentages of 40.17% for chlorpyrifos and of 5.24% for pentachlorophenol, indicating more efficient functioning of bacteria as individual strains41.

A more detailed analysis of microbial interactions suggests that several mechanisms may explain the reduced efficiency of the consortium observed in this study. Competition for aclonifen as the sole carbon and energy source likely occurred between strains with overlapping metabolic capabilities, particularly among Pseudomonas spp., which are known to exhibit strong substrate competition and rapid resource uptake. Such competition can limit the effective utilization of the compound by reducing the access of slower-growing or less competitive strains29,39. Antagonistic interactions mediated by the production of secondary metabolites may have contributed to the observed effects. Members of the genus Streptomyces are well known producers of antibiotics and bioactive compounds, which can inhibit the growth or metabolic activity of coexisting bacteria, including Pseudomonas species. This may explain the reduced overall degradation despite the high individual efficiency of strain D129,40. The lack of metabolic complementarity within the consortium may have limited synergistic degradation. Although microbial consortia can enhance biodegradation through division of metabolic labor, this effect typically requires distinct and complementary enzymatic pathways38. Ecological factors such as niche overlap and spatial competition, particularly in soil microenvironments, may have further constrained consortium performance. The observed decline in CFU in the consortium samples supports the hypothesis that interspecies interactions negatively affected microbial survival or activity37. These mechanisms are consistent with the experimental observations in this study, where the consortium showed both lower degradation efficiency and no clear advantage in population stability compared to individual strains. Despite similar initial inoculum levels, the consortium did not exhibit enhanced growth or persistence, suggesting that interspecies interactions limited its functional performance.

To better interpret the observed biodegradation patterns, probable metabolic pathways of aclonifen transformation were considered. Due to its structure as a diphenyl ether herbicide containing nitro- and chloro-substituted aromatic rings, aclonifen is likely to undergo initial reductive and oxidative transformations typical for nitroaromatic compounds. A key step may involve nitroreduction catalyzed by bacterial nitroreductases, leading to the formation of amino derivatives, which are generally more reactive and susceptible to further degradation. Such reactions are widely reported in the microbial transformation of nitroaromatic pesticides and xenobiotics28. In parallel, hydroxylation reactions mediated by monooxygenases or dioxygenases may introduce hydroxyl groups into aromatic rings, increasing polarity and facilitating subsequent ring cleavage. Oxidative cleavage of the diphenyl ether bond is another reliable step, potentially catalyzed by oxidoreductases, resulting in smaller aromatic intermediates that can be further metabolized via central metabolic pathways32. Furthermore, reported metabolites of aclonifen, including amino- and hydroxylated derivatives, are consistent with these proposed pathways2. These transformations likely enhance the bioavailability of the compound and facilitate its further mineralization. Although the exact metabolic routes require confirmation through metabolite identification, the proposed mechanisms provide a reliable biochemical explanation for the degradation efficiencies observed in this study.

Concluding, the results indicate that individual, well-adapted bacterial strains may be more effective in aclonifen bioremediation than mixed consortia. The demonstrated ability of diflufenican-degrading isolates to also degrade aclonifen highlights their metabolic versatility and supports their potential use as targeted inoculants for the remediation of soils contaminated with diphenyl-ether herbicides. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence that selective strain application can be more efficient than multi-strain mixtures in the biodegradation of persistent organic pollutants.

Conclusion

A key finding of this study is the demonstration of cross-adaptation between diflufenican and aclonifen, showing that bacterial strains previously adapted to one herbicide can effectively degrade another structurally related compound. This highlights the potential of using pre-adapted microbial populations as versatile tools in the bioremediation of chemically related contaminants. Among the tested microorganisms, A2 and D1 demonstrated the highest biodegradation efficiencies in MSM, achieving removal rates close to 60%, while strain D1 exhibited the greatest performance in soil, reaching a degradation level of 69% after 28 days. Statistically significant differences in degradation (p < 0.05) were observed for all tested samples on all sampling days (except day 4 for the soil). The results further indicate that single-strain applications were more effective than the four-strain consortium, suggesting that antagonistic interactions or competitive effects may limit the efficiency of mixed microbial cultures in aclonifen degradation. Despite showing lower performance than individual strains, the consortium still achieved substantial removal efficiencies, confirming its overall biodegradative potential. These results highlight the metabolic versatility of the tested bacteria and their strong potential for application in the bioremediation of environments contaminated with aclonifen, diflufenican, and structurally related herbicides. This finding expands the current understanding of microbial metabolic flexibility in pesticide degradation. The study provides a scientific basis for the development of targeted, strain-specific biological strategies including bioaugmentation, for the sustainable remediation of pesticide-contaminated soils. Future research should focus on validating these results under field conditions, as well as on metabolite identification and genomic or enzymatic analyses to better understand the molecular basis of aclonifen degradation.
