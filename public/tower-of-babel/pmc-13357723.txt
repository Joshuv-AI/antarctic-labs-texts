Comparative microbiome profiling of betel quid chewers and non-chewers to identify dysbiotic microbial signatures
Authors: Mayank Bahuguna, Prerna Diwan, James Wahlang, Rakesh Kumar Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent studies are showing an association between oral microbiome community changes and oral diseases, including oral cancer. The prevalence of betel quid chewing has been linked to the incidence of oral cancer, particularly in the Northeast region of India. Hence, the study to understand the bacterial community shifts induced by betel quid to identify dysbiotic microbial signatures will not only aid in early diagnosis of oral cancers but also facilitate the development of strategies to restore microbial balance and potentially prevent disease progression. The present cross-sectional study evaluated the oral microbiome of 92 adults in Meghalaya, India, using a 16 S rRNA sequencing approach. Significant differences were observed in the bacterial community in betel quid chewers and non-chewers. Alpha diversity, assessed using Chao1 and observed genera metrics, was significantly higher in betel quid chewers as compared to non-chewers, especially among long-term users and male individuals. Beta diversity analysis revealed significant community compositional differences between Betel quid chewers and non-chewers, more notably in males and individuals aged 40–60 years. Betel quid chewers demonstrated a higher number of unique taxa (63 Overall; 102 Male; 123 in 40–60 years old) compared to non-chewer samples. Linear discriminant analysis identified Haemophilus, Fusobacterium, and Lautropia enriched in non-chewers, while Lachnoclostridium, Ottowia, and Prevotella were enriched in chewers. Additionally, Pediococcus pentosaceus and Leuconostoc citrenum were found exclusively in older, long-term chewers; Lactobacillus reuteri and Lactobacillus salivarius in non-chewers. These findings highlight profound BQ-associated oral microbiome dysbiosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46533-7.

Introduction

The human oral cavity hosts a diverse and complex community of microorganisms, collectively referred to as the oral microbiome. Over recent decades, an increasing number of investigations have pointed towards an association between the changes in microbiome, especially bacteriome, with various human health conditions1–5. Studies have shown a correlation between dysbiosis of the human gut microbiome with several gastrointestinal tract (GIT) diseases6–10. Emerging evidence suggest that disturbances in the oral microbiome may also contribute to oral diseases, including cancers. The oral sampling studies, performed through non-invasive procedures, followed by DNA isolation and 16 S rRNA sequencing, can elucidate a complete profile of the oral bacterial community11–14. This community is typically in a well-balanced eubiotic state, which may get disrupted in immunocompromised states, infections, or by masticatory products including Betel Quid (BQ), a preparation of betel leaf, Betel nut (or Areca Nut), and slaked lime, with or without tobacco15. Such disruptions lead to dysbiosis with decline in beneficial microorganisms like probiotic strains, causing a tangential shift towards an increase in harmful organisms16. Betel nut ranks among the most commonly used psychoactive substances globally with deep cultural roots in Asian regions, including India17,18. In North Eastern region of India, it is a socially endorsed masticatory product in many tribal and urban areas19, due to several reasons including its psycho-stimulant effects such as hunger depletion, constipation relief, and as mouth freshener20. Furthermore, BQ chewing has laxative, anti-ulcer, carminative, diarrhoeal, helminthic, hypertension, and antibacterial properties that advocate for its prevalence21,22. BQ users show early signs of oral cancer, such as lesions in the oral cavity, including oral leukoplakia (OL), erythroplakia, and oral submucosal fibrosis (OSF)23,24. Long-term BQ chewing, besides having an etiological correlation with chromosomal abnormalities, oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), and Oral cancer (OC), has also been linked with microbial community alterations or dysbiosis25,26. A meta-analysis of studies from the South-Asian continent indicates a betel nut chewing association with a 6-fold increased risk of oral cancer27. The changes in oral microbiota may arise due to the chemical components released in the oral cavity upon BQ chewing, which include alkaloids, polyphenols, along with other piper-betel leaf agents28. NCRP ICMR, 2012–16 data in the North-East (NE) region of India, displayed a significant upsurge in cancer cases diagnosed every year29–31. A recent secondary data analysis32 supported by other studies33,34, confirmed that the NE region encountered the highest age-adjusted rate of incidence for Oral Cancer, with low survival among male individuals32–34. The prevalence of smokeless tobacco (SLT) products was also observed to be higher in the NE region31. The East Khasi hills in the NE region seem to be linked with the highest relative proportion of oral cancer in India31,33,35–37. Several studies have reported that the long term BQ chewers are at a potentially higher risk38–42.

Our previous study conducted in Delhi, India, also identified differences in the oral microbiome of BQ chewers as compared to non-chewers, with the abundance of bacteria reported in association with oral cancer and other oral diseases43. Study by Hernandez et al. 201744 identified Streptococcus as the abundant genus, with S.infantis, S.anginosus as common species in chewers, along with Actinomyces, and Parascarvodia. Another study by Uehara, O. et al., 202145 displayed a lower abundance of Streptococcus, Lautropia, and Actinobacillus in the betel quid non-chewer groups and abundance of Actinomyces, Tannerella, and Prevotella in the case of periodontal disease state45. Our recently published study from the Delhi region also identified an abundance of Prevotella and Bacteroides among the BQC category, and BQNC with an abundance of Streptococcus and Neisseria43. As betel quid chewing is associated with alterations in the oral microbiome, therefore, probiotic strains that confer promising oral health benefits could be explored as a viable approach to regain healthy microflora in the BQ chewing population and alleviate the damaging effects46. The role of bacteriome is also being studied in the development of oral premalignant lesions in BQ chewers, differentiating from cancerous and pre-cancerous tissues compared to non-malignant tissues47–49. However, the definitive role of the oral bacterial microbiome changes in Betel Nut chewers and oral carcinogenesis has not been fully established yet.

In Northeast India, the traditional habit of chewing betel quid has been linked to oral sub-mucosal fibrosis (OSF) and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), the most common type of oral cancer. Early detection of oral cancer remains challenging due to the lack of effective diagnostic tools, often resulting in late diagnosis, poor prognosis, and low survival rates. The present study delves into the microbiome profiling of NE population of India to elucidate the microbial diversity differences between BQ chewers and non-chewers in the local community (Khasi). This study will lay the foundation for identification of specific oral bacterial taxa as potential biomarkers for early diagnosis or prognosis of betel quid-induced oral cancer. Additionally, this could pave the way for the designing of probiotic microbial consortia in future, aimed at restoring oral microbial balance and promoting oral health in long-term betel quid chewers50.

Materials and methods

Demographic distribution

The study design and the protocols were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of Ram Lal Anand College, University of Delhi This study was conducted in accordance with the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017) (https://ethics.ncdirindia.org/asset/pdf/ICMR_National_Ethical_Guidelines.pdf). Informed written consent was taken from all the participants. The oral samples for this cross-sectional study were taken from 92 subjects (62 males and 30 females) visiting a dental camp organized in Shillong, Meghalaya, and assigned unique codes for protecting the individuals personal information in this study. The samples were grouped into three major divisions: (i) BQ chewers (BQC): Regular habit of BQ chewing. (ii) BQ Non-chewers (BQNC): Individuals with no history of BQ chewing, as informed by the participants. (iii) BQ chewers consuming associated substances (smoking and/or alcohol).

For comparative analysis, the 92 samples were stratified into 13 categories as detailed in Table 1. Within each category, participants were divided into two groups, Chewers and Non-Chewers with equal sample sizes. The samples were distributed to reduce biases, ensuring matching either on the basis of age or gender between the groups. Categories I, II, III, and IV had all male subjects with age matching. Category V had all males with no associated habit of Smoking and Alcohol intake, and Category VI consisted of all females with no associated habit of Smoking and Alcohol intake. Categories VII, VIII, and XI had all males with either smoking, smoking + Alcohol, or Alcohol habits, respectively. Categories X, XI, and XII included both male and female chewers with age matching. Category XIII includes all males in the age group of 40–60 years.

Oral sampling and dossier

Informed consent

Consents were obtained from all participants included in the study, providing comprehensive information about the research. Participation was voluntary. A structured questionnaire was used for collecting data, printed in different languages (*English, Hindi, Assamese, and Khasi) and interpretations. Demographic data and betel quid chewing behavior, and associated substance consumption (smoking and alcohol), were collected, excluding subjects with an intake of antibiotics in the last three months.

The protocol was adapted from the NIH Human Microbiome Project51. The microbiome collection was carried out as per the guidelines and regulations provided by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR, Government of India). The oral samples were obtained by using sterile swabs from different sites of the oral cavity, including the glossa, hard and soft palate, subgingival region, cheeks, and buccal mucosa under the supervision of oral-dental experts. The swabs were suspended in 1 ml sterile saline solution (0.85%) of Himedia Transport Swab Kit (https://www.himedialabs.com/media/TD/MS001). The samples obtained were instantly processed, with swirling action of the swab and pulse agitation using a vortex instrument. The residue was dislodged into the saline solution, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min to settle down the cells. The volume obtained was used for the DNA extraction protocol with QIAamp DNA Microbiome Kit (QIAGEN) www.qiagen.com/hb-1792 with modification steps including high-speed vortexing of the reaction mix (10 min). The DNA samples were stored at -20 °C till their transfer to the sequencing facility.

16 S rDNA Sequencing

The DNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer before processing for PCR. Gel-electrophoresis was performed for quality check and further sequencing for the V3-V4 region in the bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA gene through Illumina Miseq 2 × 300 platform. The targeting primers were barcoded for 341F (5’ CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG 3’) and 805R (5’ GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC3’) sequences52. Amplicon purification was done via ampure beads, and sequencing libraries were prepared with Illumina barcoded adapters (additionally with 8 cycles of PCR). The library quantification was done using Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity assay kit (https://www.thermofisher.com/order/catalog/product/Q32851) and processed with 2 × 300PE v3 sequencing kit (https://sapac.illumina.com/products/by-type/sequencing-kits/cluster-gen-sequencing-reagents/miseq-reagent-kit-v3.html). The 0.2 to 0.5 million paired-end reads were provided by Biokart India Pvt. Ltd.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Initially, the demultiplexing of bcl. data format was done to convert the sequence files into .fastq raw data format and checked with Multiqc (1.10.1) (https://multiqc.info/). The MultiQC-satisfied samples were further processed with TRIMGALORE, for trimming of adapters, removal of chimeras, and low-quality reads. The sequences were processed through (Biokart 16 S metagenomic pipeline), and normalization for contrast comparison was done for visualization. The sequence alignment was done against the NCBI database through a reference-based approach, obtaining the OTU table with a 97% threshold. The following community diversity profiling was primarily done at the genus level by employing Vegan R packages in Microbiome Analyst (online tool: https://www.microbiomeanalyst.ca/)53, except otherwise mentioned. The low-count features with (reads < 4) in 20% of samples were removed to reduce the impact of sequencing errors. The features with minimal variation across experimental conditions were removed using an interquartile range-based filter, with lower than 10% of variance to retain significant sequencing signals. Following filtration, total sum scaling (TSS) was used to normalize read counts across samples without applying transformation or rarefaction using MicrobiomeAnalyst. Microsoft Excel (2019 version) was used for basic analysis, including abundance feature tables and the top ten genera in each sample was identified utilizing the OTU data.

Alpha diversity (diversity within the sample group) was measured on the basis of the Shannon-diversity index (accounting for the number of unique taxa and their richness). The outlier points in box plots (Shannon indices) represented samples with high diversity; Chao1 statistical method was used for the estimation of richness, and Observed genus accounted for the number of unique taxa. Beta Diversity, defined as differences (similarity/dissimilarity) between the two groups adjoint with 2 major matrix factors: (1) Level of similarity in-between the 2 groups through non-phylogenetic Bray-Curtis index; (2) Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) [2D/3D plot] for visualizing the dissimilarity matrix in lower dimensions, where each point represented the microbiome of a single sample. The plots were constructed based on the confirmation drawn through statistical significance displayed by clustering patterns inside the plots using Permutational ANOVA (PERMANOVA), PERMDISP (variation in between the groups), and ANOSIM for studying the abundance of the genus. Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) Effect Size (LEfSe) Plots were constructed with MicrobiomeAnalyst employing the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test to detect the features and abundance for each genus in 2 groups. The evaluation was done with (p-value < 0.05) using an original filter and Log LDA score of 2.054. A unique OTU list comparison was done using the ‘eulerr’ function in RStudio version 4.5.0, displaying the percentage of unique and common organisms in each sample.

Results

Overview of the study population

92 oral samples were collected from the urban and rural locales of Shillong, Meghalaya, in the North East region of India. The information on demographic, lifestyle, and behavioral characteristics, including age, gender, BQ chewing exposure, and associated substances (smoking, alcohol) consumption patterns, was collected through a questionnaire. The final sample set used for analysis consisted of 62 males and 30 females; among whom 57 were current betel quid chewers (BQC), and 35 were Non-chewers (BQNC) as per the information provided by the subjects. The category-wise group divisions of the study individuals have been summarised in Table 1, where all the 92 samples were grouped into 13 categories for comparative analysis. Among the total sample set of 92, 14 BQC individuals reported a habit of smoking, and 17 subjects with a habit of consuming alcohol. 10 BQC individuals reported having both habits of smoking and consuming alcohol.

Sequencing outcomes

The 92 oral samples generated a total of 20,614,457 raw sequences and 20,599,586 sequences post-filtering, with an average bp length of 552 bp. The 92 paired-end reads through the Illumina MiSeq platform were submitted to the Bio project for the SRA (Sequence Read Archive) repository of NCBI (Bio sample Accession number- PRJNA931125).

Oral bacteriome taxonomic classification

A total of 1,372 OTUs were tabulated in the sample set of 92 individuals, with a filtering threshold of up to 0.5%. The OTUs were obtained through a reference-based matching approach against the NCBI database. The taxonomic classification was studied at the phylum, genus, and species levels, while class, order, and family ranks were excluded. In total, the OTUs were 100% classified at the phylum level, followed by 85.93% at the genus level, and consecutively 60.81% at the species level.

Overall taxonomic data for 92 individuals of the North East region of India revealed Proteobacteria as the most abundant phylum (40.20%), followed by Firmicutes (38.69%), Actinobacteria (10.72%), Bacteroidetes (6.79%), and Fusobacteria (3.58%). At the genus level, Streptococcus dominated with an abundance of 35.39%, Neisseria (20.67%), followed by Haemophilus, Rothia, Prevotella, Fusobacterium, and Lactobacillus. Furthermore, at the species level, a total of 869 were classified, in which Streptococcus mutans was found to be the most abundant (38.84%) among all the individuals. While 233 unclassified species were noted with an abundance of 62% recorded for Streptococcus sp. and 24% for Neisseria species.

The community structure in the overall sample set of Current Betel Quid chewers (BQC) and non-chewers was analysed to study the oral microbiome. The results of post-zero count filtration from the OTU table revealed a total of 12 phyla, 38 genera, 24 classified species, and 17 unclassified species (Fig S12) in BQC. The highest relative abundance was recorded for the Proteobacteria phylum, followed by Firmicutes. The Streptococcus genus was dominant, and S. mutans was the classified species. However, in the case of unclassified species level, the Acetobacter species showed more relative abundance; its specific role in oral microbiome is less well characterized than other genera, but it influences the oral pH (Fig S13). In BQNC, the microbiome community structure showed 5 phyla, 24 genera, 21 classified species, 10 unclassified species in the oral cavity (Fig S14). In BQNC, the highest relative abundance was recorded for the Firmicutes phylum, followed by the Proteobacteria. In the Streptococcus genus, S. mutans species was dominant (Fig S15) as seen in BQC. 20 genera were exclusively present in BQC, and 6 genera were exclusively present in BQNC (Table S6). Comparison of 22 samples (male) of BQC and BQNC showed Lautropia mirabilis to be exclusively present in BQNC, and there was a rise in relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa, Neisseria animalis, and Prevotella denticola in BQC (Fig S16). In long-term chewers of 40–60 years, the microbiome showed greater diversity with 15 phyla, 95 genera, and 88 species in the BQC samples. Proteobacteria were found to be the predominant phylum with 41% OTU counts, followed by Firmicutes (39%), Actinobacteria (8.4%), Bacteroidetes (7.6%), and Fusobacteria (1%). In terms of genus, Streptococcus covered 27%, followed by Haemophilus (15.19%) (Fig. 1a and b). Among the Streptococcus genus, Streptococcus mutans was observed with 29% abundance. On the other hand, the oral microbial community structure of BQNC individuals presented a total of 5 phyla, 37 genera, and 39 species. The predominant phylum, Firmicutes, comprised 42% OTU counts, subsequently Proteobacteria (36%) and Actinobacteria (12%). Streptococcus was again the most abundant genus with 31% of the total filtered taxa found in all the individuals of the group, and similarly at the species level (Fig. 2a and b). The second most abundant genus was found to be Neisseria (16.45%), which differed from the BQC microbiota profile. There was a rise in the relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa, Neisseria animalis, and Prevotella denticola in BQC (Fig S17).

Alpha diversity- differential measures

Using the Chao1, Shannon index, and Observed Genus with a 95% filtered diversity threshold, relative abundance was evaluated at the genus level. BQC displayed a higher number of unique taxa with a p-value of 0.03 through Chao1 (114.68 ± 8.71) and Observed genus (99.95 ± 28.80) indices in comparison to BQNC. On comparison of BQC and BQNC, in the age groups 21–30 and 31–40 years, the alpha diversity displayed insignificant results (p > 0.05) for all three indices measured (Table 2). However, the BQC of 41–50 years in comparison to BQNC, displayed a significant difference (i.e., Chao1 and Observed Genus; p-values = 0.03, 0.05; 113.65 ± 7.17 and 101.83 ± 17.60). Further, the comparison of BQC Vs. BQNC (overall male samples) displayed differences, for all three indices, i.e., Shannon (p = 0.01; 2.69 ± 0.43), Observed (p = 0.008; 110.30 ± 30.35), and Chao1 (p = 0.02; 120.11 ± 6.31) at the genus level. The comparison of Female BQC and female BQNC, there were no significant differences based on all three diversity measures. The effects of associated substances on differences in the microbiome, studied with the BQC with smoking habit in comparison to the BQC alone, failed to display diversity in terms of unique taxa at the genus level. A similar trend was observed for BQC with smoking & alcohol consumption habits and BQCs with alcohol consumption group with a similar control group of BQC alone.

Discussion

The oral microbiota corresponds to a healthy/eubiotic state in the host, however changes in community characteristics, including their composition and abundance, have been found to be associated with oral diseases55–57. Exposure to substances such as BQ and its long-term chewing appears to initiate changes in the overall microbial community structure. In the present cross-sectional study, comparative analysis was done to understand the bacterial diversity differences between BQC and BQNC, as BQ has been categorized as a carcinogenic substance, linked to oral cancers20,58,59. This study aimed to identify specific bacteriome signatures in Betel quid chewers and non-chewer groups.

Overall, the population of BQC exhibited differential microbial diversity, which aligns with previous studies suggesting that chronic exposure to BQ, particularly its constituents arecoline and slaked lime, modulates the oral bacteriome, fostering colonization by opportunistic and acid-tolerant taxa16,60,61. A notable shift in phylum-level abundance was observed with Proteobacteria dominating in chewers (40.38%), followed by Firmicutes, whereas Firmicutes were predominant in non-chewers (41.2%), with Proteobacteria as the second most abundant. This inversion of dominance suggests a dysbiotic transition that may be potentially driven by the alkaloid-rich chemical milieu introduced by BQ, which may selectively favour gram-negative facultative anaerobes62. Our earlier investigations report similar trends in a Delhi-based cohort, where BQ chewers exhibited elevated levels of Proteobacteria. The predominance of Proteobacteria in chewers has been linked to inflammatory responses and epithelial disruption45. Neisseria and Haemophilus, belonging to this phylum can exacerbate mucosal inflammation and facilitate carcinogenic pathways. Conversely, the dominance of Firmicutes in BQNC, particularly Streptococcus spp., may be consistent with a healthy oral microbiome. The increased representation of unclassified species in chewers (17 vs. 10 in non-chewers) may reflect the emergence of novel taxa, possibly induced by long-term exposure to BQ-associated carcinogens. This observation resonates with findings from Su et al. (2019), where unique mutational signatures and microbial shifts were found in oral squamous cell carcinoma patients with a history of BQ use63.

Furthermore, the exclusive presence of Lautropia in Betel Quid Non-Chewers (BQNC), a facultative anaerobic bacterium could possibly represent a health-associated microbial marker64. Its absence in Betel Quid Chewers (BQC) may reflect a disruption of the native oral microbiota. Studies have shown that L. mirabilis is sensitive to mucosal inflammation and tends to decline in dysbiotic conditions such as periodontitis65. In contrast, the abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa, Neisseria animalis, and Prevotella denticola in BQC indicates a shift towards opportunistic colonizers and inflammation-associated taxa. R. mucilaginosa, a biofilm former, has been implicated in mucosal colonization under altered immune conditions. Its rise in BQC may indicate colonization in response to epithelial disruption or altered nutrient availability66,67. Increased abundance of Neisseria animalis, in BQC may reflect a shift toward favouring nitrate-reducing species, which thrive in alkaline environments. The NE tribal Khasi population also showed Prevotella as unique feature in female BQC individuals. Prevotella denticola, has been reported to be associated with periodontal inflammation and endodontic infections16,68. Its enrichment in BQC, supports the hypothesis that betel quid chewing fosters a dysbiotic state, as these are known to produce virulence factors that possibly can increase mucosal inflammation and contribute to systemic dissemination.

BQC was found to possess a more diverse microbial community as indicated by the alpha diversity measures, which showed differentiating results among BQC vs. BQNC, with significance at the genus level through Chao1 (114.68 ± 8.71) and Observed genus (99.95 ± 28.80). This rise in alpha diversity, as highlighted by Cassol et al. (2024), can result from ecological imbalance, which allows colonization by transient or pathogenic taxa as documented in previous studies on oral dysbiosis in periodontitis and oral carcinogenesis69. Similar results were obtained for 40-60-year-old male BQC and BQNC, indicating that age-related long-term BQ chewing habits resulting in dysbiosis may translate into oral diseases. Also, significant changes in the microbiome of male BQC group, suggested that this group to be at a higher risk of dysbiosis.

Beta diversity assessment results also clearly suggest compositional differences among the BQC and BQNC groups, male BQC Vs BQNC, and in the 40–60 year age. The insignificant changes in alpha and beta diversity of < 20 years and ≥ 20 years of betel quid chewers, indicate that the overall genus richness and evenness remained relatively stable across durations. This suggests that while prolonged exposure may not drastically affect general microbial diversity, thus history of BQ chewing alone may not be the only factor driving dysbiosis. However, specific taxa may still undergo compositional shifts. The long term chewers may develop a distinct and stable dysbiotic profile favoring resilient taxa in the microbiome. No other studies have directly compared multiple age groups of chewers with non-chewers, further research could expand on multi-age cohorts using metagenomics. Findings from Unique Taxonomic Units also indicate the development of a distinct bacteriome alongside the commensals upon exposure to BQ. The number of unique taxa were much higher in different BQC categories such as in male BQC, tribal female BQC, with the highest unique taxa (123) in the focus category of 40–60 years.

In LefSE analysis, based on taxa abundance, Haemophilus emerged as a significant potential bacterial biomarker with an LDA score of 5.52, supported by a p-value of 0.0098 and FDR of 0.13, indicating strong discriminatory power. Its abundance in BQNC, suggests a stable colonization pattern in less chemically disrupted oral environment with intact epithelial barriers. Haemophilus was also found to be a significant feature with a high LDA score of 5.69 and p value 0.024 in 31–40 years age BQNC group.

The age group of 21–30 years only yielded one significant feature, Lachnoclostridium in BQC (LDA − 3.21). Its ability to metabolize complex carbohydrates and lipids may be advantageous in the chemically altered oral environment of betel quid chewers70. Similarly, Filifactor was observed in female BQNC (LDA score of 4.32), is worth exploring further. Filifactor alocis is the most studied species in this genus, a Gram-positive, obligate anaerobe with a role in periodontal disease and endodontic infections. It thrives in oxidative stress environments and contributes to biofilm formation through protease and collagenase production71. The reduction of Filifactor in female BQC may reflect the impact of BQ constituents on epithelial integrity, thereby suppressing its colonization. A 2024 study linked F. alocis depletion to alkaloid-induced mucosal damage, suggesting that its absence may signal a shift toward a dysbiotic, inflammation-prone oral microbiome72. Fusobacterium, a commensal anaerobic bacterium in the oral cavity, with a role in maintaining biofilm formation73, emerged as significant bacterium with a high LDA score in above 40 years BQNC group.

In our study, smoker BQCs showed Veillonella, Chrysobacterium, Olsenella, Stenotrophomonas, and Atopobium as distinct features. The presence of Veillonella in the oral cavity of smoker BQC has been previously reported as a signature microorganism43,74–76 and correlated with cases of Oral pre-malignant lesions (OPL) as high-risk periodontal pathogens77. It has also been reported in both Oral Submucosal Fibrosis (OSF) patients and BQ chewers, and thus, may be related to the risk of carcinogenesis78,79. Olsenella and Atopobium have been previously linked to periodontal inflammation and halitosis, with recent studies showing their enrichment in tobacco users and individuals with chronic periodontitis80. Stenotrophomonas has been observed in oral swabs of smokers and immunocompromised individuals, likely due to reduced mucosal immunity and biofilm restructuring81. It has also been reported in cases of Oral infections-opportunistic MDR pathogen in nosocomial infections, oropharyngeal squamous cell cancer, and smokers82,83. Additionally, Chrysobacterium has been recently reported as an MDR pathogen in dental plaque cases84, and Olsenella has been reported in endodontic infections85,86. Among alcohol-consuming smokers-BQC, Olsenella (LDA score of 3.74), and Alcanivorax (LDA = 3.16) can be prospectively identified for dysbiotic states. While in alcohol-consuming BQC individuals, the presence of Parvimonas with pathogenic potential and Lachnoclostridium was observed. The underexplored organism of oral cavity, Ottowia was found to be a significant feature in BQC with a chewing history of 0–20 years. Further study of samples with different chewing histories is required to reveal their correlation with early microbial shift due to BQ.

In the long term, BQC of 40–60 years, findings indicated exclusive presence Pediococcus pentosaceus, and Leuconostoc citreum, which are probiotic bacteria. Pediococcus pentosaceus, and Leuconostoc citrenum are versatile probiotic candidates showing antimicrobial and health benefits87,88, offering a strategic approach for dysbiosis reversal, suppressing tumor-promoting taxa16,89,90. In contrast, the BQNC group uniquely exhibited two probiotic bacteria, Lactobacillus reuteri and Lactobacillus salivarius. L.reuteri and L.salivarius are well established for their application to inhibit pathogenic colonial strains and production of beneficial organic acids, aiding in the reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines formation, and anti-microbial peptide formation, drawing various other positive effects91,92. LAB strains can interact with macrophages and T-cells, causing alteration of cytokine production, leading to overall immunity change93. These probiotic organisms, differentially present in BQC and BQNC, may be further isolated, cultured, and investigated for characteristic probiotic properties for their use in a potential consortium of microorganisms able to maintain long-term contact with oral tissues for preventing, reversing, or relieving the effects of dysbiosis leading to oral diseases.

The overall observed compositional differences in this NE region-specific study, highlighted differentially enriched genera in BQC and BQNC groups through the 16 S rRNA sequencing approach. It needs further investigation using whole metagenomic shotgun sequencing and comparison with oral microbial communities from oral cancer patients at various stages who are also betel quid chewers. Additionally, integrating oral microbial transcriptome (meta-transcriptome) profiling will enable the identification of specific microbial expression signatures associated with oral cancer progression in the betel quid chewing population. Together, these advanced multi-omics approach will enhance our understanding of the microbial contributions to oral carcinogenesis in this high-risk group.

Conclusions

There is a growing interest in understanding the role of dysbiosis in different diseases and the modulation of microbiota for health benefits. The present study showed significant differences between the BQC and BQNC, identifying potential microbial signatures based on a 16 S metagenomic approach in the Khasi population of the North-East region in India, where BQ chewing is rampant. Also, the differential presence of probiotic bacterial taxa identified in BQC and BQNC can further be investigated for a bio-therapeutic strategy targeting long-term BQ chewers, where dysbiosis eventually leads to the development of oral cancers and other oral diseases. This study has resolution limitations in terms of utilizing only the 16 S rRNA up to the species level. Hence, for a deeper analysis of the functional attributes in the oral microbiota along with the responsible gene modulations, we need to extend this study through shotgun metagenomic sequencing in the future.
