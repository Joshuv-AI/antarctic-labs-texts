ChildGuard: A Specialized Dataset for Combatting Child-Targeted Hate Speech
Gautam Siddharth Kashyap, Mohammad Anas Azeez, Rafiq Ali, Zohaib Hasan Siddiqui, Jiechao Gao, Usman Naseem

Abstract
The increasing prevalence of child-targeted hate speech online underscores the urgent need for specialized datasets to address this critical issue. Existing hate speech datasets lack age-specific annotations, fail to capture nuanced contexts, and overlook the unique emotional impact on children. To bridge this gap, we introduce ChildGuard 1 1 1 Our dataset can be found at https://github.com/RafiqAli25/ChildGaurd . The ”Unknown” label in the dataset applies to instances where the age group could not be determined or falls outside of the specific categories we are focusing on. The full version will be released upon acceptance. , a curated dataset derived from existing corpora and enriched with child-specific annotations. ChildGuard captures diverse contexts of child-targeted hate speech, spanning age groups. We benchmark existing state-of-the-art hate speech detection methods, including Large Language Models (LLMs), and assess their effectiveness in detecting and contextualizing child-targeted hate speech. To foster further research in this area, we publicly release ChildGuard , providing a robust foundation for developing improved methods to detect and mitigate such harm.

3
Overview of the Dataset

The
ChildGuard
dataset is an exceptional tool developed to tackle the issue of online hate speech aimed at children. It highlights three distinct age categories—teens (13-17), pre-teens (11-12), and younger children (below 11)—to address the unique difficulties these groups encounter based on their varying digital proficiency, cognitive growth, and online presence.
Note:
The justification for selecting the three age groups is based on cognitive development theories and online behavior patterns.
Gaillard et al. 2021
shows that cognitive maturity and digital fluency vary significantly across these age groups, influencing their vulnerability to online hate speech.
ChildGuard
extends previously established hate speech datasets outlined in Section
2
, enhancing them to focus on child-specific hate speech.

3.1
Dataset Acquisition

The
ChildGuard
dataset was created by refining and curating existing publicly available hate speech datasets including: i) Hate Offensive
Davidson et al. 2017
, ii) Hate in Online News Media
Salminen et al. 2018
, iii) Slur Corpus
Kurrek et al. 2020
, iv) HateXplain
Mathew et al. 2021
, v) ETHOS
Mollas et al. 2022
, vi) Gab Hate Corpus
Kennedy et al. 2022

Toraman et al. 2022
, viii) A Curated Hate Speech Dataset
Mody et al. 2023

, and x) Context Toxicity
Pavlopoulos et al. 2020
. Additionally, child-centric datasets such as GSHS, BullyDetect, and the WhatsApp Cyberbullying Dataset
Sprugnoli et al. 2018
are integrated.

Note:
A total of 790,385 instances were selected, with each dataset contributing based on relevance to child-targeted hate speech. The selection of instances was based on two main criteria: (1) relevance to child-targeted hate speech, and (2) the use of lexical and contextual analysis to identify content likely directed at children. The number of instances selected from each dataset varied as shown in Table
1
: for instance, Online Harassment 2017 contributed 19,838 instances, and OLID 2019 contributed 14,052 instances. Each instance was evaluated for keywords and patterns indicative of age-specific hate speech. Instances were categorized into three age groups—teens (13-17), pre-teens (11-12), and younger children (below 11)—based on age-relevant language, context, and expressions identified during analysis. Furthermore, to label the data, lexical analysis was employed to identify specific keywords and phrases associated with hate speech directed at children. Each instance of text
x
was examined for the presence of these keywords, leading to an initial labeling function. Next, contextual analysis was applied to examine interactions and relationships within the text, providing additional context for labeling. For instance, phrases indicating bullying or harassment in the social dynamics of children contributed to a refined labeling function.

We structured the dataset into three subsets—contextual analysis, lexical analysis, and
ChildGuard
dataset—to facilitate targeted evaluations. The contextual analysis subset focuses on semantic and syntactic structures, aiding in understanding discourse context. The lexical analysis subset emphasizes word-level features, sentiment, and vocabulary richness. The
ChildGuard
dataset integrates both perspectives, ensuring a comprehensive view of hate speech patterns.

3.2
Lexical Analysis

3.2.1
Dataset Composition

The dataset consists of sentences categorized into different age groups, namely teens, pre-teens, and younger children. For each age group
A_{i}
, a corresponding lexicon subset
L_{A_{i}}
is defined, where
A_{1}
corresponds to teens,
A_{2}
corresponds to pre-teens, and
A_{3}
corresponds to younger children. The sizes of these lexicons vary based on the complexity of language usage across age groups, which can be represented mathematically as according to Equation (1).

|L_{A_{1}}|>|L_{A_{2}}|>|L_{A_{3}}|

3.2.2
Dynamic Lexicon

The lexicons
L_{A_{i}}
are dynamic and evolve over time based on feedback from real-world interactions. This evolution can be modeled using a time-varying function, represented as according to Equation (2).

L_{A_{i}}(t+1)=L_{A_{i}}(t)\cup\Delta L_{A_{i}}

where
\Delta L_{A_{i}}
denotes the new set of terms added to the lexicon at time
t+1
.

3.2.3
Inclusion of Non-Verbal Cues

In addition to textual data, the
ChildGuard
dataset incorporates non-verbal cues, such as emojis and symbols. Each emoji is represented in a semantic vector space (
V_{S}
), allowing for a joint representation of words and emojis. For a sentence
S
containing both words and emojis, the augmented matrix representation becomes as according to Equation (3).

V_{S}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}&\dots&v_{m}\\
e_{1}&\dots&e_{k}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+k)\times(d+e)}

3.2.4
Syntactic and Semantic Structures

The dataset also supports analyses of syntactic structures, which can be represented through a dependency tree
T_{S}
modeled as a directed acyclic graph
G=(V,E)
. In this representation,
V
denotes the set of words in the sentence, while
E
denotes the syntactic dependencies between them.

3.2.5
Sentiment and Polarity Information

Each entry in the
ChildGuard
dataset includes sentiment and polarity scores, which can be denoted as
P(S)\in[-1,1]
. This score indicates the emotional tone of the text, where
-1
signifies extremely negative sentiment, and
1
represents positive sentiment.
Note:
The sentiment and polarity information was used later in the dataset for several purposes. First, it helped in refining the labeling process, distinguishing between different types of hate speech—explicit vs. implicit hate—based on the emotional tone of the text. Finally, this sentiment data enabled us to explore correlations between emotional tone and other dataset features, such as age group and platform, contributing to more comprehensive insights into child-targeted hate speech.

Age_Group

3.3
Contextual Analysis

3.3.1
Age-Based Categorization

This curation process categorizes social contexts for three age groups: teens (13-17), pre-teens (11-12), and younger children (below 11). Each age group experiences hate speech differently based on their social environment. To quantify the influence of various contextual factors,
ChildGuard
categorizes them into distinct variables. Let
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}
represent a set of social dynamics such as peer pressure. For each dataset entry, the intensity of these dynamics is measured and assigned a weight
w_{i}
, where
w_{i}
represents the relative importance of the contextual factor
s_{i}
in contributing to the presence of hate speech. The total contextual score for any data entry
x
in the dataset can be defined as according to Equation (4).

C(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot s_{i}(x)

This score
C(x)
helps differentiate how hate speech is constructed or amplified in different social environments. For instance, in pre-teens, peer influence may play a larger role in spreading harmful messages, whereas for younger children, exposure to hate speech may be more passive, such as being exposed to harmful content in group settings without full comprehension.
Note:
The mention of peer influence playing a role is based on common sense and general understanding of social dynamics, particularly in online environments where children and adolescents are influenced by their peers. While we do not provide specific examples from the dataset or a detailed analysis in this paper, the idea stems from well-established social and psychological theories
Gaillard et al. 2021
regarding peer influence in youth behavior, both online and offline. This influence is often seen in how children adopt language, trends, and behaviors from their social circles, which can include the use of certain types of language, including hate speech.

3.3.2
Platform-Specific Behavior

In addition to social dynamics, the dataset incorporates platform-specific behaviors. Different platforms, such as social media or online games, foster distinct types of interactions. Let
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k}\}
represent a set of platform-specific behaviors that contribute to the context of hate speech. The contribution of platform behavior to the dataset is captured as according to Equation (5).

C_{p}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}\cdot p_{j}(x)

Here,
\alpha_{j}
represents the weight assigned to the platform-specific behavior
p_{j}
, which varies depending on the age group and the platform where hate speech occurs. For instance, older children are likely to experience cyberbullying on social media platforms, while younger children might encounter inappropriate language or hate speech in online gaming environments.

3.3.3
Combined Contextual Score

Combining social dynamics and platform-specific behavior—the overall contextual score
C_{\text{total}}(x)
for an entry in the dataset can be represented as according to Equation (6).

C_{\text{total}}(x)=C(x)+C_{p}(x)

This score allows the
ChildGuard
dataset to provide a holistic view of the context in which hate speech occurs. The refined contextual labeling facilitates a deeper understanding of the nature of child-targeted hate speech, offering valuable insights into the age-specific and platform-specific nuances that influence its occurrence.

3.4
Annotation

The annotation process for the
ChildGuard
dataset was carried out directly by us, the authors, to maintain consistency and ensure high-quality labeling. We followed a structured, multi-step approach. Initially, we performed automated preprocessing (i.e. clean and format data via tokenization, stemming, or removing irrelevant content automatically) to filter out irrelevant content (i.e. refers to any data that does not align with the focus of the study, which is hate speech targeting children). Then, we manually annotated the data, specifically categorizing instances of hate speech. The annotators were deeply familiar with the dataset’s context, ensuring nuanced understanding and accurate labeling.

We created a comprehensive set of annotation guidelines as shown in Setion
A.1
to maintain consistency throughout the process. These guidelines defined the categories clearly and provided examples to illustrate different types of abuse. We also emphasized the importance of context, especially when dealing with children’s interactions, and ensured that the guidelines reflected that sensitivity. To ensure data quality, we conducted manual evaluation on a subset of the annotated data. This validation process was crucial for refining our understanding of complex cases and correcting any inconsistencies. Additionally, we reviewed the annotations periodically (i.e. annotation process was carried out in multiple stages rather than in one batch) to verify agreement between us, and the final dataset underwent a thorough review to ensure its robustness.

Note:
The annotation team consisted of three expert annotators, each with prior experience in hate speech detection and linguistic analysis. To maintain objectivity and minimize individual bias, each text instance was annotated independently by at least two annotators. To assess annotation reliability, Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) was measured using Cohen’s Kappa (
\kappa
) and Krippendorff’s Alpha (
\alpha
). Cohen’s Kappa was calculated for pairwise agreement between annotators and yielded a score of 0.82, indicating strong agreement. Krippendorff’s Alpha, which accounts for potential missing data and varying annotator consistency across multiple categories, was computed at 0.79, reflecting a high level of reliability. Discrepancies in annotation were resolved through a consensus-based review process, where a third annotator acted as a mediator to finalize the label for instances where the initial two annotators disagreed. Additionally, annotators were instructed to differentiate child-specific hate speech from general hate speech, ensuring that ambiguous cases were resolved.

4.2
Experimental Setup

Training:
Each baseline model was trained with a batch size of 32, optimizing both computational efficiency and memory usage. A learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
was chosen, as it is a conventional setting for fine-tuning pre-trained models like BERT and GPT. This smaller learning rate facilitated gradual updates to the model’s weights, ensuring steady convergence without overshooting optimal values. To minimize overfitting, dropout (set at a rate of 0.3) and early stopping were incorporated. Dropout randomly deactivated 30% of the neurons during each training cycle, which enhanced model generalization by preventing over-reliance on specific features. Early stopping monitored the model’s performance on the validation set, halting training when no further improvements were observed, thus protecting against overfitting.

Validation and Testing:
For validation, a specific subset of the dataset was used to tune hyperparameters such as the number of epochs (set to 100) and the learning rate. During this phase, the focus was on optimizing the models based on evaluation metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1 score (macro-averaged). After training and validation, the models were evaluated on an independent test set to assess their ability to detect hate speech targeted at children.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduce the
ChildGuard

Limitations

A limitation of our work is the absence of a detailed discussion on how the dataset can be leveraged to develop more effective hate speech detection models. While the dataset provides a rich source of annotated data, its potential for improving detection models has not been fully explored. Another potential limitation of our work is the lack of explicit bias evaluation in the dataset. While we ensure diverse data sources and rigorous annotation guidelines, inherent biases may still exist due to annotator subjectivity, dataset composition, or platform-specific trends. However, future work could involve the application of advanced techniques, such as fine-tuning transformer models, domain-specific embeddings, and multimodal approaches, to enhance model performance in detecting hate speech in children’s online interactions. Additionally, exploring real-time detection systems, cross-platform generalization, and context-aware models would broaden the practical applications of the dataset in real-world scenarios.

Appendix A
Appendix

A.1
Annotation Guidelines

A.1.1
Purpose and Scope

This document provides detailed annotation guidelines used to label instances of hate speech targeting children in the
ChildGuard
dataset. The objective is to ensure labeling consistency, minimize bias, and support reliable downstream tasks in hate speech detection.

A.1.2
Definitions

Hate Speech
refers to any form of communication that belittles, insults, threatens, or incites hatred against individuals or groups based on attributes such as age, race, gender, or other identity markers. Within this, child-targeted hate speech is a specific subtype directed explicitly or implicitly at minors (individuals under 18 years of age). It can manifest as
Explicit Hate Speech
, involving direct abuse, slurs, or threats, or as
Implicit Hate Speech
, which includes more subtle expressions like sarcasm, coded language, or hostile metaphors that convey animosity toward children.

A.1.3
Annotation Workflow

The annotation process followed a structured workflow to ensure quality and consistency. First, the raw data was preprocessed to remove noise and irrelevant content. Each instance was then independently annotated by at least two annotators to promote objectivity. Alongside the assigned labels, annotators were encouraged to include optional notes and confidence scores to provide additional context. In cases of disagreement, a third expert adjudicator reviewed the instance to determine the final label. To maintain overall quality, annotated samples were periodically reviewed as part of an ongoing quality assurance process.

A.1.4
Label Taxonomy

The annotation schema consists of three primary labels. The first,
Explicit-Hate
, refers to instances that contain direct abuse or hostile language specifically targeting children. The second,
Implicit-Hate
, captures cases where hostility is conveyed indirectly through sarcasm, metaphors, or cultural references that imply negative sentiment towards children. Finally,
Non-Hate
is used for content that is either neutral, positive, or irrelevant to the task of detecting child-targeted hate speech.

A.1.5
Annotation Criteria

Annotators were instructed to verify the presence of child-related references such as terms like “kid”, “teen”, or “school” in each instance. They were also required to distinguish between general hate speech and child-specific hate speech by carefully considering the context in which the language appeared. Any ambiguous or borderline cases that did not clearly fall into predefined categories were to be flagged for further discussion and adjudication.

A.1.6
Examples

The following examples illustrate the application of the annotation labels. An instance such as
These kids are a disease to society
is categorized as
Explicit-Hate
, as it involves a direct and generalizing insult toward children. A statement like
Another genius teen on TikTok ruining everything again
exemplifies
Implicit-Hate
, where sarcasm is used to convey a negative stereotype without overt hostility. In contrast, a sentence like
My daughter just learned to ride a bike
represents
Non-Hate
, reflecting positive and non-abusive content related to children.

A.1.7
Quality Control

Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) was assessed using metrics such as Cohen’s Kappa, which yielded a score of 0.82, and Krippendorff’s Alpha, calculated at 0.79, both indicating a high level of annotation consistency. To maintain and improve annotation quality, the team held regular meetings to discuss observed conflicts and revise the guidelines accordingly. Additionally, manual verification was performed on random subsets of the data by a senior annotator to ensure ongoing accuracy and reliability.

A.1.8
Ethical Considerations

Personally Identifiable Information (PII) was removed during the preprocessing stage to protect privacy. Annotators received training to handle toxic and disturbing content with care and sensitivity. Additionally, annotators were given the option to opt out of labeling any particularly sensitive or distressing examples to ensure their well-being throughout the annotation process.

A.1.9
Dispute Resolution

Disagreements between annotators were primarily resolved using a majority vote. In cases where a deadlock occurred, a third expert annotator was brought in to make the final decision.

A.1.10
Edge Cases

For meme or irony-laden posts, annotations should be based on the underlying sentiment rather than the literal content. In cases where the reference to a child’s age is ambiguous or uncertain, the instance should be annotated as "Non-Hate" and flagged for further review.
