N8‐Modified Quino[4,3,2‐Kl]acridines as Promising Antimalarial G‐Quadruplex Ligands
Authors: Steve Saulnier, Marco Antônio G B Gomes, Mariam Salim, Maxime Guichonnet, Michel Nguyen, Laure Vendier, Flore Nardella, Jean‐Michel Augereau, Dennis Gomez, Anne Robert, Françoise Benoit‐Vical
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo face the increasing multidrug resistance of the malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum, the development of new compounds that act in novel ways is urgent. G‐quadruplexes (G4), Guanine‐rich secondary structures of DNA and RNA sequences, have an essential role in cell survival and cell metabolism regulation, including in Plasmodium. We have recently demonstrated that the G‐quadruplex ligand RHPS4, an acridine derivative, displays strong antiplasmodial activity by disrupting P. falciparum through G4 stabilization, leading to parasite death. Based on this original mode of action, we propose here a series of new RHPS4 analogues. These derivatives were then tested against P. falciparum and on human cells to evaluate their selectivity index. They exhibited IC50 values ranging from 70 nM to up to 10 µM with cationic analogues more active than neutral ones. Their ability to interact and stabilize G‐quadruplex DNA structures was also evaluated. Among all RHPS4 analogues, the compound 8b (N8‐ethyl) exhibited the best activity (70 nM), a high selectivity index (143), and a significant capacity to stabilize the G4 structures.Keywords: antimalarial drug, G‐quadruplex, medicinal chemistry, nitrogen heterocycles, Plasmodium falciparum, RHPS4

Introduction

Malaria remains a dramatic global health problem. While from 2000 to 2015 the malaria incidence rates have decreased, from 2015 malaria cases have increased by 23%, leading to an estimated 610,000 malaria deaths in 2024 [1]. This highlights the inadequacy of current treatments to curb this scourge and the need to develop new effective drugs. Resistance to artemisinin, the core compound of artemisinin combination therapies (ACTs), was first reported in South‐East Asia [2, 3], leading to resistance to ACTs in the Greater Mekong subregion [4]. Recently, artemisinin resistance has emerged in South America [5] and is now spreading in Africa [6, 7, 8], where 95% of malaria‐related deaths occur; 75% of which being children under 5 years of age [1]. This resistance has a complex mechanism [9, 10, 11] and induces a significant risk of reaching a therapeutic impasse in Africa. To face this great health threat, there is an urgent need to develop innovative antimalarial drugs that retain activity against multidrug‐resistant Plasmodium parasites.

To meet this challenge, we designed new small molecules that specifically target G‐quadruplex structures, opening the way to novel therapeutic agents with an original mechanism of action.

Guanine‐rich DNA and RNA sequences can fold into four‐stranded noncanonical secondary structures called G‐quadruplexes (G4). Over the past years, numerous studies have demonstrated the essential role of G4 in cell survival and cell metabolism regulation [12], and especially with the characterization of small compounds that selectively bind and stabilize G4 structures, i.e., G‐quadruplex ligands [13]. In cells, the stabilization of DNA‐G4 structures by G4 ligands induces genomic instability, mainly characterized by the induction of DNA breaks, DNA deletions, and chromosomal translocations [14]. Other reports also show that G4 ligands can localize to mitochondria and affect the integrity of the mitochondrial genome [15]. Accordingly, G4 structures have been associated with mitochondrial DNA deletion found in patients with mitochondrial disorders [16].

It is to be noted that despite its extremely high AT content (>80%) [17], the genome of Plasmodium falciparum can form G‐quadruplex structures [18]. Interestingly, genome‐wide analyses have revealed a higher density of G4 motifs in the P. falciparum mitochondrial genome compared to the nuclear genome [19].

First, antiplasmodial activity assays with G‐quadruplex‐stabilizing ligands showed moderate activity on the growth of parasites in the asexual blood stage [20, 21]. However, we have recently shown that the G4 ligand and telomerase inhibitor RHPS4 [22] displays a stronger in vitro activity (IC50 values from 26 to 90 nM), including against multidrug‐resistant strains, along with high selectivity for the parasite over human cells (SI in the range from 100 to 300) [23]. RHPS4 also reduces the mitochondrial DNA copy number in parasites, an effect not observed with other G4 ligands or with atovaquone, an antimalarial drug inhibiting mitochondrial electron transport. The structure of RHPS4, based on an 8H‐quino[4,3,2‐kl]acridinium scaffold, is shown in Figure 1.

Structure of RHPS4 and synthesized analogues.

RHPS4 could therefore offer an original mode of action by specifically targeting the mitochondrion of malaria parasites. This prompted us to synthesize a series of RHPS4 analogues with the aim of enhancing the affinity for Plasmodium G4 to increase their antiplasmodial activity and selectivity.

Synthesis and Characterization

The structural modifications of RHPS4, shown in Figure 1, include: (i) the neutral analogue 7a; (ii) neutral derivatives substituted at N8 (7b, 7d, and 7e), along with their corresponding quaternized analogues (8b, 8d, and 8e); (iii) the neutral derivative 7h, bearing a trifluoromethyl group at C11; and (iv) compounds 11, 12, and 13, functionalized at N8 with a primary alcohol, a diamine moiety (devoid of permanent charge but protonated under physiological pH), and a triphenylphosphonium moiety, respectively. In fact, the natural polyamines spermine, spermidine, and putrescine are known to interact with mitochondrial membranes, thus modulating mitochondrial‐mediated apoptosis [24], and the lipophilic triphenylphosphonium ion was reported as an efficient strategy for mitochondrial drug delivery [25, 26, 27]. To our knowledge, modification of RHPS4 at N8 and the resulting derivatives are unprecedented in the literature. The antiplasmodial activity of these compounds was then evaluated on the F32‐Tanzania strain of Plasmodium falciparum.

Our strategy for synthesizing the quino[4,3,2‐kl]acridine skeleton characteristics of RHPS4 is based on a reported multistep synthetic methodology involving the condensation of the benzonitrile derivative 4, 2‐bromo‐4‐fluoroaniline 5a, and methyl iodide (Scheme 1) [28]. We anticipated that this methodology would provide a convenient way to access neutral analogues at N13 and to modify the RHPS4 scaffold at N8 by replacing methyl iodide with other alkyl halides, as long as the inserted group is compatible with the subsequent anionic ring closure.

Key steps of a reported synthetic methodology toward RHPS4 precursor 7a [28].

Compound 4 is readily obtained by the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling between 2‐bromobenzonitrile derivative 2a and the commercially available boronic acid 3 (Scheme 2). Instead of preparing 2a from 2‐fluoro‐4‐methylaniline via the reported bromination, diazotization, and cyanation sequence [28], we propose the direct palladium‐catalyzed ortho‐bromination [29] of the corresponding benzonitrile derivative 1a. Despite a poor yield of 34%, the direct ortho‐bromination allows the synthesis of 2a in a single step and avoids handling diazonium salts and copper cyanide.

The ortho‐position of bromination was unambiguously confirmed by NMR spectroscopy and single‐crystal X‐ray structures of 2a, 2b, and of the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling product 4 (Figure 2; see also NOESY spectra in Supporting Information).

X‐ray diffraction structure of single crystals of 2a, 2b, and 4, confirming ortho‐bromination and the position of the difluorophenyl group.

Replacing the 4‐methyl group with a hydroxymethyl group (compound 1b) might have allowed for the functionalization of RHPS4 at C6, but the ortho‐bromination proved very difficult, even with the corresponding protected benzyl and tert‐butyl ethers 1c and 1d (the tert‐butyl ether of 1d was cleaved under the reaction conditions, leading to a Ritter reaction).

The key step for inserting a substituent at N8 is the condensation between compounds 4 and 5a. Fluoroaniline 5a is first deprotonated by potassium tert‐butoxide in DMSO and then substitutes the fluorine ortho to the cyano group of compound 4. This gives an amine anion that is easily alkylated in situ by alkyl halides, such as methyl iodide, ethyl iodide, benzyl chloride, propargyl bromide, and bromoalkyl ethers (Scheme 2), whereas trityl chloride and ethylene oxide are unreactive. We found that the nucleophilic aromatic substitution proceeds best at 0 °C, which is achieved by using THF as a cosolvent with DMSO. Under these conditions, we obtained products 6a–f with yields ranging from 51% to 76%. With the less nucleophilic nitroaniline 5b, both the nucleophilic substitution and the amine methylation require heating at 40 °C, but this only leads to a 37% yield of 6g. Under the same conditions, the trifluoromethylaniline derivative 5c afforded the N8‐methylated product 6h.

The ring closure of 6 into 7 is induced by a bromine–lithium exchange that occurs with one equivalent of n‐butyllithium at −98 °C, giving the N‐methyl, N‐ethyl, N‐benzyl, N‐benzyloxypropyl, and N‐tert‐butoxyethoxyethyl derivatives 7a–e (R 2 = F) and 7h (R 2 = CF3) with yields ranging from 54% to 78%, without the need for the more reactive tert‐butyllithium. With the N‐propargyl derivative 6f, the reaction was complicated by the requirement for two equivalents of n‐butyllithium and by the very poor solubility of the cyclization product in common organic solvents. Consequently, the N‐propargyl cyclization product could not be isolated or characterized. The reaction conditions also seem incompatible with the presence of a nitro substituent at C11, as attempts to cyclize 6g resulted in complex mixtures of products.

The N‐methyl derivative 7a can be converted to RHPS4 by the reported N13‐methylation with dimethyl sulfate in refluxing nitromethane [30]. Alternatively, as shown in Scheme 3, compounds 7b, 7d, and 7e are efficiently methylated with methyl triflate under microwave irradiation in ethylene dichloride, giving very good yields of the corresponding acridinium triflates 8b, 8d, and 8e. The reaction is more difficult with 7a due to its poor solubility in ethylene dichloride. The reaction is also more difficult with alkyl triflates of longer aliphatic chains, such as 1,4‐butanediyl triflate and 3‐bromopropyl triflate, due to the competitive elimination of triflic acid and subsequent protonation of compounds 7.

N13‐Methylation of compounds 7b and 7d–e to the corresponding acridinium triflates 8. aX‐ray molecular structure determined.

We synthesized the N‐benzyl and N‐benzyloxypropyl derivatives 7c and 7d with the aim of removing the N‐ and O‐benzyl protecting groups for further functionalization of the amine and alcohol products. The deprotection reactions were performed by catalytic hydrogenation in THF/isopropyl alcohol as the reaction media for solubility reasons. The use of a slight excess of trifluoroacetic acid was crucial for the reactions to proceed. Under these conditions, 7d gave the best result with 68% of the primary alcohol 11, whereas 7c afforded only 19% of the secondary amine 10 (Scheme 4). Conversion of alcohol 11 to alkyl bromide via the Appel reaction, followed by nucleophilic substitution with a large excess of N,N‐dimethylethylenediamine, gave the diamine derivative 12 with an overall yield of 44%. The substitution was more difficult with triphenylphosphine and required heating at 160 °C for 48 h in a 1,4‐dioxane/acetone mixture. After a final bromide–acetate exchange via phase transfer, we obtained the triphenylphosphonium derivative 13 as an acetate salt with an overall yield of 56%.

Late‐stage transformations of quino[4,3,2‐kl]acridine derivatives 7a, 7c, and 7d.

Not only can the RHPS4 scaffold be modified at N8, but it can also be selectively brominated at C5, opening the way for further functionalization at this position. Compound 7a indeed reacted with N‐bromosuccinimide at 0 °C to yield 92% of the 5‐bromo derivative 9 (Scheme 4). Because 7a is poorly soluble in the usual solvents for reactions involving NBS (methylene chloride, acetonitrile, and ethyl acetate), [31] we had to use DMF as a cosolvent with methylene chloride.

Noteworthily, RHPS4 and all derivatives 7, 8, and 11 –13 are soluble in RPMI containing 5 vol% of human serum (or 10 vol% of fetal bovine serum) and 0.5 vol% DMSO, up to 50 µM, which are the conditions of evaluation of antiplasmodial activity and cytotoxicity (see Section 5).

Determination of the pKa Value of Compound 7b

Like RHPS4, the acridinium derivatives 8 exhibit a permanent positive charge at N13, which is expected to promote interaction with the G4 structures. Conversely, compounds 7 and 11 bear a trivalent N13. To some extent, this neutral structure may favor membrane crossing. To assess the possible protonation/deprotonation equilibrium at N13 of these compounds in physiological conditions, the pKa value of 7b was evaluated by UV–visible spectroscopy (Figure 4). The spectrum of RHPS4 does not change across a broad pH range, with λ max values at 295, 485, and 513 nm (Figure 4a,c), indicating the absence of protonation of N8, even at pH 1. The spectrum of 7b at low pH (1‐2) is similar to that of RHPS4 with λ max values at 291, 467, and 496 nm (Figure 4b), indicating protonation at N13. Conversely, at high pH values, the absorbance at 291 nm significantly decreases, and the two other bands exhibit a significant hypsochromic shift at 436 and 456 nm, assigned to the neutral form of 7b. The plot of absorbance against pH allows estimating the pKa value of 7b at 3.7 (Figure 4d), suggesting that the pentacyclic core of compounds 7 and 11 –13 should be predominantly neutral at physiological pH.

Antiplasmodial Activity and Cytotoxicity

The antiplasmodial activity of the compounds was evaluated using a standard chemosensitivity assay on the P. falciparum strain F32‐Tanzania. The results are shown in Table 2. The high activity of RHPS4 was confirmed [23], with an IC50 value of 63 nM. The cationic analogues of RHPS4 bearing different substituents at N8 (8b, 8d, and 8e) retained, to a certain extent, a significant antiplasmodial activity, with IC50 values in the range from 70 to 369 nM (Table 2), while their neutral analogues 7a, 7b, 7d, and 7e were completely devoid of antiplasmodial activity, with IC50 values close or above 10 µM, i.e., at least two orders of magnitude higher than those of RHPS4 and 8b. In the N13‐neutral series, the substitution of fluorine by a trifluoromethyl group at C11 (compound 7h) or the presence of a primary alcohol at N8 (compound 11) failed to provide active compounds (IC50 > 10,000 nM).

Compound 12, bearing a protonated diamine moiety under physiological conditions, shows a moderate antiplasmodial activity (IC50 = 430 nM), although weaker than that of RHPS4. The activity is reduced even further upon replacement of the diamine moiety with a triphenylphosphonium group (compound 13, IC50 = 1,007 nM).

The present results suggest that a positive charge located at N13 seems essential for the efficient activity of this series. The synthesis of a new series, taking into account this requirement, is now under way.

RHPS4 and the synthesized analogues exhibited low cytotoxicity against the hepatocellular carcinoma cells HepG2, with CC50 values in the range from 4 to 121 µM, whether they were neutral or cationic at N13. Consequently, the compounds having the higher antiplasmodial activity, i.e., RHPS4 and the closely related compound 8b, exhibited the highest security index, 111 and 143, respectively (Table 2). Noteworthily, there was no clear correlation between the substituent at N8 and the compound cytotoxicity.

Binding of RHPS4 Derivatives to G4 Structures

To determine whether the chemical modifications introduced into RHPS4 derivatives affect their ability to stabilize a model G‐quadruplex (G4) structure, we measured the melting temperature of RHPS4‐G4 complexes by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) melting assays, using the human telomeric sequence 21T (5′‐(GGGTTA)3GGG‐3′. This sequence has been extensively used as a reference model in the initial characterization of RHPS4 as a G4 ligand [22] and has since become a standard for evaluating numerous G4‐binding compounds [32]. It is worth noting that the telomeric sequence in P. falciparum slightly differs from the human sequence (GGGTT(T/C)A instead of GGGTTA). Mechanistically, RHPS4 interacts with G4 structures mainly through π‐stacking interactions, positioning itself on the terminal G‐quartet of the structure [33]. Although steric hindrance from lateral loops whose nature depends on sequence and G4 topology (parallel, antiparallel, or hybrid) may influence binding, it has been reported that different G4 conformations induced only minor variations of RHPS4 interaction [34]. Therefore, the widely used F21T model sequence can be considered relevant of RHPS4 interaction with Plasmodium G4. This oligonucleotide was tagged by 6‐carboxyfluorescein (FAM) fluorochrome as a donor fluorophore on the 5′‐ends and by tetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) as an acceptor on the 3′‐ends [35]. The interaction of ligands with G4 structures was monitored by the increase of the half‐melting temperature (T 1/2) of the G4‐forming F21T in the presence of the ligand. The stabilization of the structure was expressed by the ΔT 1/2 value resulting from the differences of half‐melting temperatures measured with and without ligands. The results are shown in Figure 5.

In the presence of RHPS4 (5 molar equiv.) compared with F21T alone, the T 1/2 was increased by 16.4 °C, indicating a significant stabilization of the G4 structure. Similarly, among the tested compounds, 8b and 8e significantly stabilized the G4 structure, with the melting temperature T 1/2 being increased by 12.6 and 9.4 °C, respectively (Figure 5). None of the compounds based on a neutral 8H‐quino[4,3,2‐kl] acridine scaffold significantly stabilize the G4 sequence. This confirms that the stacking of extended aromatic structures alone is not sufficient for a significant interaction between G4 and RHPS4 derivatives, whereas the presence of a positive charge at N13 has a crucial role in their affinity with the polyanionic DNA sequence.

Nevertheless, the positively charged 8d did not significantly stabilize the G4 structure despite exhibiting antiplasmodial activity (IC50 = 180 nM). Moreover, although both 8b and 8e stabilize the G4 structure, 8e exhibits a significantly lower antiplasmodial activity than 8b (IC50 values of 70 and 369 nM for 8b and 8e, respectively). Overall, the interaction of these compounds with the G4 structure of F21T does not appear to be strictly correlated with their antiplasmodial activity.

Selectivity of the Binding of RHPS4, 8b, and 8e for G4 With Respect to a Double‐Strand DNA

The selectivity of RHPS4 or its analogues 8b and 8e for G4 sequences was evaluated in the presence of a large excess of a self‐complementary 26‐base long oligonucleotide of mixed base content (CAATCGGATCGAATTCGATCCGATTG, ds26, up to 50 molar equivalents), according to the protocol reported in reference [36]. In the presence or in the absence of ds26, RHPS4 induced a significant and comparable stabilization of the G4 structure, with a T 1/2 value increased by 14.6 °C with respect to F21T alone, whatever the number of ds26 equivalents was (Figure 6). Similar results were obtained with 8b and 8e. These results confirm the selective binding of these derivatives on G4 F21T with respect to competitive double‐stranded DNA.

Conclusion

A series of original derivatives of RHPS4 has been synthesized using a multistep methodology which allows various substitutions of the 8H‐quino[4,3,2‐kl]acridine scaffold to obtain neutral or cationic molecules aiming to target the G4 of malaria parasite. This method potentially gives rise to extended aromatic compounds with tunable substituents. For several of these compounds, X‐ray diffraction structures were obtained, thus confirming the identification of the compounds. Moreover, these structures clearly indicate that the positive charge at N13 of compounds 8b and 8d induces a significant distortion of the planarity of the pentacyclic core with respect to the neutral analogues 7b and 7d.

In this series, only the compounds bearing a positive charge at N13, namely, RHPS4, 8b, 8d, and 8e, exhibited high antiplasmodial activities, and to some extent, compound 12 that contains a side chain expected to be protonated at physiological pH (IC50 < 500 nM), while the corresponding neutral compounds 7a, 7b, 7d, and 7e were completely devoid of antiplasmodial activity. RHPS4 itself and the N8‐ethyl analogue 8b exhibited the highest antiplasmodial activity (IC50 = 63 and 70 nM, respectively), the highest selectivity indexes (111 and 143, respectively), and the highest thermal stabilization of the F21T DNA sequence (ΔT 1/2 = 16.4° and 12.6°, respectively), which is selective with respect to a double‐stranded DNA sequence. These results suggest that, in this series, a positive charge located at N13 is mandatory but not sufficient for efficient antiplasmodial activity and selectivity and may be related to interaction with G4 DNA structures. Consequently, this chemical series appears as a promising way to new antimalarial drug candidates exhibiting a novel mechanism of action.

Experimental Section—Chemistry

General Information

Commercially available reagents and solvents were used as purchased without further purification unless otherwise indicated. 2‐Fluoro‐4‐methylbenzonitrile (1a), 2‐fluoro‐4‐(hydroxymethyl)benzonitrile (1b), 2,5‐difluorophenylboronic acid (3), 2‐bromo‐4‐fluoroaniline (5a), 2‐bromo‐4‐nitroaniline (5b), and 2‐bromo‐4‐(trifluoromethyl)aniline (5c) were commercially available. RHPS4 was purchased from MedChemtronica AB, Sollentuna, Sweden. Analytical thin‐layer chromatography was performed with commercial plates of silica gel 60 and visualized with UV light (254 and 365 nm) or with a permanganate stain. Preparative column chromatography was performed with silica gel 60 (40–63 μm). Reactions that require heating were performed in a hot plate/heating block apparatus with an internal temperature control. Reactions that require cooling were performed either in ice/water (0 °C) or in liquid nitrogen/methanol (−98 °C) cooling baths. The n‐butyllithium solution was accurately titrated with N‐pivaloyl‐o‐toluidine just before use [37].

NMR spectra were recorded on a 400‐MHz instrument and were calibrated using residual solvent signals as internal references: δ H 7.26 ppm and δ C 77.16 ppm for CDCl3; δ H 3.58 ppm and δ C 67.57 ppm for THF‐d 8; δ H 5.32 ppm and δ C 54.00 ppm for CD2Cl2; δH 1.94 ppm and δ C 1.32 ppm for CD3CN; δH 2.50 ppm and δ C 39.52 ppm for DMSO‐d 6; δ H 3.31 ppm and δ C 49.00 ppm for CD3OD. 1H NMR data are reported as follows: chemical shift (ppm), multiplicity (s = singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, m = multiplet, br = broad signal), coupling constants, and integration. Proton‐decoupled 13C NMR data are reported as chemical shifts for chemically nonequivalent carbon nuclei, multiplicity, and 13C–19F or 13C–31P coupling constants. Proton‐decoupled 19F NMR data are reported as chemical shifts, multiplicity, and 19F–19F coupling constants. Proton‐decoupled 31P NMR data are reported as chemical shifts only. Structural assignments were made with additional information from 1H–1H NOESY experiments.

High‐resolution mass spectra were recorded on time‐of‐flight (TOF) instruments using either desorption chemical ionization (DCI, positive‐ion mode) or electrospray ionization (ESI, positive‐ or negative‐ion mode).

Reverse‐phase high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analyses were performed to confirm the chemical purity of the compounds before biological evaluation using a C18 column (5 μm, 150 mm length x 4.6 mm diameter) with ultrapure water (eluent A) and acetonitrile (eluent B), both containing trifluoroacetic acid (0.1 vol%). Elution was carried out at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min under the following conditions: A/B ratio of 90/10 for 2 min, followed by a gradient to 5/95 over 20 min, and finally maintaining 5/95 for an additional 4–8 min. For each compound, two wavelengths were selected for UV detection.

pKa Measurement for RHPS4 and 7b

UV–visible spectra were recorded in the range from 200 to 600 nm at room temperature using an Agilent Cary 3500 spectrophotometer. A series of ten buffer solutions covering pH values from 1.0 to 10.0 were prepared according to the literature as follows: 0.2 M HCl/KCl buffer for the pH range from 1.0 to 2.0, 0.1 M citric acid/sodium citrate buffer for the pH range from 3.0 to 6.0, 0.1 M Hepes buffer for pH 7.4, 20 mM Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4 buffer the for pH 8.0, and 0.1 M Na2CO3/NaHCO3 buffer for pH 9.2 and 10.0 [40, 41]. All UV–visible spectra were recorded in a mixture of DMSO and the appropriate buffer solution (1/1, v/v), after 15 min of incubation at room temperature. An aliquot of the compound stock solution (2.5 mM in DMSO, 8 µL) was added to 492 µL of DMSO and 500 µL of buffer solution contained in the UV–visible cuvette, resulting in a final volume of 1 mL and a final concentration of the compound of 20 µM. The UV–visible spectra obtained were corrected by subtracting the absorbance of the blank solution (DMSO/H2O, 1/1, v/v). A graph showing the absorbance at two maximum wavelengths as a function of pH was plotted, and the pKa value was determined from the inflection points of the resulting curves.

Experimental Section—Biology

In Vitro Culture of P. falciparum

The culture was performed as described by Trager and Jensen with some adjustments [42]. F32‐Tanzania was cultured in human type O erythrocytes (Etablissement Français du Sang, Toulouse, France) incubated at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco Life Technologies, RPMI 1640, 25 mM HEPES) supplemented with 5% type AB human serum (Établissement Français du Sang, Toulouse, France) in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Antiplasmodial Activity

The SYBR Green I assay was used to determine the antiplasmodial activity of the molecules [43]. For this assay, parasites were used at the ring stage. In a 96‐well plate, the parasite suspension was placed in each test well, and then dilutions of the molecules to be tested were added in triplicate. The parasites were then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a VICTOR Nivo fluorescence multi‐well plate reader, with excitation and emission wavelength bands centered at 485 and 530 nm. IC50 values were calculated using Graph Pad Prism software, version 5.

Cytotoxicity Evaluation

The cytotoxicity of compounds was evaluated on HepG2 cells using the sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay. Cells were seeded in a 96‐flat‐well plate at 3500 cells per well. Serial dilutions of various compounds were realized allowing the same solvent concentration for each condition, and cells were treated 24 h after seeding. After an incubation period of 72 h, the cells were fixed by adding 100 µL of 10% trichloroacetic acid directly to the medium and then incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. The cells were then washed with water and air‐dried. The fixed cells were stained for 30 min with 100 µL of SRB solution (0.057%), after which the excess dye was removed by several washes with 1% acetic acid. The plate was left to dry. The protein‐bound dye was solubilized in a 10 mM Tris base solution (200 µL) for the determination of the optical density at 490 nm using a microplate reader (µquant).

Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer Assay

The human telomeric sequence tested F21T is flanked by a pair of fluorochromes at both ends: a FAM (6‐carboxyfluorescein) fluorochrome at the 5′ ends and a TAMRA (tetramethylrhodamine) fluorochrome at the 3′ ends. The oligonucleotide was resuspended at a final concentration of 100 μM in a buffer containing 10 mM lithium cacodylate and 90 mM lithium chloride, pH 7.2. Just before the experiments, the oligonucleotides were diluted to 20 µM with buffer B (10 mM lithium cacodylate, 90 mM lithium chloride, 12.5 mM potassium chloride, pH 7.2). The FRET test was performed in 96‐well plates in a total volume of 25 µL containing: 0.25 µL of oligonucleotide at 20 µM (final concentration 200 nM) and made up to 25 µL with 10 mM lithium cacodylate, 90 mM lithium chloride, 10 mM potassium chloride, pH 7.2. The ligands were added at the concentrations indicated (1 µM) during the last stage of preparation. For competitive studies, the self‐complementary 26‐base long oligonucleotide CAATCGGATCGAATTCGATCCGATTG, ds26, 5, 10, or 50 molar equivalents was added. FRET was performed on the Biorad CFX96 qPCR device using the following program: incubation for 10 min at 20 °C, then 20.5 °C for 10 min, plate reading, with a temperature gradient of 0.5 °C per cycle and repeated 158 times. The data were analyzed using Biorad CFX management software. The results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software, version 5.

Crystallographic Data Collection and Refinement

Data were collected at low temperature (100 K) on an XtaLAB Synergy, Dualflex, HyPix diffractometer using a micro‐focus sealed X‐ray tube, PhotonJet (Cu) X‐ray source. The structures have been solved using the dual‐space algorithm program SHELXT [44] and refined by means of least‐squares procedures using the SHELXL‐2018 [44], program included in the software package WinGX version 1.63 [45] or with the aid of the software package Crystal [46]. The atomic scattering factors were taken from International tables for X‐ray crystallography [47]. Hydrogen atoms were placed geometrically and refined using a riding model. All non‐hydrogens atoms were anisotropically refined.

For the structure of compound 8e, it was not possible to resolve diffuse electron‐density residuals (enclosed solvent molecules). Treatment with the SQUEEZE facility from PLATON [48] resulted in a smooth refinement. Since a few low‐order reflections are missing from the data set, the electron count will be underestimated. Thus, the values given for D(calc), F(000), and the molecular weight are only valid for the ordered part of the structure.

The structure of compound 7d exhibited disordered atoms. It was treated using the “PART” commands in SHELXL, and constraints were applied to the distances and thermal parameters using, respectively, the DFIX and EADP commands.

Drawing of molecules was performed with the program Ortep [49] with 30% probability displacement ellipsoids for non‐hydrogen atoms. The crystal structures of compounds 2a, 2b, 4, 7b, 7d, 8b, 8d, and 8e have been deposited at the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (http://www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/structures) and allocated the deposition numbers CCDC 2513695‐2513702.
