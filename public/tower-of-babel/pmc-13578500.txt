Rad1–Rad10 uses different interfaces to interact with pathway-specific DNA repair factors
Authors: Javier Rodríguez González, Olivia T Herman, Kaden E Lewis, Lindsay A Matthews, Luke D Hess, Jennifer A Surtees, Alba Guarné
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Saccharomyces cerevisiae Rad1–Rad10 (XPF–ERCC1 in humans) is a 3′-flap endonuclease with key roles in DNA repair. Pathway-specific repair factors determine its recruitment to specific DNA substrates. Saw1 recruits it to 3′ non-homologous tail recombination intermediates, while Rad14 recruits it to UV-lesions repaired by nucleotide excision repair. However, the exact recruitment mechanisms are unknown. We determined the cryo-EM structure of the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex at 3.7 Å resolution. The structure reveals that Saw1 wraps around the helicase-like domain of Rad1 defining an extensive interface. Point mutations on this surface disrupt the interaction and inhibit double-strand break repair without compromising nucleotide excision repair, indicating that Rad1–Rad10 uses different surfaces to interact with pathway-specific repair factors. Mutational analyses confirm that Rad14 and Saw1 bind to opposite faces of Rad1. Accordingly, defects on the Rad14-binding interface disrupt nucleotide excision repair without affecting double-strand break repair. In contrast to XPF–ERCC1, Rad1–Rad10 does not adopt an auto-inhibited conformation in the absence of DNA indicating that substrate binding may be regulated differently across species. Collectively, our data provide structural insight into how targeting factors interact with Rad1–Rad10 to recruit it to different DNA repair intermediates.

Introduction

Structure-selective endonucleases play crucial roles in the DNA damage response by processing DNA secondary structures that arise during repair and must be resolved to preserve genomic integrity [1]. Saccharomyces cerevisiae Rad1–Rad10 (XPF–ERCC1 in humans) is a 3′-flap structure-selective endonuclease with activity in multiple DNA repair pathways [2, 3]. In nucleotide excision repair (NER), Rad1–Rad10 incises 5′ to the repair bubble to allow the excision of bulky lesions [4]; in interstrand cross-link repair, it unhooks the cross-link to enable its removal [5, 6]; and in a subset of homologous recombination pathways, including single-strand annealing (SSA) and synthesis-dependent strand annealing, it catalyzes the cleavage of 3′ non-homologous tails (NHTs) [7]. Rad1–Rad10 also plays a minor role in removing 3′ blocked termini at single-strand breaks and abasic sites during base excision repair [8]

The recruitment of Rad1–Rad10 to its different repair intermediates is mediated by interactions with pathway-specific repair factors. Saw1 (SSA weakened 1) recruits it to recombination intermediates with 3′ NHTs [9–11], assisted by Msh2–Msh3 [12], while Rad14 (XPA in humans) targets Rad1–Rad10 to DNA bubbles containing UV-lesions in NER [13]. Similarly, SLX4 recruits human XPF–ERCC1 to interstrand crosslink repair substrates [14, 15]. Genetic studies have identified separation of function mutations in Rad1–Rad10 that inhibit a specific repair reaction without disrupting the others [12, 13, 16].

Saw1 enhances the affinity of Rad1–Rad10 for 3′-flaps and recruits it to SSA and synthesis-dependent strand annealing sites [9, 11]. Intriguingly, Rad1–Rad10 does not require Saw1 to process short 3′-flaps (∼10 nt) but the interaction is increasingly important for longer flaps becoming strictly required for flaps longer than 50 nt [11, 17, 18]. While the importance of targeting factors in regulating the activity of Rad1–Rad10 is well established, the mechanisms by which they impose substrate specificity to its nuclease remain largely unknown. Structures of human XPF–ERCC1 and fragments of its archaeal homologues exist, but none of them have been solved with a DNA repair intermediate bound to the active site [19–23]. This has hindered our understanding of a critical aspect of how this nuclease is regulated in the DNA damage response.

Rad1 and Rad10 belong to the Rad1/XPF/MUS81 nuclease family. In eukaryotes, proteins of this family form heterodimers composed of a catalytic and a non-catalytic subunit (Fig. 1). The catalytic module of Rad1 consists of a nuclease domain followed by a tandem helix–hairpin–helix domain [(HhH)2]. Rad1/XPF/MUS81 enzymes have a highly conserved GDXnERKX3D nuclease motif distantly related to type II restriction endonucleases [24]. This motif is mutated in the dimer’s non-catalytic protomer (Rad10/ERCC1/EME1), which forms an inactive nuclease-like domain. Although inactive, this nuclease-like domain mediates the dimerization with the nuclease domain of Rad1/XPF/MUS81 and is strictly required for the activity and stability of the complex [22, 25]. In addition to this interface, many Rad1/XPF/MUS81 nucleases use their (HhH)2 domains as an ancillary dimerization interface. In humans, the ERCC1 protomer contains a canonical (HhH)2 motif that binds to duplex DNA [26]. The (HhH)2 domain of XPF lacks the conserved residues for dsDNA binding but retains single-stranded DNA binding [27]. This asymmetry between protomers has been proposed to assist in positioning DNA substrates with unpaired splayed arms by allowing the nuclease to engage both single- and double-stranded parts of the DNA substrate [27]. Recent structures of XPF–ERCC1 revealed that the (HhH)2 domain of ERCC1 restricts access to the nuclease active site of XPF in the absence of DNA [20]. The autoinhibited state of XPF–ERCC1, which is released upon substrate binding, prevents promiscuous DNA cleavage.

The catalytic nuclease domains and tandem (HhH)2 domains are shaded gray. The inactive nuclease-like domains (NLD) and pseudo (HhH)2 domains are shown in white.

Rad1–Rad10 differs from other homologues in that its non-catalytic protomer, Rad10, lacks the (HhH)2 domain [28, 29]. Therefore, it lacks one of the interfaces stabilizing the dimer [30]. This domain organization also suggests that Rad1–Rad10 may not adopt an autoinhibited state in the absence of DNA and that regulation of its nuclease activity may be through a mechanism distinct from that of XPF–ERCC1. Whether the (HhH)2 domain of Rad1 plays a role in DNA binding or regulation of the nuclease activity is unknown, but sequence conservation analysis reveals that, like XPF, this domain lacks the key residues mediating DNA binding [31].

To elucidate whether Rad1–Rad10 also adopts an autoinhibited state and to determine how Saw1 engages/interacts with Rad1–Rad10 to allow recruitment to 3′ NHTs, we determined the cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex. The structure reveals that Saw1 interacts with Rad1 through an extensive interface with the Rad1 helicase-like domain but has no contacts with the Rad10 subunit of the complex. We find that Saw1 enhances the nuclease activity and cleavage precision of Rad1–Rad10, and mutations in the Saw1–Rad1 interface abrogate SSA repair without disrupting NER. This is consistent with AlphaFold predictions of the Rad1–Rad10–Rad14 complex showing Rad14 interacting with a surface of Rad1 opposite to the Saw1-binding surface. As expected from the lack of the (HhH)2 domain in Rad10, Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 does not adopt an autoinhibited state. Collectively, our work unveils key differences in the regulation of Rad1–Rad10 and XPF–ERCC1 and allows us to propose a model for how the Rad1–Rad10 nuclease is targeted to specific DNA repair intermediates.

Materials and methods

Generation of a Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 co-expression vector

Full-length S. cerevisiae RAD1 (residues 1–1100, Supplementary Fig. S1), RAD10 (residues 1–210, Supplementary Fig. S2), and SAW1 (residues 1–261, Supplementary Fig. S3) were subcloned into the pQLink vector system [32] from the pJAS21 and pAG8815 vectors [11] (Supplementary Table S1). To this end, BamHI/AvrII restriction flanking sites were added to the RAD1, RAD10, and SAW1 genes by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The products were ligated into the pJET plasmid using the CloneJET PCR cloning Kit (Thermo Scientific) and validated by DNA sequencing (Génome Québec). The RAD1 gene was then subcloned into pQLinkH (N-terminal 7xHis tag, Addgene #13667), while RAD10 and SAW1 were subcloned into pQLinkN (tagless, Addgene #13670). The three resulting plasmids (pQLinkH-Rad1 (pAG9198), pQLinkN-Rad10 (pAG9199), and pQLinkN-Saw1 (pAG9200)) were sequentially subcloned following vendor instructions to generate the pQLink-7×HisRad1–Rad10–Saw1 co-expression vector (pAG9202, Supplementary Fig. S4A). All point mutants were generated in the pAG9202 plasmid using the Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs) and validated by DNA sequencing (Génome Québec, and Plasmidsaurus, Supplementary Table S1).

Expression and purification of Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complexes

Chemically competent E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) cells (Novagen) including a plasmid carrying additional copies of rare tRNA genes were transformed with the pAG9202 plasmid (or derivatives of this plasmid including specific point mutations, Supplementary Table S1). The Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex was produced by auto-induction. Cells were grown in terrific broth media including ampicillin (100 µg/ml) and chloramphenicol (25 µg/ml) and supplemented with 5052 sugar and trace-metal mixes as described previously [33]. Cell cultures were grown for 60 h at 20°C and harvested by centrifugation. Cell pellets were resuspended in His-Trap buffer (300 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, pH 6.8, 30 mM imidazole, 5% glycerol, 1.4 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with 0.05% LDAO, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 1 mM benzamidine, 5 µg/ml leupeptin, and 0.7 µg/ml pepstatin and lysed by sonication at 4°C. The cell lysate was clarified by centrifugation (40 min at 39 000 × g) and loaded onto a 5-ml His-Trap HP column (Cytiva). The column was washed with buffer supplemented with 75 mM imidazole and Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 was eluted with 225 mM imidazole. Fractions containing Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 were pooled and diluted in MonoQ buffer [50 mM HEPES, pH 6.8, 5% glycerol, 0.1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol] to adjust the NaCl concentration to 150 mM. The sample was then loaded into a MonoQ 5/50 GL column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated with MonoQ buffer. Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 was eluted using a linear gradient to 250 mM NaCl. The purified complex was concentrated (Supplementary Fig. S4B), flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until further use.

Expression and purification of Rad14

The coding region of the S. cerevisiae RAD14 gene was codon-optimized for expression in E. coli (GenScript) and cloned into the pQLinkH vector (N-terminal 7xHis tag, Addgene #13667) as described above. This generated the plasmid pAG9428 (Supplementary Table S1). All rad14 point mutants and deletions were generated from this plasmid using the Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs) and validated by DNA sequencing (Génome Québec, and Plasmidsaurus, Supplementary Table S1). For protein expression, chemically competent E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) cells (Novagen) including a plasmid carrying additional copies of rare tRNA genes were transformed with the pAG9428 plasmid (or derivatives). Transformed cells were grown in LB media supplemented with 30 µg/ml ampicillin and 25 µg/ml chloramphenicol at 37°C to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.6, prior to inducing protein expression by addition of 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and overnight incubation at 16°C with orbital agitation. Cell pellets were then harvested by centrifugation and stored at −80°C. To purify Rad14 (pAG9428), Rad14R314A (pAG9641), and Rad14183-371 (pAG9622), cell pellets were resuspended in Rad14-NiA buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, 1.4 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 5% glycerol) supplemented with protease inhibitors (1 mM PMSF, 1 mM benzamidine, 5 µg/ml leupeptin, and 0.7 µg/ml pepstatin) and lysed by sonication. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation (50 min at 39 000 × g), and the supernatant was immediately loaded onto a His-Trap HP column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with Rad14-NiA buffer. The column was washed with 60 mM imidazole prior to protein elution at 240 mM imidazole. Fractions containing Rad14 were pooled and diluted to adjust the NaCl concentration to 125 mM and injected into a HiTrap Q HP column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with Q-Sepharose buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 1.4 mM, β-mercaptoethanol, 5% glycerol, 125 mM NaCl). Rad14 was eluted using a linear gradient to 400 mM NaCl. The histidine-tag was then cleaved with TEV protease at 4°C overnight, and a His-Trap HP column pre-equilibrated with Rad14-NiA buffer was used to separate the tagless and tagged Rad14 species. Tagless Rad14 was eluted with 45 mM imidazole and buffer-exchanged to Q-Sepharose buffer supplemented with 300 mM NaCl and 20% glycerol. The purified protein was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further use (Supplementary Fig. S4B). The deletion mutant Rad14183–333 (pAG9623) was purified similarly but using buffers at pH 7.5 and cation-exchange chromatography rather than anion-exchange chromatography. After the nickel affinity column, the protein was diluted to 150 mM NaCl and then loaded onto a HiTrap SP column (Cytiva). Rad14183–333 was then eluted from the column with a gradient to 400 mM NaCl. As with the other Rad14 proteins, the His tag was cleaved with TEV protease, and the untagged protein was resolved on a His-Trap HP column prior to concentration, buffer exchange, and flash-freezing.

Expression and purification of Saw1

Chemically competent E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) cells (Novagen) carrying a plasmid coding for rare tRNAs were transformed with the pET28a-Saw1 plasmid (pAG8815, or derivatives of this plasmid encoding for specific point mutations) [11]. Saw1 was produced by auto-induction. Cells were grown in terrific broth media including ampicillin (100 µg/ml) and chloramphenicol (25 µg/ml), supplemented with 5052 sugar and trace-metal mixes as previously described [33]. Cell cultures were grown for 60 h at 20°C and harvested by centrifugation. Cell pellets were resuspended in Saw1-NiA buffer (500 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, pH 6.8, 9 mM imidazole, 5% glycerol, and 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with 0.05% LDAO, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM benzamidine, 5 µg/ml leupeptin, and 0.7 µg/ml pepstatin A. The resuspended cells were lysed by sonication and the lysate clarified by centrifugation (40 min at 39 000 × g). Clarified lysates were loaded onto a 5-ml His-Trap HP column (Cytiva) equilibrated with Saw1-NiA buffer. The column was washed with 60 mM imidazole and Saw1 was eluted with 225 mM imidazole. Fractions containing Saw1 were pooled and diluted in HepA buffer (50 mM Bis–Tris propane, pH 6.8, 5% glycerol, and 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol) to a final NaCl concentration of 400 mM. The sample was then loaded onto a HiTrap Heparin HP 1 mL column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with HepA buffer. Saw1 was eluted using a linear gradient to 800 mM NaCl. The protein was concentrated, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until further use (Supplementary Fig. S4B).

Mass photometry and native gel assays

Mass photometry measurements were performed using a Refeyn TwoMP mass photometer (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK). Rad1–Rad10 (2 μM) was incubated with Saw1 variants in a 1:1 ratio for 1 h on ice in reaction buffer (50 mM Bis–Tris propane, pH 6.8, 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol). Samples were rapidly diluted in reaction buffer to a final concentration of 10 nM and 60-s movies recorded using Refeyn AcquireMP software (v2025.1.2). Ratiometric contrast was converted to mass using the MassFerence P1 Calibrant prepared in reaction buffer and mass histograms were then fitted to Gaussian curves using the DiscoverMP software (2025.1.0) to determine the averaged molecular masses (kDa) and relative abundance (%) of individual species. All measurements were repeated at least three times and analyzed using the instrument’s software.

For native gel electrophoresis, Rad1–Rad10 complexes (2 μM) were mixed with equimolar ratios of Saw1 or Rad14 variants in reaction buffer. Mixtures were incubated at 4°C for 1 h. Glycerol was then added to a final concentration of 20%, and samples were resolved in 1% agarose gels run at 50 V for 1 h in 1× Tris–Glycine buffer. Gels were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue and imaged on a Sapphire Biomolecular imager (Azure Biosystems).

DNA binding and nuclease assays

All oligonucleotides used for DNA binding and nuclease assays were purchased HPLC-purified from IDT, Inc (Supplementary Table S2). The 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM) labeled oligonucleotide JR1FAM and JR3FAM (200 nM) were annealed with unlabeled JR2 or JR4 at a 1:1.2 ratio to generate a Y-splayed substrates with either 30 bp duplex and 30 nt flaps (JR1FAM + JR2) or 20 bp duplex and 30 nt flaps (JR3FAM + JR4). The control double-stranded DNA (50 bp) was prepared by annealing JR3FAM:JR5 (1:1.2). All annealing reactions were assembled in 10 mM Bis–Tris, pH 6.8 and 50 mM NaCl, heated to 95°C and cooled down to 4°C over 60 min using a thermal cycler. DNA substrates were stored at −20°C.

For the electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA), DNA substrates (20 nM) were incubated with increasing amounts of protein (0–320 nM) in EMSA buffer [50 mM Bis–Tris propane, pH 6.8, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol, 50 mM NaCl, 0.1 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 10 µg/ml LightShift poly(dI-dC) (Thermo Scientific)] for 1 h at 4°C. Protein:DNA mixtures (10 µl) were supplemented with 50% glycerol (4 µl) and resolved in 5% native polyacrylamide gels run at 4°C (100 V for 1.5 h) using 0.5× Tris–glycine running buffer. All EMSAs were done in the absence of Mg2+ ions to prevent DNA cleavage. Gels were imaged on a Sapphire Biomolecular imager (Azure Biosystems) and quantified using ImageJ [34]. Kd values were calculated by fitting the data (n = 3) to a Hill slope using GraphPad Prism 10 for Mac (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA).

Endonuclease assays were performed as previously described with some modifications [11]. Briefly, 10 µl reactions were prepared in nuclease buffer (50 mM Bis–Tris, pH 6.8, 5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol, 50 mM NaCl) by mixing the DNA substrates (10 nM) with increasing amounts of protein (0–2560 nM) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Mixtures were treated with 100 µg proteinase K and 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and incubated for 15 min at 37°C. The reaction products were mixed with 10 µl of 96% formamide with 0.1% bromophenol blue, boiled for 10 min and resolved in 10% urea–polyacrylamide gels run in 1× TBE buffer at 200 V for 20 min. The mass of the cleaved products was measured by liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS) at the McGill Chemistry Characterization Facility.

Cryo-electron microscopy sample preparation and data collection

Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 was incubated with the Y-splayed DNA substrate (JR1:JR2, Supplementary Table S2) in EM buffer (50 mM Bis–Tris, pH 6.8, 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, and 5 mM of 2-mercaptoethanol). The complex was assembled in a 10-µl reaction including Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 (90 µM) and DNA (135 µM), incubated overnight at 4°C and resolved in a Superose 6 Increase 3.2/300 (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated in EM buffer. The peak corresponding to the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1-DNA complex was concentrated in an Amicon Ultra-0.5 ml centrifugal device 10 kDa MW cut-off (Millipore Sigma) and supplemented with 0.07% octyl-beta-d-glucopyranoside (Sigma–Aldrich). The concentrated complex (3 µl at 3.4 µM) was deposited onto C-flat 2/1 300 mesh grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) pre-treated with chloroform and glow discharged in air at 15 mA for 30 s. Grids were vitrified in liquid ethane using a FEI Vitrobot Mark IV instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) blotting for 3 s with a blot force of +3. The vitrobot chamber was set to 4°C and 100% relative humidity. The Rad1–Rad10–Rad14 sample was prepared by incubating Rad1–Rad10 (4 µM) with Rad14 at a 1:1.2 molar ratio in EM buffer at 4°C for 1 h. Immediately before freezing as described above, the sample was diluted to 3.6 µM Rad1–Rad10 and supplemented with 0.1% glutaraldehyde (Sigma–Aldrich). Micrographs for both samples were collected on a Titan Krios microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 keV and equipped with a Gatan BioQuantum LS K3 direct electron detector (Supplementary Table S3), using the SerialEM software [35].

Cryo-electron microscopy image processing

Cryo-EM data for Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 (Supplementary Fig. S5) and Rad1–Rad10 (Supplementary Fig. S6) were processed using cryoSPARC v4.5.1 [36]. Movies were corrected for beam-induced motion using patch motion correction with default settings. The Contrast Transfer Function (CTF) parameters were estimated using the Patch CTF estimation using default settings. The micrographs were then manually curated to discard low-quality images.

For Rad1–Rad10–Saw1, the resulting micrographs were used for automated blob picking with an elliptical blob 114–146 Å, followed by two consecutive rounds of 2D classification, during which junk particles were discarded. The remaining high-quality particles were used to train a TOPAZ model for further particle picking [37]. To train the model, an estimated particle diameter of 110 Å and 150 estimated particles were used. For TOPAZ extraction, a 25-Å radius of extracted regions was set as a non-default setting. The unique clean particles from blob-picking and from the TOPAZ training were pooled, resulting in a stack of 1 085 035 particles, which were further cleaned using ab initio reconstruction. The ab initio job was run requesting three classes, and with initial and final minibatch sizes of 300 and 1000 particles, respectively. The particles that yielded maps with clear protein features were then subject to heterogeneous refinement using as a reference the ab initio models previously generated and four additional decoy noise models to sieve out junk particles. The decoy volumes were generated by prematurely aborting an ab initio reconstruction job after the noise model estimation step. The best map (517 980 particles) was then subject to non-uniform refinement. The resolution was estimated using a Fourier shell correlation cut-off of 0.143. Local resolution maps were generated using cryoSPARC (Supplementary Fig. S5).

For Rad1–Rad10, a similar processing pipeline was followed. However, the TOPAZ model was iteratively trained through two cycles of TOPAZ training, particle extraction with the optimized model, 2D classification, and selection of the best classes. This yielded a stack of 427 492 particles, which were subjected to ab initio reconstruction requesting three classes (initial and final minibatch sizes of 300 and 1000 particles, respectively), followed by heterogeneous refinement. The two volumes revealing clear protein features were then subjected to an additional round of heterogeneous refinement with three decoy noise models. The best map was then subjected to non-uniform refinement and used for model building (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Model building and refinement

The structure predictions of Rad1 (AF-P06777-F1-v4), Rad10 (AF-P06838-F1-v4), and Saw1 (AF-P39735-F1-v4) were generated with AlphaFold2 [38]. The models were fitted into the cryo-EM map using the Fit to Map tool in ChimeraX [39], and subjected to iterative rounds of manual (Coot 0.9.8.93 [40]) or automated (iSOLDE [41]) model building, and refinement in PHENIX 1.21 [42]. The final model was validated using PHENIX [42]. Figures were generated using PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC) and UCSF ChimeraX [39].

Yeast plasmid and strain construction

All yeast transformations were performed using the lithium acetate method [43]. All plasmids and strains are listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S4. We previously generated 2 micron plasmids encoding either RAD1 (pRD1) or SAW1 (pCY1), including each gene’s endogenous promoter [12]. These are high copy number plasmids maintained at ∼14–34 copies per cell, as determined by quantitative PCR [44]. Both genes were cloned into pRS424 [45], which carries a TRP1 nutritional marker. Here, we created mutations in either RAD1 or SAW1 by site-directed mutagenesis (Genscript) or by subcloning fragments of the overexpression plasmids described above. To create the low copy number SAW1 and saw1 plasmids, a SacII–BamHI fragment was taken from pCY1 (SAW1) and ligated into pRS414 digested with SacII and BamHI. The Saw1-ΔLys25-Pro59 and Saw1-L188S/F226S/Y228S were then created by site-directed mutagenesis. All plasmids were fully sequenced (Plasmidsaurus and Genscript).

Double-strand break survival via single-strand annealing and mating-type switching

These assays were performed as previously described [12]. We used two different double-strand DNA break repair (DSBR) assays to determine functionality in 3′ non-homologous tail removal (3′ NHTR) in vivo, both of which require RAD1 and SAW1 for wild-type repair activity. First, we used an SSA assay, a pathway that leads to repair of a double-strand break (DSB) occurring between direct homologous repeats. All assays were performed in derivatives of EAY1141 [46], which encodes two 205-bp repeats derived from URA3 separated by 2.6 kb containing an HO cleavage site. To test functionality of alleles of RAD1 we transformed EAY1141 rad1Δ [12] with pRD1 (RAD1), pRS424 (empty vector; EV), or one of the rad1 plasmids described above (Supplementary Tables S1 and S4). To test the functionality of alleles of SAW1, EAY1141 saw1Δ [11] was transformed with pCY1 (SAW1), pRS424 (EV) or with one of the saw1 plasmids described above (Supplementary Tables S1 and S4). All of these 2 micron plasmids were derived from pRS424 [45], and carried the TRP1 nutritional marker.

Each strain was struck to single colonies on synthetic complete media plates lacking tryptophan (SC-trp), with 2% glucose as a carbon source. Liquid overnight cultures (3 ml) were grown in a 30°C shaker to saturation from individual colonies in SC-trp+glucose and then diluted into SC-trp+2% lactate as a carbon source (10 ml cultures) and grown in a 30°C shaker to mid-log phase. The culture was divided into two. In one culture, expression of HO endonuclease was induced by the addition of 2% galactose for 5 h in a 30°C shaker to generate DSBs. In the other, 2% glucose was added, and the culture was grown similarly with no HO induction. After 5 h, induction was quenched by addition of 3% glucose. Cultures were serially diluted in TE (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA) and plated in triplicate on SC-trp media and incubated at 30°C for 3 days.

The function of the rad1 alleles was also tested in a mating-type switching assay in the EAY1042-derived strains. This pathway resolves induced DSBs with non-homology on both sides of the HO-induced break by gene conversion at the MAT locus [7, 47]. EAY1115 [47] was used to test function of rad1 alleles. Cultures were established as described above. When cultures grown in liquid SC-trp+lactate media reached mid-log phase, the cultures were divided into two cultures, with the uninduced control treated with 2% glucose. In the second culture, HO endonuclease expression was induced with 2% galactose. Both cultures were grown in a 30°C shaker for 45 min, then diluted in TE (pH 7.4) and plated on SC-trp plates. For both assays, percent survival was calculated as the ratio of colonies that grew following DSB induction relative to the number of colonies that grew without induction. For each plasmid, at least three independent transformants were tested in triplicate and each transformant was tested at least three times. The average for each experiment was plotted on a semi-log plot with error bars showing the standard error of the mean (±SEM). Statistical significance was tested with the Mann–Whitney test.

To determine whether DSBR was accomplished through the predicted mating-type switching, we tested mating type of a sampling of the colonies that survived DSB induction. Repair via mating-type switch gene conversion would result in a switch from MATa to MATα. To determine the rate of switching, we mated 20–30 survivors with FY23 (MATa) or FY86 (MATα) on YPD and replica plated on SC lacking leucine and lysine to select for diploids. Those cells that were able to mate with FY23 (MATa) had switched from a to α following DSB induction and were therefore competent in 3′ NHTR.

UV sensitivity assays

FY23 rad1Δ::KANMX (JSY1540, [12]) was transformed with an empty plasmid or plasmids encoding RAD1 or rad1 alleles. FY23 saw1Δ::NATMX (JSY5777) was transformed with an empty plasmid, SAW1 or saw1 allele plasmids. Each strain was grown to saturation overnight in SC-trp. Optical densities of the cultures were determined by spectroscopy (OD600); densities were adjusted so that they were equal and the adjusted cultures were then serially diluted and plated on SC-trp. After drying, these plates were exposed to UV light at 1 J/m2/s for increasing times. Control plates were diluted and plated as described but not exposed to UV light. Plates were wrapped in aluminum foil and incubated at 30°C for 3–4 days. Colonies were counted and % survival was calculated as the ratio of the number of colonies that formed after UV exposure to the number of colonies that formed without UV exposure. For each plasmid, at least three independent transformants were tested in triplicate and each transformant was tested at least three times. The average for each experiment was plotted on a semi-log plot with error bars showing the standard error of the mean (±SEM). Statistical significance was tested with the Mann–Whitney test.

Western blots to detect Rad1 and rad1 mutant protein levels

We used western blots to determine Rad1 and rad1 mutant protein expression in cells, when expressed from the plasmids encoding RAD1, rad1∆, rad1E680A, rad1E706A, rad1E680A, E706A, rad1P1, rad1DP1, rad1P2, and rad1P3 in the JSY2888 background (Supplementary Table S4). Cultures were grown in 1 l synthetic complete media lacking tryptophan (SC-TRP) until mid-log phase. Cells were harvested and resuspended in IPP150 buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40). Resuspended cells were flash frozen drop-by-drop in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Cell pellets were thawed and resuspended in 20 ml IPP150 buffer, with the addition of PMSF and β-mercaptoethanol to final concentrations of 1 and 10 mM, respectively. Cell lysates were collected using French Pressure Cell Press (Aminco), at a pressure of 1000 psi. Cleared lysates were obtained through centrifugation and concentrated using Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter Unit 100 kDa cutoff. Total protein concentration was measured by Bradford assay.

Protein from each lysate (750 μg) was incubated on ice for 20 min and pre-cleared using 30 μl of a 50% slurry of Protein A/G-agarose beads (Pierce), followed by incubation with 2 μl anti-Rad10 primary mouse antibody at 4°C for 1 h with rocking [12]. Results

Recombinant Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 has structure-selective nuclease activity

Saw1 is difficult to produce and unstable on its own [10, 48]. However, it forms a stable complex with Rad1–Rad10 [11]. Therefore, we co-expressed the three proteins together with a single component (Rad1) including a histidine tag (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Once purified, we used differential scanning fluorimetry to assess the stability of the recombinant Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex and found that, like Saw1 [48], it was most stable in mildly acidic buffers (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). Hence, we used a pH of 6.8 in all subsequent experiments.

Consistent with previously published results [11], recombinant Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex efficiently bound and cleaved Y-splayed DNA substrates (Fig. 2A). Binding to single- and double-stranded DNA was also visible when Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 was present in excess (Fig. 2A). However, Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 did not cleave these DNA substrates (Fig. 2B). Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 bound the Y-splayed DNA with high affinity, and the DNA-bound complex could be resolved by size exclusion chromatography (Fig. 2C and D), when MgCl2 in the buffer was replaced with CaCl2 to prevent DNA cleavage [24, 49, 50]. The increase of the A260/280 ratio from 0.4 for Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 to 1.4 for the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1-DNA complex suggested the presence of DNA in the faster eluting species. The presence of DNA, as well as the presence of the three proteins, was subsequently confirmed by gel electrophoresis (Fig. 2D, inset).

It has been shown that XPF–ERCC1 and Rad1–Rad10 cleave 3′ single-strand DNA tails two nucleotides into the duplex part of the DNA substrate [50, 51]. To assess how Saw1 affected cleavage precision of Rad1–Rad10, we compared the nuclease activity of Rad1–Rad10 on its own, when Saw1 was added to Rad1–Rad10 after purification, and when the three proteins were produced together, using the splayed substrate including 30 nt unpaired single-strand tails. Both samples showed enhance nuclease activity and precision when compared to Rad1–Rad10 on its own as indicated by the prominent band right below the 20-nt molecular weight marker (Fig. 2E). This enhanced cleavage precision was more pronounced when the three proteins were co-expressed, rather than when Saw1 and Rad1–Rad10 were produced separately and mixed in the reaction (Fig. 2E). We reasoned that the complex might form less efficiently when Saw1 is added after purification leading to a more heterogeneous sample, with the nonspecific cleavage products resulting from the nuclease activity of Rad1–Rad10 alone. These results also suggested that, once formed, the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex does not dissociate. To confirm that Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 cleaved with the same specificity as previously described, we used LC-MS (Supplementary Fig. S8). The cleavage product eluted as a major species from the liquid chromatography column with a molecular mass corresponding to Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 cleaving the flap two nucleotides into the duplex section of the substrate.

Architecture of the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex

We then visualized the complex assembled in the presence of CaCl2 by cryo-EM (Supplementary Fig. S5). The resulting cryo-EM map had a global resolution of 3.7 Å and showed clear density for the three proteins (Fig. 3A). However, despite the high affinity of Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 for DNA (Kd = 115 ± 7 nM, Fig. 2C and D), the maps showed no density for the DNA substrate, probably due to dissociation of the complex at the air–water interface during grid vitrification, as it has been previously described for other protein–DNA complexes [52, 53].

The cryo-EM map was of sufficient quality to model most of the complex using the AlphaFold predictions of the individual proteins as starting points (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S9) [38]. Rad1–Rad10 conserves the domain architecture and overall relative domain organization of its human homolog XPF–ERCC1 [19, 20]. Like its human homolog, the helicase-like module of Rad1 adopts a C-shaped architecture (Fig. 3C). Superimposition of the nuclease domains of Rad1 and XPF (PDB 9QED) returns a root mean square deviation of 1.11 Å for 823 atoms. Rad1–Rad10 also retains the characteristic dimerization interface of the XPF–ERCC1/MUS81 family mediated by the nuclease and nuclease-like domains of Rad1 and Rad10 (Supplementary Fig. S10) [2]. The α30-β19-α31 motif in Rad1 interacts with the corresponding α5-β5-α6 motif in Rad10, occluding a surface area of 1 544 Å2 (Supplementary Fig. S10). Analogous to the corresponding interfaces in XPF–ERCC1 [20], Mus81–EME1 [54], and FANCM–FAAP24 [55], this interface is primarily hydrophobic but is further stabilized by polar interactions.

In contrast to the structure of human XPF–ERCC1, the (HhH)2 tandem repeat of Rad1 is disordered and could not be traced in the structure (Fig. 3B and C). Human ERCC1 and EME1, but not Rad10, contain terminal (HhH)2 repeats that dimerize with the (HhH)2 repeats in their nuclease partners, XPF and MUS81 [2, 20]. In the absence of DNA, the (HhH)2 domains of XPF–ERCC1 block the active site (Supplementary Fig. S10), defining the autoinhibited conformation of the complex [20]. It is plausible that the nuclease (HhH)2 repeat only becomes ordered when paired to the (HhH)2 repeat from the binding partner, in which case Rad1–Rad10 could not adopt a similar autoinhibited conformation (Fig. 1). In the autoinhibited conformation, the helical domain of XPF interacts with the (HhH)2 domain of ERCC1, holding the helical and nuclease domains of XPF close to each other [19, 20]. In the absence of this interaction, the helical domain of Rad1 is free to swing outward from the active site (∼15 Å), resulting in a wider conformation (Fig. 3C). This conformational change is unlikely to be caused by Saw1 binding because the structure of XPF–ERCC1 bound to the N-terminal domain of Slx4IP [19], which binds to the same surface as Saw1, retains the auto-inhibited, compact conformation of apo XPF–ERCC1 (Fig. 3D).

Saw1 and Rad1 interact through an extensive interface

Saw1 adopts an overall Y-shaped structure with the central seven-stranded antiparallel β-sheet (β1–β2, β5–β8, β11) defining the stem and two long β-hairpin insertions (β3–β4, β9–β10) protruding in opposite directions defining the arms (Fig. 4A and B). The core domain packs against the Rad1 RecA1 and RecA2 domains, as well as the RecA2 insert, primarily through electrostatic interactions (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S11). Remarkably, the Saw1 core and the structured domain of Slx4IP have the same topology (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S3). While both proteins bind the same surface of Rad1/XPF, the presence of Saw1 arms changes the relative orientation of the two proteins (Fig. 3C and D). The “left” arm, defined by the β3–β4 hairpin, wraps around the RecA1 domain, forming a small β-sheet with the β1 strand of Rad1 (Fig. 4D). The “right” arm, defined by strands β9 and β10, and the intervening disordered region threads through the RecA2 insert and interacts with the RecA2 domain (Fig. 4E).

Deleting the left arm (Saw1ΔL, Δ25–59) does not abrogate complex formation as judged by mass photometry (Fig. 4F and G), although it alters the migration of the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex on a native gel (Supplementary Fig. S12A). Removal of the disordered portion of the right arm (Saw1ΔR, Δ198–222) does not affect complex formation either (Fig. 4G). However, point mutations within the ordered region of this arm (Saw1-3S, Saw1-L188S/F226S/Y228S) completely disrupt the interaction (Fig. 4G). Interestingly, the simultaneous removal of both the left arm and the disordered portion of the right (Saw1ΔLR, Δ25–59, and Δ198–222) results in two different migrating species on a native gel corresponding to the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex and Rad1–Rad10 alone (Supplementary Fig. S12A). These results indicate that the core and ordered region of the right arm drive the interaction with Rad1–Rad10, but the arm extensions further stabilize the complex.

It has been previously shown that the Saw1-Δ18–24 deletion variant, or a variant carrying a single point mutation in this region (Saw-R19A), compromises 3′-flap removal by Rad1–Rad10 in a plasmid-based 3′-flap removal assay [11]. Saw1-R19A weakens the interaction with Rad1–Rad10, as judged by the presence of bound and unbound species detected by mass photometry (Fig. 4G). The Saw1-R19A variant also reduces the nuclease activity of Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex to levels comparable to Rad1–Rad10 (Fig. 4H). The side chain of Arg19 is opposite to Glu706 in Rad1 (Fig. 4I). A point mutation in this residue has been shown to abolish 3′ NHTR without affecting NER [11]. The Rad1E680A, E706A variant, which also carries a point mutation in a residue shown to be important for the interaction with Saw1 [16], also weakens the interaction between Saw1 and Rad1–Rad10 (Supplementary Fig. S12B).

Disrupting the Rad1–Saw1 interface inhibits 3′ NHTR but not nucleotide excision repair

Saw1 recruits Rad1–Rad10 to SSA intermediates [10, 11], whereas Rad14 recruits it to UV lesions repaired by NER [13]. Therefore, we reasoned that disrupting the Saw1–Rad1 interface would compromise 3′ NHTR in DSBR activity without significantly affecting the repair of UV-lesions. To test this idea, we first characterized the in vivo effects of SAW1 alleles carrying defects on the arms of the protein. We constructed an ARS CEN unit copy plasmid carrying SAW1 under the control of its endogenous promoter to test complementation of a saw1Δ in 3′ NHTR activity. We induced a DSB between two direct 205 bp URA3 repeats [46]. This type of DSB is repaired via SSA; 5′-3′ resection of the DSB ends generates 3′ single-stranded DNA from the repeats, which form recombination intermediates with 3′ NHTs. Failure to repair the break is lethal and, therefore, reduced survival rates indicate SSA repair defects. The SAW1 plasmid was able to complement the saw1Δ in our SSA assay, resulting in survival rates of ∼80%, similar to the wild-type background (Fig. 5A). We then constructed two saw1 alleles, encoding altered left and right arms of the protein. For the left arm, we used the deletion we assayed in vitro (saw1ΔL). For the right arm, we used a variant of Saw1 with a triple mutation in strand β10 (saw1L188S, F226S, Y228S; saw1-3S) because the original deletion removed the single sumoylation site of Saw1 [56] and could have potentially confounded the interpretation of the results. The SSA survival rates for saw1ΔL and saw1-3S were significantly reduced when compared to wild type (Fig. 5A), providing functional evidence that the extensive Saw1–Rad1 interaction network is important for the function of the complex. We also tested the UV sensitivity of cells in the presence of SAW1 or the saw1 alleles. It has been previously shown that saw1Δ has a mild UV sensitivity at high UV doses [56]. Saw1 is thought to promote UV survival in a pathway that is independent of Rad1 but dependent on Saw1 sumoylation and its interaction with Slx4 [56]. We observed a similar UV sensitivity in saw1Δ that was complemented by the low copy SAW1 plasmid. The saw1 alleles predicted to disrupt the interaction with Rad1 also complemented this UV sensitivity. Therefore, these saw1 alleles are separation-of-function mutations, which is consistent with the presence of functional Saw1 or saw1 protein in these cells (Fig. 5A).

We also created 2 micron (high copy) versions of the SAW1 and saw1 plasmids and tested their activity in SSA and UV survival (Supplementary Fig. S13). The saw1 alleles exhibited a milder, but still significant, defect in SSA compared to the low copy plasmids, consistent with increased protein expression suppressing some of the defect. Interestingly, the high copy SAW1 plasmid did not completely complement the UV sensitivity as well as the low copy version, although survival was increased relative to saw1Δ. The reduced complementation suggested the possibility that excess Saw1 in the cell disrupts this Saw1 sumoylation/Slx4-dependent pathway. The high copy saw1 allele plasmids exhibited a UV sensitivity similar to saw1Δ, suggesting a possible dominant negative effect.

To probe the effect of the interactions mediated by the core region of Saw1 on Rad1–Rad10 activities, we generated the rad1E680A, rad1E706A, and rad1E680A,E706A alleles, subcloned them under the control of the endogenous RAD1 promoter, and tested their ability to complement rad1Δ cells in DSBR that requires 3′ NHTR and in UV sensitivity assays. We previously demonstrated that the high copy RAD1 (2 micron) plasmid complements rad1Δ in 3′ NHTR and UV sensitivity assays [12]. The Rad1-E706K variant was previously shown to be a separation-of-function variant with defects in 3′ NHTR but normal UV sensitivity [16]. To assess the effect of a milder mutation at this position, we used the rad1E706A allele to determine whether this variant also conferred a separation-of-function phenotype. As expected, the mutants retained NER activity comparable to WT cells (Fig. 5B). This observation confirms that Saw1 does not play a role in NER and indicates that these Rad1–Rad10 variants are stable in vivo. Consistent with this observation, qualitative western blots indicated similar Rad1 and rad1 mutant protein levels in these cells (Supplementary Fig. S14). In contrast, SSA survival rates were reduced relative to wild-type RAD1 in the presence of all three rad1 alleles (Fig. 5B). However, the reduction for the rad1E680A mutant was not statistically significant. This result is consistent with previous work demonstrating an SSA defect in the presence of rad1E706K [11], and links this defect to the interaction with Saw1.

To confirm the function of these mutants and to verify 3′ NHTR specifically, we used a second assay to measure 3′ NHTR, inducing a break at the MAT locus [7]. Repair of this break is via gene conversion from HMLa via an intermediate with double non-homology at the break, resulting in two 3′ NHTs that must be resolved. MAT locus gene conversion results in mating-type switching, allowing us to distinguish between appropriate gene conversion and other DSBR repair pathways that allow survival. Failure to repair these breaks is also lethal and, therefore, reduced survival rates indicate 3′ NHTR defects. The survival rates of the three rad1 mutants were significantly reduced in this assay and were similar for all the mutants (∼43%). This level of survival is comparable to that of rad1Δ cells (38%), while wild-type RAD1 supported ∼80% survival (Fig. 5C). The three alleles did, however, show different levels of mate-type switching (Fig. 5D), which indicates differences in the proportion of breaks that are repaired via the mating-type switching gene conversion pathway, which requires 3′ NHTR, versus other mechanisms of DSBR. In the presence of RAD1, ~80% of the survivors repair the DSB via Rad1-dependent gene conversion. In contrast, in rad1Δ, almost none of the breaks are repaired by the Rad1-dependent pathway despite the 38% survival rate. In this assay, rad1E680A supported only ~40% survival of the induced DSB, but over 80% of the survivors repaired the break via Rad1-dependent mating-type switching [7]. In contrast, rad1E706A was more significantly impaired for mating-type switching, with ∼45% of the survivors undergoing this pathway. Finally, in the presence of rad1E680A,E706A, ∼70% of the survivors repaired the break via mating-type switch, indicating that the presence of the E680A mutation counteracts the effect of the E706A mutation on gene conversion. Combined, these data strongly indicate that rad1E680A, rad1E706A, and rad1E680A,E706A cells are separation-of-function mutants deficient in SSA but proficient in NER. Interestingly, our data indicate that the mating-type switch assay is more sensitive to Saw1–Rad1 interaction defects than the SSA repair assay.

Rad14 and Saw1 bind to opposite surfaces of Rad1

To understand how Saw1 and Rad14 impose different substrate specificities on Rad1–Rad10, we pursued the structural characterization of the Rad14-Rad1–Rad10 complex (Supplementary Fig. S6). Even though it has been shown that the Rad1–Rad10–Rad14 complex can withstand multiple purification steps [57], the cryo-EM maps did not reveal any density for Rad14 (Supplementary Fig. S15), therefore this structure represents Rad1–Rad10 alone. Rad1–Rad10 adopted the same conformation as in the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex and could be fitted into the density maps with minor rigid body adjustments. The RecA2 insert region of Rad1, which was stabilized by the interaction with Saw1 in the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 structure, is disordered in this structure (Supplementary Fig. S15). The comparison of these two structures confirmed that Saw1 binding does not impose major rearrangements on the Rad1–Rad10 complex but stabilizes some of its flexible regions.

The AlphaFold prediction of the Rad14-Rad1–Rad10 complex returned a template modelling score (pTM) of 0.56, indicating that the overall fold of the complex might resemble the real structure [58]. Accordingly, superimposition of Rad10 or the individual domains of Rad1 to our cryo-EM structure of the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complex returned r.m.s. deviations ranging from 0.8–1.6 Å. As expected, the AlphaFold prediction for the DNA-binding domain of Rad14 was identical to the crystal structure [59]. Beyond the DNA-binding domain, Rad14 is unstructured (Supplementary Fig. S16A) and returned a low interface-predicted template modelling score (ipTM of 0.47). Despite the poor ipTM score, the interface was consistent with previously published biochemical data showing that a variant of Rad1 carrying the E750K,S752N point mutations (Rad1E750K,S752N), which is part of the predicted interface, does not interact with Rad14 [13]. Additionally, Met340 is also at the interface and the Rad1M340P variant is extremely sensitive to UV [16]. Therefore, we used the predicted align error (PAE) matrix to identify additional regions within the predicted Rad1–Rad14 interface that were highly correlated (Supplementary Fig. S16B).

Using this approach, we identified three small patches in Rad14 that were highly correlated to regions in Rad1 (Fig. 6A). Patch 1 involves helices α4 and α6 in the DNA-binding domain of Rad14 interacting with the RecA2 insert sequence (residues 559–591) of Rad1 (Fig. 6A). This region of the RecA2 insert is predicted to form helices α18 and α19 but is disordered in both of our structures, suggesting that Rad14-binding may be required to stabilize this conformation. The second patch (patch 2) involves one face of helix α7. This interaction positions Glu303 and Tyr306 from Rad14 near Gln334 and Met340 in Rad1, and Arg314 at a distance compatible with the formation of a hydrogen bond with Glu750 from Rad1 (Fig. 6A-B). The last patch (patch 3) involves the C-terminal region of Rad14, which defines an electrostatic network between the side chains of Asn324, Arg325 and Lys333 from Rad14 and Asp9 and Asp11 from Rad1 (Fig. 6A-B).

We then generated a series of Rad1 and Rad14 variants to probe whether these patches were necessary for the Rad14–Rad1 interaction. To validate patch 1, we generated a deletion variant of Rad1 in which helices α18 and α19 from the RecA2 insert were replaced by a 10-glycine-serine linker (Rad1ΔP1–Rad10, Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table S1). This region is disordered in the Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 structure. Therefore, we reasoned that replacing it with a glycine-rich linker would not affect the integrity of the protein. Accordingly, we could over-express and purify this variant similarly to wild-type Rad1–Rad10 complex, suggesting that this variant was also stable. However, Rad14 interacted similarly with Rad1–Rad10 and Rad1ΔP1–Rad10 (Fig. 6C, compare lanes 1–2 and 3–4), indicating that either this region of the Rec2 insert was not important for the interaction or only had a marginal role.

To probe patch 2, we subcloned the Rad14R314E variant as this residue is the most likely to interact with Glu750 in Rad1 [13]. We found that this variant weakened the interaction with Rad1–Rad10 as observed by the relative increase of free Rad14 and Rad1–Rad10 in the mixture when compared to the complex assembled with wild-type proteins (Fig. 6C, compare lanes 3–5 and 8–9). This finding confirms that helix α7 mediates the interaction and independently explains why Rad1E750K,S752N does not interact with Rad14 [13]. Lastly, we subcloned fragments of Rad14 including either the C-terminal region of the protein (Rad14183-371) or just the DNA-binding domain and helix α7 (Rad14183-333) to probe patch 3 (Fig. 6A). Most interestingly, neither variant interacted with Rad1–Rad10 (Fig. 6C, compare lanes 6–7 to 4) indicating that, although helix α7 may be necessary for the interaction, it is not sufficient for complex formation. This result also indicates that AlphaFold provides a fragmented description of the interface of the complex because regions of Rad14 preceding residue Glu183 are also necessary for the interaction.

Disrupting the interaction with Rad14 inhibits nucleotide excision repair without affecting 3′ NHTR

We next used our in vivo assays to assess the functional relevance of the residues predicted to mediate Rad1–Rad14 interactions. We predicted that disruptions to this interface would impair NER, resulting in increased UV sensitivity. Since Rad14 does not play a role in SSA, we also predicted that mutations disrupting this interface would not affect survival in SSA. We constructed mutations in RAD1 targeted to the predicted interface mediated by patch 1 [rad1K571E,R572E,R576E; rad1P1 and rad1Δ550–594::2x(GGGGS); rad1ΔP1], patch 2 (rad1Q334A,Q336S; rad1P2); and patch 3 (rad1D9A,D11A; rad1P3), and confirmed that all mutant proteins were expressed like rad1 by western blot (Supplementary Fig. S14). The rad1P1 and rad1ΔP1 mutations resulted in severe defects in nucleotide-excision repair (NER), with UV sensitivity increasing to levels nearly as severe as for rad1Δ (Fig. 6D). In contrast, the rad1P2 mutation resulted in wild-type levels of UV sensitivity, indicating that these changes did not significantly affect the interaction with Rad14 (Fig. 6D). This surface of Rad1 is probably important for the interaction, but Alphafold fails to correctly predict the relative orientation between Rad14 and the helical domain of Rad1. The mutation designed to compromise patch 3, rad1D9A,D11A, did not compromise NER either (Fig. 6D). In fact, this mutation demonstrated slightly better survival in response to UV light than wild type at the highest dosages tested. As expected, rad1ΔP1, rad1P2, and rad1P3 did not affect survival in SSA (Fig. 6E). Similarly, these mutants showed similar survival and mate-type switching rates to wild-type RAD1 (Supplementary Fig. S17). In contrast, rad1P1 exhibited a small but significant decrease in survival in both SSA and mating-type switch assays. Of the three patches, this is the one closest to the surface recognized by Saw1. Therefore, this mutation could induce conformational changes that affect Saw1 and/or Msh2–Msh3 binding.

Discussion

Most DNA repair pathways rely on structure-selective endonucleases to process repair intermediates. The precise regulation of these endonucleases is crucial, as their inappropriate or nonspecific activation could not only hinder repair but aggravate the damage. Structural studies of structure-selective endonucleases in the past decades have shed light on mechanisms used by these enzymes to prevent nonspecific cleavage. Some of them, like the 5′-flap endonuclease FEN1 sharply bend DNA to validate their substrates [60]. Others, like Mus81-Eme1, rely on extensive DNA-induced conformational rearrangements to position the incised strand on the active site [54]. The mechanisms by which binding partners spatially and temporally regulate the activity of structure-selective nucleases in various pathways is increasingly recognized as an essential aspect of the DNA damage response.

The recruitment of XPF–ERCC1 to UV-lesions or inter-strand crosslinks is mediated by binding to either XPA or SLX4, respectively [14, 16, 61]. While the yeast and human nucleases are highly conserved (32.4% sequence identity), the mechanisms by which they are recruited and activated seem to have diverged. We now show that Saw1 and Rad14 recognize opposite surfaces of the Rad1–Rad10 complex to target this nuclease to recombination intermediates and UV-lesions, respectively. Previous work has demonstrated that Rad1–Rad10–Rad14 complexes and Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 complexes likely exist in vivo [12, 57], but it is unknown whether quaternary complexes also exist in vivo. Saw1 and Rad1–Rad10 interact through an extensive interface. Notably, Saw1 has the same topology as Slx4IP and both proteins bind to the same surface of Rad1/XPF. Saw1 and Slx4IP are conserved at the sequence and structure levels (Supplementary Fig. S3), but their specific contacts with Rad1/XPF differ (Fig. 3C). The different interaction mode may also lead to functional differences. Slx4IP does not stimulate the nuclease activity of XPF–ERCC1 in the absence of Slx4 [19]. In contrast, Saw1 stimulates both the nuclease activity and enhances the cleavage precision of Rad1–Rad10, indicating that the extended Saw1 topology suffices to properly orient DNA substrates for catalysis.

In the absence of Saw1, Rad1–Rad10 has weak endonuclease activity and cleaves Y-splayed DNAs off-target, into the single-stranded 3′-flap of the substrate. This contrasts with the activity of other Rad1/XPF/MUS81 nucleases like archaeal Pyrococcus furiosus Hef and XPF–ERCC1, which, even in the absence of other factors, still cleave splayed substrates two to eight nucleotides into the duplex [50, 62]. Human XPF–ERCC1 adopts an autoinhibited conformation in the absence of DNA. However, the structures of Rad1–Rad10 and Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 do not adopt this autoinhibited conformation, likely from Rad10 lacking the (HhH)2 domain. The autoinhibited state of XPF–ERCC1 is achieved through contacts between the helical domain and the ERCC1 (HhH)2 domain (Fig. 3C). It is possible that Saw1-mediated DNA substrate binding bypasses the need for an autoinhibited state.

Sarangi et al. [56] proposed that Saw1 functions as a scaffolding protein for Rad1–Rad10 and the 5′ flap endonuclease Slx1–Slx4. This is similar to the human proteins, in which XPF is proposed to bridge the interaction between Slx4IP and Slx4 [19]. The exchange of Saw1 from one nuclease to the other is dependent on the sumoylation of the residue Saw1K221. In our structure, this residue is positioned in the flexible loop that extends from the right arm of Saw1 leaving it accessible for the modification to occur even when bound to Rad1–Rad10. How the sumoylation of this arm disrupts complex formation with Rad1–Rad10 and enables the exchange to Slx1–Slx4 remains to be elucidated, but it reinforces the idea that Saw1 heavily relies on interactions with other proteins for proper folding and stability.

Slx4 and Saw1 operate in the same replication-coupled ICL repair pathway [16], as well as in SSA, with Slx4 playing a role downstream of Rad1–Rad10 recruitment [10, 11, 63]. Slx4IP is also involved in inter-strand crosslink repair [64], but its involvement in 3′ NHTR remains to be explored. In yeast, the recruitment of Rad1–Rad10 to recombination substrates is dependent on the SSA protein Rad52. However, their interaction is indirect and mediated by Saw1 [10, 12]. Human RAD52, on the other hand, directly interacts with XPF and stimulates its endonuclease activity during recombination [65]. The mismatch repair Msh2–Msh3 complex also facilitates recruitment of Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 to recombination intermediates with 3′ NHTs [12, 66–70]. In yeast, Msh2–Msh3 interacts directly with both Rad1–Rad10 and with Saw1 [12, 71], and recruits Rad1–Rad10 to B-/Z-DNA junctions [72]. Human MSH2–MSH3 has also been shown to interact with NER factors, including XPF–ERCC1, to promote repair of interstrand cross-links [73–76]. MSH2–MSH3 interacts with the SLX1-SLX4-MUS81-EME1-XPF–ERCC1 (SMX) complex to stimulate its Holliday junction resolution activity [73]. The Msh2–Msh3/Rad1–Rad10–Saw1 interfaces are currently unknown but are also predicted to be critical for the appropriate recruitment of the Rad1–Rad10 complex. We cannot exclude the possibility that some of the rad1 and/or saw1 mutations tested here also affect interactions with Msh2–Msh3 to compromise 3′ NHTR. Surface exposed mutations on the helical domain of Rad1/XPF identified in Fanconi anemia patients also affect SSA [16], but these defects cannot be explained by the interaction between Saw1 and Rad1.

Collectively, our results start to unveil how pathway-specific binding partners modulate the recruitment of the structure-selective nuclease Rad1–Rad10 to specific substrates. Further structural and functional studies with relevant repair factors and DNA substrates will be necessary to illuminate the intricate regulation mechanisms that recruit and position these enzymes at lesion sites to preserve genome integrity.
