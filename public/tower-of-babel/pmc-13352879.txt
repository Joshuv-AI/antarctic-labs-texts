Extensive frontal cortex abnormalities and cognitive impairment in methamphetamine use disorder: a comprehensive 3T structural MRI analysis
Authors: Yanan Zhou, Winson Fu Zun Yang, Qiuxia Wu, Yuejiao Ma, Honghong Ren, Yuzhu Hao, Manyun Li, Yunfei Wang, Pu Peng, Ning Yuan, Yifan Xiong, Yizhuo Wang, Tieqiao Liu, Qianjin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMethamphetamine (MA) is a substance commonly misused globally, with unclear brain imaging mechanisms impacting cognitive functions. This investigation sought to understand the cognitive traits of individuals with methamphetamine use disorders (MUDs) and relate these to structural brain changes, examined from a comprehensive perspective.MethodsThis research encompassed 45 individuals with MUDs (MUD group) and 43 healthy controls (HC group). The MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB), including 10 subtests covering 7 cognitive domains, was administered to evaluate cognitive abilities in participants, while a 3.0T MRI scanner was employed to gather structural brain data.ResultsCompared to the HC group, the MUD group displayed reduced gray matter volume in the right insula, left middle frontal, left postcentral, and bilateral angular gyrus, while the left precentral, left postcentral, left superior frontal, left parstriangularis, left supramarginal, and left parsopercularis exhibited decreased cortical thickness (PFDR-corr < 0.05, cluster > 100). The digit-symbol coding test regression model demonstrated a noteworthy interaction effect between group and cortical thickness at the left precentral (p = 0.01) and left parsopercularis (p = 0.04). In the neuropsychological assessment battery-mazes (NAB-Mazes), a positive correlation was found between cortical thickness in the left parsopercularis and NAB-Mazes scores (p = 0.02), with a significant group interaction (p = 0.001). For the continuous performance test (CPT), a negative correlation emerged between cortical thickness in the left superior frontal and CPT scores (p = 0.03).ConclusionMUDs exhibit altered gray matter morphology in several brain regions, where alterations in frontal cortex thickness may underlie the neuroanatomy of cognitive deficits common to MUDs.

Introduction

Chronic methamphetamine (MA) use can lead to diminished cognitive functioning or impair the development of normative cognitive traits [1]. Meta-analyses indicate that patients with MA use disorder (MUDs) exhibit various cognitive impairments compared to healthy controls (HCs), mainly related to learning ability, memorization functions, execution skills, language/verbal fluency and attention, and the effect sizes of these impairments are usually in the moderate range [2]. Cognitive dysfunctions heighten the risk of relapse, contribute to treatment failure, and are strongly associated with individuals with MUDs [3–5]. The exact pathways and mechanisms responsible for these cognitive issues are still poorly understood, emphasizing the critical need for further research to unravel these underlying processes.

Historically, voxel-based morphometry (VBM) has been the predominant method in studying in vivo brain morphology in MUDs, revealing extensive reductions in gray matter volume (GMV) across several brain regions relative to HCs. Key affected areas include the frontal cortex [6, 7], temporal cortex, fusiform [7, 8], caudate nucleus [9], parietal cortex, insula, and hippocampus [10–12]. These structural alterations are believed to impact a range of cognitive-behavioral abilities critical for inhibitory control, executive capabilities, memory abilities, and reward processing, which are essential for achieving and maintaining abstinence [13–15]. It is evident that although previous studies have consistently reported a decrease in GMV in MUDs, suggesting that this decline may lead to cognitive impairments, the results vary and merit further investigation.

Additionally, earlier research has indicated that VBM alone may not adequately explain certain cognitive difficulties in the brain, as pinpointing the specific neural basis of individual cognitive functions using VBM is challenging [16]. This limitation stems from the fact that VBM results may be influenced by variations in cortical thickness, surface area, GMV, and cortical folding, or any combination thereof. Conversely, surface-based morphometry (SBM) offers complementary insights into brain morphology by measuring cortical thickness, which reflects the structural integrity of gray matter by gauging the distance from the brain’s surface to the gray matter/white matter boundary, indicating neuron density and count per unit area [17, 18]. Research suggests that changes in cortical thickness may be more indicative of disease status than alterations in GMV or cortical surface area [19].

Despite the potential of SBM, research on the association between cortical thickness and cognitive capabilities among MUDs remains sparse and yields inconsistent findings. For instance, research reveals that cortical thickness in the orbitofrontal cortex, precuneus, and inferior parietal limbic is reduced in young individuals with MUDs compared to HCs, with a significant negative correlation with executive function. Conversely, in adults with MUDs, reduced cortical thickness in the temporal limbic region showed no correlation with executive capabilities [20]. Another study reported no significant differences in overall mean cortical thickness between MUDs and HCs; however, in MUDs, cortical thickness negatively correlated with years of cumulative MA use and drug craving, but not with usage frequency or number of uses [21]. The variability in these findings may stem from population heterogeneity, brain imaging methodologies, cognitive function assessment dimensions, data preprocessing steps, and statistical analysis techniques. Further research is necessary to elucidate the relationship between cortical thickness changes and cognitive functions in MUDs.

Given the distinct and complementary insights offered by cortical thickness and GMV into structural brain changes, and considering the limited knowledge about how these changes correlate with cognitive functions in MUDs, it becomes essential to further explore these relationships. This study aims to investigate the structural brain alterations in MUDs and their association with cognitive functions by integrating VBM and SBM techniques. The goal is to elucidate the possible neuroanatomical mechanisms behind cognitive deficits in MUDs from a morphological perspective, which could pave the way for more targeted and effective treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Forty-five individuals diagnosed with MUD according to the DSM-5 criteria were enrolled in August 2019 and January 2020 at the Changsha Kangda Voluntary Drug Rehabilitation Center in Hunan, China. Eligibility criteria included being right-handed, Han Chinese men aged 18 to 45, capable of independently completing questionnaires. These individuals had only used MA apart from nicotine and had no significant neurological issues, other mental disorders, or family history of psychiatric conditions. They were also required to have no magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contraindications such as claustrophobia or a pacemaker. All were recent abstainers, having ceased use for less than three months. The facility maintained a strict drug-free policy, though smoking was permitted, capped at five cigarettes daily. Participants received structured mental health education, physical exercise programs, and necessary medications.

Additionally, 43 HCs were recruited from the local community through advertisements and referrals, matching the MUD participants in all criteria except for the diagnosis of MUD.

Evaluation of clinical features

Essential information for sociodemographic and MA use

This section’s data were gathered via a self-completed survey on demographic specifics and patterns of MA consumption. Key points collected included demographic details like age and education, alongside usage metrics for substances such as tobacco, alcohol, and betel nuts. The questionnaire further delved into the onset age of MA use, the overall length of usage, average amounts consumed during each use, and total period of abstinence.

Evaluation of cognitive abilities

The computerized MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB), administered in Chinese, was used to assess the multidimensional cognitive abilities of participants on the same day as MRI scanning. This battery includes ten different subtests: trail making test-A (TMT-A), digit-symbol coding test (DSCT), category fluency test (CF), spatial span test (SSP), letter number span test (LNSP), neuropsychology assessment battery-mazes test (NAB-Mazes), brief visuospatial memory test-revised (BVMT-R), continuous performance test (CPT), Hopkins verbal learning test-revised (HVLT-R), and Mayer-Salovey-Caruso emotional intelligence test: managing emotions (MSCEIT-ME). These subtests collectively measure seven cognitive domains, with six assessing neurocognitive and one evaluating social cognitive abilities. All scores are standardized T-scores, and higher scores indicate better cognitive performance.

Conducted by the same psychiatrist in a consistent environment, the complete battery requires about an hour. The test automatically adjusts scores for age, gender, and educational background, providing standardized T-scores for individual subtests and overall performance. Notably, the Chinese adaptation of the MCCB demonstrates high sensitivity, specificity, and retest reliability [22].

Acquiring and pre-processing MRI data

Acquiring MRI data

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing involved using a 3.0T Siemens Skyra scanner with a 16-channel head coil. The process captured whole-brain 3D T1-weighted structural MRI images via MPRAGE, utilizing the parameters: TR/TE = 2530/2.98 ms, flip angle = 7°, field of view = 256 × 256 mm, slice thickness=1 mm, no slice gap, voxel dimensions=1 mm³, with a total of 176 slices, and a scan duration of 363 s.

Pre-processing MRI data

For preprocessing, MRI scans were processed using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox12 (CAT12) on the Statistical Parametric Mapping12 (SPM12) platform within Matlab2021b. Key steps included initial scans reviewed by a radiologist to rule out intracranial anomalies, including stroke, traumatic brain injury, tumor, or other structural lesions, which were strict exclusion criteria, image quality assessments to exclude artifacts, and manual adjustments of image origins to the anterior commissure to enhance alignment quality. Further steps involved spatial normalization of T1 images to MNI space using DARTEL, segmentation of structural images into different tissue types, and modulation of gray matter images. Post-processing checks ensured the removal of poorly aligned or segmented images and confirmed sample homogeneity. Final steps in the analysis included calculating the estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) to adjust for head size variance and applying Gaussian kernels of 8 mm full-width at half maximum for GMV smoothing and 15 mm for cortical thickness smoothing in subsequent analyses.

VBM analysis

The VBM method estimated regional volumes of interest (ROI) using the Neuromorphometrics atlas [23] within the CAT12 software for gray matter volume (GMV) analysis [24]. Differences in GMV across groups were assessed with an SPM 12 t-test matrix, adjusted for covariates such as age, educational attainment, and lifestyle factors including smoking, alcohol, and betel nut usage, as well as eTIV. Statistical significance was corrected for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method, with thresholds set at p < 0.05 and clusters larger than 100 voxels.

SBM analysis

The SBM method was employed to assess cortical thickness using projection-based thickness estimation [25]. This method quantified the thickness of the cortical layers in both brain hemispheres [24]. Furthermore, cortical metrics for each ROI were extracted using the Desikan-Killiany atlas [26] for subsequent ROI-based cortical thickness analysis. This analysis paralleled the ROI-based VBM approach, employing an SPM independent samples t-test matrix to examine differences in cortical thickness across groups, factoring in covariates like age, education, and lifestyle choices such as smoking, alcohol, and betel nut consumption. Statistical significance was maintained through corrections for multiple comparisons using the FDR, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05 and a minimum cluster size of 100.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were executed in R statistical software. Prior to statistical analyses, data normality, variance chi-square, and multicollinearity were checked using histograms, Q-Q plots, standardized residual plots of predicted values, and variance inflation factors (VIF). To analyze demographic and clinical differences between two subject groups, independent samples t-tests and chi-square tests were utilized. Subsequently, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) incorporating age, educational attainment, and lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and betel nut usage was employed to evaluate cognitive function variations between the groups, applying a Bonferroni correction for post hoc analyses (k = 10). Age, education, smoking, and betel nut use were included as covariates to isolate MUD-specific effects by eliminating confounding variance, which is a standard approach in neuroimaging studies of substance use disorders. We also performed a retrospective subgroup matching by age, education, and smoking status, and the key findings remained consistent. Furthermore, to further understand the correlation between variations of GMV or cortical thickness in differential brain regions and cognitive function, this study determined the anatomical locations of the differential clusters according to the Desikan-Killiany atlas. The mean GMV or cortical thickness values of the differential brain regions were extracted by CAT12 software, and the cognitive subtests that differed from those that differed between groups were selected for further regression analysis, with results corrected using Bonferroni correction (k = 4).

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical profiles

The sociodemographic and clinical profiles of the participants are detailed in Table 1. The mean age of MUDs was significantly higher than that of the HCs (31.37 ± 5.51 vs. 26.05 ± 6.81 years; t = 4.00, p < 0.001). Additionally, MUDs had significantly fewer years of education compared to HCs (11.46 ± 3.15 vs. 14.00 ± 3.20 years; t = 3.86, p < 0.001). High rates of smoking were observed among MUDs (95.7%) compared to HCs (45.5%), with a significant difference in prevalence (χ²=25.46, p < 0.001). Differences in betel nut use were also notable, with 67.4% of MUDs using betel nuts compared to 40.9% of HCs (χ²=4.52, p = 0.034). However, alcohol consumption did not show a statistically significant difference between the two groups (54.3% of MUDs vs. 31.8% of HCs; χ²=2.39, p = 0.12). Additional clinical details specific to MUDs included the age of first MA use (mean 25.59 ± 6.09 years), average MA dose (0.37 ± 0.24 g), cumulative duration of MA use (6.08 ± 3.16 years), and continuous duration of MA abstinence (71.60 ± 10.80 days).

Relationship of structural MRI indices with cognitive abilities

Correlation between GMV and cognitive abilities

The results of the four regression models with statistically significant intergroup differences, namely DSCT, CPT, HVLT-R, and NAB-Mazes, showed no relationship with GMV.

Correlation between cortical thickness and cognitive abilities

The DSCT regression analysis revealed a significant model [F (18, 69) = 3.47, p < 0.001], explaining 48% of the variance (R²) with notable interactions between group characteristics and cortical measurements in the left parsopercularis (β = -0.43, p = 0.04) and left precentral regions (β = 0.58, p = 0.01). Similarly, the NAB-Mazes model was robust [F (18, 69) = 4.16, p < 0.001], with an R² of 52%. This model showed a positive correlation between the left parsopercularis cortical thickness and NAB-Mazes performance (β = 0.43, p = 0.02) in the HC group, alongside a significant negative interaction with group characteristics (β = -0.68, p = 0.001). The CPT model also demonstrated significance [F (12, 75) = 4.69, p < 0.001], accounting for 43% of variance in scores. Here, cortical thickness in the left superior frontal gyrus significantly predicted CPT results (β = -0.31, p = 0.03) in the MUD group, with thinner cortex associated with worse sustained attention. However, the HVLT-R regression did not reveal significant predictors, as detailed in Table 5; Fig. 4.

Discussion

This study first utilized a combination of VBM and SBM to investigate the alterations in GMV and cortical thickness in MUDs, while also investigating these changes’ associations with various cognitive abilities. VBM and SBM provide complementary morphological information: VBM reflects regional gray matter volume changes integrating density and surface area, while SBM specifically quantifies cortical thickness representing neuronal layer integrity. The structural MRI indicated a decrease in the left frontal cortex areas (covering the superior, middle frontal, parstriangularis, and parsopercularis regions), the right insula, and the bilateral inferior parietal lobule (comprising the angular gyrus and left supramarginal gyrus), as well as the sensorimotor cortex (encompassing left precentral gyrus and postcentral gyrus) in MUD group compared to HC group. Further regression analysis revealed that cortical thickness in the left frontal cortex (including the precentral, superior frontal, and parsopercularis regions) was associated with sustained attention, information processing speed, and executive capability. The study by Zhou et al. found extensive structural abnormalities in the frontal cortex of individuals with methamphetamine use disorder, which were linked to cognitive dysfunction [28].

Intergroup differences in structural alterations of the frontal cortex and their associations with cognitive deficits

In the current study, the MUD group exhibited significantly thinner cortical thickness in the left superior frontal, parstriangularis, parsopercularis, and precentral regions, alongside decreased GMV in the left middle frontal, highlighting substantial frontal cortical structural changes in MUDs. This finding aligns with several prior studies, notably one from our team [29], which showed reduced GMV in the bilateral superior frontal in short-term abstinent MA users compared to both long-term abstinent and HC groups. Similarly, research by Gong et al. [7] observed lesser GMV in the right superior frontal and bilateral middle frontal in short-term abstinent MA users than in those abstaining from heroin. Additionally, some studies reported a reduction in GMV and gray matter density in the middle frontal and parsopercularis areas in MA users [30, 31]. A meta-analysis by Hall et al. [32] noted a GMV decline in the left precentral in cocaine and MA dependence. Contrarily, a study by Nie et al. [11] comparing long-term abstinent MUDs to HCs discovered thicker cortices bilaterally on the frontal surface in the MUD group, likely due to glial cell proliferation from extended MA use, suggesting that abstinence or recovery duration could influence neuroimaging outcomes.

Current findings highlight significant correlations between the cortical thickness of several frontal cortex subregions (superior frontal, parsopercularis, precentral) and various cognitive tests, indicating that frontal cortex changes may contribute to the typical cognitive deficits seen in MUDs. The directionality of associations differed by region and group: left superior frontal cortical thickness was negatively associated with CPT scores in MUD patients (thinner cortex = worse sustained attention); left parsopercularis cortical thickness was positively associated with NAB-Mazes scores only in HCs, and this association was absent in MUD patients. The specific frontal subregions correlated with cognitive outcomes differ depending on the cognitive test, suggesting that distinct frontal areas play specialized roles in cognitive functions. Previous research supports that different behavioral and cognitive functions are modulated by specific frontal regions, and damage to these areas can result in distinct clinical deficits [33].

The superior frontal region, a component of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), is central to higher executive functions. It integrates information from various cortical and subcortical areas, orchestrating responses that regulate emotion, motivation, and impulsivity [34]. Both preclinical and clinical studies underscore the crucial role for mPFC in addiction, particularly how its connections to subcortical zones facilitate compulsive drug-related behaviors [35]. Moreover, alterations in the synaptic structure and plasticity of mPFC neurons are pivotal in the cognitive deficits associated with addiction [36]. The middle frontal gyrus is implicated in self-regulation and forms a critical part of the brain’s cognitive control circuit [37, 38]. Dysfunctions in this area have been linked to compromised decision-making abilities in MUDs [39]. The inferior frontal gyrus, encompassing pars triangularis, pars opercularis, and pars orbitalis, is integral to response inhibition and cognitive modulation. This region’s role in controlling impulses has been documented across various studies [40, 41]. Although its connection to substance abuse has been observed, the specific impacts on pars triangularis and pars opercularis need further clarification [42, 43].

The pars triangular, part of the ventral lateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), has been implicated in attention and memory processes [44], and reduced VLPFC activity has been observed in MA abusers during cognitive conflict tasks [45]. The pars opercular is associated with speech-related executive functions and has shown involvement in motor inhibition deficits in MA-dependent individuals [43]. Previous research indicates that individuals dependent on MA often struggle with motor inhibitory control, experience emotional instability, and exhibit structural changes in the bilateral pars opercular [31, 46].

Intergroup differences in cortical structural changes in sensorimotor brain regions and their association with cognitive deficits

The precentral gyrus, known as an inhibitory motor area, typically activates during response inhibition tasks [47]. However, activation in this region is diminished in substance-dependent groups, which might reflect deficits in planning and executing actions [48, 49], and has been linked to enhanced drug-motivated behavior [50].

This study also observed that the cortical thickness in the left postcentral gyrus was reduced in the MUD group compared to HC group, accompanied by a decrease in GMV. Despite limited research connecting structural abnormalities in the postcentral gyrus to cognitive functions in MUDs, functional MRI studies have identified functional changes in this area [48, 51, 52]. These findings imply a potential impairment in the sensory-motor cortex associated with MUD. Yalachkov et al. [53] have highlighted the significant relationship between sensorimotor cortex activity and addiction development. They pointed out that the sensorimotor cortex’s response to drug-related cues might predict relapse, correlating with psychological cravings, dependency levels, and automatic responses to such stimuli. The impact of drug use on the sensorimotor cortex remains underexplored, especially concerning MA, highlighting a critical area for future research.

Intergroup differences in cortical structural changes in other brain regions and their association with cognitive deficits

This investigation has illuminated the phenomenon of a pronounced decrement in the volume of the right insula within MUDs, corroborating the observations of Mackey et al. [54], who reported a marked diminution in the cortical thickness of the insula bilaterally among individuals habituated to substances, in stark contrast to their non-using counterparts. This volumetric reduction in the insula manifests ubiquitously across a spectrum of substance-related disorders, thereby suggesting a pervasive influence on the gray matter volume integral to addictive pathologies. From a functional standpoint, the insula, which is intricately woven into the fabric of the brain’s limbic architecture, plays an indispensable role in the orchestration of a myriad of cognitive processes [55, 56]. It underpins the neurological substrate essential for addiction, linked intricately with the capabilities of detecting salient stimuli, propelling motivational states, exerting cognitive governance, and synchronizing behavioral responses. These capacities are indispensable for the genesis and the sustenance of addictive conduct [57, 58].

The current study confirmed a thinning in the cortical thickness for the left supramarginal gyrus and a reduction in the GMV for the bilateral angular gyrus, aligning with findings from earlier research. These regions are part of the inferior parietal lobule, which a meta-analysis found to be significantly affected in cocaine and MA dependent individuals compared to HCs, with more severe reductions observed in the MA group [32]. Moreover, the inferior parietal lobule is crucial for diverse functions ranging from perceptual processing to social cognition [59] and is involved in the neuropathology of addiction. It is linked to self-referential processing and attentional shifts towards significant stimuli like rewards and drug cues, which are known to provoke cravings and drug-seeking behaviors [60, 61]. Accordingly, this research hypothesized that prolonged MA use may lead to structural changes in this lobule among MUDs, potentially affecting their attentional bias and decision-making capabilities.

This study is subject to several limitations that warrant attention. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of the research suggests the need for longitudinal studies to observe gray matter changes and MCCB score variations in MUDs over abstinence periods. Although the participants had no other substance dependencies, the majority were smokers. Existing research demonstrates that smoking can alter the structure of gray matter [62], complicating the isolation of MA’s specific effects in this sample, despite statistical controls for smoking history. Secondly, abstinence duration was self-reported without biological verification (e.g., hair or fingernail testing), which may introduce recall bias. Future studies should include larger cohorts to clearly differentiate the joint and separate effects of MA use and smoking on brain development.

Conclusion

In summary, employing a combination of VBM and SBM methodologies, this study has revealed significant reductions in GMV and/or cortical thickness within key brain regions such as the frontal cortex, inferior parietal lobule, insula, and sensorimotor cortex among individuals in early abstinence from MUDs. VBM and SBM results are complementary: VBM identifies volume loss in the middle frontal, insula, and angular gyrus, while SBM detects cortical thinning in the superior frontal, precentral, and inferior frontal regions, together providing a full characterization of MUD-related brain structural damage. Additionally, a noteworthy correlation has been established between cortical thickness in the left frontal cortex and crucial cognitive functions, including information processing speed, sustained attention, and executive function. These findings suggest that structural alterations in the left frontal cortex may play a pivotal role in the cognitive deficits commonly observed in MUDs, highlighting the necessity of considering these neuroanatomical changes when evaluating cognitive impairment within this demographic.
