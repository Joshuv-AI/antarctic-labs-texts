Surface dynamics of glassy polymers with different molecular weight distributions
Authors: Siming Wang, Yongjian Zhu, Yuchen Fu, Xinyu Zhang, Biao Zuo, Liang Dai, Yu Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The surface dynamics of polymers are of great interest in the field of polymer physics. It is generally believed that a ‘liquid-like’ layer exists at the glassy polymer surface, exhibiting enhanced segmental dynamics compared to the bulk. While much effort has been dedicated to elucidating the underlying mechanism of this simple yet fascinating phenomenon, most studies have focused on polymers with a single molecular weight or a narrow molecular weight distribution. In practical applications, polymeric products consist of a broad range of molecular weights. Consequently, composition of polymers may not be expected to be uniform in space, especially in the presence of an interface. In this study, we investigate the surface dynamics of glassy polymers with a bimodal molecular weight distribution using a droplet wetting-dewetting experiment. The results reveal that the surface dynamics of polymer blends are predominantly influenced by a layer of segregated short-chain polymers. However, upon the removal of this segregated layer, the liquid-like layer begins to dictate the surface behaviors.

Introduction

When glassy polymers are confined to a nanoscopic regime, they exhibit many abnormal behaviors, such as a reduced thermal glass-transition temperature (Tg), easily accessing low energy states by aging1, and increased plasticity2. This phenomenon, drawing significant attention in the field of polymer physics, is commonly attributed to a liquid-like or, precisely, a rubbery-like layer3 on the glassy polymer surface4–8. Such a layer, exhibiting enhanced segmental and molecular mobility, has also been observed in various other glass systems, such as metallic glasses and ordinary molecular glass formers9–13.

Therefore, understanding the molecular origin of this liquid-like layer, its associated abnormal dynamic and thermodynamic behavior, and how it affects material properties remains an important task in the glass community14–17. While substantial progress has been made in recent decades, most studies have primarily focused on polymers with a monodisperse molecular weight distribution. In practice, however, many polymeric products have a wide range of molecular weights, resulting in enrichment of shorter chains at the surface because surface-induced entropic loss is smaller for shorter chains than longer chains, as shown in many recent studies18–23. When such segregation occurs, the surface dynamics would be dominated by shorter chains. Given the distinct dynamic behaviors between short-chain and long-chain polymers, it is crucial to determine what governs the surface dynamics of glassy polymer blends: the liquid-like layer or the surface-segregated layer24,25.

Despite both the liquid-like layer and the surface-segregated layer originating from the presence of a surface, they arise from fundamentally different physical mechanisms. Here, the liquid-like layer refers to a reduction in intermolecular constraints at the surface due to the absence of neighboring segments, resulting in enhanced segmental mobility. In contrast, the surface-segregated layer arises from compositional inhomogeneity, in which shorter chains preferentially accumulate near the surface.

Importantly, these two mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and may coexist at polymer surfaces. For example, a surface region enriched with shorter chains may simultaneously exhibit liquid-like dynamics. However, because these mechanisms have distinct physical origins, it remains unclear which effect primarily governs the observed surface dynamics in glassy polymer systems, and under what conditions one mechanism dominates over the other. Addressing this question is essential for a quantitative understanding of surface mobility in realistic polymer systems with polydisperse molecular weight distributions.

To clarify the effect of surface segregation, we prepared polymer blends with a bimodal molecular weight distribution and investigated the surface dynamics of thin polymer films by observing the distinctive interfacial wetting behavior of an immiscible ionic liquid droplet on their surfaces, as we previously reported3. This study aims to unravel the distinctions between the effects of surface segregation and the liquid-like layer on the dynamics at the polymer surface near Tg.

Considering the lack of correlation between the Ttransition and φ of the polymer blends, it is reasonable to conclude that this unusual pinning-wetting behavior is caused by a surface layer of polymer blends with a different chain composition compared to the bulk, rather than the presence of a free surface layer. Namely, the surface dynamics of polymer blends are dominated by a layer of short PS chains enriched at the sample surface, referred to as a surface segregation layer. It is worth noting that in our experiments, φ is limited to a range from 50% to 80%. For φ less than 50%, the difference between the segregation layer and the bulk is too weak to detect a step-change profile and measure the d value. It shows that the low-φ data will demonstrate no d → 0 transition. That will confirm that the PS601k component is essential for the d → 0 transition. However, for φ greater than 80%, the pre-annealing temperature would cause the evaporation of short-chain polymers.

Surface segregation is not unique to polymers; it can arise in many multicomponent systems with size disparities29. In addition to entropy-driven segregation, solvent evaporation during sample processing can also drive smaller components toward the surface, an effect particularly pronounced in colloidal systems30. In our measurements, however, the segregation-induced wetting behavior is markedly stronger in thermally annealed samples than in samples prepared without pre-annealing. Since all blend films were annealed above Tg for an extended time (120 h), the nonequilibrium concentration gradients generated during spin coating should largely relax, allowing the system to approach thermodynamic equilibrium.

Comparison of simulations and experiments

Our experiments cannot directly quantify the surface enrichment of short chains observed in the simulations. To enable a semi-quantitative comparison, we evaluated the short-to-long chain ratio in the simulations as a function of distance from the free surface. The results suggest that the thickness of the surface-enriched layer is approximately four bead diameters (Fig. S6).

Experimentally, the thickness of the surface segregation layer is expected to be comparable to the trench height, such as the one shown in Fig. 3e. Using an estimated length of 0.25 nm per styrene repeat unit, the segregation-layer thicknesses inferred from the simulations and experiments are found to be of the same order, indicating reasonable consistency between the two approaches.

Discussion

We investigated the wetting-pinning behavior of an ionic liquid droplet on polymer thin films. We observed a wetting-pinning transition at a temperature higher than the bulk Tg for polymers with monodisperse molecular weight distribution. In contrast, for polymer blends with a binary molecular weight distribution, a much lower wetting-pinning transition temperature was observed. The transition temperature, Ttransition, was found to be independent of the blend ratio of the high-molecular-weight component, φ. Guided by our simulation results on surface segregation of chains with different lengths, we conclude that in polymer blends composed of both short and long-chain polymers, the enrichment of short-chain polymers at the polymer blend’s surface dominates the overall surface behavior of polymer blends. By omitting the pre-annealing step during sample preparation, we eliminated surface segregation of short-chain polymers. All wetting-pinning behaviors of the ionic liquid on these samples were consistent with those of the monodisperse samples. In short, short-chain surface segregation significantly alters the surface composition of polymer blends, leading to a surface behavior dominated by short-chain polymers. In practical applications, considering the inherent polydispersity of all polymers, one should be very cautious in interpreting any unusual glassy surface behaviors observed.

We then sought to experimentally investigate the surface dynamics of glassy polymers with a bimodal molecular weight distribution using a droplet pinning-wetting experiment. Atactic polystyrene (PS) (PS601k, Mw = 601 kg/mol, PDI = 1.07, and PS2.5k, Mw = 2.5 kg/mol, PDI = 1.08) was procured from Polymer Source Inc. (Canada) and served as the high (long-chain) and low (short-chain) molecular weight components in the polymer blends. PS (PS3k, Mw = 3.0 kg/mol, PDI = 1.09; PS4.4k, Mw = 4.4 kg/mol, PDI = 1.05; PS7.1k, Mw = 7.1 kg/mol, PDI = 1.07, and PS10.9k, Mw = 10.9 kg/mol, PDI = 1.09) with different molecular weights and narrowly dispersed distribution from the same company were employed as the control groups for the experiments. The ionic liquid 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate ([Eim]BF4), purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (USA), was used as the testing liquid for the wetting experiments. [Eim]BF4 is immiscible with the PS; no plasticizing or swelling effects were observed at the surface of PS3.

Silicon (100) wafers were cut into 1 × 1 cm2 pieces, serving as the substrates for the polymer films. The cleaning process of silicon wafers involved submersion in piranha solution (H2SO4/H2O2, 7:3) preheated to 373 K for 5 min, followed by rinsing with deionized water and drying with nitrogen gas. Thin films were fabricated by spin-coating. The weight percentage of the high molecular component in the polymer blends was set at 50%, 60%, 70%, and 80%, respectively. The monodisperse samples were annealed under vacuum at a temperature above their Tg for 24 h to eliminate residual organic solvents and release internal stress. Alternatively, the blend films with different composition ratios were annealed in a vacuum at 368 K for 120 h to induce short-chain surface segregation.

The thermal Tg of the film of monodispersed PS is approximately 370 and 337 K for PS601K and PS2.5 K, respectively, measured by an RC2 Ellipsometer (J. A. Wollman Inc.) during cooling at 1 K/min. These Tg values for the blend samples align with the Fox equation.

In the wetting experiment, 4 µL of the ionic liquid was placed on the surface of the polymer film, which had been preheated for 5 min on a heat stage to reach thermal equilibrium. The time-dependent contact angle of the ionic liquid was then measured using a surface tension apparatus (OCA 15EC from Dataphysics-Instruments Inc.). After 600 s of wetting experiments, the films with a droplet atop were quenched by quickly transferring the films from the heat stage to an ice bag. The ionic liquid droplet was then removed using filter paper, and the films were rinsed with deionized water.

The capillary force of the droplet induces nanoscale deformation on the mobile surface layer of the glassy films10–12. The three-dimensional profile of the wetting-induced deformation on the film surfaces was imaged by an atomic force microscope (AFM) (Cypher-ES; Oxford Instruments; UK) under tapping mode at a temperature of 288 K.
