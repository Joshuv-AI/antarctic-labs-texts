The interplay between homocysteine, oxidative stress, and butyrylcholinesterase activity in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia
Authors: Manel Naifar, Wassim Guidara, Manel Maalej, Khalil Jmal, Lobna Zouari, Fatma Makni Ayadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchizophrenia (SCZ) is a serious mental illness linked to neurobiological problems, such as hyperhomocysteinemia (HHC), increased butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) activity, and a pro-oxidant status. However, it remains unclear how these different markers interact with each other. This study aimed to analyze oxidative status, Hcys, and BChE activity variations in male drug-free SCZ patients compared to healthy controls.MethodsWe conducted a case-control study on male drug-free patients with SCZ. Metabolic parameters, oxidative status, Hcys, BChE activity, and ultrasensitive C-reactive protein (CRP) were tested for both groups.ResultsCRP and BChE levels were significantly higher in SCZ. A significant positive correlation was identified between BChE activity and PANSS Positive and General scores. Patients had lower levels of folate and vitamin B12 and concurrently higher levels of Hcys compared to controls. A significant positive correlation was found between Hcys levels and the PANSS Positive (r = 0.423, p < 0.001) and Negative scores (r = 0.414, p < 0.001). The correlation matrix shows strong associations between Hcys and oxidative markers, namely, reduced glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Using a binary logistic regression model, the occurrence of psychotic decompensation was independently associated with BChE, GSH, and MDA.ConclusionThis study offers valuable insights into the intricate cholinergic, oxidative, and inflammatory processes linked to SCZ, which further exacerbate the clinical symptoms into a vicious cycle. Elevated Hcys levels appear mainly as a consequence of folate and vitamin B12 deficiencies rather than a direct pathogenic factor, underlining the need for integrated biomarker-based and nutritional approaches to improve patient outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07711-5.

Introduction

Psychiatric disorders are complex, frequently underdiagnosed, and have a significant socioeconomic impact. Schizophrenia (SCZ) is considered among the most severe mental illnesses, contributing substantially to global disability. Their pathophysiology remains poorly understood, and current diagnostic and risk prediction tools are limited, based on clinical examination and self-reported symptoms. Unveiling the genomic and molecular mechanisms underlying the acute decompensation of neuropsychiatric disorders is challenging [1, 2]. The identification of biomarkers would help patient stratification and offer personalized treatment strategies.

It has been documented that neuropsychiatric diseases involving anomalies of central cholinergic transmission, such as SCZ, exhibit high blood cholinesterase isoenzyme activity [3] and proinflammatory status with hyperhomocystenemia (HHC) [4]. A recent review conducted by Zhu et al. showed that high plasma Hcys levels were associated with a higher risk of SCZ (OR = 1.110, 95% CI = 1.028–1.198) [5].

Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that oxidative stress plays a central role in the pathophysiology of SCZ, contributing to neuronal damage, synaptic dysfunction, and disease progression [6, 7]. Recent papers have reported a strong correlation between HHC and symptom severity in SCZ patients compared to controls [8]. Thus, they propose Hcys as an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment in SCZ.

However, few studies have focused simultaneously on the oxidative stress, Hcys, folate, and B12 variations, which interfere with Hcys metabolism during psychotic decompensation. The role of HHC as either an independent risk factor or an auxiliary contributor to specific psychotic symptoms remains controversial. Zhilyaeva T.V. et al. demonstrated that Hcys was negatively correlated with folate and B12 and concluded that Hcys should be considered a marker of disturbances in vitamin metabolism rather than a direct risk factor [9].

Thus, the interplay between HHC, vitamin deficiency, inflammation, and BChE activity remains unclear.

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the association between BChE activity, low-grade inflammation, and cognitive impairment in SCZ.

Secondly, we aimed to determine whether HHC is a direct risk factor or a consequence of folate/B12 deficiency, thereby worsening the severity of symptoms in SCZ.

Finally, we analyzed the predictive value of Hcys, CRP, and BChE activities for psychotic decompensation in drug-free SCZ patients.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a case-control study (2022–2023) on patients hospitalized in the psychiatry department “C” of the Hedi Chaker University Hospital in Sfax, Tunisia, which only receives male patients. Forty-five male patients with SCZ, according to DSM-5 criteria [10], and one hundred healthy matched controls participated in this cross-sectional study.

Inclusion criteria

All patients included in this study were symptomatic and had stopped psychotropic medication (such as mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, or antidepressants) for at least three months. Age-matched healthy volunteers from the same region were recruited as the study progressed (control group). Saliva and blood samples were taken at 8:00 a.m. on the first day of hospitalization, prior to initiation of medication for each participant.

DSM-5 criteria were used to establish the diagnosis of SCZ. The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) was applied to all patients. The Total PANSS Score includes 30 items (7 positive, 7 negative, and 16 general), assessing overall severity in individuals with SCZ [11].

Exclusion criteria

Patients who had been receiving antipsychotic medication during the 3 months before hospital admission and those with a known history of stroke, head injury, intellectual disability, or dementia were not included in this study.

Besides, patients with a state of extreme agitation requiring immediate initiation of antipsychotic medication and those with chronic documented somatic pathology (renal failure, dyslipidemia, endocrine disorder) or intercurrent infection were excluded at admission.

Clinical data collection and biological investigations

For all subjects included in the study, a pre-established form was completed with sociodemographic data, personal and family history, and disease characteristics for the patients. Height, weight, and blood pressure were measured, and body mass index was calculated for all enrolled subjects. Following an overnight fast, blood samples were collected in tubes containing heparin and EDTA and immediately placed on ice awaiting processing. Within one hour, blood samples were centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min, at room temperature, to separate plasma. Plasma obtained from heparin-containing tubes was immediately used for a metabolic parameters analysis, while the plasma obtained from EDTA-containing tubes was stored at -80 °C until the oxidative stress analysis.

Plasma was immediately used for testing of metabolic parameters: glycemia, total cholesterol, proteins, butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), ultrasensitive C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine, and homocysteine (Hcys). Homocysteine and BChE were tested by electrochemiluminescence (Roche-Diagnostics, Germany). For the oxidative stress analysis, detailed methods were previously described [12]. The estimation of malondialdehyde (MDA) was determined using a spectrophotometric method based on the reaction between MDA and thiobarbituric acid (TBA). Protein carbonyls (PCs) were determined via the spectrophotometric method of Reznick et al. [13]. Advanced oxidation protein products (AOPPs) were assayed on duplicate samples using a microplate reader according to Witko-Sarsat et al. [14]. Reduced glutathione (GSH) was measured by the method of Jollow et al. [15]. The catalase plasma activity and total thiols were assessed using spectrophotometric methods as previously described [16, 17]. Oxidative stress parameters were assessed at the end of the sampling period, approximately one year later. During this storage period, samples were kept at − 80 °C without repeated freeze-thaw cycles to preserve their stability. Furthermore, quality control procedures included running internal standards and duplicates for each assay to ensure reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Data analysis

Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) (version 20.0) and R version 4.3.2 for Windows software were used to conduct the statistical analysis. Data with a Gaussian distribution were examined using Student’s t-test and expressed as mean (standard deviation), and the variables were correlated by the Pearson test. The Mann-Whitney test was used for variables with a non-Gaussian distribution, expressed as median and median interquartile range [25–75%], and the variables were correlated by the Spearman test.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the accuracy of a diagnostic test, i.e., to discriminate between patients and healthy controls. A binary logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between parameter levels and patients’ risk. A combinatorial analysis of multiple biomarker signatures was carried out to determine the diagnostic accuracy of marker combinations of the tested plasma BChE, CRP, and Hcys using the CombiROC method [18]. This allows determining optimal combinations of biomarkers through a combined analysis of ROC curves, considering the sensitivity (Se) and specificity (Sp) of all possible markers, which is implemented as a freely available web application (http://CombiROC.eu). Therefore, permutation tests were performed to assess the statistical significance of AUC values generated by each ROC curve analysis.

The mean age of patients was 37.38 ± 11.28 years. Metabolic parameters are represented in Table 1. A significantly lower BMI and low concentrations of lipids, proteins, and vitamins were observed in patients than in controls (Table 1). However, Hcys, CRP, and BChE levels were significantly higher in patients (Table 1).

BChE activity and low-grade inflammation in relation to cognitive impairment in SCZ

There was a significant positive correlation between BChE activity and PANSS Positive (r = 0.318, p < 0.001) and General scores (r = 0.229, p = 0.009). No correlation was found with MOCA scores, number of hospitalizations, or the number of medications.

Moreover, a significant negative correlation was observed between CRP levels and the MoCA cognitive scale (r = − 0.361, p = 0.039). The MoCA subdomains that showed significant negative correlations included language, abstraction, and orientation.

Hcys with pro-oxidant activity: direct risk factor or consequence of Folate/B12 deficiency worsening symptom severity in SCZ?

Patients had lower levels of folate and vitamin B12 and concurrently higher levels of Hcys compared to controls (Table 1). Conversely, in the SCZ group, a significant negative correlation was found between Hcys and its cofactors: folate (r = − 0.494, p = 0.001) and vitamin B12 levels (r = − 0.585, p < 0.001).

SCZ patients had a 4.11-fold higher risk of developing hyperhomocysteinemia (HHC) compared to controls [70.5% vs. 36.7%; OR = 4.24 (1.925–9.35); p < 0.001].

Similarly to BChE, a significant positive correlation was found between Hcys levels and the PANSS Positive (r = 0.423, p < 0.001) and Negative scores (r = 0.414, p < 0.001).

Interestingly, the correlation matrix in Table 2 showed a strong positive association between Hcys and the lipid peroxidation marker MDA and a negative correlation with the antioxidative marker GSH.

Predictive value of Hcys, CRP, and BChE for psychotic decompensation in SCZ

Using the ROC curve, Hcys was identified as the most predictive inflammatory marker for psychotic decompensation in schizophrenia, with 0.744 and 0.7 of sensitivity and specificity, respectively, at a threshold of 17 µmol/L. The second more predictive biomarker was BChE, with a sensitivity of 0.718 and specificity of 0.5 at a threshold of 7800.5 IU/L (Fig. 2A).

Discussion

This study investigated plasma homocysteine, oxidative status, and BChE activity variations in drug-free patients with SCZ during acute decompensation.

Inflammation, BChE activity, and cognitive dysfunction in SCZ

BChE is an enzyme involved in the hydrolysis of acetylcholine (ACh) and other choline esters. ACh is an anti-inflammatory molecule. Hence, an increase in the concentrations of the enzymes acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) would lead to reduced levels of ACh and a pro-inflammatory status [19]. In addition, BChE plays a role in modulating cholinergic neurotransmission, which is crucial for cognitive function and neuronal plasticity [20]. It is well-known that SCZ is associated with deficits in the cholinergic system, which regulates cognition and memory. Increased BChE activity may contribute to ACh breakdown, worsening cognition deficits in SCZ [21].

Our results showed higher CRP and BChE levels with a positive correlation between BChE activity and Positive and General PANSS scores in SCZ compared to controls.

Conversely, a significant negative correlation was observed between CRP levels and the MoCA cognitive scale, including language, abstraction, and orientation. The observed correlation suggests that systemic inflammation may contribute to cognitive deficits in psychotic disorders. Elevated CRP can reflect peripheral and central inflammatory processes, including microglial activation, which disrupts synaptic function and neuroplasticity [22, 23];. These findings support the hypothesis that inflammation-mediated microglial activation may underlie both molecular dysregulation and cognitive impairment.

While prior studies linked BChE activity to cognitive dysfunction in SCZ [24] and to neuroinflammation [25], our study demonstrates a concurrent association of BChE with both inflammatory markers (CRP) and clinical symptom severity, suggesting that BChE might serve as a converging point between inflammation and cognitive impairment.

Moreover, the observed correlation between BChE activity and oxidative stress markers underscores the complex interplay between cholinergic dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative imbalance. They may act synergistically to exacerbate cognitive dysfunction in SCZ. This integrative interpretation deepens the understanding of SCZ pathophysiology and invites further investigation into whether targeting BChE activity or related inflammatory pathways might offer therapeutic benefit.

Hyperhomocysteinemia in schizophrenia: direct pathogenic factor or the consequence of vitamin deficiency and prooxidative status?

Several studies reported that HHC is linked to an increased risk of bipolar disorder and schizophrenia [3], due to the low activity of the trans-sulfuration pathway [26]. Multiple hypotheses have been proposed to explain the exact mechanisms through which elevated Hcys contributes to SCZ, based on its neurotoxic effects, interaction with neurotransmitter systems, and disruption of the methylation process (Fig. 3).

However, the role of HHC as either an independent factor or an auxiliary contributor to specific psychiatric symptoms remains unclear [27].

Hcys is a non-protein-forming sulfur amino acid that has two metabolic pathways: re-methylation to methionine (requiring folate and vitamin B12 cofactors) and trans-sulfuration [28].

In cases of folate or B12 deficiency (whether due to genetic mutations, polymorphisms, or nutritional deficiencies), the remethylation of Hcys to methionine is disrupted, leading to HHC. Therefore, it is possible that in vivo, Hcys serves merely as a marker of folate metabolism disturbance [9] rather than acting as a direct pathogenetic factor, according to our findings and those of Mabrouk H et al., who reported significantly elevated homocysteine and low folate levels in Tunisian SCZ patients compared to healthy controls [29]. Vitamin deficiency could be related to malnutrition frequently observed in SCZ because of unhealthy dietary patterns, socioeconomic factors, and disordered eating behaviors [30]. In fact, Table 1 shows low levels of BMI and metabolic markers in patients, probably related to inadequate dietary intake. This strengthens the argument that HHC observed in SCZ is caused by folate and B12 deficiency.

Some studies reported an association between HHC and oxidative stress during SCZ, and it has been suggested that oxidative stress may be induced by the elevated Hcys in patients [9]. However, the authors did not examine GSH levels or investigate potential causes of hyperhomocysteinemia, such as disruptions in folate metabolism. As a result, the possibility of a reverse hypothesis cannot be ruled out—namely, that elevated Hcys levels may arise from oxidative stress, particularly through inhibition of the methylation pathway. This could represent a compensatory mechanism to enhance reduced glutathione (GSH) synthesis.

GSH is the primary non-enzymatic antioxidant that protects cells from the toxic effects of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species by binding and eliminating them [31].

Multiple investigations have demonstrated that GSH depletion contributes to schizophrenia [32]. Recent studies showed that the levels of antioxidant enzymes (GSH, SOD, GSH-Px) were significantly low, whereas lipid peroxidation markers [12], catalase, and metalloproteinase levels were high in patients with schizophrenia when compared with healthy controls [33].

Hurşitoğlu et al. identified serum NOX1 and raftlin as promising new biomarkers, underlining the complexity of redox dysregulation and its potential clinical relevance for diagnosis or monitoring [34].

Interestingly, in our cohort, we found a positive association between Hcys and the lipid peroxidation marker MDA and a negative correlation with the antioxidative marker (GSH).

Zhenlei Peng reported a strong correlation of plasma oxidative markers with symptom severity and cognitive deficits observed in SCZ [33, 35]. Moreover, recent studies showed that high Hcys levels were correlated positively with the PANSS score in SCZ [36]. In this respect, our findings are in line with these results as they show a significant positive correlation between Hcys levels and the PANSS Positive and Negative scores. Thus, Hcys could be proposed as a prognostic marker for symptom severity during SCZ.

Nevertheless, after multivariate analysis and adjusting for confounding factors, the occurrence of psychotic decompensation was independently associated with oxidative biomarkers and not Hcys. In fact, this correlation may reflect the indirect effect of vitamin deficiencies and oxidative stress rather than a direct pathogenic role of Hcys.

These findings highlight the central role of oxidative imbalance in SCZ neuroinflammatory processes that contribute to symptom severity [37]; oxidative stress may have a direct pathogenic effect in SCZ, while HHC might rather reflect underlying vitamin deficiency, thus perpetuating the vicious cycle (Fig. 3).

Vicious circle (Fig. 3)

During SCZ, the primary initiating factor is thought to involve an inflammatory or infectious state during pregnancy or early childhood: this is considered the first “hit” and may alter microglial activation in the fetus [38]. Such disruption can heighten susceptibility to a later “second hit” that ultimately leads to disease onset [38], further contributing to anomalies of central cholinergic transmission (“Two-Hit Model”) [38].

Increased BChE activity would lead to reduced levels of ACh and a pro-inflammatory status [27]. Secondly, impaired oxidative balance is characterized by low anti-oxidant agents (GSH) and high prooxidant markers (MDA), enhancing neurodegeneration and neuronal apoptosis.

Once the disease emerges, it is often accompanied by malnutrition, leading to vitamin B9/B12 deficiency and secondary HHC. HHC, characterized by its neurotoxic effects, further worsens cognitive decline by disrupting NMDA receptors, mitochondrial function, and DNA methylation.

Neurotoxicity hypothesis of HHC

HHC is neurotoxic, inducing oxidative stress by increasing the production of free radicals while reducing antioxidant levels (e.g., vitamin C, glutathione) [12]. This oxidative stress damages lipids, proteins, and DNA, potentially contributing to neurodegeneration. Additionally, Hcys acts as a partial antagonist of NMDA glutamate receptors, disrupting glutamatergic signaling, which is critical for cognition and synaptic plasticity [9]. When plasma glycine levels are low, homocysteine’s antagonistic effects on NMDA receptors are exacerbated, potentially leading to neuronal apoptosis and excitotoxicity. These mechanisms suggest that elevated Hcys may worsen both neurodegeneration and cognitive deficits observed in SCZ, beyond their role as a metabolic marker.

Clinical implications and future directions

The potential role of homocysteine in schizophrenia suggests that therapeutic interventions aimed at lowering HHC could be beneficial. Multiple studies have indicated that supplementation with folate and vitamin B12 may help reduce homocysteine levels and improve cognitive function in schizophrenia patients. A randomized multicentre study conducted by Roffman JL et al. found that combined supplementation with folate and vitamin B12 led to a reduction in negative symptoms of schizophrenia [39]. However, responses to supplementation varied based on genetic factors affecting folate metabolism, suggesting the need for personalized treatment approaches [39]. These findings highlight the importance of integrating metabolic and oxidative markers when designing therapeutic strategies, rather than focusing solely on HHC. Further controlled studies are needed to clarify whether lowering HHC translates into meaningful clinical improvement or whether it mainly reflects correction of underlying deficiencies.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the intricate cholinergic, oxidative, and inflammatory processes linked to SCZ. Elevated homocysteine levels contribute to neurotoxicity and inflammation, while altered BChE activity affects cholinergic neurotransmission and cognitive function. Our results reinforce the growing recognition of the direct pathogenic effect of oxidative stress imbalance on the onset of SCZ. Thus, the observed HHC would be the consequence of folate and/or B12 deficiency associated with SCZ, further exacerbating the clinical symptoms into a vicious cycle.

These findings emphasize the importance of integrated approaches in deepening our understanding of the mechanisms behind mental illnesses, advancing biomarker-based diagnostics, and identifying targeted therapeutic strategies in psychiatry. The limitations of the present investigation are the small sample size and the lack of female members in the studied population. In addition, there is a need for additional large mechanistic studies that specifically investigate the interplay between cholinergic, oxidative, and inflammatory processes linked to SCZ. These findings apply specifically to drug-free male patients with acute schizophrenia, highlighting the need for further studies to evaluate whether similar biochemical signatures are present in other patient populations, including females and patients under medication.
