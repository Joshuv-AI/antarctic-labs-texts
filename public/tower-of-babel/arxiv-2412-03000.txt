Hyperdeterminantal Total Positivity
Kenneth W. JOHNSON a and Donald St. P. RICHARDS b

Abstract
For a given positive integer m m , the concept of hyperdeterminantal total positivity is defined for a kernel K : ℝ 2 ​ m → ℝ K\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}\to{\mathbb{R}} , thereby generalizing the classical concept of total positivity. Extending the fundamental example, K ⁡ ( x , y ) = exp ⁡ ( x ​ y ) K(x,y)=\exp(xy) , x , y ∈ ℝ x,y\in\mathbb{R} , of a classical totally positive kernel, the hyperdeterminantal total positivity property of the kernel K ( x 1 , … , x 2 ​ m ) = exp ( x 1 ⋯ x 2 ​ m ) K(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})=\exp(x_{1}\cdots x_{2m}) , x 1 , … , x 2 ​ m ∈ ℝ x_{1},\dots,x_{2m}\in\mathbb{R} is established. By applying Matsumoto’s hyperdeterminantal Binet–Cauchy formula, we derive a generalization of Karlin’s basic composition formula; then we use the generalized composition formula to construct several examples of hyperdeterminantal totally positive kernels. Further generalizations of hyperdeterminantal total positivity by means of the theory of finite reflection groups are described and some open problems are posed.

Introduction

The theory of total positivity has, for the past 95 years, played an increasingly important role in many aspects of the mathematical sciences. The theory was largely initiated by the work of Schoenberg
[
35
]
and Krein
[
25
]
in the 1930’s, Pólya
[
33
]
in the 1940’s, and Gantmacher and Krein
[
9
]
and Karlin
[
21
]
in the 1950’s, and it is remarkable that each of those early authors was motivated by a wide variety of considerations such as approximation theory, frequency sequences and functions, integral operators, mathematical economics, ordinary differential equations and related Green’s functions, reliability theory, splines, statistics, and variation-diminishing transformations.

Since the appearance of Karlin’s monograph
[
21
]
, the theory of total positivity has broadened immensely, to the point where it arises in combinatorics, classical special functions, Gröbner bases, matrix analysis, Lie groups, harmonic analysis, special functions of matrix argument, mathematical physics, operations research, partial differential equations, stochastic processes, actuarial mathematics, conformal field theory (high energy physics), statistical mechanics, evolutionary biology, computer-aided geometric design, and many other areas. We refer to Gantmacher and Krein
[
9
]
, Gasca and Micchelli
[
10
]
, Karlin
[
21
,
22
]
, Pinkus
[
32
]
, and Gelfand, Kapranov, and Zelevinsky
[
13
]
for various accounts and applications of the classical theory and numerous references to the larger literature.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In the classical setting, a kernel
K\colon\mathcal{D}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
totally positive of order

d
, denoted TP

, if the
n\times n
determinant

\det(K(x_{j},y_{k}))=\begin{vmatrix}K(x_{1},y_{1})&K(x_{1},y_{2})&\cdots&K(x_{1},y_{n})\\
K(x_{2},y_{1})&K(x_{2},y_{2})&\cdots&K(x_{2},y_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
K(x_{n},y_{1})&K(x_{n},y_{2})&\cdots&K(x_{n},y_{n})\end{vmatrix}

(1.1)

is nonnegative for all
n=1,\dots,d
, and for all
x_{1}>\cdots>x_{d}
and
y_{1}>\cdots>y_{d}
such that
{(x_{j},y_{k})\in\mathcal{D}}
for all

. Equivalently, for a kernel
K\colon\mathcal{D}\to[0,\infty)
, total positivity of order

means that all minors of the

matrix
(K(x_{i},y_{j}))
are nonnegative whenever
{x_{1}>\cdots>x_{d}}
and

, i.e., all

(K(x_{i},y_{j}))

n=1,\dots,d
.

Consider the kernel

K(x,y)=\exp(xy),\qquad(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

(1.2)

a function that is the fundamental example of a totally positive kernel. This kernel will provide a guiding role in our subsequent results. It is well known that this kernel is totally positive of order
d
for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a property that is denoted by TP
∞
. Moreover, the minor (
1.1
) is positive for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, so we say that
K
is
strictly totally positive of order infinity
, denoted STP
∞
.

Returning to the general definition in (
1.1
), it may be observed that the classical concept of total positivity is defined (only) for kernels defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and for all orders
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. On the other hand, for kernels
K\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, where
m\geq 2
, the concept of total positivity is defined only for orders
d\leq 2
, with
d=1
signifying that
K
is nonnegative; specifically, the concept of
multivariate totally positivity of order
2
for kernels
K\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to[0,\infty)
was defined in the article
[
23
]
. Thus no general treatment has yet been developed for the concept of multivariate total positivity of order
d
, for any
d>2
, when the kernel
K
is defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, for arbitrary
m>2
.

In this article, for a given positive integer
m
, we follow
[
20
,
38
]
by applying the theory of Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant to define the concept of
hyperdeterminantal total positivity
(HTP) for a kernel

. For the case in which

, this definition reduces to the classical concept of total positivity which was defined through the minors in (
1.1
). Generalizing the fundamental example in (
1.2
) of a classical totally positive kernel, we shall establish that the kernel

K(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})=\exp(x_{1}\cdots x_{2m}),\qquad(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2m},

(1.3)

is a HTP kernel of arbitrarily large order. We show, moreover, that the resulting hyperdeterminants are strictly positive, which generalizes the STP
∞

1.2

We remark that the articles
[
38
]
derived results for the total positivity of Hankel and Vandermonde arrays that are equivalent to the hyperdeterminantal properties of those kernels. However, to the best of our knowledge, our results in the present article appear to be the first to connect the theory of HTP arrays with the generalized hypergeometric functions of matrix argument.

In Section
2
, we provide the definition of the hyperdeterminant and several of its properties. We provide in Section
3
a hyperdeterminantal Binet–Cauchy formula, due to Matsumoto
[
30
]
, and then we apply that result to obtain a Schur function summation formula for the hyperdeterminant constructed from the kernel (
1.3
).

In Section
4
, we extend to the hyperdeterminantal setting an integral of Harish-Chandra
[
17
]
that now appears prominently in the theory of total positivity
[
15
,
16
]
. Further, we apply the extended integral to derive Schur function summation formulas for certain hyperdeterminants defined in terms of the classical generalized hypergeometric series. In Section
5
, we provide the definition of hyperdeterminantal total positivity, obtain several examples of such kernels, and generalize the classical basic composition formula
[
21
, p. 17]
. As consequences of the hyperdeterminantal Binet–Cauchy formula and the generalized basic composition formula, we demonstrate how numerous examples of HTP kernels can be constructed.

Finally, in Section
6
, we describe some directions for future research that are opened by this article. In particular, we raise the possibility of investigating further generalizations of HTP kernels by means of the theory of finite reflection groups, and we raise the problem of deriving hyperdeterminantal generalizations of the FKG inequality.

Hyperdeterminants

For any collection of indices
r_{1},\dots,r_{l}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
A(r_{1},\dots,r_{l})\in{\mathbb{C}}
and form the multidimensional
array
, or
tensor
,

A=(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{l}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{l}\leq n}
. Also let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the symmetric group on
n
symbols. Cayley’s first definition in
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
of the
hyperdeterminant
of the array
A
is

\operatorname{Det}\bigl(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{l})\bigr)_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{l}\leq n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{l}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{l}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}A(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{l}(j)).

By replacing each
\sigma_{r}
in the multiple summation by
\sigma_{0}\sigma_{r}
, where
\sigma_{0}
is an odd permutation, we find that
\operatorname{Det}\bigl(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{l})\bigr)=(-1)^{l}\operatorname{Det}\bigl(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{l})\bigr)

\operatorname{Det}\bigl(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{l})\bigr)\equiv 0
if
l
is odd, a well known result; see, e.g.,
[
28
, Section 2]
. Therefore, we assume henceforth that
l
is even,
l=2m
, so that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq n}

\displaystyle\qquad:=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{2m}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}A(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m}(j)).

(2.1)

For simplicity, we will denote
\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq n}
by
\operatorname{Det}(A)
if there is no possibility of confusion.

The hyperdeterminant satisfies many properties that generalize the properties of the classical determinant. For a wide range of such properties, and numerous applications, we refer to

Let us express the formula (
2.1

\sigma_{2m}
is a classical determinant: For fixed permutations
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{2m}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{2m})\prod_{j=1}^{n}A(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m}(j))

\displaystyle\qquad\equiv\det(A(\sigma_{1}(i),\dots,\sigma_{2m-1}(i),j))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

\displaystyle\qquad=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{2m-1})\det\bigl(A\bigl(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2m-1}^{-1}(i),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\sigma_{2m-1}^{-1}(i),i,j\bigr)\bigr)_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle n!\operatorname{Det}(A)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\times\det\bigl(A(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2m-1}^{-1}(i),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\sigma_{2m-1}^{-1}(i),i,j)\bigr)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

Replacing
\sigma_{k}
by
\sigma_{k}\sigma_{2m-1}
,
1\leq k\leq 2m-2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Det}(A)=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\right)\cdot\det(A(\sigma_{1}(i),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}(i),i,j))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

(2.2)

This represents
\operatorname{Det}(A)
as an alternating multisum of classical determinants.

The second way to express the hyperdeterminant is by rewriting (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle n!\operatorname{Det}(A)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{2m-1},\sigma_{2m}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=2m-1}^{2m}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}A(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}(j),\sigma_{2m-1}(j),\sigma_{2m}(j)).

For fixed
r_{2m-1},r_{2m}\in\{1,\dots,n\}

{\sigma_{2m-1},\sigma_{2m}\hskip-0.15pt\in\hskip-0.15pt\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}A(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}(j),\sigma_{2m-1}(j),\sigma_{2m}(j))

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}B_{\sigma_{2m-1}(j),\sigma_{2m}(j)}(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}(j))

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}B_{\sigma_{2m-1}\sigma_{2m}^{-1}(j),j}\bigl(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2m}^{-1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\sigma_{2m}^{-1}(j)\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}B_{\sigma_{2m-1}\sigma_{2m}^{-1}(j),j}(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}(j)).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Det}(A)={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma_{2m-1},\sigma_{2m}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=2m-1}^{2m}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}B_{\sigma_{2m-1}\sigma_{2m}^{-1}(j),j}(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}(j)).

On replacing
\sigma_{2m-1}
by
\sigma_{2m-1}\sigma_{2m}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Det}(A)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{2m-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{2m-1})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m-2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}B_{\sigma_{2m-1}(j),j}(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}(j))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle n!\sum_{\sigma_{2m-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{2m-1})\operatorname{Det}(B_{\sigma_{2m-1}(j),j}(\sigma_{1}(j),\dots,\sigma_{2m-2}(j)).

(2.3)

This represents

A Binet–Cauchy theorem for hyperdeterminants

In reviewing the hyperdeterminantal literature, one observes that hyperdeterminants satisfy various generalizations of the classical Binet–Cauchy formula. Variants of the Binet–Cauchy formula for multidimensional arrays have appeared in the literature going back to
[
11
,
19
]
, and other analogs of the formula have appeared in
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
8
,
24
,
30
]
. In all of those cited articles, the proof of the corresponding generalized Binet–Cauchy formula is analogous to the proof of the classical Binet–Cauchy formula
[
21
]
and also to generalizations of the formula that are based on the theory of finite reflection groups
[
15
,
16
]
.

The statement and proof of the following generalized Binet–Cauchy formula are due to Matsumoto
[
30

.

Proposition 3.1

(Matsumoto
[
30
]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},\mu)
be a sigma-finite measure space and let
\{\phi_{k,r}\mid\allowbreak 1\leq k\leq 2m,\,1\leq r\leq n\}
be a collection of complex-valued functions on
\mathcal{M}
such that, for all
{r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}}
satisfying
1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq n
, the integral

A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\phi_{k,r_{k}}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(3.1)

converges absolutely. Then, with
\mathcal{M}^{n}:=\mathcal{M}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{M}

(n
factors
)
,

\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathcal{M}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\det(\phi_{k,r}(x_{s}))_{1\leq r,s\leq n}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{j}).

(3.2)

Moreover, if
\mathcal{M}
is totally ordered then

\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))=\idotsint\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}>\cdots>x_{n}{\phantom{\big|}}\\
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{M}\end{subarray}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\det(\phi_{k,r}(x_{s}))_{1\leq r,s\leq n}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{j}).

(3.3)

Proof.

By (
2.1
),

\displaystyle n!\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{2m}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\right)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\phi_{k,\sigma_{k}(j)}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}^{n}}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{2m}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\Biggl(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi_{k,\sigma_{k}(j)}(x_{j})\Biggr)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{j}).

On writing the multi-sum over
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2m}
as a product of summations, we obtain

\displaystyle n!\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\Biggl(\sum_{\sigma_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi_{k,\sigma_{k}(j)}(x_{j})\Biggr)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\det(\phi_{k,r}(x_{s}))_{1\leq r,s\leq n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{j}),

(3.4)

and this establishes (
3.2
).

Consider next the case in which
\mathcal{M}
is totally ordered. On observing that all
k
determinants in the integrand in (
3.4
) are identically zero if any two
x_{s}
coincide, it follows that the integral in (
3.4
) reduces to an integral over the union of disjoint open
Weyl chambers
,

\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{M}^{n}\mid x_{\sigma(1)}>\cdots>x_{\sigma(n)}\}.

For each
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, consider the corresponding Weyl chamber

\mathcal{C}_{n}(\sigma):=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{M}^{n}\mid x_{\sigma(1)}>\cdots>x_{\sigma(n)}\};

then

n!\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{n}(\sigma)}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\det(\phi_{k,r}(x_{s}))_{1\leq r,s\leq n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{j}).

On the chamber
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\sigma)
we make the change-of-variables
(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})\to(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, thereby obtaining

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{n}(\sigma)}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\!\det(\phi_{k,r}(x_{s}))_{1\leq r,s\leq n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{j})=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\det(\phi_{k,r}(x_{s}))_{1\leq r,s\leq n})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{j}),

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{M}^{n}\mid x_{1}>\cdots>x_{n}\}
is the
fundamental Weyl chamber
. Noting that there are
n!
chambers, each of which corresponds to a unique permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and also that
(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma))^{2m}\equiv 1
, we obtain (
3.3
).
∎

We now give an application of Proposition

Example 3.2
.

In Proposition
3.1
set
\mathcal{M}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}

\mu

,

On applying (
3.3

\operatorname{Det}(\exp(x_{1,r_{1}}\cdots x_{2m,r_{2m}}))=\sum_{i_{1}>\cdots>i_{n}\geq 0}\frac{1}{i_{1}!\cdots i_{n}!}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\det\bigl(x_{k,r}^{i_{s}}\bigr)_{1\leq r,s\leq n}.

(3.5)

Define
\lambda_{s}=i_{s}-n+s
,

; then
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=(x_{k,1},\dots,x_{k,n})

s_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=\frac{\det\bigl(x_{k,r}^{i_{s}}\bigr)_{1\leq r,s\leq n}}{V(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})}

(3.7)

\det\bigl(x_{k,r}^{i_{s}}\bigr)_{1\leq r,s\leq n}=V(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})s_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}),

(3.8)

(3.9)

where the sum is taken over all partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
of all nonnegative integers such that
\lambda
is of length
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
.

A hyperdeterminantal extension

of an integral of Harish-Chandra

Further, we apply the extended integral to derive Schur function summation formulas for some hyperdeterminants defined in terms of the classical generalized hypergeometric series, and this will also connect the theory of hyperdeterminatal total positivity with the hypergeometric functions of Hermitian matrix argument.

Denote by

For

to denote

are the eigenvalues of

. Since the matrices

and

have the same eigenvalues, then
{s_{\lambda}(X_{1}X_{2})=s_{\lambda}(X_{2}X_{1})}
.

For the case in which
m=1
, the summation formula (
3.9
) reduces to a sum that arose crucially in
[

]
, having been used there to derive a new proof that the fundamental classical example,

∞
). Let
{\beta_{n}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(j-1)!}

and denote by

the group of
n\times n
unitary matrices. For generic
{U\in U(n)}
let
\mathrm{d}U
be the Haar measure on

As noted in

Proposition 4.1

(Harish-Chandra

Suppose that

have eigenvalues

and

\frac{\det(\exp(x_{1,r_{1}}x_{2,r_{2}}))_{1\leq r_{1},r_{2}\leq n}}{V(x_{1})V(x_{2})}=\beta_{n}^{-1}\int_{U(n)}\exp\bigl(\operatorname{tr}UX_{1}U^{-1}X_{2}\bigr)\mathrm{d}U.

(4.1)

Setting

in (

\int_{U(n)}\exp\bigl(\operatorname{tr}UX_{1}U^{-1}X_{2}\bigr)\mathrm{d}U=\beta_{n}\sum_{\lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{j}+n-j)!}\cdot s_{\lambda}(x_{1})s_{\lambda}(x_{2}).

(4.2)

As it turns out, an alternative proof of (

by means of the theory of zonal polynomials and the hypergeometric functions of matrix argument. To extend the formula (

) to the setting of hyperdeterminants, we will again apply the theory of zonal polynomials. In the proof of the next result, we will largely retain the notation of
[
15
, Section 2]
and will follow closely the exposition given there. In particular, if
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
is a partition of length

then the

of
\lambda

|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}

For

(a)_{j}=\frac{\Gamma(a+j)}{\Gamma(a)}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+j-1).

For

(4.4)

Define

and set

Then it is straightforward to see that

(4.5)

Further, for any
n\times n
Hermitian matrix

As shown in

and

(4.8)

The mean-value property also implies that

\displaystyle\int_{U(n)}\int_{U(n)}Z_{\lambda}\bigl(U_{1}X_{1}U_{1}^{-1}U_{2}X_{2}U_{2}^{-1}\bigr)\mathrm{d}U_{1}\mathrm{d}U_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\equiv\int_{U(n)}\int_{U(n)}Z_{\lambda}\bigl(U_{1}X_{1}U_{1}^{-1}\cdot U_{2}X_{2}U_{2}^{-1}\bigr)\mathrm{d}U_{1}\mathrm{d}U_{2}=\int_{U(n)}\frac{Z_{\lambda}(X_{1})Z_{\lambda}\bigl(U_{2}X_{2}U_{2}^{-1}\bigr)}{Z_{\lambda}(I_{n})}\mathrm{d}U_{2}

Since

for all

(4.9)

Proof.

It follows from (

5
Hyperdeterminantal total positivity

In this section, we define the concept hyperdeterminantal total positivity, construct several examples of such kernels, and generalize the classical basic composition formula. Further, we apply the hyperdeterminantal Binet–Cauchy formula and the generalized basic composition formula to show how examples of HTP kernels can be constructed.

5.1
Definition of hyperdeterminantal total positivity

We now define the concept of hyperdeterminantal total positivity of general order
d
for a kernel
{K\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}\to[0,\infty)}

3
, we use the notation

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid x_{1}>\cdots>x_{n}\}

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

Definition 5.1
.

A kernel
K\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}\to[0,\infty)
is
hyperdeterminantal totally positive of order

d
(HTP
d
) if, for any collection of vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=(x_{k,1},\dots,x_{k,n})\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
,
1\leq k\leq 2m
, we have

\operatorname{Det}(K(x_{1,r_{1}},x_{2,r_{2}},\dots,x_{2m,r_{2m}}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq n}\geq 0

(5.1)

for all
n=1,\dots,d
. If the hyperdeterminants (
5.1
) are nonnegative for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then we say that
K
is
hyperdeterminantal totally positive of order

\infty
(HTP
∞
).

If the hyperdeterminants (
5.1
) are positive for all
n=1,\dots,d
, then we say that
K
is
hyperdeterminantal strictly totally positive of order

d
(HSTP
d
). Similarly, if strict inequality holds for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then we say that
K
is
hyperdeterminantal strictly totally positive of order infinity
(HSTP
∞
).

We remark that Definition
5.1
was given in
[
20
]
and, independently, in
[
38
]
. Intuitively, a kernel
K\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}\to[0,\infty)

(K(x_{1,r_{1}},x_{2,r_{2}},\dots,x_{2m,r_{2m}}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq d}

(5.2)

is nonnegative for all vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=(x_{k,1},\dots,x_{k,n})\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
,
k=1,\dots,2m
. Note that the concept of a

of an array was treated in
[

]
and, when expressed in that terminology, a kernel
{K\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}\to[0,\infty)}

{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2m}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}}

5.2

Example 5.2

The case

{K\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to[0,\infty)}

n=1,\dots,d
, the determinant

\det(K(x_{1,r_{1}},x_{2,r_{2}}))_{1\leq r_{1},r_{2}\leq n}=\begin{vmatrix}K(x_{1,1},x_{2,1})&K(x_{1,1},x_{2,2})&\cdots&K(x_{1,1},x_{2,n})\\
K(x_{1,2},x_{2,1})&K(x_{1,2},x_{2,2})&\cdots&K(x_{1,2},x_{2,n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
K(x_{1,n},x_{2,1})&K(x_{1,n},x_{2,2})&\cdots&K(x_{1,n},x_{2,n})\end{vmatrix}

is nonnegative whenever
x_{1,1}>x_{1,2}>\cdots>x_{1,d}
and
x_{2,1}>x_{2,2}>\cdots>x_{2,d}
. This condition is well known to be the classical definition of total positivity of order
d
.

For
m\geq 2
, the concept of HTP
d
appears to be new even for
d=2
, and the following example illustrates the difference between HTP and the concept of multivariate total positivity developed in
[
23
]
.

Example 5.3
.

The case
d=m=2
: For vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=(x_{k,1},x_{k,2})\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
,
k=1,\dots,4
, we apply (
2.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Det}(K(x_{1,r_{1}},\dots,x_{4,r_{4}}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{4}\leq 2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\begin{vmatrix}K(x_{1,1},x_{2,1},x_{3,1},x_{4,1})&K(x_{1,1},x_{2,1},x_{3,1},x_{4,2})\\
K(x_{1,2},x_{2,2},x_{3,2},x_{4,1})&K(x_{1,2},x_{2,2},x_{3,2},x_{4,2})\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}-\begin{vmatrix}K(x_{1,1},x_{2,2},x_{3,1},x_{4,1})&K(x_{1,1},x_{2,2},x_{3,1},x_{4,2})\\
K(x_{1,2},x_{2,1},x_{3,2},x_{4,1})&K(x_{1,2},x_{2,1},x_{3,2},x_{4,2})\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}-\begin{vmatrix}K(x_{1,2},x_{2,1},x_{3,1},x_{4,1})&K(x_{1,2},x_{2,1},x_{3,1},x_{4,2})\\
K(x_{1,1},x_{2,2},x_{3,2},x_{4,1})&K(x_{1,1},x_{2,2},x_{3,2},x_{4,2})\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}+\begin{vmatrix}K(x_{1,2},x_{2,2},x_{3,1},x_{4,1})&K(x_{1,2},x_{2,2},x_{3,1},x_{4,2})\\
K(x_{1,1},x_{2,1},x_{3,2},x_{4,1})&K(x_{1,1},x_{2,1},x_{3,2},x_{4,2})\end{vmatrix}.

(5.3)

Define the vectors

\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=(x_{1,1},x_{2,2}),\qquad\boldsymbol{u}_{2}=(x_{1,2},x_{2,1}),\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=(x_{3,1},x_{4,2}),\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{2}=(x_{3,2},x_{4,1}).

Since each
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
,
k=1,\dots,4
, then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{u}_{2}=(x_{1,1},x_{2,1}),\qquad\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{u}_{2}=(x_{1,2},x_{2,2}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{v}_{2}=(x_{3,1},x_{4,1}),\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{v}_{2}=(x_{3,2},x_{4,2}).

Therefore, (
5.3
) becomes

\displaystyle\operatorname{Det}(K(x_{1,r_{1}},\dots,x_{4,r_{4}}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{4}\leq 2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\begin{vmatrix}K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1})\\
K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}-\begin{vmatrix}K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1})\\
K(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}-\begin{vmatrix}K(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1})\\
K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}+\begin{vmatrix}K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1})\\
K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\end{vmatrix}.

(5.4)

Let
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1})
,
\boldsymbol{w}_{2}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
. Then,
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{w}_{2}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
,
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{w}_{2}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
, and the first determinant on the right-hand side of (
5.4
) equals

\begin{vmatrix}K(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\vee\boldsymbol{w}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})\\
K(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})&K(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{w}_{2})\end{vmatrix}.

(5.5)

Therefore, if
K
is multivariate TP
2
in the sense defined in
[
23
]
then it follows that the determinant (
5.5
) is nonnegative. However, for arbitrary multivariate TP
2
kernels, it appears to be difficult to derive general criteria that imply the nonnegativity of the alternating sum of all four determinants in (
5.4
).

Turning to the construction of HTP
d
kernels, we remarked in the introduction that the fundamental example of a classical STP
∞
kernel is the function
K(x_{1},x_{2})=\exp(x_{1}x_{2})
,
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We now generalize this example to the setting of hyperdeterminantal total positivity.

Define a
slice of the array
A=(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq n}
in the
j
th direction
to be the subset of all indices
(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m})
where the
j
th index,
r_{j}
, is fixed. Gelfand,
et al.

[
12
, Corollary 1.5 (b)]
used the action of certain general linear groups to show that
\operatorname{Det}(A)
is a homogeneous polynomial in the entries of each slice; this homogeneity property can also be deduced directly, albeit with more work, from the expansions (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).

Proposition 5.4
.

The following kernels are HSTP
∞
:

(i)

K_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})=\exp(x_{1}\cdots x_{2m})
,
x_{1},\dots,x_{2m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(ii)

K_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{2m})=x_{1}^{x_{2}x_{3}\cdots x_{2m}}
,
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{2m}>0
.

Proof.

(i) Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=(x_{k,1},\dots,x_{k,n})
,
k=1,\dots,2m
, and suppose that each
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in[0,\infty)^{n}
, the nonnegative orthant in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. By
(
3.9
),

\operatorname{Det}(\exp(x_{1,r_{1}}x_{2,r_{2}}\cdots x_{2m,r_{2m}}))=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{2m}V(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\right)\cdot\sum_{\lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{j}+n-j)!}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{2m}s_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}),

(5.6)

where the sum is over all partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
of length
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
.
It is a well-known result
[
15
,
29
]
that
s_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\geq 0
for all
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in[0,\infty)^{n}
, and therefore each summand on the right-hand side of (
5.6
) is nonnegative. Moreover, the sum is positive since
s_{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\equiv 1
, where
(0)
denotes the zero partition.

Suppose also that each
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Then, by (
3.6
), the Vandermonde polynomials
V(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
on the right-hand side of (
5.6
) are positive. Therefore, the right-hand side of (
5.6
) is positive for
{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap[0,\infty)^{n}}
,
k=1,\dots,2m
, so we obtain

\operatorname{Det}(\exp(x_{1,r_{1}}x_{2,r_{2}}\cdots x_{2m,r_{2m}}))>0.

This proves that the kernel
K_{1}
is SHTP
n
, and since
n
was chosen arbitrarily then it follows that
K_{1}
is HSTP
∞
.

Now consider the case in which
x_{k,n}<0
for some
k
. For each such
k
, we multiply the corresponding slice of the array,
viz.
,

\{(\exp(x_{1,r_{1}}x_{2,r_{2}}\cdots x_{2m,r_{2m}}))\mid 1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{2m}\leq n,\,i_{k}=n\}

by exponential factors as follows: Form the vectors
x_{k,n}x_{i}=(x_{k,n}x_{k,1},\dots,x_{k,n}x_{in})
,
1\leq i\leq n
; next, multiply the corresponding slice by

\exp(-x_{1,i_{1}}x_{2,i_{2}}\cdots x_{k-1,i_{k-1}}x_{k,n}x_{k+1,i_{k+1}}\cdots x_{2m,i_{2m}}).

thereby changing the generic entries of that slice to

\exp(-x_{1,i_{1}}x_{2,i_{2}}\cdots x_{k-1,i_{k-1}}(x_{k,i_{k}}-x_{k,n})x_{k+1,i_{k+1}}\cdots x_{2m,i_{2m}}).

On carrying out these multiplications for each
k
such that
x_{k,n}<0
, the outcome is that we have created a new multidimensional array,
(\exp(y_{1,r_{1}}y_{2,r_{2}}\cdots y_{2m,r_{2m}}))
, where

y_{k,r_{k}}=\begin{cases}x_{k,r_{k}}&\hbox{if }x_{k,n}\geq 0,\\
x_{k,r_{k}}-x_{k,n}&\hbox{if }x_{k,n}<0.\end{cases}

Noting that
y_{k,r_{k}}\geq 0
for all
k=1,\dots,2m
, it then follows from the preceding argument that
{\operatorname{Det}(\exp(y_{1,r_{1}}y_{2,r_{2}}\cdots y_{2m,r_{2m}}))>0}
.

As noted earlier, the hyperdeterminant is a homogeneous polynomial in the entries of each slice. Hence
\operatorname{Det}(\exp(y_{1,r_{1}}y_{2,r_{2}}\cdots y_{2m,r_{2m}}))
and
\operatorname{Det}(\exp(x_{1,r_{1}}x_{2,r_{2}}\cdots x_{2m,r_{2m}}))
differ only by powers of the multiplying factors. Since those factors are all exponential terms then the hyperdeterminants
\operatorname{Det}(\exp(y_{1,r_{1}}y_{2,r_{2}}\cdots y_{2m,r_{2m}}))
and
\operatorname{Det}\bigl((x_{1,r_{1}}x_{2,r_{2}}\cdots x_{2m,r_{2m}}))
differ only by positive factors, and therefore
\operatorname{Det}(\exp(x_{1,r_{1}}x_{2,r_{2}}\cdots x_{2m,r_{2m}}))>0
.

(ii) Suppose that
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{2m}>0
. We now make the change-of-variables
x_{1}\to\log x_{1}
in the kernel in (i). Then the resulting kernel is
K_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{2m})=x_{1}^{x_{2}x_{3}\cdots x_{2m}}
, and it follows from the HDTP
∞
property of
K_{1}
and strictly increasing nature of the mapping
x_{1}\to\log x_{1}
that
K_{2}
also is HDTP
∞
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

For the case in which some of the
x_{j}

K_{1}

x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{2m}>0

Example 5.6
.

Consider the classical case in which
m=1
. We want to show that if
x_{1,1}>\cdots>x_{1,n}
and
x_{2,1}>\cdots>x_{2,n}
, then the determinant

\begin{vmatrix}\exp(x_{1,1}x_{2,1})&\exp(x_{1,1}x_{2,2})&\cdots&\exp(x_{1,1}x_{2,n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\exp(x_{1,n}x_{2,1})&\exp(x_{1,n}x_{2,2})&\cdots&\exp(x_{1,n}x_{2,n})\end{vmatrix}

is positive.

If
x_{1,n}\geq 0
and
x_{2,n}\geq 0
, then the strict positivity of the determinant follows from (
3.9
) with
{m=1}
.

For
x_{2,n}<0<x_{1,n}
, we multiply the
j
th row of the determinant by
\exp(-x_{1,j}x_{2,n})
,
j=1,\dots,n
. This converts the determinant to

\begin{vmatrix}\exp(x_{1,1}(x_{2,1}-x_{2,n}))&\exp(x_{1,1}(x_{2,2}-x_{2,n}))&\cdots&\exp(x_{1,1}(x_{2,n}-x_{2,n}))\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\exp(x_{1,n}(x_{2,1}-x_{2,n}))&\exp(x_{1,n}(x_{2,2}-x_{2,n}))&\cdots&\exp(x_{1,n}(x_{2,n}-x_{2,n}))\end{vmatrix}.

On the other hand, if
x_{1,n}<0<x_{2,n}
, then we multiply the
j
th column by
\exp(x_{1,n}x_{2,j})
,
j=1,\dots,n
; and if
x_{1,n}<0
and
x_{2,n}<0
, then we carry out both multiplications.

The outcome of these multiplications is to transform the original determinant to a new determinant of the same type, with generic entries
\exp(y_{1,i}y_{2,j})
where
y_{1,1}>y_{1,2}>\cdots>y_{1,n}\geq 0
and
y_{2,1}>y_{2,2}>\cdots>y_{2,n}\geq 0
, and we have seen before that the resulting determinant is positive. Moreover, the two determinants differ in value only by positive (exponential) factors, therefore the initial determinant is positive.

For nonnegative integers
p
and
q
, denote by
\mathcal{D}_{p,q}
the set of all
(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}
such that the generalized hypergeometric series
{}_{p}F_{q}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p};b_{1},\dots,b_{q};x_{1}\cdots x_{2m})
converges. The next result is motivated by Example
4.3
, where the hyperdeterminant was constructed using this generalized hypergeometric series, and it extends the previous example involving the exponential function.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\geq 0
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{q}>0
. The kernel
K\colon[0,\infty)^{2m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

K(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})={}_{p}F_{q}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p};b_{1},\dots,b_{q};x_{1}\cdots x_{2m})

is HTP
∞
on the region
\mathcal{D}_{p,q}\cap[0,\infty)^{2m}
.

The proof of Proposition
5.7
follows from the hyperdeterminantal summation formula (
4.17
) given in Example
4.3
.

Example 5.8
.

In Proposition
5.7
, suppose that
(p,q)=(1,0)
and
a_{1}\equiv a\geq 0
. It is well known that
{}_{1}F_{0}(a;x)=(1-x)^{-a}
,
|x|<1
, and therefore
K(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})=(1-x_{1}\cdots x_{2m})^{-a}
,
|x_{1}\cdots x_{2m}|<1
. Hence the kernel
K
is HTP
∞
on the region
\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{2m})\mid x_{1}\cdots x_{2m}<1,\,x_{1}\geq 0,\dots,x_{2m}\geq 0\}
.

5.3
The basic composition formula

In the classical setting, examples of kernels that are hyperdeterminantal totally positive often are constructed using the
basic composition formula

[
21
]
: If
K_{1},K_{2}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are TP
d
kernels, then so is the kernel

K(x_{1,1},x_{2,1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{1}(x_{1,1},t)K_{2}(t,x_{2,1})\mathrm{d}\mu(t),

where
\mu
is a positive Borel measure such that the integral converges absolutely. In the hyperdeterminantal setting, the following generalization of the basic composition formula follows from the hyperdeterminantal Binet–Cauchy formula in Proposition
3.1
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a totally ordered sigma-finite measure space, and let
\{\phi_{k,r}\mid 1\leq k\leq 2m,\,1\leq r\leq d\}
be a collection of real-valued functions on
X
. Suppose for each
k=1,\dots,2m
that the kernel
K_{k}(t,r)=\phi_{k,r}(t)
,
(t,r)\in X\times\{1,\dots,d\}
, is TP
d
. Define the multidimensional array

A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m})=\int_{X}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\phi_{k,r_{k}}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

where
1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq d
, and the integral is assumed to converge absolutely. Then the array
{(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq d}}
is HTP
d
.

Proof.

For any
n
such that
1\leq n\leq d
, consider the hyperdeterminantal minor

\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq n}.

By applying (
3.3
) in Proposition
3.1
, we can represent this minor as an integral whose integrand is nonnegative, hence the minor is nonnegative. Since
n
is arbitrarily chosen, then it follows that the array
(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq d}
is HTP
d
.
∎

As a consequence of this result, numerous HTP kernels can be constructed. For example, by choosing
\phi_{k,r}(t)=t^{k+\lambda_{r}}
, where
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}
are the parts of a partition
\lambda
, then the resulting minor
\operatorname{Det}(A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}))_{1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq n}
is a hyperdeterminantal generalization of the Vandermonde determinant, and we obtain positivity results similar to those given in
[
38
]
. If we choose
\phi_{k,r}(t)
as a classical generalized hypergeometric series and if the measure
\mu
is chosen suitably, then the entries
A(r_{1},\dots,r_{2m})
,
1\leq r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}\leq n
, of the resulting array can be made to contain a wide range of the classical special functions, such as the Bessel or the Gaussian hypergeometric series. By choosing
\phi_{k,r}(t)
in terms of the bivariate normal distributions with negative correlations
[
23
]
, the entries of the resulting array can be made to include numerous multivariate probability distributions.

6
Concluding remarks

The results presented in this article raise many open problems in a variety of areas. For the purposes of making applications in statistics and probability, as in
[
23
]
, we are especially interested in hyperdeterminantal generalizations of the FKG inequality. Here, even the most basic questions are still open, e.g., the determination of conditions under which the familiar multivariate probability distributions, such as the multivariate normal distributions, are HTP of any order.

A second problem arises from the definition of the hyperdeterminant. Suppose that the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
in the sums that define
\operatorname{Det}(K(x_{1,r_{1}},x_{2,r_{2}},\dots,x_{2m,r_{2m}}))
in (
5.1
) were to be replaced by an arbitrary finite reflection group. Following the approach in
[
16
]
, corresponding generalizations of the Binet–Cauchy formula can be obtained and examples of hyperdeterminantal totally positive kernels can be constructed. It would be a fundamental achievement to derive analogs of the FKG inequality in this setting.
