Genetic risk stratification of common diseases in breast cancer survivors: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Annabelle Ashworth, Zhuqing Shi, Huy Tran, Jun Wei, Ashley J Mulford, Siqun Lilly Zheng, David Duggan, Brian T Helfand, Alan R Sanders, Tariq Azam, Bruce Brockstein, Allison DePersia, Poornima Saha, Jianfeng Xu, Ted A James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients diagnosed with breast cancer are at increased risk of multiple common diseases; however, the spectrum of these diseases and the contribution of inherited genetic susceptibility remain incompletely characterized.MethodsWe evaluated 15 common diseases and tested their associations with breast cancer exposure and disease-specific polygenic risk scores in the UK Biobank (n = 254 736). Analyses were performed using cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models within a full-cohort framework, with time-updated breast cancer status, delayed entry at study recruitment, and age as the underlying time scale.ResultsAfter recruitment, incident breast cancer was diagnosed in 11 386 women (4.47%), including 2742 (24.08%) with metastatic breast cancer. Patients with breast cancer had an increased risk of 9 diseases spanning cardiovascular, metabolic, and neuropsychiatric domains (P < .003, Bonferroni corrected). Elevated risks were generally observed among patients with both early-stage and advanced breast cancer. Inherited susceptibility further stratified disease risk, with the highest risks observed among patients with breast cancer and elevated disease-specific polygenic risk scores. For example, compared with women without breast cancer, the hazard ratio for osteoporosis was 2.33 (95% CI = 2.15 to 2.52) among women with any breast cancer, 2.38 (95% CI = 2.18 to 2.59) among women with nonmetastatic breast cancer, and 2.12 (95% CI = 1.78 to 2.54) among women with metastatic breast cancer; the hazard ratio was 4.48 (95% CI = 3.99 to 5.02) among patients with breast cancer in the highest quartile of osteoporosis-specific polygenic risk scores (all P < .001). In contrast, breast cancer was not statistically significantly associated with risk of coronary artery disease.ConclusionBreast cancer and inherited genetic susceptibility jointly contribute to increased risk of multiple common diseases, supporting the integration of genetic risk stratification into survivorship care.

Introduction

With advances in early detection and treatment, breast cancer survival has improved substantially, resulting in an estimated 4 million breast cancer survivors in the United States.1 As survival times increase, long-term health outcomes beyond cancer recurrence have emerged as a major determinant of morbidity and mortality. Multiple population-based studies have demonstrated that cardiovascular disease (CVD), metabolic disorders, osteoporosis, and second primary cancer collectively account for a substantial proportion of late adverse outcomes among breast cancer survivors, in many cases exceeding breast cancer–specific mortality.2-4 For example, a study examining 364 230 patients with breast cancer in the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database reported that two-thirds of patients died of causes other than breast cancer, with heart disease accounting for 24% of non–breast cancer deaths.5

Unlike many other cancers, where systemic therapies are largely reserved for advanced or metastatic disease, systemic and hormone therapy are routinely administered in early-stage breast cancer.6 Chemotherapy, chest radiation, targeted immunotherapy, and long-term endocrine therapy are widely used, even among patients with nonmetastatic breast cancer. These treatments exert persistent effects on the cardiovascular, skeletal, metabolic, and nervous systems, creating a substrate for long-term complications that may manifest years after cancer treatment, independent of metastatic status.7-12

Despite the recognized burden of noncancer outcomes, the long-term risk of common diseases among breast cancer survivors remains incompletely characterized.6,13 Prior studies have often focused on clinically selected patient populations, single outcomes, specific therapies, or short follow-up periods, limiting their ability to capture the full spectrum of survivorship complications.14-20 Moreover, many analyses do not adequately address time-dependent exposure, competing risks, or baseline disease susceptibility, leading to potential misestimation of risk.

Inherited genetic susceptibility represents a complementary and underutilized dimension of survivorship risk assessment. Common diseases that disproportionately affect breast cancer survivors, including cancers, coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure, ischemic stroke, atrial fibrillation (AF), hypertension, venous thromboembolism (VTE), obesity, type 2 diabetes, osteoporosis, and depression, have substantial heritable components.21-30 Both rare pathogenic variants of monogenic genes and common polygenic variants contribute meaningfully to disease risk, and disease-specific polygenic risk scores have consistently been associated with susceptibility in population-based cohorts.31-41 In the context of near-universal exposure to cardiotoxic, osteotoxic, and metabolically active therapies, inherited risk may help identify survivors who are particularly vulnerable to late adverse outcomes.

The objectives of this study were to (1) estimate the observed incidence of several reported common diseases among women with and without breast cancer, (2) evaluate whether patients with breast cancer have increased risks of these diseases after adjustment for potential confounders and competing risks, and (3) assess the independent and joint associations of breast cancer and disease-specific polygenic risk scores with these outcomes. By integrating inherited risk with clinical trajectories, this study aimed to inform genetics-guided surveillance and personalized survivorship care for the growing population of breast cancer survivors and those living with metastatic breast cancer.

Methods

Participants were from the UK Biobank, a population-based cohort comprising approximately 500 000 individuals across the United Kingdom, aged 40 to 69 years at recruitment, with extensive phenotypic and genomic data available.42 The UK Biobank has ethical approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee. Access to the UK Biobank data was approved under application No. 50295.

Information about breast cancer diagnosis and common diseases was obtained from self-reported data and inpatient hospital records using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes (Table S1). The 15 outcomes were selected a priori to represent common, clinically consequential diseases that have been reported or are biologically plausible long-term complications among breast cancer survivors, spanning second primary cancers, CVD, metabolic and skeletal disorders, and neuropsychiatric conditions.21-30 Selection was further guided by the availability of sufficiently reliable outcome ascertainment in the UK Biobank, including at least 500 incident events, and an established disease-specific polygenic risk score. The evaluated outcomes included second primary cancers (colorectal, lung, melanoma, endometrial, and ovarian), CVD (CAD, heart failure, AF, ischemic stroke, hypertension, and VTE), metabolic and skeletal diseases (obesity, type 2 diabetes, and osteoporosis), and depression. Metastatic status was obtained using International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes C77 through C79.

Genome-wide single-nucleotide variation (formerly single-nucleotide polymorphism) array as well as imputed single-nucleotide variations were available. Genetic ancestry background was estimated using principal components based on 16 309 ancestry-informative markers distributed across the genome.

A polygenic risk score was selected for each condition from multiple available models in the PGS Catalog based on larger sample size, broader ancestry representation, and minimal involvement of UK Biobank participants in polygenic risk score model training and testing (Table S2).31-41,43 For each polygenic risk score, raw scores were calculated for each individual using the provided scoring files and the patient’s genotype data. Ancestry-adjusted polygenic risk score values were then derived by adjusting for the first 4 principal components, as previously described.44

We used a full-cohort, time-to-event framework with time-updated breast cancer status to accurately quantify exposure time.45,46 Data were structured in a start-stop format with delayed entry at study recruitment, using age as the underlying time scale. Women with a history of breast cancer or any investigated diseases before recruitment were excluded. Breast cancer status was modeled using time-varying indicators that transitioned from 0 (no breast cancer) to 1 (nonmetastatic breast cancer), and then to 2 (metastatic breast cancer) at the recorded diagnosis and metastasis dates, allowing individuals to contribute both unexposed and exposed person-time (Figure S1). Participants were followed until the first occurrence of the complication, death, loss to follow-up, or administrative censoring (the last data access was on December 9, 2025).

For each common disease, we fit cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios and 95% CIs for breast cancer status (any breast cancer, nonmetastatic breast cancer, and metastatic breast cancer) and disease-specific polygenic risk score, adjusting for the top 4 genetic principal components. Death was treated as a competing event and censored in cause-specific Cox models, thereby estimating the instantaneous hazard of each disease among women who remained alive during follow-up. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals and were met. To account for multiple testing across outcomes, Bonferroni correction was applied. A multiplicative interaction between breast cancer and polygenic risk score was formally tested using a breast cancer × polygenic risk score product term.

Polygenic risk score was modeled as a continuous variable (per SD) in all inferential analyses; polygenic risk score categories (quartiles) were used only for descriptive presentation and figure visualization. Model discrimination was quantified using the Harrell concordance index (C index). Incremental discrimination between nested models was assessed using bootstrap resampling.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 254 736 women met inclusion criteria and had available genetic data (Table 1). The median (IQR) age at recruitment was 57.88 (50.37-63.34) years. Most participants were of European ancestry (93.92%).

After a median (IQR) follow-up of 14.3 (13.59-15.01) years, incident breast cancer was diagnosed in 11 386 women (4.47%). Among these, metastatic breast cancer was identified in 2742 women (24.08% of breast cancer cases).

Association between exposure to breast cancer and common diseases

We calculated disease-specific exposure time across 3 time-updated breast cancer–related exposure states (non–breast cancer, nonmetastatic breast cancer, and metastatic breast cancer) in the full cohort and estimated the observed incidence rates for each exposure group (Table S3 and Figure 1). Women exposed to breast cancer, particularly those exposed to metastatic breast cancer, had higher observed incidence rates for all evaluated common diseases compared with women without breast cancer.

To account for potential confounding due to differences in age and genetic ancestry, associations between breast cancer exposure and disease risk were evaluated using cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models, with age as the underlying time scale and adjusted for genetic ancestry (Table S4). After adjustment, a diagnosis of breast cancer was statistically significantly associated with increased risk of 9 diseases, including 1 second primary cancer (lung cancer), 4 CVD (VTE, heart failure, AF, and hypertension), 3 metabolic diseases (osteoporosis, obesity, and type 2 diabetes), and depression (all P < .003, corresponding to a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold for 15 tests). When associations were evaluated stratified by metastatic status (Table S4; Figure 2, B and C), increased risks were generally observed among women exposed to both nonmetastatic breast cancer and metastatic breast cancer. For several diseases, hazard ratio estimates were stronger among women with metastatic breast cancer than among those with nonmetastatic breast cancer, including VTE (5.24 vs 2.71), heart failure (2.19 vs 1.29), lung cancer (2.88 vs 1.71), and AF (1.52 vs 1.25). In addition, a statistically significantly increased risk of colorectal cancer was observed exclusively among women with metastatic breast cancer. In contrast, hazard ratio estimates were stronger among women with nonmetastatic breast cancer than among those with metastatic breast cancer for metabolic diseases and hypertension. The association for depression was similar between women with metastatic breast cancer and nonmetastatic breast cancer.

Independent effect of breast cancer exposure and polygenic risk score on common diseases

For diseases in which increased risk was associated with exposure to breast cancer, the corresponding disease-specific polygenic risk score, modeled as continuous variables, were also independently associated with risk in multivariable cause-specific Cox models (Table 2). As illustrated by hazard ratio estimates stratified by polygenic risk score categories (Table S5; Figure 3), hazard ratios increased consistently across higher polygenic risk score quartiles for all evaluated diseases. For example, compared with women without breast cancer, those with breast cancer and increasing quartiles of the osteoporosis-specific polygenic risk score had hazard ratios of 1.02, 1.83, 2.13, and 4.48. Formal testing for a multiplicative interaction between breast cancer and continuous polygenic risk score did not yield statistically significant results (P > .05).

Discussion

Three major findings emerged from this large population-based cohort using a full-cohort framework with time-updated breast cancer status. First, although observed incidence was higher for all evaluated diseases among women exposed to breast cancer, statistically significant increases in risk after adjustment for confounding and bias were observed for 9 common diseases spanning cardiovascular, metabolic, malignant, and neuropsychiatric domains. Second, although associations were generally stronger for several diseases among women with metastatic disease, elevated risks were evident even among women with nonmetastatic disease, indicating that excess disease burden is not confined to advanced cancer. Third, inherited genetic susceptibility further stratified disease risk, with the highest risks observed among patients with elevated disease-specific polygenic risk scores. Together, these findings highlight the multifactorial nature of long-term disease risk after breast cancer and support the integration of baseline risk assessment and genetic risk stratification into survivorship care.

A novel feature of our study is the adoption of the full-cohort, time-to-event analytical framework with time-varying breast cancer status. Instead of classifying women into static case and control groups, all participants were followed for incident complications according to their evolving breast cancer status (no breast cancer, nonmetastatic breast cancer, and metastatic breast cancer) after study recruitment (Figure S1).45,46 For example, a woman diagnosed with nonmetastatic breast cancer 5 years after recruitment who subsequently progressed to metastatic breast cancer 7 years later and developed osteoporosis 2 years thereafter, contributed 5, 7, and 2 person-years of exposure time to the no–breast cancer, nonmetastatic breast cancer, and metastatic breast cancer states, respectively. Combined with cause-specific Cox models that accounted for death as a competing event and used age as the underlying time scale, this approach enabled more accurate temporal classification of breast cancer exposure and minimized immortal-time bias in estimating associations with subsequent disease risk.47,48 Furthermore, the simultaneous assessment of inherited genetic risk for these complications provides a more comprehensive framework for risk stratification among breast cancer survivors.

The observed pattern of disease risk across breast cancer stages suggests a coherent and internally consistent risk trajectory rather than a collection of distinct disease-specific phenomena. This pattern contrasts with other malignancies, such as prostate cancer, in which systemic therapies are largely reserved for advanced disease,1,49 and highlights a defining feature of breast cancer survivorship: early and pervasive systemic exposure, with long-term multisystem consequences. In breast cancer, systemic and hormone-based therapies are commonly administered in early-stage disease, resulting in widespread exposure to cardiotoxic, osteotoxic, and metabolically active treatments, even among women without metastatic disease.7-12, 50,51 As disease advances, cumulative treatment intensity, prolonged inflammation, and increasing symptom burden may further amplify risk, contributing to the stronger associations observed among women with metastatic breast cancer. Together, these findings indicate that excess risk of common diseases after breast cancer reflects a continuum of survivorship exposure and vulnerability rather than a threshold effect confined to advanced cancer.

Established associations for many common diseases in this study are broadly consistent with prior reports in breast cancer survivorship, particularly for VTE, heart failure, AF, metabolic disorders, and second primary cancers.14-20 In contrast, we did not observe a statistically significant association between a diagnosis of breast cancer and CAD, a finding that differs from several previous studies.9,52,53 Several factors may contribute to this discrepancy. Our population-based design reduced selection bias inherent to treatment-based or clinic-based cohorts, which may overrepresent individuals with higher baseline cardiovascular risk. The large sample size and long follow-up enabled more stable risk estimation while allowing careful handling of time-dependent exposure. Furthermore, our use of a full-cohort time-to-event framework with time-updated breast cancer status minimized immortal time bias arising from misclassification of prediagnosis follow-up as exposed time, which can inflate associations in survivorship analyses. Finally, CAD is strongly age dependent, and use of age as the underlying time scale, together with explicit consideration of competing risks, may further attenuate associations observed in analyses that do not account for these factors. Together, these considerations suggest that previously reported associations between breast cancer survivorship and CAD may, in part, reflect methodological differences rather than biological inconsistency.

The demonstrated stratification of disease risk by inherited genetic susceptibility is novel in this setting and highlights the potential clinical utility of integrating genetic risk assessment into breast cancer survivorship care. Baseline evaluation of inherited risk before initiation of cancer therapy may help identify women who are predisposed to specific long-term diseases, enabling more informed treatment selection and surveillance planning. For example, osteoporosis risk is strongly influenced by genetic susceptibility and is further amplified by endocrine therapy, particularly aromatase inhibitors; incorporation of osteoporosis-specific polygenic risk scores could inform earlier bone density assessment and targeted preventive strategies. Similarly, inherited susceptibility to heart failure may help identify survivors who are more vulnerable to the cardiotoxic effects of systemic therapy, supporting intensified cardiac monitoring during and after treatment. In addition, elevated genetic susceptibility to lung cancer, when considered alongside clinical factors such as prior chest radiation and smoking history, may help identify breast cancer survivors who warrant heightened clinical vigilance within existing screening frameworks. Importantly, clinical-grade disease-specific polygenic risk scores are increasingly available and analytically validated, making genetics-informed survivorship care both feasible and actionable in current clinical settings.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, detailed treatment information was not available because comprehensive and reliable treatment histories are difficult to ascertain in the UK Biobank. Consequently, we could not attribute observed outcomes to specific therapies, infer treatment-specific causality, or estimate treatment-specific risks for individual outcomes. In addition, outcome ascertainment was based on self-reported and linked inpatient hospital records because complete, longitudinal outpatient diagnostic data were not available. This approach may have underascertained conditions managed predominantly in outpatient settings, particularly hypertension and depression. Women after a breast cancer diagnosis may also have more frequent clinical encounters and diagnostic evaluations than women without breast cancer; therefore, differential diagnostic intensity and surveillance bias may have contributed to associations for some outcomes. Although the time-updated exposure design addressed temporal classification and minimized immortal-time bias, it did not eliminate the potential for surveillance bias. Second, tumor biomarker data, including estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and ERBB2 (formerly HER2 or HER2/neu) status, were also unavailable in the standard UK Biobank cancer registration data; therefore, receptor-defined subgroups could not be evaluated as proxies for likely treatment exposures. Third, the vast majority of participants were of European ancestry. Although all analyses were adjusted for genetic ancestry using ancestry principal components, the generalizability of our findings to women from underrepresented ancestry groups remains uncertain. The sample size of participants with non-European ancestry (n = 15 491 [6.08%]) was limited, and the number of incident events for most diseases in these groups was small, precluding reliable ancestry-specific analyses. Fourth, monogenic pathogenic variants were not incorporated into the genetic risk assessment. This decision reflects both limited and inconsistent evidence linking specific genes to many of the evaluated diseases and the low prevalence of pathogenic variants for most conditions (typically <0.5%), which substantially limits statistical power in population-based analyses.

In conclusion, using a novel study design that minimized several potential sources of confounding and bias in assessing the risk of common diseases in a large population-based cohort, we found that a diagnosis of breast cancer and inherited genetic susceptibility independently and jointly contribute to the risk of multiple diseases. Integration of genetic risk with clinical factors improved stratification of long-term adverse health outcomes among breast cancer survivors and those living with metastatic disease, supporting the potential for genetics-informed, personalized survivorship care in clinical practice.
