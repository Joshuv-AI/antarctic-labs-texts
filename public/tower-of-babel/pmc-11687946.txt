Arterial thoracic outlet syndrome caused by a cervical rib: a combined thoracoscopic and supraclavicular approach for ‘en bloc’-resection
Authors: Jon Andri Lutz, Emmanouil Psathas, Benoît Rouiller, Luis Filipe Azenha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA cervical rib is the cause of ∼5% of thoracic outlet syndromes (TOS). We report the case of a patient with arterial TOS due to the presence of a cervical rib, managed by combined thoracoscopic and supraclavicular approach. An 18-year-old female patient presented with symptoms of arterial TOS. Magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography angiography scans showed a tortuous subclavian artery due to a fused cervical and 1st rib. Three-port thoracoscopy was performed, which allowed to mobilize the ventral part of the 1st rib. Supraclavicular access allowed mobilization as well as central division of the ribs. After removal of the ribs, the subclavian artery presented a normal calibre and aspect. In recent years, there has been a trend towards minimally invasive approach to TOS, either by thoracoscopy or by robotic-assisted surgery. The advantages of this approach are the ‘enbloc’-resection of both ribs and the possibility to evaluate the subclavian artery during the same procedure and perform repair if necessary.Keywords: Thoracic outlet syndrome, Thoracic surgery, Combined approach, Cervical rib, Thoracoscopy

INTRODUCTION

Thoracic outlet syndromes (TOS) can manifest with a variety of symptoms, depending on the compressed anatomic structure at the upper thoracic aperture. More than 90% of TOS are of neurogenic origin and manifest through pain, weakness, numbness or paresthesia of non-radicular distribution [1]. Symptoms occur during daily activities and are enhanced by above-the-shoulder activities. About 3–5% of TOS are of venous origin and have a particular panel of symptoms. Upper extremity swelling associated with pain, which worsens after physical activity, constitute the more frequent symptoms. More frequently, the subclavian vein is compressed at the costo-clavicular junction. Patients with venous TOS might develop venous thrombosis known as Paget-Schroetter syndrome, following repetitive injury [1]. At last, arterial TOS accounts for up to 5% of TOS. The symptoms comprise pallor, pain and coolness of the extremity, which can be mistaken with Raynaud syndrome. Repetitive intimal lesion can sometimes lead to thrombosis, embolism and limb-threatening ischaemia. Thus, it is of outmost importance to recognize and treat the underlying cause [1]. A congenital cervical rib is a predisposing factor and accounts for up to 5% of TOS cases [1]. We present a patient with arterial TOS caused by a cervical rib. Combined thoracoscopic and supraclavicular approach allowed ‘en bloc’ resection of the cervical and 1st rib as well as perioperative assessment of the subclavian artery.

Case presentation

An 18-year-old female patient was referred complaining about pain in the right upper extremity after a fall 2 years earlier. Pain occurred mainly during above-the-head activities. At clinical examination, right-sided radial pulse was weak and the Wright’s and Adson’s Tests were positive. Magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography angiography scans showed a tortuous subclavian artery with significant narrowing of the lumen due to a complete fusion of the cervical rib to the 1st rib forming a bony pseudarthrosis (Fig. 1A). Due to the burden of her symptoms and the risk of damaging the subclavian artery by repeated compression, surgical treatment with perioperative assessment of the subclavian artery was suggested.

(A) 3D reconstruction of the thoracic outlet showing a tortuous subclavian artery (red arrow) and the cervical rib (yellow arrow). (B) ‘en bloc’-specimen of the cervical and first ribs.

The patient was installed in a supine position with slight elevation of the right side. A three-port thoracoscopy was performed, which allowed to mobilize the ventral part of the 1st rib from the scalene musculature and the section of the rib ventrally at the level of the costo-chondral transition. A uniportal or even biportal approach was not feasible due to limitations because of the angles of the instruments. Further mobilization of the 1st rib was precluded by the cervical rib. A supraclavicular access allowed mobilization as well as central division of the cervical rib. The fused cervical and 1st ribs were dissected from the remaining tissue and sectioned dorsally. After removal of the ribs, the subclavian artery returned into a straight, harmonious position. The freed subclavian artery presented a normal calibre and aspect, thus no further vascular reconstruction was performed. The ‘enbloc’-resection of the right cervical and 1st ribs was performed in 157 min (Fig. 1B).

Postoperatively, normal arterial flow without acceleration during Doppler ultrasound was established, and the chest tube could be removed on day 1 (Fig. 2). The patient was discharged after 72 h. At 1-year follow-up, the patient remains asymptomatic.

DISCUSSION

Treatment recommendations depend on the aetiology of TOS. It is recommended that neurogenic TOS should be managed conservatively for 3–6 months before considering surgery [2]. Conservative treatment consists of physiotherapy, analgesia and postural training [2].

In case of vascular and neurogenic TOS persisting for more than 6 months, surgery must be suggested to the patient [1]. In the case of Paget–Schroetter syndrome, surgical decompression of the subclavian vein following a period of oral anticoagulation or thrombolysis can achieve permanent venous patency in up to 98% of cases [3].

In the largest series on cervical rib resections for TOS, authors used mostly a supraclavicular or a transaxillary approach [4, 5], with combined approach being uncommon. In recent years, there has been a trend towards minimally invasive approach to treat TOS, either by thoracoscopy or even by robotic-assisted surgery [2, 3], which gives a perfect vision of the neuro-vascular structures from below. It does, however, not give access to the root of the cervical rib. By combining a thoracoscopic and a supraclavicular approach, we were able to perform an ‘enbloc’-resection of both ribs under continuous vision of the neuro-vascular bundle. The supraclavicular access allows for clinical evaluation of the artery and offers the possibility of vascular reconstruction if needed.

CONCLUSION

Combining thoracoscopy and the classic supraclavicular approach allows a safe ‘en bloc’ removal of the cervical and 1st ribs to treat TOS.
