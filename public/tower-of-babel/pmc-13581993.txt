Development of multiple disease resistant rice variety through marker assisted selection
Authors: Mohammad Abdul Latif, Md Omar Kayess, Rakibul Hasan, A.L. Nayeem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing reliance on pesticides to manage major rice diseases has raised concerns about resistance development. To address this, the present study aimed to genetically improve the widely cultivated mega variety BRRI dhan29, which already carries the tungro resistance gene tsv1, by pyramiding multiple disease resistance genes. Unlike previous pyramiding studies, this work integrates resistance to three major rice diseases blast, bacterial blight, and tungro into a single mega-variety under Bangladesh-specific agro-ecological conditions. The blast resistance genes Pi9 and Pb1 and bacterial blight resistance gene Xa21 were introgressed into BRRI dhan29 using BR(Path)13800-BC2-224 and IRBB58 as donor parents, while the endogenous Xa4 and tsv1 alleles were monitored and retained during marker-assisted backcrossing. Importantly, tsv1 an endogenous homozygous-recessive resistance allele in BRRI dhan29 rather than a newly introgressed gene; therefore, it was monitored during backcrossing and re-fixed in homozygous form during subsequent selfing generations. Marker-assisted selection was conducted using gene specific-functional markers for all target genes. Following two backcross generations, selected BC2F1 plants were self-pollinated and advanced from BC2F2 to BC2F7 under marker-assisted foreground selection and phenotypic selection. The recessive tsv1 gene was carefully tracked and fixed through marker-assisted selection across segregating generations to ensure stable tungro resistance. Seven lines (G14, G5, G10, G17, G19, G22, and G4) recorded higher yields (8.67–8.75 t ha−1) than BRRI dhan29 (7.09 t ha−1) and showed low disease scores (0–3) against three diseases. These lines demonstrate durable, broad-spectrum resistance to three major pathogens alongside numerically superior yield and potential agronomic performance, highlighting them as promising candidates for multi-location and multi-season evaluation prior to varietal release consideration.

Introduction

Rice is the primary dietary energy source for great portion of the world's population [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Bangladesh being the world's third-largest rice producer, half of its protein and two-thirds of its caloric need fulfil by it [[5], [6], [7]]. This country is among the top ten most climate-vulnerable countries of the world [[8], [9], [10]], where climate change has severely affected rice production because of various biotic and abiotic stresses [[11], [12], [13]]. Between them, biotic stressors impacted most [[14], [15], [16]]. Among these, major diseases such as blast, bacterial blight, and tungro pose a persistent constraint to sustainable rice production in Bangladesh.

Rice production is severely constrained by major biotic stresses, among which rice blast (RB), bacterial blight (BB), and rice tungro (RT) are the most destructive diseases affecting yield and stability worldwide [17,18]. Rice blast is widely distributed across major rice-growing regions and causes lesions on leaves, culms, and panicles, ultimately reducing photosynthetic efficiency and grain yield [19]. Globally, RB is responsible for annual yield losses of 10–30%, which may increase up to 70–80% under severe epidemic conditions [[19], [20], [21], [22]], and contributes to food loss affecting ∼ 60 million people each year [23]. Consequently, effective rice blast management across diverse rice-growing environments is essential for limiting yield losses and mitigating their broader food-security, economic, and environmental consequences [[24], [25], [26]]. In Bangladesh, blast remains a primary threat, causing yield reductions ranging from 11% to 46.4% depending on environmental conditions and crop stage [27,28].

Bacterial blight is another highly destructive disease that significantly reduces rice productivity [29]. It can cause yield losses of 20–50% globally [30] and up to complete crop failure under severe outbreaks [31]. In Bangladesh, BB contributes to yield reductions ranging from 5.8% to 30.4% [32]. The presence of multiple pathogen races, including at least 12 identified strains of Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo), further complicates disease management [33].

Rice tungro is a major viral disease that severely affects plant growth and yield. Infection leads to stunted growth, chlorosis, delayed development, and reduced grain filling, which can result in yield losses of up to 100% under severe conditions [[34], [35], [36]]. The disease is caused by the synergistic interaction between Rice tungro bacilliform virus (RTBV) and Rice tungro spherical virus (RTSV), both transmitted by green leafhoppers [[37], [38], [39]]. Collectively, these diseases account for 20–30% of global rice yield losses and up to 50% under extreme conditions [3,40]. In Bangladesh, their combined impact results in annual yield losses ranging from 5.8% to 30.4%33, posing a serious threat to food security and necessitating the development of durable and broad-spectrum disease resistance strategies.

Despite extensive research on individual disease resistance, the development of rice cultivars with simultaneous resistance to multiple major diseases remains limited, particularly under Bangladesh-specific pathogen pressure.

A highly economical and environmentally friendly approach to combat these diseases is to improve host plant resistance [41,42] by pyramiding of multiple R genes [3,43,44]. Molecular marker technology offers an efficient means of selecting desirable genes [45,46] and has proven effective in rice breeding programs [47,48]. Marker-assisted selection (MAS) accelerates the integration of desirable traits into new cultivars [49,50] and plays a crucial role in gene pyramiding, enabling the combination of multiple resistance genes into a single genetic background [[51], [52], [53], [54], [55]]. However, most previous pyramiding studies have primarily focused on one or two diseases, often lacking validation under combined disease pressure or failing to utilize widely adopted mega varieties.

Although gene pyramiding involving blast and bacterial blight resistance genes such as Pi9, Pb1, Xa4, and Xa21 has been reported, their combined deployment in a high-yielding mega variety which carry tungro resistance (tsv1) gene is underexplored. Few studies have assessed pyramided lines under both controlled and field conditions in Bangladesh, were diverse pathogen populations and environmental variability challenge resistance durability. To our knowledge, this is the first study to introduce the blast-resistance genes Pi9 and Pb1 and the bacterial blight-resistance gene Xa21 into the BRRI dhan29 background while simultaneously monitoring and retaining the endogenous Xa4 and recessive tsv1 alleles, with the resulting lines evaluated under both artificial inoculation and natural multi-disease field conditions.

BRRI dhan29 is a mega variety of Bangladesh due to its high yield and popularity among farmers and consumers due to premium grain quality. However, despite its wide adaptability and productivity, it is highly susceptible to blast, bacterial blight, but moderately resistant to tungro diseases, leading to significant annual yield losses that threaten food security and economic stability.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop multi-disease-resistant lines in the BRRI dhan29 background by introducing the blast-resistance genes Pi9 and Pb1 and the bacterial-blight-resistance gene Xa21, while retaining the endogenous Xa4 and tsv1 alleles through marker-assisted selection. The resulting lines were evaluated for resistance stability and agronomic performance under both controlled and field conditions, providing a practical framework for multi-disease resistance breeding in climate-vulnerable rice production systems.

Result

Foreground selection

A total of 145 F1 progenies from the cross between BRRI dhan29 × BR(Path)13800-BC2-224 were screened for blast resistance using NMSM-Pi9 and RM206 markers, respectively while 170 F1 progenies from a cross between BRRI dhan29 and IRBB58 were evaluated for BB resistance using markers MP1/MP2, and pTA248. Following initial screening, the F1 hybrids were backcrossed with BRRI dhan29, producing 222 BC1F1 progenies. At the same time RM21801 marker were used to identify parent carrying tsv1 gene for the above-mentioned plants as they naturally contain. The BC1F1 plants were re-evaluated with the same markers to identify desired resistant alleles, and heterozygous plants resembling the recurrent parent were selected for further backcrossing, leading to production of 270 BC2F1 seeds. Populations carrying targeted R genes were then combined and advanced from BC2F1 to BC2F7 generations through continuous backcrossing and selfing using marker-assisted selection. This stepwise marker-assisted selection ensured the progressive enrichment, eventual fixation and presence confirmation of target resistance genes in each advanced generation. Genotyping and phenotyping of BC2F2 population

A total of 380 BC2F2 individuals were evaluated for resistance to major rice diseases under controlled, artificially inoculated conditions using virulent isolates of blast and BB. Phenotypic screening identified 280 blast-resistant and 275 bacterial-blight-resistant plants. For tungro, 97 plants were resistant and 283 were susceptible, consistent with the expected 1:3 phenotypic ratio for a recessively inherited resistance allele. Concurrently, all 380 plants were genotyped using molecular markers as detailed in Table 1, Table 2. From this population, a subset of 108 BC2F6 plants was developed and selected based on numerically superior morphological traits, presence of resistance or recessive genes through molecular markers and disease reactions. Segregation analysis of BC2F2 population

Chi-square analysis was performed to determine the inheritance pattern of the genes under investigation. The results of this analysis for both phenotypic and genotypic evaluations are presented in Table 2, Table 3, respectively. The chi-square values calculated for the phenotypic reactions were statistically insignificant (p > 0.05), meaning they were below the critical value of 3.84 at one degree of freedom (df = 1). Similarly, the chi-square value for the genotypic data was also found to be statistically insignificant (p > 0.05, χ2<3.84). The blast- and bacterial-blight-resistance reactions did not substantially deviate from the expected 3:1 resistant-to-susceptible ratios. Tungro resistance did not significantly deviate from the expected 1:3 resistant-to-susceptible ratio because resistance conditioned by tsv1 is recessive. Genotypic segregation at each locus was evaluated against the expected 1:2:1 ratio. However, gene-specific marker analysis enabled independent evaluation of each resistance locus. Marker–trait association

All five molecular markers showed statistically significant associations with their corresponding disease-resistance traits. For blast resistance, NMSM-Pi9 was significantly associated with the Pi9 gene, with a coefficient of −3.77, a p-value of 0.000164, and an R2 value of 0.45. Similarly, RM206 showed a strong association with Pb1 (coefficient = −2.89, p = 0.00266, R2 = 0.32). These markers explained 45% and 32% of the observed variation in blast disease scores, respectively.

For bacterial blight resistance, pTA248 was remarkable associated with Xa21 (coefficient = −3.40, p = 0.00012, R2 = 0.47), while MP1/MP2 showed a noticeable association with Xa4 (coefficient = −5.63, p = 0.00345, R2 = 0.30). For tungro resistance, RM21801 was significantly associated with the recessive tsv1 gene, recording a coefficient of −3.08, a p-value of 0.0000413, and an R2 value of 0.51.

The negative regression coefficients observed for all markers indicate that the presence of resistance-associated marker alleles was related to lower disease scores under the marker-coding system used in the analysis. Overall, the significant associations observed for NMSM-Pi9, RM206, pTA248, MP1/MP2, and RM21801 demonstrate the usefulness of these gene-linked markers for predicting resistance to blast, bacterial blight, and tungro in the evaluated rice population. Multivariate analysis

PCA and clustering analysis of BC2F7 population

The first two principal components explained over 90% of the total variation, indicating that the dataset was well represented in reduced dimensions (Supplementary Fig. 1). The PCA biplot illustrated the relationships among the evaluated traits, showing that traits with longer vectors, such as days to flowering and days to maturity, contributed substantially to Dim1, while filled spikes per panicle were more closely associated with Dim2. It also revealed strong correlations among tiller number, plant height, grain number, grain weight, and yield, whereas days to flowering and maturity exhibited an opposite trend relative to the yield-related traits (Fig. 2). PCA based cluster analysis of individual genotypes showed cluster 1 consisted of G2, G3, G6, G7, G8, G9, G10, G11, G12, G13, G14, G15, G16, G19, G20, G21, and G23 while cluster 2 consisted of G1, G4, G5, G17, G18, and G22. The PCA based traits and genotype cluster analysis revealed that Clusters 1 (G1, G4, G5, G17, G18 and G22) and 2 (All ALs excluding the genotypes of cluster 1) tended to associate with yield and yield-contributing traits, including total tiller number, effective tiller number, plant height, grain number, grain weight, and yield. Molecular analysis genotyping by SNP panel

The genotyping results of ALs, and check varieties were assessed using a 5-SNP panel targeting five R genes. Each SNP marker was associated with a specific gene, allowing the determination of gene presence or absence based on homozygous alleles. In this analysis, the allele combinations C:C or T:T indicated the presence of a target gene, while alternate alleles (e.g., A:A) denoted its absence, as validated by the check varieties. Among the ALs, the majority (e.g., G1, G4, G5, G7–G11, G14–G15, G17, G19–G22) carried all five resistance genes, representing fully pyramided lines. These lines displayed homozygous alleles corresponding to the presence of each gene, confirming successful incorporation of multiple disease-resistance loci. In contrast, certain lines exhibited partial pyramiding; for instance, G12 and G13 lacked Xa21, possessing only Xa4, tsv1, Pi9, and Pb1, whereas G16 and G18 lacked Pb1. AL G6, conversely, lacked Pi9 while consisting the other genes. Comparison with check varieties validated the SNP-based genotyping approach. BRRI dhan29, which carry Xa4 and tsv1, exhibited homozygous alleles while showing absence for Xa21, Pb1, and Pi9. The donor parent, IRBB58, confirmed the presence of Xa4 and Xa21, while another donor parent BR(Path)13800-BC2-224, containing Pi9 and Pb1, confirmed the presence of these genes (Table 7). These results demonstrate that the 10-SNP panel effectively identifies specific resistance genes, distinguishes fully pyramided lines from partial or single-gene lines, and provides precise genetic material for developing broad-spectrum disease-resistant rice cultivars. Discussion

The present study successfully developed advanced rice lines with combined resistance to blast, bacterial blight, and tungro through marker-assisted gene pyramiding in the BRRI dhan29 background. The pyramided lines demonstrated enhanced resistance across all three diseases while maintaining favourable agronomic performance in the evaluated field trial, indicating the utility of integrating multiple resistance genes into the BRRI dhan29 background. Continuous increase in rice production is essential to ensure food security [[61], [62], [63]]. But the increasing frequent incidence of rice blast, BB and tungro diseases, pose a significant threat to rice cultivation in Asia particularly in Bangladesh [64].

In Bangladesh, where 267 blast races have been identified, genes such as Pi9 and Pb1 have proven effective against these races [65], as confirmed by studies [16,42]. In the present study, ALs carrying the combination of Pi9 and Pb1 exhibited greater resistance than those with a single gene and also demonstrated high blast resistance when pyramided with other R genes via MAS [16].

The Pi9 gene plays a crucial role in plant immunity by recognizing specific effector proteins of the blast pathogen, trigger the activation of downstream defense signaling pathways [66] Pi9 encodes an NBS-LRR protein that contributes to enhanced blast resistance by promoting the production of antimicrobial compounds and inhibiting fungal proliferation [67,68]. In addition, Pb1 enhances plant defense by interacting with the WRKY45 transcription factor, which regulates the salicylic acid and diterpenoid phytoalexin pathways, thereby strengthening resistance to the blast pathogen [[69], [70], [71]]. It also encodes an atypical CC-NBS-LRR protein with an unconventional NBS domain that distinguishes it from other resistance proteins [72].

Among the bacterial blight resistance genes in rice, the Xa4 gene encodes a cell wall-associated kinase (WAK) that reinforces the plant's cell walls [60,68,73,74]. Another bacterial blight resistance gene, Xa21, has been reported to confer resistance to ∼94.9% of the twelve virulent BLB races [74,75]. The Xa21 gene, a member of the leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinase (LRR-RLK) class, functions as a pattern recognition receptor on the cell membrane, triggering a robust immune response upon detecting pathogen-associated molecular patterns. This recognition triggers a signaling cascade involving Xa21-binding protein and MAPKs, which activate transcription factors such as OsWRKY62 and OsWRKY76 to regulate downstream defense responses and inhibit the proliferation of Xoo. [76,77]. The combined action of Xa4 and Xa21 provides a multifaceted defense strategy that enhances broad-spectrum resistance against Xoo and contributes to improved yield performance in the developed advanced lines.

Rice tungro is increasingly epidemic in Bangladesh. Most of the widely cultivated varieties are susceptible in various RT hotspot regions like Cumilla [37,78]. The tsv1 gene confers resistance to Rice tungro virus through a recessive mutation in the translation initiation factor eIF4G, essential for viral RNA translation [37,79]. The tsv1 gene encodes a modified eIF4G protein containing specific SNPs or deletions in exon 9 that alter its structure, thereby preventing the tungro virus from utilizing host translational machinery and inhibiting viral RNA translation [62]. This mechanism, further validated by genome editing studies, highlights the potential of eIF4G-based mutations to confer resistance under the tested inoculation and field conditions and may be extended to other plant viruses beyond tungro [80]. These mechanisms support the strong resistance observed in the pyramided lines developed in this study and demonstrate their potential as valuable breeding materials for developing high-yielding, multi-disease-resistant rice cultivars in future breeding programs.

Marker-assisted selection is a widely used breeding approach that enables the rapid and efficient development of disease-resistant cultivars through the identification and utilization of functional genes and their associated markers [30,53,81]. For instance, several markers like pTA248, NMSMPi9, and RM206 are closely linked to the, Xa21, Pi9, and Pb1 genes, respectively were used in marker-assisted breeding programs to develop varieties resistant to blast and BB [40,56,58,65,67,[82], [83], [84]]. In the present study, all five gene-linked markers showed statistically significant associations with their corresponding disease responses. Based on the R2 values, RM21801, pTA248, and NMSM-Pi9 explained relatively greater proportions of phenotypic variation, whereas RM206 and MP1/MP2 showed comparatively lower explanatory values. These differences indicate variation in marker performance under the evaluated population and disease-screening conditions. Nevertheless, marker-assisted selection should be integrated with phenotypic screening because linked markers may not consistently predict functional resistance across different genetic backgrounds and environmental conditions.

Interestingly, some advanced lines lacking one or more target resistance genes still exhibited strong phenotypic resistance. This suggests that individual genes may provide sufficient resistance under specific environmental or pathogen conditions, or that gene redundancy and epistatic interactions among resistance loci contributed to disease suppression. Additionally, the possible presence of minor or undetected resistance QTLs, along with environmental influences, may have affected phenotypic expression. These findings highlight the complexity of genotype–phenotype relationships in pyramided lines and emphasize the importance of integrating both molecular and phenotypic selection approaches.

The development of rice varieties with durable, broad-spectrum disease resistance is essential to safeguard global food security, as the rapid mutation of pathogen races often compromise single-gene resistance [58,59]. To overcome this challenge, pyramiding multiple resistance (R) genes has been successfully employed through the additive effects of combined R genes [85]. Chi-square analysis indicated that resistance to blast, BB, and tungro follows a single-gene model, with the gene acting dominantly or recessively and its effectiveness influenced by pathogen diversity and specific gene combinations [51] thereby emphasizing the superior multi-disease resistance performance of gene-pyramided lines compared with single-gene resistance.

From an agronomic perspective, the advanced lines retained broadly favourable morphological and yield-related characteristics in the BRRI dhan29 background. Several lines recorded higher mean values for yield and associated traits than the recurrent parent. Because quantitative agronomic traits are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, these results should be interpreted together with the line-versus-parent statistical comparisons rather than as evidence that gene pyramiding itself caused improved yield. Given that many quantitative traits are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, selecting traits closely associated with yield hill−1 is crucial for an effective breeding program [34,86] thereby confirming the agronomic superiority of the selected pyramided lines with consistent performance over the recipient parent.

Multivariate analyses further supported these findings by demonstrating strong The multivariate grouping of several pyramided lines with genotypes displaying favourable agronomic traits demonstrates phenotypic association but does not establish a causal relationship between resistance-gene pyramiding and overall plant performance [87,88]. Unlike many previous studies that focused on resistance to one or two diseases, this study successfully integrated resistance to three major rice diseases within a single high-yielding mega variety. Furthermore, evaluation of these lines under both controlled and field conditions in Bangladesh provides important insights into their stability and performance under diverse environmental and pathogen pressures.

Overall, the results demonstrate the practical utility of marker-assisted gene pyramiding for developing multi-disease-resistant rice lines while retaining favourable yield performance under the evaluated conditions. A further limitation is that visual selection of candidate lines and disease scoring were not performed under blinded conditions. Although standardized phenotypic and disease-scoring criteria were applied, awareness of line identity could have introduced observer or selection bias. Genome-wide background selection was not performed in this study. Therefore, recurrent-parent genome recovery was not quantified. Nevertheless, phenotypic selection during backcrossing resulted in advanced lines showing agronomic characteristics broadly resembling BRRI dhan29. Overall, this study demonstrates the practical utility of marker-assisted gene pyramiding in developing multi-disease resistant rice varieties without compromising yield. The identified advanced lines represent valuable genetic resources for future breeding programs and hold strong candidates for further multi-location evaluation, contributing to sustainable rice production in disease prone climate-vulnerable regions.

Conclusions

Rice production is suffering from the various major diseases which significantly reduces its total yield. The study emphasizes the significance of gene pyramiding as a sustainable approach for managing major diseases rice. By combining multiple resistance genes into a single variety, this strategy offers a more durable resistance compared to chemical control measures, which are not only costly but also pose environmental risks. The promising advanced lines, particularly G14, G5, G10, G17, G19, G22, and G4, combined favourable disease reactions with high mean yield and therefore warrant further multi-location and multi-season evaluation before consideration for varietal release. Overall, this study provides a practical framework for developing multi-disease resistant rice varieties and contributes valuable genetic resources for future breeding programs aimed at enhancing crop resilience and ensuring sustainable rice production under changing environmental conditions.

Limitations

The present experiment was conducted in a single season and in a single year; therefore, multi-season and multi-location field trials are recommended to confirm the accuracy, stability, and reliability of the results. In addition, disease scoring and the selection of 23 lines from 108 BC2F6 candidates were based on visual assessment and were not performed under blinded conditions with respect to genotype. Therefore, the possibility of observer or selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Future studies should consider blinded disease scoring and genotype-independent selection procedures to minimize potential subjective bias and improve the robustness of the findings.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

The study utilized BRRI dhan29 as the recurrent parent. BR(Path)13800-BC2-224 was used as the donor of the blast resistance genes Pi9 and Pb1, whereas IRBB58, carrying the bacterial blight resistance genes Xa4 and Xa21, was used primarily as the source of Xa21. Because Xa4 was already present in BRRI dhan29, this gene was monitored for retention rather than considered a newly introgressed locus. Similarly, the endogenous recessive tsv1 allele of BRRI dhan29 was monitored throughout the breeding generations to ensure its retention and subsequent fixation. The donor parent BR(Path)13800-BC2-224 was developed by crossing between Pi9 gene donor parent Pi9-US2 and Pb1 gene donor parent Pb1-US2. After successful crosses and several years of field evaluation with help of MAS, this line was developed. However, the candidate gene Pi9, Pb1, Xa4, and Xa21 are dominant in nature while tsv1 is recessive. Although BRRI dhan29 naturally possesses the tsv1 gene, the other donor parents lack this allele; therefore, it is imperative to monitor its presence with gene-based markers at the F1 stage, throughout successive backcross generations, to ensure its retention and fixation in homozygous form. Since tsv1 confers recessive resistance, crosses with non-carrier donors produce F1 plants heterozygous at this locus, which are phenotypically susceptible to tungro, and without marker-assisted selection, the allele can be readily lost during backcrossing and selfing. So, the presence of this gene in the subsequent segregating generation was confirmed by bioassay test, phenotyping and genotyping methods. To ensure synchronous flowering for hybridization, five parental crossing combinations were done in experimental plots at the Plant Pathology Division of the Bangladesh Rice Research Institute (BRRI), Gazipur-1701, Bangladesh. Seedlings of 35 days old were transplanted in the main field during the boro season (November 2023 - April 2024). Fertilizer application, irrigation, weed control, and pest management were done according to standard protocols developed by BRRI, 2012. Heterozygous individuals (Tsv1/tsv1) were intentionally selected during early backcross generations (F1, BC1F1, BC2F1) to retain the allele, whereas homozygous recessive (tsv1/tsv1) individuals were selected from BC2F2 onward to ensure stable tungro resistance in advanced lines.

Crossing scheme

BRRI dhan29 was used as the recurrent parent. Pi9 and Pb1 were introduced from BR(Path)13800-BC2-224, whereas Xa21 was introduced from IRBB58. Although IRBB58 also carried Xa4, Xa4 was already present in BRRI dhan29; therefore, Xa4 was monitored for retention rather than treated as a newly introgressed locus. The tsv1-associated allele was also endogenous to BRRI dhan29 and was similarly monitored throughout the breeding process. Because tsv1-mediated resistance is recessive, heterozygous plants carrying the desired allele were retained during the F1, BC1F1, and BC2F1 generations. DNA isolation, PCR analysis and marker-assisted foreground selection

Marker-assisted foreground selection was conducted concurrently with PCR-based genotyping to identify and advance individuals carrying target resistance genes. To perform polymerase chain reaction (PCR), genomic DNA was isolated from 21-day-old seedlings of advanced lines and their progenitors using a modified CTAB method as described by Ref. [65]. A PCR was conducted using a 10 μL reaction mixture composed of the following: 3 μL genomic DNA, 0.5 μL of each forward and reverse primer, 5 μL of G2 GoTaq Flexi Master Mix (Promega, USA), and 1 μL of nuclease-free water. The specific primer sequences and PCR conditions employed for each gene of target are presented in Table 1. Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to detect PCR amplicons, which allowed us to identify progenies carrying the target resistance genes. The gel concentration was adjusted for each gene: 1.5% for Xa21, and 3% for Xa4, Pi9, and Pb1. The presence of PCR amplicons of 168 bp and 147 bp, respectively, indicated homozygous resistance for Pi9 and Pb1. The BB-resistance was evaluated using markers targeting Xa4, and Xa21. Homozygous resistance for these genes was confirmed by the presence of PCR products of 150 bp, and 1000 bp. Homozygous susceptibility for the genes in question was denoted by the presence of bands at 310 bp, and 750 bp. Heterozygous individuals were identified by the existence of both resistant and susceptible alleles. For tsv1 genotyping, young leaves (5–7 cm) were collected, lyophilized, and ground to a fine powder in 2-mL microcentrifuge tubes using a GenoGrinder. Genomic DNA was extracted using the TPS method consisting of Tris–HCl, EDTA, and KCl. For each sample, 700 μL of pre-warmed TPS extraction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 1 M KCl, and 10 mM EDTA) was added, thoroughly mixed, and incubated at 65°C for 1 h. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (≈13,760 × g; Hitachi Himac CT15RE, T15A62 rotor, outer row) for 10 min at room temperature to pellet the cellular debris. The resulting supernatant (500 μL) was transferred to a fresh microcentrifuge tube and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol. The samples were incubated at 4°C for 30 min to precipitate the genomic DNA and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (≈13,760 × g; Hitachi Himac CT15RE, T15A62 rotor, outer row) for 10 min at 4°C to recover the DNA pellet. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and dissolved in 200 μL TE buffer containing RNase A (10 mg/mL). DNA quality and concentration were assessed using 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the DNA concentration was adjusted to 25–50 ng/μL for PCR analysis. PCR amplification for tsv1 was performed using the SSR marker RM21801, which is closely linked to the tsv1 locus [[89], [90], [91]]. The 10 μL reaction mixture contained 3 μL of template DNA (30 ng), 0.5 μL of each primer (5 pmol; Invitrogen), 1 μL of reaction buffer, 1 μL of dNTPs, and 0.2 μL of Taq DNA polymerase. The PCR profile included an initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 1 min, 55°C for 30 s, 72°C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 7 min. Amplified products were resolved on 3.5% agarose gels with 0.5 × TBE buffer containing SYBR Safe (0.1 μg ml−1) and visualized using a gel documentation system. Gel scoring was based on the banding patterns of donor and recurrent parents. Alleles matching the homozygous resistant BRRI dhan29 control were classified as resistant, whereas alleles matching the susceptible donor controls were classified as susceptible. Individuals carrying both parental alleles were classified as heterozygous. In the F1, BC1F1, and BC2F1 generations, heterozygous offspring were deliberately selected using gene-based or linked markers. Genetic variability was initially preserved while maintaining the desired target genes through this strategy; however, from the BC2F2 generation onward, selection pressure was substantially intensified. Progenies producing amplicons with fragment sizes identical to those of the resistant parents were preferentially selected to ensure retention of target genes while advancing top performing breeding lines within the breeding program. During early backcross generations, heterozygous plants were intentionally selected to maintain the recessive tsv1 allele, whereas in later generations (BC2F2 to BC2F7), homozygous resistant individuals were selected to ensure fixation and stable expression of tungro resistance.

Foreground selection in backcrossed and selfed populations was conducted using the molecular markers listed in Table 1. These markers were applied to genotype a total of 380 BC2F1 individuals. Following this, backcrossing was performed on the selfed populations. The aforementioned markers were subsequently used to genotype an additional 300 BC2F1 lines. Plants exhibiting banding patterns identical to Pi9 and Pb1 were deemed blast resistant, whereas those matching BRRI dhan29 were classified as susceptible. For BB resistance, plants exhibiting banding patterns corresponding to Xa4 and Xa21, were classified as resistant, whereas those displaying bands similar to BRRI dhan29 were considered susceptible. On the other hand, for tungro resistance, plants exhibiting banding patterns corresponding to tsv1, were classified as resistant, whereas those displaying bands similar to BR(Path)13800-BC2-224 were considered susceptible. Individuals presenting two distinct bands were designated as heterozygous and subsequently advanced through both backcrossing and selfing up to the BC2F7 generation. Individuals with bi-allelic banding patterns were considered heterozygous and subjected to recurrent backcrossing and self-pollination through to the BC2F7 generation. Notably, in the F1, BC1F1, and BC2F1 generations, heterozygous plants were selectively advanced using the molecular markers. In contrast, from BC2F2 to successive backcrossing up to BC2F7 generations, plants displaying banding profiles matching with the resistant parental lines were preferentially chosen, thereby ensuring the effective fixation of the target resistance genes.

Inoculum preparation of for blast, bacterial blight and tungro pathogens

A highly contagious strain (BD642) of the blast fungus was used to screen for resistance. The plants were artificially inoculated with the fungus following the protocol described by Ref. [65]. A suspension of fungal spores was prepared at a concentration of ∼1.5 × 105 spores mL−1.

To induce BB infection, plants were inoculated with a highly pathogenic Xoo strain BXo822, following the methodology of [36] with the inoculum concentration of ∼3.3 × 108 cells mL−1. An ideal optical density reading of 1 at 600 nm was applied to induce BB-disease [64], using the leaf clipping technique [92].

For tungro disease, seedlings were exposed to RT virus infection through the release of viruliferous green leafhoppers (GLH), which had previously fed on infected plants for five days. The seed boxes containing the test seedlings were placed in water trays and enclosed with a screen, after which viruliferous GLH were introduced at a density of 6–7 insects per seedling for an inoculation access period of 2–3 h. To ensure uniform insect distribution, the GLH were disturbed at 30-min intervals during inoculation. Following 21 days of infestation, disease symptoms, incidence, and severity were systematically assessed following the outlined made by Ref. [37]. However, all pants (380) were inoculated and or challenged with the respective isolates in BC2F2 generation.

Seedling production and phenotyping of BC2F2 generation

The BC2F2 progenies were evaluated under both greenhouse and natural field conditions. For the BC2F2 greenhouse screening, all 380 segregating individuals were evaluated for each disease assay. Each plant constituted an individual phenotyping unit, and disease reactions were scored separately for each plant rather than as pooled observations. For each disease, the 380 BC2F2 individuals were evaluated in a single experimental run. For blast resistance assessment, 21-day-old seedlings were inoculated with the blast pathogen under controlled greenhouse conditions as well as in a natural blast-prone hotspot. Lesion lengths were measured 7 days post-inoculation following the Standard Evaluation System (SES) of the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) [90], where scores of 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 corresponded to highly resistant (HR), resistant (R), moderately resistant (MR), moderately susceptible (MS), susceptible (S), and highly susceptible (HS), respectively.

To determine bacterial blight resistance, the pathogen was inoculated at the maximum tillering stage in both net house and natural field conditions. Lesion lengths were recorded for progenies and parental lines 21 days after-inoculation, following methodology described by Ref. [90]. Advanced lines were classified as highly resistant (HR) when leaf area infection was 1–5%, resistant (R) at 6–12%, moderately resistant (MR) at 13–25%, susceptible (S) at 26–50%, and highly susceptible (HS) at 51–100%. This classification allowed for the identification and selection of lines with effective resistance to BB for further breeding, according to SES scale, developed by IRRI [90].

For tungro disease resistance, advanced lines were evaluated based on plant height reduction, leaf discoloration, and disease index (adapted from IRRI, Philippines; [37]. According to this scale, ALs were classified as highly resistant (HR) when plant height reduction was 0% with no leaf discoloration (Disease Index 1), resistant (R) when height reduction ranged from 1 to 10% with no leaf discoloration (Disease Index 3), moderately resistant (MR) when height reduction was 11–30% with no distinct discoloration (Disease Index 5), susceptible (S) when height reduction was 31–50% with yellow to orange discoloration (Disease Index 7), and highly susceptible (HS) when height reduction exceeded 50% with yellow to orange discoloration (Disease Index 9). Segregation analysis of BC2F2 population

The BC2F2 segregation population comprised 380 individual plants. Each plant was treated as an independent segregating individual for phenotypic and genotypic classification; individual plants were not technically replicated for the segregation analysis. ALs were classified into three categories: resistant (R), heterozygous (Rr), and susceptible (rr). Disease susceptibility was determined phenotypically, classifying lines as resistant or susceptible based on their reaction to the disease. Dominant resistance genes conferred resistance in both homozygous (RR) and heterozygous (Rr) genotypes, while recessive resistance genes required the homozygous recessive (rr) genotype. A chi-square test was performed to assess segregation ratios in the BC2F2 population, considering both phenotypic and genotypic data by chi-square (χ2) analysis with the expected 3:1 Mendelian segregation ratio typical of backcrossed populations. Although segregation was analysed for each gene independently following Mendelian expectations, the presence of multiple resistance genes may result in more complex segregation patterns due to gene interactions. However, gene-specific marker analysis allowed independent evaluation of each resistance locus.

Field evaluation of BC2F7 population

Twenty-three out of 108 BC2F6 lines were selected for advancement to the BC2F7 generation based on presence of resistance or recessive genes and reactions of three diseases. These ALs were evaluated for blast resistance in two field trials conducted in Gazipur, Bangladesh one on a farm and the other at the Plant Pathology Division of BRRI located at 24°00′22.2″N, 90°26′35.7″E and 23°59′27.5″N, 90°24′18.3″E, respectively. After that, a controlled inoculation experiment was done to assess the susceptibility to blast, BB and tungro diseases for further confirmation. Using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with 5 × 2 m2 plots were established to evaluate the lines’ response under field conditions with three replications. For this, seedlings were initially raised in a seedbed and were transplanted at 35 days of age, into the main field. Phenotypic data were recorded from ten randomly selected plants per plot, excluding plants from the border rows to minimize potential border effects. The agronomic performance of the ALs was evaluated by measuring plant height, number of tillers, days to flowering and maturity, panicle length, grain quality, and yield components. For measurements requiring the selection of an individual tiller or panicle, only the mother tiller (main culm) was used as the reference, while secondary and ineffective tillers were excluded to maintain consistency in phenotypic measurements. The field disease reaction was scored according to the scale mentioned in section 2.5. Marker-trait association analysis

Marker–trait association analysis was conducted using individual markers along with genotypic and phenotypic data from the BC2F2 population, and associations between SSR markers and disease resistance were evaluated using QTL Cartographer V2.5011 to explain the observed phenotypic variation. The linear regression model accounted for this analysis as y = b0 + b1x + e. The outcomes were acquired in the form of approximations of the adjusted R2 value and the F statistic corresponding to each marker. Results were presented as estimates of the adjusted R2 and F-statistic for each marker. Statistical significance of each marker–trait association was assessed using the p-value associated with the regression model, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The coefficient of determination (R2) represented the proportion of phenotypic variation explained by the marker. Marker–trait associations were evaluated using individual gene-linked markers to determine their contribution to phenotypic resistance in the BC2F2 population.

Validation of BC2F7 advanced lines by SNP markers

SNP-based validation was performed to confirm the presence and fixation of the target resistance genes at higher resolution and to complement the SSR-based genotyping results. The selected advanced gene-pyramided BC2F7 lines were validated using SNP markers targeting five QTLs associated with resistance to blast, bacterial blight (BB), and tungro diseases, as detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

For SNP validation, DNA was analysed from independently sampled plant material representing each selected BC2F7 line. Each DNA sample was genotyped for the five target SNP loci, with two leaf samples from each individual plant of each replication representing the biological experimental unit. SNP genotyping was used to verify the presence of the desired resistance-associated alleles and their fixation within the advanced pyramided lines. This higher-resolution validation complemented the SSR-based screening, reduced the likelihood of retaining lines based on potentially ambiguous or false-positive SSR signals, and enabled more precise identification of lines carrying the intended combinations of resistance loci.

Statistical analysis

Phenotypic segregation for dominant resistance traits was tested against the expected 3:1 resistant ratio, whereas recessive tungro resistance was tested against a 1:3 resistant ratio. Genotypic segregation at each marker locus was tested against the expected 1:2:1 homozygous resistant: heterozygous susceptible ratio, with locus-specific interpretation for the recessive tsv1 allele. Chi-square (χ2) goodness-of-fit tests were used to compare observed and expected frequencies, and deviations from the expected segregation ratios were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.A comprehensive suite of statistical methods, including correlation analysis, clustering, PCA, heatmap, and radar-plot was utilized to elucidate the agronomic performance as manifested by morphological characteristics. Agronomic traits of the BC2F7 lines were analysed using analysis of variance appropriate for a randomized complete block design with three replications. For plant-level agronomic traits, measurements from the ten sampled plants within each plot were averaged to obtain a single plot-level value. The plot was treated as the experimental unit, resulting in three independent plot observations per genotype corresponding to the three RCBD replications. Individual plants within a plot were treated as subsamples and were not considered independent replicates in the ANOVA. The BC2F7 materials were homozygous, non-segregating advanced breeding lines evaluated using standardized phenotypic measurements under uniform experimental conditions; together with the use of plot-level means, these factors contributed to the relatively low within-genotype residual variation and consequently low CV values. Genotype and replication effects were tested for each trait. When genotype effects were significant, overall mean separation among genotypes was performed using the least significant difference (LSD) test at p < 0.05. Because BRRI dhan29 was the recurrent parent and served as the principal control for evaluating agronomic performance, Dunnett's multiple-comparison test was additionally performed to compare each advanced line directly with BRRI dhan29 while controlling the family-wise error rate at α = 0.05. The LSD groupings are presented for general comparisons among genotypes, whereas the Dunnett-adjusted comparisons were used specifically to determine whether individual advanced lines differed significantly from BRRI dhan29. Coefficients of variation were calculated as CV (%) = [√(MSerror)/grand mean] × 100, where MSerror represents the residual mean square from the RCBD ANOVA. Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.2.3. The ‘metan’ and ‘ggplot2’ packages within R facilitated correlation analysis, while clustering and principal component analysis (PCA), heatmap, and radar-plot were conducted using the ‘FactoMineR’, ‘factoextra’, and ‘ggplot2’ packages.
