Introduction

According to the latest 2022 Global Cancer Statistics report released in 2024, breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy in women [1]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is a key treatment for locally advanced breast cancer, aiming to reduce tumor size preoperatively to facilitate surgery [2]. Studies [3, 4, 5, 6] confirm that patients who achieve a pathological complete response (pCR) after NAC show obvious short‐term benefits. Thus, early, accurate, and noninvasive prediction of pCR is important in breast cancer treatment due to the key role of NAC [7, 8].

Diffusion‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) is a noninvasive tool for probing tumor microstructures, where the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) is the most commonly used quantitative metric [9, 10]. However, during treatments, including NAC, the evolution of ADC is typically influenced by changes in multiple pathologic and microstructural features with competing effects [11, 12]. Essentially, ADC represents nonspecific diffusivity within a voxel [13]. Therefore, conventional MRI examinations and the corresponding quantitative analyses show limited effectiveness at predicting pCR [14, 15], and there is a need for a more sensitive imaging method to capture specific pathological changes during NAC.

MR cytometry provides a novel approach for microstructural imaging of in vivo tissues, which incorporates multiple diffusion times tdiff into conventional diffusion‐weighted protocols with multiple b‐values [16, 17]. This enables the quantitative estimation of specific microstructural features, including the cell diameter d, intracellular volume fraction vin, and cellularity ρ [18, 19]. Previous laboratory investigations, including numerical simulation, in vitro cell experiments, and in vivo animal experiments, have further evaluated the accuracy of MR cytometry in reconstructing microstructural features [17, 20, 21]. Moreover, the clinical feasibility of MR cytometry has been validated in multiple tumor studies, including breast cancer [18], prostate cancer [19], gliomas [22], endometrial cancer [23], and thyroid cancer [24]. Particularly for breast cancer, a series of studies have demonstrated the diagnostic efficacy of MR in distinguishing benign from malignant tumors [25, 26], as well as in identifying their molecular subtypes [27]. In addition, a recent prospective study based on pretreatment baseline data preliminarily demonstrated the clinical performance of MR cytometry in predicting pCR [28].

However, there is a major limitation in the most commonly used MR cytometry method, that is, IMPULSED (Imaging microstructural parameters using limited spectrally edited diffusion):dMRI signals are assumed as arising separately from intracellular and extracellular compartments, while the transcytolemmal (crossing cell membrane) water exchange was neglected. The previous study has indicated that this simplification leads to biased model fitting, particularly the underestimation of the intracellular volume fraction and cell density [29]. Notably, cell membrane permeability varies considerably in tumors, becoming more pronounced during therapeutic responses. Such an impermeability assumption in IMPULSED leads to the loss of important information regarding changes in membrane permeability or water exchange rate, resulting in inaccurate and delayed prediction of pCR [13].

The recently developed methods, that is, JOINT and EXCHANGE (water Exchange, Confined, and Hindered diffusion under Arbitrary Gradient waveform Encodings), overcome this limitation by incorporating transcytolemmal water exchange into biophysical modeling [20, 21]. The Kärger model was integrated into the IMPULSED framework in JOINT, enabling the derivation of a new expression that additionally describes the signal evolution induced by water exchange [20]. EXCHANGE further relaxed the assumptions made in JOINT and provided a more comprehensive description of the multiple diffusion modes within tumors [21, 23]. Simulations and cell experiments demonstrated that the introduction of water exchange improves the accuracy of parameter estimation, while animal experiments confirmed that a specific metric, the water exchange rate constant, can provide timely feedback on membrane permeability changes during tumor treatment.

Recently, artificial intelligence‐based approaches, including machine learning models and large language model frameworks, have shown promise in integrating clinical and imaging data for predicting NAC response [24, 25]. These data‐driven approaches complement biophysical models like MR cytometry by capturing complex, nonlinear relationships in high‐dimensional data. The current study focuses specifically on the biophysical modeling of diffusion MRI data, which offers the advantage of providing interpretable microstructural parameters directly linked to tumor biology.

Currently, the clinical value of the advanced MR cytometry quantitative models that integrated transcytolemmal water exchange remains unclear in predicting the efficacy of breast cancer treatment. Therefore, this prospective study aims to apply different MR cytometry methods, especially JOINT and EXCHANGE, in breast tumor imaging to predict pCR after NAC, then to validate the clinical significance of incorporating transcytolemmal water exchange into biophysical modeling, thereby providing valuable insights for model selection in future breast cancer imaging studies.

Materials and Methods

Patient Characteristics

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Medical Ethics Committee of Fudan University Affiliated Cancer Hospital, Approval No.: 2410‐Exp087‐KY. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients undergoing NAC for breast cancer were prospectively enrolled from two centers (A and B) between November 2022 and November 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of breast tumor/malignancy by mammography/ultrasound, followed by treatment with NAC; (2) completed conventional MRI examinations and MR acquisitions; (3) no treatment prior to enrollment; (4) pCR and non‐PCR status after NAC confirmed by pathological examination; and (5) no special type of invasive carcinoma confirmed by pathology. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete clinicopathological data; (2) incomplete pre‐ and post‐NAC MRI data; and (3) poor image quality, including image artifacts and low signal‐to‐noise ratios.

All patients received standard NAC regimens determined by the multidisciplinary team based on tumor subtype and stage. For hormone receptor‐positive/HER2‐negative tumors, regimens included anthracycline‐cyclophosphamide followed by taxanes (e.g., doxorubicin plus cyclophosphamide followed by paclitaxel). For HER2‐positive tumors, trastuzumab and pertuzumab were added to the taxane‐based regimen. For triple‐negative breast cancer, carboplatin was added to the anthracycline‐taxane backbone. Dosages were calculated based on body surface area, and all patients completed the prescribed cycles without dose reductions that would affect the analysis.

MRI Data Acquisition

Breast MRI examinations were performed on 3.0 T MRI scanners (MAGNETOM Skyra for Center A and MAGNETOM Prisma for Center B); Siemens Healthineers AG, (Forchheim, Germany) using dedicated 16‐channel phased‐array breast coils. The protocol consisted of pulsed gradient spin‐echo (PGSE) and oscillating gradient spin‐echo (OGSE) sequences, with OGSE diffusion frequencies set to 17/33 Hz in Center A and 25/50 Hz in Center B. MRI scans were performed both prior to NAC and after two cycles of treatment. The acquisition parameters are presented in Table S1. Routine clinical sequences were also implemented, including T2‐weighted imaging and T1‐weighted dynamic contrast‐enhanced (DCE) gradient‐echo imaging. All other MRI scans were performed before DCE scans.

Data Preprocessing and Region of Interest (ROI)

Diffusion‐weighted images of PGSE, OGSE‐17/25 Hz, and OGSE‐33/50 Hz sequences were preprocessed in MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Image alignment was performed using built‐in imregtform and imwarp functions, with the intermediate‐frequency OGSE sequence (17 or 25 Hz) used as the reference template as exhibiting the least misalignment relative to the other sequences. Marchenko‐Pastur principal component analysis was applied for MR image denoising. The effect of intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) was eliminated by refitting the nondiffusion‐weighted signals through log‐linear fitting of the signals of 200 < b < 1000 s/mm2.

Images were loaded into the ITK‐SNAP software (Version 3.8, www.itksnap.org), and ROIs were manually delineated on OGSE‐17 or 25 Hz images with the highest b‐value by two radiologists (S.Y.S. and J.D.) with 5 and 10 years of experience, respectively, who were blinded to the pathological results. For each lesion, a 3D ROI was manually delineated slice‐by‐slice to cover the entire tumor volume, targeting the most diffusion restricted areas (hypointense on ADC maps) that reflect the highest cellularity. Intra‐tumoral regions of necrosis, liquefaction, and hemorrhage were deliberately retained within the ROIs. The Dice similarity coefficient was calculated to assess the inter‐observer agreement. The final ROIs were determined by the more experienced radiologist (J.D.). A flow chart of data processing is shown in Figure 1.

MR Cytometry Methods and Data Fitting

The microstructural parameters were fitted based on three biophysical models: IMPULSED, JOINT, and EXCHANGE. Their schematic representations are summarized in Figure 2. The IMPULSED‐derived parameters include cell diameter d, intracellular volume fraction vin, extracellular diffusivity Dex, and cell density ρ. The intracellular signal is modeled as Sin=exp−b·ADCr, where ADCr is the apparent restricted diffusion coefficient, a function of the intracellular intrinsic diffusivity Din and cell diameter d. EXCHANGE (13): This method is also based on the Kärger model and IMPULSED framework, but relaxes JOINT's assumption that water exchange only influences PGSE signals. Specifically, a two‐mode diffusion model is proposed to describe the actual intracellular diffusion, accounting for both restricted and hindered diffusion in the presence of water exchange. In addition, a dimensional analysis‐based expression was used to correct the restriction‐induced edge enhancement effect, and a discretization‐based computational framework was proposed to accommodate arbitrary gradient waveforms. Further details on EXCHANGE are provided in the Appendix S15–S27 and Figure S1.

All three models were fitted using the data‐fitting platform MATI [30]. The image‐derived cellularity ρ was calculated as vin/d×100, based on the established methodology [19]. Din was fixed at 1.56 μm2/ms in the data analysis to stabilize the fitting outcomes as reported previously [18]. In addition, the ADC metrics corresponding to different acquisition sequences (including PGSE, OGSE‐17/25 Hz, and OGSE‐33/50 Hz), that is, time‐dependent ADCs, were calculated by fitting the multi‐b‐value signals to S=exp−b·ADC.

Pathological Analysis

A pathologist W.T.Y. with 15 years of experience performed the pathological diagnoses and quantitative analyses of hematoxylin and eosin (HE)‐stained sections from formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded breast tumor tissues. High‐resolution digital images (40× magnification) of the sections were obtained using a panoramic digital scanning system (Ningbo Jiangfeng Biological Information Technology Co. Ltd.). From the total cohort of 209 patients, HE slides from 69 patients were randomly selected for analysis in a 1:3 ratio. The nuclei in H&E‐stained images were segmented using CellPose, a generalist deep learning based model pretrained on diverse microscopy images encompassing various cell types and imaging conditions [31, 32]. The volume‐weighted mean nuclear diameter was calculated using the following formula: dmean=∑ndn4/∑ndn3, where n is the number of CellPose‐identified cells, and dn is the size of the segmented cell nucleus. Subsequently, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to quantify the correlation between the image‐derived cell diameter d and above pathology‐derived nuclear size dmean. A strong correlation (the correlation coefficient r > 0.7) between them would support the rationality of MR cytometry imaging.

The therapeutic response to NAC was evaluated postoperatively using the Miller‐Payne (MP) grading system [33]. By comparing prechemotherapy biopsy specimens with postoperative surgical specimens, the response was categorized into five grades: G1 (MP1), defined as little or no reduction in tumor cellularity; G2 (MP2), defined as ≤ 30% reduction in tumor cellularity; G3 (MP3), defined as a 30%–90% reduction in tumor cellularity; G4 (MP4), defined as > 90% reduction in tumor cellularity; and G5 (MP5), defined as no residual invasive cancer in the tumor bed (ductal carcinoma in situ may be present). Grades G1–G4 were defined as nonpathological complete response (non‐pCR), whereas pCR was defined as the absence of invasive carcinoma at the primary breast site (in situ carcinoma was allowed) and negative regional lymph nodes.

Statistical Analysis

For each patient's data, three time‐dependent ADC metrics and model‐fitted microstructural parameters were calculated for each voxel and averaged over the entire ROI. The variation in each imaging metric between the two time points, that is, the baseline prior to NAC serving as the first time point and two cycles after NAC treatment serving as the second time point, was further calculated as ∆u=utime point2−utime point1 (u represents all relevant imaging parameter, such as d, vin, etc.). For the two centers, the following statistical analyses were independent of each other. The Mann–Whitney U test, that is, rank‐sum test, was used to evaluate the significance of intergroup differences between patients in the pCR and non‐pCR groups. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the clinical performance of single and combined imaging metrics (and their change between the two time points) in predicting pCR. For multivariate logistic regression analysis, the regression models were constructed based on the results of time‐dependent ADCs, IMPULSED, JOINT, and EXCHANGE, respectively; furthermore, the combined models incorporating MR cytometry methods and ADC measurements, that is, IMPULSED‐ADC, JOINT‐ADC, EXCHANGE‐ADC, were also constructed. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify the diagnostic efficacy of each regression model. To prevent overfitting in multivariate logistic regression, a hybrid method combining Lasso and Ridge regressions was used for feature selection [34, 35]. The number of features included in a multivariate model should be less than 1/20 of the sample size to ensure no overfitting, that is, up to six features for Center A and three features for Center B. DeLong's test was used to compare the ROCs of different regression models. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

Figure 4 presented the feature mappings at two time points from two representative patients with breast tumors who underwent NAC. The patients were recruited from Centers A and B, and the treatment responses were pathologically confirmed as pCR and non‐pCR. Regardless of the pCR status, ADC metrics increased with decreasing diffusion time (ADCPGSE < ADC17Hz < ADC33 Hz, ADCPGSE < ADC25Hz < ADC50Hz).

Tables 2 and 3 and Figure 5 showed the statistical results of the intergroup comparisons between the pCR and non‐PCR groups. For Center A, the three ADC metrics at the time point 2, along with the change in ADCPGSE and ADC33 Hz, demonstrated significant differences between the pCR and non‐pCR groups: the values of ADCPGSE, ADC17Hz, ADC33 Hz at time point 2, ΔADCPGSE, and ΔADC33 Hz were all significantly higher for pCR. Diagnostic Performance of Imaging Metrics

Figure 6a illustrated the results of multivariate regression analysis of the data from Center A. The models based on time‐dependent ADC metrics and the three MR cytometry methods (IMPULSED, JOINT, and EXCHANGE) yielded AUC values of 0.709, 0.716, 0.747, and 0.745, respectively. Combining ADC analysis and a specific MR cytometry method can yield a higher AUC; specifically, the combined models IMPULSED‐ADC, JOINT‐ADC, and EXCHANGE‐ADC improved the AUCs to 0.737, 0.761, and 0.758, respectively. The Delong test indicated that only the ROC curves of the IMPULSED and JOINT‐ADC models showed significant differences. All other pairwise comparisons were insignificant (p = 0.061–0.970), with the highest significance level observed between the IMPULSED and JOINT models. The detailed results and comparisons were presented in Table S2.

ROC curves of multivariate logistic regression models for predicting pathological complete response of breast cancer.

Figure 6b showed the similar results obtained from Center B. Multivariate regression models for time‐dependent ADC, IMPULSED, JOINT, and EXCHANGE obtained AUC values of 0.777, 0.809, 0.846, and 0.864, respectively. The combined models achieved AUC values of 0.818, 0.860, and 0.868 for the IMPULSED‐ADC, JOINT‐ADC, and EXCHANGE‐ADC, respectively. The ROC curves of the time‐dependent ADCs and EXCHANGE‐ADC models showed significant differences. All other pairwise comparisons were insignificant (p = 0.169–0.922), with the highest significance level observed between the time‐dependent ADCs and JOINT‐ADC models. The detailed results and comparisons were presented in Table S3.

The predictive performance of the same method under different acquisition protocols was also compared, as shown in Figure 6c, but the results of the paired comparisons were all insignificant: for IMPULSED, AUC = 0.716 from 17/33 Hz versus 0.809 from 25/50 Hz with p = 0.208; for JOINT, AUC = 0.747 from 17/33 Hz versus 0.846 from 25/50 Hz with p = 0.214; for EXCHANGE, AUC = 0.745 from 17/33 Hz versus 0.864 from 25/50 Hz with p = 0.053.

Discussion

This prospective study demonstrated that the MR‐cytometry‐derived microstructural parameters vin, ρ, and kin were relatively more sensitive to the changes in tumor microenvironment during NAC. The observed decrease in vin and ρ in the pCR group reflected several concurrent biological processes during successful chemotherapy: (1) direct tumor cell death leading to reduced cell density; (2) cell shrinkage due to apoptosis; and (3) replacement of cellular tumor tissue with fibrotic or necrotic material. Notably, vin decreased more prominently in the pCR group compared to non‐pCR, suggesting that the changes in intracellular volume fraction may be served as an early indicator of treatment efficacy. The increase in d observed in some pCR patients may appear counterintuitive, as successful chemotherapy is expected to shrink cells. However, this phenomenon can be explained by selective killing of smaller, more proliferative cancer cells, leaving behind larger, more differentiated cells or inflammatory cells that are more resistant to chemotherapy. This interpretation is consistent with the previous histopathological observations that surviving cells in responding tumors typically showed increased nuclear size and atypia, exhibiting clinical potential as new biomarkers for monitoring the efficacy of NAC and predicting pCR. On the other hand, although the recently developed EXCHANGE and JOINT models have provided higher predictive performances for pCR, their improvements over IMPULSED were insignificant based on the current data and sample sizes.

IMPULSED assumes that cells are impermeable spheres, and ignores the water exchange across cell membranes, resulting in an underestimation of vin. However, transcytolemmal water exchange is typically associated with tumor malignancy and cellular metabolic activity, and has been shown to serve as a biomarker for disease status at the cellular level [21, 29]. JOINT and EXCHANGE enable the detection and quantification of water exchange between intra‐ and extracellular compartments [30], such diffusion‐based information can indirectly reflect the cell membrane permeability. Monitoring changes in permeability before and after NAC treatment using MR cytometry may help to assess early treatment responses. This study observed trends in the changes of permeability during NAC for breast cancer. These findings highlighted the necessity of incorporating transcytolemmal water exchange into biophysical modeling, especially since the membrane permeability usually changes before and after treatment. Thus, the JOINT and EXCHANGE models hold promising potential for evaluating early treatment response, but this requires further validation in future studies.

The results of the Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated good agreement between the cell diameters calculated using the three MR cytometry methods and the nuclear sizes obtained from histopathological examinations. However, there were also differences in the fitted d values obtained from different methods. A prior methodological study using the joint acquisition of PGSE and OGSE‐25/50 Hz showed that JOINT tended to overestimate cell diameter, EXCHANGE typically underestimated it, and IMPULSED provided the estimates closest to the ground truth [20, 21]. In this work, the results obtained for Center B (OGSE‐25/50 Hz) were consistent with the above findings: when sorted by fitted d values from smallest to largest, the three methods ranked as follows: EXCHANGE, IMPULSED, and JOINT; however, for Center A (OGSE‐17/33 Hz), the diameters obtained from EXCHANGE were the largest. We speculate that this inconsistency in cell diameter estimation bias may be attributable to differences in acquisition protocols, particularly the used frequencies of OGSE. We will investigate such acquisition‐dependency of cell diameter fitting in future reproducibility studies on MR cytometry imaging.

The performance of MR cytometry methods at different OGSE frequencies was further compared for predicting pCR. The results indicated that all three methods performed better when higher OGSE frequencies (25/50 Hz) were used. Although limited by the sample size, no significant differences can be observed; the current comparative results still highlighted the potential advantages of higher‐frequency MR cytometry acquisition.

The two acquisition protocols (17/33 Hz for Center A and 25/50 Hz for Center B) were implemented to explore the feasibility and potential advantages of higher‐frequency OGSE acquisitions. Since the centers utilized different protocols, all statistical analyses were performed independently within each center. The comparison of model performance between different protocols was presented as an exploratory analysis, to generate foundation and hypotheses for further investigations. Future studies could improve the pCR prediction by integrating MR cytometry‐derived microstructural parameters with artificial intelligence approaches, which have shown great promise in integrating multimodal medical data [24, 25]. For example, machine learning models could combine MR cytometry parameters with clinical features (tumor subtype, stage), radiomic features, and genomic data to build more accurate and robust predictive models.

Limitations

First, there was an obvious difference in the number of cases between the two centers. A multicenter study with a more balanced sample size distribution could provide more reliable conclusions. Second, the patients who were scanned under the two acquisition protocols (17/33 and 25/50 Hz) were recruited from two centers, resulting in potential patient differences in the comparison between the two protocols. In the future, such differences could be avoided by recruiting the same cohort of volunteers or patients and arranging them to undergo scans using the different protocols. Finally, statistical analysis was conducted based on the whole‐tumor ROI averaging, which neglected inter‐tumoral heterogeneity. Histograms and radiomic features of microstructural parameter mapping should be investigated and incorporated into regression models to capture the full complexity of tumors.

Conclusion

In this prospective study, the performance of different MR cytometry methods in predicting pCR after NAC was evaluated and compared. The results from both centers indicated that the JOINT and EXCHANGE models, which integrate transcytolemmal water exchange, obtained higher AUC values compared to the impermeable IMPULSED model and traditional ADC measurements. Furthermore, the predictive performance can be improved by combining MR cytometry parameters with time‐dependent ADCs. These findings demonstrated the clinical potential of such advanced imaging methods in the early monitoring of breast cancer treatment and provide valuable insights into tumor imaging and prognostic assessment.
