Exploring the potential of large language models for assessing medication adherence to the ESC heart failure guidelines
Authors: Noman Dormosh, Machteld Boonstra, Ameen Abu-Hanna, Folkert W Asselbergs, Iacer Calixto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate large language models (LLMs) for automating the assessment of clinician adherence to ESC heart failure pharmacotherapy guidelines.Materials and MethodsWe used electronic health records (EHRs) data pertaining to hospitalized heart failure patients. The task was to assess whether discharge medications followed the guidelines. We labeled each record as: (1) all recommended medications are present and target doses achieved; (2) all recommended medications are present, but target doses not achieved; (3) one or more recommended medications are missing. We evaluated three general-domain (GLM-4-9B-chat, Llama3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2) and three medical-specific (Med42-v2-8B, Llama-3-8B-UltraMedical, OpenBioLLM-8B) open-source LLMs under different prompt settings (zero-shot, few-shot and Chain-of-Thought). We fine-tuned the models using synthesized preference data from our EHR data with the Monolithic Preference Optimization without Reference Model (ORPO) method. We performed a learning curve analysis to determine optimal training data size for performance. We assessed LLM performance using the macro F1 score.ResultsWe included data of 1,141 patients. Adherence to medication and doses was 5.3%. All LLMs scored F1 < 0.40 across most prompt settings (baseline F1 = 0.333). After fine-tuning, four LLMs scored F1 ≥ 0.90; the other two LLMs namely Llama3-8B-Instruct and OpenBioLLM-8B scored F1 = 0.794 and 0.787, respectively. GLM-4-9B-Chat reached peak performance with 40% of the training data, while Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 required 50%. Other models needed more data.ConclusionTask-specific fine-tuning of LLMs is necessary for optimal performance, and selecting the appropriate LLM for this is important. Without fine-tuning, both general-domain and medical-specific LLMs performed close to random guessing, revealing key limitations in their adaptability to specialized tasks. Medical-specific LLMs showed no clear advantage over general-domain LLMs.Keywords: large language models, natural language processing, clinical guidelines, preference optimization, electronic health records

Introduction

Clinical guidelines serve as invaluable tools for healthcare professionals, offering evidence-based recommendations for the diagnosis, treatment, and management of various medical conditions.1 In the domain of heart failure, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) provides comprehensive guidelines for the management of heart failure,2,3 hereafter referred to as ESC-HF. The ESC-HF guidelines aid healthcare professionals in their decision-making to optimize symptom control, prevent disease progression, and reduce the burden of heart failure in both patients and healthcare systems.

Within this multifaceted guidance described in the ESC-HF guidelines, an important aspect centers on medication treatment, encompassing intricate considerations such as drug interactions, potential side effects and the existence of co-morbidities. Adherence to the ESC-HF medication treatment is expected to decrease hospitalization rate and improve patient outcomes.4,5 However, manually assessing general medication adherence is both labor-intensive and prone to errors, particularly given the evolving nature of the ESC-HF guidelines, which undergo updates over time. Automating the assessment of guideline adherence can help pinpoint areas where adherence to ESC-HF guidelines is lacking, therefore increasing efficiency, real-time monitoring and improve patient care.6

Recent advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP), particularly the rise of Large Language Models (LLMs), have opened doors to novel opportunities and applications in health care.7,8 LLMs are generally pretrained on massive amounts of textual data to learn general language constructs (including grammar, syntax, and semantics), and subsequently fine-tuned on instructions for multiple tasks and to mimic human preferences.9 They have exhibited non-trivial reasoning capabilities,10,11 and have demonstrated several competencies and on wide range of tasks like answering medical questions,12 summarizing clinical text,13 and analyzing medical images.14 However, LLMs are inherently limited to the knowledge gained during training even if they have been instruction-tuned on multiple tasks, and can struggle with tasks outside their specific training scope. For example, applying an LLM directly to assess patient medication adherence to the ESC-HF guidelines is likely to be ineffective due to the model lacking (1) knowledge of the ESC-HF guidelines and of (2) the necessary medical context and vocabulary. To effectively assess clinicians’ adherence to ESC-HF medication treatments using LLMs, we must thus provide the LLM with this domain knowledge.

Several approaches can be used to leverage LLMs with specific domain knowledge. One common method is in-context learning, where the LLM is provided with task instructions in natural language either without any labelled examples (zero-shot) or with one or few example–answer pairs (one-shot and few-shot).15 Another approach is Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting,16 which guides the LLM to generate intermediate reasoning steps before delivering a final response. Even though these techniques are relatively easy to implement, as they do not require large amounts of training data or involve fine-tuning the model’s weights, the result for more complex tasks is suboptimal. For complex tasks, supervised fine-tuning is necessary,17,18 which involves training the model utilizing task-specific datasets, typically containing hundreds or thousands of annotated examples. However, fine-tuning entails both extensive computational costs and requires access to high quality data, which is often not available.

Another option to improve LLM performance for complex tasks is retrieval augmented generation (RAG).19 In RAG, relevant (parts of) one or more documents are retrieved from a database, and then this retrieved content would be included as part of input to the LLM so an answer can be generated taking that information into account. However, applying RAG presents several challenges because it is difficult to ensure that the retrieval process consistently identifies the most relevant and contextually appropriate sections of the ESC-HF guidelines, which could lead to incomplete or misleading inputs for the LLM. This makes it hard to isolate whether LLM performance reflects the reasoning capabilities of the LLM or simply the quality of the retrieved content.

This study explores the potential of LLMs to automate the evaluation of adherence to the ESC-HF guidelines, specifically focusing on medication therapy. These guidelines can be viewed as a set of standardized rules or decision criteria that define the appropriate use of medications in the treatment of heart failure. Although such rule-based assessments could, in principle, be implemented through traditional programming, our aim is to evaluate how well LLMs can handle this task without relying on explicitly coded rules by interpreting clinical input and applying guideline logic through language understanding. We employ a comparative approach and evaluate the performance of several open-source LLMs in multiple in-context learning settings. Additionally, we fine-tune the LLMs on a synthesized dataset derived from electronic health records (EHRs) data to enhance their performance in this specific task and investigate how model performance varies as a function of the number of training examples.

Materials and methods

Study design

We employed a comparative approach to investigate the potential of LLMs for assessing medication adherence to ESC-HF guidelines. We evaluated the performance of several open-source LLMs under different settings and prompting techniques including zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting, to assess their ability to perform this complex task. Additionally, we compared their performance after fine-tuning them for preference optimization on a synthesized dataset derived from EHR data, and investigated the number of training data points required to achieve optimal performance.

Data source, clinical setting and patients

We used EHR data pertaining to individuals having a diagnosis for heart failure who were admitted to the Amsterdam UMC, an academic tertiary care hospital in Amsterdam in the Netherlands, between 2015 and 2023. Patient data contained demographics, diagnoses, medication use, and free text notes. We included only the most recent hospitalization for each patient who had a valid left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) value and a primary diagnosis of heart failure.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local institutional ethics review board (Medical Ethics Review Committee of Amsterdam UMC, protocol number 2023.0154).

Data extraction and pre-processing

Patient data consisted of both structured and unstructured data. The structured data included demographics, namely age and sex; physiological measurements, including systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, pulse, pulse oximetry, and body mass index (BMI); diagnoses using the ICD-10 classification,20 which we grouped into broader categories (see Supplementary Appendix A); and discharge medications. In cases with multiple measurements, we extracted the one closest to discharge, as the focus of this study was on discharge medications, and the most recent measurement reflects the patient’s condition at discharge. LVEF, an important element for classifying heart failure patients, was extracted from structured data (echocardiography) and complemented with values extracted from unstructured data (letters) using regular expressions. In all cases, the lowest available LVEF was used for classification.

Creation of ground truth/gold standard labels

Adherence to heart failure pharmacotherapy was defined according to the ESC guidelines, initially published in 20212 and updated with a focused revision in 2023.3 Patients were classified as having heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) if their LVEF was ≤40%, heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) if their LVEF was between 41% and 49%, and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) if their LVEF was ≥50%. To minimize potential misclassification of heart failure subtype, we classified patients based on the lowest LVEF recorded at any time prior to discharge, incorporating measurements from both previous hospitalizations and outpatient evaluations, as LVEF may improve with medical therapy. The analysis focused on guideline-directed pharmacotherapies carrying Class I, Level of Evidence A recommendations for patients with HFrEF, HFmrEF, and (HFpEF). For HFrEF patients, the guidelines recommend a quadruple therapy regimen, which includes angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE-I), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), or angiotensin receptor- neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI); beta-blockers; mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA); and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2-i), all titrated to the maximum tolerated dose. For HFmrEF and HFpEF patients, the guidelines recommends having SGLT2 inhibitors in the treatment plan.

We created a predefined set of rules and automatically labeled patient records based on clinician adherence to medication therapy according to the ESC-HF guidelines (described above). Each record was assigned one of three labels:

all the recommended medications are present and target doses are achieved.

all the recommended medications are present and target doses are achieved.

all the recommended medications are present, but target doses are not achieved.

all the recommended medications are present, but target doses are not achieved.

one or more recommended medications are missing.

one or more recommended medications are missing.

Prompt synthesis

Typically, a LLM receives a prompt consisting of an instruction (task description) followed by the input data, and is prompted to generate an answer. To further guide the LLM, demonstrations (also called shots) can be provided consisting of labeled examples where the input and the desired outcome are presented to the LLM. This is shown to the LLM before the new input is entered in the prompt, so the LLM can learn from demonstrations and hopefully generate better answers.

Figure 1 illustrates the structure of the synthesized prompts we used. For each medical record, we synthesized 11 different prompts. Zero-shot: One prompt was in a zero-shot setting, ie, without demonstrations. Zero-shot + CoT: The second prompt was in a zero-shot setting but used Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, by including the phrase “Let’s think step by step.” at the end of the prompt. Form template: The third prompt included a predefined form assessment template (see Supplementary Appendix B) to assist the LLM. Distilled information: The fourth prompt provided task-specific information and instructions distilled from relevant sections of the guidelines to the LLM for assessing medication adherence (see Supplementary Appendix C). Raw guideline context: The fifth prompt included relevant textual sections from the guidelines (see Supplementary Appendix D). Few-shot (with and without CoT): The remaining six prompts were in a few- shot setting (n = 3), where n demonstrations were given in the prompt to enable in-context learning. Of these, three prompts included CoT intermediate reasoning steps before reaching the final answer. The template that we used to synthesize the CoT from the medical records is detailed in Supplementary Appendix E. Demonstrations (shots or examples) were randomly sampled from the medical records. To mitigate LLMs’ bias toward a particular label,21 we randomly shuffled the definitions of labels (always shown in lexicographic order) for all demonstrations as well as for the example we prompt the LLM to solve.

The structure of the prompts synthesized from the medical records.

Large language models and model configuration

We employed a range of open-source LLMs in our study, including general-domain LLMs such as Llama-3-8B-Instruct,22 Mistral-0.2-7B-Instruct,23 and GLM-4-9B-Chat.24 We also used medical-specific LLMs, including Llama-3-8B-UltraMedical,25 Med42-v2-8B,26 and OpenBioLLM-8B.27 Proprietary LLMs were not viable options for our study due to privacy constraints associated with handling EHR data.

We used the vLLM package in all experiments,28 and use a temperature of 0 when sampling outputs to obtain deterministic and reproducible results

Fine-tuning of large language models

When enough data is available, fine-tuning is often used as it leads to much better performance compared to zero- or few- shot in-context learning.29–31 Thus, we further fine-tuned all the LLMs considered earlier using the Monolithic Preference Optimization without Reference Model (ORPO).32 ORPO works by directly optimizing the odds ratio between favored and disfavored generations during supervised fine-tuning, without the need for an additional reference model.

To perform ORPO fine-tuning, we created a dataset with three columns: prompt, accepted output, and rejected output. The prompt column included the instruction, the medical record, and answer options (A, B, C). The accepted output column featured our (ground-truth) synthesized CoT as described earlier, while the rejected output column contained the LLM’s response when prompted before fine-tuning (using the form assessment template described further in Supplementary Appendix B). During fine-tuning, the model learns to penalize the generation of rejected outputs and increase the likelihood of the accepted outputs.

We fine-tuned ORPO with Low-Rank Adaptation33 as implemented in the Llamafactory framework.34 The LORA hyperparameters were set to rank (lora rank = 16), alpha (lora alpha = 32), and dropout (lora dropout = 0.05), while the ORPO hyperparameters were set to beta (beta = 0.1), learning rate (learning rate = 8 × 10−6), warmup steps (warmup steps = 10), a linear scheduler (lr scheduler type =“linear”), and 3 epochs (num train epochs = 3).

Evaluation

We divided the dataset (medical records and their corresponding synthesized prompts) into two parts: 70% for training and 30% for testing. The training set was exclusively used for fine-tuning the LLM, while the test set was strictly reserved for evaluation in all our experiments. To assess the performance of the LLM, we first extracted the labels (A, B, or C) from the LLM output using regular expressions. If the output did not explicitly include one of the labels (A, B, or C), we assigned a random label. We assessed LLM performance using the macro F1 score, which computes the average of per-class F1 scores, ie, it gives equal weight to each class regardless of its frequency in the dataset.

Learning curve analysis

We conducted a learning curve analysis to determine the optimal number of training data points needed to fine-tune the LLM for best performance. We started by using 10% of the training data, then increased the training set size in 10% increments up to 100% of the training set (total of 10 steps). For each increment, we fine- tuned the LLM with the current training subset and evaluated its performance on the same full test set. We used the same hyperparameters as described earlier.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The study cohort comprised 13,734 patients (33,526 admissions). Within this cohort, 3,722 patients were identified with heart failure as the primary diagnosis. Patients whom had missing LVEF values (n = 2,581) were excluded, leaving 1,141 patients for the analysis. Among these, LVEF values were obtained from structured echocardiography data in 659 patients and from unstructured notes in 879 patients, with 397 patients having LVEF available from both sources. Concordance in HF subtype classification between structured and note-derived LVEF data was observed in 298 of these 397 patients (75%).

Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the included patients. The median age was 72.9 IQR [63.4,80.2], with a majority being male (50.5%). Of the patients, 61.9% had HFrEF, 15.1% had HFmrEF, while 23.0% had HFpEF. The median length of stay was 6 days IQR 6.0 [2.0,12.0]. The median number of discharge medications was 11 IQR [8.0,14.0]. The most prevalent conditions were valvular disease (42.7%), hypertension (42.5%), and ischemic heart disease (41.8%).

Performance of the LLMs

Figure 2 presents a comparative analysis of the employed LLMs across various prompt settings, and Table 3 highlights the best- performing LLM in each setting (see Appendix F for all performance metrics). Overall, all the LLMs exhibited relatively low F1 scores (below 0.4) in the standard prompt settings despite the inclusion of demonstrations to improve in- context learning. Providing the adherence form template within the prompt yielded marginal F1 score improvements for certain LLMs. Likewise, the use of CoT prompting enhanced F1 scores, with a positive correlation between the number of provided examples (ie, 1-shot-CoT, 2-shot-CoT, and 3-shot-CoT) and the resulting F1 score for most of the LLMs (see Figure 3). After fine-tuning, all the LLMs showed substantial improvement in performance compared to their results in other settings, albeit the performance of Llama-3-8B-Instruct and OpenBioLLM-8B remained suboptimal relative to the other LLMs. GLM-4-9B-Chat achieved the highest F1 score of 0.989, followed by Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 (0.977) and Llama-3-8B-UltraMedical (0.936). Supplementary Appendix G shows the percentage of instances where the labels were randomly assigned. Almost all cases of random label assignment occurred with OpenBioLLM-8B.

Learning curve analysis

The learning curves of the fine-tuned LLMs across various sample sizes are illustrated in Figure 4. We observed a general trend of performance improvement with increasing training data for most the LLMs. However, medical-specific LLMs tended to need more training samples to increase the performance compared with general-domain LLMs. Llama-3-8B-Instruct deviated from this trend, with its performance plateauing early and remaining consistently lower than that of the other LLMs. GLM-4-9B-Chat and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 needed 10% (80 samples) of the training data to surpass their performance before fine-tuning in all settings, and about 50% (400 samples) of the data to reach peak performance.

Discussion

Main finding

In this study, we explored the potential of LLMs for automating the assessment of medication adherence based on the ESC-HF guidelines. Our goal was to evaluate whether LLMs, without explicitly programmed rules, can effectively interpret clinical cases and apply guideline-based decision logic. We evaluated various open-source LLMs using different prompting techniques and fine-tuned them on synthesized instructions derived from EHR data. While all the LLMs demonstrated suboptimal performance across all prompt settings, fine-tuning produced the best results.

Poor in-context learning performance

Our results indicate that both general-domain and medical-specific LLMs performed poorly under standard zero-shot and few-shot prompting, with results no better than- or slightly better than random guessing for most of the LLMs. Providing a few demonstrations did not improve performance, likely due to the task’s complexity or misinterpretation of the task details by the LLMs.15,35 Consistent with literature, CoT prompting did lead to some performance gains compared to standard prompting.36–38 However, these improvements were only observed when demonstrations were provided, with gains proportional to the number of demonstrations used for some models. Additionally, providing a detailed instruction in the form of an assessment template yielded a performance comparable to that achieved with CoT prompting. This finding was also reported by39 who found that detailed illustration of the task solution process to LLMs can improve the performance. Nevertheless, all these improvements are modest compared to the performance gains realized through fine-tuning the LLMs on our specific task.

Improved performance for fine-tuned models

LLM fine-tuning substantially enhanced the performance of the LLMs across the board, with many LLMs achieving F1 scores above 0.9, reinforcing the importance of specialized training in adapting LLMs to domain-specific tasks. However, the extent of improvement after fine-tuning varied across LLMs. Llama3-8B-Instruct, the instruction-tuned variant of Llama3-8B, underperformed relative to other models. This underperformance may reflect limitations in its ability to adapt to the medical domain, likely due to the limited medical knowledge in the underlying Llama3-8B model. Notably, all medical-specific LLMs—despite being based on the same Llama3-8B backbone, outperformed Llama3-8B-Instruct after fine-tuning them with our medical adherence task. These findings underscore the importance of carefully selecting the LLM for fine-tuning. A medical-specific LLM does not necessarily provide a better starting point for fine-tuning for specific medical tasks.

General domain vs medical LLMs

In line with our fine-tuning results, the learning curve analysis indicated that general-domain LLMs are generally better than medical-specific LLMs for task-specific fine-tuning. While most LLMs eventually achieved high performance, they varied in the amount of training data needed. Remarkably, GLM-4-9B-Chat outperformed many other LLMs across many prompt settings and after fine-tuning, while also required the least training data during fine-tuning to excel. This advantage is likely due to its larger parameter size compared to the other models. For our task, we used medical-specific LLMs derived from fine-tuning the base Llama-8B-Instruct (or Llama-8B) model on medical content. These medical-specific LLMs required less training data and achieved better performance compared to the original Llama3 8B-Instruct model. This can be explained by the prior domain-specific fine-tuning, which allowed the models to acquire specialized medical knowledge that enhanced their performance when further fine-tuned for our task. Recent research suggests that the amount of training data required for optimal performance depends on task complexity.31 Our results further suggest that it also varies based on the specific LLM used.

Guidelines complexity and LLM gaps

Assessing the adherence to ESC-HF guidelines for pharmacotherapy is complex and not straightforward. It involves navigating scattered and sometimes implicit information within the guidelines and connecting various elements to reach a conclusion. Therefore, it was not surprising to observe suboptimal performance of the LLMs for this complex task before fine tuning. However, contrary to expectations, the performance of medical-specific LLMs did not align with the anticipated results typically reported for LLMs fine- tuned with domain-specific medical data. Despite being reported as state-of-the-art in various medical benchmarks, these models often fall short in real-world clinical applications, with their results failing to generalize beyond the specific tasks they were trained on. Previous research by40 highlighted the limited performance of medical-specific LLMs across various tasks, including clinical case challenges. Similarly,41 found that fine-tuning OpenBioLLM- 8B for clinical note generation resulted in poor performance, even lower than baseline models. Moreover,42 observed that many state-of-the-art medical-specific LLMs performed at the level of random guessing in medical concepts question answering. Therefore, domain-specific fine-tuning may not be a universal solution for specialized tasks, especially complex or unseen tasks, and there is a need for more rigorous evaluation frameworks in healthcare AI beyond existing benchmarks.

Implications

Our study carries important implications for the development and use of LLMs in healthcare. Based on the findings in the current study, fine-tuning LLMs is crucial for optimizing their performance on specialized and complex tasks. When compared with medical-specific LLMs, general-domain LLMs seem to offer a more flexible foundation for task-specific fine-tuning, both in terms of expected performance and reduced training data requirements. When properly developed and validated, LLMs could be integrated into clinical decision support systems and implemented within EHR systems. This integration could enhance healthcare workflows by providing real-time insights, such as evaluating treatment adherence, identifying patients who may not be receiving optimal care, and offering personalized, guideline-based recommendations directly within the EHR environment.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of the present study is our comprehensive approach to evaluate, compare, and fine-tune various LLMs, including both general-domain and medical-specific LLMs, to highlight their respective advantages. In addition, we demonstrated how synthesized instructions derived from EHR data can be effectively used for fine-tuning and validated this approach with real-world EHR data to ensure its practical relevance.

Our study has several limitations that should be noted. First, we only evaluated LLMs with fewer than 9 billion parameters, which may have constrained performance, as larger models often exhibit better capabilities. Nevertheless, the LLMs selected for our study were within a similar size range, making them suitable for meaningful comparison. Second, while LLMs have demonstrated the capacity to apply prior knowledge to new tasks, the generalizability of our fine-tuned models to other medical adherence tasks such as adherence to different ESC guidelines for various diseases, remains uncertain. Third, our analysis was confined to a single task with a specific prompt structure, leaving open questions about how results might vary with multiple tasks or changes in prompt design (eg, incorporating clinical notes or altering the sequence of elements in medical records). Fourth, although fine-tuning improved the LLMs’ performance on our task, it remains unclear whether the models truly acquired clinically valid reasoning abilities (eg, reasoning with medication names and dosage regimens) or simply learned to reproduce patterns from the training data, including potential artifacts from the synthetically generated CoT reasoning outputs. Fifth, although we relied on the lowest recorded LVEF to minimize misclassification due to improvement over time, some misclassification may persist, particularly among patients with incomplete or infrequent LVEF measurements documented in the EHR. Sixth, temporal mismatches between LVEF measurements and discharge medications may affect adherence labeling accuracy. For instance, patients may be misclassified as HFpEF if the lowest LVEF recorded in the EHR is >50%, even though they were diagnosed with HFrEF and their LVEF improved due to medication use. Finally, our adherence classification did not account for clinically justified deviations, such as medication avoidance due to contraindications, side effects, or physician judgment. As a result, some appropriate clinical decisions may have been misclassified as non-adherence, potentially affecting the observed adherence rates.

Conclusions and future work

Our comparative analysis of LLMs for automating the assessment of clinician adherence to discharge medication therapy in line with ESC-HF guidelines demonstrated that task-specific fine-tuning is essential for the LLMs to achieve optimal performance. The amount of training data required for effective fine-tuning on our task varied among the LLMs, and determining this quantity should be a key focus during the fine-tuning process. Out of the box prompting, even with LLMs pre-trained or fine-tuned for medical tasks, produced results close to random guessing for our task, highlighting critical gaps in their adaptability to specialized healthcare tasks. Medical-specific LLMs did not show a clear advantage over general-domain LLMs.

Future work should investigate the impact of model size and simultaneous fine-tuning on multiple tasks of varying complexity, both in terms of performance and the data points required for optimization. Furthermore, further research is needed to assess the robustness and generalizability of our fine-tuning approach in clinical settings where medical information may be represented differently. For instance, medications may be listed by brand names rather than scientific names, or patient information, including discharge medication details, may be embedded within clinical notes. An important direction for future work is applying LLM-based methods to other clinical guidelines (eg, for diabetes, hypertension) to assess their scalability and adaptability. In addition, future studies could explore leveraging LLMs to incorporate longitudinal patient data and treatment adjustments, providing a more complete view of adherence over time. Finally, we believe future work on the use of RAG techniques can lead to improvements compared to in-context learning methods, and it would be interesting to compare such methods to approaches involving fine-tuning.
