Informing the patient of the placebo option: an ethical necessity likely to reduce inappropriate psychotropic prescriptions?
Authors: Fabrice Berna, Guillaume Durand, Rémy Boussageon, Julien Nizard, François Paille, Laurence Verneuil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing interest in deprescribing drugs highlights the importance of re-evaluating prescriptions, but also of limiting inappropriate prescribing. This paper reflects on the ethical use of a placebo as a way of limiting inappropriate prescribing. We focus on psychotropic drugs, but our reasoning may also concern other medical conditions. We analyze the conditions in which placebos are usually prescribed in medicine and analyze four situations to determine which would be best ethical. Although the use of placebos, whether pure (i.e., containing no active substance) or impure (i.e., containing active substances but used in a non-specific manner), is commonplace in medicine, patients are not duly informed: this practice respects neither patient autonomy nor freedom of choice. In contrast, the use of open-label placebos (i.e., prescribed to the patient in full transparency) solves this ethical problem. Importantly, informing the patient about the placebo does not seem to alter its therapeutic effect. Furthermore, the information given to the patient as part of the shared-medical decision generally does not mention the benefits that a placebo could bring to the patient in place of a pharmacologically active drug. However, scientific data on the efficacy of a placebo is available in randomized controlled trials or meta-analyses for almost all drugs. This effect is sometimes high under specific clinical conditions. We conclude that there is an ethical imperative to inform patients of the efficacy of a placebo in a clinical context where the prescription of a pharmacologically active drug is discussed. The open-label placebo option should become more systematic in medicine, particularly in psychological symptoms or conditions with a well-documented significant placebo effect. Aside from this ethical aspect, prescribing more placebos could be a way of combating overprescription, reducing inappropriate drug use and iatrogenia, and supporting the eco-responsible approach to prescribing in medicine.Keywords: deprescribing, ethics, open-label placebo, placebo, psychotropics

Introduction

The deprescription of medications, particularly psychotropic drugs, has become a subject of interest not only to doctors and patients, but also to policy-makers 1 given the growing consumption of psychotropic drugs and their high rate of inappropriate use.2,3 As deprescribing occurs after these inappropriate prescriptions have been initiated, it is essential to reflect on ways of limiting their onset. We propose here an ethical reflection focusing on the use of placebos in medicine, and seek to show that presenting the placebo option to the patient as part of the shared-medical information could represent a way of reducing inappropriate use of medication.

We argue that if physicians systematically provided patients with scientific data indicating the extent to which their symptoms could be alleviated by either an active drug or a placebo compared to no therapeutic intervention, patients would have fairer information on which to base their decisions. Patients would be informed about the “clinical relevance” of a proposed active drug, not only on its “statistically greater” efficacy compared to placebo. The same is true regarding the kind and level of adverse effects induced by the active drug and the placebo, respectively. However, genuine informed choice is not possible when physicians merely explain the benefits and adverse effects of the active drug they intend to prescribe. This situation typically corresponds to what is mistakenly referred to as “shared decision-making” in contemporary medicine.

Systematically offering transparently prescribed placebos is an ethical implication of recent research on open-label placebos. This research shows that transparently prescribed placebos are as effective as—if not more effective than—placebos administered without informing the patient. 4 Providing systematic information about placebo efficacy and no intervention would enable both patients and physicians to assess more accurately whether the additional benefits of an active drug justify its use, taking the additional adverse effects it may cause into account. In this sense, it could help reduce inappropriate drug prescribing.

We focus here on psychotropic drugs, keeping in mind that our reasoning may also concern other medical conditions in which substantial placebo effect has been reported, such as analgesics for pain management. We structure our argumentation in reporting the current use of placebos in medicine, the ethical concerns it raises, and the ethical solutions provided by open-label placebos. We then analyze the ethics of systematically presenting the option of a placebo to the patient before each drug prescription. Four clinical situations are detailed and discussed.

The use of placebos in medicine

Long regarded in medicine as a synonym for “deception,” since the second half of the 20th century, the placebo has gradually earned its spurs in clinical research, where it has become the golden rule. However, its use in research5,6 but even more so in medical practice, gives rise to scientific and ethical debates.6,7

In practice, prescribing substances with a placebo effect is a common practice in general medicine. According to a meta-analysis of studies carried out in 13 countries, 8 three-quarters of doctors claim to have done so at least once a year, and more than half at least once a month. In the vast majority of cases, 9 these placebos are prescribed without the patient's knowledge (the doctor knowingly prescribes a drug with no pharmacological effect, with the intention of obtaining a potential therapeutic effect). The nature of the placebos used varies across studies, but often consists of impure placebos (54%–57% in one meta-analysis 9 ). Impure placebos consist of substances with a (generally weak) pharmacological action that are used outside the context where their effect has been demonstrated. These include non-deficiency vitamins (used by 23%–75% of GPs 9 ), dietary supplements (35%–59% 9 ), but also antibiotics for viral infections (17%–69% 9 ). The use of pure placebos (i.e., a substance devoid of any active principle 10 ) is estimated at between 1% and 7% in a meta-analysis in general practice, 8 between 17% and 80% according to other studies. 9 The definition of pure placebos varies across studies, and placebos are mainly used for insomnia, pain, and/or anxiety. 9

The reasons given by doctors for prescribing a placebo can be grouped into two categories. 9 The first is explicitly aimed at benefiting the patient: satisfying his request, calming him down, improving pain control or avoiding dependence (particularly on anxiolytics and analgesics). The second is aimed more at responding to situations of diagnostic uncertainty: the doctor wants to respond to non-specific complaints, to determine whether a complaint is “organic”/”objective” or “functional”/”subjective” in nature (in other words, as a diagnostic tool), to avoid a request for medication that he considers unjustified, or to provide a response to the patient when all the medications indicated in his case have been tested.

These intentions raise several ethical questions, which we will discuss in detail below. Starting with the situation of the placebo delivered without the patient’s knowledge, our reflection opens on the ethical questions resolved and the new questions raised by the use of an open-label placebo (see also Richard et al. 11 for review). Our analysis leads us to conclude that proposing open-label placebo in shared-medical decisions is an ethical necessity, in the sense that it represents the situation that best respects the fundamental principles of ethics in medicine in conditions for which a significant placebo effect is well-documented (see Bschor et al. 12 ).

Ethical issues raised by placebo prescribing

The ethical framework that is the international reference in bioethics today is that of principlism. 13 These include respect for patient autonomy, the principles of beneficence and non-maleficence, and the principle of justice or fair treatment for all. These can be summed up schematically as the moral obligation to respect the autonomy of the individual, not to harm, to act in a way that benefits the individual, and to be fair to the individual, to other patients, and to society.

Respect for patient autonomy and freedom of choice is one of the cornerstones of shared-medical decision. It goes beyond simply providing information to the patient, and represents a key step in the break with the medical paternalism that previously prevailed.

Case of placebos used without the patient’s knowledge

The clinical use of placebos without the patient’s knowledge raises a number of ethical issues, including the acceptability of this practice. 14 These questions involve the trustful relationship between doctor and patient. First and foremost, they call into question the principles of beneficence and patient autonomy, as well as non-maleficence and justice. 13

Some motivations for prescribing a placebo are based on the principle of beneficence (such as satisfying a patient’s request) or non-maleficence (the wish to avoid unjustified prescriptions of pharmacologically active substances or to limit potential side effects). But deception on the part of the doctor implies that the patient is kept in ignorance of this use. It speaks against the contract of trust necessary to the doctor-patient relationship. It speaks against the requirement for clear, fair, and proportionate information and consequently fails to respect the principle of autonomy: it is therefore one of the ways in which medical paternalism is put into practice.

However, some authors argue that this “benevolent deception” of the patient by the physician does not represent an infringement of the patient's autonomy in some circumstances. The debate here concerns the nature of what constitutes “essential information” that patients should receive from their doctors 14 and the uncertainty as to what information the patient wishes to receive. 15

Could “doctor deception” jeopardize the trustful relationship with the patient? Quantitative and qualitative studies have shown that a majority of patients seem to accept that a doctor may prescribe a placebo without informing them, under certain conditions; just a minority consider this practice not acceptable.16 –18 A utilitarian point of view could justify the use of a placebo without informing the patient, on the grounds of giving priority to the majority of patients who value efficacy over honesty. Gold et al. 19 criticize this utilitarian point of view. They question the ability of the doctor to anticipate how a patient will react when discovering this “deception.” The risk that the patient could break off the relationship with the doctor by discovering the deception may be too high. The patient could cut himself off from beneficial treatments, this representing a greater loss of chance than the beneficial effect hoped for the patient by the “deception.” These questions seem to receive little consideration from caregivers, some of whom believe that this practice is acceptable and ethically sound or that it does not alter the trustful relationship between caregiver and patient. 9

The case of the open-label placebos

Over the last few decades, research has focused on the use of the open-label placebos. An open-label placebo is a substance with no pharmacological action that is transparently prescribed to the patient, after explaining that its administration may help relieve some symptoms. This use resolves the tension between the principle of beneficence and the principle of autonomy: it aims to derive the benefits of the placebo while avoiding the ethical problems posed by blinding. Yet, while some consider this prescription to be ethically sound20,21 others contest this argument.15,22 –24

One of the central points of the discussion is again the nature of the information provided to the patient when an open-label placebo is offered (for a systematic review, see Richard et al. 11 ). Four pieces of information are generally provided to accompany an open-label placebo:

Does this information, although explicitly mentioning that a placebo contains no active substance, contain elements of deception that could undermine the relationship of trust with the doctor? A critical analysis of each of these elements has been carried out by Blease et al. 20 The first statement is correct: several studies and meta-analyses have provided evidence of significant improvements in certain clinical conditions.4,25 The efficacy of open-label placebos provides better outcomes than both “doing nothing” (i.e., treatment as usual, no treatment or waiting list) and even than blinded placebos, although those effects are rather observed on subjective measures and not on objective ones. 4 The second statement, concerning one of placebo’s mechanisms of action, is also well-documented. 26 The absence of prerequisites for a positive attitude toward placebo was demonstrated in a pilot study on chronic rhinitis. 27 It shows that the taking of the placebo explains its clinical effect independently of the effects of expectations linked to the instruction.

Even if this instruction does not involve deception, it is necessary to check that the patient is able to understand its meaning; otherwise, it would consist of using a placebo without the patient's knowledge. This practice also implies that the doctor is able to anticipate the patient's reaction when proposing a placebo (in place of a non-pharmacological option or no prescription). A telephone survey of 853 patients 17 showed that 85% of patients considered open-label placebos to be acceptable, and 61.5% were open to using placebos if their doctor suggested that they might be effective (to relieve abdominal pain, for instance). Explanation contributed to a good acceptability to this option, as 80% of patients before explanation thought that the placebo could only work if they were kept in the dark. Other qualitative studies have also shown that, while some patients prefer the doctor to make his placebo proposal explicit, a minority of others find it unacceptable, despite the doctor's transparency. As mentioned above (The use of placebo in medicine), the personal representation and definition of placebo, the capacity to choose or not the placebo option, the medical condition concerned, the representation of both illness and cure explain these disparities16 –18,28 (e.g., patients with a more biological representation of illness and healing are less likely to accept a placebo than those whose model of illness includes the doctor-patient relationship and psychological factors such as expectation.)

Placebo and informed patient information: Analysis of four situations

In the context of the ethical principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice 13 we analyze common situations in medicine with regard to the use of a placebo and the information given to the patient on a placebo. We take the example of antidepressants for depressive or anxiety disorders with various levels of severity.

Situation 1: The doctor prescribes a placebo without the patient’s knowledge

The placebo could be either a pure or impure (e.g., vitamins or phytotherapeutic substances without scientifically validated efficacy) placebo without proven efficacy on anxiety or depression. This situation speaks against the duty to inform the patient and is unanimously criticized as it fails to respect the principle of autonomy. Yet it is very common, even widespread, in medicine (see Introduction), probably justified by the principle of beneficence: acting with the intention of relieving the patient’s suffering.

Situation 2: The doctor prescribes an active drug without informing the patient of the efficacy of the placebo in this indication

Here, the doctor prescribes, for instance, an antidepressant. Antidepressants have shown a higher efficacy than placebo for severe depression or anxiety disorders, but are commonly prescribed in depressive or anxiety disorders with low-to-moderate severity. The level of evidence of antidepressants is particularly questioned for depressive/anxiety disorders with moderate severity. Their efficacy is not superior to placebo in disorders with low severity, where they should not be prescribed. 33

In practice, patients are almost never informed about the size of the placebo response in the clinical condition for which they are being treated. This is questionable, however, as randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses provide this information, at least in part. This information is useful for two reasons: to estimate the placebo response in this indication (namely the decrease of symptoms observed in the placebo group that can be attributed together to true placebo effect, natural disease course, and other nonspecific effects), and to estimate the added value of the drug (namely, the proportion of clinical response observed when taking a placebo and the remaining proportion of clinical response that can be attributed to the active drug). For instance, the mega-analysis by Hieronymus et al. 34 (see figures 2 and 3 of Hieronymus et al. 34 ) showed that placebo response explains 67% to 85% of the global response to antidepressants in acute depressive disorders and that the added value of antidepressants in terms of response rate compared to placebo is estimated between 11.2% and 37.8% (depending on the dose of antidepressants or the tools used to measure depression). This mega-analysis was performed on acute depression, which may overestimate the efficacy of antidepressants in other non-acute situations.

Some authors have tried to systematize the use of “Drugs Fact Boxes” by doctors. These are summary sheets presenting essential information on drug efficacy and side effects in a concise, fair, and easily understandable way for patients.35,36 The figures and graphs they contain make it possible to assess the clinical relevance of the drug in relation to the effect obtained with placebo, and provide more than just the information that the proposed drug is “statistically more effective than a placebo.” In the case of antidepressants, communicating this information to patients in a fair manner would enable them to understand the lack of benefit, in certain cases, of an antidepressant compared to a placebo, and thus to fairly inform their choice for a placebo (and thus respect their autonomy).33,34

By failing to communicate clearly on placebo response data, the physician does not present the scientific data available on the various therapeutic choices in a fair manner. The doctor presents proposals and choices in such a way that the person concerned believes to exercise free choice, but is merely following the influences exerted on them. This nudging by the doctor is rooted in a disqualification of the placebo as a resource. In medicine, a placebo is often associated with “uselessness” (as in the frequent analogy: “this drug is ineffective because its efficacy is not significantly better than the response to placebo”). Another example is the a priori assumption that the placebo is simply a variable used in clinical trials to compare with “real drugs,” rather than a drug in its own right and with its own therapeutic effects. Unknowingly, the doctor is led to offer the patient only a restricted choice, ignoring other options such as placebo, which may be effective or relevant. He would use the following schematic reasoning: “this drug has marketing authorization, so it has a proven efficacy, and is therefore, on average, more effective than the placebo; as it is ethically sound to offer the best treatment to the patient, I offer this drug.”

In practice, this reasoning is questionable, as the choice of treatment is not based solely on efficacy, but on the efficacy/side-effect ratio. Admittedly, taking a placebo can generate side effects in randomized double-blind trials, where the patient does not know whether he is taking the placebo or the active drug, 37 but side effects are less likely to occur if the patient chooses the placebo option 38 than an antidepressant (including antidepressant-induced side effects and withdrawal symptoms associated with antidepressant discontinuation).

Situation 3: The physician offers an informed choice between an open-label placebo and a clinically proven drug

To this end, it presents the best available scientific data to enable patients assessing the ratio between the importance of symptoms release and of side effects associated with the use of the drug or the placebo.

This situation meets all the requirements of patient information. It does not omit any of the possible therapeutic options available to the patient, and fully respects the principle of patient autonomy. It emphasizes the size of the therapeutic potential of the placebo option (which was not proposed in situations 1 and 2) and the size of the added response to the drug, according to the severity of its anxiety or depressive symptoms. It provides patients with all the information they need to make a free and autonomous decision, without preventing them from asking the doctor for advice on the best strategy to adopt, in complete confidence. Typically, such fair information should make both the doctor and patient aware that anxiety/depression disorders of moderate or low severity do not represent conditions in which the ratio between clinical efficacy and side effects clearly favors psychotropic drugs.

In addition, it is important for both doctor and patient to be aware that the use of open-label placebo in many clinical conditions was more efficient compared to no intervention. 39 It is this placebo response that we want to capitalize on. Admittedly, such studies are still rare. It should also be remembered that the placebo response has been evaluated at around 30% on average 40 and that its importance may vary according to clinical conditions.12,41

The open-label placebo option could be considered for psychological symptoms or conditions in which a clinically meaningful placebo response has been well-documented, such as major depressive disorders, general anxiety disorder, panic disorder, and ADHD, for instance. 12 In those conditions, it could be offered as a first-line treatment, with the active drug retained as a second-line treatment in case of no or only partial improvement (this aligns with the German National Medical Council proposal, see Box 1). This strategy respects the duty to provide fair information to the patient and represents limited risk-taking thanks to the close re-evaluation consultation. Of course, it should not be proposed in the case of curable or severe diseases with treatments with clear efficacy superior to placebo. 21

This graduated strategy had already been advocated by Brown 42 for moderate depression without suicidal ideation. The use of a placebo as a first-line treatment for 4–6 weeks for depression also enables a finer distinction between the “first consultation” effect (with its implications for the relationship, the first prescription, the specific course of the illness and regression to the mean) and the specific effect of an antidepressant (often confused with the “first consultation” effect if an antidepressant is prescribed at the first consultation).

Situation 4: The doctor offers an informed choice between a drug with proven efficacy or an open-label placebo, and psychosocial support, different options that can be combined

As in situation 3, the context is that of conditions with a well-documented placebo effect. We would like to emphasize here the value of psychosocial support aimed at developing the patient’s confidence in his own resources to cope with symptoms, independently of medication. However, it must take the patient’s preference into account: some patients do not like the option of psychosocial support and prefer an easier or quicker drug solution (sometimes used as a transient option). In such cases, an open-label placebo appears to be a pragmatic solution, responding to the patient’s request, even if this may also mask other implicit or less conscious demands.

This situation seems ethically the best one, but its implementation requires to take the pragmatic reality of medical consultations today into account: long delays in obtaining a consultation, short consultation times (barely longer for consultations with psychological issues), low rate of prescription-free in general practice. 43 If the consultation time is short, one option may be for the doctor to provide the patient with information documents on these options, and to see the patient later to clarify their decision.

Discussion

On the basis of these typical situations, we propose to combine respect for the ethical principle of patient autonomy, the objective of reducing risky practices (to what extent it can be concretely applied given the known constraints of general medical practice taken as a reference) and ease of implementation to identify the situation that is least ethically questionable, pragmatically achievable and associated with the lowest iatrogenic risk.

We deduce from this analysis that systematically informing patients of the open-label placebo option is an ethical necessity: it represents one of the least ethically questionable possibilities, given the frequent use of placebos delivered without the patient’s knowledge, and of potentially iatrogenic impure placebos.

From the perspective of reducing inappropriate prescribing of psychotropic drugs, we argue that systematically informing patients about the placebo option would be beneficial in two common clinical scenarios.

Case 1: The efficacy of an active drug is significantly greater than that of a placebo. In this situation, the physician may be inclined to prescribe the drug, and the patient may be inclined to take it. Information about the efficacy of the placebo would allow both the physician and the patient to assess more objectively the clinical relevance of this “greater efficacy,” which may be substantial or, conversely, very small, even when statistically significant. This assessment can be made in light of the potentially higher incidence of adverse effects associated with the active drug. The patient would thus be better able to evaluate whether their expectations are more likely to be met by taking the active drug or the placebo.

Case 2: The efficacy of the active drug is not clearly established. This may occur either because the scientific literature shows no significant difference between the drug and a placebo, or because it provides no reliable data for the patient’s specific clinical context. In such cases, although the physician lacks scientific evidence to justify prescribing an active drug, they may nonetheless be tempted—as often happens in medical practice—to do so “for placebo purposes” or on the assumption that the drug might “still work” or “produce some beneficial effect.” In this context, information about the efficacy of placebos would enable both the physician and the patient to recognize that prescribing a placebo instead of an active drug would offer the most favorable benefit–risk ratio, if both agree that a prescription is truly necessary. Alternatively, this information could support the decision not to prescribe any treatment, thereby avoiding inappropriate prescribing.

It is, however, worth clarifying that in both Case 1 and 2, having data supporting the efficacy of the placebo compared to no intervention would be the prerequisite to firmly justify placebo prescription, being aware that this information is often missing. On the contrary, patients should be clearly informed that if a placebo is proposed, its efficacy may not be different from “doing nothing.”

In addition, we would like to underscore that the use of placebos also contributes to an eco-responsible approach by limiting unnecessary prescriptions and the carbon footprint of prescriptions (i.e., 25% of the carbon footprint of hospitals 44 ). It is also eco-responsible thanks to the very low carbon cost of its production, and contributes to reducing certain care-related costs, even if optimization strategies are undoubtedly possible in terms of financial and environmental costs.

Limitation

A critical aspect before proposing the option of open-label placebo is to take some factors into account that likely influence their proneness to accept it, namely, health literacy, socioeconomic status, or cultural beliefs about medication. For instance, patients with greater health literacy could be less likely to believe in or expect benefits from open-label placebos and dislike this proposition. 45 However, as mentioned above, the acceptability of open-label placebos was predominantly good.16 –18,28

Additional considerations also justify the use of an open-label placebo. This may reduce the medicalization of care and keep its therapeutic virtue.27,46 In fact, it helps to reduce both health damages and costs of adverse drug events.47,48 However, some authors challenge this view. 49 They argue that there is a risk of overprescription of placebos for any condition a patient may have, which could reinforce the patient’s dependence on external interventions and hinder the development of patient autonomy. This is why an open-label placebo should be accompanied by an intervention fostering empowerment, as presented in situation 4, which represents a concrete way to lower this risk. We should also emphasize that open-label placebos could be useful for accompanying psychotropic deprescription, with promising results already shown for children treated with methylphenidate 50 or patients willing to reduce methadone dosage. 51

Conclusion

Our analysis leads us to conclude the ethical necessity of including both the placebo effect and the placebo option in patient information. Our article focused on psychological conditions, but we should keep in mind that our reasoning may also concern other medical conditions, particularly those with a well-documented placebo effect. This is not part of current practice, but should be, while including the option of a non-pharmacological and/or psychosocial support. This approach is more ethical than prescribing placebos, particularly impure ones, which are common in medicine. These considerations on the ethical issue of placebo use in clinical practice should also lead us to reflect on clinical trial methodology and evidence-based decision-making. In ethical terms, the concept of value-based medicine, integrating the patient’s perceived value, seems more appropriate. 52
