“We feel like orphans”: listening to users’ expectations to ensure more responsive healthcare services in rural Peru
Authors: Maria Amalia Pesantes, Silvana Perez-León, María Lazo-Porras, Maria Kathia Cárdenas, J Jaime Miranda, David Beran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe goal of health systems is to meet the health needs of the population in order to improve and/or maintain their health. To do so, it is necessary to understand the expectations regarding local health services. Meeting users’ expectations is key to responsive health systems. However, there is little understanding about the expectations of rural populations in low- and middle-income countries regarding Primary Health Care. Through interviews with rural people affected by a chronic condition (n = 15), local leaders (n = 7), and focus groups with users of health services in Northern Peru (n = 109), this paper explores the perceptions they have of local Primary Health Care services and their expectations of how services ought to be. We used Atlas-ti to conduct a thematic analysis, and found that peoples’ concerns about the health system go beyond commonplace elements such as lack of medications or healthcare workers. Rather, participants’ concerns revolve around not feeling respected and that their health needs were less important than the ones of those with money or who live in the city. This was captured in their feeling of abandonment by the State who sends young and inexperienced doctors to work in rural communities and ineffective medications. Interventions to improve the responsiveness of health systems cannot be based on blanket approaches but rather should be specific to each context and take into consideration the expectations and experiences of those it seeks to serve. For instance, re-consider the policy of sending young doctors to work in rural areas and/or providing a sustained mentorship from more experienced doctors, during their year of service.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13535-6.Keywords: Responsiveness, Primary health care, Rural health, Peru, Patient’s experiences

Introduction

The goal of health systems is to meet the health needs of the population in order to improve and/or maintain their health [1]. In order to attain those goals, health systems require a thorough understanding not only not only of needs but also the expectations of the population regarding the health services that are delivered, as these will in turn, promote effective healthcare service utilization and thus improve health outcomes. Most efforts in LMIC have concentrated on pursuing universal health coverage ensuring availability and accessibility of health services. There is still, little understanding about the expectations of rural populations in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) regarding healthcare services and most of the literature to date has focused on patient satisfaction using quantitative approaches [2–4]. Yet, much remains to be done to ensure that they are also responsive and of high-quality [5]. To reach that goal, governments should understand the expectations of vulnerable groups about health services and place them at the center of primary health care (PHC) design and service delivery. Research shows that unmet expectations may have an impact in future health-seeking behaviors as people might delay care or try to address their health problems using private services [6].

Despite the fact that public health services ought to serve all citizens equally, this is not always the case [7]. Oftentimes, people in poor countries have less access to health services than those in better-off countries, and within countries poor people have less access than better-off citizens [8]. In LMICs one in three people have negative experiences with their health system, specifically in the areas of attention, respect, communication, and length of visit, as well as disrespectful treatment and abuse [5]. Furthermore, whilst the accessibility of health services is essential, this per se is not a guarantee to ensure high quality healthcare services. It is common to hear that the provision of healthcare services is not tailored to the local needs and expectations [9]. Furthermore, many Latin American countries lack robust primary care services and adequately trained workforce in rural areas [10].

Meeting the expectations of the local population regarding health services is, according to the World Health Organization (WHO), key to have responsive health systems [11]. The concept of “responsiveness” is, defined by the WHO “as the outcome that can be achieved when institutions and institutional relationships are designed in such a way that they are cognizant and respond appropriately to the universally legitimate expectations of individuals” [11]. The WHOs health systems responsiveness framework includes the following core components: dignity, autonomy, confidentiality, clear communication, prompt attention, quality of amenities, and access to social networks [11]. It considers both the expectations of the people and health system actors about how the population should be treated, and the interactions that influence people’s experiences within a context [12]. This framework is appropriate to explore patients’ expectations because rather than focusing on issues associated with financial resource allocation, it stresses the importance of the interaction between patients and providers in the context of healthcare provision.

To date, most of the research using the responsiveness framework has been largely conducted in high-income countries (HIC), specifically in Europe [13], and in hospitals [14]. Studies from LMIC that use the responsiveness framework in LMIC such as Philippines [15] or Ethiopia [16] use a quantitative approach (surveys) and have not been done exclusively in public facilities. Among the limitations of the WHO responsiveness measurement tool is that we cannot ignore that health and non-health expectations of people are embedded in broader sociocultural norms and thus, the importance that some domains receive in HIC are not so important or relevant in LMIC [17]. Our paper provides an opportunity to deeper our understanding of those aspects of PHC that people in rural communities in Northern Peru expect to receive from public services. Listening to the voices of rural communities is of importance not only in Peru but in Latin America. Rural populations in the region face the highest poverty levels as a result of increased urbanization that has made governments prioritize development efforts in cities where most of the population resides [18].

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was part of the formative phase of a bigger research project aimed at co-designing an intervention to improve the health system responsiveness in four LMICs. We conducted a qualitative study that collected data through in-depth interviews and focus group discussions (FGD). Interviews included: people with chronic conditions, specifically: hypertension (n = 7), type 2 diabetes(n = 4) and epilepsy (n = 5) and their relatives and/or caregivers (n = 11). We tried to have an equal number of men and women affected by the conditions, but the majority were women (13/15). These three chronic conditions were chosen as “tracer conditions” to look at the health system. Patients were identified either by referral of local health workers or by community members who participated in a meeting where researchers presented the study to both local authorities and community members. To ensure we had a similar number of participants from each health condition we used the snowball sampling technique. The number of patient interviews was defined a priori, based on previous experience conducting qualitative research which has shown us that having at least ten participants with a similar health condition provides substantial data on their experiences with the condition and the health services. We anticipated interviewing at least one caregiver and/or relative knowledgeable of the patient’s experiences.

Participants received verbal explanation of the research goal the interview process and the overall project activities as the interviews were to be followed by community meetings to co-create an intervention in a later phase. Interview participants were given the opportunity to ask questions before confirming their interest in participating in the study. Afterwards, the researcher and the participant read together the written consent to ensure clarity. The team in charge of data collection were all between 20 and 25 years of age while participants were 40 and over. We believe that despite the differences in socio-economic and educational background, the generational difference made the participants more at ease in asking questions about the research. Furthermore, most of the authors in this paper lead other participatory research activities in these communities that enabled a better interpretation of the research findings.

We complemented the information with key informants (n = 7), such as community leaders and health promoters. They were identified in the aforementioned meeting. We also conducted nine focus groups with community members without any specific health condition. We held three FGD with only men (n = 23) and six with only women (n = 59). Interviews with community members were done when focus groups were not possible (n = 27). The rationale for including community members who use the PHC but are not necessarily affected by a health problem was our interest in documenting the general information among community members around health service provision for people with diabetes, hypertension and epilepsy. These would inform future project activities to co-design an intervention for these conditions [19]. We also wanted to ensure we had a similar number of men and women from different ages represented not to do a gender analysis of their responses, but rather to make sure all views by gender and generation were taken into account for the following project activities.

Data was collected by four research assistants (two men and two women), all with a university degree in sociology or anthropology and with previous experience in conducting in-depth interviews in rural areas. They all underwent a training with the principal investigator before going to the field where, in addition to going over the interview guide and focus group guide, we emphasized the importance of being aware of power dynamics between the interviewee and the interviewer. All interviews were conducted in Spanish, the mother tongue of both the community members and the interviewers.

The interviews and FGDs covered a wide variety of topics described elsewhere [20]. In this paper we focus on responses to the following topics: Local perceptions of local health services; Experiences with local health services; General use of health services; Use of health services for chronic conditions. Through the analysis of the transcripts, one emerging theme was the unmet expectations of local people and patients around the health system. This made it relevant to analyze the data using the responsiveness framework.

All data was de-identified, and each participant was assigned a code. Only the Principal Investigator had access to the personal information of each participant which was recorded in a separate excel spreadsheet.

The interview guide (Appendix 1) and FGD guide (Appendix 2) were developed by the research team for this study. Ethics approval for the study was obtained from the Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia (approval No. 393–22–16, dated 16th October 2016).

Setting

This research was conducted in four rural communities from two districts from the province of Ayabaca, in the region of Piura, in Northern Peru. All four communities had a health facility staffed by a different number of healthcare provided based on the population served by the facility (See Tables 1 and 2). Throughout the manuscript we do not use the name of the communities but rather a pseudonym (Community A, Community B, Community C and Community D).

Piura is the region with the lowest per capita healthcare expenditure in the country, translating into the lowest geographic distribution of health care workers (7.2 in Piura vs 12.8 national average) and the lowest availability of hospital beds in the country (3.6 per 10,000 people in Piura vs 15.9 national average) [21]. While 78.4% of the Piura region’s population has access to sanitation, only 8.1% have access to safe water, and 42% of residents live in moderate or extreme poverty [21]. Further, according to the local health workers 100% of the population of the communities in the study is enrolled in the Free National Comprehensive Health Insurance (SIS gratuito), which is only for people who are classified by the Ministry of Social Inclusion as Poor or Extremely Poor.

Healthcare provision in the four communities is delivered through small healthcare facilities called “Postas de Salud” or “Centros de Salud.” Postas usually provide very basic primary health services focused mostly on maternal and child health which are national priorities, as well as infectious conditions that result in diarrheic and respiratory conditions. Centros de Salud provide services for a bigger population and usually have at least a physician. When a patient requires specialized care or diagnostic tests, they are referred to another health facility. The distance and costs related to accessing health facilities that can provide laboratory tests and specialized care represent a barrier. Another challenge in rural health services is the limited number of health human resources that since 1981 is compensated by the Rural and Urban Marginal Health Service (SERUM) program.

The SERUM is a community service program aimed at staffing primary health care facilities with trained health professionals. These health professionals are people who have recently graduated from private and public universities in a health profession [22, 23]. Currently, the SERUM is the main instrument of the Peruvian health sector for staffing the health system in remote areas. These recent graduates have little or no experience working in PHC facilities, with most of their training in urban universities and only exposed to hospital settings [24].

Data analysis

The information collected through interviews and FGD was transcribed verbatim and analyzed using the Atlas.Ti 8.0 software. The research team developed the code book based on the interview and FGD guides. Three researchers coded one interview to standardize the use of the codes and introduced changes in the codebook for consistency.

Only one person was responsible for coding the rest of the material with the other two overviewing the process. When the coding was completed, the quotes were exported to be analyzed and summarized. Codes related to the use of health services within the public health system were selected due to their relevance for the topic of health system’s responsiveness. Then, the quotes were summarized in a matrix, where each row was a participant, and each column was a code. Finally, the lead author read all the matrices and organized the information along the eight domains of the Responsiveness Framework: dignity, autonomy, confidentiality, prompt attention, adequate quality of care, clarity of communication, access to social support networks and choice of care provider [25]. The testimonies of participants provided relevant information on three constructs: dignity, prompt attention, adequate quality of care which are described in the following section. It is important to note here that those domains that are not part of this manuscript reflect the limited information that participants shared on such issues or the lack of relevance of those dimensions in a poor rural setting such as the one where this research was done. For example, the dimension “choice of care provider” is based on the assumption that there is more than one doctor available. In the communities we visited there are often no doctors but only nurses and nurse technicians. Other key dimension such as “autonomy”, is deeply connected to a patient’s right to make decisions on their own treatment based on a horizontal dialogue and discussion of options. Participants -as we will see- were more bothered by the limited availability of medications and poor quality of them, than not being able to choose another course of treatment. We have explored this issue under the section “Adequate quality of care”, as this was the emphasis given by participants.

Results

This section presents the results of the interviews and FGD organized around the three dimensions of the responsiveness framework that most closely aligned with the views expressed by our participants: Dignity, Prompt Attention and Quality of Care. Details about which type of participant is quoted, is provided in brackets and it also includes information about the community where they come from.

Dignity

According to the responsiveness literature, dignity is a domain closely related to human rights. It refers to “the state of being worthy or esteemed” [26, p. 3] and to ensuring the respect for the rights and freedom of health care seekers when receiving health services [25, 27]. Individuals are treated with dignity when they are seen as a whole person [28], are treated with respect and empathy, receive full attention when being treated, receive the same treatment as other people [28, 29], and when their privacy is protected [30].

Dignity: people’s perceptions

Participants’ insights about dignity in the context of healthcare provision allows us to understand whether compared to others, they considered everybody was treated the same or not. One salient issue brought up by participants was that your economic status plays a role in the quality of care you can receive. Participants stated that those with money can afford better care, even if health services at the local PHC facility are free. They can choose to seek care at a private clinic in the area and/or afford transportation expenses required to go to a better health facility in the city. People with more availability of cash could also buy the medications prescribed that are not available in public health facilities due to shortages. Female focus group participants in Community B expressed bluntly that people who do not have money will not receive quality care as they don’t have the means to pay to travel to a nearby city in search for better care. They were thus left to deal with their health problem at home with herbal remedies.

The stories shared showed the poor consideration they feel is given to their lives. For instance, participants stated that the lack of an ambulance in the community, meant that patients are often taken in a regular vehicle to the hospital usually hours away from where they live:

Sometimes, emergency cases are taken in a truck to XX [health center of reference], we are like orphans over here, [orphans] of medications, technical personnel, trained professionals that are trained to provide care with the minimum that a patient and their family members deserve.(Male focus group, Community B)

Throughout the interviews it was obvious that people are aware that the way they are being treated by the health system (and not only by healthcare providers) ought to be improved.

People who have an unexpected health emergency often have to ask for help from their neighbors and relatives “A patient who fainted had to rely on their neighbors to be evacuated. She did not receive support from the health personnel who told her she was going to die.”(Female Patient with diabetes, Community A). This participant stressed that it appeared that healthcare workers did not to care about them, adding to the feeling of abandonment.

Making people wait despite the urgency of their condition, from the perspective of the relatives, is disrespectful and a mistreatment. Many interpret this mistreatment as rooted in discrimination against the poor rural population, because they cannot afford to pay the consultation. Administrators or healthcare workers at private services assess a person will not be able to pay the consultation and ignore them. One participant shared his story taking his father to a private clinic in the city:

The doctor arrived, put him in his wheelchair, left him there abandoned. The doctor left and then I said: “is the doctor going to his office to see my father?” because I was already in the process of paying for the consultation …(Focus Group with young men Community B)

Another male participant shared a similar feeling regarding the attitude of healthcare providers in the public sector. When his brother had a medical emergency, the doctor told them that unless the patients’ family covered for his (the doctor’s) transportation expenses, he could not go to see the patient. It is worth noting that most of the criticism came from experiences during emergencies and urgent care, rather than routine visits to healthcare facilities.

Prompt attention

Prompt attention refers to respecting the time and feelings of people [27] as it relates to offering a fast and timely healthcare service to all, especially to vulnerable groups in case of emergencies and non-emergency situations including appointments, consultations, exams and treatment [25–27]. Prompt attention includes not only reduced waiting times, but having health facilities geographically located at a reasonable distance from people’s houses, facilitating people’s access to health care [27, 31]. By providing prompt attention, people’s feelings of discomfort, anxiety and inconvenience are avoided [25].

Prompt attention: people’s perceptions

From the perspective of the local population, the failure for prompt attention is expressed with regards to two factors, the limited hours of operation of health facilities and long waiting times.

PHC facilities in Peru have established hours of operation by law. Postas (Health Posts) are open from 8 to 1 p.m. Monday through Saturday and they provide emergency care at any time. On the other hand, Centros de Salud (Health Centers, which are facilities that can tend to more complex health needs, have more personnel and better infrastructure) like the ones in Community A and B have two shifts that allow them to see patients until 8 p.m. Postas and Centros are part of a network (Red de Salud), which means that when the PHC is closed or the health problem is more complex, people need to go to the Health Center located one hour away. Health centers are mandated to see emergency cases at any moment during the day and even on weekends and holidays. But the regular hours of operation are only until 8:00 p.m.

However, people express dissatisfaction with the hours of operation. People from Community A, argue that, despite the Heath Centre having a sign saying that they receive patients 24 hours, the doctor is not always available, and they have to wait and beg to be seen.

…it says that there are doctors 24 hours a day at the health post, but when you go it is not like that. They tell you: “There is no doctor”, and then? Or sometimes they tell you “Wait for the doctor” it is up to the doctor at what time he arrives. We are there an hour, two hours, waiting and still, when he arrives, we still have to beg “please, see me …” (…) and this has happened to me several times, several times… (Focus Group with women, Community A)

For example, yesterday … I think it was yesterday morning, or it was before yesterday, that the doctor was not there. He [husband] had gone to see him, to measure his sugar levels [and] he was not there and, well, he had to return home. My husband does not always find [health workers], or the staff …. (Relative of patient with Diabetes, Community B)

A Promotor de Salud (Community Health Worker who does preventive health activities on a volunteer basis) explained that sometimes people go to his house at night because the health facility is closed. He then must go with the patient in the middle of the night to knock on the health facility’s door to ask the doctor to tend to the emergency.: “At night you die, “bien muerto” because there is no one to see you.” (Female community leader, Community B). The tension between the expectations of people to be seen at any time of the day and the “emergency only” attention after the facility is closed comes from a misunderstanding of the sign stating, “Open 24 hours.”

Not only the hours of operation are a problem according to the local population, the fact that they are not open during weekends is also problematic. For instance, the Health Post in one of the communities is not open on Sundays. If somebody has a health problem during the weekend, they have to spend their own money to travel to Community A (45 minutes by car) and buy medications that otherwise could have been received for free at the health post.

For those participants who had travelled to the City of Piura (5 hours away in private vehicle) or Sullana (4 to 6 hours away in private vehicle) to seek care at a public hospital, the main problem were the waiting times. In the region of Piura, there are few specialists available and few appointments in public hospitals, to satisfy the high demand. People who want to secure an appointment, need to sleep outside the hospital the night before and hope that doctors are available to see you.

I went once, to the Hospital to see the endocrinologist. There, the consultation is 15 soles. But the worst for those of us who live far away is that sometimes when we go, they are on strike. [There is always a reason why the doctor is not available] “She’s on strike … she’s on leave, she’s sick, she only sees patients once a week”. Imagine, you queue from 6 in the morning until 9 in the morning and … Oh, how difficult is it to receive care in that hospital(Female Patient with Diabetes, Community B).

In the case of the Postas, a participant said that if people anticipate that they will have to wait and not receive the care they need, they will not go:

“Sometimes, for a small ailment, people choose not to go to the health post because they know that the care is not good, they know that there is no doctor, that they are not treated well, that they make them wait …” (Female relative of a patient with Diabetes, Community B).

Adequate quality of care

Quality of care can cover a wide spectrum of experiences. For example there are components related to “structural quality” related to dimensions such as continuity of care, costs, accommodation and accessibility while “process quality” involves the dimensions of courtesy, information, autonomy and competence [27, 32].

Patient’s perspectives on adequate quality of care

Perceptions about health professionals

Patients were critical of the health services because healthcare professionals that work in them are inexperienced, are constantly changing and are clueless about health needs of the rural population. Throughout the interviews people expressed concern and annoyance about the fact that the health facilities in their communities were staffed by healthcare providers that are recent university graduates (serumistas). They described them as young and amateur, which translates into people feeling that serumistas are “practicing” with them. This idea was expressed in a Focus Group with men, where participants said: “They are sent to the provinces to practice; they are not good doctors.”

People feel that often, these young doctors only accept to work in rural areas such as Ayabaca and Montero because the wages are better. As part of a policy to promote doctors to go to work in remote rural areas, the Ministry of Health in Peru offers financial bonuses.

“As the lady said: “Send us good doctors, not interns.” They have to have a vocation, because they send us [doctors who accept] only because they get good money, nothing more. They come to work here because of the money, not because they have a vocation.”

They don’t love their work either, they are just sitting there with their bored face …” (Focus Group with women in community A)

Given that these young doctors are serumistas fulfilling their 1-year service in these rural facilities, people complain about the constant change of health workers:

They come here to practice; they do not stay for more than a year. The doctors come for one year from and then go away. Then comes another intern…(Patient with Hypertension and his relative, Community C)

According to the participants, another problem with having young healthcare workers is that they are unfamiliar with the rural setting. They do not know the most common ailments in the communities and do not know how to address them such as the case of neurocysticercosis. Furthermore, people are aware that the working conditions are not ideal or attractive for a doctor. Therefore, from peoples’ perceptions, the doctors that accept to go and work in their communities, are not the best clinicians.

Can’t you see that doctors don’t want to come here? Do you know why they don’t come? Because here they are going to earn less; here you can’t put their private office outside the medical post, you can’t work. It’s not like in the city. For example, in the city he works in a health center then he also has his private practice, but not here. Here the population is small.(Focus group with men, Community B)

Overall, there is awareness that the health system is designed to have the most inexperienced health professionals in rural areas and the best ones in the cities, and this does not sit well with the participants.

Poor quality of medications and lack of medications

Another big issue mentioned by participants through which they assess the quality of the care provided is whether health facilities have the medications they want/need. People mentioned that the medications in the health facilities were “not strong” and therefore useless. The perception that free medications provided by the government are not effective was expressed by several participants. One female participant stated: “In the health center they give basic medicines, they don’t give good medicines.”

As a result of such perception, people would skip going to the health facility and go directly to the pharmacy to buy medications. Patients living with diabetes mentioned that they often did not have the medications they have been prescribed: “In the health center, they only have two types of medications for diabetes, and they are not the ones doctors prescribe you.” (Female patient with Diabetes, Community B). Similarly, the assessment of health services as “good” is related to the capacity of doctors to give them the medications they need, and to solve their problems.

With that doctor [things are well], he has given me medicines and he is giving me medicines so far. The problem is when you run out of medicines … and [you live in] poverty, there is nothing to buy medications with. The doctor is terrific, he gives me handfuls of pills, I only (pay) for the consultation, which is five soles.(Female patient with Diabetes, Community C)

Similarly, bad quality of care is associated with not receiving the medications they need:

I went to the health post, because of a blow [in my head]. I could no longer bear (the pain). I told him: “Blood has accumulated where I was hit.” I went to the post and then I told them to prescribe me something for the blood because the blood was clotting (…),and they prescribed me only one thing, just one thing to take when my head burns, they did not prescribe anything else.(Female Hypertension Patient, Community A)

This patient is frustrated with the fact that health workers only gave her some generic pills for the pain (probably Ibuprofen). From her perspective, her condition was more serious and deserved another medication since there was a possibility that her blood will coagulate inside her head. Different assessments about the seriousness of a condition or the causes of a condition impacts the evaluation of the care received. Furthermore, in this particular case, there is a possibility that the gender of the patient might also influence the attention she received for her complain.

The lack of medications at health facilities affects mostly those with chronic conditions that rely on them for managing their condition. This was the case of a patient with epilepsy that needed to travel to Piura for her medications but could not afford the transportation costs, resulting in an increase in the frequency of seizures.

Besides the poor quality and availability of medications, poor quality of health services is also related to having to wait to see a doctor only to receive little information about your condition. Participants also mentioned that the local health facilities do not have basic equipment for laboratory tests, x-rays and other tests that will require them to travel outside of their communities that implies investing time and money that they do not have. This results in people just “skipping” those tests and figuring out their own ways of tending to their health needs, including using herbal remedies or buying medications from the local pharmacies. The lack of diagnostic tools for neurocysticercosis is worrisome, considering that in the selected communities, this condition is endemic and the long-lasting effects of seizures mentioned by several participants.

Uhmm of course, we trust [the doctors], but let’s say [the quality of care] doesn’t depend on them. For example, let’s say they provide very good care [but] because they lack, let’s say medical tools, many things are missing, the doctor does not [heal you]. The professionals here, they do what they can do. (Local authority, Community B)

Even when patients understand the limitations of doctors at the local health facilities, they still experience the lack of comprehensive medical infrastructure which adds to the feeling of being neglected by the state.

Distances and lack of ambulances

The roads in the smaller communities (C and D) are not paved and distances from a community with a PHC facility, to a bigger location with a health center (Community A or B) and then to a city (Sullana) with a hospital are big. It can take around six hours to travel from Community B to Sullana considering that there is no regular transportation services between these places. Transportation is provided by individuals who own pickup trucks and provide daily transportation services to and from the city of Piura. For the local population, the distances between one PHC to a hospital where an emergency can be solved are a major problem that is aggravated by the lack of ambulances. As one Focus Group participant explained:

For example, some patients are sometimes taken to the health post, but there is nothing at the health post, mostly they are referred straight away to Sullana and until he reaches Sullana he might have already died.(Focus Group with women, Community B)

As Fig. 1 shows, patients’ concerns about the health system appear to focus on concrete issues such as medications, capacity of human resources and inadequate hours of operation. Yet, their criticism goes beyond these isolated elements. The stress placed on being treated appropriately as an element of satisfaction, and the frustration with actions that went against their dignity are noteworthy. Participants did not feel respected at healthcare services and felt their health needs were less important than the ones of those with money or those who live in the city.

Factors that contribute to patient’s perceptions of healthcare services

Discussion

The responsiveness framework emphasizes the importance of meeting users’ expectations to improve the use of health services and in turn, health outcomes. Our research used a qualitative approach to better understand those expectations among rural populations in a middle-income country like Peru. Most of the evidence around the responsiveness of health systems comes from surveys in hospital settings [33] and from HIC [14], our study offers insights into PHC services. We used the responsiveness framework to analyze data gathered via interviews and focus groups among users of public PHC facilities. Our analysis unveils many weaknesses in health care delivery that aligns mostly with three dimensions of WHOs responsiveness framework: Dignity, Prompt Attention and Quality of Care. The poor assessment of these dimensions were the result of disappointment for being seen mainly by inexperienced doctors; low levels of trust in the quality of medicines being prescribed even if they are available at no cost; long-waiting times, inadequate hours of attention and; difficulties in accessing specialists, senior doctors, and hospitals located in urban areas. All these factors result in the disenfranchisement of rural populations, showing how health systems can be spaces where social divisions and patterns of exclusion can be reinforced [34, 35].

Although there are studies from LMIC assessing the responsiveness of their health system, it is difficult to draw comparisons as all used surveys to collect the data and have not focused specifically in public PHC in rural areas. It is nevertheless worth noting that in Nigeria [36], South Africa [37], and Bangladesh [38] prompt attention is one of the domains that researchers found needing improvement. Furthermore some studies in LMIC that compared responses among people from different income levels, found that autonomy is usually rated low among low-income individuals [36, 39]. Autonomy, within the responsiveness framework is tightly connected with individual’s right to make decisions around the type of treatment they want to receive, including refusing it [1]. Participants in our study did not express a significant concern about being given or not the option to choose a treatment but rather, their responses pointed to the quality of medications provided by the public health system which they considered were not effective. This underscores the importance of understanding how the domains of the responsiveness framework are interpreted in different settings and, as other studies have found, the relationship between empowerment and financial autonomy to treatment-related decisions [36].

In addition to prompt attention, and the quality of medications, the key criticism expressed by participants was having recently graduated healthcare workers assigned to the local PHC. The rationale for this policy is addressing the limited number of health professionals in the country. World Bank Data from 2022, shows that Peru has 1.6 physicians per 1000 people [40]. Although from a resource-allocation perspective the Serumista policy makes sense, the impact in the ground is a sense of institutionalized discrimination towards rural communities. People resent having inexperienced doctors and – as a recent study shows – these young doctors or nurses themselves feel unprepared to work in PHC as their training is hospital-centered [41]. Other Latin American countries such as Mexico [42], Ecuador [43], Brazil [44] and the Dominican Republic [45] have similar human resource strategies to address the limited number of doctors in the country, but they all found that they are not willing to work in rural areas. In Brazil, doctors sent to work in remote rural areas felt insecure in their new sociocultural environment, and described the working environment as a form of “exile” [44]. Studies from other areas of Peru have also found that cultural differences between health care providers, and rural and indigenous populations result in feelings of discrimination by the local population [46].

Our study shows that having inexperienced doctors is read through the lens of a government that does not care for them. Rural dwellers expressed a feeling of abandonment that, in Latin America is tied with a paternalistic view of the state as a “father” (Papá Estado). This figure is rooted on past colonial relations between indigenous peoples and European invaders, and later the hacienda system of the region where rural peasants were treated as children by the hacendados [47].

The experiences shared by participants show that, rather than addressing the inequalities they already face due to their poverty and rurality, the health system compounds them. As the responsiveness framework suggests, health systems need to find creative ways to address people’s perceived needs and truly be people-centered. Much of the health service quality improvement literature [9, 48] provide examples of “technical” straightforward solutions such as an attention to the number and permanence of human resources in a given area. Yet, our study shows it is not only about providing quantitative solutions (increase the number of doctors) but also, a qualitative improvement by ensuring their training is relevant to the area. A responsive health system should reconsider the policies that assign young doctors to rural areas where more experienced doctors with knowledge of the local culture would be more appropriate. A study in Brazil among rural doctors identified that having experienced doctors working with them was helpful in their professional development as mentors provided orientation on how to deal with the challenges of working in rural and remote areas [44].

Striving towards high quality healthcare is a laudable goal, and our study focusing on the provision of healthcare through public services in rural Peru signals various weaknesses that are not easily solved with more financial resources or improvements. The perceived quality of care is intertwined with a health system that is not capable of listening to vulnerable people’s expectations. Working towards improving the responsiveness of a health system has shown to increase public trust and demands for healthcare [49]. In line with the rationale that responsive health systems are those in which the expectations of both users and providers are aligned, our study provides insights into users’ experiences and disappointments specifically during their encounter with a health system.

Our results have contributed with the development of an intervention that uses participatory approaches (specifically co-creation) to address some of the weaknesses found [19]. The intervention on the one hand, offers training support on chronic conditions for doctors working in rural areas as well as a communication campaign oriented to promote a more proactive role of users when they feel they are not satisfied with the information provided to them regarding their treatment or medical condition.

We acknowledge that a limitation is that the data comes from vulnerable groups with an emphasis of people with chronic conditions that we know are not a public health priority in Peru. Probably the experiences of those who seek maternal care – a national priority – would be very different. Our study did not collect the perspectives of those of health workers which would also enable us to understand the barriers and challenges they face. Finally, at the methodological level, the use of snowball sampling to identify people affected by the conditions was useful to access hard-to -reach participants but probably limited the representativeness of these participants as there might be people in the community who suffer from these conditions that might have more complex experiences [50].

Conclusions

In this manuscript, we focused on the experiences of poor rural populations requiring chronic instead of short-term care. We took advantage of the constructs of the responsiveness framework, to navigate through people’s expectations of existing local health services. This includes interactions with individuals (e.g. service providers), processes (e.g. service use) or organizational and institutional arrangements (e.g. payment for services, reference to hospital).

The domains of the framework provided useful guidance to identify issues to improve in the interaction between users and healthcare services. In so doing, we encountered many weaknesses with existing services in rural areas resulting in populations being disenfranchised from their health services at the PHC level. Our results signal a basic divorce between health professionals in charge of the delivery of a ‘highly technical’ provision of care, focused on diseases, and key non-negotiable principles such as dignity and various other indicators of respect that people value.

Although PHC can help address inequalities [51] our results show that the Peruvian health system, particularly in rural areas, does not appear to invest sufficiently in PHC. This can be observed in the training health professionals receive in universities and technical institutions where they are largely prepared for future work in hospital and urban settings. As Kruk [5] suggests, health systems should focus not only on better health but on its equitable distribution without discrimination. This implies building trust in healthcare users and ensuring competent care and positive user experience.

As the responsiveness framework suggests, health systems need to find creative ways to address people’s perceived needs and truly be people centered. As we have shown, it is essential to make sure that users’ feel that they are being treated with dignity and this feeling goes beyond the interaction between the health provider and them as patients. Being treated with dignity, encompasses perceiving that the government in charge of allocating resources, prioritizes their health: investing in good quality medications and allocating well-trained human resources to work in rural areas.

One avenue to address this is rethinking the strategy of sending young doctors to work in rural areas or at least ensuring that they receive a comprehensive training. Such training should emphasize that quality care is deeply connected with an understanding of the local sociocultural context. Improving the existing training system for new rural doctors, providing constant mentorship from more experienced doctors, and establishing a feedback mechanism in line with the ways in which the local population make decisions would be a good starting point. Further nuanced understanding of the way in which local populations perceive the different components of the responsiveness framework would also be an important avenue of research for those using that framework.
