Conditional diffusion model for high-accuracy brain tumor segmentation in MRI images
Authors: Baolong Yu, Chuanbing Xu, Qiang Yin, Baoying Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The segmentation accuracy of deep learning–based brain tumor MRI images still requires further improvement. We proposed a conditional diffusion network that incorporates image information into the mask’s perturbed diffusion process. By optimizing the introduction of conditional supervision signals and employing an attention mechanism, our model accelerated convergence and improved predictive performance on the BraTS 2020 dataset. In the public MRI brain tumor segmentation dataset, both performance metrics have improved, with Dice metric increasing by approximately 1.99% compared to the second best metric and IoU metric increasing by 1.61% compared to the second best metric. This suggests the model may provide more stable MRI segmentation, potentially supporting clinical decision-making in research settings.

Introduction

Brain tumors are one of the leading causes of cancer-related death in children and adults. In 2018, approximately 300,000 new cases were diagnosed worldwide, resulting in 241,000 deaths from brain tumors1 (Sung 2021). In non-invasive examinations, MRI is the primary imaging modality for the diagnosis and treatment planning of brain tumors2 (Ranjbarzadeh 2021). With the development of artificial intelligence, segmentation based on deep learning (DL) has become the mainstream method for brain tumor image segmentation3 (Kouli 2022). Therefore, accurate and reliable automatic segmentation of brain tumors holds significant clinical value.

Among the existing MRI image processing techniques for brain tumors4 (Fassia et al. 2024), the mainstream models mainly include Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Deep Belief Networks (DBNs), Deep Residual Networks (DRNs), and Vision Transformers (ViTs). These models leverage deep learning to autonomously learn and extract features from MRI images, overcoming the limitations of manual observation and successfully performing classification tasks5 (Li et al. 2024).

Unlike traditional architectures based on UNet, image generation tasks can also be applied to image segmentation6–8 (Azad et al. 2022, Heidari 2023, Agrawal & Choudhary 2023). Currently, the most common generative models in the field of image generation include Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), while DDPM (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models) - known as “diffusion models” - have been proposed and developed as image generation methods. Diffusion models have demonstrated strong capabilities in downstream decision-making tasks and are increasingly being applied to medical image segmentation9 (Wang et al. 2025).

However, constructing a segmentation network based on generative diffusion models is inherently complex, and integrating segmentation outputs as conditional guidance directly into the model profoundly impacts its performance10 (Azad et al. 2022). Therefore, it is necessary to design a framework that effectively adapts generative diffusion models to segmentation tasks via conditional guidance and to establish an appropriate diffusion‑based loss function to ensure the model can produce accurate medical image segmentations. Additionally, the conditional guidance mechanism should be specifically tailored to medical imaging characteristics in order to fully extract features relevant to medical images.

In each sampling step of the diffusion model, labeled conditional sampling and the presence of labeled conditions can provide gradient guidance. Typically, an additional classifier with a UNet encoder architecture is required to generate conditional gradients for specific labels. At this point, labels can be text labels or categorical labels11,12 (Dhariwal & Nichol 2021, Lu 2022), binary labels13,14 (Wolleb et al. 2022, Packhäuser et al. 2023), or even extracted feature vectors15 (Chen et al. 2023). For unlabeled conditions, aside from label‑guided sampling, guidance can rely solely on self‑derived information via self‑supervised methods16 (Meng 2023), which are commonly applied to denoising17 (Hu et al., 2023) and non‑inpainting tasks18 (Tashiro et al., 2021).

In this study, we developed a conditional diffusion model CMDiff that incorporates attention mechanisms and Fourier preprocessing and achieves higher accuracy in brain tumor segmentation with limited annotated data, especially in cases where tumors have complex shapes and images contain heavy noise. Adding channel attention significantly enhances the supervision signal, preventing the model from losing small‑target information in deep layers and thereby reducing false negatives and false positives. Fourier filtering as a preprocessing step effectively removes explicit noise and provides the diffusion network with cleaner inputs, although the diffusion framework’s own iterative denoising already offers strong noise robustness. Moreover, the slower training convergence reflects the diffusion model’s superior ability to suppress overfitting and deliver better generalization, which is particularly important for medical imaging tasks characterized by high heterogeneity and abundant noise. This method provides precise and reliable MRI segmentation capabilities, helping to improve clinical downstream decision‑making.

Methods

Research framework

To enhance the model’s accuracy in medical image segmentation tasks, we proposed a fully automated brain tumor segmentation model based on conditional diffusion. The main improvements were as follows: (1) we added a conditional guidance module on top of the diffusion model to steer convergence toward the true segmentation labels; (2) we introduced an attention mechanism during the UNet encoding stage to guide the model’s focus toward channel‑wise features; and (3) we incorporated image preprocessing to remove Gaussian noise, thereby improving the model’s predictive performance.

Superposition signal of conditional diffusion model

The signal superposition method in the conditional diffusion model is based on the attention mechanism. As shown in Fig. 3:

Fig. 3Attention Block.

Attention Block.

In the neck of the UNet structure, the conditional diffusion model adopts a channel attention mechanism structure. This paper proposes an image segmentation algorithm based on a multi-channel attention mechanism. The main function of this module is to incorporate an attention mechanism into each channel of the feature map to better preserve and amplify the unique features of the target channel. This method can effectively improve recognition performance and reduce computational complexity. In the environments of UNet and UNet++, multiple feature extractions and encodings allow different channels to capture various characteristics of the target features.

For each extracted channel, its weight is calculated based on the features it contains. Since unified upsampling and downsampling operations are performed on all channels, edge information will be lost during multiple downsampling operations (i.e., max pooling). To extract the detailed regions of interest, they must be recalculated. Thus, this leads to these models easily missing the unique attributes of certain small targets. To compensate for this shortcoming, a new method needs to be designed to extract more target features. This study uses global pooling techniques and fully connected layers (FC layers) to deeply explore the characteristic images of targets at different scales. Next, use the attention mechanism to convey the results of feature extraction to multiple channels, reducing the loss of target feature information.

Experimental results and analysis

Datasource

The dataset used in this study originated from the public Brain Tumor Segmentation Challenge (BraTS) hosted by the Medical Image Computing and Computer‑Assisted Intervention Society (MICCAI)19 (Baid 2021). To verify the algorithm’s segmentation accuracy for tumors of different sizes and types, multiple comparative experiments were conducted on the training set. The analysis of results demonstrated that the method achieved high segmentation accuracy. In the BraTS 2020 dataset, each case comprised image volumes of size 240 × 240 × 155, totaling 480 samples, of which 355 were used for training and the remainder served as test samples.

Each case in the BraTS 2020 dataset provided four imaging modalities:

Implementation details

The training adopts a bilateral optimization mechanism. The division of the training dataset and test machine is based on the BraTS-2020 dataset. The experimental platform used in this study: Windows 11 system, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090, PyTorch 1.12.0 compilation framework, CUDA 11.0. During training, the learning rate is 0.001, sampled 500,000 times, with the Adam optimizer. Test set: 10% fixed for final performance evaluation. Training set: 72% were used for parameter optimization. Validation set: 18% were monitored for overfitting, driving an early stop strategy. Furthermore, we compares the adjusted diffusion model with traditional models such as the UNet model20 (Ronneberger et al. 2015), UNet + + model21 (Zhou et al. 2018), Dense UNet model22 (Li et al. 2018), UNet3plus model23 (Huang 2020), nnUNet model24 (Isensee et al. 2021), SegNet model25 (Badrinarayanan et al. 2017) and our model.

In our experiments, all models were trained and tested using the PyTorch framework on a unified NVIDIA RTX4090 GPU environment. The full training process for our proposed diffusion model took approximately 22 h, with a batch size of 4 during training and peak GPU memory usage around 8GB. Regarding inference efficiency, the diffusion model’s iterative sampling (1000 steps) required about 15 s per image segmentation task, significantly longer than UNet’s feedforward inference time. To balance efficiency and performance, we implemented a fast sampler like DDIM to reduce the number of sampling steps to 50, cutting the inference time to 3 s at the cost of some performance trade-offs. As discussed in the discussion section, the current model’s inference speed remains the primary bottleneck for clinical deployment. Future work will focus on integrating knowledge distillation, optimizing architecture design, and developing faster sampling algorithms to enhance its practical applicability.

Unlike natural images, medical images (such as MRI, CT, etc.) typically exhibit more pronounced noise interference, which may originate from electronic noise during equipment acquisition, motion artifacts, and tissue heterogeneity. To address this characteristic, this study innovatively introduces Fourier frequency-domain filtering as a preprocessing method to enhance image quality by suppressing high-frequency noise components. To systematically evaluate the effectiveness of this approach, we selected three representative models—nnUNet, SegNet, and the proposed model—for comparative experiments, with detailed data presented in Table 3.

The iterative denoising process in CMDiff enables capture of finer structural details and contextual information, thereby enhancing performance. In conclusion, experimental results validate CMDiff’s effectiveness and superiority in complex clinical data. Its robust feature learning capability provides a novel and effective solution for medical image segmentation.

Discussion

The conditional diffusion network proposed in this paper can more robustly capture fine details in low-contrast or morphologically complex regions of brain tumor MRI through iterative denoising and conditional supervision. On the BraTS-2020 dataset, it achieves performance improvements over several classic U-Net variants and other mainstream networks, such as nnUNet, SegNet, and DenseUNet. The improvement in performance holds significant value for clinical decision-making: brain tumors often exhibit blurred boundaries and highly variable shapes, making it difficult for traditional convolutional networks to accurately segment tumor edges. In contrast, the “denoising and reconstruction” process of diffusion models can capture lesion contours more precisely, providing pixel-level image accuracy control, thereby aiding downstream treatment planning.

During the downsampling process, the traditional U-Net architecture is prone to losing critical information from small tumors or regions with blurred edges. In our approach, we introduce a multi-scale channel attention module at the bottleneck of the U-Net: we apply global pooling to the feature maps at each downsampling stage, then use a bottleneck-style fully connected layer to generate channel weights, which are subsequently applied to the feature maps of the same scale. The ablation experiment (Table 2) demonstrates that even without attention, our diffusion model already outperforms the nnUNet with attention. However, when attention is added, mdevice improves to 89.98%, m_PPV to 83.01%, and mSensitivity to 88.93%. The network’s ability to enlarge small target channels is fully leveraged, reducing false negatives. This is particularly important in a clinical setting, where even minute lesions (e.g., multiple small glioma nodules) can significantly impact prognosis.

Medical images such as magnetic resonance imaging typically contain a significant amount of high-frequency interference due to factors such as electronic noise generated by the equipment, motion artifacts, and tissue heterogeneity. In this study, we employed Fourier frequency domain filtering as a preprocessing step to effectively remove explicit noise. Meanwhile, the diffusion model inherently suppresses implicit noise and random artifacts through its T→0 reverse iterative denoising process (as shown in Table 3, our model achieves an mdevice of 0.8524 even without Fourier preprocessing). By combining these two methods, we achieve a dual denoising effect—first eliminating explicit noise and then iteratively correcting implicit noise—thereby producing more stable and reliable predictions. This strategy is highly versatile and can be applied to different imaging modalities (such as CT, PET, and ultrasound), requiring only adjustments to the frequency domain filtering parameters to match the noise characteristics of each modality, without altering the core diffusion network architecture.

The ablation experiment (Fig. 6) demonstrates that when relying solely on “denoising” as the supervisory signal, the model converges rapidly but is prone to falling into local optima, leading to final segmentation results that deviate from the ground truth. By introducing channel attention and conditional supervision, the network receives clearer segmentation guidance in each iteration, avoiding the mere “reconstruction of image appearance” while neglecting the actual segmentation objective. This indicates that for medical image segmentation, diffusion models cannot solely depend on noise reconstruction; they must incorporate the true mask as a condition to ensure that the denoising process aligns with the segmentation task.

It can be observed that in the early stages of training, our model converges slightly slower than nnUNet, but achieves better final performance on the test set (Fig. 5). This indicates that the diffusion process effectively adds an extra form of “denoising regularization” to the network, forcing it to learn robust segmentation mappings rather than merely memorizing noise or spurious features that lead to overfitting on the training data. In contrast, while nnUNet exhibits rapid loss reduction during training, it tends to show performance stagnation on the test set, implying a risk of overfitting. For medical imaging, where sample heterogeneity is high and noise is prevalent, stable generalization is often more important than rapid training convergence.

However, the method still has certain limitations, such as its reliance on large-scale annotated data, longer inference times, and insufficient cross-modal generalization capabilities. Future research could explore lightweight model architectures, few-shot adaptive training strategies, multi-modal data joint modeling, and the development of interpretability modules to further enhance the method’s practicality.

Additionally, the diffusion-based architecture requires more computational resources and longer training times compared to traditional CNN-based models. While the model shows improvements on the BraTS-2020 dataset, further validation on external datasets (e.g., BraTS-2021 or clinical data from other centers) is needed to confirm its generalization ability. Moreover, the current framework focuses solely on tumor segmentation and does not incorporate multimodal clinical factors, which may limit its application in broader diagnostic tasks.

The primary objective of this study is to explore the application potential of the diffusion model—a powerful generative framework—in brain tumor segmentation tasks. Although our model demonstrates superiority in specific metrics, we cautiously maintain that no universal “optimal solution” exists due to the complexity and diversity of medical imaging. Our work provides a valuable new option for this field. The research remains in the academic validation phase. While the trained model performs well on strictly controlled test sets, its real-world clinical performance still requires further verification.

Conclusion

We propose a novel brain tumor image segmentation method based on a conditional diffusion model. By incorporating multi‑scale channel attention and a task supervision module into the diffusion network, and integrating Fourier frequency‑domain preprocessing, we effectively enhance the segmentation accuracy of low‑contrast and blurred tumor regions. Experiments on the BraTS‑2020 dataset show that our approach significantly outperforms traditional U‑Net variants (including nnUNet, SegNet, and DenseUNet) on key metrics such as Dice and IoU, and visualization confirms its robustness in segmenting small lesions and complex morphologies. Moreover, the inherent iterative denoising mechanism of the diffusion framework suppresses overfitting during training and ensures strong generalization, while the embedded attention modules compensate for small‑target features potentially lost during downsampling, reducing false negatives and false positives. The combined “preprocessing denoising + diffusion denoising” strategy provided by Fourier filtering and the diffusion model delivers more stable and reliable inputs across different imaging modalities.
