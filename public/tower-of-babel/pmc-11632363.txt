Adaptation During the Shift from Entomopathogen to Endosymbiont Is Accompanied by Gene Loss and Intensified Selection
Authors: Chris M Ward, Cristobal A Onetto, Anthony R Borneman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungi have been found to be associated with many insect species, with some species transitioning to reside within insects as symbionts. However, the evolutionary pressures and genomic consequences associated with this transition are not well understood. Pathogenic fungi of the genus Ophiocordyceps have undergone multiple, independent transitions from pathogen to endosymbiont lifestyles, where they reside within the fatty tissues of infected soft-scale insects transgenerationally without killing their hosts. To gain an understanding of the genomic adaptations underlying this life history shift, long-read sequencing was utilized to assemble the genomes of both the soft-scale insect Parthenolecanium corni and its Ophiocordyceps endosymbiont from a single insect. Assembly and metagenomic-based binning produced a highly contiguous genome for Pa. corni and a chromosome-level assembly for the Ophiocordyceps endosymbiont. The endosymbiont genome was characterized by 524 gene loss events compared to free-living pathogenic Ophiocordyceps relatives, with predicted roles in hyphal growth, cell wall integrity, metabolism, gene regulation, and toxin production. Contrasting patterns of selection were observed between the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes specific to the endosymbiont lineage. Intensified selection was most frequently observed across orthologs in the nuclear genome, whereas selection on most mitochondrial genes was found to be relaxed. Scans for positive selection were enriched within the fatty acid metabolism pathway with endosymbiont specific selection within three adjacent enzymes catalyzing the conversion of acetoacetate to acetyl-coenzyme A, suggesting that the endosymbiont lineage is under selective pressure to effectively exploit the lipid rich environment of the insect fat bodies in which it is found.

Significance

Life history transition from free-living fungi to vertically inherited insect endosymbiont requires a multitude of adaptive events to occur. This transition has occurred multiple times in entomopathogenic Ophiocordyceps. However, the selective landscape of this transition remained unknown. Genomic analysis of the Ophiocordyceps endosymbiont of Parthenolecanium corni revealed the role of intensified selection and gene loss, coupled with adaptation within fatty acid metabolism, during this complex life history shift.

Introduction

Microbes interact with many organisms through diverse and specific mechanisms (Iturbe-Ormaetxe et al. 2011; Fan et al. 2015; Flórez et al. 2017; Kobmoo et al. 2018; Trivedi et al. 2020) within a spectrum of mutualism, commensalism, and parasitism relationships (Duron et al. 2015; Keeling and McCutcheon 2017; Drew et al. 2021). In insects, symbiotic relationships with prokaryotic and fungal species have been shown to provide benefit to the host via increasing nutrient availability (Noda and Koizumi 2003; Vogel and Moran 2011) or through detoxification of noxious compounds (Welte et al. 2016). Yet, under certain environmental constraints, endosymbionts have been shown to switch from beneficial to parasitic relationships with their host (Chrostek et al. 2013; Le Clec'h et al. 2017). The relationship between an endosymbiont and its host is therefore complex and influenced by environmental stressors (Drew et al. 2021), rather than being constrained to a single relationship mode. After the transition to symbiosis, the selection pressures acting across the symbiont genome can be drastically different to those of their nonsymbiont sister taxa (Wernegreen 2015; Kinjo et al. 2021). This can result in differences in the strength or efficiency of selection being observed between symbiont and nonsymbiont orthologs. For example, relaxed selection (a reduction in the intensity of either positive or purifying selection), can allow previously constrained genes to escape selective pressures (Wernegreen 2011; Wertheim et al. 2015). Relaxed selection has been observed within the genomes of facultative and obligate γ-proteobacteria endosymbionts of insects when compared to their free-living relatives (Wertheim et al. 2015). Furthermore, the shift to a vertically inherited, asexual lifestyle can result in reduced rates of recombination and lead to the accumulation of deleterious mutations via Muller's Rachet (Muller 1964).

Gene loss and genomic minimization are also common features of the switch to a symbiotic lifestyle (Silva et al. 2001), as genes encoding now superfluous metabolic pathways (Osvatic et al. 2023), or those involved in DNA regulation or repair (Shigenobu et al. 2000), are lost from the symbionts genome. Gene loss occurs mainly through the accumulation of deleterious mutations that compromise gene function while still being retained in the genome (van der Burgt et al. 2014; Esfeld et al. 2018).

Ophiocordyceps are mostly entomopathogenic fungi that have evolved highly complex life histories that target, control and kill their host (Kobmoo et al. 2018). Many species are highly host-specific, with species complexes evolving via unique mechanisms to infect their host (Evans et al. 2011; Beckerson et al. 2023). However, in soft-scale insects (Hemiptera: Coccomorpha) Ophiocordyceps have undergone multiple independent shifts from a pathogenic to an endosymbiont lifestyle, in which they are inherited vertically (Szklarzewicz et al. 2021). Vertically inherited Ophiocordyceps live within the lipid rich storage tissues of their scale insect host and upon female maturation and egg production migrate to the ovum to infect eggs (Szklarzewicz et al. 2021). Despite this, the fitness impacts on both host and endosymbiont that are associated with the transition symbiotic relationship are unknown. Therefore, the Ophiocordyceps species that host within soft-scales insect cells have been described as “fungal associates” in past literature (Szklarzewicz et al. 2021) and we opt to follow this nomenclature here.

In this study, long-read sequencing was used to produce a highly contiguous genome from a single representative individual of the soft-scale insect, Parthenolecanium corni (Coccomorpha: Coccidae), which also captured the genome of an associated Ophiocordyceps symbiont. Analysis of the Pa. corni genome identified multiple structural rearrangements and a decrease in the frequency of the conserved Mariner repeat family. Phylogenetic reconstruction of the Ophiocordyceps associate confirmed its closest ancestor as Hirsutella rhossiliensis and identified gene loss and signals for intensified selection within the nuclear genome. In contrast, the mitochondrial genome exhibited signals for relaxed selection. Positive selection was identified within three adjacent enzymes catalyzing the conversion of acetoacetate to acetyl-coenzyme A (CoA), suggesting that the Ophiocordyceps associate may have undergone adaptation to exploit its lipid rich new environment. This work provides the first genome assembly of an Ophiocordyceps endosymbiont and suggests that although the Ophiocordyceps associate is adapting to exploit its intracellular environment, the retention of many virulence associated genes suggests that it may still be operating parasitically.

Results

Assembly of DNA Sourced From a Single Scale Individual Recovers Contiguous Genomes for Pa. corni and a Putative Ophiocordyceps Associate

Sequencing the total genetic content within a single insect allows bacterial and/or fungal symbionts to be co-assembled (Ward and Baxter 2017). Due to the small size of soft-scale insects (<4 mm), recovery of sufficient high molecular weight DNA was challenging; however, sufficient DNA (∼190 ng) was recovered for sequencing on a Nanopore P2 flow-cell, which produced 76.1 Gb of long read data (>q10, 71.9 Gb simplex; 4.2 Gb duplex). After removing shorter reads (<500 bp), 62 Gb (N50 of 8.05 kb) was retained for metagenomic assembly, resulting in a 261.1 Mb assembly that was distributed across 1,331 contigs. This assembly was subsequently binned into eight metagenome assembled genomes (MAGs) that varied in size from 212 Mb to 220 kb (Fig. 1a, supplementary table 1, Supplementary Material online). Combining MAGs based on taxonomic classification of the bins identified a single Insecta/Hemipteran and two Fungal genomes (Fig. 1a) that were predicted to belong to the genera Parthenolecanium (Hemiptera), Aureobasidium (Dothideales), and Ophiocordyceps (Hypocreales), respectively (Fig. 1a). Aureobasidium growth is known to be promoted by honey dew secretion and therefore reads were likely present as a contamination within the metagenome assembly.

Duplex and simplex reads that mapped to the Ophiocordyceps and Pa. corni MAGs were then extracted and used to perform assemblies for each organism, producing two highly contiguous genomes that were contained within 6 and 85 contigs, respectively. Assessment of duplex and simplex 21-mer depth revealed two clear peaks in the approximate location of mapped contig read depth distributions (Fig. 1b). Average read depth of reads mapped back to their respective MAG was found to be 246× for Pa. corni and 48× for its Ophiocordyceps associate.

The 21-mer distribution was then used to determine the estimated genome size of each of the MAGs. The difference between recovered and estimated genome size provides insight into over clustering or bin misassignment during the assembly and separation stages. Calculating the area under each local maxima estimated the genome size of Ophiocordyceps to be 33 and 212 Mb for Pa. corni, compared to the 27.7 and 227.8 Mb recovered genomes (Fig. 1c), suggesting that the genomes for both Pa. corni and its Ophiocordyceps associate were well recovered. Overlap in the read depth distributions between bin 4 and bin 3 likely contributes to the difference between the k-mer estimated and observed genome size.

Structural Rearrangements and Decreased Repetitive Elements in the Pa. corni Genome

Analysis of completeness of the Pa. corni genome revealed similar levels of BUSCO gene presence relative to genome assemblies of other coccoid species (supplementary fig. 1, Supplementary Material online) with 85.6% single-copy, 5.5% duplicated (91.1% complete), and 6.8% missing BUSCOs in the Pa. corni genome. Gene annotation of the Pa. corni genome identified 14,951 protein coding genes, which is well within the estimated 10,000 to 30,000 present in most insects (Adams et al. 2000; Holt et al. 2002; Honeybee Genome Sequencing Consortium 2006; International Aphid Genomics Consortium 2010; Ward et al. 2021).

Clustering the Pa. corni genes with other publicly available coccoid genomes (supplementary table 2, Supplementary Material online) identified 23,726 orthogroup clusters containing 86.3% of all genes within the gene space, 951 of which represented single-copy orthologs present across all genomes. Phylogenetic reconstruction of the Pa. corni single-copy orthologs against the background of mealybug and scale insect coccoid species identified the related scale insect Ericerus pela as the closest sequenced relative (Fig. 2a). Scale insects were found to be in reciprocal monophyly with mealybugs (Fig. 2a), agreeing with past literature (Vea and Grimaldi 2016). Synteny analysis of contigs >1 Mb in length (n = 24, 97% of total genome size) from the Pa. corni genome against the chromosome level Planococcus citri (mealybug) genome identified almost no macrosynteny at the chromosome level (Fig. 2b). To determine if this was due to misassembly in the Pa. corni genome, duplex Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) reads were mapped against the Pa. corni assembly. One hundred and sixty-nine putative structural variants (SVs) were identified within the Pa. corni genome and manual investigation of read alignments surrounding these loci confirmed 13 heterozygous indels. No evidence for widespread misassembly was observed with called major SVs (e.g. inversions and translocations) being caused by mapping errors (supplementary fig. 2, Supplementary Material online).

Repeat modeling of the Pa. corni genome identified 1,881 DNA, 38 LINE, 268 SINE, and 1,364 long terminal repeat (LTR) repetitive elements (Fig. 2c), which represents a far lower repeat content than the estimates performed on either its nearest neighbor, E. pela (Yang et al. 2019) or the model Hemipteran, Acyrthosiphon pisum (International Aphid Genomics Consortium 2010). For example, Mariner repeats are present throughout Hemiptera (Filée et al. 2015; Bouallègue et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2019) and while 812 were observed in the E. pela repeat annotation (Yang et al. 2019), assembled Pa. corni genome was found to only have 193.

The Ophiocordyceps Associate of Pa. corni Has Undergone Gene Loss in Conserved Orthologs

Ophiocordyceps and Hirsutella are synonymous genera, with Hirsutella being used to refer to the anamorphic, asexual life stage of this fungus (Simmons et al. 2015), rather than the two genera representing phylogenetically distinct lineages. Phylogenetic reconstruction of the Ophiocordyceps associate of Pa. corni (referred to as OAc) and publicly available Ophiocordyceps (Hirsutella) and Cordyceps genomes (supplementary table 3, Supplementary Material online) identified H. rhossiliensis as the closest sequenced relative to OAc, with high posterior support (Fig. 3). OAc, H. rhossiliensis, Ophiocordyceps sinensis, and Hirsutella minnesotensis formed a monophyletic clade separate from all other Ophiocordyceps genomes (Fig. 3; Clade 1), suggesting that this lineage shared a most recent common ancestor (MRCA) separate from other Ophiocordyceps species.

Analysis of the completeness of each genome revealed similar levels of BUSCO gene presence across all the sequenced Ophiocordyceps species (supplementary fig. 3, Supplementary Material online) with 96% single-copy, 0.9% duplicated, and 2.7% missing BUSCOs identified in the OAc genome. EukCC was also utilized to further investigate the completeness of the Ophiocordyceps genome estimating 99.92% completeness and 0% contamination. The proportion of genes assigned to orthogroups was also similar across the sequenced Ophiocordyceps, with no species having a significant number of species-specific genes that were not able to be grouped (Fig. 3). Of the 7,357 predicted gene models in OAc, 7,247 were successfully assigned to an orthogroup (total number of orthogroups = 10,690) with at least one other Ophiocordyceps or Cordyceps gene model and the single-copy orthogroups present in all species totaled 2,319.

Gene gain and loss are essential sources of genetic variation, having been observed to occur in many fungi as an adaptation to novel environmental stressors (Osvatic et al. 2023). To investigate gene loss in OAc, two complementary approaches, a birth–death based gene family estimation and a pangenomic analysis, were applied. First birth–death based gene family size was estimated across the phylogeny utilizing all orthogroups present in the MRCA of Cordyceps and Ophiocordyceps (n = 7,376). OAc was observed to have the third highest contraction rate (n = −1,017), behind Ophiocordyceps australis and O. sinensis, of all species within the phylogeny (Fig. 1a). This suggests that OAc may have undergone gene family contraction since its MRCA with H. rhossiliensis. OAc was found to have a similar genome size to gene content relationship as other Ophiocordyceps species outside of Clade 1 (supplementary fig. 4, Supplementary Material online). Despite this, within Clade 1, OAc was found to have both smallest genome size and fewest numbers of genes (supplementary fig. 4, Supplementary Material online), further supporting the hypothesis that OAc has undergone genome shrinkage.

Second, a pangenomic approach was utilized to investigate gene loss in the OAc lineage across all 10,690 orthogroups. To reduce the effect that taxon specific gene gain has on the analysis, only orthogroups with genes present in H. rhossiliensis, O. sinensis, and H. minnesotensis were considered (n = 5,255). 524 orthogroups were found to be present in H. rhossiliensis, O. sinensis, and H. minnesotensis (Clade 1, Fig. 3) but absent from the OAc annotation (Fig. 3). Of these 524 orthogroups, 159 were present in all other taxa throughout the phylogeny (Fig. 3), suggesting that they are likely to play important roles in Hypocreales biology. Orthogroups present in all other taxa, but absent from OAc, are therefore referred to as the “conserved set” throughout the remainder of the text. Investigating gene loss specific to each taxon across all orthogroups, OAc was found to have the highest rate of gene loss relative to all other Ophiocordyceps species except O. sinensis (supplementary fig. 5, Supplementary Material online), consistent with the CAFE estimations above. Functional annotation of the H. rhossiliensis orthologs that were absent in OAc revealed roles in amino acid and xenobiotic metabolism, genetic information processing, and environmental signal processing (supplementary fig. 6, Supplementary Material online). Furthermore, gene loss was observed across the fungal mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, with genes required for proper hyphal/cell wall growth absent from OAc genome, including WSC1, RHO1, YPD1, and SSN6, which have all been implicated in hyphal generation and/or cell wall integrity (Verna et al. 1997; Hwang et al. 2003; Sohn et al. 2003; Krysan et al. 2005; Richthammer et al. 2012). Enrichment analysis of KEGG annotations within the “conserved set” showed no significantly enriched pathways. However, when considering all orthogroups present within other Clade 1 taxa, significant enrichment (supplementary table 4, Supplementary Material online) of oxidative phosphorylation (ko00190, P-value = 0.0027) and biodegradation of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450 (ko00980, P-value = 0.0083) was observed.

Gene family size reduction without complete loss was investigated using CAFE to identify orthogroups that have undergone contraction specific to the OAc lineage. Five orthogroups were observed with OAc specific contraction since the MRCA of Clade 1 (supplementary table 5, Supplementary Material online). These included three orthogroups, which contained domains (collagenase, lysin motif domain, glycoside hydrolase family 18 [Chitinase], and catalytic domain), linked to fungal pathogenicity (Bolton et al. 2008; Chen et al. 2020) and one orthogroup, which contained a domain (secreted thaumatin-like protein cetA-like) linked to conidial germination (Belaish et al. 2008).

Toxin and Hydrolase Gene Loss along the OAc Branch

Toxic proteins and compounds, such as enterotoxins and mycotoxins, allow pathogenic species of Ophiocordyceps to kill or negatively impact their infected host (Lacadena et al. 2007; Kobmoo et al. 2018). To investigate if loss of toxin producing orthogroups occurred during the transition to an associate life history in OAc, orthogroups without an OAc ortholog were queried for genes involved in toxin production and pathogenicity. This identified 10 orthogroups related to toxin production that were absent from the OAc annotation, which ranged from nonconserved (present in few taxa) to highly conserved (present in all taxa other than OAc) throughout Ophiocordyceps (Fig. 4a). Two highly paralogous conserved orthogroups that were both predicted to encode heat labile enterotoxins (HLEs) were found to be absent from OAc (Fig. 4a; HLE). Four orthogroups that were predicted to encode proteins containing either aflatoxin (Fig. 4a; AfT) or mycotoxin (Fig. 4a; MyT) domains were also absent from the OAc lineage, along with other toxin domain containing orthogroups including diphtheria-like toxin-like (Fig. 4a; OG0000073) and ribotoxin (Fig. 4a; OG0008875).

Hydrolases, such as chitinases and glycoside hydrolases, have also been shown to play an important role in entomopathogenicity (Sánchez-Pérez et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2020) through breaking down structural components of the insect and disrupting host immune response (Sánchez-Pérez et al. 2014). Therefore, hydrolase (chitinase, serine protease, glycoside hydrolase, and lipase) gene loss was investigated in OAc utilizing the same method as toxin genes. This identified OAc specific loss within eight hydrolase orthogroups (chitinase = 1, lipase = 5, glycosyl hydrolase = 3; Fig. 4b); however, no serine proteases were found to be lost.

Intensified and Positive Selection are Operating on the Ophiocordyceps Associate Genome

The transition of life history from an entomopathogenic fungus to an associate likely requires complex changes in gene function and transcription. To understand the selection landscape of this transition, orthogroups were constructed using only species present in Clade 1 (Fig. 3) to maximize the number of nucleotide single-copy orthogroups for testing, identifying 2,711 single-copy orthogroups for further analysis (supplementary table 6, Supplementary Material online). The effect of mutational saturation on the analysis was investigated by estimating Ti:Tv for each species pair. Ti:Tv ranged from 1.03 to 1.67 and showed a correlation with genetic distance between the same pair (supplementary fig. 7, Supplementary Material online) suggesting minor levels of mutational saturation may have occurred since the crown of Clade 1.

The intensity of selection quantifies the relative strength of purifying and positive selection along a branch compared to background branches. Genome wide scans for relative changes in the intensity of selection in OAc against the background of nonassociate Ophiocordyceps branches within Clade 1 revealed 1,120 (41.3%) orthologs that had k parameter values suggestive of intensified selection (k ≥ 1.1). In addition, 912 (34.4%) orthologs had values consistent with relaxed selection (k ≤ 0.9) (Fig. 5a and b), while only 631 orthologs (23.3%) had a near-identical strength of selection (1.1 ≥ k ≥ 0.9) across the Clade 1 (Fig. 5b). After testing for significant differences in the strength of selection between the OAc and background Clade 1 Ophiocordyceps branches, 408 orthologs were identified that displayed a significant signal of either relaxed selection (n = 198) or intensification (n = 310) (Fig. 5b, supplementary table 6, Supplementary Material online). This suggests that intensified selective pressure, rather than relaxed, occurred more frequently in the OAc lineage since its separation from its most MRCA with H. rhossiliensis.

Differences in function between genes under significant intensified or relaxed selection were then investigated. To achieve this, genes with KEGG annotations (supplementary table 7, Supplementary Material online) within each group (n intense = 161, n relax = 77) were extracted. In the intensified gene set, significant enrichment of KEGG pathways (supplementary table 8, Supplementary Material online) were observed in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (supplementary fig. 8, Supplementary Material online; ko04621, P-value = 0.00084) along with carbon metabolism (supplementary fig. 9, Supplementary Material online; ko01200, P-value = 0.018) specifically in the carbohydrate metabolism (ko00051, P-value = 0.0091) and reductive pentose phosphate cycle (ko00710, P-value = 0.0057) pathways. RNA degradation (supplementary fig. 10, Supplementary Material online; ko03018, P-value = 0.045), spliceosome (supplementary fig. 10, Supplementary Material online; ko03040, P-value = 0.025) and MAPK signaling (supplementary fig. 11, Supplementary Material online; ko04011, P-value = 0.029) pathways were also enriched within the intensified set suggesting a clear role for selection within transcription and extracellular stress related genes. However, KEGG pathways significantly enriched (supplementary table 9, Supplementary Material online) in the relaxed set were within the phagosome (ko04145, P-value = 0.026) and endocytosis (ko04144, P-value = 0.032) pathways, along with cAMP (ko04024, P-value = 0.014) and Ras signaling pathways (ko04014, P-value = 0.034).

Although intensified selection was found to be common across the OAc genome, this does not provide insight into the type of selection (positive or purifying). Therefore, the OAc genome was investigated for signatures of positive selection. ω (dN/dS) was calculated separately along the OAc terminal branch (Fig. 5c; Foreground; ω2) and all other branches in Clade 1 (Fig. 5c; Background; ω1) for each single-copy orthogroup (supplementary table 10, Supplementary Material online). Comparing ω1 and ω2 values for each orthogroup revealed signals for elevated ω along the OAc terminal branch (ω2) compared to the background (ω1) (Fig. 5c). Testing the foreground branch for positive selection in each single-copy ortholog identified 97 genes with a significant signal of selection (P-value ≤ 0.05) along the OAc lineage after its MRCA with H. rhossiliensis (Fig. 5c). After filtering out orthologs with significant (P-value ≤ 0.05) signal for positive selection across background branches, 82 genes were identified as displaying OAc specific positive selection. Of the positively selected genes, 32 genes had KEGG pathway annotations and contained functions involved in metabolism, cellular signaling, and genetic information processing. Enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways under positive selection (supplementary table 11, Supplementary Material online) identified significant enrichment within the fatty acid metabolism (supplementary fig. 12, Supplementary Material online; ko01212, P-value = 0.0077), valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation (supplementary fig. 13, Supplementary Material online; ko00280, P-value = 0.010) and butanoate metabolism (supplementary fig. 14, Supplementary Material online; ko00650, P-value = 0.027) pathways.

Within the fatty acid metabolism pathway, significant (P-value ≤ 0.05) signals for positive selection were detected within the acetoacetate metabolism (supplementary fig. 15, Supplementary Material online). The three genes (acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase, acetyl-CoA acyltransferase 1, and succinyl-CoA-3-oxaloacid CoA transferase), which are involved in catalyzing the reactions from acetyl−CoA↔acetoacetyl−CoA↔acetoacetate in the presence of CoA, showed clear signals for positive selection that were absent from the background branches (supplementary fig. 15, Supplementary Material online). The observed positive selection within the fatty acid degradation pathway coupled with experimental evidence that the associate is localized within lipid rich tissue inside Pa. corni (Szklarzewicz et al. 2021), suggests that the associate is undergoing adaptive evolution, driven by the nutrient availability of its new environment when compared to its pathogenic sister species.

The functional annotation of orthogroups under intensified/relaxed and positive selection was queried to identify those related to toxin or hydrolase activity. Due to selection testing being carried out on only single-copy orthogroups (n = 2,711), only a minority of toxin or hydrolase related orthogroups were investigated for selection (n single copy orthogroups: HLE = 1, mycotoxin = 0, ribotoxin = 0, chitinase = 2, serine protease = 8, lipase = 18, and glycosyl hydrolase = 34). No HLEs, mycotoxins, ribotoxins, or chitinase orthogroups were found to be under significant selection. Significant intensified selection was observed within one serine protease, two lipase and five glycosyl hydrolase orthogroups (supplementary table 12, Supplementary Material online). Significant relaxed selection was observed across two lipase and two glycosyl hydrolase orthogroups (supplementary table 12, Supplementary Material online). Positive selection was observed within a single lipase orthogroup (OG0002055).

Twenty-three gene duplications were identified that were specific to the OAc lineage in the Clade 1 orthogroup set. Tests for positive selection on tandemly duplicated paralogs revealed four OAc duplications with significant (P-value ≤ 0.05) signal for positive selection. Duplicated paralogs had increased omega values compared to all other lineages within their respective gene trees (Fig. 5d), suggesting that they may be undergoing neofunctionalization. Gene function of putatively neofunctionalized genes included the Zinc Finger Protein 598 (OG0001049), TPR-like (OG0002189), fungal specific transcription factor (OG0002481), and DNA repair protein MutS (OG0002645) domain containing proteins.

In contrast to intensified selection across nuclear orthogroups (Fig. 5a), mitochondrially encoded genes exhibited frequent relaxed selection along the OAc lineage (Fig. 5e). Six mitochondrial genes exhibited significant (P-value ≤ 0.05) signal for relaxed selection (NAD2, COX1, COX2, COX3, and ATP8) whereas only two (NAD6 and NAD3) showed significant intensification. However, the majority (n = 7) of mitochondrial genes were found to lack significant signal for disparate strength of selection (Fig. 5e).

Discussion

Long-read genome assembly of small insects is difficult due to the low amount of total DNA within individuals. To extract sufficient DNA researchers generally pool multiple individuals from the same clutch to carry out sequencing and assembly (International Aphid Genomics Consortium 2010; You et al. 2013; Yang et al. 2019). Although pooling individuals can benefit read length through the ability to carry out size selection, variant reads and those spanning recombination breakpoints can result in fragmented assemblies (You et al. 2013). In this study, genome assembly was performed from a single individual of Pa. corni producing contiguous assemblies for the insect and its Ophiocordyceps fungal associate.

Scale insects have a diverse set of reproductive life histories ranging from parthenogenic to sexual. Pa. corni is thought to have a parthenogenic life history, although males have been observed to exist within populations (Gimingham 1934; Santas 1985) and sexual reproduction is suggested to occur. In this study, almost no heterozygous SVs were observed, suggesting that the sequenced genome represents a highly homozygous diploid, with only a small number of minor SVs differentiating the two haplotypes. This may be due to the complexities of non-Mendelian chromosome assortment during reproduction in Pa. corni (Gavrilov and Kuznetsova 2004). Pa. corni exhibits a Comstockiella chromosome system (Gavrilov and Kuznetsova 2004; Gavrilov 2007), whereby paternally inherited chromosomes are condensed into a heterochromatic body in somatic cells and then eliminated (Hodson et al. 2023). This, coupled with a suggested founders effect (Ward et al. 2023), may explain the limited genetic diversity present in the sequenced genome. Chromosome number (1n) variability is high in scale insects (Coccidae), with various scale species estimated to have 5 to 18 chromosomes (Gavrilov 2007), compared to 4 to 32 chromosomes in mealybugs (Pseudococcidae) (Gavrilov 2007). High levels of structural rearrangements were observed between the chromosome level genome of the mealybug Pl. citri and the Pa. corni genome sequenced in this study, with low levels of macrosynteny observed between the two taxa. Taken together this suggests that the chromosome evolution of scale insects and mealybugs are exceptionally complex, and more research is needed to more fully understand their evolutionary and population genomics.

Changes in gene function when transitioning life histories are well recorded, with strong selective pressures being imposed across the genome as the transitioning organism is exposed to novel stressors (Schluter et al. 1991; Kelly and Griffiths 2021). Symbionts are no exception with rapid diversification of key mechanisms such as metabolism and growth being observed (Brownlie et al. 2007; Wernegreen 2015). While adapting to life as an obligate, relaxed selection is generally observed in the genome as the symbiont becomes shielded from many of the selective pressures that affect their free-living sister taxa (Wernegreen 2011; Wertheim et al. 2015). In the Ophiocordyceps associate of Pa. corni (OAc), frequent intensification of selection was observed across the genome; enriched within immune response, carbon metabolism, and transcriptional regulation pathways. The transition to an endosymbiont life history occurred before the species diversification of the Parthenolecanium genus, with all species of Parthenolecanium containing an endosymbiont (Szklarzewicz et al. 2021). Intensified selection along the OAc branch may be explained by the complexity of the transition to an endosymbiont life history, as the associate must first prevent host death while rapidly switching to vertical inheritance. Despite this, pathogenic fungi already have the ability for survival inside the insect body at the early stage of infection (Kobmoo et al. 2018), potentially requiring fewer alterations in cell growth and response to abiotic and biotic stimuli to occur.

Gene loss and genome minimization are a common occurrence during the transition to an associate or symbiotic lifestyle (Moran and Mira 2001; Fan et al. 2015; Osvatic et al. 2023). Loss was observed in the Ophiocordyceps associate across many conserved genes with putative function in metabolism, cell wall integrity, and gene regulation. During the adaptation to an intracellular or endosymbiotic life history, microbes have been shown to co-opt fundamental nutrients from the host (Fisher et al. 2017). For example, the fungal endosymbiont of plant hoppers (Homoptera: Delphacidae) has been shown to aid in host sterol metabolism (Noda and Koizumi 2003; Body et al. 2013; Oliver 2023). During the transition to an associate life history in Ophiocordyceps, it was expected that evolutionary changes would occur in genes involved in fundamental pathways such as toxicity, cell growth, and sexual reproduction. In line with these predictions, gene loss was observed across genes involved in hyphal growth and cell wall integrity as part of the fungal MAPK signaling pathway, suggesting that filamentous growth may not be necessary in this Ophiocordyceps associates. Therefore, the Ophiocordyceps associate of Pa. corni may have reverted to a yeast-like life history as has been observed in other independent transitions in fungal insect endosymbionts (Suh et al. 2001).

Pathogenicity or host control traits in Ophiocordyceps are suggested to be underpinned by complex interactions between toxin-related genes and the host organism (Kobmoo et al. 2018; Will et al. 2023a, 2023b). Gene loss was observed in multiple highly paralogous toxin and hydrolase related genes including HLEs, which have been suggested to play an important role in Ophiocordyceps pathogenicity (Kobmoo et al. 2018). Ribotoxins also play a key role in entomopathogenicity of Ophiocordycipitaceae and other fungal taxa (Lacadena et al. 2007; Foresman and Tartar 2023), which are thought to avoid exoskeleton drilling by favoring the production of ribotoxins early in the infection process (Foresman and Tartar 2023). However, only a single highly conserved ribotoxin gene was observed to be lost from the genome of the associate indicating that widespread loss of this gene family has not occurred. This suggests that not all genetic pathways that are key to entomopahtogenicity were lost and that OAc might still operate through a continuous spectrum of antagonism–commensalism–mutualism (Drew et al. 2021). While negative fitness effects may be imposed by retained entomopahtogenicity related genes, the Ophiocordyceps associate may be providing positive fitness effects to the host under specific environmental conditions, as has been described in Wolbachia endosymbionts (Chrostek et al. 2013). Future work should investigate the fitness effects of Ophiocordyceps associate infection through curing experiments under diverse environmental conditions.

Positive selection provides an evolutionary mechanism for neofunctionalization of preexisting genes (Brownlie et al. 2007; Murrell et al. 2012). The selection analysis carried out in this study was limited by its reliance on single-copy orthologs present within Clade 1 taxa, capturing only 37% of the total genes present within the Ophiocordyceps associate. Despite this, the Ophiocordyceps associate of Pa. corni has undergone positive selection in multiple important mechanisms, such as genetic information processing, potentially changing the transcriptional landscape. In addition to changes in transcriptional control we observed positive selection across three sequential enzymes in the acetoacetate metabolism pathway. Although this change may be in response to the production of acetoacetate by the associate for uptake by Pa. corni, the presence of the Ophiocordyceps within the lipid bodies of Pa. corni (Szklarzewicz et al. 2021) may suggest that the Ophiodordyceps associate is co-opting extracellular acetoacetate from the Pa. corni host. Furthermore, relaxed selection was observed within the OAc mitochondrial genome. This provides support that the Ophiocordyceps associate may have a different energy source or reduced energy requirements relative to their pathogenic ancestors.

This study investigated the evolutionary landscape during the shift from pathogenic to endosymbiont life history in the OAc. These results highlight metabolism and transcription as important targets for reprograming during this transition. To achieve this, a long-read genome assembly was produced for Pa. corni, representing the first Coccoidea species assembled from a single individual, revealing divergent transposable element frequencies compared to other Hemipterans. Contiguous assembly of Pa. corni allowed co-recovery of a chromosome level assembly for its Ophiocordyceps associate producing the first genome sequence for a nonpathogenic Ophiocordyceps. Investigation into the evolutionary history of the transition to an associate life history identified widespread gene loss of conserved orthogroups involved in toxicity, pathogenicity, and transcriptional regulation. Despite this, OAc retains many pathogenicity related genes suggesting that OAc may still operate in antagonism to its host. The selective landscape of the nuclear genome was intensified since the separation of OAc from its closest pathogenic relative, contrasted with relaxed mitochondrial selection. Furthermore, positive selection was observed that was specific to OAc in genetic information processing and metabolism. Taken together these results suggest gene loss, transcriptional reprograming, and acetoacetate metabolism are important mechanisms when adapting to an intracellular, endosymbiont life history in this Ophiocordyceps species.

The Ophiocordyceps genome and insights produced in this study highlight the importance of sequencing complete genomes for fungal endosymbionts of insects. Future work should focus on assembling Ophiocordyceps endosymbionts that have undergone independent life history transitions to determine if convergent gene loss has occurred and if similar orthogroups are under selection in other, independently derived lineages.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection, DNA Extraction, and Sequencing

A single Pa. corni individual was collected from a grapevine in the Langhorne Creek wine region of South Australia. The Pa. corni individual was inspected under a stereomicroscope and washed using 95% ethanol and brushed three times to remove potential environmental contaminants. DNA was then extracted via gentle homogenization of the insect tissue using a glass rod in lysis buffer followed by 24-h proteinase-K digestion, RNAse A incubation, a modified phenol–chloroform extraction (Ward et al. 2021) and glycogen/isopropanol precipitation at 4 °C. DNA molecular weight and quality was determined using a TapeStation (Agilent) at the Australian Genome Research Foundation. A total of 222 ng of DNA was obtained and used to prepare a nanopore library using the SQK-LSK114 kit (Oxford Nanopore). The library was loaded into a FLO-PRO114M flow-cell and sequenced in a P2-Solo instrument. Basecalling was performed using Guppy v.6.4.6 with the SUP (r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup) model. Duplex paired reads were identified using duplex-tools and re-basecalled using Guppy. After quality filtering (Q > 10) a total sequencing yield of 76.17 Gb (71.98 simplex and 4.19 Gb of duplex reads) was obtained.

k-mer Analysis

Simplex reads were used to carry out k-mer analysis using Canu v2.2 (Koren et al. 2017) to investigate 21-mer depth distribution and estimate ploidy and genome size. Genome size estimation was carried out by calculating the area under the curve for local maxima present in the 21-mer depth distribution (bioinformatics.uconn.edu/genome-size-estimation-tutorial/).

Genome Assembly and Separation

Metagenome assembly was carried out using Meta-Flye v2.9.3 (Kolmogorov et al. 2020) using the combined simplex and duplex reads under default settings. Combined simplex and duplex reads were then mapped to the metagenome using Minimap2 v2.26 (Li 2018) with the map-ont preset. Mapped reads and the metagenome were then passed to MetaBat2 v2.15 (Kang et al. 2019) for metagenome binning. A combined approach was used to determine if binned subgenomes should be combined. First, binned subgenome average read depth and variance were compared to the 21-mer depth distribution; second, contig guanine/cytosine content distribution was compared between bins; third, a custom database containing all insect, fungal, and bacterial proteins on NCBI was constructed and contigs were queried using Kaiju v1.10.0 (Menzel et al. 2016), EukCC v v2.1.0 (Saary et al. 2020), and the BUSCO v4.1.2 (Seppey et al. 2019) Insecta and Fungi datasets. NgsReports v2.4 (Ward et al. 2020b) was then used to visualize and investigate BUSCO outputs to identify differences in completeness between organisms.

Simplex and duplex fastq files were then mapped separately to the complete metagenome with Minimap2 v2.26 (Li 2018) using the map-ont preset without allowing for secondary mapping. Reads with a MAPQ ≥ 20 were extracted from their respective fastq files and separated into subgenome fraction (i.e. Parthenolecanium or Ophiocordyceps subgenome) using seqtk v75 (https://github.com/lh3/seqtk). The Parthenolecanium and Ophiocordyceps derived reads were then assembled separately using Verkko v2.0 (Rautiainen et al. 2023) by providing duplex reads as the “high accuracy” and simplex reads as the “low accuracy” long-read arguments. The two Verkko assemblies were then combined along with Meta-Flye contigs that were not assigned to Parthenolecanium or Ophiocordyceps. All simplex and duplex reads were then mapped to the Meta-Flye/Verkko assembly and contig error correction was carried out using medaka (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka). Finally, corrected contigs were reassigned into Parthenolecanium or Ophiocordyceps derived genomes with SeqKit v2.7.0 (Shen et al. 2016).

BUSCO v4.1.2 (Seppey et al. 2019) was run at each stage of the assembly process using the Fungi and Insecta databases to determine single-copy ortholog fraction being recovered and identify potential contamination.

Gene Prediction and Annotation of the Coccoidea and Ophiocordyceps Genomes

Gene prediction was carried out on the Pa. corni assembly and publicly available Coccoidea genomes without gene models (supplementary table 1, Supplementary Material online) using the braker2 pipeline (Brůna et al. 2021) providing all Arthropod OrthoDB (Kriventseva et al. 2019) orthogroups and Hemipteran proteins available on NCBI to generate hints for annotation.

The Ophiocordyceps genome was annotated using the Funannotate pipeline (10.5281/zenodo.2604804) by passing Ophiocordyceps proteins along with the SwissProt and UniProt databases to generate hints. Gene predictions were then functionally annotated for all genomes using the Funannotate (10.5281/zenodo.2604804) annotation function and Interproscan v5.59_91.0 (Jones et al. 2014). Gene ontology (GO) terms were generated using the IPR domain to GO term table available through GO (https://current.geneontology.org/ontology/external2go/interpro2go). KEGG terms were then generated using KEGG Mapper (Kanehisa and Sato 2020) based on Cordyceps militaris pathways and pathway maps were constructed using KEGG Reconstruct (Kanehisa 2017).

Analysis of the Parthenolecanium Genome

Synteny analysis between the Pa. corni genome and Pl. citri (GCA_950023065.1) was carried out using MCScanX (Wang et al. 2012) under default settings using contigs >1 Mb in total length. Orthogroups were identified between Pa. corni and publicly available Coccomopha genomes (supplementary table 1, Supplementary Material online) protein annotations using OrthoFinder v2.2.7 (Emms and Kelly 2019) under default settings.

Repeat annotation was carried out by first modeling denovo repeats in the genome using RepeatModeler v2.0.5 (Flynn et al. 2020) with the RepBase database 28.05 (Jurka et al. 2005) and RepeatMasker databases (Chen 2004). Repeats were then annotated and masked in the genome using RepeatMasker v4.1.5 (Chen 2004).

Structural variation was characterized within the Pa. corni genome by mapping the duplex reads to the Pa. corni sub genome with Minimap2 v2.26 (Li 2018). Mapped reads were then called using Sniffles v2.2 (Sedlazeck et al. 2018) to identify SVs and integrative genomics viewer (Thorvaldsdóttir et al. 2013) was used to manually inspect read mapping around these loci.

Analysis of the Ophiocordyceps Genome

Orthogroups were identified between Ophiocordyceps and Cordyceps genomes (supplementary table 2, Supplementary Material online) protein annotations using OrthoFinder v2.2.7 (Emms and Kelly 2019) under default settings. A species tree was then constructed by combining individual single-copy ortholog gene trees using ASTRAL-III v5.7.1 (Zhang et al. 2018). Gene loss analysis was carried out by parsing the OrthoFinder orthogroups output to identify genes present in the closest taxa, H. rhossiliensis, O. sinensis, and H. minnesotensis, as identified using phylogenetic analysis. Two different levels of gene loss were then assessed by extracting genes using geaR v1.0 (Ward et al. 2020a). First, genes present in all species within the same clade and second those that are present in all assessed Ophiocordyceps and Cordyceps genomes. GO and KEGG terms of lost genes were determined by first annotating the H. rhossiliensis genome using the same methodologies as the Ophiocordyceps associate genome above. Annotations were then parsed for their GO and KEGG pathways. Enrichment analysis of GO terms was carried out using the R package clusterProfiler v3.18 (Yu et al. 2012). KEGG “K” terms were converted to “ko” pathway terms using the bitr_kegg() function and enrichment was carried out using the enricher() function with genes under relaxed, intensified or positive selection tested against the entire gene spaces “ko” annotation separately.

Toxin and hydrolase related genes were determined using a combination of P-fam, SuperFamily, and InterPro domains for each gene family. Domain annotation codes used can be found in supplementary tables 13 and 14, Supplementary material online.

CAFE v5.1 (Mendes et al. 2021) was used under default settings to estimate gene family expansion/contraction on protein based orthogroups. Each orthogroup was treated as a separate gene family for the analysis and the species tree in Fig. 3 was used for estimations.

Selection Testing

Single-copy orthologs between the Ophiocordyceps associate of Pa. corni (OAc), O. sinensis, H. rhossiliensis, and H. minnesotensis were identified using OrthoFinder v2.2.7 (Emms and Kelly 2019) and translation informed codon alignment was carried out with translatorX v1.1 (Abascal et al. 2010). Gene alignments were trimmed with TrimAl v1.5 (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009) to remove gaps. Trimmed alignments were then manually inspected to identify genes with abnormally high genetic distance in Geneious (Kearse et al. 2012) removing 34 genes before selection testing. Ti:Tv ratios and genetic distance was calculated for each single copy orthogroup using the APE R package (Paradis and Schliep 2019). Gene alignments were then passed to HyPhy v2.5.59 (Pond et al. 2005) for positive selection testing using the BUSTED model (Murrell et al. 2015). Two positive selection hypotheses were tested. First, only the OAc branch was tested for positive selection for each gene utilizing the species topology. Second, all other internal and terminal branches excluding the OAc terminal branch were tested for positive selection. Genes were flagged as under positive selection if a statistically significant signal (P-value ≤ 0.05) was observed in the OAc terminal branch without signal (P-value > 0.05) detected across all other branches.

Intensification/relaxation of selection was calculated using RELAX model (Wertheim et al. 2015) in HyPhy v2.5.59 (Pond et al. 2005) utilizing the gene alignments of OAc, Ophiocordyceps sine, H. rhossiliensis, and H. minnesotensis single-copy orthologs. The OAc terminal branch was set to the foreground and evolutionary rate was determined against all other internal and terminal branches.

Duplicated orthogroups were identified from the nucleotide OrthoFinder output. Orthogroups that were found to be duplicated in only OAc were tested for positive selection using the ABSREL model (Smith et al. 2015) in HyPhy v2.5.59 (Pond et al. 2005). Each terminal branch was tested for selection and signal for positive selection was considered if significant signal (P-value ≤ 0.05) in either one or both OAc paralogues.

Mitochondrial selection was investigated by using the mold codon model in HyPhy v2.5.59 (Pond et al. 2005) with the ABSREL (Smith et al. 2015) and RELAX (Wertheim et al. 2015) models following the same methodology as nuclear genes.
