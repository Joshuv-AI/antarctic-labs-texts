Revealing tensions in neutron star observations with pressure anisotropy
Peter T. H. Pang, Stephanie M. Brown, Thibeau Wouters, Chris Van Den Broeck

Abstract
Pressure isotropy, i.e., radial pressure equal to tangential pressure, is often assumed when studying neutron stars. However, multiple physical mechanisms, including pion/kaon condensation, magnetic fields, superfluidity, dark matter clustering, and violations of general relativity, can lead to a pressure anisotropy. This work presents a comprehensive measurement of pressure anisotropy in neutron stars. Our analysis incorporates both an extensive set of nuclear experimental constraints and multi-messenger astrophysical observations, including gravitational wave detections and electromagnetic observations. The Bayesian framework employed marginalizes over equation of state uncertainties and allows the anisotropy to vary between individual neutron stars. We find the Bayes factor for anisotropy against isotropy is e 0.46 = 10 0.20 = 1.58 ≳ 3 : 2 e^{0.46}=10^{0.20}=1.58\gtrsim 3:2 . Additionally, the posterior indicates a population-wide preference for negative pressure anisotropy, with GW170817 serving as the primary contributor. The individual variability suggests density-scale-independent anisotropy, potentially attributable to magnetic fields, dark matter clustering, or deviations from general relativity rather than phase transitions. While the evidence for pressure anisotropy remains inconclusive, these results demonstrate that pressure anisotropy can be utilized as a valuable diagnostic tool for identifying missing physics in neutron star modeling or revealing tension among observations in the era of multi-messenger astronomy.

Second, we incorporate a comprehensive list of nuclear experimental constraints and utilize an extensive collection of neutron stars observed through both gravitational and electromagnetic channels. Third, we employ a robust, state-of-the-art Bayesian framework that allows us to make statistically strong conclusions. Fourth, we allow the anisotropy to vary between different neutron stars rather than assuming a single value for the population. This choice breaks the degeneracy between anisotropy and the equation of state. Finally, we do not assume any particular physical origin but instead use anisotropy as a generic tool for probing this sector of physics.

, speed-of-sound parameterizations
(

II.1.1
Metamodel

Our equation-of-state model varies across different density regimes within neutron stars. At low densities up to
0.08\ {\rm fm}^{-3}
, we employ the crustal equation of state from
F. Douchin & P. Haensel 2001
. Above the crust-core transition, we assume the matter consists purely of nucleonic degrees of freedom in
\beta
-equilibrium and apply a Metamodel parametrization
(
J. Margueron et al. 2018a
;
J. Margueron et al. 2018b
;
R. Somasundaram et al. 2021
)
.

The Metamodel framework expresses the energy per nucleon as a systematic expansion in the isospin asymmetry,

\displaystyle e(n,\delta)=e_{\rm{sat}}(n)+e_{\rm{sym}}(n)\,\delta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4}),

(1)

where
n
is the baryon number density and
\delta=(n_{n}-n_{p})/n
is the isospin asymmetry, with
n_{n}
and
n_{p}
representing the neutron and proton number densities, respectively.

Both the saturation energy
e_{\rm{sat}}(n)
and symmetry energy
e_{\rm{sym}}(n)
are expressed as Taylor expansions around saturation density
n_{\rm sat}
using the dimensionless variable
x=(n-n_{\rm{sat}})/3n_{\rm{sat}}
, which in this work are truncated at second order:

\displaystyle e_{\rm{sat}}(n)

\displaystyle=E_{\rm{sat}}+\frac{1}{2}K_{\rm{sat}}x^{2}+\mathcal{O}(x^{3})\,,

(2)

\displaystyle e_{\rm{sym}}(n)

\displaystyle=E_{\rm{sym}}+L_{\rm{sym}}x+\frac{1}{2}K_{\rm{sym}}x^{2}+\mathcal{O}(x^{3})\,.

(3)

Throughout this work, we fix the saturation energy coefficient
E_{\rm{sat}}=-16\ {\rm MeV}
and the saturation density
n_{\rm{sat}}=0.16\ {\rm{fm}}^{-3}
. The remaining parameters
E_{\rm sym}
,
L_{\rm{sym}}
,
K_{\rm{sym}}
, and
K_{\rm sat}
are referred
to as the nuclear empirical parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\rm NEP}
.

The pressure and energy density are given by,

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=n^{2}\frac{\partial e}{\partial n},

(4)

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=ne.

For the case of symmetric matter (
\delta=0
), the saturation pressure becomes,

p_{\rm sat}(n)=n^{2}\frac{\partial e_{\rm sat}}{\partial n}=\frac{n^{2}}{3n_{\rm sat}}K_{\rm sat}x,

(5)

while the symmetry pressure is,

p_{\rm sym}(n)=n^{2}\frac{\partial e_{\rm sym}}{\partial n}=\frac{n^{2}}{3n_{\rm sat}}\left(L_{\rm sym}+K_{\rm sym}x\right).

(6)

This Metamodel framework offers an intuitive approach to incorporating laboratory constraints on nuclear matter properties, as detailed in Sec.
II.4
.

II.1.2
Metamodel + peak

While the Metamodel provides a flexible description of nucleonic matter, it is expected to break down when new degrees of freedom become relevant at higher densities
(
J. M. Lattimer & M. Prakash 2006
;
M. Oertel et al. 2016
;
A. Kumar et al. 2023
)
. To capture the potential emergence of exotic matter phases, we extend the Metamodel using a hybrid approach similar to that described in
S. K. Greif et al. 2019
.

The construction of this model has two parts. We first employ the Metamodel parametrization up to a break-off density
n_{\rm break}=1.5\ n_{\rm sat}
, then transition to a speed-of-sound-based description for higher densities. For densities
n>n_{\rm break}
, we parameterize the speed-of-sound squared as,

\displaystyle c^{2}_{s}

\displaystyle=c^{2}_{s,{\rm break}}+\frac{\frac{1}{3}-c^{2}_{s,{\rm break}}}{1+e^{-l_{\rm sig}(n-n_{\rm sig})}}+c^{2}_{s,{\rm peak}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n-n_{\rm peak}}{\sigma_{\rm peak}}\right)^{2}},

(7)

where
c^{2}_{s,\text{break}}
is the speed-of-sound at the transition density, determined by the Metamodel.

This parameterization incorporates two physically motivated components.
The first term is a sigmoid function that captures the asymptotic approach toward the conformal limit
c_{s}^{2}=1/3
at ultra-high densities predicted by perturbative QCD calculations
(
T. Gorda et al. 2018
;
T. Gorda et al. 2021
;
T. Gorda et al. 2023
)
. This term is characterized by a stiffness parameter
l_{\rm sig}
and a midpoint density
n_{\rm sig}
that control the rate and location of the transition.
The second term is a Gaussian peak designed to capture intermediate-density features inferred from astrophysical observations
(
I. Legred et al. 2021
;
S. Huth et al. 2022
;
P. T. H. Pang et al. 2021
;
P. T. H. Pang et al. 2024
;
H. Koehn et al. 2025
)
. This peak is defined by its maximum amplitude
c^{2}_{s,{\rm peak}}
, center location
n_{\rm peak}
, and width
\sigma_{\rm peak}
.

The pressure and energy density in the high-density regime are constructed by integrating the speed-of-sound,

\displaystyle\log\mu

\displaystyle=\log\mu_{\rm break}+\int^{n}_{n_{\rm break}}c^{2}_{s}\,d\log n^{\prime},

(8)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=p_{\rm break}+\int^{n}_{n_{\rm break}}c^{2}_{s}\mu\,dn^{\prime},

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\mu n-p,

where
\mu
is the chemical potential and quantities with subscript “break” are evaluated at
n_{\rm break}
using the Metamodel, ensuring continuity across the transition.

Throughout this work, this hybrid construction, i.e., employing the Metamodel below
n_{\rm break}
and the speed-of-sound parameterization above
n_{\rm break}
, is referred to as the
Metamodel + peak
approach.

II.2
Pressure anisotropy

To model the pressure anisotropy, we employ the commonly used anisotropic parameter
\sigma
, which is defined as

\sigma\equiv p-p_{t},

(9)

where
p
is the radial pressure and
p_{t}
is the tangential pressure. The inclusion of this parameter leads to a modification of the standard Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) equations. The anisotropic structure equations for static, spherically symmetric neutron stars are
(
A. Rahmansyah et al. 2020
)
:

\displaystyle\frac{dm}{dr}

\displaystyle=4\pi r^{2}\epsilon,

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{dr}

\displaystyle=-\frac{m\epsilon}{r^{2}}\frac{\left(1+\frac{p}{\epsilon}\right)\left(1+\frac{4\pi r^{3}p}{m}\right)}{1-\frac{2m}{r}}-\frac{2\sigma}{r},

where
\epsilon
is the energy density,
r
is the radial coordinate, and
m(r)
is the enclosed mass within radius
r
. The stellar radius
R
is defined where the radial pressure vanishes,
p(R)=0
, and the mass is
M=m(R)
.

For binary neutron stars, the component stars experience the tidal force generated by their companion. The dimensionless tidal deformability characterizes the response of a neutron star to external tidal fields,

\Lambda=\frac{2}{3}k_{2}C^{-5},

(11)

where
k_{2}
is the second tidal Love number and
C=M/R
is the compactness.

To determine
k_{2}
, we consider static, azimuthally symmetric metric perturbations of the form

\displaystyle ds^{2}=

\displaystyle-e^{2\Phi(r)}\left[1+H(r)Y_{20}(\theta,\varphi)\right]dt^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle+e^{2\mu(r)}\left[1-H(r)Y_{20}(\theta,\varphi)\right]dr^{2}

\displaystyle+r^{2}\left[1-K(r)Y_{20}(\theta,\varphi)\right]\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\varphi^{2}\right).

The perturbation function
H(r)
satisfies the modified tidal equation
(
A. Rahmansyah et al. 2020
)
:

\displaystyle H\Bigg[e^{2\mu}\left(-\frac{6}{r^{2}}+\frac{4\pi(p+\epsilon)\left(1+\frac{d\epsilon}{dp}\right)}{1-\frac{d\sigma}{dp}}+4\pi(8p+4\epsilon)\right)

(13)

\displaystyle+16\pi\sigma e^{2\mu}-(\Phi^{\prime})^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle+\frac{dH}{dr}\left[\frac{2}{r}+e^{2\mu}\left(\frac{2m}{r^{2}}+4\pi r(p-\epsilon)\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{d^{2}H}{dr^{2}}=0.

The second Love number is then given by

\displaystyle k_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{8C^{5}}{5}(1-2C)^{2}\left(2+2C(y-1)-y\right)

(14)

\displaystyle\times\Big[2C\left(6-3y+3C(5y-8)\right)

\displaystyle+4C^{3}\left(13-11y+C(3y-2)+2C^{2}(1+y)\right)

\displaystyle+3(1-2C)^{2}\left(2-y+2C(y-1)\right)\log(1-2C)\Big]^{-1},

where
y=RH^{\prime}(R)/H(R)
.

To solve the coupled system of Eqs. (
10
) and (
13
), we require closure relations connecting the radial pressure, energy density, and anisotropic parameter. For the radial pressure, we employ the barotropic equation of state models (Metamodel and Metamodel + peak) described in Sec.
II.1
. The anisotropic parameter is modeled following
D. D. Doneva & S. S. Yazadjiev 2012
:

\sigma=\gamma\frac{2mp}{r},

(15)

where
\gamma
is a free parameter quantifying the anisotropy strength, with
\gamma=0
corresponding to the isotropic case. This anisotropy model is preferred over the other proposed models (e.g.,
R. L. Bowers & E. P. T. Liang 1974
;
L. Herrera & W. Barreto 2013
), since it satisfies the energy conditions of a perfect fluid and is symmetrically valid for both positive and negative anisotropies
(
A. M. Setiawan & A. Sulaksono 2019
)
, which facilitates an unbiased search for anisotropies in observations.

Fig.
1
illustrates the impact of non-zero pressure anisotropy on neutron star structure. Notably, a positive anisotropy, i.e., the radial pressure exceeds the tangential pressure, leads to smaller yet stiffer neutron stars. In contrast, a negative anisotropy tends to produce larger but softer stars.

)
, which is a TOV-solver with support for GPU acceleration based on

II.3
Bayesian statistics

According to Bayes’ theorem, the posterior probability
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|d,\mathcal{H})
on parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
under the hypothesis
\mathcal{H}
and conditioned on the data
d
is given by

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{\theta}|d,\mathcal{H})=\frac{P(d|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathcal{H})P(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{H})}{P(d|\mathcal{H})}\to\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\mathcal{Z}}\,,

(16)

where
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
,
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
,
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and
\mathcal{Z}
are the posterior, likelihood, prior, and the evidence, respectively.
The evidence marginalizes the likelihood with respect to the prior, i.e,

\mathcal{Z}=\int d\boldsymbol{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,.

(17)

To quantify the plausibilities between two hypotheses,
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, one can calculate the odds ratio between them,
\mathcal{O}^{1}_{2}
, given by

\mathcal{O}^{1}_{2}\equiv\frac{P(d|\mathcal{H}_{1})}{P(d|\mathcal{H}_{2})}\frac{P(\mathcal{H}_{1})}{P(\mathcal{H}_{2})}\equiv\mathcal{B}^{1}_{2}\Pi^{1}_{2}\,,

(18)

where
\mathcal{B}^{1}_{2}
and
\Pi^{1}_{2}
are the Bayes factor and prior odds, respectively.
If
\mathcal{O}^{1}_{2}>1
,
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is more plausible than
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, and vice versa. Throughout this work, we set the prior odds to
1
whenever comparing two hypotheses. Therefore, the Bayes factor is equivalent to the odds ratio.

To combine information across multiple independent observations, we adopt a hierarchical Bayesian technique. In this approach, a set of hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is introduced to describe the distribution of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, which are specific to individual observations. We condition the prior on these hyperparameters, i.e.,
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, and assign a hyperprior
\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
.

Given
N
independent experiments
\{d_{i}\}
, the likelihood of the hyperparameters
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int d\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})\,\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\lambda})

(19)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{Z}_{i}\int d\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})\,\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int d\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})\,\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}

where
\mathcal{P}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
denotes the posterior corresponding to the
i
-th observation, obtained using an unconditional prior
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
. Throughout this work, we refer to this as the unconditioned posterior distribution.

Nuclear experimental constraints

Our analysis incorporates the astrophysical observations of neutron stars listed in Tab.
2
. For these observations, the hyperparameters are divided into two parts: the equation of state parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\rm EOS}
and the anisotropy parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\gamma}
.

The equation of state parameterization depends on the chosen model. For the Metamodel approach, the equation of state is fully characterized by the nuclear empirical parameters, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\rm EOS}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\rm NEP}
. For the Metamodel + peak approach, the parameter space expands to include the additional speed-of-sound parameters from Eq. (
7
), i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\rm EOS}=\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\rm NEP},c^{2}_{s,{\rm peak}},n_{{\rm peak}},\sigma_{{\rm peak}},l_{{\rm sig}},n_{{\rm sig}}\}
.

We implement two scenarios to parametrize the anisotropy in the neutron star population. In the “
{\rm identical\ }\gamma
” scenario, all neutron stars share a universal anisotropy parameter
\gamma
, representing a single global parameter characterizing anisotropic strength, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\gamma}=\{\gamma\}
. The “
{\rm independent\ }\gamma
” scenario allows variation between neutron stars by drawing each star’s anisotropy parameter from a population-level hyper-distribution. We model this as a truncated Gaussian with mean
\mu_{\gamma}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{\gamma}
(i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\gamma}=\{\mu_{\gamma},\sigma_{\gamma}\}
), bounded to
\gamma\in[-0.5,0.5]
for numerical stability while permitting both positive and negative anisotropies. This scenario effectively removes the assumption that all neutron stars follow a single
M
-
R
-
\Lambda
relation, while keeping the assumption that all neutron stars are made of the same matter.

IV
Discussion

Figure
3
, along with the Bayes factors in Tab.
4
, shows that allowing anisotropies to vary between neutron stars slightly favors anisotropic models, regardless of the equation of state, with the population exhibiting overall negative anisotropies. By examining the posterior of
\gamma
for individual observations, we conclude that GW170817 is the primary driver of this negative trend. Since negative anisotropy means that the radial pressure exceeds the tangential pressure (see Eq. (
9
)), GW170817 shows weaker radial pressure than expected from nuclear constraints and other neutron star observations.

Since the detailed calculations and follow-up work necessary to determine the cause of this apparent anisotropy are beyond the scope of this work, we instead aim to provide qualitative estimates of whether such phenomena can be explained by various physical mechanisms.

There is stronger statistical evidence for anisotropy when the anisotropy is allowed to vary between neutron stars, and it notably does not correlate with the observed mass scale. GW170817 and PSR J0030+0451, despite having similar mass scales (and equivalently density scales), do not share the same degree of preference for negative anisotropy. This suggests that the anisotropy does not arise from the equation of state, making a phase transition or density-induced superfluidity less likely to be the underlying cause.

In contrast, the impact of strong magnetic fields, clustering of dark matter, and violations of general relativity would not be expected to remain constant across neutron stars or to correlate strongly with mass scale. These mechanisms, therefore, remain viable explanations.

The above discussion represents speculations that require detailed modeling and examination to confirm or dismiss explanations. However, these findings indicate that anisotropy serves as a valuable generic tool for probing missing sectors in multi-messenger neutron star studies. Because anisotropy is not strongly degenerate with equation-of-state parameters, it allows us to probe individual variations and identify potential tensions between observations.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we conducted a comprehensive measurement of pressure anisotropy by utilizing an extensive set of nuclear experimental constraints and astrophysical observations of neutron stars. By employing machine learning techniques to enhance Bayesian analysis, we are able to perform this measurement while effectively marginalizing over the uncertainties of the supranuclear equation of state. Additionally, we arrived at a statistically robust conclusion regarding the existence of this anisotropy.

Our findings indicate that across the various equation of state models considered, there is slight evidence of anisotropy when it is allowed to vary between neutron stars. The posterior analysis suggests an overall negative anisotropy across the population driven by GW170817 rather than by a tension between the nuclear experiments and the astrophysical observations. We speculate that this individual variability points to a density-scale independent anisotropy, which cannot be attributed to a phase transition. Instead, mechanisms such as magnetic fields or violations of general relativity seem more plausible.

However, given the weak statistical evidence, we cannot definitively conclude that anisotropy has been observed. Nevertheless, due to the variety of physical origins for anisotropy and its weak degeneracy with the equation of state, we propose using anisotropy as a tool for probing missing physics in modeling neutron star observations. This approach could help us investigate individual variations and identify potential tensions between observations.

Appendix A
Numerical methods and implementation

A.1
Normalizing flow

A key step in evaluating the likelihood of the hyperparameters is to estimate the unconditioned posterior distribution
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. The posterior distribution is estimated from a given set of posterior samples using
normalizing flow

(
I. Kobyzev et al. 2021
;
G. Papamakarios et al. 2021
)
.

Normalizing flows are a neural network-based method designed to learn a bijection transformation
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a latent distribution
\boldsymbol{z}\sim p_{Z}
, which is typically simple and easy to sample, to the true distribution. The true complicated and unknown distribution
\boldsymbol{x}\sim p_{X}
is represented by samples
\{x_{i}\}
. After training, the model allows efficient sampling from and computation of the true distribution’s probability density, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{z}),

(A1)

\displaystyle p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=p_{Z}(f^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}))\left|\frac{\partial f^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right|.

In order to estimate and sample from the unconditioned posterior distribution, we employ a masked auto-regressive flow
(
D. P. Kingma et al. 2016
;
G. Papamakarios et al. 2017
)
, as implemented in
FlowJAX

(
D. Ward 2024
)
.

A.2
Markov chain Monte Carlo

To obtain posterior samples of the hyperparameters, we use
Markov Chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) sampling, specifically the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. In MCMC parameter estimation, the posterior is approximated by a collection of walkers. The walkers will converge asymptotically to the posterior distribution
\mathcal{P}(\lambda)
.

At each iteration, the sampler proposes a new point
\lambda^{\prime}
based on the current state
\lambda_{t}
and a proposal density
Q(\lambda^{\prime}|\lambda_{t})
. The proposed point is accepted with a probability
a
,

a=\min\left(1,\frac{\mathcal{P}(\lambda^{\prime})}{\mathcal{P}(\lambda_{t})}\frac{Q(\lambda_{t}|\lambda^{\prime})}{Q(\lambda^{\prime}|\lambda_{t})}\right).

(A2)

If the proposal is rejected, the chain remains unchanged.

This process is repeated until the chain has sufficiently explored the posterior distribution. The resulting samples are then treated as representative draws from the posterior distribution. This analysis utilizes the machine learning-enhanced MCMC algorithm implemented in
flowMC

(
K. W. k. Wong et al. 2023
;
M. Gabrié et al. 2022
)
, in which the proposal distribution is trained on the fly using preliminary posterior samples.

A.3
Learned harmonic mean estimator

The
learned harmonic mean estimator

(
A. Polanska et al. 2024
;
D. Piras et al. 2024
;
A. Spurio Mancini et al. 2023
)
is used to estimate the evidence given a set of posterior samples and their corresponding likelihood and prior values. This method offers a scalable, sample-based approach to evidence estimation that is independent of the methodology used to obtain the posterior samples. While the original harmonic mean estimator is known to suffer from instabilities
(
M. A. Newton & A. E. Raftery 1994
)
, the learned version mitigates these issues using machine learning techniques
(
A. Polanska et al. 2024
)
.

In particular, the reciprocal of the evidence,
\mathcal{Z}^{-1}
, is estimated via:

\mathcal{Z}^{-1}=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\left[\frac{\varphi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right],

(A3)

where
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim p(x)}[\cdot]
denotes the sample average under the distribution
p(x)
.
\varphi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
represents a learned, normalized target distribution that is required to concentrate within the true posterior. As demonstrated in
A. Polanska et al. 2024
, normalizing flows offer a robust approach to ensure this concentration condition is met.

We use the machine learning-assisted harmonic mean estimator implemented in
harmonic

(
J. D. McEwen et al. 2024
)
.
