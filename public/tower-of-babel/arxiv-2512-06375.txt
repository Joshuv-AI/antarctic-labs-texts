Extended Argmin-Theorems for multiple nets of multivariate càdlàg stochastic processes
Dietmar Ferger, Niklas Rosar

Abstract
Consider finitely many nets of multivariate càdlàg stochastic processes. We show that the vectors consisting of the respective minimizing points converge in distribution to a random closed set. This set is given as a cartesian product with factors which are equal to the set of all minimizing points of stochastic processes occurring as functional limits of the respective nets. If these limit processes have almost surely exactly one minimizer, then the vectors converge classically in distribution to the vector of these minimizers.

Introduction

Assume that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the random map
\xi_{n}
is a minimizing point of a stochastic process
X_{n}
defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
with trajectories in a
function space
F
endowed with some topology
\mathcal{T}
. If
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in distribution to a limit process
X
, then the question arises what can be said about a possible distributional convergence of the sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and how does the limit variable looks like? Argmin-theorems give answers to that question. Since
\xi_{n}
is a maximizing point of
-X_{n}
, every Argmin-theorem yields an Argmax-theorem and vice versa. The first Argmin-theorem in the above spirit goes back to Kim and Pollard (1990)
[
21
]
. They deal with
\textbf{F}=l^{\infty}(H)
, the space of all locally bounded real-valued functions on
H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the topology
\mathcal{T}
of uniform convergence on compacta. Here, the problem occurs that the stochastic processes
X_{n}
need not to be measurable maps into
l^{\infty}(H)
endowed with the Borel-
\sigma
algebra
\sigma(\mathcal{T})
.
As a way out, Kim and Pollard
[
21
]
use the general concept of distributional convergence in the sense of Hoffmann-Jørgensen (1998)
[
20
]
, where no measurability is required. This type of convergence is denoted by

X_{n}\rightsquigarrow X\;\text{ in }l^{\infty}(H).

(1)

It is (for general metric spaces
H
) equivalent to, e.g.

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{*}(X_{n}\in O)\geq\mathbb{P}(X\in O)\quad\text{for all open }O\subseteq l^{\infty}(H),

(2)

where
\mathbb{P}_{*}
is the inner probability of
\mathbb{P}
. See Theorem 1.3.4 in van der Vaart and Wellner
[
35
]
for several other equivalent characterisations of (
1
).
From (
2
), one can immediately see how the Hoffmann-Jørgensen-convergence
\rightsquigarrow
depends on the underlying topology
\mathcal{T}
on
l^{\infty}(H)
. Therefore, we occasionally write more precisely
X_{n}\rightsquigarrow X\;\text{ in }(l^{\infty}(H),\mathcal{T})
.
Suppose that in addition to
(\ref{Hoffmann})
, the sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is stochastically bounded, i.e

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}^{*}(||\xi_{n}||>k)=0,

(3)

where
||\cdot||
denotes any norm on the euclidian space
H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{P}^{*}
is the outer probability of
\mathbb{P}
.
If moreover
X
possesses an unique minimizing point
\xi
with probability one, then Kim and Pollard
[
21
]
prove that

\xi_{n}\rightsquigarrow\xi\;\text{ in }H.

(4)

van der Vaart and Wellner (1996)
[
35
]
extend this result from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a metric space
H
. In their proof, which is much simpler than that of Kim and Pollard
[
21
]
, they use that (
1
) is equivalent to the convergence of the restrictions
X_{n}\rightsquigarrow X\;\text{ in }l^{\infty}(K)
for all compact
K\subseteq H
, confer Theorem 1.6.1 in
[
35
]
. However, they have to pay a small price for the more general set up, because the assumption (
3
) must be replaced by uniform tightness of
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, i.e. for each
\eta>0
there exists a compact
K\subseteq H
such that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}^{*}(\xi_{n}\notin K)\leq\eta.

(5)

Indeed, in metric spaces
H
closed and bounded sets are in general not compact, so that the requirement (
5
) is stronger than the generalisation of (
3
) to metric spaces. (Of course, when
H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
they are equivalent.)
It should be mentioned that
[
21
]
and
[
35
]
both consider more generally
\xi_{n}
, which are
\epsilon_{n}
-optimal solutions, i.e.
X_{n}(\xi_{n})\leq\inf_{t\in H}X_{n}(t)+\epsilon_{n}
with
(\epsilon_{n})
converging to zero in probability. Clearly, if
\epsilon_{n}=0
, then
\xi_{n}
is a minimizer of
X_{n}
.

These two Argmin-theorems are no longer applicable, when the limit process
X
has more than one minimizer with positive probability.
This situation is far away to be pathological as it frequently occurs in applications. It typically arises in the analysis of regression functions with a
break-point. Here, the limit
X
turns out to be two-sided compound Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}
with drift upwards, which in general has a finitely union of compact intervals as its set of all minimizing points. See for instance, Pons (2003)
[
30
]
, Kosorock and Sen (2007)
[
22
]
, Kosorock (2008)
[
23
]
, Lan et al. (2009)
[
25
]
, Ferger and Klotsche (2009)
[
9
]
or Albrecht (2020)
[
1
]
. Notice, that a compound Poisson process with upwards drift has with probability one a smallest and largest minimizing point, say
\sigma
and
\tau
, which do not coincide:
\sigma<\tau
. In Ferger (2004)
[
11
]
we give a first solution for
H=\mathbb{R}
in the non-unique case. Under
(\ref{Hoffmann})
and
(\ref{stochasticallybounded})
it is shown that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}^{*}(\xi_{n}\leq x)\leq\mathbb{P}(\sigma\leq x)

(6)

and

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{*}(\xi_{n}<x)\geq\mathbb{P}(\tau<x),

(7)

where
\sigma
and
\tau
are the smallest and largest minimizer of
X
. These results carry over without problems to

\textbf{F}=C(\mathbb{R})
, the space of all continuous real functions on
\mathbb{R}
equipped with the topology of uniform convergence on compacta.
Here, the convergence in (
1
) reduces to usual distributional convergence, i.e.

X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}X\quad\text{ in }C(\mathbb{R}).

(8)

Moreover, the outer and inner probability occurring in (
3
), (
6
) and (
7
) can be replaced by
\mathbb{P}
, because all participating maps are measurable. Notice that similarly as for
\textbf{F}=l^{\infty}(H)
the requirement (
8
) is equivalent to
the convergence of the restrictions on compact intervals:

X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}X\quad\text{ in }C[-a,a]\quad\text{for all }a>0,

(9)

confer Theorem 5 in Whitt (1970)
[
38
]
. This is useful to know, because there are several sufficient conditions for (
9
), confer
Theorems 8.1 and 8.2 and especially the nice moment-criterion in Theorem 12.3 in Billingsley (1968)
[
4
]
.

The space
\textbf{F}=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of all sequences
f=(f(k):k\in\mathbb{Z})
with the product topology occurs naturally in change-point analysis. Here, the counterpart of (
1
) is the convergence of the finite dimensional distributions of
X_{n}
to
X
.
Ferger
[
11
]
shows this and (
3
) for a class of change-point estimators.
As a result we obtain limit theorems of type (
6
) and (
7
) without asterisks (by measurability as above), where the limit process
X
is a two-sided random walk on the integers.

Now, in the above mentioned examples from regression analysis the trajectories of the involved processes live in the space
D(\mathbb{R})
of all
right-continuous functions
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with left limits endowed with the Skorokhod-topology.
Also in this case Ferger
[
11
]

proves the following Argmin-theorem: If the restrictions of the
X_{n}
to compact intervals
[-a,a]
converge, i.e.

X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}X\quad\text{ in }D[-a,a]\quad\text{for all real }a>0,

(10)

and
\xi_{n}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
(stochastical boundedness), then

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n}\leq x)\leq\mathbb{P}(\sigma\leq x)\text{ and }\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n}<x)\geq\mathbb{P}(\tau<x).

(11)

Analogously to continuous stochastic processes (
10
) is equivalent to
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}X\text{ in }D(\mathbb{R})
, confer Theorem 16.7 in Billingsley
[
5
]
. As a consequence, one can use e.g. the easy to handle criterion in Theorem 13.5 in Billingsley (1999)
[
5
]
to ensure (
10
).

If
\mathcal{O}_{<}:=\{(-\infty,x):x\in\mathbb{R}\}\cup\{\emptyset,\mathbb{R}\}
and
\mathcal{O}_{>}:=\{(x,\infty):x\in\mathbb{R}\}\cup\{\emptyset,\mathbb{R}\}
denote the left-order topology and right-order topology on
\mathbb{R}
, then we obtain from (
6
) and (
7
) that

\xi_{n}\rightsquigarrow\sigma
in
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{O}_{>})
and
\xi_{n}\rightsquigarrow\tau
in
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{O}_{<})
. Recall that our notation expresses that
\mathcal{O}_{>}
and
\mathcal{O}_{<}
are the respective underlying topologies. For
\textbf{F}\in\{D(\mathbb{R}),C(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}\}
one gets
\xi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}\sigma
in
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{O}_{>})
and
\xi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}\tau
in
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{O}_{<})
, i.e. traditional distributional convergence, however not with respect to the natural topology
\mathcal{O}_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}
, but with respect to the weaker topologies
\mathcal{O}_{>}
and
\mathcal{O}_{<}
.
Assume that
\mathbb{E}[\sigma]=\mathbb{E}[\tau]
as for instance when
\sigma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}\tau
. Then actually
\sigma=\tau
almost surely (a.s.), since
0=\mathbb{E}[\tau]-\mathbb{E}[\sigma]=\mathbb{E}[\tau-\sigma]
and
\tau-\sigma\geq 0
by definition. So,
X
has a unique minimizing point a.s. if and only if
\sigma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}\tau
. In particularly, in this situation

\xi_{n}\rightsquigarrow\sigma
in
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{O}_{>})
and
\xi_{n}\rightsquigarrow\sigma
in
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{O}_{<})
, whence
by Example 3.5 in Ferger (2024)
[
14
]
,
\xi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}\sigma
in
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{O}_{n})
so that we obtain classical convergence in distribution.

In
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with dimension
d\geq 2
the notion of smallest and largest minimizer is not given a priori.
Theorem 3.2 of Seijo and Sen (2011)
[
33
]
goes exactly in this direction for
\textbf{F}=D(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the multivariate Skorokhod-space. In Definition 2.4
[
33
]
they introduce the functionals sargmin and largmin, which by using the idea of lexicographic order give a smallest and largest minimizing point of a function
f\in D(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
X_{n}
and
X
with some specific trajectories in
D(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and so-called associated jump processes
\Gamma_{n}
and
\Gamma
it is shown that if
(X_{n},\Gamma_{n})
converges in distribution to
(X,\Gamma)
and
(\mbox{sargmin}(X_{n}),\mbox{largmin}(X_{n}))=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1),
then in fact

(\mbox{sargmin}(X_{n}),\mbox{largmin}(X_{n}))\rightsquigarrow(\mbox{sargmin}(X),\mbox{largmin}(X))\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(12)

An alternative approach to Seijo and Sen
[
33
]
that does not use the concept of smallest and largest minimizer in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is introduced in Ferger (2015)
[
12
]
.
We prove that, if
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}X
in
D(\mathbb{R}^{d})
endowed with the multivariate Skorokhod-topology, then

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n}\in K)\leq\mu(K)\quad\text{ for all compact }K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},

(13)

where
\mu(K)=\mathbb{P}(A(X)\cap K\neq\emptyset)
and
A(X)
is equal to the set of all minimizing points of the process
X
.
The set-function
\mu
is called
capacity functional
of the random closed set
A(X)
. In particularly, it is a
Choquet-capacity
.
Now, to every Choquet-capacity
\mu
there exists a random closed set
C
such that
\mu(K)=\mathbb{P}(C\cap K\neq\emptyset)
for all compact
K
. Further, every Choquet-capacity can be extended to the Borel-
\sigma
algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mu(B)=\mathbb{P}(C\cap B\neq\emptyset)
for all Borel-sets
B
, where
\{C\cap B\neq\emptyset\}\in\mathcal{A}
. The extension in general is not a probability measure, because it lacks
\sigma
-additivity. For facts on random closed sets and Choquet-capacities we refer to Molchanov (2017)
[
26
]
. By Proposition 3.3 in Ferger
[
12
]

\mu
is a probability measure if and only if the corresponding random closed set
C
is equal to a singleton
\{\xi\}
almost surely for some random variable
\xi
.

Notice that for (
13
) beside
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}X
no further requirement is needed.
However, if in addition
(\xi_{n})
is stochastically bounded, then (
13
) holds even for all closed sets:

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n}\in F)\leq\mu(F)\quad\text{ for all closed }F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(14)

Since (
14
) formally looks exactly like the characterisation of weak convergence in the Portmanteau-Theorem and moreover
\mu
uniquely corresponds to
A(X)
, we say that the sequence
(\xi_{n})
of
points
converges in distribution to the
set

A(X)
of all minimizing points. Finally,
if
\mu
is a probability measure, then as we have seen above
A(X)=\{\xi\}
a.s., whence

\mu(B)=\mathbb{P}(A(X)\cap B\neq\emptyset)=\mathbb{P}(\{\xi\}\cap B\neq\emptyset)=\mathbb{P}(\xi\in B)\quad\forall\;B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Consequently, (
14
) shows in this case that

\xi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}\xi\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(15)

Notice that
A(X)=\{\xi\}
a.s. means exactly that
X
has a unique minimizing point (namely
\xi
) with probability one.

Actually, in Ferger
[
12
]
we consider more generally non-empty closed sets
\varphi_{n}
, which are a.s. subsets of all minimizing points
A(X_{n})
of
X_{n}
. Then given
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}X
in
D(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it is shown that

\varphi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}A(X)\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{F},\tau_{\text{uF}}),

(16)

where
\mathcal{F}
is the family of all closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\tau_{\text{uF}}
is the upper Fell-topology on
\mathcal{F}.
It follows from the definition of
\tau_{\text{uF}}
(see the next section) in combination with the Portmanteau-Theorem that (
16
) is equivalent to

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{K\in\mathcal{K}^{*}}\{\varphi_{n}\cap K\neq\emptyset\}\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{K\in\mathcal{K}^{*}}\{A(X)\cap K\neq\emptyset\}\Big)\quad\text{for all }\mathcal{K}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{K},

(17)

where
\mathcal{K}
denotes the class of all compact subsets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If in addition the sequence of subsets
(\varphi_{n})
is
stochastically bounded
in the sense that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\varphi_{n}\nsubseteq[-k,k]^{d})=0,

(18)

then

\varphi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}A(X)\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{F},\tau_{\text{uV}}),

(19)

where
\tau_{\text{uV}}
is the upper Vietoris-topology on
\mathcal{F}.
This is the same as

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}}\{\varphi_{n}\cap F\neq\emptyset\}\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}}\{A(X)\cap F\neq\emptyset\}\Big)\quad\text{for all }\mathcal{F}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{F}.

(20)

Finally, if furthermore
A(X)=\{\xi\}
a.s., then

\varphi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}A(X)\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{F},\tau_{F}),

(21)

where
\tau_{F}
denotes the Fell-topology. When comparing the convergence results (
16
), (
19
) and (
21
) notice that
\tau_{\text{uF}}\subseteq\tau_{\text{uV}}
and
\tau_{uF}\subseteq\tau_{F}
, so that the statements become stronger each time.

Consider the special case that
\varphi_{n}=\{\xi_{n}\}
. Then the choice
\mathcal{K}^{*}=\{K\}
in (
17
) immediately gives (
13
). Similarly,
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{F\}
in (
20
) directly yields (
14
).

In stochastic optimisation it is convenient to work with the space
\textbf{F}=S(H)
of all lower semicontinuous functions
f:H\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
equipped with the epi-topology
\mathcal{T}_{e}
, confer e.g. Salinetti and Wets (1986)
[
32
]
, Pflug (1992, 1995)
[
28
,
29
]
, Vogel (2005, 2006)
[
36
,
37
]
or Gersch (2006)
[
17
]
. For
\epsilon\geq 0
let
A(f,\epsilon)
be the set of all
\epsilon
-optimal solutions. Assume that

X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}X\quad\text{in }(S(H),\mathcal{T}_{e}),

(22)

where
H
is a locally compact second countable Hausdorff-space. If moreover

\epsilon_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}\epsilon\text{ with }\epsilon\text{ is constant a.s.},

(23)

then by Theorem 5 in Ferger (2025)
[
16
]

A(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}A(X,\epsilon)\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{F},\tau_{\text{uF}})

(24)

with
\mathcal{F}
is equal to the family of all closed subsets in
H
. Next, suppose for every
\eta>0
there exists a compact
K\subseteq H
such that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(A(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})\nsubseteq K)\leq\eta.

Then it follows from (
24
) and Corollary 2.2 in Ferger (2024)
[
15
]
that

A(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}A(X,\epsilon)\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{F},\tau_{\text{uV}}).

(25)

By Proposition 2.1 and Remark 2.3 in Ferger
[
15
]
we know that
(
24
) and (
25
) also hold for non-empty subsets
\varphi_{n}\subseteq A(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})
and that the equivalent characterisations (
17
) and (
20
) are valid analogously.

If (
23
) holds with
\epsilon=0
and
X
has at most one minimizing point
\xi
a.s., then by Theorem 6 and Remark 5 in Ferger
[
16
]

A(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}\{\xi\}\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{F},\tau_{F}).

(26)

Clearly, for the constant sequence
(\epsilon_{n})\equiv 0
, i.e.
A(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})=A(X_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the requirement (
23
) is automatically fulfilled.

As to
\epsilon_{n}
-optimal solutions
\xi_{n}
the special choice
\varphi_{n}:=\{\xi_{n}\}
yields that (
22
) and (
23
) entail

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n}\in K)\leq\mu_{\epsilon}(K)\quad\text{ for all compact }K\subseteq H,

(27)

where
\mu_{\epsilon}
is the capacity-functional of

, and

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n}\in F)\leq\mu_{\epsilon}(F)\quad\text{ for all closed }F\subseteq H,

(28)

provided
(\xi_{n})
is uniformly tight, i.e. (

) holds without asterisks. If in addition
\epsilon=0
and

a.s., then

\xi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}\xi\text{ in }H.

(29)

For the last two statements confer Theorem 7 in Ferger
[
16
]
.

Back to
H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here, the situation becomes very easy when the stochastic processes
X_{n}
and
X
are not only lower semicontinuous, but also convex. Indeed, Ferger (2021)
[

]
shows that then the basic requirement (

) can be replaced by the much weaker assumption

(X_{n}(t_{1}),\ldots,X_{n}(t_{k}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}(X(t_{1}),\ldots,X(t_{k}))\quad\text{in }\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{k}

(30)

for all

, where

is a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
convergence of the finite dimensional distributions on
D
).
But not only this! Also, any condition of stochastic boundedness can be omitted. So, if only (
30
) and (
23
) hold, then (
25
)-(

) follow. In particular,
we obtain as special cases earlier results by Geyer (1996)
[
18
]
, Davis, Knight and Liu (1992)
[

]
and Hjort and Pollard (2011)
[

]
.

Moreover, by Theorem 1.4 in Ferger
[
13
]
there exists a stronger version of (
26
):

A(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})\rightsquigarrow\{\xi\}\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{F},\tau_{V}).

(31)

Here,
\tau_{V}
is the Vietoris topology on
\mathcal{F}
, which is the strongest one among all other hyperspace topologies occurring so far:

\tau_{V}\supseteq\tau_{\text{uV}}
and
\tau_{V}\supseteq\tau_{F}\supseteq\tau_{\text{uF}}
.

In this paper, we consider more generally a multidimensional vector
(\xi_{\alpha,1},\allowbreak\ldots,\allowbreak\xi_{\alpha,k},\allowbreak\sigma_{\alpha})
consisting of
a.s. minimizing points
\xi_{\alpha,j}
of
X_{\alpha}^{(j)},1\leq j\leq k
. Here, each
X_{\alpha}^{(j)}
is a multivariate càdlàg stochastic process
with trajectories in
D(\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}),\;d_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover,
\sigma_{\alpha}
is a random variable in some metric space
S
and the index
\alpha
runs through a directed set
(I,\leq)
. Thus,
(\xi_{\alpha,1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k},\sigma_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in I}
is a net in the product space
H=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}\times S
. Suppose that each
(X_{\alpha}^{(j)})_{\alpha\in I}
is tight and that

(X_{\alpha}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{\alpha}^{(k)},\sigma_{\alpha})\rightarrow_{\text{fd}}(X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(k)},\sigma)
, where
\rightarrow_{\text{fd}}
means convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions. If in addition
(\xi_{\alpha,1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k})_{\alpha\in I}
is stochastically bounded, then we show in Theorem
3
that:

\limsup_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,1}\in F_{1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k}\in F_{k},\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)\leq\mu(F_{1}\times\cdots\times F_{k}\times B)

(32)

for all closed
F_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}},1\leq j\leq k,
and all
\sigma
-continuity-sets
B\subseteq S
. Here,
\mu
is the capacity-functional
of the random closed set

C:=A(X^{(1)})\times\cdots\times A(X^{(k)})\times\{\sigma\}.

That
C
in fact is a random closed set in

H=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}\times S
endowed with the product-topology follows from Theorem 1.3.25 in Molchanov
[
26
]
. Recall that
\mu(E)=\mathbb{P}(C\cap E\neq\emptyset)
for all Borel-sets
E
in
H
. Moreover, it is also proved in Theorem
3
that

\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,1}\in G_{1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k}\in G_{k},\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)\geq\nu(G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{k}\times B)

(33)

for all open
G_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}},1\leq j\leq k,
and all
\sigma
-continuity-sets
B\subseteq S
. Here,
\nu
is the
containment-functional
of
C
, i.e.
\nu(E):=\mathbb{P}(C\subseteq E)
. It is related to
\mu
by
\nu(E)=1-\mu(E^{C})
with
E^{C}:=H\setminus E
the complement of
E
in
H
. Moreover, one sees immediately that

(provided
C
is a.s. non-empty as it is in our case.) Notice that (

) does not follow from (
32
) by complementation.
Comparing (
32
) and (
33
) with the limsup- and liminf-characterization in the Portmanteau -Theorem makes us to say that
the points
(\xi_{\alpha,1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k},\sigma_{\alpha})
,
\alpha\in I
, converge in distribution to the set

, formally written as:

(\xi_{\alpha,1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k},\sigma_{\alpha})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}A(X^{(1)})\times\cdots\times A(X^{(k)})\times\{\sigma\}.

(34)

If
\xi_{\text{min}}^{(j)}:=\text{sargmax}(X^{(j)})
and
\xi_{\text{max}}^{(j)}:=\text{largmax}(X^{(j)})
are the smallest and largest minimizer of

, then in Corollary

we obtain as a special case that

\limsup_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\leq x_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\text{\text{min}}}^{(j)}\leq x_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right),

(35)

and

\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}<x_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\text{max}}^{(j)}<x_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right),

(36)

for all

,
1\leq j\leq k
and for all
\sigma
-continuity sets

. Here, the relations
\leq
and

in the multi-dimensional euclidean space are defined componentwise.

Finally, if each process
X^{(j)}
possesses a.s. an unique minimizing point

, then the limit-set in (

and as a consequence usual convergence in distribution follows, see Corollary

(\xi_{\alpha,1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k},\sigma_{\alpha})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{k},\sigma)\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}\times S.

The adjunction of
\sigma_{\alpha}
in all of our findings might seem artificial. However, in section 3 we show how useful this is
in regression analysis. On the other hand, if in fact

is omitted, then we obtain the corresponding results for

(\xi_{\alpha,1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k})

2
Extended Argmin-Theorems

For a natural number
d
let
X=\{X(t):t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be a real-valued stochastic process defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
with trajectories in the
multivariate Skorokhod-space

D=D(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For the definition of
D
we consider
d
-tuples
R=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})\in\{<,\geq\}^{d}
with the usual relations
<
and
\geq
in
\mathbb{R}
. If
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a point in the euclidean space, then

\displaystyle Q_{R}:=Q_{R}(t):=\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:s_{i}R_{i}t_{i},1\leq i\leq d\}

is the
R-quadrant of t
. Given a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the quantity

\displaystyle f(t+R):=\lim_{s\rightarrow t,s\in Q_{R}(t)}f(s)

is called the
R-quadrant-limit of f at t
. Then
D
consists of all functions
f
such that for each
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

a)

f(t+R)
exists for all
R\in\{<,\geq\}^{d}
,

b)

f(t+R)=f(t)
for
R=(\geq,\ldots,\geq)
.

Relations (
a
) and (
b
) extend the notions ,,limits from below” and ,,continuous from above” from the univariate case (
d=1
) to the multivariate one. Therefore it is convenient to call
f\in D
a
càdlàg function
(continue à droite limite à gauche).
D
endowed with the
Skorokhod-metric
s
is a complete separable metric space, confer Lagodowski and Rychlik (1986)
[
24
]
, p. 332. The pertaining Borel-
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{D}
is generated by the sets of all cylinders, confer Theorem 2 of Lagodowski and Rychlik
[
24
]
. Therefore,
X
can be identified with a random element
X:(\Omega,\mathcal{A})\rightarrow(D,\mathcal{D})
.

Let

\mbox{Argmin}(f)\equiv A(f):=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\min_{R\in\{<,\geq\}^{d}}f(t+R)=\inf_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(s)\},\quad f\in D,

(37)

be the set of all
minimizing points
of
f
. It should be noted that we call a point
t\in A(f)

minimizing
point even though the function
f
in general does not attain its minimal value at that point. One reason for this is that the pertaining lower semicontinuous regularisation
\bar{f}
of
f
is minimized at each
t\in A(f)
and conversely every (proper) minimizer
t
of
\bar{f}
lies in
A(f)
, confer Lemma 2.2 in Ferger
[
12
]
. Therefore,
A(f)
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(possibly empty). So, if the functional
A:D\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
is applied to

X
one obtains a map from
\Omega
into the family
\mathcal{F}
of all closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
including the empty set
\emptyset
:

A(X)=A\circ X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{F}.
We endow
\mathcal{F}
with a topology suitable for our purposes. To this end introduce for every subset
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
the system

\mathcal{M}(C):=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:F\cap C=\emptyset\}
of all
missing sets
of
C

and for later use

\mathcal{H}(C):=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:F\cap C\neq\emptyset\}
of all
hitting sets
of
C
.
Put

\mathcal{S}:=\{\mathcal{M}(K):K\in\mathcal{K}\}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{F}}.

Then the topology on
\mathcal{F}
generated by
\mathcal{S}
is called
upper Fell-topology
and denoted by
\tau_{\text{uF}}
.
It induces the pertaining Borel-
\sigma
algebra
\mathcal{B}_{\text{uF}}:=\sigma(\tau_{\text{uF}})
. Proposition 2.7 in Ferger
[
12
]
shows that

A(X)
is
\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B}_{\text{uF}}
measurable. Such maps are called
random closed set
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
A(X)
is non-empty a.s., then the
Fundamental selection theorem
, confer Molchanov
[
26
]
, guarantees the existence of a Borel-measurable map

\xi:(\Omega,\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\xi\in A(X)
a.s. This random variable
\xi
is called a
measurable selection
of
A(X)
. In particular,
\xi
is a minimizing point of the stochastic process
X
.

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the projection (evaluation map)
\pi_{t}:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined by
\pi_{t}(f):=f(t)
. If
T=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\pi_{T}:=(\pi_{t_{1}},\ldots,\pi_{t_{k}})
. Finally, we introduce

\displaystyle T_{X}:=\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\pi_{t}\text{ is continuous at }X\text{ a.s.}\right\}.

To emphasize the dependence on the dimension
d
we write
D_{d},\mathcal{D}_{d}
and
\mathcal{F}_{d}
for
D,\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{F}
. But for notational convenience, we avoid the index for
A
and
\tau_{\text{uF}}
, the readers should keep in mind that they depend on
d
as well.

Our first result is the starting point for the Extended-Argmin Theorem. It is a generalisation of Theorem 5.1 in Ferger (2010)
[
10
]
.

Proposition 1
.

For finitely many natural numbers

(X_{\alpha}^{(j)})

For notational convenience let

where the limit in (

is continuous at

a.s. For the same reason

is continuous at

From (

\displaystyle\left(\pi_{T}\left(X_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}\right),\pi_{U}(Y_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}),\sigma_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}\right)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\left(\pi_{T}(X),\pi_{U}(Y),\sigma\right)\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{|T|}\times\mathbb{R}^{|U|}\times S

and

. Put

,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{T_{0}}:=\left\{\pi_{T}^{-1}(B):B\in\mathcal{B}\left(\mathbb{R}^{|T|}\right),\,T\subseteq T_{0},\,T\text{ finite}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{U_{0}}:=\left\{\pi_{U}^{-1}(B):B\in\mathcal{B}\left(\mathbb{R}^{|T|}\right),\,U\subseteq U_{0},\,U\text{ finite}\right\}.

Since

[

(46)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\left(A\left(X^{(1)}_{\alpha}\right),\ldots,A\left(X^{(k)}_{\alpha}\right),\sigma_{\alpha}\right)\in\mathcal{M}(K_{1})\times\ldots\times\mathcal{M}(K_{k})\times\mathring{B}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(A\left(X^{(j)}\right)\cap K_{j}=\emptyset,1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in\mathring{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A\left(X^{(j)}\right)\subseteq K_{j}^{\mathrm{C}},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right).

Here, the inequality
\geq
is a consequence of (
43
) and the Portmanteau-Theorem, because

\mathcal{M}(K_{1})\times\ldots\times\mathcal{M}(K_{k})\times\mathring{B}
is open in
\mathcal{F}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{F}_{d_{k}}\times S.
The last equality holds, since
B
is a
\sigma
-continuity-set.

Next, let
I_{j}:=[-a,a]^{d_{j}}
for each
1\leq j\leq k
and
a>0
. Then

\displaystyle\left\{\xi_{\alpha,j}\in G_{j},\xi_{\alpha,j}\in I_{j}\right\}=\left\{\xi_{\alpha,j}\in\left(G_{j}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap I_{j}\right)^{\mathrm{C}}\cap I_{j}\right\},

because
(G_{j}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap I_{j})^{\mathrm{C}}\cap I_{j}=G_{j}\cap I_{j}
. Hence we obtain

\displaystyle\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in G_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)

(47)

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in G_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B,\|\xi_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\leq a\right)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in K_{j}^{\mathrm{C}},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B,\|\xi_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\leq a\right),

where
K_{j}:=G_{j}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap I_{j}
is compact for each
1\leq j\leq k
. Since
\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)\geq\mathbb{P}(A)-\mathbb{P}(B^{\mathrm{C}})
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
, the last limit inferior in (
47
) has the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in K_{j}^{\mathrm{C}},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)-P_{2}(a)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(A\left(X^{(j)}\right)\subseteq K_{j}^{\mathrm{C}},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right)-P_{2}(a)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(A\left(X^{(j)}\right)\subseteq G_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right)-P_{2}(a).

Here, the first inequality holds by (
46
) and the last one holds, because
K_{j}\subseteq G_{j}^{\mathrm{C}}
, whence
K_{j}^{\mathrm{C}}\supseteq G_{j}
. Taking the limit
a\rightarrow\infty
yields (
42
) upon noticing that
P_{2}(a)\rightarrow 0
by (
3
).
∎

Remark 4
.

Notice that for general sets
E_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
the following equivalences
hold:

A(X^{(j)})\cap E_{j}\neq\emptyset,1\leq j\leq k\;\Longleftrightarrow\;A(X^{(1)})\times\cdots\times A(X^{(k)})\cap(E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{k})\neq\emptyset

and

A(X^{(j)})\subseteq E_{j},1\leq j\leq k\;\Longleftrightarrow\;A(X^{(1)})\times\cdots\times A(X^{(k)})\subseteq E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{k}.

Consider
C:=A(X^{(1)})\times\cdots\times A(X^{(k)})\times\{\sigma\}
. We already know that each
A(X^{(j)})
is a random closed set in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}},1\leq j\leq k,
and clearly
\{\sigma\}
is a random closed set in
S
. Thus, by Theorem 1.3.25 in Molchanov
[
26
]

the cartesian product
C
is a random closed set in
H=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}\times S
. Let
\mu
and
\nu
be the capacity-functional and the containment-functional, respectively, of
C
in
H
, i.e.

\mu(E)=\mathbb{P}(C\cap E\neq\emptyset)\;\text{ and }\;\nu(E)=\mathbb{P}(C\subseteq E)\;\text{ for all Borel-sets }E\text{ in }H.

If
P_{\alpha}
is the distribution of
(\xi_{\alpha,1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k},\sigma_{\alpha})
, then our limit-results (
41
) and (
42
) can equivalently be rewritten as

\limsup_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}(F_{1}\times\cdots\times F_{k}\times B)\leq\mu(F_{1}\times\cdots\times F_{k}\times B)

and

\liminf_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}(G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{k}\times B)\geq\nu(G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{k}\times B).

Therefore, we say that the
P_{\alpha}
converge weakly to
(\mu,\nu)
.

Assume that each limit process
X^{(j)}
has a unique minimizing point
\xi_{j}
with probability one. Then
C
simplifies to

C=\{\xi_{1}\}\times\cdots\times\{\xi_{k}\}\times\{\sigma\}
and hence
\mu=\nu=P
. Thus, we may expect weak convergence

P_{\alpha}\rightarrow_{w}P
. The following corollary shows that this is indeed the case

Corollary 5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3
suppose in addition that there are random variables
\xi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
such that
A(X^{(j)})=\{\xi_{j}\}
a.s. for every
1\leq j\leq k
. Then

\displaystyle\left(\xi_{\alpha,1},\ldots,\xi_{\alpha,k},\sigma_{\alpha}\right)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k},\sigma\right)\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\cdots\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}\times S.

(48)

Proof.

Firstly, observe that

\displaystyle\left\{A\left(X^{(j)}\right)\cap F_{j}\neq\emptyset\right\}=\{\xi_{j}\in F_{j}\}\text{ and }\left\{A\left(X^{(j)}\right)\subseteq G_{j}\right\}=\{\xi_{j}\in G_{j}\}.

Now, let
B_{j}
be a
\xi_{j}
-continuity set for
1\leq j\leq k
and
B
a
\sigma
-continuity set. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in\mathring{B}_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in\mathring{B}_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)

by (
42
)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in B_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in B_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in\overline{B}_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in\overline{B}_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right)

by (
41
)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in\mathring{B}_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right).

(49)

To see the last equality put

\displaystyle M_{j}:=\left\{\xi_{j}\in\mathring{B}_{j},\sigma\in B\right\},\qquad N_{j}:=\left\{\xi_{j}\in\overline{B}_{j},\sigma\in B\right\},\qquad 1\leq j\leq k.

Then
M_{j}\subseteq N_{j}
for each
1\leq j\leq k
and consequently

\displaystyle M:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}M_{j}\subseteq\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}N_{j}=:N.

Thus

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(N)-\mathbb{P}(M)=\mathbb{P}(N\setminus M)=\mathbb{P}\Big(N\cap\Big(\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}M_{j}^{\mathrm{C}}\Big)\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\Big(N\cap M_{j}^{\mathrm{C}}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\Big(N_{j}\cap M_{j}^{\mathrm{C}}\Big)\Big)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{j}\setminus M_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(\mathbb{P}(N_{j})-\mathbb{P}(M_{j})\Big)=0,

because every summand vanishes To see this, recall that
\overline{B}_{j}=\mathring{B}_{j}\cup\partial B_{j}
, whence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(N_{j})=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in\overline{B}_{j},\sigma\in B\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in\mathring{B}_{j},\sigma\in B\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in\partial B_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(M_{j}).

This shows the equality (
49
), and further that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in\mathring{B}_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in B_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right),

because
\mathring{B}_{j}\subseteq B_{j}\subseteq\overline{B}_{j}
. So, finally we arrive at

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{\alpha,j}\in B_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma_{\alpha}\in B\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{j}\in B_{j},1\leq j\leq k,\sigma\in B\right),

and the assertion follows from Theorem 2.8 in
[
5
]
, which -as its proof shows- also holds for nets.
∎

3
Application

We consider the following regression setting: Let
X
and
Y
be two real-valued random variables and suppose that there exists a function
m:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
Y=m(X)+\epsilon
, where
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]<\infty
and
\epsilon
denotes a centered stochastic error.
\epsilon
is not necessarily independent of
X
. However, it is assumed to satisfy the weaker condition
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon|X]=0
a.s. In consequence,
m(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y|X]
a.s. and

\displaystyle m\in\argmin\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-g(X))^{2}\right]:g\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),Q)\right\},

(52)

where
Q
is the distribution of
X
. Following the approach in Bühlmann and Yu (2002)
[
7
]
as well as Banerjee and McKeague (2007)
[
2
]
, we do not estimate
m
itself. Rather we want to estimate the minimizer of the
L_{2}
-distance in (
52
) on a smaller domain than
L_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),Q)
. For this purpose, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

\displaystyle\Lambda_{<}^{k}:=\left\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\right\}.

Each tuple of parameters
(t,a)=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k+1})\in\Lambda_{<}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
uniquely determines a step function
g_{(t,a)}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
k
jumps by

\displaystyle g_{(t,a)}(x):=a_{1}\mathbf{1}_{x\leq t_{1}}+a_{2}\mathbf{1}_{t_{1}<x\leq t_{2}}+\ldots+a_{k}\mathbf{1}_{t_{k-1}<x\leq t_{k}}+a_{k+1}\mathbf{1}_{x>t_{k}}.

Suppose there exists exactly one step function
g_{(\tau,\alpha)}
, that minimizes the
L_{2}
-distance in (
52
) among all step functions with
k
jumps, i.e.

\displaystyle(\tau,\alpha)\in\argmin\left\{S(t,a):=\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-g_{(t,a)}(X))^{2}\right]:(t,a)\in\Lambda_{<}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\right\}.

(53)

Notice that

\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-g_{(t,a)}(X))^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[(m(X)-g_{(t,a)}(X))^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon^{2}\right],

whence
g_{(\tau,\alpha)}
is the best-approximation of
m
in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),Q)
among all
k
-step functions.

The estimation of
(\tau,\alpha)=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k+1})
is a parametric problem, even if the regression function
m
is non-parametric. It is assumed that
m
satisfies the assumptions of Chapter 4 in Rosar
[
31
]
, where
m
itself has discontinuities in the jumps of its best
L_{2}
-approximation
g_{(\tau,\alpha)}
. In detail we suppose for each
1\leq j\leq k
that
m
is continuous in an open neighbourhood of
\tau_{j}
with the exception of
m(\tau_{j}-)\neq m(\tau_{j}+)
.

(a)

Exemplary sketch of a regression function and its best
L_{2}
-approximation for
k=2
.

(b)

Decision tree that results from minimizing parameters
(\tau,\alpha)
in (
53
) for
k=2
.

Note that the parameter
(\tau,\alpha)
can be interpreted as a decision tree in the tradition of Breiman (1993)
[
6
]
.
To estimate
(\tau,\alpha)
we use the least squares method: For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq n
, be i.i.d copies of
(X,Y)
. Then we define an estimator
(\tau_{n},\alpha_{n})
as (any) minimizer of the empirical counterpart
S_{n}
of
S
,

S_{n}(t,a):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-g_{(t,a)}(X_{i})\right)^{2},\text{ i.e. }

\displaystyle(\tau_{n},\alpha_{n})=(\tau_{n,1},\ldots,\tau_{n,k},\alpha_{n,1},\ldots,\alpha_{n,k+1})\in A(S_{n}).

(54)

Theorem
3
enables us to derive a limit theorem including convergence rates for
(\tau_{n},\alpha_{n})
in (
54
). We can prove the convergence of

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}n(\tau_{n}-\tau)\\
\sqrt{n}(\alpha_{n}-\alpha)\end{pmatrix}

and identify the limit variable. To do this, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce the
rescaled processes

Z_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in
D(\mathbb{R}^{k})
in the following way:

\displaystyle Z_{n}(t)=n\left\{S_{n}(\tau+n^{-1}t,\alpha_{n})-S_{n}(\tau,\alpha_{n})\right\},

and see from (
54
) that

. Rosar
[
31
]
decomposes for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the multivariate

into the sum of
k

in
D(\mathbb{R}),1\leq j\leq k
, where

\displaystyle n(\tau_{n,j}-\tau_{j})\in A(Z_{n}^{(j)}).

(55)

By Lemma 6.10 in Albrecht
[
1
]
it turns out that for each
1\leq j\leq k
the processes
(Z_{n}^{(j)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge weakly to a certain compound Poisson process
Z^{(j)}
in
D(\mathbb{R})
. The exact form of these
Z^{(j)}
is given in Definition 4.10 in
[
31
]
. This means that (
1
) in Theorem
3
is fulfilled.

Moreover,
\sqrt{n}(\alpha_{n}-\alpha)
converges in distribution to a centered normal vector
W=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{k+1})
, defined in Theorem 4.20 in Rosar
[
31
]
. Here,

Z^{(1)},\ldots,Z^{(k)},W_{1},\ldots W_{k+1}\;\text{ are independent.}

(56)

Among other things, this follows from Lemma 4.22 in Rosar
[
31
]
, which says that

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\Big(\pi_{T}\left(Z_{n}^{(1)}\right),\ldots,\pi_{T}\left(Z_{n}^{(k)}\right),\sqrt{n}(\alpha_{n}-\alpha)\Big)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\left(\pi_{T}(Z^{(1)}),\ldots,\pi_{T}(Z^{(k)}),W\right)

(57)

in
\mathbb{R}^{k\cdot|T|}\times\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
for all finite

.
So, we see that condition (
ii*
) is also satisfied.
As to (
3
), the stochastic boundedness of

holds by Theorem 4.16 in Rosar
[
31
]
, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{a\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|n(\tau_{n}-\tau)\right\|_{\infty}>a\right)=0.

(58)

In conclusion, we can apply Theorem
3
. This leads to Theorem

below.
For its formulation, let
\mu_{j}
and
\nu_{j}
be the capacity functional and the containment-functional, respectively, of the random closed set

, i.e.
\mu_{j}(E)=\mathbb{P}(A(Z^{(j)})\cap E\neq\emptyset)
and
\nu_{j}(E)=\mathbb{P}(A(Z^{(j)})\subseteq E)
for every Borel-set

. Recall that
\mu_{j}
in particularly is a Choquet-capacity and that

Theorem 8
.

Let the assumptions 2.1, 2.2, 2.4, 4.1 and 4.2 in Rosar
[
31
]
be valid. Further, suppose that

\displaystyle(\tau_{n},\alpha_{n})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(\tau,\alpha),\qquad n\rightarrow\infty.

Then

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(n(\tau_{n,j}-\tau_{j})\in F_{j},1\leq j\leq k,n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_{n,i}-\alpha_{i})\in B_{i},1\leq i\leq k+1)\Big)

(59)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{j}(F_{j})\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\mathbb{P}(W_{i}\in B_{i}),

for all closed sets
F_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and for all
W_{i}
-continuity sets
B_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(n(\tau_{n,j}-\tau_{j})\in G_{j},1\leq j\leq k,n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_{n,i}-\alpha_{i})\in B_{i},1\leq i\leq k+1)\Big)

(60)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}(G_{j})\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\mathbb{P}(W_{i}\in B_{i}),

for all open sets
G_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and for all
W_{i}
-continuity sets
B_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Recall that we can apply Theorem
3
. Thus, by (
41
) the upper limit in (
59
) is less than or equal to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A(Z^{(j)})\cap F_{j}\neq\emptyset,1\leq j\leq k,W_{i}\in B_{i},1\leq i\leq k+1)

(61)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}(A(Z^{(j)})\cap F_{j}\neq\emptyset)\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\mathbb{P}(W_{i}\in B_{i}),

where the last equality holds by (
56
). Thus, (
59
) follows from the definition of the capacity functionals
\mu_{j}
.
Similarly, the second part of Theorem
3
says that the lower limit in (
60
) is greater than or equal

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A(Z^{(j)})\subseteq G_{j}\neq\emptyset,1\leq j\leq k,W_{i}\in B_{i},1\leq i\leq k+1)

(62)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}(A(Z^{(j)})\subseteq G_{j}\neq\emptyset)\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\mathbb{P}(W_{i}\in B_{i}).

Now, (
60
) follows immediately from the definition of the containment-functionals
\nu_{j}
.
∎

This convergence result can be used for the construction of confidence rectangles. Here, first of all it should be noted that
Z^{(j)}(t)\rightarrow\infty
a.s as

Lemma 12

\displaystyle B(x,\epsilon)\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left(T_{X_{j}}^{\mathrm{C}}\cap I_{a}\right).

(66)

We know that for each
