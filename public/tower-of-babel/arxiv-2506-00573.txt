Neural Estimation for Scaling Entropic Multimarginal Optimal Transport
Dor Tsur, Ziv Goldfeld, Kristjan Greenewald, Haim Permuter

Abstract
Multimarginal optimal transport (MOT) is a powerful framework for modeling interactions between multiple distributions, yet its applicability is bottlenecked by a high computational overhead. Entropic regularization provides computational speedups via the multimarginal Sinkhorn algorithm, whose time complexity, for a dataset size n n and k k marginals, generally scales as O ⁡ ( n k ) O(n^{k}) . However, this dependence on the dataset size n n is computationally prohibitive for many machine learning problems. In this work, we propose a new computational framework for entropic MOT, dubbed Neural Entropic MOT (NEMOT), that enjoys significantly improved scalability. NEMOT employs neural networks trained using mini-batches, which transfers the computational complexity from the dataset size to the size of the mini-batch, leading to substantial gains. We provide formal guarantees on the accuracy of NEMOT via non-asymptotic error bounds. We supplement these with numerical results that demonstrate the performance gains of NEMOT over Sinkhorn’s algorithm, as well as extensions to neural computation of multimarginal entropic Gromov-Wasserstein alignment. In particular, orders-of-magnitude speedups are observed relative to the state-of-the-art, with a notable increase in the feasible number of samples and marginals. NEMOT seamlessly integrates as a module in large-scale machine learning pipelines, and can serve to expand the practical applicability of entropic MOT for tasks involving multimarginal data.

1.1
Contributions

We develop a methodology for scaling up the entropic MOT (EMOT) computation. Drawing upon recent neural estimation approaches
belghazi2018mutual
;
sreekumar2022neural
;
tsur2023neural
;
wang2024neural
, we propose the neural EMOT (NEMOT) framework, which optimizes an approximation of the dual EMOT objective over a set of neural network parameters. NEMOT estimates both the EMOT cost and transportation plan by harnessing deep learning tools, such as mini-batch gradient-based optimization. This transfers the computational cost of our algorithm to the mini-batch size
b
(as opposed to
n
), which results in a marked increase in scalability compared to multimarginal Sinkhorn. Furthermore, while existing approaches often rely on restrictive assumptions on the cost function (e.g., sparsity) to alleviate some computational burden, NEMOT can handle massive datasets with arbitrary costs. As such, NEMOT can be seamlessly integrated as a module into larger machine learning pipelines as a loss or regularizer.

To facilitate a principled implementation of NEMOT, we provide non-asymptotic bounds on the cost and plan estimation erros. Under mild assumptions, a NEMOT realized by a
m
-neuron shallow ReLU network and
n
data samples from each of the
k
distributions is guaranteed to estimate the cost
\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c}(\bm{\mu}^{k})
within an additive error of
O(\mathsf{poly}(\epsilon^{-1})k(m^{-\frac{1}{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}))
. We further show, that the same bound, up to an extra multiplicative factor of
1/\epsilon
, holds for the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the optimal EMOT plan and its neural estimate. The theory is supplemented by empirical results that demonstrate the computational advantage of NEMOT over the multimarginal Sinkhorn algorithm. NEMOT exhibits speedups in orders of magnitude, unlocking new regimes of possible
(b,n,k)
values for EMOT and applications thereof. We present extensions of NEMOT to the multimarginal Gromov-Wasserstein (MGW) problem, which gained recent popularity for tasks involving alignment of heterogeneous datasets
alvarez2018gromov
;
xu2019gromov
. To that end, we reformulate the MGW as an alternating optimization between NEMOT and certain auxiliary matrices.

1.2
Related Work

MOT has seen increasing applications as a means to alter the distribution of learned representations in a way that enforces criteria across multiple domains. In generative modeling,
cao2019multi
introduces a multimarginal extension of the Wasserstein GAN
arjovsky2017wasserstein
to address the multimarginal matching problem. In multi-view learning, the multimarginal Sinkhorn algorithm is employed for distribution matching of multiple learned embeddings
piran2024contrasting
.
mehta2023efficient
leveraged MOT with
1
-Wasserstein cost to regularize data representations, with applications to fairness, harmonization, and generative modeling. In the context of adversarial classification,
trillos2023multimarginal
shows the equivalence between MOT and certain adversarial multiclass classification problems, allowing the derivation of optimal robust classification rules. More recently,
noble2024tree
proposed to solve high-dimensional star-structured EMOT problems via diffusion processes. However, this approach does not generalize to other cost functions, leaving barycenter computation as its main application. For unregularized MOT,
zhou2024efficient
suggests solving a simplified surrogate problem over a directed tree, but their computational complexity also scales with
n
.

When only two marginals are present, EMOT reduces to the ubiquitous entropic optimal transport problem (EOT). Neural estimation of EOT has seen significant success via several different approaches. For instance,
seguy2017large
introduced a neural estimation framework based on the semi-dual formulation of the entropic OT problem. Their method efficiently computes the optimal transport plan by parametrizing the dual potentials with neural networks. Building on this,
daniels2021score
proposed a score-based generative approach that not only estimates the Sinkhorn coupling via neural network parametrization but also incorporates stochastic dynamics to sample from the resulting transport plan. More recently,
mokrov2023energy
combined the semi-dual entropic OT formulation with energy-based models, further increasing modeling capacity by capturing complex cost structures through expressive neural networks. Theoretical aspects of neural entropic OT were studied in
wang2024neural
, providing sharp estimation bounds on the optimal transport cost and approximation guarantees for the neural bimarginal transport plan. We anticipate that similar advances will be attainable in the multimarginal setting, building on the neural estimation approach proposed herein.

Overall, existing EMOT estimation methods for more than two marginals (i.e., excluding the reduction to EOT) suffer from two main problems: (1) most approaches are tailored to specific cost functions, which restricts applicability
beier2023multi
;
noble2024tree
, and (2) they often suffer from scalability issues, with computational complexity growing as
O(n^{k})

piran2024contrasting
;
zhou2024efficient
. The NEMOT framework we propose addresses these issues, facilitating broad applicability of MOT methods at scales not possible before.

3.1
Performance Guarantees

We provide non-asymptotic bounds on the NEMOT estimation error, both for the cost and the induced neural plan. Generally, the proposed approach entails three sources of error: (i) function approximation of the dual MOT potentials by neural nets; (ii) empirical estimation of the means by sample averages; and (iii) optimization, which comes from employing suboptimal (e.g., gradient-based) routines. We provide sharp bounds on the errors of types (i) and (ii), leaving the account of the optimization error for future work. To simplify the presentation of our bound, we assume that
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,k
(although our results readily extend to arbitrary compact spaces) and that the pairwise cost
\tilde{c}
is continuously differentiable. Many transportation costs of interest satisfy this assumption, e.g., the

are fixed throughout this section.

\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{nn}}^{\mspace{1mu}m}(a)
be the class of neural nets

f(z)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{i}\phi\left(\langle w_{i},z\rangle+b_{i}\right)+\langle w_{0},z\rangle+b_{0}

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|w_{i}\|_{1}\vee|b_{i}|\leq 1,\ \ \max_{1\leq i\leq m}|\beta_{i}|\leq\frac{a}{2m},\ \ |b_{0}|,\|w_{0}\|_{1}\leq a

parameter bounds, where

Neural Multimarginal Gromov-Wasserstein

The Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) problem
memoli2011gromov
provides a OT-based framework for alignment of heterogeneous datasets (e.g., of varying modalities or semantics). Representing each dataset as a metric measure (mm) spaces, say
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{d},\mu)
, we look for an alignment plan that optimally matches the mm spaces’ internal structures by minimizing distance distortion. GW alignment has seen a variety of applications, including single-cell genomics
cao2022manifold
;
demetci2022scot
, matching of language models
alvarez2018gromov
, and shape/graph matching
memoli2011spectral
, with recent interest in the multimarginal extension of the problem
beier2023multi
. We propose utilizing NEMOT as a primitive for a neural entropic MGW (NEMGW) algorithm.

We consider the quadratic entropic MGW (EMGW) problem between
k
Euclidean mm spaces
(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathsf{d}_{1},\mu_{1}),\ldots,(\mathcal{X}_{k},\mathsf{d}_{k},\mu_{k})
of (possibly) different dimensions, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
, and
\mathsf{d}_{i}(x,y)=\|x-y\|_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
. The EMGW problem with parameter
\epsilon>0
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}\mathsf{G}\mathsf{W}_{\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\bm{\mu}^{k})}\int\Delta(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{y}^{k})d\pi\otimes\pi(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{y}^{k})+\epsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\bm{\mu}^{k}),

(8)

where
\Delta(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{y}^{k})\coloneqq\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}\left|\mathsf{d}_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})^{2}-\mathsf{d}_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})^{2}\right|^{2}

\mathsf{M}\mathsf{G}\mathsf{W}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\coloneqq\mathsf{M}\mathsf{G}\mathsf{W}_{0}(\bm{\mu}^{k})

Entropic approximation gap.

Entropic GW is a popular computationally tractable proxy of the unregularized GW, which is an instance of the NP-hard quadratic assignment problem
Commander2005
. However, a formal justification for this approximation was provided only recently as an
O(\epsilon\log(1/\epsilon))
bound on the entropic GW cost gap
zhang2024gromov

Proposition 4.1

(Entropic gap)
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,
\mathsf{M}\mathsf{G}\mathsf{W}_{\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})-\mathsf{M}\mathsf{G}\mathsf{W}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\lesssim_{d}k\epsilon\log\left(\frac{|\mathcal{E}|}{k\epsilon}\right)

6
Conclusion

This paper proposed NEMOT, an EMOT neural estimation algorithm utilizing modern neural network optimization schemes. NEMOT is applicable at scale with arbitrary cost functions and operates in a data-driven manner; these features align well with large-scale machine learning pipelines.
We derive formal guarantees on the NEMOT estimation error, for both the cost and optimal plan, under mild smoothness assumptions on the cost function. The resulting error bounds decay at the parametric rate, rendering NEMOT a minimax optimal estimator (provided an idealized optimizer, as the optimization error is not accounted for in our analysis).
Compared to the Sinkhorn algorithm—the prior go-to approach for EMOT computation—NEMOT offers a substantial speedup that becomes increasingly pronounced as the dataset size grows, while preserving comparable accuracy.
We further proposed to utilize NEMOT for a neural EMGW alignment solver. Due to the massive speedups achieved and increased practicality of the algorithm, we believe that NEMOT will unlock EMOT estimation and alignment for real-world datasets that were previously intractable.

\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact and let
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that there exists an open set
\mathcal{U}\supset\mathcal{X}
,
b\geq 0
and
\tilde{g}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{s_{\mathsf{KB}}}(\mathcal{U})
with
s_{\mathsf{KB}}\coloneqq\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor+3
, such that
g=\tilde{g}\big|_{\mathcal{X}}
. Then, there exist
f\in\mathcal{F}_{m,d}(\bar{c}_{b,d,\|\mathcal{X}\|})
where
\bar{c}_{b,d,\|\mathcal{X}\|}
is a constant that depends on
(b,d)
and is proportional to
\max_{\|\alpha\|_{1}}\|D^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}
is given in
[
27
]
,Eqn. A.15, such that

\|f-g\|_{\infty}\lesssim\bar{c}_{b,d,\|\mathcal{X}\|}d^{\frac{1}{2}}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

A preliminary step to use Proposition
A.1
is to that for any
i
,
\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{s_{\mathsf{KB}}}
, i.e., that the function we aim to approximate adhere to the required smoothness conditions.
Furthermore, for an explicit characterization of
\bar{c}_{b,d,\|\mathcal{X}\|}
in terms of the problem’s parameters, the smoothness of the partial derivatives of the dual potentials should also be characterized.
To that end, we propose the following lemma.

Lemma A.2
.

There exist dual EMOT potentials
\left(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}\right)
for
\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})
such that

\displaystyle\max_{i=1,\dots,k}\|\varphi_{i}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}_{i}}\lesssim_{d}|\mathcal{G}_{c}|

(15)

\displaystyle\max_{i=1,\dots,k}\|D^{\alpha}\varphi_{i}\|\lesssim_{d,s}(1+\epsilon^{1-s})\left(\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{x})\right)^{s},\quad 1\leq|\alpha|\leq s.

(16)

for any
s\geq 2
and some constant
C_{s}
that depends only on
s
.

The proof of Lemma
A.2
, which is given in Supplement
A.3
, is a generalization of
[
29
]
Lemma 1, which accounts for the dual potential in the bimarginal EOT case. Specifically, given a set of optimal potentials
(\varphi_{1}^{\star},\dots,\varphi_{k}^{\star})
we can construct a set of dual potentials
(\varphi_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\varphi_{k}^{\prime})
that benefit from an explicit representation via the Schrödinger system, while agreeing with
(\varphi_{1}^{\star},\dots,\varphi_{k}^{\star})

\mu_{k}
-a.s., rendering them optimal as well for the considered EMOT.
Accordingly, the proof follows from similar arguments.
Using Lemma
A.2
we result with the following error on the approximation error

\left|\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})-\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}^{\epsilon}_{c,m,a}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\right|\lesssim_{d,M_{\mathcal{X}}}\left(1+\epsilon^{-(2+\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor)}\right)\left(\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})\right)^{\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor+3}km^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(17)

We now turn to bound the estimator error, which is given by the following lemma.

Lemma A.3

(Estimation Error)
.

Under the considered setting, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}^{\epsilon}_{c,m,a}-\widehat{\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}}_{c,\epsilon}^{m,a}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\right|\right]\lesssim_{d}n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\max\left\{(1+\epsilon^{-(d+4)})\left(\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})\right)^{d+6},(1+\epsilon^{-(\frac{d}{2}+2)})k\right\}.

(18)

The proof of Lemma
A.3
is given in Supplement
A.4
and follows the steps of the corresponding estimation error bound from
[
29
]
. It uses the established smoothness of the integrands to construct bounds on the empirical error using covering and bracketing numbers of the corresponding function classes, combined with maximum inequalities of empirical processes
[
53
]
.
We note that the maximum between the two terms in (
18
) depends on the cost graph, as
\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})
is independent on
k
for sparse graphs.

Finally, combining (
17
) and (
18
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}}_{c,\epsilon}^{m,a}(\mathbf{X}^{k\times n})-\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon^{-(\frac{d}{2}+1)})\left(\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})\right)^{\frac{d}{2}+2}km^{-\frac{1}{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\max\left\{(1+\epsilon^{-(d+4)})\left(\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})\right)^{d+6},(1+\epsilon^{-(\frac{d}{2}+2)})k\right\}.

To obtain a simpler bound, we consider two cases, distinguishing between the dependence of
\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})
on
k
.
In the worst case, when
\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})=k
, the first term is dominant in the error bound, leaving us with

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}}_{c,\epsilon}^{m,a}(\mathbf{X}^{k\times n})-\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\right|\right]\lesssim(1+\epsilon^{-(\frac{d}{2}+1)})k^{d+6}(m^{-\frac{1}{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}),

while, in the sparse case where
\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})
is a constant that is independent of
k
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}}_{c,\epsilon}^{m,a}(\mathbf{X}^{k\times n})-\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\right|\right]\lesssim(1+\epsilon^{-(\frac{d}{2}+1)})k(m^{-\frac{1}{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

This concludes the proof.
\hfill\square

Remark A.4

(Dependence on dimension)
.

In the bimarginal case, it is well known that the EOT empirical plug-in estimator attains the parametric rate of convergence
n^{-1/2}
with a multiplicative factor of
\epsilon^{-d/2}

[
20
,
21
]
. Early bounds assumed that both population measures were supported in Euclidean spaces of the same dimension
d
. Recently,
[
54
]
showed that if the dimensions of the supports differ, say
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
, then
\min(d_{1},d_{2})
appears in the empirical convergence rate. This phenomenon is known as the
lower complexity adaptation
principle for EOT, and it also holds for its neural estimator
[
29
]
.
In contrast, the bound from Theorem
3.1
depends on the largest dimension. The argument used in the bivariate setting achieves a rate depending on the smaller dimension by using the fact that one dual potential determines the other via the
(c,\epsilon)
-transform. Similarly for
k>2
, any one dual potential can be expressed in terms of the remaining ones, which reduces the problem from
k
to
k-1
potentials. In particular, this allows expressing the largest-dimensional marginal in terms of the others. By analogy to the
k=2
case, we therefore conjecture that the bound of Theorem
3.1
can be improved from using
\max_{i}d_{i}
to using the
second largest
dimension (which in the
k=2
case would revert to the minimum). Adjusting our proof accordingly would be an interesting followup.

A.2
Proof of Theorem

The proof extends the technique from
[
29
]
.
Let
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}
and denote the corresponding random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
.
We begin by showing that for a pair of couplings
(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon},\pi_{f}^{\epsilon})
with
\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}
being the optimal coupling and optimal potentials
f^{\star}\coloneqq(f^{\star}_{1},\dots,f^{\star}_{k})
, the KL divergence
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}\|\pi_{f}^{\epsilon})
is upper bounded by the subtraction of EMOT costs, up to a multiplicative factor.
Specifically,

\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}\|\pi_{f}^{\epsilon})\leq\epsilon^{-1}\left(\Gamma(f^{\star})-\Gamma(f)\right),

for any continuous
f
.
To that end, we consider the
(c,\epsilon)
-transform, which stems from the Schrödinger system of the EMOT
[
39
]
, and allows us to represent a single potential in terms of the rest, which for any
f_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma(f)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k-1}}\bar{f}d\bar{\mu}+\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}f^{c,\epsilon}d\mu_{k},\quad f^{c,\epsilon}(y)\coloneqq-\epsilon\log\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k-1}}\exp\left(\frac{\bar{f}(\bar{x},y)}{\epsilon}\right)d\bar{\pi}(\bar{x})\right),

where
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1})
,
\bar{f}(\bar{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}f_{i}(x_{i})
,
\bar{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k-1})
and
\bar{\pi}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\pi(\bar{x},y)d\mu_{k}(y)
.
We define the energy function
E_{\bar{\pi}}
.
Furthermore, note that in this setting, the conditional plan is given by:

\frac{d\pi(\bar{x}|y)}{d\bar{x}}=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\tilde{f}(\bar{x})-c(\bar{x},y)}{\epsilon}\right)}{\int\exp\left(\frac{\tilde{f}(\bar{x}^{\prime})-c(\bar{x}^{\prime},y)}{\epsilon}\right)d\bar{x}}.

Define
F_{\bar{f}}(y)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k-1}}\exp\left(\frac{\bar{f}(\bar{x})-c(\bar{x},y)}{\epsilon}\right)d\bar{x}
and
Z\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k-1}}\exp\left(-E_{\bar{\pi}}(\bar{x})\right)
.
We therefore have
\bar{f}^{c,\epsilon}(y)=-\epsilon\log(F_{\bar{f}}(y))+\epsilon\log(Z)
.
We have the following:

\displaystyle\Gamma(f_{\star})-\Gamma(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}c\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}+\epsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i})-\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k-1}}\bar{f}\,d\bar{\pi}-\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}f^{c,\epsilon}\,d\mu_{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{=}}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\bigl(c(\bar{x},y)-\tilde{f}(\bar{x})\bigr)\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(\bar{x},y)-\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}h(\cdot|y)d\mu_{k}(y)+h(\bar{\pi})+\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}\log\bigl(F_{\tilde{f}}(y)\bigr)\,d\mu_{k}(y)+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\,\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\frac{\tilde{f}(x)-c(\bar{x},y)}{\epsilon}\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x,y)+\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\log\bigl(F_{\tilde{f}}(y)\bigr)\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x,y)-\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}h\bigl(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(\cdot\mid y)\bigr)\,d\mu_{k}(y)+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\,\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\log\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{F_{\tilde{f}}(y)}\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{\tilde{f}(x)-c(\bar{x},y)}{\epsilon}\bigr)\Bigr)\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x,y)-\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}h\bigl(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(\cdot\mid y)\bigr)\,d\mu_{k}(y)+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\,\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\log\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d\pi_{f}^{\epsilon}(x\mid y)}{d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x\mid y)}\Bigr)\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x,y)-\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}h\bigl(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(\cdot\mid y)\bigr)\,d\mu_{k}(y)+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\,\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}\!\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k-1}}\log\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d\pi_{f}^{\epsilon}(x\mid y)}{d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x\mid y)}\Bigr)\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x\mid y)\,d\mu_{k}(y)+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}\!\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k-1}}\log\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x\mid y)}{d\pi_{f}^{\epsilon}(x\mid y)}\Bigr)\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x\mid y)\,d\mu_{k}(y)+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\log\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x,y)}{d\pi_{f}^{\epsilon}(x,y)}\Bigr)\,d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}(x,y)+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}\,\big\|\,\pi_{f}^{\epsilon}\bigr)+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right),

where
h(\mu)=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\left(\frac{d\mu}{dx}\right)d\mu(x)
is the differential entropy of
\mu
and (a) follows from

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}}{d(\otimes_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{d\pi_{\star}^{\epsilon}}{d\mu_{k}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\log\bar{\pi}_{\star}^{\epsilon}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{d\bar{\pi}_{\star}^{\epsilon}}{d(\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}h(\cdot|y)d\mu_{k}(y)+h(\bar{\pi})+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k-1}\mu_{i}\right).

Next, we bound the EMOT cost difference.
We have the following:

\displaystyle\Gamma(f_{\star})-\Gamma(f)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})-\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}^{m,a}(\bm{\mu}^{k})}_{(i)}+\underbrace{\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}^{m,a}(\bm{\mu}^{k})-\widehat{\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}}_{c,\epsilon}(\textbf{X}^{n,k})}_{(ii)}+\underbrace{\hat{\Gamma}(\hat{f}^{\star})-\Gamma(\hat{f}^{\star})}_{(iii)},

where
\Gamma(\hat{f}^{\star})
is the EMOT cost, calculated from the optimal neural net within the class
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{nn}}^{\mspace{1mu}m}(a)
.
Note that (i) and (ii) quantify the NEMOT approximation and estimation error, respectively. Therefore, we can simply use the bounds from Supplement
A.1
.
Furthermore, for (iii), as noted in
[
29
]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\Gamma}(\hat{f}^{\star})-\Gamma(\hat{f}^{\star})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{nn}}^{\mspace{1mu}m}(a)}\left|\hat{\Gamma}(\hat{f}^{\star})-\Gamma(\hat{f}^{\star})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{nn}}^{\mspace{1mu}m}(a)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{j}(X_{ij})-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{j}}[f_{j}])\right|\right],

which can correspondingly bounded by the estimation error.
Combining the bounds, we result with the same bound as the neural estimation bound (
7
). This concludes the proof.
\hfill\square

By the invariance of the EMOT to additive constants we may without loss of generality take optimal MOT potentials such that

.
We consider a set of dual potentials
(\varphi_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
that satisfy the Schrödinger system with the optimal potentials, i.e.,

\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{i}^{\star}(x_{i})-c(x^{k})\right)d\mu^{-i}(x^{-i})=1,\quad\mu_{i}-\text{a.e.},

\mathcal{X}^{-i}(\times_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{X}_{j})\setminus\mathcal{X}_{i}

\mathcal{X}_{i}
. First, by Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}^{-}i}c(x^{k})-\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}\varphi_{j}^{\star}(x_{j})d\mu^{-i}(x^{-i})

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{G}_{c}|C(d)-\frac{k-1}{k}\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{G}_{c}|C(d),

C(d_{i},d_{j})

C(d)
such that
d=\max_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}
.
For example, when the pairwise cost is quadratic,
C(d_{i},d_{j})=2\max(d_{i},d_{j})
.
Next, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}^{-}i}\exp\left(\frac{c(x^{k})-\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}\varphi_{j}^{\star}(x_{j})}{\epsilon}\right)d\mu^{-i}(x^{-i})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}^{-}i}\exp\left(\frac{(1+\frac{k-1}{k})|\mathcal{G}_{c}|C(d)}{\epsilon}\right)d\mu^{-i}(x^{-i})

\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}_{c,\epsilon}(\bm{\mu}^{k})\leq|\mathcal{G}_{c}|C(d)

As the bound is uniform we can conclude that

\max_{i=1,\dots,k}\|\varphi_{i}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}_{i}}\lesssim_{d}|\mathcal{G}_{c}|.

By the concavity of the logarithm function we can conclude that
\varphi_{i}=\varphi^{\star}_{i}

\mu_{i}
-a.s. for
i=1,\dots,k
.

[
55
]

\alpha

\varphi

\alpha

\displaystyle-D^{\alpha}(\varphi_{i})(x)=\epsilon\sum_{r=1}^{\alpha}\sum_{p(\alpha,r)}\frac{\alpha!(r-1)!(-1)^{r-1}}{\prod_{j=1}^{|\alpha|}(k_{j}!)(\beta_{j}!)^{k_{j}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\times\prod_{j=1}^{|\alpha|}\left(\frac{D^{\beta_{j}}\int\exp\left(\frac{\sum_{\ell=1,\ell\neq i}^{k}\varphi^{\star}_{\ell}(x_{k})-c(x^{k})}{\epsilon}d\mu^{-i}(x^{-i})\right)}{\int\exp\left(\frac{\sum_{\ell=1,\ell\neq i}^{k}\varphi^{\star}_{\ell}(x_{k})-c(x^{k})}{\epsilon}d\mu^{-i}(x^{-i})\right)}\right),

(20)

where

is the collection of all tuples
(k_{1},\dots,k_{|\alpha|;\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{|}\alpha|})\in\mathbb{N}^{|\alpha|}\times\mathbb{N}^{d_{i}\times\alpha}
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{|\alpha|}k_{i}=r,\sum_{i=1}^{|\alpha|}k_{i}\beta_{i}=\alpha
and for which there exit
s\in(1,\dots,|\alpha|)
such that
k_{i}=0
and
\beta_{i}=0
for all

and

. We refer the reader to
[
55
]
for more information on the Faa di Bruno formula.

D^{\beta_{j}}\int\exp\left(\sum_{\ell=1,\ell\neq i}^{k}\varphi^{\star}_{\ell}(x_{k})-c(x^{k})\right)d\mu^{-i}(x^{-i})
.
To that end, we apply the Faa di Bronu formula to

yielding

and the cost derivative can be bounded using the pairwise cost bound, i.e., for any

Here
N(X_{i})
are the neighbors of the variable
X_{i}
in the cost graph
\mathcal{G}_{c}
,

is a uniform bound on the pairwise costs derivatives that depends only on
d
, and
\mathsf{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{c})
is the degree of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{c}
. The sum
\sum_{r^{\prime}=1}^{|\eta_{j}|}\left(\frac{-1}{2\epsilon}\right)^{r^{\prime}}
is upper bounded by

when

, and by
\epsilon^{-1}
when

. Consequently, we have uniformly on

which concludes the proof.

where
\vec{f}_{\theta,k}\coloneqq(f_{\theta_{1}},\dots,f_{\theta_{k}})

(a)

\pi^{\ell}

G(\cdot)

We begin by bounding the cost terms. We denote with
\mathcal{X}^{k}

\displaystyle G(\pi^{\ell})-G(\pi^{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}\left|\|x_{i}-y_{i}\|^{2}-\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|^{2}\right|^{2}d\pi^{\ell}\otimes\pi^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{y}^{k})

\displaystyle G(\pi^{\ell})-G(\pi^{0})

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}\int_{\mathcal{X}_{i}\times\mathcal{X}_{j}}\|x_{i}\|^{2}\|x_{j}\|^{2}d(\pi^{\ell}-\pi^{0})(x_{i},x_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq m\leq d_{i}\\

[
56

Finally, we construct an entry-wise bound on the resulting covariance matrix, which leaves us with a bound of the form

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi^{\ell}\|\otimes_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}+d_{i}\log(d_{i}!(M_{2}(\mu_{i})+d_{i}(1+M_{1}(\mu_{i}))))-d_{i}\log\ell.

\ell

\ell

\mathsf{M}\mathsf{G}\mathsf{W}^{\epsilon}(\mu^{k})-\mathsf{M}\mathsf{G}\mathsf{W}^{2}(\mu^{k})\lesssim_{k,(d_{i})_{i=1}^{k}}\epsilon\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right).

Appendix C
Computational Complexity of NEMOT via U-statistics

Herein, we consider the U-statistic estimator of the exponential term in the NEMOT.
For a given dataset
\mathbf{X^{n,k}}
, the
k
-sample U-statistic estimator considers all possible
k
-tuples that can be generated from the dataset and takes the sample mean over the exponential term evaluated over these tuples.
Formally it is given by the following expression

\hat{E}_{\mathsf{U}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n^{k}}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}e^{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{j_{i}})-c(X_{j_{1}},\dots,X_{j_{k}})\right)/\epsilon}+\epsilon.

where
(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
are all possible
k
-tuples, in which each element takes a value in
\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Generally
U
-statistics form low variance estimators.
However, for a given batch size
b
, to calculate
\hat{E}_{\mathsf{U}}
, one must take the average of a data structure with
b^{k}
.
This is indeed a significant speedup over
n^{k}
, which is presented by Sinkhorn, but is limited to relatively small
(b,k)
values.

However, as was shown for Sinkhorn, when the cost graph is sparse, the
U
-statistic can be reformulated in terms of matrix-matrix or matrix-vector operations, leading to polynomial complexity in
(b,k)
.
We focus on a circle cost, and outline the steps to derive similar results for tree cost graphs.
When
\mathcal{G}_{c}
is circular, i.e.,
c(\mathbf{x}^{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tilde{c}(x_{i},x_{i+1})
, we identify
\hat{E}_{\mathsf{U}}
as the trace of some matrix product, which corresponds to a loop along the circle:

Proposition C.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{c}
be a circle cost graph,
\vec{f}_{i}=[f_{\theta_{i}}(x_{i1}),\dots,f_{\theta_{i}}(x_{in})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the vector of dual potential outputs,
\mathrm{C}^{i}[j,l]=\tilde{c}(x_{ij},x_{i+1,l})
be the pairwise cost matrix for
i=1,\dots,k
with
k+1=1
and let
\mathrm{L}_{i}\coloneqq\exp\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}(\vec{f}_{i}\bigoplus\vec{f}_{i+1})-\mathrm{C}_{i}}{\epsilon}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
where
\vec{f}_{i}\bigoplus\vec{f}_{i+1}
is the direct sum of the vectors, resulting in an
n\times n
matrix.
Then

\hat{E}_{\mathsf{U}}=\frac{1}{n^{k}}\mathrm{tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{i}\right)+\epsilon,

(27)

and the complexity of calculating (
27
) is
O(kn^{3})
.
Furthermore, the neural plan tensor can be represented as

\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\theta^{\star}}(\mathbf{X}^{n,k})[i_{1},\dots,i_{k}]=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{j}(i_{j},i_{j+1}).

(28)

Furthermore, we have an alternative representation of the resulting optimal transport cost, due to the representation of the neural plan:

Proposition C.2
.

Let
(\mathrm{C}^{i})_{i=1}^{k}
be the pairwise cost matrices such that
\mathrm{C}^{i}_{j,l}=c(x_{ij},x_{i+1,l})
for a given circle cost graph
\mathcal{G}_{c}
.
The corresponding unregularized transport cost is given by

\langle C,\widehat{\Pi}_{\theta^{\star}}^{\epsilon}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle C_{j},m\left(\widehat{\Pi}_{\theta^{\star}}^{\epsilon},j,j+1\right)\rangle

where
m\left(\widehat{\Pi}_{\theta^{\star}}^{\epsilon},i_{1},i_{2}\right)
is the pairwise marginalization of
\widehat{\Pi}_{\theta^{\star}}^{\epsilon}
between
(\mu_{i_{1}},\mu_{i_{2}})
, which is given by

m\left(\widehat{\Pi}_{\theta^{\star}}^{\epsilon},i_{1},i_{2}\right)=\left(\prod_{j=i_{1}}^{i_{2}-1}\mathrm{L}_{j}\right)\odot\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i_{1}-1}\mathrm{L}_{j}\right)^{\intercal}\left(\prod_{j=i_{2}}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{j}\right)^{\intercal}\right]

(29)

where
\mathrm{A}\odot\mathrm{B}
is the Hadamard product of
(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})
.

At the heart of Proposition
C.2
is the efficient marginalization, which is the main computational bottleneck of the Sinkhorn algorithm.

Similar results can be drawn for the tree cost graph.
When
\mathcal{G}_{c}
is a tree, the resulting alternative form of
\hat{E}_{\mathsf{U}}
corresponds to a set of matrix-vector operations, which correspond to a DFS traversal along the tree that represents
\mathcal{G}_{c}
.

Proof of Proposition
C.1

Recall that the NEMOT is given by the expression

\widehat{\mathsf{M}\mathsf{O}\mathsf{T}}_{c,\epsilon}(\textbf{X}^{n,k})\coloneqq\sup_{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{ij})-\frac{\epsilon}{n^{k}}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}e^{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{j_{i}})-c(X_{j_{1}},\dots,X_{j_{k}})\right)/\epsilon}+\epsilon.

The first sum directly follows from the definition of the direct sum.
For the exponential term, we have

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{j_{i}})-c(X_{j_{1}},\dots,X_{j_{k}})}{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=\exp\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{j_{i}})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tilde{c}(X_{j_{i}}X_{j_{i+1}})}{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=\exp\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\left(f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{j_{i}})+f_{\theta_{i+1}}(X_{j_{i+1}})\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tilde{c}(X_{j_{i}}X_{j_{i+1}})}{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}\left(\vec{f}_{\theta_{i}}[j_{i}]+\vec{f}_{\theta_{i+1}}[j_{i+1}]\right)-\mathrm{C}^{i}[j_{i},j_{i+1}]}{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{i}[j_{i},j_{i+1}].

Note that by the definition of the neural plan (
6
) we have obtained the desired representation.
Consequently, the second sum is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{i}[j_{i},j_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}\mathrm{L}_{1}[j_{1},j_{2}]\mathrm{L}_{2}[j_{2},j_{3}]\cdots\mathrm{L}_{k}[j_{k},j_{1}].

(30)

Note that each index we sum over corresponds only to a specific pair of matrices, and is equivalent to their matrix multiplication.
Thus, after taking the product of the set of matrices, we remain with the sum

\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{i}\right)[j_{1},j_{1}]=\mathrm{tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{i}\right),

We now move on to prove the circle cost marginalization formula.
Let
0\leq u,v\leq k
with
u\neq v
. The marginalization follows from noticing that the considered summations over the given product can be represented with products of the matrices
\mathrm{L}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{L}_{k}
. For a given entry of the marginalized coupling, we have:

\displaystyle m\left(\widehat{\Pi}_{\theta^{\star}}^{\epsilon},u,v\right)(i_{u},i_{v})=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\setminus[i_{u},i_{v}]}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{\ell}[i_{\ell},i_{\ell+1}]\right).

We divide the sum into its consecutive parts and define the matrices

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{<u}\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{u-1}\mathrm{L}_{j},\qquad\mathrm{A}_{u<v}\coloneqq\prod_{j=u+1}^{v-1}\mathrm{L}_{j},\qquad\mathrm{A}_{>v}\coloneqq\prod_{j=v+1}^{k}\mathrm{L}_{j}.

We obtain the following product:

\displaystyle m\left(\widehat{\Pi}_{\theta^{\star}}^{\epsilon},u,v\right)[i_{u},i_{v}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}}\mathrm{A}_{<u}[i_{1},i_{u}]\mathrm{A}_{u<v}[i_{u},i_{v}]\mathrm{A}_{>v}[i_{v},i_{1}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{A}_{u<v}(i_{u},i_{v})\sum_{i_{1}}\mathrm{A}_{<u}^{\intercal}[i_{u},i_{1}]\mathrm{A}_{>v}^{\intercal}[i_{1},i_{v}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{A}_{u<v}[i_{u},i_{v}]\left(\mathrm{A}_{<u}^{\intercal}\mathrm{A}_{>v}^{\intercal}\right)[i_{u},i_{v}],

which completes the proof.
\hfill\square
