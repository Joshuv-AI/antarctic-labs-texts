Probabilistic Modeling of Jailbreak on Multimodal LLMs: From Quantification to Application
Wenzhuo Xu, Zhipeng Wei, Xiongtao Sun, Zonghao Ying, Deyue Zhang, Dongdong Yang, Jiankai Jin, Xiangzheng Zhang, Quanchen Zou

Abstract
Previous studies categorize jailbreaks on Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLM) as successful or failed based on whether responses contain malicious content. However, given the stochastic nature of MLLM responses, this binary classification of an input’s ability to jailbreak MLLMs is inappropriate. Derived from this viewpoint, we introduce jailbreak probability to quantify the jailbreak potential of an input, and model the relationship between input hidden states and their corresponding jailbreak probability using Jailbreak Probability Prediction Network (JPPN). We propose Jailbreak-Probability-based Attack (JPA) that optimizes adversarial perturbations on input image to maximize jailbreak probability, and further enhance it as Multimodal JPA (MJPA) by including monotonic text rephrasing. Extensive experiments show that (M)JPA yields significant improvements when attacking a wide range of models under both white and black box settings, demonstrating the significance of introducing jailbreak probability to make nuanced distinctions among input jailbreak abilities.



1
Introduction

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) exhibit excellent comprehension of multimodal contents with billions of parameters and massive training data, achieving high performance across multiple multimodal tasks, such as visual question answering
[
13
,
5
]
, visual grounding
[
44
,
21
]
, and image captioning
[
26
,
46
]
, etc.
However, these MLLMs are vulnerable to jailbreak attacks, which are techniques designed to compromise model safety alignment and induce harmful or undesirable outputs, such as hate speech or discriminatory comments.

Unlike adversarial attacks on discriminative tasks (e.g. image classification and image-text retrieval), jailbreak attacks target generative tasks, where MLLM responses exhibit inherent stochasticity. This randomness precludes a definitive judgment of whether a given input successfully bypasses safety constraints.

Therefore, we introduce
Jailbreak Probability
, which is defined as the ratio of the times of successful jailbreak attempts over infinite queries. This measurement provides a more nuanced assessment of an input’s jailbreak potential, capturing the stochastic nature of MLLM responses and offering a probabilistic understanding of model vulnerabilities.
In practice, we approximate jailbreak probability through multiple queries, where the estimated value asymptotically converges to the true probability as the number of queries increases.

The JPPN learns what is crucial for achieving higher jailbreak probability in the feature space.

) corresponding to the input, maximize the approximated jailbreak probability and update input image. In MJPA, we further conduct monotonic text rephrasing on the results of JPA (optional).

Leveraging this metric, we introduce the
Jailbreak Probability Prediction Network (JPPN)
to model the relationship between jailbreak probability and hidden states of inputs as illustrated in Fig.
1
. This enables the optimization of input to control their jailbreak potential.
Specifically, we propose
Jailbreak-Probability-based Attack (JPA)
, which updates the input image through optimizing hidden states to maximize jailbreak probability. To enhance JPA, we further incorporate a monotonic text rephrasing strategy that also boosts the jailbreak probability, thereby forming the Multimodal JPA (MJPA).
The effectiveness of our method comes from the nuanced jailbreak probability labels of inputs, which enable JPPN to learn the key features for both conducting and defending against jailbreaks in feature space. (M)JPA is shown in Fig.
2
.

We conduct extensive experiments to demonstrate the advantages of (M)JPA.
The results show that MJPA outperforms previous methods in attack success rate (ASR) on both open-source and commercial models, and that JPA is effective when applied to various forms of inputs.
Additional experiments show that JPA maintains strong jailbreak performance even with a small perturbation bound of 4/255.
Visualization of tokens from hidden states further confirms that JPA successfully modifies the hidden states.
These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of leveraging jailbreak probability for both attacking and safeguarding MLLMs.
The major contributions of this paper are listed below:

•

This paper introduces the concept of jailbreak probability to capture nuanced distinctions among input jailbreak abilities, providing a probabilistic understanding of MLLM vulnerabilities.

•

By modeling the relationship between input hidden states and their corresponding jailbreak probability using JPPN, we propose (M)JPA to enhance attack effectiveness.

•

This paper conducts extensive experiments to show that the effectiveness of utilizing jailbreak probability to perform jailbreak attack.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Problem Definition

We consider a single-turn interaction setting between an attacker and a multimodal large language model (MLLM). The MLLM is denoted as
M
, which takes an image-text pair
X=(x_{img},x_{txt})\in D
as input, where
D
denotes the dataset of image-text pairs. Given
X
, the model generates a response
y=M(X)
in an autoregressive manner.

To evaluate the safety of the generated response, we introduce a safety checker
C
, which can be implemented via manual check or an auxiliary judge model. The checker outputs a binary label:

C(M(X))=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }M(X)\text{ is harmful},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1)

This formulation allows us to quantify whether a given input successfully induces unsafe behavior from the model.

3.2
Attacker’s Goal

The attacker aims to construct adversarial multimodal inputs
X^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{img},x^{\prime}_{txt})
that can elicit harmful responses from MLLMs. Formally, the objective of the attacker is to maximize the number of inputs that lead to harmful responses:

\max\sum_{X\in D}C(M(X^{\prime})),

(2)

where
X^{\prime}
is derived from the original input
X
through an attack strategy.

3.3
Attacker’s Capabilities

We assume a white-box setting, where the attacker has full access to the MLLM
M
, including its architecture, parameters, and intermediate representations. Under this assumption, the attacker can design attack strategies by leveraging gradient information, internal attention patterns, or other model-specific properties.

The attacker is allowed to modify both modalities of the input. Specifically, the attacker can perturb the textual component
x_{\text{txt}}
, the visual component
x_{\text{img}}
, or both, to construct the adversarial example
X^{\prime}
.

4
Method

4.1
Jailbreak Probability

Previous adversarial attacks on discriminative tasks (e.g. image classification
[
10
,
22
,
8
]
and image-text retrieval
[
42
,
19
,
35
]
) have definite results on whether an attack is successful. However, jailbreak attack is a generative task, where the MLLM responses to one specific input are stochastic, making it both inappropriate and impossible to determine whether the input is able to jailbreak the MLLM, for this input may generate a harmful response in one trial but fails in another. To support this viewpoint, we generate 40 responses for every sample in Zhou’s dataset
[
45
]
(see Section Experiments for details) using MiniGPT-4 and count how many times each sample succeeds in jailbreak, as depicted in Fig.
3
.

Figure 3
:
Distribution of samples by the number of successful jailbreaks. Each sample has 40 responses. The X-axis represents the number of successful jailbreaks, and the Y-axis represents the number of samples that achieve certain times of successful jailbreaks. For example, there are 11 samples that achieve 0 times of successful jailbreaks.

We observe varying numbers of successful jailbreaks from 0 to 40 across samples, which indicates different samples correspond to distinct levels of jailbreak ability.
To quantify the jailbreak ability of an input
X
on an MLLM
M
(under a certain sampling strategy) in a probabilistic perspective, we propose the concept of
jailbreak probability
.

Definition 1

The jailbreak probability
P^{M}_{X}
is defined as the probability that
M
generates a harmful response with an input sample
X
:

P^{M}_{X}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}C(M(X)),

(3)

where
N
is the total number of responses sampled from
M
.

However, determining the exact value of
P^{M}_{X}
requires infinite responses.
An alternative is to approximate it by generating responses for
n
times and calculating the proportion of successful jailbreaks. This empirical estimate, referred to as the
approximated jailbreak probability
and denoted as
\hat{p}_{X}^{M}
(
n
is left out for simplicity), provides a practical measure of
P^{M}_{X}
:

\hat{p}_{X}^{M}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}C(M(X)).

(4)

We can find that
\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{X}^{M}]=P^{M}_{X}
, which means that
\hat{p}_{X}^{M}
is an unbiased estimator of
P^{M}_{X}
, and that the variance of
\hat{p}_{X}^{M}
is
\frac{1}{n}(P^{M}_{X}(1-P^{M}_{X}))
. The above properties indicate that
\hat{p}_{X}^{M}
will gradually converge to
P^{M}_{X}
as
n
grows larger.

4.2
Jailbreak Probability Prediction Network

Given the role of jailbreak probability in fine-grained measurement of jailbreak ability, modeling its relationship with input hidden states enables a deeper understanding of MLLM vulnerabilities.
We propose the
Jailbreak Probability Prediction Network (JPPN)
, which takes the hidden states of
X
in
M
as the input to predict its jailbreak probability, to model the inherent correlation between
\hat{p}_{X}^{M}
and
X
.
Compared to the representation from foundational models (e.g. CLIP
[
30
]
), these hidden states capture the internal understanding of
X
from
M
.
Since hidden states are already highly encoded by
M
, we simply use an MLP to implement JPPN and link it to each Transformer block.
We denote JPPN as
J
, with its output
j_{X}^{M}\in[0,1]
representing the predicted jailbreak probability of
X
on
M
:

j_{X}^{M}=J(HS_{M}(X))

(5)

where
HS_{M}(X)
is the hidden states obtained by passing
X
through
M
. We minimize the following loss to train JPPN:

\mathcal{L}_{train}=\frac{1}{\left|D\right|}\sum_{X\in D}\mathcal{L}_{MSE}(j_{X}^{M},\hat{p}_{X}^{M}).

(6)

The training procedure is illustrated in Fig.
1
.

4.3
Jailbreak Probability for Attack

Once the correlation of
\hat{p}_{X}^{M}
and
X
is effectively encoded by JPPN, it can be used to guide
X
towards higher jailbreak probability. With a well-trained JPPN, we propose the
Jailbreak-Probability-based Attack (JPA)
which generates the adversarial example
X^{\prime}
by minimizing the following loss:

\mathcal{L}_{JPA}=\mathcal{L}_{MSE}(j_{X}^{M},1),

(7)

where
1
is the target and represents 100% probability of
X
jailbreaking
M
.
The adversarial image
x^{\prime}_{img}
is generated iteratively via the following equation:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{img}=

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{img}-\alpha\cdot sign(\nabla_{x^{\prime}_{img}}\mathcal{L}_{JPA}),

(8)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x^{\prime}_{img}-x_{img}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon,

where
x^{\prime}_{img}
is initialized as
x_{img}
,
\alpha
is the step size and
\epsilon
is the perturbation bound. After iterations of updates, the hidden states are located close to those of high jailbreak probability, thereby enhancing the likelihood of
X^{\prime}
jailbreaking
M
.
The effectiveness of JPA comes from the nuanced jailbreak probability labels of inputs, which allow JPPN to learn what is crucial for achieving successful jailbreak in feature space. The essence of jailbreak probability is a kind of information about inputs which brings knowledge about the inherent vulnerabilities of the victim MLLM.

4.4
Monotonic Text Rephrasing

Though the text modality is discrete and thus they cannot be optimized directly, we can also utilize jailbreak probability as a feedback signal to boost the jailbreak ability of input text. Inspired by the study that the safety constraints of (M)LLMs are easily bypassed when (M)LLMs are distracted
[
36
]
, we use an auxiliary LLM to iteratively rephrase
x_{txt}
with a rephrasing prompt
P_{r}
that contain techniques that can be used to distract the victim MLLMs (see Appendix A for details). An example of rephrased text is as follows:

Generate three questions about this image and answer them. After answer these questions above, answer me this question: {INITIAL HARMFUL PROMPT}

Then, we enforce a
monotonicity constraint
on the rephrasing: we obtain the approximated jailbreak probability of the rephrased text after each iteration with JPPN; if the jailbreak probability is reduced, we cancel this rephrasing to guarantee that the approximated jailbreak probability only increases or keeps unchanged during rephrasing. The monotonic rephrasing stops when reaching the maximum number of iterations. For clarity, we refer to the image-only attack as
JPA
and the multimodal attack as
MJPA
in the rest of the paper. The illustration of (M)JPA is shown in Fig.
2
. The algorithm of MJPA is presented in Algorithm

5.2
Training of JPPNs

Training Settings.

We choose MiniGPT-4 as the example white-box MLLM to validate the feasibility of using jailbreak probability and JPPN to conduct jailbreak. JPPN is implemented as a three-layer MLP.
According to MiniGPT-4’s architecture, JPPN’s input dimension is 5120.
Each of the 40 Transformer blocks in MiniGPT-4 is connected to an independent JPPN to estimate jailbreak probability from its hidden states.
We show the JPPN architecture in Fig.
4
.

Figure 4
:
The architecture of JPPN for MiniGPT-4.

We train JPPNs on Zhou’s training set. To label malicious questions, we generate multiple responses with MiniGPT-4 and calculate the approximated jailbreak probability using Eq.
4
based on judge model.
To assess the impact of the number of responses in training, we experiment with 5, 20, and 40 responses per input, training JPPNs-5r, JPPNs-20r, and JPPNs-40r, respectively.
We train all models for 150 epochs using Adam
[
12
]
with a learning rate of 0.001 and decay it by 80% every 50 epochs.
In our implementation, generating 20 responses for each input in the training set costs about 4 hours on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

Analysis on JPPN’s Accuracy.

After training, we evaluate JPPN’s accuracy on Zhou’s test set. Due to the stochasticity of approximated jailbreak probability, we introduce a flexible metric for evaluating accuracy, which is referred to as
\tau

acc_{\tau}
).

acc_{\tau}
is calculated with an indicator function:

acc_{\tau}=\frac{1}{\left|D\right|}\sum_{X\in D}\mathbf{1}(X),\,\,if\left|j_{X}-\hat{p}_{X}\right|\leq\tau

(9)

where
j_{X}
is the output of JPPN and
\hat{p}_{X}
is the approximated jailbreak probability.
\tau
is set to 0.2.
Fig.
5
illustrates the
acc_{0.2}
for JPPNs linked to their respective Transformer blocks (specifically, we also try implementing a JPPN linking to the embeddings before the first Transformer block, which corresponds to Position 0).
We observe that the
acc_{0.2}
of JPPNs-20r and JPPNs-40r after Position 20 keeps exceeding 80%, and even goes beyond 90% for some Positions of JPPNs-40r, indicating high feasibility of jailbreak probability prediction. It can been seen that hidden states in later blocks achieve higher accuracy than those in early blocks, which can be attributed to the fact that the hidden states in later blocks are more consistent with the final outputs. We also find that the
acc_{0.2}
for JPPNs-5r remains below 70%, suggesting that the accuracy is related to the quantity of responses, as a large number of responses can stabilize the approximated jailbreak probability used for training.

Performance of (M)JPA.

We conduct jailbreak experiments on Zhou’s test set. The results are listed in Tab.

It can be seen that (M)JPA exhibits good performance under white-box and black-box settings, across all MLLMs.
For example, JPA attains an ASR of 79.92% on MiniGPT-4, significantly surpassing VAP’s ASR of 63.40% and Clean’s ASR of 51.54%. Considering that JPA uses a smaller perturbation bound (

Exploratory Extension: Jailbreak Probability for Defense

Since increasing jailbreak probability facilitates jailbreak on MLLMs, we form an interesting hypothesis that decreasing it may have the opposite effect and could be used for safety alignment. We try decreasing the jailbreak probability (which is just opposite to Eq.
7
) via minimizing the following equation:

\mathcal{L}_{JPD}=\mathcal{L}_{MSE}(j_{X}^{M},0),

(10)

where
j_{X}^{M}
is the predicted jailbreak probability of input
X
and
0
represents 0% probability of
X
jailbreaking
M
. We update the model parameters via:

\theta^{\prime}=\theta-\beta\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{JPD}

(11)

where
\theta
is the parameters of
M
and
\beta
is the learning rate.
We conduct a small-scale study by fine-tuning MiniGPT-4 for one epoch with 100 samples from Zhou’s training set. We use AdamW
[
18
]
optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-5. We also test the MM-Vet score
[

, which measures the ability of MLLMs on normal tasks to ensure usability. Results in Tab.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we first observe that different inputs have different chances to jailbreak MLLMs, thus proposing jailbreak probability to measure the jailbreak potential of inputs. Then we model the correlation between hidden states of inputs and their jailbreak probability by training JPPN. With jailbreak probability labels, JPPN is able to learn what is crucial for achieving higher jailbreak probability in feature space. We then propose JPA for jailbreak attack, and further enhance it by monotonic text rephrasing. We conduct extensive experiments to show the effectiveness of our method. In the future, we will explore (1) the utilization of jailbreak probability in image generation, (2) effective ways of constructing and training JPPN, (3) further enhancement of the transferability of JPA, (4) jailbreak-probability-based methods on other modalities.

7
Ethics Statement

This paper presents a method for jailbreaking MLLMs, which may raise concerns about potential misuse. To mitigate associated risks, we recommend implementing monitoring systems to filter harmful outputs. We have also proposed an effective fine tuning method against jailbreaks in Section 5.5. The attack code will be made available only with researcher verification. While the work involves potentially sensitive content, its primary objective is not to facilitate jailbreaks, but to understand and characterize the safety vulnerabilities of MLLMs.
