Transcriptomics-based analysis of key genes and potential mechanism and therapeutic agents in cadmium-induced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma progression
Authors: Rui Zhu, Jiongyu Chen, Xiarong Zhang, Qian Chen, Yingming Jin, Mingqin Zeng, Jianan Luo, Yiteng Huang, Lin Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic cadmium exposure is linked to esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) progression, treatment resistance, and poor prognosis, but its molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Based on whole-transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) on chronic cadmium-treated (CCT) and untreated human EC109 ESCC cells, we performed lncRNA-mRNA co-expression and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analyses to screen the hub genes related to cadmium exposure. Then, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses depicted that these genes were mainly enriched in nucleocytoplasmic transport, ferroptosis, Huntington disease, and cellular senescence. Based on the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA potential interaction, three lncRNAs (AC107068.1, TTN-AS1, MAPKAPK5-AS1) and ten mRNAs (IPO5, NUDCD1, OSTM1, CCNB1, FANCD2, TFRC, POLR2B, HTT, NUP43, NBN) were selected for competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network construction. Connectivity map (CMap) analysis for the target mRNAs showed that JAK3-inhibitor-I was the most promising therapeutic drug for cadmium-induced ESCC progression. The prognostic value and abnormal expression of the candidate mRNAs were then validated in TCGA cohorts, cadmium-treated cancer cells and 41 ESCC specimens. Moreover, migration and invasion assays were performed to assess the effects of the identified gene on cell malignant phenotypes. As a result, IPO5 was identified as the pivotal gene and MAPKAPK5-AS1-hsa-miR-379-5p-IPO5 was deemed as a potential ceRNA regulatory mechanism for cadmium carcinogenesis in ESCC. Using TIMER and EPIC algorithms, IPO5 was correlated with increased infiltration of CD4+ T cells and macrophages, while negatively associated with CD8+ T cells and NK cells in ESCC tissues. Collectively, our study provides valuable information in understanding the molecular mechanisms involved in cadmium-induced ESCC progression and treatment resistance. Furthermore, we predict potential agents for cadmium carcinogenicity prevention and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32732-1.

Esophageal cancer ranked as the eleventh most common malignancy worldwide, with an estimated 511,000 new cases, and was the 7th leading cause of cancer-related death globally, accounting for approximately 445,000 deaths in 2022. Eastern and South central Asia and South Africa ranks the top high-prevalence regions. It was reported that about 70% of cases occur in men, whose incidence and mortality rates were 2- to 3-fold than that of women1. Esophageal cancer consists of two most common histologic subtypes—esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma—with ESCC accounting for approximately 85% of cases globally. Notably, the variability in ESCC incidence between high- and low- risk areas across the world was up to ten folds2. This peculiar distribution imbalances in gender and geography draw questions on the specificity of certain risk factors for ESCC development and progression3,4.

Emerging epidemiologic evidence indicates environmental and lifestyle factors, genetic factors, and nutritional deficiency collectively drive ESCC risk. Notably, persistent exposure to specific environmental pollutants has been implicated as a potential pathogenic etiologic factor for esophageal cancer, particularly within high-risk clusters4. However, definitive evidence for the causative roles of environmental carcinogens in esophageal cancer is still scarce. Cadmium (Cd), a ubiquitous heavy metal pollutant was classified as Group Ⅰ human carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) in 19935. Occupational or environmental exposure to cadmium exposure has been linked to elevated risks of lung, prostate, kidney, breast, bladder, and pancreatic cancer. We previously demonstrated that blood cadmium concentrations were markedly elevated in ESCC patients relative to healthy controls and that high cadmium levels independently predicted shorter progression-free survival (PFS). Additionally, 12-week exposure of ESCC cell lines to cadmium chloride promoted a malignant phenotype and conferred resistance to both radiotherapy and chemotherapy6. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects remain to be clarified fully.

Epigenetic and transcriptomic dysregulation, particularly aberrant expression of non-coding RNAs such as microRNA and long non-coding RNA, has been implicated in heavy metal carcinogenesis7. LncRNAs act as molecular scaffolds, forming competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks with mRNAs, miRNAs, and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), thereby playing regulatory roles in multiple cancers, including ESCC8. Although ceRNA networks have been extensively characterized in ESCC, their role in cadmium-driven oncogenesis remains largely unexplored.

Whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing affords a panoramic, high-resolution snapshot of cellular transcription. Hence, we employed this approach to compare the transcriptomic profiles of cadmium-treated and control EC-109 ESCC cells. Comprehensive bioinformatics analyses were then performed to construct cadmium-related mRNA-lncRNA-miRNA regulatory networks, thereby identifying key genes and pathways, and assessing their prognostic values in ESCC cohorts. Meanwhile, candidate hub genes were validated in additional cadmium-exposed tumor cell lines, and a putative therapeutic agent capable of reversing cadmium-induced malignant progression was predicted. Collectively, this study elucidates the molecular circuitry underlying cadmium-driven ESCC progression from an environmental etiology perspective, providing mechanistic leverage points for public health strategies aimed at mitigating cadmium-related carcinogenesis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

EC109, KYSE30 and KYSE150 ESCC cell lines were supplied by the Central laboratory of Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Yeasen, China) at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2. The EC109 cell line continually exposed to 5 µM cadmium chloride (purity 99%; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, United States) for a duration of 90 days (d) was previously developed and confirmed for its malignant phenotypes6. To obtain longer and lower-dose cadmium-treated cells, both KYSE30 and KYSE150 cells were treated with a concentration of 1 µM for durations of 90 and 150 days. Exposure doses and timelines were chosen according to lethal concentration 50% (LC50) (Supplementary Fig. 1) and published literature6,9. The chronic cadmium-treated ESCC (CCT-ESCC) cells were denoted CCT-EC109, CCT90d-KYSE-30, CCT150d-KYSE-30, CCT90d-KYSE-150, CCT 150d-KYSE-150, respectively.

Total RNA extraction

The malignant-phenotype differences between CCT-EC109 and EC109 cells were previously verified by our group6. Total RNA intended for transcriptome sequencing was isolated with the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, German), whereas RNA for RT-qPCR was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA). RNA concentration and purity were subsequently assessed with a Qubit®3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) and a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Whole transcriptome sequencing

With an integrity index higher than 7.0 determined by the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, USA), the purified RNA was utilized to construct a cDNA library, and then sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. A data volume of roughly 10G per sample and a Q20% higher than 90% were required for quality control.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes

Raw lncRNA and mRNA data was processed using the “limma” R package for microarray data. Hisat2 (version 2.0.5) was used as the reference database for raw data comparison, and the results of sequence alignments were stored in files in the Sequence Alignment/Map (SAM) format and the Binary Alignment/Map (BAM). Read counts of the RNA-seq data were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments (FPKM) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEG), including mRNAs, miRNAs and lncRNAs, between the cadmium-exposed and control groups (n = 3 per group) with P < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1 using R software’s edgeR tool. Finally, the DE lncRNAs, DE-miRNAs and DE mRNAs were visualized by clustering heatmaps using OmicShare (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/Home/Soft/heatmap).

LncRNA-mRNA co-expression network analysis and protein-protein interaction analysis for hub gene identification

Using the ENCORI website (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn), the interactions between DE lncRNAs and DE mRNAs was assessed and cross-referenced by Pearson correlation analysis based on a coefficient value (r) ≥ 0.9 along with a p < 0.05. The top 25 pairs were then mapped and visualized via Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1). Subsequently, the mRNAs in the top 25 lncRNA-mRNA co-expression pairs were inputted for protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis using the String database (http://string-db.org) based on betweenness centrality with a cut-off value of combined score ≥ 0.4. Afterwards, the PPI network was visualized by Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) and the sorted critical mRNAs and their associated lncRNAs were used to construct a key lncRNA-mRNA co-expression network.

Function enrichment analysis

The Kyoto Genome Encyclopedia (KEGG) pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment tools were performed to analyze the functional enrichment of hub mRNAs in the PPI network. Briefly, the R package “enrich” was utilized for GO functional annotation, and the KEGG database (http://www.genome.ad.jp/kegg) was performed for pathway enrichment analysis10,11. The cut-off criterion of significance was set with an adjusted p < 0.05 and count > 1.

Hub CeRNA network construction

The WebGestalt database (http://www.webgestalt.org/option.php) was used to further forecast the hub mRNAs and predicted lncRNAs in the PPI network (p < 0.05). Key miRNA nodes in the ceRNA network were obtained by intersecting the predicted miRNAs of the mRNAs and lncRNAs, as mentioned above, with the DE-miRNAs. TargetScan was used to predict the binding sites in the putative ceRNA network, and an expression correlation study of the RNA within the ceRNA network was carried out. Hub triple-regulatory networks were then established using the Cytoscape module cytoHubba and displayed using Cytoscape software (version 3.7.0). LncATLAS was performed to determine the subcellular localization of the candidate DElncRNAs. The LNCipedia (https://lncipedia.org) provided the DElncRNA sequences, and the LncATLAS (https://lncatlas.crg.eu) database was utilized to ascertain the cellular localization of the DElncRNAs based on their sequences. The hub triple-regulatory networks were ultimately visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.7.0).

Functional module identification

The expression of important genes in both normal and pan-cancer tissues was examined using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database for gene expression profile interaction analysis. Additionally, RNA from ESCC samples and control groups from TCGA was subjected to differential analysis.

Prognostic potential assessment of candidate genes in ESCC

To explore the candidate genes screened as above in relation to ESCC progression, we validated the prognostic value of these genes in a TCGA cohort of esophageal cancer (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Expression levels of the selected genes were categorized into low-expression and high-expression groups using X-Tile (https://www.giuspen.com/x-tile) based on the median expression level. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank test were then utilized to compare overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) between subgroups.

RT- qPCR validation

Total RNA of CCT-ESCC cells (Cd90-KYSE-30, Cd150-KYSE-30, Cd90-KYSE-150, Cd150-KYSE-150) and their parental cells were reverse transcribed into cDNA utilizing a HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA). The following primers were used for qPCR analysis: IPO5 forward 5′-CTGCTGAAGAGGCTAGACAAATG-3′ and reverse 5′-TCTGCCGCAATATCACAAACTT-3′; β-actin forward 5′-AGCGAGCATCCCCCAAAGTT-3′ and reverse 5′-GGGCACGAAGGCTCATCATT-3′. Gene expression relative to β-actin was determined by the comparative CT method (2 − ΔΔCT). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Western blot

Western blot was carried out referred to standard methods, as described previously6. In brief, proteins were extracted from cultured cells and separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins of interest were determined using corresponding specific antibodies after transferring to PVDF membranes. The following antibodies were used for determination: mouse monoclonal antibody against β-actin (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc), mouse monoclonal antibody against IPO5 (1:1000; Proteintech Group, Inc).

Immunohistochemistry evaluation

A total of 41 paraffin-embedded tissue sections pathologically diagnosed as ESCC were recruited from Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to determine the expression of IPO5 in tissue specimens. Briefly, slides were incubated at 4 °C overnight with anti-IPO5 antibody (1:500, Proteintech Group, Inc, China) and with biotin-labeled secondary immunoglobulin (1:100, DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark) at 37 °C for 30 min. The results were semi-quantitatively estimated by two pathologists independently without prior knowledge of the clinicopathological outcomes of the specimens. The scoring criteria has been described previously12. Correlations between IHC scores and blood cadmium levels was ultimately evaluated with the Spearman’s correlation coefficient. We confirm that this study was conducted in full compliance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods were performed in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, this study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (2019026).

Transfection with small-interfering RNA (siRNA)

Knockdown of IPO5 expression was performed in CCT90-KYSE30, CCT90-KYSE150 and their parental cells using specific siRNA against IPO5 (si-IPO5). Twenty-four hours prior to transfection, approximately 1 × 105 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and grew to 70–80% confluence. Subsequently, cells were separately transfected with si-IPO5 (sense: GGAAGCAACUCUACAGCUATT; antisense: UAGCUGUAGAGUUGCUUCCTT) and control siRNAs produced by GenePharma, Inc Suzhou, China. Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc California, US) was used for transfection following the manufacturers protocol. The transfected cells were then collected after 48 h for RT-qPCR and 72 h transwell assay.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion ability was determined using transwell chambers (8 μm membrane pores; Corning Inc, New York, USA) according to the protocol described previously6. Briefly, 50 µl matrigel (50 mg/l; BD biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, US) was pre-coated on the upper chamber for invasion assay. Cells in 200 µl serum-free RPMI-1640 medium were seeded onto the upper chambers with a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, and 500 µl medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After incubation at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 48 h (migration experiment) or 72 h (invasion experiment), the cells on the upper chamber were gently removed and the cells that migrated to the lower side of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Images of stained cells per membrane were counted for five random fields under a light microscope. Each experiment was independently performed in triplicate.

Immune infiltration analysis

It has been observed that in certain malignancies, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are independent predictors of lymph node status and survival. Using the TIMER2.0 database (http://cistrome.org/TIMER) and the EPIC database (https://www.epicgames.com/store/zh-CN), we assessed and verified any possible correlation between IPO5 expression and immune infiltration levels in ESCC. The number and prognostic significance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells (B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic ceneutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells) in ESCC were examined in connection to IPO5 expression. Finally, we assessed the effect of immune infiltration on ESCC patient clinical prognosis in more detail.

CMap analysis

The genome-wide expression profiles of small active chemicals estimated were available in the Link Mapping (CMap) database, which also includes details on 7056 expression profiles and roughly 6100 small-molecule interference studies (https://clue.io/). The ten DE mRNAs that were chosen above were submitted to CMap analysis. Drug candidates for reversing biological processes were identified by looking for compounds with negative linkage enrichment fractions.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 19.0 software and GraphPad Prism 5 software were performed for Statistical procedures. Data with normal distribution were presented by mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed by Student’s t-test, while data with a non-normal distribution were represented by median and analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationships between cadmium concentrations and IPO5 mRNA levels. Differences with P < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Transcriptional profiling of ESCC cells exposed to chronic low-dose cadmium

The workflow of the research is depicted in Fig. 1. Based on the high-throughput RNA-seq analysis of CCT-EC109 and EC109 cells, we obtained the transcriptional profile of ESCC cells subjected to chronic low-dose cadmium exposure. Clustering hierarchy revealed that the expression patterns of mRNAs, lncRNAs and miRNAs could distinctly distinguish CCT-EC109 and EC109 cells (Fig. 1). There were 1845 upregulated and 2176 downregulated DE mRNAs, 970 upregulated and 1212 downregulated DE lncRNAs, and 167 upregulated and 116 downregulated DE miRNAs. Prior to establishing the chronic cadmium (Cd)-exposure models, we conducted LC₅₀ assays to determine cadmium toxicity on ESCC cell lines. As a result, the LC50 values of EC109, KYSE30 and KYSE150 cells were 52.22 µM, 56.68 µM and 57.96 µM (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Identification of candidate cadmium-related genes through lncRNA-mRNA interacting network and PPI analysis

Utilizing the ENCORI database combined with Pearson’s correlation analysis, a lncRNA–mRNA interaction network based on DE lncRNAs and DE mRNAs was constructed, which contained 2550 co-expressed lncRNA-mRNA pairs with 103 DE mRNAs and 26 lncRNAs (Fig. 2A). PPI analysis for the association of the 103 candidate genes showed that there were 33 nodes and 78 edges in the PPI network (Fig. 2B). Therefore, the top 33 genes with larger betweenness were identified and included to reestablish lncRNA-mRNA co-expression networks involving 26 lncRNAs with correlation coefficient > 0.9 (Fig. 2C). Ultimately, 13 hub mRNAs were determined and sorted by betweenness decreasing.

Functional analyses for hub genes in relation to cadmium exposure in ESCC

To further clarify the potential mechanisms of the candidate cadmium-related genes, functional enrichment analysis was performed with 13 hub mRNAs as stated above. As shown in Fig. 2D, the significantly enriched pathways noted in the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were as follows:1 nucleocytoplasmic transport;2 ferroptosis;3 Huntington disease;4 cellular senescence, and5 hematopoietic cell lineage. Then, GO analysis showed that terms were mainly involved in1 nuclear lumen;2 nucleoplasm;3 membrane-enclosed lumen and4 intracellular organelle lumen (Fig. 2E).

Construction of the CeRNA network

The 26 lncRNAs and 33mRNAs obtained from the identified lncRNA-mRNA co-expression networks were employed to construct a lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA interaction network. Twenty-two miRNAs regulated by the 26 hub lncRNAs and 122 miRNAs targeting the 13 hub mRNAs were predicted, and then 9 intersecting miRNAs (upregulated: hsa-miR-455-5p, hsa-miR-200c-3p, hsa-miR-1286, hsa-miR-379-5p, hsa-miR-154-5p, hsa-miR-103a-3p, hsa-miR-3150a-3p; downregulated: hsa-miR-193a-5p, hsa-miR-429; Fig. 2F) were identified. Based on the ceRNA hypothesis, three lncRNAs (AC107068.1, TTN-AS1, MAPKAPK5-AS1; all upregulated) and ten mRNAs (IPO5, NUDCD1, OSTM1, CCNB1, FANCD2, TFRC, POLR2B, HTT, NUP43, NBN; all upregulated) were ultimately selected as key nodes for ceRNA network construction. The pivotal lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network diagrams were then established and visualized with Cytoscape (Fig. 2G), elucidating the contribution of non-coding RNAs in mechanisms of cadmium carcinogenicity in ESCC.

Small molecular drugs against carcinogenic effect of cadmium

To identify candidate small molecules that may reverse pathological processes induced by cadmium, the screened DE mRNAs in CCT-EC109 cells were included in the CMap online database for analysis. The top 10 antagonistic compounds with the highly negative correlation in turn are listed in Table 1. Among them, JAK3-inhibitor-I, with the highest enrichment score (enrichment score = − 99.89), was identified to have the maximum potential to reverse or alleviate the pathologic processes induced by cadmium in ESCC (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Identification and validation of selected genes associated with ESCC prognosis and cadmium exposure

To evaluate the prognostic potential of the 10 candidate mRNAs, an esophageal cohort available in TCGA was utilized for validation by GEPIA. Patients were stratified into high- and low- expression groups according to the median value of each gene. High-IPO5 expression was significantly associated with poorer DFS (P = 0.032; Fig. 3A), while none of the hub mRNAs correlated with OS (P > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 2). Pan-cancer analysis revealed that IPO5 was frequently upregulated across multiple cancer types, including esophageal carcinoma (Fig. 3B). Elevated IPO5 expression in esophageal carcinoma was corroborated in a TCGA cohort comprising 82 ESCC samples and 1456 normal samples (Fig. 3C) and in the GEO dataset GSE23400 containing 51 ESCC- normal pairs (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, we showed a consistent upregulation of IPO5 in cadmium-treated MCF-7 breast cancer cells (GSE136595; P < 0.01; Fig. 3E). Likewise, increased mRNA levels IPO5 were also observed in four CCT-ESCC cell lines (CCT90-KYSE-30, CCT150-KYSE-30, CCT90-KYSE-150, CCT 150-KYSE-150), implying a time-dependent induction (P < 0.05; Fig. 3F). Western blot results corroborated this trend at the protein level (P < 0.05; Fig. 3G). Notably, a positive correlation between IPO5 protein expression and blood cadmium concentration was observed in 41 paraffin-embedded ESCC tissue sections (r = 0.7491, P < 0.001; Fig. 3H).

Knockdown of IPO5 attenuates the invasiveness of CCT-ESCC cells

CCT90-KYSE-30, CCT90-KYSE-150 and their parental cells were then transfected with IPO5-targeting siRNA to interrogate its role in cadmium-driven invasion. As shown in Fig. 4A, the knockdown efficiency achieved over 70% (P < 0.001). IPO5 knockdown markedly inhibited both cell migration and invasion in control cells and these effects were even more pronounced in chronic cadmium-treated groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 4B, C, D), suggesting that IPO5 suppression attenuates cadmium-induced migratory and invasive phenotypes in ESCC cells. Construction of an IPO5-centered CeRNA network contributing to cadmium carcinogenesis in ESCC

Considering only cytoplasmic lncRNA can act as ceRNAs, we inspected the subcellular localizations of 2 candidate IPO5-related lncRNAs by lncLocator. As depicted in Fig. 5A, MAPKAPK5-AS1 was mainly present in the cytoplasmic compartment, while the subcellular localization of Ac107068.1 could not be determined, suggesting that MAPKAPK5-AS1 may act as a sponge for hsa-miR-379-5p or hsa-miR-154-5p to regulate expression of IPO5. Correlation analysis of TCGA data demonstrated that the correlation coefficient of MAPKAPK5-AS1 with IPO5 was 0.25 (P = 0.023; Fig. 5B). Afterwards, the binding sites of hsa-miR-379-5p with IPO5 and MAPKAPK5-AS1 were respectively predicted (Fig. 5C), but the binding sites for hsa-miR-154-5p could not be found. Therefore, MAPKAPK5-AS1-hsa-miR-379-5p-IPO5 was deemed as the potential ceRNA regulatory mechanism for cadmium carcinogenesis in ESCC.

Discussion

Cadmium carcinogenesis is a multifactorial process driven by the interplay of numerous genes and signaling cascades. Nevertheless, the precise molecular events that underlie cadmium-mediated tumorigenesis and progression of ESCC remain poorly understood. In this study, we screened cadmium-associated transcriptomic profile from CCT-EC109 cells, which comprised 2182 DE lncRNAs, 283 DE miRNAs and 4021 DE mRNAs. Integration of lncRNA-mRNA co-expression analysis with a PPI network pinpointed thirteen hub genes. KEGG and GO analyses was then performed for functional annotation of these genes. CMAP analysis identified JAK3-inhibitor-I as the top scoring compound predicted to counteract cadmium-induced oncogenic reprogramming. Subsequently, IPO5, a karyopherin superfamily protein related to the importin β family13, was established as a prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target in ESCC patients with cadmium exposure. Finally, we delineated a novel MAPKAPK5-AS1-hsa-miR-379-5p/hsa-miR-154-5p-IPO5 ceRNA axis that appears to orchestrate cadmium-driven ESCC progression, thereby suggesting an IPO5-centered regulatory framework for future mechanistic dissection of cadmium carcinogenesis.

Functional enrichment analyses revealed that the cadmium-related hub mRNAs are predominantly linked to nucleocytoplasmic transport, ferroptosis, Huntington’s disease and cellular senescence and hematopoietic cell lineage—pathways previously implicated in both carcinogenesis and cadmium toxicity. Our finding aligns with prior observations that long-term, low-dose cadmium exposure confers cell growth and ferroptosis resistance in prostate cancer14. Interestingly, short-term cadmium exposure was reported to cause ferroptosis of vascular endothelial cells in vivo and in vitro15 , underscoring exposure duration-dependent effects of cadmium on iron-dependent cell death. The hub genes overlapped with pathway implicated in Huntington’s disease, supporting the concept that cancer, inflammation, and neurodegenerative diseases share convergent molecular mechanisms16,17. For example, targeting mTOR signaling has been proposed as a novel therapeutic strategy in both Huntington’s disease models and chronic myelogenous leukemia, as well as colorectal cancer metastases18. Whether cadmium exposure raises Huntington’s disease risk in ESCC patients warrants further investigation. Nucleocytoplasmic transport was identified as the most significant item in the current study, which corroborates with previous observations that low-dose, chronic cadmium exposure stimulates nuclear translocation of thioredoxin-1 and metal-responsive transcription factor-1, resulting in NF-κB activation and increased metallothionein upregulation, respectively19,20.

JAK3 mutations occur across diverse neoplasms, including ESCC21, and JAK3 has been widely considered as a promising target in preclinical models of acute myeloid leukemia22 and glioblastoma multiforme23 by suppressing proliferation and stemness respectively. Notably, enhancing cancer stemness was also proposed to play a role in the carcinogenic potential of cadmium in our previous study6. In the present study, JAK3-inhibitor-I stands out as the premier small-molecule candidate to reverse cadmium-mediated carcinogenesis in ESCC. This finding aligns with prior observation in human immature T-cell lines, where acute cadmium treatment triggered JAK3-dependent transcriptional programs while suppressing Fas expression24. However, little evidence has shown the effect of JAK3 inhibitor on ESCC cell response to heavy metal stimuli. Further experiments are warranted to explore the biological functions of JAK3-inhibitor-I in cadmium carcinogenicity.

IPO5 emerged as the core gene associated with cadmium-induced ESCC progression. IPO5 encodes a 124 kDa protein that mediates nuclear import of cellular and viral proteins13. IPO5 is overexpressed in esophageal cancer and correlates with advanced pathological stage, poor OS, and increased immune infiltration25,26. Mechanically, IPO5 promotes cell growth and migration through RAS-ERK signaling and MMP7 activation in esophageal cancer cells25,26. In the current study, elevated IPO5 mRNA was validated across multiple cadmium-treated cancer cell lines, and its protein abundance in tumor specimens positively correlated with blood cadmium concentrations in ESCC patients. Functionally, IPO5 knockdown attenuated cadmium-induced invasion and metastasis in Transwell assays. Intriguingly, prior study has linked IPO5 overexpression to 5-fluorouracil resistance in colorectal cancer cells27, corroborating our earlier observation that CCT-EC109 cells exhibit resistance to both 5-fluorouracil and cisplatin6. Collectively, these findings establish IPO5 as a pivotal orchestrator of both tumor aggressiveness and chemoresistance in CCT-ESCC cells, underscoring its therapeutic potential for intercepting cadmium-driven carcinogenesis. Nevertheless, the definitive role IPO5 in cadmium-induced malignancy warrants further validation in genetically engineered mouse models or in three-dimensional (3D) spheroid models (such as 3D spheroid invasion assays), which more faithfully recapitulate tissue architecture and the intricate cellular behaviors observed in human.

IPO5 has been shown to interact with IQGAP1, a regulator of β-catenin, and thus activates canonical Wnt signaling through mediating nuclear localization of β-catenin28. Notably, our previous research demonstrated that cadmium could cause an increase in total β-catenin and its accumulation in the nuclei of both nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells and ESCC cells6,29. Hence, we infer that cadmium-induced IPO5 upregulation drive nuclear translocation of β-catenin and subsequent Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation, thereby contributing to cadmium carcinogenesis in ESCC.

Nevertheless, a definitive functional landscape and hierarchical regulatory network for IPO5 remain to be systematically elucidated and experimentally validated. Future work should integrate IPO5 knock-out/knock-in and over-expression models with multi-omics profiling to dissect IPO5-dependent signaling in cadmium-driven carcinogenesis. Gain- and loss-of-function screens, together with co-immunoprecipitation and chromatin-conformation assays, will be essential to identify the upstream regulators and downstream nodes that propagate IPO5-mediated oncogenic signaling in ESCC. To validate the predicted ceRNA network, dual-luciferase reporter assays, RIP-qPCR, and siRNA-based lncRNA/miRNA perturbation followed by rescue experiments will be required. Moreover, JAK3-inhibitor-I was suggested as a candidate potential for reversing cadmium carcinogenesis in ESCC. However, exhaustive database searches, structural modelling and biochemical evidence currently provide no support for a direct, high-affinity interaction between JAK3-inhibitor-I and IPO5. In view of pathway logic, JAK3 and IPO5 appear to reside in a sequential rather than physical combination within the JAK/STAT nuclear-import axis. Consequently, rigorous validation combining pharmacological or genetic JAK3 inhibition with functional assays and matched transcriptomic profiling will be required to clarify the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Moreover, IPO5 participates in regulating tumor immune microenvironment and mediating tumor immune response27.Persistent low-dose cadmium exposure leads to cadmium accumulation in immune cells, triggering apoptosis, skewed differentiation, composition shifts, aberrant cytokine secretion and inflammatory reactions30. Concurrently, cadmium-exposed cancer cells could upregulate an array of pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory factors and chemokines, such as IL-6, IL-8 and CCL231,32, which orchestrate the recruitment of diverse immune cell populations. However, the precise mechanisms by which cadmium reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in ESCC remain elusive. In the present study, elevated IPO5 expression positively correlated with increased CD4+ T cell and macrophage abundance, yet inversely associated with both CD8+ T cell and NK cell infiltration. Consistent with the established prognostic significance of IPO5 in ESCC, these findings corroborate previous reports demonstrating that elevated tumor-associated macrophages density and an increased CD4/CD8 T cells ratio predict poorer OS in ESCC patients33,34, whereas robust NK cell infiltration had a significantly better five-year cumulative incidence of recurrence and reduced tumor-related mortality35. As IPO5 is currently identified as a key cadmium-responsive target gene in ESCC cells, we postulate that it mediates cadmium-driven reprogramming of the tumor immune microenvironment in ESCC. The precise molecular mechanisms by which IPO5 orchestrates immune cell chemotaxis and functional polarization remain to be elucidated.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study suggests IPO5 is the hub gene related to cadmium exposure, serving as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for cadmium-induced ESCC progression and therapeutic resistance. The prediction of JAK3-inhibitor-I as the prospective therapeutic agent and MAPKAPK5-AS1-hsa-miR-379-5p-IPO5 as the potential regulatory mechanism for cadmium carcinogenesis provides valuable clues for prevention and therapeutic strategies on cadmium-associated tumorigenesis and progression. Nevertheless, validation experiments are warranted to verify the above results further.
