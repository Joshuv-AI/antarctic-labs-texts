A mechanism-aided transformer may transform in-flight aviation safety
Authors: Ji Song, Fusheng Hou, Xing Wang, Xiaowei Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn accurate forecast on the in-flight parameters and aviation status is crucial for aviation safety, enabling timely responses to potential upcoming urgent risks during flights. Conventional physics-based method, the F-Factor, a wind shear hazard index associated with the total aircraft energy and its rate of change, face limitations such as sensitivity to physical assumptions, high false alarm rate, and high uncertainties. The advent of machine learning (ML) has significantly influenced various prediction tasks, offering new possibilities for aviation-related forecasting and hazard alarming. Here, we present a physical mechanism-aided TwinTransformer approach that integrates aviation dynamic constraints with deep learning to improve multivariate in-flight prediction, with targeted enhancement of windshear hazard alarming. Tested on real-world in-flight datasets, the proposed approach can consistently outperform cutting-edge benchmarks and provide pilots with reliable 15-s-ahead windshear hazard warnings. These results demonstrate a practical path toward intelligent decision support in aviation safety by AI and highlight how combining physical knowledge with ML can enhance in-flight aviation safety against windshear hazards.

A precise forecast of in-flight parameters and aviation status is essential for ensuring aviation safety, as it allows for timely responses to potential urgent risks that may arise during flights. This article presents a physical mechanism-aided TwinTransformer approach that integrates aviation dynamic constraints with deep learning to improve multivariate in-flight prediction, with targeted enhancement of windshear hazard alarming. Tested on real-world in-flight datasets, the proposed approach can consistently outperform cutting-edge benchmarks and provide pilots with reliable 15-s-ahead windshear hazard warnings. These results demonstrate a practical path toward intelligent decision support in aviation safety by AI and highlight how combining physical knowledge with machine learning can advance in-flight aviation safety against windshear hazards.

Introduction

Aviation safety is of paramount importance as it safeguards the lives of passengers and crew, and ensures the smooth operation of global transportation networks, compelling humanity to develop an extensive range of technologies to ensure secure flights. Machine learning (ML) and AI techniques have been developed and significantly improved the safety of autonomous vehicles patient care (1), cybersecurity (2), and detecting extreme weather hazards (3), offering new possibilities for improving the in-flight hazard forecasting and alarming, as well as enhancing aviation safety. In this article, we focus on the wind-related factors crucial to aviation safety, in particular, windshear (Fig. 1). Windshear, a physical phenomenal description of rapid variation in wind speed or/and direction, has significant impacts on aviation safety (4). It generally comes from complicated topographical and weather conditions including orographic winds, land and sea breezes, microbursts, and so forth. Almost 20% of aviation accidents recorded from 2020 to 2024 were caused by wind-related factors, with windshear itself resulting in 18% accidents in 2022 alone (5, 6). Pilots can suffer from insufficient reaction time, low controllability, and limited effective solutions when they fail to break away from high-risk areas affected by windshear (7).

Aircraft encounters microbursts during takeoff/approach processes. Generally an aircraft would firstly be exposed in increasing head wind which brings higher airspeed and leads to more lift force; while subsequently the aircraft would suffer from an abrupt strong tail wind, which causes an undesired airspeed drop and results in dangerous stalling, out-of-balance, and out-of-control, until it escapes from areas affected by low-level windshear.

Existing windshear detection and forecast (WDF) methods can be classified into three categories, including runway-based, region-based, and aircraft-based methods, according to the original data sources. Both runway-based and region-based methods rely on comprehensive monitoring systems installed at the airport, where runway-based methods raise real-time wind mutation alarms along runways, while region-based methods provide estimation and prediction of wind conditions in the airport with the help of large-scale simulation model for wind and turbulence (8–10). Aircraft-based methods can make full utilization of parameters monitored by in-flight devices, with little support from the powerful and expensive airport systems. Therefore, aircraft-based methods are more crucial for ensuring aviation safety in real-world scenes. On one hand, aircraft may encounter windshear beyond the maximum monitoring ability of airports, when pilots need to be alarmed timely; On the other hand, some airports may not be equipped with the expensive low-level windshear warning system (11) to support runway-based or region-based WDF, especially in airports from developing countries. Therefore, an efficient aircraft-based WDF method is of paramount importance as it safeguards the lives of passengers and crew for aviation safety.

Existing aircraft-based WDF methods involves vertical airflow estimation (12) or low-energy state prevention (13). These methods require instruments like a millimeter-wave radar to collect necessary data such as temperature, flow rate, or water vapor of air mass along the flight path, and they have limitations in predictability due to sensing accuracy. As far as physical mechanism-based methods, F-factor metrics (14) is widely adopted and recognized as a reliable intensity indicator of windshear detection. As expressed in Equation (1), F-factor physically represents aircraft energy variation caused by wind. It is monitored to prevent uncontrolled aircraft energy loss, which is generally manifested as stalling or the loss of lift of an aircraft. However, F-factor can be used to detect windshear to some extent but fails to predict potential wind variation, which has restricted its application in predictive windshear system (15, 16). According to Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) (17) and European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) (18), 15 s or more should be set aside before aircraft encounter the core zone affected by windshear so that pilots possess sufficient time to conduct efficient operations. An efficient timeseries forecast method is urgently needed to provide accurate prediction of future key flight parameters, so that reliable aviation hazard warnings can be accomplished in advance of windshear risks.

Cutting-edge AI methods have demonstrated certain effectiveness in dealing with timeseries data, such as recurrent neural network (19), decision tree (20, 21), deep learning (22, 23), reinforcement learning (24) and transformer (25, 26) . These breakthroughs in AI have significantly improved the safety of autonomous vehicles (1), patient care (2), cybersecurity (3), and detecting extreme weather hazards (27). However, the in-flight multivariate forecasting and windshear prediction is uniquely challenging compared to other applications due to the following perspectives: (i) high safety-critical nature: a single error in wind shear detection or safety monitoring could potentially lead to catastrophic accidents. Thus, the margin for error is much smaller compared to other applications; (ii) complex and dynamic environment: aircraft operate in complex weather conditions, various altitudes, air masses, and geographical locations, each with unique characteristics, resulting in large uncertainties in windshear patterns; (iii) strict regulatory requirements: pure data-driven methods, without mechanism-aided (MA) efforts, may lack high levels of transparency, explainability, and reliability for aviation applications. Extreme AI risks (28) also draw attentions in the public.

Though recent studies have highlighted the application of advanced AI techniques to enhance flight safety assurance and incident prediction, most of them focus on reactive analysis, such as investigations of flight accident reports (29) or performance evaluations based on simulated flight data (30). Existing AI-based WDF and timeseries forecast methods do not address long-term flight parameter prediction (31) or rely extensively on ground-based monitoring data (32). These studies are limited by a lack of onboard deployment capability and insufficient predictive performance, thereby constraining their ability to deliver real-time safety assurances against high-risk in-flight incidents. There is an urgent need for an efficient onboard forecast technique with real-world physical mechanisms incorporated to improve the forecast reliability (33).

In this article, we propose a physical MA transformer-based model. This novel approach illuminates a path toward transforming in-flight windshear forecasting and ensuring aviation safety by incorporating physical mechanisms with deep learning. Our key contributions comprise an efficient forecasting network for real-time aircraft in-flight status, a robust theory integrating mechanism-driven modeling with deep learning from the perspective of uncertainty quantification, and an accurate estimation of external wind-related factors without relying on remote sensing devices such as radar.

Results

A novel mechanism-data integrated (MDI) architecture is proposed in this study to integrate physical mechanisms into deep learning, which enhances the forecast accuracy, robustness and explainability. The key idea of the proposed MDI architecture is utilizing “reactive” physical mechanisms to calibrate “predictive” deep learning models, thereby obtaining more reliable and accurate predictions on real-world aircraft status.

As shown in Fig. 2, the MDI architecture consists of two subsequent phases: The first-phase ML model generates a data-driven prediction and then the second-phase MA model further improves the prediction. Under this architecture, a representative model named mechanism aided TwinTransformer (MA-TwinTran) is proposed to provide reliable in-flight parameter forecast in aircraft-based WDF scenes.

Enhancing safety: MDI architecture produces robustly reliable forecast

To further verify the reliability and robustness of the proposed MDI architecture on real-world in-flight parameter forecast, expanded experiments were conducted using more comprehensive first-phase ML models.

As shown in Fig. 5, three transformer-based models with different emphases on feature extraction are selected to produce the first-phase basic prediction, respectively. Subsequently, those predictions are improved by an identical MA model with the same physical MA. It is expected that our proposed architecture demonstrates reliable forecast capabilities under different model combinations, with the second-phase MA model consistently offering a significant improvement in the basic data-driven predictions.

As illustrated in Fig. 4, the proposed architecture’s performance on NASA’s real-world in-flight data has been sufficiently evaluated through the 10-fold cross-validation. Benefiting from the second-phase MA model, the MA-TwinTran model achieved a 1.64% improvement in prediction accuracy and a 3.03% uncertainty reduction on wind-related factors compared to purely data-driven forecasts. Consistent with the results shown in Fig. 3, the model demonstrates stably promising forecasts across the 5-/10-/15-s horizons. This positions the MA-TwinTran model, a representative instance of the proposed MDI architecture, as an effective tool for reliable multivariate forecast and flight safety assurance.

Moreover, by adjusting the first-phase ML models, the second-phase MA model can further enhance the basic prediction, reaching up to 18.23% accuracy gain and 25.78% uncertainty reduction. This clearly demonstrates the calibration and improvement effect of the proposed second-phase MA model on purely data-driven predictions. The proposed MDI architecture has brought about significant positive effects on the reliability and interpretability of in-flight parameter forecast by incorporating real-world physical mechanisms. These advancements provide robust support for windshear prevention and significantly contribute to flight safety. This breakthrough underscores the transformative potential of hybrid modeling in aviation meteorology, paving the way for more reliable and proactive flight decision-support systems.

Enhancing transparency: physical MA ML

The proposed MDI architecture takes t-long data of key flight parameters as input X and output the final prediction Y^ with respect to a targeted future horizon Y. The whole forecast process is expressed as Equation (2).

As illustrated in Fig. 2B, a basic forecast Y0^ is firstly generated by the first-phase ML model f(⋅) with an original input X. Subsequently, with Y0^ as input, the second-phase MA model s(⋅) would refine the basic forecast as m(Y^0) based on a real-world physical mechanism m(⋅). The forecast error is then modeled as s(Y0^−m(Y0^)), which is utilized to calibrate the basic forecast Y0^ into the final forecast Y^.

In the aforementioned real-world in-flight experiments, Equation (3) is incorporated into the second-phase MA model as an example of available physical mechanisms. This mechanism comes from the windshear simulation model recorded in ETSO-C117b (18), which utilizes the aircraft’s airspeed V and groundspeed Vg to approximately estimate the real-time horizontal wind speed Wx. Motivated by the importance of wind forecasting highlighted by the F-factor, this mechanism is utilized to targetedly calibrate the Wx predictions of first-phase models, so as to further improve the forecast precision of wind-related factors.

Thorough real-world experiments summarized in Figs. 3 and 4 have verified the robustness and stability of the proposed approach. Within our proposed MDI architecture, other existing reactive mechanism formulas can also be incorporated into the second-phase MA model, with lenient and flexible requirement of the first-phase feature extractors. This positions the architecture for broader application in the pursuit of improved aviation safety.

Discussion

This article has proposed a novel MDI architecture to incorporate aviation physical mechanisms into ML methods for better multivariate timeseries forecast in aviation safety. MA-TwinTran, one representative method of the proposed MDI architecture, is taken as a classic example to show its practicality and superiority. The model is able to provide accurate and reliable forecast of in-flight key parameters. It sufficiently satisfied the need for windshear detection beyond 15-s-long horizon within real-world flights. The adoption of the proposed method would efficiently complement the monitoring ability of airports and significantly contribute to aviation safety.

Long-term forecast

As discussed before, multilevel experiments illustrated in Fig. 3 have guaranteed robustly reliable forecasts across all of the 5-, 10-, and 15-s-long horizons.

Notably, although the proposed MA-TwinTran maintains an acceptable prediction error for a 15-s forecast horizon, its long-term performance degrades relative to short-term forecasts. Specifically, as the forecast horizon increases from 5 to 15 s, the mean of bias histograms in Fig. 3 becomes increasingly skewed to the right. This indicates that the model gradually approaches its full capacity with longer horizons, leading to a certain degree of overestimation.

This observation suggests that, the incorporated “reactive” mechanism exhibits limitations in long-term prediction scenarios. This is a natural consequence of the substantial reduction in temporal dependence over long forecast horizons. Nevertheless, the model remains reliable enough to support flight safety assurance within the required 15-s-long horizon.

Model comparison

As illustrated in the aforementioned Fig. 5, the three first-phase ML models aim at integrating features from both temporal and multivariate dimensions for more sufficient information extraction. Comprehensive real-world experiments in Fig. 4 have shown that: ZonalTransformer has limitations in capturing temporal features without the help of globally attentive extractor; TwinTransformer conducts sufficient feature extraction on in-flight data for robustly reliable forecast; while ZT-Transformer fails to improve the performance by integrating more redundant temporal features.

The relative performance loss of the ZT-Transformer is mainly attributed to integrating excessively redundant features. It simultaneously integrates globally attentive features and locally convolutional features in the temporal dimension, which results in redundant temporal features. Those redundancies are assigned with nonzero weights in the decoder to suppress noise and correlation. This results in dilution of effective gradient, inefficient convergence, and higher overfitting risks.

Furthermore, with real-world mechanisms aided, the differences between MA-TwinTran and MA-ZT-Tran are significantly smaller than those between their corresponding first-phase ML models. This demonstrates a powerful capability of the proposed second-phase MA module and MDI architecture to reduce the prediction uncertainties of data-driven ML models by leveraging real-world physical mechanisms.

The above analysis indicates that, within the proposed MDI architecture, integrating attentively extracted features from both temporal and multivariate dimensions is sufficient to provide reliable forecasts for real-world in-flight parameters. A representative approach is proposed and implemented as TwinTransformer in this study, which is further aided by physical mechanisms and refined as MA-TwinTran.

Robustness analysis

The proposed MDI architecture possesses another significant advantage. There is a nonnegligible challenge when introducing aviation physical mechanisms in forecasting flight parameters: to avoid the influence of biased mechanism. Generally, existing physical mechanisms are theoretically reliable but may not be accurate in real-world flight records. Take Equation (3) as an example. This formula is biased but unable to further improve the estimation since other wind-related data is insufficient in most real-world flight records. Similarly, available mechanisms can be too sketchy to provide reliable estimation under limited conditions without support from ground facilities.

However, the proposed MDI architecture provides a reliable approach to approximate that estimation bias by utilizing information embedded in large-scale real-world data. As explained in Fig. 7 and Equation (2), the second-phase MA model s(⋅) would extract deep features within residuals between basic forecast f(X) and mechanism re-estimation m(f(X)), which efficiently predict the mechanism bias via a data-driven approach. Both more accurate forecast and more reliable mechanism models are produced during that process. It is expected that the second-phase MA model can not only integrate mechanisms and lead to better forecast but also make up for the high bias which is unavoidable when applying mechanisms. This provides another valuable engineering accomplishment in enhancing aviation safety.

Furthermore, the proposed two-phase MDI architecture has flexibility and expansibility because far more mechanism constraints can be integrated into the second-phase improve model, so that more comprehensive and accurate forecast could be accomplished after fine model tuning.

There certainly exist some exploration space for the proposed methods. For example, ML models utilized in both two phases may have better alternatives. A scheme to organize more integrated mechanisms can be further researched. Far more efforts are needed for the method’s multilevel validation for aviation regulations.

Data acquisition and performance metrics

The architecture’s forecast ability was tested on the NASA DASHlink Sample Flight Dataset (NASA Dataset for short) (34), a large-scale and multivariate dataset of real-world quick access recorder records. One-hundred pieces of flight records were randomly selected and preprocessed to construct a 10-fold dataset with a proportion of train, validation, and test datasets being 7:2:1.

Two performance metrics including root mean standard error (RMSE) and sdjusted r2-score (radjust2 for short) are selected in order to evaluate the accuracy and reliability of predictions. RMSE metrics is calculated according to RMSE=1n∑i=0n(yi−yi^)2, while radjust2 metrics is calculated based on radjust2=1−(1−r2)(n−1)(n−p−1), with r2=1−∑i=0n(yi−yi^)2∑i=0n(yi−yi¯)2. Here, n is the sample size, ie forecast time steps in this study; p is the feature size, ie selected variables number in this study; yi and yi^ are the underlying truth and the forecast result at the ith time step, separately; yi¯ is the mean of all yi, with i ranging from 0 to n.

Both RMSE and radjust2 metrics range from 0 to 1, thus a theoretically perfect forecast model is expected to have (RMSE, radjust2) = (0, 1). Therefore, the optimization objective is to decrease RMSE and increase radjust2 as much as possible.

Input/output variable selection

There are totally 186 parameters with different sampling rate and respective flight length ranging from hundreds to thousands of seconds in each flight record of the NASA dataset. Among all those available variables, 19 key parameters which are valuable for WDF are selected according to the windshear simulation model constructed in ETSO-C117b document from EASA (18). Those parameters, as aforementioned in Fig. 2C, are primarily from mechanical-, power-, and control system of an aircraft, as well as the external flight environment during flights.

Data preprocessing

There are four preprocessing operations subsequently conducted on the selected flight records:

Removing invalid values: Timeseries data with little variation at the start and end of most flights can be reasonably seen as the initialization statuses of devices. Such data typically indicate that the aircraft is still parked, making wind shear forecasts unnecessary. These data are considered invalid and are removed.
