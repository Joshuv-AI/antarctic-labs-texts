Cost-effectiveness of intradiscal injection with condoliase versus microendoscopic discectomy for lumbar disc herniation: a retrospective multicenter study
Authors: Takuya Takahashi, Hiroyuki Sato, Yohei Takahashi, Kota Watanabe, Tomohiro Banno, Kyohei Sakaki, Yoshiyasu Arai, Yuichi Takano, Yawara Eguchi, Yuki Taniguchi, Satoshi Maki, Yasuchika Aoki, Toshitaka Yoshii, Hiroshi Yamada, Takashi Kaito, Yutaka Hiraizumi, Masatune Yamagata, Masaya Nakamura, Naobumi Hosogane, Akihiro Hirakawa, Seiji Ohtori, Takashi Hirai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntradiscal condoliase injection has been described as a safe treatment with relatively favorable results. In Japan, the direct medical cost of intradiscal condoliase injection is significantly lower than that of microendoscopic discectomy (MED). The objective of this study was to assess the cost-effectiveness of condoliase injection compared to MED in patients with lumbar disc herniation (LDH). This retrospective study included patients who received either an intradiscal condoliase injection or MED for LDH at nine participating centers. Pretreatment magnetic resonance imaging revealed that the patients had subligamentous-type herniation. A Markov model was used to evaluate cost-effectiveness. The robustness of the results was assessed using one-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses. The cost-effectiveness was assessed using data from 229 patients treated with condoliase injection and 98 patients treated with MED. Condoliase injection had a significantly lower mean direct cost compared to MED, while the quality-adjusted life years were equal, indicating similar effectiveness and potential for cost savings. A one-way sensitivity analysis revealed that condoliase injection remained superior in nearly all scenarios. A probabilistic sensitivity analysis revealed that condoliase injection was cost-saving compared to MED in 92.6% of cases. These findings indicate that intradiscal condoliase injection is cost-saving compared to MED for the treatment of LDH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27802-3.Keywords: Condoliase, Microendoscopic discectomy, Lumbar disc herniation, Cost-effectivenessSubject terms: Neuropathic pain, Outcomes research, Spinal cord diseases

Introduction

Condoliase was approved in Japan in 2018 as a novel intradiscal treatment for lumbar disc herniation (LDH). This enzyme (chondroitin sulfate ABC endolyase) is highly substrate-specific for glycosaminoglycans, including chondroitin sulfate and hyaluronic acid, which are abundant in the nucleus pulposus1. Condoliase’s indication is limited to subligamentous-type LDH, and it is currently only approved for one use per patient for safety reasons. Unlike chymopapain, condoliase lacks protease activity, allowing it to cause chemonucleolysis without affecting nerve or ligamentous tissues2. Intradiscal condoliase injection is a safe and effective treatment for radiculopathy caused by LDH3,4. Improvement rates for leg pain range from 70 to 85%, with 0–13% of patients requiring surgery5,6. In the United States, a phase three randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial found that condoliase significantly improved radicular leg pain by week 13,7 consistent with findings from a Japanese study4.

Microendoscopic discectomy (MED) is widely considered the gold standard treatment for LDH. This minimally invasive procedure is performed through a small 16-mm incision, preserving dorsal muscles and resulting in less postoperative wound pain8. Although the type of herniation is not specified, the improvement rates for pain after MED have been reported to be between 88 and 94%8,9, with reoperation rates ranging from 2.0 to 10.7%10,11.

The reimbursement for MED was 303,900 Japanese yen, whereas the reimbursement for intradiscal condoliase injection was only 53,500 yen, indicating a significant cost difference. The condoliase injection requires specialized techniques, with precise injection into the center of the disc identified as a critical factor12 for successful outcomes. Consequently, mature techniques for proper injection must be developed and approved by spine experts. Evaluating the cost-effectiveness and reviewing technical fees for these treatments is critical for recommending appropriate reimbursement rates. However, no multicenter trials have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of condoliase injection compared to MED. Therefore, this multicenter study sought to assess the comparative cost-effectiveness of both procedures.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective, multicenter study was conducted by the Japanese Society for Spine Surgery and Related Research. It included patients diagnosed with LDH who received either intradiscal injection of condoliase (Hernicore®, Seikagaku Corp., Tokyo, Japan/Kaken Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) or MED between February 2017 and September 2021 at nine participating centers. Patients were followed for a year after treatment. The indication for treatment was nerve root compression due to LDH, as confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and patients were required to be resistant to conservative therapy. Cases with transligamentous or sequestration types of herniation were excluded for both condoliase and MED, as well as those with missing pretreatment MRI data or no postoperative follow-up.

Evaluation

Demographic and clinical data were gathered, including age, gender, body mass index, disease duration, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, herniation level, and numeric rating scale (NRS) scores for leg and low back pain. Furthermore, the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores, adverse events (such as low back pain, leg pain, skin symptoms, fever, headache, dural injury, postoperative hematoma, and wrong-level surgery), and any reoperations due to adverse events were recorded. The ODI is divided into 10 sections, each of which assesses a different aspect of daily life. Higher ODI percentages indicate more disability, with 0 representing the best condition and 100 representing the worst13. Adverse events were defined as sudden occurrences during or immediately following treatment. The costs were obtained from the hospital’s financial system and classified as direct medical expenses from a healthcare standpoint. The initial treatment costs included medical service fees for the procedure, medication, anesthesia, and any associated anesthetic medications. The other initial costs included outpatient and inpatient expenses incurred during treatment. Outpatient costs included diagnosis, medication, and examination fees after the initial treatment. The reoperation costs included medical service fees and associated inpatient expenses.

Model structure

In Japan, residents are required to have health insurance. Patients usually pay 10–30% of their medical expenses out-of-pocket, depending on their age and income, with the rest covered by insurance. There are also limits on maximum out-of-pocket expenses to ensure affordability. The cost-effectiveness of intradiscal condoliase injection versus MED was evaluated using a Markov state-transition model. Following the initial treatment with either condoliase injection or MED, patients transitioned from the “initial treatment” state to one of two possible health states: (1) “reoperation” or (2) “no need for surgery” (Fig. 1). In this study, the “reoperation” state represents a condition requiring additional surgery due to residual or recurrent herniation, whereas the “no need for surgery” state indicates that surgery is not required. Previous research has shown that the average time to reoperation ranges from 1 to 4 years14–16. As a result, the analysis period was set to 5 years, with each model cycle lasting 6 months. The Markov model’s transition probabilities between health states were estimated using data on the number of patients who underwent reoperation versus those who did not require surgery at 6-month intervals following the initial treatment. These transition probabilities were assumed to be constant regardless of the time elapsed since the initial treatment. This assumption is consistent with a previous study that found reoperation rates stabilize after the first 6 months of post-surgery15. In our study, we estimated transition probabilities using data collected at 6 and 12 months post-surgery. Due to the unavailability of data beyond 12 months, a time-dependent transition model could not be applied for a longer follow-up period.

A simulated patient enters the model at the first presentation and receives a condoliase injection or MED. Following treatment, the patient may progress to the “reoperation” or “no need for surgery” status. At 6-month intervals, patients may transition to a different health state or remain in their current one.

Utilities and costs

Each health state was assigned unique utilities and costs. The overall utilities and costs were calculated using the relative contribution of each initial treatment to each state over successive cycles during the analysis period. Health state utilities were evaluated in terms of health-related quality of life (QOL) using the Short Form-6D (SF-6D) score, which was derived from the ODI score using the following transformation formula17: SF-6D = 0.78275 − 0.00518 × ODI. An annual discount rate of 2% was used for both utilities and costs, as recommended by the Central Social Insurance Medical Council18. Because the model ran on a 6-month cycle, the annual rate was converted to a 6-month discount rate of 0.995% (/6 months).

Utilities and costs for the “initial treatment” state were determined using the median ODI scores and costs for each treatment group, which included patients who received a condoliase injection or MED as their first treatment. For utilities in the “no need for surgery” category, QOL was expected to improve over time after the initial treatment. A linear model was selected for its simplicity and interpretability in approximating the QOL improvement. The model was fitted to log-transformed ODI scores for stable variance, with the log transformation chosen over alternatives (e.g., Box-Cox) for better model fit and interpretability. This model was then applied to predict SF-6D scores 6 months to 5 years after the initial treatment. Costs in the “no need for surgery” state were assumed to be stable after 12 months post treatment. Each treatment group’s median costs were calculated at the 6-month and 12-month marks. Given the small number of patients who needed reoperation in each treatment group, it was difficult to estimate the utilities and costs associated with the “reoperation” state. In particular, ODI scores for the MED group were completely missing, necessitating the assumption of consistent utilities across treatment groups based on the median ODI scores at retreatment for the entire patient population. Cost data for reoperation were available separately for each group, allowing us to estimate and use group-specific costs in the cost-effectiveness analysis.

Statistical analysis

The cost-effectiveness outcome measure was calculated using the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), which represents the ratio of the change in total costs associated with the condoliase injection treatment strategy to the change in total effects when compared to the MED treatment strategy. Total effects were expressed as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), which were calculated using a Markov model based on QOL and model cycles. ICERs less than 5,000,000 yen, the price adjustment threshold in Japanese health technology assessments19, were considered to favor condoliase injection over MED.

Data are expressed as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Variables between treatment groups were compared using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Cost-effectiveness analyses with the Markov model were carried out using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Sensitivity analysis

The robustness of the base–case analysis results was assessed using one-way parameter and probabilistic sensitivity analyses. For these analyses, parameters expected to have a significant impact on the cost-effectiveness results were chosen, such as the transition probability from the initial treatment to reoperation, QOL at the first treatment, and costs of the first treatment. The one-way parameter sensitivity analysis involved varying each input parameter by ± 20%. In the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, all parameters were sampled simultaneously and randomly from their respective probability distributions to calculate total costs and QALYs for each treatment group. A total of 1000 iterations of input parameter variations were performed. The beta distribution was used for the transition probabilities from initial treatment to reoperation, as well as the QOL parameters, whereas the gamma distribution was used for the cost parameters. A scatter plot illustrates the cost and QALYs differences between condoliase injection and MED.

Supplementary analysis

Three additional analyses were performed in this clinical study. First, we conducted a supplementary analysis to examine how differences in baseline characteristics between treatment groups affected the cost-effectiveness results. Specifically, propensity scores20 were determined using logistic regression, with all baseline characteristics listed in Table 1 as explanatory variables, including age, gender, BMI, disease duration, smoking, diabetes mellitus, herniation level, baseline NRS for leg pain, baseline NRS for back pain, and baseline ODI. A cost-effectiveness analysis was performed on a matched dataset of 64 patients (32 for condoliase injection and 32 for MED), with the number of patients and distribution of baseline characteristics between the two groups determined using propensity score matching. Following the matching process, we conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis using a Markov model based on the matched patient data (Tables S1 and S2). Second, to assess the effect of missing data on the cost-effectiveness analysis results, we performed supplementary analysis by imputing missing data on QOL and costs using multiple imputation methods, yielding a dataset of 327 patients (229 for condoliase injection and 98 for MED). Table S3 shows the input parameters and transition probabilities used in the base–case analysis of imputed data. Finally, unlike in the Markov model analysis, the ICER was calculated based on the observed numbers of patients in each health state at baseline and 6 months, as well as the observed ODI and costs. In this analysis, we assumed that the QOL observed at each time point (baseline and 6-month follow-up) remained constant over the same 6-month period. Under this assumption, QALY was calculated by multiplying the observed QOL at each time point by the duration of the corresponding cycle (6 months) and applying a 0.995% discount rate to the 6-month follow-up values, which corresponds to a 2% annual discount rate. For patients who underwent reoperation within the 6-month period, QALY contributions were weighted according to the time spent before and after reoperation, and a monthly discount rate of approximately 0.165% (derived from a 2% annual rate) was applied based on the timing of the event. Similarly, total costs were calculated by adding the observed costs for each treatment group over 6 months with discounting applied in the same manner depending on the timing of cost occurrence. The ICER was then calculated as the incremental cost per QALY gained when comparing the condoliase injection and MED groups. This analysis only included patients who had complete ODI and cost data at baseline and 6 months (Table S4).

Results

Clinical and cost outcomes

The study included 327 patients, with 229 receiving intradiscal condoliase injections and 98 undergoing MED (Fig. 2). There was no significant difference in disease duration between the condoliase and MED groups. Table 1 shows the patients’ demographic and baseline characteristics. The herniation levels were significantly different between the two groups (P = 0.0005). Patients treated with MED had higher NRS scores for leg pain and ODI scores compared to those treated with condoliase injection, although NRS scores for back pain were similar between the two groups.

Flowchart of patients in this study.

Table 2 summarizes the clinical results. Among the adverse events following the initial treatment, leg pain was reported by 3.1% of patients in the condoliase injection group and 10.2% in the MED group. Reoperation rates for adverse events were 10.0% in the condoliase injection group and 5.1% in the MED group. The reasons for reoperations in the condoliase injection group were poor response, whereas in the MED group, 60% of reoperations were due to herniation recurrence, 20% to a postoperative hematoma, and 20% due to level error. Overall, the condoliase injection group had slightly higher NRS scores for leg and back pain, as well as ODI scores, 3–6 months after surgery; however, the scores were similar 1 year later.

Base–case analysis

Tables 3 and 4 present the input parameters for the Markov model used in the base–case analysis, as well as the analysis results. Changes in QOL over time were similar between the condoliase and MED groups. Direct medical costs in the condoliase group were higher than those in the MED group in the “no need for surgery” state. We further classified the costs in this state into medical (including rehabilitation), examination costs (including X-ray, CT, and MRI), drug costs, and other costs (including injections and emergency visits). Although the medical examination and drug costs were similar between the two groups, the condoliase group had higher overall costs than the MED group. However, the costs for condoliase injection were significantly lower during the “initial treatment” phase. Consequently, the total costs for the condoliase injection and MED groups were 552,688 and 987,531 yen, respectively, indicating a difference of −434,843 yen in favor of the condoliase group. The effectiveness was essentially similar between the two groups, with a difference in total effect (condoliase injection − MED) of 0.037 QALYs. Given that the condoliase injection group achieved similar QALYs at a lower cost than the MED group, this intervention can be considered cost-saving compared to MED.

Sensitivity analysis

Table 5 shows the input parameters and results of the one-way sensitivity analysis. The ICER was calculated by individually varying parameters, such as the probability of transitioning from the initial treatment to reoperation, QOL at the first treatment, and first treatment costs. In nearly all of the scenarios tested in the one-way sensitivity analysis, condoliase injection dominated MED. However, when a lower QOL was assumed for the initial treatment with condoliase injection and a higher QOL for the MED group, condoliase injection was linked to lower costs and QALY than MED, although the difference in QALY was minor. The probabilistic sensitivity analysis sampled each input parameter independently and randomly from the probability distributions shown in Table 6. The total costs and QALYs were then calculated using the Markov model and the given input parameters. Figure 4 shows a scatter plot of the differences in total costs and incremental effects (i.e., QALYs) of condoliase injection versus MED after 1000 iterations in the probabilistic sensitivity analysis. This analysis revealed that condoliase injection outperformed MED in 92.6% of iterations, whereas in 7.4% of iterations, condoliase injection was both less expensive and less effective than MED. When the condoliase injection cost was increased by 300,000 yen, it outperformed MED in 86.6% of iterations.

Supplementary analysis

In the supplementary analysis assessing the impact of differences in baseline characteristics between treatment groups, the intervention resulted in a decrease of 0.231 QALYs but also a cost reduction of 479,127 yen when compared to the control (Table S5). The supplementary analysis found that missing data resulted in a QALY difference of −0.013, saving 427,188 yen (Table S6). Finally, analyzing 6 months without assumptions about QOL and cost data revealed a QALY difference of −0.148 and a cost reduction of 565,675 yen (Table S7). These findings show that while condoliase injection reduced QALYs compared to MED in all supplementary analyses, it also provided significant cost savings.

Discussion

This study assessed the cost-effectiveness of condoliase injection to MED for LDH patients using a Markov state-transition model. The base–case analysis revealed that condoliase injection had a lower total cost than MED, while the QALYs were equal to or greater than those of MED. This finding indicates that condoliase injection is cost-saving. Both the one-way parameter and probabilistic sensitivity analyses confirmed that condoliase injection was cost-saving compared to MED, even when the input parameters were changed. A one-way sensitivity analysis revealed that condoliase injection provided superior cost savings in nearly all scenarios, including variations in QOL values and costs at the time of initial treatment. Similarly, the probabilistic sensitivity analysis showed that condoliase injection outperformed MED in 92.6% of iterations. When a lower QOL was assumed for the initial treatment with condoliase injection and a higher QOL for the MED group, condoliase injection was linked to lower costs and lower QALY than MED, although the difference in QALY was −0.01 to −0.02. In previous studies, the mean minimally important difference for SF-6D ranged from 0.03 to 0.0421,22, so the difference in QALY was almost negligible. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare the clinical and cost outcomes of condoliase injection versus MED using real clinical data, providing valuable insights for clinical decision-making in LDH management.

According to a study, the improvement rate for condoliase treatment is 72%, implying that giving condoliase as a first-line treatment followed by open or endoscopic surgery in cases of poor response is less expensive than performing surgery first23. This study used actual improvement rates, allowing for a direct comparison of condoliase injection and MED as the first treatments. The reoperation rates for condoliase injection and MED were estimated to be 10.0% and 5.1%, respectively, with transition probabilities of 8% and 4% every 6 months. MED had lower reoperation rates, but the condoliase injection was cost-saving in the long run. The supplementary analyses revealed that condoliase injection reduced QALYs in all scenarios compared to MED and resulted in substantial cost savings. These findings are consistent with those from a previous study23. Importantly, this is the first study to report such findings with no conflicts of interest from pharmaceutical or surgical device companies, allowing for a direct comparison of chemonucleolysis with condoliase and surgical methods. Although there may be additional medical costs for revision after condoliase injection, the cost-effectiveness results are expected to remain favorable. A further sensitivity analysis showed that even if the cost of condoliase injection increases by 300,000 yen, there is an 86.6% probability of it being cost-saving compared to MED.

Some studies have shown that chemonucleolysis with chymopapain is both effective and less expensive than laminectomy24,25. In a comparison of clinical outcomes between chemonucleolysis with condoliase and microscopic surgery, NRS scores for leg and back pain, as well as ODI scores, were slightly lower for MED than for condoliase injection at 3–6 months postoperatively. However, these scores were comparable at 1-year post-surgery, with condoliase injection providing slightly more QALYs. Previous research has shown that MED promotes early recovery, shorter hospital stays, and faster returns to work26,27. One study found that leg pain improved within 1 month of condoliase treatment and continued to improve for another 3 months28. Another study found no significant difference in pain relief 1 month after treatment, but there was significant improvement after 3 months29. In our study, the results were similar, with symptoms improving faster after MED than after condoliase injection, but the long-term outcomes were comparable. Notably, condoliase injection resulted in slightly higher QALYs than MED at the final time point. This suggests that MED may provide better short-term results, but condoliase injection can lead to better long-term outcomes in appropriately selected patients.

Regarding cost outcomes, the two groups had comparable outpatient and reoperation costs, but injections and off-appointment outpatient visits with condoliase injections were more expensive. Condoliase injections resulted in significantly lower overall treatment costs. The significant cost difference between the two groups in this study was primarily due to the treatment costs, such as drugs, anesthesia, and other inpatient or outpatient expenses. Previous research has shown that MED is significantly more expensive than microsurgical and open discectomy30 and that percutaneous transforaminal endoscopic discectomy incurs significantly higher surgical and total hospital costs than MED31,32. Although condoliase injection was ultimately cost-saving compared to MED, we found that patients treated with MED had faster pain relief at 3–6 months post treatment than those treated with condoliase. The supplementary analyses revealed that condoliase injection reduced QALYs in all scenarios compared to MED and resulted in substantial cost savings. Our findings show that for patients with LDH who have not responded to conservative therapy, MED may be a better option for those with acute neuropathic pain seeking early relief, despite the higher cost. Conversely, condoliase injection may be more appropriate for patients with chronic pain that can be managed conservatively, particularly those looking for a less expensive treatment option. Although condoliase treatment may be less effective in terms of rapid pain relief than operative treatments, it may still be an acceptable first-line option for LDH due to its low cost. Recently, condoliase injections have grown in popularity annually33, due to their efficacy and safety. More profitable treatments are more likely to be used in clinical settings. As a result, increasing the reimbursement for low-cost and high-quality treatments increases their likelihood of being chosen. Based on economic principles, more cases are expected to shift the treatment strategy from MED to condoliase treatment, potentially lowering national medical costs and the economic burden.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. First, as a retrospective and non-randomized analysis of medical records, we were unable to collect all potentially relevant data, such as costs associated with missed work, unpaid caregiver time, and missed housekeeping. Additionally, the sample size for MED was relatively small, with only 1 year of actual follow-up and a 5-year modeled follow-up period. Second, due to the small number of reoperations and missing ODI values in the MED group, we assumed that the QOL values and retreatment costs were identical for both condoliase injection and MED. Third, since MED is performed under general anesthesia, the overall medical costs may be higher than those for condoliase injection. Therefore, transforaminal percutaneous endoscopic discectomy, which is typically performed under local anesthesia similar to condoliase injection, may be a better comparison in future studies. Fourth, not all baseline characteristics were evenly distributed, and supplementary analyses addressing this imbalance revealed lower QALYs for condoliase injection. Despite these limitations, our study analyzed the cost-effectiveness of condoliase injection using one-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses, as well as the primary analysis. Additional prospective studies are required to address these limitations and validate our findings.

Conclusions

The cost-effectiveness analysis showed that intradiscal condoliase injection is cost-saving compared to MED. Changes in QOL over time were similar and comparable between the condoliase and MED groups.
