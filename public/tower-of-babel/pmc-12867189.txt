Modified Colonna Capsular Arthroplasty Procedure to Treat Neglected Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip
Authors: Kaveh Gharanizadeh, Hadi Ravanbod, Ahmad Hassan, Amin Nemati, Mohammad Shahsavan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The management of neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) in adolescents and young adults presents a significant challenge. This study aimed to assess the clinical and radiological outcomes of the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty technique in patients with neglected DDH.Matеrials and Methods:This retrospective cohort study included four patients (aged 13–24 years) with unilateral neglected DDH who underwent the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty procеdurе between 2012 and 2021. Preoperative and postoperative radiographic parameters were assessed, such as lateral center-edge angle (LCEA), acetabular index (AI), joint space width, and medial head border-ilioischial line distance. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Harris Hip Score (HHS), Postel-Merle d’Aubigné (PMA) score, and range of motion (ROM).Results:The mean follow-up duration was 40.75 months (12–90 months). Postoperatively, the mean LCEA was 36° (range: 29–45°), and the mean AI was -0.5° (range: -6 to 4°). The joint space width was 2.5 mm (range: 1–4 mm), and the medial head border-ilioischial line distance was 4.5 mm (range: 3–8 mm). The mean postoperative PMA score was 17 (range: 16–18), and the mean HHS was 96.5 (range: 93–100). All patients achieved normal hip ROM, with no reported cases of avascular necrosis or femoral neck fractures. One patient developed class II heterotopic ossification; however, no further intervention was required.Conclusions:Modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty seems to be a reliable and efficient treatment for neglected DDH in young patients, providing satisfactory joint preservation, improved function, and minimal complications.

INTRODUCTION

Dеvеlopmеntal dysplasia of thе hip (DDH) is a frеquеntly occurring congеnital abnormality еncompassing a spеctrum of hip disordеrs from nеonatal instability to truе hip dislocation.[12] The management approach for developmental dysplasia of the hip may vary according to the patient’s age. For infants undеr 6 months, utilizing thе Pavlik harnеss can bе bеnеficial. Surgical treatment for adult developmental dysplasia of the hip has favored joint preservation since the early 20th century. In the late 20th century, total hip arthroplasty (THA) was a significant breakthrough for several reasons.[7] These include a prolonged immobilization period associated with joint preservation and a higher incidence of avascular necrosis (AVN), stiffness, and reduced functional outcomes of the lower extremity compared with THA. As a result, THA has been widely regarded as a surgery in the past century.[8] Although the long-term survival rate of THA decreases significantly in patients aged <50 years, this has led to a resurgence of interest in joint preservation techniques.[9] Despite the significant improvement in joint function provided by THA, its high failure rate is a cause of concern. Hip arthrodesis offers a viable and safe option, resulting in a stable and pain-free hip joint that does not require multiple surgeries. Ilizarov’s pelvic support osteotomy, which combines femur osteotomy and lengthening, effectively treats adolescent hip dysplasia or dislocation, while preserving the hip, restoring limb length, and significantly improving gait. Resection arthroplasty is a dependable method that provides pain-free joints and positive functional outcomes in 90% of patients with dysplasia, although it is currently reserved as a last resort.[10]

Capsular arthroplasty, introduced in 1901 and later refined by Hey Groves and Colonna in 1926 and 1932, has been a widely used surgical technique for over a century to preserve the structural integrity of joints.[11] The joint capsule covered the femoral head before the hip was reduced to the reamed true acetabulum. It effectively maintains joint function and prevents degeneration.[912] However, this technique has historically been associated with high complication rates, such as avascular necrosis, stiffness, and the need for further surgical procedures.[11]

Ganz et al.[8] presented a modified version of the original capsular arthroplasty involving a hip dislocation approach. Their technique includes several joint preservation procedures, such as femoral derotation, femoral head reduction, femoral shortening, relative neck lengthening, and acetabular roof augmentation. Although they initially reported favorable outcomes for this procedure, the functional and radiological outcomes require further clarification.

Although surgical interventions for developmental dysplasia of the hip have advanced significantly, there remains a notable need for research on the long-term efficacy and safety of joint-preserving methods. In particular, little is known about the effectiveness of the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty technique in patients with neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip. This study aimed to address this gap by thoroughly evaluating the clinical and radiological outcomes of this approach. Such investigations are essential for improving our understanding of the technique’s potential contribution to the comprehensive management of neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip, with the ultimate aim of enhancing patient care in orthopedic surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a referral hospital between between 2012 and 2021. This study focused on patients aged >10 who had unilateral neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip, severe chronic subluxation, and accompanying acetabular cartilage degeneration or complete low and high dislocation. Patients who had previously undergone femoral and pelvic surgeries to treat developmental dysplasia of the hip were excluded from the study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (Approval No: IR.ARI.MUI.REC.1402.124), and all patients provided informed consent for their data to be used in this research.

Surgical procedure and postoperative protocol

A senior surgeon performed all the surgeries according to the Ganz surgical protocol. One-stage capsular arthroplasty was performed via hip dislocation during the surgical procedure. To ensure joint preservation, additional procedures, such as relative neck lengthening, augmentation of the roof, and shortening/derotation of the femur for containment and congruency, were performed as required. Furthermore, head–neck junction trimming and fascia lata autografts from the same wound were used to augment capsular defects in the inferior part of the head [Figure 1].

Surgical exposure of the femoral head: (a) surgical hip dislocation, (b) femoral head–neck junction trimming, and (c) capsulorrhaphy

After the surgical procedure, the affected hip was placed in a single-leg spica cast for 2 days for immobilization. The cast was then replaced with an abduction splint that allowed for movements between 0 and 45° of hip flexion/extension and up to 20° of hip abduction. At the follow-up visit, 2 weeks after the operation, the splint was replaced with a hip abduction brace, which improved the range of motion to 100° flexion and full extension at the same 20° abduction. After 6 weeks, the brace was discontinued, and full hip range of motion (ROM) with abductor strengthening exercises was initiated.

After the surgery, the patient underwent a specific weight-bearing plan for 12 weeks. For the first 6 weeks, they were only allowed to touch down on their operated hip while using a walker. They could then perform partial weight-bearing on their legs for 6 weeks with two crutches. Subsequently, they were allowed to put full weight-bearing on their legs. Individuals over 20 years of age were prescribed aspirin (80 mg) once daily, 2 weeks after surgery to prevent thromboembolic events.

Data collection and outcome measures

Patients’ medical records were reviewed to collect data on age, sex, body mass index (BMI), surgical techniques, and postoperative complications, including avascular necrosis, thromboembolic events, femoral neck fractures, infection, and nonunion. Postoperative radiographs were evaluated to assess radiographic characteristics, including, the lateral center-edge angle, acetabular index, and medial head border-ilioischial line distance. Surgical outcomes were assessed by measuring hip ROM (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, and external rotation) during the final follow-up visit. Hip function was evaluated using the harris hip score and Postel-Merle d’Aubigné scores.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to present the patients’ demographics, clinical features, and outcomes. The results are reported as mean ± standard deviation or frequency (percentage).

Postoperative complications

At the final follow-up, only two patients (Patients 3 and 4) showed slight limping, and all had negative Trendelenburg test results. Fortunately, no femoral neck fractures or avascular necrosis cases were reported during follow-up. However, one patient (Patient 3) had class II heterotopic ossification according to the Brooker classification, but since she had no ROM limitations, no further treatment was necessary.[13] The series recorded no other complications, such as infection, nonunion, or venous thromboembolism. None of the patients required conversion to total hip arthroplasty.

DISCUSSION

The study found that The modified Colonna procedure is an effective treatment for hip subluxation and dislocation in patients with neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip. The results of the radiology tests, such as the lateral center-edge angle, acetabular index, width of the joint space, and medial head border-ilioischial line distance, were within the acceptable ranges. Most patients had proper Shenton lines and joint space congruity [Figure 3]. Functional outcomes were outstanding, as demonstrated by a mean Harris Hip Score of 96.5 points and a Postel-Merle d’Aubigné score of 17. The patients had full or nearly full hip ROM. During the average follow-up period of 40.75 months, no significant complications were reported and none of the patients required THA conversion.

The LCEA and AI measured 29° and 0°, respectively. The joint space width was 4 mm, and the distance between the medial head border and the ilioischial line was 4 mm lateral to the ilioischial border. The Shenton line remained intact, and the joint space exhibited congruency

Capsular augmentation is a promising solution for young patients with dysplasia who suffer from chronic hip dislocation and seek pain relief and improved mobility. Without the need for THA, this approach offers successful outcomes, such as pain reduction, increased ROM, and femoral head reduction. The combination of surgical hip dislocation with capsular arthroplasty and decellularized dermal allografts can benefit patients. An iliac crest bone graft may be added to shelf autograft acetabuloplasty to improve the hip joint stability and function. Long-term research has shown high levels of patient satisfaction and positive hip scores among patients who have undergone capsular arthroplasty.[11]

Our results are consistent with those of the 2023 study by Zhang et al.,[14] which examined 90 patients (92 hips) with developmental dysplasia of the hip. They highlighted the significance of capsular quality in determining the success of the procedure and recommended that patients with poor capsular quality avoid treatment. Our research produced even more impressive functional outcomes, with an average postoperative Harris Hip Score of 96.5 points compared with Zhang et al.’s[14] 83.61. Although these differences could be attributed to various factors, our study demonstrated complete or nearly complete hip ROM in all patients after surgery, indicating a successful surgical outcome. All four patients exhibited anatomically correct femoral head contours after surgery, with no significant complications such as femoral neck fractures or avascular necrosis. This indicates that careful application of the modified Colonna procedure may result in promising joint preservation outcomes. While our research did not specifically examine capsular quality, our findings indicate that the procedure can succeed even in neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip patients.

The modified capsular arthroplasty procedure has demonstrated encouraging outcomes in terms of minimizing postoperative complications. This was accomplished by safeguarding the primary blood supply to the femoral head and implementing appropriate management throughout the recuperation phase. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of this procedure is significantly dependent on the surgeon’s proficiency and meticulous evaluation of the proper indications.[15]

Capsular arthroplasty has been used for over a century to treat hip dislocations under various conditions such as developmental dysplasia of the hip.[11] However, the outcomes of the original version may not have been satisfactory, mainly because of the high rate of complications.

In 2012, Ganz et al.[8] introduced a modified Colonna procedure. The surgical hip dislocation approach and capsulotomy near the acetabular rim were used in the modified technique, followed by true acetabular reaming. To avoid femoral head avascular necrosis and joint stiffness, subtrochanteric femoral shortening osteotomy was performed with or without femoral derotation to achieve proper femoral neck anteversion. The capsule was wrapped around the femoral head and was reduced to the true acetabulum.

A retrospective analysis of the modified Colonna procedure was conducted on the results of nine patients in the initial report. Their study evaluated the surgical outcomes using Harris Hip Score and radiographic characteristics. The Harris Hip Score significantly improved after surgery, with a mean score of 84. Only one patient required conversion to THA. Postoperative complications included deep vein thrombosis and femoral neck fractures.[8]

Our study revealed better functional outcomes as assessed by the Harris Hip Score than the findings of Ganz et al.[8] (Harris Hip Score: 96.5 vs 84). This can be attributed to the absence of complications or comorbidities in our patients rather than developmental dysplasia of the hip. Regardless, in their report, patients had comorbidities, such as arthrogryposis, which could influence the functional outcome. Our study did not include patients who experienced postoperative thromboembolic events or femoral neck fractures. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that the hip had almost full ROM after the surgical procedure; previous studies did not consider this particular factor.

Ahmad et al.[16] reported five patients over 10 years who were treated with the modified Colonna procedure and showed significant improvement in Harris Hip Score in at least 2 years of follow-up. According to McKay’s classification, all the patients had good outcomes. After the operation, the Severin class was IA in all the patients. They did not assess the hip range of motion before and after surgery. There were no reported cases of avascular necrosis during follow-up. However, one patient had to undergo conversion to THA due to persistent postoperative subluxation.

Dumlao et al.[17] presented a case study of a 7-year-old boy with chronic traumatic posterior hip dislocation. The patient was treated using a modified Colonna procedure and was subsequently protected with an Ilizarov hinged frame, after which the patient underwent a normal physical examination for extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. However, the flexion arc of motion was 0–80 degrees. They did not evaluate functional outcomes based on available hip scores.

The present study examined the potential benefits of modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty as a treatment option for neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip. Although the findings are encouraging, it is essential to note some inherent limitations. The study’s retrospective design and limited sample size may have affected its applicability and introduced selection bias. Furthermore, the absence of a control group makes it difficult to compare outcomes with alternative treatments, and specific patient characteristics could not be evaluated before surgery due to the retrospective approach. The narrow demographics of the patients included in this study may also limit the generalizability of the results to larger populations.

The implications underscore the importance of preserving joints in young patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip and underscore the necessity for extensive, multicenter studies involving various patient groups to verify the results. Conducting long-term follow-up to evaluate outcomes and identify delayed complications is crucial. Future research should focus on identifying predictive factors to enhance surgical outcomes, patient selection, and preoperative planning. These measures are vital for establishing evidence-based recommendations for treating neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip by using the modified Colonna procedure.

CONCLUSION

This study provides compelling evidence that modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty is a safe and effective treatment option for unilateral neglected developmental dysplasia of the hip in younger patients. The positive outcomes of hip joint preservation observed include improved joint anatomy, increased ROM, and high levels of patient satisfaction, as indicated by Harris Hip Score and Postel-Merle d’Aubigné scores. Additionally, the low incidence of postoperative complications, such as avascular necrosis and femoral neck fractures, emphasizes the safety of the procedure. These findings suggest that the modified Colonna approach could be a promising alternative to THA for the management of developmental dysplasia of the hip in younger individuals. Further research is necessary to validate these results and to enhance the surgical approach.
