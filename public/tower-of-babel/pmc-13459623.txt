Hybrid plate-and-UHMWPE fixation for step-cut subtrochanteric osteotomy in crowe IV hip dysplasia: a finite element–supported case report
Authors: Askarjan Beknazarov, Kairat Ashimov, Olzhas Bekarissov, Durdana Oktyabrova, Timur Baidalin, Bekzhan Suleimenov, Daniyar Yestay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTotal hip arthroplasty (THA) in Crowe type IV developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is technically demanding due to high dislocation, abnormal femoral morphology, and soft-tissue contractures. Subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy (SSO) is often required to restore the anatomic hip center and prevent excessive lengthening, yet the ideal osteotomy design and fixation remain controversial. The step-cut SSO offers greater rotational stability and bone contact but is technically complex and biomechanically under-evaluated. This report describes a clinical case supplemented by computational biomechanical analysis evaluating a hybrid fixation technique using a titanium plate reinforced with ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) tape.Case presentationIn this clinical case, a 35-year-old female with bilateral Crowe IV DDH underwent staged THA with step-cut SSO at the National Scientific Center of Traumatology and Orthopedics, Astana, Kazakhstan. Approximately 4.5 cm of femoral shortening was performed per side. The osteotomy was stabilized with a locally designed titanium plate and UHMWPE tape cerclage. Both procedures were uneventful, achieving equal limb lengths, early mobilization, and complete bone union by four months. Finite element analysis (FEA) was performed to evaluate mechanical behavior under single-leg-stance loading (1000 N vertical, 200 N horizontal). The hybrid construct demonstrated favorable stress distribution: peak von Mises stress values of 137 MPa in cortical bone and 94 MPa in the plate – both below yield limits. Maximum displacement was 0.16 mm, and safety factors exceeded 2.9, confirming high rigidity. Clinically, both hips showed stable fixation, pain-free gait, and excellent functional outcomes with a Harris Hip Score of 90 for the right hip and 88 for the left hip at one-year follow-up.ConclusionsIn this case, the combination of step-cut SSO with hybrid titanium-plate and UHMWPE-tape fixation demonstrated favorable biomechanical characteristics and successful clinical outcome. Finite element analysis supported the mechanical stability of the construct. These findings should be considered preliminary and represent a proof-of-concept observation. Further experimental and multicenter clinical studies are required to confirm the generalizability of this approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09937-0.

Introduction

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) in its Crowe type IV form represents the most severe spectrum of the disorder, characterized by complete femoral head dislocation, pseudoacetabulum formation, and pronounced limb shortening [1]. Total hip arthroplasty (THA) in such patients remains among the most technically demanding procedures in reconstructive orthopedics due to complex anatomy, soft-tissue contractures, and altered biomechanics that increase the risk of neurovascular injury and postoperative instability [2, 3].

To achieve anatomical hip center restoration, surgeons must place the acetabular component in the true acetabulum, perform extensive soft-tissue release, and, when indicated, subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy (SSO) to prevent excessive lengthening [4–6]. However, there is still no consensus regarding the optimal osteotomy configuration or fixation strategy in Crowe IV hips. Several osteotomy geometries – including transverse, oblique, V-shaped, and Z-shaped (“step-cut”) – have been described, each with specific biomechanical advantages and limitations [7–9]. The step-cut design theoretically offers superior rotational stability and greater bone contact surface, though its technical complexity and uncertain fixation strength have limited its widespread use [9].

Stable fixation of the osteotomy site is critical to ensure bone union, allow early mobilization, and prevent nonunion or implant failure [10, 11]. While modern uncemented stems such as the Wagner or S-ROM can bridge the osteotomy, discrepancies in canal diameter or bone quality often necessitate additional fixation using plates, cables, or cerclage systems. The biomechanical effectiveness of such hybrid constructs, particularly in complex dysplastic femora, remains insufficiently investigated, despite its potential impact on fixation stability and bone healing.

In this context, finite element analysis (FEA) provides an important tool to assess stress–strain distribution and optimize fixation design [12]. Despite its growing use in orthopedic research, few studies have combined FEA validation with clinical outcomes in Crowe IV reconstructions.

Therefore, the aim of this report is to present a clinical case illustrating the surgical technique of step-cut subtrochanteric osteotomy stabilized with a titanium plate reinforced by UHMWPE tape in bilateral Crowe IV hip dysplasia and to provide preliminary biomechanical insight using finite element analysis.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old female presented to the National Scientific Center of Traumatology and Orthopedics (Astana, Kazakhstan) with progressive bilateral hip pain, restricted motion, and gait disturbance since childhood. Previous conservative management, including physiotherapy and nonsteroidal medication, had provided only temporary relief.

On examination, she demonstrated a waddling gait with bilateral abductor weakness and marked restriction of hip motion, while the knee joints remained unaffected. Radiographs and CT revealed bilateral high dislocation of the femoral heads with pseudoacetabular formation at the iliac level, hypoplastic true acetabula, and proximal femoral deformity–consistent with Crowe type IV DDH (Fig. 1).

Given the severity of the deformity and limb-length discrepancy, staged bilateral total hip arthroplasty (THA) with step-cut subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy (SSO) was planned to restore the anatomical hip center, equalize limb lengths, and minimize sciatic nerve tension. Preoperative CT templating indicated a need for approximately 4–5 cm of femoral shortening per side.

The right hip was operated first, followed by the contralateral side four months later, both using the same technique. Under spinal anesthesia via a standard lateral approach, a Z-shaped (step-cut) SSO was performed after femoral head removal. About 4.5 cm of bone was resected to permit reduction into the true acetabulum without undue lengthening. A cementless press-fit acetabular component (Trilogy® IT Acetabular System, 44 mm; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) was implanted. The femoral canal was subsequently prepared for implantation of a size 15 Wagner Cone femoral stem (Zimmer Biomet, Winterthur, Switzerland).

Osteotomy fragments were anatomically aligned and fixed using a locally designed titanium stabilizing plate (Kazakhstan patent No. 8955) reinforced by ultra–high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) tape cerclage to enhance rotational stability. Intraoperative fluoroscopy confirmed accurate alignment and stable fixation (Fig. 2). Both procedures were uneventful, with a combined blood loss of approximately 600 mL.

Postoperative rehabilitation included early physiotherapy and partial weight-bearing, progressing to full ambulation by eight weeks. The wounds healed primarily without infection or neurovascular complications. Radiographs at 1.5, 3, and 12 months demonstrated progressive union and stable component positioning. Clinically, the patient achieved equal limb lengths, pain-free gait, and near-normal hip mobility (Fig. 3). At the 12-month follow-up, the Harris Hip Score was 90 for the right hip and 88 for the left hip, indicating excellent functional recovery. No implant migration, loosening, or nonunion was observed. Both procedures were performed by the same experienced orthopedic surgeon using identical surgical techniques and perioperative protocols.

Given the excellent clinical course, a finite element analysis (FEA) was subsequently performed to evaluate the biomechanical stability and stress distribution of the fixation construct under physiological loading conditions. Mesh convergence testing confirmed that further refinement produced < 5% change in peak stress values.

Mesh convergence analysis

The finite element mesh was generated using four-node tetrahedral elements in Autodesk Inventor Professional. Initial mesh parameters included an average element size of 0.100, a minimum element size of 0.200, and a heterogeneity coefficient of 1.5. Subsequent mesh refinement was performed to achieve an optimal balance between numerical accuracy and computational efficiency. In the final model, the mesh parameters were set to an average element size of 0.100, a minimum element size of 0.050, and a heterogeneity coefficient of 1.5. Mesh convergence analysis demonstrated that further refinement resulted in a 3.024% change in the maximum von Mises stress, satisfying the commonly accepted convergence criterion of less than 5%. The final mesh consisted of approximately 34,000 tetrahedral elements and 77,000 nodes and was selected as an optimal balance between computational accuracy and computational cost. It should be noted that the mesh used for convergence analysis represents an intermediate model, whereas the final refined mesh used for the simulations is described in the “Model development” section.

Contact conditions

Contact interactions between model components were defined using frictional and bonded conditions depending on the interface. The interface between the femoral stem and bone, as well as screw–plate and screw–bone interfaces, were modeled as bonded, representing stable fixation and commonly accepted assumptions in orthopedic finite element analysis. The interfaces between the plate and bone, the osteotomy surfaces, and the UHMWPE tape and bone were defined as frictional contacts. Friction coefficient values were assigned based on previously published experimental and computational studies in orthopedic biomechanics [13–18].

Only joint reaction forces corresponding to single-leg stance were applied in the present simulation, while active muscle forces were not included. The UHMWPE tape used clinically for circumferential stabilization of the osteotomy was represented in the finite element model as a circumferential constraint providing compressive stabilization of the osteotomy fragments.

Study design

This work represents a clinical case report supplemented by a computational biomechanical analysis using finite element modeling (FEA). The clinical component describes the surgical treatment and follow-up of a single patient with Crowe type IV developmental dysplasia of the hip, while the computational component was performed to evaluate the biomechanical behavior of the fixation construct.

Biomechanical modeling study

A finite element model (FEM) was developed to assess the biomechanical behavior of the prosthesis-femur-stabilizing plate system following step-cut subtrochanteric osteotomy in Crowe IV DDH.

Model development

A three-dimensional digital model was constructed to include the femur with a Z-shaped osteotomy, a cementless femoral stem, and a titanium stabilizing plate anatomically contoured to the lateral surface. The construct incorporated UHMWPE cerclage elements to replicate the clinical fixation technique. The femoral geometry used in the finite element model was based on a representative generic STL femur model, which was subsequently modified in a CAD environment to reproduce the surgical configuration analyzed in this study, including the step-cut subtrochanteric osteotomy, cementless stem implantation, lateral plate fixation, and UHMWPE tape reinforcement. In the present model, the femur was represented as a homogeneous structure with material properties corresponding to cortical bone, and separate modeling of cortical and cancellous bone compartments was not performed in order to reduce computational complexity. Geometry reconstruction and meshing were performed using professional CAD software to ensure accurate bone–implant contact and realistic load transfer. The final model contained approximately 74 000 tetrahedral elements and 120 000 nodes, providing sufficient computational resolution (Fig. 4).

Material parameters and safety factor

All materials in the finite element model were assumed to be linear elastic, isotropic, and homogeneous. Mechanical properties for cortical bone, titanium alloy, and UHMWPE tape were assigned according to values reported in previous experimental and computational studies in orthopedic biomechanics [13–18]. The safety factor was calculated as the ratio of the material yield strength (or ultimate strength) to the maximum von Mises equivalent stress obtained in the corresponding component of the model. Boundary and loading conditions

A single-leg stance configuration was simulated, representing a simplified and conservative loading condition corresponding to early partial weight-bearing rather than peak physiological loading during normal gait. The distal femur was rigidly fixed, while the femoral head was subjected to a vertical load of 1000 N and a horizontal shear component of 200 N [19]. This loading scenario approximates combined compressive and shear forces transmitted through the hip joint under controlled conditions (Fig. 5).

Model validation and interpretation of results

Because experimental validation of the finite element model was not performed in the present study, the absolute values of stress and displacement should be interpreted considering the limitations of the computational model. Therefore, the finite element analysis was primarily intended to provide a qualitative assessment of stress distribution, load transfer, and mechanical stability of the hybrid fixation construct rather than an exact prediction of in vivo mechanical conditions.

Results

Finite element analysis demonstrated that equivalent von Mises stresses in all components remained well below the yield limits of the materials, indicating a substantial safety margin. The highest stresses were concentrated in the mid-plate region and around screw holes but did not exceed critical values for titanium alloys (~ 900 MPa). Linear displacement and safety-factor distributions confirmed high construct rigidity and mechanical stability of the hybrid fixation system (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). Interpretation

The FEA demonstrated that the combination of a step-cut osteotomy with hybrid plate-and-UHMWPE-tape fixation provides favorable stress distribution and overall mechanical stability of the construct. These findings suggest adequate stabilization of the osteotomy site; however, micromotion was not directly evaluated in the present model. The biomechanical profile of this construct aligns with the clinical observations of early union and durable stability in both hips. Collectively, these findings establish a mechanical rationale for the favorable postoperative outcomes.

The integration of clinical outcomes with biomechanical modeling offers a deeper understanding of the mechanical and biological determinants of success in complex Crowe type IV reconstructions. Building upon the finite element results, the following discussion analyzes how the hybrid fixation strategy influences stability, bone healing, and long-term function in the context of current literature and surgical experience.

Discussion

Crowe type IV DDH, one of the most technically demanding indications for THA, involves combined deformities [1, 2]. Achieving anatomic hip-center restoration while preserving limb-length equality and neural safety requires both accurate preoperative planning and mechanically reliable fixation of the subtrochanteric osteotomy site [3, 10].

Among available shortening osteotomies – transverse, oblique, V-shaped, and Z-shaped (step-cut) – the latter offers broad cancellous contact and intrinsic rotational stability, promoting rapid osseous union [7, 8]. However, its technical complexity and the need for rigid fixation have limited wider adoption [20, 21]. In the present case, the step-cut SSO fixed with a titanium plate reinforced by UHMWPE tape resulted in bilateral bone union and restoration of the hip center. While this observation is limited to a single patient, it suggests that hybrid fixation may provide adequate mechanical stability in complex reconstructions. These findings are broadly consistent with previous reports indicating that step-cut configurations can reach union rates of 93–100% when stable fixation is ensured [20].

Hybrid plate-and-UHMWPE fixation

Fixation strategy critically determines mechanical performance and healing after osteotomy. Conventional cerclage wiring, though simple, is prone to stress concentration, plastic deformation, and wire breakage under cyclic loading [13, 22]. Modern non-metallic UHMWPE cables demonstrate superior fatigue resistance and maintain tension across one million load cycles without deformation [13, 14]. The broad footprint of UHMWPE tape decreases the risk of cortical cut-through, an important advantage in dysplastic or osteoporotic femora [23]. Clinical series comparing polymer and metallic cerclage systems in periprosthetic fractures showed comparable healing but fewer cable-related failures and soft-tissue complications with UHMWPE [14]. In the present study, the use of a patented stabilizing plate combined with UHMWPE tape cerclage (Kazakhstan patent No. 8955) provided additional mechanical advantages. The implant was designed to ensure stable fixation of osteotomy fragments, to eliminate rotational instability, and to improve axial stability of the construct. Its design allows anatomical adaptation to the femoral surface and secure fixation around the femoral component. In addition, the use of UHMWPE tape may facilitate early mobilization and contribute to improved conditions for bone healing compared to conventional fixation methods.

Biological rationale of UHMWPE tape fixation

The use of UHMWPE tape in hybrid fixation constructs may also provide biologically favorable conditions for bone healing. In contrast to metallic cerclage cables, polymer tapes exert a broader contact surface on the cortical bone, potentially reducing focal pressure and preserving periosteal blood supply. Preservation of periosteal vascularity is considered an important factor for successful osteotomy healing and early callus formation. Sakai et al. demonstrated that the contact pressure generated by UHMWPE cables influences periosteal blood flow and that appropriate clamping force may prevent vascular compromise around the femoral cortex [24]. Furthermore, circumferential polymer tape fixation can generate controlled compression across the osteotomy interface while minimizing excessive stress shielding. This balance between mechanical stability and biological preservation may facilitate primary bone union in subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy. Clinical observations reported showed reliable fracture union following fixation with UHMWPE fiber cables, supporting their ability to provide stable fixation in femoral osteosynthesis. In addition, polymer cerclage systems have been associated with favorable biological compatibility and minimal tissue reactivity. Sogawa et al. reported that UHMWPE fiber cables demonstrate good clinical performance and limited biological reaction in surrounding tissues during osteosynthesis procedures [25].

Role of finite element analysis in orthopedic biomechanics

Finite element analysis (FEA) has become an important tool in orthopedic biomechanics for evaluating stress distribution and implant stability in complex bone–implant systems. It allows researchers to analyze mechanical behavior of fixation constructs and optimize implant design in situations where experimental testing may be difficult or impractical. Numerous studies have demonstrated the usefulness of FEA in orthopedic research, surgical planning, and implant development [26, 27].

Finite element analysis has also been increasingly applied to investigate the biomechanical behavior of dysplastic hip joints and surgical reconstruction techniques. Recent studies have used patient-specific finite element models to evaluate stress distribution and implant stability in developmental dysplasia of the hip and hip arthroplasty reconstruction [28, 29].

Biomechanical validation and comparison with literature

Finite element analysis (FEA) confirmed that the hybrid construct maintains a high safety margin, with peak cortical bone stress of 137 MPa and plate stress of 94 MPa – both below the yield limits of cortical bone (≈ 150 MPa) and titanium alloys (≈ 830–900 MPa) [16, 17]. Maximum displacement at the femoral head (0.16 mm) and a minimum safety factor above 2.9 indicated excellent rigidity and stability, consistent with mechanical requirements for primary bone healing which meets accepted biomechanical thresholds for stable osteosynthesis [16, 18]. The localization of maximal tensile stress along the lateral cortex near the distal screws corresponded to expected distraction zones during stance phase, validating the physiological relevance of the model [18, 30].

These findings align with prior biomechanical comparisons showing that overall construct stability depends more on fixation quality than on osteotomy geometry. Yıldız et al. [9] demonstrated that step-cut and oblique osteotomies yield equivalent stability when adequately fixed, whereas Turgut et al. (2024) [21] reported similar union and function between step-cut and transverse designs but shorter operative times for the transverse cut. Nevertheless, our results emphasize that hybrid fixation can compensate for geometric variability by improving overall construct stability and promoting more uniform load distribution along the osteotomy plane.

Clinical translation and functional outcomes

Clinically, the patient achieved equal limb lengths, full radiographic union within four months, and a Harris Hip Score of 90 for the right hip and 88 for the left hip at one-year follow-up. These results are comparable to outcomes reported in recent large clinical series of Crowe IV hips treated with modular stems or step-cut osteotomies [33, 34]. The absence of sciatic neuropathy and correction of abductor insufficiency are consistent with studies reporting improved gait and muscle recovery after controlled shortening of 3–5 cm [35]. These parallel clinical and biomechanical outcomes highlight the value of integrating computational modeling into personalized surgical planning.

While the hybrid plate-and-UHMWPE fixation demonstrated favorable mechanical behavior and successful clinical outcome in the present case, its broader clinical applicability requires further evaluation. In particular, future studies involving larger patient cohorts are needed to assess operative complexity, implant-related factors, and long-term implant tolerance in comparison with other fixation strategies used for subtrochanteric osteotomy in Crowe IV DDH.

Limitations and perspectives

Despite encouraging results, this study has several limitations, including the absence of experimental validation of the finite element model. First, the clinical component is based on a single-patient case, which limits the external validity of the findings and prevents direct generalization of the clinical outcomes. Therefore, the clinical findings should be interpreted as preliminary observations rather than definitive evidence of clinical effectiveness of the proposed fixation method. Second, the finite element model used simplified assumptions regarding material properties and loading conditions. Bone and soft tissues were modeled as linear elastic and isotropic materials, whereas real biological tissues demonstrate anisotropic and viscoelastic behavior. In addition, the simulation considered a single quasi-static loading scenario corresponding to single-leg stance and did not include cyclic loading, fatigue effects, or muscle forces acting around the hip joint. Therefore, the long-term durability of the fixation construct cannot be inferred from static FEA alone. Furthermore, the current finite element analysis focused primarily on global mechanical parameters such as stress distribution, displacement, and safety factor. Micromotion at the osteotomy interface and local contact pressures at the bone–plate interface were not quantitatively evaluated and should be investigated in future studies to better correlate computational results with biological processes of bone healing. In addition, the biomechanical findings are specific to the analyzed fixation construct and the anatomical configuration used in the model and therefore cannot be directly generalized to other implant systems or patient anatomies. Future research should include multi-patient cohorts, experimental fatigue testing, and simulation of cyclic torsional stresses to validate long-term construct durability. Comparative biomechanical studies of plate-only, cable-only, and hybrid fixation systems under identical step-cut geometry may further refine optimal fixation strategies. Despite these limitations, the integration of computational modeling with the presented clinical observations provides a preliminary framework for future research and potential personalized approaches to DDH reconstruction.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates that step-cut subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy combined with hybrid titanium-plate and UHMWPE-tape fixation can provide stable fixation and satisfactory early clinical outcome in a patient with Crowe IV DDH. Finite element analysis supported the mechanical stability of the construct under simulated loading conditions. However, these findings are based on a single clinical case and computational modeling. Further experimental studies and larger clinical series are required to confirm the biomechanical and clinical advantages of this hybrid fixation approach.
