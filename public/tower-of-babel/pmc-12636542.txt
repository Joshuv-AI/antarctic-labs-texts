The mechanisms of kidney–lung interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases: A two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Shengyi Yang, Changxian Wang, Zhenwei Li, Liyuan Sun, Yulu He, Qun Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between chronic kidney disease and pulmonary diseases, and explore the mechanisms of kidney-lung interactions.MethodsTwo-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) was performed to explore the causal effect of chronic kidney disease (CKD) on pulmonary diseases, and two-step MR was performed to explore the mechanisms of lung–kidney interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) meta-analysis was used as the main method for obtaining the MR estimate, and complementary analysis were performed using the weighted median method, maximum-likelihood, MR-RAPS.ResultsIVW analysis showed that genetically predicted CKD was positively associated with the increased risks of asthma (OR:1.005, 95%CI:1.001, 1.009), pneumonia (OR:1.066, 95%CI: 1.009, 1.127), adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (OR:2.110, 95%CI: 1.053, 4.231), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (OR, 1.004; 95%CI:1.000, 1.008). Mediation analysis revealed that interleukin-1β mediated CKD’s effect on pneumonia risk, tumor necrosis-α mediated its effects on both asthma and COPD, and parathyroid hormone (PTH) mediated its effects on pneumonia and ARDS, with mediated effects size ranging from 3.53% to 27.15%.ConclusionsPredicted CKD was positively associated with the increased risk of asthma, pneumonia, ARDS, and COPD. Furthermore, interleukin-1β and tumor necrosis-α, 25-hydroxyvitamin D and PTH mediated the kidney–lung interactions in CKD-associated pulmonary diseases.Keywords: Kidney–lung interactions, chronic kidney disease, pulmonary diseases, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as abnormalities in kidney structure or function, present for a minimum of 3 months, with implications for health [1]. CKD affects > 10% of the general population worldwide, accounting for >800 million individuals [2]. A nationally representative cross-sectional study reported a weighted estimate of 82 million adults with CKD in China, and the prevalence appeared to have decreased by 30% over the past decade [3]. CKD is the fifth leading cause of years of life lost worldwide by 2040 [4].

Lung diseases are the major cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide, with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) alone causing more than 2.7 million deaths [5]. Some studies have found that CKD predisposes patients to lung disease. Evidence from Sweden by Hideyuki Mukai indicates the following:

The prevalence of obstructive, especially restrictive lung dysfunction, increases with declining glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and with higher comorbidity burden [6]. Elucidating kidney–lung interorgan crosstalk is key to formulating novel treatment plans for patients. The mechanisms of kidney–lung interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases involve various factors, including immunological triggers and immune responses, such as anti-glomerular basement membrane antibody (anti-GBM) antibody disease and increased interleukin-18 levels [7]. Additionally, disturbances in fluid regulation can lead to tissue injury in both respiratory and renal systems.

Investigating the causal association between CKD and the risk of pulmonary diseases and the mechanisms of kidney–lung interactions are challenging due to reverse causation and confounding. Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variation associated with diseases to mitigate biases that affect traditional observational study designs and largely overcomes confounders with random assignment of an individual’s genetic variants at conception [8–10]. Furthermore, a two-step MR can be used for mediation analysis to decompose the impact of exposure on the outcome, estimating the causal relationships between the three variables through mediation variables [11]. Hence, we applied this MR analysis to investigate the relationship between chronic kidney disease and pulmonary diseases and explored the mechanisms of kidney–lung interactions.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study conducted two-sample MR analysis and two-step MR analysis using summary data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to assess the mechanisms of lung–kidney interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with CKD were used to assess their causal effects on pulmonary diseases (asthma, pneumonia, lung cancer, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease). Subsequently, to explore the mechanisms of lung–kidney interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases, the following two-step MR was performed: first, SNPs associated with CKD were used to assess the effect on immune response (interleukin-1β, interleukin-18, interleukin-6, and TNF-α), phosphate metabolism (25-hydroxyvitamin D, fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF-23)), and hormonal imbalances (parathyroid hormone (PTH)), and then, mediation-related SNPs were used to assess the effect on pulmonary diseases. The study design is illustrated in Figure 1. This study was conducted according to the STROBE-MR guidelines [12].

Data retrieval for MR analysis

Summary statistics for CKD were derived from a meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies for estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), combining data across 133,413 individuals of European ancestry from the Chronic Kidney Disease Genetics Consortium (CKDGen) with replication in up to 42,166 individuals [13]. CKDGen has focused on identifying genetic loci associated with CKD-classifying quantitative traits, most importantly GFR and albuminuria [14].

Summary statistics for asthma [15], pneumonia, lung cancer [16], and COPD were obtained from the UK Biobank with European ancestors (484,598 for asthma, 486,484 for pneumonia, 492,803 for lung cancer, and 112,583 for COPD). The UK Biobank is a large-scale biomedical database and research resource containing in-depth genetic and health information from half a million UK participants (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/). For acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), summary statistics were obtained from the FinnGen Biobank with 216,528 individuals of European ancestors.

Publicly available summary data for interleukin-1β, interleukin-18, interleukin-6, and TNF-α were obtained from a meta-analysis conducted by the SCALLOP consortium, which mapped and replicated protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) for 90 cardiovascular proteins in over 30,000 individuals, resulting in 451 pQTLs for 85 proteins [17]. For FGF23 and PTH, summary statistics were obtained from the KORA F4 study of 3,800 individuals. The KORA F4 study was a population-based cohort living in southern Germany. Study participants were recruited between 2006 and 2008, comprising individuals who were 32–81 years [18]. Publicly available summary data for 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels were obtained from the UK Biobank with European ancestors. Detailed information on the GWAS dataset is provided in Table 1.

Genetic instrumental variable selection

Within the scope of this investigation, the selection of nucleotide polymorphisms with a robust association was implemented as instrumental variables (IVs) for exposure [19]. Genetic variants that reached genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−6) were selected as instruments for MR analysis. Additionally, to select the sufficient SNPs as IVs associated with these significance levels, we lowered the genome-wide significance threshold of the parathyroid hormone (PTH) to p < 5 × 10−5 [20]. The corresponding linkage disequilibrium was tested to confirm that there were any SNPs in a linkage disequilibrium state, and the SNPs were independently by pruning SNPs within a 10,000kb window with an R2< 0.001 threshold. For the GWAS data on PTH, a more lenient LD threshold (R2 < 0.1, window size = 1,000 kb) was applied because of the smaller sample size [21]. SNPs should be independent of any confounders of exposure and outcomes [22]. In this study, we used the PhenoScanner tool to exclude any SNP associated with the confounders of CKD with outcome events (pulmonary diseases) (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) [23]. Subsequently, proxy SNPs were used with a maf_threshold 0.01 in this analysis. Furthermore, we ensured the harmonization of exposure and outcome data sources and systematic exclusion of palindromic SNPs.

To measure the strength of the instrumental variables and the degree of explanation for phenotypes, we calculated the F statistic and R2 for the instrumental variables. The F statistic, defined as the ratio of the model’s mean square to that of the error, is related to the degree of interpretation of the phenotype by genetic variation (R2), sample size (N), and the number of instrumental variables (k), using the following calculation formula: F = R2 (N-k-1)/(k(1 − R2)) [24,25]. The R2 for each instrument was calculated using the following formula: R2=2EAF (1 − EAF)/(β/SD) [2], where EAF is the effect allele frequency, β is the estimated genetic effect on exposure, and SD is the standard deviation. An F-statistic >10 indicated a relatively low risk of weak instrument bias [26].

In the study design, we employed various MR methods to minimize bias and obtain reliable estimates of modifiable exposures of interest and their relationship with the outcomes. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method is the primary analytical approach employed, which employs weighted linear regression and presupposes the validity of all instrumental variables [28]. It is intended to be flexible even when the data are diverse [29]. Complementary analyses were performed using maximum likelihood [30] and MR-Egger methods [30]. Furthermore, the MR-Robust-adjusted profile score (MR-RAPS) with ‘Huber’ loss function was used to model a random-effects distribution of the pleiotropic effects of genetic variants [31]. Mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) test was performed to support the results by IVW method, which could identify the horizontal pleiotropic outliers.

Two-step MR analysis

We conducted a two-step MR analysis to assess the mechanisms of lung–kidney interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases. The first step was to estimate the causal effect of genetically determined CKD on the mediating factors (β 1). The second step was to estimate the causal effect of the mediating factors on pulmonary diseases (β2). The third step is to divide the result of the mediated effect (β1*β2) by the total effect of Two-Sample Mendelian Randomization (TSMR) to estimate the mediated effect proportion of immune response, phosphate metabolism, and hormonal imbalances that mediate the association between CKD and pulmonary diseases [32]. We applied the Delta method, using the effect estimates derived from the two-sample MR analysis to derive the SE of the mediation proportion, and further calculate the confidence interval [33].

Sensitivity analysis

Cochran’s Q test was used to assess the heterogeneity between individual genetic variant estimates. If the P-value of Cochran’s Q test was less than 0.05, the final results of MR were referred to a multiplicative random-effects model of IVW; otherwise, the impact of heterogeneity on the estimation of causal effects could be ignored, and a fixed-effects model was used [34]. MR-Egger regression analysis was employed to assess the bias due to genetic pleiotropy, where the intercept of the regression can evaluate the magnitude of pleiotropy [30,35]. If p > 0.05, there is a weak possibility of genetic pleiotropy in the causal analysis [35]. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were implemented by removing a single SNP each time to assess whether the variant was driving the association between exposure and the outcome variable.

Statistical analysis

All evaluations were two-sided, and the MR study was performed in R version 4.0.5 (The R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria) using the packages ‘Two-Sample-MR’ (version 0.5.7) and ‘MR.RAPS’ (version 0.4.2).

A total of 77 SNPs were selected as IVs, with an average F-statistic of 20. IVW analysis revealed that genetically predicted CKD was suggested associated with an increased risk of asthma (OR: 1.005, 95%CI:1.001, 1.009), pneumonia (OR: 1.066, 95%CI: 1.009, 1.127), ARDS (OR: 2.110, 95%CI: 1.053, 4.231), and COPD (OR, 1.004; 95%CI:1.000, 1.008) (Table 3). The same trend was observed in the maximum-likelihood analysis (for asthma, pneumonia, ARDS, and COPD (Table 3). There was no directional pleiotropy but heterogeneity in the some of the results (Supplementary Table 29-30).

The mechanisms of kidney–lung interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases

Immune response

IVW analysis revealed that genetically predicted CKD was suggested associated with an increased risk of interleukin-1β (OR, 1.068; 95%CI: 1.008, 1.131) and TNF-α (OR: 1.082; 95%CI: 1.015, 1.154) (Figure 2(A), Supplementary Table 31). And interleukin-1β was suggested associated with an increased risk of pneumonia (OR: 1.071, 95%CI: 1.012, 1.133), while TNF-α was suggested associated with an increased risk of asthma (OR: 1.009; 95%CI: 1.002, 1.015) and COPD (OR: 1.005, 95%CI: 1.001, 1.010).

According to the above analysis, interleukin-1β mediating the effect of CKD on pneumonia risk, and TNF-α mediated its effects on both asthma and COPD risk (Figure 3). Phosphate metabolism

Figure 2B shows the estimated MR for CKD on phosphate metabolism. Genetically predicted CKD was suggested associated with decreased risk of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (OR: 0.975, 95%CI: 0.957, 0.993). These results were consistent in all complementary analyses (Supplementary Table 32). Regarding pulmonary diseases, 25-hydroxyvitamin D was associated with a decreased risk of asthma (OR: 0.987, 95%CI: 0.976, 0.998) and ARDS (OR: 0.349, 95%CI: 0.168, 0.529). It showed that 25-hydroxyvitamin D mediates the effect of CKD on asthma and ARDS risk (Figure 3). The proportions of CKD-25-hydroxyvitamin D-asthma and CKD-25-hydroxyvitamin D-ARDS were 6.50% and 3.53%, respectively (Table 4).

Hormonal imbalances

IVW analysis revealed that genetically predicted CKD was positively associated with an increased risk of PTH (OR: 1.736, 95%CI: 1.031, 2.924) (Figure 2B). It also provided strong evidence that genetically determined PTH levels had a positive effect on pneumonia (OR: 1.034, 95%CI: 1.008, 1.061) and ARDS (OR: 1.381, 95%CI: 1.019, 1.671) (Supplementary Table 32). It showed PTH mediated the effect of CKD on pneumonia and ARDS risk (Figure 3). And the proportions of mediated effects for CKD-PTH-pneumonia and CKD-PTH-ARDS were 27.15% and 22.03%, respectively (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted the first systematic exploration using MR analysis to investigate the potential causal link between CKD and pulmonary diseases and explore the mechanism of kidney–lung interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases. Our findings indicated that CKD is positively associated with an increased risk of asthma, pneumonia, ARDS, and COPD. Furthermore, we found that the immune response (interleukin-1β and TNF-α), phosphate metabolism (25-hydroxyvitamin D), and hormonal imbalances (PTH) mediated kidney–lung interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases.

In 1996, a cohort study with 183 index subjects who developed contrast media-associated renal failure and 174 paired subjects indicated that renal failure was associated with an odds ratio of dying of 5.5. Subjects who died after developing renal failure had complicated clinical courses characterized by respiratory failure [36]. However, pulmonary diseases may also contribute to the onset or progression of CKD through several potential mechanisms: Hypoxia and renal ischemia, where chronic hypoxemia (e.g., due to COPD) can induce renal vasoconstriction and glomerular injury; systemic inflammation, as pulmonary diseases release pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-a that may accelerate renal fibrosis); drug toxicity, where long-term use of glucocorticoids or antibiotics (e.g., for asthma or pneumonia treatment) may cause direct kidney damage. In our mediation MR analysis, IL-1β levels were identified as a significant mediator in the CKD-to-pneumonia pathway. The indirect effect of CKD on pneumonia through IL-1β was estimated at β = 0.064 via the product-of-coefficients method, accounting for 7.01% of the total effect. This suggests that elevated systemic inflammation (as proxied by IL-1β) may partially explain the increased pneumonia risk in CKD patients, consistent with prior evidence linking uremic toxins to immune dysregulation.

Kramer found that pulmonary histology demonstrated interstitial edema, alveolar hemorrhage, and red blood cell sludging after renal ischemia/reperfusion, which was partially attenuated by CNI-1493 [37]. To elucidate the effect of renal failure on aseptic lung injury, another murine two-hit model consisting of acute uremia and subsequent aseptic lung injury was developed. This indicated a PMN-dependent decrease in arterial partial pressure of O2 (>70%) in nonuremic mice. In contrast, uremic mice showed significant protection from acute lung injury (decrease in arterial partial pressure of O2 <40%) [38]. Grigoryev further described local and systemic inflammatory transcriptomes following kidney injury. Sixty-minute kidney ischemia resulted in a higher number of kidney inflammatory gene changes at both 6 and 36 h compared to 30-min ischemia. At 6 h, Significance Analysis of Microarrays identified 55 IRI-affected inflammatory genes after 60 min of ischemia compared to 31 genes after 30 min of ischemia, including 24 genes that were responsive to both mild and severe ischemia [39]. Other in vivo animal studies have highlighted the inflammatory consequences of kidney injury in the lungs. In mice fed a 0.2% adenine diet for 4 weeks, an established model to induce murine CKD has been shown to have significantly greater lung injury than mice fed control chow [40].

Vitamin D deficiency is widespread in both pediatric and adult CKD populations. CKD is characterized by dysregulation of vitamin D and mineral metabolism [41]. Kalkwarf found that nearly half of 182 patients (ages 5–21) with kidney disease (stages 2–5) and a third of age-matched 276 healthy children were 25-hydroxyvitamin D deficient (<20 ng/ml) [42]. The risk of deficiency is significantly greater in advanced CKD [42]. Furthermore, several clinical trials have indicated that vitamin D repletion is recommended for vitamin D insufficiency in CKD [43,44]. Mouse models used to replicate vitamin D deficiency, achieved by feeding the animals a diet lacking sufficient vitamin D, have shown that these conditions increase tissue inflammation and exacerbate COPD-like phenotypes in the lungs of cigarette smoke-exposed mice [45,46]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of aggregate data from randomized controlled trials revealed that vitamin D supplementation was safe and reduced the risk of acute respiratory infections compared with placebo [47].

In patients with advanced CKD, secondary hyperparathyroidism is an almost universal complication in patients with advanced CKD. Data from the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) study pointed out that PTH concentrations start to increase once the estimated glomerular filtration rate drops below 45 mL/min per 1.73 m2. Another recent Swiss population-based cohort study showed a steady increase in PTH concentrations paralleling the estimated glomerular filtration rate decline and is already significant at an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 126 mL/min per 1.73 m2 [48]. A study from the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey reported that lung function was inversely related to PTH, and the PTH value was independently associated with the EQ5D-index [49]. The parathyroid (in humans) is a key regulator of calcium and phosphorus homeostasis through its effects on the bone, kidney, and intestine, and by regulating 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D. PTH and vitamin D are the two major regulators of mineral metabolism [50]. PTH and vitamin D form a tightly controlled feedback cycle; PTH is a major stimulator of vitamin D synthesis in the kidney, whereas vitamin D exerts negative feedback on PTH secretion [51]. A study by Nasser A. Dhayat indicated that plasma PTH (β: 0.002768) was negatively correlated with plasma 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 [48]. Therefore, the mediating effect mediated by parathyroid hormone may be related to 25-hydroxyvitamin D. Basic experiments and large population studies should be carried out to further clarify.

This study is innovative in the following ways. First, the application of the MR method is one of the key strengths, enabling us to successfully eliminate confounding concerns and the problem of reverse causality. Second, this study is based on GWAS data with a large sample size and is the first relevant study on the mechanisms of kidney–lung interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases, which could provide evidence for subsequent studies and help provide new approaches for the targeted treatment of pulmonary diseases. In addition, there was no evidence that any IV was correlated with potential confounding factors by searching PhenoScanner. The IVs in this study were strong enough (F statistic much greater than 10) to diminish potential bias from sample overlap. Finally, although the observed effect sizes were the modest, it remained important to frame conclusions around risk stratification. Even small increases in risk may help identify subgroups of CKD patients who are more susceptible to pulmonary complications and who might benefit from closer respiratory monitoring or earlier intervention.

Our investigation is subject to several other inherent limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, it is worth noting that our findings were limited to European populations, which may constrain their applicability to other ethnic groups. Future studies should include large-scale GWAS from diverse populations. Second, despite performing sensitivity analyses to minimize potential violations of MR assumptions, the possibility of residual pleiotropy cannot be completely ruled out. Third, our study could not rule out the effect of canalization (i.e., dilution of the gene-exposure association), and thus, the estimate might be inflated [52]. Fourth, the existence of certain heterogeneities in SNPs may lead to potential bias, affecting the robustness of the MR results. Fifth, one restriction is that the instrumental variables we chose have very low thresholds (p < 5 × 10−5 for parathyroid hormone). This may result in pleiotropy, in which a single instrumental variable influences numerous pathways or attributes, distorting the results. Finally, owing to the numerous factors influencing CKD and pulmonary diseases, there may be other other plausible mechanisms (e.g., uremic toxins, pulmonary-renal crosstalk via endothelial dysfunction) that are well-documented in CKD-associated lung injury. More studies are needed to explore other mechanisms of kidney–lung interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrated that CKD is positively associated with an increased risk of asthma, pneumonia, ARDS, and COPD. Furthermore, immune response (interleukin-1β and TNF-α), phosphate metabolism (25-hydroxyvitamin D and PTH) mediate kidney–lung interactions in chronic kidney disease-associated pulmonary diseases. This finding provides new insights for the study of the etiology and pathogenesis of pulmonary diseases. Our findings need to be further confirmed through more basic experiments in the future.
