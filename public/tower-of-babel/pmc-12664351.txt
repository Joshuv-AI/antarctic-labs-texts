Effect of β-glucans with different molecular weights on pasting, rheological, texture properties, and in vitro digestibility of normal and waxy highland barley starches
Authors: Xueqing Wang, Ruoqi Sun, Xuebing Wang, Xiangping Zhang, Shuoya Zeng, Jingjing Yang, Lang Jia, Jingfeng Li, Bei Fan, Jia Liu, Yatao Huang, Fengzhong Wang, Lili Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the impact of low, medium, and high molecular weight β-glucans on physicochemical properties and in vitro digestibility of normal (NHBS) and waxy highland barley starches (WHBS). Results indicated that β-glucans with different molecular weights had varying effects on HBS properties. High molecular weight β-glucan (HBG) significantly increased the pasting temperature and gelatinization enthalpy of two HBS, and reduced the apparent viscosities. The firmness of NHBS was decreased after adding β-glucans, while the opposite result was observed in WHBS. Structural analysis showed HBG formed stronger interactions with two HBS, resulting in a more ordered structure. LF-NMR analysis revealed that β-glucans had water-binding capacities, and their interaction with water limited water access to HBS and reduced water mobility. In vitro digestion indicated that HBG significantly decreased the digestibility of NHBS and WHBS to 67.87 % and 73.85 %, respectively. HBG improved the HBS properties through its interaction with HBS and water.

Introduction

Highland barley, known as naked barley, is mainly planted in high-altitude areas, such as Qinghai and Tibet in China. Highland barley contains high amounts of starch, dietary fiber, polyphenols, etc., and has been used as raw material for the production of bread, noodles, and biscuits (Ge et al., 2023). Compared to wheat products, highland barley products exhibit a lower digestibility and glycemic index, providing nutrients and health benefits to humans (Ge et al., 2023). Starch is the main component of highland barley, thus the properties of highland barley starch (HBS), especially the amylose/amylopectin ratio, are the critical factors affecting the quality of highland barley products (Xie et al., 2023). For instance, HBS with higher amylose content possessed lower crystallinity (20.63 %) and higher solubility, whereas HBS with higher amylopectin content exhibited higher crystallinity (26.97 %) and peak viscosity, and lower gelatinization temperature (∼63 °C) (Li et al., 2001; Ma et al., 2020). Moreover, the amylose/amylopectin ratio in HBS has an effect on its final product quality. HBS with higher amylose content had good anti-digestive properties (91 %), which is very beneficial to human health, while HBS with higher amylopectin content showed higher in vitro digestibility (97 %) (Asare et al., 2011; Li et al., 2004). These properties of HBS limit its application in the food industry. Previous studies have used physical methods (roasting, adding food hydrocolloids, germination) and enzymatic methods (fermentation) to modify HBS and further improve the quality of starch-based products (Pan et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2020).

Adding food hydrocolloids is a simple, safe, and feasible modifying technology that has attracted extensive attention. Barley β-glucan, a mixed-linked (1 → 3, 1 → 4)-D-glucan with health benefits such as lowering post-prandial glucose and cholesterol levels, has been proven to improve the physicochemical properties of HBS. Li et al. (2024) found that β-glucan changed the pasting behavior of HBS, increased its pasting temperature, and reduced peak viscosity. Moreover, the presence of β-glucan increased the resistant starch content and inhibited the in vitro digestion of waxy HBS (Xie et al., 2024). However, β-glucan is hydrophilic, and water is a crucial factor influencing starch pasting; the binding of β-glucan and water may limit starch for accessing water. And the molecular weight of β-glucan affected its water-binding capacity, with higher molecular weight β-glucan having a stronger affinity for water (Lee et al., 2015). Additionally, β-glucans with different molecular weights possess different chain lengths and spatial conformation, and will interact differently with starch (Feng et al., 2019). To the best of our knowledge, the effect of β-glucans with different molecular weights on the properties of HBS with different amylose/amylopectin ratios is unclear. Therefore, the study aimed to explore the impact of low (LBG), medium (MBG), and high molecular weight β-glucans (HBG) on pasting, rheological, texture properties, and in vitro digestibility of normal HBS (NHBS) and waxy HBS (WHBS). This study can provide theoretical support for improving the processing characteristics of HBS and expanding the application of β-glucans in foods.

Materials and methods

Materials

Normal highland barley (Dulihuang) and waxy highland barley (Gankengnuo 2) grains were provided by the Qinghai Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences (Qinghai, China) and the Gansu Academy of Agri-engineering Technology (Gansu, China), respectively. LBG (179 kDa, purity>95 %), MBG (251 kDa, purity>95 %), HBG (495 kDa, purity>90 %), and GOPOD were purchased from Megazyme (Ireland). α-Amylase and pepsin were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China).

Chain conformation of β-glucans

The chain conformation of β-glucans was determined using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Park Inc., Korea). β-Glucans were mixed with distilled water and stirred at 80 °C for 1 h to prepare 100 μg/mL solutions. The solutions were filtered using a 0.22 μm filter and diluted to 5 μg/mL, then stirred for 5 h at room temperature. The solutions (8 μL) were pipetted onto the mica carrier, and the microstructure was observed using AFM (Zhang et al., 2022).

HBS extraction

HBS was isolated using the method of Zhao et al. (2020) with some modifications. Highland barley grains were pearled at the rate of 20 %. After pearling, grains were mixed with 0.5 % sodium sulfite solution in a 1:5 ratio for 60 h at 4 °C to ensure complete softening. Then, grains were washed with distilled water and homogenized with a blender (Domex-E, Hangzhou, China). The starch-water suspension was passed through 80 sieves and stood for 3 h. The suspension was centrifuged (3000 g, 20 min), and the sediment was further washed with water and ethanol three times each. The starch obtained was dried for 24 h at ambient temperature and screened with a 100-mesh sieve. The amylose contents in NHBS and WHBS were measured using assay kits (Megazyme, Ireland), with the values of 25.06 % and 7.59 % respectively. The amylopectin contents in NHBS and WHBS were calculated based on the amylose contents, with the values of 74.94 % and 92.41 %, respectively.

Preparation of HBS/β-glucan mixtures

The HBS/β-glucan mixtures (10 %, w/v) were prepared as the following procedure.

The β-glucans (0.50 g) were weighed and solubilized in 100 mL of distilled water. The solutions were stirred in an 80 °C water bath until their white solids disappeared. The solutions were cooled to room temperature, and then HBS (9.50 g) was added to the solutions and heated at 90 °C for 20 min (Xiao et al., 2020). The obtained mixtures were used for further analysis.

HBS/β-glucan mixtures properties

Determination of pasting properties

Raw HBS (2.85 g) and β-glucans (0.15 g) were weighed into the aluminum container. The total weight of the mixture was 28 g after adding distilled water. The samples were transferred to a rapid-viscosity analyzer (RVA, Perten Inc., Australia). The test procedure was as follows: the shear rate was 160 rpm, the temperature was increased from 50 °C to 90 °C at a rate of 9 °C/min, kept at 90 °C for 3 min, and cooled to 50 °C at a rate of 9 °C/min. The peak viscosity (PV, cP), trough viscosity (TV, cP), breakdown viscosity (BV, cP), final viscosity (FV, cP), setback viscosity (SV, cP), and pasting temperature (PT, °C) were recorded.

Determination of thermal properties

The HBS/β-glucan mixtures prepared in Section 2.4 were frozen with liquid nitrogen and lyophilized at −69 °C and 1 Pa for 3 days. The lyophilized samples were ground, weighed, and mixed with distilled water in a 1:3 mass ratio. They were sealed in the aluminum pan and placed at room temperature for 24 h to equilibrate. The test procedure was as follows: the temperature was increased from 30 °C to 100 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. The onset temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), conclusion temperature (Tc), and gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH) were recorded using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, TA Instruments Inc., USA). The gelatinization degree (GD) was calculated based on the following formula (Schneider Teixeira et al., 2018):(1)GD%=ΔHrawstarch−ΔHpasted starch/ΔHrawstarch×100%where ΔHraw starch and ΔHpasted starch (J/g) are the enthalpies of raw starch and pasted starch, respectively.

Determination of dynamic rheological properties

The HBS/β-glucan mixtures after RVA analysis were subjected to rheological tests. A rheometer (Anton Paar Inc., Austria) equipped with a parallel plate (50 mm diameter) was used to measure the rheological behavior. The HBS/β-glucan mixtures were cooled to 50 °C and positioned onto the parallel plate with a 1.00 mm gap. The scanning angular frequency ranged from 1 to 100 rad/s at a strain of 1 %, which was within the linear viscoelastic region. The apparent viscosity (Pa∙s), storage modulus (G′), and loss modulus (G″) were recorded.

Determination of textural properties

The HBS/β-glucan mixtures prepared in Section 2.4 were stored at 4 °C for 24 h for texture profile analysis. Samples were compressed to 50 % using a P/0.5R probe. The trigger was 2 g, and the test speed was 1.0 mm/s. Firmness (g), resilience, cohesiveness, springiness, gumminess (g), and chewiness (g) were recorded.

Determination of crystalline structures

The lyophilized HBS/β-glucan powders were scanned at 2θ in the range of 5°-40° using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker Inc., Germany) (Zehra et al., 2024). The instrument was operated at 40 kV and 100 mA with the scanning rate at 5°/min. The relative crystallinities of samples were calculated using Jade 6.0 software.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The lyophilized HBS/β-glucan powders were mixed with KBr (1:100, w:w) and pressed into tablets. The FTIR spectra were scanned using a spectrometer (Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany). The scanning parameters were as follows: wavenumber ranged from 400 cm−1 to 4000 cm−1, scanning times were 32, and the resolution was 4 cm−1. Absorbance ratios of 1047 cm−1 to 1022 cm−1 and 995 cm−1 to 1022 cm−1 were calculated (Zhao et al., 2024).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The lyophilized HBS/β-glucan mixtures were cut into squares, fixed on the plate with double-sided tape, and sprayed with gold. The morphological structures of samples were observed by SEM (Hitachi Inc., Japan) at a voltage of 10 kV and 400× magnification.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

The HBS/β-glucan mixtures prepared in Section 2.4 were stored at 4 °C for 24 h to form gels. The gels were cut into cylinders and placed in a test tube with a diameter of 10 mm. The transverse relaxation time (T2) was determined using the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill sequence of the LF-NMR instrument (MesoMR23-060 V-I, Niumag Co., Ltd., China). The parameters were as follows: the number of echoes was 15,000, the echo time was 0.15 ms, and the number of scans was 16. Additionally, β-glucan solutions prepared in Section 2.3 were poured into small bottles and placed in the test tube to carry out LF-NMR measurements.

In vitro digestibility

The lyophilized HBS/β-glucan powders (50 mg) were mixed with 2 mL distilled water in 50 mL tubes, and the mixture was stirred and placed in a 37 °C water bath for 2 min. Three glass beads and 1 mL α-amylase solution (50 U/mL) were added to the tube to simulate oral digestion. After 2 min, pepsin solution (400 U/mL, 5 mL) was added and shaken for 30 min at 200 rpm to simulate gastric digestion. Subsequently, 5 mL NaOH (0.02 M), 20 mL sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.0), and 5 mL pancreatin/amyloglucosidase mixture were added to simulate small intestine digestion. After incubation for 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 90, 120, and 180 min, 1 mL sample was removed to the ethanol solution (1 mL) to stop the reaction and centrifuged (Wang et al., 2022). The content of glucose in the supernatant was determined using the GOPOD kit and converted to the HBS hydrolysis rate. The contents of rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS) were calculated according to the previous study (Wahab & Janaswamy, 2024).

The following formula is used to describe the kinetics of starch hydrolysis (Wang et al., 2022):(2)C=C∞×1−exp−ktwhere C represents the hydrolysis rate of starch at t time, C∞ represents the equilibrium concentration of starch hydrolyzed after 180 min, k represents the kinetic constant, and t represents time.

The hydrolysis index (HI) was measured based on the following formula (Graça et al., 2021):(3)HI=AUCsample/AUCfresh white breadwhere AUC represents the area under the hydrolysis curve.

The measurement of the estimated glycemic index (eGI) was calculated (Wang et al., 2022):(4)eGI=39.71+0.549×HI

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently conducted three times, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. SPSS 26.0 was used for one-way analysis of variance with the Tukey test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Graphing was with GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Pasting properties

The pasting parameters of samples are shown in Table 1. In the NHBS system, the pasting temperature of NHBS was 81.18 °C, and the addition of β-glucans significantly increased its pasting temperature. An increase in pasting temperature was thought to be caused by incomplete gelatinization of starch, because the presence of β-glucans may reduce the content of available water during the pasting process and decrease starch gelatinization degree (Ma et al., 2019). Moreover, the addition of LBG and MBG significantly reduced the peak and trough viscosities of NHBS. This may be attributed to the presence of β-glucans, on the one hand, β-glucan competes with starch for free water, limiting the solubilization of starch granules (Xie et al., 2020); on the other hand, β-glucan interacts with starch granules to form closer cross-linkages, hindering starch gelatinization (Xiao et al., 2020), thus decreasing the viscosity of the system. However, HBG increased the peak and trough viscosities. Cui et al. (2023) observed similar results, possibly because HBG exhibits higher viscosity in water, and its own viscosity further affects the viscosity of the NHBS system (Kim & White, 2013). Furthermore, the presence of β-glucans increased the final viscosity, possibly because the viscosity of β-glucan solutions affects the viscosity of the system (Cui et al., 2023). Additionally, β-glucans increased the breakdown and setback viscosities of the NHBS system. Breakdown viscosity (the difference between peak viscosity and trough viscosity) reflected the damage degree of starch granules during pasting, and setback viscosity (the difference between final viscosity and trough viscosity) was associated with the rearrangement of starch molecules during cooling (Zhao et al., 2023). The phenomenon implies that the addition of β-glucans promoted starch pasting and short-term aging, which was contrary to the result of pasting temperature. A similar finding was obtained by Liu et al. (2018). Compared with NHBS, WHBS began gelatinization at a lower temperature and had higher peak and trough viscosities. Previous studies showed that starch with higher amylopectin content was more likely to absorb water for pasting, while high amylose content hindered their swelling (Xie et al., 2020). Moreover, results showed that LBG did not increase the pasting temperature of WHBS, whereas LBG had a significant effect on NHBS. This may be attributed to the amylose content of NHBS and WHBS, and LBG may tend to interact with amylose, inhibiting its gelatinization (Xie et al., 2020). In addition, adding LBG and MBG to WHBS significantly decreased the peak and trough viscosities, whereas WHBS-HBG showed higher values, which showed similar trends as the NHBS system. In summary, the molecular weights of β-glucan affected the pasting properties of HBS.

Thermal properties

Starch was gelatinized during the heating process, changing from an ordered structure to an amorphous structure, the process that was accompanied by thermal transition (Luo et al., 2021). Table 2 shows the thermal transition parameters of HBS/β-glucan mixtures. The addition of HBG significantly increased the To and Tc values of the NHBS/HBG mixture, suggesting that HBG increased the thermal stability of NHBS. Moreover, the To values of NHBS/HBG mixtures were higher than their pasting temperature, which may be attributed to the states of the samples and the interactions between starch granules, β-glucans, and water molecules in the samples during testing. A similar result was observed in the study of Zhang et al. (2024). ΔH represents the energy required to destroy the double helix structure of starch (Luo et al., 2021). The presence of HBG significantly improved ΔH values in the NHBS system, suggesting that the addition of HBG stabilized the structure of starch and required higher energy to destroy its structure (Wang et al., 2023). Previous studies explained the result from two aspects: First, HBG exhibited a higher water-binding capacity, limiting the contact between starch and water molecules, making starch gelatinization incomplete, so higher energy is required to destroy its remaining crystalline regions; On the other hand, HBG possessed many hydroxyl groups and longer chain length, it formed a stronger interaction with starch, thus needing higher energy to destroy its structure (Wang et al., 2023). WHBS had a lower ΔH value compared with NHBS, and it was more prone to pasting. Additionally, MBG and HBG significantly reduced the gelatinization degree of WHBS, possibly because the binding of β-glucans and water affects the interactions between starch and water, resulting in a lower gelatinization degree. The result indicated that the molecular weights of β-glucan affected the pasting behavior of HBS, particularly WHBS.

Rheological properties

The dynamic flow curves of HBS/β-glucan mixtures are shown in Fig. 2. The apparent viscosities of the NHBS system were gradually decreased with the increase of shear rate, indicating that the gels were non-Newtonian. The addition of LBG, MBG, and HBG reduced the apparent viscosity of the NHBS system. The formation of the cross-linked network of starch gels is attributed to amylose (Kong et al., 2020), so the lower apparent viscosity of HBS/β-glucan mixtures may be because β-glucans envelop starch granules, inhibiting the dissolution of amylose and thus decreasing the viscosity of the system (Jia et al., 2022). G′ and G″ reflect the solid- and liquid-like properties of the system (Xiao et al., 2020). In the NHBS system, with the increase of angular frequency, the G′ and G″ values increased, and G′ was greater than G″, which meant that NHBS/β-glucan mixtures possessed solid properties. The presence of β-glucans reduced G′, and the value was decreased by 27.16 %, 12.69 %, and 82.09 % at the angular frequency of 100 rad/s, indicating that β-glucans weakened the elasticity of the system. This may be because β-glucans adhere to the surface of amylose, hindering amylose-amylose interactions and inhibiting the formation of elastic gels (Kong et al., 2020; Zhao, Jin, et al., 2023). However, in the presence of β-glucans, G′ did not exhibit a molecular weight-dependent changing trend, which needs further exploration. In WHBS, apparent viscosity, G′, and G″ showed similar trends to those of NHBS, whereas the values were lower than those of NHBS. Sasaki et al. (2004) found that the amylose content affected the rheological properties of starch because amylose was mainly involved in forming the network structure. Textural properties

Table 3 presents the texture parameters of HBS/β-glucan gels. In the NHBS system, the addition of β-glucans changed the texture parameters of NHBS, with a decrease in firmness, resilience, springiness, gumminess, and chewiness, and an increase in cohesiveness. The firmness was related to the rearrangement of amylose. The presence of β-glucans significantly reduced firmness, which may adhere to starch granules and inhibit the dissolution and rearrangement of amylose, resulting in a decrease in firmness (Zhang et al., 2023). Moreover, NHBS-HBG exhibited higher firmness than NHBS/LBG and NHBS/MBG, which is possible due to the longer chain length of HBG, as it promotes the formation of a denser network structure in the NHBS system (Cui et al., 2023). Chewiness reflects the energy required to chew (Cui et al., 2023). Results showed that β-glucans reduced the chewiness of the system, and the study of Li et al. (2024) supported the result. WHBS exhibited lower firmness and chewiness than NHBS. However, the addition of β-glucans significantly increased the firmness of the WHBS system. This is possible because WHBS contains a higher amount of amylopectin, and β-glucan with a longer chain length is more tightly entangled with amylopectin, leading to an increase in the firmness. Structure of HBS/β-glucan mixtures

XRD is used for determining the crystal structure of starch. As shown in Fig. S1, raw NHBS exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks at 15.18°, 17.32°, 17.82°, and 23.26°, suggesting WHBS was a typical A structure (Pan et al., 2023). The pasting process significantly destroyed the crystal structure of NHBS, and only 8.24 % of the relative crystallinity was retained. The relative crystallinity of raw NHBS was 21.66 %, meaning that 61.96 % of crystalline regions were destroyed during the pasting process. The addition of β-glucans did not change the peak shape of starch but significantly increased its relative crystallinity (Table S1). The study of Chung et al. (2011) found that amylopectin played an important role in the formation of crystalline regions. An increase in relative crystallinity of NHBS/β-glucan mixtures is possibly because β-glucans promote double helix structural rearrangement of amylopectin. WHBS showed a higher relative crystallinity than NHBS, mainly because it contains more amylopectin. The relative crystallinity of raw WHBS was 31.44 %, and 69.91 % of the crystalline regions were damaged. WHBS/β-glucan mixtures exhibited higher relative crystallinity than WHBS.

FTIR can be used to reflect the functional group of starch and evaluate the interaction between HBS and β-glucans (Zhao et al., 2023). The FTIR spectra of the samples are shown in Fig. S1. NHBS and WHBS showed characteristic peaks of polysaccharides. The peaks in 3344 and 3332 cm−1 were related to the -OH stretching; the peaks in 2929 cm−1 corresponded to the stretching vibration of CH2; the signals at 1645 and 1654 cm−1 were caused by the -OH bending vibration of water; the bands at 1020 cm−1 were attributed to the C—O stretching (Kong et al., 2020). The addition of β-glucans did not change the characteristic peaks of starch, suggesting that β-glucans non-covalently interacted with HBS. Li et al. (2024) found that the β-glucan-HBS interactions were mainly driven by hydrogen bonding. R1047/1022 and R995/1022 were used to reflect the short-range ordered structure and double helix structure of starch (Zhao et al., 2024). Table S1 showed that the addition of MBG and HBG increased the R1047/1022 and R995/1022 values, indicating that β-glucans promoted the formation of a more ordered and compact double helix structure of two HBS. This is mainly attributed to the interaction between starch and β-glucans; studies showed that HBG had a higher chain length and more hydroxyl groups, leading to stronger interactions with starch and improving short-range ordered structure (Feng et al., 2019).

Microstructure of HBS/β-glucan mixtures

As shown in Fig. 3, HBS/β-glucan mixtures exhibited a “honeycomb” structure. The gel networks of NHBS and WHBS had large pores that were unevenly distributed (white arrows). After the addition of β-glucans, particularly HBG, the pore sizes of the gels became smaller, and the structures were more compact. The finding was consistent with the results of FTIR, which confirmed that the presence of β-glucans enhanced the short-range ordered structure of starch. Additionally, Li et al. Relaxation properties

During the pasting process, starch granules in contact with water undergo swelling, the disappearance of birefringence, reduction of the crystalline regions, granules rupture, and finally solubilization (Donmez et al., 2021). The process mainly involves the interactions between starch molecules and water. LF-NMR is used to investigate the mobility and distribution of water molecules in the starch system (Luo et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 4A, strongly bound water (T21, 0.01–1 ms), weakly bound water (T22, 1–10 ms), and free water (T23, 100–10,000 ms) were found in the system. β-Glucans are hydrophilic polysaccharides; β-glucans with different molecular weights exhibited different water binding capacities, and HBG solution had lower T21 and T22, which indicated that there was a tighter association between water molecules and HBG. In the NHBS system, compared with NHBS, T22 and T23 shifted towards lower relaxation time after adding β-glucans, indicating that β-glucans, particularly HBG, decreased the degree of freedom of water. Possibly because β-glucans fill into the starch gel network, limiting the movement of water molecules. Additionally, in this study, the β-glucans were first dissolved in water, and then NHBS was added to the solution, meaning that β-glucans first bound a portion of the water and reduced the free water content used for starch pasting. Some studies showed that hydrophilic polysaccharides competed with starch for water molecules, thus inhibiting the pasting of starch and increasing its pasting temperature (Ma et al., 2019). This was consistent with the results of RVA and DSC. Compared with NHBS (T21: 0.22 ms, T22: 5.54 ms, and T23: 212.15 ms), WHBS exhibited higher T21 (1.52 ms), T22 (8.31 ms), and T23 (344.90 ms). Hu et al. (2019) found that a high proportion of amylose promoted the interactions between starch and water molecules and reduced water mobility. WHBS contains higher amylopectin content, thus exhibiting higher water mobility than NHBS. Similarly, the addition of β-glucans affected water distribution in the WHBS system. In summary, the molecular weight of β-glucan affected water availability and further influenced starch pasting and thermal properties.Fig. 4LF-NMR T2 relaxation time distribution curves of β-glucan solutions and HBS/β-glucan mixtures (A); starch hydrolysis curves of HBS/β-glucan mixtures (B). In vitro digestibility

The starch hydrolysis curves of HBS/β-glucan mixtures are shown in Fig. 4B. In the first 60 min, the hydrolysis rate of the NHBS system was rapidly increased, and the hydrolysis rates of NHBS, NHBS-LBG, NHBS-MBG, and NHBS-HBG were 73.30 %, 71.60 %, 69.23 %, and 67.87 %, respectively. The result indicated that β-glucans had an inhibitory effect on starch hydrolysis, and the HBG exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory effect. Starch hydrolysis is associated with the presence of amylase, and the presence of HBG may increase the viscosity of digestive digesta and reduce the mobility of water, thereby reducing the contact between amylase and starch (Regand et al., 2011). Moreover, as shown in Fig. 3, a more compact structure was formed between HBG and NHBS, which may prevent amylase from contacting starch (Liu et al., 2021). These factors further influenced RDS, SDS, and RS contents of NHBS (Cui et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2020). NHBS/β-glucan mixtures with lower digestibility showed higher RS content and lower RDS and SDS contents. Additionally, C∞, k, and eGI showed decreasing trends with the increase of β-glucan molecular weight (Table S2). Feng et al. (2019) found that β-glucans reduced postprandial blood glucose levels. The property of β-glucans is very beneficial to humans, especially for people with diabetes. WHBS contained more amylopectin and was hydrolyzed faster than NHBS, with a hydrolysis rate of 81.22 % in 60 min. The addition of LBG, MBG, and HBG slowed down the hydrolysis rate of WHBS to 78.91 %, 75.33 %, and 73.85 %.

Conclusion

The study investigated the effect of β-glucans with different molecular weights on the pasting, rheological, texture, and digestion properties of NHBS and WHBS. RVA and DSC analyses showed that the addition of HBG increased the pasting temperature and thermal stability of two HBS. Dynamic rheological tests indicated that β-glucans decreased the apparent viscosity and G′ of the HBS systems. Textural analysis found that β-glucans changed HBS texture properties. Moreover, XRD, FTIR, and SEM analyses indicated that the addition of HBG protected the crystal structure of HBS and made its structure more ordered and compact. Furthermore, HBG significantly increased RS content and lowered HBS digestibility and eGI. Changes in the properties of HBS were related to the β-glucans-HBS and β-glucans-water interactions, as confirmed in FTIR and LF-NMR. The proposed mechanism of HBG and HBS is shown in Fig. 5. The study contributes to the development and application of β-glucans in HBS-based products.
