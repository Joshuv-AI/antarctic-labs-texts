Distributionally Robust Deep Q Q -learning
Chung I Lu

Abstract
We propose a novel distributionally robust Q Q -learning algorithm for the non-tabular case accounting for continuous state spaces where the state transition of the underlying Markov decision process is subject to model uncertainty. The uncertainty is taken into account by considering the worst-case transition from a ball around a reference probability measure. To determine the optimal policy under the worst-case state transition, we solve the associated non-linear Bellman equation by dualising and regularising the Bellman operator with the Sinkhorn distance, which is then parameterised with deep neural networks. This approach allows us to modify the Deep Q-Network algorithm to optimise for the worst case state transition. We illustrate the tractability and effectiveness of our approach through several applications, including a portfolio optimisation task based on S&P 500 data. We also establish convergence guarantees for exact and approximate robust fitted Q Q -iteration, decompose the numerical RDQN error into interpretable components, and discuss extensions to compact continuous action sets.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for training intelligent agents to make optimal decisions in complex environments
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
)
.
A central concept within RL is Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), which provide a mathematical formalism for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. Traditional RL algorithms often assume a perfectly known model of the environment’s dynamics.
However, in many real-world applications, the state transition probabilities of the underlying MDP are subject to uncertainty due to factors such as limited data, noisy measurements, or inherent stochasticity.
This model misspecification can lead to policies that perform poorly when deployed in the actual environment, simply because the agent is trained in the wrong model.

To address the challenge of model uncertainty, Distributionally Robust Optimisation (DRO)
(
Rahimian and Mehrotra, 2019
)
offers a principled approach by considering a set of plausible probability distributions, an ambiguity set, and optimising against the worst-case distribution within this set.
Recent research has begun to integrate DRO into MDPs
(
Wiesemann et al., 2013
)
, leading to the development of distributionally robust RL algorithms.
One of the types of ambiguity sets that has gained traction is the Wasserstein ball around a possibly estimated reference measure, which quantifies the distance between probability measures using the Wasserstein metric
(
Villani, 2009
)
.
While significant progress has been made in the tabular setting with discrete state and action spaces for this type of ambiguity sets, extending these techniques to continuous state spaces presents substantial challenges, particularly in solving the associated non-linear Bellman equation.

In this paper, we propose a novel distributionally robust Q-learning algorithm specifically designed for continuous state spaces where the state transition of the underlying Markov decision process is subject to model uncertainty.
We model this uncertainty by considering the worst-case transition from a ball around a reference probability measure, quantified by the Sinkhorn distance which is a regularised version of the Wasserstein distance
(
Cuturi, 2013
)
.
To determine the optimal policy under these worst-case transitions, we tackle the associated non-linear Bellman equation by dualising the Bellman operator
(
Wang et al., 2021
)
.
This regularised problem yields a more tractable dual formulation, which we then parameterise using deep neural networks (see
Hornik et al. (1990)
,
Kidger and Lyons (2020)
,
Pinkus (1999)
,
Scarselli and Tsoi (1998)
), allowing us to adapt the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm
(
Mnih et al., 2015
)
to optimise for the worst-case state transition.
The case of Wasserstein uncertainty can be approximated by choosing a small value for the regularisation parameter.

1.1
Contributions

Our main contributions can be summarised as follows:

1.

We introduce a distributionally robust Q-learning framework for continuous state spaces and discrete action spaces based on the Sinkhorn distance.

2.

We prove that the dynamic programming principle applies to the robust MDP using the Sinkhorn ball as the ambiguity set, hence allowing us to derive a robust Bellman equation.

3.

We address the intractability of the robust Bellman equation by dualising the optimisation problem leading to a more tractable formulation.

4.

We develop a practical algorithm,
Robust DQN (RDQN)
, by parameterising the robust Q-function with deep neural networks and deriving a modified loss function based on the dual formulation, enabling optimisation using stochastic gradient descent.

5.

We provide theoretical guarantees for the existence of solutions when the state space is compact.

6.

We extend the robust operators to compact continuous action sets, quantify action-grid and entropy-regularisation errors, and relate critic accuracy to robust policy performance. These results concern exact or error-controlled fitted iteration, not unrestricted actor-critic training.

7.

We provide evidence for the applicability of RDQN through three illustrative applications: first a toy example involving agent-environment interaction, and two more realistic and complex settings for portfolio optimisation based on the S&P 500 index and a set of US stocks.

1.2
Related Literature

The application of DRO to RL has led to the emergence of distributionally robust RL, aiming to develop policies that are resilient to uncertainties in the underlying MDP parameters.
The theoretical foundations were laid in
Iyengar (2005)
and
Nilim and El Ghaoui (2005)
which established the concept of distributional robustness in discrete time MDPs and derived the distributionally robust Bellman equation under the assumption of discrete state and action spaces.
The result was extended to continuous state and action spaces in
Bäuerle and Glauner (2022)
and
Neufeld and Sester (2024b)
for finite time horizon and
Neufeld et al. (2023)
for infinite time horizon.
Central to the results is that the ambiguity set has a rectangularity property which means that the ambiguity related to any state-action pair is independent of the ambiguity related to other state-action pairs.
Ambiguity sets that fall within this framework include the balls around a reference measure, which are defined using distances such as the Wasserstein distance or Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence.

Algorithms in the literature aiming to solve robust MDPs can be classified based on several factors: the type of ambiguity set employed, the nature of the state and action spaces (e.g., continuous vs. discrete), and the approach taken to solve the MDP.

One of the earlier works in this area is
Xu and Mannor (2010)
which defines the ambiguity based on uncertainties in parameters of a distribution but the algorithm solving the problem sequentially is meant for smaller state and action spaces.

Liu et al. (2022)
,
Wang et al. (2023b)
and
Wang et al. (2024)
focus on the finite state and action space setting using a KL divergence ball as the ambiguity set.
All three works use
Q
-learning approaches (see
Watkins and Dayan (1992)
,
Hasselt (2010)
,
Tan et al. (2017)
,
Fan et al. (2020)
), each improving on the sample complexity.
The use of the KL divergence is motivated by the use of a duality to derive the robust Bellman operator.
Computing the operator requires sampling the transition from the same state-action pair under the reference measure multiple times.
This can be simplified in the analogous Sinkhorn duality where the sampling can be based on a suitably chosen prior measure.

Smirnova et al. (2019)
also uses the KL divergence but the ambiguity is instead defined on the policy and they use function approximation to extend to continuous state and action spaces with an actor-critic algorithm.

Panaganti et al. (2022)
uses a ball based on the total variation in a finite state and action space and uses a fitted
Q
-iteration approach.

Ramesh et al. (2024)
uses Gaussian processes to model the transition and derives sample complexity for error bounds depending on the type of ambiguity set, assuming the optimal robust policy can be obtained by some chosen algorithm.

Wang and Zou (2021)
and
Wang and Zou (2022)
use the R-contamination model to define the ambiguity set and solve the continuous state and action space robust MDP using a
Q
-learning and policy gradient based algorithm respectively.

Decker and Sester (2024)
uses a finite set of pre-defined measures in a discrete state and action space and also utilises
Q
-learning.

Yang (2017)
and
Yang (2020)
consider the Wasserstein ball but frame the problem as a convex optimisation problem.

Neufeld and Sester (2024c)
also uses the Wasserstein ball for finite state and action spaces and solving the robust MDP using a
Q
-learning approach but it has a similar sampling issue as the KL divergence approach.

There are also works that focus on scenarios where the ambiguity set is not state-action rectangular leading to general robust MDPs which are known to be NP-hard
(
Wiesemann et al., 2013
)
.
The complexity of algorithms used to solve these general MDPs varies based on the specific structure of the ambiguity set.
In
Wang et al. (2023a)
, a double loop policy gradient based method is proposed to solve robust MDPs with state rectangular ambiguity sets.
For generally non-rectangular ambiguity sets,
Li et al. (2023)
proposed an actor-critic algorithm that approximates a solution with the complexity scaling with inverse-quartic dependence on the error.
Other works that consider non-sa-rectangular ambiguity sets include
Kumar et al. (2023)
,
Wiesemann et al. (2013)
,
Mannor et al. (2016)
,
Neufeld et al. (2024)
and
Goyal and Grand-Clément (2023)
.

Another line of work investigates the relationship between regularisation and robustness in the context of MDPs.
These include
Eysenbach and Levine (2021)
and
Derman et al. (2023)
which show the equivalence between the regularised MDP and certain classes of robust MDPs.

In terms of the use of the Wasserstein or Sinkhorn distance, it comes from the field of optimal transport
(
Villani, 2009
)
.
The original Wasserstein distance is computationally expensive to compute, especially in high-dimensional spaces
(
Genevay et al., 2019
)
.

Cuturi (2013)
introduced the Sinkhorn distance, which regularises the Wasserstein distance by adding an entropic term to the optimisation problem thereby enforcing some smoothness.
It is named after the algorithm
(
Sinkhorn, 1964
)
used to compute the distance and has better computational and sample complexity than the Wasserstein distance (see
Lin et al. (2022)
,
Genevay et al. (2019)
).
There are additional variants that have been proposed, such as the Sinkhorn divergence, unbalanced optimal transport and combinations of these (see e.g.
Feydy et al. (2019)
,
Séjourné et al. (2019)
).

We are using a more general definition of the Sinkhorn distance similar to
Wang et al. (2021, Definition I)
where the entropic regularisation is defined with respect to the product measure of the reference measure and a prior measure.
The prior measure is chosen such that all measures in the ambiguity set are absolutely continuous with respect to it.
This grants us added flexibility when computing the robust Bellman operator as it allows us to sample from the prior measure instead of the reference measure as in the KL divergence case.

followed by experiments in Section

.
We conclude in Section

2.1
Distributionally Robust Markov Decision Processes

To model the
state space
of the Markov decision process, we consider a closed but not necessarily compact subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
which we use to define the space on which the infinite time horizon stochastic process takes values, given by the infinite Cartesian product

\Omega:=\mathcal{X}^{\mathbb{N}}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\times\cdots.

We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
the set of all probability measures on
\Omega
equipped with its Borel-
\sigma
-algebra.
We model the evolution of the attained states via an infinite horizon time-discrete stochastic process and, to this end, we define on
\Omega
the stochastic process
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by the canonical process

X_{t}(\left(\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{t},\dots\right)):=\omega_{t},\text{ for }(\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{t},\dots)\in\Omega,~~t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

To model the set of actions (also called controls), we fix a
finite
set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(a_{t}(X_{t})\right)_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}~\middle|~a_{t}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow A\text{ Borel measurable for all }t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}.

Instead of fixing the distribution of the state transition between states from the underlying state space (in dependence of a chosen action), we want to account for a possible model misspecification by allowing for a range of possible distributions.
We model distributional uncertainty through optimal transport distances.

(i)

The map

\mathcal{X}\times A\times\mathcal{X}\ni(x_{0},a,x_{1})\mapsto r(x_{0},a,x_{1})

Dynamic Programming: The Bellman Equation

In

In particular, it applies when the ambiguity set is a Wasserstein ball around a reference measure.
We extend this result to the Sinkhorn distance and the associated ambiguity set.

Proposition 2.8

(Robust Bellman equation for the Sinkhorn ball ambiguity set)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0,\delta\geq 0

V_{\delta}(x)=\mathcal{T}_{\delta}V_{\delta}(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\ni x\mapsto\mathcal{T}_{\delta}V_{\delta}(x):

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a,X_{1})+\gamma V_{\delta}(X_{1})\right].

2.4
Q
-learning

The goal of
Q
-learning, as introduced in
Watkins and Dayan (1992)
, is to make use of the Bellman equation (
2.7
) to learn the optimal policy
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathcal{A}
to maximising (
2.6
).
By
Neufeld et al. (2023, Theorem 2.7 (iii))

{\mathbf{a}}=(a^{*}(X_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
for some measurable decision rule

Hence, it suffices to determine an optimal action
a^{*}(x)
for each state

\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a^{*}(x))\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a^{*}(x),X_{1})+\gamma V_{\delta}(X_{1})\right]=V_{\delta}(x).

(2.8)

We define the optimal robust

\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\mapsto Q_{\delta}^{*}(x,a):=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a,X_{1})+\gamma V_{\delta}(X_{1})\right].

(2.9)

With this definition, we obtain via Equation (

\sup_{a\in A}Q_{\delta}^{*}(x,a)=V_{\delta}(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X},

and thus computing
Q_{\delta}^{*}
allows us to determine the optimal action in each state
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Now we observe by Equation (
2.10
) that

\begin{split}(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{*})(x,a)&:=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a,X_{1})+\gamma\sup_{b\in A}Q_{\delta}^{*}(X_{1},b)\right]\\
&=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a,X_{1})+\gamma V_{\delta}(X_{1})\right]=Q_{\delta}^{*}(x,a).\end{split}

(2.11)

Under Assumptions
2.4
–
2.7
the map
\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\mapsto Q_{\delta}^{*}(x,a)
is continuous and bounded (compare Lemma
6.8
). This motivates extending the notation in (
2.11
) to general continuous and bounded functions
Q
. To this end, let
C_{b}(\mathcal{X}\times A)
denote the space of continuous and bounded functions
Q:\mathcal{X}\times A\to\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the supremum norm.
For
Q\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}\times A)
, we define

(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q)(x,a):=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a,X_{1})+\gamma\sup_{b\in A}Q(X_{1},b)\right].

(2.12)

Applying the above defined operator
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
iteratively to an arbitrary initial
Q_{0}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}\times A)
then converges to
Q_{\delta}^{*}
.

Theorem 2.9

(Convergence of exact robust
Q
-iteration)
.

For any
Q_{0}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}\times A)
, define

Q_{k+1}=\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{k},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.

Then there exists a unique fixed point
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}\times A)
satisfying
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}=Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
, and
Q_{k}
converges uniformly to
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
. Moreover,

\left\lVert Q_{k}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{k}\left\lVert Q_{0}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

(2.13)

This leads directly to the idea of
Q
-learning which is to solve the fixed point equation
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{*}=Q_{\delta}^{*}
via iteratively applying
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
.
In the case of a discrete state and action space, a corresponding algorithm exists for the Wasserstein case (see
Neufeld and Sester (2024c)
).
For infinitely many states and actions, the algorithm is however infeasible which is why we propose to approximate the optimal
Q
function with neural networks by pursuing a similar approach as in
Mnih et al. (2015)
or
Van Hasselt et al. (2016)
in the non-robust case.

In particular, the convergence statement from Theorem
2.9
should be read separately from the stochastic optimisation routine (RDQN) we propose to train the neural network in Section
3
: the exact robust Bellman iteration is contractive, while RDQN is a numerical implementation whose approximation errors must be controlled or monitored, which we discuss in more detail in Section
3.3.5
.

Q

3.1
Dualising and Regularising the Robust Optimisation Problem

As discussed in Section
2.4
, our goal is to solve the fixed point equation
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{*}=Q_{\delta}^{*}
.
Directly computing the infimum over the Sinkhorn ball is intractable in practice.
Instead, we follow the procedure proposed in
Wang et al. (2021)
and consider the dual formulation of the robust optimisation problem which is in a tractable form.
Throughout this section, we assume that Assumptions
2.4
,
2.7
and
2.5
hold.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
. Assume that
\nu\left(\{0\leq\|y-X_{1}^{\nu}\|<\infty\}\right)=1
, and that

\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}^{\nu}\sim\nu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-\|y-X_{1}^{\nu}\|}{\delta}\right)\right]<\infty

for
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
-almost every y, for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
.
Then, we have for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
and for
\varepsilon,\delta>0
such that

\overline{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon+\delta\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}^{\mathbb{P}}\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}\left[\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}^{\nu}\sim\nu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-\|X_{1}^{\mathbb{P}}-X_{1}^{\nu}\|}{\delta}\right)\right]\right)\right]\geq 0

the following duality

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{*}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda>0}\bigg\{-\lambda{\varepsilon}-\lambda\delta\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}^{\mathbb{P}}\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}\left[\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}^{\nu}\sim\nu}\left[\exp\left(\tfrac{-r(x,a,X_{1}^{\nu})-\gamma\sup_{b\in A}Q_{\delta}^{*}(X_{1}^{\nu},b)-\lambda\|X_{1}^{\mathbb{P}}-X_{1}^{\nu}\|}{\lambda\delta}\right)\right]\right)\right]\bigg\}.

Proof.

This follows from
Wang et al. (2021, Theorem I)
and from using that
\sup_{z}f(z)=-\inf_{z}-f(z)
.

Wang et al. (2021, Theorem I)
in addition requires that the function
f(z):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is measurable and that every joint distribution on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
with
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
as the first marginal has a regular conditional distribution given the value of the first marginal.
For our setting, the function
f(z)=r(x,a,z)+\gamma\sup_{b\in A}Q_{\delta}^{*}(z,b)
is measurable as it is continuous due to Assumption
2.7
and the continuity of
Q_{\delta}^{*}
as shown in Lemma
6.8
.
By
Klenke (2013, Theorem 8.29)
, real-valued random variables always have a regular conditional distribution.
∎

The optimisation problem over a set of probability measures in the Sinkhorn ball in Proposition
3.1
is transformed into one that only requires maximising over the scalar
\lambda
. The inner expectation is under the measure
\nu
which offers flexibility in sampling.
If one were mainly interested in the Wasserstein-1 ball as the ambiguity set, the following observation offers a direct connection to the Sinkhorn distance.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and assume that the prior measure
\nu
has full support on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
\supp(\nu)=\mathcal{X}
. Then, for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
, we have

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{0}^{*}(x,a)=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,0}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a,X_{1})+\gamma\sup_{a\in A}Q_{0}^{*}(X_{1},a)\right]:=\mathcal{H}_{0}Q_{0}^{*}(x,a)=Q_{0}^{*}(x,a)

Proof.

The full-support condition ensures that the vanishing-entropy limit does not retain a restriction to a proper subset of
\mathcal{X}
. The assertion then follows from
Wang et al. (2021, Appendix EC.4)
and from (
2.11
).
∎

In other words, as
\delta
approaches
0
, the Bellman operator
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
converges to the Bellman operator
\mathcal{H}_{0}
associated with the Wasserstein ball.
In the classical dual formulation of the Wasserstein ball robust Bellman operator, the computation of
\mathcal{H}_{0}Q_{0}^{*}
involves the computation of the so called
\lambda-c
transform
4
4

4

The
\lambda-c
transform of a function
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is given by
f^{\lambda c}(x):=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\{f(y)-\lambda c(x,y)~|~f(y)<\infty\}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, for some distance function

.
on the dual side of the problem (compare
Bartl et al. (2020, Remark 2.1)
), which in most cases for non-finite state spaces
\mathcal{X}
is intractable (
Wang et al. (2021, Remark 3)
).
The use of a small regularisation parameter
\delta
allows us to avoid this issue and instead approximate the solution using the Sinkhorn distance.

Due to the assertion from Proposition
3.1
, our main goal will be to minimise the error between

\sup_{\lambda>0}\bigg\{-\lambda{\varepsilon}-\lambda\delta\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}^{\mathbb{P}}\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}\left[\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}^{\nu}\sim\nu}\left[\exp\left(\tfrac{-r(x,a,X_{1}^{\nu})-\gamma\sup_{b\in A}Q_{\delta}^{*}(X_{1}^{\nu},b)-\lambda\|X_{1}^{\mathbb{P}}-X_{1}^{\nu}\|}{\lambda\delta}\right)\right]\right)\right]\bigg\}

and
Q_{\delta}^{*}(x,a)
with respect to
Q_{\delta}^{*}:\mathcal{X}\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which still is an infinite dimensional intractable optimisation problem due to our setting of continuous states.
Thus, to finally obtain a numerically tractable, finite-dimensional optimisation problem, we parameterise
Q_{\delta}^{*}(x,a)
by fully connected feed-forward neural networks.

3.2
Q
-learning with Neural Networks

To introduce neural networks, we follow closely the presentation in
Neufeld and Sester (2024a)
.
By fully connected feed-forward

\begin{split}\mathbb{R}^{d_{\operatorname{in}}}&\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\operatorname{out}}}\\

(3.1)

where
({A_{i}})_{i=0,\dots,l}
are affine

5

5

This means for all

{A_{i}}({x})={M_{i}}{x}+{b_{i}}

{M_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{i+1}\times h_{i}}

(3.2)

Then, we denote by

It is well-known that the set of neural networks possesses the so-called

, see, e.g.,

Proposition 3.3

(Universal approximation theorem)
.

For any compact set
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\operatorname{in}}}
the set
\mathfrak{N}_{d_{\operatorname{in}},{d_{\operatorname{out}}}}|_{\mathbb{K}}
is dense in
{C}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{R}^{d_{\operatorname{out}}})
with respect to the topology of uniform convergence on

.

Since
\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\rightarrow Q_{\delta}^{*}(x,a)
is a continuous function (see Lemma

), we can approximate

arbitrarily well on compact sets with neural networks.
Our goal will then be to solve the following optimisation problem.

Optimisation Problem 3.4
.

Given some tolerance
\rm TOL>0
, find
Q^{*}_{\rm NN}\in\mathfrak{N}_{d+m,1}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(x,a)-Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(x,a)\right|<\rm TOL

uniformly on
\mathcal{X}\times A
.

As we will show in the subsequent Proposition

of Optimisation Problem
3.4
exist if
\mathcal{X}
is bounded, and they approximate the optimal
Q
function
Q_{\delta}^{*}
arbitrarily well.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
such that
\overline{\varepsilon}\geq 0
as defined in Proposition
3.1
. Moreover, let
\mathcal{X}
be bounded, and let
\rm TOL>0
be some tolerance. Recall that
\gamma
is the discount factor which by Assumption
2.7
(iii) satisfies
0<\gamma<1
.

(i)

A solution
Q^{*}_{\rm NN}\in\mathfrak{N}_{d+m,1}
of Optimisation Problem
3.4
exists for
\delta>0
.

(ii)

Any solution
Q^{*}_{\rm NN}\in\mathfrak{N}_{d+m,1}
of Optimisation Problem
3.4
for
\delta>0
will also satisfy

\sup_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A}|Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(x,a)-Q_{\delta}{{}^{*}}(x,a)|\leq\frac{{\rm TOL}}{1-\gamma}

(iii)

There exists some
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that for all
\delta\in(0,\delta^{\prime})
solutions

of Optimisation Problem
3.4
w.r.t.
\delta
will also satisfy
\sup_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A}|Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(x,a)-Q_{0}{{}^{*}}(x,a)|\leq 2\cdot\frac{{\rm TOL}}{1-\gamma}
.

Proposition
3.5
shows three results regarding solutions to Optimisation Problem
3.4
in the case
\mathcal{X}
is bounded.
Firstly, it shows the existence of a neural network which can approximate a function that will satisfy the condition of Optimisation Problem
3.4
.
Note that this is not simply showing that the neural network can approximate
Q_{\delta}^{*}
, but rather that there is a neural network that can approximate a function such that applying the robust Bellman operator
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
will yield a function that is close to the original function in the sense of the uniform norm for all state-action pairs from the compact set
\mathcal{X}\times A
.
Secondly, we show that if we find a neural network fulfilling the condition of Optimisation Problem
3.4
, then it will also be close to
Q_{\delta}^{*}
justifying the formulation of Optimisation Problem
3.4
.
Thirdly, we can find a
\delta>0
such that if we have a solution to Optimisation Problem
3.4
, it will approximate
Q_{0}^{*}
, which is the optimal
Q
function for the Wasserstein case.

The results rely on Proposition
3.3
which ensures the neural network can approximate the
Q_{\delta}^{*}
arbitrarily well and hence the condition for a bounded
\mathcal{X}
.
However, it is not necessarily the case that a neural network cannot approximate
Q_{\delta}^{*}
for unbounded
\mathcal{X}
as this is an area of ongoing research (see, e.g.,
Neufeld and Schmocker (2024)
).

3.3
The Robust DQN Algorithm

In the following, to emphasise the dependence of neural networks on its parameters, i.e., its weights and biases, we write
Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(\theta;x,a)=Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(x,a)
for a neural network
Q^{*}_{\rm NN}\in\mathfrak{N}_{d+m,1}
evaluated at a state action pair
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
, given the parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, where
\Theta
denotes the Euclidean set of all possible parameters of the neural network, i.e., the possible choices of weights and biases.
Motivated by Proposition
3.1
, our goal of solving Optimisation Problem
3.4
now becomes minimising the following loss function

L(\theta;(x,a)):=\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(\theta;x,a)-Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(\theta;x,a)\right)^{2}

(3.3)

for any state action pair
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
, with respect to
\theta\in\Theta
.
Given a batch of states
x^{B}:=(x_{i})_{i=1,\dots,B}\subset\mathcal{X}
and actions
a^{B}:=(a_{i})_{i=1,\dots,B}\subset A
, we obtain the loss function on the batch as

L^{B}(\theta;(x^{B},a^{B})):=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(\theta;x_{i},a_{i})-Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(\theta;x_{i},a_{i})\right)^{2}

(3.4)

Our setting of continuous state space and finite action space is the same as in
Mnih et al. (2015)
where the authors propose the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm to learn the optimal
Q
function.
We show how the DQN algorithm can be modified to the robust case in Algorithm
1
.
The DQN algorithm uses an experience replay buffer to store transitions
(x_{t},a_{t},r_{t},x_{t+1})
, where
r_{t}=r(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1})
, and samples a batch of transitions to compute targets that are then used to update the neural network approximating the
Q
function.
For the Robust DQN (RDQN) algorithm, we store transitions obtained from interacting with the environment which is assumed to be following the reference distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
.
The targets in the DQN algorithm are also computed using a target network, which is a copy of the neural network approximating the
Q
function.
The target network is updated less frequently than the neural network approximating the
Q
function for better stability as described in
Mnih et al. (2015, Training algorithm for deep Q-networks)
.

The main modification to DQN is to use Proposition
3.1
to calculate the targets
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q^{*}_{\rm NN}
to update
Q^{*}_{\rm NN}
.
From the replay buffer, only the state
x_{t}
, the action
a_{t}
and the next state
x_{t+1}
are needed to calculate the modified target.
In particular, the outer expectation in Proposition
3.1
will be approximated by the single unbiased sample of the next state
x_{t+1}
and the action
a_{t}
.
The inner expectation can be approximated by sampling multiple times from the distribution
\nu
.

Remark 3.6

(Reward evaluation in the RDQN target)
.

The reward appearing in Proposition
3.1

r(x,a,X_{1}^{\nu})

X_{1}^{\nu}

Accordingly, the RDQN target evaluates the reward function

the expectation under
\nu

The algorithm therefore requires access to the reward

Mnih et al., 2015
)
.
A closed-form expression for

provided these evaluations can be computed.
In the dual expression, the realised next state

\|x_{1}^{\mathbb{P}}-X_{1}^{\nu}\|

This modification leads then to the numerical routine summarised in Algorithm

We omit the details of the agent interacting with the environment, the experience replay buffer, the updating of the target network, and refer the reader to
Mnih et al. (2015, Algorithm 1)

Remark 3.7

(One-sample approximation of the dual objective)
.

Fix a bounded continuous critic
Q
, and write

F_{Q}(x,a,y):=r(x,a,y)+\gamma\sup_{b\in A}Q(y,b).

With the inner expectation evaluated exactly, define

h_{Q}(\lambda,z;x,a):=-\lambda\varepsilon-\lambda\delta\log\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\nu}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{F_{Q}(x,a,Y)}{\lambda\delta}-\frac{\|z-Y\|}{\delta}\right)\right].

Under the conditions of Proposition
3.1
,

(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q)(x,a)=\sup_{\lambda>0}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}[h_{Q}(\lambda,Z;x,a)].

Algorithm
1
approximates this outer expectation
by one nominal next-state sample and maximises the resulting
Monte Carlo estimate. For each fixed
\lambda
, this estimate
is unbiased, but maximising it generally introduces an upward bias.
Indeed, with exact inner integration and per-transition
maximisation, and without transition exclusions, the expected
target is

(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}Q)(x,a):=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}\left[\sup_{\lambda>0}h_{Q}(\lambda,Z;x,a)\right]\geq(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q)(x,a).

When both values are finite, define this bias by

b_{Q}(x,a):=(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}Q)(x,a)-(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q)(x,a).

Averaging more independently maximised one-sample targets,
increasing the number of candidates from
\nu
, or improving
the per-transition optimisation does not generally remove this bias.
It vanishes, for example, when a common multiplier maximises

h_{Q}(\lambda,Z;x,a)
for
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
-almost every
Z
.

To approximate the intended expected objective, one can instead
optimise a common multiplier over several nominal next-state
samples, or learn a multiplier for each state–action pair through
successive stochastic updates using fresh nominal samples.
The same issue arises in the distributionally robust

Q
-learning algorithm of
Liu et al. (2022)
, which
approximates the dual value with a multilevel Monte Carlo scheme
built from several next-state samples drawn from a simulator for
the same state–action pair at each update.
We use a single nominal next-state sample because this keeps the
algorithm applicable to settings in which only the realised
transition is observed, so that counterfactual next states for the
same state–action pair cannot be generated.
Appendix
B
estimates
b_{Q}
in the S&P 500
experiment of Section
4.2
and finds it small relative
to the robust penalty, in particular for long positions.
For the present algorithm, the fitted-iteration bounds in
Section
3.3.5
must account for

b_{Q}
in addition to the numerical errors.
The per-transition supremum need not be finite; feasibility
checks and the bias from excluding transitions are discussed
in Section
3.3.2
.

3.3.1
Sampling from
\nu

The inner expectation in Proposition
3.1
is taken
under the chosen measure
\nu
. Throughout this subsection, all densities are defined with respect to a common
\sigma
-finite dominating measure
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
. In particular,

p_{\nu}:={\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\nu/{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\rho
, and
p_{\lambda}(\,\cdot\mid x^{\mathbb{P}})
denotes the density with respect to
\rho
of the associated conditional law given the nominal next state
x^{\mathbb{P}}
.
For fixed
(x,a)
and
\lambda>0
, by
Wang et al. (2021)
, this density is

{p_{\lambda}(y\mid x^{\mathbb{P}}):=\frac{p_{\nu}(y)e^{-\|x^{\mathbb{P}}-y\|/\delta}e^{Z(y)/(\lambda\delta)}}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}p_{\nu}(z)e^{-\|x^{\mathbb{P}}-z\|/\delta}e^{Z(z)/(\lambda\delta)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\rho(z)},}\qquad\text{ with }Z(y):=-r(x,a,y)-\gamma\sup_{b\in A}Q_{\delta}^{*}(y,b).

For small
\delta
, the transport factor

e^{-\|x^{\mathbb{P}}-y\|/\delta}
concentrates near
x^{\mathbb{P}}
.
Direct sampling from
\nu
may miss this region, while large
values of
Z
can make distant candidates important as well.

We therefore use importance sampling. For transition
i
, let

q_{i}
be a proposal density satisfying
q_{i}>0

\nu
-almost
everywhere. Candidates
x^{\nu}_{i,j}
receive weights

w_{i,j}:=\frac{p_{\nu}(x^{\nu}_{i,j})}{q_{i}(x^{\nu}_{i,j})},

(3.5)

and expectations under
\nu
are approximated by

\widehat{\mathbb{E}}^{(i)}_{\nu}[f]:=\frac{1}{N_{\nu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}w_{i,j}f(x^{\nu}_{i,j}).

(3.6)

This estimator is unbiased for random draws from
q_{i}
,
provided
f
is integrable, and reduces to the usual sample
average when
q_{i}=p_{\nu}
. The deterministic candidate sets
described below instead give numerical integration rules.

In the financial applications, we combine three groups of
candidates, using the mixture weights of

Veach and Guibas (1995)
:

q_{i}=\frac{N_{\rm fix}}{N_{\nu}}q^{\rm fix}+\frac{N_{\rm loc}}{N_{\nu}}q_{i}^{\rm loc}+\frac{N_{\rm far}}{N_{\nu}}q^{\rm far},\qquad N_{\nu}=N_{\rm fix}+N_{\rm loc}+N_{\rm far}.

(3.7)

Here
d
denotes the dimension of the variable sampled under
\nu
.

Fixed candidates
cover the distribution
\nu
and are
reused across transitions. For
d=1
, we use the quantiles

F_{\nu}^{-1}(k/(N_{\rm fix}+1))
,
k=1,\ldots,N_{\rm fix}
.
For
d>1
, we transform a scrambled Sobol’ sequence

Owen (1998)
using the elliptical representation
of
\nu
, pairing points with their reflections about its
location. The corresponding density in
(
3.7
) is
q^{\rm fix}=p_{\nu}
.

Local candidates
cover the region near
x_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}
.
We use

x^{\nu}_{i,j}=x_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}+\delta R_{j}\Theta_{j},\qquad R_{j}\sim\Gamma(d,1),

where
\Theta_{j}
is independent of
R_{j}
and uniform on the
unit sphere
S^{d-1}
. This gives the density

q_{i}^{\rm loc}(y)=\frac{e^{-\|y-x_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}\|/\delta}}{\Gamma(d)\delta^{d}|S^{d-1}|}.

(3.8)

Thus, the proposal matches the normalised transport factor
and has mean distance
d\delta
from
x_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}
.
We generate these candidates using scrambled Sobol’ points
with paired opposite directions.

Tail candidates
are added when extreme returns can
strongly affect the objective. For example, the log return
of a fully short position can become very negative as the
asset price approaches a doubling. To cover such outcomes,
we divide the tails into intervals
B_{k}
using a geometric
spacing of tail probabilities. Each interval has mass

m_{k}=\nu(B_{k})
and is represented by its conditional mean.
The corresponding mixture density is

q^{\rm far}(y)=\frac{1}{N_{\rm far}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm far}}\frac{p_{\nu}(y)\mathbf{1}_{B_{k}}(y)}{m_{k}}.

Using conditional means rather than random draws introduces
a numerical integration error.

In the financial experiments,
\nu
is a Student-
t

distribution restricted to a bounded box and fitted to the
training data.
For
d=1
, we fix the degrees of freedom and choose the
location and scale so that the truncated distribution
matches the sample mean and variance.
For
d>1
, we fit the location, scale, correlation and degrees
of freedom by maximum likelihood, using a common location
parameter for all assets.

The truncation bounds are chosen so that portfolio gross
returns remain bounded away from zero, ensuring bounded
log rewards as required by Assumption
2.7
(i).

3.3.2
Feasibility of the sampled dual problem

Proposition
3.1
requires

\bar{\varepsilon}\geq 0
. Its transition-wise estimate is

\bar{\varepsilon}_{i}=\varepsilon+\delta\log\left(\frac{1}{N_{\nu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}w_{i,j}e^{-\|x_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}-x^{\nu}_{i,j}\|/\delta}\right).

This estimate can be negative even when the condition in
the proposition holds, because we use one nominal next
state and finitely many candidates. If

\bar{\varepsilon}_{i}<0
, the sampled dual objective tends
to
+\infty
as
\lambda\to\infty
and cannot provide a
finite target. This concerns the sampled problem, rather
than the validity of the exact duality.

We use two checks. Before training, we evaluate

\bar{\varepsilon}_{i}
on a large sample of nominal transitions
and proceed only if more than
99.5\%
are positive.
During training, we exclude transitions with

\bar{\varepsilon}_{i}\leq 0
and skip the critic update if none
remain.

Excluding transitions introduces selection bias, particularly
if adverse outcomes are more often excluded. The preliminary
check limits their overall frequency but does not, by itself,
bound the resulting bias.

3.3.3
Optimisation of
\lambda

For fixed
(x,a)
, define

S(z,\lambda):=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\nu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{Z(Y)-\lambda\|z-Y\|}{\lambda\delta}\right)\right],\qquad\frac{dP_{z,\lambda}}{d\nu}(y):=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{Z(y)-\lambda\|z-y\|}{\lambda\delta}\right)}{S(z,\lambda)}.

The dual objective and its derivatives are

\displaystyle g(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\varepsilon-\lambda\delta\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}[\log S(X,\lambda)],\qquad\lambda>0,

(3.9)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon-\delta\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}[\log S(X,\lambda)]+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P_{X,\lambda}}[Z(Y)]\right],

(3.10)

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}\delta}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}\left[\operatorname{Var}_{Y\sim P_{X,\lambda}}(Z(Y))\right]\leq 0.

(3.11)

Hence
g
is concave in
\lambda
. The sampled derivatives
use the same exponential weights as the objective and
require little additional computation.

In Algorithm
1
, we optimise the sampled
objective for each retained transition using safeguarded
Newton iterations, with a stopping tolerance and an
iteration limit. We parameterise
\lambda=e^{u}
to ensure
positivity and store the resulting
u
with the transition
for use as the next starting value. Concavity in
\lambda

does not imply concavity in
u
.

3.3.4
Stable evaluation of the exponential term

Small
\delta
or
\lambda
can produce large exponents.
For each transition, we therefore evaluate the sampled
logarithm using

\log\left(\frac{1}{N_{\nu}}\sum_{j}e^{E_{i,j}}\right)=C_{i}+\log\left(\frac{1}{N_{\nu}}\sum_{j}e^{E_{i,j}-C_{i}}\right),\qquad C_{i}=\max_{j}E_{i,j},

where

E_{i,j}=\frac{Z(x^{\nu}_{i,j})}{\lambda\delta}-\frac{\|x_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}-x^{\nu}_{i,j}\|}{\delta}+\log w_{i,j}.

The shifted exponents are non-positive, preventing overflow.

3.3.5
Approximation error bounds

RDQN approximates the Bellman update using a neural network,
sampled targets and numerical optimisation. To describe how
one-step errors accumulate, consider a fitted iteration

Q_{k+1}=\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{k}+e_{k},\qquad\left\lVert e_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\eta_{k}.

(3.12)

Theorem 3.8

(Approximate robust fitted
Q
-iteration)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
is a
\gamma
-contraction with fixed
point
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
, and

\left\lVert Q_{k+1}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\eta_{k},\qquad k=0,1,\ldots.

(3.13)

Then, for every
K\geq 1
,

\left\lVert Q_{K}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{K}\left\lVert Q_{0}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma^{K-1-k}\eta_{k}.

(3.14)

If
\eta_{k}\leq\eta
for all
k
, then

\left\lVert Q_{K}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{K}\left\lVert Q_{0}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{1-\gamma^{K}}{1-\gamma}\eta\leq\gamma^{K}\left\lVert Q_{0}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{\eta}{1-\gamma}.

(3.15)

When separate uniform error bounds are available, we may use

\eta_{k}\leq\eta_{\mathrm{fit},k}+\eta_{\mathrm{MC},k}+\eta_{\lambda,k}+\eta_{\mathrm{opt},k}+\eta_{\mathrm{struct},k}.

(3.16)

These terms account for function approximation, target
estimation, the numerical dual solve, and incomplete
regression optimisation, respectively.
Here
\eta_{\mathrm{MC},k}
must include both sampling and
numerical integration errors, as well as bias from excluding
transitions.
The separate term
\eta_{\mathrm{struct},k}
bounds the
operator discrepancy in Remark
3.7

for the critic used to construct the targets; it is not a
Monte Carlo or numerical optimisation error and need not
vanish as those errors decrease.

Corollary 3.9

(RDQN error decomposition)
.

Suppose (
3.13
) and
(
3.16
) hold, with

\eta_{j,k}\leq\eta_{j}
for all
k
and

j\in\{\mathrm{fit},\mathrm{MC},\lambda,\mathrm{opt},\mathrm{struct}\}
.
Then, for every
K\geq 1
,

\left\lVert Q_{K}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{K}\left\lVert Q_{0}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{fit}}+\eta_{\mathrm{MC}}+\eta_{\lambda}+\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}+\eta_{\mathrm{struct}}}{1-\gamma}.

(3.17)

These bounds describe the effect of controlled update errors;
they do not establish that RDQN satisfies the required
uniform bounds.

3.4
Extensions to Compact Continuous Action Sets

We consider two extensions of the robust Bellman operator to a nonempty
compact action set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
: action discretisation and
entropy-regularised action selection.
Throughout this subsection, Assumptions
2.4
,
2.7

and
2.5
are imposed on this enlarged action set.
The ambiguity correspondence is therefore nonempty, weakly compact and
continuous, by the arguments of Lemmas
6.4
,

6.6
and
6.7
.
Write
\mathcal{Q}:=C_{b}(\mathcal{X}\times A)
, equipped with the supremum norm,
and take all critics in this complete space.
Compactness of
A
makes
M_{Q}(x):=\max_{b\in A}Q(x,b)
continuous
by Berge’s maximum theorem.
The finite-grid and soft continuation values defined below are also
continuous and bounded.
Consequently, the continuity argument of Lemma
6.8

applies to each corresponding robust operator.
Non-expansiveness of the continuation maps makes these operators

\gamma
-contractions on
\mathcal{Q}
, so their fixed points exist uniquely.

These are guarantees for the stated operators.
Numerical implementations additionally require controlled sampling,
regression and optimisation errors, including the multiplier
discrepancy in Remark
3.7
.

3.4.1
Action discretisation with an explicit error bound

Let
A_{h}\subset A
be a finite
h
-net, meaning that for every
a\in A
there exists
a_{h}\in A_{h}
such that

\left\lVert a-a_{h}\right\rVert\leq h.

For
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, define for all
x\in\mathcal{X}

M_{Q}(x):=\sup_{b\in A}Q(x,b),\qquad M_{h,Q}(x):=\max_{b\in A_{h}}Q(x,b).

The discretised robust Bellman operator is

\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\mapsto(\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}Q)(x,a):=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a,X_{1})+\gamma M_{h,Q}(X_{1})\right].

(3.18)

Here only the continuation maximisation is discretised.
Keeping the current action
a
in
A
places both operators on the
same function space; restricting their grid fixed point to

\mathcal{X}\times A_{h}
gives the optimal critic for the grid-constrained problem.
Neither
A_{h}
nor this construction alters the state-transition
ambiguity sets.

Lemma 3.10

(Action-grid approximation of the maximisation)
.

Assume that
Q
is Lipschitz in the action variable with constant
L_{A}
, i.e.,

|Q(x,a)-Q(x,a^{\prime})|\leq L_{A}\left\lVert a-a^{\prime}\right\rVert\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X},\;a,a^{\prime}\in A.

Then, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

0\leq M_{Q}(x)-M_{h,Q}(x)\leq L_{A}h.

(3.19)

Consequently,

\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q-\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}Q\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma L_{A}h.

(3.20)

Proof.

Since
A_{h}\subset A
, we have
M_{h,Q}(x)\leq M_{Q}(x)
. For the reverse approximation bound, fix
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\rho>0
. Choose
b_{\rho}\in A
such that
Q(x,b_{\rho})\geq M_{Q}(x)-\rho
. Since
A_{h}
is an
h
-net, there exists
b_{h}\in A_{h}
with
\left\lVert b_{\rho}-b_{h}\right\rVert\leq h
. By Lipschitz continuity,

Q(x,b_{h})\geq Q(x,b_{\rho})-L_{A}h\geq M_{Q}(x)-\rho-L_{A}h.

Because
M_{h,Q}(x)\geq Q(x,b_{h})
, we have
M_{Q}(x)-M_{h,Q}(x)\leq\rho+L_{A}h
. Letting
\rho\downarrow 0
proves (
3.19
).

For (
3.20
), fix
(x,a)
. For any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
, the expectations defining
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q
and
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}Q
differ only in the continuation value. By (
3.19
), this difference is between
0
and
\gamma L_{A}h
. Taking infima over the same ambiguity set preserves this upper bound, and taking the supremum over
(x,a)
gives (
3.20
).
∎

Theorem 3.11

(Continuous actions by discretisation)
.

Let
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
and
Q_{\delta,h}^{\ast}
be the unique fixed points
of
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}
, respectively.
Assume that
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
is uniformly Lipschitz in the action variable
with constant
L_{A}
.
Then
Q_{\delta,h}^{\ast}\leq Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
pointwise, and

\left\lVert Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-Q_{\delta,h}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma L_{A}h}{1-\gamma}.

(3.21)

Proof.

Using the fixed-point identities and Lemma
3.10

applied to
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-Q_{\delta,h}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}Q_{\delta,h}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma L_{A}h+\gamma\left\lVert Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-Q_{\delta,h}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

Rearranging gives (
3.21
).
Since
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\leq\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}=Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}
is monotone, its iterates
started from
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
remain below
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
.
Their uniform limit is
Q_{\delta,h}^{\ast}
, proving the pointwise order.
∎

Corollary 3.12

(Approximate fitted iteration with action discretisation)
.

Suppose
Q_{k}\in\mathcal{Q}
and the discretised fitted updates satisfy

\left\lVert Q_{k+1}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta,h}Q_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\eta_{k}.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.11
,

\left\lVert Q_{K}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{K}\left\lVert Q_{0}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma^{K-1-k}\eta_{k}+\frac{1-\gamma^{K}}{1-\gamma}\gamma L_{A}h.

(3.22)

Proof.

Let
d_{k}:=\left\lVert Q_{k}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}
.
The contraction property and the grid error at
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
give

d_{k+1}\leq\eta_{k}+\gamma d_{k}+\gamma L_{A}h.

Iterating this inequality gives (
3.22
).
∎

The constant
L_{A}
is an additional regularity assumption on the
optimal critic; continuity alone does not give a Lipschitz rate.
More generally, all the grid bounds hold with
L_{A}h
replaced by the
uniform action modulus

\omega_{\ast}(h):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{X},\ a,b\in A\\
\left\lVert a-b\right\rVert\leq h\end{subarray}}|Q_{\delta}^{\ast}(x,a)-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}(x,b)|.

Thus convergence follows whenever
\omega_{\ast}(h)\to 0
, in particular
when
\mathcal{X}
is compact.
Action grids are practical in low dimensions, but their size can grow
rapidly with the action dimension.
The bound on
\eta_{k}
remains an assumption on the numerical update;
discretisation alone does not provide replay coverage or uniform
regression accuracy.

3.4.2
Entropy-regularised continuous actions

For higher-dimensional action spaces, a smoother alternative is the
soft action value

V_{\tau,Q}(x):=\tau\log\int_{A}\exp\left(\frac{Q(x,b)}{\tau}\right)\mu_{A}({\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}b),\qquad\tau>0,

(3.23)

where
\mu_{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(A)
is a fixed probability reference
measure with full support on
A
.
The action temperature
\tau
is distinct from the transport
regularisation parameter
\delta
.
Define the entropy-regularised robust Bellman operator

(\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}Q)(x,a):=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x,a,X_{1})+\gamma V_{\tau,Q}(X_{1})\right].

(3.24)

Lemma 3.13

(Soft maximum is non-expansive)
.

For any
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathcal{Q}
,

\left\lVert V_{\tau,Q_{1}}-V_{\tau,Q_{2}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

(3.25)

Proof.

Let
c:=\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}
. Then
Q_{1}(x,b)\leq Q_{2}(x,b)+c
for all
(x,b)
, and hence

\int_{A}\exp\left(\frac{Q_{1}(x,b)}{\tau}\right)\mu_{A}({\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}b)\leq e^{c/\tau}\int_{A}\exp\left(\frac{Q_{2}(x,b)}{\tau}\right)\mu_{A}({\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}b).

Taking logarithms and multiplying by
\tau
gives
V_{\tau,Q_{1}}(x)\leq V_{\tau,Q_{2}}(x)+c
. Interchanging
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
proves (
3.25
).
∎

Lemma 3.14

(Contraction of the entropy-regularised robust operator)
.

For all
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathcal{Q}
,

\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}Q_{1}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}Q_{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

(3.26)

Proof.

Lemma
3.13
gives

|V_{\tau,Q_{1}}(x)-V_{\tau,Q_{2}}(x)|\leq\left\lVert Q_{1}-Q_{2}\right\rVert_{\infty},

and the same infimum inequality over the common ambiguity set
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
yields the stated
\gamma
-contraction bound.
∎

Theorem 3.15

(Convergence of entropy-regularised robust
Q
-iteration)
.

For
\tau>0
and any
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}
, define

Q_{k+1}=\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}Q_{k}.

Then
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}
has a unique fixed point

Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{Q}
, and
Q_{k}
converges uniformly to it.
Moreover,

\left\lVert Q_{k}-Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{k}\left\lVert Q_{0}-Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.14
,
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}
is a
\gamma
-contraction on the complete space
\mathcal{Q}
. Banach’s fixed-point theorem gives existence and uniqueness of
Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}
, convergence of the Picard iteration, and the stated geometric error bound.
∎

Proposition 3.16

(Soft-to-hard approximation)
.

For
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, the soft values converge pointwise:

V_{\tau,Q}(x)\longrightarrow\sup_{b\in A}Q(x,b)\qquad\text{as }\tau\downarrow 0

for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
For
\rho>0
, define

\omega_{Q}(\rho):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{X},\ a,b\in A\\
\left\lVert a-b\right\rVert\leq\rho\end{subarray}}|Q(x,a)-Q(x,b)|,\qquad m_{A}(\rho):=\inf_{a\in A}\mu_{A}(B_{\rho}(a)\cap A).

Compactness and full support imply
m_{A}(\rho)>0
.
The following quantitative bound holds for every
x
and
\rho>0
:

0\leq M_{Q}(x)-V_{\tau,Q}(x)\leq\omega_{Q}(\rho)+\tau\log\frac{1}{m_{A}(\rho)}.

(3.27)

In particular, if
\omega_{Q}(\rho)\to 0
as
\rho\downarrow 0
,
the soft-to-hard convergence is uniform in
x
.
Set

\beta_{\tau}(Q):=\inf_{\rho>0}\left\{\omega_{Q}(\rho)+\tau\log\frac{1}{m_{A}(\rho)}\right\}.

For the fixed points, we have
Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}\leq Q_{\delta}^{\ast}

pointwise and

\left\lVert Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma\beta_{\tau}(Q_{\delta}^{\ast})}{1-\gamma}.

(3.28)

Hence
Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}\to Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
uniformly as

\tau\downarrow 0
whenever the optimal critic is uniformly continuous
in the action variable, uniformly over states.

Proof.

Let
M_{Q}(x):=\sup_{b\in A}Q(x,b)
. Since
\mu_{A}
is a probability measure,

V_{\tau,Q}(x)=\tau\log\int_{A}\exp\left(\frac{Q(x,b)}{\tau}\right)\mu_{A}({\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}b)\leq M_{Q}(x).

For the lower bound, fix
x
and
\xi>0
. By continuity and compactness, choose
a_{x}\in A
with
Q(x,a_{x})=M_{Q}(x)
. There is a radius
\rho_{x}>0
such that
Q(x,b)\geq M_{Q}(x)-\xi
for all
b\in B_{\rho_{x}}(a_{x})\cap A
. Since
\mu_{A}
has full support,

\displaystyle V_{\tau,Q}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\tau\log\left[\exp\left(\frac{M_{Q}(x)-\xi}{\tau}\right)\mu_{A}(B_{\rho_{x}}(a_{x})\cap A)\right]

\displaystyle=M_{Q}(x)-\xi+\tau\log\mu_{A}(B_{\rho_{x}}(a_{x})\cap A).

Letting
\tau\downarrow 0
and then
\xi\downarrow 0
gives pointwise convergence.

To see that
m_{A}(\rho)>0
, cover
A
by finitely many balls

B_{\rho/2}(a_{j})
with centres in
A
.
For any
a\in A
, some
a_{j}
satisfies
\left\lVert a-a_{j}\right\rVert<\rho/2
,
so
B_{\rho/2}(a_{j})\cap A\subseteq B_{\rho}(a)\cap A
.
Full support makes the minimum of the finitely many masses

\mu_{A}(B_{\rho/2}(a_{j})\cap A)
positive.
The same lower-bound argument around a maximiser of
Q(x,\cdot)

now gives (
3.27
).
If
\omega_{Q}(\rho)\to 0
, first choose
\rho
to make this modulus
small and then let
\tau\downarrow 0
, proving uniform convergence.

Applying (
3.27
) at
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
gives

\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma\beta_{\tau}(Q_{\delta}^{\ast}).

The fixed-point identities and contraction of
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}

therefore imply

\left\lVert Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma\beta_{\tau}(Q_{\delta}^{\ast})+\gamma\left\lVert Q_{\delta}^{\ast}-Q_{\delta,\tau}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty},

which proves (
3.28
).
Since
V_{\tau,Q}\leq M_{Q}
and the soft operator is monotone,
iterating it from
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
proves the pointwise order as in
Theorem
3.11
.
∎

For approximate soft updates satisfying

\left\lVert Q_{k+1}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta,\tau}Q_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\eta_{k}
,
the same contraction argument yields

\begin{split}\left\lVert Q_{K}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq{}&\gamma^{K}\left\lVert Q_{0}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma^{K-1-k}\eta_{k}\\
&+\frac{1-\gamma^{K}}{1-\gamma}\gamma\beta_{\tau}(Q_{\delta}^{\ast}).\end{split}

(3.29)

Indeed, the one-step recursion is

d_{k+1}\leq\eta_{k}+\gamma d_{k}+\gamma\beta_{\tau}(Q_{\delta}^{\ast})

for
d_{k}:=\left\lVert Q_{k}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}
.
This separates action regularisation from the numerical update error.

The soft-value bound also quantifies the role of the action reference measure.
For example, if
m_{A}(\rho)\geq c_{A}\rho^{p}
for

0<\rho\leq\rho_{0}
, with
c_{A},p>0
, and
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
is
uniformly
L_{A}
-Lipschitz in actions, then

\beta_{\tau}(Q_{\delta}^{\ast})\leq L_{A}\rho+\tau\log\frac{1}{c_{A}\rho^{p}}.

Choosing
\rho=\tau
for sufficiently small
\tau
gives
an
O(\tau\log(1/\tau))
soft-value error.
Full support alone does not give this particular rate.

For implementation with a stochastic actor, the relevant entropy
is relative to
\mu_{A}
, not necessarily to Lebesgue measure.
The entropy variational formula is

V_{\tau,Q}(x)=\sup_{\pi\ll\mu_{A}}\left\{\int_{A}Q(x,a)\pi({\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}a)-\tau H(\pi\mid\mu_{A})\right\}.

(3.30)

Its maximiser is the Gibbs action law

\frac{{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi_{\tau,Q}(\cdot\mid x)}{{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mu_{A}}(a)=\exp\left(\frac{Q(x,a)-V_{\tau,Q}(x)}{\tau}\right).

For an actor
\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x)\ll\mu_{A}
of finite relative
entropy, write
p_{\phi}(a\mid x):={\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x)/{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mu_{A}(a)
.
The implementable actor value is

\mathcal{V}_{\tau,Q}^{\phi}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x)}[Q(x,a)-\tau\log p_{\phi}(a\mid x)].

Substituting the Gibbs density in the entropy chain rule gives the
exact actor-gap identity

V_{\tau,Q}(x)-\mathcal{V}_{\tau,Q}^{\phi}(x)=\tau H(\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x)\mid\pi_{\tau,Q}(\cdot\mid x))\geq 0.

(3.31)

This proves (
3.30
) and shows that a
uniform actor KL bound gives an explicit continuation-value error.
Replacing
V_{\tau,Q}
by this actor value changes the robust critic
target by at most

\gamma\tau\sup_{x}H(\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x)\mid\pi_{\tau,Q}(\cdot\mid x))
,
before sampling and regression errors.
If densities are instead written relative to Lebesgue measure and

\mu_{A}
has density
p_{A}
, the logarithm is

\log(p_{\phi}^{\mathrm{Leb}}/p_{A})
.
Omitting
p_{A}
generally changes the regularised objective.

A deterministic actor cannot implement this variational expression
when
\mu_{A}
is non-atomic, because its point-mass action law has
infinite relative entropy.
It may instead approximate the hard maximum, provided its uniform
action-suboptimality gap is controlled.
The deterministic RTD3 continuation in Section
3.5

is not the soft operator (
3.24
); an entropy bonus on
portfolio weights is also distinct from
H(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\mid\mu_{A})
.
The contraction results do not establish convergence of either
actor’s gradient updates or eliminate the sampled-multiplier
discrepancy.

3.4.3
From critic accuracy to robust policy performance

The following bound concerns the original, unregularised robust
return and makes the action-approximation errors relevant to
decision quality.
For a measurable stationary policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to A
, let
V_{\delta}^{\pi}

be its worst-case discounted value under the same state–action
rectangular ambiguity sets as in (
2.6
).

Proposition 3.17

(Robust performance of an approximate greedy policy)
.

Let
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
satisfy

\left\lVert Q-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq e
.
If a measurable policy
\pi
satisfies

M_{Q}(x)-Q(x,\pi(x))\leq\xi\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X},

then

0\leq V_{\delta}(x)-V_{\delta}^{\pi}(x)\leq\frac{2e+\xi}{1-\gamma}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

(3.32)

Alternatively, if
\pi_{h}(x)\in\argmax_{a\in A_{h}}Q(x,a)
and

Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
is
L_{A}
-Lipschitz in actions, then

0\leq V_{\delta}(x)-V_{\delta}^{\pi_{h}}(x)\leq\frac{2e+L_{A}h}{1-\gamma}.

(3.33)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{\pi}
be the robust policy-evaluation operator,

(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{\pi}v)(x):=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,\pi(x)))}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[r(x,\pi(x),X_{1})+\gamma v(X_{1})].

It is a
\gamma
-contraction, and

V_{\delta}^{\pi}=\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{\pi}V_{\delta}^{\pi}
.
Since
V_{\delta}=M_{Q_{\delta}^{\ast}}
, critic accuracy and the assumed
action gap give

0\leq V_{\delta}(x)-(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{\pi}V_{\delta})(x)=M_{Q_{\delta}^{\ast}}(x)-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}(x,\pi(x))\leq 2e+\xi.

Consequently,

\left\lVert V_{\delta}-V_{\delta}^{\pi}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2e+\xi+\gamma\left\lVert V_{\delta}-V_{\delta}^{\pi}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

Rearranging and using optimality of
V_{\delta}
proves
(
3.32
).
For
\pi_{h}
, the grid bound at
Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
gives

\displaystyle M_{Q_{\delta}^{\ast}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{a\in A_{h}}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}(x,a)+L_{A}h

\displaystyle\leq Q(x,\pi_{h}(x))+e+L_{A}h

\displaystyle\leq Q_{\delta}^{\ast}(x,\pi_{h}(x))+2e+L_{A}h.

The same contraction argument proves
(
3.33
).
∎

The critic-error bounds in (
3.22
),
(
3.28
) and
(
3.29
) can therefore be substituted
for
e
to obtain robust decision-quality guarantees for grid-greedy
or hard-greedy policies.
For a learned critic,
e
must also include training and structural
operator errors.
These conclusions are conditional on the stated uniform bounds;
they do not assert global convergence of RDQN or RTD3.

3.5
The Robust TD3 Algorithm

The RDQN algorithm of Section
3.3
evaluates the supremum of the critic over the action space which is feasible when the neural network outputs all values of the critic for a finite action space.
For the portfolio optimisation problem we consider in Section
4.3
, it is a continuous action space and hence this approach is not feasible.
Therefore, we also give an actor-critic variant, which we call Robust TD3 (RTD3), obtained by applying the same modified target to the Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic policy gradient algorithm (TD3) of
Fujimoto et al. (2018)
.

TD3 maintains a deterministic actor
\pi_{\rm NN}(\phi;\cdot)
and two critics
Q_{\rm NN}(\theta_{1};\cdot,\cdot)
,
Q_{\rm NN}(\theta_{2};\cdot,\cdot)
, each with a target network, and forms its target from the stored reward and the smaller of the two target critics evaluated at the target actor’s action at the realised next state.
The actor is trained to maximise the critic’s value at the next state and hence it approximates the supremum over the action space.
As in the discrete-action case, the key change we make is to the target.
Algorithm
2
replaces the two lines of
Fujimoto et al. (2018, Algorithm 1)
that compute the smoothed target action
\tilde{a}
and the target
y
.

The hard maximum
\sup_{b\in A}Q_{\delta}^{*}(x^{\nu},b)
in Proposition
3.1
is not available for a continuous
A
, and is replaced by the continuation value that the current target policy induces,

x^{\nu}\mapsto\min_{k\in\{1,2\}}Q_{\rm NN}\left(\theta_{\text{target},k};x^{\nu},\pi_{\rm NN}(\phi_{\text{target}};x^{\nu})\right),

(3.34)

evaluated at each of the
N_{\nu}
candidates.
Everything else in the target, the candidate construction of Section
3.3.1
, the feasibility mask of Section
3.3.2
and the dual solve of Section
3.3.3
, is shared with Algorithm
1
without modification. Beyond the target, our implementation differs from
Fujimoto et al. (2018)
in the following respects.

Remark 3.18

(What operator RTD3 approximates)
.

The results of Sections
2
and
3
concern the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
built with the exact maximum over
A
, whose fixed point is the robust optimal
Q
function
Q_{\delta}^{*}
.
Substituting (
3.34
) changes the object being computed: with the continuation value supplied by a fixed policy, the corresponding operator is one of robust policy
evaluation
, whose fixed point is the worst-case value of that policy rather than
Q_{\delta}^{*}
.
The actor is then improved against that critic in the usual deterministic policy gradient manner, so RTD3 is a robust policy iteration scheme in which neither half is solved exactly before the other is updated.

The actor carries an entropy bonus.

The actor loss is the usual deterministic policy gradient objective augmented by
\beta H(w)
, where
w
is the vector of portfolio weights the actor outputs and
H
is the Shannon entropy, in the spirit of the entropy regularisation used in policy gradient methods
(
Mnih et al., 2016
)
.
The entropy bonus encourages exploration and discourages the actor from collapsing to a single asset, which is important in a portfolio context.
TD3 has no such term.

The exploration noise is autocorrelated and applied on the simplex.

Rather than the independent Gaussian perturbations of
Fujimoto et al. (2018)
, we add an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck perturbation with autocorrelation
\rho
, decaying in magnitude over training, and project the perturbed weights back onto the simplex.
Using independent noise would create excessive turnover which comes at a cost, so an autocorrelated perturbation achieves the same exploration reach at a materially lower cost in reward.

3.6
Worst Case Distribution and
\delta

Note that as we lower
\delta
, the Sinkhorn distance approaches the Wasserstein distance but the transport plan
\pi
that realises the Sinkhorn distance
W_{\delta}
becomes more sparse and hence the worst case distribution tends to become more discrete in nature (see e.g.
Wang et al. (2021, Remark 4)
).
Therefore, even if the reference distribution is a smooth and continuous distribution, the worst case distribution may not be.
This may or may not be desirable depending on the specific application.

The regularisation term in the Sinkhorn distance allows us to control the smoothness of the worst case distribution by adjusting the parameter
\delta
.
Our definition using
\nu
allows us to choose a prior, whereby if we increase
\delta
, we can make the worst case distribution more similar to
\nu
.

We illustrate this with a specially designed environment where we have an action space with a single action value and a continuous state space in the closed interval
[0,1]
.
From the equivalence in Equation (
2.10
) and since there is only a single action, the
Q
function
Q_{\delta}(x,a)
in this case is equivalent to the value function
V_{\delta}(x)
.

If we choose the reward function to be
r(x_{0},a,x_{1})=1\hskip-3.0pt\mathrm{l}_{\{x_{1}\leq x_{0}\}}
and set the discount factor
\gamma
close to zero, then from Equation (
2.6
), we have

\begin{split}V_{\delta}(x)&=\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{0},a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[r(x_{0},a,X_{1})+\gamma V_{\delta}(X_{1})\bigg]\\
&\approx\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{0},a)\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[1\hskip-3.0pt\mathrm{l}_{\{X_{1}\leq x_{0}\}}\right]\\

(3.35)

which is the cumulative distribution function of the worst case distribution.
In this particular case, the worst case distribution would be one leading to high values of
X

\delta
on the smoothness of the worst case distribution.

With a reference distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}=\text{Beta}(2,2)

\delta
pushes all distributions in the Sinkhorn ball, including the worst case distribution, towards

4.1
Gambling on the Unit Square

We start by illustrating our approach by letting an agent play an easy-to-understand environment.
In this environment, the state
X_{t}
is a single number in the interval
[0,1]=:\mathcal{X}
.
The agent can choose to gamble on this number by choosing an action
a\in A:=\{-1,0,1\}
.
The reward is then given by

r(x_{0},a,x_{1})=a(x_{1}-x_{0})(1+(M-1)1\hskip-3.0pt\mathrm{l}_{a(x_{1}-x_{0})<0}),

(4.1)

for some
M\geq 1
.
In other words, the agent wins or loses the amount of money equal to the difference between the next state and current state multiplied by the action chosen and a reward factor
M
(relevance to be explained subsequently).
The goal is to maximise the total reward.

In the following, we describe the
true distribution
of the environment.
The initial state
X_{0}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
, i.e., it is distributed according to a Beta distribution with parameters
\alpha^{\prime}=1.2,\beta^{\prime}=2
.
The realised state
x_{0}
and action
a
are used to determine the parameters of the Beta distribution used to sample the next state.
Specifically, when
a\neq 0
, the next state,
X_{1}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)=:\mathbb{P}(x_{0},a)
where
\alpha=g(\alpha^{\prime}-ax_{0})
and
\beta=g(\beta^{\prime}+a(1-x_{0}))
. The function
g(x)=\log(1+e^{x})
is the softplus function to ensure
\alpha,\beta>0
.
Put simply, the agent’s action and the current state combine to shift the
\alpha,\beta
parameters used to sample the next state.
To be clear, for each state transition, the parameters
\alpha,\beta
are always calculated using the original values
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
.
If
a=0
, then the next state is sampled from the initial distribution
\text{Beta}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}(x_{0},0)
.

4.1.1
Ambiguity in the distribution

We create ambiguity in the
true distribution
of the state transition in the following manner.
The agent knows that the initial state follows a Beta distribution but is not given the parameters.
The agent is also aware of how the actions affect the parameters of the Beta distribution in the state transition.
Before the game starts, 5 samples from the initial Beta distribution are given.
The agent will construct a
reference distribution
by inferring a Beta distribution based on these 5 samples.
We use the method of moments to estimate
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
, which was shown to have better performance for small samples compared to maximum likelihood estimation with the Beta distribution
(
Ali et al., 2023
)
.
In other words, we have
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{t},0)=
Beta
(\hat{\alpha^{\prime}},\hat{\beta^{\prime}})
where
\hat{\alpha^{\prime}},\hat{\beta^{\prime}}
are the estimated parameters and
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{t},a)=
Beta
(g(\hat{\alpha^{\prime}}-ax_{t}),g(\hat{\beta^{\prime}}+a(1-x_{t})))
for
a\neq 0
.
For
\nu
, we choose the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
since it is a simple and valid choice for the state space.

4.1.2
The case for robustness

As the goal of robust optimisation is to ensure that the policy performs well under worst case scenarios, the result should be to reduce the occurrence of very unfavourable outcomes at the expense of reducing the reward under the most favourable outcomes.
Therefore, we would expect the RDQN algorithm to be at its most effective when the agent is penalised more heavily for the wrong action than it is rewarded for the right action.

To this end, we experiment by tweaking the reward function to penalise the agent more heavily for the wrong action.
We multiply the reward by a factor of 1, 5 and 10
only when it is negative
to create three different environments that vary in the severity of the penalty for the wrong action.
This means
M=1,5,10
in (
4.1
).

and

4.2
Portfolio Optimisation with RDQN

We now turn to a financial trading environment.
The S&P 500 index is a stock market index that tracks the performance of 500 large companies listed on stock exchanges in the United States.
It serves as a case study in which the
reference distribution
is a generative model fitted to historical data and the
true distribution
is the realised out-of-sample data, so that the distributional shift the agent faces is precisely the shift between the simulator and the market.

4.2.1
The environment

The action,
a\in A:=\{-1,-0.75,\dots,0.75,1\}
, is the weight of the portfolio in the S&P 500 index which starts from -1 and goes in increments of 0.25 until 1.
In other words, the agent is allowed to short the index up to an amount equal to the value of the portfolio.
The reward is the log return of the portfolio, i.e., the difference in the log value of the portfolio from one time step to the next.
The goal is to maximise the cumulative log return, effectively maximising the portfolio value.

Before constructing the state, we require the log returns for each time step to be bounded.
This is a reasonable assumption for financial markets and ensures our setting satisfies Assumption
2.7
.
As described in Section
3.3.1
, the prior measure
\nu
is truncated to a bounded box, and for the S&P 500 we take that box to be
[\underline{y},\overline{y}]:=[\log 0.05,\log 1.95]
.
These bounds keep the reward for every action in
A
bounded; see Lemma
6.10
.
The state
X_{t}
at time
t
consists of three components:

1.

(X_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{t}^{(60)})
are the log returns of the past 60 time steps
\subset\mathbb{R}^{60}
,

2.

X_{t}^{(61)}
is the current weight of the portfolio in the S&P 500 index
\subset\mathbb{R}
,

3.

X_{t}^{(62)}
is the time delta from current time step to the next time step in calendar years
\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
6
6

6

The time delta is relevant in two ways. It is used to compute the interest that will be accrued for cash holdings and it is part of the input for the generative model.
.

Therefore,
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{62}
.
Note that the time delta is a variable that is exogenous as it is a function of the trading calendar.
Note also that component
2
is the weight the portfolio actually holds at the start of the period, which is in general
not
one of the values in
A
.
The action sets the weight at the moment of trading, after which the position drifts with the price of the index to the next time step, as made precise in (
4.5
) below.
It remains bounded, because the gross return of the portfolio is bounded by Lemma
6.10
, so
\mathcal{X}
is bounded.

We include a transaction cost that is
c:=0.05\%
of the value of the notional amount being traded.
This is factored into the reward that results from the action.
The interest rate is set to a continuously compounded rate of
r_{f}:=2.4\%
which is the per annum log return from investing in 3-month treasury bills for the period 1 Jan 1995 to 31 Dec 2020.

Note that given the current action and state, the only uncertainty that remains is the next log return.
Every other coordinate of
X_{t+1}
is a deterministic function of
(x_{t},a_{t})
and that single log return.
We train a generative model from
Lu and Sester (2024, Section 5)
on the S&P 500 historical data to simulate the next log return.
To make this dependence explicit, we first introduce the two quantities that both the reward and the transition are built from.
The
gross return
of the portfolio over one period, as a function of the next log return
y
, is

\mathcal{X}\times A\times\mathbb{R}\ni(x_{t},a_{t},y)\mapsto G(x_{t},a_{t},y):=1+a_{t}\left(e^{y}-1\right)+(1-a_{t})\left(e^{r_{f}x_{t}^{(62)}}-1\right),

(4.2)

where the two terms represent the return on the index position and the interest earned on cash holdings
1-a_{t}
over a period of length
x_{t}^{(62)}
.
The transaction cost is charged on the notional actually traded, which is itself reduced by the cost, so we solve the following equation to arrive at the cost
k
expressed as a fraction of the pre-trade portfolio value such that the resulting weights after the trade are exactly
a_{t}
.

k=c\left|(1-k)a_{t}-x_{t}^{(61)}\right|,\;\text{equivalently}\;k(x_{t},a_{t})=\frac{c\cdot s\left(a_{t}-x_{t}^{(61)}\right)}{1+c\cdot s\cdot a_{t}},\quad s:=\operatorname{sgn}\!\left(a_{t}-x_{t}^{(61)}\right),

(4.3)

which admits a unique solution, depending continuously on
(x_{t},a_{t})
, by Lemma
6.10
.
If there is no change in the position then
k=0
.
The reward is the log return of the portfolio net of this cost,

\begin{split}\mathcal{X}\times A\times\mathcal{X}\ni(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1})&\mapsto r(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1}):=r^{\prime}(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1}^{(60)}),\\
\mathcal{X}\times A\times\mathbb{R}\ni(x_{t},a_{t},y)&\mapsto r^{\prime}(x_{t},a_{t},y):=\log\Big(\big(1-k(x_{t},a_{t})\big)\,G(x_{t},a_{t},y)\Big)\in\mathbb{R},\end{split}

(4.4)

and the next state is obtained from the next log return by the map

\mathcal{X}\times A\times\mathbb{R}\ni(x_{t},a_{t},y)\mapsto f_{X}(x_{t},a_{t},y):=\left(x_{t}^{(2)},\ldots,x_{t}^{(60)},\;y,\;\frac{a_{t}\,e^{y}}{G(x_{t},a_{t},y)},\;\Delta_{t+1}\right)\in\mathcal{X},

(4.5)

where
\Delta_{t+1}
is the time delta from time step
t+1
to
t+2
.
The window of log returns is shifted and
y
appended.
The position becomes the weight that
a_{t}
drifts to as the index moves over the period, which is why this component is a function of
y
and not the constant
a_{t}
.
The time delta advances along the calendar.
With the function
f_{X}
, the reference transition kernel is the pushforward of the generative model under it,

\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x_{t},a_{t})\mapsto\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{t},a_{t}):=\big(f_{X}(x_{t},a_{t},\cdot)\big)_{\#}\,\mathbb{P}_{\text{gen}}(x_{t},a_{t}),

(4.6)

where
\mathbb{P}_{\text{gen}}(x_{t},a_{t})
is the generative model that generates the next log return based on the current state and action, i.e.,
X_{t+1}^{(60)}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\text{gen}}(x_{t},a_{t})
.

Since the key uncertainty lies with the single log return in the state, it only makes sense to have the Sinkhorn ball around the measure
\mathbb{P}_{\text{gen}}(x_{t},a_{t})
rather than the entire state space.
We define for all
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
and
a_{t}\in A
, the ambiguity set

\mathcal{P}(x_{t},a_{t}):=\left\{\big(f_{X}(x_{t},a_{t},\cdot)\big)_{\#}\,\mathbb{P}:\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\text{gen}}(x_{t},a_{t})\right)\right\}

(4.7)

that is, the image under
f_{X}
of a Sinkhorn ball taken in the one-dimensional space of log returns.

The same map
f_{X}
generates the candidate states that appear in the dual below: replacing the next log return in (
4.5
) by a sample
Y\sim\nu
yields the state
f_{X}(x_{t},a_{t},Y)
, which carries with it the position that
a_{t}
would have drifted to under that sample.

In the setting of Section

\begin{split}(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}\widetilde{Q}_{\delta}^{*})(x_{t},a_{t})&:=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}(x_{t},a_{t})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(x_{t},a_{t},X_{t+1})+\gamma\sup_{a_{t+1}\in A}\widetilde{Q}_{\delta}^{*}(X_{t+1},a_{t+1})\right]\\

rather than a Sinkhorn ball around the reference distribution

for the entire state space.
This means the choice of

is only relevant for the log return and not the entire state space.

4.2.2
Ambiguity in the distribution

The learned generative model serves as the
reference distribution
while the
true distribution
is the actual S&P 500 out-of-sample data.
For
\nu
, we chose the Student’s t-distribution with the location-scale representation, i.e.,
t(\mu,\sigma,\mathrm{df})
, where
\mu
is the location parameter,
\sigma
is the scale parameter and
\mathrm{df}
is the degrees of freedom, truncated to the box
[\underline{y},\overline{y}]=[\log 0.05,\log 1.95]
of Section
4.2
.
We fit the degrees of freedom to the data and choose
(\mu,\sigma)
so that the
truncated
law reproduces the first two sample moments of the training data.
The choice of the Student’s t-distribution as a prior stems from the fact that it is a bell shaped distribution with heavier tails than the normal distribution, fitting stylised facts of financial data
(
Cont, 2001
)
.
The tighter truncation compared to Section
4.3
is because
A
permits a short position of up to the full value of the portfolio.
When fully shorting the index, the gross return (
4.2
) approaches zero as the index approaches a doubling, so an unbounded support would leave the reward unbounded below.
For the same reason, the tails of
\nu
are economically important, which is why the far block of Section
3.3.1
is used in this experiment.

4.2.3
The case for robustness

The
true
distribution is likely to deviate from the
reference
distribution.
In other words, there will be a distributional shift that can cause a policy that performs well in the
reference
distribution to perform poorly in the
true
distribution.
The source of the distributional shift can be due to a number of reasons.
For example, the generative model may not be able to capture the true distribution of the S&P 500 index.
This can be due to missing features, the model being too simple or simply that the market behaves differently for other reasons.

A policy trained to optimally exploit the
reference
distribution may not be robust to this distributional shift.
Using a distributionally robust policy, such as the one we propose, can help mitigate this issue.

4.2.4

We train DQN and RDQN agents on the simulator, i.e., the generative model from
Lu and Sester (2024, Section 5)
using the same five seeds for every agent.
The DQN control uses the same implementation as RDQN with the only difference being the computation of the target in Algorithm

.
Any difference between the two arms is therefore attributable to the target calculation alone.
For implementation details, we refer the reader to
https://github.com/luchungi/Sinkhorn-DRL
.

Post-training we evaluate every agent on the simulator it was trained against, which we refer to as the
validation

out-of-sample
evaluation.
For the validation evaluation we generate 1,000 paths independent of the training data.
The out-of-sample evaluation replays the realised index over the five years from 1 Jan 2021 to 31 Dec 2025.

4.3
Multi-Asset Portfolio Optimisation with RTD3

The experiment of Section
4.2
has a one-dimensional uncertainty and a finite action set.
We now move to an allocation problem across five assets, where
A
is a continuous action space and a simplex.
The RTD3 algorithm of Section
3.5
is designed for this setting, and we use it to show that the robust target can be applied to a more complex environment.
The construction of the environment, the ambiguity set and the dual is the one already given in Section
4.2
, so we only describe what changes in the following subsections.

4.3.1
The environment

The universe is five US stocks Ford (F), Johnson & Johnson (JNJ), JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Coca-Cola (KO) and Walmart (WMT).
The action is the vector of portfolio weights
a\in A:=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{5}:w_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i}w_{i}=1\right\}
, so the agent is long-only and fully invested.
The reward is the log return of the portfolio net of transaction costs, defined exactly as in (
4.4
), with the gross return (
4.2
) now summing over the five assets and the cost
k
of (
4.3
) solving the same self-financing fixed point.
The state consists of the daily log returns of the past 21 time steps for each of the five assets together with the five weights held before trading, so
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{110}
.
Since the portfolio is always fully invested, the interest rate plays no part in the reward here but is still used in calculating the excess returns for the Sharpe and Sortino ratios at the same
r_{f}=2.4\%
used in Section
4.2
.
The transition map
f_{X}
of (
4.5
) carries over coordinatewise, the position component again being the weights that the action drifts to as prices move over the period.

The reference distribution is an extension of the simulator in
Lu and Sester (2024, Section 5)
fitted to the realised daily log returns of the five stocks from 3 January 1991 to 30 June 2021.
We withhold 500 simulated paths for the validation evaluation.

4.3.2
Ambiguity in the distribution

The ambiguity set is (
4.7
) with the Sinkhorn ball now taken in the five-dimensional space of next-period log returns, and Proposition
4.2
applies except with the supremum over
A
replaced by the continuation value of the target actor in the sense of Remark
3.18
.

The reference distribution is the simulator and the true distribution is the realised price history of the five stocks.
Following Section
3.3.1
,
\nu
is a multivariate Student’s t-distribution truncated to a box, fitted by maximum likelihood to the realised daily log returns up to 30 June 2021.
The truncation boundaries can be wider than in Section
4.2
because the action set is long-only with no singularity of the kind a short position creates.
It is typically the case that single stocks have fatter tails than the index, so the truncation is also wider to accommodate this fact.
The box has only to be finite for Assumption
2.7
(i) to hold so we accordingly truncate to
[\log 0.001,\log 1000]
per coordinate, and verify at run time that the mass it truncates is negligible, so that
\nu
is to all intents the fitted law.
The consequence for the sampler is that the far block of Section
3.3.1
is not needed here.

4.3.3
Experiments

We train RTD3 and TD3 agents on the simulator, using the same five seeds for every agent.
As in Section
4.2
the control is the same implementation with the robust target replaced by the TD3 target, and every other component is shared by both algorithms.
Agents are evaluated on the 500 held-out paths, which we call the validation evaluation, and on the realised prices of the five stocks over the five years from 1 July 2021 to 30 June 2026.
We use two baselines for comparison: a daily rebalanced equally weighted portfolio and a buy-and-hold equally weighted portfolio.

Table
5
shows the validation results and similar to the S&P 500 experiment, the simulator is exploitable.
The TD3 control earns almost twice the return of the baselines at similar volatility with significantly better risk-adjusted performance.
However, the robust agents display a different pattern from that in Section
4.2
.
The small radius configurations earn more than the control with stronger risk-adjusted returns, but the large radius configurations are only able to achieve comparable returns to the baselines.

The reader will note that the
\varepsilon
value is roughly an order of magnitude higher when the regularisation coefficient
\delta
is increased by an order of magnitude.
Recall that the duality in Proposition
4.2
requires that
\bar{\varepsilon}\geq 0
.
This condition ensures there are feasible solutions to the optimisation problem and is an equivalent condition to the Sinkhorn ball being non-empty as discussed in Remark
2.6
.
As we increase
\delta
, the
\varepsilon
value that keeps
\bar{\varepsilon}
non-negative also increases, which is why the
\varepsilon
values are higher for the larger
\delta
configurations.

Table
6
shows the out-of-sample results.
The performance of the control is significantly worse than the baselines, which is a sign that the distributional shift is more severe than in Section
4.2
.
We see a reversal in fortunes where the large radius configurations handle the distributional shift much better than the small radius configurations.
The log returns and risk-adjusted measures of the large radius configurations are still, however, underperforming the baselines.

5
Conclusion

We introduced a novel distributionally robust
Q
-learning algorithm, Robust DQN (RDQN), for continuous state spaces and discrete action spaces subject to model uncertainty in the state transitions.
By formulating the problem within the framework of distributionally robust Markov decision processes and utilising the Sinkhorn distance to define an ambiguity set around a reference probability measure, we overcome the computational difficulties associated with the robust Bellman equation by the derivation of a more tractable dual optimisation problem.
We provided theoretical justification for our approach, showing in Proposition
2.8
that the dynamic programming principle holds for our setting.

The resulting robust Q-function was then parameterised using deep neural networks, allowing for the adaptation of the successful Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm to the distributionally robust setting.
The RDQN algorithm, presented in Algorithm
1
, leverages a modified target calculation based on the dual formulation and can be trained using standard deep learning techniques.
We provide theoretical guarantees for the existence of solutions in Proposition
3.5
when the state space is compact.
We further showed that the robust Bellman operator is contractive, that exact robust
Q
-iteration converges uniformly to the robust fixed point, and that approximate robust fitted
Q
-iteration remains stable up to a neighbourhood determined by fitting, Monte Carlo, dual-optimisation, and training errors.

Our empirical evaluations on a carefully designed gambling environment and on two real-world portfolio optimisation tasks demonstrated the practical viability and benefits of the algorithms.
In the gambling task discussed in Section
4.1
, the RDQN agent exhibited a greater resilience to unfavourable outcomes and achieved a higher mean reward per step compared to the standard DQN algorithm, particularly when the reward structure penalised wrong actions more heavily.

The two portfolio experiments are a natural setting for the use of distributionally robust reinforcement learning.
In both, the non-robust agent exploits its simulator convincingly, and in both that advantage does not survive distribution shift to the out-of-sample data.
In contrast, the RDQN and RTD3 agents are able to handle the distributional shift more robustly.

Despite these promising results, several avenues for future research remain.
The continuous-action extensions in Section
3.4
provide a first rigorous route through action discretisation and a scalable entropy-regularised route; future work can sharpen the latter in high-dimensional action spaces.
Choosing appropriate values for the Sinkhorn radius
\varepsilon
and regularisation level
\delta
in a principled manner is another area of interest, as the current approach relies on empirical tuning, and the two experiments here show that the choice matters more than any other we make.
The same algorithm is indistinguishable from its non-robust control at one
\varepsilon,\delta
pair and clearly better than it at another.
Since the pair enters the dual jointly, a calibration procedure would have to select them together rather than one at a time.

6.1
Auxiliary results

Lemma 6.1
.

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

A sequence of measures
(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
converges to
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
in the Wasserstein-1 topology, i.e.,
W_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

2.

A sequence of measures
(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
converges to
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
weakly and converges in the first moment, i.e.,
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(x)\to\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}(x)
as
n\to\infty
.

In other words, convergence in the Wasserstein-1 topology is equivalent to a combination of weak convergence and convergence of the first moment.

Proof.

This follows from
Villani (2009, Definition 6.8)
combined with
Villani (2009, Theorem 6.9)
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\delta\geq 0
, and fix the prior measure
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.
The extended-valued map
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})\ni(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})\mapsto W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})\in[0,\infty]
is jointly lower semi-continuous with respect to weak convergence.
For
\delta>0
, we extend Definition
2.2
using the convention
H(\alpha\mid\beta)=\infty
whenever
\alpha
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\beta
; for
\delta=0
, we use
W_{0}=W
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(n)}\to\mathbb{P}_{i}
weakly in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
for
i=1,2
, and set

L:=\liminf_{n\to\infty}W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbb{P}_{2}^{(n)}).

If
L=\infty
, the desired inequality is immediate.
Otherwise, choose a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that the finite values

W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n_{k})},\mathbb{P}_{2}^{(n_{k})})
converge to
L
.
Define the objectives with their respective first marginals by

f_{n}(\pi):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi(x,y)+\delta H(\pi\mid\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)}\otimes\nu),&\delta>0,\\
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi(x,y),&\delta=0.\end{cases}

For each
k
, choose
\pi_{k}\in\Pi(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n_{k})},\mathbb{P}_{2}^{(n_{k})})
such that

W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n_{k})},\mathbb{P}_{2}^{(n_{k})})\leq f_{n_{k}}(\pi_{k})\leq W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n_{k})},\mathbb{P}_{2}^{(n_{k})})+\frac{1}{k}.

In particular,
f_{n_{k}}(\pi_{k})\to L
.

Weak convergence of both marginals implies that their families, and hence the couplings
(\pi_{k})
, are tight (see
Villani (2009, Lemma 4.4)
).
Since
\mathcal{X}
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, Prokhorov’s theorem gives a further subsequence, without changing notation, such that
\pi_{k}\to\pi
weakly in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})
.
For every
g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X})
,

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}g(x){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi(x,y)&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}g(x){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi_{k}(x,y)\\
&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(x){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n_{k})}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(x){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}_{1}(x).\end{split}

(6.1)

The same argument for the second coordinate shows that
\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})
.

The nonnegative continuous cost satisfies, by Portmanteau’s theorem,

\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi(x,y)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi_{k}(x,y).

Relative entropy is jointly weakly lower semicontinuous: its variational representation

H(\alpha\mid\beta)=\sup_{g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})}\left\{\int g{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\alpha-\log\int e^{g}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\beta\right\}

expresses it as a supremum of jointly weakly continuous functions.
Since
\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n_{k})}\otimes\nu\to\mathbb{P}_{1}\otimes\nu
weakly, we therefore have

H(\pi\mid\mathbb{P}_{1}\otimes\nu)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}H(\pi_{k}\mid\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n_{k})}\otimes\nu).

Let
f
be the objective defined as above with
\mathbb{P}_{1}
in place of
\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)}
.
Combining these inequalities, using nonnegativity of both terms and omitting the entropy term when
\delta=0
, gives

W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})\leq f(\pi)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}f_{n_{k}}(\pi_{k})=L.

This proves joint lower semi-continuity.

∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\delta\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
have a finite first moment for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
, then for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
and
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
, it holds that
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}(x)\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)(y)+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By our definition of the Sinkhorn distance, we have

W(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a),\mathbb{P})\leq W_{\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a),\mathbb{P})

where
W
is the standard Wasserstein-1 distance since
H(\pi|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)\otimes\nu)\geq 0
.
Therefore, for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
, we have

W(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a),\mathbb{P})\leq\varepsilon.

(6.2)

Due to the metric property of the Wasserstein distance and the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality
(
Villani, 2009
, Theorem 5.10)
, we have

W(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a),\mathbb{P})=W(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(x){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}(x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(y){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)(y)\right\}

where
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is the set of Lipschitz continuous functions with Lipschitz constant 1.
Since
g(x)=\|x\|
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant 1, we must have

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}(x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)(y)\leq W(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a),\mathbb{P})\leq\varepsilon

which implies

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}(x)\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)(y)+\varepsilon

Since
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
has a finite first moment, we have

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}(x)<\infty

(6.3)

Therefore, all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
have a finite first moment which is uniformly bounded.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\delta\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
have a finite first moment for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
, then the Sinkhorn ball
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
is weakly compact for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
.

Proof.

The proof follows
Yue et al. (2022, Theorem 1)
.
First, we show that
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
is tight.
Due to Lemma
6.3
and Assumption
2.4
, we can find
C>0
such that for all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
,
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}(x)\leq C
.
Then for any
\eta>0
, we have a compact set
B(\frac{C}{\eta})=\{x\in\mathcal{X}~|~\|x\|\leq\frac{C}{\eta}\}
such that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}\setminus B(\frac{C}{\eta}))\leq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}(x)}{\frac{C}{\eta}}\leq\eta

\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
, where the first inequality is due to Markov’s inequality which shows tightness.

Next, due to Lemma
6.2
, if we have a sequence
(\mathbb{P}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
such that
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}\to\mathbb{P}
weakly as
n\to\infty
, then we have

W_{\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a),\mathbb{P})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}W_{\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a),\mathbb{P}^{(n)})\leq\varepsilon

which implies
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
.
Therefore, the Sinkhorn ball
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
is weakly closed.
We can now conclude that
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
is weakly compact by Prokhorov’s theorem.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\delta\geq 0
, and let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
be the fixed prior measure from Definition
2.2
.
Let
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbb{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
have finite first moments, and assume that
W(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbb{P}_{1})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
If
\delta>0
, assume additionally that
H(\mathbb{P}_{2}\mid\nu)<\infty
.
Then all the following Sinkhorn distances are finite, and

\left|W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbb{P}_{2})-W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})\right|\leq W(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbb{P}_{1}).

In particular,
\lim_{n\to\infty}W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)},\mathbb{P}_{2})=W_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})
.

Proof.

For
\delta=0
, finiteness follows from the first-moment assumptions, and the claimed estimate is the reverse triangle inequality for the Wasserstein-1 distance.
Hence, suppose that
\delta>0
.
For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
with a finite first moment, define

S_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2}):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2})}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi(x,y)+\delta H(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\mathbb{P}_{2})\right\}.

(6.4)

The relative-entropy chain rule gives, for every
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2})
,

H(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\nu)=H(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\mathbb{P}_{2})+H(\mathbb{P}_{2}\mid\nu).

Taking the infimum yields

W_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2})=S_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2})+\delta H(\mathbb{P}_{2}\mid\nu)
.
The product coupling
\mu\otimes\mathbb{P}_{2}
has zero relative entropy with respect to itself and finite transport cost, so both distances are finite.

Let
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
have finite first moments, and fix
\eta>0
.
Choose
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2})
such that

\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi(x,y)+\delta H(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\nu)\leq W_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2})+\eta.

Let
\tau\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
be an optimal coupling for
W(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
.
Since
\mathcal{X}
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can disintegrate it as

\tau({\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x,{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime})=\mu({\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x)K(x,{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime})
for a probability kernel
K
.
Form the joint law
\pi({\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x,{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}y)K(x,{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x^{\prime})
, and denote its
(x^{\prime},y)
-marginal by
\widetilde{\pi}\in\Pi(\mu^{\prime},\mathbb{P}_{2})
.
Applying
K
to the first coordinate maps
\mu\otimes\nu
to
\mu^{\prime}\otimes\nu
.
Thus, the data-processing inequality for relative entropy gives

H(\widetilde{\pi}\mid\mu^{\prime}\otimes\nu)\leq H(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\nu).

Moreover, the triangle inequality implies

\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x^{\prime}-y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{\pi}(x^{\prime},y)\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\pi(x,y)+W(\mu,\mu^{\prime}).

Combining these estimates yields

W_{\delta}(\mu^{\prime},\mathbb{P}_{2})\leq W_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2})+\eta+W(\mu,\mu^{\prime}).

Letting
\eta\downarrow 0
and interchanging
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
proves

|W_{\delta}(\mu^{\prime},\mathbb{P}_{2})-W_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{2})|\leq W(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
.
Applying this estimate with
\mu=\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\mu^{\prime}=\mathbb{P}_{1}^{(n)}
proves the result.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\delta\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
. The set valued map
F:\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
is upper hemicontinuous.

Proof.

Let
(x_{n},a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times A
such that
(x_{n},a_{n})\to(x,a)\in(\mathcal{X},A)
converge as
n\to\infty
and let
(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
where
\mathbb{P}_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{n},a_{n}))
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We want to show that
\mathbb{P}_{n}\to\mathbb{P}
weakly as
n\to\infty
for some
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
, then upper hemicontinuity follows from
Aliprantis and Border (2006, Theorem 17.20)
.

First, we show that
(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight.
By Assumption
2.4
and Lemma
6.3
we have that
\mathbb{P}_{n}
has a uniformly bounded first moment for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In other words, there exists
C>0
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(x)\leq C.

Then for any
\eta>0
, we can find a compact set
B(\frac{C}{\eta}):=\{x\in\mathcal{X}~|~\|x\|\leq\frac{C}{\eta}\}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus B(\frac{C}{\eta}))\leq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|x\|{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(x)}{\frac{C}{\eta}}\leq\eta

where the first inequality is due to Markov’s inequality.
Therefore,
(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight.
By Prokhorov’s theorem, there exists a subsequence
(\mathbb{P}_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{n_{k}}\to\mathbb{P}
weakly as
k\to\infty
for some
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
.

Since we assume that the map
(x,a)\mapsto\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
is continuous, we have
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{n_{k}},a_{n_{k}})\to\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
weakly as
k\to\infty
.
This combined with Lemma
6.2
, we have

W_{\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a),\mathbb{P})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}W_{\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{n_{k}},a_{n_{k}}),\mathbb{P}_{n_{k}})\leq\varepsilon.

Therefore, we have
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
.
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\delta\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Assume that Assumptions
2.4
and
2.5
hold. The set valued map
F:\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a))
is lower hemicontinuous.

Proof.

Let
(x_{n},a_{n})\to(x,a)
in
\mathcal{X}\times A
, and fix an arbitrary
\mathbb{P}\in F(x,a)
.
Write
\mu_{n}:=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{n},a_{n})
and
\mu:=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
.
By Assumption
2.4
,
d_{n}:=W(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
.
Assumption
2.5
gives
\mu_{n}\in F(x_{n},a_{n})
and
\mu\in F(x,a)
.
We construct
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}\in F(x_{n},a_{n})
converging weakly to
\mathbb{P}
.

Every
Q\in F(x,a)
has a finite first moment by Lemma
6.3
.
If
\delta>0
, projection onto the second coordinate and the data-processing inequality give

\delta H(Q\mid\nu)\leq W_{\delta}(\mu,Q)\leq\varepsilon.

Thus, the Lipschitz estimate of Lemma
6.5
applies with any such
Q
as the second marginal.
Also,
W_{\delta}(\mu,\cdot)
is convex: mixing couplings preserves the first marginal
\mu
, the transport cost is linear, and relative entropy is convex.

First, suppose that there is a strictly feasible measure
\mathbb{P}^{0}
, with

g:=\varepsilon-W_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}^{0})>0
.
Choose
N
such that
d_{n}<g
for all
n\geq N
, and define

\mathbb{P}^{(n)}:=\begin{cases}\mu_{n},&n<N,\\
(1-t_{n})\mathbb{P}+t_{n}\mathbb{P}^{0},\quad t_{n}:=d_{n}/g,&n\geq N.\end{cases}

For

,
so
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}\in F(x,a)
.
Lemma

therefore yields

\displaystyle W_{\delta}(\mu_{n},\mathbb{P}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq W_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}^{(n)})+d_{n}

\displaystyle\leq(1-t_{n})W_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P})+t_{n}W_{\delta}(\mu,\mathbb{P}^{0})+d_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon-t_{n}g+d_{n}=\varepsilon.

Hence

for all

, and

implies
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}\to\mathbb{P}
weakly.

Next, suppose that no strictly feasible measure exists.
Since

, this case requires
\delta>0
.
Every

then satisfies

.
The infimum defining this value is attained, because

is weakly compact and the transport-plus-entropy objective is weakly lower semicontinuous.
If

were feasible, let

and
\pi_{0}
be minimizing couplings for

and

, respectively.
These couplings are distinct and have finite objective value

.

has objective strictly below

would therefore be strictly feasible, a contradiction.
Thus

, and setting

gives

and
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}\to\mu=\mathbb{P}

In both cases, every

admits such a sequence of feasible approximations.
Lower hemicontinuity follows from

Lemma 6.9

(Contraction of the robust Bellman operator)
.

For all

-contraction on

.

\displaystyle\sup_{(x^{\prime}a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times A}|Q^{*}_{\rm NN}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q_{0}^{*}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})|<\frac{2{\rm TOL}}{1-\gamma}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

First, we note that for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
, the reference measure
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)=\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
where
\alpha=g(\alpha^{\prime}-ax)
and
\beta=g(\beta^{\prime}+a(1-x))
for
g(x)=\log(1+e^{x})
if
a\neq 0
and
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta=\beta^{\prime}
if
a=0
for some
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

To verify Assumption
2.4
, we aim at applying Lemma
6.1
. To this end, let
(x_{n},a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{X}\times A
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(x_{n},a_{n})=(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
. Since the probability density function of the Beta distribution is continuous in its parameters, the densities of
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{n},a_{n})
converge pointwise to the density of
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
By
Jacod and Protter (2012, Theorems 18.1, 18.5)
, the weak convergence
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x_{n},a_{n})\rightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
follows then by the pointwise convergence of their densities.
The first moment of the Beta distribution is

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}[Y]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}=\frac{g(\alpha^{\prime}-ax)}{g(\alpha^{\prime}-ax)+g(\beta^{\prime}+a(1-x))}

(6.16)

which is continuous in
(x,a)
. Therefore, the first moment converges to the first moment of
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
as
(x_{n},a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{X}\times A
converges to
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times A
.
By Lemma
6.1
, the map
(x,a)\mapsto\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
is continuous in the Wasserstein-1 topology.
Therefore, Assumption
2.4
is satisfied.

The reward function is
r(x_{0},a,x_{1})=a(x_{1}-x_{0})(1+(M-1)1\hskip-3.0pt\mathrm{l}_{a(x_{1}-x_{0})<0})
where
M\geq 1
is the reward factor,
x_{0},x_{1}\in[0,1]
and actions
a\in\{-1,0,1\}
.
We note that
\lim_{(x_{1}-x_{0})\rightarrow 0^{+}}r(x_{0},a,x_{1})=0=\lim_{(x_{1}-x_{0})\rightarrow 0^{-}}r(x_{0},a,x_{1})
.
Therefore, the reward function is continuous.
Since the domain is bounded, the reward function is also bounded, hence Assumption
2.7
is satisfied.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

Let
d_{k}:=\left\lVert Q_{k}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}
. Using (
3.13
), the fixed-point identity
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}=Q_{\delta}^{\ast}
, and the contraction property, we obtain

\displaystyle d_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert Q_{k+1}-Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert Q_{k+1}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{k}-\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q_{\delta}^{\ast}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{k}+\gamma d_{k}.

Applying this recursion gives

d_{K}\leq\gamma^{K}d_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma^{K-1-k}\eta_{k},

which proves (
3.14
). If
\eta_{k}\leq\eta
, the geometric-series bound yields (
3.15
).
∎

Proof of Corollary
3.9
.

The uniform bounds and (
3.16
) imply

\eta_{k}\leq\eta_{\mathrm{fit}}+\eta_{\mathrm{MC}}+\eta_{\lambda}+\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}+\eta_{\mathrm{struct}}=:\eta.

Substituting this value into (
3.15
) gives (
3.17
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

In the first part, we want to show that the set valued map

\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\mapsto\mathcal{P}(x,a)\subset\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})

(6.17)

where
\mathcal{P}(x,a)
is as defined in (
4.7
), is weakly compact and continuous.
First, we show, by applying Lemma
6.1
, that the map

\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{\text{gen}}(x,a)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})

(6.18)

is continuous in the Wasserstein-1 topology
\tau_{1}
.
The generative model is a neural network as described in
Lu and Sester (2024, Section 4)
.
Note that the measure actually does not depend on the action
a
.
Given the current state
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the generative model maps from a 4-dimensional Gaussian random variable to a 1-dimensional log return.
Let
Z
be the 4-dimensional Gaussian random variable, and let the output of the generative model in dependence of state
x\in\mathcal{X}
and realization of the Gaussian random variable
Z=z
be defined via

\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}\ni(x,z)\mapsto f_{\theta,x}(z)\in\mathbb{R}

(6.19)

where
f_{\theta,x}
is a neural network with parameters
\theta
.
Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
be the probability measure of the 4-dimensional Gaussian random variable
Z
.
If we have
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{X}
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}=x\in\mathcal{X}
then to show weak convergence of the generative model, we need to show that for any continuous and bounded function
g\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}g(f_{\theta,x_{n}}(z))d\mu(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}g(f_{\theta,x}(z))d\mu(z).

(6.20)

The function
f_{\theta,x}
is continuous in
x
as the neural network is a composite of continuous functions and since
g
is continuous and bounded, we can apply the dominated convergence theorem to show that the above limit holds.
Similarly, our assumption that log returns are bounded implies that we have convergence in the first moment.
This shows that the map (
6.18
) is continuous in the Wasserstein-1 topology due to Lemma
6.1
.

Next, by Lemma
6.10
(ii) the map
f_{X}
of (
4.5
) is continuous on
\mathcal{X}\times A\times[\underline{y},\overline{y}]
, and bounded.
Consequently the pushforward map

\mathcal{X}\times A\times\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})\ni(x,a,\mathbb{P})\mapsto\big(f_{X}(x,a,\cdot)\big)_{\#}\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{X})

(6.21)

is continuous in the Wasserstein-1 topology since pushing forward along a continuous bounded map preserves weak convergence, and boundedness gives convergence of the first moment, so Lemma
6.1
applies.

In addition, by Lemma
6.4
, Lemma
6.6
and Lemma
6.7
, the set valued map

\mathcal{X}\times A\ni(x,a)\mapsto\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{\text{gen}}(x,a))\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})

(6.22)

is weakly compact and continuous.
Since
\mathcal{P}(x,a)
is by (
4.7
) the image of
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{\text{gen}}(x,a))
under the continuous map (
6.21
), and continuous images of weakly compact sets are weakly compact, we can apply the same arguments as in
Neufeld et al. (2023, Lemma 6.1, Proposition 3.1)
to conclude that the set valued map (
6.17
) is weakly compact and continuous.

In the second part, we want to show that Assumption
2.7
is satisfied.
The reward is the log return of the portfolio for the period when the state transitions from
x_{t}
to
x_{t+1}
.
Recall that the log return
x_{t+1}^{(60)}
of the underlying asset, which we require to be bounded, and the current weight of the portfolio in the asset
x_{t}^{(61)}
, are both part of the state.
The reward function is

r(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1})=r^{\prime}\!\left(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1}^{(60)}\right)=\log\Big(\big(1-k(x_{t},a_{t})\big)\,G\!\left(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1}^{(60)}\right)\Big).

By Lemma
6.10
(i) and (iii), the gross return satisfies
G\geq G_{\min}>0
and the rebalancing cost satisfies
1-k\geq 1-c(1+W)/(1-c)>0
, so the argument of the logarithm takes values in a compact subset of
(0,\infty)
.
The reward is therefore continuous and bounded, which is Assumption
2.7
(i).

For Assumption
2.7
(ii), Lemma
6.10
(iv) gives a finite Lipschitz constant for
r^{\prime}
in
(x_{t},a_{t})
, uniformly in the log return: the logarithm is Lipschitz on the compact range just identified,
G
is smooth with bounded derivatives on the compact domain, and
k
is Lipschitz as the fixed point of a uniform contraction with Lipschitz parameter dependence.
Composing the three yields

\left|r(x_{t},a_{t},x_{t+1})-r(x_{t}^{\prime},a_{t}^{\prime},x_{t+1})\right|\leq L\left(\|x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime}\|+\|a_{t}-a_{t}^{\prime}\|\right)

(6.23)

for some
L>0
and all
x_{t},x_{t}^{\prime},x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{X}
and
a_{t},a_{t}^{\prime}\in A
.
Note that the cost term is what makes this estimate non-trivial:
k
enters through an absolute value and is therefore not differentiable where the traded notional changes sign, but it is Lipschitz there, which is all that Assumption
2.7
(ii) requires.
Therefore, Assumption
2.7
is satisfied.

Analogous to Proposition
2.8
, we can conclude by
Neufeld et al. (2023, Theorem 2.7)
that the Bellman equation holds true in the setting of Section
4.2
.

Finally, we note that the duality follows directly from
Wang et al. (2021, Theorem I)
using the same arguments as in Proposition
3.1
with

\mathbb{R}\ni z\mapsto f(z)=-r(x_{t},a_{t},f_{X}(x_{t},a_{t},z))-\gamma\sup_{a_{t+1}\in A}Q^{*}_{\delta}(f_{X}(x_{t},a_{t},z),a_{t+1})

(6.24)

where
f(z)
is the function being
minimised
in the notation of
Wang et al. (2021)
.

Appendix B
Bias of the one-sample target in the S&P 500 example

This appendix estimates the bias
b_{Q}
of Remark
3.7
, which arises because Algorithm
1
maximises over
\lambda
separately for each nominal next-state sample instead of maximising the expectation over
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
.
We use the trained RDQN agent of Section
4.2
with
\varepsilon=2\times 10^{-3}
and
\delta=5\times 10^{-5}
, and take
Q
in Remark
3.7
to be its trained critic.

States and nominal samples.

We roll out the greedy policy of the agent on the simulator and, after a burn-in of 252 steps, select the first state whose latest log return falls in each of the seven ranges in Table
8
.
These ranges cover ordinary days as well as rare large moves, at which the conditional distribution of the next log return is widest.
From each selected state
x
we draw
M=8192
next log returns
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{M}
from the generative model, i.e. from
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)
.
Since the next log return does not depend on the action, the same draws are used for every action
a
.

Inner expectation.

Given
(x,a)
, the next state is a deterministic function of the next log return (Section
4.2
), so the expectation under
\nu
in
h_{Q}
is a one-dimensional integral.
We evaluate it with a deterministic quadrature rule that integrates the kernel
e^{-\|z-y\|/\delta}
exactly on each cell.
This removes the error from sampling
N_{\nu}
candidates from
\nu
and isolates the outer expectation, which is the source of
b_{Q}
.

Estimators.

For each
(x,a)
we compute

\widehat{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}_{\delta}Q(x,a):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sup_{\lambda>0}h_{Q}(\lambda,Z_{m};x,a),\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}Q(x,a):=\sup_{\lambda>0}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}h_{Q}(\lambda,Z_{m};x,a).

The first is the average of
M
independent one-sample targets of Algorithm
1
with the inner expectation evaluated exactly, and estimates
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}Q(x,a)
.
The second uses one multiplier
\hat{\lambda}
common to all
M
samples, and estimates
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q(x,a)
.
Their difference

\hat{b}_{Q}(x,a):=\widehat{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}_{\delta}Q(x,a)-\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}Q(x,a)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left[\sup_{\lambda>0}h_{Q}(\lambda,Z_{m};x,a)-h_{Q}(\hat{\lambda},Z_{m};x,a)\right]

is an average of non-negative terms, and we use their sample standard deviation for the standard error of
\hat{b}_{Q}
.
Every estimate in Table
8
lies at least
4.8
standard errors above zero.
The pooled estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}Q
is itself biased upwards by
O(1/M)
, so
\hat{b}_{Q}
slightly underestimates
b_{Q}
.
Recomputing
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}Q
on disjoint subsets of
M/16
and
M/4
draws and extrapolating in
1/M
puts this residual below
0.4\%
of
\hat{b}_{Q}
in every cell of the table.
For every draw, the feasibility condition
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
of Section
3.3.2
holds, so no transitions are excluded.

Scale.

To judge whether
\hat{b}_{Q}
is small, we compare it with the robust penalty

\Pi_{Q}(x,a):=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(x,a)}\left[F_{Q}(x,a,Z)\right]-(\mathcal{H}_{\delta}Q)(x,a),

i.e. the amount by which the robust target lies below the non-robust one, estimated with the same draws.
The ratio
\hat{b}_{Q}/\hat{\Pi}_{Q}
is the fraction of the intended conservatism that the one-sample target gives back.
We report
\hat{b}_{Q}
in the units of the reward, a daily log return.
A constant bias
b
in the target has the same effect on the fixed point as adding
b
to the reward at every step.
