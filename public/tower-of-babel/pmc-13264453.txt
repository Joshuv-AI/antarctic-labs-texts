Artificial Intelligence for Regulatory Evidence: A Systematic Document Analysis of European Medicines Agency Regulatory Advice and Public Reports
Authors: Lucina‐May Nollen, Gerard J.P. van Westen, Gabriel Westman, Anna M.G. Pasmooij
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) is receiving increasing attention across the entire lifecycle of medicines, from early development to postauthorization use. While various AI tools have been developed in commercial and academic settings, the extent of their use in regulatory contexts within the European Union remains unknown. In this study, we systematically analyzed the use of AI for regulatory evidence by reviewing Public reports and internal development advice documents from the European Medicines Agency (EMA). Of 26,480 documents screened, 52 documents contained AI use, reflecting 43 unique AI tools used across quality, nonclinical, clinical, and pharmacovigilance domains. The majority of AI tools were deployed in clinical applications, and outputs were used either directly as endpoints, in shaping clinical trial methodology, or in ancillary analyses. Comments and advice from the EMA regarding AI use varied according to the context of use and covered aspects of documentation, methodology, validation, and lifecycle management. Our findings indicate that AI use in regulatory evidence is still limited but showing an increasing trend. As the field is still novel, there are limited regulatory precedents, and AI‐specific guideline development and harmonization across regulatory jurisdictions are still ongoing. In this light, the quantitative characterization of AI tools and AI‐related regulatory comments captured in this analysis provide concrete insights into AI use and frequent regulatory considerations, aiding in both AI tool and AI guideline development.

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Regulatory authorities and stakeholders have identified various potential applications of artificial intelligence (AI) in regulatory contexts. However, it is yet unknown how these observations and expectations relate to actual AI usage. Available analyses on this topic provide only limited insights into AI methodology and related regulatory comments and do not cover the regulatory sphere of the European Medicines Agency.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This study examined how AI is actually used in regulatory evidence across the European Union, with the aim of mapping this usage against previously identified or foreseen applications.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

The characterization of the current AI‐based regulatory evidence landscape and related comments, questions and concerns from regulator–developer interactions allow more streamlined development of AI‐enabled regulatory evidence tools.

Artificial intelligence (AI) in pharmaceutical research is advancing rapidly, with applications across the medicinal product lifecycle. 1 , 2 Whereas some AI tools support discovery and early development, others are used in later stages of development: examples include in‐process quality monitoring in production, toxicity prediction in nonclinical development, patient selection in clinical development, and adverse drug event detection in pharmacovigilance. 2 , 3 Outputs of these AI models may serve as information supporting regulatory decision‐making, hereafter termed regulatory evidence. Model‐supported regulatory evidence can be especially valuable in settings with limited trial population (e.g., pediatric studies) and in context of the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, and refinement) of animal research. 4

Nevertheless, several AI‐inherent characteristics complicate its use to support regulatory decision‐making, including dependence on data quality and representativeness, difficulties in quantifying and interpreting prediction uncertainty, and performance degradation over time when deployed across diverse clinical settings. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 These challenges should be addressed through good machine learning practices 8 and proper validation of the AI tools for regulatory contexts. Such validation can occur within the context of a specific medicine development program, or in a qualification for repeated use, such as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) “Qualification of Novel Methodologies” 9 or the US Food and Drug Agency (FDA) “Qualification Process for Drug Development Tools.” 10 In either context, applicants must carry out general validation, in addition to more AI‐specific analyses. For these analyses, the recent International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) M15 guideline 11 provides a risk‐based assessment framework to guide analyses for model‐supported drug development, albeit with intentionally limited model‐specific technical criteria. Overall, the relative novelty of the field and limited regulatory precedents mean that AI‐specific requirements are still evolving.

To support the use of AI, regulatory authorities worldwide are developing frameworks and guidelines, as well as stimulating research on responsible AI use in regulatory contexts. 8 , 12 , 13 , 14 Among World Health Organization Listed Authorities (WLAs), 15 several authorities have published documents on AI use for medicines development and regulatory decision‐making. The FDA has published various documents including discussion papers 16 , 17 and draft guidance 7 ; the Korean Ministry for Food and Drug Safety has published a collection of AI use cases, 18 as well as the first finalized guidance 19 among WLAs; and the EMA has published various documents, 20 , 21 most importantly the Reflection Paper on AI use in the medicinal product lifecycle. 6 These regulatory documents have been complemented by related publications, including a landscape analysis of AI use in FDA submissions, 22 AI case studies, 23 and perspectives from stakeholders. 1 , 24 , 25 , 26 Together, these documents contain initial insights into anticipated AI applications and considerations of AI use in regulatory contexts.

However, we do not yet know how these anticipated applications and considerations compare to observed AI prevalence and regulatory comments. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated AI use in EMA regulatory documents. Moreover, while a previous study characterizes AI mentions in FDA submission documents, 22 it provides limited details into the AI tools themselves, and does not cover the recent period of 2022–2024, a time marked by substantial scientific progress in AI applications. This study characterizes applicant AI use for regulatory evidence in the European Union through a systematic analysis of EMA public reports and internal development advice. We include quantitative analyses, comparisons to previously identified applications, and regulatory comments, offering insights that can inform both regulatory prioritization and future AI development.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

EMA regulatory documents published between 1995 and 2024 were systematically analyzed to identify the use of AI for regulatory evidence. The analysis covered publicly available reports (hereafter “Public Reports”) (European Public Assessment Reports (EPARs), Qualification Opinions (QOs), and Letters of Support (LoS)), as well as confidential regulatory development advice reports (hereafter “Development Advice”) consisting of Scientific Advice (SA) and Qualification Advice (QA) (Tables S1 and S2). The unit of analysis throughout this study is single unique documents. The scripts used to download and scan Public Reports and create plots from the annotated data are publicly available at https://github.com/CDDLeiden/ema‐ai‐paper.

Document retrieval and initial filtering

We extracted all Public Reports and their metadata from the EMA website by using the “document type” filters to download public reports by category, using Python v3.13. 27 For confidential Development Advice, bulk‐downloading of documents was not possible, and the documents could only be searched through the internal EMA system “Scientific Explorer,” for which we considered all SA (Including Protocol Assistance), QA and follow‐up procedures from 1995 to 2024 (Table S1). From this collection, we applied initial exclusion criteria to define the dataset used for our analysis. We excluded joint EMA‐FDA Development Advice to comply with confidentiality agreements. Among downloaded public documents, duplicate PDF files were detected through byte‐wise comparison, keeping only the first one in the list and assigning it consolidated metadata. Documents containing only a redirection link were also excluded.

The EMA also publishes draft versions of QOs and LoS for public consultation. When both draft and final versions were available, only the final versions were included. Documents summarizing consultation feedback (“Overview of comments”) were excluded.

We obtained the following metadata for all documents: date, procedure type, and medicine ATC class. Metadata for the Development Advice documents was obtained from Scientific Explorer. The Development Advice's “Opinion date” served as the document date. Public Reports publication dates were extracted with a Python script from the EMA website HTML and procedure types from the site's tag filters (Table S2). For EPARs tagged only as “EPAR”, we identified Referral Procedure EPARs 28 and Refusal EPARs 29 using file names. All remaining unspecified EPARs were categorized as Initial Authorisation EPARs (Table S1).

Detecting AI mentions

AI definition and keyword set

In this analysis, we adopted the EMA Reflection Paper's AI definition “all models developed through the process of ML [Machine Learning], regardless of the specific model architecture” as our starting point. 6 We excluded rule‐based and traditional statistical methods from machine learning (ML) by excluding models with less than two learning hyperparameters.

Based on this definition, we devised a keyword set combining machine learning terminology, model architectures, and terms observed during exploratory analysis. Abbreviations were generally omitted to prevent false‐positive matches from homonyms (e.g., “AI” as “aromatase inhibitor”), but included in combination with the word “based” or “model” (e.g., “AI based” and “LLM model”) to preserve search sensitivity. This resulted in a 33‐term keyword set (Table S3).

Keyword search and semantic verification

Development Advice (SA and QA) was searched for keyword presence through Scientific Explorer's search function. Public Reports (EPAR, QO, and LoS) were searched using a Python script on the downloaded PDFs. We allowed full‐word matches, including variants with hyphens or spaces (e.g., “LLM model,” “LLM‐model,” and their plurals). All matched documents were manually verified to exclude false‐positive search results (i.e., the keyword not actually being present), withdrawn documents (e.g., title‐only matches without available documents), and “non‐content matches” (e.g., keywords appearing only in cited journal names or matches in abbreviation lists without the abbreviations being used).

Simple keyword filtering is semantically agnostic and could match homonyms unrelated to AI (e.g., “neural network” as a biological neural network). Through manual semantic verification on all matches, we excluded all keyword matches with non‐AI‐related meanings.

Document annotation

All selected Public Reports and Development Advice were downloaded to the Dutch Medicines Evaluation Board (MEB) environment, and each document was annotated by L. Nollen (LN), recording AI mention context, use objective, area of development, therapeutic area, model architecture, and EMA comments. Uncertain or ambiguous cases were discussed between LN and A.M.G. Pasmooij (AP) until a unanimous decision was reached. When an aspect was not mentioned in a document, its respective annotation was left blank.

Context of mention

As AI can be mentioned in various contexts (e.g., direct use by applicant vs. citing an external AI‐based study as supportive evidence), context categories were assigned using inductive, iterative categorization. For each category, we included an example quoted directly from public reports where available.

Objective

The objective of the AI use was recorded as free text and included the AI tool's explicit task (i.e., input to output) and its intended contribution to the total regulatory evidence. From the resulting collection of free‐text objective annotations, thematic labels were manually identified for the model's task, input data type, and output type.

Development area, therapeutic area, and ATC classification

The area of development that the AI tool was used in was categorized into quality, nonclinical, clinical, or pharmacovigilance, choosing the most prominent area when used across multiple areas.

Therapeutic areas were classified using the ICD11 coding tool 30 ; when multiple areas were present, all were included. Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) class 31 was derived from the medicinal product's EMA medicine data sheet 32 or the DrugBank site 33 when applicable, and otherwise left blank.

Model architecture

Reported model architectures were annotated with inductively and iteratively created categories. If the architecture was not reported in a document, we examined the following external sources: submission materials (excluding annexes), related Development Advice documents, referenced commercial software information, and cited scientific articles. When none of these sources provided architecture details, the architecture was classified “unreported”.

EMA comments

Any EMA comments relating to the AI use were collected per document within the secure MEB environment. Comment categories and topics to each of the comments were assigned iteratively and inductively by LN in discussion with AP. Subsequently, overarching themes were identified from the annotated topic labels using the large language model “Lumo” 34 , and were manually corrected and assigned to the respective topics by LN.

Analyses

Comparison to previous results and expectations

We compared the AI tools identified in our analysis with those reported or anticipated by regulatory authorities that were part of the World Health Organization (WHO) Listed Authorities 15 as of January 2025. 6 , 18 , 22 Applications were categorized by development area and function in data handling (acquisition, transformation, analysis, and interpretation) introduced in the EMA Reflection Paper. 6 Smaller sub‐applications were grouped under broader corresponding categories where appropriate (Figure S1). After compiling and organizing all applications, we mapped each AI tool from the main document analysis to the most relevant application whenever possible.

Longitudinal analysis: Development threads

As authorization and qualification processes can span several documents (e.g., Scientific advice to follow‐up Scientific Advice to Marketing Authorization), we performed longitudinal analyses for related documents (“development threads”) on the AI tools. Development Advice documents were considered related if their Case title–Applicant combination or Research Product Identifier (RPI) numbers were identical. Additionally, we manually matched any remaining related documents including Public Reports during annotation. We noted any changes implemented over time through the advice iterations.

RESULTS

We analyzed a total of 26,480 EMA regulatory documents published 1995–2024, of which 11,351 Development Advice and 15,075 Public Reports (Figure S2). Among these, 84 documents contained mentions of AI, of which 32 were excluded due to containing only potential future use (alternative/hypothetical option, n = 17), cited publications (n = 8), or mentions of an anecdotal or illustrative nature (n = 7) (Figure S3, Table S4). The remaining 52 documents reflect actual AI use and form the basis for consecutive analyses. These 52 documents represented 43 unique development threads, defined as the collection of documents belonging to a single medicine or methodology. An increasing trend was observed in the number of documents over time, with most AI mentions occurring in the last 5 years (Figure S4). The earliest document was a 2011 Qualification Advice describing Support Vector Machines for multicomponent stratification biomarker generation. The most recent document in the analysis set was Scientific Advice from November 2024 discussing risk score prediction for trial enrichment using a model with an unreported architecture.

The majority of AI tool use was reported in Qualification Opinions (n = 23), which discuss qualification of tools and methodologies rather than single medicines. Relative to total documents per procedure, Qualification Advice mentioned AI most frequently (11.1%), followed by Qualification Opinions (3.33%) (Figure 1a, Table S5). Overall, Public Reports contained fewer AI mentions (n = 11, 0.08%) than Development Advice (n = 41, 0.36%), both in absolute and relative terms (Figure S4, Table S5). Out of 11 Public Reports, only three had a preceding Development Advice discussing the AI tool used (Figure S5). An overview of annotated Public Report documents is given in Table S8.

Characteristics of AI tool use

Therapeutic area, medicine ATC class, and area of development

Subsequently, we investigated details provided regarding the AI use. Almost one third of AI tools were used in the therapeutic area of oncology (26.9%, n = 14). Other therapeutic areas included nervous system diseases (11.5%, n = 6); mental, behavioral or neurodevelopmental disorders (9.6%, n = 5); and endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases (9.6%, n = 5) (Figure S6). The majority of AI tools were either not linked to a specific medicine or were used in development of medicines without an available ATC classification (n = 35, 67.3%). Among AI tool use in medicines with ATCs, the majority was in code L: Antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents (n = 14, 26.9%).

Most reported AI tools focused on the clinical domain (n = 50), with only one AI tool each in quality and pharmacovigilance domains, and no documents reporting applicant AI use in nonclinical development (Figures 2 and 3). While two documents mentioned AI in the nonclinical context, they did not imply actual applicant AI use and were thus filtered out as described in the methodology.

All models that were denoted AI or ML were included, regardless of underlying architectures, leading to some architectures being labeled as “non‐artificial intelligence” when the underlying model was rule‐based or statistical (see Methods – Definition of AI for details on the exact definition adopted).

Types of tasks

Tasks included analysis of high‐dimensional data (n = 21), information extraction (n = 10), feature selection (n = 8) and future outcome predictions (n = 8), among others (Figure S7). The AI tools were used on various types of data, mostly in imaging and histology (n = 11), standard lab or clinical results (n = 10), health records and other text (n = 10), and motion sensor data (n = 8) (Figure S8). Types of AI output included risk, prognosis and propensity scores (n =12); event detection and diagnoses (n = 12), patient behavior and experience (n = 9), and general statistical insights (n = 8) (Figure S9). Figure 2 compares observed tasks to previously identified ones.

Contribution to regulatory evidence

Overall, we identified three main types of AI contribution to overall regulatory evidence: direct use as an endpoint (n = 14); use in shaping evidence generation methodology (n = 12); and use in ancillary analyses (n = 26) (Table S4). Ancillary analyses ranged from systematic literature review data analysis, cohort selection for additional noninterventional studies, and feature selection for use in statistical models or for identification of biomarkers, among others.

AI architecture and model types

Architecture and model types often remained unreported in the regulatory documents and main briefing documents (n = 13). This does not preclude potential architecture mentions in annexes to the briefing documents, which may contain additional methodological details. In three cases, model types were primarily statistical or rule‐based and were labeled “non‐AI” architectures but were included in the analysis because the document described them as AI. Other cases reported multiple separate model options for the intended tool (n = 7), most often in the context of prediction of future outcomes (n = 5). Neural networks made up the majority of reported decided model types (n = 9), followed by model ensembles (n = 7)—mostly for high‐dimensional data analysis (n = 6)—and tree‐based models (n = 5). Neural networks were mostly used on image data (imaging or histology, n = 3) and motion sensor data (n = 4) (Figure 3). None of the documents mentioned reinforcement learning or generative AI use. Unsupervised learning appeared once, in the form of clustering as an ancillary analysis for a stopping rule.

EMA comments regarding AI tool use

Comments and advice from the EMA regarding the use of AI tools discussed various aspects of methodology development, such as analytical and clinical validation (n = 33), method and algorithm (n = 31), and documentation (n = 27), where n is the number of documents mentioning this aspect at least once. Data‐driven theme identification resulted in the following six themes: Validation and Performance (n = 41), Algorithmic design and Development (n = 33), Explainability (n = 27), Context of Use and Regulatory Landscape (n = 25), Model lifecycle and Governance (n = 13), and Operational and Ethical considerations (n = 7) (Figure 4).

Recurring topics included generalizability across AI input (e.g., data obtained from different clinical test centers or device types, n = 22), documentation of methods used (n = 22), and overall generalisability of validation results (at least one mention for either model or analytical/clinical validation, n = 29). Another prevalent concept was that of prospective validation and predefining of development and validation (n = 22), related to statistical best practices and data leakage prevention. Fifteen documents contained guideline references to either EMA or external guidelines such as GxP standards. Following development of tools across documents

Among documents discussing AI use, we identified seven “development threads” with two or more documents concerning the same product or methodology, allowing us to follow changes to the tool across follow‐ups. Two out of seven threads contained AI‐related discussions or adaptations to the methodology. In the other five threads, EMA comments were not related to the AI use or focused only on clarification of terminology. In three out of seven threads, the discussed AI tool ended up in a public report, one as part of clinical methodology in an EPAR, 35 one as a Qualification Opinion, 36 and one as a Letter of Support 37 (Figure S5). The other threads did not have any respective public report mentioning AI tools, nor did the other public documents have any preceding AI discussions in Development Advice. One development thread discussed the same AI tool in two simultaneous Qualification Advice procedures for slightly different regulatory use cases and was therefore not a “follow‐up”. In one of the two threads with AI‐related components, the applicant aimed to qualify an overall AI validation framework, something that the EMA stated to be outside of the scope of Qualification Advice; its follow‐up procedures therefore focused mostly on the specific AI tool proposed as a proof‐of‐concept. In the other, the applicant increased the validation data and simulations and provided more documentation of architecture and development steps in response to the EMA's request, resulting in the above‐mentioned Letter of Support. 37 We included findings from the AI‐related discussions and the overall EMA comments in Box 1.

DISCUSSION

Our analysis shows that current AI use described in regulatory documents remains limited and predominantly clinical in scope. Most reported AI tools were used to analyze various multidimensional data such as medical imaging, movement sensor, text or ‐omics analyses, in therapeutic areas with a strong emphasis on such data types, such as oncology and diseases or disorders of the nervous system. Overall, we identified three main contribution modes of AI outputs in regulatory evidence: as study endpoints, in shaping evidence generation methodology, and in ancillary analyses. EMA comments on the AI use covered various topics and often discussed generalizability of validation results (relating to model overfitting and bias), inclusion of detailed methodology reporting, and requirement of analytical or clinical validation in independent or prospective data.

AI use in regulatory documents remains limited and clinically focused

The limited prevalence of AI use mentions in regulatory documents reflects two factors: a latency between scientific development and regulatory applications, and the selective scope of the investigated documents. The field of advanced AI methods is still new, and there is a delay between scientific frontiers, applicant development activities, and their discussion in Development Advice and subsequently in Public Reports (Figure 1b). Previous analyses of the earliest regulatory interactions Innovation Task Force meetings—identified more upcoming AI applications 41 ; these may take substantial time to reach Development Advice. Moreover, both Development Advice and Public Reports capture only a subset of regulatory evidence, with a frequent emphasis on clinical development in Public Reports, and a focus on the specific questions raised by applicants in Development Advice. One can therefore expect less AI mentions than numbers previously observed in broad‐scope, extensive documents such as submission materials. 22

Additionally, development area‐specific characteristics further limit the presence of AI in Public Reports and Development Advice for quality, nonclinical, and pharmacovigilance settings. Whereas clinical development allows for some tailoring of study design to the disease setting, guidelines in quality and nonclinical domains are more prescriptive. For example, in nonclinical safety assessment, ICH safety guidelines specify a fixed set of in vivo and in vitro studies as recommended regulatory evidence. 42 Consequently, complementary AI outputs may remain unreported as they are not mandated. In this light, the increasing emphasis on replacing, reducing, and refining animal experiments (3Rs) in nonclinical studies may extend possibilities for AI contributions. 43 In quality domains, AI use might be additionally underreported due to proprietary methodologies. Lastly, pharmacovigilance AI use may not be put forward for regulatory assessment or advice if used only to complement traditional signal detection surveillance.

Regulatory comments and illustrative AI qualification cases

Across documents, EMA comments mirrored general methodological guidelines for novel methodologies, for example, clinical and analytical validation methodologies, generalizability of models across different settings, and context‐specific performance requirements. 44 , 45 More AI‐specific topics such as preventing “data leakage” (i.e., information leaking directly or indirectly from validation and/or test data into training data and/or development choices), lifecycle management, and explainability were also raised. These comments largely aligned with recommendations from the 2024 EMA Reflection paper, 6 which both regulators and applicants referred to in later documents. While we did not observe explicit terms or assessment frameworks presented in the 2024 ICH M15 draft, 46 such as “Model Impact” and “Consequences of Wrong Outcomes,” EMA comments did address these concepts implicitly, and the comments were broadly consistent with the M15 guidance.

Qualification Opinions provide extensive reports on these validation steps and thus serve as concrete examples of how regulatory expectations are applied in practice. Out of two resulting Qualification Opinions, there was only one qualification of an actual AI tool, the draft Qualification Opinion of AIM‐NASH. 47 The other Qualification Opinion concerned the statistical adjustment method PROCOVA, 36 for which AI was used only to generate prognostic scores for a proof‐of‐concept ancillary analysis in the tool's validation. By contrast, AIM‐NASH is an AI tool for automated disease feature scoring from histology slides. After its draft qualification, it was fully qualified in April 2025 for use as both a clinical endpoint and a patient selection tool. 48

As of December 2025, the FDA has also fully qualified AIM‐NASH, with its qualification review revealing some divergence in regulatory expectations, including differences in context of use statements and validation requirements regarding data leakage. 49 While not formally assessed and based only on publicly available information of one AI tool, these discrepancies illustrate previously reported jurisdictional differences 50 , 51 and the potential value of currently ongoing harmonization of AI guidelines for consistent evaluation and acceptance. 52

Risk mitigation in AI deployment

A notable clause in the approved AIM‐NASH tool's Context of Use is the requirement of human oversight, with intended use of the tool only as helping human pathologists in their decisions. 48 This was a recurring theme across other tools as well and reflects caution on both the sides of developers and regulators regarding AI use in high regulatory impact settings. No AI tool has a 100% accuracy, and a human‐in‐the‐loop approach, where human review is required alongside AI output, remains a practical safeguard to mitigate effects of wrong predictions. In similar light, the majority of observed AI tools were not used as endpoints or in shaping main methodologies, but rather in ancillary analyses. Ancillary analyses, such as post hoc analysis of results or predefined noninterventional studies provide useful insights for further development and contextualize main regulatory evidence results, but they seldom have a decisive impact on final regulatory decisions. Furthermore, an absence of reported use of generative AI and LLMs further illustrates this risk management: objective validation of these AI types remains difficult, complicating appropriate risk assessment. Overall, human oversight, the restriction of AI to ancillary evidence, and restriction to objectively validatable architectures reduce consequences of wrong predictions, and current precedents of AI use remain within these boundaries. As of now, there is no precedent of a fully autonomous AI tool contributing to main regulatory evidence, nor is there a consensus on which, if any, level of model accuracy would enable qualification of high regulatory impact autonomous AI deployment.

Limitations and strengths

First, a key limitation of our analysis is the narrow scope of the Development Advice and Public Reports. The analysis did not include original applicant submissions and full internal assessment reports, which contain detailed methodology descriptions and may reference additional AI tools absent from the analyzed documents. Nevertheless, any tools with direct regulatory impact are likely discussed in Development Advice and Public Reports, making our dataset a more focused representation of AI use contributing to regulatory evidence compared with previous studies. 22 Second, some AI tools may have been missed due to keyword limitations or proprietary commercial naming. We mitigated this by a broader keyword set with post‐detection filtering for false‐positives, so these factors are unlikely to substantially affect results. Notwithstanding these limitations, this study provides the first systematic analysis of AI in EMA regulatory reports, with a selective focus on AI tools directly contributing to regulatory evidence. It also includes the first structured assessment of AI‐related regulatory comments. Together, these findings provide a detailed quantitative characterization of AI use for regulatory evidence, contextualized against earlier findings and regulatory expectations.

Conclusion and future perspectives

AI is gradually gaining traction in regulatory evidence contexts, with limited but steadily increasing numbers of observed AI use, exemplified by the first AI tool achieving full EMA qualification in 2025. This milestone establishes a concrete regulatory precedent that will likely reinforce current trends toward increasing AI integration in regulatory contexts. Further research identifying adoption barriers, continued regulatory framework evolution to accommodate emerging evidence types, and the harmonization of guidelines across regulatory spheres will further support AI adoption across the medicinal lifecycle. With coordinated scientific, regulatory, and infrastructural efforts, AI could strengthen regulatory evidence, especially in complex areas such as personalized medicine and rare diseases, ensuring medicine quality, safety, and efficacy.
