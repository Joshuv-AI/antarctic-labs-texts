Integrate bulk RNA and single-cell sequencing to identify prognostic genes associated with dietary restriction and circadian rhythm in colorectal cancer and conduct experimental verification
Authors: Dongqiang He, Li Ma, Yalan Zhang, Jiaxing Zhang, Wenzhang Wu, Fan Liu, Yumin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) is an aggressive malignancy with a poor prognosis. Recent research has focused on the impact of dietary restriction (DR) and circadian rhythm (CR) on cancer; however, the roles of DR-related genes (DRRGs) and CR-related genes (CRRGs) in CRC remain poorly understood. Bulk RNA and single-cell sequencing data for CRC were retrieved from public databases, while DRRGs and CRRGs were compiled from existing literature. Prognostic genes were identified through univariate Cox regression and machine learning approaches. A prognostic risk model and a clinically applicable nomogram were developed. Differences in functional pathways, tumor microenvironment characteristics, and drug sensitivities were analyzed between the high-risk cohort (HRC) and the low-risk cohort (LRC). The expression of prognostic genes was assessed at the single-cell level and validated in clinical samples. Five prognostic genes, namely SOCS3, MMP3, SLC3A1, FABP4, and SERPINE1, were identified. Both the model and nomogram demonstrated strong predictive power. LRC was primarily associated with DNA replication pathways, whereas HRC exhibited higher ESTIMATE, immune, stromal, and TIDE scores, with significant differences across 24 immune cell types. Sensitivity to 61 drugs was observed in HRC, while 56 drugs were sensitive to LRC. Single-cell analysis highlighted fibroblasts as key cells, with dynamic expression of FABP4 and SOCS3 during differentiation. With the exception of SERPINE1, prognostic genes showed differential expression in clinical samples. The risk model effectively predicts CRC prognosis and emphasizes the prognostic relevance of DRRGs and CRRGs, offering novel insights for personalized CRC therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01993-5.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Dietary restriction, Circadian rhythm, Prognostic genes

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a heterogeneous malignancy originating from the epithelial cells of the colon or rectum, encompassing distinct histological subtypes such as adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, and molecular subtypes including microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), chromosomal instability (CIN), and CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) [1]. The etiology of CRC is multifactorial, driven by a combination of genetic mutations (e.g., APC, KRAS, TP53), environmental factors (e.g., diet, obesity, smoking), and chronic inflammatory conditions. Clinical presentations often include altered bowel habits, rectal bleeding, and unexplained weight loss [2]. Globally, CRC is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer, with an estimated 1.9 million new cases annually, predominantly affecting individuals over the age of 50, particularly those with a family history of CRC or predisposing conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [3]. Despite advances in treatment modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, managing CRC remains challenging due to high treatment costs, significant healthcare burdens, and suboptimal outcomes, especially in advanced stages where recurrence and metastasis are prevalent [4]. These challenges highlight the need for novel prognostic biomarkers that can improve early detection, guide personalized treatment strategies, and enhance prognostic accuracy, ultimately reducing the socioeconomic burden of CRC and improving patient outcomes.

Dietary restriction (DR), which includes calorie restriction and other forms of dietary modulation, involves limiting nutrient intake while ensuring adequate provision of essential amino acids, vitamins, and nutrients to prevent malnutrition [5]. Circadian rhythm (CR) refers to the endogenous 24-hour biological cycles that regulate physiological processes such as metabolism, sleep, and hormone secretion [6]. Emerging evidence suggests that both DR and CR play pivotal roles in CRC development and progression. Studies have shown that approximately half of CRC risk can be attributed to modifiable factors, including diet, positioning DR as a potential preventive strategy [7]. In contrast, disruption of CR has been linked to accelerated CRC progression by promoting the loss of heterozygosity in Apc, a key tumor suppressor gene [8]. DR and CR interact through bidirectional regulation, jointly influencing the risk of CRC development. DR, such as regular fasting, helps establish a stable CR by enhancing the expression of CR-related genes (CRRGs) and regulating nutrient sensing- and DR-related genes (DRRGs). This coordination synchronizes metabolic rhythms in organs like the intestines and liver, thereby improving metabolic efficiency [12, 13]. In turn, a well-regulated CR optimizes metabolic outcomes by preventing nutrient excess and the buildup of metabolic waste products. Conversely, irregular dietary patterns, such as late-night eating or binge eating, disrupt the rhythmic expression of CRRGs, leading to imbalanced expression of DRRGs, such as those involved in insulin signaling, and perpetuating a detrimental cycle [16]. Furthermore, the DR-CR interplay affects CRC initiation through various interconnected pathways, including metabolism, gut microbiota homeostasis, and cellular repair. When circadian rhythms are intact, DR reduces insulin resistance and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) via DRRGs. It also modulates the gut microbiome, reducing the production of toxic metabolites like secondary bile acids [28, 80]. Meanwhile, CR regulates the rhythmicity of cell cycle progression and DNA repair via CRRGs, working synergistically with DR-induced autophagy to enhance DNA repair in intestinal epithelial cells and mitigate genetic mutations [81, 82]. In the absence of proper DR-CR balance, the anti-tumor effects of DR mediated by DRRGs are significantly diminished. Moreover, pro-carcinogenic effects induced by high-sugar and high-fat diets, through DRRGs, are further exacerbated by CRRG dysregulation, increasing CRC susceptibility [20, 21]. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of DRRG and CRRG expression profiles and their regulatory networks is crucial for developing more clinically relevant prognostic frameworks for CRC.

Previous multi-omics studies on CRC have encountered significant technical limitations. One approach relies solely on bulk RNA-seq to profile gene expression at the tissue level. While effective at identifying diagnosis-related genes, this method is inadequate for exploring cell type-specific mechanisms. Additionally, many of these studies focus on surface-level analyses, such as immune infiltration and pathway enrichment, without delving into the regulatory networks among genes [18]. Another approach uses single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to investigate cellular heterogeneity in the tumor microenvironment (TME). However, despite its ability to characterize cell subpopulations, this method often lacks correlation with clinical prognostic factors (e.g., overall survival [OS], tumor stage), making it difficult to translate into actionable clinical tools [19].

This study integrated publicly available bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data on CRC to explore the biological roles of CRRGs and DRRGs in CRC prognosis using bioinformatics techniques. Prognostic genes were identified and used to construct a risk model, followed by functional enrichment analysis, TME assessment, and drug sensitivity profiling to reveal differences in prognosis across patient groups. Additionally, scRNA-seq data were analyzed to investigate the cell type-specific expression patterns of prognostic genes at the single-cell level. These findings offer new theoretical insights and practical references for CRC prognosis, clinical research, and therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

CRC-related datasets, including TCGA-CRC, GSE39582 (GPL570 platform), GSE161277 (GPL20795 platform), and GSE29621 (GPL570), were retrieved from the TCGA (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) and GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) databases. The GSE39582 dataset comprises 566 tumor tissue samples and 19 control tissue samples, with survival data available for 562 patients, which were used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The GSE29621 dataset contains 16 PBMCs and 16 control samples, which were used for nomogram construction. Additionally, 544 samples with survival information and survival times exceeding 60 days were designated as the training cohort to develop the risk model. The TCGA-CRC dataset includes 585 tumor tissue samples and 48 control tissue samples, with 549 samples and survival data (survival time > 60 days) serving as the validation cohort for model validation. The GSE161277 dataset consists of 4 CRC tissue samples and 3 control samples, utilized in scRNA-seq analysis. Data access for this study occurred on November 6, 2024. Participant identification information was not accessible during or after data collection. Furthermore, based on previously published literature, 300 CRRGs [24] and 276 DRRGs [25] were separately obtained, with further details available in Online Resources 1–2.

Analyzing the differential expression

Differential expression analysis was conducted to identify DEGs between CRC and control samples in the GSE39582 dataset, applying a |log2 fold change (FC)| >1 and p < 0.05 threshold. Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated to visualize the top 10 upregulated and downregulated DEGs.

Consensus clustering analysis

The intersection of CRRGs and DRRGs was determined through Venn analysis. Subsequently, consensus clustering analysis was performed on samples with survival data from the GSE39582 dataset, using Pearson for distance measurement and the Kaplan-Meier (K-M) method for clustering. The optimal k-value was determined based on the results of the consensus clustering, categorizing samples into distinct clusters. Differential expression analysis was then performed on the clusters to identify cluster-specific genes (|log2FC| >1, p < 0.05), with volcano plots visualizing the top 5 upregulated and downregulated genes. K-M survival curves were plotted to compare survival differences across clusters, with statistical significance evaluated using the log-rank test (p < 0.05).

Recognition and functional analysis of candidate genes

The intersection of DEGs and cluster genes was analyzed to identify genes associated with CR and DR in CRC, which were designated as candidate genes. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were then performed on the candidate genes to explore their biological significance and related signaling pathways (p < 0.05). GO enrichment analysis included biological processes (BPs), cellular components (CCs), and molecular functions (MFs). Additionally, the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was used to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network (confidence score > 0.15) to explore the protein-level interactions among candidate genes.

Screening of prognostic genes

In the training cohort, the proportional hazards (PH) assumption for candidate genes was first tested (p > 0.05). Genes passing the PH assumption test were then subjected to univariate Cox regression analysis to identify significantly CRC survival-related genes (SRGs) (hazard ratio [HR] ≠ 1, p < 0.1). SRGs were subsequently included in least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis, with 10-fold cross-validation (model parameter: cox). The optimal model fit was determined by the minimum log(lambda) value, selecting prognostic genes and their corresponding risk coefficients.

The 544 CRC samples in the training cohort, with complete survival data and OS times greater than 60 days, were divided into a high-risk cohort (HRC) and a low-risk cohort (LRC) based on the optimal cutoff value of risk scores. Plots of risk scores, survival outcome distributions, and K-M survival curves were generated, with statistical significance assessed using the log-rank test (p < 0.05). The model’s predictive performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) values for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS were calculated to assess model effectiveness (AUC >0.6). The risk model’s efficacy was further validated in the validation cohort.

Association analysis within risk scores and clinical features and establishment of a nomogram

To explore the relationship between the risk score and clinical features (age, gender, M stage, N stage, and T stage), patients were stratified into different clinical subgroups. The Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05) was performed to assess differences in risk scores across these subgroups.

For clinical application, independent prognostic factors were identified via univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (HR ≠ 1, p < 0.05) in the training cohort. A nomogram incorporating these independent prognostic factors was then developed to estimate the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS probabilities for CRC patients. Calibration curves and ROC curves were also used to assess the discriminative power and accuracy of the nomogram. Additionally, the same method for nomogram construction and ROC analysis was applied to the GSE29621 dataset.

Enrichment analysis

To explore the signaling pathways involved in HRC and LRC of CRC, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on the training cohort. Differential expression analysis was first conducted between HRC and LRC, and genes were ranked by their log2FC values in descending order. GSEA was then performed using the “c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt” gene set from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/) as the background set, with criteria set as |normalized enrichment score| >1, p adj < 0.05, and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25. The top 5 pathways for each cohort were presented, sorted by the |enrichment score| in descending order.

Tumor microenvironment analysis

To investigate the TME during CRC progression, the infiltration scores of 28 immune cell types for each patient in HRC and LRC were computed using the ssGSEA algorithm [35]. Stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were also calculated for both cohorts in the training dataset. Differences between the cohorts were assessed using the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05) and visualized.

Next, to assess the potential response of CRC patients to immune checkpoint blockade therapy, TIDE (Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion) score data were obtained from the TIDE portal (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/). TIDE scores for patients in HRC and LRC were calculated using the TIDE algorithm [26], with differences compared via the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05). A Spearman correlation analysis was performed between the TIDE score and the risk score (|R| >0.3, p < 0.05). CRC patients were then divided into immune-responsive and non-responsive groups based on the median TIDE score. The distribution of patients in both groups within HRC and LRC was presented, and differences in risk scores between the two groups were evaluated using the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05).

Chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity

To identify potential clinical therapeutic drugs for CRC, the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for 198 chemotherapeutic agents were obtained from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) using the oncoPredict algorithm in the training cohort. The correlation between the IC50 values of each drug and the risk score was assessed (p < 0.05). Box plots were generated to display the top 5 chemotherapeutic agents with the most significant differences in IC50 values between the two risk cohorts.

Mutation analysis and evaluation of the diagnostic value of prognostic genes

To further explore the genetic mutations involved in CRC, the cBio Cancer Genomics Portal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/) was used to analyze the mutation status of prognostic genes. Additionally, to evaluate the diagnostic potential of these genes in the GSE39582 dataset, ROC curves were plotted for each prognostic gene, and the AUC values were calculated (AUC > 0.7).

Prognostic gene expression analysis and prognostic value

The Wilcoxon test was performed to evaluate the differential expression of prognostic genes between CRC and control samples in the GSE39582 dataset (p < 0.05). Based on the expression levels of each prognostic gene, CRC patients in the training cohort were categorized into high-expression and low-expression groups. Survival differences between these groups were assessed using the log-rank test (p < 0.05), and K-M survival curves were generated. The expression levels of prognostic genes were further validated in the TCGA-CRC validation cohort using the same methodology.

To further confirm the expression of prognostic genes, reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed. Five control and five CRC tissue samples were obtained from patients at Lanzhou University Second Hospital. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Lanzhou University Second Hospital, Lanzhou, China (ethical code: 2025 A-425), and informed consent was obtained from all patients. All procedures followed the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Total RNA was extracted from five pairs of tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA). mRNA was then reverse-transcribed to synthesize cDNA using a SureScript-First-strand-cDNA-synthesis-SERPINE1 kit (Servicebio). The reverse transcription product (cDNA) was diluted with RNase/DNase-free reagents. Details of the specific reaction system are provided in Online Resource 3. RT-qPCR analysis was performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (BIO-RAD, USA), with amplification conditions and primer sequences outlined in Online Resource 3. The collected data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the reference gene for normalization [27]. GraphPad Prism 10 [29] was used to calculate p-values and generate plots.

scRNA-seq analysis

In the GSE161277 dataset, quality control (QC) was performed to exclude low-quality cells, specifically those with more than 20% mitochondrial genes, those with an nCount_RNA value below 200 or exceeding 8,000 genes, and those with an nFeature_RNA count below 200. Following this, data normalization and selection of highly variable genes (HVGs) were performed on the processed dataset. Data scaling was then conducted prior to principal components analysis (PCA). PCA was applied to the HVGs, and a scree plot of the top 50 principal components (PCs) was generated to identify those contributing significantly to the variation for further analysis (p < 0.05).

Next, cell cluster analysis was performed on the dimensionally reduced cells (resolution = 0.07, dimension = 35), and cell clusters were visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Cell-type annotation was conducted using SingleR (parameters: min.pct = 0.2, only.pos = TRUE), with “HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData,” “BlueprintEncodeData,” and “ImmuneCellExpressionData” as reference datasets. Additionally, marker genes for each cell cluster were retrieved from the CellMarker database (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/) to assist with annotation. Differences in cell proportions between CRC patients and controls were assessed using chi-square tests, with multiple testing corrected by the Benjamini-Hochberg method (p < 0.05). Differential cell proportion analyses were conducted for further investigation. Functional enrichment analysis was performed on the differential cells to identify associated pathways. Subsequently, the expression levels of prognostic genes in the differential cells were examined, identifying cells with the highest expression of prognostic genes as key cells. Key cells were subjected to secondary clustering via UMAP, using the same method as before. The differentiation trajectory of key cells was analyzed, and density curves were generated to visualize the developmental stages of each cell cluster, along with the dynamic expression of prognostic genes at different differentiation stages. The distribution and functional features of these key cells were further characterized by comparing the proportions of their distinct subclusters between the CRC and control groups. Additionally, the expression patterns of activation markers [30], reflecting cellular functional states, were evaluated across the different subtypes of key cells. Cell-cell communication between key cells and other cell types was analyzed, and a ligand-receptor bubble plot was generated to visualize this communication.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses were conducted using R (v 4.2.2). Detailed information on the R packages used is provided in Online Resource 4. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Wilcoxon test and log-rank test. For RT-qPCR, comparisons of 2−ΔΔCT values were made using the t-test. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

A total of 59 candidate genes were identified

Differential expression analysis of the GSE39582 dataset between CRC and control samples identified 2,031 DEGs, of which 1,113 genes were upregulated, and 918 genes were downregulated in CRC (Fig. 1a-b). Additionally, 26 intersection genes were identified by taking the overlap of CRRGs and DRRGs (Fig. 1c). Consensus clustering analysis revealed the highest accuracy when k = 2, resulting in the allocation of samples into two clusters: cluster1 (274 samples) and cluster2 (288 samples) (Online Resource 5). Differential analysis between cluster1 and cluster2 led to the identification of 108 cluster-specific genes (Fig. 1d). The OS probability for cluster1 was significantly lower than that of cluster2 (p = 0.036) (Fig. 1e). The intersection of the 108 cluster genes and the 2,031 DEGs yielded 59 candidate genes (Fig. 1f).

SOCS3, MMP3, SLC3A1, FABP4, and SERPINE1 were recognized as prognostic genes

To identify genes significantly associated with survival in CRC, the 59 candidate genes underwent the PH assumption test (p > 0.05). A total of 54 candidate genes passed the screening (p > 0.05) (Online Resource 8). Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed, resulting in the identification of 10 SRGs (HR ≠ 1, p < 0.05). The univariate Cox regression results showed that 2 of these genes were protective factors (HR < 1), while 8 were risk factors (HR > 1) (Fig. 2d). These 10 SRGs were subsequently included in LASSO regression analysis. When log(lambda.min) was − 4.19876941261084, the regression coefficients of 5 genes were retained, and the others were set to zero. As a result, 5 prognostic genes for CRC were identified: SOCS3, MMP3, SLC3A1, FABP4, and SERPINE1 (Fig. 2e-f).

Robust predictive ability of risk model

Based on the above data, the risk score for each CRC sample was calculated using the following formula: Risk score = (0.0475649 × SOCS3) + (0.0842849 × SERPINE1) + (0.0606066 × FABP4) + (-0.0621530 × MMP3) + (-0.0316164 × SLC3A1), providing a quantitative tool for CRC prognostication. Based on the optimal risk score thresholds (0.4164923 for the training cohort and 0.3220827 for the validation cohort), CRC patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. In the training cohort (n = 544), 321 patients were classified as high-risk and 223 as low-risk, while in the validation cohort (n = 549), 196 were high-risk and 353 were low-risk. The distribution of risk scores and survival outcomes revealed a clear trend: as the risk score increased, the number of deaths also increased (Fig. 3a-b). Survival differences between different risk strata were further evaluated, showing that patients in HRC had significantly poorer survival rates (p = 1 × 10− 4) (Fig. 3c). ROC curve analysis demonstrated the model’s strong predictive performance, with AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival in the training cohort of 0.62, 0.63, and 0.60, respectively (Fig. 3d). Validation of the model in the validation cohort confirmed its robustness, with results remarkably consistent with the training cohort, highlighting the model’s reliable predictive ability (Fig. 3e-h).

There were differences in risk scores in different clinical features

Within the training cohort, risk scores varied significantly across different clinical feature subgroups, including the T stage, N stage, and M stage (p < 0.001). Specifically, for the T stage, risk scores were lower in T1/T2 than in T3/T4. A similar trend was observed for the stage, with lower risk scores in early-stage (I/II) compared to advanced-stage (III/IV) patients. Additionally, in the N stage, risk scores were lower in N0 than in N1/N2/N3 (Fig. 4a). These results suggest that the associations between risk scores and clinical features such as T stage, N stage, and stage could provide valuable insights for more accurate prognosis predictions. This information could assist healthcare providers in making more informed decisions regarding follow-up and monitoring for CRC patients.

Nomogram demonstrated excellent predictive value

To enhance the clinical utility of the risk model, clinical features and risk scores derived from the previous analyses were subjected to further screening. The results revealed that only risk score and M stage were independent prognostic factors for CRC patients (HR ≠ 1, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4b-c, Online Resources 9–10). A nomogram model was developed to evaluate the combined predictive capacity of risk score and M stage in estimating CRC patients’ survival probabilities. The nomogram indicated that higher total points corresponded to greater OS probability for CRC patients. Subsequently, a nomogram was constructed using the GSE39582 dataset to assess the predictive power of the combination of risk scores and M stage in estimating the likelihood of survival for CRC patients (Fig. 4d). The predictive accuracy of the nomogram was evaluated through calibration and ROC curves. The calibration curve demonstrated that the nomogram accurately predicted the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities for CRC patients (Fig. 4e). This model provides clinicians with an intuitive and effective tool, enabling the formulation of more targeted and personalized treatment regimens for CRC patients. In the GSE29621 dataset, the higher the total nomogram score incorporating clinical features, the lower the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates for CRC patients (Fig. 4f). The calibration curve confirmed that the nomogram accurately predicted patient prognosis (Fig. 4g). This model represents a significant shift from traditional empiric treatment approaches to a precision-based treatment model, grounded in the individual characteristics of CRC patients.

There were marked differences in pathways replete with HRC and LRC

GSEA identified 47 pathways enriched in HRC and 39 enriched in LRC (Online Resource 11). Figure 4h shows the top 5 and bottom 5 pathways with the highest and lowest enrichment scores, such as “complement and coagulation cascades,” “DNA replication,” and “one carbon pool by folate.” These findings provide a deeper understanding of the molecular biological mechanisms underlying CRC in different risk states.

HRC and LRC exhibited marked heterogeneity in the tumor microenvironment and chemotherapeutics

Immune infiltration analysis identified 24 immune cell types with notable differences between HRC and LRC (p < 0.05). Except for memory B cells, infiltration scores for the remaining cell types were higher in HRC than in LRC (Fig. 5a). Additionally, the Estimate, immune, and stromal scores were significantly higher in HRC (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5b-d), suggesting a poorer prognosis for patients in HRC The abundance of stromal cells could provide a more supportive environment for tumor growth, and immune escape may also occur. The TIDE score, which reflects the likelihood of immune evasion, was notably higher in HRC than in LRC (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5e). There was a significant correlation between TIDE score and risk score (R = 0.36, p = 8.66 × 10− 18) (Fig. 5f), indicating that tumor cells in HRC were more capable of evading immune surveillance, providing a stronger basis for personalized treatment and monitoring plans. Moreover, the proportion of immune responders was higher in HRC than in LRC (Fig. 5g), and a significant difference in risk scores was observed between the two groups (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5h), suggesting the potential for more refined stratification of CRC patients.

Furthermore, drug sensitivity analysis revealed significant differences in IC50 values across 117 drugs between the two risk cohorts, with 61 drugs sensitive to HRC and 56 drugs sensitive to LRC (Fig. 5i). Genetic variations, expression analysis, and diagnostic and prognostic values of prognostic genes

Gene mutation analysis revealed that FABP4 had the highest mutation rate, accounting for 8%, followed by SERPINE1 at 4%, with the primary mutation type being amplification (Fig. 6a). ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the AUC values for each prognostic gene exceeded 0.7, indicating excellent diagnostic performance (Fig. 6b). In the GSE39582 dataset, significant differences in the expression of prognostic genes were observed between the CRC and control groups (p < 0.001). Specifically, FABP4 and SLC3A1 had higher expression in the control group, whereas MMP3, SERPINE1, and SOCS3 were more highly expressed in the CRC group (Fig. 6c). Notably, K-M survival analysis revealed significant differences in the survival outcomes based on the expression of the five prognostic genes between HRC and LRC (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6d), confirming their strong prognostic value. Fibroblasts were regarded as key cells

In the GSE161277 dataset, after QC, 22,010 genes and 24,832 cells met the criteria (Online Resource 13a-b). Data normalization revealed 2,000 HVGs, with the top 10 HVGs highlighted (Fig. 7a). T cells, B cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, and endothelial cells were identified as differential cells (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7g). Pseudo-time trajectory analyses

After secondary clustering, fibroblasts were categorized into three distinct cell clusters (Online Resource 13c). The bubble plot further confirmed the specificity of marker genes (Online Resource 13d). Additionally, fibroblasts underwent two key differentiation stages during their development. Cluster 0 represented the earliest stage of differentiation, while clusters 1 and 2 represented the later developmental stages (Fig. 8a). Density curves supported these findings (Fig. 8b). Expression analysis of prognostic genes across different developmental stages revealed that FABP4 expression progressively increased during fibroblast differentiation, while MMP3, SERPINE1, and SLC3A1 maintained stable expression throughout the differentiation process. SOCS3 expression remained stable in the early stage, increased during the middle stage, and stabilized in the later stage (Fig. 8c). These findings highlight the significant roles of FABP4 and SOCS3 in CRC progression, suggesting that monitoring their expression in key cells could aid in treatment strategies. Analysis of the proportions of different fibroblast subclusters between the CRC and control groups revealed significantly higher proportions of Cluster 0 and Cluster 1 in the control group compared to the CRC group, while Cluster 2 was more prevalent in the CRC group (Online Resource 14a). Discussion

CRC is one of the most prevalent malignancies globally, with high morbidity and mortality rates. Its complex etiology and heterogeneity present significant challenges in both prognosis and treatment [2, 31, 32]. Recent research has demonstrated that DR and CR can influence the composition and functionality of the gut microbiota, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of CRC [33–35]. This study aimed to identify novel prognostic biomarkers and develop a robust risk model to improve CRC prognosis prediction. Five key prognostic genes—SOCS3, SERPINE1, FABP4, MMP3, and SLC3A1—were identified, and a prognostic risk model with strong predictive capability was constructed. A reliable nomogram was then developed based on independent prognostic factors to predict CRC survival probabilities. Further analyses, including GSEA, TME assessment, and drug sensitivity profiling, were performed to explore differences between HRC and LRC. The expression of prognostic genes was validated in clinical samples, and their diagnostic and prognostic value was evaluated. scRNA-seq identified fibroblasts as a critical cell type in CRC progression. This study provides new theoretical insights and references for CRC prognosis, clinical research, and therapeutic strategies, emphasizing the potential of these prognostic genes and the fibroblast population in improving patient outcomes.

The Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling 3 (SOCS3) gene is a key negative regulator of cytokine signaling, primarily controlling the JAK/STAT pathway [36]. SOCS3 proteins play a pivotal role in regulating energy metabolism and serve as natural suppressors of inflammation and immune responses, significantly influencing various cellular processes [37, 38]. Its expression is tightly controlled, and dysregulation can lead to immune system dysfunction, contributing to inflammatory diseases and cancer development, especially in CRC [39]. Empirical studies suggest that SOCS3 deficiency in bone marrow-derived cells exacerbates colitis induced by dextran sulfate sodium (DSS). The absence of SOCS3 results in heightened immune activation, potentially advancing IBD and increasing the risk of CRC [40]. Beyond its immune regulatory role, SOCS3’s involvement in CRC pathogenesis is influenced by interactions with microRNAs, particularly miR-4449. MiR-4449 regulates SOCS3 expression and enhances STAT3 signaling, a pathway often associated with cancer cell proliferation, survival, and metastasis. This dysregulation contributes to CRC cell growth and may have prognostic significance, as elevated SOCS3 levels are correlated with poor survival outcomes [41]. Our findings revealed that SOCS3 expression was significantly higher in CRC tissues than in control tissues, suggesting its upregulation in tumor progression. Furthermore, SOCS3 is part of the gene set that distinguishes high-risk from low-risk groups, and its expression level is linked to poorer prognosis in CRC patients. These preliminary results indicate that SOCS3 may represent a promising target for personalized therapy in CRC. However, further in vitro and in vivo studies, including experiments targeting SOCS3 through overexpression or silencing to assess changes in tumor proliferation, drug resistance, or immune microenvironment, are needed to fully validate its therapeutic potential.

SERPINE1 (Serine Protease Inhibitor Clade E Member 1), also known as plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), is a critical regulator of fibrinolysis and ECM remodeling by inhibiting tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA) [42]. In addition to its role in hemostasis, SERPINE1 is involved in cancer progression by modulating cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis [43]. In CRC, overexpression of SERPINE1 has been associated with advanced tumor stages, metastasis, and poor prognosis, highlighting its dual function as both a protease inhibitor and an oncogenic driver [46]. A recent study revealed that senescent tumor cells in CRC secrete extracellular vesicles (EVs) enriched with SERPINE1, which promote nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 in recipient cells, facilitating tumor growth and chemoresistance [83]. This mechanism aligns with our study’s findings of SERPINE1 upregulation in CRC tissues compared to controls. While RT-qPCR validation showed a non-significant trend of elevated SERPINE1 expression in CRC samples (p > 0.05), the consistent directional increase across datasets suggests biological relevance. Potential explanations for the lack of statistical significance include a small sample size (n = 5 per group) or TME heterogeneity, which may dilute SERPINE1’s expression signal.

FABP4, a member of the fatty acid-binding protein family, plays a pivotal role in lipid metabolism and inflammatory regulation [47]. Its involvement in cancer progression has gained increasing attention, though its functional role varies depending on the cancer type and stage [48, 49]. FABP4 regulates cell proliferation, stemness, apoptosis, and glycolysis in CRC by modulating the ROS/ERK/mTOR pathway [44]. Targeted inhibition of the FABP4/UCP2 axis has also been shown to reverse cetuximab resistance in obesity-related CRC mediated by drug-tolerant persister cells [45], offering indirect evidence of FABP4’s therapeutic relevance. The expression pattern and prognostic significance of FABP4 in CRC remain controversial. Previous studies have reported FABP4 overexpression in CRC tissues, which correlates with advanced tumor stages and poor prognosis [52]. However, our RT-qPCR validation (p < 0.05) revealed lower FABP4 expression in CRC tissues compared to control tissues, and FABP4 acted as a protective factor in our prognostic model (HR < 1). This discrepancy may arise from differences in patient cohorts, molecular subtypes (e.g., CMS1-4), or tumor stages. While most prior studies focused on advanced-stage cases, our cohort includes a broader range of patients, potentially encompassing early-stage tumors. The observed downregulation of FABP4 in these cases may reflect alterations in tumor metabolic demands or stromal interactions. Based on current data, it is hypothesized that FABP4 downregulation in tumor cells could exert a protective effect by inhibiting lipid-driven proliferation and metastasis. However, given the higher TIDE scores in the high-risk group, FABP4 expression in the stromal compartment may still contribute to immune evasion or therapeutic resistance, a hypothesis that requires direct experimental validation. While preliminary inferences suggest a potential therapeutic role for FABP4, its validity as a therapeutic target for CRC must be confirmed through direct functional validation experiments (e.g., modulating FABP4 expression to observe tumor phenotype changes). Additionally, targeted functional assays (e.g., overexpressing or silencing FABP4 to assess its effects on tumor proliferation, drug resistance, and the immune microenvironment) should be conducted to provide further evidence.

Matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP3), also known as stromelysin-1, is a key enzyme in the stromelysin subfamily of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [71]. MMP3 facilitates the breakdown of the ECM, disrupts tight junctions formed by E-cadherin, promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and enables epithelial cells to acquire migratory properties essential for breaching the basement membrane and initiating metastasis [53, 54]. In colitis-associated cancer (CAC), MMP3 plays a critical role in carcinogenesis by regulating cellular growth, differentiation, neovascularization, ECM remodeling, and pro-inflammatory cascades [55]. Furthermore, Li et al. demonstrated that glabridin, a key phytochemical from Huangqin Decoction, exerts chemopreventive effects against ulcerative colitis (UC)-induced CRC progression by modulating both STAT3 and MMP signaling pathways [56]. The present study identified MMP3 as one of five prognostic genes significantly linked to DR and CR pathways in CRC. Experimental validation in clinical samples confirmed higher MMP3 expression in tumor tissues compared to normal controls (p < 0.05), supporting its role as an oncogene [56, 57].

Solute Carrier Family 3 Member 1 (SLC3A1) is a transmembrane protein essential for amino acid transport, particularly cysteine uptake. It is a vital component of the heterodimeric amino acid transporter system, which also involves SLC7A9 [58]. SLC3A1 is known to influence cellular proliferation, differentiation, and immune regulation. Recent studies have highlighted its role in cancer biology, particularly in modulating the cellular response to oxidative stress and chemotherapy agents like N-acetylcysteine (NAC) [59, 60]. While SLC3A1 has been implicated as a potential therapeutic target in breast cancer, its role in CRC remains unexplored. This study is the first to examine the prognostic and therapeutic potential of SLC3A1 in CRC. However, the protective mechanism of SLC3A1 in CRC remains unclear. Future research is needed to clarify its molecular mechanisms and potentially develop more effective CRC treatment strategies.

The nomogram developed in this study, integrating risk scores with M-stage, demonstrated strong predictive performance, with a calibration curve slope approaching 1.0 and ROC-AUC values exceeding 0.65 across all validation cohorts. M-stage emerged as a critical prognostic factor in CRC, quantifying tumor dissemination and disease progression. This clinical relevance is supported by epidemiological data showing a significant survival disparity between metastatic (M1-stage; 14%) and non-metastatic (91%) CRC patients [61, 62]. The nomogram serves as a visual prognostic tool, synthesizing key multifactorial regression predictors to estimate OS, with M-stage as a pivotal component in this clinically accessible prediction model [63]. In CRC risk assessment, model precision and reliability are paramount [64]. This research model offers notable benefits in prediction accuracy and stability. The AUC values for this model at 1, 3, and 5 years are 0.72, 0.72, and 0.66, respectively. In comparison, the CRC model by Li et al. demonstrates AUC values of 0.846, 0.841, and 0.852 for the same time periods [65]. While our model has a slightly lower AUC at the 1-year point, it demonstrates a calibration curve slope close to 1.0, indicating better long-term prognostic reliability. Compared to the AUC models for associated genes developed by Wang et al. at 1, 3, and 5 years (0.6, 0.61, 0.6), our model shows more consistent performance and enhanced calibration accuracy regarding AUC [66]. Cheng et al.‘s rectal cancer nomogram, with AUC values of 0.75, 0.71, and 0.65 for 1, 3, and 5 years, excels in short-term predictions [67], but our model maintains superior calibration curve slopes across all time points. The strength of our model lies in its ability to link CRC prognosis with modifiable factors such as diet and circadian rhythms, offering actionable therapeutic targets like SOCS3, while maintaining minimal AUC variation and calibration curve slopes close to 1.0, ensuring high reliability for clinical decision-making. Our nomogram integrates prognostic gene risk scores (including SOCS3, MMP3, SLC3A1, FABP4, and SERPINE1) with the M-stage of CRC to estimate OS probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years. Emphasizing M-stage highlights its clinical significance, as metastatic spread significantly affects patient outcomes. By linking molecular data (related to diet and circadian rhythm) with clinical staging, this nomogram represents a significant advancement in CRC prognosis. Implementing it in clinical practice could improve risk-adapted treatment approaches. Future studies could explore incorporating immune scores to improve short-term predictive accuracy and validate SERPINE1 expression in larger cohorts.

GSEA comparison of high-risk and low-risk CRC subgroups revealed several significantly enriched signaling pathways, particularly the “complement and coagulation cascade” and “DNA replication” pathways. The complement-coagulation cascade pathway plays a critical role in disease development by regulating liver-derived coagulation factors and vascular endothelial components in a coordinated manner [68]. It helps maintain a balance between coagulation, anticoagulation, fibrinolysis, and complement activation [69]. Due to its complex role in disease progression, this pathway warrants focused research. In CRC progression, the pathway promotes tumor-related blood clotting through increased clotting activity, alters the TME via inflammatory mediators, and aids immune evasion by affecting complement-dependent immune responses [70]. Furthermore, SERPINE1 is abundant in this pathway. SERPINE1 may contribute to the poor prognosis of CRC by participating in the complement and coagulation cascade pathways, suggesting a potential therapeutic avenue. These insights highlight the potential for targeted therapies, such as thrombin inhibitors or anti-C5 antibodies, to interfere with cancer progression in CRC, particularly in patients with liver metastases [71].

An analysis of clinical data from CRC, combined with computational immune profiling, has identified distinct prognostic patterns linked to the variability of the TME. Increased ESTIMATE, immune, and stromal scores were effective in differentiating CRC subtypes. Notably, myeloid-derived cells, particularly monocytes and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), played a significant role in prognosis due to their dual function: promoting tumor growth through the release of inflammatory mediators while simultaneously inhibiting cytotoxic T-cell activity [72]. This immunosuppressive effect accounts for the strong positive correlation between myeloid infiltration scores and poor clinical outcomes in high-risk patients, consistent with findings by Wang et al. [73]. These immune microenvironment characteristics are critical in determining the effectiveness of immunotherapy for CRC. AZD8055, a dual mTOR inhibitor, is proposed as a targeted treatment option for high-risk CRC patients. It exerts anti-tumor effects by inhibiting both mTORC1 and mTORC2 within the mTOR signaling pathway, reducing tumor cell growth and metastasis potential [74]. In CRC, AZD8055 induces cell death, triggers caspase-dependent apoptosis, and causes G1-phase cell cycle arrest, enhancing its anti-tumor efficacy [75]. Preclinical studies also demonstrate its effectiveness against various cancers, including bladder cancer [76]. However, as an experimental drug, AZD8055 may present risks of immune system disruption and metabolic complications, emphasizing the need for thorough clinical trials to assess its safety and effectiveness in high-risk CRC patients.

The drug sensitivity analysis in this study revealed significant differences in how high-risk and low-risk CRC patients respond to chemotherapy. A total of 61 drugs were effective against high-risk CRC, while 56 were effective against low-risk CRC. AZD8055, which inhibits both mTORC1 and mTORC2, can induce apoptosis and arrest the G1 phase in CRC cells by blocking the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Its effectiveness may be linked to the modulation of immune-related signaling pathways, such as NF-κB and JAK/STAT [75, 76]. Elevated TIDE scores and immune/stromal infiltration in high-risk patients suggest that immune evasion mechanisms contribute to treatment resistance. Activation of the NF-κB pathway induces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, while enhancing TGF-β-mediated immunosuppression through increased SERPINE1 expression [83]. Dysregulated SOCS3 activity drives JAK/STAT3 signaling, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and reducing CD8 + T cell function, fostering a pro-tumor environment [36]. Targeting these pathways could offer therapeutic benefits. For example, combining STAT3 inhibitors with mTOR inhibitors may synergistically suppress CRC growth, while NF-κB inhibitors could enhance chemotherapy efficacy by reducing ECM remodeling driven by SERPINE1. Additionally, the heightened complement-coagulation cascade in high-risk CRC presents a potential therapeutic target. Inhibiting thrombin or complement C5 could interfere with pro-tumor interactions between coagulation factors and immune cells [70]. These findings offer new insights into personalized treatment strategies based on signaling pathway characteristics. Future research should validate the effectiveness of combined therapies targeting mTOR, NF-κB/STAT3 pathways, or immunomodulators in preclinical models.

In this study, fibroblasts were identified as key cellular components significantly influencing tumor progression and metastasis [77]. Agorku’s research categorized CRC into four fibroblast subtypes: myofibroblastic cancer-associated fibroblast (myCAF)-like, inflammatory CAF (iCAF)-like, proliferating fibroblasts, and T cell-inhibiting CAFs (TinCAF). TinCAF is characterized by its role in promoting immune interaction receptor and ligand expression through the NECTIN2 signaling pathway [78]. This leads to a reduced immune compartment, impaired T cell proliferation, and an increased failure rate of effector T cells. The interaction between MFAP5 + fibroblasts and tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells contributes to shaping the malignant microenvironment in CRC [79]. In CRC, the crosstalk between CAFs and TME components results in a marked decrease in anti-tumor immune cells, such as natural killer (NK) cells and monocytes, which further promotes tumor progression and metastasis [9]. Fibroblasts play a pivotal role in CRC by facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis through the secretion of ECM proteins like MMP3 and by modulating inflammatory pathways such as IL-6/STAT3 signaling. They also interact with immune cells to hinder anti-tumor immune responses [10, 11]. Targeting fibroblasts through FABP4 inhibitors and SOCS3 modulators can suppress pro-tumorigenic fibroblast subtypes and disrupt interactions between fibroblasts and immune cells [14, 15, 17]. In summary, fibroblasts provide a novel direction for precision immunotherapy strategies by regulating the TME. Intervening in specific signaling pathway axes may improve clinical outcomes for CRC patients in the future.

This study identified notable differences in drug sensitivity between high-risk and low-risk CRC subgroups by integrating multi-omics data and single-cell sequencing analysis, while also examining the impact of immune response-related signaling pathways. These findings offer a new theoretical foundation for prognostic evaluation, clinical research, and therapeutic strategy development in CRC. However, there are certain limitations to this study. First, the sample size for the RT-qPCR validation cohort was small (only 5 pairs of tissue samples), limiting statistical power and hindering the detection of intergroup differences in SERPINE1 expression. In future studies, the sample size will be expanded through multicenter collaboration to validate the expression patterns of prognostic genes. Second, the validation was primarily based on public TCGA and GEO datasets, with no additional external datasets incorporated as test sets. This may limit the verification of the model’s generalizability. To improve the reliability and clinical applicability of the findings, future research will involve multicenter collaborations to systematically collect CRC clinical samples, perform transcriptome sequencing, and conduct long-term follow-up studies. Additionally, a local independent external validation cohort will be established for cross-population and cross-center validation of the model to comprehensively assess its generalization performance. Third, the biological functions of the prognostic genes still need further validation. Future studies will systematically explore their regulatory roles and molecular mechanisms in CRC initiation and progression through in vitro cell experiments and in vivo animal models.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically identified five prognostic genes (SOCS3, SERPINE1, FABP4, MMP3, and SLC3A1) by integrating multi-omics CRC datasets with literature-derived CRRGs and DRRGs. A robust risk stratification model and a clinically applicable nomogram were developed to predict survival outcomes in CRC patients, demonstrating strong prognostic performance. Multi-omics analyses, including functional enrichment, TME deconvolution, and drug sensitivity profiling, revealed distinct molecular and immunological features between HRC and LRC. Notably, scRNA-seq data identified tumor-associated fibroblasts as key drivers of CRC progression, highlighting their potential role in modulating therapeutic responses and immune dynamics.
