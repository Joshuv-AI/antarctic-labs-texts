Solute carrier-correlated gene signature in predicting the prognosis and immunity in patients with acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Delei Zhang, Gongli Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSolute carrier (SLC) is involved in diverse malignancies. This research analyzed the involvement of SLC-related genes in acute myeloid leukemia (AML).MethodsThis study analyzed transcriptomic and clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), with SLC-related genes from GeneCards. SLC scores were obtained using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), and key modules were identified with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Functional enrichment was performed using the clusterProfiler package. Prognostic genes were screened by univariate Cox and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, and a RiskScore model was built. Tumor immune microenvironment features were assessed by Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) and the ESTIMATE algorithm. Mutation profiles and pathways were compared between risk groups, and hub gene expression and function were validated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay.ResultsThis study identified the turquoise module significantly associated with the SLC score using WGCNA, and enrichment analysis showed immune-related pathways. A RiskScore model (FERMT3, CLCN5, DUSP7, and CSRP1) was constructed. High-risk patients showed significantly worse survival, and a stronger prognostic efficacy was seen in the model proposed in this study with an overall C-index of 0.626. Immune profiling revealed higher immune cell infiltration and checkpoint expression in the high-risk group. Additionally, the genes with higher mutation rates in the high-risk group were DNMT3A, RUNX1, and NPM1. The RiskScore showed significant positive correlations with multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, such as EGFR and MAPK. In-vitro cell assays demonstrated that FERMT3 and CLCN5 were significantly upregulated in AML cell lines, and gene knockout significantly inhibits cell viability.ConclusionOverall, these SLC-related genes may have predictive relevance in AML, and our study identified some promising targets for AML.Keywords: Bioinformatics, Acute myeloid leukemia, Tumor microenvironment, Therapeutic response, RiskScore, Solute carrier

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly aggressive hematologic malignancy originating from bone marrow stem cells, characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of malignant marrow stem cells, leading to infection, anemia, and bleeding [1, 2]. With the development of curative treatment approaches like cytostatic chemotherapy, autologous as well as allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation, significant outcomes have been made in adult patients [3]. In the meantime, the advent of hypomethylating agents contributes to the development of therapeutic strategies with generally mild side effects and good tolerability in the elderly [4]. Nonetheless, the response of patients remains heterogeneous, and relevant biomarkers tailoring the treatment are lacking, which has made it an urgency to reveal more reliable biomarkers for the treatment of AML in clinical practice [5].

Aberrant metabolism has been underlined as a major hallmark of cancer, and abnormal metabolism like aerobic glycolysis plays an important role in tumorigenesis [6]. Considering the critical role of metabolism in cancer cells, understanding the metabolic pattern of AML may provide insights into the development of precise therapeutic strategies [6]. The solute carrier (SLC) transporters family is known as the “metabolic gating” of cells responsible for the inward and outward transport of cellular nutrients and metabolites across the cellular membrane [7]. Such a dynamic process has been documented to be critical for maintaining the metabolic demands of malignant cells so as to enhance their survival, proliferation, and adaptation to the tumor microenvironment (TME) [8]. Over the past decades, the profoundly accelerated pace of research in SLC biology has led to the development of opportunities to treat certain diseases like cancer [9]. Some previous reviews have addressed the efficacy and safety of SLC transporters in AML cohorts, while others have underlined the prognostic significance of the SLC2 family gene in AML [10, 11]. Additionally, the methylation levels and expression levels of genes within the SLC family may serve as indicators of poor prognosis in AML. Research suggests that the expression of methylated and silenced SLC22A3 may hold potential guidance for the anti-leukemic efficacy of demethylating agents [12]. Howbeit, currently, the specific involvement of SLC transporters in AML awaits being expounded in detail.

Bioinformatics is a critical tool for the research of biological phenomena via integrating the biological and computational sciences, which can be applied to understand the disease-relevant mechanisms using discrete data [13, 14]. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-sequencing, RNA-seq) from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database has been extensively applied in the research of identifying biomarkers and understanding their roles in the TME [15]. In the meantime, existing studies have applied bioinformatics in the research of AML. For example, high-throughput single-cell genomics has revealed the clonal evolution of AML [16]. Here, this study first constructs and validates an AML prognostic model based on SLC-related genes, systematically revealing its association with the immune microenvironment, gene mutations, and oncogenic pathways. We constructed the AML prognostic model using multi-cohort transcriptomic data and multiple bioinformatics methods. Furthermore, we performed a systematic analysis integrating immune infiltration, mutation profiles, and signaling pathways, while validating core gene expression through cellular experiments. This study not only reveals the potential mechanisms of SLC-related genes in AML development but also provides novel biomarkers and research directions for AML risk stratification and personalized therapy.

Methods

Data collection and pre-processing

The somatic mutation and clinical phenotype data relevant to AML were downloaded from TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The samples lacking the information on survival time and survival status were removed to ensure the survival was > 0 days. The RNA-seq expression profile was downloaded and converted into FPKM format, followed by log2 transformation. In this study, 130 AML samples were applied for the analyses.

The chip data of the datasets GSE10358 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE10358) and GSE106291 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE106291) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Firstly, the probes were converted to symbol based on the provided annotation file. Then the samples with the absence of clinical follow-up information and overall survival (OS) were removed. A total of 554 tumor samples were retained for subsequent analyses, including 304 samples from the dataset GSE10358 and 250 samples from the dataset GSE106291.

Weighted co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

502 SLC-related genes were downloaded from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) beforehand. The trait score of these genes was then calculated via single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), and then the WGCNA was initiated to recognize the gene modules related to the trait score via the “WGCNA” R package [17]. The soft threshold (β) was confirmed via the “pickSoftThreshold” function of the package, followed by hierarchical clustering to identify the gene module based on the parameter minModuleSize = 60 and the analysis on the correlation between the gene module and the trait score. Genes in the module with a relatively high coefficient were obtained for subsequent analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis on genes specific to the module

The relevant genes to the module identified from WGCNA were applied for the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analyses via “clusterProfiler” R package [18].

Construction and validation of risk model

Univariate Cox regression analysis was applied to identify the prognostic relevance of the obtained genes, which were then narrowed down via LASSO Cox regression analysis using the “glmnet” R package [19]. Then, according to the median of the RiskScore, the patients were allocated to high or low-risk groups via the “survminer” R package, and the difference in the prognosis was compared [20]. The efficacy of the calculated RiskScore in predicting the prognosis based on the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve and the AUC was accordingly calculated.

Tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) analysis

The immune infiltration was analyzed via ssGSEA. The abundance of 28 types of immune cells was calculated via the “GSVA” R package [21]. Meanwhile, the ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATE score were calculated via the ESTIMATE algorithm. Further, the gene expression status of immunity-related genes in high/low-risk groups was determined.

Mutation landscape of SLC

The TCGA dataset incorporating gene mutation information under the processing of mutect2 was downloaded to depict the mutation of SLC in tumor samples.

Exploration on the efficacy of SLC-related gene prognostic model

To further explore the effectiveness of our SLC-related gene prognostic model, the prognostic efficacy of our current model was compared with those of published studies based on the overall C-index calculated based on the random effects models [22–24].

Cell culture and cell transfection

Human bone marrow stromal cell line HS-5 (RRID: CVCL_3720, BNCC339313, BeiNa Culture Bio, Xinyang, China) and leukemic cell line HAP1 (RRID: CVCL_Y019, C631, Horizon Discovery, Waterbeach, UK) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (D6540, Solarbio, Beijing, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (S9020, Solarbio, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P1400, Solarbio, China) and were incubated in the incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2. We have performed STR identification on the cells, and the mycoplasma detection results turned out to be negative. The si-FERMT3 and si-CLCN5 were transfected into HAP1 cells using the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent kit (11668027, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

The target sequences of si-FERMT3 were as follows: si-FERMT3#1: 5′-GAGTTTGATGAACACATCAATGT-3′, si-FERMT3#2: 5′-GCCGAATTGTACACGAGTATATC-3′. And the target sequences of si-CLCN5 were as follows: si-CLCN5#1: 5′-TGGAGGAATAGGTTCTTCAAATA-3′, si-CLCN5#2: 5′-GAGGAATAGGTTCTTCAAATAGG-3’.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted using a commercial assay kit (15,596,026, ThermoFisher, USA) based on the protocols of the producer, and the concentration was tested in a spectrophotometer (RRID: SCR_018042, ND-2000, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Hereafter, following the synthesis of complementary DNA, the PCR was initiated in the CFX384 Touch Real-time PCR system (RRID: SCR_018057, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using the SYBR Green PCR Mastermix (SR1110, Solarbio, China). The conditions for the PCR reaction are set as follows: 30 s at 95 °C, followed by 10 s at 95 °C, then 30 s at 60 °C, and finally 34 s at 70 °C, completing a total of 40 cycles. The relative mRNA levels were finally calculated via the 2−ΔΔct method with GAPDH as the reference gene [25]. The primers applied are listed in Table 1.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The log phase cells were plated in a 96-well format at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and maintained in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for periods of 0, 24, 48, or 72 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 was introduced into the medium, and the samples were allowed to incubate at 37 °C for 2 h. For the CCK-8 curve, absorbance was measured at 450 nm and plotted against time. The data were averaged from three separate experiments.

Statistical analysis

R software (version 3.6.0, Irving Medical Center, Columbia University, New York, NY, USA) was applied for the computational analysis. The data of two groups of continuous variables were compared via the Wilcoxon test, and the difference in the survival of patients was plotted in Kaplan–Meier curves and compared via the log-rank test. Experimental data were compared with two-way analysis of variance and analyzed in GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2, GraphPad, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). The data were statistically significant when the p-value was below 0.05.

Results

Identification of key SLC-related genes

In the beginning, the SLC score of each sample was analyzed and applied for the WGCNA analysis to determine the SLC-related gene module. The soft threshold (β) was confirmed as 14 to ensure the scale-free network (Fig. 1A). Then the hierarchical clustering was initiated to identify the gene modules, and 10 gene modules were obtained for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1B). The correlation between the SLC score and the gene modules was analyzed, and a relatively stronger correlation was seen in the turquoise module (Fig. 1C). The number of genes in the modules, meanwhile, is also displayed in Fig. 1D.

C Correlation between the traits and the modules (numbers without brackets were the correlation coefficients, while those within the brackets were the p-values). Functional enrichment analysis

Then the functional enrichment analysis of genes in the turquoise module was evaluated via “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package. In Fig. 2A–D, the relevant results of KEGG functional enrichment analysis have shown that the genes were mainly enriched in immunity-related pathways like B cell receptor signaling pathway and antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 2A). In the meantime, the genes were mainly enriched in the biological processes like T cell activation, neutrophil activation, regulation of lymphocyte activation and leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, neutrophil-mediated immunity, positive regulation of cytokine production (Fig. 2B), in the cellular components like endocytic vesicle, secretory granule membrane, vacuolar membrane, tertiary granule, and specific granule (Fig. 2C) and in the molecular functions like immunoglobulin binding, Toll-like receptor binding, actin binding, amide binding and Ras GTPase binding (Fig. 2D).

The RiskScore of each sample was calculated, and the samples were stratified into high/low-risk groups based on the median value. The Kaplan–Meier curve has depicted the significance of RiskScore on the survival of patients using the data from TCGA-LAML, and patients of high RiskScore tend to have a worse prognosis (Fig. 3D). The ROC curve and the corresponding AUC were plotted and calculated to further determine the efficacy of the RiskScore model in predicting survival. A relatively high AUC value was then observed (Fig. 3E), which was indicative of the good stratification efficacy of RiskScore. Further analysis on the survival status of patients in high or low-risk groups has suggested a higher mortality in patients of the high-risk group than that of the low-risk group (Fig. 3F). These results collectively indicated the potential value of our RiskScore model in predicting the prognosis of AML patients.

Immunity characteristics of high or low-risk group

The ImmuneScore of patients in the high or low-risk group was calculated and the positive correlation was seen in ImmuneScore and the RiskScore (Fig. 4A). The correlation between the RiskScore and the cell infiltration degree calculated via ssGSEA, and the higher infiltration of immature B cells, activated B cells, neutrophils, Type 1 T helper 1 cells, macrophages, effector memory CD4 T cells, effector memory CD8 T cells, natural killer cells, and activated dendritic cells was seen in patients of high risk (Fig. 4B). Also, we analyzed the correlation between the RiskScore and immune checkpoints, and a positive correlation was also displayed (Fig. 4C). These evidences pointed out the potential occurrence of immune evasion of AML patients of high risk.

Gene mutation status and abnormal signaling pathway of high or low-risk group

The gene mutation status of high- or low-risk group was determined. Notably, the following genes, including DNMT3A, RUNX1, and NPM1, were the top 3 genes with a relatively high mutation status in AML patients of the high-risk group (Fig. 5A), while ADGRG4, AK9, and CALR were the top 3 genes with a relatively high mutation status in AML patients of the low-risk group (Fig. 5B). Further, the synergistic effect of tumor mutation burden and RiskScore was explored, and a better prognosis was seen in patients of the low-risk group (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the activities of oncogenic pathways in different risk groups were computed and compared, and a positive correlation was seen in RiskScore and some oncogenic pathways like EGFR, MAPK, and NFκB, for instance (Fig. 5D). These evidences thus provided reference on the relevant mutational drivers and signaling pathways.

Exploring the efficacy of the SLC-related gene prognosis model

Finally, we compared the efficacy of our SLC-related gene prognosis model with those of published studies based on the overall C-index. Based on the data, an overall higher C-index was seen in our study (C-index: 0.626) (Fig. 6A–D). These evidences proved the strong prognostic efficacy of our SLC-related gene model, thus laying a foundation for potential drug candidates for the treatment of AML.

In vitro validation

Thereafter, we aimed to determine and validate the efficacy of the 4 hub genes in AML using a relevant leukemic cell line HAP1. The relevant qPCR assay was implemented to calculate their expression levels in the cell line and human bone marrow stromal cells HS-5 as needed. The results have suggested the higher expression of FERMT3 (Fig. 7A, p < 0.0001) and CLCN5 (Fig. 7A, p < 0.05) in HAP1 in comparison with those in HS-5 cells. Subsequently, we selected the FERMT3 and CLCN5 genes to validate the efficiency of gene knockout (Fig. 7B, p < 0.01). CCK-8 assay results indicate that FERMT3 and CLCN5 knockout significantly downregulated the viability of HAP1 cells, suggesting that FERMT3 and CLCN5 exert potential effects on AML cells (Fig. 7C-D, p < 0.05).

Discussion

AML is a common malignancy of the hematopoietic system which is characterized by the rapid replacement of healthy white blood cells by immature bone marrow cells, which develop more frequently in the elderly [26–28]. Prior investigations have suggested the involvement of SLC family members in AML [10, 11]. Nonetheless, the identification of an SLC-relevant gene signature in AML remains inadequately discussed. In this study, we aimed to fill this blank via a series of computational analyses. In detail, we proposed a 4-hub SLC-relevant gene signature in determining the prognosis and therapeutic response in AML, which, to some extent, contributes to the development of novel diagnostic biomarkers and treatment strategies for the therapeutic strategies of AML.

With the development of bioinformatics, accumulating studies have demonstrated and identified the prognostic signature in AML. For instance, Zhang et al. have developed a prognostic model in AML via a pyroptosis-related signature, where an evident correlation between elevated pyroptosis risk score and the increased stemness of AML was revealed [29]. Moreover, Fu et al. have identified and proposed an autophagy-related signature in determining the prognosis and depicting the TIME in AML, and a strong immunosuppressive microenvironment was seen in high-risk patients [30]. Besides, a 10-gene ferroptosis-related prognostic signature was noticed in Zhu et al.’s study, where 8 drugs have also been revealed to be beneficial for the treatment of AML [31]. Nonetheless, the SLC-related gene signature has not been developed in AML so far, which gives us the opportunity to fill the blank. Our current study applies a series of computational analyses and develops such an SLC-related gene signature in AML. Specifically, we applied WGCNA to identify the relevant gene modules for our study. Then, 10 gene modules were obtained, where a relatively stronger correlation between the SLC score and the gene modules was seen in the turquoise module. This evidence made us curious whether the genes in this module could be applied for the establishment of the prognostic signature in AML. In light of this, the functional enrichment analysis was implemented, and the relevant results manifested that the genes specific to the turquoise module were mainly enriched in immunity-related pathways. Further, the number of genes in the turquoise module was narrowed down to 4. Namely, FERMT3, CLCN5, DUSP7, and CSRP1. Alternatively known as Kindlin-3, FERMT3 is a member of the kindlin family of local adhesion proteins which can bind to integrin beta-chain cytoplasmic domains and plays a promoting role in AML [32, 33]. CLCN5, which encodes a voltage-gated chloride ion channel, was previously identified in Dent’s disease [34]. In this study, the overexpression of CLCN5 in AML correlates with poor prognosis. Beyond its classical role in renal disease, we hypothesize that it may contribute to leukemia progression in tumor cells by influencing intracellular chloride homeostasis, pH regulation, and metabolic reprogramming. Previous studies have indicated that chloride channels/transporters play crucial roles in cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolic pathways. Chloride channels maintain cell volume, regulate intracellular pH and ion homeostasis, thereby influencing metabolic enzyme activity and mitochondrial function [35]. Therefore, we hypothesize that in AML cells, CLCN5 may participate in pH buffering or chloride ion channel flux at the endosome/lysosome stage, thereby influencing cellular metabolism and apoptosis. Future studies should validate whether CLCN5 mediates AML cell survival, proliferation, and drug resistance by regulating intracellular chloride flux or acid–base balance at the protein level and through metabolomics analysis. DUSP7 is a member of the DUSP family which dephosphorylates both phospho-tyrosine and phospho-serine/phospho-threonine residues [36]. An existing study based on some large population cohorts has identified DUSP7 as an RNA marker for prognostic stratification in AML [37]. CSRP1 is a cysteine-rich protein which plays a critical role in a variety of diseases like AML and is correlated with immune-related pathways, immune cells, and immune checkpoints in AML [38]. These 4 hub genes contributed to the development of a RiskScore system in our study, which divided patients into either a high or low-risk group. The established RiskScore based on 4 hub genes showed efficacy in predicting the prognosis. These findings reveal that the four biomarkers identified in this study can not only be used for risk stratification in AML patients, but also provide new evidence for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of their immune microenvironment and potential therapeutic targets.

Existing studies have demonstrated that AML may blast and leukemic stem cells can evade and suppress the immune systems of the hosts [39]. Moreover, some research has demonstrated that the AML-related signature is involved in immunosuppression and defines checkpoint blockade-unresponsive microenvironments in AML [40, 41]. In accordance with the results of our study, the 4-gene SLC-related RiskScore was positively correlated with the immune checkpoint and the oncogenic pathways, hinting at the potential occurrence of immune evasion in high-risk AML patients. Existing studies have stressed the cytogenetics of AML and revealed the recurrent gene alternations in AML, like DNMT3A [42], RUNX1 [43], and NPM1 [44]. A 5-gene prognostic signature in AML has revealed the mutation of FLT3 and NPM1 in AML [45]. An increased tumor mutation burden was witnessed in AML patients with the high RiskScore based on the results of this study, with a relatively higher gene mutation frequency of DNMT3A, RUNX1, and NPM1. These results provided reference for monitoring the mutational drivers. Besides, in-depth studies have proposed a strong efficacy of our SLC-related gene model in predicting the prognosis, thus demonstrating the potential of our SLC-related gene model in aiding decision-making in AML.

In short, our study preliminarily analyzed the relationship between the SLC-related genes and the development of AML and explored 4 hub genes as the targets to construct the prognostic model and to predict the potential immunotherapy candidates. Some inevitable shortcomings, however, should be addressed. First, the comprehension of the involvement of SLC in AML remains poorly defined. Second, despite their prognostic values, the specific involvement and effects of these 4 hub genes in AML need to be interpreted in detail based on relevant in vitro and in vivo assays. Third, some follow-up clinical trials should be performed in order to confirm the therapeutic effects of the drugs and biomarkers. Collectively speaking, this study emphasized the role of SLC-related genes in AML, clarified the potential clinical utility of the SLC, and directed the effective therapeutic strategies for AML.
