Engineering an arginine-nitric oxide circuit in Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 to remodel the tumor microenvironment for enhanced immunotherapy: a review
Authors: Sujuan Li, Yuanhang Zhang, Pengfei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), faces a major obstacle: the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by abnormal vasculature, T cell exhaustion, and infiltration of suppressive immune cells including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs). Nitric oxide (NO), a critical gasotransmitter, exhibits concentration-dependent dual roles in tumor biology. However, conventional NO delivery methods are limited by poor targeting specificity and systemic toxicity. Engineered Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), with its century-long safety record, facultative anaerobic nature enabling tumor-selective colonization, and well-characterized genetic background, has emerged as a promising living therapeutic platform. This review systematically examines EcN engineered with a synthetic arginine-NO circuit (EcN-NO), in which deletion of argR eliminates feedback inhibition, while plasmid-encoded argAᶠᵐʳ, argG, argH, and Bacillus subtilis NO synthase (BsNOS) drive constitutive, sustained NO production after intratumoral colonization. We first provide a comparative analysis positioning EcN-NO within the historical landscape of engineered bacterial cancer therapy, including seminal work by Danino et al. (EcN tumor detection, 2015), Din et al. [synchronized lysis circuit (SLC), 2016], Chowdhury et al. (CD47 nanobody-mediated systemic antitumor immunity, 2019), and the framework of Gurbatri, Arpaia, and Danino [2022]. We then present a focused evidence-based analysis of EcN-NO’s multidimensional mechanisms: tumor vascular normalization, dendritic cell (DC) recruitment and activation, reprogramming of immunosuppressive myeloid cells (TAM repolarization and MDSC reduction), direct cytotoxicity, and induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD). The review further details how this TME remodeling synergizes with αPD-L1 checkpoint blockade to expand functional CD8+ T cells, reverse T cell exhaustion, and establish long-term immunological memory across colorectal and breast cancer models. Finally, we outline unresolved translational questions regarding biosafety and biocontainment, optimal dosing and administration routes, good manufacturing practice (GMP) manufacturing, predictive biomarkers for patient stratification, and rational combination regimens. This review aims to provide a comprehensive theoretical foundation for overcoming immunotherapy resistance through engineered bacterial NO delivery.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has revolutionized the treatment of many cancers by enabling durable remission. However, response rates remain low, and many patients derive no benefit (1). A major obstacle is the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME)—a complex ecosystem characterized by abnormal, leaky blood vessels that induce hypoxia and acidosis, which impair immune cell function while promoting immunosuppressive factors (2). The TME is densely populated with suppressive immune cells, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (3). In osteosarcoma, single-cell RNA sequencing reveals a high infiltration of MDSCs, Tregs, and exhausted T cells, with MDSCs driving NF-κB upregulation and establishing an immunosuppressive niche. These cells suppress immune responses by secreting cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10 and by depleting nutrients, thereby impairing the function of CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells (4). Tumor-derived exosomes delivering PD-L1 further inhibit T cell function, resulting in a “cold” tumor phenotype characterized by a lack of T cell infiltration and correlating with primary resistance to ICIs (4). This underscores the urgent need for strategies to convert “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors that are responsive to immunotherapy (Figure 1).

T cell exhaustion within the immunosuppressive TME—characterized by reduced proliferation, diminished cytokine secretion (IFN-γ, TNF-α), and high co-expression of inhibitory receptors—poses a significant challenge to effective immunotherapy (3). Resistance to ICIs involves both tumor-intrinsic and extrinsic TME factors, including impaired antigen presentation, defective interferon signaling, T cell exclusion, and the presence of immunosuppressive metabolites and exosomes (3). Reversing this exhaustion is critical; however, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies have achieved only partial success. Next-generation approaches aim to achieve a more comprehensive reversal through metabolic targeting and combination therapies, including nanoparticle co-delivery systems (5), metformin-mediated enhancement of CD8+ T cell respiration, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of RASA2, IL-15 therapy (which is limited by toxicity concerns) (6), and modulation of retinoic acid pathways. Overcoming resistance requires reprogramming the TME by targeting immunosuppressive pathways, depleting suppressive myeloid cells (7), or employing nanomedicine for the precise delivery of immunomodulators (8) (Figure 2).

Within this context, nitric oxide (NO) emerges as a critical signaling molecule with the potential to remodel the TME. NO exhibits a complex, concentration-dependent dual role in tumor biology, acting as both a promoter and an inhibitor of cancer progression (2). Low physiological concentrations promote tumor growth by reducing oxidative stress and facilitating angiogenesis, whereas sustained high concentrations induce apoptosis through p53 activation and peroxynitrite formation (4). This behavior is influenced by exposure duration, cellular origin, and the redox conditions within the TME (9). In lung cancer, NO’s dose-dependent effects on tumor development and dissemination underscore its context-specific nature (10). Therapeutic strategies include either inhibiting pro-tumorigenic low-concentration NO or delivering cytotoxic high-concentration NO to tumor sites (11) (Figure 3).

NO plays a critical role in regulating vascular homeostasis and tumor angiogenesis. Endothelial NO synthase (eNOS)-derived NO promotes endothelial cell survival and vasodilation; physiological levels of NO produced by eNOS are essential for maintaining vascular health (12). Endothelial dysfunction associated with aging and obesity can be reversed by restoring the GTPCH1/BH4/eNOS axis. In contrast, dysregulated NO produced by inducible NO synthase (iNOS) contributes to vascular dysfunction and immunosuppression in cancer. Within the TME, dysregulation of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-NO axis leads to abnormal, leaky angiogenesis that supports tumor growth and induces hypoxia with elevated interstitial pressure, thereby hindering drug delivery (13). Therapeutic strategies that enhance eNOS-derived NO or deliver NO donors can normalize vasculature—improving perfusion, reducing hypoxia, and enhancing the penetration of drugs and immune cells (14). Engineered nanoparticles that release NO promote vessel normalization and degrade the extracellular matrix, thereby enhancing drug delivery and synergizing with chemotherapy and immunotherapy (7) (Figure 4A).

Beyond its vascular effects, NO plays a context-dependent role in immunity, both suppressing and activating anti-tumor responses (15). Immune cells within the TME produce NO (16). Low NO levels promote immunosuppression by inducing M2 macrophage polarization and inhibiting T cell activity, whereas high NO levels stimulate immunogenic cell death (ICD) and enhance CD8+ T cell function (17). The modulatory capacity of NO makes it a promising immunotherapy adjuvant (18), and its strategic delivery or inhibition could improve cancer treatment outcomes (19). Exploiting NO biology can thus reprogram the immunosuppressive TME by simultaneously modulating blood vessels and immune cells, converting a “cold” tumor into a “hot”, therapy-permissive environment (Figures 1,4B).

Conventional NO delivery methods face challenges related to targeting specificity and systemic toxicity. To address these issues, engineered Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) has been developed as a “living microrobot” that selectively targets hypoxic tumors and achieves complete tumor regression in mice (20). EcN produces antitumor compounds, expresses tumoricidal proteins, and modulates the TME by secreting cytokines and lactate oxidase. When combined with immunotherapies, EcN delivering IL-15 enhances the efficacy of checkpoint blockade therapies. Furthermore, synthetic gene circuits enable precise (21), TME-responsive payload release, improving both safety and therapeutic efficacy. These advances establish EcN as a versatile platform for overcoming resistance to immunotherapy.

Engineered bacterial therapy: EcN as a tumor-targeted delivery platform

Biological characteristics and tumor-targeting advantages of EcN

EcN has a century-long safety record as a probiotic for gastrointestinal disorders, making it a promising chassis for tumor-targeted therapy (22). Its facultative anaerobic nature enables selective colonization of hypoxic tumor cores, allowing localized therapeutic delivery while minimizing systemic toxicity (23). Studies in multiple murine tumor models demonstrate high tumor-to-normal tissue accumulation (24). Following treatment, immune clearance typically eliminates EcN strains within days, with no persistent colonization detected in the bloodstream or non-target tissues. Early cancer trials involving engineered EcN report manageable safety profiles, with cytokine release syndrome identified as a dose-limiting but monitorable adverse event (25).

EcN’s well-characterized genetic background facilitates advanced genetic engineering (26). Its genome demonstrates high transformation efficiency and stability for heterologous plasmids, enabling the construction of complex synthetic gene circuits. Researchers have integrated therapeutic genes into the chromosome using CRISPR/Cas9 and phage integration methods to achieve stable, plasmid-free expression—an essential feature for clinical translation, as it eliminates the need for antibiotic resistance markers (27). Engineered circuits provide inducible and tunable control over the production of therapeutic proteins, including cytolytic toxins, cytokines, and enzymes (28). The combination of safety, tumor-homing capability, and genetic programmability makes EcN an ideal vehicle for targeted delivery aimed at remodeling the TME and enhancing immunotherapy (Figure 5).

The seminal study by Li et al. (7) first demonstrated the feasibility of programming EcN as an in vivo diagnostic tool. In this work, EcN was engineered to overexpress a LacZ reporter and was administered orally to mice bearing liver metastases. The bacteria selectively colonized tumor tissue, exhibiting approximately a 106-fold enrichment compared to normal organs. The LacZ-cleaved substrate (LuGal) produced a detectable signal in urine, enabling noninvasive tumor detection at sizes as small as 1–2 mm. This study established that systemically administered EcN can home to tumors with remarkable specificity and provided the technological foundation for subsequent therapeutic applications, including the development of EcN engineered with a synthetic arginine-NO circuit (EcN-NO) (29).

Applications of synthetic biology in bacterial therapy

Synthetic biology enables the engineering of bacteria as living therapeutics with precise control in complex environments, such as the TME. A key strategy involves using genetic circuits to sense TME signals (e.g., hypoxia) and trigger on-demand therapeutic production (20). For example, bacteria equipped with hypoxia-sensing modules can express cytotoxic agents specifically within solid tumors, thereby reducing off-target effects (20). This approach transforms bacteria into autonomous, tumor-responsive systems through modular designs for payload production, targeting, and safety (30). Additionally, synthetic biology rewires microbial networks to detect biomarkers and deliver calibrated responses, as demonstrated by EcN engineered to sense NO and secrete anti-TNF-α nanobodies for treating gut inflammation (31). These genetic circuits enable bacteria to perform Boolean logic operations, thereby enhancing therapeutic specificity.

The advancement of bacterial therapy relies on controllable expression systems that enable spatiotemporal regulation, thereby enhancing both safety and precision. Inducible promoters and quorum sensing (QS) systems are essential tools for achieving such control (32). For example, QS-based genetic circuits allow engineered bacteria to coordinate their behavior and synchronize therapeutic release only when a critical tumor cell density is reached, preventing premature activation (33). This population-level regulation is crucial for designing synthetic bacterial consortia, such as those targeting colorectal cancer, where multiple sensing modules can operate synergistically (34). Beyond chemical induction, external physical triggers—such as light, ultrasound, and magnetic fields—offer noninvasive and spatially precise control. For instance, bacteria have been engineered with temperature-responsive switches that activate in response to focused ultrasound-induced hyperthermia, enabling targeted release of payloads like ICIs specifically at the tumor site. Similarly, near-infrared (NIR) light-responsive systems utilize upconversion nanoparticles to convert NIR light into visible wavelengths, thereby activating optogenetic circuits that induce bacterial lysis or therapeutic secretion. These systems provide programmable control over bacterial lifestyles—planktonic, biofilm, or lytic states—through modulation of light intensity, allowing precise regulation of adhesion, colonization, and drug release during therapy (35). Integrating controllable expression systems with biocontainment strategies, such as synthetic auxotrophies or kill switches, is essential to ensure safety by preventing unwanted colonization and maintaining genetic stability. These synthetic biology approaches are driving the development of next-generation bacterial therapies that are both safe and effective within the TME (36) (Figure 6).

The synchronized lysis circuit (SLC), developed by Zhang et al. (37), represents a groundbreaking advancement in programmable bacterial therapy. This QS-based genetic oscillator utilizes the LuxI/LuxR system to monitor bacterial population density. When the acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL)-mediated threshold is reached, the φX174E lysis gene is activated, triggering synchronized bacterial lysis and payload release (36). A fraction of the bacteria survive lysis and re-seed the population, enabling repeated cycles of growth, lysis, and release. Thus, the SLC transforms bacteria from static delivery vehicles into autonomous, pulsatile drug-release systems. Although EcN-NO employs a fundamentally different circuit architecture—a constitutive metabolic pathway rather than periodic lysis—the SLC’s conceptual framework—that bacteria can be programmed to sense their in vivo environment and respond with controlled therapeutic output—directly informed the rational design of EcN-NO’s arginine-NO circuit (37).

Comparative analysis: distinctive features of EcN-NO compared to previous bacterial therapy platforms

Liu et al. (38) provided a comprehensive framework for understanding engineered bacteria as “interactive cancer therapies”. Their review outlined the evolution from using bacteria as single agents to rational combinations with other modalities, such as checkpoint inhibitors, chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells, and radiotherapy. They introduced the concept of “programmable medicines” that can be engineered to interact dynamically with both the TME and external therapeutic technologies. They emphasized that the next generation of bacterial therapies must move beyond simple payload delivery toward multidimensional remodeling of the TME (39). EcN-NO exemplifies this vision: rather than delivering a single immunotherapeutic payload (e.g., a nanobody), it generates a continuous metabolic output (NO) that simultaneously normalizes vasculature, remodels myeloid cell phenotypes, recruits dendritic cells (DCs), and synergizes with checkpoint blockade—achieving multidimensional TME remodeling through a single, self-sustaining circuit (20).

Fan et al. (40) provided the first demonstration that programmable bacteria can induce systemic (abscopal) antitumor immunity. Using an SLC-equipped EcN strain that releases CD47 nanobodies intratumorally, the authors showed that local treatment not only cleared injected tumors but also reduced the growth of uninjected contralateral lesions—a phenomenon known as the abscopal effect (41). Mechanistically, CD47 blockade enhanced macrophage phagocytosis of tumor cells, leading to cross-presentation of tumor antigens and activation of tumor-specific T cells that circulated systemically. This finding established a critical proof of concept: locally delivered bacterial immunotherapy can prime adaptive immunity that extends beyond the treated site. EcN-NO builds upon this principle but achieves systemic immunity through a distinct mechanism—NO-mediated vascular normalization and immune cell remodeling—rather than checkpoint nanobody blockade (42).

As shown in Table 1, EcN-NO differs from previous platforms in two primary aspects. First, its circuit architecture is based on a constitutive metabolic pathway rather than a lytic release system (43) or a passive reporter system. This design enables sustained, continuous production of NO without the cyclic population dynamics characteristic of the SLC, potentially providing more stable therapeutic concentrations within the TME. Second, its payload—NO—is a small, diffusible gasotransmitter rather than a protein therapeutic (such as HlyE, CD47 nanobody, or LacZ). NO’s membrane permeability allows it to act simultaneously on multiple cell types—including endothelial cells, macrophages, DCs, and T cells—without requiring bacterial lysis for release. These two features are interdependent: the constitutive metabolic circuit is uniquely suited to a diffusible payload whose therapeutic effect depends on sustained local concentration rather than pulsed bolus delivery. However, EcN-NO shares its chassis (EcN) and tumor-homing mechanism with prior platforms, and its current reliance on intratumoral injection remains a translational limitation, as also noted by (41).

Evidence-based section: EcN-NO—design, mechanisms, and synergistic efficacy

Circuit design and validation for NO production

The construction of a synthetic arginine-NO circuit in EcN involves three genetic modifications to sustain NO production. First, disrupting feedback inhibition by knocking out the argR gene enables continuous L-arginine synthesis (2). Second, introducing a feedback-resistant N-acetylglutamate synthase gene (argAfbr), along with the co-expression of argG and argH from the arginine regeneration pathway, stabilizes arginine supply (20). Third, incorporating a heterologous NO synthase (NOS), such as Bacillus subtilis NOS (BsNOS) from Bacillus subtilis, converts arginine into NO. Unlike mammalian NOS, BsNOS is a simpler enzyme that uses L-arginine and oxygen to produce NO and L-arginine-NO circuit. This design links enhanced arginine biosynthesis to NO production, with arginine-NO circuit recycled via the argG/argH pathway for continuous synthesis. Consequently, EcN is transformed into a localized, self-sustaining NO producer (Figure 7).

“Engineered arginine-NO circuit-NO circuit” to “engineered arginine-NO circuit (EcN-NO)”. EcN, Escherichia coli Nissle 1917; EcN-NO, EcN engineered with a synthetic arginine-NO circuit; NO, nitric oxide; NOS, NO synthase.

Engineered EcN-NO autonomously synthesizes arginine to provide substrates for BsNOS, enabling sustained NO production. In contrast, mammalian L-arginine transport and asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) regulation are tightly controlled by specialized transporters (44). This autonomous arginine synthesis allows engineered EcN to overcome nutrient limitations imposed by the TME and liberates bacterial BsNOS from substrate constraints, ensuring stable catalysis. NOS activity depends on substrates, oxygen, and cofactors, with bacterial NOS isoforms exhibiting distinct kinetic properties (45). Therefore, integrating autonomous arginine synthesis with kinetically optimized BsNOS ensures stable and efficient NO production.

Bacterial colonization combined with synthetic genetic circuits enables sustained NO production in hypoxic tumors, addressing the short-lived and poorly targeted nature of exogenous NO donors (46).

The circuit functions constitutively after intratumoral colonization; once established, the ΔargR knockout eliminates feedback inhibition, while the plasmid-encoded argAfbr, argG, argH, and BsNOS genes drive continuous NO production without requiring environmental sensing or regulatory feedback. Release kinetics depend on bacterial dynamics, analogous to sustained-release drug delivery. Mathematical models simulating key parameters in engineered probiotics are essential for predicting NO delivery in vivo (31). At controlled levels produced by EcN-NO, NO modulates immune cells and vascular permeability, potentially enhancing immunotherapy. This approach combines synthetic biology and therapeutic delivery to overcome the limitations of traditional NO therapies (Figure 8).

TME remodeling evidence: vascular normalization, DC recruitment, TAM repolarization, MDSC reduction, ICD induction

Tumor vascular normalization

Sustained NO production by EcN-NO induces tumor vascular normalization. NO acts locally on endothelial cells, enhancing pericyte coverage and reducing vascular permeability via eNOS signaling (20). Bioresponsive nanoreactors also contribute to vascular normalization (47), while NO-releasing nanoparticles, such as DNMF/PLGA, improve vessel integrity and drug penetration (48). This vascular remodeling significantly alleviates tumor hypoxia and immunosuppression, thereby creating a more favorable environment for immune cell infiltration and function.

Establishing a vascular normalization window is essential for enhancing the delivery of therapeutic agents, including chemotherapeutics and immunotherapies. NO-mediated normalization improves blood flow and reduces vascular pressure, thereby facilitating more effective drug distribution within tumors. This effect is exemplified by nanodelivery systems such as polymeric vesicles that co-deliver an NO precursor and paclitaxel for lung cancer; NO release in the acidic TME normalizes vasculature and enhances therapeutic outcomes (49). Chitosan-based nanospheres (ZFO/NO@CS), which release NO under acidic conditions, normalize blood vessels and improve doxorubicin efficacy in mouse models (50). Additionally, the ALMn nanoreactor demonstrated that vascular normalization combined with lactate depletion synergistically sensitizes tumors to αPD-L1 therapy, suppressing tumor growth and prolonging survival. The importance of vascular normalization for subsequent immunotherapy is further discussed in the “Synergy with αPD-L1: tumor regression, CD8+ T cell expansion, T cell exhaustion reversal, and immunological memory” section (Figure 9).

DC recruitment and activation

NO remodels the immunosuppressive TME by promoting DC recruitment and activation. EcN-NO constitutively produces NO through enhanced arginine regeneration and BsNOS expression (20). This sustained NO production by intratumorally colonizing EcN-NO has been shown to induce vascular normalization (the “Tumor vascular normalization” section) and recruit DCs to the tumor site (20). DC recruitment and maturation are essential for antigen presentation and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) priming. Other engineered EcN platforms, such as Te@EcN—a biohybrid synthesizing tellurium nanorods—promote DC maturation and act as immunoadjuvants in photothermal immunotherapy (51). The mechanism linking NO to DC function may involve modulation of the local metabolic and inflammatory milieu, as NO production can counteract immunosuppressive factors (52). EcN-based nanodrug-bacteria conjugates that degrade oncogenic collagen in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) further illustrate how microbial metabolic modulation overcomes immunosuppression (53). IL-2-secreting EcN biohybrid microrobots induce ICD—specifically pyroptosis—exposing tumor antigens and, in combination with PD-L1 blockade, reprogram the TME to elicit robust antitumor immunity (54). This NO-triggered cascade, from DC recruitment to T cell expansion, enables EcN to remodel the immunosuppressive TME into an immunostimulatory environment. The implications for αPD-L1 combination therapy are detailed in the “Synergy with αPD-L1: tumor regression, CD8+ T cell expansion, T cell exhaustion reversal, and immunological memory” section (Figure 10).

Reprogramming immunosuppressive myeloid cells

The EcN-NO circuit reprograms immunosuppressive myeloid cells, including MDSCs and TAMs, by shifting their phenotypes toward immunostimulatory states. Exogenous NO produced by EcN-NO may alter MDSC function, linking arginine metabolism to immunosuppression (55). Sustained NO production disrupts reactive nitrogen species (RNS)-dependent barriers in tumors such as PDAC, thereby overcoming T cell exclusion (55). Similarly, cannabinoids such as cannabidivarin (CBDV) reduce the expression of iNOS and Arg-1 in MDSCs (56).

EcN-NO also skews TAMs toward the anti-tumor M1 phenotype by modulating NO and arginine metabolism (57), a mechanism supported by BELAC therapy (41)and other Escherichia coli-based approaches. Sepiapterin restores tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), redirecting arginine metabolism toward NO synthesis and reprogramming M2-like macrophages into M1-like macrophages with enhanced antigen-presenting capabilities (57). EcN-NO likely induces a similar metabolic shift in TAMs by creating an NO-rich microenvironment. By reducing MDSC-mediated immunosuppression and reprogramming TAMs, EcN-NO removes barriers to effector T cell infiltration. The downstream effects on T cell function and synergy with αPD-L1 therapy are discussed in the “Synergy with αPD-L1: tumor regression, CD8+ T cell expansion, T cell exhaustion reversal, and immunological memory” section (Figure 11).

Direct cytotoxicity

NO plays a concentration-dependent role in cancer. At high concentrations, it directly kills tumor cells by causing DNA damage and inhibiting mitochondrial respiration, leading to apoptosis (58). Engineered bacteria, such as EcN-NO, produce sustained high levels of NO within tumors (20). Other approaches utilize bacteria or nanomaterials to generate peroxynitrite, which disrupts cellular metabolism and enhances immunotherapy (59). This direct cytotoxic effect complements the immune-mediated mechanisms discussed in the “DC recruitment and activation” to “Reprogramming immunosuppressive myeloid cells” section.

Synergistic ICD

The antitumor efficacy of EcN involves both direct cytotoxicity and the induction of ICD. Bacterial colonization acts as an immunological adjuvant through pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), recruiting macrophages and DCs (60). This inflammatory response is further amplified by sustained NO production from EcN-NO. Elevated NO levels damage tumor cells, triggering the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including calreticulin (CRT) and HMGB1—hallmarks of ICD (61). These signals, together with tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), are enhanced by bacterial presence and NO-induced cellular stress (62). This synergistic effect promotes the phagocytosis of dead tumor cells by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly DCs, facilitating cross-presentation of TAAs and activation of tumor-specific T cells. Engineered bacteria expressing CXCL16 recruit T cells, while those expressing CCL20 attract DCs, coordinating a robust immune response (63). Strategies combining bacteria with photothermal agents or ICD inducers demonstrate that bacterially enhanced ICD potentiates antitumor immunity, resulting in increased T cell infiltration and long-term immunological memory (61). The role of this in situ vaccination effect in combination with αPD-L1 is described in the “Synergy with αPD-L1: tumor regression, CD8+ T cell expansion, T cell exhaustion reversal, and immunological memory” section.

Synergy with αPD-L1: tumor regression, CD8+ T cell expansion, T cell exhaustion reversal, and immunological memory

Efficacy across multiple solid tumor models

The engineered EcN-NO strain demonstrated broad antitumor efficacy when combined with αPD-L1 in preclinical solid tumor models. Intratumoral colonization by EcN-NO remodels the TME through mechanisms described in the “TME remodeling evidence: vascular normalization, DC recruitment, TAM repolarization, MDSC reduction, ICD induction” section—vascular normalization, myeloid reprogramming, DC recruitment, and induction of ICD—thereby enhancing αPD-L1 immunotherapy and resulting in durable tumor regression across multiple cancer types, including colorectal and breast cancers. This synergy is further supported by other EcN-based strategies, such as adenosine deaminase expression under hypoxic conditions (52), IL-15 delivery combined with photothermal therapy (6), and copper selenide nanoparticle synthesis for macrophage repolarization (64), all of which demonstrate tumor suppression when combined with ICBs. These findings underscore EcN’s versatility as a platform for localized immunomodulation aimed at overcoming immunotherapy resistance.

Expansion of CD8+ T cells and reversal of T cell exhaustion

The combined treatment with EcN-NO and αPD-L1 establishes durable systemic antitumor immunity by expanding functional CD8+ T cells and reversing T cell exhaustion within the remodeled TME. The expanded T cell clones exhibit high expression of effector molecules, including IFN-γ and granzyme B, which confer potent cytotoxic activity (65). NO induction enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation (66); NO-driven nanomotors that normalize tumor vasculature increase T cell infiltration from approximately 0.5% to over 27% in mouse models (67). The underlying mechanisms—vascular normalization (the “Tumor vascular normalization” section), DC recruitment for antigen presentation (the “DC recruitment and activation” section), and myeloid cell reprogramming that removes immunosuppressive barriers (the “Reprogramming immunosuppressive myeloid cells” section)—collectively create the conditions necessary for this T cell expansion.

This therapeutic approach reverses the dysfunctional, exhausted state of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Exhausted T cells express multiple inhibitory receptors that impair their function (68). Combined blockade of PD-1 and IL-10 reverses T cell exclusion, effectively restoring TIL activity in fibrolamellar carcinoma (69). Modulation of NO reduces nitrosative stress induced by MDSCs (70), and NO-scavenging liposomes restore T cell function (71). This functional restoration is accompanied by transcriptional changes, as single-cell analyses reveal that exhausted T cell states are enriched in tumor-dense regions (72). Similar long-term immune memory has been observed with other EcN platforms, including personalized cancer vaccines (73), nanocomposite systems targeting triple-negative breast cancer (74), and platforms that inhibit metastasis and recurrence (51).

Establishment of immunological memory

Generating long-lived memory T cells is essential for sustained antitumor immunity. Combined treatment with EcN-NO and αPD-L1 promotes the development of central memory T cells (Tcm) and effector memory T cells (Tem) (75). In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), tumor-specific exhausted CD8+ and helper CD4+ T cells are associated with lymph node progenitors, supporting persistent immune responses (76). In pancreatic cancer, the combination of anti-CD40, αPD-L1, and IL-15C reduces T cell exhaustion and enhances the generation of long-lived memory cells (72); responsive patients maintain tumor-specific T cell clones for years (76). These findings confirm that EcN-NO, by remodeling the TME through the mechanisms described in the “TME remodeling evidence: vascular normalization, DC recruitment, TAM repolarization, MDSC reduction, ICD induction” section and synergizing with αPD-L1 as outlined above, induces tumor regression and durable immunological memory, effectively functioning as an in situ vaccine (Figures 12,13).

Unresolved questions

Biosafety and biocontainment: constitutive NO output, off-target toxicity, genetic stability, and clearance mechanisms

Engineered EcN strains require comprehensive biosafety assessments for clinical applications, focusing on colonization control, clearance, and risks associated with localized therapeutic production. Probiotics must survive within the TME while remaining confined to prevent colonization of healthy tissues (52). Strategies such as lactate-induced coagulase expression enhance confinement by creating a physical barrier. Following treatment, immune clearance typically eliminates EcN strains within days, with no persistent colonization detected in the bloodstream or non-target tissues (77,78). The bacteria’s immunogenicity facilitates clearance, and in situ lysis systems ensure payload release followed by bacterial elimination (37,79). Advanced platforms employing macrophage-encapsulated bacteria activated by dual tumor-specific signals provide additional biocontainment (80). EcN-NO is constitutively active upon intratumoral colonization; its NO production results from a constitutive metabolic circuit (81). Overall, the biosafety of EcN-NO relies on a multilayered approach: engineering tumor-restricted colonization using microenvironment-sensing circuits (82), implementing fail-safe mechanisms such as inducible lysis for post-therapy clearance (37), and employing controllable gene expression to restrict NO production to the tumor sit (81). Continuous monitoring of bacterial dissemination and immune responses will be essential to validate these safety strategies.

The clinical translation of engineered EcN strains necessitates rigorous preclinical safety and biodistribution profiling in relevant animal models to support an investigational new drug (IND) application. Although the parental EcN strain has a well-established safety profile in humans for gastrointestinal indications (83). the incorporation of synthetic circuits for therapeutic NO production modifies its risk profile. Therefore, GLP-compliant toxicology studies in immunocompetent, tumor-bearing models are essential to evaluate acute and chronic toxicity, pharmacokinetics (including colonization dynamics, persistence, and clearance), and risks such as systemic bacteremia or off-target NO production (84). Pharmacokinetic studies should quantify bacterial load in tumors and organs over time to confirm tumor-specific tropism (85,86). Additionally, the immunogenicity of both the bacterial chassis and synthetic proteins must be assessed, as neutralizing antibodies may reduce efficacy or trigger hypersensitivity reactions (87). Although synthetic biology tools enable compact circuits such as CRISPReader systems, they require thorough toxicological evaluation to predict in vivo behavior (88). This integrated preclinical package—encompassing toxicology, pharmacokinetics, and immunogenicity—is critical for de-risking clinical translation, defining safe starting doses, and monitoring NO-related toxicities (89).

The immunogenicity of bacterial vectors, such as EcN, poses a significant challenge for repeated therapeutic use. Although engineered EcN strains can stimulate antitumor immunity, repeated administration may induce host-neutralizing antibodies that eliminate the bacteria before tumor colonization, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy (73). Potential strategies to address this issue include evading immune recognition through surface modifications or leveraging immunogenicity to enhance adjuvant effects and promote ICD (36). Additionally, modulating host immune responses and thoroughly evaluating EcN’s safety profile—particularly regarding colibactin-induced DNA damage—are critical considerations (90). Therefore, a nuanced understanding of the interplay among bacterial vector immunogenicity, therapeutic payload delivery, and host immune activation is essential for developing sustainable and effective multi-dose treatment regimens.

Dosing, route, and timing: optimal bacterial dose, intravenous (IV) vs. intratumoral delivery, and the temporal window for αPD-L1 administration

Optimizing the dose, frequency, and timing of engineered EcN administration is essential to maximize synergy with ICIs, particularly by promoting vascular normalization prior to immunotherapy. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that remodeling tumor vasculature enhances immune cell infiltration (91). Dose optimization must balance effective intratumoral colonization and payload delivery with safety considerations. Tunable control systems, such as programmable encapsulation, have increased tolerated doses tenfold in mice (92). For EcN-NO, dosing strategies should ensure robust colonization to sustain NO production, with dosing frequency adjusted according to bacterial persistence within the hypoxic TME. Studies have shown that EcN can deeply penetrate tumors, producing therapeutic effects such as tumor regression over extended periods (e.g., 21 days) (93). Therefore, it is critical to determine whether a single sustained dose or multiple booster doses of EcN-NO are more effective.

The timing of αPD-L1 administration relative to EcN-NO is critical for achieving synergy. Administering immunotherapy after a TME-remodeling preconditioning phase is supported by evidence from other combination therapies. For example, in pancreatic cancer—and similarly in colorectal cancer models—an engineered EcN strain that modulates the TME demonstrated synergy with low-dose chemotherapy, suggesting that bacterial remodeling can prime tumors for enhanced efficacy of subsequent treatments (52). In the case of EcN-NO, a sequential strategy involves initial bacterial administration to induce NO-mediated effects—such as vascular normalization, modulation of macrophage polarization, and potential ICD—thereby converting the TME from immunosuppressive to immunoreactive (94). Following vascular and immune preconditioning, αPD-L1 can block the PD-1/PD-L1 axis on reinvigorated T cells, resulting in a more potent antitumor response. Clinical evidence from radio-immunotherapy underscores the importance of timing, where a delay between radiotherapy and immunotherapy was necessary to achieve a synergistic “PULSAR effect” (95). This principle likely applies to bacterial therapy as well, since immune signals from bacterial colonization and NO release require time to remodel the TME before checkpoint inhibition. Future studies should investigate the interval between EcN-NO and αPD-L1 dosing in preclinical models to identify the optimal sequence for tumor control and survival while minimizing adverse events (Figure 14).

The route of administration is critical for clinical applications. Intratumoral (IT) injection enables direct delivery but is limited to accessible tumors, making systemic approaches (e.g., IV) essential for treating metastatic disease. A key challenge for IV delivery is ensuring efficient tumor homing while minimizing off-target effects. Engineered EcN strains naturally target the TME. For example, EcN has been utilized in self-propelled microrobots for drug delivery, demonstrating enhanced tumor accumulation and deep penetration into hypoxic regions (93). EcN expressing a tumor-binding protein (HlpA) has also shown therapeutic benefits in a colorectal cancer model (96). To improve IV targeting, advanced strategies are being explored, such as engineering bacteria with surface modifications or genetic circuits to enhance specificity. A programmable encapsulation system for EcN, which provides temporary immune evasion, increased bacterial translocation to tumors, including distal sites (92). An innovative approach employs “Trojan horse” delivery systems, where bacteria are loaded into carrier cells, such as Salmonella-loaded macrophages, to evade clearance and enhance tumor accumulation (97). This method could protect EcN-NO during circulation and facilitate its release within the TME. Other targeted strategies include combining bacterial therapies with nanotechnology to create hybrid systems, such as an ultrasound-controlled bacterial system for localized release or EcN hybridized with gold nanorods for photothermal therapy and drug delivery. For deep-seated tumors, leveraging bacterial anaerobic metabolism—using obligate anaerobes like Bifidobacterium—is critical (98). Engineering EcN for stricter hypoxia-dependent gene expression could further improve its safety and specificity. Research on other bacteria, such as a consortium inducing intratumoral inflammation to synergize with oncolytic virotherapy, also offers insights for future EcN-NO targeting strategies (99).

Manufacturing and regulatory pathway: good manufacturing practice (GMP) production, quality control, IND-enabling studies

Transitioning genetically engineered EcN from the laboratory to clinical use requires overcoming significant manufacturing and quality control challenges. Scaling production under GMP standards involves unique complexities, including maintaining viability, genetic stability, purity, and potency. Key challenges include optimizing fermentation and purification processes without compromising function, developing preservation methods for long-term storage, and establishing assays to verify the absence of contamination and ensure genetic integrity (e.g., the arginine-NO pathway). These issues are further complicated for strains with complex inducible circuits, as population drift or plasmid loss could compromise safety or efficacy. The regulatory framework for these synthetic biology products is still evolving. Proactive engagement from scientists is essential to help develop policies that ensure safety without stifling innovation. Establishing scalable, reproducible, and cost-effective GMP manufacturing pipelines is a critical engineering challenge for enabling the widespread clinical availability of engineered bacterial therapies.

Predictive biomarkers and patient stratification

The clinical success of engineered EcN therapy depends on identifying responsive patient groups and developing predictive biomarkers. Given its mechanism of remodeling the immunosuppressive TME through localized NO release, initial patient selection should focus on solid tumors exhibiting features such as hypoxia and leaky vasculature, which facilitate bacterial colonization and maximize NO’s effects (86). Biomarker development must be integrated into early clinical trials. Imaging biomarkers, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or positron emission tomography (PET) scans assessing perfusion and hypoxia, can predict bacterial colonization. Additionally, fibroblast activation protein (FAP) imaging using (68Ga)Ga-FAPI-46 PET can identify tumors with a modifiable stromal compartment (100). Baseline immune profiling of biopsies is essential to characterize the “cold” TME—marked by immunosuppressive cells and low CD8+ T cell infiltration—that the therapy aims to activate (101). Patients with “cold” or immune-excluded tumors, who derive limited benefit from ICIs alone, represent a key target population. Predictive biomarkers include molecular indicators of NO activity, such as low baseline intratumoral cyclic GMP or elevated reactive oxygen species levels (102). Pharmacodynamic biomarkers for in vivo therapy monitoring are equally critical; serial or liquid biopsies can assess changes in immune cell populations, cytokine profiles, and transcriptomic signatures indicative of TME reprogramming (103). Therapy-induced reductions in immunosuppressive cells, such as FAP-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts or M2-TAMs, may serve as markers of biological activity (104). Noninvasive monitoring of bacterial colonization through reporter gene imaging could correlate colonization success with clinical response (87). By prospectively enrolling patients based on TME characteristics and evaluating these biomarkers in early-phase trials, the development of EcN-NO therapy can be guided toward those most likely to benefit.

Rational combination regimens: future exploration with CAR-T, cancer vaccines, radiotherapy

The therapeutic potential of EcN-NO is best realized when combined with established and emerging cancer treatments rather than used as monotherapy. NO modulates the TME by enhancing vascular permeability and promoting immune cell infiltration, thereby creating a favorable environment for adjunct therapies. Combining EcN-NO with ICIs can convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, overcoming resistance (20). Engineered bacteria also synergize with cancer vaccines and CAR-T cells by producing antigens or costimulatory molecules in situ (105). Combinations with radiotherapy are promising, as bacteria can enhance immune infiltration and amplify the abscopal effect (106). Additionally, EcN-NO may radiosensitize tissues. In chemotherapy, this platform can activate prodrugs or counteract chemoresistance. Understanding NO’s role in remodeling the TME, such as lactate scavenging, is crucial. Therefore, systematic preclinical and clinical investigations into rational combination regimens are essential to unlock the full potential of EcN-NO against diverse, resistant, and metastatic cancers.

Conclusions

The development of EcN-NO represents a transformative approach to remodeling the TME through localized and sustained NO production. This strategy addresses abnormal vasculature and immunosuppression, converting a “cold” TME into a “hot” one, and synergizes with ICIs (e.g., αPD-L1) to achieve potent antitumor efficacy and long-term immunity. However, clinical translation requires optimizing safety, determining optimal combination regimens, and overcoming manufacturing and regulatory challenges. Furthermore, the platform’s potential extends beyond oncology, and realizing its promise demands interdisciplinary efforts to balance innovation with practical considerations.
