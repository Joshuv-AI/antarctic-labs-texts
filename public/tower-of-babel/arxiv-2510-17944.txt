Abstract
Pearl’s do-calculus is a complete axiom system for interventional identification in acyclic causal models under classical (Boolean) logic. We generalize it to j j -stable causal inference inside a topos of sheaves 𝐒𝐡 𝒥 ​ ( 𝐂 ) \mathbf{Sh}_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{C}) , where regimes form a site ( 𝐂 , 𝒥 ) (\mathbf{C},\mathcal{J}) and observations/interventions are sheaves on that site. Our framework is an elaboration of the recently proposed framework of Topos Causal Models (TCM) Mahadevan 2025a , where causal interventions are defined as subobjects. We generalize the original setting of TCM using the Lawvere-Tierney topology on a topos, defined by a modal operator j j on the subobject classifier Ω \Omega . We introduce j j -do-calculus, where we replace global truth with local truth (Kripke–Joyal semantics) and formalize causal interventions as structure-preserving morphisms that are stable along 𝒥 \mathcal{J} -covers. j j -do-calculus is a sound rule system whose premises and conclusions are formulas of the internal (intuitionistic) logic of 𝐒𝐡 𝒥 ​ ( 𝐂 ) \mathbf{Sh}_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{C}) . We define j j -stability for conditional independences and interventional claims as local truth in the internal logic of 𝐒𝐡 𝒥 ​ ( 𝐂 ) \mathbf{Sh}_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{C}) . We give three inference rules that strictly generalize Pearl’s insertion/deletion and action/observation exchange, and we prove soundness in the Kripke–Joyal semantics. We show how these rules specialize back to classical do-calculus when 𝒥 \mathcal{J} is the trivial topology (Boolean case) and to regime-aware identification when 𝒥 \mathcal{J} encodes experimental covers. This paper develops the theory of j j -stable causal inference and a j j -do-calculus inside 𝐒𝐡 J ​ ( 𝒞 ) \mathbf{Sh}_{J}(\mathcal{C}) . Our focus is conceptual: we assume access to the theoretical objects (e.g., stages U U , J J -covers of U U , and the internal interventional distribution 𝖯 int \mathsf{P}^{\!\mathrm{int}} ) and study their logical consequences. A companion paper in preparation provides the algorithmic side – how to estimate the required entities from data and how to instantiate j j -do with standard discovery procedures (e.g., score-based and constraint-based methods) – which describes experimental results on how to (i) form data-driven J J -covers (via regime/section constructions), (ii) compute chartwise CIs after graph surgeries, and (iii) glue them to certify the premises of the j j -do rules in practice

Introduction

In this paper, we build on the recently proposed Topos Causal Models (TCMs)
[
Mahadevan 2025a
]
that formulates causal inference in the category of toposes. TCMs are part of a recent series of papers on categorical models of causality, including those using symmetric monoidal categories
[

,

[

can be translated into a categorical language. Operations on causal models, such as interventions, can be modeled as functors on the objects of the associated symmetric monoidal category or simplicial set. Categorical approaches to causality also extend to the

TCM is also part of a line of previous research of ours

[

[

using the Yoneda Lemma. For example, a structural causal model (SCM)
[

[

[

Causal Models Over a Topos of Sheaves

The categorical framework underlying Topos Causal Models (TCMs) introduced in
[
Mahadevan 2025a
]
defines causal inference in a topos by using the property that Yoneda embeddings of presheaves forms a topos
[
MacLane and leke Moerdijk 1994
]
. To ensure consistent extension into a unique global function, we build on the theory of sheaves
[
Mac Lane and Moerdijk 1992
]
, which ensures local functions can be “collated" together to yield a unique global function. In our setting, we will construct sheaves from categories over causal models through the Yoneda embedding
\text{\char 72\relax}(x):{\cal C}\rightarrow{\bf Sets}^{{C}^{op}}
and impose a Grothendieck topology. TCMs were originally defined over Grothendieck topologies on categories (known as sites), and we will generalize that formulation here to Lawvere-Tierney topologies.

3.3
Kripke-Joyal Semantics for Sheaves

Every topos has an internal intuitionistic logic that derives from the fact that the subobject classifier
\Omega
yields a poset of subobjects on which the semantics of a formal Mitchell-Bénabou language describing objects and arrows in the category can be defined. This formal language is associated with a Kripke-Joyal semantics, which we will specialize to a topos equipped with a Grothendieck topology, that is a site. This specialized structure captures how causal inference is woven in the fabric of the internal logic of a causal topos. Define
{\tt Sh}({\cal C,J})
be a topos of sheaves with a specified Grothendieck topology
{\cal J}
, defined by the following diagram, where
H
is the Yoneda embedding, and
{\cal P}
is a presheaf:

{\cal C}\xrightarrow{\text{\char 72\relax}}{\cal P(C)}\xrightarrow{a}\mathbf{Sh}({\cal C,J})\cong{\cal C}

where we know that the Yoneda embedding
H
creates a full and faithful copy of the original category
{\cal C}
. Let us define the semantics for a sheaf element
\alpha\in X(C)
, where
X(C)=\mathbf{Sh}({\cal C},J)({\cal C}(-,C),X))
. We will describe the Kripke-Joyal semantics in more detail later in the paper, but for now, a concise summary for the topos category of sheaves is as follows:

1.

C\Vdash\phi(\alpha)\wedge\psi(\alpha)
if it holds that
C\Vdash\phi(\alpha)
and
C\Vdash\psi(\alpha)
.

2.

C\Vdash\phi(\alpha)\vee\psi(\alpha)
if there is a covering
\{f_{i}:C_{i}\rightarrow C\}
such that for each
i
, either
C_{i}\Vdash\phi(\alpha)
or
C_{i}\Vdash\psi(\alpha)
.

3.

C\Vdash\phi(\alpha)\rightarrow\psi(\alpha)
if for all
f:D\rightarrow C
, and
D\Vdash\phi(\alpha\circ f)
, it holds that
D\Vdash\psi(\alpha\circ f)
.

4.

C\Vdash\neg\phi(\alpha)
holds if for all arrows
f:D\rightarrow C
in
{\cal C}
, if
D\Vdash\phi(\alpha\circ f)
holds, then the empty family is a cover of
D
.

5.

C\Vdash\exists y\ \phi(x,y)
holds if there is a covering
\{f_{i}:C_{i}\rightarrow C\}
and elements
\beta_{i}\in Y(C_{i})
such that
C_{i}\Vdash\phi(\alpha\circ f_{i},\beta_{i})
holds for each
i
.

6.

Finally, for universal quantification,
C\Vdash\forall y\ \phi(x,y)
holds if for all arrows
f:D\rightarrow C
in the category
{\cal C}
, and all
\beta\in Y(D)
, it holds that
D\Vdash\phi(\alpha\circ f,\beta)
.

-Do-Calculus on Sites

To transition from classical do-calculus to

Stages and generalized elements.

Let
(\mathbf{C},J)

5
Probabilistic Inference in TCM

In
[
Mahadevan 2025a
]
, causal models were defined either as objects of a topos category called
{\cal C_{TCM}}
(which will be often denoted simply by
\mathcal{E}
in this paper), or as functor objects in a presheaf topos
\mathbf{Set}^{{\cal C}^{op}_{TCM}}
. Here, we will get into a more specialized modeling framework, which is less general, but gives us the opportunity to develop a more refined language for translating classical do-calculus into an intuitionistic framework. We begin by introducing a categorical framework for probabilistic inference. Some of the main ideas that will be explained in detail below are summarized at a high level in Figure
4
and Table
5
, and Figure
5
gives a high-level summary. A standard way to model probability distributions categorically is through monads
[
Giry 1982
]
. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the case of distribution monads over finite sets. Let
\mathbf{FinSet}
(or more generally
\mathcal{E}
) denote the base category of finite sets (or objects of the ambient topos). The
distribution monad

[
Jacobs 2016
]

\mathsf{Dist}:\mathbf{FinSet}\longrightarrow\mathbf{FinSet}

maps an object
X
to the set of finitely supported probability measures on
X
:

\mathsf{Dist}(X):=\Big\{\,p:X\to[0,1]\ \big|\ \sum_{x\in X}p(x)=1\,\Big\}.

For a morphism
f:X\to Y
,
\mathsf{Dist}(f):\mathsf{Dist}(X)\to\mathsf{Dist}(Y)
is the pushforward of measures,

\mathsf{Dist}(f)(p)(y):=\sum_{x:\,f(x)=y}p(x).

The unit
\eta_{X}:X\to\mathsf{Dist}(X)
sends
x\mapsto\delta_{x}
(Dirac measure), and the multiplication

\mu_{X}:\mathsf{Dist}(\mathsf{Dist}(X))\to\mathsf{Dist}(X)
is integration (flattening of distributions of distributions).

The
Kleisli category

\mathbf{FinStoch}
of
\mathsf{Dist}
has:

•

the same objects as
\mathbf{FinSet}
,

•

morphisms
X\to Y
given by stochastic kernels
X\to\mathsf{Dist}(Y)
,

•

composition given by convolution:

(g\ast f)(x):=\int_{Y}g(y)\,df(x)(y).

We write
\mathsf{Dist}(Y)
also when
\mathcal{E}
is a topos and
\mathsf{Dist}
is lifted to an
internal distribution monad
,
so that morphisms
X\to\mathsf{Dist}(Y)
represent internal stochastic maps.
All constructions in this paper are interpreted internally in the co-Kleisli (Markov) category of
\mathsf{Dist}
.

5.1
The Co-Kleisli (Markov) Category of the Distribution Monad

Definition of the distribution monad.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a topos (or any cartesian closed category with finite coproducts)
equipped with a
distribution monad

\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E},

interpreting probabilistic choice or convex combination.
Each object
X\in\mathcal{E}
is mapped to an object
\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X)
of finitely supported
distributions over
X
, and each morphism
f:X\to Y
is mapped to the

pushforward

\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(f):\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X)\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y)
.
The monad structure consists of:

\displaystyle\eta_{X}

\displaystyle:X\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X),

\displaystyle\eta_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=1\,|x\rangle\quad\text{(Dirac embedding)},

\displaystyle\mu_{X}

\displaystyle:\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X))\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X),

\displaystyle\mu_{X}\!\left(\sum_{j}q_{j}\Big|\sum_{i}p_{ji}\,|x_{ji}\rangle\Big\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,i}q_{j}p_{ji}\,|x_{ji}\rangle,

which flatten a distribution of distributions.
This construction generalizes the classical Giry or finitely supported distribution
monad on
\mathbf{Set}
to internal settings such as presheaf toposes.

The co-Kleisli category.

The
co-Kleisli category
of
\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}
, denoted

\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}),

is the category having:

•

the same objects as
\mathcal{E}
;

•

morphisms
X\!\to\!Y
given by arrows
X\!\to\!\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y)
in
\mathcal{E}
,
written
f:X\!\Rightarrow\!Y
;

•

composition defined by
Kleisli convolution
:
for
f:X\!\Rightarrow\!Y
and
g:Y\!\Rightarrow\!Z
, their composite is

g\odot f\;:=\;\mu_{Z}\circ\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(g)\circ f\;:\;X\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Z).

The identity on
X
is the Dirac morphism
\eta_{X}:X\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X)
.
Associativity and unitality of
\odot
follow from the monad laws.

Intuition.

A morphism
f:X\Rightarrow Y
represents a
stochastic kernel
:
it assigns to each generalized element
x:N\!\to\!X
an internal probability
distribution
f(x):N\!\to\!\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y)
.
Composition
g\odot f
corresponds to
marginalizing over the intermediate
variable
Y
:

(g\odot f)(x)\;=\;\int_{Y}g(y)\,df(x)(y)\;=\;\sum_{y\in Y}g(y)\,f(x)(y)\quad\text{(internally in $\mathcal{E}$)}.

Hence
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}})
behaves as the internal version of the category of
finite-state Markov kernels, and is often called the

Markov category
of
\mathcal{E}
.

Commutative and strong structure.

The monad
\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}
is
commutative
and
strong
:
there exists a natural transformation

st_{X,Y}:X\times\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y)\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X\times Y),\qquad st_{X,Y}(x,p)=\sum_{y}p(y)\,|(x,y)\rangle,

which allows one to handle dependent random variables
and to interpret causal composition diagrammatically.
This strength is precisely what defines interventions:
given a context
\Gamma
, policy
\mu:\Gamma\Rightarrow Z
, and structural kernel

k:\Gamma\times Z\Rightarrow Y
, the interventional composite

\mathsf{Do}_{Z}(k;\mu)\;=\;\mu_{Y}\circ\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(k)\circ st_{\Gamma,Z}\circ\langle\textup{id},\mu\rangle

is the co-Kleisli composition
\Gamma\Rightarrow Y

that corresponds to integrating
k
against
\mu
.

Categorical properties.

The co-Kleisli category
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}})
satisfies the axioms of a

Markov category
(Fritz, 2020):

•

it has a symmetric monoidal structure inherited from
\mathcal{E}
;

•

the comonoid structure
(X\leftarrow X\times X\leftarrow X)

represents duplication and deletion of deterministic information;

•

every morphism
X\Rightarrow Y
is a stochastic map,
and deterministic maps arise from the embedding

\eta_{Y}\circ f:X\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y)
.

Thus
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}})
internalizes the category of

probabilistic processes, kernels, and causal mechanisms
.
It is the natural semantic environment for Topos Causal Models.

Connection to TCMs.

In a TCM, every causal mechanism

P(Y\mid\mathrm{Pa}(Y)):\mathrm{Pa}(Y)\!\Rightarrow\!Y

is a morphism in
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}})
.
Interventions and observations are morphism replacements or pullbacks
within this category, and all do-calculus equalities are internal equalities
between co-Kleisli arrows of
\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}
.

5.2
Example: Causal Models as Functors

1. Causal graph as a category.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{G}
be the free category generated by the graph

X\;\longrightarrow\;Y\;\longrightarrow\;Z.

Objects:
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C}_{G})=\{X,Y,Z\}
.
Morphisms:

\mathrm{Hom}(X,Y)=\{f\},\quad\mathrm{Hom}(Y,Z)=\{g\},\quad\mathrm{Hom}(X,Z)=\{g\circ f\},\quad\mathrm{Hom}(X,X)=\{\textup{id}_{X}\},\;\dots

2. Target category of stochastic maps.

Let
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist})
denote the co-Kleisli category of the
finite-support distribution monad
\mathsf{Dist}
on
Set
.
Objects are finite sets, morphisms
A\to B
are stochastic matrices

A\to\mathsf{Dist}(B)
, and composition is

(h\circ f)(a)(c)=\sum_{b\in B}f(a)(b)\,h(b)(c).

3. Causal model as a functor.

A causal model is a functor

F:\mathcal{C}_{G}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}),

given on objects and morphisms by

F(X)=\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\quad F(Y)=\{y_{1},y_{2}\},\quad F(Z)=\{z_{1},z_{2}\},

and stochastic matrices

F(f):X\to\mathsf{Dist}(Y)=\begin{bmatrix}0.8&0.2\\
0.3&0.7\end{bmatrix},\qquad F(g):Y\to\mathsf{Dist}(Z)=\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.1\\
0.4&0.6\end{bmatrix}.

Composition in
\mathcal{C}_{G}
gives

F(g\circ f)=F(g)\circ F(f),

which in
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist})
is the matrix product:

F(g\circ f)=\begin{bmatrix}0.8&0.2\\
0.3&0.7\end{bmatrix}\!\!\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.1\\
0.4&0.6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.8\!\times\!0.9+0.2\!\times\!0.4&0.8\!\times\!0.1+0.2\!\times\!0.6\\
0.3\!\times\!0.9+0.7\!\times\!0.4&0.3\!\times\!0.1+0.7\!\times\!0.6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.80&0.20\\
0.57&0.43\end{bmatrix}.

Thus
F(g\!\circ\!f):X\to\mathsf{Dist}(Z)
represents
the induced causal influence from
X
to
Z
.

5. Functorial laws.

The functor
F
preserves identities and composition:

F(\textup{id}_{X})=\textup{id}_{F(X)},\qquad F(g\circ f)=F(g)\circ F(f).

Hence, causal composition (via functional or stochastic dependence)
is represented as categorical composition in the Markov category.

6. Generalization inside a topos.

If
\mathcal{E}
is a topos, we can internalize this by taking

F:\mathcal{C}_{G}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}),

where each variable is now an object of
\mathcal{E}
,
each arrow a stochastic morphism in
\mathcal{E}
,
and the same functorial laws hold internally.
This internalizes causal semantics in any intuitionistic setting,
allowing higher-order, context-dependent, or sheaf-based models.

5.3
Interventions as Natural Transformations

Given a causal graph category
\mathcal{C}_{G}
,
a causal model is a functor

F:\mathcal{C}_{G}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}).

An intervention
do(X\!\sim\!\mu_{X})

produces a modified functor

F_{do(X)}:\mathcal{C}_{G}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}})

that agrees with
F
on all nodes except
X
and its outgoing arrows,
which are replaced by constant kernels using
\mu_{X}
.

The relationship between
F
and
F_{do(X)}

is expressed by a
natural transformation

\eta^{(X)}:F\Longrightarrow F_{do(X)}.

Each component
\eta^{(X)}_{V}:F(V)\to F_{do(X)}(V)

represents the “surgical” replacement effect of the intervention
propagated through the functorial semantics.

6
Internal logic of toposes

We review the concept of internal logic that is intrinisc to every topos, including TCM’s, and this logical language will play a central role in the extension of classical do-calculus to
j
-do-calculus.

6.1
Mitchell-Bénabou Language

We define the Mitchell-Bénabou language (MBL), a typed local set theory (see Section
6.3
) associated with a causal topos. Given the topos category
{\cal C}_{\Omega}
, we define the types of MBL as causal model objects
M
of
{\cal C}_{\Omega}
. For each type
M
(e.g., an SCM), we assume the existence of variables
x_{M},y_{M},\ldots
, where each such variable has as its interpretation the identity arrow
{\bf 1}:M\rightarrow M
. We can construct product objects, such as
A\times B\times C
, where terms like
\sigma
that define arrows are given the interpretation
\sigma:A\times B\times C\rightarrow D
. We can inductively define the terms and their interpretations in a topos category as follows (see
[
Mac Lane and Moerdijk 1992
]
for additional details):

•

Each variable
x_{M}
of type
M
is a term of type
M
, and its interpretation is the identity
x_{M}={\bf 1}:M\rightarrow M
(e.g.,
M
may be an SCM or a causal model on a Markov category).

•

Terms
\sigma
and
\tau
of types
C
and
D
that are interpreted as
\sigma:A\rightarrow C
and
\tau:B\rightarrow D
can be combined to yield a term
\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle
of type
C\times D
, whose joint interpretation is given as

\langle\sigma p,\tau q\rangle:X\rightarrow C\times D

where
X
has the required projections
p:X\rightarrow A
and
q:X\rightarrow B
.

•

Terms
\sigma:A\rightarrow B
and
\tau:C\rightarrow B
of the same type
B
yield a term
\sigma=\tau
of type
\Omega
, interpreted as

(\sigma=\tau):W\xrightarrow{\langle\sigma p,\tau q\rangle}B\times B\xrightarrow{\delta_{B}}\Omega

where
\delta_{B}
is the characteristic map of the diagonal functor
\Delta B\rightarrow B\times B
. These diagonal maps correspond to the “copy" procedure in Markov categories
[
Fritz 2020
]
.

•

Arrows
f:A\rightarrow B
and a term
\sigma:C\rightarrow A
of type
A
can be combined to yield a term
f\circ\sigma
of type
B
, whose interpretation is naturally a composite arrow:

f\circ\sigma:C\xrightarrow{\sigma}A\xrightarrow{f}B

•

For exponential objects, terms
\theta:A\rightarrow B^{C}
and
\sigma:D\rightarrow C
of types
B^{C}
and
C
, respectively, combine to give an “evaluation" map of type
B
, defined as

\theta(\sigma):W\rightarrow B^{C}\times C\xrightarrow{e}B

where
e
is the evaluation map, and
W
defines a map
\langle\theta p,\sigma q\rangle
, where once again
p:W\rightarrow A
and
q:W\rightarrow D
are projection maps.

•

Terms
\sigma:A\rightarrow B
and
\tau:D\rightarrow\Omega^{B}
combine to yield a term
\sigma\in\tau
of type
\Omega
, with the following interpretation:

\sigma\in\tau:W\xrightarrow{\langle\sigma p,\tau q\rangle}B\times\Omega^{B}\xrightarrow{e}\Omega

•

Finally, we can define local functions as
\lambda
objects, such as

\lambda x_{C}\sigma:A\rightarrow B^{C}

where
x_{C}
is a variable of type
C
and
\sigma:C\times A\rightarrow B
.

We combine terms
\alpha,\beta
etc. of type
\Omega
using logical connectives
\wedge,\vee,\Rightarrow,\neg
, as well as quantifiers, to get composite terms, where each of the logical connectives is now defined over the subobject classifier
\Omega
.

•

\wedge:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
is interpreted as the
meet
operation in the partially ordered set of subobjects (given by the Heyting algebra).

•

\vee:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
is interpreted as the
join
operation in the partially ordered set of subobjects (given by the Heyting algebra). This operation gives the definition of a disjunction of two properties.

•

\Rightarrow:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
is interpreted as an adjoint functor, as defined previously for a Heyting algebra. Thus, the property of implication over SCMs is modeled as an adjoint functor.

We can combine these logical connectives with the term interpretation as arrows, relegating some details to
[
Mac Lane and Moerdijk 1992
]
. We now turn to the Kripke-Joyal semantics of this language.

6.2
Kripke-Joyal Semantics for a Causal Topos

We now define the Kripke-Joyal semantics for the Mitchell-Bénabou language of a causal topos. Any free variable
x
must have some causal model
X
of
{\cal C}_{\Omega}
as its type. For any causal model
M
in
{\cal C}_{\Omega}
, define a
generalized element
as a morphism
\alpha:N\rightarrow M
. To understand this definition, note that we can define an element of a causal model by the morphism
x:{\bf 1}\rightarrow M
. Thus, a generalized element
\alpha:N\rightarrow M
represents the “stage of definition" of
M
by
N
. We specify the semantics of how an SCM
N
supports any formula
\phi(\alpha)
, denoted by
N\Vdash\phi(\alpha)
, as follows:

N\Vdash\phi(\alpha)\ \ \ \mbox{if and only if }\ \ \ \mbox{Im}\ \alpha\leq\{x|\phi(x)\}

Stated in the form of a commutative diagram, this “forcing" relationship holds if and only if
\alpha
factors through
\{x|\phi(x)\}
, where
x
is a variable of type
M
(recall that objects
M
of a topos form its types), as shown in the following commutative diagram.

Kripke–Joyal Forcing and Internal Semantics in a TCM

Before explaining how to prove the validity of do-calculus statements in a TCM, we need to provide more detail on the Kripke-Joyal intuitionistic semantics that constitutes the “semantic engine" in a topos.

Motivation.

In a classical set-based semantics, a statement such as
“
\forall x\in X,\,\varphi(x)
”
is true if
\varphi(x)
holds for each element
x\in X
.
In a topos, however,
elements
are generalized—they are morphisms

\alpha:N\to X
from a test object
N
.
The Kripke–Joyal forcing relation provides the inductive definition of what it means
for a formula
\varphi
to hold at a stage
N
and under a generalized element

\alpha:N\to\Gamma
.
This endows the internal logic of a topos with semantics analogous
to intuitionistic Kripke models.

Definition (Forcing relation).

For an elementary topos
\mathcal{E}
, the
forcing relation

N\Vdash_{\mathcal{E}}\varphi[\alpha]

reads “
\varphi
holds at stage
N
under assignment
\alpha:N\to\Gamma
.”
The semantics is defined inductively:

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\Vdash(x=y)[\alpha]

\displaystyle\text{iff }\alpha^{*}x=\alpha^{*}y\text{ in }\mathcal{E},

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\Vdash(\varphi\wedge\psi)[\alpha]

\displaystyle\text{iff }N\Vdash\varphi[\alpha]\text{ and }N\Vdash\psi[\alpha],

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\Vdash(\varphi\Rightarrow\psi)[\alpha]

\displaystyle\text{iff for all }u:N^{\prime}\to N,\;N^{\prime}\Vdash\varphi[\alpha\!\circ\!u]\Rightarrow N^{\prime}\Vdash\psi[\alpha\!\circ\!u],

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\Vdash(\exists x:A)\,\varphi(x)[\alpha]

\displaystyle\text{iff there exists an epi }e:M\to N\text{ and }a:M\to A\text{ s.t. }M\Vdash\varphi[a,\alpha\!\circ\!e],

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\Vdash(\forall x:A)\,\varphi(x)[\alpha]

\displaystyle\text{iff for all }u:N^{\prime}\to N,\text{ and all }a:N^{\prime}\to A,

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826ptN^{\prime}\Vdash\varphi[a,\alpha\!\circ\!u].

Truth is thus
monotone
:
if
N\Vdash\varphi[\alpha]
and
u:N^{\prime}\to N
, then
N^{\prime}\Vdash\varphi[\alpha\!\circ\!u]
.
This matches intuitionistic semantics where information grows along morphisms.

Interpretation in TCMs.

In a Topos Causal Model, contexts
\Gamma
denote joint variable spaces,
and a stage
\alpha:N\to\Gamma
represents a local assignment or partial observation.
A judgment

\Gamma\vdash\varphi

is true in
\mathcal{E}
if
N\Vdash_{\mathcal{E}}\varphi[\alpha]
for all
\alpha:N\to\Gamma
.
For instance, the sequent

\Gamma\vdash P(Y\mid do(Z),X)=P(Y\mid X)

is internally true iff for every
\alpha:N\to\Gamma
,
the two stochastic morphisms
N\to\mathsf{Dist}(Y)

given by
P(Y\mid do(Z),X)\circ\alpha
and
P(Y\mid X)\circ\alpha

coincide in
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}})
.
Hence equality of arrows is verified
stagewise
.

Forcing for interventions.

For
k:\Gamma\times Z\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y
and policy
\mu:\Gamma\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Z
,
the formula

\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{Do}_{Z}(k;\mu)=k_{0}

is true in
\mathcal{E}
iff
for every stage
\alpha:N\to\Gamma
,

\int_{Z}k(\alpha,z)\,d\mu(\alpha)(z)=k_{0}(\alpha)\quad\text{in }\mathsf{Dist}(Y).

Thus the “proof” of a do-calculus identity
reduces to verifying equality of integrals pointwise
at every generalized element
\alpha
.
This is precisely how the proof of Rule 1 was phrased.

Semantic intuition.

Each object
N
of
\mathcal{E}
is a “stage of information,” and morphisms

u:N^{\prime}\to N
represent refinements of context.
Kripke–Joyal forcing guarantees that
truth of causal assertions (e.g. conditional independence, intervention equalities)
is preserved by refinement:
if a relation holds at a stage, it holds at all more informative stages.
Hence, causal reasoning in a TCM corresponds to

constructive reasoning about local data
that remains stable
under restriction.

Summary.

Kripke–Joyal forcing provides the bridge between
syntactic sequents of internal logic and semantic equality of morphisms.
In particular:

•

generalized elements
\alpha:N\to X
replace concrete elements of
X
;

•

truth values are subobjects (elements of the internal Heyting algebra);

•

forcing ensures stagewise stability of causal equations.

Therefore, to prove a statement like

(Y\!\perp\!Z\mid\Gamma)\Rightarrow(P(Y\mid do(Z),\Gamma)=P(Y\mid\Gamma))

in a TCM,
one checks the equality at each stage
N
—exactly the Kripke–Joyal semantics
of internal equality.

A defining assumption of the Topos Causal Model (TCM) framework is that
causal semantics are given by functors

To obtain a universal semantics, we require that
\mathbf{C}
be

This ensures that every such functor extends uniquely to a

The classical case.

In the original formulation of TCM, the semantic category is

\mathbf{Prob}=\mathbf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist})

Because:

•

Context.

(4)

Equality and entailment symbols

.

10.4

Presheaf topos and internal distributions.

Fix a small category
\mathcal{C}
(of “contexts” or shapes). Let

\mathcal{E}\;=\;\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\;=\;\mathbf{Set}^{\mathcal{C}^{op}}

be the presheaf topos. For
F\in\mathcal{E}
, write
F(c)
for sections at stage
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
F(u):F(c)\to F(c^{\prime})
for restriction along
u:c^{\prime}\to c
.
Define the internal finite-support distribution monad pointwise:

(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}F)(c)\;=\;\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathbf{Set}}\!\big(F(c)\big),\qquad(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}F)(u)\;=\;\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathbf{Set}}\!\big(F(u)\big).

The co-Kleisli (“Markov”) category
\mathsf{Kl}(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}})
has the same objects as
\mathcal{E}
; morphisms
F\to G
are natural transformations
F\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}G
(“stochastic natural transformations”). Composition is pointwise convolution.

Objects and kernels.

A variable
X
is a presheaf
X\in\mathcal{E}
. A
stochastic kernel
in context
\Gamma
with a controlled variable
Z
and response
Y
is a natural transformation

k\;:\;\Gamma\times Z\;\Rightarrow\;\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y,

i.e. for each
c\in\mathcal{C}
a stochastic map
k_{c}:\Gamma(c)\times Z(c)\to\mathsf{Dist}(Y(c))
that is natural in
c
.

Observation via comprehension + normalization.

An internal predicate
\chi:\Gamma\times Z\to\Omega
yields its comprehension mono
\iota_{\chi}:\Gamma{\mid}\chi\hookrightarrow\Gamma\times Z
.
Given a prior
p:\Gamma\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Z
and likelihood
k:\Gamma\times Z\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y
, define the observed posterior

\mathsf{Obs}_{\chi}(k,p)\;:\;\Gamma\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y

stagewise by

\big(\mathsf{Obs}_{\chi}(k,p)\big)_{c}(\gamma)\;=\;\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{z\in Z(c)}\mathbf{1}_{\chi}(\gamma,z)\;k_{c}(\gamma,z)\;p_{c}(\gamma)(z)}{\displaystyle\sum_{z\in Z(c)}\mathbf{1}_{\chi}(\gamma,z)\;p_{c}(\gamma)(z)}.

(This is pointwise restriction along
\iota_{\chi}
followed by normalization.)

Intervention as kernel replacement + integration.

An intervention policy is a stochastic nat. transf.
\mu:\Gamma\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Z
.
The interventional kernel is the co-Kleisli composite

\mathsf{Do}_{Z}(k;\mu)\;:=\;\mu_{Y}\circ\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(k)\circ st\circ\langle\textup{id},\mu\rangle\;:\;\Gamma\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y,

whose
c
-component is the usual integral

\big(\mathsf{Do}_{Z}(k;\mu)\big)_{c}(\gamma)\;=\;\sum_{z\in Z(c)}k_{c}(\gamma,z)\;\mu_{c}(\gamma)(z).

Independence as factorization.

Conditional independence
Y\perp Z\mid\Gamma

in the cut model

M_{\overline{Z}}
is the internal naturality equation

k\;=\;k_{0}\circ\pi_{\Gamma}\;:\;\Gamma\times Z\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y,

i.e. each
k_{c}(\gamma,z)
ignores
z
and equals
k_{0,c}(\gamma)
.

Rule 1 inside the presheaf model.

If
k=k_{0}\circ\pi_{\Gamma}
in
M_{\overline{Z}}
, then for every policy
\mu:\Gamma\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Z
,

\mathsf{Do}_{Z}(k;\mu)\;=\;k_{0}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{E},

because pointwise
\sum_{z}k_{c}(\gamma,z)\,\mu_{c}(\gamma)(z)=k_{0,c}(\gamma)
.

Worked example (constant presheaves).

Let
\mathcal{C}
be arbitrary and take
constant
presheaves

X,Y,Z,\Gamma
with values the two-point set
\{0,1\}
, so all restrictions are identities.
Fix pointwise kernels (same at every stage
c
):

P(Y{=}1\mid X{=}0)=0.1,\quad P(Y{=}1\mid X{=}1)=0.9,\qquad P(Z{=}1\mid Y{=}0)=0.2,\quad P(Z{=}1\mid Y{=}1)=0.8.

Thus
k:\Gamma\times Z\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y
is
independent of
Z
(take
\Gamma:=Y
or impose the cut to
Z
), yielding
k=k_{0}\circ\pi_{\Gamma}
.
For any policy
\mu:\Gamma\Rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Z
(e.g.
\mu(\gamma)=\delta_{z_{0}}
for
do(Z{=}z_{0})
),

\big(\mathsf{Do}_{Z}(k;\mu)\big)_{c}(\gamma)=\sum_{z}k_{c}(\gamma,z)\,\mu_{c}(\gamma)(z)=k_{0,c}(\gamma),

so internally
P(Y\mid do(Z),\Gamma)=P(Y\mid\Gamma)
at every stage
c
.
By contrast, observation with
\chi(z){:=}[z{=}1]
produces

\big(\mathsf{Obs}_{\chi}(k,p)\big)_{c}(\gamma)=\frac{k_{0,c}(\gamma)\;p_{c}(\gamma)(1)}{p_{c}(\gamma)(1)}=k_{0,c}(\gamma)\quad\text{whenever }p_{c}(\gamma)(1)>0,

exhibiting the action/observation equivalence under independence.

Takeaway.

In presheaf models,
all causal operators are computed objectwise
, so translation of do-calculus into TCM language (independence
\Rightarrow
equality of interventional conditionals) reduces to pointwise finite-sum identities that are natural in
c\in\mathcal{C}
. This yields internal (Heyting) entailments that hold at every stage, hence globally in
\mathcal{E}
.

10.5
Exponential Objects and Their Role in TCMs

Definition.

Every topos
\mathcal{E}
is
cartesian closed
:
for objects
A,B\in\mathcal{E}
there exists an exponential object
B^{A}

and an evaluation map
\mathrm{ev}:B^{A}\times A\to B

such that for all
X
there is a natural isomorphism

\mathcal{E}(X\times A,B)\;\cong\;\mathcal{E}(X,B^{A}).

Hence morphisms depending on parameters in
A

can be re-expressed as internal elements of
B^{A}
.

Conditional kernels.

In a Topos Causal Model,
a stochastic kernel
k:\Gamma\times Z\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y)

can equivalently be seen as

\tilde{k}:\Gamma\to(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y)^{Z},\qquad k=\mathrm{ev}\circ\langle\tilde{k},\textup{id}_{Z}\rangle.

This interpretation allows causal mechanisms and conditionals
to be treated as
elements of an exponential object
.

Interventions as higher-order morphisms.

The intervention operator acts internally as a morphism between exponentials:

\mathsf{Do}_{Z}:(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y)^{\Gamma\times Z}\times(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Z)^{\Gamma}\longrightarrow(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y)^{\Gamma},

whose externalization sends

(k,\mu)
to

\mathsf{Do}_{Z}(k;\mu)=\mu_{Y}\circ\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(k)\circ st\circ\langle\textup{id},\mu\rangle
.
Thus interventions are higher-order arrows
within the cartesian closed structure of
\mathcal{E}
.

Quantifiers and forcing.

Exponentials support internal quantification over function spaces.
For example, the formula

(\forall f:Z\to Y)\,\varphi(f)

is interpreted using
Y^{Z}
, and its Kripke–Joyal clause reads:
for all
u:N^{\prime}\to N
and all
f:N^{\prime}\to Y^{Z}
,

N^{\prime}\Vdash\varphi(f)[\alpha\!\circ\!u]
.
Hence statements such as
“for every policy
\mu
” or
“there exists a kernel
k
”
are expressed directly in the internal logic.

Presheaf case.

When
\mathcal{E}=\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
,
exponentials are computed pointwise:

(F^{G})(c)\;=\;\mathrm{Nat}(G\times h_{c},F),

so an internal arrow
\Gamma\to(\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}Y)^{Z}

assigns to each
c\in\mathcal{C}

a natural family of stochastic maps

Z(c)\to\mathsf{Dist}(Y(c))
varying functorially in
c
.

10.6
Example: Generalizing Do-Calculus in a Simple TCM

Setup.

Consider a simple causal system with two observable variables

X
(treatment) and
Y
(outcome), and an optional confounder
Z
.
In the ordinary probabilistic semantics we have a factorization

P(X,Y,Z)=P(Y\mid X,Z)\,P(X\mid Z)\,P(Z),

and interventions replace
P(X\mid Z)
by a chosen policy
\mu_{X}
.

Internal TCM formulation.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a topos (e.g. the presheaf topos
\mathbf{Set}^{\mathcal{C}^{op}}
)
equipped with the internal finite-support distribution monad
\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}
.
Objects
X,Y,Z
represent the corresponding variables as presheaves,
and the causal mechanisms are stochastic morphisms

k_{Y}:X\times Z\longrightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y),\qquad k_{X}:Z\longrightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X).

The joint is the co-Kleisli composite

P(X,Y,Z)=(\textup{id}_{Z}\otimes k_{X}\otimes k_{Y}):1\longrightarrow\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X\times Y\times Z).

Observation and intervention.

Observation of
X=x
corresponds to restricting along the
comprehension subobject
\iota_{x}:Z{\mid}X{=}x\hookrightarrow Z\times X

and renormalizing.
An intervention
do(X\!\sim\!\mu_{X})
replaces
k_{X}

by a constant kernel
\mu_{X}:1\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X)
and composes via the
co-Kleisli operation:

\mathsf{Do}_{X}(k_{Y};\mu_{X})=\mu_{Y}\circ\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(k_{Y})\circ st\circ\langle\textup{id},\mu_{X}\rangle:Z\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y).

At each stage
c\in\mathcal{C}
this reduces to the ordinary formula

(\mathsf{Do}_{X}(k_{Y};\mu_{X}))_{c}(z)=\sum_{x\in X(c)}k_{Y,c}(x,z)\,\mu_{X,c}(x).

Independence and the first rule.

Suppose that inside the cut model
M_{\overline{X}}
the kernel

k_{Y}
does not depend on
X
, i.e.

k_{Y}=k_{0,Y}\circ\pi_{Z}
in
\mathcal{E}
.
Then by Kripke–Joyal semantics, for every stage
\alpha:N\to Z
,

\int_{X}k_{Y}(\alpha,x)\,d\mu_{X}(x)=\int_{X}k_{0,Y}(\alpha)\,d\mu_{X}(x)=k_{0,Y}(\alpha),

so internally

Z\vdash P(Y\mid do(X),Z)=P(Y\mid Z).

This is precisely Rule 1 (insertion/deletion of observations)
now expressed as an
internal equality of morphisms
.

Action/observation equivalence.

If, in the model
M_{\overline{X(W)}}
, we have

Y\!\perp\!X\mid(Z,W)
,
then both the observational restriction
and the interventional replacement yield the same morphism

k_{0,Y}:\Gamma\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Y)
,
giving the internal version of Rule 2.

Compositionality.

Nested interventions compose through the monad multiplication:
for any two controlled variables
X,Z
with policies

\mu_{X}:\Gamma\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(X)
and
\mu_{Z}:\Gamma\to\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}(Z)
,
the associative law of
\mathsf{Dist}_{\mathcal{E}}
ensures that

\mathsf{Do}_{Z}(\mathsf{Do}_{X}(k;\mu_{X});\mu_{Z})=\mathsf{Do}_{X\times Z}\big(k;\ \mathrm{mix}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Z})\big),

generalizing the classical “do–composition” of Pearl’s calculus
to any topos with a commutative distribution monad.

Intuitive summary.

At each stage
c\in\mathcal{C}
the TCM reproduces the ordinary
set-level equations of do-calculus,
but these equations now hold
internally and naturally

across all stages.
Kripke–Joyal forcing expresses this as:

\forall\alpha:N\to\Gamma,\quad N\Vdash P(Y\mid do(X),\Gamma)=P(Y\mid\Gamma),

so the familiar algebraic laws of interventions become

Heyting-algebra entailments
valid in the internal logic.
Thus, do-calculus in TCMs is not merely a translation:
it is a categorically natural generalization of Pearl’s rules
to any intuitionistic, higher-order setting.

Exchangeable
j
-Stable Causality

A central theme in modern causal inference is
symmetry
: many datasets consist of partially interchangeable units (patients, households, pixels). The recent “do-Finetti” viewpoint
[
Guo et al. 2023
]
studies do-calculus under exchangeability. In our setting, we formulate the analogue at the level of a
j
-site and show how
j
-stability interacts with permutation invariance.

Setup.

Let
\mathbf{C}
be a causal site and
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
its presheaf topos. Fix a Lawvere–Tierney topology

on
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
.
Write
\mathrm{Sym}(I)
for the finite permutation group of a finite index set
I
of units.
A family of random variables
X=(X_{i})_{i\in I}
, outcomes
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in I}
, and covariates

is represented by an arrow

\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{i})_{i\in I}:\;1\to\prod_{i\in I}\mathsf{X}_{i}\qquad\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{i})_{i\in I}:\;1\to\prod_{i\in I}\mathsf{Y}_{i}\qquad\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{Z}_{i})_{i\in I}:\;1\to\prod_{i\in I}\mathsf{Z}_{i},

with the natural
\mathrm{Sym}(I)
–action by permuting factors.

Definition 17

(
j
–invariant (exchangeable) family)
.

The family
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
is
j
–exchangeable
if for every
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}(I)
and every stage

, the equality in distribution

j

j

\pi

j

.

Let

j

G
–invariant

S
on
U
and every
g\in G

is again
j
–covering. Intuitively,
j

Interventions with symmetry.

For a finite treatment set
S\subseteq I

G

Proposition 18

Assume (i)

G

(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
is
j

j

\text{$j$\textendash}\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{Y}\mid\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X}_{S}=x),\mathbf{Z}\big]\;=\;\text{$j$\textendash}\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{Y}\mid\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X}_{\pi(S)}=x),\mathbf{Z}\big],

U

Related Work.

Closest to our setting is the recent “do–Finetti” line of work

Summary

In this paper, we introduced the concept of
j
-do-calculus, which extends classical do-calculus to the recently proposed Topos Causal Model (TCM)
[
Mahadevan 2025a
]
. Pearl’s do-calculus is a complete axiom system for interventional
identification in acyclic causal models under classical (Boolean) logic.
We generalize it to
j
-stable causal inference
inside a topos of

, where regimes form a site
(\mathbf{C},\mathcal{J})
and

We generalize the original setting of TCM using the Lawvere-Tierney topology on a topos, defined by a modal operator
j

j

(Kripke–Joyal semantics) and formalize causal interventions as
structure-preserving morphisms

\mathcal{J}

j
-do-calculus is a sound rule system whose premises and conclusions are formulas of
