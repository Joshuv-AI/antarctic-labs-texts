Impact of Teaching Strategies on Caries Detection and Management
Authors: Swetha Sriram, Swetha Perumal, Latha Nirmal, Priya Jayakumar, Gayathri Thiruvengadam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundThe increasing prevalence of dental caries highlights the need for effective diagnostic tools. Advances in e-learning and hybrid methods offer promising solutions to improve dental education and assessment. The study aimed to assess which learning method (e-learning, tutorial, and a combination of both) garnered a better response among clinical dental students for the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) and International Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS).Materials and methodsA sample of 90 dental students in the third, fourth, and final years of their dental education was selected to take part in the study, which was conducted in three phases. The learners were requested to answer a set of 15 questions given in a booklet, following which they were grouped into three groups (I, II, and III) by randomization: Group I (e-learning), group II (tutorial), and group III (e-learning + tutorial). Three weeks later, the groups were re-evaluated using the same set of questions. The data were analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare pre- and posttest scores across the three learning groups.ResultsOn comparing the three groups, there appears to be a substantial difference among all three groups, with group III showing a better significance than groups I and II.ConclusionThough the use of all three learning strategies showed an improved performance by the dental students, it was the use of a combination of e-learning and tutorial that produced a better outcome compared to the other two.Clinical significanceBlended learning enhances dental students' proficiency in ICDAS and ICCMS, emphasizing its efficacy in clinical education.How to cite this articleSriram S, Perumal S, Nirmal L, et al. Introduction

The increasing trends in the quality of food that is consumed and poor maintenance of oral hygiene are a few among the various physical, biological, environmental, behavioral, and lifestyle-related factors that have led to an increase in the prevalence of dental caries worldwide, causing pain and early loss of teeth, leading to masticatory problems.1,2 Hence, detection and diagnosis of caries become important to make an effort to reduce its prevalence. With dental caries being prevalent worldwide, a standardized visual examination tool was put forth to help with reproducibility and accuracy.3 International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS), a scoring system, was developed around 2002–2005 following two consensus conferences to help assess the severity of the lesion as early as an incipient caries.3,4 Recently, around 2012, based on ICDAS, the International Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS) was developed by a group of researchers that helps in the comprehensive management of dental caries, where the lesions were treated to preserve as much natural tooth as possible.5 It also enables patients to adopt home-care preventive measures to control caries.5 Considering the adroitness required for using ICDAS and ICCMS, early sensitization to these scoring criteria helps in better use of the system over the years.

The increase in the rate of students enrolling in the healthcare fraternity, the lack of clinical staff, and the emergence of diverse learning content pose a challenge in meeting the increasing demands of a better quality of learning and education.6 Advances in technology and integrating them into classic didactic lectures increase the availability of the learning material. Concepts of various learning strategies, such as e-learning, digital learning, and hybrid learning, have garnered widespread attention, as they help in overcoming the growing need of the healthcare education fraternity. Several papers have been published on the use of e-learning and hybrid learning in dentistry, such as the use in radiology, prosthetic dentistry, and histopathology.6–8 These learning strategies have also been explored in the management of dental caries, such as evaluating the performance of the detection of caries by the dental students.3 However, on the contrary, didactic lecture is still preferred among some learners and faculty as they facilitate better interactions between them.

Hence, the objective of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of three distinct teaching strategies (e-learning, tutorial-based instruction, and a blended approach combining both) in enhancing undergraduate dental students' understanding and application of the ICDAS and ICCMS. The study further aimed to evaluate which method resulted in the most significant improvement in knowledge retention and assessment performance.

Methods

Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) Approval

The study was approved by the Institution Ethics Committee (REF: CSP/20/JAN/83/34).

Study Setting

This was a randomized interventional educational study conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Sri Ramachandra Dental College and Hospital, Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research.

Study Sample

The cohort comprised officially registered dental undergraduate learners comprising the third, fourth, and final year (internship) of the Bachelor of Dental Surgery (BDS) program. The participants were sensitized about the conduct of the study. Written informed consent was obtained from those who agreed to participate. No learners were compelled to participate and were informed that they had the right to withdraw anytime during the study.

Inclusion Criteria

Third, fourth, and final year (interns) of students in the BDS program of Sri Ramachandra Dental College and Hospital, Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research.

Exclusion Criteria

(1) Undergraduates from other streams in the university, and (2) learners not willing to participate.

Study Plan

Though the study was planned to be conducted for around 3 months in three phases, the lockdown phase due to the COVID-19 pandemic extended the duration of the study. A flowchart explaining the study process is given in Figure 1.

Flowchart explaining the process of the study

First Phase

Learners undertook a theoretical pretest of 15 questions in a well-illuminated and ventilated classroom during regular working hours. The learners were informed of the place and time of the pretest. They were given a formulated questionnaire booklet and requested to choose the most appropriate answer. The participants were requested to answer all the questions within 20 minutes. Data were collected, and pretest scores were calculated.

Questionnaire

A set of 15 unordered closed-ended questions was formulated into a booklet. These questions were prepared only in the English language based on concepts of ICDAS and the ICCMS. The formulated questionnaire had been validated by two internal and one external examiner. The questionnaire was prepared under two domains. The first part included the year of study and the participant's mailing address, and the second part had 15 multiple-choice questions based on ICDAS and ICCMS. Specific instructions about the study were emphasized, and doubts were clarified by the research team. The participants were requested to circle the most appropriate answer and leave questions unanswered if they were not sure of the answer. The booklet aided in safe collection, better handling, and maintenance of the data.

Second Phase

Each participant was allotted a serial number that started from number 1 and proceeded onwards in a continuous fashion (e.g., 1, 2, 3, and so on). Later, a random number generator was used to randomize the participants into three learning groups (group I—e-learning, group II—tutorial, and group III—e-learning + tutorial) (Fig. 2).

Sample Size

As this study is exploratory and not directly based on previously published data, the sample size was determined using assumptions specific to the expected outcomes of this research. An improvement of 20% was anticipated in group I and 55% in group III, reflecting a 35% difference between groups. With a power of 80% and a significance level of 5%, the required sample size was estimated to be 29 participants per group. This was adjusted to 30 participants per group, resulting in a total of 90 participants for the study. After randomization of the students into the three learning groups, the first 30 of each group were selected to form a sample of 90 clinical dental undergraduates. In case of the participant's inability to join on the day of the study, the next participant was selected from the randomized groups.

Third Phase

It was conducted online 3 weeks post-phase II, during the lockdown period of the COVID-19 pandemic. The protocol was deviated, and Google Forms were created encompassing the same 15 closed-ended questions. The Google Form link was mailed to the participants of the three groups. The data were collected and exported for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) test was used to compare the postintervention test values after adjusting for pretest values among the three groups. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used to compare the mean difference in the pretest and posttest values. Data were analyzed by the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 16 (SPSS, IBM).

Discussion

This study was undertaken to evaluate clinical dental undergraduates' best favorable reception among the three learning methods. In recent times, clinical evaluation has been inclined more toward the diagnosis of an incipient lesion. The application of ICDAS and ICCMS aids in scoring a lesion before its cavitation and guides to better treatment outcomes.9 As ICDAS and ICCMS provide standardized protocols in detecting both cavitated and noncavitated lesions along with their treatment modalities, they were included as an assessment tool to sensitize undergraduate learners. It helped learners to improve their skills in distinguishing carious lesions in daily practice, paving the way for more preventive therapy and preservation of tooth structure.

This research compared three learning strategies to find out which method was best preferred by learners, including self-learning through a link mailed to the students, a tutorial-based program, and a combination of both. A key component of the study methodology was the structured questionnaire employed for both pre- and postintervention assessments. This tool consisted of 15 multiple-choice questions specifically designed to evaluate the students' theoretical knowledge and applied understanding of ICDAS and ICCMS concepts. The questionnaire encompassed a broad spectrum of topics, including lesion staging, diagnostic coding, caries risk assessment, and management protocols. Furthermore, the inclusion of questions targeting different levels of cognitive processing from factual recall to clinical reasoning provided a well-rounded evaluation.

In a study conducted by Luz et al., the performance of dental students was evaluated after the introduction of digital learning and e-learning methods in the detection of caries clinically in patients.3 The same training methods were compared using extracted teeth using the ICDAS scoring system in a study by Alves et al.10 In our study, similar research was carried out using three training systems in a nonclinical setup. Our study revealed an outcome that the use of combined learning produced better posttest results. Though all three groups showed improvement in the posttest, group III (tutorial + e-learning) showed better results than group II (tutorial) and group I (e-learning).

Though e-learning is available and accessible at any point in time, learners seem to prefer a more physical interactive session with the facilitator to understand certain clinical concepts better, which can be brought through these combination learning strategies (tutorial + e-learning).11 It has become evident in recent COVID times, where everyone had to resort to online teaching, that the quality of students' performance through self-learning was not substantial enough when compared to face-to-face learning and blended learning, which was apparent in our test results.

This study demonstrates several strengths. Firstly, its methodology is well-structured, incorporating a systematic approach that includes pretest assessments, random group assignments, and posttest evaluations. Secondly, the study is attuned to contemporary trends in dental education, notably the integration of technology through e-learning, which is compared against conventional tutorial methods. Thirdly, by including a combined group (e-learning + tutorial), the study offers a thorough examination of diverse learning strategies, enhancing its comprehensiveness and relevance to modern educational practices in dentistry. Consistent with existing literature,12–15 the present study underscores the effectiveness of structured educational strategies incorporating ICDAS and ICCMS in enhancing diagnostic accuracy and learner competence in caries assessment. Our limitations are the fact that the study was conducted only in one dental college, and hence, the results cannot be generalized. Further, it carries the risks associated with online surveys, where there could be the possible exchange of answers with the learners during their participation. More research is warranted regarding the combined impact of e-learning and tutorial methods.

Conclusion

The following conclusions can be drawn from the study:

The integration of e-learning and tutorial-based instruction significantly improved students' retention and posttest performance in ICDAS and ICCMS, surpassing the effectiveness of each method used independently.

Clinical Significance

The combination of e-learning and tutorial sessions optimizes dental education by reinforcing students' comprehension and application of ICDAS and ICCMS. This approach enhances diagnostic precision, supports early intervention strategies, and strengthens clinical decision-making, ultimately contributing to improved patient care and oral health outcomes.
