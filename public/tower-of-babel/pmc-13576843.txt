Development and Expert Consensus of a Stage-Based, Needs-Oriented Non-Pharmacological Sleep Management Program for Liver Transplant Recipients
Authors: Ningning Su, Jiawei Zhang, Jin Xia, Yanfei Wu, Lan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSleep disturbance is common among liver transplant recipients and is associated with impaired daytime functioning, psychological distress, impaired post-transplant recovery, and reduced quality of life. However, sleep-related problems and care needs may change across the transplant trajectory, whereas existing non-pharmacological interventions have not been systematically structured to address these stage-specific needs. This study aimed to develop a stage-based, needs-oriented, non-pharmacological sleep management program for liver transplant recipients.MethodsThis methodological study comprised two phases. In Phase 1, a preliminary program was developed through a literature review, qualitative interviews with 13 dyads of liver transplant recipients and their primary caregivers, and research team discussions. These findings were integrated to identify stage-specific sleep-related needs and inform intervention selection and adaptation. In Phase 2, 16 experts in liver transplantation medicine and liver transplantation nursing, sleep medicine, and traditional Chinese medicine nursing participated in a two-round Delphi consultation to evaluate and refine the program.ResultsBoth Delphi rounds achieved 100% response rates, with expert authority coefficients of 0.89 and 0.91, respectively. Item importance ratings were high, variability decreased across rounds, and expert agreement was statistically significant across all three program levels. The final program comprised 3 primary components, 11 secondary components, and 29 tertiary items spanning the pre-transplant, postoperative, and early transitional stages. Components were classified as core, optional adjunctive, with a referral-informed management pathway incorporated when additional clinical assessment or specialist collaboration was required.ConclusionA structured, consensus-informed, stage-based, needs-oriented sleep management program was developed for liver transplant recipients. It aligns intervention components with stage-specific sleep-related needs, symptom severity, safety considerations, and transplant-specific care requirements, providing a framework for subsequent feasibility testing and controlled evaluation.Keywords: liver transplantation, sleep initiation and maintenance disorders, nursing care, consensus, delphi technique

Introduction

Liver transplantation is a critical treatment for improving prognosis and prolonging survival in patients with end-stage liver disease.1 Despite advances in surgical techniques, perioperative care, and immunosuppressive therapy,2 recipients continue to experience substantial physical and psychological symptom burden during perioperative recovery and the transition to life after transplantation.3 Sleep disturbance is common during this period and may manifest as difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, fragmented or non-restorative sleep, reduced sleep efficiency, and daytime sleepiness.4 These problems are clinically consequential because they may coexist with fatigue, psychological distress, impaired daytime functioning, impaired post-transplant recovery, and reduced quality of life.5

The challenge is that sleep disturbance in liver transplant recipients is not static across the transplant course. Sleep problems are evident before transplantation and may persist after transplantation,6,7 while their clinical context changes with disease burden, psychosocial adjustment, and increasing self-management demands.8 As patients move from the pre-transplant stage through postoperative recovery to the early transitional stage, the factors relevant to sleep—and the feasibility and safety of available interventions—also change.9 A strategy appropriate before transplantation or after discharge may not be feasible or optimal during acute postoperative recovery.10 Effective sleep management must therefore account not only for what sleep-related problem is present, but also for when it occurs in the transplant course.9,11

This stage-dependent complexity has important implications for how existing intervention evidence is translated into care. A network meta-analysis showed that several non-pharmacological interventions may improve sleep and related psychosocial outcomes in liver transplant recipients, with relative benefits differing across sleep disturbance, anxiety, depression, and quality of life; sleep hygiene education ranked highest for sleep outcomes.12 These findings informed the inclusion of multiple non-pharmacological strategies in the present program and supported an approach that does not rely on a single modality to address heterogeneous sleep-related needs. A randomized controlled trial further showed that a multicomponent postoperative nursing program integrating psychological care, lifestyle guidance, pain management, and rehabilitation training improved sleep and psychological outcomes during follow-up.13 This trial provided direct evidence that multiple nursing components can be integrated within post-transplant care and informed the multicomponent orientation of the present program. However, it evaluated a predefined postoperative care package rather than a sleep-focused pathway in which intervention selection changes according to transplant stage, individual sleep-related need, and clinical risk.13 Likewise, the network meta-analysis compared intervention effects but did not specify how available strategies should be selected, combined, or escalated in routine practice.12 The remaining gap is therefore not the absence of comprehensive interventions, but the lack of an explicit framework for translating available strategies into stage-appropriate, needs-matched, and clinically actionable sleep management.

To bridge this gap, the program was organized around three linked clinical decisions. First, transplant stage defines what can be offered appropriately and safely.14 The pre-transplant, postoperative, and early transitional stages differ in their dominant sleep-disrupting factors, treatment priorities, functional limitations, and safety constraints; the same intervention is therefore not assumed to be equally appropriate across stages.15 Second, individual assessment determines what is needed within each stage.16 Core components provide a consistent foundation for routine sleep management, whereas optional adjunctive components are selected according to specific sleep problems, contributing factors, patient preferences, and clinical context. Third, a referral-informed management pathway defines when routine nurse-led management is insufficient. Greater symptom severity, psychological risk, suspected sleep disorders, medication-related concerns, or other clinical risks prompt additional assessment or specialist collaboration. In practical terms, transplant stage constrains the available options, individual sleep-related needs guide intervention selection, and clinical risk triggers escalation. This structure integrates core and optional adjunctive interventions with a referral-informed management pathway, thereby linking assessment, interpretation, intervention selection, and escalation of care.

Accordingly, this study aimed to develop a stage-based, needs-oriented sleep management program for liver transplant recipients by integrating current evidence with multidisciplinary expert consensus. The intended contribution was not a new individual sleep intervention, but a structured framework for organizing existing strategies according to transplant stage, individual sleep-related needs, and the boundaries of nurse-led management. The resulting program provides a basis for subsequent evaluation of its feasibility and clinical effectiveness.

Materials and Methods

Phase 1: Development of the Preliminary Program

Formation of the Research Team

A nine-member research team was established to oversee the development of the sleep management program. The team comprised two postgraduate supervisors, including one head nurse from the department of hepatobiliary surgery and one professor from a school of nursing; one director and one deputy director of hepatobiliary surgery; and five nursing postgraduate students. The postgraduate supervisors, director, and deputy director were responsible for study design, methodological guidance, quality control, and selection of experts for the Delphi consultation. The nursing postgraduate students conducted the literature review, extracted and organized relevant evidence, summarized the qualitative findings, developed the preliminary item pool, prepared the Delphi consultation questionnaires, coordinated questionnaire distribution and collection, and performed data entry and statistical analysis. Regular research team meetings were held throughout program development to review methodological and clinical issues, integrate information from different sources, and refine the content and structure of the program.

Operationalization of Sleep Disturbance for Program Development

For the purpose of program development, sleep disturbance was operationalized as clinically relevant sleep-related problems occurring during the pre-transplant, postoperative, and early transitional stages in liver transplant recipients. These problems could involve sleep quantity, quality, continuity, sleep-wake rhythm, or sleep-related behaviors and included difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, sleep fragmentation, non-restorative sleep, daytime sleepiness, and fatigue when accompanied by subjective distress, impaired daytime functioning, reduced recovery experience, or increased health-related risk.17 The program focused primarily on sleep-related problems potentially manageable through nurse-led assessment, comfort measures, environmental optimization, sleep education, behavioral strategies, family support, and early follow-up.

The Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) was used to assess insomnia symptom severity and associated daytime impact. Conventional severity bands were 0–7 for no clinically significant insomnia, 8–14 for subthreshold insomnia, 15–21 for moderate insomnia, and 22–28 for severe insomnia.18 An ISI score of ≥11 was used as the clinical-sample case-detection threshold prompting additional clinical assessment.19 Psychological risk was screened using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21), with each subscale score multiplied by 2 before interpretation.20 Depression, anxiety, and stress scores of ≥10, ≥8, and ≥15, respectively, were considered indicative of elevated symptoms and prompted additional clinical assessment rather than serving as diagnostic criteria.20

The program was not intended to establish formal diagnoses of sleep or mental health disorders. Further clinical assessment was prompted by an ISI score of ≥11, DASS-21 subscale scores reaching the above thresholds, suspected sleep-disordered breathing, abnormal sleep behaviors, marked daytime hypersomnolence, severe psychological symptoms, complex medication-related concerns, or other findings requiring assessment beyond routine nursing scope. Referral-informed management was initiated when further assessment indicated that nurse-led non-pharmacological management alone was insufficient to address the patient’s clinical needs or risks. Nurses performed preliminary screening, communicated relevant findings to the transplant physician, and facilitated specialist consultation or collaborative assessment involving sleep medicine, mental health, pharmacy, or other relevant disciplines when clinically indicated. Appropriate core nursing components could continue during this process, whereas optional adjunctive interventions were individualized according to the patient’s clinical condition and safety assessment.

Literature Review

A literature review was conducted to identify non-pharmacological interventions relevant to sleep management in transplant recipients and to generate candidate components for the preliminary program. The review considered both direct evidence from liver transplant recipients and potentially transferable evidence from other solid organ transplant populations. Evidence involving liver transplant recipients was prioritized, whereas studies involving other transplant populations were considered according to the clinical relevance and transferability of their population, intervention context, and timing to liver transplant care.

The literature search was conducted in the English-language databases PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Embase, and CINAHL and the Chinese-language databases Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, Wanfang Data, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and VIP Database. The search period was January 2015 to December 2025. Detailed database-specific search strategies and the study-selection process are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) studies involving liver transplant recipients or other solid organ transplant recipients whose population and intervention context were considered relevant and transferable to liver transplant care; (2) studies addressing non-pharmacological interventions related to sleep disturbance, insomnia symptoms, sleep quality, or sleep-related psychological symptoms; and (3) randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, clinical guidelines, expert consensus documents, or other evidence sources relevant to intervention development. The exclusion criteria were duplicate publications, unavailable full text, insufficiently described intervention content, review articles, and studies in which the population, intervention context, outcomes, or study focus were not applicable to the scope of sleep management in liver transplant recipients.

After screening, six randomized controlled trials13,21–25 and four quasi-experimental studies26–29 were included. Extracted intervention components included sleep hygiene education, cognitive and behavioral sleep strategies, mindfulness-based interventions, traditional Chinese medicine nursing techniques, environmental modification, and follow-up management. The level of evidence supporting the extracted intervention components was classified according to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Levels of Evidence.30 The evidence level and clinical transferability of each component were subsequently considered during the development of the preliminary program.

Qualitative Interviews to Identify Stage-Specific Needs

To identify stage-specific sleep-related needs, data from a broader qualitative study conducted by the research team were used for program development. The broader study was ongoing at the time of program development and remained unpublished. Guided by the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM),31 the qualitative study explored sleep-related experiences, perceived causes, coping strategies, care-related barriers, support resources, and unmet needs among liver transplant recipients and their primary caregivers. At the time of program development, interview data from 13 liver transplant recipient-primary caregiver dyads were available and were included in the present analysis.

Semi-structured dyadic interviews were conducted using an interview guide developed around these domains. Interviews were undertaken by two trained researchers, comprising a head nurse and a nursing postgraduate student. Neither interviewer was involved in the participants’ direct clinical treatment or routine nursing care, and neither had a prior relationship with the participating recipients or caregivers. Interviews were conducted in a private single-patient hospital room during hospitalization or in a private consultation room at the follow-up clinic. Each interview lasted approximately 20–38 min. Audio recordings and field notes were transcribed within 24 h after each interview, and transcripts were imported into NVivo 15.0 for data management and coding. The interview guide is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Two researchers analyzed the data using directed content analysis.32 Disagreements were resolved through discussion; when disagreement persisted, a third researcher participated until agreement was reached. For the program-development analysis, no new categories relevant to the targeted transplant stages emerged after the 11th dyadic interview, and two additional dyads were analyzed to confirm analytic sufficiency. Recruitment for the broader qualitative study continued because its scope extended beyond the stages addressed in the present program and included liver transplant recipients up to 2 years after transplantation.

For program development, sleep-related experiences and needs were organized into three clinically relevant stages: the pre-transplant stage, the postoperative stage, and the early transitional stage.14 During the pre-transplant stage, prominent sleep-related concerns included liver disease-related somatic symptoms, psychological stress associated with transplant waiting, disease uncertainty, and financial concerns. During the postoperative stage, sleep was disrupted by intensive monitoring, frequent clinical care activities, pain, abdominal drainage tubes, diarrhea, and other sources of physical discomfort. During the early transitional stage, sleep-related problems were associated with maladaptive sleep behaviors, inaccurate sleep-related beliefs, uncertainty regarding recovery, and insufficient self-management support. These findings were used to identify the predominant sleep-related needs and sleep-disrupting factors at each stage and to inform the subsequent matching and adaptation of candidate intervention components.

The overarching research project was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. [2025(353)]) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All liver transplant recipients and primary caregivers participating in the qualitative interviews provided written informed consent before data collection. Participants were informed of the study purpose, interview procedures, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any time without affecting their clinical care.

Drafting the Preliminary Program

The preliminary program was developed through a three-step mapping process. First, the research team summarized the predominant sleep-related needs, sleep-disrupting factors, and care-context characteristics identified at each transplant stage. Second, literature-derived candidate components relevant to these needs were identified and considered according to the level, directness, and clinical applicability of the supporting evidence. Where not directly corresponding, components were identified in the literature review, additional candidate components and operational details were informed by the qualitative findings and transplant-specific clinical considerations. Third, the resulting candidate components were mapped to stage-specific needs and adapted to the liver transplantation context through repeated research team discussions.

Candidate components were considered according to: (1) their relevance to stage-specific sleep-related needs; (2) the level and clinical applicability of the supporting evidence; (3) transplant-specific safety considerations, including immunosuppression, medication-related risks, infection prevention, postoperative physical condition, and psychological status; (4) professional scope and implementation requirements; and (5) the need for individualized selection according to symptoms, clinical condition, patient preference, or available resources.

During development of the preliminary program, the research team established an operational classification of intervention components as core or optional adjunctive, together with a referral-informed management pathway. Core components were those considered broadly applicable to eligible patients at the relevant stage and suitable for routine nurse-led care. Optional adjunctive components were selected according to individual symptoms, needs, preferences, clinical condition, and the availability of appropriately trained personnel or resources. The referral-informed management pathway was applied when symptom severity, diagnostic uncertainty, psychological or medication-related risks, transplant-specific safety considerations, or professional scope requirements warranted additional assessment or collaborative management with relevant specialists. The initial classification and referral pathway were subsequently refined based on Delphi feedback.

The level and directness of the supporting evidence were considered when determining whether an intervention was positioned as a core or optional adjunctive component, whereas clinical safety and professional scope considerations informed activation of the referral-informed management pathway. Appropriate core nursing components could continue while additional clinical assessment or collaborative management was undertaken, whereas optional adjunctive interventions requiring further clinical assessment or safety review were implemented only when considered appropriate for the individual patient.

When information from the literature review, qualitative findings, and clinical judgments was not fully consistent, decisions were guided by clinical relevance, transplant-specific safety, and applicability of the supporting evidence. Direct evidence from liver transplant recipients and transplant-specific safety considerations were given priority when conflicting recommendations arose. Qualitative findings were used to determine stage-specific needs and contextual fit, whereas clinical judgment and subsequent Delphi expert feedback were used to refine intervention content, delivery procedures, and professional roles. Disagreements within the research team were resolved through discussion until agreement was reached. A logic model was developed to illustrate the relationships among transplant stages, sleep-related needs, intervention matching, transplant-specific adaptations, professional involvement, and expected outcomes. Phase 2: Delphi Consensus Process

A two-round modified Delphi consultation was used to obtain iterative judgments from a multidisciplinary expert panel regarding the importance of and proposed refinements to program components.33 Independent rating, anonymity among panelists, and controlled feedback between rounds were maintained throughout the consultation process.33 The study protocol was not prospectively registered. Reporting of the Delphi phase was checked against the Delphi studies in social and health sciences—Recommendations for an interdisciplinary standardized reporting (DELPHISTAR) guideline.34 The completed checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 4.

Selection of Delphi Experts

Purposive sampling was used to recruit experts with experience relevant to liver transplantation care, sleep management, and nursing intervention development. Sixteen experts were invited from four tertiary Grade A hospitals located in Hunan Province, Sichuan Province, and Chongqing Municipality, China. The panel primarily comprised professionals in liver transplantation medicine and liver transplantation medicine nursing and also included two sleep medicine experts, one of whom held a degree in psychology and had professional training in psychological training in clinical psychology, as well as experts in traditional Chinese medicine nursing. This composition was intended to include professional perspectives relevant to clinical decision-making, nursing implementation, and specialist consultation within the proposed program.

Experts were eligible if they: (1) held a bachelor’s degree or higher; (2) held an intermediate-level or higher professional title; (3) had at least 10 years of professional experience in liver transplantation medicine, liver transplantation nursing, sleep medicine, or traditional Chinese medicine nursing; (4) were familiar with clinical care for liver transplant recipients, sleep-related problems, or intervention development; and (5) were willing to participate in the Delphi consultation.

Questionnaires were distributed individually by Email and completed independently. Panelists were anonymous to one another and did not participate in direct discussion during the consultation. Their identities were known to the research team for questionnaire administration and to link individual responses across rounds. Expert comments were de-identified before being summarized for group feedback.

The Delphi consultation was covered by the ethics approval for the overarching research project (Approval No. [2025(353)]). The study purpose and consultation procedures were described in the invitation materials, and return of a completed questionnaire was considered to indicate informed consent to participate.

Questionnaires were distributed by email, and experts were given approximately 1–2 weeks to complete and return the questionnaire. Predefined quantitative criteria for item retention were a mean importance score of ≥4.0 and a coefficient of variation (CV) of <0.25.36 Items not meeting both criteria were reviewed by the research team for possible revision or deletion rather than being excluded automatically. Items meeting the quantitative criteria could also be revised in response to substantive expert comments. No fixed number of Delphi rounds or separate overall consensus threshold for terminating the Delphi process was specified in advance.

After Round 1, quantitative ratings and qualitative comments were reviewed together by the research team. Decisions regarding item retention, addition, deletion, or revision took into account the predefined quantitative criteria, expert comments, clinical relevance, supporting evidence, and stage-specific needs identified in the qualitative interviews. The revised program was subsequently prepared for further expert evaluation. Before Round 2, controlled feedback from Round 1 was incorporated into the revised questionnaire. For each item, panelists were provided with the group mean importance score and CV, a de-identified summary of expert comments, modifications made after Round 1, and the research team’s disposition of the comments received. Added, deleted, and revised content was identified, and each panelist was also provided with their own previous rating.

Round 2 Delphi Consultation

Round 2 was conducted 2–3 weeks after completion of Round 1, in mid-to-late April 2026. The revised questionnaire was distributed to the same 16 experts by email, with approximately 1–2 weeks allowed for completion. The Round 2 questionnaire comprised 3 primary components, 11 secondary components, and 29 tertiary items. Experts independently re-rated the importance of the revised items using the same 5-point Likert scale and could provide additional comments or suggestions.

After Round 2, the research team again reviewed the quantitative ratings and qualitative comments using the same item-level criteria. All retained items met the predefined quantitative criteria, and no substantial new comments, major modifications, or additional items requiring another round of expert reassessment were identified. The Delphi consultation was therefore concluded after Round 2.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered and organized using Excel 2016, and statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Expert participation was described using questionnaire response rates.

Item importance was summarized using mean importance scores, and dispersion of expert ratings was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV), calculated as the standard deviation divided by the mean. Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) was calculated separately for the primary, secondary, and tertiary levels in each Delphi round to assess concordance among expert importance ratings. For each analysis, W, the corresponding chi-square statistic (χ2), degrees of freedom (df), P value, and number of items included in the analysis were reported. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The expert authority coefficient (Cr) was calculated as the mean of the judgment basis coefficient (Ca) and familiarity coefficient (Cs), according to the formula Cr = (Ca + Cs) / 2.36 Cr was reported descriptively as a characteristic of the expert panel.

Both rounds of the Delphi consultation achieved a 100% response rate, with all 16 questionnaires returned and considered valid in each round. Program Refinement Across the Delphi Rounds

The preliminary program entering the first Delphi round comprised 3 primary components, 11 secondary components, and 26 tertiary items. Following the first round, no items were deleted, three new tertiary items were added, and several existing items were revised or reworded, resulting in primary components, 11 secondary components, and 29 tertiary items for the second round. After the second round, no items were added or deleted, while four tertiary items underwent further refinement. The final program therefore retained 3 primary components, 11 secondary components, and 29 tertiary items.

Most substantive refinements occurred after the first round and focused on standardized assessment, patient safety, transplant-specific adaptation, and behavioral sleep management. Sleep and psychological assessments were strengthened to support more systematic identification of insomnia symptoms and psychological distress, including the addition of the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) and psychological screening before selected psychological or behavioral interventions. Safety requirements were also clarified for selected complementary and mindfulness-based interventions, including eligibility considerations, precautions for aromatherapy massage, and adjustment of intervention intensity according to the patient’s postoperative clinical condition. Transplant-specific content was refined to better address sleep-disrupting factors associated with the ward environment, postoperative symptoms, drainage management, treatment schedules, and medication-related issues.

Behavioral and complementary intervention components were further refined to improve clarity and clinical applicability. Overlapping content between sleep hygiene education and cognitive interventions was reduced, progressive muscle relaxation was added, and selected traditional Chinese medicine nursing interventions were revised with respect to their content and implementation requirements, including auricular acupressure and five-element (wuxing) music therapy. In the second round, further modifications focused on selected clinical details, including the timing of fluid and diuretic administration, management considerations during diarrhea, multidisciplinary assessment before selected mindfulness-based interventions, and post-discharge health education. The detailed expert comments and corresponding revisions across both rounds are provided in Supplementary Material 6.

Final Stage-Based, Needs-Oriented Sleep Management Program

The final program comprised 3 primary stages, 11 secondary components, and 29 tertiary items. Specifically, the pre-transplant stage comprised 4 secondary components and 11 tertiary items, the postoperative stage comprised 5 secondary components and 13 tertiary items, and the early transitional stage comprised 2 secondary components and 5 tertiary items. The intervention components were classified as core or optional adjunctive according to their intended role in clinical delivery, with a referral-informed management pathway incorporated for situations requiring additional clinical assessment or specialist collaboration. Discussion

Necessity of Developing a Stage-Based Sleep Management Program for Liver Transplant Recipients

The present study showed that the factors contributing to sleep disturbance differed across the liver transplantation trajectory. Before transplantation, sleep problems were closely related to disease burden, transplantation-related uncertainty, and psychological distress; during early postoperative recovery, pain, treatment-related procedures, and environmental disruption became more prominent; after discharge, medication management, self-management demands, and restoration of daily routines assumed greater importance. This pattern is consistent with previous research showing that supportive and continuing-care needs in liver transplant recipients vary across treatment and recovery contexts.37,38 Viewed specifically in relation to sleep, our findings indicate that the problems requiring attention also change across transplantation stages. The clinical rationale for stage-based sleep management therefore lies not simply in the persistence of sleep disturbance, but in the changing dominant contributing factors across stages of care and the corresponding shifts in management priorities.

Existing evidence has identified promising nonpharmacological interventions, but provides less guidance on their stage-specific operationalization within transplant care. A network meta-analysis ranked sleep hygiene education highest for improving sleep disorders in liver transplant recipients.12 This ranking suggests potential benefit rather than definitive clinical superiority, because sleep hygiene education was represented by a single study in the sleep-disorder network and the review itself called for further high-quality trials to strengthen the evidence.12 In the primary study underpinning this intervention, a one-week sleep hygiene education program was associated with improved sleep quality.26 These findings support sleep hygiene education as a relevant component of post-transplant sleep care, while leaving an important practical question unresolved. During early postoperative recovery, pain, invasive devices, frequent monitoring, treatment procedures, restricted mobility, and medication schedules may constrain conventional sleep-hygiene recommendations. Effective implementation therefore requires more than transferring general sleep-hygiene principles into the postoperative setting; the content and delivery of these strategies need to be adapted to the clinical conditions of this stage.

The development of multicomponent nursing interventions represents a further advance. Xi et al reported that a postoperative program integrating psychological care, lifestyle guidance, pain management, and rehabilitation training improved insomnia and psychological outcomes during the first year after liver transplantation.13 This study demonstrates the value of moving beyond isolated sleep interventions and incorporating sleep-related outcomes into broader postoperative care. However, a multicomponent postoperative program and a stage-based management framework address different problems. The former broadens the range of interventions delivered after transplantation, whereas the latter concerns how management priorities are organized as recipients move from pretransplant preparation through postoperative recovery to early home-based self-management. The remaining gap is therefore organizational rather than simply interventional. Because the determinants of sleep disturbance are closely intertwined with transplant-related symptoms, treatment procedures, medication management, and recovery demands, evidence-supported sleep strategies need to be positioned according to stage-specific priorities and integrated into specialized liver transplant care rather than delivered as an isolated sleep intervention. This need for stage-specific organization and clinical integration provides the central rationale for developing a stage-based sleep management program.

Novel Contribution and Contextual Features of the Stage-Based, Needs-Oriented Program

The principal contribution of the present program lies in how sleep-management strategies are positioned across the transplantation trajectory rather than in the novelty of individual intervention techniques. Existing evidence already supports several nonpharmacological approaches for liver transplant recipients, including sleep hygiene education,26 psychological and behavioral interventions,21 mindfulness-based strategies,22 and rehabilitation.24 The present program reorganizes these strategies according to the clinical priorities of successive transplantation stages. Before transplantation, the emphasis is placed on sleep assessment, education, and management of sleep-related concerns arising in the waiting and treatment context. During early postoperative recovery, priorities shift toward environmental and comfort measures, symptom-related management, and sleep-supportive strategies compatible with intensive treatment and physical recovery. After discharge, greater emphasis is placed on rebuilding sleep–wake routines, coordinating sleep-related behaviors with medication and self-management requirements, and maintaining continuity of sleep care. Thus, stage-based design does not simply divide the same intervention package by time; it assigns different management priorities and functions to intervention components according to the clinical context of each stage.

The needs-oriented design complements this stage-based structure by combining predefined program content with flexibility in the use of additional components. Core components, including sleep assessment, environmental optimization, comfort measures, sleep education, and basic behavioral strategies, provide a common foundation for sleep management, while optional components allow additional approaches to be considered when relevant to the recipient’s identified needs and clinical circumstances. For example, adjunctive measures such as auricular acupressure or five-element (wuxing) music therapy are not intended for universal delivery as part of a fixed intervention bundle. This distinction makes the program modular rather than cumulative: its comprehensiveness derives from the range of needs it is designed to address, not from delivering all available components to every recipient. Accordingly, needs-oriented denotes structured flexibility within a defined program framework, allowing the emphasis of care to vary without implying a prespecified patient-level allocation algorithm.

A further contribution is the explicit incorporation of transplant-specific clinical conditions into the organization of sleep care. Observational studies have associated poorer sleep after liver transplantation with transplant-related symptom burden, depression, anxiety, and other psychosocial and recovery-related factors.7,39 These associations do not establish that integrating transplant-care processes into sleep management will improve sleep outcomes, but they support considering the transplantation context when sleep interventions are designed. Accordingly, postoperative sleep-supportive strategies are planned in relation to pain, invasive devices, monitoring, treatment schedules, and mobility restrictions; activity and sleep planning are coordinated with treatment and rehabilitation requirements; and post-discharge sleep routines are considered alongside immunosuppressant administration, symptom monitoring, and self-management. These transplant-specific activities are not reclassified as sleep interventions; rather, their potential influence on sleep and on the practicability of sleep-related recommendations is incorporated into care planning. The logic model further makes explicit the expected relationships among transplantation stages, identified sleep-related needs, intervention components, transplant-specific adaptations, and outcomes. Such explicit consideration of intervention–context relationships is consistent with contemporary guidance for developing and evaluating complex interventions.40 The program’s contextual contribution therefore lies in translating existing sleep-management evidence into a stage-specific, needs-oriented structure that is embedded within the clinical realities of liver transplant care.

Multidisciplinary and Referral-Informed Implementation

The proposed program is nurse-led but supported by targeted multidisciplinary input when sleep-related problems extend beyond routine nursing management. This model reflects the clinical complexity of liver transplantation, in which sleep disturbance may coexist with postoperative symptoms or complications, psychological distress, medication-related problems, impaired physical recovery, and specific sleep disorders. Multidisciplinary care is already integral to liver transplantation,2 and psychosocial guidance for solid-organ transplantation likewise emphasizes continued involvement of mental health professionals when clinically indicated.41 Within the proposed program, nurses serve as the continuity hub by conducting routine sleep assessment, delivering core components and selecting appropriate optional adjunctive components according to individual needs and clinical context, monitoring changes in symptoms and sleep, providing education, and reassessing needs across transitions in care. Transplant surgeons and physicians provide medical assessment when sleep disruption may reflect postoperative complications, treatment-related symptoms, or other conditions requiring modification of medical management. The nurse-led designation therefore refers to continuity and coordination of routine and individualized non-pharmacological sleep management rather than to independent nursing management of all sleep-related problems.

Additional professional involvement is activated according to the nature of the problem identified. Persistent or clinically complex insomnia, suspected sleep-disordered breathing, or sleep problems requiring diagnostic clarification warrant assessment by sleep medicine specialists; contemporary insomnia guidance similarly recommends investigation for other sleep disorders when clinically suspected or when insomnia is resistant to routine treatment.42 Clinically significant depression, anxiety, or other psychiatric concerns require specialist mental health assessment rather than management solely as manifestations of poor sleep.41 When sleep complaints intersect with adverse drug effects, complex medication schedules, potential interactions, or concerns regarding immunosuppressive therapy, pharmacists can contribute medication reconciliation and review in collaboration with the transplant medical team. In liver transplant recipients, pharmacist-led medication review has identified adverse drug reactions, nonadherence, unnecessary medication, dosing problems, and other clinically relevant medication-related problems, with proposed changes discussed with patients and treating physicians.43 Rehabilitation professionals may support safe restoration of mobility, daytime activity, and functional recovery when physical limitations constrain sleep-related behavioral strategies,44 while caregivers can reinforce daily routines, observe emerging problems, and facilitate communication with the clinical team during home recovery. Qualitative work in liver transplantation indicates that recipients and close caregivers often manage medical, role-related, and emotional self-management demands together, supporting a complementary role for caregivers without positioning them as substitutes for professional assessment.45

The referral-informed management pathway provides the operational link between these roles. At each stage, core components provide the routine foundation of nurse-led sleep management, whereas optional adjunctive components are selected according to individual sleep-related needs, preferences, clinical condition, and safety considerations. When assessment identifies predefined referral indications or a problem that exceeds routine nursing scope, the referral-informed management pathway is activated, and the relevant professional is engaged for targeted evaluation or collaborative management. Appropriate core nursing components may continue during this process when clinically appropriate, whereas optional adjunctive components requiring further clinical assessment or safety review are initiated or modified accordingly. The purpose of referral-informed management is not to transfer responsibility for sleep care to another discipline, but to incorporate specialist assessment or treatment recommendations into the ongoing transplant care plan. Subsequent nursing reassessment then determines whether the sleep-related problem has improved, whether the care plan requires further adjustment, and whether unresolved concerns need to be carried forward during transition from inpatient care to discharge and early home recovery. This creates a clinically interpretable sequence of assessment, individualized intervention selection, escalation, specialist input, care-plan adjustment, and reassessment. Such a pathway also provides a safety boundary by reducing the risk that medically driven sleep disruption, significant psychological distress, medication-related problems, or a specific sleep disorder is managed solely with routine behavioral or comfort measures. Multidisciplinary collaboration in this program therefore does not require simultaneous involvement of all disciplines for every recipient; its function is to connect nurse-led longitudinal sleep care with problem-specific expertise when needed, thereby supporting interdisciplinary coordination, continuity of care, and patient safety.

Practical Implementation Considerations

The core pathway was designed to be embedded within routine transplant nursing rather than to require a separate sleep service. However, repeated assessment, documentation, referral coordination, and post-discharge follow-up may increase workload. A designated nursing responsibility for coordinating these activities would therefore be needed within each implementation unit, although this need not constitute a new full-time post. When staffing capacity is constrained, priority should be given to core functions, including assessment and risk recognition, essential environmental and behavioral measures, and referral when indicated, before optional components are added. This preserves the defining functions of the program without requiring uniform delivery of its full content in every clinical circumstance.46

Standardized preparation is necessary to prevent needs-oriented flexibility from becoming inconsistent practice. Nurses delivering the core pathway should receive initial training in stage-specific program content, sleep assessment and interpretation, core nonpharmacological strategies, safety boundaries, referral indications, and standardized documentation. Competency should be verified before independent delivery, with refresher training prompted by protocol changes or identified fidelity problems rather than an arbitrary fixed interval.47 A brief structured record of core components delivered, referral actions, and clinically relevant adaptations would also support consistent implementation and subsequent fidelity assessment.

Additional competency is required for selected adjunctive components. Hands-on interventions such as auricular acupressure and acupoint massage should be provided by personnel who have received procedure-specific theoretical and supervised practical training and demonstrated competency in the technique. A nurse-delivered auricular acupressure implementation study used structured instruction, hands-on practice, and competency assessment and also identified integration into routine workflow as a practical challenge.48 Mindfulness-based components similarly require provider competency proportionate to their intensity; structured mindfulness-based stress reduction programs in transplant populations have involved specifically trained instructors.49 These interventions should therefore remain optional rather than prerequisites for delivery of the core pathway.

Liver transplantation is delivered within specialized services, but transplant centers and follow-up settings may differ in access to sleep medicine, mental health, rehabilitation, pharmacy, and complementary-therapy support. Broader implementation should therefore preserve core program functions while adapting resource-dependent delivery methods to local service configurations.50 Remote support may be useful for sleep reassessment, reinforcement of education, review of home routines and self-management, and referral coordination. Liver transplant telemedicine studies have demonstrated that post-discharge follow-up can incorporate review of sleep, medication use, exercise, and other recovery issues while maintaining access to transplant specialists.51 In contrast, new medical warning signs, problems requiring physical examination, and hands-on adjunctive therapies require direct local assessment or care. A hybrid approach may therefore extend continuity without treating remote follow-up as a substitute for necessary face-to-face clinical management.

Role of Expert Consensus in Program Development

Expert consultation provided an important clinical interpretive step in translating the needs and evidence identified during earlier phases into a structured program for liver transplant care. Qualitative findings identified sleep-related needs across the transplantation trajectory, while the literature provided evidence for potentially relevant intervention strategies. The Delphi process enabled these sources to be considered collectively from different professional perspectives, particularly where intervention priorities, procedural details, and safety considerations required further clinical specification. In this way, expert input helped move the program beyond an aggregation of evidence-supported components toward a clinically coherent framework in which those components were prioritized and refined for the liver transplant context.

The modest Kendall’s W values indicate that expert judgments showed convergence but retained meaningful heterogeneity. Such variation is relevant in a multicomponent program spanning different transplantation stages and clinical domains, where professionals may assign different priorities to particular components according to their disciplinary perspectives and clinical experience. Nevertheless, convergence across the predefined Delphi criteria suggested sufficient stability to proceed with the current development-stage version of the program. Methodological literature similarly emphasizes that Delphi consensus should be understood through explicit consensus and stopping criteria rather than through a single statistical indicator.52 The residual variation in expert judgments is therefore not simply a statistical limitation; it also highlights areas in which the relative importance and practical value of individual components may become clearer only when the program is applied and evaluated in clinical practice.

Study Limitations and Directions for Further Validation

Several limitations should be considered. First, the present study remains at the program-development stage, and the feasibility, acceptability, fidelity, safety during implementation, and clinical effectiveness of the program have not yet been empirically evaluated. The Delphi consultation focused on item importance and did not include feasibility as a separate rating domain, limiting the extent to which expert judgments on implementability were incorporated during program development. Feasibility therefore requires explicit evaluation in subsequent pilot implementation. The literature review classified evidence by JBI level but did not formally assess methodological quality or risk of bias, limiting confidence in the supporting evidence for individual program components. In addition, the population-specific validity of some assessment tools remains uncertain; for example, the 12-item Pruritus Severity Scale was developed in dermatological populations rather than in patients with cholestatic pruritus associated with end-stage liver disease.53

Second, stage-specific sleep-related needs and influencing factors were identified primarily through qualitative inquiry. Although this approach captured recipients’ experiences and stage-specific care needs, it could not quantify the severity and temporal trajectories of sleep disturbance or the strength and patterns of associations between sleep disturbance and relevant factors across transplantation stages. In addition, recipient–caregiver dyads contributed to the needs-identification phase but were not involved in the Delphi prioritization and refinement of program items. Their direct perspectives on acceptability, burden, and preference-sensitive components were therefore not incorporated into this stage of program development. The Delphi panel also did not encompass all professional groups likely to contribute during subsequent implementation, which may have limited the range of perspectives represented.

Further validation should address both the stage-specific basis of the program and its clinical application. Prospective longitudinal studies could characterize changes in sleep-disturbance severity and associated factors across the transplantation trajectory, helping to identify periods of greater sleep burden and refine stage-specific management priorities. Future updates of the program should also incorporate formal methodological quality assessment of the supporting evidence. Further program refinement should incorporate direct input from recipients, caregivers, and implementation-relevant professionals, followed by pilot testing of feasibility, acceptability, fidelity, and safety. Subsequent controlled and multicenter studies can then evaluate clinical effectiveness, transferability across transplant-care settings, and sustainability during longer-term recovery.

Conclusion

This study developed a stage-based, needs-oriented, non-pharmacological sleep management program for liver transplant recipients through a literature review, qualitative findings, and two rounds of Delphi expert consultation. The program operationalized sleep disturbance as a clinically meaningful and nursing-sensitive construct and linked stage-specific sleep-related needs across the transplant trajectory to evidence-informed, context-adapted intervention components. The final program comprised three primary components, 11 secondary components, and 29 tertiary items spanning the pre-transplant, postoperative, and early transitional stages.

By distinguishing core and optional adjunctive components and incorporating a referral-informed management pathway, the program provides a structured framework for nurse-led sleep management within multidisciplinary liver transplant care and defines the intended scope and safety boundaries of non-pharmacological management. Further empirical validation is needed to determine its feasibility, implementation, and clinical effectiveness in practice.
