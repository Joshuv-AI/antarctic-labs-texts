Leveraging transfer learning-driven convolutional neural network-based semantic segmentation model for medical image analysis using MRI images
Authors: Amal Alshardan, Nuha Alruwais, Hamed Alqahtani, Asma Alshuhail, Wafa Sulaiman Almukadi, Ahmed Sayed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recognition and segmentation of brain tumours (BT) using MR images are valuable and tedious processes in the healthcare industry. Earlier diagnosis and localization of BT provide timely options to select effective treatment plans for the doctors and can save lives. BT segmentation from Magnetic Resonance Images (MRI) is considered a big challenge owing to the difficulty of BT tissues, and segmenting them from the healthier tissue is challenging when manual segmentation is done through radiologists. Among the recent proposals for the brain segmentation method, the BT segmentation method based on machine learning (ML) and image processing could be better. Thus, the DL-based brain segmentation method is extensively applied, and the convolutional network has better brain segmentation effects. The deep convolutional network model has the problem of a large loss of information and a large number of parameters in the encoding and decoding processes. With this motivation, this article presents a new Deep Transfer Learning with Semantic Segmentation based Medical Image Analysis (DTLSS-MIA) technique on MRI images. The DTLSS-MIA technique aims to segment the affected BT area in the MRI images. At first, the presented method utilizes a Median filtering (MF) approach to optimize the quality of MRI images and remove the noise. For the semantic segmentation method, the DTLSS-MIA method follows DeepLabv3 + with a backbone of the EfficientNet model for determining the affected brain region. Moreover, the CapsNet architecture is employed for the feature extraction process. Lastly, the crayfish optimization (CFO) technique with diffusion variational autoencoder (D-VAE) architecture is used as a classification mechanism, and the CFO technique effectively tunes the D-VAE hyperparameter. The simulation analysis of the DTLSS-MIA technique is validated on a benchmark dataset. The performance validation of the DTLSS-MIA technique exhibited a superior accuracy value of 99.53% over other methods.

Review of literature

Kiran et al.11 propose a cutting-edge DL method using a binary CNN (BCNN) method. This technique is used to segment the prevailing BT kinds, and it is an essential development over existing methods that are only limited to segmenting four types. This method starts with obtaining MRI imageries, tracked by a complete pre-processing phase where imageries experience dual alteration utilizing an adaptive thresholding technique and morphological process. The subsequent phase is segmentation, which produces the data. Rajaragavi and Rajan12 recommend an effective brain cancer classification and segmentation based on DL methods. At first, Squirrel search optimization (SSO) bi-directional ConvLSTM U-net with attention gate was presented for brain cancer segmentation. Next, the Inception and Hybrid Deep ResNet method is employed for classifications. SSO imitates the hunting behaviour of flying squirrels and their efficient method of the drive. Now, the squirrel optimizer is used to fine-tune the hyper-parameters of the U-net method. Additionally, the channel and position module’s bi-directional attention module is integrated into U-Net to mine many feature characteristics. The authors13 introduce an original lightweight attention-guided CNN (AG-CNN) method for BT classification in MR images. The intended structure employs a channel-attention block to concentrate on related areas of the image for BT classification. This method aids the network mining. Improved features are necessary to separate a normal brain and a cancer MR imagery. Rajendirane et al.14 develop a complete method for improving brain cancer recognition in MRI images by integrating advanced methods. At First, an enhanced K-means clustering method is presented to segment cancer areas effectually. After this, MF is used to improve the quality of the image and decrease noise interference. Afterwards, a DNN structure is used for accurate cancer classifications. Ullah et al.15 presented a new hybrid method that unites handcrafted features with the CNN model. In this study, handcrafted features are mined from MRI scans that comprised texture-based, shape-based, and intensity-based features. At the same time, a single CNN structure was trained and developed to spontaneously identify the features from the data. The presented hybrid technique has been integrated with the handcrafted features and recognized by CNN in various paths to a novel CNN. In this research, the BT Segmentation test dataset has been utilized.

Samee et al.16 propose a lightweight, efficient execution of the U-Net deep networks for executing precise real-time segmentation. In addition, a basic DCNN structure for the classification of BT is proposed for extracting features automatically and identifying the segmented ROIs. 5 convolutional layers, max-pooling layers, normalization, and ReLU create DCNNs that offer simple structures. Aljohani et al.17 examined structure categorizing patients with brain cancers with higher efficiency and accuracy by the CNNs, pre-trained methods, and the Manta Ray Foraging Optimizer (MRFO) method on MRI and X-ray imageries. In addition, the Transfer Learning (TL) and CNN hyper-parameters will be enhanced with the MRFO technique, improving the performance of the pre-trained method. In18, a new automatic classification and detection technique was presented. In the pre-processing stage of an MRI scan, an AF was used to remove noise in the background. For the extraction of features, the local-binary grey level co-occurrence matrix (LBGLCM) was utilized and enhanced fuzzy c-means clustering (EFCMC) was used for the image segmentation. The method also employs a DL method to identify MRI images. The classifications are produced by using convolutional recurrent neural networks (CRNNs). Anwar, Abrar, and Ullah19 explore TL for brain tumour classification, which also reviews existing models. Ullah et al.20 present a lightweight methodology utilizing MRI. This model integrates weighted averages with XGBoost decision trees, improving prediction explainability through feature extraction and ensemble classification. Ullah et al.21 introduce a federated learning (FL) framework for collaborative training of a segmentation model by utilizing distributed data from various medical institutions. Employing a U-Net architecture accentuates the scalability of the model.

Ullah et al.15 introduce a hybrid methodology by integrating handcrafted features and a CNN model to improve brain tumour segmentation. Ullah, Nadeem, and Abrar22 develop a model that enhances CNN performance for glioma segmentation by incorporating prior knowledge from handcrafted feature-based ML models. Touijer et al.23 evaluate the efficiency of in-domain fine-tuning on small-scale prostate MRI datasets related to utilizing only ImageNet pre-training to enhance DL performance in MRI segmentation. Messaoudi et al.24 propose improving medical image segmentation by transferring knowledge from a 2D classification network trained on natural images to 2D and 3D segmentation tasks, utilizing novel architectures that integrate weight and dimensional transfer techniques. Mecheter et al.25 explore the TL model between T1-weighted and T2-weighted MR sequences to enhance bone segmentation effectualness, presenting diverse TL mechanisms and a multi-parametric segmentation model utilizing T2-weighted MR as an intensity augmentation technique. Kumar et al.26 introduce a novel CNN method that employs TL to precisely classify brain tumours in MRI images as benign or malignant. Pani and Chawla27 introduce a hybrid methodology that incorporates transfer and self-supervised learning to improve brain tumour segmentation efficiency, employing unannotated medical imaging data and utilizing a specialized 3D UNET architecture for fine-tuning with limited labelled data. Alzate-Grisales et al.28 introduce SAM-UNETR, a novel model that incorporates a transformer-encoder from the Segment Anything Model with a residual-convolution decoder for effective segmentation of csPCa regions in MRI images, employing various modalities and advanced preprocessing-techniques. Mathivanan et al.29 explore the efficiency of DTL models, namely ResNet152, VGG19, DenseNet169, and MobileNetv3, in improving the accuracy of brain tumour diagnosis, leveraging pre-trained models.

The existing studies present various approaches for brain tumour segmentation and classification, each with specific limitations. Some methodologies may restrict their applicability to complex tumour types or need help distinguishing subtle differences in tumour characteristics, resulting in misclassifications. Others rely on the intrinsic incorporation of diverse methods, which can complicate implementation and tuning. While hybrid methods incorporating handcrafted features with CNNs present some merits, they may limit scalability and adaptability to new data distributions. Lightweight techniques may compromise segmentation quality in complex cases, and the quality of dispersed data can hinder the FL model. Moreover, the reliance on local features or prior knowledge can restrict adaptability to unseen tumour types, and the computational overhead of specific architectures may restrict real-time clinical application. Finally, findings from studies based on small-scale datasets may not generalize well to broader clinical practice, limiting the overall applicability of these methodologies. Despite enhancements in brain tumour segmentation and classification using DL methods, crucial research gaps remain. Many existing methods need help with generalization across diverse datasets and tumour types, specifically in clinical settings. Additionally, there is a requirement for more robust frameworks that effectually integrate both handcrafted features and DL while maintaining computational efficiency. Further exploration of techniques that address privacy concerns and enable collaborative learning in dispersed medical environments is also significant.

The DTLSS-MIA technique comprises noise reduction, semantic segmentation, feature extractor, parameter selection, and classification processes to accomplish that. Noise reduction

At first, the presented method includes the MF approach to optimize the quality of MRI images and remove the noise. The MF model is selected for its efficiency in improving MRI image quality by selectively mitigating noise while conserving critical anatomical details. This method outperforms in handling diverse noise types typical in MRI scans, which can obscure crucial features necessary for accurate diagnosis. Unlike other methodologies that may introduce artefacts or overly smooth images, the MF approach maintains the integrity of the underlying structures. Furthermore, it needs fewer computational resources than more complex denoising algorithms, making it practical for clinical settings. Its robustness and adaptability to diverse imaging conditions justify its selection over alternative methods. Figure 2 illustrates the flow of the MF model.

Fig. 2Overall structure of the MF technique.

Overall structure of the MF technique.

MF is a non-linear digital filtering method more commonly applied in image pre-processing, especially to remove noise from MRI images of BTs30. This method effectively reduces impulsive noise while retaining edges by replacing all the pixel values with the median values of the intensity in its neighbourhood, which makes it better for medical imaging where detail preservation is crucial. In brain tumour MRI images, MF enhances the visibility of tumour boundaries and other essential patterns, facilitating accurate analysis and diagnosis. This pre-processing step enhances succeeding image processing tasks, namely classification and segmentation.

Semantic segmentation process

This work employs DeepLabV3 + for semantic segmentation and BT detection31. This method is chosen for its superior balance between accuracy and effectiveness in semantic segmentation tasks. EfficientNet’s architecture allows for scalable performance by optimizing depth, width, and resolution, making it specifically adept at capturing complex details in medical images. Furthermore, the DeepLabv3 + model employs atrous convolution, which efficiently captures multi-scale contextual information, which is significant for precisely detecting affected brain regions. This integration improves feature extraction while minimizing computational overhead, confirming faster processing times without sacrificing precision. Compared to other methods, the technique’s capability to adapt to varying image resolutions and conditions further solidifies its merit for medical applications.

The EfficientNet is employed as the backbone of the segmentation model. In computer vision (CV), semantic segmentation is a vital task that involves pixel-wise object identification in imagery. At present, notable developments in DL have moved the area of semantic segmentation to unparalleled levels of efficiency and accuracy. Initially, FC networks took major development in semantic segmentation by presenting endwise dense feature learning. Conversely, FCNs undergo restricted contextual and spatial information due to the narrow local receptive areas. Therefore, many researchers mainly concentrated on taking superior context and spatial information in semantic segmentation. Atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) grabs various ranges of contextual information. HRNet upholds higher-resolution representation in the system, which certifies the protection of fine particulars. The OCRNet structure has been presented for additional developments, which combines object-contextual representation, letting the system consider relations among objects within a scene. These state-of-the-art models acquire perception capability by utilizing contextual information in a distinct image, but there is a restriction on the ability of global feature perception.

DeepLabv3 uses atrous convolution to remove the features intended by deep CNNs (DCNN) at a random resolution32. Here, the output stride is defined as the spatial proportion of the input image to the last output resolutions (earlier global pooling or fully connected (FC) layer). For the image classification task, the previous spatial resolution feature map was generally 32 times less than the input image resolution, so the output stride was 32. For the semantic segmentation task, one can take output stride = 16 (or 8) for extraction of denser feature by eliminating the stride in the previous one (or dual) block and using atrous convolution respectively (e.g., rate = 2 and rate = 4 is utilized to the last dual blocks correspondingly for output stride = 8). Moreover, DeepLabv3 enlarges the Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling component that analyses convolution features at numerous measures using atrous convolution with dissimilar rates and image level features. The previous feature map from the original DeepLabv3 is utilized as the encoding output in the proposed encoder-decoder framework. The feature map of encoding output covers 256 channels and rich semantic data. Moreover, one can remove features at a random resolution by using the atrous convolution, dependent on the computation price. Figure 3 illustrates the structure of DeepLabV3 + technique.

CapsNet model

Next, the CapsNet architecture is applied for the feature extraction34. The CapsNet architecture is selected for feature extraction due to its unique ability to capture spatial hierarchies in data, making it specifically efficient for medical imaging tasks. Unlike conventional CNNs, CapsNet employs capsules that conserve the relationship between features, allowing it better to comprehend anatomical structures’ orientation and spatial associations. This characteristic is significant for precisely detecting intrinsic patterns in medical images. Moreover, CapsNet is more robust to discrepancies in viewpoint and deformation, which can be common in medical datasets. Its mitigated requirement for extensive data augmentation also improves efficiency, making it a robust option for tasks such as breast cancer detection, where precise feature representation is crucial. Figure 5 demonstrates the framework of CapsNet.

Fig. 5Framework of CapsNet model.

Framework of CapsNet model.

In CapsNet, architecture is introduced, which stores the entity properties in an image (viz., orientation, position, pose, and angle) in capsules and offers vector output rather than scalar output in CNN. While in CNN, the pooling layer ensures that the neuron has perspective invariability, in CapsNet, capsules replace the neuron, and the dynamic routing mechanism offers the relations between the high and low-level capsules. CapsNet has digit capsule layers and convolutional and primary capsule layers. After the convolution process, the essential features of the image are extracted first and then forwarded to the primary capsule layer. Now, the features are divided into capsules through the reshaping method, which is once again subjected to the convolution process. In this layer, the capsule passed over the squash function is provided as input to the digit capsule layer. The capsule with the high probability produces the output values where the dynamic routing function is employed. Consequently, image reconstructions are implemented by the loss estimation.

This methodology also reduces the risk of overfitting by systematically exploring diverse parameter settings, which is significant in a sensitive domain such as medical imaging. Additionally, the CFO’s adaptability to diverse hyperparameter combinations confirms that the CapsNet model is finely tuned for specific tasks, resulting in enhanced accuracy and reliability in feature extraction. Classification using D-VAE model

Finally, the D-VAE model was employed to identify the presence of BT36. This technique is selected for classification because it can effectually capture complex data dispersions while employing the merits of both diffusion processes and variational inference. This hybrid methodology enhances the capacity of the approach to learn robust latent representations, which is specifically beneficial for high-dimensional medical imaging data. The D-VAE model outperforms in generating diverse samples and enhancing feature disentanglement, enabling better classification performance. Moreover, its capability to integrate noise and uncertainties in the data allows for more resilient and reliable predictions, addressing threats often encountered in medical contexts. The model’s inherent flexibility in adapting to diverse types of data additionally solidifies its suitability for tasks like classification in complex domains. Fig. 7Architecture of the D-VAE approach.

Architecture of the D-VAE approach.

The D-VAE integrates the principles of VAE with the diffusion process to generate accurate predictions and capture complex data distribution in uncertain and volatile market conditions. This approach applies stochastic diffusion to generate synthetic data similar to the training dataset, enriching the model’s ability to simulate and understand market dynamics.

The D-VAE architecture involves the mean, encoder, log-variance, decoder reparameterization trick, and diffusion process with Gaussian noise. All the components transform the input dataset into a more informative and compact hidden representation and recreate the information from the hidden representation while accounting for uncertainty and stochasticity in the data. The encoder converts the input dataset into a hidden representation through a dense layer with ReLu activations.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ReLU\left(x\right)=\:\text{m}\text{a}\text{x}\left(0,\:x\right)$$\end{document}

This function assists in addressing the gradient vanishing problems and presents nonlinearity in the model, allowing the NN to capture the complicated pattern in the dataset. The sequence of dense layers processes the input dataset to reduce the dimension gradually. The dense layer in the encoder is used to compress significant data from the input dataset, transforming it into a more compact and simpler hidden representation. The dataset is divided into mean (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mu\:$$\end{document}) and log variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\:\text{l}\text{o}\text{g}\:{\sigma\:}^{2}\right)$$\end{document} vectors after passing through dense layers in the encoder, which describe the hidden distribution. The reparameterization trick is used for generating the hidden parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:z$$\end{document} as follows:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:z=\mu\:+\sigma\:.\epsilon$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ϵ\sim\:N\left(\text{0,1}\right))$$\end{document}. This process retains differentiability during backpropagation and captures uncertainty in the dataset. In the D-VAE, a diffusion method is added by integrating the Gaussian noise into the hidden representation:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Z}_{diffused}=Z+\epsilon$$\end{document}

In Eq. This simulates the stochastic nature of stock price, which helps the model to capture variability in the market dataset. Then, the decoder processes the diffused hidden variables, reconstructing the input dataset from this hidden representation using a dense layer with ReLu activation. The last layer exploits linear activation to produce stock price predictions. Conclusion

In this article, a new DTLSS-MIA technique on MRI images is presented. The DTLSS-MIA technique aims to segment the affected BT region in the MRI images. To accomplish that, the DTLSS-MIA technique comprises noise reduction, semantic segmentation, feature extractor, parameter selection, and classification processes. At first, the presented method includes the MF approach to optimize the quality of MRI images and remove the noise. For the semantic segmentation method, the DTLSS-MIA method follows DeepLabv3 + with a backbone of the EfficientNet model for determining the affected brain region. Moreover, the CapsNet architecture is applied for the feature extraction process. Lastly, the CFO approach with D-VAE architecture is employed as a classification mechanism, and the CFO approach effectively tunes the D-VAE hyperparameter. The simulation analysis of the DTLSS-MIA approach is validated on a benchmark dataset. The performance validation of the DTLSS-MIA technique exhibited a superior accuracy value of 99.53% over other methods. The DTLSS-MIA technique’s limitations include reliance on a single dataset, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to diverse clinical scenarios. Furthermore, the computational complexity of the proposed model could pose threats in real-time applications, necessitating additional optimization. The study also acknowledges potential biases in the training data that could affect the model’s performance. Future work may concentrate on validating the model across diverse datasets to improve its robustness, exploring incorporating multimodal data for enhanced accuracy and optimizing the architecture for faster processing times in clinical settings. Moreover, investigating interpretability methods could give insights into the model’s decision-making process, ultimately improving clinician trust in automated systems.
