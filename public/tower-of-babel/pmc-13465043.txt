From lab to field: accelerated cultivar release via integrated speed breeding framework in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)
Authors: Jin-Kyung Cha, Hyeonjin Park, Youngho Kwon, So-Myeong Lee, Woo-Jae Kim, Jong-Hee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWheat (Triticum aestivum L.) breeding must accelerate to meet increasing global demand, yet traditional field-based programs remain a major bottleneck. While speed breeding (SB) and speed vernalization (SV) have emerged as transformative tools for generation acceleration, their practical application for official cultivar release through comprehensive field validation remains remarkably limited.ResultsWe developed the new high-yielding bread wheat cultivar ‘Irum’ using an integrated speed breeding (ISB) system that combines SV with SB protocols. The ISB system facilitated the rapid advancement of F1 to F5 generations within only 15 months, effectively reducing the total breeding cycle from 128 months to 80 months, representing a significant 4-year reduction. Principal component analysis confirmed that the ISB-advanced population maintained the trait diversity required for effective selection, allowing for the strategic identification of elite lines. Multi-year and multi-environment field trials demonstrated that ‘Irum’ exhibited a consistent agronomic performance across environments, achieving a significant 14.6% yield increase over the check cultivar ‘Keumgang’. Background

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a major global food crop that provides about 20% of the calories and proteins in human diet [1]. However, the global wheat supply chain faces pressure from rapid population growth, shifting dietary preferences, and the increasing frequency of extreme climate events [2]. To meet the projected global demand for an annual increase of 132 million metric tons by 2050, wheat production must increase significantly [3]. This necessitates a continuous supply of new cultivars that simultaneously exhibit enhanced yield potential and superior end-use quality to satisfy both agricultural productivity and industrial standards [4].

Despite the urgent requirement for rapid varietal improvement, traditional field-based breeding remains a bottleneck in agricultural innovation. The conventional breeding cycle for inbred cereals typically spans up to ten years for line development, followed by an additional three to five years of field testing [5]. This prolonged timeline is largely due to the slow pace of achieving genetic fixation through consecutive self-fertilization and the necessity for multi-year, multi-location field trials to ensure agronomic stability. In an era where climate patterns are shifting faster than traditional breeding cycles can adapt, this time lag presents a critical risk to sustainable food systems [6].

In response, speed breeding (SB) and speed vernalization (SV) technologies have emerged as transformative paradigms in modern wheat breeding. By manipulating photoperiod and temperature within controlled environments, these systems can achieve five to six generations per year for both spring and winter wheat [7, 8]. While SB systems accelerate spring wheat through an extended 22-h photoperiod, integrated speed breeding (ISB) systems, which combine SV and SB, facilitate the rapid advancement of both spring and winter wheat by utilizing relatively high vernalization temperature (8–10 °C) in conjunction with an extended 22-h photoperiod. These technologies offer a resource-efficient alternative for small-scale breeding programs with limited land and labor resources.

However, the ‘lab-to-field’ transition remains a significant challenge. Lines advanced in artificial environment may exhibit reduced field adaptation or a loss of genetic variability, raising concerns about whether speed-bred lines can maintain the robust agronomic traits required for commercial farming [9]. Consequently, despite successful proof-of-concept studies in laboratory settings, the practical delivery of officially registered cultivars through national-level trials remains remarkably limited.

In this study, we present the development and release of the new bread wheat cultivar ‘Irum’ via an ISB system. By systematically combining ISB protocols with field validation, we successfully bridged the gap between indoor innovation and large scale agricultural application. This paper details the accelerated breeding process and provides a comprehensive evaluation of the agronomic stability and superior end-use quality of the new cultivar across diverse environments. Overall, this study provides a practical roadmap connecting laboratory innovation to official registration for the rapid delivery of new wheat cultivars to farmers.

Materials and methods

Development of plant materials

Two Korean bread wheat cultivars, ‘Jokyoung’ and ‘Joongmo2008’, were sown in a greenhouse in November 2018, and artificially crossed in April 2019. ‘Jokyoung’ is a representative Korean bread wheat cultivar primarily recognized for its high yield potential. ‘Joongmo2008’ is characterized by its superior protein content and baking quality, although it exhibits a relatively lower yield capacity. The cross was strategically designed to combine the high productivity of ‘Jokyoung’ with the premium quality traits of ‘Joongmo2008’.

To accelerate generation advancement from the F1 to F5 generations, the integrated speed breeding (ISB) system, which combines speed vernalization (SV) and speed breeding (SB) according to the protocol of Cha et al. [7], was utilized. Briefly, SV was conducted at 10 °C under a 22-hour photoperiod with a light intensity of 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Subsequently, plants were transferred to the SB condition, maintained at 22 °C (day/night) under the same 22-hour photoperiod with a light intensity of 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Starting with ten F1 plants, generations were advanced via the Single seed descent (SSD) method without intentional phenotypic selection; at each stage, one seed per plant was planted to advance the generation. Strategic population expansion was incorporated at the F3 and F4 stages to ensure a robust genetic pool for selection (Table S1). Following the initial F1:2 seeds harvest in November 2019, the F5:6 seeds were successfully obtained by November 2020 under ISB conditions (Fig. 1).

Comparison of breeding timeline for new wheat cultivar ‘Irum’ (‘Milyang53’) using an integrated speed breeding (ISB) system versus the conventional breeding method. a Rapid generation advancement pipeline using the ISB framework. b Conventional field-based pedigree breeding method. The red dashed line indicates the 4-year reduction in breeding time achieved using the ISB system

A field evaluation of 213 F6 lines during the 2020/2021 growing season led to the selection of seven lines for advanced yield trials (AYT). Following the 2021/2022 AYT, two elite lines—‘Milyang52’ and ‘Milyang53’—were selected and concurrently evaluated in additional AYT and regional yield trials (RYT) during the 2022/2023 season. Upon completion of the three-year RYT in June 2025, ‘Milyang53’ was ultimately selected for its superior performance and subsequently registered as ‘Irum’.

Evaluation of agronomic traits of breeding lines

For field evaluation, F6 lines were grown in an unreplicated observational nursery at Miryang. Each line was sown in a single 3.5 m row (plot area: 1.05 m²) with a spacing of 30 cm between rows and 15 cm between plants (one seed per hill), ensuring a population of at least 20 plants per line. For the AYT, the experiment was conducted in Miryang. Seeds were drill-seeded at a rate of 14 kg/1,000 m² in plots consisting of five 5 m rows with 30 cm inter-row spacing (total plot area: 7.5 m²). A randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications was employed, with the check cultivars randomly assigned within each block alongside the test lines to ensure unbiased comparisons. The RYT trials were conducted at the same five locations for three consecutive years (2022/2023–2024/2025) using a RCBD with three replications. The test sites consisted of three paddy fields (Miryang, Jinju, and Naju) and two upland fields (Daegu and Jeju). All trials were performed under rainfed conditions, while drainage ditches were thoroughly maintained to prevent waterlogging in paddy fields. Seeding rates and plot dimensions were adjusted by location: in paddy fields, broad ridge seeding was performed at 16 kg/1,000 m² with a plot area of 7.5 m² (1.5 m ridge width × 5 m length). In upland fields, drill seeding was performed at 14 kg/1,000 m². The plot areas for upland trials were 9.0 m² in Daegu (25 cm row spacing × 6 rows × 6 m length) and 12.0 m² in Jeju (40 cm row spacing × 5 rows × 6 m length). Fertilizer was applied at 9.1–7.4–3.9 kg (N–P2O5–K2O) per 1,000 m², following the RDA Standard Evaluation Manual for Agricultural Experiments and Research [10]. The white hard wheat cultivars ‘Keumgang’ and ‘Baekkang’ served as check varieties as they are representative certificated seeds in Korea [11]. ‘Keumgang’ is extensively utilized as a standard check cultivar in Korean wheat breeding programs due to its high cultivation stability. ‘Baekkang’ is a leading bread-making variety developed after ‘Jokyoung’ and is a major cultivar in the domestic baking industry owing to its superior processing quality.

Agronomic traits were assessed according to the RDA manual [10]: heading date, maturity date, culm length, spike length, number of spikes (per plant or m²), number of grains per spike, thousand-grain weight, test weight, and lodging index. Days to heading (DTH) was calculated from sowing to the heading date, while the days to maturity (DTM) was determined as the period from heading to maturity. Plant height was recorded as the combined length of the culm and the spike. Cold tolerance and lodging indices were evaluated on a scale of 0 (tolerant) to 9 (susceptible). Cold tolerance was assessed based on the degree of leaf necrosis after wintering, while lodging was determined by the percentage of fallen plants: 0 for no damage/lodging, 1 for ≤ 20%, 3 for 21–40%, 5 for 41–60%, 7 for 61–80%, and 9 for ≥ 81%.

Evaluation of flour quality and baking suitability

The 213 F6 breeding lines were milled using a Brabender Quadrumat Junior mill (CW Brabender Instruments, Inc., South Hackensack, NJ, USA) to evaluate flour quality. For the AYT and RYT samples, a Buhler MLU 202 laboratory mill (Bühler AG, Uzwil, Switzerland) was employed. High-molecular-weight glutenin subunits (HMW-GS) were identified using a lab-on-a-chip system (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with Protein 230 Kit; Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany), following the protocols of Shin et al. [12].

Flour protein content was measured using a LECO FP928 nitrogen analyzer (Laboratory Equipment Co., St. Joseph, MI, USA) with a conversion factor of 5.7. Dry gluten content and gluten index were determined with a Glutomatic 220 system (Perten Instruments AB, Sweden). The SDS-sedimentation volume was assessed according to Axford et al. [4], with a modification for 3 g sample. Water absorption and mixing properties were determined using a 10 g Mixograph (National Manufacturing Co., Lincoln, NE, USA) following AACC Method 54-40.02 [5].

Baking quality was assessed per AACC Method 10-10.03. The baking formula included 100 g flour, 5.3 g yeast, 6.0 g sucrose, 1.5 g salt, and 3.0 g shortening. Water absorption was adjusted for each sample based on Mixograph results. Following a 90 min fermentation, loaf volume was determined 1 h post-baking by rapeseed displacement. Crumb firmness was measured 24 h after baking using a DuraVision macrohardness tester (ZwickRoell GmbH & Co. KG, Ulm, Germany) following AACC Method 74-09.01.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1; R Core Team) within the RStudio environment (version 2025.09.2 + 418; Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA). Data were subjected to distinct statistical treatments tailored to each trial stage and dataset. For the single-season advanced yield trial (AYT) presented in Table S2, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. For the two-season AYT data presented in Table S3, a two-way ANOVA framework was applied to evaluate the main effects of genotype (G), year (Y), and their interaction (G × Y). Mean separations for these ANOVA models were determined using Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) at a significance level of p < 0.05. Conversely, for the multi-year and multi-location regional yield trials (RYT) presented in Table 1, performance comparisons between ‘Irum’ and the standard check ‘Keumgang’ were evaluated strictly using location-specific independent t-tests at each individual regional site. While this approach does not account for complex, higher-order interaction effects across all environments simultaneously, it facilitates a direct and straightforward pairwise comparison between the two cultivars under each specific localized environment. Additionally, paired t-tests were utilized to compare the multi-environment agronomic performance and flour quality traits between ‘Irum’ and ‘Baekkang’ presented in Table S4 and Table S5. The R packages dplyr, agricolae, and ggplot2 were used for data processing and visualization.

Results

Accelerated development of new cultivar ‘Irum’ via integrated speed breeding

The utilization of the ISB systems combined with traditional field tests significantly reduced the breeding period for ‘Irum’. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the total period from the initial sowing of parental lines in November 2018 to the final harvest of the cultivar candidate ‘Milyang53’ in June 2025 was 80 months. This represents a 48-month (4-year) reduction compared to the 128 months typically required for a conventional field-based breeding cycle. The most substantial efficiency gain occurred during early generation advancement (F1 to F5), which was completed within only 15 months using laboratory-based ISB systems (Fig. 1).

Following this acceleration, field evaluations of 213 F6 lines were conducted to assess agronomic and quality traits. PCA biplot analysis of this population clearly illustrated the trait diversity and the inherent trade-offs between yield potential and grain quality (Fig. 2a). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 68.9% of the total variation, revealing strong positive correlations between DTH and GN (r = 0.82***), while a significant negative correlation was observed between GN and PC (r = -0.33***) (Fig. S1). Based on their distribution along the PC1 axis, seven lines representing the population’s trait diversity were selected for AYT (Fig. 2a). The selection was categorized into two target groups: three yield-representative lines characterized by high GN and DTH (maximized vegetative growth), and four quality-representative lines with high PC and DTM (extended grain-filling period). Final selection within these groups was strictly based on visual assessments of plant architecture and field uniformity to ensure practical agronomic performance and stability.

Evaluation of these seven lines in the AYT clarified their distinct characteristics (Table S2). Lines AYT5–7 recorded the highest grain yields (4.04–4.66 t/ha) and grain numbers per spike (44–48). Among them, AYT6 (subsequently named ‘Irum’ or ‘Miryang53’) produced a yield of 4.66 t/ha, showing high grain numbers per spike (48). Conversely, lines AYT1–4, including ‘Milyang52’, exhibited significantly higher protein contents (17.2–18.6%) and SDS-sedimentation volumes (63–82 mL), highlighting their potential for high-quality flour production. This strategic selection process identified ‘Milyang52’ and ‘Milyang53’ as the most promising candidates for final field validation, with ‘Milyang53’ closely aligned with the yield-related GN vector and ‘Milyang52’ characterized by superior quality and longer DTM (Fig. 2b).

The distinct characteristics of ‘Milyang52’ and ‘Milyang53’ were further validated through consecutive AYT and RYT evaluations (Fig. 2b, Table S3). While ‘Milyang52’ possessed superior flour quality, its grain yield (2.69 t/ha) was significantly lower than that of the standard check cultivars ‘Keumgang’ and ‘Baekkang’ (4.28–4.68 t/ha). In contrast, ‘Milyang53’ maintained an optimal balance between high yield potential and superior quality traits (PC, DGC, GI, SDSS, and mixograph properties including mixing time and dough strength) compared to the checks. Consequently, ‘Milyang53’ was selected as the final cultivar candidate for the three-year RYT. Following the completion of the final trials in 2025, it was officially registered and approved as a new cultivar by the Korea Seed & Variety Service under the name ‘Irum’ (meaning ‘achievement’ in Korean; Application No. 2025 − 407).

Agronomic performance and yield stability across diverse environments

The regional adaptability and yield potential of ‘Irum’ (‘Milyang53’) were evaluated across five locations from 2023 to 2025. As summarized in Table 1, ‘Irum’ exhibited superior yield performance compared to the standard check cultivar ‘Keumgang’. The pooled mean yield of ‘Irum’ was 4.32 t/ha, a significant increase of 14.6% over ‘Keumgang’ (3.77 t/ha, p < 0.05).

The yield advantage of ‘Irum’ was primarily attributed to a substantial increase in the number of grains per spike. ‘Irum’ produced significantly more grains per spike (28.6) than ‘Keumgang’ (24.2, p < 0.001), effectively compensating for its relatively lower spike number and thousand-grain weight. This result aligns with the PCA in Fig. 2a, where ‘Irum’ was strongly associated with the GN vector.

Regarding growth characteristics, ‘Irum’ showed no significant differences in DTH and DTM compared to ‘Keumgang’, maintaining the early-maturing traits required for double-cropping systems in Korea. Furthermore, ‘Irum’ exhibited high lodging resistance (index 1) and a consistent plant height (81 cm) across all locations, demonstrating its agronomic stability and field standing ability (Fig. 3). The cold tolerance index of ‘Irum’ was recorded as 2, while that of ‘Keumgang’ was 1 (Table 1).

Flour quality and enhanced bread-making properties

In addition to its stable agronomic performance, ‘Irum’ demonstrated grain yield and flour quality parameters comparable to ‘Baekkang’, a representative high-quality bread wheat cultivar, across multi-environment RYT tests (Table S4, Table S5). Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between ‘Irum’ and ‘Baekkang’ in terms of grain yield and growth characteristics, confirming that ‘Irum’ maintains the high productivity standards required for commercial cultivation (Table S4). Furthermore, ‘Irum’ showed no significant difference from ‘Baekkang’ in key flour quality parameters, including the milling rate (67.4%), protein content (11.7%), dry gluten content (9.2%), gluten index (89.5), and SDS-sedimentation volume (55.1 mL), confirming its consistently high-quality characteristics (Table S4). HMW-GS composition analysis identified that ‘Irum’ carries subunits 1, 17 + 18, and 5 + 10 at the Glu-A1, Glu-B1, and Glu-D1 loci, respectively (Table S2).

The baking performance of ‘Irum’ was validated through actual baking tests, where it outperformed ‘Baekkang’ in essential baking performance parameters (Fig. 4). ‘Irum’ produced a significantly larger loaf volume (680 mL) than ‘Baekkang’ (600 mL, p = 0.043) (Fig. 4a and b). Furthermore, the crumb firmness of ‘Irum’ was significantly lower (2.5 N) than that of ‘Baekkang’ (3.5 N, p = 0.032), reflecting a softer and more desirable texture for bread products (Fig. 4c).

Discussion

Practical effectiveness and various availability of speed breeding

To our knowledge, the development of ‘Irum’ represents the first successful cultivar officially registered through the practical application of ISB systems in South Korea. While SB has been extensively studied in laboratory settings, transitioning these technologies to field environment has remained a challenge [13]. By adopting ISB protocols [7, 8], we successfully bridged the gap between laboratory innovation and field-based cultivar development.

The most notable outcome was the dramatic reduction of the breeding cycle. The total duration from the initial cross to cultivar development was shortened from the conventional 128 months to only 80 months. This four-year reduction was primarily achieved by accelerating the F1 to F5 generations within only 15 months using the ISB systems, combined with a strategic overlap of AYT and RYT. Conventional field-based breeding programs generally require up to 13 years for inbred crop cultivar release [14]. Achieving genetic fixation through self-fertilization requires the longest period in the breeding timeline, followed by seed increase and evaluation [15].

Although alternative generation acceleration methods, such as doubled haploid (DH) and genetic modifications (GM) offers distinct advantages, they serve as complementary approaches rather than simple substitutes for ISB. For instance, DH technology enables the rapid production of completely homozygous lines, although its efficiency can be limited by low induction rates and reduced genetic gain from a single recombination event [16, 17]. Similarly, GM allows for the precise introduction of exogenous elite genes, yet it faces hurdles such as low transformation efficiency and high public sensitivity [18]. ISB specifically addresses the bottleneck of generation progression in conventional breeding, maintaining the benefits of multiple recombination cycles while significantly reducing the breeding time. This efficiency demonstrates that SB systems are no longer just research tools but a viable infrastructure for addressing food security by rapidly delivering improved cultivars to farmers.

One important aspect to consider is the initial population size. In this study, we began with ten F1 plants. While this was a relatively small starting group, we increased the population size in later generations to maintain a strong genetic pool. To secure more F2 seeds from the start, we suggest using more F1 plants or larger pots in the early stages. These adjustments can provide a wider genetic base and improve the reliability of field-based selection.

Biological trade-offs and necessity of precision breeding

Despite the successful acceleration of the breeding cycle, the PCA biplot analysis in Fig. 2a highlighted the fundamental biological trade-offs in wheat breeding. The clear separation of the population along the PC1 axis underscored a persistent trade-off between yield potential and grain quality. While ‘Irum’ successfully maximized grain numbers to achieve a 14.6% yield increase over ‘Keumgang’, it reached a ‘yield-quality equilibrium’ that remains difficult to surpass through phenotypic selection alone [19]. Importantly, although the absolute grain yield of the check varieties fluctuated across trials due to differences in cultivation years, environmental conditions, and seeding methods, ‘Irum’ consistently maintained its yield superiority over the check varieties throughout the entire evaluation process. However, it should be noted that while this location-specific t-test approach provides a direct and localized performance comparison at each regional site, it has limitations in fully capturing the complex, multi-environmental mixed-model interactions across all years and locations simultaneously. Regarding flour quality and baking characteristics, although the protein content of ‘Irum’ was comparable to that of ‘Baekkang’, ‘Irum’ exhibited a significantly larger loaf volume; this is likely attributed to the presence of the HMW-GS subunit 17 + 18, which is recognized for its positive effect on bread-making quality (Fig. 4, Table S2) [20].

Furthermore, a critical limitation of rapid generation advancement is the omission of early-stage environmental stress screening. Since the F1 to F5 generations were advanced under controlled laboratory conditions, these lines did not experience the selective pressure of natural field conditions. This ‘greenhouse domestication’ can lead to compromised performance in traits that are difficult to evaluate indoors, such as drought tolerance, cold tolerance, and disease resistance. For instance, ‘Irum’ showed lower cold tolerance compared to ‘Keumgang’ and ‘Baekkang’ (Table 1, Table S5), which stands as a clear agronomic limitation of this cultivar under severe winter conditions. Practically, these results imply that commercial cultivation of ‘Irum’ should be strategically targeted toward the southern regions of South Korea, where winter temperatures are relatively mild, or require optimized field management, such as adjusted sowing windows, to prevent winter freezing injury.

To overcome these hurdles, future SB programs must combine multiple screening protocols. Recent studies have shown that phenotypic selection for certain biotic stresses such as leaf rust, stripe rust, yellow spot, and Fusarium head blight can be effectively conducted within SB environments [21–24]. For traits where indoor phenotypic selection remains challenging, molecular breeding technologies such as marker-assisted selection (MAS) and genomic selection (GS) should be prioritized to introgress essential adaptation and resistance alleles [25, 26]. In particular, as flour quality analysis is limited during the indoor SSD phase due to the insufficient seed volume, MAS serves as a crucial tool for selecting target HMW-GS compositions. For instance, the synergy between SB and marker-assisted backcrossing (MABC) was successfully employed to introgress a specific Glu-B1 allele for developing a near-isogenic line within 3.5 years [27]. By pre-screening for specific alleles related to environmental adaptation and end-use quality during the indoor laboratory phases, breeders can ensure that the speed of the program does not compromise the stability required for diverse field environments. While ‘Irum’ stands as a groundbreaking proof-of-concept for rapid cultivar development via ISB systems, its development process highlights a critical transition from purely speed-oriented breeding to a more robust, high-precision paradigm that integrates molecular diagnostics at every accelerated step.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study demonstrates the first successful registration of a bread cultivar ‘Irum’, through the practical application of an ISB system in South Korea. The integration of SV and SB protocols enabled a dramatic reduction in the breeding cycle, shortening the total duration from the initial cross to cultivar release from 128 months to 80 months. Multivariate analysis using PCA confirmed that the SB population effectively maintained the trait diversity required for selection. This diversity allowed for the strategic identification of elite lines that successfully navigated the inherent trade-off between yield potential and grain quality. Consequently, ‘Irum’ was selected for 14.6% yield increase over ‘Keumgang’ and its superior baking suitability, producing a significantly larger volume than the high-quality check ‘Baekkang’. While ‘Irum’ serves as a proof-of-concept for rapid development, its observed reduction in cold tolerance highlights a critical agronomic limitation that necessitates a high-precision breeding paradigm. Integrating molecular technologies such as MAS and GS into SB infrastructures will be critical for pre-screening environmental adaptation and end-use quality. In conclusion, this study provides a robust framework for enhancing national food security by significantly accelerating the delivery of new wheat cultivars to the agricultural sector.
