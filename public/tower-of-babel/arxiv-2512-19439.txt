An Inverse Scattering Inspired Fourier Neural Operator for Time-Dependent PDE Learning
Rixin Yu

Abstract
Learning accurate and stable time-advancement operators for nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) remains challenging, particularly for chaotic, stiff, and long-horizon dynamical systems. While neural operator methods such as the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) and Koopman-inspired extensions achieve good short-term accuracy, their long-term stability is often limited by unconstrained latent representations and cumulative rollout errors. In this work, we introduce an inverse scattering inspired Fourier Neural Operator (IS-FNO), motivated by the reversibility and spectral evolution structure underlying the classical inverse scattering transform. The proposed architecture enforces a near-reversible pairing between lifting and projection maps through an explicitly invertible neural transformation, and models latent temporal evolution using exponential Fourier layers that naturally encode linear and nonlinear spectral dynamics. We systematically evaluate IS-FNO against baseline FNO and Koopman-based models on a range of benchmark PDEs, including the Michelson-Sivashinsky and Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equations (in one and two dimensions), as well as the integrable Korteweg-de Vries and Kadomtsev-Petviashvili equations. The results demonstrate that IS-FNO achieves lower short-term errors and substantially improved long-horizon stability in non-stiff regimes. For integrable systems, reduced IS-FNO variants that embed analytical scattering structure retain competitive long-term accuracy despite limited model capacity. Overall, this work shows that incorporating physical structure—particularly reversibility and spectral evolution—into neural operator design significantly enhances robustness and long-term predictive fidelity for nonlinear PDE dynamics.



I
Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) provide a fundamental mathematical description of spatio-temporal dynamics arising in a wide range of physical systems, including fluid flows, combustion, climate dynamics, and nonlinear wave propagation. Despite their central role, the numerical solution of nonlinear PDEs—especially over long time horizons or in regimes characterized by stiffness, instability, or chaos—remains computationally demanding. High-fidelity solvers often incur prohibitive costs, motivating the development of reduced-order or data-driven alternatives that can deliver accurate and efficient predictions.

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) have substantially reshaped the computational treatment of PDEs. In particular,
operator learning
has emerged as a powerful framework for approximating mappings between infinite-dimensional function spaces. Early data-driven approaches relied on convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
15
;
51
;
2
;
3
;
20
;
37
;
43
, which interpret discretized PDE solutions as images and learn grid-dependent input-output mappings. While effective in many settings, such approaches are inherently tied to fixed discretizations.

Neural operator methods
29
;
23
overcome this limitation by learning operators directly in function space, enabling discretization-invariant generalization. Representative examples include the Deep Operator Network (DeepONet)
30
and the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)
28
. DeepONet employs a branch-trunk architecture to approximate nonlinear operators, whereas FNO exploits spectral convolution to efficiently represent global interactions in space. Subsequent extensions have incorporated wavelet representations
16
;
40
, complex geometries
5
, and parameter-dependent operators
48
.

Among the operators of interest in PDE modeling, the
solution time-advancement operator
plays a particularly important role. Given a solution field
\phi(\bm{x},t)
at time
t
, this operator maps it to the solution at a later time
t+\Delta_{t}
under prescribed boundary conditions, where
\bm{x}
denotes the spatial coordinates and
t
denotes time. Iterative application of such an operator enables long-horizon prediction of dynamical evolution, which is essential in applications such as unsteady fluid dynamics, flame front propagation, and pattern-forming systems.

Learning accurate and stable time-advancement operators is, however, challenging. While many neural operator models achieve impressive single-step accuracy, small errors can accumulate rapidly during rollout, leading to degraded long-term predictions—particularly for chaotic or unstable PDEs. Prior work has sought to mitigate this issue through recurrent or multi-step training strategies
28
;
49
.
Nevertheless, long-term robustness remains limited when the latent representations lack physical structure or consistency constraints.

Motivated by
Koopman operator theory

21
;
32
;
4
, which asserts that nonlinear dynamical systems can be represented as linear evolution in an appropriately lifted observable space, our recent work
45
introduced the
Koopman Fourier Neural Operator
(kFNO). By learning a latent linear (or weakly nonlinear) evolution operator, kFNO demonstrated improved performance over standard FNO in modeling unstable flame front dynamics, capturing both short-term evolution and long-term statistical behavior.

In kFNO, the physical-state field is first lifted into a high-dimensional latent function space, evolved forward in time via repeated application of Fourier layers, and then projected back to the physical space. The lifting and projection maps are learned independently and are not constrained to be inverses of one another. From a physical perspective, however, a desirable property of a consistent time-advancement operator is
near reversibility
: in the limit of vanishing time step, the combined lift-evolve-project operation should recover the original state.

A closely related structural principle appears in the classical
inverse scattering transform
(IST)
12
;
1
;
34
, which provides exact solution formulas for certain integrable nonlinear PDEs. In the IST framework, the solution
\phi(\bm{x},t_{0})
is mapped to scattering data
\mathcal{S}(t_{0})
via a forward transform,

\mathbb{S}:\;\phi(\bm{x},t_{0})\mapsto\mathcal{S}(t_{0}),

(1)

the scattering data evolve trivially in time,

\mathbb{A}:\;\mathcal{S}(t_{0})\mapsto\mathcal{S}(t),

(2)

and the solution at time
t
is recovered through the inverse transform,

\mathbb{S}^{-1}:\;\mathcal{S}(t)\mapsto\phi(\bm{x},t).

(3)

A key feature of this construction is exact reversibility,
\mathbb{S}^{-1}\circ\mathbb{S}(\phi)=\phi
. A concise overview of IST on an example equation is provided in Appendix
A
.

Inspired by this structure, we propose an
Inverse Scattering-inspired Fourier Neural Operator
(IS-FNO). The IS-FNO architecture enforces a near-invertible pairing between the lifting and projection networks and introduces an
exponential Fourier layer
to model latent temporal evolution in a manner analogous to the simple spectral dynamics of scattering data. This design embeds physical structure—reversibility and spectral evolution—directly into the neural operator.

We assess the proposed IS-FNO on a suite of benchmark PDEs chosen to span a range of dynamical behaviors and modeling challenges. The Michelson-Sivashinsky and Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equations serve as prototypical examples of unstable and chaotic dissipative systems relevant to flame front and pattern-forming dynamics. The Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation provides a canonical integrable model with soliton solutions, while its higher-dimensional extension probes the generalization of operator learning frameworks beyond one-dimensional settings. Together, these benchmarks enable a systematic evaluation of accuracy, stability, and long-horizon robustness.

V

VI
summarizes the main findings and discusses future directions.

II
Problem Setup for Operator Learning

In this section, we outline the problem setup for learning PDE operators, together with a description of recurrent training approaches.

Consider a system governed by a PDE, typically involving mappings between functional spaces. A general operator can be written as

G:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime};\quad v(x)\mapsto v^{\prime}(x^{\prime}),

(4)

where the input function
v(x)
, with
x\in\mathcal{D}
, belongs to the functional space
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{D},\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
. The output function
v^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
, with
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, belongs to
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}^{\prime}})
, where
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
denote the input and output domains, respectively.

In this work, we are primarily interested in the time-advancement operator of the PDE solution, defined as

G:\phi(x,t)\mapsto\phi(x,t_{1}),

(5)

where
\phi(x,t)
denotes the PDE solution at time
t
, and
t_{j}=t+j\Delta_{t}
for
j=1,2,\dots
represents future time instances separated by a time step
\Delta_{t}
. For simplicity, we assume that the input and output share the same domain and codomain, i.e.
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=\mathcal{V}
,
d^{\prime}=d
, and
d_{v}^{\prime}=d_{v}
, with periodic boundary conditions on
\mathcal{D}
.

In some cases, it is preferable to work with a related operator that outputs solutions over multiple consecutive steps:

\bar{G}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{n};\quad\phi(x,t)\mapsto(\phi(x,t_{1}),\phi(x,t_{2}),\dots,\phi(x,t_{n})),

(6)

where
\mathcal{V}^{n}
denotes the Cartesian product of
n
copies of
\mathcal{V}
. These operators satisfy the relationship

\bar{G}=(G,G^{2},\dots,G^{n}),

(7)

where the superscript denotes iterative composition:
G^{n}=G\circ\cdots\circ G
(
n
times).

To approximate the operators
G
and
\bar{G}
using neural networks, we introduce the parameterized models

\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{V}\times\Theta\to\mathcal{V},\text{ or }\mathcal{G}_{\theta}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V},

(8)

and

\bar{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{V}\times\Theta\to\mathcal{V}^{n},\text{ or }\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{n},

(9)

where
\theta\in\Theta
, denoting the space of trainable parameters. The learning objective is to find
\hat{\theta}\in\Theta
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\theta}}\approx G
or
\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}\approx\bar{G}
.

Our previous studies
49
;
48
;
47
focused on learning the single-step advancement operator
G
. Starting from an initial solution
\phi(x,t)
, recurrent application of the learned operator
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\theta}}
enables long-range rollout predictions by iteratively feeding each output back into the model. A well-learned operator should yield accurate short-term predictions. Although long-term predictions generally diverge for chaotic PDEs, it is still desirable for them to reproduce key statistical properties of the underlying nonlinear system.

To promote numerical stability, the works
49
;
48
;
47
employed a one-to-many training strategy, in which
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
was trained to predict multiple successive steps from a single input. Specifically, given a dataset

\{\,v_{j},\;(G^{1}v_{j},\dots,G^{n}v_{j})\,\}_{j=1}^{Z}

consisting of
Z
input-output pairs arranged in a
1
-to-
n
format, the loss function is

\hat{J}=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\chi}\left[C\left((\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{1}v,\dots,\mathcal{G}_{\theta}^{n}v),\;(G^{1}v,\dots,G^{n}v)\right)\right],

(10)

where
v
is sampled from a distribution
\chi
over the input space, and
C
denotes the relative mean squared error

C(a,b)=\frac{\|a-b\|_{2}}{\|b\|_{2}}.

Minimizing this loss,

\hat{\theta}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\hat{J},

yields the trained operator
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\theta}}
.

In our earlier work on Koopman-inspired neural operators
45
, we proposed an alternative approach that directly learns the multi-step operator
\bar{G}
through the network
\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}
. In the present work, we continue to focus on learning
\bar{G}
. Using the same training dataset, the loss function becomes

\hat{J}=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\chi}\left[C\left(\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}v,\;(G^{1}v,\dots,G^{n}v)\right)\right],

(11)

and minimizing it produces the trained model
\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}
.

After training, recurrent application of
\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}
enables long-horizon predictions by repeatedly feeding the last predicted state (i.e. the
n
th component) back as the next input. More precisely, given an initial input
\phi(x,t)
, the predicted state at time
t+j\Delta_{t}
for integer
j\geq 1
is obtained as

\bigl(\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}^{\lceil j/n\rceil}\,\phi(x,t)\bigr)\,[\,j\bmod n\,].

(12)

III
Operator Learning Methods

This section describes the operator-learning architectures used in this work. We begin with a brief review of the baseline Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)
28
, followed by the Koopman-theory-inspired extension (kFNO)
45
. We then introduce the proposed inverse scattering inspired Fourier Neural Operator (IS-FNO).

III.1
Baseline Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)

FNO
28
learns infinite-dimensional operators by parameterizing integral kernels in Fourier space. When applied to learning the single-step time-advancement operator
G
, FNO models the mapping
\phi(x,t)\mapsto\phi(x,t_{1})
through the composition

\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\big|_{\text{FNO}}=P\circ H^{\prime}\circ L,

where
L
is a lifting map,
H^{\prime}
is a hidden evolution map, and
P
is a projection map.

The lifting map
L:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}
embeds the input field into a higher-dimensional feature space,
\phi(x,t)\mapsto\varepsilon_{0}(x)
, where
\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}:=\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{\varepsilon}})
with
d_{\varepsilon}>d_{v}
. A common implementation is a single linear layer,

\varepsilon_{0}(x)=\bm{w}_{L}\,\phi(x,t)+\bm{b}_{L},

(13)

where
\bm{w}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{\varepsilon}}
and
\bm{b}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\varepsilon}}
are trainable parameters.

The hidden evolution map
H^{\prime}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}
performs a sequence of updates
\varepsilon_{0}\mapsto\varepsilon_{1}\mapsto\cdots\mapsto\varepsilon_{L}
. Each update
\varepsilon_{l}\mapsto\varepsilon_{l+1}
is implemented via a Fourier layer of the form

\varepsilon_{l+1}=\alpha\varepsilon_{l}+\sigma\!\left(\bm{w}_{\mathcal{F}}\,\varepsilon_{l}+\bm{b}_{\mathcal{F}}+\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{\bm{r}^{*}\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon_{l}\}\right\}\right),

(14)

where
\bm{w}_{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\varepsilon}\times d_{\varepsilon}}
and
\bm{b}_{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\varepsilon}}
are learnable parameters,
\sigma
is a pointwise nonlinearity, and
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
denote the Fourier transform and its inverse. The coefficient
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
controls the residual connection:
\alpha=0
disables it, while
\alpha=1
enables a full skip connection.

The tensor
\bm{r}^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2\kappa_{1}^{\max}-1)\times\cdots\times\kappa_{d}^{\max}\times d_{\varepsilon}\times d_{\varepsilon}}

contains complex-valued learnable weights that mix Fourier modes across the feature dimension accroding to

\bigl(\bm{r}^{*}\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon\}\bigr)_{\bm{\kappa},\,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{r}^{*})_{\bm{\kappa},\,i,j}\,\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon\}_{\bm{\kappa},\,j},\qquad i=1,\ldots,d_{\varepsilon},

(15)

where
\bm{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{d})
denotes the multi-dimensional wave-number index.
For real-valued
\varepsilon(x)
, conjugate symmetry is enforced by restricting independent modes to

\kappa_{m}\in(-\kappa_{m}^{\max},\kappa_{m}^{\max})
for
m<d
and
\kappa_{d}\in[0,\kappa_{d}^{\max})
.
Fourier modes outside a user-prescribed cutoff
\bm{\kappa}^{\max}
are truncated to zero.

Finally, the projection map
P:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}
maps the final hidden state back to the physical space,
\varepsilon_{L}(x)\mapsto\phi(x,t_{1})
. In practice,
P
is typically implemented as a two-layer perceptron.

III.2
Koopman Fourier Neural Operator (kFNO)

The kFNO architecture
45
generalizes the baseline FNO by learning the
n
-step solution-advancement operator
\bar{G}
. This is achieved through the composite mapping

\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}\big|_{\text{kFNO}}=\bar{P}\circ\bar{Q}\circ\bar{K}\circ H\circ L,

as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Following the baseline FNO, the input field undergoes a two-stage lifting,

\phi(x,t)\;\mapsto\;\varepsilon_{0}(x)\;\mapsto\;\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}(x),

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}
is obtained via the lifting map
L
followed by a hidden evolution map
H:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}
, analogous to
H^{\prime}
.

The Koopman-inspired extension introduces the operator

\bar{K}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}^{\,n};\qquad\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}(x)\mapsto(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}(x),\ldots,\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n}(x)),

where future hidden states are generated via repeated application of a single advancement operator
A:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}
:

\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}(x)=A^{\,j}\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}(x),\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.

This recursive structure enables kFNO to approximate multi-step temporal evolution directly in the lifted feature space. Prevous work
45
recommends
A
to be implemented as a stack of Fourier layers.

The map

\bar{Q}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}^{\,n}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}^{\,n};\qquad(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n})\mapsto(\varepsilon^{\dagger}_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon^{\dagger}_{n}),

applies additional transformations to each intermediate hidden state. A simple and effective choice
45
is to apply a shared operator
Q:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}
independently to each
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}
.

Finally, the projection map

\bar{P}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}^{\,n}\to\mathcal{V}^{n}

maps each refined hidden state
\varepsilon^{\dagger}_{j}
to the corresponding physical output
\phi(x,t_{j})
, typically by independently applying the same projection operator
P
.

III.3
Inverse Scattering inspired Fourier Neural Operator (IS-FNO)

Figure 1:

Schematic comparison of inverse scattering based and Koopman inspired Fourier Neural Operator architectures.

We now introduce the proposed IS-FNO, which learns the
n
-step solution-advancement operator
\bar{G}
through the composition (see Fig.
1
)

\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\theta}\big|_{\text{IS-FNO}}=\bar{L}_{0}^{\dagger}\circ\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}\circ\bar{K}\circ\mathcal{R}\circ L_{0}.

The first lifting map
L_{0}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon};\phi(x,t)\mapsto\varepsilon_{0}(x)
is implemented by stacking
(d_{\varepsilon}-d_{v})
zero-valued channels onto the input field. This corresponds to using the linear lifting map (
13
) with

\bm{b}_{L}=\bm{0},\qquad(\bm{w}_{L})_{ij}=\delta_{ij},

(16)

i.e., an identity embedding padded with zeros.

The second map
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon};\varepsilon_{0}(x)\mapsto\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}(x)
, is constructed as a reversible residual network (RevNet)
14
.
The input and output are split along the co-dimension direction:

\varepsilon_{0}=a\cup b,\qquad\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}=a^{\prime}\cup b^{\prime},

with

a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon_{a}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{\varepsilon_{a}}}),\qquad b,b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon_{b}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{\varepsilon_{b}}}),

where
1\leq d_{\varepsilon_{a}}\leq d_{\varepsilon}-1
and
d_{\varepsilon_{b}}=d_{\varepsilon}-d_{\varepsilon_{a}}
.
The map takes the form

\displaystyle a^{\prime}

\displaystyle=a+g\!\left(b+f(a)\right),

\displaystyle b^{\prime}

\displaystyle=b+f(a),

(17)

with

f:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon_{a}}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon_{b}},\qquad g:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon_{b}}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon_{a}}.

Each of the sub-maps
f
and
g
is implemented in three parts:
(i) an “adapter” that maps the input to an intermediate functional space
\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon_{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{\varepsilon_{*}}})
via a single linear layer (or identity if the co-dimension already matches),
(ii) a few stacked Fourier layers operating in the fixed co-dimension
d_{\varepsilon_{*}}
, and
(iii) a two-layer perceptron that adjusts the co-dimension, first raising it to a large number (we use 128) and then reducing it to the target dimension (
d_{\varepsilon_{b}}
for
f
and
d_{\varepsilon_{a}}
for
g
).

Importantly,
\mathcal{R}
admits an exact inverse

\mathcal{R}^{-1}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon};(a^{\prime}\cup b^{\prime})\mapsto(a\cup b)
,
given by

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=a^{\prime}-g(b^{\prime}),

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=b^{\prime}-f\!\left(a^{\prime}-g(b^{\prime})\right).

(18)

As in kFNO, the Koopman map

\bar{K}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}^{n};\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}(x)\mapsto(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}(x),\ldots,\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n}(x))

is realized by repeated application of an advancement operator
A:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}
, so that
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}(x)=A^{j}\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}(x)
with
A:\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}\mapsto\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j+1}
.
While
A
can be implemented using vanilla Fourier layers, we propose a more effective
exponential Fourier layer
:

\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j+1}=\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}+\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{[\exp(\bm{r}^{\prime})-\bm{I}]\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}\}\right\}+\gamma\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{[\exp(\bm{r}^{o})-\bm{I}]\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}\}\right\}\right)^{2},

(19)

where
\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{o}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2\kappa_{1}^{\max}-1)\times\ldots\times\kappa_{d}^{\max}\times d_{\varepsilon}\times d_{\varepsilon}}
are learnable parameters, the matrix exponential is applied over the last two dimensions,

\bm{I}
is the identity matrix broadcast to match the appropriate shape, and
Fourier modes with frequencies above
\bm{\kappa}^{\max}
are truncated.

This layer may be interpreted as sequentially updating the scattering data as

\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}\}\mapsto\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j+1}\}.

The parameter
\gamma\in\{0,1\}
is user-specified.
When
\gamma=0
, the layer reduces to a purely linear evolution:

\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}\}=\exp(j\bm{r}^{\prime})\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}\},\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,n,

(20)

of which the KdV scattering relation (
39
) is a special case.
When
\gamma=1
, the additional quadratic term accommodates nonlinear effects.

The fourth map
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}^{n}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}^{n}
applies
\mathcal{R}^{-1}
to each time step:

\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}(x)\mapsto\varepsilon^{\dagger}_{j}(x),\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.

Finally,
\bar{L}_{0}^{\dagger}
maps the multi-step hidden states back to the solution space, producing

(\phi(x,t_{1}),\ldots,\phi(x,t_{n}))
.
Each update
\varepsilon^{\dagger}_{j}(x)\mapsto\phi(x,t_{j})
is achieved via the simple dimension-truncation map

L_{0}^{\dagger}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\varepsilon}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
,
which extracts the first
d_{v}
components of
\varepsilon^{\dagger}_{j}(x)
.
Thus, the remaining
(d_{\varepsilon}-d_{v})
co-dimensions of
\varepsilon^{\dagger}_{j}(x)
need not be computed.
If we further set
d_{\varepsilon_{a}}=d_{v}
, the second part of
f
in (
18
) is unnecessary and may be skipped to reduce computation.

We conclude by noting a generalization of the pair
(L_{0},L_{0}^{\dagger})
.
The zero-stacking implementation of
L_{0}
can be seen as a special case of the learnable lifting map (
13
) through using (
16
).
If
L_{0}
is instead implemented as a learnable
L
, the paired projection becomes a pseudo-inverse:

\phi(x,t_{j})=\bm{w}_{L}^{\dagger}\bigl(\varepsilon^{\dagger}_{j}(x)-\bm{b}_{L}\bigr),

where

\bm{w}_{L}^{\dagger}=(\bm{w}_{L}^{T}\bm{w}_{L})^{-1}\bm{w}_{L}^{T}.

III.4
Models and Variants

The kFNO and IS-FNO architectures admit several interchangeable design choices, resulting in a family of model variants. Below, we summarize all variants evaluated in this work.

Koopman Fourier Neural Operator variants.

Following
45
, the intermediate maps
Q
and

are implemented using
one and three-stacked vanilla Fourier layers, respectively. Variants differ primarily in the implementation of the advancement operator
A
:

•

kFNO
∗
:
A
is implemented using two stacked vanilla Fourier layers (Eq.
14
,
\alpha=1
);

•

kFNO
o
:
A
is implemented using the nonlinear exponential Fourier layer (Eq.
19
,
\gamma=1
);

•

kFNO
′
:
A
is implemented using the linear exponential Fourier layer (Eq.
19
,
\gamma=0
).

Baseline FNO.

A single-step operator learning model is recovered as a special case of the Koopman architecture:

•

baseline FNO
: obtained from
kFNO
∗
by setting the rollout horizon to
n=1
, in which case the composed map
\bar{P}\circ\bar{Q}\circ\bar{K}\circ H\circ L
reduces to the standard

through absorption of
Q
and
K
into
H^{\prime}=Q\circ K\circ H
.

In all Koopman-based models (including baseline FNO), the vanilla Fourier layers in
H
,
A
, and

use skip connections with coefficient
\alpha=1
.

Inverse Scattering FNO variants.

Within the IS-FNO framework, we study the parallel set of variants:

•

IS-FNO
∗
:
A
uses two stacked vanilla Fourier layers (
\alpha=1
with skip connection).

•

IS-FNO
o
:
A
uses the nonlinear exponential Fourier layer (
\gamma=1
);

•

IS-FNO
′
:

uses the linear exponential Fourier layer (

);

To more accurately evaluate performance on the 1d KdV equation—whose scattering data evolve exactly as
\propto e^{-\kappa^{3}t}
(Eq.

)—we further introduce two KdV-specific IS-FNO variants.
These employ a

, in which the frequency-dependent weights are parameterized as

\bm{r}^{\prime}(\kappa)=\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\kappa_{\max}}\right)^{p},\qquad\kappa=0,\ldots,\kappa_{\max},

where
\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\varepsilon}\times d_{\varepsilon}}
is a reduced-size learnable weight matrix and

The two variants considered are:

•

{}^{\prime}_{\kappa 3}
: exponent fixed to

, matching the analytical KdV scattering law;

•

IS-FNO
{}^{\prime}_{\kappa}
: exponent

IV
Governing Equations and Training Datasets

This section presents the governing PDEs and the construction of training datasets used to evaluate the proposed operator-learning models.
We focus on three families of nonlinear equations exhibiting distinct dynamical regimes:

•

the Michelson–Sivashinsky (MS) and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky (KS) equations, which model flame-front evolution driven by Darrieus–Landau (DL)
9
;
26
and diffusive–thermal (DT)
50
;
38
instabilities;

•

the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation
22
and its two-dimensional extension, the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP) equation
17
, which describe weakly nonlinear dispersive waves and soliton interactions.

High-resolution pseudo-spectral solvers are used to generate all datasets, covering eight distinct cases:
five one-dimensional configurations (MS and KS at two parameter settings each, and the KdV equation) and three two-dimensional configurations (MS, KS, and KP equation).
These problems collectively span chaotic dynamics, stiff cusp formation, multiscale flame-front evolution, and integrable soliton behavior, providing a comprehensive benchmark for assessing operator-learning architectures.

IV.1
Governing Equations for Flame-Front Instabilities

The spatiotemporal evolution of a statistically planar flame front is described by its displacement
\hat{\phi}(\hat{x},\hat{t}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The full
d
-dimensional Sivashinsky equation
11
reads

\partial_{\hat{t}}\hat{\phi}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\hat{\phi}\right)^{2}=-4(1+\mathrm{Le}^{*})^{2}\nabla^{4}\hat{\phi}-\mathrm{Le}^{*}\nabla^{2}\hat{\phi}+(1-\Omega)\,\Gamma(\hat{\phi}),

(22)

where
\Gamma
is a linear nonlocal operator,

\Gamma(\hat{\phi})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left(|\kappa|\,\mathcal{F}(\hat{\phi})\right),

and
\Omega
is the density ratio between products and reactants.
Here
\nabla
,
\nabla^{2}
, and
\nabla^{4}
denote the gradient, Laplacian, and biharmonic operators.
\mathrm{Le}^{*}
is the effective Lewis number, representing the ratio of thermal to mass diffusivity in the deficient reactant.

With suitable nondimensionalization
47
of time
t\sim\hat{t}
, space
x\sim\hat{x}
, and the flame displacement
\phi\sim\hat{\phi}
, the Sivashinsky equation reduces to two limiting cases:

1.

Michelson–Sivashinsky (MS)
33
(DL instability):

\frac{1}{\tau}\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}(\nabla\phi)^{2}=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\nabla^{2}\phi+\frac{1}{\beta}\Gamma(\phi),

(23)

2.

Kuramoto–Sivashinsky (KS)
25
(DT instability):

\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}(\nabla\phi)^{2}=-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\nabla^{2}\phi-\frac{1}{\beta^{4}}\nabla^{4}\phi.

(24)

We consider periodic domains
\mathcal{D}=[0,2\pi]^{d}
.
The parameter
\beta
corresponds to the largest unstable wave number according to linear stablity analysis;
\tau=\beta/10
adjusts the time scales of DL instality
47
.

Known dynamical behavior.

The KS equation exhibits spatiotemporal chaos for sufficiently large
\beta
and is widely used as a benchmark problem in PDE learning.
The MS equation admits an exact pole-decomposition formulation
39
, reducing the PDE to a finite-dimensional ODE system.
At small
\beta
, MS solutions converge to a stable “giant-cusp” profile.
At large
\beta
, the cusp tip becomes highly curved and extremely sensitive to round-off noise, leading to persistent small-scale wrinkles that convect along the front.
For very large
\beta
(e.g.,
\beta\gtrsim 50
), resolving peak curvature requires significantly finer meshes
49
.
Additional background may be found in
41
;
42
;
35
;
10
;
24
;
18
;
8
;
7
;
36
;
44
.

IV.2
Governing Equations for Nonlinear Wave Dynamics

The 1d Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation
22
,

\partial_{t}\phi+6\phi\partial_{x}\phi+\partial_{x}^{3}\phi=0,

(25)

describes weakly nonlinear, weakly dispersive waves and is a classical integrable system.
Its 2d extension, the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP) equation
17
of type II, is

\partial_{x_{1}}\!\left(\partial_{t}\phi+6\phi\partial_{x_{1}}\phi+\partial_{x_{1}}^{3}\phi\right)+\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}\phi=0,

(26)

for which
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
.
We consider periodic domains
\mathcal{D}=[0,l_{\mathrm{KDV}}]^{d}
with
l_{\mathrm{KDV}}=20
.
Well-posedness of KP requires intial condition
\phi_{0}(x):=\phi(x,t_{0})
to satisfy the constraint

\int_{0}^{l_{\mathrm{KDV}}}\left(\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}\phi_{0}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{1}=0.

(27)

IST structure (summary).

As outlined in Appendix
A
, the 1d KdV equation is solvable via the inverse scattering transform (IST)
12
;
1
;
34
.
The initial field serves as a potential in a stationary Schrödinger problem (
36
); discrete eigenvalues correspond to solitons, and continuous spectra to dispersive radiation.
Time evolution is encoded through the isospectral Lax pair
27
, and the solution is reconstructed via the Gelfand–Levitan–Marchenko equation
13
;
31
, (
40
).

The one-soliton solution is

\phi(x,t)=\frac{s}{2}\,\mathrm{sech}^{2}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2}(x-st)\right),

(28)

whose associated Schrödinger operator has one discrete eigenvalue
\lambda=-s/2
.

IV.3
Training Dataset Construction

All datasets are generated using a pseudo-spectral solver with integrating factors for linear terms and a fourth-fifth order Runge–Kutta scheme for nonlinear terms
19
.

IV.3.1
One-Dimensional Datasets

We construct five 1d datasets: MS and KS at
\beta=10
and
40
, and the KdV equation. All 1d simulations use 256 grid points.

MS and KS dynamics.

For KS at both values of
\beta
, long-time solutions are chaotic. For MS at
\beta=10
, the solution relaxes to a stable giant-cusp profile, whereas at
\beta=40
the dynamics become highly sensitive to round-off noise, producing persistent small-scale wrinkles riding on a large cusp.
Note that at even larger
\beta

.

MS and KS datasets.

For each MS and KS case we generate:

•

250 short
trajectories on
0\leq t\leq 75
, with 500 snapshots and
\Delta_{t}=0.15
;

•

one long
trajectory on
0\leq t\leq 18{,}750

Initial conditions are random physical-space perturbations

\phi_{0}(x_{j})\sim\mathcal{U}(0,0.03),

(29)

where
x_{j}
are the grid points and
\mathcal{U}(a,b)
denotes the uniform distribution on
[a,b]
.
For more details about MS and KS datasets, the reader is referred to
45

KdV dataset.

We generate 350 sequences on
0\leq t\leq 1.22
, each with 500 snapshots at
\Delta_{t}=0.0024
.
Two classes of initial conditions are used:

1.

Low-wavenumber randomization:

250 sequences are initialized by prescribing Fourier coefficients as:

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}\{\phi_{0}\}=\xi_{\kappa}e^{i\theta_{\kappa}},

(30)

where
\theta_{\kappa}\in[0,2\pi)
and
\xi_{\kappa}\in[0,256/2]
for
\kappa\leq 9
, and zero otherwise.

2.

Random soliton superposition:

The remaining 100 sequences are initialized by superimposing
M\in[3,8]
randomly selected soliton solutions (Eq.

):

\phi_{0}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{s_{j}}{2}\,\mathrm{sech}^{2}\!\left[\frac{\sqrt{s_{j}}}{2}(x-c_{j})\right],

(31)

where
s_{j}\in[0.4,2]
and
c_{j}\in[0,l_{\mathrm{KDV}}]

x-c_{j}
falls outside
\mathcal{D}

If the initial wave packets do not overlap, each soliton propagates at its own speed until interactions occur.

We construct three 2d datasets: MS and KS at
\beta=15

grid.

For 2d MS and KS, we generate 45 sequences over

\xi_{\kappa}\in[0,0.01]

The MS and KS datasets were used in previous work

Initial conditions follow the randomized spectrum (

) with
\xi_{\kappa}\in[0,128^{2}/15]

Validation sets.

For each of the eight total datasets (five 1d and three 2d), a validation dataset is constructed, comprising at least 10% of the corresponding training data.

V.1
Evaluation Metrics

Model accuracy is assessed by direct comparison between predictions and reference numerical solutions.
Short-term predictive accuracy is quantified using the relative
L_{2}
error averaged over
n=20
prediction steps, computed on the training and validation datasets using Eq. (
10
) for the baseline model and Eq. (
11
) for the others.

Long-horizon behavior is characterized using time-accumulated errors over extended rollouts. For a
j
-step prediction this is defined as

\displaystyle J(j)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{0}\sim\chi}\!\left[C\big(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\theta}}^{j}\phi_{0},\;G^{j}\phi_{0}\big)\right],

\displaystyle\text{for baseline {FNO}},

\displaystyle J(j)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{0}\sim\chi}\!\left[C\big((\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\hat{\theta}}^{\lceil j/n\rceil}\phi_{0})[j\bmod n],\;G^{j}\phi_{0}\big)\right],

\displaystyle\text{for other models}.

(32)

For chaotic or noise-sensitive stiff systems such as KS and MS, trajectory-wise agreement eventually becomes infeasible, even for the numerical solver itself. Therefore, we additionally evaluate the spatial autocorrelation

\mathfrak{K}(r)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{0}\sim\chi}\!\left(\frac{\int_{t_{*}}^{t_{*}+T}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\phi(x,t)\,\phi(x-r,t)\,\text{d}x\text{d}t}{\int_{t_{*}}^{t_{*}+T}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\phi(x,t)\,\phi(x,t)\,\text{d}x\text{d}t}\right),

(33)

where
\phi(x,t_{*})
denotes the model prediction after suffcient late time (e.g.
t_{*}\geq 1000\Delta_{t}
).
This statistic measures long-range structural coherence and provides a robust diagnostic of dynamical fidelity in chaotic regimes.

VI
Summary and Conclusion

This work proposed an inverse scattering inspired neural operator framework for learning solution time-advancement operators of nonlinear partial differential equations.
The design is motivated by the structural insight of the classical inverse scattering transform (IST), which decomposes nonlinear evolution into a forward scattering map, a simple evolution in spectral space, and an inverse scattering reconstruction.
Adapting this idea to the neural-operator setting, the proposed IS-FNO enforces a near-reversible pairing between lifting and projection maps and employs exponential Fourier layers to model latent temporal evolution in a structured and physically motivated manner.

A comprehensive set of numerical experiments was conducted on eight benchmark datasets spanning integrable, chaotic, stiff, and noise-sensitive regimes, including one- and two-dimensional variants of the Michelson–Sivashinsky, Kuramoto–Sivashinsky, Korteweg–de Vries, and Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equations.
Across these datasets, IS-FNO consistently outperformed both the baseline FNO and Koopman-inspired FNO architectures in terms of short-term accuracy and long-horizon stability, particularly when nonlinear exponential Fourier layers were used to parameterize latent dynamics.

Several key observations emerge from the results.
First, enforcing near-reversibility between lifting and projection maps significantly stabilizes nonlinear latent-time evolution, leading to reduced error accumulation during rollout.
This advantage is most pronounced when combined with nonlinear exponential Fourier layers, which outperform both linear exponential layers and stacked vanilla Fourier layers in non-stiff regimes.
Second, the benefit of nonlinear latent evolution is equation dependent: for integrable systems such as the KdV equation, linear exponential dynamics already capture the dominant scattering behavior, while for chaotic systems, nonlinear latent interactions are essential for accurate long-term modeling.
Third, in extremely stiff and noise-sensitive regimes, such as the Michelson–Sivashinsky equation at large
\beta
, simpler vanilla Fourier layers can offer improved robustness at the expense of short-term accuracy, underscoring an inherent trade-off between expressiveness and numerical stability.

The study also demonstrates that embedding analytical structure into reduced models can be highly effective.
In particular, a restricted IS-FNO variant that hard-codes the
\kappa^{3}
scattering scaling of the KdV equation achieves competitive long-horizon performance despite limited parameterization, outperforming unconstrained baseline models in long-term predictions.

Overall, this work highlights the importance of architectural inductive biases—specifically reversibility and physically motivated spectral evolution—in neural operator design.
The proposed IS-FNO framework provides a flexible yet principled approach for learning time-advancement operators of nonlinear PDEs, offering improved stability, interpretability, and generalization across a broad range of dynamical regimes.

Possible futher works include refining the architecture to better align with classical scattering structure, where integrable systems exhibit continuous spectrum (radiation) and discrete eigenvalues (solitons); disentangling these components could improve interpretability and accuracy.
Extending IS-FNO to continuous-time modeling via adjoint methods as in neural ODEs
6
is another avenue, offering adaptive time-stepping and improved handling of irregularly sampled data.
Additional directions include improved treatment of diverse boundary conditions and noisy data, applications to more complex multi-physics systems, adaptive spectral parameterizations, and tighter integration of analytical structure for partially integrable or weakly chaotic dynamics.

Appendix A
Inverse Scattering Transform of 1d KdV Equation

The one-dimensional KdV equation (
25
) can be recast as a
Lax equation

\partial_{t}\mathcal{L}=[\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L}]=\mathcal{M}\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}\mathcal{M},

(34)

for a pair of differential operators
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})
defined as

\mathcal{L}:=-\partial_{x}^{2}+\phi(x,t),\qquad\mathcal{M}:=-4\partial_{x}^{3}+6\phi\partial_{x}+3\phi_{x}.

(35)

Here,
\mathcal{L}
is a Schrödinger-type operator with potential
\phi(x,t)
. The corresponding eigenvalue problem is

\mathcal{L}\psi=-\psi_{xx}+\phi(x,t)\psi=\lambda\psi,

(36)

where
\psi
is an eigenfunction associated with eigenvalue
\lambda
.
From the Lax equation (
34
), it follows that
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{M}
evolve compatibly such that the eigenvalues
\lambda
are time-invariant (the
isospectral property
), while the eigenfunctions evolve according to

\partial_{t}\psi=-\mathcal{M}\psi.

(37)

The inverse scattering transform (IST) method
12
;
1
;
34
for Eq. (
25
) consists of three conceptual steps corresponding to Eqs.
1
-
3
in the introduction section:

1.
Forward scattering transform
:
Given an initial condition
\phi(x,0)
, one solves the stationary Schrödinger problem (
36
) to determine the scattering data

\mathcal{S}(0)=\{\,T(k),R^{\pm}(k,0);\,c^{\pm}_{1}(0),\dots,c^{\pm}_{N}(0)\,\},

(38)

where
T(k)
is the transmission coefficient,
R^{\pm}(k,t)
are the left and right reflection coefficients corresponding to the continuous spectrum, and
c_{j}^{\pm}(t)
are the norming constants associated with discrete eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}=-k_{j}^{2}
.

2.
Time evolution of scattering data
:
The time evolution of these coefficients follows directly from (
37
) as

\displaystyle R^{-}(k,t)

\displaystyle=R^{-}(k,0)\,e^{-8ik^{3}t},

\displaystyle R^{+}(k,t)

\displaystyle=R^{+}(k,0)\,e^{+8ik^{3}t},

\displaystyle c^{-}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=c^{-}_{j}(0)\,e^{+4k_{j}^{3}t},\quad j=1,\dots,N,

\displaystyle c^{+}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=c^{+}_{j}(0)\,e^{-4k_{j}^{3}t},\quad j=1,\dots,N,

(39)

while the transmission coefficient
T(k)
and eigenvalues
k_{j}
remain constant in time.

3.
Inverse scattering transform
:
The field
\phi(x,t)
is reconstructed from
\mathcal{S}(t)
by solving the Gelfand–Levitan–Marchenko integral equation
13
;
31

K(x,z,t)+F(x+z,t)+\int_{x}^{\infty}K(x,y,t)F(y+z,t)\,\mathrm{d}y=0,

(40)

where

\phi(x,t)=-2\,\partial_{x}K(x,x,t),

(41)

and the kernel
F(x,t)
is defined in terms of the scattering data as

F(x,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R^{+}(k,t)e^{ikx}\,\mathrm{d}k+\sum_{j=1}^{N}c^{-}_{j}(t)^{\,2}\,e^{-k_{j}x}.

(42)

The IST method thus maps the nonlinear PDE (
25
) into a linear evolution in the scattering space.
For reflectionless potentials (
R^{\pm}\equiv 0
), only discrete eigenvalues contribute, leading to
N
-soliton solutions.
The entire procedure can be viewed as a nonlinear generalization of the Fourier transform for integrable PDEs:
the forward transform converts
\phi(x,0)
into scattering coefficients, which evolve linearly in time,
and the inverse transform reconstructs
\phi(x,t)
from the evolved scattering data.

Model hyper-parameters and training details

All IS-FNO models employ the zero-stacking lifting map
L_{0}
and set
d_{\varepsilon_{a}}=d_{v}
, which avoids the final
f
-inversion step and therefore reduces computational cost.
To learn the four 1d MS and KS datasets, which contain significant high-frequency content, all 1d models use Fourier layers (either vanilla or exponential) with
\kappa^{\max}=128
.
For the 1d KdV dataset, which is less demanding, we set
\kappa^{\max}=32
to allow truncation of higher modes.
To learn the three 2d datasets, all models use Fourier layers with
\kappa^{\max}_{1}=\kappa^{\max}_{2}=64
.
In all kFNO models, the projection map
P

All models are trained for 1000 epochs using the Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.0025, weight decay 1e-6 and epsilon value 1e-6. A learning rate scheduler with step size 100 and decay factor 0.5 is applied. Gradient clipping with maximum norm 10 ensures training stability.
