Phylogenomic and comparative analyses of plastomes of the tribe Marsdenieae (Apocynaceae: Asclepiadoideae): insights into plastome characteristics and generic relationships with an emphasis on Asian genera
Authors: Rong Chen, Xin Yao, Jin-Yue Li, Yun-Xue Xiao, Li-Qiong Chen, Hai-Tao Li, Jun-Bo Yang, De-Zhu Li, Wen-Bin Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe tribe Marsdenieae is one of the five tribes within the subfamily Asclepiadoideae of the Apocynaceae. To date, the evolutionary dynamics of plastomes and phylogenetic relationships within the tribe Marsdenieae (Apocynaceae) remain poorly understood, particularly regarding the structural diversity driven by variations in the boundary of inverted repeat (IR). This study aimed to elucidate the evolutionary history of plastome structure and refine the taxonomic framework of Marsdenieae by analyzing IR boundary dynamics, repeat sequence features, and phylogenetic signals.ResultsIn this study, 76 plastomes of tribe Marsdenieae were analyzed using phylogenomic approaches and ancestral state reconstruction to investigate plastome structural variation and its evolutionary trajectory. Phylogenetic signals associated with variations of IR boundary were evaluated using EM Mantel tests and Delta statistics. Although Marsdenieae plastomes retain a conserved quadripartite structure, they exhibit substantial size variation (156,364–179,121 bp), primarily driven by IR expansion. Five types of the IR boundary (I–V) were identified, with Type III inferred as ancestral state; Type IV further diversified into three subtypes (Ⅳa, Ⅳb, and Ⅳc) corresponding to distinct clades (Clades A1, A2, and A5). Plastome phylogenetic analyses supported two major clades within Marsdenieae but revealed non-monophyly in genera Hoya, Marsdenia, and Dregea, and two Asian monotypic genera, Sinomarsdenia and Dolichopetalum, were supported as distinct genera. Moreover, based on molecular and morphological evidence, we propose merging Gongreos into Sinomarsdenia. Moreover, Asian Marsdenia species resolved into two subclades, while Dregea sinensis formed a represented lineage clearly separated from core Dregea taxa, indicating a well-supported phylogenetic divergence between these groups.ConclusionsIR boundary dynamics were central to plastome diversity in Marsdenieae, and IR structural variations were strongly consistent with phylogenetic signals, thus supporting relevant taxonomic revisions. However, complex relationships within Hoya and allied genera underscore the limitations of plastome data alone, highlighting the need for future integration of nuclear genomic data together with morphological and biogeographical evidence. This study advances our understanding of plastome evolution in Apocynaceae and highlights the urgency of resolving polyphyly and refining generic boundaries of Asian genera in the tribe Marsdenieae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09171-9.

Background

The tribe Marsdenieae comprises around 39 genera and approximately 740 species, making it the third-largest tribe in the subfamily Asclepiadoideae of Apocynaceae [1, 2]. The Marsdenieae species are primarily distributed in the tropical and subtropical Asia to Australia, and extend to Africa and the Americas, with only one species in Europe [3]. The Marsdenieae species are mostly herbs and lianas, and many are succulent epiphytic vines. They exhibit a wide variety of floral structures (Fig. 1). The tribe Marsdenieae can be easily distinguished from the other tribes of Asclepiadoideae by a characteristic combination of floral and pollinial features, including the presence of an apical membranous appendix on the anthers, erect pollinia, and a reduced or marginal pellucid germination zone [4–6]. A fair number of Marsdenieae species are highly valued in ornamental and medicines [1]. For example, species of Hoya R.Br. are very popular in the global flower market due to their distinctive flower fragrance and attractive appearance, and they have been extensively domesticated and cultivated [7]. Gymnema sylvestre (Retz.) R.Br. ex Sm., Marsdenia sinensis Hemsl., and Gongronemopsis tenacissima (Roxb.) S.Reuss, Liede & Meve (≡ Marsdenia tenacissima (Roxb.) Moon) contain important phytochemicals, including organic acids, alkaloids, and glycosides [8]. The tribe Marsdenieae has been well supported as monophyletic in recent phylogenetic analyses [9–11]. However, phylogenetic relationships of the Marsdenieae genera are not well resolved, and the genus-level classifications within the tribe have been repeatedly revised and modified. Endress et al. [12] proposed 26 genera in the Marsdenieae based on both morphological and phylogenetic evidence, establishing a preliminary classification framework. Among these genera, 20 genera were found in Asia, with 13 out of them being endemic to this region. Following the concept of Endress et al. [12], Hoya and Marsdenia R.Br. (> 150 spp.) were the two most species-rich genera in the tribe, and much of the taxonomic controversy in Marsdenieae could be attributed to these highly diverse genera and their close relatives. Both Hoya and Marsdenia have been recognized as non-monophyletic genera, and work in recent years has focused on delimiting monophyletic units in these two complexes [1, 7]. Based on morphological and phylogenetic evidence, Rodda et al. [7] treated Anatropanthus Schltr. as a synonym of Hoya s.s., and proposed a separate genus Papuahoya Rodda & Simonsson including some Hoya and Oreosparte-like species. Furthermore, the phylogenetic relationship between Hoya s.s. and Dischidia R.Br. is not full resolved [1, 13]. In addition, the genus Marsdenia had no consensus on its classification until the study by Liede-Schumann et al. [1]. Firstly, Espírito Santo et al. [3] proposed that all the American Marsdenia species should be treated as an independent genus Ruehssia H. Karst. ex Schltdl. Then, Liede-Schumann et al. [1] revised the tribe Marsdenieae into 39 genera with more species sampled by integrating morphological, geographical and phylogenetic evidence using 28 genera and 171 species. Of the 11 genera unsampled by Liede-Schumann et al. [1], Treutlera Hook.f [2]. and Gymnemopsis Costantin [14] have since been shown to be monophyletic and are still recognized at generic rank; nine genera remain to be sampled, with eight of them Asian endemics. In addition, the phylogenetic placement of certain species from genera that have been merged or split (e.g., Dregea E. Mey., Marsdenia) remains unresolved [2, 15]. The current development of the next generation sequencing techniques that allow us to reconstruct phylogeny with genomic data, which could improve the phylogenetic resolutions of the tribe Marsdenieae.

Recently, many plastome sequences of the Apocynaceae have been reported, representing 13 genera and more than 70 species from the tribe Marsdenieae (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/GenBank/), and the whole plastome sequences can provide a high capability for species delimitation and phylogenetic resolution in this tribe [16–18]. Rodda and Niissalo [19] achieved the isolation of an early-diverging genus, Papuahoya, from the Hoya group, based on plastome exon datasets. Wang et al. [15] extracted 77 protein-coding gene (PCG) sequences from the complete plastome to construct the phylogenetic relationships of the Apocynaceae and found that utilizing multiple PCG sequences provided better phylogenetic resolution compared to using only a few gene fragments. Meanwhile, a growing number of researchers conducted molecular evolution analyses and genomic comparisons with plastomes [19, 20]. Wang et al. [15] investigated plastome structural characteristics across Apocynaceae and found that pronounced plastome structural variations occur exclusively in species of the tribe Marsdenieae and its sister tribe Ceropegieae L. These variation types include IR expansion into the LSC region (Type III sensu Wang et al. [15]), as well as large-scale bidirectional expansion of the IR into both the SSC and LSC regions (Type IV sensu Wang et al. [15]). Notably, within the Hoya group, a dramatic expansion of the IR region into the SSC region was repeatedly reported, with the IR/SSC boundary shifting from ycf1 to ndhF. As a result, the SSC region in the Hoya group is one of the shortest known lineages among angiosperms [13, 15, 19]. However, there are still few comprehensive publications on plastome characteristics at the genus level of Asian Marsdenieae, and the plastome evolution within this group is still largely unaddressed, which restrict the development of phylogenetic studies and the mining of its genetic data.

The present study reconstructs the phylogeny of Marsdenieae genera using whole plastome sequences and conducts comparative analyses of plastome characteristics. We increased sampling coverage for Asian Marsdenieae species including one Asian monotypic genus, Sinomarsdenia P.T.Li & J.J.Chen. Twenty plastome sequences are newly assembled in this study. Together with the available Marsdenieae plastomes in the GenBank, we reconstruct the generic phylogeny and investigate the plastome characteristics in the tribe Marsdenieae. This study aims to: (1) infer for the phylogenetic relationships among Marsdenieae genera; and (2) explore the evolutionary tendency of plastome variations in the tribe Marsdenieae.

Results

Plastome characteristics of the tribe Marsdenieae

All the Marsdenieae plastomes displayed a typical quadripartite structure, consisting of one LSC and one SSC that are separated by two IRs (Table S1). Their sizes ranged as follows: the plastome size was 156,364 bp—179,121 bp, the LSC region was 85,994 bp—93,427 bp, the SSC region was 2,265 bp—19,767 bp, and the IR region was 25,808 bp—42,291 bp, respectively. The GC content in these plastomes ranged from 36.8%—38.0%. The GC content varied within the LSC, SSC, and IR regions, which were 35.2%—36.2%, 27.8%—33.3% and 38.3%—43.4%, respectively. A total of 114 unduplicated genes were annotated, including 80 PCGs, 4 rRNA genes and 30 tRNA genes, with the total number of genes ranging from 133 to 145.

Sequence repeats and SSR analyses

In the 72 complete plastomes, a total of 8,583 scattered repetitive sequences (including 4,765 forward and 3,818 palindromic) were identified (Fig. S6). The number of repeats varied widely among samples, ranging from 12 to 330. The plastome of Dischidia milnei Hemsl. contained the fewest repeats (12), whereas Hoya lyi H.Lév. had the highest number (330).

A total of 4,727 SSRs were detected, and the number of detected SSR markers for each sample fell between 89 (Hoya omlorii (Livsh. & Meve) L.Wanntorp & Meve) to 37 (Gymnema sylvestre), including mono- to hexanucleotides, of which mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant SSRs (3,950), while pentanucleotide repeats were the least (4) (Supplementary Fig. S7). According to base complementarity, the six types of SSR can be classified into 12 categories (Fig. 5B). The mononucleotide (A/T) motif was the most abundant (83.12%, 3,929), followed by the dinucleotide (AT/AT) motif (9.96%, 471). The least abundant motif types were tetranucleotide (AATT/AATT) and hexanucleotide (AACTAT/AGTTAT), each occurring only once. Moreover, the distribution of SSR varied across the genera within the tribe Marsdenieae (Fig. 5C). The LSC region contained the majority of SSRs among the four regions of the plastome. The SSC region exhibited a higher number of SSR in the species of the Clade B and the Clade A4. Conversely, more SSRs were discovered in the IR regions compared to the SSC region in species from Clades A1, A2, A3, and A5.

Identification of hypervariable regions

In general, the IR regions exhibited the lowest sequence variations across the three regions of the plastome (Supplementary Fig. S7). Nucleotide variation in non-coding regions (intergenic spacer and introns) was higher than that in coding regions. Among the functional genetic regions, the tRNA and rRNA were highly conserved, while the PCGs ycf1, ycf2 and accD showed higher sequence variations than other PCGs.

The nucleotide diversity calculation showed that the π values ranged from 0.00005 to 0.07251 in the 72 Marsdenieae plastomes, with a mean value of 0.01222 (Fig. 2). Evolutionary analyses of plastome types

The ‘ER’ likelihood model analyses showed that the Type III (55.48%) represented the most ancestral plastome state in the tribe Marsdenieae, rather than the Type I, which is the conserved type similar to that of Amborella trichopoda (Fig. 5A). Most species in Clade B were Type III. Although five species in Clade B possessed the Type I (i.e. Gymnema sylvestre) and Type V (i.e. Dolichopetalum kwangsiense Tsiang, Gymnema longiretinaculatum, Gongronemopsis cavaleriei (H.Lév.) S.Reuss, Liede & Meve and Gongronemopsis tenacissima) plastomes, these taxa were distributed across three distinct genera. Type Ⅳ was inferred to have evolved independently at least three times, and the three subtypes Ⅳa, Ⅳb, and Ⅳc were found in three separate clades, i.e., Clade A1, Clade A2, and Clade A5, respectively (Fig. 5A). The EM Mantel test and Delta Statistic further revealed a significant phylogenetic signal in the variations of the IR/SC boundaries (Mantel test: r = 0.82, P.Null = 0.01, P.BM = 0.01; DeltaA = 39.58, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5A).

Discussion

Plastomes diversity of the tribe Marsdenieae

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the plastomes in the tribe Marsdenieae, revealing both conservatism and variability in their plastome architecture. The findings indicate that plastomes of the tribe Marsdenieae are highly conserved in the quadripartite structure, gene order, and GC content, which is similar to most species in the sister group Ceropegieae [21–23]. Meanwhile, no gene loss was observed in any species of the tribe Mardenieae. These findings were consistent with previous studies comprising a few species of the tribe Marsdenieae [13, 19, 20]. Notably, the position exchange of the gene ycf1 adjacent to the IR/SSC junctions was detected for the first time in four newly sequenced species, i.e., D. kwangsiense, Gymnema longiretinaculatum, Gongronemopsis cavaleriei and Gongronemopsis tenacissima (Figs. 4, 5A).

Despite the conservatism of gene content and GC content, plastome sizes of the tribe Marsdenieae varied significantly, ranging from 156,364 bp (Gongronema napalense (Wall.) Decne., P2230) to 179,121 bp (Marsdenia sp.2, S8599), which is close to the size variations in other tribes of Apocynaceae [15]. There was no gene loss in plastomes of the tribe Marsdenieae (Table S1). However, the plastome size showed variations, which is primarily attributed to the expansion of the IR regions, with duplication of some genes near IR/SSC junctions (Fig. 4). Previous studies also found that variations of the plastome size across the family Apocynaceae is typically influenced by the contraction and expansion of the IR regions [15, 24]. This study highlights the significant impact of the IR expansion on variations of the plastome size within the tribe Marsdenieae, particularly in the Types II and IV, where the IRa region elongated into the SSC region by more than 14 kb, from the gene rps15 to the gene ndhD, even to the gene rpl32, respectively (Fig. 4).

Repeat sequence analyses revealed that the numbers of forward and palindromic repeats vary widely among plastomes of species belonging to Marsdenieae (Supplementary Fig. S6). Notably, species in the Marsdenia II clade have more long repeats, with all exceeding 200. Additionally, A/T repeats were found to be far more abundant than G/C repeats, consistent with previous findings [25, 26]. This expansion of the IR region also leads to the duplication of SSRs originally located in the SSC region, influencing their distribution and abundance (Fig. 5C).

Nucleotide polymorphism analysis identified four hypervariable regions (π > 0.035) in the plastome of the tribe Marsdenieae, i.e., accD, clpP, ycf1 and ndhF (Fig. 2), which were similar to the findings in previous study of the family Apocynaceae [15]. These regions, particularly ycf1 and ndhF, are located near the IRa/SSC and the SSC/IRb junctions and contribute to the phylogenetic resolution of the tribe Marsdenieae. Such as, five clades correspond to five types or subtypes of the plastome, and only the Clade B has three types of the plastome (Fig. 5A). The Clade B contained ten genera, and variations of plastomes were found among these genera (Supplementary Fig. S8). The absence of the gene clpP as a hypervariable region in Hoya species [19, 20] suggests lower plastome variability and phylogenetic resolution within this genus (Fig. 2). Considering the large number of Hoya species, future phylogenetic studies of them should focus on sequences with higher variability from nuclear genes to improve phylogenetic resolution. Overall, these hypervariable regions can provide important information for phylogenetic analyses and species identification of genera and species in the tribe Marsdenieae.

Updating the phylogenetic relationships of the tribe Marsdenieae, with emphasis on Asian genera

Reconstructing a comprehensive phylogeny of the tribe Marsdenieae continues to present substantial challenges. Through comprehensive sampling of 72 species representing 17 genera, (including 19 newly sequenced plastomes of this tribe), our phylogenetic analyses yielded well-resolved intergeneric relationships within the tribe Marsdenieae, demonstrating strong nodal support across most branches (Fig. 3A). Our current phylogenetic results align with previous phylogenetic frameworks [1, 3, 12], with the exception of several taxonomically complex genera that exhibited incongruent placements. The phylogenetic delineation of Hoya and its allied genera [1, 7, 19, 20] remains one of the most contentious issues in Marsdenieae systematics. Our plastome phylogenies demonstrated strong support for the monophyly of Oreosparte, Dischidia and Papuahoya (BS = 100%, PP = 1.00), while also revealing substantial phylogenetic complexity within the Hoya alliance, which includes Hoya s.l., Dischidia, Oreosparte and Papuahoya (Fig. 3A). The ML analysis based on the WPS dataset resolved Hoya s.l. into two main clades (Hoya I and Hoya II) with a topology structure (((Hoya II: Oreosparte): Hoya I): Dischidia), which corroborates the findings of Rodda et al. [7] and Odago et al. [20]. However, the BI analysis yielded a different topology ((Hoya II: Oreosparte): (Hoya I: Dischidia)) (Supplementary Fig. S1), which is congruent with a recent report by Wang et al. [15]. These topological discrepancies may stem from methodological discrepancies (ML vs. BI) and low information sites in the dataset. Crucially, the remarkably conserved plastome structure observed among Hoya, Oreosparte and Dischidia—characterized by the identical IR boundary architecture (the Type IVc), comparable plastome sizes (over 175,000 bp), and identical gene content (145)—presents significant challenges for phylogenetic resolution using plastome sequences alone. This genomic stasis, coupled with analytical discordance, underscores the necessity for future studies to: (1) expand taxon sampling across critical lineages, (2) incorporate nuclear genomic data with higher phylogenetic informativeness, and (3) employ coalescent-based methods to address potential incomplete lineage sorting.

Phylogenetic analyses have consistently demonstrated the non-monophyly of the genus Marsdenia [3, 27], prompting the reclassification of the American lineage as the distinct genus Ruehssia [3]. Our expanded sampling of Asian Marsdenia species revealed persistent non-monophyly within the Asian clade, resolving into two subclades, Marsdenia Ⅰ and Marsdenia Ⅱ (Fig. 3A). Subclade Marsdenia Ⅰ includes four taxa: the type species M. tinctoria, M. schneideri and two undescribed taxa (i.e., Marsdenia sp. 2 and sp. 3). Subclade Marsdenia Ⅱ comprises five described species (M. koi, M. griffithii, M. yarlungzangboensis, M. sinensis, M. burmanica) and one undescribed taxon (i.e., Marsdenia sp. 1) (Fig. 3A). As M. tinctoria (the nomenclatural type of Marsdenia) resides within the subclade Marsdenia Ⅰ, this subclade retains the genus name, consistent with the treatment by Liede-Schumann et al. [1]. Notably, in both the concatenated plastid and nrITS phylogenies, Marsdenia II also includes M. oreophila W.W.Sm., corresponding to the M. oreophila subclade sensu Liede-Schumann et al. [1]. Building upon the framework of Liede-Schumann et al. [1], Liao et al. [2] further demonstrated that the genus Treutlera Hook.f. is nested within the M. oreophila subclade. Based on morphological and genetic evidence, they advocated that Treutlera, the earliest known generic name for this subclade, be recognized and tentatively kept as monospecific. Taken together with the distinctly different plastid IR/SC boundary characteristics of Marsdenia II and Marsdenia I (Type IVb vs. Type IVa; see Fig. 5A), these findings provide further support for recognizing this lineage as another separate genus. Our results underscore critical knowledge gaps in the systematics of Marsdenia s.l. across tropical-subtropical Asia, Australia, and Papua New Guinea. Future studies should prioritize three key objectives: (1) expanded taxon sampling to resolve persistent polyphyly, (2) integrative taxonomic frameworks incorporating molecular, morphological, and biogeographic evidence, and (3) systematic reassessment of generic boundaries in light of phylogenetically distinct clades.

The genus delimitation of Dregea was also controversial. Phylogenetic analyses by both Liede-Schumann et al. [1] and the present study have shown the non-monophyly of the genus Dregea. Liede-Schumann et al. [1] resolved a strongly supported clade comprising Dregea (including its type species D. floribunda E. Mey., D. volubilis, and five additional species), Stephanotis, and Marsdenia brevisquama. Based on these findings, they proposed merging Dregea into an expanded genus Stephanotis. Yet, there are still some East Asian species of Dregea (e.g., D. sinensis) that were not sampled. In the present study, the newly sampled D. sinensis does not cluster within the Stephanotis clade, but instead forms a distinct lineage nested within the Ruehssia–Gymnema clade (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Figs. S1–5). These findings imply that the taxonomic revision of Dregea may prove more complex as additional species are sampled in future studies. Thus, for systematic studies of underrepresented Asian taxa (e.g., Dregea), it is essential to integrate broader taxonomic sampling and genomic data to reconcile molecular phylogenies with morphological and biogeographic evidence.

Sinomarsdenia and Dolichopetalum were two monotypic genera in Asia [1]. The phylogenetic placement of Dolichopetalum has been resolved [28, 29], whereas the phylogenetic position of Sinomarsdenia within the tribe Marsdenieae remained unresolved due to insufficient sequence data. Our study reported the first collection of Sinomarsdenia species, uncovering a unique IR/SC boundary type (Type II; see Fig. 5A). Moreover, our phylogenetic analyses based on the concatenated plastid dataset revealed that Sinomarsdenia incisa nested within genus Gongreos (Supplementary Fig. S4). Based on the shared morphological characters between Gongreos species and Sinomarsdenia incisa, including an urceolate corolla and staminal corona with elongate lobes, together with their overlapping distributions, we propose merging Gongreos into Sinomarsdenia. Notably, our analysis revealed that Gymnema longiretinaculatum was a sister to Lygisma inflexum separating from three congeneric species (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Figs. S1–3). As G. longiretinaculatum belongs to Gymnema sect. Rhionanthus Tsiang & P. T. Li, a monotypic section, whereas the other three species are assigned to Gymnema sect. Gymnema [30], its phylogenetic placement merits particular attention. In both the concatenated plastid and nrITS phylogenies, G. longiretinaculatum clusters with species of the genus Gymnemopsis (Supplementary Figs. S4–5). Notably, it also exhibits an extremely elongated corpusculum, a diagnostic morphological character of Gymnemopsis [14, 30, 31]. Therefore, a conclusive determination of the phylogenetic status of G. longiretinaculatum and the interspecific boundaries within Gymnema will require expanded taxonomic sampling.

Evolutionary history of IR boundaries in Marsdenieae

Comparative analysis of IR boundary structural variations across the Marsdenieae plastomes revealed five distinct organizational types, with Types II, IVa, and V reported here for the first time within the tribe. These five types showed strong correspondence with the genus-level classification proposed in previous plastid phylogenetic studies [15, 32, 33]. These patterns were statistically validated through EM Mantel and DeltaA analyses, with the IR boundary variations demonstrating strong phylogenetic signals (Fig. 5A). Notably, types II and IV exhibited the most substantial IR expansion. Three subtypes of the type IV showed phylogenetically informative: the subtype IVa restricted to Clade A1 taxa, the subtype IVb characterizing Clade A2 members, and the subtype IVc exclusive to Clade A5 species. Among the three subtypes, the subtype IVc differed from the former two subtypes in that IR regions, which were expanded more dramatically and only single ndhF gene located in the SSC region (Fig. 4). The extreme expansion of IRs in the subtype IVc was detected in all sampled Hoya, Oreosparte, Dischidia and Papuahoya species, which was also documented by Rodda and Niissalo [19], Odago et al. [20] and Wang et al. [15]. Consistent with this structural distinctiveness, the subtype IVc-bearing plastomes exhibited larger genome sizes compared to other Marsdenieae species (Table S1), directly attributable to their expanded IR regions (Fig. 5A).

Ancestral state reconstruction revealed that the Type III is the most likely ancestral type of the IR boundary configuration in the Marsdenieae plastome, with Types II, IV and V likely evolving as derived secondary states through subsequent modification (Fig. 5A). Phylogenetic reconstruction showed that species within the earliest diverging clade predominantly exhibit Types I, III, and V. Notably, the ycf1 inversion observed in the type V plastomes can be attributed to the IR contraction from an ancestral Type IV configuration. Meanwhile, the type I displays the canonical IR boundary architecture characteristic of angiosperm plastomes, potentially originating through IR contraction events in Clades B and A4, respectively. Therefore, the evolutionary trajectory of the IR region in Asian Marsdenieae plastomes can be reconstructed into five distinct phases. (1) Initial IRa Expansion: extension of IRa into the LSC region, establishing the ancestral Type III configuration. (2) First IRa contraction: two independent constrictions of IRa in the LSC region to create Type I. (3) Secondary IRa expansion and diversification: subsequent expansion of the IRa into the SSC region, producing three distinct Subtypes (IVa, IVb, and IVc). (4) Regional IRa constriction: Secondary contraction of IRa in the LSC region, resulting in the specialized Type II architecture. (5) Dual IR boundary modification: Coordinated contractions of IRa (spanning rpl32 to rps15 in the SSC) and IRb (partial ycf1 retention in the SSC), culminating in the evolution of Type V. This phased model suggests alternating patterns of IR expansion and contraction, with spatial modifications in both LSC and SSC regions driving structural diversification in Marsdenieae plastomes. While Wang et al. [15] have documented bidirectional IR expansion (towards both LSC and SSC regions) in plastomes of the "Asia–Pacific Clade" within the tribe Marsdenieae, our findings further demonstrate: an ancestral IRa extension restricted to the LSC region, subsequent progressive IRa expansion into the SSC region, and a complex history of IRa contractions involving both regional specificity (e.g., Type II) and dual-IR coordination (e.g., Type V).

Conclusions

This study elucidates the evolutionary dynamics of plastomes within the tribe Marsdenieae using 76 plastomes (including 19 newly sequenced), revealing a complex interplay of conserved structure and lineage-specific innovation. Despite plastome maintaining a conserved gene content and quadripartite structure, significant plastome size variations (156,364–179,121 bp) were driven by expansions and contractions of the inverted repeat (IR) regions, particularly at IR/SC boundaries. Five distinct IR boundary types were identified, with Type III inferred as the most ancestral state, and Types II, IV, and V emerging through subsequent evolutionary modifications. These structural variations, coupled with hypervariable regions (accD, clpP, ycf1, and ndhF), provided critical phylogenetic signals, resolving six major clades and highlighting persistent non-monophyly in genera such as Hoya, Marsdenia, and Dregea. Notably, IR expansions in Hoya, Dischidia, and allied genera (Type IVc) correlated with plastome enlargement, underscoring their role in plastome diversification. Phylogenetic discordances, particularly within Hoya group and Marsdenia, emphasize the limitations of plastome data alone and advocate for integrative approaches combining nuclear genomic data, expanded taxon sampling, and morphological analyses. The evolutionary history of IR boundaries was reconstructed into five phases, characterized by alternating expansion–contraction cycles, offering a model for plastome structural evolution in plants. This work advances understanding of plastome evolution and tribal systematics while highlighting unresolved taxonomic complexities in the tribe Marsdenieae, urging future studies to reconcile molecular phylogenies with biogeographic and morphological evidence to refine generic boundaries and resolve polyphyly.

Methods

Taxon sampling, DNA extraction, and genome sequencing

A total of 77 whole plastome sequences were used for this study, including 76 Marsdenieae plastomes and one outgroup species. These 76 Marsdenieae plastomes represent 17 genera and 72 species. The outgroup species Heterostemma siamicum belongs to the tribe Ceropegieae, which is sister to the tribe Marsdenieae [1, 3, 9]. Among the 77 plastomes, 20 accessions were newly sequenced and assembled in this study (19 Marsdenieae samples and one outgroup). The remaining 57 accessions consisted of eight that were assembled using the raw data from the NCBI-SRA database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra), eleven samples derived from a publication by Yao et al. [28], and 38 plastome sequences were achieved from the NCBI GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/, accessed January 1 st, 2026) (Supplementary Table S2). All samples for the newly sequenced data were either collected from the field or botanical gardens. Voucher specimens were deposited at herbaria of Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences (HITBC) and of Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (KUN) (Supplementary Table S2).

For the newly sequenced samples, total genomic DNA was extracted from leaves (silica gel-dried or fresh materials) with the verified CTAB protocol [34]. Purified DNA was fragmented to 350–500 bp for library construction, following standard protocols (NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina). The 150 bp pair-end reads were generated using an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. Finally, about 2.0 Gb raw data was obtained for each sample. The newly generated plastome sequences are available in Figshare (https://figshare.com/) (Supplementary Table S2).

Plastome assembly and annotation

All new plastome and nuclear ribosomal ITS sequences were de novo assembled from the raw reads data using the GetOrganelle toolkit v1.6.2 with default parameters [35]. The software Bandage v0.8.1 was used to check the final results of the assembled graph with manual removal non-target contigs when it was necessary [36]. New plastome sequences were initially annotated using online tool Ge-Seq [37]. Then, the codons and gene boundaries were checked and manually corrected using Geneious v2022.2.1 [38] in accordance with the published plastome of the Catharanthus roseus (L.) G. Don (GenBank accession KC561139) as the reference [39]. In addition, the ORFs function in Geneious was used to recheck annotations of the protein-coding gene (PCG). The ITS sequences were manually annotated in Geneious using a published, well-annotated sequence of Marsdenia tinctoria R.Br. (GenBank accession MW226616) as the reference [38].

Identification of repeats and highly variable regions

To analyze sequence repeats and highly variable regions within plastomes, we selected only 72 complete plastomes of Marsdenieae for these analyses. The forward and palindromic repeats in the plastomes of the tribe Marsdenieae were detected using VMATCH [40], with a minimum repeat length of 50 bp and a sequence identity threshold of ≥ 95% (Hamming distance = 3). Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were detected with MISA-web [41] using minimum thresholds of ≥ 10 repeat units for mono-; ≥ 6 repeat units for di-; ≥ 5 repeat units for tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide repeats. In addition, if the distance between two SSRs was less than 0 bp, the two SSRs were considered composite microsatellite SSRs. For identifying hypervariable regions, the plastome alignment of 19 representative species of the tribe Marsdenieae was visualized using the Shuffle-LAGAN mode in the software mVISTA [42]. Further, the nucleotide diversity (π) was calculated in the 72 Marsdenieae plastomes using software DnaSP v6.12.03 with sliding window method (window length 600 bp and step size 200 bp) [43].

Phylogenetic analyses

To determine the phylogenetic relationships among Marsdenieae species, both whole plastome sequence and PCG datasets were used to reconstruct phylogenetic trees. The 77 whole plastomes (retaining only one IR region) were aligned using MAFFT with default parameters [44]. Then, the whole plastome sequence (WPS) dataset was removed ambiguously aligned sites using trimAl v1.4 with the parameter ‘tgapthreshold = 0.9, tsimthreshold = 0.001’ [45]. For the PCG dataset, 80 PCG sequences were extracted from the plastome sequences, and each PCG sequence aligned using MAFFT with default parameters [44]. Then, all PCG alignments were concatenated into a supergene matrix or the PCG dataset.

To further resolve the phylogenetic position of the newly sampled Asian genera in the tribe Marsdenieae, sequence data for the four plastid loci (trnT-L intron, trnL-F intergenic spacer, rps16 intron, and psbA-trnH intergenic spacer) and the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) were compiled for the tribe and its sister group from previous studies (Liede-Schumann et al. [1], Kidyoo et al. [14], and Liao et al. [2]). For the four plastid loci dataset, accessions with fewer than two loci were excluded, resulting in 215 retained samples (Supplementary Table S3). Homologous regions were extracted from the 77 plastomes, yielding a total of 292 accessions. Each region was aligned using MAFFT with default parameters, then four matrices were concatenated into a supermatrix. To maintain alignment continuity, some samples with missing loci were filled with 'N' characters. For the nrITS dataset, 127 sequences were retrieved and combined with 38 newly assembled ITS sequences generated in this study, resulting in a final dataset of 165 accessions representing 157 species (Supplementary Table S4).

Both Bayesian Inference (BI) and Maximum Likelihood (ML) methods were used for phylogenetic inferences. The BI trees were inferred using MrBayes v3.2.7a [46], and the best substitution models (WPS: TVM + I + G; PCG: GTR + I + G; 4-loci: GTR + I + G; ITS: TIM1 + I + G) were calculated by jModeltest v2.1.10 [47]. Two independent Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs were conducted for 2,000,000 generations, with trees were sampled every 1,000 generations. Convergence of runs was checked in Tracer v.1.7 to ensure adequate mixing and that all parameters reached an effective sample size (ESS) greater than 200 [48]. The first 20% of the trees were discarded as burn-in, with the remaining trees were used to calculate the Bayesian posterior probability (BIPP) values of each node of the consensus tree as the support values in the BI tree. ML analyses were performed using RAxML v8.2.12 [49] with the model GTRCAT. Rapid bootstrap with 1,000 times was utilized to compute bootstrap support values (MLBS) for each node of the ML tree.

To minimize the influence of phylogenetic noise in the 4-loci matrix, which contains relatively few informative sites, the plastome phylogeny was used to constrain twelve well-supported clades in the concatenated plastome tree (see Supplementary Fig S4 for constrained nodes). Constraint information was incorporated using BEAUTi and subsequently analyzed in BEAST v2.7.1 for Bayesian inference [50], with MCMC chains run for 250,000,000 generations and trees sampled every 1,000 generations to ensure convergence. The consensus tree was generated in TreeAnnotator (http://beast.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/TreeAnnotator) based on the maximum clade credibility method, applying a 20% burn-in to remove initial samples. The resulting BI tree was then employed as a constraint in RAxML via the '-g' option, guiding the ML tree search and allowing the estimation of node support values and branch lengths under the GTRCAT model (options: -n 4 -m GTRGAMMA -g BI.nwk -p 12,345 -x 12,345 -# 1000). The final results were visualized with the FigTree 1.4.3 [51] and further decorated by using tvBOT [52].

Evolution analysis of plastome structure

Comparisons of IR/SC boundaries

Previous studies have documented length variations of the IR regions in the tribe Marsdenieae [15], particularly in the genus Hoya and Dischidia [19, 20]. To further explore the variation of IR/SC boundaries, we selected only 72 complete plastomes of the tribe Marsdenieae in the following analyses. The basic characteristics of the plastomes were calculated using Geneious [38], including the lengths of the large single-copy region (LSC), the small single-copy region (SSC) and the inverted repeats (IR), the GC content of the three regions as well as gene contents (including number of genes). The SC/IR boundary locations at four junctions (LSC/IRa, IRa/SSC, SSC/IRb and IRb/LSC) of the plastomes were annotated using Geneious and classified into five types of plastome structure (see the ‘Results’ section) with the Amborella trichopoda Baill. plastome as the reference. Additionally, gene rearrangement of the Marsdenieae plastomes was analyzed using Amborella trichopoda as the reference.

Ancestral state reconstruction of plastome types and their phylogenetic signal detection

To infer the evolutionary history of the five types of the IR/SC boundary, the ancestral state was reconstructed based on the ML tree of the WPS dataset. This tree was pruned to exclude one outgroup and four incomplete plastome species. The reconstruction was conducted in the R package “phytools” [53], with the ‘equal-rates’ (ER) likelihood model [54]. Most likely ancestral states of each node are indicated with colored circles. Furthermore, we used the EM Mantel test [55] and the Delta Statistic [56] to assess the degree of phylogenetic signal of the IR boundary type using the mantel() function in the R package “vegan” [57] and the delta() function in the R package “ape” [58].
