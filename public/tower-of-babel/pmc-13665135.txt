Placental Iron Homeostasis: Navigating the Delicate Balance Between Transport and Ferroptosis
Authors: Dariusz Szukiewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As an essential micronutrient, iron plays a vital role in oxygen transport, deoxyribonucleic acid synthesis, and energy production and is a redox-active cofactor for many biological processes. However, ferrous iron (Fe2+) is toxic to cells because it reacts with hydrogen peroxide in the Fenton reaction to generate the hydroxyl (free) radical, a key player in oxidative stress, leading to lipid peroxidation and iron-dependent cell death (ferroptosis). Therefore, iron homeostasis is precisely regulated. Maintaining appropriate iron metabolism during pregnancy is a major challenge, as it requires adaptation at the level of the utero–placental–fetal unit to the dynamic changes in the demand for iron in the developing fetus during the later weeks of pregnancy. The distinction between physiological (normal) anemia, which results from hemodilution and leads to the depletion of intracellular iron stores (ferritin) in placental cells, and the manifestation of the toxic effects of iron overload and/or oxidative stress is not always clear. Moreover, chronic inflammation during pregnancy associated with increasingly common metabolic disorders such as obesity, diabetes and hypertension may induce ferroptosis. This review presents the current understanding of placental iron homeostasis and its disturbances, focusing on the role of ferroptosis in placental dysfunction. The rationale for potentially effective anti-ferroptotic treatment is also presented.

1. Introduction

Iron, abbreviated as Fe (from “ferrum”, the ancient Latin word for the metal), is a crucial micronutrient for almost all living organisms. Despite the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) of iron for humans, which ranges from 8 mg to 18 mg per day and depends on age and biological sex, corresponding to the definition of a trace element (micronutrient), iron plays a disproportionately vital role in the functioning of the body [1,2]. In humans, it supports oxygen metabolism, muscle function, and energy production as a key component of hemoglobin and myoglobin [3,4]. The maintenance of iron homeostasis during pregnancy is particularly important, as confirmed by the recommendations of both the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, which recommend 27–30 mg of elemental iron as a universal daily oral supplement during pregnancy [5].

Mild iron deficiency anemia, which often accompanies pregnancy, is caused primarily by the body’s drastically increased demand for iron to support the rapidly growing fetus and placenta, along with a 40% to 50% increase in maternal blood volume, which dilutes the existing red blood cell (RBC) count [5,6]. Additionally, it may be caused or exacerbated by nutritional deficits, multiple pregnancies, short intervals between subsequent pregnancies and hyperemesis gravidarum [6,7]. Iron deficiency during pregnancy can lead to severe maternal and fetal complications. Maternal risks include preterm labor, postpartum hemorrhage, cesarean delivery, heart strain and higher rates of postpartum maternal depression [7,8,9]. Fetal and infant risks include low birth weight, neonatal anemia and impaired brain development, particularly altered neurocognitive development with long-term behavioral and mental health challenges [7,8,10,11]. However, maintaining the correct iron balance is critical, as both extremes carry serious risks [12]. Excessive iron or iron overload during pregnancy can be dangerous and is linked to severe complications for both mothers and fetuses, including embryo malformation (embryotoxicity), preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, impaired fetal growth and preterm birth [12,13,14]. The toxic effects of excess iron during pregnancy are directly linked to ferroptosis—an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by overwhelming lipid peroxidation [14,15,16]. Maternal iron overload results in the dysregulation of placental iron transporters, leading to toxic iron accumulation and severe oxidative cellular damage [17,18,19].

Symptoms of disturbances in iron homeostasis during pregnancy can be clearly observed in the placenta because trophoblasts—specifically the multinucleated syncytiotrophoblasts (STB) that line the maternal–fetal interface—are the primary gatekeepers of fetal iron acquisition [20,21]. These cells extract maternal iron, shuttle it via endosomes into fetal circulation, and simultaneously balance placental iron storage and local metabolism [22,23]. Ferroptosis and normal iron homeostasis are maintained in a delicate, dynamic balance [24].

The aim of this narrative review is to present the current understanding of iron metabolism during pregnancy, referring to placental tissue under physiological conditions and considering the involvement of ferroptosis in the pathomechanisms of various pregnancy complications.

2. Methodology Used for Literature Search

Through a review of the current literature, a comprehensive analysis of the current state of knowledge on placental iron metabolism in normal and complicated pregnancies was performed, referring to ferroptosis as a consequence of abnormal or dysregulated iron transport leading to cellular iron overload. While trying to maintain maximum criticism and objectivity, a synthesis of relevant research and generalization of the collected data was performed. This approach established a coherent framework for presenting the findings, supporting a qualitative analysis of the topic and facilitating the achievement of the predefined objectives.

Highly cited, peer-reviewed publications in journals from the Master Journal List (MJL) in the fields of anatomy/histology, physiology/pathophysiology, obstetrics, nutrition and dietetics were selected. An important selection criterion for review articles was their topicality (date of publication). The electronic databases PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science (WoS) and Google Scholar were reviewed. Priority was given to studies providing observational or experimental evidence. The core of the literature search consisted of publications within the time frame of 2015–2026, and in the case of review articles with the same topic defined by keywords, publications from journals positioned in Q1 were preferred. During manuscript preparation, the citation list was then supplemented with key milestone articles that captured the phenomena and the biologically active compounds’ properties, cited regardless of publication date.

Ultimately, this narrative review included 269 publications, including 109 original research papers and 160 review articles.

3. General Principles of Iron Metabolism in the Body

3.1. Iron Content and Distribution

The average iron content in the adult human body is approximately 42 to 55 milligrams per kilogram of tissue [25,26]. Assuming that for the general adult population in Western countries, the average measured weight is approximately 172 lb (78 kg) for women and 199 lb (90 kg) for men, with an average body mass index (BMI) of 26.6, this translates to a total body iron content of 3.3 to 5.0 g for women and men, respectively [27]. Sex-related differences in iron content stem from the lower erythrocyte mass and iron stores (myoglobin and ferritin) in women owing to their lower muscle mass and the effects of estrogen (see the next section: Estrogen–iron axis) [28,29].

The total iron content of the body is distributed across three main pools: functional, storage and transport [30,31,32].

The functional pool comprises approximately 65% to 75% of the total iron—which is found in the hemoglobin of red blood cells (RBCs) and erythroid precursors as well as in the myoglobin of muscle cells—and is primarily involved in oxygen transport (hemoglobin) and the formation of intracellular oxygen reservoirs (myoglobin) [33]. The remaining trace amounts of iron within the functional pool, sometimes referred to as the enzymatic protein pool, are integrated into the cytochromes and iron-sulfur clusters necessary for cellular respiration and adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) generation at the mitochondrial level [34,35]. Iron is also found in catalases—i.e., antioxidative enzymes that convert hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen to protect cells from oxidative damage—which are composed of four subunits containing heme (iron-porphyrin) as a prosthetic group [36,37].

The storage pool, also known as the reserve pool, comprises 20% to 30% of the total iron in the body, approximately 0.8 g of which is stored in liver parenchymal cells, primarily in the form of ferritin [38]. This water-soluble, spherical protein safely stores excess iron in its core to prevent cellular toxicity [39]. In states of significant hepatocyte iron overload—where the amount of excess iron exceeds the cells’ capacity to safely store the trace element in a soluble form—it is also stored as hemosiderin, an insoluble iron-storage complex derived from degraded ferritin within lysosomes [40,41]. In addition to hepatocytes, reticuloendothelial macrophages in the spleen, liver, and bone marrow—which are involved in iron transport—serve as iron reserves [42,43].

The transport (transit) pool of iron consists primarily of the glycoprotein transferrin, which binds to circulating iron in the bloodstream [44]. By binding virtually all plasma iron produced by the liver, transferrin protects cells from the high toxicity of free iron and allows for its safe transport from intestinal cells and macrophages to primarily bone marrow cells for erythropoiesis [45,46]. The iron transport system operates via a highly dynamic, recycling-based process, and despite the small amount of the transferrin pool, which comprises approximately 3 mg to 4 mg of iron in circulation, the daily iron turnover reaches 20 mg to 25 mg [47].

In addition, a small fraction of transported iron—typically constituting less than 5% of total cellular iron—serves as a transient, intracellular reservoir of loosely bound, redox-active iron [48]. Because ferrous iron reacts with hydrogen peroxide, which serves as a catalyst to generate highly reactive cell-damaging hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction, its levels are strictly regulated by intracellular proteins [49]. Dysregulation of this pool is closely linked to severe conditions, such as oxidative stress, ferroptosis (see Section 5.3. Iron overload and placental ferroptosis) and cancer [50].

3.2. Intestinal Iron Absorption

Strict control of daily intestinal iron absorption by systemic hormones and local cellular mechanisms is particularly important because the human body does not have an active, regulated mechanism to excrete excess iron [52].

The iron regulatory protein/iron responsive element (IRP/IRE) system acts as the primary cellular and basal regulator of intestinal iron absorption by determining how much iron is imported, stored, or exported by absorptive cells in the duodenum [53,54]. This regulation occurs through post-transcriptional management of translation and stability of key iron transporter and storage mRNAs in duodenal enterocytes [55,56]. IRP1 and IRP2 are cytosolic RNA-binding proteins that sense intracellular iron levels and bind to IREs [57]. IREs, in turn, are specific hairpin structures found in the untranslated regions (UTRs) of target mRNAs [58,59]. When IRPs bind to an IRE in the 5′-UTR of an mRNA (such as ferritin or ferroportin), they block translation, reducing the production of these proteins [60]. Conversely, when IRPs bind to an IRE in the 3′-UTR of an mRNA (such as DMT1 or transferrin receptor 1), they protect the transcript from degradation, increasing protein expression by mRNA stabilization [61].

Hepcidin, a hormone produced in the liver, serves as the main systemic regulator; by binding to the iron exporter ferroportin [also known as solute carrier family 40 member 1 (SLC40A1)] on the basolateral membrane of duodenal enterocytes, it triggers its degradation, thereby inhibiting the transfer of iron into the bloodstream [62]. Hepcidin production increases owing to elevated systemic iron levels and inflammation, actively limiting iron absorption to prevent toxicity [62,63]. Conversely, during iron deficiency or increased erythropoiesis, hepcidin production decreases, allowing ferroportin to remain stable and export more iron into circulation [51].

Mucosal iron sensing involves the expression of the transcription factor hypoxia-inducible factor 2-alpha (HIF-2α) in enterocytes, which responds directly to intracellular iron and oxygen levels [64]. In low-oxygen or systemic iron-deficient states, intestinal HIF-2 α is stabilized and binds directly to HREs on genes for key iron transporters at the apical membrane, including divalent metal-ion transporter 1 (DMT1) and the enzyme duodenal cytochrome b (DcytB) [64,65,66]. The reduction and transport of dietary Fe3+ into enterocytes are accelerated. Additionally, HIF-2α directly increases the transcription of the ferroportin (FPN/SLC40A1) gene, which leads to an adaptive increase in ferroportin protein expression and enhanced iron export (efflux) from duodenal enterocytes into the bloodstream [67]. In an indirect mechanism, via the renal-hepatic axis, HIF-2α leads to a reduction in the production of hepcidin [64]. This is because the main function of HIF-2α is to strongly activate erythropoietin (EPO) production in the kidneys and liver. Increased EPO concentrations stimulate erythropoiesis in the bone marrow, leading to the secretion of erythroferrone (ERFE) by erythroblasts. ERFE then reaches the liver and inhibits the bone morphogenic protein/small mother against decapentaplegic (BMP/SMAD) pathway, lowering hepcidin levels to free iron stores for hemoglobin production [68,69,70]. Low hepcidin levels allow upregulated ferroportin to remain open and active for a prolonged duration.

HIF-2α is also involved in another mammalian iron-sensing axis via the IRP and IRE machinery [71]. This is due to the presence of a stem-loop structure hidden within the HIF2α mRNA within its 5′ untranslated region (UTR), corresponding to the IRE [72,73,74]. This suggests the existence of a further iron- and oxygen-dependent mechanism for translational regulation of HIF-2α.

Non-heme iron (Fe3+) absorption is strongly influenced by dietary factors and is usually low, ranging from 2 to 20%, while the food components have a minor effect on heme iron (Fe2+) from meat, the bioavailability of which is significantly higher and amounts to 15 to 35% [75,76]. The mechanism of intestinal absorption of heme iron differs from that of non-heme iron primarily in that heme is taken up as an intact metalloporphyrin complex, whereas non-heme iron requires chemical reduction [77]. Heme iron therefore uses a separate, specialized pathway to enter the intestinal enterocyte intact. This apical uptake is mediated by heme carrier protein 1 (HCP1) and potentially heme responsive gene-1 (HRG1) or via receptor-mediated endocytosis [76,78,79]. Inside the cell, under the influence of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), enzymatic cleavage of the porphyrin ring of the heme molecule occurs [80]. Fe2+ is liberated, which joins the common pool with iron from the non-heme pathway, and bilirubin, which can be removed from the cell by the efflux proteins feline leukemia virus subgroup C cellular receptor (FLVCR) and ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2, also known as breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP)) [78,81].

Heavy chain ferritin (H-ferritin) plays a critical role in preventing iron overload and limiting iron basolateral efflux into the bloodstream [82]. H-ferritin possesses ferroxidase activity, rapidly converting toxic ferrous iron (Fe2+) into safe, stored ferric iron (Fe3+) within the enterocytes. Therefore, intestinal H-ferritin acts as a local brake by trapping absorbed dietary iron inside the mucosal cells. Since hepcidin-mediated regulation alone is not sufficient to limit iron absorption, H-ferritin participates in a key mechanism of fine-tuning, in parallel with hepcidin-related regulation [82,83].

3.3. Iron Transport and Utilization

Divalent iron (ferrous ion, Fe2+), which is transported out of enterocytes into the bloodstream by ferroportin, is immediately oxidized in the plasma by a ferroxidase enzyme (such as hephaestin or ceruloplasmin) into the trivalent form (ferric ion, Fe3+), which can bind to transferrin [24]. Each transferrin molecule can safely carry up to two iron atoms, and iron-loaded transferrin (holo-transferrin; holo-Tf) delivers iron to all cells possessing transferrin receptors (primarily TfR1) on their surface. The entire holo-Tf/TfR1 complex is internalized via endocytosis [84]. Intracellular iron is released owing to the high acidity of the endosome interior, causing the iron to dissociate from transferrin; it is then transported into the cell’s cytoplasm to be used or stored in ferritin. Transferrin carries iron mainly to the bone marrow, where it is utilized to produce mature RBCs (hemoglobin) [85]. The remainder of the iron is delivered to tissues with high metabolic needs, such as the liver and spleen, for storage and systemic distribution. Iron-free transferrin (apo-transferrin; apo-Tf) remains bound to TfR1 and is recycled back to the cell surface, where it is subsequently released into the bloodstream [44].

Cellular iron homeostasis is regulated by posttranscriptional feedback mechanisms, which control the expression of proteins involved in iron uptake, release and storage mainly through two cytoplasmic proteins with mRNA-binding properties, iron regulatory proteins 1 and 2 (IRP1 and IRP2) [86]. By binding to specific noncoding sequences, known as iron-responsive elements (IREs), within the untranslated regions (UTRs) of mRNA transcripts, IRP1 (90 kDa) and IRP2 (105 kDa) form a system that regulates ferritin translation and thus iron storage. For example, when intracellular iron levels are low, IRPs bind to IREs within the 5′ UTR of ferritin mRNA, resulting in reduced translation rates [87]. Additionally, through mRNA stabilization, IRP/IRE interactions increase TfR1 expression and iron uptake via TfR1-mediated endocytosis of iron-loaded transferrin [88]. PCBP1 (poly(rC)-binding protein 1) is a vital cytosolic iron chaperone that safely binds and delivers iron to key intracellular targets such as ferritin, iron-dependent enzymes, iron-sulfur cluster assembly complexes and ferroportin. It prevents cellular toxicity by controlling iron reactivity [65].

3.4. Storage and Its Disorders

Ferritin is a 24-subunit protein composed of two types of chains: heavy (H) and light (L). As a storage protein, ferritin safely encapsulates up to 4500 iron atoms, which remain readily available when erythropoiesis increases in the body [89]. Hemosiderin, which forms stable protein–iron complexes, serves as a long-term iron reservoir, particularly when iron levels become significantly (and dangerously) elevated [89]. The physiological profiles of iron storage and distribution exhibit sex-related differences. Adult males typically store approximately 1000 mg of iron in intracellular reserves, whereas menstruating females often have storages closer to 500 mg because of higher iron turnover [90,91].

Disorders associated with iron overload are known as hemochromatosis and hemosiderosis [92,93]. Hemochromatosis involves excessive systemic iron absorption and deposition within the parenchymal cells of organs such as the liver, heart, and pancreas, leading to organ damage; this can be caused by genetic factors (usually a mutation of the homeostatic iron regulator (HFE) gene) or occur as a secondary reaction because of frequent blood transfusions or severe chronic liver disease [94]. In hemochromatosis, iron is deposited in amounts exceeding the cell’s normal ferritin storage capacity, resulting in a substantial pool of labile (free) intracellular iron, leading to toxic effects such as ferroptosis [95,96].

The primary causes of hemosiderosis, i.e., a focal or systemic accumulation of hemosiderin, include localized bleeding, chronic hemolysis and repeated blood transfusions [97]. Iron deposits are located primarily within tissue-resident macrophages of the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) [98]. Unlike hemochromatosis, hemosiderosis typically does not cause structural or functional damage to affected organs because the excess iron is safely bound and sequestered inside hemosiderin; thus, it cannot easily participate in the Fenton reaction to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS). As a result, the molecular triggers for ferroptosis are largely avoided [89].

3.5. Recycling and Excretion

Over 90% of the body’s iron stores undergo recycling, and the small amount of iron lost daily—on the order of 1–2 mg—is replenished through intestinal absorption during the digestion of nutrients containing bioavailable iron [4]. The primary source of iron recovery is the degradation of the hemoglobin heme group, which occurs as a result of the destruction of aging erythrocytes by specialized reticuloendothelial macrophages in the spleen and liver in a process termed erythrophagocytosis. The iron released from the macrophage phagolysosome is transported into the cytosol via natural resistance-associated macrophage protein 1 (NRAMP1), a divalent metal transporter and paralog of DMT1 [99]. Afterward, the liberated iron can be stored in ferritin or released back into systemic circulation by ferroportin-mediated transport across the plasma membrane, and after binding with transferrin in plasma, it reaches the bone marrow to synthesize new RBCs [100]. Heme is transported from the phagolysosome to the cytoplasm by heme responsive gene 1 (HRG1), an essential transmembrane protein for macrophage iron homeostasis. HRG1 is strongly expressed in macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system and specifically localizes to the phagolysosomal membranes during erythrophagocytosis [101]. Heme in the cytoplasm can be degraded by inducible form of heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), a catabolic enzyme whose action creates the rate-limiting step in the oxidative breakdown of heme into three primary products: biliverdin, carbon monoxide (CO), and free ferrous iron (Fe2+) [102,103,104,105].

A key role in iron metabolism is played by liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), highly specialized cells lining the tiny blood vessels, which act as the body’s main iron sensors by producing bone morphogenetic protein 6 (BMP6), the signaling protein regulating systemic iron balance [106]. LSECs detect changes in blood iron levels, including non-transferrin-bound iron and hemoglobin [106,107]. LSECs are characterized by the presence of open pores called fenestrae without diaphragms, the lack of a structured basement membrane, and an exceptionally high capacity for endocytosis [108,109]. They have the highest permeability of any mammalian endothelial cell [108,110]. When the level of iron in the blood increases, LSECs release BMP6 into the narrow gap in their environment, the space of Disse (also called the perisinusoidal space), which transmits a signal to hepatocytes in a paracrine manner [111]. Binding of BMP6 to hepatocyte receptors triggers the production of hepcidin to block ferroportin. Recent research shows LSECs also directly clear hemoglobin from the blood (via a process called macropinocytosis) to recycle iron safely and prevent toxicity during hemolysis [106,107].

The system tightly governing iron absorption, utilization, storage, recycling, and excretion is semiclosed in nature, as the only routes of iron excretion are passive loss via menstrual blood in premenopausal women and the shedding of gastrointestinal epithelial cells and skin cells [52].

3.6. Estrogen—Iron Axis

The regulatory mechanism based on the physiological feedback loop that allows estrogen to influence iron metabolism is known as the estrogen–iron axis [112]. Clinical and experimental evidence suggests that periods with increased estrogen levels (e.g., the ovulatory phase of the menstrual cycle, pregnancy) are characterized by more efficient dietary iron absorption from the gut and maximum systemic iron bioavailability [113,114]. The estrogen–iron axis has been shown to act through both direct (through estrogen response elements (EREs)) and indirect (e.g., through G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor 1 (GPR30) and bone morphogenetic protein 6 (BMP6) pathways) mechanisms in duodenal enterocytes, macrophages and hepatocytes [115,116]. Estrogen suppresses hepcidin and limits ferroportin mRNA transcription in estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) cells. In this way, E2 prevents ferroportin degradation, and because this effect dominates the reduction in ferroportin mRNA transcription, intracellular iron (ferritin) stored in duodenal enterocytes, macrophages and hepatocytes is redistributed into the plasma [115]. This increase in systemic iron availability to meet hematologic (erythropoiesis) and nonhematologic demands is particularly important during pregnancy and is essential for uterine growth and placental and fetal development [21,23,117]. However, the direct involvement of pregnancy-related hormones, including those in the estrogen–iron axis, in the mechanism of hepcidin suppression during pregnancy remains uncertain and requires further investigation [21,114].

The effects of the estrogen–iron axis can be reversed in states characterized by low estrogen levels (e.g., during menopause and in certain hormonal disorders associated with amenorrhea), where iron absorption is restricted, accompanied by an increased risk of localized iron deposition or deficiency [118,119]. Thus, while the activity of the estrogen–iron axis helps compensate for the monthly blood loss that occurs in premenopausal women, in postmenopausal women using hormone replacement therapy, it may increase susceptibility to iron overload if not monitored [120,121].

4. Iron Metabolism and Pregnancy

Adaptive Changes in Iron Metabolism During Pregnancy.

Of the approximately 1000 mg of additional iron required by the mother during pregnancy, ~300 mg is transferred to the fetus and placenta, ~500 mg is involved in the increase in the number of maternal red blood cell mass, and ~200 mg is lost through normal bodily excretion (e.g., exfoliation of dead skin, blood loss and turnover of the intestinal epithelium) [21]. The temporary cessation of menstrual bleeding during pregnancy allows only approximately 200–300 mg of iron to be preserved [122]. Avoiding a massive imbalance between iron supply and demand during pregnancy requires many adaptive changes, with hepcidin inhibition playing a key role. The strong inhibition of hepcidin synthesis often results in undetectable levels in the third trimester of pregnancy [114]. Hepcidin suppression occurs through the maximization of dietary iron absorption, which is particularly pronounced from week 24 of gestation until term, and the increased release of the intracellularly stored iron into the bloodstream [123]. The active transport of nonheme iron across the apical membrane of duodenal enterocytes is increased because of increased local expression of DMT1; moreover, the lack of hepcidin prevents the degradation of ferroportin at the level of the basolateral membrane, which increases the export of ferrous iron from the inside of cells such as enterocytes, macrophages and hepatocytes [124,125].

The mechanisms of pregnancy-related hepcidin suppression are not fully understood, and recent studies have not confirmed the previously suggested direct effect of elevated estradiol and prolactin levels during pregnancy [21,114]. Currently, when the causes of the reduced hepcidin concentration are analyzed, three key factors are considered:

The natural decrease in blood iron levels and the depletion of iron reserves in hepatocytes in a pregnant woman’s body—resulting from the uptake of significant amounts of iron by the placenta and the growing fetus—directly inhibit hepcidin synthesis in the liver [21,126].

The increased iron demand driven by intensified erythropoiesis—associated with the significant increase in circulating blood volume during pregnancy—acts as a bone marrow-derived signal that suppresses hepcidin; however, this excludes the erythroid-derived hepcidin-suppressing factor erythroferrone (ERFE) [127,128].

An increase in the serum concentration of the soluble form of hemojuvelin (sHJV) during gestation—which acts as a decoy receptor within the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)/small mother against decapentaplegic (SMAD) family–hepcidin axis—inhibits BMP signaling and reduces hepcidin expression [129,130,131].

Rising estrogen levels during pregnancy stimulate the liver to increase the synthesis of transferrin, which increases total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) in the blood [132]. This adaptive change serves as a compensatory mechanism to meet the increased iron needs of both the mother and the developing fetus. While the average serum transferrin concentration is in the range of 200–400 mg/dL (2.5–5.0 μmol/L) in nonpregnant women, the concentration of this transport protein gradually increases and reaches values in the range of 288–530 mg/dL (3.5–6.5 μmol/L) during the third trimester [133,134,135]. However, despite the increase in the total amount of iron in the body, transferrin saturation (iron saturation) tends to decrease slightly during pregnancy because of the disproportionate increase in blood volume (hemodilution) [21,136,137]. This may be misinterpreted as iron deficiency.

Dynamic increases in estrogen levels during pregnancy transcriptionally upregulate the expression of lactoferrin through specific EREs located within the lactoferrin gene promoter. Lactoferrin is a specialized multifunctional protein belonging to the transferrin family that binds iron [138]. The increase in plasma lactoferrin levels during pregnancy is accompanied by its presence in breast milk, with particularly high concentrations found in colostrum [138,139]. The core functions of lactoferrin include immune support through antimicrobial, antiviral, and anti-inflammatory activities; iron regulation to ensure its safe absorption in the body; antioxidant action; and the modulation of coagulation and hemostasis during pregnancy [138,139]. Lactoferrin, which is upregulated by estrogen, is responsible for physiological pregnancy-associated hypercoagulability by interacting with and potentiating factor XIa (FXIa), an active blood plasma enzyme that facilitates the production of blood clots [140]. While this physiological shift promotes hemostasis to mitigate peripartum hemorrhage risk, it also exacerbates maternal hypercoagulability, thereby increasing the incidence of thromboembolic morbidity [140].

5. Role of the Placenta in Iron Metabolism

In the hemochorial placenta found in humans and rodents, maternal blood is in direct contact with fetal chorionic villi and trophoblast cells, with no intervening maternal tissue layers [141]. Iron is therefore transferred from maternal circulation via the blood flowing through placental STBs. Iron transport across the placenta is unidirectional and requires cellular energy because it occurs against a concentration gradient [22]. Because iron builds up in the fetus at higher levels than it does in the mother, receptors and channels handle iron intake and release within placental circulation in a way that prioritizes the fetus, which means that iron is acquired by the fetus even when iron is deficient in maternal circulation [22,142]. However, several human and animal studies challenge the classic dogma of exclusive fetal control over the iron transfer process, as compromised fetal iron stores have been demonstrated in cases of severe maternal iron deficiency [8,143]. Thus, maternal and local signals may also be considered in iron regulation.

5.1. Normal Placental Iron Transport

Iron transport at the placental level occurs in several precise stages, and its intensity increases as pregnancy progresses [144]. The rate of this increase in iron transport cannot be explained by a simple increase in the number of transferrin receptors (TfRs) due to an increase in their surface area caused by placental growth [144,145]. The most important components (stages and mechanisms) of this process are as follows:

Iron uptake by the placenta occurs through the binding of transferrin to appropriate receptors on the surfaces of trophoblasts [20];

The intracellular release of iron occurs through its detachment from transferrin and, via PCBP1, storage as ferritin and/or subsequent release as a transport ligand/ion for ferroportin [146];

Iron trafficking occurs across STBs and through the fetal endothelium into the fetal blood [127];

At the peak of iron transport, which occurs in the third trimester of pregnancy, the fetus accumulates the greatest reserves of this trace element, which is stored mainly in the liver and used during the first months of life after birth [147].

The villi (and the syncytiotrophoblast microvilli covering them) in the human placenta are directly immersed in the maternal blood located in the intervillous spaces (placental sinuses) [148]. Iron transported in the placenta must travel at the level of the fully developed villi (approximately 18–20 weeks of pregnancy), including two plasma membranes of STBs (the microvillous plasma membrane contacting the maternal blood and the basal membrane facing the stromal core of the villi) [149]. Afterward, iron moves in the connective tissue within the space between the STBs and the fetal vessels, called the villous core stroma (or extraembryonic mesoderm), which contains mesenchymal cells, fibroblasts, and Hofbauer cells (specialized fetal macrophages), toward the fetal capillary endothelium, i.e., the inner lining of the fetal blood vessels, which consists of polarized cells with a luminal surface facing the bloodstream and a basal (basolateral) surface facing the underlying basement membrane and stroma [150,151]. Iron transport across fetal capillary endothelial cells (ECs) relies on regulated transcellular pathways involving specific uptake and efflux mechanisms rather than passive paracellular leakage [20,152].

Several transporters expressed in the abovementioned plasma membranes participate in the transfer of iron across the placenta to the fetal blood, and the intercellular spaces of the stroma of the villi also participate in the regulation of maternal–fetal signals, forming a system of fluid-filled channels containing extracellular vesicles (stromal macrovesicles), among other components [145,153].

The main mechanism of iron transport between maternal and fetal blood involves diferric transferrin [Tf-Fe(III)2], which forms a complex on the apical side of STBs after binding to TfR1 [22]. The expression of both holo-Tf and TfR1 on the apical surface of placental microvilli increases as pregnancy advances and fetal iron demand increases [154]. The key role of transferrin in placental iron transport has been confirmed by studies on hypotransferrinemic mice, whose offspring showed an extremely severe form of iron deficiency anemia (IDA) and survived only up to 2 weeks without transferrin or serum administration [155]. The holo-Tf/TfR1 complex is endocytosed in clathrin-coated vesicles, and the intracellular mechanism for holo-Tf-bound iron processing after endocytosis is similar to that of enterocytes, macrophages and hepatocytes [156]. Because TIBC is higher in maternal blood than in umbilical cord blood, iron transport due to placental transferrin transcytosis may occur only in trace amounts [157]. The decrease in the pH inside the endosome from 7.4 to less than 5.5 results in the release of ferric iron from Tf, which is subsequently reduced to ferrous iron by the ferric reductases of six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate (STEAP) family members, specifically STEAP3 and STEAP4 [21,158]. Both DMT1 and ZRT/IRT-like protein 14 (ZIP14) enable the release of Fe2+ from the endosome into the cytoplasm, where it can be stored by ferritin, incorporated into heme and heme-derivative proteins within the placenta, or released into fetal capillaries via ferroportin [159,160]. Similar to hephaestin in the gut, a multicopper ferroxidase, zyklopen, is expressed in the cellular membrane and oxidizes ferrous iron to ferric iron after it is transferred through ferroportin [161,162]. In this way, the exported, less toxic ferric iron can safely bind to fetal transferrin in the placental stroma and passively diffuse (along with free Fe3+ ions) in the extracellular fluid over a short distance [20,146]. The loose connective tissue of the placental villous stroma acts as a simple aqueous space, known as the wet stromal matrix, which fills the narrow physical gap between the trophoblast basement membrane and the outer wall of the fetal capillary [153,163]. In addition to the basolateral syncytiotrophoblast membrane, ferroportin is expressed in the core villous stroma cells to transfer iron into the fetal capillary endothelium [163].

The least understood stage of placental iron transfer is the mechanism of movement of this element within the villous stroma toward the capillary endothelium and the subsequent transendothelial transport to fetal blood [20,145]. A “classic mechanism” is assumed in which the diferric fetal Tf complex approaches the fetal capillary endothelium and binds to TfR1 located on the abluminal (outer/stromal-facing) side of the capillary ECs. The holo-Tf/TfR1 complex is subsequently internalized into ECs via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Owing to the acidity of the endosome interior, the ferric ion detaches from Tf and is converted to ferrous iron mainly by STEAP4; then, iron may be stored in the form of ferritin or exported to fetal blood [164]. How iron subsequently traverses the microvascular ECs of the fetal villous capillaries to reach the circulation remains unclear, and this polarized endothelial expression and luminal vs. abluminal distribution must be further investigated [21]. Typically, this polarized placement allows controlled iron transendothelial efflux from the blood into local tissue or the brain interstitium, and ferroportin, the only known cellular iron exporter in vertebrates, is expressed on the abluminal (basolateral) surface of ECs [17,165].

Notably, there is no clear evidence that the dominant, transferrin-related placental transfer of iron to the fetus is the only way for fetal tissues to acquire this element. In mouse studies, disruption of TfR1 alleles significantly reduced, but did not prevent, the generation of substantial numbers of RBCs in embryos, suggesting the existence of an alternative iron uptake mechanism during early embryogenesis [123,145]. Other transferrin-independent iron transport mechanisms may involve primarily heme transporters that are highly expressed in the placenta, including the transmembrane receptor low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) [166], the feline leukemia virus subgroup C receptor 1 (FLVCR1) [167,168], and the proton-coupled folate transporter (PCFT), also known as heme carrier protein 1 (HCP1) [169]. Additionally, iron transport independent of transferrin may occur with the participation of DMT1 and heme oxygenase (HO)-1 and HO-2 [170,171,172]. In pathological conditions associated with elevated transferrin iron saturation, placental transport of non-transferrin-bound iron (NTBI) may occur, which increases the risk of toxic effects if ferrous iron (Fe2+) triggers the Fenton reaction [173]. Additionally, zinc transporters belonging to the solute carrier family 39A (known as the ZIP or ZRT- and IRT-like protein family), such as ZIP 8 (SLC39A8) and ZIP 14 (SLC39A14), can transport both non-transferrin-bound and transferrin-bound iron in the placenta [174]. Further studies are needed to determine the role of non-transferrin-mediated placental iron transfer in the physiological and pathophysiological mechanisms of pregnancy at different stages [18,175].

5.2. Placental Function in Iron Deficiency

Maternal iron deficiency impairs normal placental development, which limits the effective placental transfer of oxygen and nutrients [177,178]. Serious consequences of maternal IDA include increased maternal mortality, severe postpartum hemorrhage, preterm birth, low birth weight and long-term fetal neurodevelopmental deficits [179,180,181]. Because maternal iron deficiency can result from various causes, each of which can independently affect the placenta and fetus, typical treatment with iron supplements is not always effective [177]. Therefore, a detailed understanding of the molecular mechanisms of placental developmental disorders in individuals with iron deficiency may be crucial for the future development of optimal treatments for anemia during pregnancy [5,182].

5.2.1. Physiological Anemia

Physiological (normal) anemia resulting from the disproportionately greater increase in the plasma volume compared with the increase in red blood cell mass in pregnant women, i.e., the dilution of erythrocytes, usually has a mild course in individuals with hemoglobin levels above 10.5 g/dL. In this case, red blood cell size, measured as the mean corpuscular volume (MCV), remains within the normal range [80 to 100 femtoliters (fL)], which distinguishes this condition from fully developed iron deficiency anemia, which is accompanied by a reduction in MCV (microcytosis) [183]. However, the MCV can remain completely normal during the early phase of iron shortage because stored iron (ferritin) levels decrease before the cell size changes [184,185]. Therefore, major health organizations recommend routine low-dose iron supplementation during pregnancy to support increased nutritional demands and prevent iron deficiency, even if mild hemodilution (physiological anemia) is a normal part of pregnancy [186,187]. One well-described approach to selective supplementation is based on an estimation of iron stores by serum ferritin. A plasma ferritin concentration > 70 µg/L indicates that iron stores are adequate to support pregnancy and that no supplementation is needed, whereas a plasma ferritin concentration less than 30 µg/L may indicate an almost complete lack of iron stores and indicate the need for iron supplementation [188,189].

Reduced blood viscosity caused by physiological hemodilution decreases vascular resistance and facilitates optimal blood flow and increased perfusion within the placenta [190]. Optimal microvascular circulation through the low-pressure intervillous spaces of the placenta supports effective nutrient and gas exchange. Mild decreases in hemoglobin and hematocrit linked to hemodilution may help trigger beneficial vascular growth factor signaling, which is crucial for trophoblast differentiation and placental vessel development [191]. The hypoxic environment in the first trimester of pregnancy plays an important role in hypoxia, which is related to the occlusion of uterine spiral arteries by extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs), resulting in the activation of hypoxia inducible factors (HIFs), which are the main transcription factor regulators that control cellular responses to low oxygen levels [192,193]. Hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) activation in the developing placenta stimulates angiogenesis by upregulating the expression of angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and enables metabolic adaptation by switching metabolic pathways toward anaerobic processes to reduce oxygen consumption while maintaining energy balance [194,195]. Moreover, the action of HIFs affects almost all stages of trophoblast differentiation and placental growth in both normal and pathological pregnancies, and in addition to low oxygen levels, the secretion of HIFs is influenced by the renin–angiotensin system (RAS) and cytokines and growth factors that regulate placental function and homeostasis [196].

The absence of normal plasma expansion in pregnant women with dilutional anemia may therefore alter healthy microvascular development in the placenta through increased blood viscosity and a lack of modulation of local oxygen tension in placental tissue [177]. Moreover, excessive intake of iron supplements in pregnant women with normal iron stores (ferritin) may increase oxidative stress and potentially disrupt the normal development of small blood vessels in the placenta [15].

5.2.2. Pathological Iron Deficiency Anemia

Iron deficiency during pregnancy, which is associated with a decrease in hemoglobin levels below the values typical for hemodilution and depletion of the intracellular stores of this element, leads to serious disorders of placental function, in which the pathomechanisms of chronic hypoxia play major roles [5,197,198].

Hypoxia itself often exacerbates oxidative stress because of a paradoxical increase in ROS levels [199,200]. Normally, because oxidative metabolism is necessary to meet the needs of the growing fetus, the placenta is a source of ROS. Therefore, in physiological pregnancy, placental antioxidant protective systems function efficiently and neutralize free radicals formed within the uteroplacental–fetal unit [201]. However, a low, physiological level of free radical production is necessary and constitutes an important component of cellular signaling pathways and downstream activities during normal placental development [202]. Only in the case of placental hypoxia, which is often caused by iron deficiency and may be accompanied by microcirculation disorders that limit blood flow or cause fluctuations in blood flow, is the risk of hypoxia–reoxygenation or ischemia–reperfusion injury significantly increased [203,204].

At the mitochondrial level, low oxygen levels slow the activity of the electron transport chain, causing electron carriers to back up and leak single electrons directly to oxygen, resulting in the generation of superoxide [205,206,207]. This occurs because hypoxia triggers enzymes such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase and xanthine oxidase (XO). NADPH oxidase, a multiprotein enzyme complex, produces ROS by transferring electrons from NADPH to molecular oxygen, whereas xanthine oxidase is converted from xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) under cellular stress conditions, resulting in the accumulation of purine breakdown products (hypoxanthine and xanthine) [208,209]. While XO cannot fully function without oxygen during the pure hypoxic phase, a critical surge in production occurs upon reoxygenation/reperfusion, where XO uses reintroduced molecular oxygen to produce massive amounts of ROS (mainly hydrogen peroxide and superoxide radicals) [209]. Over time, antioxidant depletion also occurs [210]. Cellular defense systems become less effective and cannot neutralize additional free radicals. Tissue damage occurs, which affects the parenchymal and circulatory (fetal and maternal) compartments. Excessive ROS cause severe damage to cellular lipids (lipid peroxidation), proteins, and DNA, leading to trophoblast dysfunction and cell death [211]. This results in aberrant placentation and immune disturbances, which are linked to many pregnancy-related disorders, including early and recurrent pregnancy loss, fetal death, spontaneous preterm birth, preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction [212,213].

Placental dysfunction is accompanied by morphological changes, which usually represent compensatory but ultimately insufficient adaptations to hypoxia [214]. Placentas from pregnancies complicated by IDA often exhibit hypertrophy with a disproportionately increased weight relative to the birth weight of the fetus. Areas of necrosis (infarcts) and calcifications caused by inadequate blood flow are found in the placental tissue. Deposits of fibrin-like material are present within the intervillous spaces and placental villi, indicating fibrinoid necrosis, which further impairs/blocks nutrient and gas exchange [215]. Chronic villitis occurs with the excess production of syncytial knots, indicating increased trophoblast turnover and apoptosis [216]. While increased capillary proliferation within the villi can be observed in the placenta, endothelial damage and impaired local blood flow regulation can occur because of reduced levels of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) [215,217].

5.3. Iron Overload and Placental Ferroptosis

As a typical micronutrient, iron is characterized by a U-shaped risk curve [218]. Therefore, both iron deficiency and excess iron lead to increased health risks. Iron overload in the human placenta disrupts normal cellular function and is strongly linked to pregnancy complications [219]. Regardless of the many possible causes of placental iron overload, dysregulated transport pathways play major roles in the mechanism of intracellular iron accumulation. Imbalances in the levels of iron-transporting proteins (such as increased TfR1 and DMT1 levels and decreased ferroportin levels) may result in the retention of excess iron within placental trophoblast cells [16]. Moreover, a decrease in the level of intracellular ferritin may reduce the ability of cells to safely store iron, resulting in high levels of reactive free iron, which can result in oxidative damage and cell death [220].

5.3.1. Labile Intracellular Iron and the Fenton Reaction

Iron overload has toxic effects, which are primarily a consequence of the presence of excess labile (unbound or loosely bound), i.e., redox-active ions of Fe2+ inside the cell, which act as chemical catalysts and transform ordinary cellular byproducts into highly destructive ROS via the Fenton reaction [221]. The Fenton reaction is a catalytic process in which ferrous iron (Fe2+) reacts with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) under acidic conditions to produce highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH) (Figure 3).

Lysosomes and endosomes constitute acidic cell compartments that have a pH of 4.5 to 5.0, which allows iron-mediated radical generation to proceed more efficiently [222]. The hydroxyl radicals generated during the unchecked Fenton reaction fiercely attack nearby biomolecules, causing lipid peroxidation with subsequent ferroptosis (iron-dependent regulated cell death) and protein damage owing to the loss of their normal shape and function [223]. Nucleic acid (DNA/RNA) oxidation leads to damage with an increased risk of genetic mutations (e.g., due to large breaks in nucleic acid strands and the disruption of vital purine bases such as guanine) [224,225].

Moreover, excess labile iron levels via Fenton reactions influence epigenetics by acting as crucial cofactors for iron-dependent modifying eraser enzymes (e.g., ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes and Jumonji C (JmjC) domain-containing demethylases), leading to altered DNA and histone demethylation patterns [226,227]. Consequently, excess redox-active Fe2+ activity leads to altered chromatin dynamics, with a shift in the balance toward widespread DNA or histone hypomethylation, including reductions in activating marks such as H3K4me3 (trimethylation at the 4th lysine residue of the histone H3 protein) [228]. Thus, as a sensor capable of modulating cAMP signaling pathways, the labile iron pool may reflect and respond to the immediate metabolic demands of cells [229].

In hemochorial placentae, iron is transferred from maternal blood to the fetus through STBs, i.e., the outer lining of the placenta that contacts maternal blood; these cells show very high activity in iron metabolism and may be susceptible to excessive iron accumulation under certain conditions [16,230]. Moreover, recent reports have provided evidence that in states of severe iron deficiency during pregnancy, the placenta begins to prioritize its own metabolic needs and, as a compensatory reaction, may excessively store iron instead of—as previously thought—unconditionally eliminating ferritin and providing iron to the fetus [23,157,173].

5.3.2. Ferroptosis in Placental Tissue

Ferroptosis: An overview.

Ferroptosis was discovered and named in 2012 by scientist Dr. Scott W. Dixon and principal investigator Dr. Brent R. Stockwell alongside their research team at Columbia University [231]. They identified it as a unique form of regulated, iron-dependent regulated cell death driven by the unchecked accumulation of lipid peroxides. Although some types of autophagy (e.g., ferrinophagy), a process that usually keeps cells alive by degrading, clean up, and recycling their own damaged or unnecessary cellular components, can actually help trigger ferroptosis, the two processes should be treated separately [232]. Ferroptosis differs significantly from apoptosis, which is dominated by cell shrinkage and caspase activation, and from necrosis, which is characterized by uncontrolled swelling and bursting [233]. In contrast, iron-dependent cell death is defined by specific physical and biochemical traits [234,235,236,237].

The physical and morphological traits of ferroptosis include mitochondrial alterations with reduced volumes (mitochondrial shrinkage), increased membrane density, elevated membrane potentials, reductions in the levels of or total disappearance of inner mitochondrial cristae, and outer mitochondrial membrane rupture alongside intact plasma or cellular membrane breakdown at later stages [238,239]. A characteristic feature of ferroptosis is the preservation of nuclear integrity, i.e., no chromatin condensation or DNA fragmentation occurs, as observed in apoptosis [240,241].

The biochemical traits of ferroptosis include the following:

Iron overload, where the accumulation of labile, redox-active intracellular iron triggers the generation of ROS via the Fenton reaction [50];

Lipid peroxidation associated with the destructive accumulation of oxidized polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in cell membranes [242];

Antioxidant failure, including the depletion of glutathione (GSH) and inactivation of the lipid repair enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), as well as decreased activity of ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) [243,244];

Loss of key regulatory metabolic drivers and inhibition of membrane transporters that provide vital antioxidant building blocks (e.g., system Xc–, which is the functional cystine–glutamate antiporter), as well as increased activity of cell-surface iron transporters (such as transferrin) [245,246].

Two mechanisms are involved in ferroptosis: the intrinsic mechanism, which is an enzyme-regulated cell death pathway driven by the iron-dependent accumulation of lethal lipid peroxides within cell membranes due to failing internal antioxidant defense mechanisms, and the extrinsic (transporter-dependent) mechanism, which is triggered by extracellular signals and the regulation or inhibition of specific cell membrane transporters that control amino acid exchange and iron uptake [247]. Importantly, a definitive demonstration of ferroptosis requires a multi-parametric experimental validation combining genetic, biochemical, and morphological evidence, rather than relying on a single marker. Because many molecular changes seen in ferroptosis also occur in other forms of cell death or cellular stress, specific criteria must be met to definitively claim that ferroptosis has occurred. In the field of cell death research, distinguishing between ferroptosis-associated molecular alterations and the definitive demonstration of ferroptosis is the difference between identifying potential clues or indicators (e.g., lipid peroxidation accumulation, antioxidant system depletion, iron overload signaling and morphological alterations) and proving a cause-of-death verdict. Definitive demonstration of ferroptosis requires functional proof that the cell death process is mechanistically driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and can be actively halted by specific, targeted inhibitors. According to guidelines established by the Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death (NCCD), a definitive demonstration requires the following evidence [248]:

Pharmacological rescue (the gold standard), when the cell death must be prevented, delayed, or significantly mitigated by treatment with highly specific ferroptosis inhibitors (e.g., lipid trapping antioxidants, iron chelators). If these agents do not save the cells, the death is not ferroptosis.

Genetic or pharmacological sensitization, where manipulation of specific enzymes responsible for fueling or preventing ferroptosis must predictably alter the death rate.

Exclusion of other death pathways, when blocking other major regulated cell death pathways (e.g., apoptosis, necroptosis) does not prevent the cell death.

Following the criteria for definitively demonstrating ferroptosis can undermine the results of most recent studies, which usually identify potential indicators of ferroptosis.

5.3.3. Placental Ferroptosis and Pregnancy Complications

The subtle shift from normal placental iron homeostasis to ferroptosis.

Maintaining iron homeostasis in trophoblast cells and endotheliocytes of the villous microcirculation is a challenge at every stage of pregnancy [22,146,249]. The delicate threshold between normal placental iron metabolism and ferroptosis depends on the balance among iron uptake, storage, and antioxidant defense mechanisms [19]. Under normal conditions, the placenta tightly regulates iron uptake via transferrin receptors, controls its intracellular reserve pool via ferritin stores, and exports iron via ferroportin to nourish the fetus while preventing toxicity [157]. When this threshold is crossed, iron overload in trophoblasts triggers destructive, iron-dependent lipid peroxidation that compromises placental function and drives severe obstetrical complications [19,157].

Under physiological conditions, excess iron typically results in the downregulation of TfR1; however, pathological placental overexpression of TfR1, which is found in preeclampsia, accelerates iron accumulation, triggering oxidative stress and cellular ferroptosis [13,16].

An example of the narrow boundary between the control of cellular iron cycling and the occurrence of programmed iron-dependent cell death is ferritinophagy [250]. During this process of selective autophagy, the receptor protein nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4, also known as androgen receptor activator 70 (ARA70)) binds to the heavy chain 1 subunit of ferritin (FTH1) within the iron-storage ferritin complex [251]. The NCOA4–ferritin complex is subsequently transported toward and engulfed by autophagosomes and ultimately delivered to lysosomes for degradation [252]. After such ferritin decays, trivalent iron ions are reduced to divalent iron (ferrous Fe2+) and almost simultaneously released into the cytosol, which directly increases the intracellular labile iron pool to meet cellular iron demands [253]. The replenishment of or an increase in the labile iron pool is a necessary compensatory mechanism (to adjust demand) for intensive maternal–fetal iron transfer, and the autophagic pathway is utilized to mobilize iron from intracellular stores [254]. Expanding the cytosolic iron pool ensures that the developing placenta and fetus receive adequate amounts of iron even when maternal supply fluctuates. However, unchecked ferritinophagy can result in the overexpansion of the labile iron pool, which is located primarily in the cytosol but is also present in other cellular compartments, such as mitochondria, the nucleus and lysosomes [255]. Under such conditions, excess weakly bound, redox-active iron has toxic effects, generating ROS and triggering iron-dependent cell death (ferroptosis) [256,257].

The coexistence of oxidative stress, during which excess ferrous iron (Fe2+) reacts with hydrogen peroxide via the Fenton reaction, resulting in the generation of massive amounts of ROS, and antioxidant collapse, which prevents the neutralization of lipid hydroperoxides due to a critical reduction in the levels of or failure of protective enzymes—most notably GPX4—almost always indicates the occurrence of placental ferroptosis [18,258].

Iron homeostasis may also shift toward ferroptosis in the placenta owing to decreased activity or downregulation of the activity of ferroportin, the primary cellular iron exporter [259]. The iron trapped intracellularly in trophoblasts and endothelial cells causes toxic lipid peroxidation and the generation of ROS, which directly drives ferroptosis and contributes to placental dysfunction in several pregnancy complications [16,18,260].

Ferroptosis is associated with pregnancy complications.

In recent years, increasing evidence has shown that an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation may contribute to placental insufficiency and related pregnancy complications. Ferroptosis plays an important role in the pathophysiology of placental abnormalities associated with often overlapping pregnancy complications, such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus, fetal growth restriction, spontaneous abortion (miscarriage), and preterm birth [260].

In preeclampsia, a high blood pressure disorder that typically develops after the 20th week of pregnancy with symptoms of multiorgan damage (e.g., liver, kidneys, and central nervous system), iron dyshomeostasis with iron overload and subsequent ferroptosis contributes to placental damage, especially within the STB [261]. The significant contribution of iron metabolism disorders to the still insufficiently understood pathomechanisms of preeclampsia is suggested by elevated hemoglobin levels, increased transferrin saturation (↑ holo-Tf), elevated ferritin and serum iron levels during the second trimester, and placental iron overload [262,263]. Analyses of iron transporters by single-cell/nuclear RNA sequencing revealed increased expression of the TFRC and SLC11A2 genes, which encode TfR1 and DMT1, respectively, and reduced expression of SLC40A1, which encodes ferroportin [16]. The above changes, indicating iron overload, were accompanied by typical signs confirming increased ferroptosis in trophoblast cells, such as increased ROS levels and increased levels of 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE), a toxic, reactive aldehyde produced as a major end product during the lipid peroxidation of PUFAs, as well as reduced expression of the ferroptosis inhibitor GPX4 [264]. The above symptoms related to oxidative stress manifested as reduced placental secretion of progesterone (P4) and placental growth factor (PlGF) [264]. Preeclampsia involves a systemic hyperinflammatory state and immune dysregulation, which amplify endothelial damage and placental dysfunction [265,266]. Elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) stimulate the liver and placenta to produce more hepcidin, which targets ferroportin and regulates iron metabolism by preventing the release of iron into the bloodstream, trapping it inside cells and promoting its storage [267,268]. Some researchers have proposed that high hepcidin expression in preeclampsia acts as a protective response (compensatory mechanism) to limit free iron levels, oxidative stress, and endothelial damage [268,269,270]. Elevated serum hepcidin levels during early pregnancy are associated with preeclampsia [270]. However, meta-analyses suggest elevated serum hepcidin levels in preeclampsia, a definitive clinical consensus has not yet been reached because conflicting individual studies suggest no significant correlation among iron status, hepcidin levels and IL-6 levels or even question the increase in hepcidin levels in preeclampsia [271,272]. In the latter case, the authors concluded that high serum iron levels in preeclamptic women are likely caused by low hepcidin production [272].

The inconsistent correlations observed in preeclampsia research, including those related to hepcidin and iron metabolism, may be related to the heterogeneous nature of the condition, which has diverse underlying causes and clinical presentations [273].

Genetic studies have shown that in preeclampsia, reduced expression of the FTH1 (ferritin heavy chain 1) and FTL (ferritin light chain) genes, which encode the respective subunits of ferritin, may be associated with disturbances in the safe intracellular storage of iron in the form of Fe3+ and an increase in the labile pool of Fe2+ involved in the Fenton reaction [274,275]. For example, specific microRNAs (such as miR-150-3p) are upregulated in preeclampsia and directly target and downregulate FTH1 expression [275].

In preeclampsia, trophoblast ferroptosis may occur owing to the influence of the microRNA miR-30b-5p, which may simultaneously suppress the antiporter cystine/glutamate (system Xc–) and transcription factor paired box gene 3 (PAX3) [276]. This suppression promotes ferroptosis by decreasing the level of GSH and increasing the concentration of Fe2+ in the labile pool. In turn, the abnormally upregulated expression of miR-210 in preeclampsia leads to iron accumulation and autophagosome formation in trophoblast cells and hemosiderin deposition in placental stroma trophoblasts [277].

In terms of the link between the pathomechanism of preeclampsia and iron metabolism, the influence of iron supplementation in iron-deficient women, which may inhibit maternal hepcidin production and function, remains unclear. Absorption of dietary iron may increase, which may subsequently lead to an increase in hemoglobin levels, an increase in blood viscosity, and ultimately a reduction in placental blood flow (placental ischemia), which predisposes patients to endothelial dysfunction and the release of factors that increase blood pressure due to vasoconstriction (e.g., endothelin 1) [51]. Moreover, excessive dietary iron intake increases the risk of increased NTBI content in blood plasma, which is transported through the placenta and causes placental dysfunction because of increased oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and DNA damage [22].

In gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), a common metabolic complication of pregnancy associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, ferroptosis contributes to the development of placental dysfunction through an interconnected network involving oxidative stress, inflammation and impaired angiogenesis [278,279]. Placental tissue studies in GDM patients revealed elevated levels of oxidative stress markers, including phagocyte NADPH oxidase 22-kilodalton subunit (P22PHOX) and thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP), and decreased levels of antioxidant markers such as heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) [221]. Consistently, the levels of the anti-ferroptotic markers GPX4, SLC7A11, and NRF2 were significantly decreased, whereas the levels of the pro-ferroptotic markers TfR1 and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) were increased, suggesting increased iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation [258,280]. The development of a chronic inflammatory background in diabetic placental tissue was confirmed by increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)) and the activation of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), as well as the reduced expression of interleukin-10 (IL-10), which is primarily an anti-inflammatory cytokine [281,282]. Recent studies have shown that placental hepcidin mRNA and protein levels are significantly increased in diabetic pregnancies compared with those in healthy controls [18]. This upregulation of hepcidin expression is likely driven by the inflammatory response in diabetic placental tissue. Increased hepcidin expression occurs predominantly in the STB layer of the placenta and is correlated with downregulated ferroportin expression. The expression of hepcidin in individuals with GDM may also be influenced by polymorphisms of the hepcidin gene, in addition to iron nutritional status and the degree of compensation for glycemic disorders [283]. Ferritin translation and iron storage in the placenta are often suppressed in complicated or poorly controlled diabetic pregnancies, particularly when accompanied by fetal iron deficiency [284]. The expression of TfR1 varies depending on the specific type of diabetes, local iron status, and the presence of oxidative stress or hyperglycemia. Studies have reported conflicting patterns; some studies have reported increased TfR1 expression or mRNA accumulation in diabetic pregnancies complicated by low fetal iron stores, whereas other recent studies on GDM and diabetes in pregnancy (DIP) have reported decreases or no significant changes in TfR1 protein levels in the STB [18,285,286]. It is assumed that, especially in poorly controlled diabetes during pregnancy, hyperglycemia and lipid levels alter iron handling pathways and trigger oxidative stress, which can decrease TFR1 expression and the expression of other iron transporters, such as DMT1 and FPN1, as a protective cellular response [287].

Ferroptosis also acts as a central driver of impaired placental angiogenesis and dysfunction in GDM [258,288]. The combined burden of oxidative stress and ferroptosis suppresses vital vascular markers (key vascular development regulators), such as vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A), hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) 1 α subunit (HIF-1α) and stromal cell–derived factor 1 (SDF-1α, also known as chemokine CXCL12), thereby stunting healthy blood vessel growth in the placenta [280]. Angiogenesis disorders in the diabetic placenta are characterized mainly by excessive, abnormal branching of vessels (hypervascularization) and disturbances in their maturation and structure [289].

Emerging obstetric research has confirmed that ferroptosis may contribute to fetal growth restriction, spontaneous abortion, and preterm birth by triggering placental trophoblast injury and disrupting maternal–fetal tolerance [260]. Excessive cell death at the maternal–fetal interface compromises syncytialization, nutrient and gas transport, and placental hormone secretion, compromising proper growth and development of the fetus at various stages of pregnancy [264]. Disturbed iron metabolism and severe oxidative stress, which overwhelm placental antioxidant defense mechanisms and drive extensive ferroptotic cell death in trophoblasts, are responsible for the following processes:

Impaired early embryonic tissue invasion and breakdown in the maternal–fetal immune tolerance necessary to sustain pregnancy [290,291];

Fetal growth restriction in the subsequent weeks of pregnancy due to limited angiogenesis and blood vessel remodeling, causing tissue underperfusion and hypoxia–reoxygenation cycles, which lead to the acceleration and accumulation of lipid ROS [257,292];

Preterm birth, as ferroptosis prematurely damages the integrity of placental membranes and chorionic tissues and leads to hormonal disruptions in the placenta, which may trigger signaling cascades that stimulate premature uterine contractile activity [19,293].

Recommended remedies and potential therapeutic options to prevent placental ferroptosis-related complications.

General recommendations, although not yet formulated into clinical practice guidelines, may include avoiding cellular iron overload and excessive lipid peroxidation.

Iron supplementation during pregnancy should be monitored, preferably under medical supervision, to avoid unnecessary administration/taking an excessive dose. Treatment with iron preparations should be initiated after a clinical diagnosis of IDA because excess circulating non-transferrin-bound iron promotes placental oxidative damage (ferroptosis) [258,294].

Any inflammation during pregnancy should be treated effectively, considering the level of oxidative stress associated with inflammation [219,257].

The BMI should be normalized, as larger BMI values indicating obesity (BMI ≥ 30) are associated with the development of chronic low-grade inflammation (CLGI or metainflammation). To this end, obesogenic dietary habits (avoiding high-fat/high-sugar diets) that disrupt maternal–placental iron handling and increase the expression of markers of iron-dependent lipid damage should be minimized [295,296].

Pregnant women should especially avoid environmental toxins such as cigarette smoke (cessation of smoking) and exposure to environments with poor air quality (smog) or the risk of exposure to substances classified as endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) such as bisphenol A (BPA) [292,297,298].

Antioxidant defenses should be supported by maintaining adequate dietary intake of natural antioxidants (e.g., green tea and resveratrol from grapes) and nutrients that support GSH and cellular defense pathways [299,300].

Pregnancy-related conditions, especially blood pressure and metabolic homeostasis (especially glucose control), should be closely managed because hypertension (preeclampsia) and GDM actively increase susceptibility to placental ferroptosis [260,279,301].

Vitamins with antiferroptotic effects include water-soluble vitamin B6 (pyridoxine), which helps restore the proper level of glutathione; fat-soluble vitamin E (tocopherol) and vitamin K, which inhibit lipid peroxidation directly in the cell wall; and vitamin A (retinol) and vitamin D (calciferol), which activate genes that protect cells against ferroptosis [302,303].

The above recommendations show that following the general principles of proper pregnancy management, including nutrition, significantly reduces the risk of placental ferroptosis. However, with regard to monitoring iron supplementation for the prevention of ferroptosis, there are currently no official guidelines for use in clinical practice. Standard antenatal care focuses instead on preventing and treating maternal IDA. Similarly, there are currently no official clinical guidelines or standard recommendations for using “anti-ferroptotic” vitamin treatments during pregnancy.

The pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis remains in the preclinical development phase and involves three main groups of compounds:

Iron chelators that bind tightly to excess iron to safely remove trivalent (e.g., deferoxamine) or divalent (e.g., alpha-lipoic acid) ions outside the cell [304,305];

Ferroptosis inhibitors or radical-trapping agents, such as liproxstatin-1 and ferrostatin-1, that act as free radical scavengers and capture ROS before they cause lipid peroxidation [306,307,308];

Repurposed drugs, including dipyridamole and promethazine, in new therapeutic applications such as lipid peroxidation inhibitors, GSH preservation agents, and inhibitors of harmful antiangiogenic factor release [e.g., soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1 or sVEGFR-1)] in preeclamptic placental tissue [309,310].

Once again, none of the above pharmacological treatments have yet been included in official clinical guidelines or standard recommendations for the treatment of ferroptosis.

The effectiveness of future therapeutic approaches in limiting the harmful effects of ferroptosis in the placenta depends on the ability to locally enhance antioxidant pathways, e.g., by activating GPX4, replenishing GSH (e.g., supplying precursors or increasing cellular pools of GSH) and targeting protective enzymes [e.g., calcium-independent phospholipase A2 group VI (PLA2G6)] that repair peroxidized phospholipids in placental cells [311,312]. Considering that nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) regulates genes responsible for antioxidant and detoxification responses, activation of the p62 (sequestosome 1, SQSTM1)-kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1)–NRF2 signaling axis in trophoblasts may support the maintenance of redox homeostasis and limit the severity of the Fenton reaction [313,314]. Restricting excessive iron uptake (such as by targeting high TfR1 expression) or preventing NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy could also prevent dangerous local iron overload during hypoxia in early pregnancy [251,315].

6. Concluding Remarks

Achieving and maintaining iron homeostasis during pregnancy is a major challenge for the regulatory systems of pregnant women. The dynamics of iron metabolism during fetal development are particularly complex, and the placenta, as an intermediary organ between the mother and the fetus, plays a central role. Syncytiotrophoblast (STB) cells are specialized placental cells that efficiently absorb, store, and transport iron within the uteroplacental–fetal unit. In a normal pregnancy, maternal iron stores—measured as serum ferritin levels—frequently decrease because of the increase in the plasma volume and high fetal–placental demands, whereas the placental STB, i.e., the outer epithelial layer of the hemochorial placenta, adapts by upregulating TfR1s expression to maximize iron transfer to the fetus. Owing to the higher receptor density, the STB can effectively capture deficient iron from maternal circulation and quickly transport it via the prominently expressed ferroportin, an iron scavenger. This decrease in placental iron content reflects normal iron utilization rather than an immediate pathological state, although severe decreases may result in iron deficiency. The lack of iron accumulation and efficient antioxidant defense mechanisms indicate that the labile intracellular ferrous iron (Fe2+) pool does not pose a significant risk for ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and the unchecked accumulation of ROS in cell membranes. However, in cases of placental iron overload (e.g., due to unmonitored excessive iron supplementation) and pathological pregnancy with concomitant oxidative stress, an increased pool of toxic Fe2+ combined with high oxygen fluctuations (such as early placental hypoxia/reperfusion) can trigger Fenton reactions and subsequent lipid peroxidation, leading to ferroptosis-related placental dysfunction. Placental dysfunction due to lipid peroxidation often develops in the context of chronic inflammation, which exhausts the antioxidant defense capacity. This transition from physiology to pathophysiology in the placenta is often difficult to capture in clinical studies. CLGI is closely linked to obesity during pregnancy, GDM and hypertensive disorders such as preeclampsia, which frequently co-occur as a pregnancy-specific metabolic syndrome. Therefore, considering the global epidemic of metabolic disorders in pregnant women, targeting placental ferroptosis could help in the prevention and treatment of serious pregnancy complications, thereby improving obstetric outcomes. However, despite the well-known health-promoting recommendations for pregnant women, including maintaining a healthy body weight and optimal dietary composition, including supplementation of iron, vitamins and natural antioxidants, research on the pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis has not progressed beyond the preclinical phase.
