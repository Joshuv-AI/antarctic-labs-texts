Abstract
We provide the first OPEN 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d CLOSE ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right) -sample algorithm for sampling from unbounded Gaussian distributions under the constraint of OPEN ( ε , δ CLOSE ) \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right) -differential privacy. 1
Introduction

Given a sample from a probability distribution, output a sample from the same probability distribution.
As stated, this problem is trivial: one can simply output the sample received.
However, when
privacy
is a concern, this is clearly inappropriate, as the sample may contain sensitive personal information.
To address this issue, one may turn to the concept of
differential privacy
(DP)
[
DMNS06
]
, a rigorous notion of privacy that ensures that the output of a procedure does not reveal too much information about any individual’s data.

One well-studied option is to privately
learn
the underlying distribution.
That is, given samples from a distribution, output a (differentially private) estimate of it.
After this estimated distribution is released, one may freely output any desired number of samples from the distribution without any further privacy concerns (appealing to the post-processing property of differential privacy).

However, this strategy may be costly.
Indeed, in both the private and non-private setting, learning a distribution is clearly a much harder problem than simply outputting a single sample from it.
In an influential paper, Raskhodnikova, Sivakumar, Smith, and Swanberg
[
RSSS21
]
introduced the problem of privately
sampling
from a distribution.
Given a collection of samples from a distribution, privately output a single sample from (approximately) the same underlying distribution.
The authors provided upper and lower bounds for private sampling on discrete distributions and binary product distributions, showing that, in various cases, private sampling is either no easier or dramatically easier than private learning.

We note that this problem has immediate implications for machine learning in the context of private generative models.
Rather than privately training a generative model (which may be data-intensive), one could instead use private sampling if only a limited number of samples (e.g., prompt completions) are needed.

Subsequently, Ghazi, Hu, Kumar, and Manurangsi
[
GHKM23
]
studied private sampling of Gaussian distributions.
Technically speaking, the most interesting case is when we are sampling from distributions with unknown covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
The authors provide two algorithms for privately sampling from
d
-dimensional Gaussians subject to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP:

1.

If
\mathbb{I}\preceq\Sigma\preceq K\mathbb{I}
(i.e., the covariance is bounded), they give an algorithm with sample complexity
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{dK^{2}}{\varepsilon}}}\right)
;

2.

Without any assumptions (i.e., a potentially unbounded covariance), they give an algorithm with sample complexity
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon}}}\right)
.

They also prove a sample complexity lower bound of
\widetilde{\Omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}}}\right)
(for the case
\mathbb{I}\preceq\Sigma\preceq 2\mathbb{I}
), thus establishing that, when the covariance is bounded up to constant factors, their algorithm is near-optimal.
However, this leaves open the case where
K=\omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
.
For example, if
K=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{d}}}\right)
, then the best known upper bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d^{2}}}\right)
is off from the lower bound of
\widetilde{\Omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)
by a quadratic factor.
Is it always a choice between an algorithm whose sample complexity depends linearly on the dimension
d
(but also suffers from a dependence on the “range” of the covariance
K
), and one whose sample complexity is quadratic in
d
?
Or is there an
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)
-sample algorithm, regardless of how large
K
may be?

1.1
Results and Techniques

Our main result is an algorithm with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)
sample complexity, for private sampling from Gaussians with arbitrary (i.e., potentially unbounded) covariance.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists an
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-differentially private algorithm such that, given:

n=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\delta}}}\right)}{\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d+\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\delta}}}\right)}{\alpha\varepsilon}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right),

samples drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\Sigma}}\right)
, outputs a sample from a Gaussian distribution which is
\alpha
-close to
\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\Sigma}}\right)
.

Observe that, in terms of its dependence on
d
in the sample complexity, this nearly matches the
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon\sqrt{\log d}}}}\right)
lower bound of
[
GHKM23
]
for the same problem.
Additionally, it is quadratically less than the sample complexity of privately
learning
Gaussians in the same setting
[
KLSU19
,
AAK21
,
KMS+22b
,
AL22
,
KMV22
,
KMS22a
]
, which is
\widetilde{\Theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}}{\alpha\varepsilon}}}\right)
.

We give a brief technical overview of our approach.
As a starting point, we use the algorithm of
[
GHKM23
]
which privately samples from a Gaussian with covariance
\mathbb{I}\preceq\Sigma\preceq K\mathbb{I}
using
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dK^{2}}}\right)
samples.
Recall the following basic property of Gaussians: given
n
samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
from
\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\Sigma}}\right)
, their average
\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}X_{i}
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\frac{1}{n}\Sigma}}\right)
.
Simply outputting this average is insufficient for two reasons: i) the variance of the resulting distribution is too small by a factor of
n
, and ii) the algorithm is not differentially private.
Ideally, one could solve both problems at once by adding
\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{n-1}{n}\Sigma}}\right)
noise, but recall that
\Sigma
itself is unknown.
As a proxy for this,
[
GHKM23
]
uses the
samples themselves
to sample from this distribution.
Another basic property of Gaussians is
2
-stability: it implies that, if
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a random unit vector and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are samples from a Gaussian, then
\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}z_{i}X_{i}
is a sample from the same Gaussian.
1
1

1

See Lemma
B.10
for a proof of this property.

Thus, letting
Y_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{n+i}-X_{n+m+i}}}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\Sigma}}\right)
, the random variable
\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}X_{i}+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{n}}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]}z_{i}Y_{i}
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu,\Sigma}}\right)
.
To complete the privacy analysis, two additional components are needed: a) “clipping” the datapoints to a predetermined range (based on the upper bound
\Sigma\preceq K\mathbb{I}
) and b) a “Propose-Test-Release” (PTR) step
[
DL09
]
to ensure that the collection of
Y_{i}
’s have large (empirical) variance in all directions (which is implied with high probability given the lower bound
\mathbb{I}\preceq\Sigma
).
Unfortunately, due to these two steps, the resulting sample complexity is
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(dK^{2}}}\right)
.

We modify this general framework, turning to recent results of Brown, Hopkins, and Smith
[
BHS23
]
.
2
2

2

A concurrent work to theirs of Kuditipudi, Duchi, and Haque
[
KDH23
]
obtains a similar result for covariance-aware mean estimation via similar techniques. We focus on
[
BHS23
]
since we directly appeal to their primitives; it is conceivable that methods in
[
KDH23
]
could have been used instead.

Their main result is an
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)
sample algorithm for covariance-aware private mean estimation – that is, mean estimation where the error in the estimate may scale proportional to the underlying distribution’s variance in that direction.
To do this, they design algorithms
StableMean
and
StableCov
.
Focusing on
StableMean
(
StableCov
is similar), this algorithm takes in a dataset
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, and outputs a vector of weights
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
and a score
s
.
3
3

3

As in given in
[
BHS23
]
,
StableMean
outputs a non-private estimate for the mean of the distribution which is a weighted version of the empirical mean that uses the weight vector
v
.
We have slightly changed the format of the output, since it facilitates aspects of our exposition.
For more details on this, we point readers to Section
3
.

This algorithm has two key properties: a) if the data is truly Gaussian, then the weights will be uniform (i.e.,
v_{i}=\frac{1}{n}
for all
i
) with high probability, and b) if the score
s
is sufficiently small (which can be verified using a PTR step), then the weighted empirical mean
\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}v_{i}X_{i}
will have bounded sensitivity when modifying one datapoint.
We highlight that bounded sensitivity is a property of the overall sum, and individual summands may change dramatically if a single datapoint is changed – this is in contrast to clipping-based strategies where individual summands also have bounded sensitivity.
These elegant primitives allow one to perform private mean estimation without any explicit clipping.

We employ these primitives in the framework of
[
GHKM23
]
: we output the quantity
\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}v_{i}X_{i}+\sqrt{m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}}}\right)}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]}z_{i}\sqrt{w_{i}}Y_{i}
, where the
v_{i}
’s and
w_{i}
’s are the weights outputtted by
StableMean
and
StableCov
, respectively.
That is, we incorporate these weights to bound the sensitivity, as an alternative to clipping.
We also require a preceding PTR step in order to ensure that the scores output by
StableMean
and
StableCov
are both sufficiently small.
To perform the utility analysis, it suffices to show that the weights are uniform with high probability, and the analysis is similar to before.
The privacy analysis however requires newfound care.

We illustrate in the univariate case (i.e.,
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
), focusing exclusively on the term
\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]}z_{i}U_{i}
, where we use
U_{i}
to denote
\sqrt{w_{i}}Y_{i}
.
We accordingly define
U_{i}^{\prime}
, which is defined with respect to a neighboring dataset
Y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,Y_{n}^{\prime}
.

[
GHKM23
]
previously used a coupling-based argument to reduce to examining only the term involving the
Y_{i}
that differs (i.e., the datapoint such that
Y_{i}\neq Y_{i}^{\prime}
).
But, as mentioned before, changing a single
Y_{i}
may result in changes to
all

U_{i}
’s.
Since the stability property of
StableCov
tells us that the empirical variance with these weights has bounded sensitivity, all we know is that
\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]}U_{i}^{2}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1\pm\frac{1}{m}}}\right)\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]}U_{i}^{\prime 2}
.
Our goal is to argue that, for fixed
U
and
U^{\prime}
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle z,U}}\right\rangle
is similar in distribution to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle z,U^{\prime}}}\right\rangle
, where all the randomness is in the choice of the random unit vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Perhaps surprisingly, if
U
and
U^{\prime}
satisfy the aforementioned bounded sensitivity property, then this suffices to prove the desired statement.
To this end, it is convenient to think of
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
as a vector.
By rotational symmetry,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle z,U}}\right\rangle
is equal in distribution to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|U}}\right\|_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle z,e_{1}}}\right\rangle
, where
e_{1}
is the first standard basis vector, and similarly,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle z,U^{\prime}}}\right\rangle
is equal to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|U^{\prime}}}\right\|_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle z,e_{1}}}\right\rangle
.
It is not too hard to argue that the two transformed distributions are close to each other (as required in Hockey-Stick divergence), as
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle z,e_{1}}}\right\rangle
can be seen to be a Beta distribution, and the two distributions are scalings of this distribution by similar constants.
This sketch exposes some of the key ideas in the univariate case for the “variance” term of the output, handling the general case requires additional care.

We now give the formal definition of this notion.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
P,Q\in\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X}}}\right)
such that
\mathrm{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P}}\right)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q}}\right)
.
4
4

4

If

the definition is still valid, but can result in

.
However, for the

-divergences considered in present work, this will not be the case, and we will be able to calculate them using simplified versions of the general definition without concern about the distributions’ supports.

Also, let

be a convex function such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|<\infty,\forall x>0

, and

D_{f}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P\middle\|Q}}\right)\coloneqq\rmintop\nolimits\limits_{\mathcal{X}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}}}\right)dQ.

.

D_{e^{\varepsilon}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P\middle\|Q}}\right)\coloneqq\max\limits_{S\subseteq\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)-e^{\varepsilon}Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)}}\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\rmintop\nolimits\limits_{\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)-e^{\varepsilon}q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|\,dx-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{\varepsilon}-1}}\right).

.

2.2
Privacy Preliminaries

We define differential privacy here and introduce some of its most fundamental properties.

Definition 2.4

(Approximate Differential Privacy (DP)
[
DMNS06
]
)
.

A randomized algorithm
M\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
satisfies
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
-DP if for every pair of neighboring datasets
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
(i.e., datasets that differ in at most one entry), we have:

\underset{M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)\in Y}}\right]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\underset{M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X^{\prime}}}\right)\in Y}}\right]+\delta,\forall Y\subseteq\mathcal{Y}.

Remark 2.5
.

The algorithm’s goal is to privately obtain an estimate

\mu

\Sigma
that satisfies
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widetilde{\mu}-\mu}}\right\|_{\Sigma}\leq\alpha

Roughly speaking, the algorithm involves producing a pair of non-private estimates

These estimates come from

This is a class of algorithms which, given well-behaved input datasets (i.e., datasets that do not contain significant outliers), produce outputs which are resilient to small perturbations of the input dataset, despite having no formal robustness and/or privacy guarantees.

Appendix A
Facts from Linear Algebra

In this appendix, we state a number of useful facts from linear algebra.
We start with a fact about the semi-definite ordering.

Fact A.1
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive-definite matrices.
Then,
A\preceq B\iff\mathbb{I}\preceq A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}}\iff B^{-\frac{1}{2}}AB^{-\frac{1}{2}}\preceq\mathbb{I}
.

Next, we have a fact about matrix norms.
Specifically, we recall the property known as
rotational invariance
.

Fact A.2
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.
For any rotation matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|UA}}\right\|_{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A}}\right\|_{2},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|UA}}\right\|_{F}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A}}\right\|_{F}
, and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|UA}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
.
The above also holds for
AU
, for any rotation matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.

We now introduce basic matrix factorizations, namely the
spectral decomposition
for symmetric matrices, and the
singular value decomposition (SVD)
for general matrices.

Fact A.3
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.
Then,
A
can be factorized as follows:

•

If
n=m
and
A=A^{\top}
, we can write
A=S\Lambda S^{\top}
, where
\Lambda\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\lambda_{i}}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}
are the eigenvalues of
A
, and
SS^{\top}=\mathbb{I}
.
For the special case of PSD matrices,
S
can be assumed to be a rotation matrix.
The previous can equivalently be written as
A=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}\lambda_{i}s_{i}s_{i}^{\top}
, where
s_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the vectors representing the columns of
S
.

•

If
n\geq m
, we can write
A=UDV^{\top}
, where
D\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}}}\right\}\middle|0_{m\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-m}}\right)}}}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma_{i}}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]}
are the singular values of
A
, and
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},V\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
are rotation matrices.
The previous can equivalently be written as
A=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]}\sigma_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{\top}
, where
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the vectors representing the columns of
U
and

, respectively.

Remark A.4
.

The statement given above for the SVD is phrased in terms of matrices whose number of rows is lower-bounded by the number of columns.
However, the decomposition is valid even without this assumption and, given a matrix that does not satisfy this, we can obtain its SVD by taking its transpose, and then applying the previous statement.

We include one standard inequality here that upper-bounds the determinant of a positive-definite matrix in terms of its trace.
The inequality follows directly from AM-GM.

Fact A.5
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a positive-definite matrix.
Then, we have
\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)}{n}}}\right)^{n}
.

Finally, we have a lemma which establishes that, if a symmetric positive-definite matrix
A
has small distance from the identity matrix, the same holds for
A^{-1}
as well.

Lemma A.6
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric positive-definite matrix with
A\succeq\mathbb{I}
.
Then, it holds that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\leq\alpha\implies\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A^{-1}-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\leq\alpha
,

Proof.

We only prove the first case, because the proof for the second is analogous.
Since the matrix
A
is symmetric and positive-definite, by Fact
A.3
, it can be written in the form
A=U\Lambda U^{\top}
, where
\Lambda
is the eigenvalue matrix of
A
, and
U
is a rotation matrix.
This implies that we have
A^{-1}=U\Lambda^{-1}U^{\top}
.
Thus, by Fact
A.2
, we get
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A^{-1}-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
.
Let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\lambda_{i}}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}
be the spectrum of
A
.
By the previous and our assumptions that
A\succeq\mathbb{I}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\leq\alpha
, we can reduce the problem of upper-bounding
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A^{-1}-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
to the following constrained maximization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda_{i}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}}}\right)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{i}-1}}\right)\leq a<\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\geq 1,\forall i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right],

The above can be solved exactly via an application of the KKT conditions, yielding the bound
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A^{-1}-\mathbb{I}}}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{\alpha n}{n+\alpha}\leq\alpha
.
∎

We conclude our facts about distributions with a lemma that generalizes the stability property of the Gaussian distribution.
Namely, stability says that any sum of independent Gaussians or product of a Gaussian with a number also follows a Gaussian distribution.
We extend this to random linear combinations when the weights come from a distribution over the unit sphere.

Lemma B.10
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}}\right)

\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}a_{i}X_{i}\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\mathbb{I}}}\right)

Proof.

Let
X\coloneqq\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}a_{i}\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\mathbb{I}}}\right)
.
Let
a_{0}
be a fixed realization of
a
.
Observe that:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X\middle|a=a_{0}}}\right)=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}a_{0,i}\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\mathbb{I}}}\right)\overset{d}{=}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,a_{0,i}^{2}\mathbb{I}}}\right)\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum\limits_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]}a_{0,i}^{2}}}\right)\mathbb{I}}}\right)\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\mathbb{I}}}\right).

is a weighted average of the densities of

of

f_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\rmintop\nolimits\limits_{\mathrm{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}}}\right)}f_{\mathcal{D}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{0}}}\right)f_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X\middle|a=a_{0}}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\,da_{0}=\rmintop\nolimits\limits_{\mathrm{supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{D}}}\right)}f_{\mathcal{D}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{0}}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\,da_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}},\forall x\in\mathbb{R},
