Updated guide to RNA quantification by RNA sequencing and reverse transcription-qPCR
Authors: Dajeong Bong, Kyungjin C. Lee, Gee-Yoon Lee, Seung-Jae V. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) are widely used for RNA quantification. RNA species with distinct structural and biogenetic features require specific computational and experimental approaches. Here, we provide an updated MiniResource that extends our previous guides to RNA-seq analysis and RT-qPCR-based RNA quantification. We introduce available tools and key considerations for analyzing circular RNAs, double-stranded RNAs, ribosomal RNAs, and transfer RNAs. This guide will help researchers choose appropriate methods for RNA species-specific quantification.

INTRODUCTION

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) are generally used methods for quantifying RNA (Ahn et al., 2025, Gao et al., 2024, Jeong et al., 2025, Ku et al., 2025, Kwon et al., 2018, Lee et al., 2024a, Lee et al., 2024, Lee et al., 2025, Oh et al., 2025, Park et al., 2024, Shin et al., 2025, Tae et al., 2024). Our previous MiniResource manuscripts provided concise guides to RNA-seq analysis and RT-qPCR-based RNA quantification (Lee et al., 2024c; Bong et al., 2024). Because circular RNAs (circRNAs), double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs), ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), and transfer RNAs (tRNAs) have distinct structural and biogenetic features, accurate quantification requires RNA species-specific experimental and computational analyses (Ha et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Rothschild et al., 2024; Sohn et al., 2026). General Methods for RNA-seq and RT-qPCR

RNA-seq enables transcriptome-wide RNA quantification through sequencing-read quality assessment, alignment or pseudoalignment, transcript quantification, normalization, differential expression analysis, and downstream functional analysis. Library preparation, reference sequences, and computational tools are selected based on the experimental aim and RNA type (Lee et al., 2024c).

RT-qPCR enables sensitive quantification of selected RNAs through RT, sequence-specific amplification, and absolute or relative quantification. Reliable analysis depends on appropriate RT primers, specific qPCR primers, and suitable reference genes (Bong et al., 2024). The following sections describe methods for specific RNA species.

circRNA

circRNAs are covalently closed RNAs generated by back-splicing (Liu and Chen, 2022). Their unique back-splice junctions (BSJs) and lack of polyadenylated tails require specialized RNA-seq approaches for accurate profiling (Hwang and Kim, 2024). For circRNA-enriched profiling, rRNA depletion and RNase R treatment, followed by polyadenylation of residual linear RNAs and depletion of the resulting polyadenylated RNAs, reduce linear RNA background and increase BSJ-spanning reads (Mun et al., 2024; Pandey et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2022). Sequencing reads are mapped to the genome, and circRNA-specific tools identify BSJs and annotate circRNAs. For example, CIRCexplorer2 (Zhang et al., 2016) uses TopHat-Fusion-based alignments (Kim and Salzberg, 2011), whereas CIRI2 (Gao et al., 2018) detects BSJs from reads aligned using BWA-MEM (Li, 2013). To assess circRNA abundance relative to cognate linear transcripts from the same host genes, circRNA-to-linear RNA ratios are calculated by integrating circRNA-enriched RNA-seq with matched mRNA-seq or total RNA-seq data, using tools such as CIRCexplorer3-CLEAR (Kim et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2019). For targeted analysis of annotated circRNAs, circRNA microarrays have advantages for sensitivity because BSJ-specific probes enable efficient profiling of predefined circRNA sets (Li et al., 2019) (Fig. 1A).

For RT-qPCR analysis, circRNAs are typically quantified using back-splice junction-specific assays (Jeck et al., 2013; Memczak et al., 2013). circRNA levels in RNase R-treated samples are compared with those in untreated controls and with cognate linear RNA levels (Panda and De, 2017, Vromman et al., 2023). Random or target-specific primers are used for RT, but oligo (dT) primers are not (Panda et al., 2017b). Divergent qPCR primers are used to amplify the back-splice junction, and assay specificity is examined by melt curve analysis, gel electrophoresis, and PCR product sequencing (Panda and Gorospe, 2018; Ren et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2023) (Fig. 1A).

dsRNA

dsRNAs are RNA duplexes produced from bidirectional transcription of circular mitochondrial DNA, sense-antisense transcript overlap, or transcription of repetitive elements (Jeong and Jeong, 2025, Sadeq et al., 2021 Sohn et al., 2026). dsRNA analyses focus on detecting double-stranded regions rather than total transcript levels, using two approaches: adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (ADAR)-based analysis (Reich and Bass, 2019) and antibody-based transcriptomic analysis (Schönborn et al., 1991, Shadle et al., 2019). ADAR-based analysis infers double-stranded regions from clustered A-to-G mismatches or hyper-edited reads using RNA editing detection tools, including Hyper-editing (Porath et al., 2014), REDItools2 (Flati et al., 2020), SPRINT (Zhang et al., 2017), and JACUSA2 (Piechotta et al., 2022), but does not capture dsRNAs lacking detectable mismatch signals, including unedited or weakly edited mitochondrial dsRNAs. Antibody-based transcriptomic analysis using J2 or K1, both of which are dsRNA-specific monoclonal antibodies, identifies dsRNA-enriched regions using dsRNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (dsRIP-seq), with peak callers including MACS2 (Zhang et al., 2008) and RIPSeeker (Li et al., 2013), or J2 formaldehyde crosslinking immunoprecipitation sequencing (fCLIP-seq) with CLIPper (Lovci et al., 2013) and Piranha (Uren et al., 2012). Candidate repeat-containing regions are annotated using RepeatMasker (Smit et al., 2013) and BEDTools (Quinlan and Hall, 2010), and signals from candidate regions and repetitive or transposable elements are quantified using deepTools2 (Ramírez et al., 2016) and TEtranscripts (Jin et al., 2015) (Fig. 1B).

For RT-qPCR analysis, strand-specific assays separately quantify individual RNA strands from putative dsRNA-forming regions at candidate loci (Dhir et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2023; Sohn et al., 2026; Waast et al., 2025). Although strand-specific RT-qPCR does not directly quantify dsRNA, changes in complementary RNA strands provide information on dsRNA formation (Sohn et al., 2026). Gene-specific RT primers are used for strand-specific cDNA synthesis, followed by qPCR using primers designed across the predicted overlap or repeat-containing region (Kawakami et al., 2011, Kim et al., 2023) (Fig. 1B).

rRNA

rRNAs are highly abundant structural RNAs generated from long precursor rRNAs through sequential processing, making it important to distinguish mature rRNAs from precursor ribosomal RNA (pre-rRNA) intermediates (Hwang et al., 2025, Pastore et al., 2026) (Fig. 2A). rRNA-seq analysis assesses mature rRNA abundance and pre-rRNA processing, including changes in external transcribed spacer/internal transcribed spacer (ETS/ITS) removal and ribosome biogenesis. rRNA reads are aligned to annotated rRNA references using SortMeRNA (Kopylova et al., 2012) and riboPicker (Schmieder et al., 2012). Mature rRNA reference sequences, including 18S, 5.8S, and 28S rRNAs, are obtained from databases such as SILVA (Quast et al., 2013) or predicted from reference genomes using tools such as RNAmmer (Lagesen et al., 2007). Annotation of pre-rRNA regions, including ETS and ITS, is obtained from species-specific rDNA reference sequences from GenBank (Sayers et al., 2025) or RefSeq (O'Leary et al., 2016) and aligned using STAR (Dobin et al., 2013) and Bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). Mature-rRNA and ETS/ITS signals are quantified using featureCounts (Liao et al., 2014), BEDTools (Quinlan and Hall, 2010), and SAMtools (Li et al., 2009). rRNA modifications are assessed using RiboMeth-seq for 2′-O-methylation (Birkedal et al., 2015), HydraPsiSeq for pseudouridylation (Marchand et al., 2020), and ac4C-seq for N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) (Sas-Chen et al., 2020).

For RT-qPCR analysis, random or target-specific primers are used for RT (Weigel et al., 2021). Mature rRNAs are quantified using 18S-, 5.8S-, or 28S-targeting primers, whereas pre-rRNA intermediates are assessed using ETS- or ITS-targeting primers (Tafforeau et al., 2013, Yerlici et al., 2024). cDNA dilution and normalization strategies need optimization because of the high abundance of mature rRNAs (Weigel et al., 2021). Separate quantification of mature rRNAs and ETS/ITS-containing precursor species enables assessment of rRNA processing (Smith et al., 2026, Pastore et al., 2026) (Fig. 2A).

tRNA

tRNAs are small noncoding RNAs that mediate codon-dependent amino acid delivery during protein synthesis. Because of their small size, high sequence similarity, extensive nucleotide modifications, and processing into tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs), tRNA-seq analysis requires specialized reference annotation, read alignment, and quantification strategies (Ha et al., 2026, Ha and Lee, 2023) (Fig. 2B). Mature tRNA levels are analyzed by mapping reads to curated or predicted tRNA references, such as GtRNAdb 2.0 (Chan and Lowe, 2016) or tRNAscan-SE 2.0 annotations (Chan et al., 2021), followed by quantification with mim-tRNAseq (Behrens et al., 2021) and tRAX (Holmes et al., 2022). Cleaved tRNAs, including tRFs, are analyzed using SPORTS1.1 (Shi et al., 2018), tRAX (Holmes et al., 2022), MINTmap (Loher et al., 2017), and tDRmapper (Selitsky and Sethupathy, 2015). tRNA modifications are analyzed using RT-derived signatures, including mismatches, RT stops, and misincorporations, and tRNA-focused methods such as mim-tRNAseq (Behrens et al., 2021) and Induro-tRNAseq (Nakano et al., 2025).

For RT-qPCR analysis, mature tRNAs and tRFs require different RNA preparation and assay designs. Total RNA or tRNA-enriched fractions are used, depending on the target RNA (Clark et al., 2016; Shigematsu et al., 2025). Mature tRNAs are quantified by four-leaf clover RT-qPCR using T- and D-arm-targeting primers and a stem-loop adaptor-specific TaqMan probe from Applied Biosystems (Honda et al., 2015). For tRFs, stem-loop RT-qPCR, adaptor-ligation-based TaqMan RT-qPCR, or Dumbbell-PCR are used to improve specificity (Ha et al., 2026; Honda and Kirino, 2015; Shigematsu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024). Modification-dependent RT efficiency and assay specificity require consideration when comparing tRNA or tRF levels (Shigematsu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2015) (Fig. 2B).

CONCLUDING REMARKS

Here, we provide a brief follow-up to our previous MiniResource papers on RNA-seq and RT-qPCR (Lee et al., 2024c; Bong et al., 2024) by summarizing key methods for quantifying selected RNA species. Methods and analytical conditions vary by RNA species, with updated tools and databases guiding method selection. For RNA species other than those highlighted here, including small RNAs and long noncoding RNAs, we recommend that users consult relevant literature for RNA-specific RT-qPCR primer design and validation, as described in relevant studies (Boo et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025, Yoon et al., 2025).
