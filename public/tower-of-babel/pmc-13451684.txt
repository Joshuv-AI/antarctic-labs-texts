Guideline recommendations versus real-world management in early-stage testicular cancer: a multicenter survey of contemporary practice
Authors: Angelina Strauch, Hans Schmelz, Justine Schoch, Axel Heidenreich, Tim Nestler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Early-stage testicular cancer is highly curable; however, overtreatment may result in substantial long-term toxicity. Current guidelines aim to balance relapse risk and treatment-related morbidity, yet real-world adherence remains unclear.Objectives:To assess real-world management of clinical stage (cS) I and cSIIA testicular cancer in specialized centers and to evaluate the extent to which guideline recommendations are implemented or deviated from to avoid overtreatment.Design:Multicenter questionnaire-based cross-sectional study.Methods:A structured 17-item survey was distributed to 59 urological, medical oncology, and radiation oncology experts from the German Testicular Cancer Study Group (GTCSG) and university hospitals in Germany and Switzerland. Survey topics included staging, repeated imaging, and treatment strategies for seminoma and non-seminomatous germ cell tumors (NSGCT) in cSI and cSIIA with negative serum tumor markers (S0). Statistical analyses were performed using descriptive statistics and chi-square testing.Results:Fifty-three questionnaires (89.8%) were evaluable. Although current guidelines recommend active surveillance as the preferred management for cSI seminoma, irrespective of risk factors, 52.9% of institutions administered adjuvant therapy in patients with risk factors. For cSI NSGCT, only 11.5% always applied active surveillance, independent of risk factors, despite guideline-supported recommendations for this approach. In cSIIA seminoma, 43.4% routinely repeated staging imaging prior to treatment initiation, although this strategy is not uniformly guideline endorsed. Conversely, only 31.4% consistently repeated imaging in cSIIA NSGCT, despite explicit guideline recommendations supporting repeat imaging before treatment. Treatment decisions after repeat imaging were predominantly based on lymph node (LN) progression (⩾20% increase). Additional biomarkers such as microRNA-371 were used only in a minority of centers.Conclusion:Substantial discrepancies exist between guideline recommendations and real-world practice in early-stage testicular cancer management. While guideline adherence is essential to prevent overtreatment, repeated imaging in cSIIA seminoma may represent a reasonable de-escalation strategy and should be considered for consistent future guideline integration.Keywords: adjuvant therapy, germ cell tumor, guideline adherence, overtreatment, staging imaging, testicular cancer

Introduction

Testicular cancer is the most common malignancy in young men between 20 and 40 years and remains highly curable even in metastatic stages. 1 Despite excellent overall cure rates, testicular cancer survivors frequently experience acute treatment-related adverse effects as well as long-term toxicities associated with systemic chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery.2,3 Given the young age at diagnosis, this may translate into a reduced relative survival and impaired quality of life over decades. 4 Consequently, avoiding unnecessary treatment is of particular importance in this patient population.

At initial diagnosis, most patients present with early-stage disease: clinical stage (cS) I (non-metastasized) in 78%, cSII (retroperitoneal lymph node (LN) metastasis) in 11%, and cSIII (distant LN or organ metastasis) in 11% of cases. 5

In cSI, relapse risk has been extensively studied due to the established feasibility of active surveillance. In seminoma, histopathological risk factors include primary tumor size (⩾4 cm) and invasion of the rete testis; however, these have never been prospectively validated. 6 Without these risk factors, relapse rates range from 5% to 14%, whereas the presence of risk factors is associated with relapse rates of 18%–32%.6,7 Recent prognostic risk models have additionally identified lymphovascular invasion (LVI) as well as hilar soft tissue invasion, elevated pre-orchiectomy human chorionic gonadotropin, and lactate dehydrogenase as independent risk factors for relapse.8,9 In non-seminomatous germ cell tumors (NSGCT), LVI represents the most relevant risk factor, with relapse rates of approximately 14% in the absence of LVI (low risk) and up to 44% when LVI is present (high risk). 10 Recent studies have also described hilar soft tissue invasion, tumor size, and the proportion of embryonal carcinoma as novel risk factors associated with an increased risk of relapse. 11

Depending on the cS and associated relapse risk, different treatment strategies are recommended following orchiectomy (Table 1). There are slight but clinically relevant differences between the German S3 guideline and the European Association of Urology (EAU) guideline in this context.12,13

To avoid unnecessary morbidity related to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgery, current guidelines already incorporate several de-escalation strategies. These include active surveillance as the standard of care for cSI seminoma (independent of risk factors), for cSI NSGCT low risk and as a valid option that should be discussed with patients for cSI NSGCT high risk; as well as restaging after 6 weeks in marker-negative NSGCT patients with questionable cSIIA disease.12,13

Moreover, new prognostic risk models aim at improving clinical decision-making in patients with cSI seminoma to reduce overtreatment.8,9 In addition, recent studies on nerve-sparing retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (RPLND) or combined dose-reduced radiochemotherapy (SAKK) for cSIIA seminoma further reflect the ongoing effort to safely de-escalate treatment while maintaining excellent oncological outcomes.14–17 Compared with the German S3 guideline, the EAU, the American Urological Association, and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines have recently implemented RPLND as a primary therapeutic option for seminoma in cSIIA and cSIIB, as well as the repetition of imaging after approximately 6 weeks in marker-negative seminoma with unclear cSIIA status, similar to the restaging strategy that has long been established for marker-negative cSIIA NSGCT.12,13,18,19

Given the overall rarity of testicular cancer, many treating centers may have limited case volumes and varying levels of experience with contemporary management strategies. Previous studies have demonstrated that non-adherence to guideline-recommended management in testicular cancer is associated with inferior clinical outcomes, underlining the importance of guideline awareness and implementation in daily practice. 20

The aim of the present study was therefore to investigate real-world clinical practice in specialized testicular cancer centers in Germany and Switzerland and to assess the extent to which contemporary guideline recommendations are implemented in routine care. Specifically, we sought to assess how restrictively urologists, medical oncologists, and radiation oncologists manage testicular cancer patients in early cS. Furthermore, we aimed to identify diagnostic and therapeutic approaches that are already commonly applied in clinical routine to minimize treatment-related toxicity while accepting a clinically justifiable risk of relapse, even if these approaches have not yet been incorporated into current clinical guidelines.

Methods

The survey was distributed to 59 urological, medical oncology, and radiation oncology experts specializing in testicular cancer, primarily working at university hospitals and within the German Testicular Cancer Study Group (GTCSG), with participants from Germany and Switzerland. The survey period extended from August 2025 to November 2025.

It focused primarily on staging procedures, particularly imaging modalities, timing, and criteria for repeat imaging, as well as on the treatment of seminoma and NSGCT patients in cSI and cSIIA. Patients were assumed to have negative serum tumor markers (S0). Therefore, all cSIIA patients belonged to the good-prognosis group according to the International Germ Cell Cancer Collaborative Group classification. 21

We used a structured questionnaire comprising 17 items, including single-choice and multiple-choice questions (quantitative data) as well as free-text fields (qualitative data). The full translated questionnaire is shown in Supplemental Figure 1. Prior to distribution, the questionnaire was pilot tested among clinicians to assess comprehensibility and subsequently revised for clarity and content.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, v31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as n (%), while continuous variables were reported as the median with range.

We analyzed group comparisons for categorial variables using Pearson’s Chi-square test. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (Supplemental Figure 2). 22

Results

General characteristics

In total, 53 of 59 questionnaires (89.8%) were completed and included in the analysis. Four institutions did not respond, and two reported that they were not directly concerned with the topic.

Respondents included 42 (79.2%) urologists, 9 (17.0%) medical oncologists, and 2 (3.8%) radiation oncologists. Most institutions treat at least 10 testicular cancer patients per year (48/53; 90.6%), with 16/53 (30.2%) managing more than 30 patients annually, and therefore routinely perform staging investigations at initial diagnosis (Table 2).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is routinely used for initial staging in only 5/53 (9.4%) institutions. Most centers use MRI selectively (35/53; 66.0%), particularly in younger patients to reduce radiation exposure. Additional reasons include contraindications to computed tomography (CT) and MRI availability. Some institutions routinely perform CT of the chest combined with MRI of the abdomen for initial staging and use abdominal MRI for follow-up (Table 2). 13/53 (24.5%) clinics never use MRI in testicular cancer staging.

Staging is performed preoperatively by 19/53 (35.8%) respondents, postoperatively by 25/53 (47.2%), and both pre- and postoperatively by 7/53 (13.2%; Table 2). The main reasons for preoperative staging are elevated serum tumor markers and the avoidance of misinterpreting postoperative reactive LN. Postoperative imaging is mainly performed for logistical reasons and to await histological confirmation to avoid unnecessary imaging in benign tumors.

Treatment of cSI seminoma and NSGCT

Adjuvant treatment of cSI seminoma regardless of risk factors (tumor size >4 cm, rete testis invasion) is performed in only 1/51 (2.0%) institutions (2 of 53 institutions did not provide data). In the presence of risk factors (tumor size and/or rete testis invasion), 27/51 (52.9%) institutions routinely administer adjuvant therapy, whereas 23/51 (45.1%) manage all cSI seminoma patients with active surveillance (Figure 1).

For NSGCT in cSI, adjuvant therapy is administered regardless of risk factors (lymphovascular invasion) in 1/52 (1.9%) institutions, in the presence of risk factors in 45/52 (86.5%), and never in 6/52 (11.5%), with patients managed by active surveillance (1 of 53 institutions did not provide data; Figure 1).

Treatment of cSIIA seminoma

At initial diagnosis of cSIIA seminoma (LN < 2 cm) without elevated serum tumor markers (S0), 5/53 (9.4%) institutions initiate chemotherapy immediately without repeating staging imaging. In contrast, imaging is always repeated by 23/53 (43.4%) and selectively by 25/53 (47.2%) institutions, most commonly after an interval of 6 weeks (range 4–12 weeks; Figure 2).

The primary criterion for repeating imaging is the maximum LN diameter (22/25; 88.0%), whereas LN morphology is rarely used (2/25; 8.0%). Most respondents reported a size threshold of 19 mm (range 10–30 mm), measured in the axial or any other radiological plane. Additional reasons include postoperative reactive LN (5/25; 20.0%) as well as LN location (6/25; 24.0%) and number (3/25; 12.0%).

After repeated imaging, treatment decisions are based on maximum LN size in 25/48 (52.1%) institutions, typically initiating treatment at ⩾20 mm (range 10–30 mm). However, the majority rely on LN progression (43/48; 89.6%), with a ⩾20% increase in LN size considered clinically relevant (range 10–25%, or 2–5 mm). LN morphology (7/48; 14.6%), location (2/48; 4.2%), and number (4/48; 8.3%) play a minor role in treatment decisions.

When imaging criteria are met, preferred treatment modalities are polychemotherapy (28/60; 46.7%), radiochemotherapy according to the SAKK protocol (15/60; 25.0%, including 9/11 (81.8%) medical oncologists and radiation oncologists), RPLND (10/60; 16.7%), and radiotherapy (7/60; 11.7%). If imaging criteria are not met, imaging is mostly repeated again after ⩾12 weeks (20/43; 46.5%; range 4–12 weeks) before initiating therapy.

Additional diagnostic parameters beyond imaging and conventional serum tumor markers, such as microRNA-371, are used by 7/53 (13.2%) institutions.

Treatment of cSIIA NSGCT

At initial diagnosis of cSIIA NSGCT (LN < 2 cm, S0), 8/51 (15.7%) institutions (2 of 53 institutions did not provide data) initiate treatment without repeating imaging, predominantly polychemotherapy (7/8; 87.5%; RPLND in 1/8; 12.5%). Individual respondents additionally cited 100% teratoma in the primary tumor as a reason for immediate treatment (with RPLND).

Most institutions repeat imaging prior to treatment initiation, either always (16/51; 31.4%) or selectively (27/51; 52.9%), usually after approximately 6 weeks (range 4–6 weeks; Figure 2). The main criterion for repeating imaging is again the maximum LN diameter of 19 mm (range 10–25 mm; 20/27; 74.0%), predominantly measured in the axial plane. Additional reasons are like those reported for seminoma.

Following repeated imaging, treatment decisions are guided by maximum LN size in 23/43 (53.5%) institutions, generally initiating therapy at ⩾20 mm (range 10–25 mm). LN progression is used by 38/43 (88.4%), with a ⩾20% increase in LN size considered clinically relevant (range 10%–25%, or 2–5 mm). LN morphology (9/43; 20.9%) and location (2/43; 4.7%) are used far less frequently.

When imaging criteria are fulfilled, polychemotherapy is the preferred treatment (32/48; 66.7%), whereas 16/48 (33.3%) opt for primary RPLND. If criteria are not met, imaging is mostly repeated again after ⩾12 weeks (16/38; 31.6%; range 4–12 weeks) prior to treatment initiation.

Additional diagnostic parameters independent of imaging and standard serum tumor markers, such as microRNA-371, are used in 4/51 (7.8%) institutions.

Correlation analysis

Guideline adherence was not associated with the number of testicular cancer patients treated per institution per year. For this analysis, institutions treating ⩽30 patients annually (n = 36) were compared with institutions treating >30 patients annually (n = 16); one of 53 institutions did not provide corresponding data. No significant differences between institutions treating ⩽30 versus >30 patients per year were observed regarding guideline-concordant diagnostics and treatment for either seminoma in cSI (p = 0.63) and cSIIA (p = 0.13) or NSGCT in cSI (p = 0.28) and cSIIA (p = 0.99).

Whether the initial staging was performed pre- or postoperatively had no significant influence on the repetition of staging (for seminoma p = 0.45, for NSGCT p = 0.55).

For institutions applying a more restrictive strategy in cSI, defined as never administering adjuvant treatment, compared with institutions that always or selectively administer adjuvant therapy in cSI based on risk factors, no significant differences were observed for either seminoma or NSGCT regarding their management of cSIIA patients (p = 0.8, respectively). Specifically, repetition of staging imaging in cSIIA as a more restrictive approach was not significantly more frequent among institutions with a restrictive cSI strategy. Conversely, immediate initiation of treatment in cSIIA without repeated imaging was not performed significantly more often by institutions that routinely apply adjuvant treatment in cSI.

Discussion

Overall, the range of responses regarding diagnostic and therapeutic management of testicular cancer patients in cSI and cSIIA seminoma and NSGCT was very broad.

Notably, 27/51 (52.9%) institutions administer adjuvant treatment for cSI seminoma with risk factors, although this is not recommended by current guidelines. In addition, only 6/52 (11.5%) institutions always apply the option of active surveillance for cSI NSGCT, independent of risk factors, which is a legitimate alternative option according to current guidelines. In contrast, it is encouraging that adjuvant treatment in cSI without risk factors plays almost no role for either seminoma or NSGCT (98%, respectively).

Furthermore, in cSIIA seminoma, only 5/53 (9.4%) institutions initiate treatment immediately. The remaining 90.6% always or selectively repeat staging imaging based on specific imaging criteria and initiate treatment only when a LN threshold of >20 mm or a ⩾20% increase in LN size is reached. However, repeated imaging in marker-negative cSIIA seminoma is currently not recommended by the German S3 guideline, which may be attributable to the fact that the guideline was last revised in 2020. Yet this recommendation has been incorporated relatively recently into the international testicular cancer guidelines.12,13,18,19

For marker–negative cSIIA NSGCT, only 16/51 (31.4%) institutions always repeat imaging. Another 27/51 (52.9%) repeat imaging only when specific criteria are met, while 8/51 (15.7%) initiate treatment immediately. According to current guidelines, however, repeat imaging after approximately 6 weeks is recommended for all such patients, as initial LN findings may be false positive.12,13,18,19

Thus, according to the present survey, management at most institutions is not guideline-concordant and is also internally inconsistent. While treatment of seminoma and NSGCT in cSI as well as NSGCT in cSIIA should be considerably more restrictive according to all international and national testicular cancer guidelines, seminoma in cSIIA is managed more conservatively by most institutions.

Non-adherence to guideline recommendations in testicular cancer management has already been investigated in several studies. Thibault et al. (2014) demonstrated that non-adherence to guidelines in patients with advanced germ cell tumors was associated with inferior clinical outcomes and depended on the treating institution. 20 This may be attributable to the rarity of testicular cancer, resulting in substantial variability in institutional experience. Consequently, the authors concluded that treatment centralization in specialized, experienced centers is warranted. 20 However, in the present study, no significant difference in guideline adherence was observed between institutions treating a higher number of patients per year, and therefore presumably having greater experience in testicular cancer management, and institutions treating fewer patients annually.

A U.S. analysis reported a substantial proportion of inappropriate staging imaging (approximately 44%) as well as overtreatment of testicular cancer patients (approximately 40%) because of guideline non-adherence. 23 A nationwide German survey conducted in 2018 on staging, treatment, and follow-up of testicular cancer patients (n = 411, response rate 45%) found that primary staging diagnostics were largely guideline-concordant; however, relevant discrepancies were observed particularly during follow-up. 24 A small French study likewise demonstrated heterogeneous and largely non-guideline-concordant follow-up strategies during active surveillance of cSI seminoma and NSGCT patients. 25 The study further highlighted that international and national testicular cancer guidelines differ in their recommendations, thereby complicating a standardized approach in routine clinical practice. 25

Another issue that is inconsistently addressed across international and national guidelines are the differences in radiological LN measurement planes.12,13,26,27 Correspondingly, the present survey also demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in clinical practice regarding LN assessment, with many clinicians lacking a standardized approach. As this variability may contribute to divergent treatment decisions and potential overtreatment, 27 clearer and harmonized guideline recommendations appear warranted. Despite the frequent use of non-standardized measurements in routine practice, most respondents-based treatment initiation on ⩾20% LN growth, consistent with RECIST 1.1 criteria, suggesting that a standardized RECIST-based approach could be feasibly implemented in clinical routine.27,28

In the present survey, we also assessed the frequency of MRI use for initial staging due to radiation exposure from CT imaging and its association with secondary malignancies. Despite evidence suggesting comparable diagnostic accuracy to CT,29–31 current German S3 and EAU guidelines continue to recommend CT as the standard imaging modality for initial staging, while reserving MRI mainly for selected cases or contraindications.12,13 Similar to previous German surveys, 24 in our study MRI was only rarely used routinely for initial staging. Nevertheless, partial, patient-dependent MRI use increased from 50% in 2020 24 to 66% in the present survey, suggesting at least a modest upward trend.

MicroRNA-371 represents a novel, promising biomarker with clear potential for diagnostic and therapeutic de-escalation in testicular cancer patients.32–34 However, it has not yet been incorporated into clinical guidelines due to the need for further validation. Consistent with the current evidence base and limited availability, microRNA-371 was used in only a small number of centers (n = 7) in the present survey.

Regarding treatment of cSIIA seminoma, guideline-concordant management was reported by 46.7% of institutions using polychemotherapy and by 11.7% using radiotherapy, two treatment modalities with comparable oncological outcomes, with a slight preference for chemotherapy. 3 However, 25% of respondents (including 15% medical oncologists and radiation oncologists) already routinely apply combined dose-reduced radiochemotherapy according to the current SAKK protocol, or primary RPLND (16.7%). While these approaches are mentioned in current guidelines, they should only be applied in specialized centers. Nevertheless, they appear to represent safe treatment strategies and may be interpreted as a positive step toward reducing overtreatment.14–17 For NSGCT, treatment was guideline-concordant, with polychemotherapy used in 66.7% and primary RPLND in 33.3% of institutions.

Overall, strict adherence to guideline recommendations remains essential to minimize morbidity related to unnecessary radiation exposure and overtreatment in patients with cSI and cSIIA testicular cancer. At the same time, particularly for seminoma with its generally favorable prognosis, applying the same strategy recommended for NSGCT in cSIIA–namely repeated staging imaging–appears to be a reasonable and clinically legitimate option and may warrant consistent consideration in all future guideline updates. The reason why repeated imaging had long been recommended only for NSGCT and is therefore, despite recent adaptations, still described more explicitly for NSGCT than for seminoma in current guidelines may historically originate from the scenario of pure teratoma in NSGCT, which cannot be cured by chemotherapy.

A limitation of the present study is the relatively small cohort (n = 53). However, all respondents were recognized experts in testicular cancer care, with one representative per institution, supporting the validity and relevance of the findings. Nevertheless, it should be considered that the survey was not conducted anonymously. Therefore, it cannot be excluded that some respondents may have provided answers more closely aligned with guideline recommendations than their actual clinical practice, implying that real-world guideline adherence may in fact be even lower than reflected in the present data.

Conclusion

Overall, this survey demonstrates marked heterogeneity in the clinical management of early-stage testicular cancer among specialized centers. While the guideline-recommended restrictive approach for patients with cSI seminoma, cSI NSGCT, and cSIIA NSGCT is largely not implemented in routine practice, cSIIA seminoma is managed more conservatively in everyday clinical care than currently recommended, at least according to the German S3 guideline. To avoid unnecessary diagnostic- and treatment-related morbidity, adherence to restrictive guideline recommendations is strongly warranted. Further prospective studies are needed to evaluate whether de-escalated management strategies and repeat imaging for cSIIA seminoma can be safely implemented and incorporated into future guideline updates in the best interest of patients.
