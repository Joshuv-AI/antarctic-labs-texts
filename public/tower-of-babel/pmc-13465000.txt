Perioperative complications of laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy versus sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy for pelvic organ prolapse
Authors: Shada Alanazi, Najd Alshammari, Arwa Jrais, Nawal Alharbi, Mai Alsaadoon, Yaseer Alanazi, Jawaher Alsahabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPelvic organ prolapse (POP) affects 23.4% of women in Saudi Arabia. Laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy is the established surgical gold standard; however, whether concomitant hysterectomy confers additional perioperative risk remains debated.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 179 women who underwent laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy (n = 119) or laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy (n = 60) at two tertiary referral centers in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, between 2009 and 2024. Intraoperative and postoperative complications, perioperative hemoglobin change, and hospital stay duration were compared between groups.ResultsThe sacrocolpopexy group was significantly older (P = 0.004). Organ injury occurred in 0.6% of cases overall. Blood transfusion was required exclusively in the sacrohysteropexy group (3.3% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.045). Both groups demonstrated a statistically significant postoperative hemoglobin decline — sacrocolpopexy: 124.52 ± 15.07 to 110.60 ± 13.77 g/L; sacrohysteropexy: 125.27 ± 13.20 to 110.29 ± 12.18 g/L (P < 0.001) — with no significant between-group difference (P = 0.747) and neither decline reaching clinical significance. Sacrohysteropexy was associated with shorter hospitalization (44.6% vs. 24.6% discharged within two postoperative days, P = 0.012). Reoperation rates and symptom resolution were comparable between groups.ConclusionsNo significant between-group differences in perioperative morbidity were detected. Sacrohysteropexy was associated with shorter hospital stay; however, the clinical relevance of the higher transfusion rate is uncertain, given the small event count. Given baseline group non-comparability and the retrospective design, these results should be regarded as exploratory. The surgical approach should be individualized based on patient characteristics and the surgeon’s expertise. Prospective randomized trials are warranted.

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) constitutes a significant public health issue. Although estimates indicate that up to 50% of women may develop POP during their lifetime, precise prevalence data are difficult to establish, as not all affected women seek clinical evaluation [1]. Advancing age and high parity are recognized as the principal global risk factors [2–6]. In Saudi Arabia, a recent nationwide survey found that 23.4% of women report symptoms of POP [7].

Laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy is considered the gold standard for the surgical management of POP and is frequently performed in conjunction with hysterectomy [8]. The necessity of hysterectomy during prolapse repair remains debated. Hysterectomy is more clearly indicated in cases of cervical dysplasia, abnormal uterine bleeding, significant fibroids, or other uterine pathology [9, 10]. Recently, uterine-preserving surgery has gained popularity, with up to 36% of patients preferring this approach if outcomes are comparable [11]. However, uterine preservation carries a small risk of future malignancy and may require additional interventions if abnormal bleeding occurs [12].

When hysterectomy is performed during prolapse repair, its primary purpose is to facilitate access to tissues necessary for apical suspension. However, this approach increases operative morbidity, cost, and procedure duration [13–17]. A 2021 systematic review reported lower rates of visceral injury and shorter operative times with uterine-preserving procedures, with no significant difference in intraoperative blood loss [12]. Oliveira et al. found no significant difference in prolapse recurrence between approaches, although reoperation rates were lower following hysterectomy-based repair [18]. Another systematic review concluded that uterine preservation reduces mesh exposure, operative time, blood loss, and surgical costs without increasing recurrence rates. However, that analysis included open and total hysterectomy cases, which are recognized risk factors for mesh exposure [8, 17]. More recently, Sato et al. reported comparable outcomes between laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with supracervical hysterectomy and laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with uterine preservation [19].

The decision to perform hysterectomy during prolapse repair is complex and involves considerations of surgical efficacy, perioperative morbidity, patient autonomy, management of concurrent uterine pathology, and cost [17]. Comparative data specifically evaluating perioperative outcomes of laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy versus laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy are limited. To our knowledge, no comparative perioperative data from the Middle East have been published for these two procedures, and this study represents one of the largest cohorts comparing them using a unified surgical technique in the region.

Objectives

This study aims to compare intraoperative and postoperative complications, as well as perioperative blood loss objectively measured by hemoglobin change, between laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy and laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy in women undergoing surgery for POP.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective cohort study.

Study setting

This study was conducted at the urogynecology departments of two tertiary referral centers in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia: King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC) and King Fahad Medical City (KFMC). All surgical cases were performed by two experienced urogynecology surgeons. Patients were predominantly referred from central and peripheral regions of Saudi Arabia.

Study participants

We included all patients who underwent laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy (n = 119) or laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy (n = 60) at either institution between 2009 and 2024, provided they had a documented preoperative hemoglobin level and at least one postoperative follow-up visit. Patients who underwent laparotomy, robotic surgery, total hysterectomy, or other pelvic organ prolapse repair procedures were excluded.

Surgical technique

All cases were performed using the same surgical technique, sutures, and mesh materials as previously described by our research team [20]. The decision between sacrohysteropexy and sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy was guided by a combination of clinical, anatomical, and patient-preference-related factors. Hysterectomy was generally recommended for patients older than 45 years, particularly in the presence of uterine pathology such as abnormal uterine bleeding, symptomatic fibroids, or where menopausal status and concern for future uterine surveillance were relevant considerations. Younger patients and those wishing to preserve their uterus for personal or fertility-related reasons were offered sacrohysteropexy, provided no uterine pathology contraindicated this approach. Patient autonomy was explicitly documented in all cases. This study included only cases in which subtotal hysterectomy was performed.

Data collection methods

Data were retrieved from electronic medical records and included patient characteristics (age, parity, weight, height, and medical and surgical history), surgical characteristics (procedure type and concomitant procedures), and outcome data (intraoperative and postoperative complications throughout the follow-up period). Hemoglobin measurements obtained before and on the first postoperative day were recorded as the primary objective measure of blood loss. All patients had documented postoperative examinations assessing for prolapse recurrence and mesh exposure. Patients without follow-up data were excluded.

Data analysis

Data entry was performed in Microsoft Excel, followed by analysis using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as median and interquartile range (IQR) otherwise. Within-group comparisons of preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin and hematocrit values utilized the paired t-test for hemoglobin (if normally distributed) and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for hematocrit. Between-group comparisons of mean hemoglobin drop were conducted using the independent samples t-test. Hospital stay was defined as the number of postoperative nights in the hospital beginning from the day of surgery. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Clavien-Dindo system [21]: Grade I–II for minor complications managed without surgical intervention, Grade IIIa–IIIb for those requiring surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention without or with general anesthesia, respectively, Grade IV for life-threatening complications, and Grade V for death. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Analyses in cases of missing data were performed using only available information.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Ministry of National Guard-Health Affairs (MNG-HA) under study number NRC22R/371/08 and by the IRB of King Fahad Medical City (KFMC) under study number IRB00010471. Informed consent was waived by both IRBs due to the study’s retrospective nature. Data confidentiality was maintained in accordance with institutional guidelines and relevant regulations.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

Between 2009 and 2024, 179 patients were included in this study. Patient characteristics and overall surgical outcomes are summarized in Table 1. The majority of patients (138, 77.1%) were aged 55 years or younger, while 41 (22.9%) were aged 55 years or older. The follow-up period ranged from 6 weeks to 12 years, with a median duration from surgery to last follow-up of 1.7 years. Obesity was prevalent in 56.3% of the cohort (99 patients). Common comorbidities included bronchial asthma (14.6%), diabetes mellitus (14.1%), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (4.5%). A minority of patients (5.6%) had a prior cesarean delivery.

Laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy was performed in 119 cases (66.5%) and laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy in 60 cases (33.5%). Anterior mesh placement was used in 85.6% of patients, while 14.4% received both anterior and posterior mesh. Posterior vaginal repair was performed in 73.2% of cases and anterior repair in 5.0%.

Intraoperative complications occurred in one patient (0.6%). Organ injuries were rare, with one bladder injury (0.6%) and one gastrointestinal injury (0.6%) each occurring in separate patients. Blood transfusion was required in two cases (1.1%). Among the 153 patients with available hemoglobin data, 77.1% experienced a hemoglobin drop of 20 g/L or less, while 22.9% had a drop exceeding 20 g/L. Reoperation for prolapse recurrence occurred in 1.7% of patients, and mesh removal was required in 1.1%. The majority of patients (117, 68.4%) remained hospitalized for more than two postoperative days.

A significantly higher proportion of patients aged 55 years or older underwent sacrocolpopexy than sacrohysteropexy (29.4% vs. 10.0%, P = 0.004). Mesh placement patterns differed significantly. Anterior mesh was more frequently used in sacrohysteropexy (96.7% vs. 80.7%, P = 0.004), while posterior repair was also more prevalent in the sacrohysteropexy group (83.1% vs. 68.1%, P = 0.034).

Intraoperative complications were uncommon in both groups. One intraoperative complication occurred in the sacrocolpopexy group (0.8%) and none in the sacrohysteropexy group (P = 0.476). Organ injuries — one bladder injury and one gastrointestinal injury — both occurred in the sacrohysteropexy group (1.7% each, P = 0.135). Blood transfusion was required in two sacrohysteropexy patients and none in the sacrocolpopexy group (3.3% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.045). Mean estimated blood loss did not differ significantly between groups (sacrocolpopexy: 268.80 ± 139.53 mL vs. sacrohysteropexy: 239.83 ± 130.26 mL, P = 0.355).

Sacrohysteropexy was associated with a significantly shorter hospital stay, with 44.6% of patients discharged within 2 postoperative days compared with 24.6% in the sacrocolpopexy group (P = 0.008). Reoperation rates for prolapse recurrence, bowel complications, urinary issues, and mesh removal were not significantly different between groups. Postoperative symptom resolution was excellent and comparable between groups, with no significant difference in residual POP symptoms (P = 0.958).

Hemoglobin and hematocrit analysis

Preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin and hematocrit values are presented in Table 3. Within-group comparisons demonstrated statistically significant decreases in hemoglobin following surgery in both groups (sacrocolpopexy: 124.52 ± 15.07 to 110.60 ± 13.77 g/L; sacrohysteropexy: 125.27 ± 13.20 to 110.29 ± 12.18 g/L; P < 0.001 for both). The mean hemoglobin drop was 14.58 ± 7.32 g/L in the sacrocolpopexy group and 15.05 ± 8.99 g/L in the sacrohysteropexy group; the between-group difference was not significant (P = 0.747). Neither decline met the threshold for clinical significance. Hematocrit changes were not statistically significant within either group (sacrocolpopexy P = 0.623; sacrohysteropexy P = 0.577), and the 95% confidence intervals for both hemoglobin and hematocrit showed overlapping ranges between groups.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Table 4 presents the results of multivariate logistic regression analysis, with procedure type (sacrohysteropexy vs. sacrocolpopexy) as the dependent variable, and age, mesh type, posterior repair, blood transfusion, and hospital stay entered simultaneously as covariates. Younger age (≤ 55 years) was independently associated with sacrohysteropexy (OR 3.75, 95% CI: 1.478–9.515, P = 0.002). Anterior mesh placement was nearly seven times more likely in sacrohysteropexy cases (OR 6.948, 95% CI: 1.58–30.56, P = 0.004). Posterior repair was significantly less common in the sacrocolpopexy group (OR 0.426, 95% CI: 0.195–0.931, P = 0.030). Sacrohysteropexy was independently associated with shorter hospital stay, with patients more than twice as likely to be discharged within two postoperative days (OR 2.321, 95% CI: 1.193–4.516, P = 0.012). Due to complete separation resulting from zero transfusion events in the sacrocolpopexy group, an odds ratio for blood transfusion could not be calculated; this variable is therefore reported descriptively (P = 0.045, Fisher’s exact test).

Discussion

In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of POP is relatively high, with 23.4% of Saudi women experiencing symptoms [7, 22]. Laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy is considered the gold standard for the treatment of POP [8]. An increasing proportion of women express a preference for uterine preservation; a survey of patient preferences for POP surgery found that up to 36% of respondents favored uterine preservation if outcomes were equivalent [11]. Hafedh et al. attributed the relatively low uptake of uterine-preserving procedures in the region to reduced patient willingness, limited public awareness, and potentially restricted national surgical proficiency [23].

This retrospective cohort study examines the experience of two tertiary referral centers in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, involving 179 patients who underwent either laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy or laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy between 2009 and 2024, with follow-up periods ranging from 0.1 to 12 years. A previous publication reported comparable subjective and objective efficacy between these two groups [20]. However, few studies have compared laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy and laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with hysterectomy, and none are randomized controlled trials. A review of laparoscopic pelvic organ prolapse surgeries found no significant differences in outcomes between treatment groups [24]. In contrast, a recent meta-analysis suggested that sacrocolpopexy with hysterectomy may yield better outcomes than uterine preservation, possibly because suboptimal mesh placement is associated with laparoscopic supracervical hysterectomy [25].

Age distribution

A significantly higher proportion of patients older than 55 years underwent sacrocolpopexy (29.4% vs. 10.0%, P = 0.004), which may reflect surgeon decision-making and patient preferences for more conservative treatment, as documented in recent prospective studies examining patient choice in prolapse surgery [26]. Tius et al. [25] report that the decision to preserve or remove the uterus is primarily influenced by the perceived risks and benefits of each approach. In contrast, Sato et al. [19] observe that older patients with significant comorbidities may benefit from uterine preservation due to shorter operative time, although this must be weighed against the risk of malignancy and the need for ongoing uterine surveillance.

Sacrohysteropexy versus sacrocolpopexy

Sacrocolpopexy was performed in 119 cases (66.5%) and sacrohysteropexy in 60 cases (33.5%), reflecting the predominance of hysterectomy-based repair in our cohort. A recognized clinical advantage of uterine-sparing procedures is the reduced risk of mesh erosion [12, 17, 25]. In our experience, when subtotal hysterectomy is performed, the cervix is preserved, potentially combining the benefits of hysterectomy with preservation of the pericervical fascial ring anatomy, which may partly explain the favorable complication profile observed across both groups [27].

Hospital stay

Sacrohysteropexy was associated with significantly shorter hospitalization, with 44.6% of patients discharged within two postoperative days compared to 24.6% in the sacrocolpopexy group (OR 2.321, 95% CI: 1.193–4.516, P = 0.012), consistent with findings reported by Porcari et al. and Tius et al. [24, 25]. Gracia et al. [27], however, found no significant difference between groups. The variability reported in the literature, with lengths of stay ranging from two to five days, likely reflects institutional discharge practices rather than procedure-specific morbidity. In our cohort, the higher proportion of older patients in the sacrocolpopexy group may have contributed to prolonged hospitalization through greater comorbidity burden or surgeons’ concerns regarding early discharge.

Mesh exposure and complications

The majority of patients (85.6%) received anterior mesh placement, whereas 14.4% underwent both anterior and posterior mesh placement. The observed reduction in routine posterior mesh placement over the study period reflects increased awareness of the association between posterior compartment procedures and defecatory dysfunction [28]. Reoperation for mesh excision was required in one case in each group, corresponding to 0.8% for sacrocolpopexy and 1.7% for sacrohysteropexy, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.622). One sacrocolpopexy patient also underwent reoperation for abdominal mesh excision at their own request. These findings contrast with previous observational studies that reported higher mesh exposure rates when total hysterectomy or vaginal mesh procedures were included [15–24, 29].

These findings are consistent with a recent meta-analysis that demonstrated no statistically significant difference between groups in mesh extrusion or exposure [25]. This result aligns with the established advantage of subtotal hysterectomy in reducing mesh contact with vaginal bacteria and preserving blood supply to the vaginal cuff tissue [25]. Further support for this mechanism is provided by a randomized controlled trial by Illiano et al. [30], which reported a 10% mesh exposure rate in the total hysterectomy group, compared with none in the subtotal hysterectomy group.

Recent data on natural orifice approaches to sacrocolpopexy are relevant in this context. Lu et al. reported mesh exposure in 3 of 55 women (5.5%) and a recurrence rate of 5.5% following vNOTES sacrocolpopexy at a median follow-up of 24 to 46 months [31]. Liu et al. observed similar outcomes in a pilot series of 26 cases [32]. Bulutlar et al. described sacrohysteropexy via vNOTES in eight cases, noting significant improvements in POP-Q scores and no intraoperative or 30-day postoperative complications, with all patients discharged on postoperative day two [33]. Another comparative feasibility study by Bulutlar et al. demonstrated that V-NOTES sacrocolpopexy achieves outcomes comparable to laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy, with significantly shorter operative time and superior cosmetic results, supporting its potential as a safe, minimally invasive alternative [34]. Although these results are promising, larger prospective comparative studies are necessary before widespread adoption can be recommended.

Blood loss, hemoglobin, and blood transfusion

Intraoperative complications related to blood loss were uncommon in both groups. Mean estimated blood loss did not differ significantly between groups (sacrocolpopexy: 268.80 ± 139.53 mL vs. sacrohysteropexy: 239.83 ± 130.26 mL, P = 0.355). Blood transfusion was required in two sacrohysteropexy patients and none in the sacrocolpopexy group (3.3% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.045). In the first case, intraoperative transfusion was administered due to a preoperative hemoglobin of 93 g/L with an estimated blood loss of 200 mL. In the second case, blood loss of 500 mL resulted in a hemoglobin decline from 114 to 94 g/L, prompting transfusion to support postoperative recovery. With only two transfusion events, however, the clinical significance of this difference is uncertain and cannot be attributed to a procedural effect based on this retrospective dataset alone.

Both groups demonstrated statistically significant within-group decreases in hemoglobin following surgery (P < 0.001), with mean drops of 14.58 ± 7.32 g/L and 15.05 ± 8.99 g/L in the sacrocolpopexy and sacrohysteropexy groups, respectively. The between-group difference in mean hemoglobin drop was not significant (P = 0.747). The threshold for clinical significance was based on Chamsy et al. [35], who reported a mean symptomatic hemoglobin decrease of 26.6 g/L among laparoscopic hysterectomy patients; the declines observed in both groups of our cohort were substantially smaller and were therefore considered clinically insignificant. Hematocrit changes were not statistically significant in either group. These findings are consistent with those of Sato et al. [19] and align with meta-analytic data suggesting that increased blood loss associated with hysterectomy-based repair is primarily attributable to total rather than subtotal hysterectomy [17, 19, 25].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include an extensive follow-up period of up to 12 years, a relatively large cohort drawn from two major tertiary centers, and the exclusive use of a unified laparoscopic technique with subtotal hysterectomy — in contrast to prior studies that combined multiple techniques or included total hysterectomy cases. The objective assessment of blood loss through perioperative hemoglobin and hematocrit measurement provides a quantifiable metric that complements conventional subjective estimates.

This study has several important limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as both patients and surgeons participated in the decision to choose the procedure, reflected in significant baseline differences in age distribution and mesh configuration between groups. Data collected from medical records are inherently subject to ascertainment bias; patients without symptoms or complications may have been less likely to attend follow-up visits, potentially underestimating true complication rates, while those with complications may have been more likely to return, influencing long-term outcome assessments. The two groups were therefore not selected under equivalent clinical conditions, and accordingly these findings should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive. The study spans 15 years, during which surgeon experience, discharge practices, mesh materials, and patient selection criteria may have evolved, introducing temporal heterogeneity that could not be formally accounted for in a time-stratified analysis due to insufficient case numbers within individual strata. Several clinically relevant variables were unavailable for analysis, including menopausal status, smoking status, estrogen use, uterine size and pathology, all representing potential sources of residual confounding. Finally, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the involvement of only two centers and two surgeons.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort, no significant differences in perioperative morbidity were detected between laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy and laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy, with both procedures demonstrating low complication rates. Sacrohysteropexy was associated with shorter hospital stay; however, the clinical relevance of the higher transfusion rate remains uncertain, given the small event count. Given the inherent selection bias, baseline group non-comparability, and retrospective design, these findings should be regarded as exploratory. The choice between surgical approaches should be individualized based on patient preferences, surgeon expertise, and specific clinical circumstances. Future prospective randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, focusing specifically on subtotal hysterectomy, are needed to provide higher-level evidence for clinical decision-making.
