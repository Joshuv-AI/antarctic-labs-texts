Microscopic theory of spin friction and dissipative spin dynamics
Nicolas Lenzing, David Krüger, Michael Potthoff

Abstract
The real-time dynamics of local magnetic moments exchange coupled to a metallic system of conduction electrons is subject to dissipative friction even in the absence of spin-orbit coupling. Phenomenologically, this is usually described by a local Gilbert damping constant. Here, we use both linear response theory and adiabatic response theory to derive the spin friction microscopically for a generic single-band tight-binding model of the electronic structure. The resulting Gilbert damping is time-dependent and nonlocal. For a one-dimensional model, we compare the emergent relaxation dynamics as obtained from LRT and ART against each other and against the full solution of the microscopic equations of motion and demonstrate the importance of nonlocality, while the time dependence turns out to be irrelevant. In two dimensions and for a few magnetic moments in different geometries, it is found that the inclusion of nonlocal Gilbert damping can counterintuitively lead to longer relaxation times. Besides the distance dependence, the directional dependence of the nonlocal Gilbert damping turns out as very important. Our results are based on an expression relating the nonlocal Gilbert damping to the nonlocal tight-binding density of states close to the Fermi energy. This is exact in case of noninteracting electrons. Effects due to electronic correlations are studied within the random-phase approximation. For the Hubbard model at half filling and with increasing interaction strength, we find a strong enhancement of the nonlocality of spin friction.

Introduction

The understanding of the relaxation dynamics of local magnetic moments on an atomistic level represents an essential step for further progress in the field of nanospintronics

A prototypical model within atomistic spin-dynamics theory

Its Hamiltonian has the form

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{el}}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}}

{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}

i_{m}

In its most simple form, assuming a single spin-degenerate orbital per site,

\hat{H}=\sum_{\langle ii^{\prime}\rangle}\sum_{\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}T_{ii^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}c_{i^{\prime}\sigma}+J\sum_{m}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\boldsymbol{S}_{m}\>.

(1)

The time dependence of the electron and the classical-spin degrees of freedom follows the general rules for quantum-classical dynamics

As the model is quadratic in the electron annihilators and creators,

The dynamics of the latter is governed by classical Landau-Lifschitz equations

\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m}=\partial\langle\hat{H}\rangle/\partial{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}

Here, we are interested in the relaxation dynamics of systems with a few impurity spins,
M=1,...,10
, for the single-orbital model Eq. (
1

The computational effort for solving the related coupled system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations roughly scales quadratically in the number of lattice sites

and linearly in the propagation time

To avoid unwanted reflections (or interferences) of propagating excitations from the system boundaries (or due to periodic boundary conditions), however, systems with a linear extension

The Gaussian electronic part can be formally integrated out within a path-integral formalism but at the cost of a highly time-nonlocal effective action for the classical spins, which, if treated exactly, does not improve the scaling.

For 1D systems, absorbing boundary conditions, e.g., using a generalized Lindblad master-equation approach to couple the edge sites of the conduction-electron tight-binding model to an external bath, have turned out as helpful

13

It has been demonstrated that this allows to exceed the characteristic femtosecond electronic scale set by the inverse nearest-neighbor hopping by more than five orders of magnitude.
For

Even at thermal equilibrium and using advanced Monte-Carlo techniques, quantum-classical hybrid systems with a linear extension

On the other hand, for physically relevant applications, the classical-spin dynamics takes place on a characteristic time scale

) electron dynamics set by the nearest-neighbor hopping

This situation has motivated theoretical efforts to approximately “integrate out” the fast electron degrees of freedom, using the interrelated assumptions of weak

\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m}=\sum_{m^{\prime}}J_{mm^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}+\sum_{m^{\prime}}\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}\times\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m^{\prime}}\>.

In the quite common continuum (as opposed to the discrete tight-binding) approach, the nonlocality of the Gilbert damping is typically accounted for by additional gradient terms

The LLG equation (

) is re-derived for the model given by Eq. (

This and the impact on the long-time dynamics and the relaxation time is studied for the

II
Full spin dynamics

Starting from the Hamiltonian, Eq. (
1
), the exact equations of motion for the classical spins
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
, with
m=1,...,M
and
|{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}|=1
, and of the one-particle reduced density matrix
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(t)
with elements

\rho_{i\sigma,i^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}(t)=\langle\Psi(t)|c_{i^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}c_{i\sigma}|\Psi(t)\rangle\>,

(3)

are readily derived (see, e.g., Ref.
11
).
We find

\frac{d}{dt}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)=J\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle_{t}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)-\sum_{m}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{m}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)\>,

(4)

where
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle_{t}
is the expectation value of the local spin at site
i_{m}
of the electron system in the
N
-electron state
|\Psi(t)\rangle
.
If
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
denotes for the vector of Pauli matrices, we have
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c_{i\sigma^{\prime}}
.
The last term on the right-hand side results from local magnetic fields
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{m}
coupling to the impurity spins
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
, i.e., we have replaced the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) by
\hat{H}\mapsto\hat{H}-\sum_{m}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{m}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
.
This will be convenient in the following.
The equation of motion for the density matrix is given by

i\frac{d}{dt}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(t)=[{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\rm(eff)}(t),{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(t)]\>,

(5)

where
{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\rm(eff)}(t)
is an effective hopping matrix with elements

T_{i\sigma,i^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}^{\rm(eff)}(t)=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}T_{ii^{\prime}}+\frac{J}{2}\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{ii_{m}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)\>.

(6)

Initially, at time
t=0
, we specify a certain start configuration
{\boldsymbol{S}}(0)=({\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}(0),...,{\boldsymbol{S}}_{M}(0))
for the impurity spins.
Furthermore, we assume that the electron system, at time
t=0
, is in its ground state for
J=0
.
The corresponding ground-state one-particle reduced density matrix

{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(0)=\Theta(\mu{\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{T}})\>,

(7)

is formally given in terms of the Heavyside step function
\Theta(\cdots)
and can be computed by diagonalizing the hopping matrix
{\boldsymbol{T}}
.

In the multi-impurity-spin case (
M>1
), real-time dynamics is initiated by suddenly switching on
\hat{H}_{\rm int}
.
For
M=1
, we suddenly switch the direction of a local magnetic field
{\boldsymbol{B}}
that couples to the impurity spin
{\boldsymbol{S}}
.

Equations (
4
) and (
5
) form a closed set of nonlinear ordinary differential equations for the spin configuration
{\boldsymbol{S}}=({\boldsymbol{S}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{S}}_{M})
and
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
.
This can be solved numerically by standard techniques for a
D
-dimensional lattice with a finite number of sites
L=l^{D}
.
Accessible propagation times
\tau
are limited by the requirement
\tau\lesssim l/v
, where
v
is the (ballistic) propagation speed.

III
Linear response theory

The effective spin-only dynamics, determined by the LLG equation (
2
), is obtained in the limit of weak
J
and short retardation times.
In the linear response approach, we start by treating
J
perturbatively.
The corresponding Kubo formula reads as:

\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle_{t}=J\sum_{m}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau){\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}(t-\tau)+{\cal O}(J^{2})\>.

(8)

The integral kernel is given in terms of the unperturbed (
J=0
), retarded, nonlocal, and time-homogenous magnetic susceptibility

\chi^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=-i\Theta(t)e^{-\eta t}\left\langle[s^{\alpha}_{i_{m}}(t),s^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}(0)]\right\rangle^{(0)}\>.

(9)

Here,
\langle\cdots\rangle^{(0)}
denotes the expectation value with respect to the unperturbed system at
J=0
.
Furthermore,
\eta>0
is an infinitesimal, and
\alpha=x,y,z
.
Since
\hat{H}_{\rm el}
is invariant under SU(2) spin rotations, the susceptibility is diagonal with respect to the directional indices and also
\alpha
independent:
\chi^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\delta^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
.

As a second approximation, we assume that the retardation time
\tau
in Eq. (
8
) is small, i.e., that the integral kernel
\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau)
is peaked at small
\tau
, on the characteristic time scale
1/T
of the electron system, as compared to the much slower time scale on which the classical spins evolve.
This justifies a Taylor expansion
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}(t-\tau)={\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)-\tau\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)+\cdots
.
Truncating the expansion after the first order and inserting into Eq. (
8
) yields:

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\sum_{m^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau)\right){\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)

(10)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle J\sum_{m^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\tau\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau)\right)\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)\>.

With Eq. (
4
) we then get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m^{\prime}}J_{mm^{\prime}}(t){\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)

(11)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{m^{\prime}}\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{m}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{m}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)\>,

where we have defined the time-dependent RKKY exchange interaction

J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=J^{2}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau)\>,

(12)

and the time-dependent Gilbert damping

\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=-J^{2}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\tau\,\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau)\>.

(13)

Equation (
11
) should be compared with the LLG equation Eq. (
2
).
Apart from the additional magnetic-field term, the main difference is the time dependence of the coupling constants.

The time-independent RKKY interaction, defined as
J_{mm^{\prime}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
, corresponds to an effective RKKY Hamiltonian
H_{\rm RKKY}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{mm^{\prime}}^{m\neq m^{\prime}}J_{mm^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}
.
We furthermore define the time-independent Gilbert damping:
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
.
The sign in the definition (
13
) of the damping matrix is chosen such that
\alpha_{mm}>0
.
(This is opposite to the convention used in Refs.
11
,
49
).
Through Fourier transformation of the susceptibility,
\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\omega)=\int d\tau\,e^{i\omega\tau}\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau)
, we get the alternative representation

\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}=iJ^{2}\frac{d}{d\omega}\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\omega=0)=-J^{2}\frac{d}{d\omega}\mbox{Im}\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\omega=0)\>.

(14)

Most convenient for the numerical simulations, however, is the representation of the damping matrix,

\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}=\frac{\pi}{2}J^{2}A_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}}}(\omega=0)^{2}\>,

(15)

in terms of the local or nonlocal tight-binding density of states
A_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)
.
In the context of a single impurity spin and local Gilbert damping (
m=m^{\prime}
), a derivation of Eq. (
15
) is given in Refs.
[
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
]
.
A general derivation is given in the Appendix
A
, see also Ref.
[
55
,
18
,
11
,
56
]
.

Assuming a translation-invariant system with lattice vectors
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}
and periodic boundary conditions, we have

A_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i^{\prime}})}\delta(\omega+\mu-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}))\>,

(16)

where
\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})=\varepsilon(-{\boldsymbol{k}})
is the tight-binding dispersion and
\mu
the chemical potential, which fixes the average particle number
\langle N\rangle
.

According to the derivation given in the Appendix
A
, the temperature dependence of the damping parameters can be obtained via

\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}=-\frac{\pi}{2}J^{2}\int dx\,f^{\prime}(x)A_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}}}(x)^{2}\>.

(17)

In the low-temperature (
\beta\to\infty
) or in the wide-band limit, we can replace the one-electron spectral density by a constant,
A_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}}}(x)\to\rho_{mm^{\prime}}
.
This yields:

\alpha^{(\infty)}_{mm^{\prime}}=\frac{\pi}{2}J^{2}\rho_{mm^{\prime}}^{2}\>.

(18)

Hence, a nontrivial temperature dependence at low temperatures is due to the variation of the spectral density near
\omega=0
.
We can make use of the Sommerfeld expansion in powers of
\beta^{-2}
to make this explicit.
A straightforward calculation yields:

\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}=\alpha^{(\infty)}_{mm^{\prime}}+\frac{\pi^{3}}{6}\frac{J^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\left[\left(\rho^{\prime}_{mm^{\prime}}\right)^{2}+\rho_{mm^{\prime}}\rho^{\prime\prime}_{mm^{\prime}}\right]+{\cal O}(\beta^{-4})\>,

(19)

where we have defined

\rho^{(n)}_{mm^{\prime}}=\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}A_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}}}(x)\Big|_{x=0}\>.

(20)

IV
Adiabatic response theory

In the limit of weak J and short retardation times, the same effective spin-only dynamics, Eq. (
2
), is obtained when first assuming that the electron system almost adiabatically follows the spin dynamics, while the weak-
J
approximation is done at a later stage.
This can be seen by starting with the adiabatic response theory as outlined in Ref.
50
and adapted to the case of spin dynamics.

The considered setup is that of an open quantum system, driven by external parameters
{\boldsymbol{S}}=({\boldsymbol{S}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{S}}_{M})
and in contact with a large thermal bath at temperature
1/\beta
.
The total Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}_{\rm total}=\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{\rm B}+\hat{H}_{\rm SB}\>,

(21)

where
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian given by Eq. (
1
), where
\hat{H}_{\rm B}
denotes the Hamiltonian of the thermal bath, and where
\hat{H}_{\rm SB}
is the system-bath interaction.
The latter terms,
\hat{H}_{\rm B}
and
\hat{H}_{\rm SB}
, are introduced for formal reasons only and can be disregarded at the end of the consideration, assuming that the system-bath coupling is sufficiently weak.
Finally, we will also take the zero-temperature limit
\beta\to\infty
.

The goal is to determine the expectation value

\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle_{t}

of the local spin of the electron system at site
i_{m}
in the many-electron quantum state, for given trajectory of the classical spins
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t^{\prime})
with
t^{\prime}\leq t
.
This is needed for Eq. (
4
) to obtain a closed set of equations of motion for the classical spins
{\boldsymbol{S}}
only.
To this end, we start with the thermal (canonical) equilibrium value
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}
for a fixed spin configuration
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
at time
t
.
In the adiabatic approximation, we would have
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle_{t}=\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}
.

Within adiabatic response theory, the difference

\langle\Delta{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}(t)\rangle=\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle_{t}-\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}\>,

(22)

is computed perturbatively in the deviation from a strictly adiabatic, infinitely slow spin dynamics
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
.
The “small parameter” is given by the dissipated work
[
50
,
57
]

W=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}(t^{\prime})\left[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}\hat{H}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t^{\prime}))-\langle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}\hat{H}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t^{\prime}))\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t^{\prime})}\right]\>,

(23)

where
\hat{H}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t))
is the total Hamiltonian (
1
) for a given spin configuration.
Note that
W
is an operator but not an observable
[
58
,
59
]
.
The dissipated work can be interpreted as the difference between the work performed on the system along a certain trajectory
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
in the spin-configuration space and the work along the same path but traversed adiabatically.
For an isothermal process, the second contribution is given by the free-energy difference between the initial and the final state at
{\boldsymbol{S}}(0)
and
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
, respectively.

As detailed in Ref.
50
, one finds

\langle\Delta{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}(t)\rangle=J\sum_{m^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{mm^{\prime}}(t,{\boldsymbol{S}}(t))\,\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)

(24)

up to first order in
W
.
Here,
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{mm^{\prime}}
is a
3\times 3
matrix for each index pair
m,m^{\prime}
, which depends on
t
explicitly, and also implicitly via
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
.
The elements of this matrix are given by

\displaystyle K^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}_{mm^{\prime}}(t,{\boldsymbol{S}}(t))=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta}du\,\langle s^{\alpha}_{i_{m}}(-iu)s^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}(t^{\prime}-t)\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta}du\,\langle s^{\alpha}_{i_{m}}(-iu)\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}\langle s^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}(t^{\prime}-t)\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}\>.

(25)

Here,
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}=x,y,z
and
u
refers to imaginary time.
Inserting
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle_{t}
, as obtained from Eqs. (
22
) and (
24
), into Eq. (
4
), we get:

\displaystyle\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)-\sum_{m}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{m}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle J^{2}\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}K_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t,{\boldsymbol{S}}(t))\dot{S}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{m}(t)\>.

In the second step, we additionally assume that
J
is weak.
For a fixed spin configuration
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
, the static, equilibrium expectation value
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}
in the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
) can be expanded in powers of
J
as

\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)}=\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{J=0}+J\sum_{m^{\prime}}\chi_{mm^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)+{\cal O}(J^{2})\>,

(27)

where
\chi_{mm^{\prime}}\equiv\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\omega=0)
is the static and unperturbed (
J=0
) magnetic susceptibility, i.e., the
\omega=0
Fourier component of
\chi_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t)=\delta^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
defined in Eq. (
9
).
The first term
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{\rm(eq)}_{J=0}
vanishes, as there is no spontaneous magnetic order for a system of noninteracting conduction electrons and since the magnetic field
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{m}
only couples to the impurity spin
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
.
With this, the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
) reduces to the RKKY term
\sum_{m^{\prime}}J^{2}\chi_{mm^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)
, if terms of order
J^{3}
are neglected.
Since the
K
matrix in Eq. (
) already carries a
J^{2}
factor, we can then disregard its dependence on
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
as this would produce terms of
{\cal O}(J^{3})
as well.
With the same argument and since the model is SU(2) symmetric and does not support spontaneous magnetic order, the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
25
) can be disregarded.
We are left with

K^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\delta^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta}du\,\langle s^{\alpha}_{i_{m}}(-iu)s^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}(t^{\prime}-t)\rangle^{(0)}\>.

(28)

Inserting a resolution of the identity
{\boldsymbol{1}}=\sum|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|
with energy eigenstates between the spin operators in Eq. (
28
) and expressing the expectation value as
Z^{-1}\sum e^{-\beta E_{n}}\langle\Psi_{n^{\prime}}|\cdots|\Psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle
, one can derive a Lehmann-type representation.
This involves matrix elements
\langle\Psi_{n}|s^{\alpha}_{i}|\Psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle
with eigenstates of the
J=0
Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm el}
.
In the sector with even total particle number
N
, the latter is invariant under time reversal,
[\Theta,\hat{H}_{\rm el}]=0
, where the representation of
time reversal is given by an antiunitary operator
\Theta
with
\Theta^{2}={\boldsymbol{1}}
,
\Theta\Theta^{\dagger}={\boldsymbol{1}}
, and
\Theta{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}\Theta^{\dagger}=-{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}
.
A straightforward consequence is that the matrix elements

\langle\Psi_{n}|s^{\alpha}_{i}|\Psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle\Theta\Psi_{n}|s^{\alpha}_{i}|\Theta\Psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle\Psi_{n}|\Theta^{\dagger}s^{\alpha}_{i}\Theta|\Psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle^{\ast}=-\langle\Psi_{n}|s^{\alpha}_{i}|\Psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle^{\ast}\in i\mathbb{R}

are purely imaginary (see also Ref.
60
).
This immediately implies that
{\boldsymbol{K}}
is symmetric
K^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=K^{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}_{m^{\prime}m}(t)
.

It is important to see that there is a finite
antisymmetric
part for strong
J
, i.e., in the regime where the weak-
J
approximation does not apply, e.g., for systems directly coupled to an external magnetic field, or systems where time-reversal symmetry is broken explicitly in the electronic sector, as in the case of the Haldane model
[
61
]
.
In the equations of motion for the classical spins, this would lead to an additional geometrical spin torque

\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t))\dot{S}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{m}(t)

(29)

resulting from the spin-Berry curvature

\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t))=J^{2}\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t))-K_{m^{\prime}m}^{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t))\right)\>.

(30)

Previously, this was derived within the framework of adiabatic spin dynamics
[
62
,
60
,
63
,
64
]
.
The spin-Berry curvature represents the feedback
[
65
]
of the Berry-phase physics
[
66
]
in the electron system on the dynamics of the classical degrees of freedom.
Here, we see that the same geometrical spin torque can be derived within the context of adiabatic response theory
[
50
]
as well.
Importantly, the spin-Berry curvature is a
geometrical
object and only depends on
t
via
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
,
if
the explicit
t
dependence of

K_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t,{\boldsymbol{S}}(t))
in Eq. (
25
) can be disregarded, i.e., in the long-time limit.

Let us return to our main focus, namely spin damping.
Generally, we define

\overline{K}_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t,{\boldsymbol{S}}(t))+K_{m^{\prime}m}^{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}(t,{\boldsymbol{S}}(t))\right)\>.

(31)

Specifically, for the time-reversal and spin-SU(2) symmetric model
\hat{H}_{\rm el}
considered here and in the weak-
J
limit, the symmetrization is superfluous and, furthermore, the
3\times 3
tensor is actually isotropic, i.e.,
\overline{K}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\delta^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\overline{K}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
.
Hence, the last term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
) reads:

\sum_{m}\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m^{\prime}}(t)\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}(t)

(32)

when identifying

\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=J^{2}\overline{K}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)\>.

(33)

With the term Eq. (
32
) and with Eq. (
27
) inserted into Eq. (
), we find the same spin-only effective equation of motion (
11
) that was derived within linear response theory and the subsequent perturbative treatment of retardation effects, but with two exceptions:
The RKKY coupling constants are time-independent, and the expression Eq. (
32
) for the damping constants differs from Eq. (
13
).

V
Computational details

The fundamental equations of motion (
4
), (
5
) as well as the effective equations of motion (
2
) can be solved using standard numerical techniques
[
67
,
68
,
69
]
.
On time scales up to
\sim 10^{9}
in units of the inverse nearest-neighbor hopping
1/T
, the achieved numerical accuracy is sufficient, i.e., effects of numerical errors are invisible in all spin-dynamics plots shown below.

We consider a tight-binding model of noninteracting electrons
\hat{H}_{\rm el}
on a
D
-dimensional lattice with periodic boundary conditions such that diagonalization of the hopping matrix
{\boldsymbol{T}}={\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{\dagger}
is achieved analytically via Fourier transformation.
Hence, the tight-binding dispersions are given by

\varepsilon(k)=-2T\cos(k)-2T^{\prime}\cos(2k)

(34)

for
D=1
, where the nearest-neighbor hopping
T=1
fixes the energy (and with
\hbar\equiv 1
the time scale) and where
T^{\prime}
is the next-nearest-neighbor hopping.
The lattice constant is set to unity as well.
For
D=2
, we have

\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})=-2T(\cos( k_x )+\cos(k_y))-4T^{\prime}\cos(k_x)\cos(k_y)\>.

(35)

The necessary ingredients for the effective equations (
2
) or, more generally, for the effective equations of motion with time-dependent parameters, Eq. (
11
), can be computed numerically as follows.

We start with the RKKY interaction parameters
J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
.
These are obtained from Eq. (
12
) by performing the
\tau
integration numerically.
For the integrand, we use the representation

\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau)=\Theta(\tau)e^{-\eta\tau}\imaginary\left[\left(\frac{e^{i{\boldsymbol{T}}\tau}}{e^{\beta({\boldsymbol{T}}-\mu{\boldsymbol{1}})}+{\boldsymbol{1}}}\right)_{i_{m^{\prime}}i_{m}}\left(\frac{e^{-i{\boldsymbol{T}}\tau}}{{\boldsymbol{1}}+e^{-\beta({\boldsymbol{T}}-\mu{\boldsymbol{1}})}}\right)_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}}}\right]

(36)

of the (
J=0
) retarded magnetic susceptibility, where we made use of the fact that the hopping matrix is diagonal in the spin indices
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
and spin independent.
With
U_{i{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}e^{-i{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}}
, and in the zero-temperature limit
\beta\to\infty
, one gets

\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau)=\Theta(\tau)e^{-\eta\tau}\imaginary\left[\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\rm occ}e^{i\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})\tau}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}})}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}^{\rm unocc}e^{-i{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})}e^{-i\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})\tau}\right]\>.

(37)

We see that the susceptibility is symmetric with respect to
m
,
m^{\prime}
.
As argued above, this is a consequence of the time-reversal symmetry of
\hat{H}_{\rm el}
.

Importantly, the wave-vector summations over the occupied or unoccupied points in the first Brillouin zone factorize.
This allows us to address fairly large systems and to control the thermodynamic limit.
The
L\to\infty
limit should be taken with a finite
\eta>0
in the regularization factor
e^{-\eta\tau}
which appears in the definition of the retarded susceptibility Eq. (
9
) and which ensures the convergence of the limit
t\to\infty
in Eq. (
12
).
The latter is necessary for the computation of the time-independent RKKY coupling
J_{mm^{\prime}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=J^{2}\chi_{mm^{\prime}}(\omega=0)
.
The limit
\eta\searrow 0
should be taken in the end.

Within linear response theory, the time-dependent Gilbert-damping parameters
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
are obtained from Eq. (
13
) via numerical integration.
The time-independent parameters
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}
are obtained from Eq. (
61
).

Within adiabatic response theory and for weak
J
, the identification Eq. (
33
) leads us exactly to the same effective equations of motion (
11
) that were obtained within linear response theory, albeit with a different expression for
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
as compared to Eq. (
13
).
For its numerical evaluation we first consider Eq. (
28
).
Making use of isotropy and time-reversal symmetry,
K_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t)=\overline{K}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\delta^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}K_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
, inserting a resolution of the identity with an orthonormal basis of eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{\rm el}
as described below Eq. (
28
), and evaluating the corresponding matrix elements of
s_{i_{m}}^{\alpha}
and
s_{i_{m^{\prime}}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}
, yields:

K_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta}du\left(\frac{e^{{\boldsymbol{T}}(it^{\prime}+u)}}{e^{\beta({\boldsymbol{T}}-\mu{\boldsymbol{1}})}+{\boldsymbol{1}}}\right)_{m^{\prime}m}\left(\frac{e^{-{\boldsymbol{T}}(it^{\prime}+u)}}{{\boldsymbol{1}}+e^{-\beta({\boldsymbol{T}}-\mu{\boldsymbol{1}})}}\right)_{mm^{\prime}}\>,

(38)

and, after diagonalization of the hopping matrix
{\boldsymbol{T}}
,

K_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}f(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})-\mu)f(\mu-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}))e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})}e^{-i{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\beta}du\,e^{(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}))(u+it^{\prime})}\>.

(39)

Here,
f(x)=1/(e^{\beta x}+1)
is the Fermi function.
We carry out the integration over imaginary time
u
with the case distinction (i)
\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})\neq\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})
and (ii)
\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})=\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})
.
Using the identity

(e^{\beta(x-x^{\prime})}-1)f(x)f(-x^{\prime})=f(-x)f(x^{\prime})-f(x)f(-x^{\prime})

in addition and carrying out the
t^{\prime}
integration, we arrive at:

K_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})\neq\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})}f(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})-\mu)f(\mu-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}))\,\frac{\imaginary[e^{i({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})}(1-e^{i(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}))t})]}{(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}))^{2}}+K^{\rm(deg)}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)\>,

(40)

where the first term refers to case (i) and where the second term, case (ii), is given by:

K^{\rm(deg)}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta t}{L^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})=\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})}f(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})-\mu)f(\mu-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}))\,e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})}e^{-i{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})}\>.

(41)

In the thermodynamic limit
L\to\infty
, this second term vanishes, unless there is a macroscopic number of degeneracies in the tight-binding dispersion
\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
Assuming that this is not the case, taking the zero-temperature limit
\beta\to\infty
, and inserting into Eq. (
33
) we find:

\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=J^{2}\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\rm occ}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}^{\rm unocc}\frac{\imaginary[e^{i({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})}(1-e^{i(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}))t})]}{(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}})-\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}))^{2}}\>.

(42)

Unfortunately, the numerical evaluation is much more time-consuming as compared to linear response theory, i.e., Eq. (
13
) and Eq. (
37
).

While LRT and ART yield the same Gilbert damping constant

In addition, the time dependence of the damping is practically irrelevant for the spin dynamics.

We start the related discussion with Fig.

Initially, the impurity spin points in the

(not shown) and

Importantly, Fig.

We have also calculated the spin dynamics within ART.
The result perfectly agrees with that shown in Fig.

.
After the previous discussion, this was only to be expected, since within ART
\alpha_{0}(t)
oscillates with the same frequency as seen within LRT (cf. Fig.

) and converges to the constant
\alpha_{0}
after a few oscillations anyway.
We conclude that the time dependence of the Gilbert damping is practically irrelevant for the resulting spin dynamics within both approaches, LRT and ART.
From this point on, we therefore use LRT with time-independent damping constants
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}
.

The predictions of the effective spin-only theory agree very well with the results obtained from the full theory, i.e., from Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
).
For a numerical evaluation of the full theory up to a propagation time
t_{\rm prop}
and avoiding unwanted finite-size effects, i.e., interferences due to excitations propagating back to the impurity spin (in case of periodic boundary conditions), one must consider systems with

L\gtrsim vt_{\rm prop}
, where
v=d\varepsilon(k_{\rm F})/dk
is the Fermi velocity.
At half filling and for
T^{\prime}=0
, we have
v_{\rm F}=2
.
This implies that a system with
L=1000
sites is sufficiently large to see complete spin relaxation, if the relaxation time is
\tau=500
.

As can be seen in Fig.
6
, this is the case, when choosing
J=0.5
and
B=1.0
(see top panel).
While
S_{x}
(and
S_{y}
, not shown) oscillates with the Larmor frequency
\omega\approx B
, the
z
component of the spin monotonously increases from
S_{z}=0
for
t=0
to
S_{z}\approx 1
for
t\approx 300
.
The dynamical evolution is qualitatively the same for both, the full theory and the effective spin-only theory.
The agreement is even quantitative, there is merely a small difference of a few per cent between full and effective theory visible in the
z
component of
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
around
t=100
, and the relaxation is somewhat faster in the full theory.

Physically relevant coupling strengths
J
and magnetic fields
B
(or effective “Weiss” fields produced via RKKY exchange in a multi-impurity-spin system) are generically much weaker.
A weaker field
B
implies a smaller precession frequency and thus a slower spin dynamics.
Hence, this implies a more adiabatic motion and thus improves the short-retardation-time approximation.
In fact, comparing the results at fixed
J=0.5
, for
B=1.0
(Fig.
6
, top panel) with those obtained for
B=0.5
(middle panel), one finds an even better agreement between full and effective theory for the smaller field strength.
Note that the relaxation time increases with decreasing
B
.
We have thus extended the maximum propagation time to
t_{\rm prop}=1000
and, accordingly, the system size to
L=2000
.

Similarly, a weaker
J
, at fixed
B
, improves the weak-coupling approximation.
In the bottom panel of Fig.
6
, for
J=0.3
and
B=0.5
, the agreement between full and effective theory is perfect on the scale of the figure.
The relaxation time increases once more.

The results discussed so far have been obtained by simultaneously switching on the coupling
J
and the field
B
at time
t=0
.
This setup for initiating the dynamics is the one that is conceptually consistent with the linear-response approach.
Alternatively, we have tentatively initiated the dynamics by starting from the coupled system at finite
J
and switching on the field
B
only.
Using this second setup and within the full theory, we did not find any significant differences in the spin dynamics.
This is easily understood, since (i) for the considered coupling strengths there is only a very weak polarization of the conduction-electron local magnetic moment
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{0}}\rangle
at
t=0
.
At
J=0.5
, for example, its magnitude amounts to
|\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{0}}\rangle|\approx 0.064
, i.e., almost an order of magnitude smaller that the saturation value, and hence its feedback effect on the spin dynamics is weak.
(ii) While in the first setup
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{0}}\rangle=0
at time
t=0
, the moment very quickly polarizes
in the course of time
to the same value
|\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{0}}\rangle|\approx 0.064
that is found at
t=0
in the second setup.
In fact, the polarization takes place on the fast electronic time scale and is fully completed already after
t=1/T=1
.
We can thus state that the spin dynamics practically does not depend on the preparation of the initial state.

VI.4
Van Hove singularity

Besides
J
, the strength of the (local) Gilbert damping also depends on the density of states at the Fermi energy, as Eq. (
15
) demonstrates.
A divergence of the density of states at the Fermi energy then implies a divergent damping constant
\alpha_{0}
.
The analysis of the effective equation of motion for a single spin
[
70
,
11
,
56
]
,

\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}(t)=\alpha_{0}\,\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}(t)\times{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)\\
-{\boldsymbol{B}}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)\>,

gives the exact analytical result

\tau\propto\frac{1+\alpha_{0}^{2}}{\alpha_{0}}\frac{1}{B}

(44)

for
\tau
.
The relaxation time diverges for
\alpha_{0}\to\infty
.

We have checked this against the numerical evaluation of the full theory, Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), for the half-filled system,
J=0.5
,
B=1.0
, and next-nearest-neighbor hopping
T^{\prime}=-0.5
, where the density of states exhibits a strong
1/\sqrt{\omega}
van Hove singularity at the Fermi energy (see Fig.
3
).
In addition, slightly smaller
T^{\prime}\lesssim-0.5
have been considered.
Contrary to the prediction of the effective theory, we find a finite relaxation time of the order of
\tau={\cal O}(10^{2})
.
This shows that the effective theory fails, if the (dimensionless) parameter, given by the product of
J
with the density of states at the Fermi energy, is large.

In case of two impurity spins at distance
d=i_{m^{\prime}}-i_{m}
and vanishing magnetic field, the effective equations of motion read:

\displaystyle\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{1}=J_{d}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}+\alpha_{0}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}\times\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{1}+\alpha_{d}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}\times\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{2}\>,

(45)

\displaystyle\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{2}=J_{d}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}+\alpha_{0}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}\times\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{2}+\alpha_{d}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}\times\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{1}\>.

(46)

Here, we have disregarded the time dependence of
\alpha_{d}(t)
, as discussed above, but also of the RKKY coupling
J_{d}(t)
[see Eq. (
12
)].
The latter shows an oscillatory time dependence with the same characteristic frequency
\omega_{\rm vH}=\pm 2
that was found for
\alpha_{d}(t)
, but decays quickly on a time scale of a few tens of inverse hoppings.
Using the same reasoning as for the damping, we can also ignore the
t
dependence of
J_{d}(t)
, if
J
is sufficiently small.

As discussed above, for half filling and if
-T^{\prime}_{\rm c}<T^{\prime}<T^{\prime}_{\rm c}
, the damping parameters
\alpha_{d}
for all even
d
are equal, while
\alpha_{d}=0
for arbitrary odd
d
.
Hence, coupling the two impurity spins to neighboring lattice sites, the nonlocal damping vanishes, and, independent of the initial spin configuration, one finds a relaxation of the spin system to its ground state that is driven by the local damping parameter
\alpha_{0}
only.

For distance
d=2
, the situation is completely different (see also the discussion in Ref.
49
):
Exploiting the fact that
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{2}
and adding the two equations (
45
) and (
46
), immediately implies that
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{\rm tot}={{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}
is a constant of motion and, consequently,
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}
is constant as well.
We conclude that the spin system does not relax at all, if
d
is even.

This is demonstrated with Fig.
7
, where the time dependence of the
x
component of
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
is shown for a system with two impurity spins at distance
d=2
, initially prepared as mutually orthogonal,
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}=0
.
In fact, there is no relaxation for
T^{\prime}=0
, and
S_{1x}
exhibits an undamped oscillation.
This appears as counterintuitive.

On the contrary, for
T^{\prime}=0.6>T^{\prime}_{\rm c}=0.5
, the two-spin system relaxes to its (ferromagnetic) ground-state spin configuration.
The rather long relaxation time
\tau\sim 10^{6}
is due to the chosen weak coupling strength
J=0.1
.
The relaxation is due to the fact that
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{2}
is no longer enforced by the arguments leading to Eq. (
43
):
At
T^{\prime}=0.6
, we find
\alpha_{0}=1.169
and
\alpha_{2}=0.581
.

Fig.
7
also shows the time evolution of
S_{1x}(t)
for the same
T^{\prime}=0.6
but neglecting the nonlocal damping, i.e., the result of a calculation where we have
ad hoc
set
\alpha_{2}=0
.
Intuitively, one would expect that switching off the nonlocal damping would lead to a longer relaxation time.
However, as is seen in the figure, the opposite behavior is found, and
\tau
in fact
decreases
if one sets
\alpha_{2}=0
.

.

VI.8
Effect of nonlocal Gilbert damping in
D=2

To study the effect of the nonlocal Gilbert damping on the relaxation dynamics of impurity spins in two dimensions we first consider a setup with two classical spins exchange coupled to next-nearest-neighbor sites, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{R}}=(1,1)
.
Figure
13
shows the relaxation time
\tau
as a function of the next-nearest-neighbor hopping
T^{\prime}
, starting from an initial state where the two classical spins are orthogonal.

Of course, the definition of
\tau
is somewhat arbitrary.
As an operational criterion for (almost) full relaxation of
R
impurity spins, we employ the condition

\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R-1}\left|{\boldsymbol{S}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{r+1}\mp 1\right|<\epsilon\;,

(49)

with the
-
sign in case of a ferromagnetic alignment in the ground state and with the
+
sign for antiferromagnetic alignment.
Furthermore, we choose
\epsilon=0.001
.
We find that the criterion Eq. (
49
) is completely fulfilled for a sufficiently long time evolution so that we can define the relaxation time
\tau
as the latest time for which Eq. (
49
) no longer applies.

As can be seen in Fig.
13
,
\tau
monotonously increases with increasing
|T^{\prime}|
up to
|T^{\prime}|=0.6
, while for
|T^{\prime}|\geq 0.7
the
\tau
dependence becomes more complicated.
This is partly related to the
T^{\prime}
dependence of the ground-state spin configuration, which is determined by the RKKY interaction.
For
T^{\prime}=0
and
{\boldsymbol{R}}=(1,1)
, we have
J({\boldsymbol{R}})<0
, i.e., ferromagnetic coupling.
A finite (positive or negative)
T^{\prime}
increases the tendency towards antiferromagnetic coupling.
In fact we find
J({\boldsymbol{R}})<0
(ferromagnetic) for the data points up to
T^{\prime}=0.6
, and for
T^{\prime}=0.8
, while
J({\boldsymbol{R}})>0
(antiferromagnetic) for
T^{\prime}=0.7,0.9,1.0
.
This “irregularity” in the ground-state spin configuration and in the relaxation time is due to the nontrivial
T^{\prime}
dependence of both, the RKKY coupling and the Gilbert damping.
In all cases the impurity-spin configuration in the fully relaxed state is identical with the ground-state configuration.

The relaxation time scale crucially depends on
J
and is clearly unreachable in practice by
full
quantum-classical theory at a local exchange coupling
J=0.1
.
The numerical results of the effective spin-only theory (Fig.
13
) show that, roughly, the relaxation time is of the order of
\tau\sim 10^{8}
(see blue symbols) and thus, at the same value for
J
, about two orders of magnitude larger than for the
D=1
case (see Sec.

).

Let us note here that the spin dynamics is numerically very stable and does not change significantly when sharpening the tolerances for numerical errors in the solution of the system of differential equations.
Furthermore, slight deviations in the initial state, e.g., a small change of the angle enclosed by the two spins, only leads to small changes of
\tau
.

Interestingly, a clearly
shorter
relaxation time is obtained when disregarding the nonlocal Gilbert damping.
At
T^{\prime}=\pm 0.3
, where the value for
\alpha(1,1)
is about
18\%
of the local damping (see Fig.
12
), a decrease of
\tau
by about
10\%
is found, when
ad hoc
switching off the nonlocal damping.
The effect becomes stronger with decreasing
|T^{\prime}|
.

Let us mention that for
T^{\prime}=0
, the relaxation time is infinite in the effective theory due to the van Hove singularity at the Fermi energy of the half-filled conduction-electron system (see the discussion in Sec.
VI.4
).
The equations of motion Eq. (
2
) are meaningless in this case, since
\alpha({\boldsymbol{R}})
is divergent.

The finding that including nonlocal Gilbert damping leads to
longer
relaxation times is reminiscent of the results found for the
D=1
lattice.
For the
D=1
case, the effect could be explained analytically as the result of an emergent conserved quantity that is not caused by a symmetry of the Hamiltonian, see Sec.
VI.5
.
The main point is that an infinite relaxation time is obtained if
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{d}
, which is exactly realized for impurity spins coupled to next-nearest-neighbor (distance
d=2
) sites on the
D=1
lattice.
For the
D=2
case, the ratio
\alpha(1,1)/\alpha(0,0)
is clearly smaller than unity (see Fig.
12
for
T^{\prime}=-0.3
) and, therefore,
there is a weak reminiscence of the effect only, but still the relaxation time
increases
due to nonlocal damping.
This is corroborated by the observation that the effect is practically absent if the impurity spins couple to nearest-neighbor sites along the
y
axis of the square lattice, where we find
\alpha(0,1)/\alpha(0,0)\approx 0.001

(see Fig.
11
for
T^{\prime}=-0.3
).

For both types of initial states there is no general

with increasing

\tau\sim 10^{8}
roughly remains constant.
This hints towards a dominantly local relaxation mechanism, where the relaxation time is mainly dominated by the approach of the local ground state, while reaching the overall collinear spin structure is not very crucial.

This view is corroborated by the observation that the local Gilbert damping still dominates the relaxation dynamics:
For both types of initial conditions we have performed computations with all nonlocal damping switched off, i.e.,
\alpha({\boldsymbol{R}})=0
for all
{\boldsymbol{R}}
except for
{\boldsymbol{R}}=(0,0)
.
Results are displayed by orange and read circles in the figure.
While there is some effect of the nonlocal damping, it is generally small.
This is remarkable since the number of nonlocal damping terms grows as
R(R-1)/2
with

, i.e., quadratically for large

opposed to the number

We also note that switching off the nonlocal damping counterintuitively leads to shorter relaxation times

some

{\boldsymbol{R}}
and is always reached as the final state of the relaxation dynamics.

For the diagonal spin chain we find that the relaxation time
\tau
increases with the number of spins
R
for both initial spin configurations, the global
\pi/2
spin spiral (filled circles) and for an initial configuration (open circles) with only the first spin
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
rotated by
\pi/2
out of the ferromagnetic ground-state configuration.
The growth of
\tau
with

As the total spin of the full quantum-classical system (including the conduction electrons) is a constant of motion, this difference must be compensated in the course of time by a corresponding difference in the conduction-electron system.
In other words, the necessary amount of spin dissipation grows with

and leads to a (superlinearly) growing relaxation time.
Note that for the second type of initial conditions, only the component of the total spin along the direction of
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}

We have checked this by analogous computations for a

Finally, switching off all nonlocal Gilbert damping constants,

{\boldsymbol{R}}\neq 0

Very consistently, the relaxation time slightly
decreases
for both types of initial states, if

– this is the counterintuitive effect observed and discussed earlier.
For
R=5,6,....
, on the other hand, we find an increasing relaxation time.
We attribute this trend reversal to the fact that the overall larger nonlocal damping (see Figs.
11
and
12
) in combination with the growing number of nonlocal terms as
R
increases, now substantially adds to the local damping mechanism.

VI.10
Weakly interacting systems

The derivation (see Appendix
A
) of Eq. (
15
) is based on the application of Wick’s theorem and thus cannot be used to compute the Gilbert damping in the case of a correlated electron system.
Therefore, we compute
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}
via the frequency derivative of the magnetic susceptibility at
\omega=0
, i.e., via Eq. (
14
).
Addressing weakly interacting systems, the random-phase approximation (RPA)
[
71
,
72
,
73
,
64
]
is employed here.
We start from Eq. (
37
) for the non-interacting retarded magnetic susceptibility, denoted here as
\chi_{mm^{\prime}}^{(0)}(t)
, to get
\chi^{(0)}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)
via Fourier transformation from real to
{\boldsymbol{k}}
space and from time to frequency representation.
The interacting RPA susceptibility is then obtained as

\chi({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=\frac{\chi^{(0)}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)}{1+U\chi^{(0)}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)}

(50)

for the
D=2
Hubbard model on the square lattice,

\hat{H}_{\text{el}}=\sum_{\langle ii^{\prime}\rangle}\sum_{\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}T_{ii^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}c_{i^{\prime}\sigma}+U\sum_{i}n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}\>,

(51)

where
U
is the strength of the on-site Hubbard interaction.

On the level of one-particle excitations, the RPA corresponds to the Hartree-Fock approach.
We consider the paramagnetic phase of the Hubbard model at weak
U
and finite next-nearest-neighbor hopping
T^{\prime}\neq 0
.
As has been found within Hartree-Fock theory
[
74
]
, the paramagnetic state of the system becomes unstable towards an antiferromagnetic state at a finite critical interaction
U_{\rm c}=U_{\rm c}(T^{\prime})
.

Our numerical calculations have been performed for lattices with
L=512\times 512
sites, periodic boundary conditions, a large inverse temperature
\beta=500
, and using a small regularization parameter
\eta=0.015
.
We have checked that the results do not significantly depend on these choices and are representative for the zero-temperature and the thermodynamical limit.
In particular, we recover the known results for
U_{c}

[
74
]
.
At
T^{\prime}=-0.3
, for instance, we find
U_{c}\approx 2.53
, which is straightforwardly obtained as the singularity of
\chi({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)
at
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(\pi,\pi)
and
\omega=0
.

In reciprocal space, the frequency derivative of the susceptibility can be computed analytically:

\frac{d}{d\omega}\chi({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=\frac{d\chi^{(0)}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)/d\omega}{[1+U\chi^{(0)}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)]^{2}}\>.

(52)

Via Fourier transformation of
\chi({\boldsymbol{k}},0)
and of
d\chi({\boldsymbol{k}},0)/d\omega
we find the distance-dependent RKKY interaction
J({\boldsymbol{R}})
and the Gilbert damping
\alpha({\boldsymbol{R}})
in real space.
Fig.
16
shows the nearest-neighbor RKKY coupling
J(0,1)/J^{2}
as well as the local Gilbert damping
\alpha(0,0)/J^{2}
as functions of
U
for
T^{\prime}=-0.3
.
In reciprocal space, on approaching the phase transition via
U\to U_{\rm c}\approx 2.53
, we have

\chi({\boldsymbol{k}},0)\sim(U_{\rm c}-U)^{-1}
at
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(\pi,\pi)
while
d\chi({\boldsymbol{k}},0)/d\omega\sim(U_{\rm c}-U)^{-2}
.
The stronger divergence of
d\chi/d\omega
as compared to
\chi
leads to the stronger enhancement of
\alpha({\boldsymbol{R}})
, as compared to
J({\boldsymbol{R}})
, that can be seen in the figure.

Generally, the increase of the local Gilbert damping with increasing
U
for the square lattice is consistent with the results of a previous study performed for a single impurity spin coupled to the
D=1
Hubbard model
[
75
]
, where a decreasing relaxation time with increasing
U
has been found in the weak-interaction regime.

More importantly, however, we find that the Hubbard interaction causes a strongly
nonlocal
Gilbert damping.
This is demonstrated by the results shown in Fig.

and of the Gilbert damping

For

(panels on left), one recovers the above-discussed directional dependence of the Gilbert damping (bottom panel).
As compared to the RKKY interaction (top), this is much more pronounced.
With increasing

, see the middle panels for

is strongly enhanced due to the strong increase of the nonlocal damping parameters, relative to

.

At

(upper right panel), the RKKY coupling exhibits a checkerboard pattern with

strongly oscillating between positive (antiferromagnetic) and negative (ferromagnetic) values.
Comparing with

, we see that this pattern extends spatially and is expected to eventually cover the entire lattice upon approaching the phase transition at
U_{c}\approx 2.53
.
This is indicated by the closed nodal line of RKKY-coupling zeros extending spatially with increasing
U
.

Similarly, in the same spatial region enclosed by this line, the Gilbert damping at
U=2.5
(lower right panel) is found to oscillate.
Eventually, for
U\to U_{c}
, the spatial structure of the Gilbert damping is expected to be given by
\alpha(R_{x},R_{y})/\alpha(0,0)\to(-1)^{R_{x}+R_{y}}
.
This is very reminiscent of the distance dependence of the Gilbert damping on the
D=1
chain,

\alpha_{d}/\alpha_{0}=\frac{1}{2}[1+(-1)^{d}]
, which has been discussed in Sec.
VI.2
for the noninteracting case.

For a system with, e.g., two impurity spins coupled to next-nearest-neighbor sites with
{\boldsymbol{R}}=(1,1)
, and if one trusted the effective theory right at the phase transition, the spatial structure of the (divergent) Gilbert damping would imply that the total spin
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}
and the enclosed angle
\arccos(\ff S_{1} \ff S_{2})
are constants of motion and thus that the system never relaxes to its ground-state spin configuration.
However, it remains an open question, how the relaxation dynamics would look like in
D=2
and for
U\to U_{c}
, since the absolute (unnormalized) Gilbert damping is divergent (Fig.
16
) and, hence, the effective spin-only theory is likely to break down at the critical point.

VII
Conclusions

Local magnetic moments, modelled as classical spins and locally exchange coupled to an extended metallic system of conduction electrons at zero temperature undergo a relaxation dynamics.
To reduce the computational complexity, it is tempting to describe this dynamics within an effective spin-only theory.
Actually, this is necessary to access the emergent relaxation times scale, which can be orders of magnitude longer than the fast femtosecond time scale of the conduction-electron dynamics.

This reduction to a spin-only theory is possible by invoking two mutually interrelated approximations:
(i) The exchange-coupling strength
J
is small as compared to, e.g., the nearest-neighbor hopping matrix element
T
.
(ii) The electron dynamics follows the spin dynamics almost adiabatically, i.e., the retardation time of the electron system is short compared to the spin-dynamics time scale.
Within the framework of linear-response theory (LRT), one focusses on step (i) first and adopts (ii) in a subsequent step, while in the framework of adiabatic-response theory (ART), one starts from (ii) and later on adopts the weak-
J
approximation (i).
Both approaches, LRT and ART, yield a system of nonlinear differential equations of motion involving the impurity spin
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
only, as well as time-dependent and nonlocal parameters, namely the RKKY interaction strengths
J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
and the Gilbert-damping parameters
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
, and possibly external magnetic fields coupling to the spins.
The parameters
J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
and
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
are properties of the conduction-electron system only and closely related to its retarded magnetic susceptibility.

The reliability of the effective spin-only theory has been tested against the numerical solution of the full coupled quantum-classical electron-spin dynamics for a single spin, driven by an external field
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, coupled to a
D=1
dimensional tight-binding electron system.
The expressions for
J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
and
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
, as obtained within LRT and ART, exhibit a quite different time dependence.
It has been found, however, that the static RKKY interaction and the static Gilbert damping
J_{mm^{\prime}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
and
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
perfectly agree.
Furthermore, the strong temporal oscillations of
J_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
and
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}(t)
seen within LRT are irrelevant for the spin dynamics as they take place on the fast electron time scale only.
In fact, if
J
is sufficiently weak and if the spin-dynamics time scale
\sim 1/B
is sufficiently slow, almost perfect agreement is found when comparing the LRT and ART results against each other
and
against the results of the full theory.

To achieve computationally manageable relaxation times (within the full theory), this comparison was done for values of
J
and
B
of the order of
T
.
Realistic values for
J
are very specific to the concrete system considered but will typically be an order of magnitude smaller than
T
:
Assuming a nearest-neighbor RKKY exchange of the order of
J_{\rm RKKY}\sim 0.01T
(with
J_{\rm RKKY}\sim 1
meV) or smaller, see, e.g., Ref.
[
76
]
, we may deduce from
J_{\rm RKKY}=J^{2}\chi\sim J^{2}/T
a typical value
J=0.1T
for the local exchange.
Realistic values for
B
are even smaller.
Hence, for computations addressing real systems, one may expect that the reliability of the effective theory is even better.

In the absence of an external driving field
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, the time scale on which the spin dynamics takes place is governed by the RKKY coupling.
Since
J_{mm^{\prime}}\propto J^{2}
, one actually has to control the exchange-coupling strength
J
only.
We have derived a simple expression for the Gilbert damping in the wide-band limit,
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}=\frac{\pi}{2}J^{2}\rho_{mm^{\prime}}^{2}
, which involves the (nonlocal) densities of states
\rho_{mm^{\prime}}
at the Fermi energy.
This shows that the dimensionless parameter
J\rho_{mm}
is a suitable control parameter for the reliability of the effective theory.
In fact, if the local density of states
\rho_{mm}
diverges, as in the case of a van Hove singularity, the effective theory is found to break down.

The
D=1
case turns out to be critical in the sense that the Gilbert damping
\alpha_{d}
does not decay as a function of the distance
d=i_{m}-i_{m^{\prime}}
but rather oscillates between the local value
\alpha_{0}
and zero for even and odd
d
, respectively.
This causes an anomalous spin dynamics where relaxation is prohibited by an emergent conserved quantity that does not derive from a symmetry of the full Hamiltonian.
This anomaly has already been discussed in Ref.
[
49
]
for
T^{\prime}=0
.
Here, we could demonstrate that it occurs for absolute value of the next-nearest neighbor hoppings
T^{\prime}
smaller than a critical value
T^{\prime}_{\rm c}=T/2
and were able to relate the critical value to a Lifschitz transition of the Fermi “surface”.
Our analysis also explains why the relaxation time counterintuitively
decreases
when disregarding the nonlocal terms
m\neq m^{\prime}
in the matrix of Gilbert-damping parameters
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}
.

This is actually a recurring theme in the discussion of relaxation dynamics in
D=2
dimension:
For two, initially orthogonal impurity spins coupled to next-nearest-neighbor sites on the square lattice and for all values of
T^{\prime}
(
|T^{\prime}|<T
), we again find a decrease of the relaxation time when
ad hoc
switching off the nonlocal Gilbert damping.
As compared to
D=1
, however, the effect is much smaller such that even disregarding the nonlocality of the Gibert damping might be a reasonable approximation, depending on the value for
T^{\prime}
.

For the
D=2
square lattice and distances
R=|{\boldsymbol{R}}|
up to
R\sim 10
lattice constants, we did not see a universal simple power law
\alpha(R)\propto R^{-(D-1)}
, which is expected for large
R
(assuming a free,
\propto k^{2}
dispersion).
More importantly, the nonlocal Gilbert damping exhibits a strong directional dependence.
This reflects itself, e.g., in the relaxation dynamics of one-dimensional spin chains coupled to the square lattice.
For chains with 2-10 impurity spins coupled to nearest-neigboring sites along the
x
or, equivalently, the
y
direction, the relaxation time
\tau
turns out to oscillate with the length of the chain without a significant overall increase of
\tau
, independent of the initial spin configuration.
Contrary, for chains of spins coupled to next-nearest-neigboring sites along a diagonal direction, there is an overall superlinear increase of
\tau
with the chain length.
The same behavior is found for an initial
\pi/2
spin-spiral configuration, as compared to an initial state, where all spins are in their ground-state (ferromagnetic) configuration, except for an edge spin rotated by
\pi/2
that is kept fixed during the time evolution.
The strong increase of
\tau
with the chain length can be explained with the necessarily increasing amount of spin dissipation.

There are several main lines of relevant future research:
(i) Our studies can straightforwardly be extended to more realistic models for the electronic structure and also combined with
ab initio
band-structure calculations.
In particular, it would be interesting to consider multi-orbital systems and to see the effect of band degeneracy on the nonlocality of the Gilbert damping.

(ii) The present study had focussed on systems with a gapless metallic electronic structure and weak local exchange
J
, where Gilbert damping is the dominant leading-order effect.
For gapped systems, on the other hand, the spin dynamics is affected by the geometrical spin torque in addition
[
62
,
77
,
60
,
64
]
.
Studying semi-metals, where one would expect a drastically reduced damping and a still well-defined geometrical spin torque
[
60
]
are worth studying.
Furthermore, somewhat relaxing the almost adiabatic time evolution, by truncating at the next higher order in the expansion in the typical retardation time
\tau_{\rm ret}
, brings in additional effects such as nutation
[
78
,
79
,
80
,
63
]
.

(iii) Modelling the impurity local magnetic moments as classical spins can only be a first step towards a theory of spin friction and dissipative dynamics of
quantum
spins.
Clearly, this is a hard correlation problem as it essentially requires a conceptually and computationally feasible treatment of the long-time dynamics in a multi-impurity Kondo model.

More promising is (iv) to study the effect of electron correlations on the relaxation dynamics of classical impurity spins.
This type of problem can be addressed within the presented LRT and ART frameworks.
It essentially requires a computation of the zero-temperature (or the finite-temperature equilibrium) retarded magnetic susceptibility close to
\omega=0
.
In a first attempt to address a weakly correlated conduction-electron system, we here have applied the RPA to the half-filled Hubbard model at finite next-nearest-neighbor hopping
T^{\prime}
.
This is already interesting, since with increasing
U
the paramagnetic phase of the electron system becomes unstable towards a
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(\pi,\pi)
antiferromagnetic phase.
Close to the phase transition, the Gilbert damping matrix
\alpha_{mm^{\prime}}
is found to be overall strongly enhanced and strongly nonlocal.
The numerical data suggest that, right at the phase transition, the distance dependence is simply given by
\alpha({\boldsymbol{R}})\propto e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{R}}}
with
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(\pi,\pi)
, which would cause spin dynamics without any dissipation.
However, right at the phase transition the effective spin-only theory itself is expected to break down.
Important next steps would therefore be to replace the RPA by a more advanced technique, to study the system for
U

above
the critical interaction, and to address the strong-coupling regime.

Appendix A
Representation of the nonlocal Gilbert damping in terms of the nonlocal density of states

To derive Eq. (
15
) for the Gilbert damping matrix, we start from the definition of the retarded susceptibility given by Eq. (
9
).
The computations are done at finite temperature
1/\beta
, and the zero-temperature limit
\beta\to\infty
is taken at the end.
Since
\langle[s^{\alpha}_{i_{m}}(t),s^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}(0)]\rangle
is a free expectation value, Wick’s theorem applies and can be used to decompose the two-particle correlation into products of one-particle correlations.
A straightforward calculation yields:

\displaystyle\chi_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\Theta(t)e^{-\eta t}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mbox{Im}\left(\langle c_{i_{m}\sigma}(t)c^{\dagger}_{i_{m^{\prime}}\sigma}(0)\rangle\langle c^{\dagger}_{i_{m^{\prime}}\sigma}(0)c_{i_{m}\sigma}(-t)\rangle\right)\>.

The one-particle correlation functions can be obtained from the (nonlocal) one-particle density of states, Eq. (
16
), as

\displaystyle\langle c_{i\sigma}(t)c^{\dagger}_{i^{\prime}\sigma}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\,f(-x)A_{ii^{\prime}}(x)e^{-ixt}\>,

\displaystyle\langle c^{\dagger}_{i^{\prime}\sigma}(0)c_{i\sigma}(-t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dy\,f(y)A_{ii^{\prime}}(y)e^{iyt}\>.

(54)

Here,
f(x)=1/(e^{\beta x}+1)
denotes the Fermi function.
Using Eq. (
54
) we find for the frequency-dependent susceptibility

\chi_{ii^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)=\delta^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\!\!\iint dxdy\,f(-x)f(y)A_{ii^{\prime}}(x)A_{ii^{\prime}}(y)\rho(x,y)

(55)

with

\displaystyle\rho(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}\Theta(t)e^{-\eta t}\mbox{Im}(e^{-ixt}e^{iyt})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\omega-x+y+i\eta}-\frac{1}{\omega+x-y+i\eta}\right)\>.

Note that
A_{ii^{\prime}}(x)
is real.
Inserting this result in Eq. (
55
) and using the identity
f(-x)f(y)-f(x)f(-y)=f(y)-f(x)
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\chi_{ii^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)=\frac{\delta^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}}{2}\iint dxdy\,(f(y)-f(x))\frac{A_{ii^{\prime}}(x)A_{ii^{\prime}}(y)}{\omega-x+y+i\eta}\>.

For the second representation of the Gilbert damping in Eq. (
14
) we only need the imaginary part of
\chi_{ii^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)
.
Using the identity
(-1/\pi)\mbox{Im}(x+i\eta)^{-1}=\delta(x)
, taking the
\omega
derivative of
\mbox{Im}\,\chi_{ii^{\prime}}^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)
, and evaluating the result at
\omega=0
, yields

\displaystyle\alpha_{ii^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}J^{2}\iint dxdy\,\delta^{\prime}(y-x)

(58)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle(f(y)-f(x))A_{ii^{\prime}}(x)A_{ii^{\prime}}(y)\>.

The prime at the
\delta
function denotes the derivative with respect to the argument.
After exchanging the integration variables
x\leftrightarrow y
for the summand involving
f(x)
and using
\delta^{\prime}(-x)=-\delta(x)
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle\alpha_{ii^{\prime}}=\pi J^{2}\iint dxdy\,\delta^{\prime}(y-x)f(y)A_{ii^{\prime}}(x)A_{ii^{\prime}}(y)\>.

To carry out the integration over
y
