Comparative effectiveness of osteochondral restoration, cell-based regeneration and cell-free implants for knee chondral defects: a systematic review
Authors: Shivani M Reddy, Caroline B Rains, Joseph Marchese, Leila C Kahwati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesChondral defects of the knee can impair function, reduce quality of life and accelerate progression to osteoarthritis. Treatment aims to delay further cartilage damage and reduce the risk of early-onset osteoarthritis and knee replacement. This systematic review evaluated the effectiveness and safety of chondral defect repair procedures on the knee.DesignSystematic review.Data sourcesPubMed and Cochrane Library.Eligibility criteria for selecting studiesInterventions were matrix-induced autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI), osteochondral autologous transplantation (OATS) and cell-free implants. Comparators included microfracture (MF). Eligible study designs were randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and comparative non-randomised studies of interventions (NRSIs). Eligible outcomes included patient-reported outcomes (PROs), clinical response, treatment failure, reoperation and harms. Risk-of-bias assessments (RoB) on all included studies were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) for the RCTs and Risk Of Bias In Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions tool for the NRSIs.Results13 studies reported effectiveness and 8 studies reported harms. MACI was more effective than MF, measured by PROs and response to treatment (moderate certainty of evidence (COE) from RCTs, very low to low COE from NRSIs). Evidence comparing OATS with MF suggested no difference in effectiveness, as measured by PROs (low COE from RCTs and very low COE from NRSIs). MACI and OATS had similar harms to MF (COE low and very low). Evidence for cell-free implants suggested greater effectiveness (moderate COE) and fewer harms (low COE) compared with MF. No studies compared these procedures to sham surgery or conservative management.ConclusionMACI and OATS showed greater or comparable effectiveness compared with MF. Harms were comparable between procedures, though reporting was sparse. Rigorous study design, standardised effectiveness outcomes and consistent reporting of harms would strengthen the evidence base. These findings can inform clinical decision-making and identify future research needs.Keywords: Knee injuries, Cartilage, Knee surgery

Introduction

Chondral defects are acquired defects in the articular cartilage surface of the knee. They are associated with pain, swelling, locking and impaired function1 limiting activities of daily living (ADL), work or physical activity, including sports. Some patients with chondral defects of the knee have quality of life (QOL) scores similar to those with severe osteoarthritis and reported pain and functional impairment similar to those of patients scheduled for knee replacement.2 Cartilage is poorly vascularised and innervated, with limited ability to repair and regenerate new cartilage.3 As a result, individuals with chondral defects can experience progressive cartilage degeneration, a greater risk of developing osteoarthritis and an earlier need for knee replacement.4,6 Treatment aims to delay further cartilage damage and reduce the risk of early-onset osteoarthritis and need for total or partial knee replacement by filling the defect with new cartilage, ideally tissue composed of collagen similar to native cartilage.

Microfracture (MF) is the most commonly performed procedure for chondral defects of the knee in the USA7 due to its wide availability, minimal invasiveness, simpler surgical technique and lower costs.8,10 MF involves using a small, sharp pick to create channels in the subchondral bone for mesenchymal stem cells from the bone marrow to migrate to the bone surface and create new cartilage. A related procedure, drilling, uses a surgical drill rather than a pick to create the holes. However, MF primarily generates fibrocartilage, which is not as durable as native hyaline cartilage. Furthermore, MF may decrease the efficacy of future defect repair procedures that generate greater amounts of hyaline cartilage,11 including osteochondral restoration or cell-based regeneration procedures. Osteochondral autologous transplantation (OATS) or osteochondral allograft transplantation (OCA) transplants articular cartilage from a non-weight bearing part of the knee or from a cadaveric source, respectively. Matrix-induced autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI), or cell-based regeneration, is a two-stage procedure in which a patient’s chondrocytes are harvested, then cultured ex vivo on a porcine or synthetic scaffold and transplanted into the defect 6–8 weeks later.

MF, drilling and OATS do not involve products or devices regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).12 The MACI porcine scaffold (Vericel Corporation) is FDA-approved through the 351 Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Products (HCT/P) pathway.13 Cadaver tissue used in OCA is approved through the 361 HCT/P pathway. The FDA approved Agili-C (CartiHeal), a cell-free aragonite implant that resorbs as stem cells migrate to create new cartilage, through the premarket approval pathway for medical devices.

The objective of this systematic review is to evaluate the comparative effectiveness and harms of chondral defect repair procedures for the knee in clinical use, including MACI, OATS, OCA and a cell-free implant. This work is derived from a Health Technology Assessment (HTA) commissioned by the State of Washington Healthcare Authority.14 The HTA programme includes public involvement at multiple points in the review process, including public comment periods after publication of the draft key question and draft evidence report and during public presentation of the report.15 The draft and final key questions, analytic framework and study selection criteria for this review were posted at the State of Washington’s Health Technology Assessment Programme public website on 22 December 2023 and 22 January 2024, respectively (https://www.hca.wa.gov/about-hca/programs-and-initiatives/health-technology-assessment/treatments-chondral-defects-knee).

Eligible studies were conducted in individuals with chondral defects of the knee only and evaluated OATS, OCA, MACI or products/devices with FDA approval. For all interventions, eligible comparators included conservative therapy, chondroplasty, MF, sham surgery or knee replacement. First- and second-generation autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI) were excluded because these procedures have been superseded by the third-generation matrix-based procedure (MACI), which has fewer complications, thus focusing the review on current clinical practice.16,18

Eligible study designs were randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and non-randomised studies of interventions (NRSIs) that included a comparison group. Studies conducted in countries not categorised as very high on the United Nation’s 2021 Human Development Index were not eligible.

Information sources

PubMed and the Cochrane Library were searched for relevant studies published in English from database inception to 12 September 2024.

Search strategy

The search strategy included medical subject headings and keyword terms for knee and cartilage surgery (search strategy in online supplemental material, appendix B). In addition, the reference lists of systematic reviews and health technology assessments were reviewed to identify relevant primary research studies not found through the electronic search.

Selection process

Two review team members independently screened titles, abstracts and full-text articles using DistillerSR version 2.35. Discrepancies in study selection at the full-text level were adjudicated by a senior investigator or by team consensus. DistillerSR’s artificial intelligence feature was used to rank abstract and full-text articles in priority order of relevance for screening.

Data collection process

One team member extracted study intervention, population characteristics, and efficacy and harm outcomes into a structured form in DistillerSR and a senior investigator checked these data for accuracy. When multiple publications reported results from the same study, these reports were grouped, with data extracted across all reports into a single data collection form.

Data items

Eligible effectiveness outcomes included validated patient-reported outcomes (PROs), return to work or sport and rehabilitation time, as well as clinical response, treatment failure and reoperations. Studies only reporting intermediate outcomes (eg, imaging results, pathological specimens) were excluded. Harms outcomes included serious adverse events (SAEs) and adverse events (AEs).

Study risk of bias assessment

Two team members conducted independent risk-of-bias assessments (RoB) on all included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) for the RCTs19 and the Risk Of Bias In Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for the NRSIs.20 Discrepancies were resolved by discussion. Relevant confounders designated for use with ROBINS-I included age, body mass index, mechanism of injury, size of lesion/depth of lesion, location of lesion, pre-surgery treatment, previous same knee surgery and concomitant knee conditions.

Effect measures

Effect measures were reported to the extent that data were provided by the study. For continuous data, comparisons of follow-up values and within-group and between-group mean differences (MDs) were reported with statistical testing when available. Categorical data outcomes included comparison of follow-up values, risk ratios and ORs.

Synthesis methods

A qualitative synthesis was performed for each research question by each procedure compared with MF in tabular and narrative formats. To determine whether quantitative synthesis was appropriate, established guidance was followed.21 22 A minimum of three studies was required to conduct meta-analyses and at least 50% of studies with a similar intervention, comparator and outcome measured at approximately the same follow-up time point with available data to calculate a pooled treatment effect. For meta-analyses, random-effects models using the inverse variance method of DerSimonian and Laird were used to generate pooled MDs for continuous outcomes.23 Stata (V.17, StataCorp) was used to conduct all quantitative analyses.24

Certainty assessment

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to grade the certainty of evidence (COE) for each comparison, category of outcome and study design.25 COE was assessed by one investigator and reviewed by another. Differences in COE assessment were adjudicated between reviewers or the larger team. Details of the GRADE approach can be found in online supplemental material, appendix C.

Results

Literature search yield

The literature search yielded 4363 unique records and 4240 records were excluded after title and abstract review. The full text of 123 articles was reviewed and a total of 13 studies reported in 17 publications published between 2005 and 2023 were included. 13 studies were included for the efficacy question and 8 studies for the safety question. Of the included studies, five compared MACI to MF, seven compared OATS to MF and one compared cell-free implants to MF/chondroplasty. No eligible comparative studies evaluating OCA were identified. MACI compared with MF

Study and population characteristics

Five studies compared the effectiveness of MACI to MF; three were RCTs26,28 and two were NRSIs.29 30 Two of the studies used a porcine scaffold for MACI procedures,26 28 two studies used an alternative scaffold27 29 and one study did not report the matrix type used.30

The 3 RCTs were conducted from 2000 to 2019, sample sizes ranged from 30 to 144 and were rated as low28 or some concerns26 27 for risk of bias. Two of the three RCTs were funded entirely by industry, 27 28and the remaining RCT did not report the study sponsor.26 The RCTs enrolled patients from the age of 18 to mid-50s, and the mean age ranged from 33 to 46 years.26,28 Study inclusion criteria allowed a range of cartilage defect sizes from 1 cm2 to 10 cm2, with actual mean defect size ranging from 3 to 5 cm2,27 28 in the four studies reporting this information. The majority of study participants incurred cartilage injuries from acute trauma or sport (range 46%–79%), followed by chronic degeneration (25%–33%) and osteochondritis dissecans (range 6%–17%). Most participants had defects of the medial or lateral femoral condyle.

The two NRSI studies29 30 were conducted from 2012 to 2018, were rated as high RoB, had sample sizes of 144 and 254 participants and were both funded by industry. One study29 was a matched-pair analysis using data from two trials. The other NRSI used claims data and only required that patients had an eligible chondral defect repair procedure in the past 2 years.30 In one NRSI, all defects were located on the femur.23 The other NRSI did not report defect location.24

Findings from RCTs

Patient-reported outcomes

All three RCTs reported statistically significant improvements in the MACI group compared with the MF group on measures of knee symptoms and function (Cincinnati Knee Rating System over 5 years,28 International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) over 2 years27 and Lysholm score over 2 years27). One of these RCTs also reported numerical improvements in IKDC favouring MACI, but these findings were not statistically significant.28 Follow-up scores for Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) subscales of pain, symptoms QOL and return to sport were not reported, though authors did report statistically significant improvements in these subscales favouring MACI compared with MF.27 28

Response, treatment failure, reoperations

Clinical response to surgery was defined based on achieving a threshold change in different PROs for the two studies reporting this outcome.26 28 In both studies, there was a statistically significant greater response for MACI compared with MF over 12–24 months. There were no significant differences observed for treatment failure, defined as reoperations within 2 years, in one RCT.28

Harms

All RCTs reported AEs. Results were mixed for two RCTs, such that a greater number of AEs occurred in the MF group for one study28 and a greater number occurred in the MACI group in the other study.27 The remaining RCT reported only one event overall but did not report by group.26 Common AEs included knee pain and joint swelling. SAEs were reported in two RCTs (2–11 events for the MACI group and 0–19 events for the MF group).26 27 SAEs reported included deep vein thrombosis, septic arthritis and muscle atrophy.

Subgroups

In one RCT, there were more responders in the MACI group than in the MF group among participants less than 34.5 years of age compared with older participants, participants with lesions larger than 4 cm2 compared with smaller lesions and in male participants compared with female participants.28

Findings from NRSIs

Patient-reported outcomes

Only one study reported PROs. The MD from baseline to 2 years for the total KOOS and for the KOOS return to sport and QOL subscales favoured the MACI group by 10–14 points, a clinically significant difference.29 Authors reported similar findings for the IKDC score.29

Response, treatment failure, reoperations

Response, which was measured as a 10-point or more improvement in KOOS total score from baseline, was reported by one study.29 94% of MACI patients responded to treatment at 2 years compared with 65% of MF patients (p<0.01).29 Reoperations were less common in the MACI group than in the MF group in one study (12% vs 22%, p<0.05).30 In the other study, unplanned surgeries after the initial treatment were numerically more common in the MACI group (six participants, 8.3%) than in the MF group (three participants, 4.2%), but no statistical significance testing was conducted.29 There were no treatment failures in either group of the study.29

Harms

Neither study specifically reported AEs or SAEs. In one study, one of the unplanned surgeries in the MACI group was assessed as treatment related (compression syndrome caused by overtightened sutures).29

MACI compared with sham surgery or conservative management

No studies comparing MACI to sham surgery or conservative management were identified.

OATS compared with MF

Study population and characteristics

The five RCTs were conducted from 2005 to 2018 and were rated as some concerns31,34 or high35 RoB. Sample sizes ranged from 25 to 109 participants. Funding sources were not reported for any study. For the three studies that included age as part of their eligibility criteria, two studies allowed patients between the ages of 18 and 5033 34; the other study was exclusively conducted in participants younger than 18 years with osteochondritis dissecans.32 Study inclusion criteria allowed a range of defect size from 2 cm2 to 6 cm2 for three studies32,34 and 1 cm2 to 4 cm2 for two studies.31 35 Actual mean defect size across studies ranged from 2.6 cm2 to 3.6 cm2. The most common mechanism of injury was trauma followed by osteochondritis dissecans. Participants had defects on the medial femoral condyle, lateral femoral condyle or trochlea.

The two NRSIs36,38 were rated as high RoB and were conducted between 2012 and 2020. Sample sizes ranged from 96 to 203 participants and each used data obtained from a patient registry at a single institution. Funding sources were not reported for either study. Participants aged 15–60 years were eligible for the two NRSIs. Only one study used defect size as an eligibility criterion (1–6 cm2).36 Most participants had defects on the medial femoral condyle or lateral femoral condyle; a smaller number of participants had defects on the trochlea, patella, or lateral tibial plateau.

Findings from RCTs

Patient-reported outcomes

Three RCTs33,35 reported the Lysholm score and the pooled estimate for between-group difference change from baseline score from meta-analysis was 3.60 (95% CI −9.66 to 16.85; 112 participants; I2=84.6%; online supplemental material, appendix I). A clinically meaningful difference on this score ranges from 3.7 to 12.39 40 One additional RCT31 reported greater Hospital for Special Surgery scores in the OATS group compared with MF at 1 and 3 years that were statistically significant.

Response, treatment failure, reoperations

The one RCT reporting response (Lysholm score greater than or equal to 80) found more participants undergoing OATS met response criteria compared with MF (60% vs 20%, p=0.01).33 Treatment failure was defined differently across three RCTs (knee replacement, symptomatic after rehabilitation and any revision surgery) and at follow-up times ranging from 2 years to 15 years.31,33 All RCTs reporting treatment failure had few events in each group, with fewer failures in the OATS group but no statistical significance testing was conducted. Reoperations were measured in three RCTs,31 34 35 in which all studies reported few and similar numbers of events.

Harms

Among the three RCTs reporting any AEs, two reported few events.31 33 The third RCT reported individual AEs including knee pain, joint swelling and crepitation that were higher in the MF group but no statistical significance testing was conducted.32 No RCTs reported SAEs.

Subgroups

For two RCTs, within the MF group, patients with defects >2 cm2 had significantly worse clinical results than lesions <2 cm231 or <3 cm2,32 using the ICRS (International Cartilage Regeneration and Joint Preservation Society) score. This association was not observed within the OATS group. In one study, for patients in the MF group, lesions in the central part of the medial condyle had worse clinical outcomes, using the ICRS score, than other weight-bearing parts of the knee joint. Outcomes for patients in the OATS group were similar regardless of the location of the lesion.31

Findings from NRSIs

Patient-reported outcomes

One study reported the IKDC measure, which captures symptom and function; scores were not statistically different at follow-up over 2 and 5 years.40 Authors of this study observed similar results for the KOOS ADL subscore.

Response, treatment failure, reoperations

No studies reported outcomes of response or reoperations. One study reported on treatment failures defined as Lysholm score less than 65 or ipsilateral knee replacement.37 Treatment failures were greater in the MF groups compared with the OATS group (OATS 8.4 years vs MF 4.0 years, p=0.011), as well as having a shorter mean time to failure (OATS 8.4 years vs MF 4.0 years, p=0.011).37

Harms

Harms were not reported.

Subgroups

One NRSI conducted a survival analysis of treatment failure and reported improved results in the OATS group compared with the MF group for ages younger than 51 compared with older ages and lesion sizes less than 5 cm2 compared with larger sizes.37

OATS compared with sham surgery or conservative management

No studies comparing OATS to sham surgery or conservative management were identified.

Cell-free implants compared with MF/chondroplasty

One RCT was identified comparing a cell-free aragonite implant used to plug osteochondral lesions to a comparator group of MF or chondroplasty, described by the authors as the surgical standard of care.41

Study population and characteristics

The study was conducted between 2017 and 2019 and was rated as high RoB because of lack of information in the randomisation domain and baseline differences in disease severity. The sample size was 251 and enrolled patients were between ages 21 and 75 years. The study was funded entirely by industry. 59% of participants in the cell-free implant group and 49% of participants in the MF/chondroplasty group had a defect size greater than 3 cm2. Defect location was not reported.

Findings

Patient-reported outcomes

The RCT reported a KOOS total score (MD 22.5 (95% CI 17.0 to 28.0), p<0001 at all timepoints, 6, 12, 18, 24 months). KOOS subdomains of pain, ADL and QOL were reported to have a greater increase in all scores for the cell-free implant group compared with the MF group, though absolute values and statistical significance were not reported.

Response, treatment failure, reoperations

Response, defined as an overall improvement in KOOS total score of 30 or more, was greater in the cell-free implant group compared with MF (77.8% vs 33.6%, p=0.0001). The outcome of treatment failure (any secondary procedure, including joint injections) was comparable between groups. The authors did not report on reoperations.

Harms

Over 24 months, fewer individuals in the cell-free implant group experienced adverse events than the MF group (59% vs 77%, p<0.01). Few SAEs related to either treatment were reported.

Cell-free implants compared with sham surgery or conservative management

No studies comparing cell-free implants to sham surgery or conservative management were identified.

Discussion

Summary of the evidence

Studies evaluating MACI generally reported improved PROs and responder rates compared with MF. Effectiveness outcomes for OATS were comparable to MF with the exception of higher response rates and lower rates of treatment failure for OATS. Evidence for cell-free implants suggested greater effectiveness and fewer harms compared with MF, though it was limited to one study. A visual representation of COE ratings for all comparisons and outcomes graded is provided in online supplemental material, appendix G.

For the MACI versus MF comparison, the COE was moderate among RCTs for greater effectiveness of MACI compared with MF for PROs and response to treatment. NRSIs reported similar results, though with very low COE primarily driven by high risk of bias and small study samples resulting in imprecision. Both RCTs and NRSIs reported comparable effectiveness of MACI and MF for treatment failure, reoperations and harms. By excluding obsolete ACI procedures that are included in most systematic reviews, this review was able to draw conclusions about the comparative effectiveness of the most contemporary and prevalent cell-based regeneration technique.

Effectiveness outcomes, including PROs from RCTs comparing OATS with MF, were similar (low to very low COE), with the exception for the outcomes of response to treatment and treatment failure (low and very low COE favouring OATS). One NRSI also reported less treatment failure in the OATS group (low COE). The reasons for low COE were generally related to a body of evidence with high risk of bias studies.

In the one RCT comparing a cell-free implant to MF or chondroplasty, the cell-free implant group was more effective as measured by PROs and response to therapy (moderate COE) and with no difference in treatment failure (low to very low COE). AEs were lower in the cell-free implant group and SAEs were comparable (both low COE). Because evidence for cell-free implants was limited to a single study, long-term durability and generalisability remain uncertain. No comparative studies of the fourth intervention specified for this review, OCA, were identified. OCA requires a size- and location-matched donor and cadaveric tissue is only viable for a short amount of time, which may have limited the feasibility of conducting a comparative study of OCA. Additionally, the lack of comparative studies of OCA may be related to the relevance of MF as a comparator. OCA is often selected for patients with larger lesions, for which arthroplasty would be considered the alternative treatment, not MF. Similarly, no studies with the comparators sham surgery or conservative management were identified. Patients who have failed conservative therapy may be more likely to present for these procedures.

MF is the most common procedure performed to repair chondral defects in clinical practice and is often used as a ‘standard of care’ comparator for more advanced procedures like MACI and OATS in comparative effectiveness research.41 However, MF may not be appropriate for some lesions based on size, depth or location. Comparative effectiveness studies are not always based on lesion-specific characteristics (eg, eligibility criteria allowing a wide range of lesion sizes), and few studies presented results stratified by lesion size or location, providing little evidence from comparative studies about which procedure to perform based on these characteristics. Furthermore, even in circumstances where one procedure may be more effective, considerations like time required for rehabilitation and preferences for return to sport may drive procedure selection. Thus, these results should be interpreted in light of the need to individualise care to different clinical contexts.

A limited number of studies reported harms, suggesting that harms may have been under-reported. The small number of harms events resulted in imprecision and downgrading of COE.

Contemporary reviews have reported improved outcomes for MACI and OATS procedures, including PROs and return to sport.42,44 However, these reviews included several studies of ACI and single-arm studies; our review provides more applicable evidence for policy-makers and clinicians and improves the ability to estimate causal effects rather than associations.45 Clinical decision making involves comparing multiple treatment options and comparative evidence better supports patient-provider decision-making than single-arm data.

Limitations

This systematic review included many RCTs and NRSIs with high risk of bias because of lack of transparency about the randomisation process, limited adjustments to address confounding and not reporting about missing data or how missing data was handled. Confounding in the NRSI evidence for this topic is particularly problematic because the selection of procedure is often based on clinical characteristics of the defect and surgeon experience or preference, both of which may be related to the outcome. Many of the studies included had extended follow-up times, which is often associated with significant attrition, and many studies did not report the number of patients with follow-up data available at various timepoints. Studies with small sample sizes also resulted in imprecise effect estimates.

In some cases, study eligibility criteria included a wider range of defect characteristics than would be typically considered for a particular procedure (eg, MF performed for a defect >4 cm2 with subchondral bone involvement). A range of follow-up times across the evidence base, ranging from 6 months to 15 years, made it difficult to systematically compare the effectiveness of chondral defect procedures over time. For example, two procedures may have similar reoperation rates 1 year after surgery, but over an extended follow-up, the difference in longevity of the procedure may become more apparent, especially when compared with MF.

This review was limited to peer-reviewed, English-language studies identified through searches of PubMed and the Cochrane Library, which may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant evidence indexed in other databases, though this is unlikely given no comments about missed studies during multiple rounds of peer review and public comment were received. Only validated measures for disease-specific PRO were eligible, while general QOL outcomes were excluded. This review included only comparative study designs, which increases the ability to draw causal inferences but may not offer a comprehensive assessment of longer-term benefits and harms, particularly for less frequently employed treatments like OCA. Data extraction was performed by a single reviewer with independent verification by a second reviewer, rather than duplicate independent extraction, which may increase the risk of undetected extraction errors. Strengths of this review include evaluating procedures commonly performed or available to clinicians; for example, excluding older versions of ACI provides a more relevant synthesis of the third-generation procedure currently used in practice. Additionally, other systematic reviews examine a single procedure or a procedure for a specific location (eg, the patellofemoral joint), while this review is inclusive of all locations.

Future research and practice needs

Studies with more robust methodology are needed to increase the certainty of the evidence. Reducing the high risk of bias in future studies includes thorough consideration of confounding factors, reporting of missing data, and use of study designs and statistical methods to limit bias.

The PROs reported were variable across studies and treatment failure and response outcomes were defined with different threshold values of PROs for each study; a standardised set of outcomes would allow for more robust quantitative synthesis. With the exception of the Tegner score and KOOS-ADL domains, measures of daily function are usually incorporated into an overall score. No studies reported on time to return to work or sport, or rehabilitation time, which are particularly important outcomes given that many rehabilitation programmes were reported to last 6–12 months with the goal of returning the individual to high-impact sports. Measuring time to pre-injury function and return to work would expand understanding of the effectiveness of cartilage repair and restoration procedures in a broader population, such as older adults, important factors in clinical and policy decisions.

Only 8 of the 13 included studies reported harms; more robust and systematic ascertainment of harms would facilitate pooling across studies and would likely increase the COE ratings that could be assigned to harm outcomes. Future trials should report harms using validated systems (eg, Clavien-Dindo for surgical complications, International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis bleeding assessment tool) to improve comparability.

No clinical practice guidelines from orthopaedic surgery societies discuss the use of articular cartilage repair procedures.46,51 The development and publication of guidelines on articular cartilage repair procedures would support improved clinical decision-making, advise future research efforts (eg, recommendations for outcome measures and definitions or prioritisation of research gaps) and inform policy decisions.

Conclusion

MACI is probably more effective than MF based on moderate COE from RCTs and very low to low COE from NRSIs. OATS and MF may have similar effectiveness (low COE for RCTs and very low for NRSIs) on most but not all measures. MACI and OATS may have comparable harms to MF (low and very low COE). Evidence for a cell-free implant suggests greater effectiveness and fewer harms compared with MF (moderate and low COE, respectively). Rigorous study design, standard effectiveness outcomes and consistent reporting of harms would strengthen the evidence base for these procedures.
