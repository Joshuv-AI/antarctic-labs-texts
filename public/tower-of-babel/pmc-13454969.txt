Delayed diagnosis of aluminum soda‐can cover impact in the upper esophagus of a toddler: A rare cause of chronic feeding difficulty and recurrent aspiration
Authors: Saleh Ibrahim Alsuqayh, Homoud Alhebbi, Mohammad Gaafar Mohammed, Mohammad Alamrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractForeign body ingestion is a common occurrence in young children and may present with a wide range of nonspecific symptoms. Diagnosis can be challenging, particularly when the ingestion is unwitnessed and imaging studies are inconclusive. We present a case of a child with recurrent hospital admissions for respiratory symptoms that did not improve with standard management, along with repeated choking episodes that led the family to rely solely on milk feeds and avoid solid foods. Despite normal chest X‐rays and magnetic resonance imaging findings, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed an aluminum soda‐can cover lodged in the upper esophagus.

INTRODUCTION

Foreign body ingestion is a common occurrence in the pediatric population, particularly among toddlers and infants exploring their environment orally. 1 While the majority of ingested objects pass through the gastrointestinal tract without incident, certain materials—especially metallic or sharp‐edged items—can remain undetected and lead to subtle but progressive clinical symptoms. 2 Here, we report a rare and delayed presentation of a 1‐year‐old child who developed progressive feeding difficulty over 4 months, ultimately found to have ingested a piece of an aluminum can. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by non‐specific symptoms and the importance of considering foreign body ingestion in infants with unexplained feeding disturbances.

CASE REPORT

A 1‐year‐old previously healthy boy presented with a 4‐month history of choking and non‐bilious vomiting after eating solid foods. During this period, he had multiple admissions for aspiration pneumonia. His mother reported significant improvement after discontinuing solid foods and switching to formula milk.

He was evaluated in several clinics, and multiple chest X‐rays were performed, all of which were unremarkable. Because of persistent symptoms, a barium swallow study was obtained, which showed dilatation of the upper esophagus at the lower cervical level with a transition zone of contrast, raising suspicion for an esophageal stricture. The study also demonstrated severe gastroesophageal reflux. A chest magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was subsequently performed, as congenital esophageal stenosis was high on the differential diagnosis, and imaging was required to determine whether the stenosis was muscular or cartilaginous. However, it demonstrated a collapsed esophagus without definitive characterization of the lesion

Based on these findings, we proceeded with upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy under anesthesia using a cuffed endotracheal tube. A pediatric scope (8 mm) was introduced, and at approximately 13 cm from the mouthpiece, there was severe narrowing with an opening of only 3–4 mm surrounded by extensive granulation tissue. At this stage, it was difficult to determine the underlying cause of the stenosis. Dilatation using an 8 mm Savary dilator was initiated, after which the pediatric scope was reintroduced, revealing a metallic foreign body resembling an aluminum soda‐can cover embedded within the granulation tissue. Several attempts were made to remove the foreign body, but it could not be retrieved initially and migrated slightly down the esophagus. The patient then experienced desaturation and possible aspiration, prompting the anesthesia team to stabilize him with suctioning, a change of the endotracheal tube, nebulized Ventolin, and intravenous dexamethasone. After stabilization, the procedure was resumed, but the stenotic segment was still too tight to allow removal of the foreign body. Therefore, sequential dilatations with Savary dilators (sizes 9, 10, and 11 mm) were performed over a guidewire and fluoroscopic guidance. After successful dilatation to 11 mm, a regular adult scope (outer diameter 9.8 mm) was introduced, and after several careful attempts, the foreign body was successfully removed using rat‐tooth forceps. Reassessment with the regular scope showed mild to moderate bleeding at the site, which resolved spontaneously within a few minutes. The scope was advanced to the second part of the duodenum, and no additional lesions were seen. The patient required oxygen therapy, close monitoring, a PPI and back‐to‐back Ventolin nebulizations postoperatively, and remained under observation with persistent wheezing and crepitations but maintained an oxygen saturation of 92%.

After that, all imaging studies (X‐ray, barium study, and MRI chest) were reviewed with two radiology consultants, and they did not find a foreign body in the esophagus. The most likely explanation is that the low density of aluminum made it difficult to visualize on routine imaging.

After foreign body removal, the child presented to the emergency department on two separate occasions with recurrent vomiting and drooling. On both occasions, repeat endoscopy was required, and esophageal dilatation was performed for stricture management. For the last 3 months after the last dilatation session, he has been doing fine with normal feeding.

DISCUSSION

Foreign body ingestion is a frequent problem in pediatrics, particularly in children between 6 months and 3 years of age, due to their natural tendency to explore objects orally. Clinical presentation can vary widely, ranging from mild, non‐specific symptoms such as drooling, vomiting, or dysphagia to severe complications like aspiration pneumonia or airway compromise. Because ingestion is often unwitnessed, diagnosis can be challenging and is sometimes delayed. 2 , 3

In our patient, the prolonged history of choking and vomiting after solids, along with recurrent aspiration pneumonia, suggested an obstructive esophageal process. However, multiple imaging studies—including chest X‐rays, a barium swallow, and even MRI—failed to reveal the underlying pathology. This reflects an important limitation of standard imaging: certain objects, such as thin aluminum or plastic materials, may be radiolucent and therefore undetectable by conventional radiography. 2 This underscores the need to maintain a high index of suspicion for foreign body ingestion in any child with persistent esophageal symptoms, even when imaging is inconclusive.

The barium swallow in our case revealed a dilated upper esophagus with a transition zone and severe gastroesophageal reflux, findings that warranted endoscopic evaluation. Endoscopy identified extensive granulation tissue causing significant luminal narrowing, behind which an aluminum soda‐can cover was eventually uncovered. This case illustrates a well‐described complication of delayed diagnosis: long‐standing esophageal foreign bodies can incite local inflammation, leading to granulation tissue formation, fibrosis, and stricture. 2 , 3

Management of esophageal strictures secondary to chronic impaction often requires endoscopic dilatation, as in our patient, who needed repeated sessions after foreign body removal. This highlights the importance of careful follow‐up to monitor for post‐removal complications and ensure adequate restoration of esophageal patency. 3

CONCLUSION

Foreign body ingestion is one of the most common occurrences in pediatrics and can present with a wide range of symptoms. Therefore, a high index of suspicion is essential. Our case highlights the importance of considering esophageal foreign bodies in children with persistent vomiting, choking, or recurrent aspiration pneumonia, even when imaging appears normal. Early endoscopic evaluation is vital to prevent complications such as granulation tissue formation and esophageal stricture. Prompt recognition and timely intervention can greatly improve outcomes and reduce morbidity.
