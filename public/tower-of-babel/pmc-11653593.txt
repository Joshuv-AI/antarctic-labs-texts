Effect of COVID-19 restriction measures on multidrug resistant tuberculosis case notifications and treatment outcomes at treatment centres in Uganda
Authors: Pamela Okwir Apio, Lydia Nakiyingi, Charles Batte, Pius Mukisa, John Mukisa, Monica Okwir Acen, Andrew Weil Semulimi, Achilles Katamba, Joan Nakayaga Kalyango
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultidrug resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) is a global public health threat. In 2021, an incidence of 3.6% was reported among new TB patients, and 18% was reported among previously treated patients. The emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic impacted the health sector, although little is known about the effect of restrictive COVID-19 measures on MDR-TB case notifications and treatment outcomes in Uganda. This study aimed to assess the effect of COVID-19 restriction measures on MDR-TB case notifications and treatment outcomes at treatment centres in Uganda.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study in which a total of 483 participants were enrolled—238 before (March 2018-February 2020) and 245 during (March 2020-February 2022) COVID-19 restrictions. The data were extracted from the Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis (DR-TB) Health Management Information System (HMIS), and patient charts, and census sampling was employed. Interrupted time series (ITSA) was used to compare MDR-TB case notifications and treatment outcomes.ResultsBefore the COVID-19 restrictions, the majority 58.0% were aged less than or equal to 38 years whereas during the restrictions, the majority 51.8% were aged greater than 38 years. A total of 238 cases of MDRTB were reported before, and 245 cases were reported during the restrictions. There was no immediate (β2; 0.134) or sustained (β3; 0.494) impact of COVID-19 restriction measures on monthly MDR-TB case notifications. The mean number of monthly MDR-TB notifications was similar for the 3-month period before (11.0 cases per month) and during (10.0 cases per month) the COVID-19 restrictions (p-value 0.661). The proportions of patients who achieved successful MDR-TB treatment before (81.5%) and during (81.7%) COVID-19 restriction was not significantly different (p- value < 0.001). During the COVID-19 restrictions, not being married (aPR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.74—0.97) and treatment delay greater than 7 days (aPR 0.87, 95% CI 0.78—0.96) were negatively associated with successful treatment outcomes.ConclusionRestrictive COVID-19 measures did not affect MDR-TB case notifications or treatment outcomes. Not being married and having a treatment delay greater than 7 days reduced the chances of a successful treatment outcome during COVID-19. The WHO and MoH should continue strengthening active case finding, contact screening and community engagement to consolidate MDR-TB control and management in preparation for similar future pandemics.Keywords: COVID-19, Multidrug resistant tuberculosis, Case notifications, Treatment outcome

Background

Multidrug resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) is a serious public health threat globally and continues to undermine the strides made in combating the tuberculosis epidemic. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Report 2023, more than 3.6% of new cases and 18% of previously treated TB cases of MDR-TB were reported in 2021, and approximately 161,746 MDR-TB/Rifampicin Resistant Tuberculosis (RR-TB) patients were started on treatment [1]. This was an 11% decrease compared to the number of people who started treatment in 2019 [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa, which has the highest burden of HIV/AIDS and tuberculosis, had an incidence of 7.3% in 2018 [2]. Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) was declared a pandemic in 2019 and continues to have devastating effects on the global health structure. To date, more than 620 million people have suffered from this infection [3]. In response to this highly infectious virus, the WHO recommended restrictive measures such as lockdowns to disrupt transmission. These measures (ban on public transport and curfews), though effective, impeded the implementation of strategies designed to fight longstanding epidemics such as tuberculosis (TB) through the WHO’s end TB strategy [4]. The similarities in the diagnosis, presentation, and stigmatization of COVID-19 and TB could have led to delays in MDR-TB care [5]. A study conducted in Vietnam showed a 1% decrease in MDR-TB case notifications between 2019 (before COVID-19) and 2020 (during COVID-19) [6]. A study conducted in Peru reported an increase in MDR-TB cases despite the effect of COVID-19 [7]. A study conducted in Uganda by Kadota and colleagues reported a 43% decrease in TB notifications during the COVID-19 lockdown, and mortality was expected to increase by 14% [8]. Another study in Uganda showed poor adherence to TB treatment during COVID-19 and this was mainly due to isolation and neglect by family members, relatives and health care workers secondary to restrictions [9]. Although a similar study in Jinja showed no significant impact on TB treatment [10]. Little is known about the impact of COVID-19 restrictions on MDRTB in Uganda. This study aimed to assess the effect of COVID-19 restriction measures on MDR-TB case notifications and treatment outcomes and to assess predictors of successful treatment outcomes during COVID-19 restriction measures in Uganda. The findings from this study will inform and guide the WHO and Uganda Ministry of Health, on how to better manage, prevent transmission and limit poor outcomes of MDR-TB amidst a pandemic, thus contributing to the WHO’s strategy to end TB by 2030.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective cohort study involving a review of records from the Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis (DR-TB) Health Management Information System (HMIS) and patient charts. In this study, the 2-year period before the COVID-19 restrictions were implemented was between March 2018 and February 2020, and the period during which the COVID-19 restrictions were implemented was between March 2020 and February 2022. Uganda had different restrictive measures, including total lockdowns; the first was from March 2020 to May 2020, and the second was from June 2021 to July 2021. A ban on all public and private transport, except that of essential workers, the closure of schools, places of work, and social gatherings [11], were among the restrictive measures. The data were collected from all 17 MDR-TB treatment centres located across the country. Treatment centres offer free diagnosis and treatment services, including free medication, directly observed therapy (DOT) and follow-up for all MDR-TB patients. All presumptive TB patients were tested for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and resistance to rifampicin using the Xpert mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB)/resistance to rifampicin (RIF) assay and drug susceptibility test (DST) to determine whether the patients had RR-TB, MDR-TB or extensively resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB). These MDR-TB centres are controlled by the Uganda National TB and Leprosy Program under the Ministry of Health.

Participants

Eligibility criteria

We retrieved files of patients whose results from culture and DST indicated multidrug resistance between March 2018 and February 2022. Patient records without dates for culture and first line DST were excluded.

Sample size estimation

A sample size of 273 was calculated using a formula for comparing two proportions.

We based the computation on assumptions of a 5% level of significance and 80% power. A pilot study was conducted at Mulago National Referral Hospital, and MDR-TB case reports from 2018 to 2019 (before) and from 2020 to 2021 (during) were used to determine the means in the different groups. We adjusted for clustering and 10% missing data. However, census sampling was employed, and all 483 patients were included.

Variables

The independent variables considered in this study included sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex, marital status, tribe, occupation, and residence status; clinical characteristics such as health facility type, patient type, adverse drug reactions, baseline weight, treatment regimen, DST results/resistance status; and HIV status. Treatment delay was defined as the difference between the date of initiation of second-line treatment and the date of diagnosis. These patients were further categorized based on the acceptable delay of 7 days [12, 13]. Diagnostic delay was defined as time from MDR-TB presumption (Rifampicin resistance detected on GeneXpert) to diagnosis (time when MDRTB is confirmed on culture and first line DST) and was categorized based on the practical guidance for clinical microbiology [14] which suggests that 42 days required for liquid culture.

The dependent variables were MDR-TB case notifications and MDR-TB treatment outcomes. MDR-TB treatment outcomes were classified as successful (cured or treatment completed) or unsuccessful (treatment failed, patient was lost to follow-up, died or was not evaluated).

Data collection procedures

An investigator-designed data extraction tool was used to retrieve data from the DR-TB HMIS and patient files.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was performed in STATA version 14. 0. Patient characteristics were compared before and during restrictive measures using the chi-square test. Changes in the number of monthly MDR-TB case notifications during the first and second total COVID-19 restrictions were assessed using the single-group Newey approach (ITSA) with two interruptions [15] using an ordinary least squares regression (ITSA) approach [16]. The assumptions for ITSA were assessed;1) Autocorrelation with a Durbson-Watson statistic which we adjusted for with a lag of 1, 2) Stationarity of the data using the Augmented Dick Fuller Test ,3) Sufficiency of the time points before and after the intervention/disruption and 4) Linearity of the relationship between the outcome and our time points. We controlled for seasonality by fixing the calendar month effect and adjusted for factors by fitting the Poisson model. The mean number of MDR-TB case notifications 3 months before (December 2019-February 2020) and 3 months after (March 2020-May 2022) the COVID-19 restrictions were compared using a dependent groups t test.

The proportions of successful treatment outcomes before and during COVID-19 restrictions were compared using McNemar’s test. These time periods were used to ensure equal durations. A generalized linear model with family (Poisson) and link (log) information with clustered robust standard errors was used. The cluster variable was the facility type. Bivariate analysis was performed, and variables with p values less than 0.20, variables that were significantly different at baseline and those consistently reported to be significant in the literature were included in the multivariable model. Multivariate analysis was performed using the stepwise backwards method, and interaction assessment was performed using the likelihood ratio test. Assessment of confounding factors was performed, and a percentage change in the PR greater than 10% indicated that there was confounding. All confounders were left in the final model. A p value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Missing data

Variables with missing data more than 20% were dropped from analysis. All the variables with missing data less than 20% were indicated in the footnote.

Quality control measures

A pretest of study tools was conducted at Mulago National Referral Hospital MDR-TB treatment centres on 20 participant records. Research assistants at the MDR-TB centres underwent an hour-long training by the study PI on study protocol and data collection procedures to minimize errors. Double data entry was done and PI continually cross-checked data collection tools for completeness.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 518 MDR-TB patient records were screened, and 483 patients were included in the study (Figs. 1), 238 were before the COVID-19 restrictions and 245 were during the COVID-19 restrictions.

Most patient characteristics before and during COVID-19 restriction were comparable, except for age (p value 0.031), adverse drug reactions (p value 0.046), treatment regimen (p value < 0.001) and DST results/resistance (p value < 0.001), which were significantly different. Before the COVID-19 restrictions, the majority 58.0% (138) were younger than or equal to 38 years, whereas during the restrictions, the majority 51.8% (127) were older than 38 years. Changes in MDR-TB case notifications

There were 238 and 245 MDR-TB case notifications before and during COVID-19 restriction measures. A slight increase of 2.9% was observed during the COVID-19 restrictions. There was no significant difference in the mean monthly MDR-TB case notifications before and during COVID-19 restrictions (P-value 0.869).

There was also no significant difference when the mean monthly MDR-TB case notifications were compared 3 months before and 3 months during the COVID-19 restrictions. (P value = 0.661).

Figure 2 shows that there were no immediate (β2; 0.134) or sustained (β3; 0.494) changes in the number of monthly MDR-TB case notifications before and during COVID-19 restrictions.

A drastic increase in the number of cases from April 2020 to a peak in June 2020 was noted, followed by a sudden decrease in July 2020. During the second total lockdown (June 2021), the number of patients with monthly MDR-TB greatly decreased.

Changes in MDR-TB treatment outcomes

During the period before COVID-19 restrictions were implemented, 79.8% (190) of the patients were cured and 8.5% (20) died, whereas during COVID-19 restrictions, 75.1% (148) were cured and 11.2% (22) died. The proportions of successful treatment outcomes before and during COVID-19 restrictions were 81.5% (194) and 81.7% (161), respectively, and the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.590). Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis was done to assess for impact of COVID-19 restrictions on treatment outcomes. We used age since the age category because it was significantly different in the two groups (Table 1). Therefore, we developed a group for before and during COVID-19 restrictions for those aged less or equal to 38 years. The proportions of successful treatment outcomes before and during COVID-19 restrictions were 81.9% (113) and 84.5% (82), respectively, and the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.594).

For the second group of participants aged more than 38 years. The proportions of successful treatment outcomes before and during COVID-19 restrictions were 81.0% (81) and 79.2% (80), respectively, and the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.750).

A second sensitivity analysis was done for sex. The group for males; the proportions of successful treatment outcomes before and during COVID-19 restrictions were 78.9% (131) and 82.4% (117), respectively, and the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.442). The group for females; the proportions of successful treatment outcomes before and during COVID-19 restrictions were 87.5% (63) and 80.0% (44), respectively, and the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.250).

Discussion

There was no significant difference in the mean number of monthly multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) case notifications before and during COVID-19 restriction measures (p- value 0.661). A slight increment of 2.9% in the number of cases during the COVID-19 restriction measures was not significant. According to the ITSA, there was no impact of total lockdowns on the number of monthly MDR-TB case notifications. The proportions of successful MDR-TB treatment outcomes before [81.5% (95% CI: 73.9—84.9%)] and during [81.7% (95% CI: 75.1—86.9%)] restrictions were not significantly different (p-value 0.590). Neither being married nor having a treatment delay greater than 7 days were negatively associated with successful treatment outcomes. Treatment regimen, sex, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status, tribe, occupation, diagnostic delay, adverse drug reaction (ADR), and drug susceptibility test (DST) results/resistance were not significantly associated with successful treatment outcomes.

There was no immediate or sustained impact of the first or second total COVID-19 lockdowns on MDR-TB case notifications. Uganda experienced two periods of total lockdowns between March 2020 and May 2020 and between June 2021 and July 2021, with intermittent periods of partial lockdowns. The duration of the total lockdown could have been too short to cause an impact on MDR-TB case notifications. Updating and strengthening existing guidelines for TB management during the pandemic to include screening for both COVID-19 and MDR-TB could have contributed to this finding [17, 18]. Patients were still allowed to walk to the hospital or use public transport with clearance from the local council. The use of village health teams in the Ugandan care health system could have promoted treatment adherence during the pandemic. Village health teams are community members who bridge gap between health workers and the community. Their main roles include identification and linkage to care as well as distribution of treatment in the community [17, 18]. The results could have been affected by the long turnaround time for the phenotypic DST test [19], as more than 60% of participants in both periods had diagnostic delays greater than 42 days.

A slight increase of 2.9% in MDR-TB case notifications during COVID-19 restrictive measures was contrary to findings from a study conducted in Vietnam, where a 1% decrease in MDR-TB notifications was reported from 2019 to 2020 [6]. In Addis Ababa, DST results before (54%) and during (56.3%) restrictions did not change (p-value 0.29), although there was an increase of 27.7% in RR-TB case notifications (p-value 0.001) [20]. The difference is probably because our study only examined MDR-TB rather than RR-TB, which has a higher prevalence. Another study conducted in Kenya showed that 45% fewer people were tested for MDR-TB in 2020 than in 2019 [21]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reported a 6.9% decrease in MDR-TB from 2019 to 2020 [1]. This finding was contrary to that of our study because in Uganda, patients were allowed to travel to hospitals despite restrictions, and the total lockdown period was short.

There was no significant difference in the proportions of successful treatment outcomes before (81.5%) and during (81.7%) COVID-19 restrictive measures (p-value 0.590), although the proportions were high and in line with the WHO target of 75% [1]. In this study, all patients underwent DOT as shown in Table 1, this is when the patients take medication in the presence of a health worker. The lack of difference in the success rate could also be attributed to the strengthening of the guidelines by the Ministry of Health (MOH) [17], TB awareness programs and community drug delivery during the COVID-19 lockdown. Patients were also allowed to walk to the hospital and use public transport with clearance from local council 1, and incentives such as transport reimbursement given to patients during follow-up visits could have motivated them to adhere to treatment despite the restrictions [22] The availability of village health teams (VHTs)/ community health systemin the Uganda health care system could have improved treatment outcomes. The VHTs help bridge the gap between the community and health care system, and they also participate in patient referrals [23].

According to modelling analysis by stop TB partnership, the second-line treatment completion rate was expected to decrease by 25% in Kenya for a month after the lockdown, and the DST test results were expected to decrease by 5% [24].

A recent study conducted in Uganda showed that there was no effect of COVID-19 on the treatment success of TB. Despite the similarity in the findings with those from our study, this study assessed TB treatment success and was limited to only one TB treatment site in Uganda [10].

The odds of successful treatment outcome were 14% times lower among patients who were not married. This is attributable to lack of social support usually offered by partners during the treatment course. Our findings contrast with those of a study conducted in Nigeria, where patients who were not married were 3.6 times more likely to have a successful treatment outcome [25]. The odds of successful treatment outcome were 14% times lower among patients who had a treatment delay of more than 7 days. However, a similar study showed that treatment initiation was not significantly associated with treatment outcome [26]. This disparity is possibly due to the difference in the study periods and the presence of a lockdown in our study.

Limitations and strengths of the study

Based on the nature of the study design which involved reviewing of secondary data, some of the predictors of successful treatment outcomes, such as nonadherence, health facility factors, religion, alcohol consumption, and smoking status, were not assessed because they were not captured in the patients’ records.

Selection bias was due to missing data on date of DST results. Some patient records were torn and thus could not be captured, and some patient charts were lost.

The study had challenges of information bias. Some of the patient records had missing patient data, and these could not be captured.

Treatment regimen was categorized without basing on the duration of the treatment regimen.

However, despite these limitations, the study was powered due to the large sample size. The study used census sampling where all records of MDRTB patients who met the eligibility criteria were included. In addition, the study was conducted in all MDR-TB treatment centres in Uganda, making the findings generalizable to Uganda. It should also be noted that the single group ITSA had a limitation of lack of controls.

Conclusion and recommendation

In Uganda, COVID-19-associated restrictive measures such as lockdown had no significant effect on MDR-TB case notifications or treatment outcomes. Not being married and having a treatment delay greater than 7 days reduced the chances of a successful treatment outcome. The WHO and MoH should continue strengthening active case finding, contact screening and community engagement to consolidate MDR-TB control and management in preparation for similar pandemics in the future. Current practices including involvement of village health teams in screening and referrals should be encouraged. Different stake holders should offer support inform of incentives to promote treatment adherence. Early detection and prompt initiation of patients on treatment should be encouraged as this is associated with treatment success. MDR-TB patients should be offered social support from partners, family members and social workers to improve treatment outcomes.

Future research should consider incorporating qualitative data to enrich the understanding of patients’ experiences during the pandemic.
