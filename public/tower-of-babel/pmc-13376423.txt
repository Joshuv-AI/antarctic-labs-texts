Interplay of oxygen vacancies and lanthanide emitters enables reversible upconversion switching
Authors: Mingye Ding, Yongqi Guo, Jun Cao, Yu Yuan, Deqi Fan, Jingru Lai, Zixia Lin, Chaorong Li, Xiaofei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dynamic modulation of upconversion with spatiotemporal spectral features is essential for advanced optical multiplexing in anti-counterfeiting applications. However, achieving reversible dynamic control of upconversion emissions in a simple nanostructure of fixed composition under external stimulation remains challenging. Here we show that orthogonal upconversion luminescence in erbium-doped bismuth oxyhalides can be temporally modulated by exploiting the interplay of oxygen vacancies and erbium activators. Alternating ultraviolet irradiation and water bleaching enables reversible regulation of the upconversion emissions with high switching contrast and excellent fatigue resistance. Experimental and theoretical evidences reveal that the introduction of oxygen vacancies and lanthanide activators as well as their interactions contributes to the photochromism-induced dynamic modulation of orthogonal upconversion luminescence. Inspired by their special luminescent performance, the developed materials facilitate evolutionary upconversion color and intensity displays in both spatial and temporal dimensions, offering a promising route for high-level anti-counterfeiting and high-capacity optical storage.

Introduction

Counterfeiting phenomena are becoming a serious global concern that poses a threat to several aspects of social life, including politics, economics, and culture1,2. To fight against the increasing sophistication of counterfeiting, various luminescent materials (e. g. dye molecules3,4, coordination polymers5,6, carbon dots7,8, quantum dots9,10, lanthanide-doped nanocrystals11,12, and inorganic phosphors13–15) with diverse excitation modes (e. g. ultraviolet/near-infrared (UV/NIR) photoexcitation, X-ray irradiation) and tunable emission colors have been developed greatly for optical information encoding, in which the encrypted/hidden information with high spatial resolution can be revealed under the external stimulus. Comparatively, lanthanide-doped upconversion (UC) materials have been regarded as one of the most promising alternatives to traditional fluorescent anti-counterfeiting materials, owing to their attractive optical properties, such as broadly color-tunable emissions, excellent photostability, low pumping threshold, long lifetimes, and low background autofluorescence16–21. Especially, UC materials remain inactive under the irradiation of commonly used UV lamps, thus offering high security naturally.

To date, considerable efforts, including host lattice regulation22,23, energy transfer modulation24–27, and micro/nano-structure engineering28–30, have been devoted to the manipulation of UC luminescence (e.g., multicolor outputs under single-wavelength NIR excitation, orthogonal luminescence by switching NIR excitation wavelength, and multimodal emissions response to various external stimuli channels) for advanced anti-counterfeiting. Nevertheless, most of the UC codes are limited by their static monochromatic luminescence under a fixed excitation mode, which lacks temporal encryption information for hindering the development of next-generation anti-counterfeiting in both spatial and temporal dimensions.

To advance the technology of high-level anti-counterfeiting, recent developments of UC dynamic modulation strategies provide a tantalizing opportunity for optical multiplexing by integrating temporal encoding information in the spatial dimension. Generally, dynamic manipulation of UC luminescence with time-dependent color outputs or temporal UC evolutions is achieved by adjusting pulsed laser frequencies or excitation power densities31–34. These strategies often require complex excitation conditions and special structural design of UC materials, making them unsuitable for easily identifying the temporal UC anti-counterfeiting codes. A more effective solution lies in the utilization of external stimulations (e.g., optical, electric, magnetic, and thermal fields) to dynamically modulate UC luminescence for optical multiplexing, owing to the unique advantages of high coding capacity, confidentiality, and non-destructive readout capability35–39. However, the unsatisfactory UC performance (low UC efficiency, single-wavelength response, or limited color outputs), disappointing modulation behaviors (low luminescent switching contrast or slow response rate) of these systems, narrow scope of effective external stimuli, as well as a limited range of suitable UC hosts (mostly macroscopic bulk oxides), hinder their practical applications in high-security anti-counterfeiting with spatially and temporally resolved features. Despite considerable efforts, the reversible dynamic modulation of orthogonal UC emissions has not yet been achieved in a nano-semiconductor host under simple external stimulation. Consequently, achieving such real-time, reversible regulation of UC luminescence within a single nanomaterial platform remains a significant challenge.

In this work, inspired by photochromic semiconductor nanocrystals (e.g., TiO2, WO3) for optoelectronics applications40,41, we propose a strategy to achieve dynamic orthogonal UC luminescence modulation by incorporating erbium activators into bismuth oxyhalide (BiOX, X = Cl, Br and I) nano-semiconductor hosts (Fig. 1a). In our design, a defect engineering strategy is employed to synthesize BiOBr ultrathin nanosheets via a surfactant-assisted hydrothermal reaction. The confined two-dimensional structure and dramatically increased surface-to-volume ratio of the BiOBr sample intrinsically drive the formation of oxygen vacancies (denoted as OVs) in the host lattice, making it possible for the construction of color centers to achieve the photochromic phenomenon42,43. Meanwhile, the dopant of Er3+ ions also creates emission centers within the lattice for the realization of high-efficiency orthogonal UC emissions through switching of NIR excitations. By simply controlling the energy transfer from UC activators to OVs induced by their complex interactions, reversibly dynamic manipulation of UC emission color and intensity can be achieved within non-core-shell structured hosts. Upon excitation of 980 or 1532 nm laser, the designed BiOX:Er3+ samples exhibit steady green or red UC luminescence, respectively. Under 391 nm UV irradiation, the OVs sites receive photo-induced electrons to form color centers, resulting in the occurrence of radiative absorption energy transfer from Er3+ to color centers owing to spectral overlap between UC emissions and host adsorption. A recovery of UC luminescence can be achieved through water-induced bleaching reaction (Fig. 1b). Results

Microstructural characterization

In the proof-of-concept experiment, Er3+-doped BiOX samples were synthesized via a facile hydrothermal method. Taking BiOBr:Er3+ (denoted as BOBE-OV) for instance, the detailed microstructural characterization results are presented in Fig. 2, Supplementary Figs. 1–5. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the undoped and doped samples verify the high purity of the resulting phosphors without impurity phase (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Rietveld refinement result (Fig. 2a) and the relevant unit cell structure (Fig. 2b) also validate the successful preparation of single-phased crystals with a two-dimensional layered structure in the tetragonal P4/nmm space group (a = b = 3.926 Å, c = 8.103 Å; Supplementary Table 1). The similar ionic radius of Er3+ (r = 0.89 Å, CN = 6) and Bi3+ (r = 1.02 Å, CN = 6)44,45, along with the crystal field effect of the four-coordinated geometry to four equivalent oxygen atoms and four equivalent bromine atoms46, facilitates the preferential occupation of the Bi3+ sites by Er3+ dopants. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the BiOBr:Er3+ sample reveals that the product consists of square-shaped nanosheets (Supplementary Fig. 2a) with the particle size ranging from 20 nm to 100 nm. The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image (Supplementary Fig. 2b) reveals that the lattice distance of 0.278 nm corresponds to the (110) crystalline planes of tetragonal BiOBr. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Supplementary Fig. 2c) also demonstrates high crystallinity of the as-prepared BiOBr:Er3+ sample. Obvious signals of all the elements (Bi, Br, Er, and O) are detected by high-angle angular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), and these elements distribute uniformly over the nanoparticle (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Combining with the microstructural characterization results of the as-obtained BiOCl:Er3+ and BiOI:Er3+ samples (Supplementary Fig. 1b, Fig. 3), it can be concluded that BiOX:Er3+ nanosheets have been successfully synthesized. In our design, the introduction of OVs and erbium ions into the BiOBr host lattice has a significant role in the realization of UC dynamic modulation. To verify the existence of OVs and Er3+ emitters in the host, the in-depth structural analysis of the synthesized BiOBr:Er3+ nanosheets has been conducted, as shown in Fig. 2c–g. Compared with the BOBE sample (Supplementary Fig. 5), the atomic-resolution HADDF-STEM images of BOBE-OV nanosheets exhibit locally incomplete lattice fringes except for regular surface lattice fringes. Especially, some defect areas with diameters of about 1–2 nm can be clearly observed in the HADDF-STEM image of the OVs-rich BiOBr sample (Fig. 2c; marked with red circles), indicating the possible existence of OVs43. In the line-scan intensity profile of atomic-resolution HADDF-STEM (Fig. 2d) image along the [110] axis with a periodic Bi atomic distribution, the deeper valleys with low contrast intensity may arise from the misalignment of Bi atoms induced by the presence of OVs42,47. Typical vibration modes shown by Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 6) also confirm the changes in the short-range structural information of the samples, whereas BOBE-OV demonstrates a red-shifted band at 109 cm−1 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${A}_{1{{{\rm{g}}}}}^{2}$$\end{document}A1g2 internal Bi-Br stretching mode) with respect to the BOBE sample. This redshift phenomenon can be ascribed to the slight microstructural distortion induced by OVs48. In the high-resolution O 1 s X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra (Fig. 2e), a new peak at 531 eV that belongs to oxygen adsorbed at OVs sites of BOBE-OV can be detected49. Moreover, electronic paramagnetic resonance (ESR) analysis (Fig. 2f) and positron annihilation lifetime (PAL) spectroscopy (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Table 2) also confirm the presence of OVs in BOBE-OV but not in the BOBE sample. In addition, the characteristic analysis of elemental distribution (Supplementary Fig. 2d) and the intense UC luminescence from Er3+ in the BOBE-OV sample (Fig. 3a, b) strongly support the successful incorporation of Er3+ ions into the crystal lattice. Accordingly, our synthetic strategy involves a two-step process, which allows for the reliable production of OVs-rich BiOBr:Er3+ with intense orthogonal UC luminescence. In particular, the hydrothermal step primarily introduces OVs during the surfactant-controlled growth of BiOBr (Supplementary Fig. 7), while the subsequent thermal treatment removes residual organics absorbed on the surface of BiOBr and enhances crystallinity of BiOBr host under the premise of preserving oxygen vacancies, thereby dramatically improving UC performance of Er3+ ions (Supplementary Fig. 8). The optimal OV concentration, controlled by the PVP amount during synthesis and quantified by XPS, exhibits a volcano-shaped correlation with the UC modulation contrast, peaking at an OV content of 38.27% (achieved with 100 mg PVP) (Supplementary Fig. 9). These PVP-induced OVs are predominantly located on the surface, a characteristic dictated by the ultrathin morphology of the nanosheets and essential for their function in interfacial photochromic reactions.

Orthogonal UC luminescence behaviors

The Er3+-doped BiOBr samples exhibit the intense orthogonal UC luminescence with color-switched outputs, as evidenced by Fig. 3a, b. Interestingly, the emission color of the as-obtained BOBE-OV sample alters from green to red upon switching the excitation wavelength from 980 to 1532 nm, owing to the different intensity ratio of green to red emission. Differing from previous reports24,50–52, the orthogonal UC emissions of Er3+ ions with high spectral purity can be realized in a nano-sized host without complex core-shell structures. Under 808 nm laser excitation, the BOBE-OV sample exhibits green-dominated UC luminescence (similar to 980 nm excitation), thereby establishing an orthogonal luminescence relationship with the red-dominated UC emission under 1532 nm laser excitation (Supplementary Fig. 10). Through a series of optimization experiments, it shows that the optimal concentration of Er3+ emitters for achieving high-efficiency orthogonal UC emissions is at 5 mol% (Supplementary Fig. 11, Fig. 12). The upconversion quantum yields of BOBE-OV sample are determined to be about 0.41% for 980 nm laser excitation and 0.29% for 1532 nm laser excitation, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13, Table 3 and Note 0). Moreover, the orthogonal UC emissions could also be observed in the OVs-deficient BOBE sample, demonstrating that the existence of OVs has little effect on steady-state orthogonal UC behavior of Er3+ emitters (Supplementary Fig. 14). To understand the orthogonal UC mechanism, pump-power-dependent UC luminescence and theoretical investigation could be conducted, as presented in Supplementary Figs. 15–17 and Note 1. Analysis indicates that the observed orthogonal UC luminescence of Er3+ ions arises from the low-symmetry crystal field of the BiOBr host, which selectively modulates the competition between different relaxation pathways. It suppresses multi-phonon relaxation from the 4I11/2 to 4I13/2, thereby ensuring efficient green emission via a two-photon process under 980 nm laser excitation, while simultaneously enhancing non‑radiative decay from 4I9/2 to 4I11/2 and cross-relaxation processes that favor red emission via a three-photon process under 1532 nm laser excitation, resulting in the excitation-wavelength-dependent color-switchable UC luminescence (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Note 2).

It is observed that the orthogonal UC emissions of BOBE-OV samples can be reversibly manipulated under the external stimuli of UV light irradiation and water bleaching. Obviously, the UC emission intensities of BOBE-OV decrease gradually with increasing illumination time of 391 nm UV light and then increase progressively with the increment of water bleaching time. To understand the actions of OVs in water-induced decoloration reaction, the interaction between OVs sites and water molecules can be verified by the ESR technique and density functional theory (DFT) calculation. As evidenced by ESR results, the photoexcited charge carriers (e− and h+) can be generated in the OVs-rich BiOBr sample under UV illumination, which can be involved in redox reaction with oxygen-containing groups (e. g. H2O and O2) to form various radicals (•OH and •O2-) (Supplementary Fig. 35. Supplementary Note 4)57. Additionally, the calculated results clearly validate that water molecules are dissociatively absorbed on the OVs sites (Brønsted acid sites) of BOBE-OV surface to generate bridged hydroxyl groups, while molecularly absorbed on Bi sites (Lewis acid sites) of OVs-poor BOBE surface58,59. Thus, the enriched OVs on the BOBE-OV surface serve as Brønsted acid sites, facilitating the dissociation of H2O and adsorption of H + OH species (the product of H2O dissociation). These adsorbed water species are readily oxidized by photogenerated holes h+, thus promoting the release of electrons e− trapped in the color centers for the production of superoxide radical •O2− (Fig. 5a–e, Supplementary Figs. 36–39, Table 5, and Supplementary Note 5). Based on the above findings, the reversible photochromic mechanism is proposed as follows (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 42). Under the irradiation of UV light, the electrons in the valance band (VB) of BiOBr host could be excited to the conduction band (CB), leaving holes in the VB. The photogenerated electrons and holes are trapped by oxygen vacancies and Bi3+ ions, respectively, resulting in an increase in the content of oxygen vacancies and an obvious increase in the valence state of bismuth. Moreover, the capturing of electrons by oxygen vacancies also effectively promotes the photoinduced charge carriers, leading to the formation of color centers. This electron trapping is a reduction reaction, which changes the local electronic structure and creates a new, broad absorption band in the visible light region, resulting in the color change from white to black. In the water bleaching process, trapped holes are consumed by the photocatalytic oxidation reaction (H2O + h+ →\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\cdot {{{\rm{OH}}}}$$\end{document}⋅OH + H+), generating the hydroxyl radicals. Meanwhile, the captured electrons in oxygen vacancies can be released into the CB and are depleted via oxygen reduction reaction (O2 + e− → \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\cdot {{{\rm{O}}}}}_{2}^{-}$$\end{document}⋅O2−). As a result, the color centers in the BiOBr:Er3+ sample disappear completely, resulting in the recovery of color to the initial state. It is critical to note that the ·O2− generation consumes the released electrons but does not consume the oxygen vacancy lattice sites themselves. The OVs remain as stable centers, enabling the highly reproducible trapping and release of electrons over photochromism/bleaching cycles.

Physics and interactions in dynamic UC emissions

Owing to the existing overlap between the absorption and UC emission spectra, the orthogonal UC luminescence of the Er3+-activated BiOBr sample could be reversibly manipulated by photochromism and water bleaching processes (Supplementary Fig. 43). This reversible UC dynamic modulation behavior is generally associated with the interaction between OVs defect (color centers) and erbium emitters (UC emission centers). To further investigate the energy transfer process between them, the UC decay dynamic behaviors of Er3+ emitters under 391 UV illumination or water bleaching for different times have also been investigated, as presented in Supplementary Figs. 44–47. Obviously, the decay lifetimes of Er3+ ions at 542 and 668 nm remain essentially unchanged during coloration and decoloration processes, demonstrating that the main type of energy transfer from UC emission centers to color centers is a radiative absorption (RA) process, not a non-radiative resonance energy transfer process56,60. As observed in Supplementary Fig. 48, the RA efficiency is positively correlated with the spectra overlap between absorption and UC emission of the BOBE-OV sample, firmly supporting our proposed mechanism. Our systematic experimental observations and theoretical analysis together exclude other possibilities where OVs act as intermediate states for sequential photon absorption or as surface recombination centers, as these would either require a specific defect state incompatible with multi-wavelength excitations or would inherently suppress the photochromism we clearly observe. It is worth mentioning that the reversible modulation of UC luminescence is critically dependent on the initial UV-triggered formation of color centers. Without this key step, the RA process can’t occur, and thus the UC intensity remains constant, as our control experiment directly verifies (Supplementary Fig. 49). Thus, the high-contrast switching is enabled by a synergy between the broad, intense absorption band of the color centers and their structural colocalization with Er3+ emitters on the nanosheet surface, which maximizes the efficiency of the radiative re-absorption process.

Accordingly, we have demonstrated the UC dynamic modulation mechanism under different external stimulations in the designed BiOX:Er3+ system (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 50). Upon UV illumination, the photogenerated electrons are captured by OVs to form color centers, which possess a broad absorption band that overlaps with the emission bands of the excited Er3+ ions, resulting in the occurrence of the RA process from Er3+ emitters to color centers. Subsequently, the orthogonal UC luminescence could be absorbed by color centers (UC luminescent quenching) with the emergence of a photochromic phenomenon, due to the overlap of emission peaks of Er3+ and absorption band of BiOX host. The introduction of water molecules into OVs-rich BiOX samples can induce the gradual release of the captured electrons in OVs sites and return to the conduction band, leading to a decrease in the content of color centers. As a result, the radiative absorption process from Er3+ ions to OVs could be suppressed greatly, and the recovery of orthogonal UC emissions could be achieved. By alternating stimulation of UV light irradiation and water bleaching, the radiative absorption from UC emitters to OVs could be manipulated gradually, leading to the reversible dynamic modification of orthogonal UC luminescence with high switching contrast. Notably, the high-contrast UC modulation is primarily governed by surface OVs, as their location enables efficient electron trapping/release with environmental molecules (e.g., H2O/O2) and effective radiative energy transfer to nearby Er3+ ions.

High-level anti-counterfeiting Applications

Inspired by the dynamic regulation of UC luminescence upon external stimuli, we designed a versatile anti-counterfeiting scheme with multichannel encoding, dynamic erasable display, and multi-stimulus-responsive abilities. To demonstrate the practical applications of Er3+-activated bismuth oxyhalides in anti-counterfeiting and optical storage, the BiOBr:Er3+/agarose composite film was successfully fabricated by uniformly mixing the as-obtained UC powder and agarose hydrogel. Benefiting from the advantages of agarose hydrogel (e.g., good transparency, porous network microstructure, and biocompatibility), the composite film has appropriate strength, good flexibility, and elasticity. The characterization results (Supplementary Fig. 51) indicate that the white composite film possesses a microporous network structure, in which the BiOBr:Er3+ particles can be dispersed homogeneously. The composite film also exhibits good reversible photochromic property under alternating stimulation of UV light illumination and water bleaching (Supplementary Fig. 52, Supplementary Movie 1). For demonstration purposes, various complicated patterns with high coloration contrast were photo-printed on the as-obtained composite films by the photomask method, as presented in Fig. 6a–c. Under UV lamp irradiation for 9 s, the white patterns, including peacock, petal, letters, and binary code, can be observed on the surface of the composite film under natural light (Fig. 6d). As expected, the as-obtained patterns emit green light under 980 nm laser excitation and red light upon 1532 nm laser excitation (Fig. 6e, f). The brightness of orthogonal UC emissions for the designed patterns decreases after the illumination of the UV lamp (Fig. 6g), indicating that the UC emission behaviors could be regulated by external stimulations. Furthermore, the binary dot arrays representing NJFU encrypted data could be written on the composite film, and read out by the UC emission modulation of photochromic composite film (Fig. 6c–f, Supplementary Fig. 53). The composite film can be written and erased serval times with no significant loss in contrast and resolution, demonstrating the recoverability and reversibility of Er3+-doped BiOBr sample (Fig. 6h). More significantly, the as-obtained composite film is capable of high-resolution patterning (Supplementary Fig. 54) and possesses excellent mechanical stability (Supplementary Fig. 55), making it highly suitable for high-level anti-counterfeiting applications. Discussion

In summary, we present a design strategy for the dynamic regulation of reversible orthogonal UC luminescence of Er3+ ions based on the photochromic reaction of bismuth halide oxides (BiOX, X = Cl, Br and I). By tailoring the oxygen vacancy variations under alternating stimulation of UV light irradiation and water bleaching, the absorption profile of BiOX could be regulated on-demand, enabling external-stimuli-controlled radiative reabsorption process from UC emitters to color centers and ultimately leading to temporal modulation of orthogonal UC luminescence. Benefiting from the reversible dynamic UC luminescent feature of Er3+-doped BiOX sample, the proof-of-concept experiments exhibit the significant application prospect of the developed UC materials in the fields of high-level anti-counterfeiting and optical information storage. Our investigation may enable great feasibility in developing advanced UC materials with dynamic reversible luminescence for broad applications.

Methods

Synthesis

The OVs-rich BiOBr:Er3+ (marked as BOBE-OV) samples were synthesized by a facile surfactant-assisted hydrothermal method. In a typical procedure, stoichiometric mixtures of high-purity Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (99%) and Er(NO3)3·5H2O (99.99%) were added in deionized water (60 mL) with vigorous stirring for 10 min, following by the addition of 0.6 g of mannitol (98%), 0.1 g of PVP (K30) and 3 mmol of KBr (99%). After stirring for another 1 h, the solution was transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 160 °C for 3 h. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitates were collected by centrifugation, washed with deionized water several times, and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 10 h. Finally, the products were annealed at 550 °C for 1 h in a sealed crucible, which was placed into a muffle furnace. For comparison, an OVs-deficient BiOBr:Er3+ (denoted as BOBE) was also prepared under the same conditions without the addition of mannitol and PVP. The OVs-enriched BiOBr:Yb3+/Tm3+ and BiOBr:Yb3+/Tm3+ samples were prepared via a similar synthetic procedure except for the use of different rare earth nitrates (Yb(NO3)3·5H2O, Tm(NO3)3·5H2O and Ho(NO3)3·5H2O).

Fabrication of composite films

Typically, 2 mL of aqueous solution containing 0.15 g of BiOBr:Er3+ nanomaterials and 2 mL of glycerin were mixed thoroughly to form a homogeneous dispersion. Subsequently, 6 mL of agarose aqueous solution (33.33 mg/mL) was slowly added into the above mixture and stirred at 80 °C for 15 min. Finally, the aqueous dispersion was poured into a glassy mold to obtain the composite film.

Characterization

The crystal phase and purity of the samples were determined by a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) was performed in the Regulus 8100 microscope. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) measurements together with energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy, high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images, and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns were carried out by a JEOL JEM-2100 UHR transmission electron microscope operating at 200 kV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) experiments were carried out on an ESCALAB 250 Xi. UV-vis diffused reflection spectrum was gained from a Lambda 950 spectrophotometer while BaSO4 was taken as a reflectance standard. Raman spectra of the samples were taken by a micro-Raman LabRAM HR800 spectrometer with laser excitation at λ = 633 nm. The UC emission spectra and corresponding decay lifetimes were recorded on an Edinburgh FS5 spectrometer with 980 nm (MDL-III-980) and 1532 nm (MDL-N-1532) laser diodes as excitation sources. The photochromic images and luminescent photographs were recorded by a Canon EDS 70D camera equipped with an IR-cut-off optical filter.

Computation method

All plane-wave density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.4) software61,62. The generalized gradient approximation by Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof was used for the exchange-correlation energy63,64. The projector-augmented-wave (PAW) pseudo-potentials are chosen to describe ionic cores. The cutoff energy of 500 eV was adopted, and a Gaussian smearing of 0.1 eV to the orbital occupation was applied for the geometry optimization and total energy computations. A tetrahedron method with Blöchl corrections was employed for accurate electronic structure calculations. The convergence threshold for the iteration in self-consistent-field (SCF) was set to be 10−5 eV, and for geometry optimizations by using the BFGS algorithm was set to be 0.02 eV/Å on the maximum force component. The k-point sampling of the Brillouin zone was obtained using a 2 × 3 × 1 grid in the Monkhorst-Pack Scheme and a large vacuum slab of 15 Å was inserted in z direction for surface isolation to prevent interaction between two neighboring surfaces. Grimme’s DFT-D3 methodology was used to describe the dispersion interactions.

The adsorption energy of water on the BiOBr or BiOBr-OV slab surface (ΔE) was calculated as follows: ΔE = Eslab - (Eslab + EH2O), where Eslab+H2O, Eslab and EH2O stand for the total energies of the slab/water adsorption system, the slab (BiOBr or BiOBr-OV) semiconductor and the isolated H2O molecule, respectively.
