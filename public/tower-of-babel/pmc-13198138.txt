An Unexpected Electrocardiogram in Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: Garrett Cotter, Richard Malone, Abdallah Abuawad, Rohit Moudgil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase SummaryA 19-year-old female with a history of tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) with prior right ventricle (RV)-pulmonary artery conduit, RV outflow tract aneurysm, intermittent ventricular arrhythmia, Chiari malformation, and scoliosis presented to establish care at our institution. Electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated an unexpected pattern consistent with dextrocardia and right axis deviation. Further investigation noted TOF with situs inversus totalis, a rare TOF variant only previously described several times. She underwent longitudinal monitoring with progression of RV dysfunction and ventricular arrhythmias.Take-Home MessageTOF produces characteristic ECG changes that have utility in monitoring RV remodeling, arrhythmogenesis, and RV volume load. When ECG presents unexpected changes, additional defects or compromised repair should be interrogated.Key words: electrocardiogram, tetralogy of Fallot, ventricular septal defect

Clinical Vignette

A 19-year-old female with a history of repaired tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) and right ventricle-pulmonary artery (RV-PA) conduit presented to our clinic to establish care. She had a history notable for RV outflow tract aneurysm, Chiari malformation, and surgically corrected scoliosis. She noted her defects were “rare” but could not discuss specific abnormalities, and outside documentation remained unavailable. She denied cardiac symptoms and presented to discuss the risks of future pregnancy. Electrocardiogram (ECG) (Figure 1A) obtained in the office is shown. What is the appropriate diagnosis?

Answer: C.

Discussion

An initial differential included inappropriate lead placement, dextrocardia, RVH, LV atrophy with repaired TOF, anteroseptal myocardial scar, regional pericardial effusion, and concurrent respiratory pathology. This ECG demonstrates global negativity in leads I and aVL, global positivity in lead aVR, dominant S waves in V1 and V2, and low-voltage QRS complexes in V3 to V6. In totality, this presentation suggests dextrocardia with inappropriate left-sided precordial lead placement. Dextrocardia classically produces RAD, upright QRS complexes in aVR, global negativity in lead I, and poor R progression. Our patient presented with concurrent situs inversus, a rare TOF variant minimally described in the literature. We repeated an ECG with intentional limb lead reversal and right-sided precordial lead placement (Figure 1B).

While a dominant R-wave in lead V1 with RAD is associated with RVH, a less pronounced S-wave would be expected (classically with R/S >1). In addition, this ECG lacks dominant S waves in leads V5 and V6 to further suggest RVH. Instead, there is a sharp decline in QRS amplitude with progression across the precordium.

Although some criteria for LVH are met (R in aVF >20 mm, S in aVR >14 mm), both traditional and modified criteria (ie, Sokolow-Lyon index or Cornell product) are misleading in the presence of underlying structural disease such as dextrocardia. TOF itself rarely causes LVH unless there is hemodynamic compromise of the surgical repair (ie, right ventricle-pulmonary artery conduit). Instead, patients often demonstrate reductions in the LV mass/volume ratio characteristic of myocardial atrophy.1

Finally, anteroseptal infarcts may present with T inversions, transient ST abnormalities, or Q waves primarily in leads V1 to V3. None of these abnormalities are demonstrated (except for T wave inversion isolated to V2).

ECG in patients with TOF (assuming levocardia) often demonstrates RAD, dominant R waves in anterior precordial leads, and dominant S waves in lateral leads. ECG is recommended to monitor for interval changes and estimate arrhythmogenic risk. Significant RVH or myocardial fibrosis may produce widened QRS morphology that suggests conduction deficits.2 QRS fragmentation may also develop due to progressive myocardial scar or RV remodeling.3 Notably, QRS duration >180 milliseconds may predict risk of ventricular arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death.3 PR prolongation may function as a prognostic marker of unstable atrial tachycardic risk. PQ and QRS intervals may estimate assumed RV volume load, although this evidence is less established.2 Ultimately, ECG remains a valuable, noninvasive tool to ensure optimal outcomes in TOF.
