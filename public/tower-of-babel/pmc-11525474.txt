Non-invasive assessment of programmed cell death ligand-1 expression using 18F-FDG PET-CT imaging in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Liming Miao, Gang Xiao, Wanqi Chen, Guisheng Yang, Denghui Hong, Zhenshan Wang, Longsheng Zhang, Weipeng Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProgrammed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) is an ideal checkpoint for immunohistochemical detection. The method of obtaining PD-L1 expression through biopsy can impact the accurate assessment of PD-L1 expression due to the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of tumors. Because of the limited sample size, biopsies often give only a localized picture of the tumor. In this retrospective study, a total of 2,386 metabolic tumor volume (MTV) features were extracted from 18F-FDG PET-CT images. A radiomics model was developed to holistically and non-invasively assess PD-L1 expression in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma by identifying seven independent factors through feature screening. The radiomics model shows effective discrimination, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.888 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.831–0.945] and 0.889 (95% CI: 0.706–1.000) for the training and validation cohorts, respectively. The results of the decision curve analysis demonstrated that utilizing the radiation model to forecast PD-L1 expression levels yielded more net benefits at threshold probabilities below 0.669. The clinical impact curves demonstrate that when the threshold probability is less than 0.501, the loss-to-benefit ratio is less than one in all cases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77680-4.Keywords: Esophageal carcinoma, PD-L1, Radiomics, 18F-FDG PET-CT, Metabolic tumor volumeSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Computational models, Machine learning

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is a prevalent malignancy worldwide, ranking among the top ten. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) represents the primary histologic subtype of EC and is disproportionately prevalent in Central and East Asia1. Early-stage ESCC is typically treated surgically. However, for locally advanced stages, a combination of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy or immunotherapy and surgery is recommended according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines (2.2023) for cancer of the esophagus and esophagogastric junction2. In recent years, the use of immunotherapy to treat multiple cancers, such as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), hepatocellular carcinoma, and esophageal cancer, has significantly increased3. Immunotherapy has played a crucial role in the treatment of ESCC by improving immune checkpoint inhibitors continually4–6. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the immune escape of tumor cells results from the activation of inhibitory signaling pathways that involve programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) and programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1), the limitation of T-cell activity, and the formation of tumor microenvironments that promote tumor cell expansion7–9. With the approval of new PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, like nivolumab, PD-L1 is now considered an ideal checkpoint for immunohistochemical (IHC) assays10–12.

Unfortunately, obtaining PD-L1 expression usually requires IHC detection of biopsies or resection specimens, which can be invasive13–15. Biopsies, in particular, do not wholly represent the tumor due to their limited sample size. Tumors may also exhibit heterogeneity, which can influence PD-L1 expression levels16,17. A gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma study involving 211 patients and 407 tumor samples revealed significant spatial and temporal heterogeneity in the PD-L1 expression18. Subsequently, a study of PD-L1 expression in biopsy and surgical specimens from 112 pairs of gastric adenocarcinoma tissues disclosed differences in PD-L1 expression between gastric biopsies and the corresponding resected tumors19. Therefore, various studies have utilized computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging to detect PD-L1 expression in tumors20–22. Recently, attempts have been made to evaluate PD-L1 expression through 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, which has shown promising progress23,24. In a study of 697 NSCLC patients from three institutions using 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging and clinical data, PD-L1 exhibited significant distinguishing abilities between PD-L1-positive and -negative patients25. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) exceeded 0.82. However, studies assessing PD-L1 expression in ESCC using multidimensional imaging data form 18F-FDG PET still need to be discovered.

Our study aims to identify the independent factors that can differentiate between PD-L1 positive and negative ESCC patients using 18F-FDG imaging. To this end, multidimensional quantitative imaging phenotypic features will be extracted from the metabolic tumor volume (MTV), and a radionics model will be developed by multivariate analysis to holistically and non-invasively assess PD-L1 expression levels in ESCC patients.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

This retrospective study included a total of 144 cases that met the inclusion criteria. Among them, 58 cases were PD-L1 positive, and 86 were negative. The study population consisted of 110 men and 34 women, with an average age of 65.3 years, ranging from 49 to 86 years. TNM staging was conducted following the guidelines of the eighth edition26. Radiomics signature construction and performance evaluation

Our study cohort consists of 144 samples, each containing 2,389 features. We used R version 4.2.0 and performed ten repeated tenfold cross-validations to split the training and validation cohorts. Z-score normalization was performed on the training cohort, and the same mean and standard deviation were applied to the validation cohort. Feature selection was performed on the training cohort using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO27) regression, resulting in the selection of 12 features. (Fig. 1).

According to the Shapiro–Wilk test, the radiomics scores of the PD-L1 expression-positive group in both the training and validation cohorts significantly followed a normal distribution, with p-values of 0.578 and 0.514, respectively. In contrast, the radiomics scores in the PD-L1 expression-negative group significantly deviated from a normal distribution, with p-values of 0.001 and 0.032, respectively. The radiomics signature demonstrates good discriminatory capability, achieving an AUC of 0.896 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.842–0.951) in the training cohort and 0.870 (95% CI: 0.677–1.000) in the validation cohort (Fig. S1). Based on the optimal Youden index (0.676) identified in the training cohort, we selected a radiomics score cut-off of 0.440.

The model’s goodness of fit was assessed in both the training and validation cohorts using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. The results suggest a correlation between the predicted values and the actual values. (training cohort: χ2 = 8.457, p-value = 0.390, validation cohort: χ2 = 7.762, p-value = 0.471). The model’s calibration curves display a gratifying alignment of predicted to actual probabilities (Fig. 2b). In the training cohort, the AUC of the model is 0.888 (95% CI: 0.831–0.945), while in the validation cohort, it is 0.889 (95% CI: 0.706–1.000). Utilizing the optimal Youden index (0.658) determined from the training cohort, we selected a radiomics score cut-off of 0.478. These results suggest that the model possesses a good discriminatory ability (see Fig. 2c). A likelihood ratio test for the radiomics signature and radiomics model was performed. The log-likelihood of the radiomics signature was -30.791, while the log-likelihood of the radiomics model was -39.010. The calculated chi-square statistic was 16.437 with a p-value of 0.0057.

Clinical utility of the radiomics model

Decision curves analysis demonstrates that utilizing a radiomics model to predict PD-L1 expression levels with a threshold probability lower than 0.669 brings about more net benefit compared to deciding on whether to conduct a full biopsy or forgo it entirely in a clinical setting (Fig. 3a). We predicted the PD-L1 expression stratification in 1000 samples using a clinical impact curve. The results showed that when the threshold probability was more significant than 0.669, the difference between the number of positive PD-L1 expressions predicted by the radiomics model and the actual number was less than 4.54%. When the threshold probability was less than 0.501, the loss-to-benefit ratios were all less than 1(Fig. 3b).

Discussion

The present clinical approach for detecting PD-L1 expression in the first-line setting involves testing biopsy or surgical tissue using immunohistochemistry. However, this method causes additional patient trauma and introduces detection bias due to limited tissue samples and tumor heterogeneity. In this scenario, we developed a radiomics model to noninvasively and holistically assess PD-L1 expression levels in ESCC patients by 18F-FDG PET-CT imaging.

Maximum standardized uptake (SUVmax) values are commonly implemented as quantitative indicators to assess tumor activity and therapy response. Several studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between SUVmax values and PD-L1 positivity in lesions24,28. However, when used as an independent factor, the model constructed using SUVmax values performed unsatisfactorily. Kuriyama and colleagues conducted a PET imaging study on 41 ESCC patients. Their results indicate that high PET tracer uptake is significantly associated with cancer aggressiveness and hot tumor immune status29. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis of 18F-FDG SUVmax yielded an AUC of 0.654. Our study included SUVmax values among the extracted features from preprocessed PET images. However, SUVmax values were not selected into the selected subset for feature screening using LASSO logistic regression. Subsequently, our radiomics signature, constructed using the screened twelve features, produced satisfactory results with an AUC of 0.896 (95% CI, 0.842–0.951) in the training cohort and 0.870 (95% CI: 0.677–1.000) in the validation cohort. Based on these results, the exclusion of SUVmax values from radiomics signature can improve the AUC of radiomic features.

Although there is a lack of studies utilizing radiomics to evaluate PD-L1 expression among ESCC patients, some progress has been made in studies using the same approach to assess PD-L1 expression in NSCLC. Li et al. analyzed 255 NSCLC patients, consisting of 73.7% adenocarcinoma and 26.3% squamous cell carcinoma, and extracted 80 features from PET images30. Additionally, the MLR was conducted using 3 PET imaging features to predict PD-L1 expression levels ≥ 50%, yielding an AUC score of 0.745. The MLR was conducted utilizing 12 PET imaging features to predict PD-L1 expression levels ≥ 1% with an AUC score of 0.728 in the test cohort. Monaco et al. analyzed 86 patients with NSCLC16. They extracted 527 radiomics features from PET images and composed a multivariate linear discriminant model based on three features to predict cases with PD-L1 expression ≥ 50% in the training set. The AUC for the model was 0.82. In this study, to ensure that the features extracted from PET-CT images adequately reflect tumor characteristics and to enhance the accuracy and reproducibility of the constructed radiomic models, we implemented a multi-step feature extraction process. Initially, we employed the SUVbw value combined with a region-growing algorithm to perform an initial segmentation of the lesion volume. Subsequently, to ensure the reproducibility and accuracy of the Regions of Interest (ROIs) delineated on the PET-CT images, we utilized the "Double Independent Drawing and Review" method, where two experienced radiologists independently mapped the ROIs, which were then reviewed by a senior radiologist. Throughout the study, we strictly followed the guidelines of the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) and used the PyRadiomics tool to extract radiomic features from the same ROIs within the PET and CT images. To ensure consistency in feature extraction, we used consistent parameter settings within PyRadiomics, minimizing potential bias due to parameter variation. We used repeated tenfold stratified cross-validation to divide the training and validation cohorts and LASSO regression to select radiomics features. The accuracy of the training-validation cohort, selected radiomics features, and accuracy were saved for each fold, while the average accuracy for each repetition was recorded. Out of 100 repetitions of cross-validation, the cohort with 12 features appeared the most often, 40 times. That is, it appeared four times in every 10 folds. These four cohorts of 12 features have four features in common. We chose the cohort with the highest accuracy as the radiomics signature. The radiomics signature’s discriminative ability was demonstrated by the results of ROC analysis. Applying the optimal Youden index to the training cohort resulted in the radiomics signature exhibiting a sensitivity of 0.962 and a specificity of 0.714.

The radiomics model we developed consists of seven independent predictors (see Table 2 for independent predictors). The correlation between the radiomics signature and the radiomics model was analyzed using the likelihood ratio test. The results showed significant differences (log-likelihood of -30.791 for the radiation feature and -39.010 for the radiation model, with a p-value of 0.0057). The likelihood ratio test results showed that radiomics signature (containing twelve features) was a better fit than radiomics model (containing seven features). However, the radiomics model has a more significant advantage in clinical applications due to the use of fewer features. Additionally, the model’s AUC in the training and validation datasets are 0.888 (95% CI: 0.831–0.945) and 0.889 (95% CI: 0.706–1.000). Compared to the more complex model with 12 features, there is a noticeable improvement in reducing overfitting. Among the seven independent predictors, five are related to PET features (with four of these predictors having adjusted p-values < 0.05), and two are related to CT features (with adjusted p-values < 0.05). Upon removing the PET imaging feature (PET Wavelet LLL GLCM Cluster Prominence) with a p-value greater than 0.05 and reconstructing the model with the remaining six predictors, the AUC on the validation cohort decreased from 0.889 to 0.870. Additionally, there was a significant deviation in the calibration curve compared to actual predictions. This result indicates that PET features, like CT features, are significantly associated with PD-L1 expression. This conclusion differs from the findings of Jiang et al.31. "Log sigma.3.0.mm.3D NGTDM Busyness" is a PET texture feature extracted from three-dimensional voxels using a Laplacian operator with a convolution kernel size of 3 × 3 mm. The Busyness in NGTDM (Neighborhood Gray Tone Difference Matrix) is related to the complexity and frequency of texture variations, indicating higher heterogeneity in tumor regions within ESCC. These findings suggest a significant correlation between PD-L1 expression and tumor heterogeneity in ESCC. The correlation between texture changes and PD-L1 expression is also reflected in the wavelet features of PET. The predictive factor "CT wavelet LHL GLSZM Small Area Low Gray Level Emphasis" primarily describes the relative significance or prominence of small to medium-sized areas with low gray values. Meanwhile, "Wavelet LHL GLSZM Small Area Low Gray Level Emphasis" highlights the importance of small areas with low gray levels within the image. Together with PET features, these CT-related factors offer a more comprehensive characterization of the tumor lesion’s texture features. In summary, the heterogeneity of radiomics texture features derived from PET-CT imaging is capable of characterizing the heterogeneity of protein expression in tumors, akin to the conclusions reached by Zheng et al.32. Differences in microvessel density and distribution of cancer cells among tumors result in alterations in the tumor microenvironment, which may impact the levels of tumor glycolysis. According to tracer kinetic models and glycolysis imaging methods, the signal intensity of 18F-FDG PET imaging is proportional to the glycolysis rate of the cells. Thus, the texture of 18F-FDG PET imaging in tumors reflects the distribution of microvessel density and cancer cells within the tumor. This basis in tumor biology informs the radiomics model we developed. The radiomics model developed in this study used the Mann–Whitney U test on both the training and validation cohorts and showed significant differences between the PD-L1 positive and negative groups. The calibration curve of the model showed that the predicted probabilities were in good agreement with the actual results.

In conclusion, in this study, a radiomics model was constructed by extracting multidimensional quantitative imaging phenotypic features from 18F-FDG PET-CT images and selecting seven independent factors. The model showed promising results for the non-invasive assessment of PD-L1 expression in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. However, as this is a retrospective study with a limited sample size in the study cohort, further validation of this radiomics model is required.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jieyang People’s Hospital (approval number 2021.079) and was conducted following the ethical standards set forth in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Patient consent was waived due to this study was retrospective research. The procedure for the study is outlined in Fig. 4. The study population comprised patients diagnosed with ESCC at Jieyang People’s Hospital between January 2019 and June 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) no prior radiation, chemotherapy, or other treatments before undergoing PET-CT examination, (2) surgery performed within four weeks of PET-CT examination, (3) initial gross total resection of the tumor followed by standard IHC testing, and (4) no cancer-related comorbidities, particularly infection or inflammation and diabetes. We identified 144 individuals who met the inclusion criteria, comprising 110 men and 34 women with a mean age of 65.3 years (49.0–86.0 years). The detailed immunohistochemistry method is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Method: Immunohistochemistry method). Details of the 18F-FDG PET-CT imaging are given in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Note: 18F-FDG PET-CT scan and imaging).

The study flowchart. Radiomics features were extracted from the cases in the study cohort. Subsequently, the radiomics signature was obtained by screening these features. Finally, a radiomics model was constructed.

Lesion volume segmenting and features extraction

For the segmentation of lesion volume, PET imaging pixel values were initially transformed into standardized uptake value (SUV) by body weight (SUVbw) values utilizing the formula (1):

where SUVbw is the SUV normalized to body weight33.

Pixel values with SUVbw > 2.3 in the area of the esophagus were selected as seed points and segmented through the region growth algorithm in SimpleITK (version 2.0.1)34. Subsequently, the segmented volume was manually corrected by two radiologists with more than five years of experience using the 3D Slicer image computing platform to retain the lesion volumes while eliminating the over-segmented volumes35. A senior radiologist reviewed and approved each lesion volume defined as an MTV. We strictly followed the guidelines of the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) and used PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1) to extract radiomics features from PET-CT images36,37. During the feature extraction process, we set the original image type to SUV and the bin width to 25. No image resampling was performed as the resampling was set to (0, 0, 0). The Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filter was applied with values of (1, 3, 5). In addition, the “correct mask” and “normalise” options were set to “False”, while the “force3D” option was set to “True”. This configuration ensures the accuracy and consistency of the feature extraction. 2,320 features were extracted from PET and CT images in the segmented lesion volume utilizing the PyRadiomics. Sixty-six statistical features related to SUV were extracted from PET images using 3D Slicer. The methods of feature extraction are explained elaborately in Supplementary Material (Supplementary Method: Feature extraction methods). The specifics of the extracted features are stated in Table S1 and Table S2.

Construction and evaluation of radiomics signature for PD-L1 expression

Our study cohort was divided into positive (58 cases) and negative (86 cases) groups based on PD-L1 expression. The study cohort dataset includes 2,320 extracted radiomics features, 66 statistical features related to SUV, and patient sex, age and TNM staging, for a total of 2,389 features. During the study, features with missing values were removed from the study cohort. One-hot coding was used to process the binary categorical variable of sex, and TNM staging was coded using integers. We used repeated tenfold cross-validation to divide the training and validation cohort. For division, the 144 samples were divided into 10 folds using a stratified cross-validation approach to ensure that the proportion of positive and negative samples in each fold was as close as possible to the overall proportion in the study cohort. A shuffle is performed after each stratification to ensure that the data distribution in each stratum is random. The z-score normalization was performed on each feature data of the training cohort. The test cohort is then z-score normalized with the mean and standard deviation of the feature data of the training cohort. LASSO regression using repeated tenfold cross-validation was performed for feature selection. The selected features were then used to construct MLR to produce radiomics scores. The radiomics score and the sigmoid function (1 / [1 + exp (- radiomics score)]) formed the MLR. The discriminatory performance of MLR was assessed using AUC, and the MLR with the highest AUC was identified as the radiomics signature.

Construction and evaluation of radiomics model

There is an overfitting phenomenon in the radiomics signature. At the same time, there is an urgent need to further reduce the number of constituent radiomics features in order to construct a more robust radiomics model. First, a variance inflation factor was utilized to examine collinearity among variables in MLR within the training cohort. Variables with variance inflation factor values ≥ 10 were identified as collinear. We then conducted backward stepwise logistic regression to choose the combination of variables with small Akaike Information Criterion values. In multivariate analyses, p-values were adjusted for the level of significance of each characteristic using the Bonferroni method. Using the manual backward stepwise logistic method, first remove the variable with the highest p-value. After removing this variable, reconstruct the MLR model. If the AIC value of the new model does not change significantly and the ROC curve shows no clear signs of overfitting or underfitting, then the variable can be removed. Continue to examine other variables with high p-values.

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to detect whether the distribution of data in the positive and negative groups in the training and validation cohort deviated from the normal distribution. Levene’s test was used to assess the variance alignment of the data. If the data deviated from normal distribution, Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the difference between the positive and negative groups. The goodness of fit of the radiomics model was assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Calibration curves were generated to illustrate the disparities between the predicted and actual values of the model. ROC curves were constructed, and AUCs were calculated separately for the training and validation cohorts. ROC curves were used to demonstrate the relationship between the true positive rate and the false positive rate of the model at different thresholds, while AUC was used to illustrate the model’s ability to distinguish between positive and negative samples. A likelihood ratio test was used to compare the difference between the radiomics signature and the radiomics model in assessing PD-L1 expression.

Clinical utility of the radiomics model

Decision curve analysis and clinical impact curves were used to assess the clinical utility of the model at various threshold probabilities38.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.0, Python version 3.10.9, and GraphPad Prism version 9. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine whether the data distribution conformed to normality. ROC curves, AUC, Youden’s index, and the optimal threshold (cut-off) were used to assess the model’s ability to distinguish between positive and negative classes at different thresholds (in GraphPad Prism). Variance inflation factors in Python’s ‘statsmodels’ library were used to test for multicollinearity in multiple linear regression. Radiomics models were built in R using backward stepwise logistic regression, and Bonferroni’s method was applied to correct for p-values (using R’s stats package). Levene’s test was used to check for homogeneity of variances of the data (using R’s car package). Mann–Whitney U-test was used to assess the significance of the difference between the PD-L1-positive and the negative groups (via R’s stats package). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to assess the goodness of fit of the radiomics model (using R’s ResourceSelection package). Likelihood ratio tests were used to assess differences between radiomics signature and radiomics models (via the lmtest package for R). Calibration curves were used to assess the degree of calibration of the model’s predictive probabilities (applying R’s rms package). Decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curves were used to evaluate the clinical utility of the radiomics model (using R’s rmda package).
