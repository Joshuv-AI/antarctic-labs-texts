Translation and Psychometric Properties of the Turkish Version of the Person‐Centred Climate Questionnaire—Patient Version: A Methodological Study
Authors: Nur Güven Özdemir, Ayşe Aydin, Münevver Sönmez, Aysel Özsaban, David Edvardsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo translate, culturally adapt and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the Person‐Centred Climate Questionnaire—Patient Version (PCQ‐P‐Tur).DesignA methodological and descriptive study design.MethodsThe PCQ‐P was translated and culturally adapted into Turkish, followed by content and face validity assessment. Subsequently, psychometric evaluation was conducted in a sample of 300 patients recruited from the acute care settings of a public hospital. Data were collected using a Patient Information Form, the PCQ‐P‐Tur and the Individualized Care Scale (ICS‐Patient‐B). Analyses included descriptive statistics, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, Spearman's rho correlations for concurrent validity, and reliability assessments using Cronbach's alpha and item–total correlations.ResultsExpert evaluation indicated strong content validity (I‐CVI = 0.857–1.00; S‐CVI = 0.969), while face validity assessment with 30 patients supported the clarity and relevance of the items. Factor analyses revealed that, unlike the original version, the PCQ‐P‐Tur consisted of two factors, Climate of Safety and Climate of Everydayness, forming a 16‐item structure. The PCQ‐P‐Tur demonstrated substantial concurrent validity, with significant positive correlations between the total PCQ‐P‐Tur score and the total ICS‐B score. Reliability was excellent, with Cronbach's α = 0.965 and item–total correlations ranging from 0.538 to 0.878. Patients reported high levels of person‐centred care, with Climate of Safety and Climate of Everydayness subscales receiving the highest ratings, indicating a care climate perceived as consistently safe, supportive and aligned with person‐centred principles.ConclusionThe PCQ‐P‐Tur is a valid and reliable scale for assessing patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate in Turkish healthcare settings.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareThe PCQ‐P‐Tur can support nurses, healthcare professionals, and managers in assessing the person‐centred care climate, identifying opportunities for improvement and informing quality improvement initiatives. It may also help explore how the physical and social dimensions of care climate shape patient experience and support safe, supportive and individualized patient care.ImpactThis study addressed the lack of a Turkish instrument for assessing patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate. The findings provide a basis for further validation and application of the PCQ‐P‐Tur among patients in Turkish acute care settings.Reporting MethodThis study was reported in accordance with the COSMIN Reporting Guideline for Studies on Measurement Properties of Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures: version 2.0.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient involvement beyond participation in the study.Keywords: care climate, perception, person‐centred care, psychometrics, surveys and questionnaires

Introduction

Healthcare services that respect and address patients' needs enhance care outcomes and perceptions of quality. This constitutes person‐centred care, an approach that keeps the individual at the centre of nursing care through creative and responsive practices (Baek et al. 2023; Edvardsson 2015; McCance and McCormack 2025). Person‐centred care recognizes patients as unique individuals rather than passive recipients of the nursing process. Incorporating their values, beliefs and preferences into the nursing process enhances communication, improves the quality of care and supports patient participation in health decisions. This approach also reflects essential ethical principles, including respect for autonomy and human dignity, contributing to a more compassionate and sustainable healthcare system (Edvardsson 2015; McCance and McCormack 2025).

The Person‐centred Nursing Framework proposed by McCance and McCormack (2025), a middle‐range theory for nursing practice, conceptualizes person‐centred care as a humanistic nursing practice that emerges through the interaction of nurse prerequisites, the care climate and person‐centred processes. Within this framework, the person‐centred climate represents a key element of the care climate/environment that supports the implementation of person‐centred care. Therefore, assessing patients' perceptions of the person‐centred climate provides valuable insight into the extent to which person‐centred care is experienced in healthcare settings.

Background

Person‐centred care views humans as individuals who must be assessed within their own nature (Phelan et al. 2020). Nursing theories and models support this view in nursing care by emphasizing sensitivity to physical, psychosocial, environmental, sociocultural and spiritual domains of the individual, empathy, relational engagement and the foundation of ethically grounded practice (Alligood 2021).

Since the identification of person‐centred care as one of six specific aims for healthcare improvement by Institute of Medicine [IOM] (2001), this aim has been considered a high‐priority indicator of international nursing care quality and patient safety. The Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) declaration emphasized in 2017 that to ensure efficient, high‐quality and sustainable healthcare, health system performance must be measured based on person‐centred healthcare outcomes that matter to patients (The Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development [OECD] 2017). Thus, nurses are expected to integrate technological and scientific advances with a holistic, existential‐humanistic and ethically informed approach in all aspects of patient care delivery, including clinical processes, services and programme structures (American Nurses Association [ANA] 2025; International Council of Nurses [ICN] 2021).

The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) has also outlined that health systems research should promote person‐centredness to achieve better health outcomes and enhance the overall quality of care (Abt Global, L.L.C. and The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality 2025). Recent evidence suggests that person‐centred care improves patients' quality of life by promoting engagement, self‐management, and physical and social safety, the care experience in healthcare settings, as well as reducing hospital admissions, costs of care and healthcare utilization across various clinical settings (Abbott et al. 2021; Berkovic et al. 2024; Kang et al. 2022; Nkhoma et al. 2022; Rossiter et al. 2020). However, as noted by Giusti et al. (2020), the studies still present a somewhat ambiguous view of the interventions and outcomes of person‐centred care, particularly in terms of its clinical implementation. Moreover, according to McCormack (2022), as patients are increasingly recognized as active partners in their own care, further research is required to develop comprehensive and contextually grounded methods for assessing person‐centred care based on qualitative and quantitative data.

Patients play a key role in co‐designing the process of person‐centred care (Santana et al. 2018). From the patients' perspective, key priorities for further research in person‐centred care include dignity, autonomy, effective communication, individualized and coordinated care, the systematic assessment of patient‐reported outcomes and experiences, as well as supportive care climates (Feldthusen et al. 2022; Santana et al. 2018). A supportive care climate that actively involves patients in its design is vital for promoting comfort, inclusion, and responsiveness to their needs (Feldthusen et al. 2022). Edvardsson et al. (2008) conceptualized the supportive care environment as a person‐centred care climate, encompassing physical and psychosocial dimensions that place the patient's needs and expectations at the heart of care. Measures of patient perceptions of person‐centred care climate address the degree to which health care settings are perceived as being person‐centred (Edvardsson et al. 2009, 2008).

The person‐centred care concept, which holds a significant place in nursing literature, has so far been largely examined in studies reflecting the perceptions and experiences of nurses (Güney et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2023). Studies addressing the subject from the patient's perspective are limited, but a remarkable increase in studies on the subject has been observed in recent years (Edvardsson et al. 2017; Shyu 2024). On the other hand, these studies are mostly conducted in high‐income countries, and only a limited number of studies examining the situation in low‐ and middle‐income countries like Türkiye have been found (Tong et al. 2025). Türkiye is a country where individuals with diverse cultural backgrounds receive care and treatment, and it has an effective healthcare system with multiple financing models, both public and private. Many factors, such as the structure of healthcare services, delivery methods and patient expectations, can influence patients' perceptions of care and treatment. The results may be affected by the healthcare environment in which the relevant population receives services, the culture and the socio‐cultural characteristics that shape individuals' expectations and judgements. To meet this growing need for person‐centred care outcomes, internationally validated and reliable scales are required. Several patient‐reported scales have been developed to assess person‐centred nursing care and related constructs, reflecting the multidimensional nature of person‐centred care. However, these scales differ in their conceptual focus and primarily evaluate patients' experiences of individualized care, patient participation or the nurse–patient relationship rather than the person‐centred climate of the care environment (Köberich and Farin 2015). In contrast, PCQ‐P was specifically developed by Edvardsson et al. (2008) to assess Swedish patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate. The PCQ‐P has been widely used in various healthcare settings and has been translated and validated in multiple languages, demonstrating strong psychometric properties across different cultural contexts (Aljuaid et al. 2023; Bergland et al. 2015; Edvardsson et al. 2009; Kobrai‐Abkenar et al. 2020; Stolt et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2019; Yoon et al. 2018, 2015). However, the validity and reliability of PCQ‐P have not yet been established in Türkiye. Additionally, no validated Turkish scale is currently available to assess patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate. Therefore, adapting and validating the PCQ‐P‐Tur will provide researchers and clinicians with a culturally appropriate scale to evaluate patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate, support quality improvement initiatives, facilitate national and international comparative research, and address an important methodological gap in Turkish healthcare research.

Aim

The aim of this study was to translate, culturally adapt and evaluate the psychometric properties of the PCQ‐P‐Tur for use in acute care settings.

Methods

Study Design

A methodological and descriptive study design was used. The findings of this study were reported in accordance with the COnsensus‐based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) Reporting Guideline for Studies on Measurement Properties of Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures: version 2.0 (Table S1) (Gagnier et al. 2025).

Sample and Settings

The sample consisted of 300 patients cared for in the acute care settings of a public hospital between October 10, 2024 and May 31, 2025. Convenience sampling was used to select patients who met the inclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1) aged between 18 and 65 years; (2) being literate; (3) having full consciousness; (4) receiving inpatient care for at least 48 h; (5) having no health conditions that impair communication or cooperation; and (6) providing informed consent to participate in the study. A total of 300 eligible patients were invited to participate in the study, and all agreed to participate (response rate: 100%).

Regarding sample size adequacy for factor analysis, some methodological recommendations suggest a minimum of 10 participants per scale item, while others indicate a sample size of 200–300 participants is sufficient for robust factor analytic procedures (Boateng et al. 2018). In this regard, a sample size of 300 was determined to be sufficient to assess the validity of the 17‐item PCQ‐P. Moreover, when exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is used to identify the factor structure of a scale, it is recommended that the resulting model be tested using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on an independent sample, as conducting both analyses on the same dataset limits generalizability by reiterating relationships identified during the EFA (Knekta et al. 2019). Accordingly, to examine the construct validity of the PCQ‐P‐Tur, the total sample was randomly divided into two independent subsamples in this study: one half for EFA and the other for CFA.

Instruments

Patient Information Form

This form was developed by the researchers as a questionnaire based on the relevant literature. It was used to collect patients' sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. It included questions on individual characteristics (age, gender, marital status, and educational status); disease‐related factors (clinical diagnosis, presence of chronic illness, length of hospital stay, prior experience of receiving care in a hospital setting and presence of companion); hospital‐related factors (general inpatient unit and number of patients in the room); and their perceptions of the care climate and nursing care (the situation of choosing this care climate again in terms of nursing care, degree of satisfaction with the care climate and degree of satisfaction with nursing care) (Kobrai‐Abkenar et al. 2020; Stolt et al. 2021).

The Person‐Centred Care Climate Questionnaire‐Patient Version (PCQ‐P)

PCQ‐P was developed by Edvardsson et al. (2008). It consists of 17 items and is structured into three subscales: Climate of Safety (10 items), Climate of Everydayness (4 items) and Climate of Hospitality (3 items). The climate of safety refers to patients' sense of security in healthcare settings, which is enhanced by approachable and competent staff, clear communication, timely responses, and a well‐organized physical environment. The climate of everydayness is characterized by a ‘de‐institutionalized’ atmosphere where patients feel surrounded by familiar, home‐like elements. Lastly, the Climate of Hospitality encompasses how individuals are received within the care setting, evoking a sense of being warmly welcomed and treated with the highest level of care and respect.

Each item is assessed using a 6‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = strongly disagree to 6 = strongly agree). Higher scores indicate a more person‐centred care climate. In the Swedish version, Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.93 for the overall scale and 0.94, 0.82 and 0.64 for the Climate of Safety, Climate of Everydayness and Climate of Hospitality subscales, respectively. The intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC) for average measures was 0.73, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.58–0.85 to evaluate the test–retest reliability of the overall scale. These findings indicate that the scale demonstrates acceptable test–retest reliability (Edvardsson et al. 2008).

The Individualized Care Scale (ICS‐Patient‐B)

ICS‐Patient‐B is a validated self‐report scale developed initially by Suhonen et al. (2005) to assess patients' perceptions of their individualized care. The ICS‐Patient‐B has been widely used in various healthcare settings and has been translated and validated in multiple languages, demonstrating strong psychometric properties across different cultural contexts (Amaral et al. 2014; Köberich et al. 2015; Petroz et al. 2011; Rovetta et al. 2012; Suhonen et al. 2010; Yi et al. 2017). Turkish validity and reliability of the scale (ICS‐Patient‐B) were conducted by Acaroglu et al. (2011) in 2006. In this study, the ICS‐Patient‐B scale was used because its assessment framework is similar to the PCQ‐P for evaluating concurrent validity and patients' perceptions of individualized care.

The scale consists of 17 items and is structured into three subscales: Clinical Situation (7 items), Personal Life Situation (4 items) and Decisional Control over Care (6 items). Each item is assessed using a 5‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = fully disagree to 5 = fully agree). Higher scores indicate that the patient perceives the care they received is more individualized to them. In the Turkish version, the Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.93 for the overall scale and 0.89, 0.80 and 0.84 for the Clinical Situation, Personal Life Situation, and Decisional Control over Care subscales, respectively. In this study, overall Cronbach's alpha coefficient for ICS‐Patient‐B was 0.977, and 0.976, 0.957 and 0.885 for the Clinical Situation, Personal Life Situation and Decisional Control over Care subscales, respectively (Table 4).

Translation Procedure

To ensure linguistic and cross‐cultural equivalence, the 10‐step translation and cultural adaptation guideline developed by the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) was followed throughout the process (Wild et al. 2005). Each stage of the adaptation was systematically planned and implemented in alignment with the procedures outlined in this guideline. Further details of the translation and cultural adaptation process are presented in Table S2.

Data Collection

Data were collected using paper‐based self‐report instruments administered in person by the researchers. Following completion of all required ethical and institutional procedures, eligible patients completed the instruments, which were collected by the researchers immediately after completion. The data collection process took approximately 25–30 min. No reminder procedures were required because all instruments were completed during hospitalization.

Data Analyses

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS version 23 for descriptive and exploratory statistical procedures. The number, percentage, mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values were assessed using descriptive analyses. Additionally, IBM AMOS version 24 was used for structural equation modelling and to assess the model's fit indices (IBM Corp.). Normality was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and the assumption of multivariate normality.

To assess content validity, the Davis technique was used, with each item rated on a 4‐point scale by experts (Davis 1992). Item‐based content validity index (I‐CVI) and scale‐based content validity index (S‐CVI) values of each item of 0.90 or above were considered to have acceptable validity (Polit and Beck 2022).

To evaluate concurrent validity, Spearman's rho correlation coefficients were utilized to examine the relationships between non‐normally distributed scale scores. The strength of the correlation was evaluated as follows: r = 0.00 to ±0.10 is a negligible correlation, r = ±0.10 to ±0.39 is a weak correlation, r = ±0.40 to ±0.69 is a moderate correlation, r = ±0.70 to ±0.89 is a strong correlation, and r = ±0.90 to ±1.00 is a very strong correlation (Schober et al. 2018).

To evaluate the scale's construct validity, both EFA and CFA were conducted. Before conducting EFA, the Kaiser‐Meyer‐Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett's test of sphericity were performed. For the EFA, principal component analysis and Varimax rotation were performed. Construct validity of the scale was assessed through CFA. The Bootstrap Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation method was used since the assumption of multivariate normality was not met in CFA. The fit indexes of the model were assessed with the goodness‐of‐fit statistics, such as Chi‐square/degrees of freedom ratio 3 < (χ 2/df) < 5, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) < 0.05, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) < 0.08, Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Tacker‐Lewis index (TLI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) > 0.90 (R. B. Kline 2023).

To evaluate the reliability, Cronbach's alpha coefficients were analysed to assess internal consistency reliability, with α value of 0.90 accepted as the criterion for good reliability for scales containing more than 11 items and a sample size greater than 300 (Streiner et al. 2024). Item–total correlations were used to evaluate test item quality, with r > 0.20 serving as the criterion for adequacy (P. Kline 2015). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Before initiating data collection, ethical approval was obtained from Atılım University Human Research Ethics Committee for this study (dated July 3, 2024; No. 604.01.02‐248), and formal authorization was obtained from the relevant hospital's administration. Furthermore, the authors secured permission via email correspondence to utilize the measurement instruments. The researchers provided patients with comprehensive information regarding the study's objectives, procedures and ethical considerations. Both verbal and written informed consent was obtained from each patient. All research procedures adhered strictly to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association 2025).

Results

Sample Characteristics

The mean age of the patients was 58.94 ± 15.03 years, and 51.0% were male. More than half had a chronic disease (58.3%), 65.7% were hospitalized in internal medicine units and 84.3% reported receiving care in a hospital setting previously. Overall, almost all (99.0%) indicated that they would choose to receive care in the same environment again, and they reported high satisfaction with both the care climate and nursing care (Table 1).

Psychometric Properties of the PCQ‐P‐Tur

Validity of the PCQ‐P‐Tur

Content Validity

Seven nurse academicians with PhD degrees and clinical experience from across various nursing fields were recruited to validate the items' content. Based on expert evaluations, I‐CVI and S‐CVI were calculated using the Davis technique, in which each item was rated on a four‐point Likert‐type scale: 1 = not appropriate to 4 = highly appropriate. The results indicated that the I‐CVI ranged from 0.857 to 1.00, while the S‐CVI was 0.969.

Face Validity

Face validity was examined to test whether patients perceive the items as relevant to the characteristic being evaluated. It was assessed using convenience sampling, with 30 patients who provided feedback but were not included in the sample. All patients reported that the translated scale was clear, easy to understand and representative of the construct being measured. In addition, all items were considered relevant, and no modifications to the translated version were required following the pilot testing.

Construct Validity

Exploratory Factor Analysis

The analysis was performed on data obtained from 150 patients for the 17‐item scale. Before factor extraction, data suitability for factor analysis was assessed. The Kaiser‐Meyer‐Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.912, indicating excellent sampling adequacy. Additionally, Bartlett's test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ 2 = 2797.434, df = 136, p < 0.001), confirming that the correlation matrix was appropriate for factor analysis (Table 2).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

Before the analysis, the dataset was examined for all potential data‐related issues (outliers, kurtosis and skewness, and missing values, etc.), and necessary corrective procedures were applied. Because ML estimation requires multivariate normality, this assumption was assessed before analysis. The multivariate normality test yielded a critical ratio of 191.495. While values below 10 indicate excellent multivariate normality and values up to 20 are generally considered acceptable in the literature (Gürbüz 2024), the results showed a violation of the multivariate normality assumption in this study. Accordingly, the Bootstrap ML estimation method was employed, with 5000 bootstrap resamples.

A first‐order CFA was initially performed on data obtained from 150 patients for the 17‐item examination. Examination of the modification indices (MI) suggested potential adjustments related to regression weights and covariances in the initial hypothesized model. First, for modification related to regression weights, Item 9 was removed from the model due to cross‐loadings on both the Climate of Safety and Climate of Everydayness subscales during the first‐order CFA. Second, for modification related to covariance, two modifications were made by adding additional covariances between error terms. Consequently, the CFA was then re‐estimated using 16 items, and the Climate of Safety subscale consisted of 11 items and the Climate of Everydayness subscale comprised 5 items (Tables S3 and S4). Additionally, all factor loadings for the items were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). The results of CFA for PCQ‐P‐Tur are presented in Table 3 and Figure 2. The model fit indices for the final model were as follows: CMIN/DF (446.429/101) = 4.42; RMSEA = 0.152; SRMR = 0.034; GFI = 0.746; IFI = 0.904; TLI = 0.885; and CFI = 0.903.

Concurrent Validity

Concurrent validity was assessed by comparing the PCQ‐P‐Tur to a known criterion, such as patients' perceptions of their individualized care (Stolt et al. 2021). A statistically significant, moderate, positive association was found between total ICS‐B score and the PCQ‐P‐Tur (r s = 0.483; p < 0.001), supporting concurrent validity. Similarly, a statistically significant, moderate, positive association was found between total ICS‐B score and the Climate of Everydayness (r s = 0.522; p < 0.001) and the Climate of Safety (r s = 0.395; p < 0.001) subscales (Table 4).

Reliability of the PCQ‐P‐Tur

The internal consistency reliability of the PCQ‐P‐Tur was high. The overall Cronbach's α coefficient for PCQ‐P‐Tur was 0.965, 0.979 for the Climate of Safety and 0.883 for the Climate of Everydayness subscales (Table 4). All items on the measure had item–total score correlations ranging from 0.538 to 0.878 (Table 2).

Perceptions of Person‐Centred Care Climate and Individualized Care

Patients reported high levels of person‐centred care across all measures. PCQ‐P‐Tur total mean score was 5.72 ± 0.57. Among its subscales, the Climate of Safety received the highest rating. Similarly, the mean total score of the ICS‐Patient‐B was 4.83 ± 0.42, with the highest ratings in the Clinical Situation. Detailed results for all subscales are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

An important gap remains in evaluating the person‐centred care climate, which reflects the extent to which healthcare settings support the delivery of person‐centred care (Aljuaid et al. 2023; Edvardsson et al. 2009). Measuring the person‐centred care climate and systematically incorporating patient‐reported outcomes into care evaluation frameworks are essential to ensure that patients' experiences, needs and priorities are comprehensively taken into account. Such evaluation may also contribute to improved patient‐relevant outcomes (McCance and McCormack 2025). Therefore, this study examined the validity and reliability of the PCQ‐P‐Tur, thereby providing a culturally adapted scale for assessing patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate in acute care settings. Consistent with previous cross‐cultural validation studies (Aljuaid et al. 2023; Bergland et al. 2015; Edvardsson et al. 2009; Kobrai‐Abkenar et al. 2020; Stolt et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2019; Yoon et al. 2018, 2015), the findings demonstrated that the PCQ‐P‐Tur possesses satisfactory psychometric properties, supporting its use in clinical practice and future research.

The findings provide strong evidence supporting the content validity of the PCQ‐P‐Tur. The high level of expert agreement, as evidenced by content validity indices exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.90 (Polit and Beck 2022), indicates that the translated items adequately captured the intended construct and maintained conceptual consistency with the original scale. Moreover, the predominance of expert ratings in the higher relevance categories suggests that the items are clear, relevant and theoretically consistent (Boateng et al. 2018; Polit and Beck 2022). Face validity findings further supported the comprehensibility and relevance of the translated instrument, with 30 patients' feedback confirming that the items were clear and understandable. Consistent with recommendations for cross‐cultural instrument adaptation, the combination of expert evaluation and feedback from patients provided complementary evidence supporting the overall validity of the translated instrument (Boateng et al. 2018).

The findings provide satisfactory evidence supporting the construct validity of the PCQ‐P‐Tur. The use of separate randomly selected subsamples for EFA and CFA strengthened the methodological rigour of the validation process by enabling the factor structure to be explored and subsequently confirmed in independent samples (R. B. Kline 2023; Orçan 2018). Although the factorial structure differed from the original three‐factor model (Edvardsson et al. 2008), the EFA supported a two‐factor structure, consistent with the English (Edvardsson et al. 2009) and Korean (Yoon et al. 2018) versions. Unlike the original version, the Climate of Hospitality subscale did not emerge as a distinct subscale in the Turkish adaptation. Instead, items originally belonging to this subscale loaded onto the Climate of Safety and Climate of Everydayness subscales, resulting in a revised two‐factor structure. Consistent with the Korean adaptation (Yoon et al. 2018), the resulting subscales were labelled Climate of Safety and Climate of Everydayness, with the approval of the scale developer. Although the composition of the subscales differed from that of the original instrument, the overall structure of the PCQ‐P remained conceptually aligned with the original version. These findings suggest that the subscale structure of the PCQ‐P may vary across cultural contexts while preserving its conceptual coherence.

The CFA findings underscore the importance of prioritizing empirical clarity and factorial distinctiveness when evaluating item performance in different cultures. The CFA findings provide supportive evidence for the construct validity of the PCQ‐P‐Tur and highlight the importance of balancing statistical model fit with conceptual interpretability during scale adaptation. Several model modifications were implemented to ensure that the goodness‐of‐fit indices reached the threshold values commonly recommended in the literature (R. B. Kline 2023). First, Item 9 was excluded from the final measurement model due to significant cross‐loadings on both the Climate of Safety and Climate of Everydayness subscales in the first‐order CFA. This issue indicated that the item did not clearly represent a single latent factor and therefore reflected overlapping dimensions (Ximénez et al. 2022). In contrast, Yoon et al. (2015) retained the same item despite similar cross‐loadings by assigning it to a single subscale based on theoretical considerations. This difference illustrates that decisions regarding item retention during scale validation may reasonably differ depending on whether conceptual or empirical criteria are prioritized. Second, only two additional covariates were added between error terms, in line with recommendations that models with more than 12 items should include no more than three such modifications (Gürbüz 2024). These modifications resulted in a 16‐item model with acceptable CMIN/DF, SRMR, IFI and CFI values, yielding a clearer and more parsimonious factor structure. However, RMSEA, GFI and TLI still indicated poor fit. Recent research findings suggest that in SEM studies using ML estimation with small samples (n < 250), reporting CMIN/df, the p‐value for CMIN, CFI and SRMR is always advisable when presenting model fit statistics, while not reporting TLI and RMSEA (Gürbüz 2024). Similarly, it is underlined that GFI and AGFI are highly sensitive to sample size (R. B. Kline 2023). Furthermore, it is reported that CMIN and absolute goodness‐of‐fit indices (RMSEA, GFI and AGFI) are highly sensitive to non‐normal data and to potential model‐related issues involving covariances (Gürbüz 2024; R. B. Kline 2023). Although the relatively modest CFA sample size, the non‐normal distribution of the data and model‐related characteristics may partly explain the less satisfactory RMSEA, GFI and TLI values observed in this study, these factors should be taken into account when interpreting the overall model fit. Taken together, the CFA findings provide supportive evidence for the factorial structure of the PCQ‐P‐Tur, while the mixed fit indices suggest interpreting the factorial structure in light of both the empirical findings and the underlying theoretical framework.

Evaluating the concurrent validity of the PCQ‐P‐Tur in this study, patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate and subscales of PCQ‐P‐Tur demonstrated moderate, positive correlations with perceptions of individualized care. As highlighted by Post (2016), moderate correlations in the range of 0.40–0.60 are often expected and acceptable when scales assess related but non‐identical constructs, particularly in the context of complex, subjective phenomena such as patient perceptions of care. Moreover, variation in correlation strengths between the subscales of PCQ‐P‐Tur and individualized care may indicate that patients perceive and evaluate the dimensions of the person‐centred care climate differently, thereby reinforcing the construct's multidimensional nature. In line with psychometric recommendations cautioning against interpreting moderate validity coefficients (Post 2016), these results suggest that the PCQ‐P‐Tur provides supportive, albeit not strong, evidence of concurrent validity. Although the correlation strength was reported as strong in the Finnish adaptation of PCQ‐P by Stolt et al. (2021), these findings align with the observation of positive relationships in the PCQ‐P‐Fin. The observed differences in correlation strength between these two studies may reflect cultural and contextual differences in patients' care expectations or healthcare delivery models, as well as differences in factor structures across adaptations.

The findings show that both the PCQ‐P‐Tur and its subscales have excellent internal consistency. The high Cronbach's alpha coefficients for both the overall scale and its subscales indicate that the items consistently measure coherent constructs of the person‐centred care climate, supporting the internal consistency of the adapted scale (Boateng et al. 2018). These values were similar to those of the original scale (Edvardsson et al. 2008) and consistent with earlier current findings (Kobrai‐Abkenar et al. 2020; Stolt et al. 2021; Yoon et al. 2018, 2015). Although Cronbach's alpha coefficients approaching 1.00 may sometimes raise concerns regarding item redundancy or excessive homogeneity (Tavakol and Dennick 2011; Vaske et al. 2017), this interpretation should be considered in light of the conceptual purpose of the instrument. The PCQ‐P‐Tur was specifically developed to assess a clearly defined and conceptually coherent construct. Therefore, while the high internal consistency may reflect precise measurement of the conceptually coherent person‐centred care climate, the possibility of some degree of item overlap cannot be completely ruled out. Furthermore, the high item–total correlations suggest that each item contributes meaningfully to the overall measurement of the construct, exceeding the recommended minimum criterion of 0.30 (Boateng et al. 2018). Overall, these findings provide additional psychometric evidence supporting the reliability of the PCQ‐P‐Tur for assessing patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate in acute care settings.

The findings suggest that patients perceived the care climate in the participating acute care settings as highly person‐centred. Compared with findings from international studies conducted in long‐term and other institutional healthcare settings (Aljuaid et al. 2023; Bergland et al. 2015; Kobrai‐Abkenar et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2019; Yoon et al. 2018, 2015), the scores observed in this study tend to be higher. However, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in healthcare settings, patient populations and cultural contexts. Additionally, consistent with previous studies (Aljuaid et al. 2023; Bergland et al. 2015; Kobrai‐Abkenar et al. 2020; Stolt et al. 2021; Yoon et al. 2018), Turkish patients highlight the Climate of Safety as the most critical dimension in care settings. The findings suggest that for Turkish patients, a home‐like atmosphere is valued, but it is the sense of being in a safe, organized and professionally managed environment that most strongly defines a person‐centred climate.

Implications for the Profession and/or Patient Care

Incorporating patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate into routine assessment may provide valuable insight into how patients experience communication, respect, involvement and responsiveness to their individual needs. Particular attention to the physical and social dimensions of the care climate may further illuminate how patients experience the care setting and how these dimensions may support or constrain person‐centred care. Such perspectives may help inform efforts to better integrate patients' needs, preferences and expectations into care delivery.

These insights may also support nurses and other healthcare professionals in reflecting on how person‐centred principles translate into routine interactions and care practices. Assessment using the PCQ‐P‐Tur may also help identify areas for improvement in communication, patient involvement and responsiveness to individual needs, thereby informing efforts to strengthen person‐centred care in acute care settings.

Beyond individual care practices, patient‐reported perceptions may provide healthcare managers with complementary information about organizational conditions that facilitate or constrain a person‐centred care climate. Staffing adequacy, workload, leadership support, resource availability, communication practices and opportunities for meaningful staff–patient interaction may warrant consideration when developing organizational and quality improvement strategies to sustain person‐centred care.

Strengths and Limitations

This study strengthens the person‐centred care literature by bringing patients' perceptions to the forefront through the cultural adaptation and validation of a widely used scale for care climate. This study is the first to report the cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the Person‐Centred Climate Questionnaire—Patient Version (PCQ‐P) in Turkish healthcare settings. Examining the scale across both medical and surgical units provides a broader understanding of how person‐centred care climates are perceived in diverse clinical contexts. In addition to introducing a Turkish perspective to the measurement of person‐centred care climate, the study provides a foundation for more consistent evaluation of care climates and supports future cross‐cultural and outcomes‐focused research.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The first methodological limitation of this study is its descriptive design, which restricts the evaluation of the scale's temporal stability and performance. Because the data were collected at a single point in time, predictive validity and test–retest reliability could not be established. Test–retest reliability could not be assessed because patients were recruited from acute care settings, where short and variable hospital stays made repeated assessments difficult. A very short retest interval was also avoided to prevent memory and practice effects from influencing participants' responses. Therefore, the temporal stability of the measurement should be examined in future studies using an appropriate test–retest interval. Second, although random allocation was used to create the EFA and CFA subsamples for internal validation, the use of convenience sampling among 300 patients from the acute care settings of a public hospital may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, the study relied on patient self‐reported data, which may be subject to response bias. Some hospitalized patients, particularly those experiencing discomfort, fatigue or other effects of their clinical condition, may have had difficulty providing fully accurate or complete responses. Fourth, the inclusion criteria required participants to be conscious, literate and able to communicate, which may have led to the exclusion of more severely ill or vulnerable patients. As a result, the findings may mainly reflect the perceptions of relatively stable inpatients. Fifth, the factor structure did not replicate the original three‐subscale, 17‐item model, as the EFA supported a two‐subscale solution and the CFA yielded a 16‐item structure. Finally, although model modifications produced a 16‐item structure with acceptable fit indices, some goodness‐of‐fit indices suggested less‐than‐ideal fit. Sixth, the very high internal consistency of the Climate of Safety subscale warrants consideration, as such a high coefficient may indicate similarity or overlap in item content. Although this finding indicates strong internal consistency, item redundancy cannot be completely ruled out. In particular, the poor RMSEA, along with the less satisfactory GFI and TLI values, represents an important limitation of the final model and suggests that the proposed factor structure does not achieve satisfactory fit across all evaluated criteria. Accordingly, the CFA findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies with larger and more diverse samples are needed to confirm the model's robustness, clarify the potential influence of cultural or contextual factors on the scale's dimensional structure, and examine alternative model specifications.

Conclusion

The PCQ‐P‐Tur demonstrated satisfactory reliability and provided supportive evidence of validity for assessing patients' perceptions of the person‐centred care climate in the acute care settings of a public hospital. Furthermore, this study adds to the existing evidence on the PCQ‐P by providing additional psychometric evidence for the adapted Turkish version. However, further validation studies across different regions of Türkiye, in diverse national or international healthcare settings, and with larger samples are recommended to further examine the reliability, factorial structure and generalizability of the PCQ‐P‐Tur for assessing patients' perceptions of person‐centred care. The significant positive moderate correlation between the ICS‐B‐Patient and the PCQ‐P‐Tur suggests that patients' perceptions of individualized care are linked to the overall person‐centredness of the care climate. Nurses working in acute care settings can use the PCQ‐P‐Tur to assess the person‐centred climate of their units. Implementing the PCQ‐P‐Tur may facilitate the evaluation of care climates and contribute to the development of safe, supportive, and individualized care.
