Serum cotinine-assessed tobacco exposure, cadmium co-exposure, and childhood obesity: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fanjia Guo, Changming Ding, Simeng Gu, Wanying Shi, Xinhan Zhang, Yingli Qu, Peiwei Xu, Zhe Mo, Xiaofeng Wang, Sujun Yan, Peijie Sun, Feng Zhao, Ying Zhu, Yuebin Lv, Zhijian Chen, Xiaoming Shi, Xiaoming Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between tobacco exposure and obesity remains controversial, with the potential modifying role of cadmium (Cd) unclear. Childhood population provide a critical window to assess low-dose tobacco effects. This study aimed to investigate the association between serum cotinine-assessed tobacco exposure and childhood obesity, and further evaluate whether Cd exposure acts as a potential effect modifier in this association.MethodsThis nationwide cross-sectional study investigated 5,595 school-aged children from 2017 to 2018 China National Human Biomonitoring. Serum cotinine levels were measured to assess tobacco exposure. General obesity was determined using BMI Z-score, and central obesity was assessed via waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio, and body roundness index. Weighted regression and restricted cubic splines (RCS) assessed associations between cotinine and obesity, with interaction analyses for Cd exposure.ResultsCompared to the lowest cotinine tertile, individuals with the highest tertile had higher odds of general obesity (OR = 1.485; 95% CI: 1.096, 2.011) and central obesity (OR = 1.333; 95% CI: 1.030, 1.723). RCS models indicated linear and positive associations. Cd exposure significantly attenuated the association between cotinine levels and general obesity (Pinteraction = 0.002), with stronger effects observed in low Cd subgroups (OR = 1.279; 95% CI: 1.133, 1.444).ConclusionsThese results indicate positive associations of tobacco exposure with general and central obesity among nonsmoking children, and Cd acted as an effect modifier. This study suggested that environmental tobacco reduction and considering Cd co-exposure could have implications for obesity in children and adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12940-026-01262-2.

Introduction

Childhood obesity has emerged as a critical global public health concern [1], with the prevalence tripling from 1990 to 2021 globally [2], and the prevalence is estimated to surge to 15.1% by 2030 in China [3]. Strong evidence has associated childhood general and central obesity with adverse health outcomes, such as metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, and increased mortality rates [4, 5]. Environmental pollutants, including tobacco exposure, are emerging as potential risk factors contributing to the rising prevalence of childhood obesity [6].

Environmental tobacco exposure resulting from secondhand smoke (SHS) is one of the major burdens on childhood health [7]. Globally, SHS has been reported to be responsible for 4.5 million disability-adjusted life-years among children under 14 years of age [8]. Notably, up to 60% of children are exposed to SHS globally [9]. Tobacco smoke releases numerous toxicological chemicals, triggering inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, leading to cellular and tissue injury [10, 11]. These mechanisms may dysregulate energy metabolism by impairing insulin signalling in adipocytes and altering hypothalamic appetite regulation, thereby contributing to childhood obesity [12].

Nevertheless, the relationship between environmental tobacco exposure and obesity remains controversial. While active smoking is consistently associated with reduced body weight, and smoking cessation with subsequent weight gain [13, 14], emerging studies have associated active smoking with central obesity [15], defined as the excessive abdominal fat accumulation, indicating potential differential impacts on fat distribution. For passive smoking, epidemiological evidence shows consistent association on general obesity (defined by body mass index, BMI) but conflicting findings concerning central obesity. Studies from the United States, Europe and low- and medium-income countries have suggested that exposure to SHS, a low-level tobacco exposure, is associated with increased BMI and a higher prevalence of general obesity [16–18]. Regarding central obesity, a cross-sectional study among Chinese schoolchildren did not find a significant association between SHS exposure and central obesity [19], whereas a longitudinal investigation in Finland reported a significantly positive association [20]. The controversial results observed across studies may arise from differences in tobacco exposure levels, variations in SHS measurements, population heterogeneity, and differences in confounding variables. Notably, cotinine, a validated biomarker for low-level tobacco exposure [21], overcomes recall biases in questionnaires, especially among nonsmokers and enables exploration of dose‒response relationships. Elucidating the relationships between serum cotinine levels and obesity in a large-scale population with diverse genetic and environmental backgrounds in China can provide a more precise understanding of the metabolic impacts associated with low-level tobacco exposure among children and adolescents [22].

Cadmium (Cd), a heavy metal present in tobacco smoke and prevalent in the environment, may act as an effect modifier in the cotinine-obesity relationship. While smoking is a known source of Cd, the association of Cd with obesity remains controversial. Epidemiological studies from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey in the United States (NHANES) and Korea (KNHANES) reported an inverse association between Cd levels and obesity [23, 24]. However, experimental studies suggest that Cd exposure causes adipose tissue dysfunction and induces an obesity-associated metabolic profile [25]. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of Cd exposure sources and metabolic effects in humans. Notably, tobacco smoke not only is a vector of nicotine but also delivers complex chemical mixtures, including Cd [26, 27]. Both cotinine and Cd are closely linked to oxidative stress and inflammation [28, 29], suggesting potential biological interaction. However, it remains unclear whether and how co-exposure to Cd modifies the metabolic impact of low-dose tobacco exposure in children, given their distinct exposure patterns compared to adults. Current studies have predominantly assessed single exposures, potentially neglecting the interactions in pediatric populations. Thus, examining the interactions between cotinine and Cd is essential for addressing the gaps in our understanding of how tobacco smoke contributes to childhood obesity.

Current epidemiological studies on tobacco exposure and childhood obesity have several limitations, with controversial results, unclear dose‒response relationships and undressed potential interactions between Cd and nicotine. Taking these gaps into account, we conducted a comprehensive cross-sectional study based on the China National Human Biomonitoring (CNHBM) 2017–2018. First, we examined the relationship between cotinine-assessed tobacco exposure and the prevalence of obesity among nonsmoking children and adolescents. The potential modifying role of Cd exposure was subsequently analysed. Understanding the effects of these exposures could contribute to more targeted public health interventions for mitigating childhood obesity and its associated risks.

Material & methods

Study population

This study analysed data from the CNHBM survey (2017–2018) conducted by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC). The detailed design, methods, and recruitment process have been described previously [30]. Briefly, the CNHBM survey employed a multistage, probability-based sampling approach to ensure a demographical representation of individuals aged 3–79 y across China. The samples were selected via a three-stage process. First, 152 counties or districts were randomly selected from 2,869 counties across 31 provinces as primary sampling units through cluster sampling. Second, three villages or communities were chosen from each unit through a probability proportional-to-size sampling method. Finally, 48 individuals were selected from each village or community via stratified random sampling based on the basis of age and sex. The survey ultimately enrolled 21,888 participants from the non-institutionalized civilian population in the CNHBM. The CNHBM (2017–2018) received approval from the ethical review boards of the National Institute of Environmental Health (NIEH) and China CDC (approval number 201701).

In this analysis, 6,340 individuals aged 7–17 years were enrolled. We excluded participants whose blood or urine samples were missing (n = 240) and those whose weight, height, or waist circumference data were not reported (n = 298). Additionally, individuals classified as active smokers, identified through a serum cotinine concentration over 15 ng/mL [31], were excluded (n = 207). The analysis ultimately included 5,595 participants (Figure S1). The demographic characteristics of the individuals included and excluded are available in Table S1.

Laboratory measurements

We collected single-spot urine and blood samples from the participants in heparin anticoagulant and non-anticoagulant tubes. Serum cotinine levels were quantified via isotope dilution high-performance liquid chromatography and atmospheric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometric (HPLC-ESI MS/MS) methods. The detection limit (LOD) for the serum cotinine concentration was 0.05 ng/mL. To focus on environmental tobacco smoke exposure rather than active smoking, individuals with serum cotinine concentrations > 15 ng/mL, a widely used cutoff indicative of active smoking [16, 32], were excluded. In the primary analysis, serum cotinine concentrations were categorized into tertiles for evaluating dose-response relationships. To characterize the population by exposure status, participants with cotinine levels below the LOD (< 0.05 ng/mL) were classified as the no SHS exposure group, whereas those with levels ≥ 0.05 ng/mL were further subdivided into low and high SHS exposure groups based on the median concentration among the exposed participants [16, 31].

With respect to Cd concentrations, previous studies introduced measurements from participants [30]. Briefly, Cd concentrations in urine (UCd) were measured via inductively coupled plasma dynamic reaction cell mass spectrometry (ICP–MS, PerkinElmer NexI ON350, Turku, Finland) at key CNHBM laboratories, adhering to standardized protocols. Quality control measures were used in all analyses. The spiked samples demonstrated a mean recovery rate between 93.2% and 100.7%, with relative standard deviations (RSDs) between 1.2% and 3.0%, which is consistent with the criteria for urine and blood trace element concentrations as outlined by China’s biological monitoring quality assurance guidelines. The LOD for UCd was 0.020 µg/L. Measurements below the LODs were observed for 0.20% of the UCd samples. For these values, adjustments were made through division of the LOD by the square root of two.

Outcomes

Trained technicians conducted physical examinations following standardized procedures to obtain anthropometric data, including body weight, height, and waist circumference (WC). BMI was derived by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. To account for age and sex variations, BMI Z-scores were calculated. Additionally, the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) was determined by dividing the WC in meters by the height in meters, with the result expressed as a percentage (WHtR%). In this study, general obesity was defined as overall adiposity assessed by BMI, whereas central obesity was defined as abdominal adiposity assessed by waist circumference and related indices. General obesity was classified according to the age- and sex-specific BMI standards for school-aged adolescents (WS/T 586–2018). Central obesity was defined according to the Chinese criteria of a high waist circumference screening threshold among children and adolescents aged 7–18 y (WS/T 611–2018). Overall, the assessment of general obesity relied on BMI Z-scores and their prevalence, whereas central obesity was evaluated through WC, the WHtR%, the BRI, and its prevalence.

Covariates

Parents or guardians completed standardized self-administered questionnaires to gather information regarding demographic and lifestyle factors. The covariates included age, sex, residential area, household income, physical activity, and food consumption (rice, red meat, vegetables, fruits, and seafood over the last year). Physical activity was categorized on the basis of the daily duration as less than 20 min, 20–60 min, and 60 or more min. Dietary intake was evaluated via the Chinese Food Frequency Questionnaire [34]. Rice consumption was categorized as less than once per day, 1–3 times per day, and 3 or more times per day. Meat consumption was classified as less than 7 times per week, 7–14 times per week, and 14 or more times per week. Vegetable consumption was classified as less than 14 times per week and 14 or more times per week. Fruit consumption was categorized as less than 7 times per week and 7 or more times per week. Seafood consumption was divided into no consumption, less than once/week and once/week or more.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for variables were stratified by obesity status, with categorical variables presented as weighted proportions and continuous variables as weighted geometric means (GM) and standard errors. The correlation coefficients between serum cotinine levels and UCd were calculated using Spearman correlation. Missing covariates were addressed via multiple imputations via the R package ‘mice’ (version 3.17.0). Log-transformation was applied to non-normally distributed exposures.

Weighted multivariate regression analyses were conducted to address the complex sampling design of the CNHBM. Associations between cotinine levels and obesity outcomes were assessed via two approaches: (1) weighted multiple logistic regression to calculate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for general and central obesity, and (2) weighted multiple linear regression for continuous indices, including the BMI Z-score, WC, WHtR, and BRI. Cotinine concentrations were classified as tertiles (with the lower tertile as a reference) in the main models. According to previous studies, cotinine levels can also be categorized into three levels on the basis of cotinine concentrations: no SHS exposure (< 0.05 ng/mL, reference), low SHS exposure (≥ 0.05 ng/mL to ≤ median of exposed participants), and high SHS exposure (> median) [16]. Linear trends were evaluated by incorporating the median values into categories as continuous variables. Furthermore, nonlinear dose‒response relationships were assessed via restricted cubic splines (RCSs) with knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles, with 10th percentile as baseline.

For Cd, weighted multivariate regression models were used to assess associations between UCd and obesity, with UCd treated as tertiles (with the lower tertile as a reference) and ln-transformed continuous variables. The interaction effects between ln-transformed cotinine and Cd exposure on obesity were subsequently evaluated via likelihood ratio tests contrasting models with and without interaction terms.

Stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modifiers of the relationships between ln-transformed cotinine levels and obesity indicators. The participants were subdivided according to age, sex, residence area, family income, physical activity, and food consumption, including meat, seafood, rice, vegetables, and fruit. Interactions between these variables and cotinine levels were assessed via a likelihood ratio test.

To validate the robustness of the observed associations, sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, we used complete data without multiple imputations. Second, general obesity was classified on the basis of a BMI Z-score ≥ 95th percentile [35], whereas central obesity was defined as a WHtR% ≥ 50% [36], to examine whether different definitions of obesity would lead to similar conclusions. Third, we further adjusted for UCd levels via Model 3 to account for potential confounding effects of Cd exposure.

Statistical analyses were performed via R software (version 4.0). All the tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 and Table S2 summarize the basic demographics according to general obesity and central obesity status. The mean age of the individuals was 11.55 (2.98) years. Of the 5595 participants, the weighted prevalence was 9.31% (N = 521) for general obesity and 22.14% (N = 1239) for central obesity. The mean BMI was 18.72 (3.90) kg/m2, WC was 65.85 (25.22) cm, WHtR% was 44.45% (18.57%), and BRI was 2.39 (1.68). Among all the participants, the overall concentration of cotinine in the serum was 0.34 (0.15, 0.71) ng/mL, and a total of 4984 (89.08%) participants reported SHS exposure. Compared with individuals without general obesity, individuals with general obesity tended to be older, predominantly boys, living in urban areas and engaging in more seafood consumption. In addition, participants with general obesity had higher cotinine levels and lower UCd levels than did those without general obesity. As indicated in Table S2, participants with central obesity tended to be older, live in urban areas, and have more seafood consumption and less fruit consumption. Participants with central obesity presented significantly higher levels of serum cotinine and lower concentrations of UCd. In addition, BMI, WC, WHtR% and BRI were found to be greater in participants with general or central obesity. In our study population, serum cotinine levels showed a weak negative correlation with UCd (Spearman’s r = -0.12, p < 0.05).

Table 2 shows the associations of cotinine with BMI Z-scores and the prevalence of general obesity, adjusted for potential confounders. A significant positive association was identified between cotinine levels and general obesity. Compared with participants in the lowest tertile, participants in the highest cotinine tertile had a higher BMI Z-score (β = 0.555; 95% CI: 0.025, 1.084) and an elevated prevalence of general obesity (OR = 1.485; 95% CI: 1.096, 2.011) in the fully adjusted models. Ln-transformed cotinine, modelled as a continuous variable, was significantly and positively related to general obesity. The adjusted OR (95% CI) of general obesity was 1.124 (1.041, 1.214), and the β (95% CI) of the BMI Z-score was 0.171 (0.025, 0.316), for each 1-unit increment in ln-transformed cotinine levels. When categorized by SHS exposure, the associations with BMI Z-score and general obesity were significant in participants with high cotinine levels compared with those with no SHS exposure (Table S3). Spline regression showed linearity in the association between serum cotinine (Poverall < 0.001, Pnonlinear = 0.244) general obesity prevalence (Fig. 1), and BMI Z-scores (Figure S2).

Table 3 displays the associations between cotinine levels and WC, WHtR%, BRI, and the prevalence of central obesity among the school-aged population. Compared with participants in the lowest tertile, those in the highest cotinine tertile had higher WC (β = 1.507; 95% CI: 0.004, 3.010), WHtR% (β = 1.654; 95% CI: 0.511, 2.797), and BRI (β = 0.229; 95% CI: 0.047, 0.411). The OR (95% CI) of central obesity in participants with the highest tertile level of cotinine was 1.333 (1.030, 1.723) compared with the lowest tertile. When cotinine was treated as a continuous variable, participants with higher cotinine levels had a higher WC (β = 0.448; 95% CI: 0.012, 0.884), WHtR% (β = 0.514; 95% CI: 0.172, 0.856), BRI (β = 0.075; 95% CI: 0.034, 0.117), and higher prevalence of central obesity (OR = 1.124; 95% CI: 1.041, 1.214), for per 1-unit increase in ln-transformed cotinine. When classified by SHS exposure, participants with high cotinine levels had an elevated BRI and a higher prevalence of central obesity than did participants with no SHS exposure (Table S4). As shown in Fig. 1, positive linear associations were evident between serum cotinine levels and central obesity prevalence (Poverall < 0.001, Pnonlinear = 0.243) in the RCS models. As shown in Figure S2, the relationships between cotinine and BRI (Poverall = 0.023, Pnonlinear = 0.582) were positive and linear in the RCS models.

The stratified analyses revealed no significant differences in the estimated associations of serum cotinine with general or central obesity according to age, sex, residence area, physical activity, or food consumption (Figure S3). A significant difference was observed in the effects of cotinine levels between the different family income groups. The association of cotinine with central obesity was significantly higher in participants with lower family income (OR = 1.279; 95% CI: 1.133, 1.444) than in those with higher family income (Pinteraction = 0.005).

The associations between serum cotinine levels and both general obesity and central obesity (Table S6) remained robust in the sensitivity analyses. The primary results were consistent in the analyses using complete data without multiple imputation. These associations were also sustained when alternative definitions of obesity were applied, including defining general obesity as a BMI Z-score ≥ 95th and central obesity as a WHtR% ≥ 50%. Additionally, the observed relationships persisted following additional adjustments for UCd levels, reinforcing the robustness of the results.

Discussion

The nationwide, representative Chinese study investigated the association of cotinine levels with obesity among a school-aged population with low-level tobacco exposure. After accounting for potential confounders, a notable positive relationship emerged between serum cotinine concentrations and both general and central obesity. Furthermore, the relationship between cotinine levels and obesity was stronger in individuals exposed to low levels of Cd. These findings highlight the independent association between tobacco exposure and general and central obesity among nonsmoking children and adolescents.

The relationship between tobacco exposure and obesity is multifaceted and not yet fully understood. While active smoking results in weight suppression through nicotine appetite regulation [14], our findings align with those of epidemiological studies on SHS exposure [16, 18], highlighting the positive association of tobacco exposure with general obesity at low doses. A study across 38 countries with low and middle incomes revealed that adolescents aged 12–15 y with daily self-reported SHS exposure exhibited a notably increased risk of general obesity compared with unexposed adolescents [18]. Similarly, studies in Europe and the U.S. have shown that childhood passive smoke exposure, indicated by urinary or serum cotinine, is significantly associated with an increased prevalence of general obesity among children [16, 17]. However, studies examining the link between tobacco exposure and central obesity remain inconsistent. A study of Chinese schoolchildren aged 6–12 years revealed a positive relationship between questionnaire-based SHS exposure and general obesity, but no significant relationship with central obesity, with the use of the age- and sex-specific 90th percentiles for WC as criteria [19]. In contrast, a longitudinal cohort study in Finland reported that parental smoking measured via questionnaires during childhood elevated the risk of central obesity later in life [20]. The metabolism of nicotine is modulated by diverse biological and behavioral determinants, including genetic polymorphisms in enzymes, age, sex, and dietary consumption [21], which may explain the inconsistency in the association between tobacco exposure and childhood central obesity to some extent. In our study, the utilization of serum cotinine as a quantitative biomarker for tobacco exposure strengthened the inference by capturing actual internal doses. The consistent results across multiple adiposity indices (WC, WHtR%, BRI) further confirm the stability of the association. Given the established link between central obesity and various health risks such as allergic asthma [37], further research is needed to investigate the potential impact of cotinine on central obesity.

In addition, as demonstrated in the stratified analysis, children from low-income households had a stronger association between elevated cotinine levels and central obesity prevalence. As cotinine serves as a biomarker of SHS exposure, it may exhibit an amplified relationship with central obesity in disadvantaged populations due to socioeconomic vulnerability. Children with a low level of socioeconomic status often experience prolonged SHS exposure [38], which is attributable to factors such as lower parental education levels, limited health awareness, and poor living conditions. Additionally, low-income populations face compounded environmental conditions, including lower-quality diets and reduced opportunities for physical activity [39, 40], which may collectively exacerbate metabolic dysregulation. Furthermore, chronic stress in low-income environments may increase cortisol and fat intake [41], which may compound the obesogenic effects of cotinine. Given the stronger link between tobacco exposure and childhood central obesity in low-income households, public health policies should prioritize this vulnerable group [42].

The pathways through which tobacco exposure contributes to obesity development are multifactorial and may vary depending on the exposure level [43]. Experimental studies in rats demonstrated that nicotine administration induced a dose-dependent decrease in body weight, with higher doses significantly attenuating weight gain independent of caloric intake, whereas dose reduction led to weight gain [44], which indicates that the regulatory effects of nicotine on body weight are directly dependent on exposure level. Elevated cotinine concentrations at relatively low exposure levels can be linked to increased risks of both general and central obesity via mechanisms related to oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and endocrine disruption [45, 46]. Cigarette smoke can stimulate the release of inflammatory mediators by activating immune and inflammatory cells, potentially increasing the risk of obesity [47]. Oxidative stress induced by cigarette smoke might interact with inflammatory pathways, further increasing the likelihood of obesity [48]. Maternal nicotine exposure in rats induces hypothalamic leptin resistance in offspring and alters orexigenic and anorexigenic neuropeptide expression, ultimately driving hyperphagia and decreased energy expenditure, which may explain the pathogenesis of general obesity [49]. In contrast, animal studies have indicated that maternal tobacco exposure increases offspring visceral adipogenesis through disruption of glucocorticoid metabolic homeostasis and impairment of vitamin D-mediated signalling pathways [50]. Future research should elucidate these mechanisms to increase the understanding of the effects of tobacco exposure on obesity.

While Cd exposure alone exhibited an inverse association with obesity prevalence, which is consistent with prior epidemiological studies [23, 24], our study further revealed that Cd modified the relationships between cotinine levels and general obesity. The weak negative correlation between serum cotinine and UCd suggests that in nonsmoking children, Cd burden is primarily driven by non-tobacco sources, such as dietary intake or environmental pollution [51], and is independent of SHS exposure. Thus, our results suggests that the metabolic disruptive effects of tobacco smoke are more discernible in relatively low Cd exposure, while the overwhelming systemic toxicity of Cd [52] such as the direct damage to mitochondrial function, insulin signalling pathways, and endocrine regulation, may alter the metabolic effects of cotinine. Experimental evidence shows that Cd accumulates in fat, activates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR)‑mediated metabolic pathways, promoting energy expenditure and potentially reducing fat mass [53]. Hence, high Cd exposure might mask the association of tobacco exposure on obesity, whereas at lower Cd levels metabolic disruption of tobacco exposure becomes more apparent. Moreover, Cd exposure may influence the activity of the hepatic nicotine-metabolizing enzyme CYP2A6 by inducing its expression [54], thereby increasing the metabolic efficiency of nicotine and cotinine, which could diminish the biological effects of cotinine. At low Cd levels, CYP2A6 inhibition can prolong nicotine exposure, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and disrupting adipose tissue metabolism [55]. Additionally, animal studies suggest that neonatal nicotine exposure can cause leptin resistance and inhibit hypothalamic leptin signaling [56], whereas Cd exposure has been shown to reduce leptin mRNA expression in adipose tissue [57]. Further exploration is essential to elucidate the mechanisms driving the interaction between Cd and cotinine.

Our study has potential public health implications. First, this study revealed that increased tobacco exposure may exacerbate childhood obesity, posing a significant threat to public health. Public health policies should place greater emphasis on tobacco control, particularly in reducing children’s exposure to tobacco smoke. Second, our findings emphasize the need to consider various environmental factors when assessing obesity risk. In regions with low heavy metal exposure, such as rural regions, strengthening smoke-free legislation could significantly reduce childhood obesity risk. In contrast, in regions with severe Cd contamination, smoking control alone may not be sufficient to curb obesity epidemics. Identifying and addressing other contributing risk factors is essential. Moreover, we found a statistically stronger association between tobacco exposure and obesity among children from low-income families. This underscores the need for targeted public health policies that prioritize this vulnerable group. Children from socioeconomically disadvantaged environments may face compounded risks, which may exacerbate the effects of tobacco exposure. Public health interventions should address both environmental exposure and social determinants of health to reduce the risk of childhood obesity and promote healthier outcomes for vulnerable populations.

This study has several strengths. First, this study was based on a nationally representative sample of the Chinese school-aged population to examine the relationship between tobacco exposure and childhood obesity. To ensure data quality, standardized methods and strict quality control protocols were employed during the data collection process and laboratory analyses. Tobacco exposure was objectively measured by assessing serum cotinine levels. While most previous studies used BMI to measure general obesity, our study incorporated several objective indicators, including WC, WHtR%, and BRI, to evaluate central obesity, thereby providing a more comprehensive exploration of the effects of low-dose tobacco exposure on childhood obesity. However, some limitations should be noted. First, owing to its cross-sectional design, the study cannot establish causal relationships or temporal effects. Further prospective research is needed to confirm these results. Second, while serum cotinine served as an objective biomarker for environmental tobacco exposure, misclassification of exposure status remains inevitable. For instance, participants classified as no SHS exposure (< 0.05 ng/mL, reference) group might still have very low-level background exposure from ubiquitous sources like thirdhand smoke. However, such misclassification would likely weaken the observed associations, suggesting that the true effects might be stronger than reported in our study. Third, we lacked data on self-reported household tobacco exposure or other detailed information, including the duration, frequency, and specific types of tobacco exposure (e.g., traditional cigarettes and electronic nicotine delivery devices). In addition, other potential toxins from tobacco smoke, such as PAHs, were not considered in this study. Moreover, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as maternal smoking during pregnancy [58] and parental BMI [59], remains a concern. Dietary patterns and physical activity levels, which are difficult to measure accurately, may also contribute to residual confounding factors.

Conclusion

Our study revealed a significant positive association between serum cotinine levels and an increased prevalence of general and central obesity among the nonsmoking school-aged population. These associations were more pronounced in individuals with lower levels of Cd exposure. Our findings underscore the positive association between cotinine and obesity at low levels of tobacco exposure and the potential modifying effects of Cd. Our results highlight the need for public health strategies targeting environmental tobacco reduction and considering Cd co-exposure, to mitigate tobacco-related obesity in children and adolescents. Further studies are crucial to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying these relationships.
