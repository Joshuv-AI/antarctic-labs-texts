Layer codes as partially self-correcting quantum memories
Shouzhen Gu, Libor Caha, Shin Ho Choe, Zhiyang He, Aleksander Kubica, Eugene Tang

Abstract
We investigate layer codes, a family of three-dimensional stabilizer codes that can achieve optimal scaling of code parameters and a polynomial energy barrier, as candidates for self-correcting quantum memories. First, we introduce two decoding algorithms for layer codes with provable guarantees for local stochastic and adversarial noise, respectively. We then prove that layer codes constitute partially self-correcting quantum memories which outperform previously analyzed models such as the cubic code and the welded solid code. Notably, we argue that partial self-correction without the requirement of efficient decoding is more common than expected, as it arises solely from a diverging energy barrier. This draws a sharp distinction between partially self-correcting systems and partially self-correcting memories. Another novel aspect of our work is an analysis of layer codes constructed from random Calderbank–Shor–Steane codes. We show that these random layer codes have optimal scaling (up to logarithmic corrections) of code parameters and a polynomial energy barrier. Finally, we present numerical studies of their memory times and report behavior consistent with partial self-correction.

Introduction

Self-correcting quantum memories (SCQM) are systems that can passively protect quantum information from noise
Ter 15
;
BLP+ 16
.
Development of SCQM in three or fewer spatial dimensions provide an attractive path to fault-tolerant quantum computers
Sho 96
;
Pre 98
, and also offers fundamental insights into to quantum many-body physics

Despite their practical and theoretical importance, the existence of SCQM in 3D remains largely unresolved while in 2D, there are many no-go results

;
HP 12
;
AFH 09
.

One may search for SCQM within systems arising from stabilizer quantum error-correcting codes

.
In that setting, the Hamiltonian describing the quantum memory is specified by the parity checks of the code, with the ground space corresponding to the codespace.
It is typically assumed that the system is weakly and locally coupled to a Markovian thermal bath

; then, a necessary condition for self-correction is an energy barrier that grows with the linear system size
L

KLCT 16
;
Tem 16
.
The energy barrier captures the largest energy cost the environment needs to pay in order to change the encoded information by applying local errors.
The first example of a SCQM is the 4D toric code

;
AHHH 10
, with an energy barrier scaling as
\Delta=\Theta(L)
.
In 3D, the cubic code

;
BH11b
has an energy barrier scaling as
\log L
and provided the first example of a partially SCQM, where the memory time increases polynomially in

but only for
L<L^{*}=e^{\Theta(\beta)}
(here,
\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature, setting
k_{B}=1
), beyond which there are no guarantees

.
Another example of a partially SCQM is the 3D welded solid code, with the memory time increasing subexponentially in
L
for
L<L^{*}=e^{\Theta(\beta)}

Mic 14
;
Mic 15
;
SY 17
.

Recently, Williamson and Baspin
WB 24
introduced the layer code construction. Layer codes are 3D topological codes that can saturate the Bravyi-Poulin-Terhal-Haah (BPTH) bounds
BPT 10
;
BT 09
;
Haa 21
, achieving optimal scaling of code parameters and energy barrier for 3D stabilizer codes.
A layer code is formed by stacking layers of the surface code
Kit 03
;
BK 98
and intricately coupling them along one-dimensional line defects according to the parity-check matrices of an input Calderbank-Shor-Steane (CSS) code
CS 96
;
Ste 96
.
Since thermal excitations within each layer can move without incurring energy penalty, one may hastily conclude that layer codes are not SCQM
LWH24a
.
This would be consistent with the fact that the 2D toric code is not a SCQM
AFH 09
.
However, when excitations hit line defects, they split and this process is energetically suppressed.
Given a layer code with many line defects, it is unclear which process is dominant, and one might hope for the code to be self-correcting.

In this work, we explore layer codes as potential SCQM.
Motivated by an intuition that layer codes with many line defects may exhibit better self-correction, we go beyond the scenario from Ref.
WB 24
and also consider layer codes constructed from random CSS codes with high-density parity-check matrices.
By developing new proof techniques, we show that, with high probability and up to logarithmic corrections, these random layer codes have optimal scaling of code parameters and energy barrier.
Next, we introduce two decoding algorithms for layer codes:
(i) the cluster decoder, which has a provable threshold against local stochastic noise, and (ii) the concatenated decoder, which corrects against adversarial noise.
Using the concatenated decoder, we then prove that layer codes constructed from quantum Tanner codes
LZ 22
, which are asymptotically good quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes, as well as random layer codes both exhibit partial self-correction stronger than that of the cubic code. In particular, their memory times increase exponentially in
L
for
L<L^{*}=e^{\Theta(\beta)}
.
We also argue that the phenomenon of partial self-correction is more common than previously expected, as it arises from diverging energy barrier.
Finally, we use statistical-mechanical methods to numerically study the memory times of random layer codes and observe scaling behavior consistent with partial self-correction.

Intuitively,

II.1
Quasiparticle excitations

The memory system associated with a stabilizer code is described by a commuting Pauli Hamiltonian
H_{\text{mem}}
, whose terms corresponding to parity checks.
The ground space of
H_{\text{mem}}
corresponds to the codespace and excited states correspond to configurations of unsatisfied checks of the stabilizer code.
In the context of the surface code, unsatisfied checks correspond to locations of quasiparticle excitations, electric charge
e
and a magnetic flux
m
.
In the bulk, excitations can only be created in pairs, as captured by the fusion rules
e\times e=m\times m=1
.
For layer codes, we also have
e
and
m
excitations.
Within individual layers, the fusion rules are the same as for the surface code except in the vicinity of line defects.
There, excitations can move or split between layers; see Fig.
1
.
For layer codes, there is a constant energy penalty to create excitations and their movement within individual layers is not energetically suppressed unless they cross line defects, where they split.

II.2
Layer codes from random CSS codes

One may hope that layer codes with many line defects give rise to SCQM, as the movement of excitations can be energetically suppressed.
Constructing such layer codes would require an input code with a dense parity-check matrix, which emphatically is not a qLDPC code.
In what follows, we prove that such layer codes can achieve favorable parameters.

Let us define a random CSS code by choosing, uniformly at random, two parity-check matrices
H_{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\rho_{X}n\times n}
and
H_{Z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\rho_{Z}n\times n}
satisfying
H_{X}H_{Z}^{\mathrm{T}}=0
.
We denote the resulting ensemble of random codes as
\mathrm{CSS}_{n}(\rho_{X},\rho_{Z})
.
Note that a random CSS code has maximum check weight
\Theta(n)
with high probability.
Consequently, the proof technique from Ref.
WB 24
gives trivial bounds on the parameters of the resulting layer code.
Nevertheless, we are able to show the following theorem; see Appendix
D
for details.

Theorem II.1
.

Let
\mathscr{L}(C)
be a layer code with the input code
C\sim\mathrm{CSS}_{n}(\rho_{X},\rho_{Z})
.
Then
\mathscr{L}(C)
has distance
d=\Omega(n^{2}/\log n)
and energy barrier
\Delta=\Omega(n/\log n)
with high probability.

Since a random layer code has block length
\Theta(n^{3})
, Theorem
II.1
implies that it has parameters
[\![\Theta(n^{3}),\Theta(n),\Omega(n^{2}/\log n)]\!]
, and energy barrier
\Omega(n/\log n)
, with high probability.
Up to logarithmic factors, this is optimal parameter scaling saturating the BPTH bounds.
Notably, this is achieved without using asymptotically good qLDPC codes, in contrast with all previous works
WB 24
;
Por 23
;
LWH24b
.
This has practical implications, as the codes are of smaller size, making them amenable to numerical simulations.

III
Layer code decoders

We now describe two novel decoding algorithms for layer codes, the cluster decoder and the concatenated decoder.
The cluster decoder builds on the renormalization group
BH11a
;
DCP 10
and union-find
DN 21
;
DLB 22
decoders.
It partitions the error syndrome into local clusters and tries to remove them independently.
The concatenated decoder is inspired by the matching
DKLP 02
;
Bro 22
and color code decoders
KD 23
.
It first removes the low-level error syndrome of individual surface code layers, which results in some high-level error syndrome of the input code that is subsequently decoded using any decoder of the input code.

III.1
Cluster decoder

The cluster decoder starts by initializing a list of clusters, each corresponding to the location of an individual excitation.
While the list is nonempty, the cluster decoder:
(i) grows each cluster by adding locations in its neighborhood,
(ii) merges any overlapping clusters, and
(iii) removes any neutral clusters.
To determine if a cluster is neutral, we check whether a system of linear equations associated with that cluster is solvable.
When the list becomes empty, the cluster decoder returns a recovery operator composed of local operators found in step (iii) (that remove neutral clusters); see Fig.
2
(a).

Using similar arguments as in Ref.
BH 13
, we prove that the cluster decoder a has nonzero QEC threshold for the independent and identically distributed depolarizing noise, i.e., for sufficiently small error rate, the logical error rate goes to zero as the layer code size grows; see Appendix
B
.

III.2
Concatenated decoder

The concatenated decoder applies minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM)
DKLP 02
to different types of surface code layers in stages and matches excitations between them with a decoder of the input code, see Fig.
2
(b).
In particular, to decode
Z
errors, it proceeds as follows.

1.

For each
Z
-layer, apply MWPM to eliminate excitations within this layer; this may create new excitations on the
Q
- and
X
-layers.

2.

For each
Q
-layer, apply MWPM to eliminate excitations on this layer; this may create new excitations on the
X
-layers.

3.

For each
X
-layer, evaluate the parity of the number of excitations on this layer. Treating the resulting bit string as a syndrome, use a decoder of the input code to obtain a correction, and apply vertical strings on the corresponding
Q
-layers.

4.

Match the excitations on the
X
-layers in an arbitrary way.

To analyze partial self-correction of random layer codes, we will also consider a modified version of the concatenated decoder that matches all excitations on the
Z
- and
Q
-layers to the top boundary in the first two steps of the algorithm.
For either version, it is important to invoke the decoder of the input code in Step 3 of the decoder to obtain an even number of excitations on each
X
layer, as boundaries of the
X
-layers do not condense
e
anyons. Decoding
X
errors is analogous, where we proceed in the opposite order of
X
-,
Q
-, and
Z
-layers. Note that if the input code decoder is efficient, then so is the concatenated decoder. We prove that the concatenated decoder can correct high-weight adversarial errors.

Theorem III.1
.

Let the input
C
be an
[\![n,k,d]\!]
code with parity checks of weight at most
w
.
Then, given a decoding algorithm for
C
that corrects all
Z
errors up to weight
\alpha d
, where
\alpha>0
, the concatenated decoder corrects all
Z
errors of the layer code
\mathscr{L}(C)
of weight
O(\alpha dn/w)
.

As an immediate corollary, we obtain that for any layer code constructed from a qLDPC code, the concatenated decoder can correct adversarial errors of weight linear in the layer code distance.

IV
Self-correction and quantum memories

In this section, we focus on the performance of families of finite-temperature systems in terms of storing quantum information.
Let
\{C_{n}\}
be a family of stabilizer codes.
Each
C_{n}
is associated with a memory system described by a Hamiltonian
H^{(n)}_{\text{mem}}
.
We work in the Davies weak-coupling limit
Dav 74
, i.e., each memory system is weakly-coupled to its own thermal bath at inverse temperature
\beta
, which is Markovian and acts locally on the physical degrees of freedom of the memory system.
At time
t=0
, the state
\rho^{(n)}(0)
of the memory system is in the ground space of
H^{(n)}_{\rm mem}
.
The evolution of
\rho^{(n)}(t)
is determined by the Lindblad master equation

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho^{(n)}(t)}{dt}=-i\left[H^{(n)}_{\rm mem},\rho^{(n)}(t)\right]+\mathcal{L}^{(n)}(\rho(t)),

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}
is a Lindblad generator describing energy dissipation from the memory system to the bath and it is chosen so that the evolution drives
\rho^{(n)}(t)
to the canonical Gibbs state
\rho^{(n)}_{\beta}\propto\exp\left(-\beta H^{(n)}_{\rm mem}\right)
; see Refs.
Lin 76
;
GKS 76
;
BP 07
.

In order to analyze how the ability to recover
\rho^{(n)}(0)
from
\rho^{(n)}(t)
changes in time
t
, we need a decoder
\Phi
, which is a quantum channel implementing an appropriate recovery and returning

\Phi\left(\rho^{(n)}(t)\right)
that, hopefully, is close to
\rho^{(n)}(0)
.
For concreteness, we consider decoders of the following form

\displaystyle\Phi(\cdot)=\sum_{\sigma}P_{\sigma}\Pi_{\sigma}(\cdot)\Pi_{\sigma}P_{\sigma},

(2)

where the summation is over different error syndromes,
P_{\sigma}
is a Pauli operator with syndrome
\sigma
, and
\Pi_{\sigma}
is a projector onto the subspace with syndrome
\sigma
.
Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
be a constant.
We define the memory time
t_{\text{mem}}
as the maximum time such that for all
t<t_{\text{mem}}
, there exists
\Phi
satisfying

\left\|\Phi\left(\rho^{(n)}(t)\right)-\rho^{(n)}(0)\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon

(3)

for any initial state
\rho^{(n)}(0)
.
We then say that the family
\{C_{n}\}
is self-correcting at inverse temperature
\beta
if the memory time diverges with
n
.

It may happen that
t_{\text{mem}}
grows with
n
for
n\leq n^{*}
and is not guaranteed to increase for
n>n^{*}
, where
n^{*}
is some cutoff size.
This motivates the following definition: we say that the family
\{C_{n}\}
is partially self-correcting if the maximum memory time
t^{*}_{\text{mem}}
scales superexponentially in
\beta
, i.e.,
t^{*}_{\text{mem}}=\exp(\omega(\beta))
. See Definition
C.2
for more details. For instance, the 4D toric code is self-correcting with
t_{\text{mem}}=\Theta(\exp(\beta n))
; the 3D cubic code and 3D welded surface code are partially self-correcting with

t^{*}_{\text{mem}}=\exp(\Omega(\beta^{2}))
and
t^{*}_{\text{mem}}=\exp(\exp(\Omega(\beta))
, respectively, with cutoff size
n^{*}=\exp(\Omega(\beta))
.

We arrive at the following general result.

Theorem IV.1
.

Let
\{C_{n}\}
be a family of qLDPC codes, where
C_{n}
has
n
physical and
k_{n}
logical qubits, and energy barrier
\Delta_{n}
.
If
\Delta_{n}=\Omega(\max(k_{n},\log n))
, then
\{C_{n}\}
is partially self-correcting with maximum memory time
t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}=\exp[\Omega(\Delta_{n^{*}}\beta)]
and cutoff size
n^{*}=\exp(\beta/2)
.

Proof.

We start by introducing the energy barrier decoder
\Phi
.
If
\sigma
is a syndrome for some Pauli error
E
with energy barrier smaller than
\Delta_{n}
, then set
P_{\sigma}=E
; otherwise, choose as
P_{\sigma}
any Pauli operator with syndrome
\sigma
.
We remark that the decoder
\Phi
is primarily of conceptual interest, as the task of finding an appropriate Pauli error
E
may be computationally intractable.

By definition of energy barrier, the decoder
\Phi
successfully corrects all Pauli errors with energy barrier smaller than
\Delta_{n}
.
Using Lemma 1 and Eq. (26) in Ref.
BH11a
, we obtain

\left\|\Phi\left(\rho^{(n)}(t)\right)-\rho^{(n)}(0)\right\|_{1}\leq O(t)n2^{k_{n}}e^{-\beta(\Delta_{n}-f)/2}

(4)

for all
n\leq n^{*}=e^{\beta/2}
, where
f
is a constant depending on locality of
C_{n}
.
We equate the right-hand side of the inequality to a constant
\epsilon
and find the maximum memory time

t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}\geq\exp(\beta(\Delta_{n}-f)/2-k_{n}\log 2-\log n).

(5)

Since
\Delta_{n}=\Omega(\max(k_{n},\log n))=\omega(f)
, the term
\beta\Delta_{n}/2
dominates in the exponent and the result
t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}=\exp[\Omega(\Delta_{n^{*}}\beta)]
follows. In particular,
\Delta_{n}=\Omega(\log n)
implies
t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}=\exp(\Omega(\beta^{2}))
, so the definition of partial self-correction is satisfied.
∎

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
IV.1
, by invoking the energy barriers from Ref.
WB 24
and Theorem
II.1
, we obtain that quantum Tanner layer codes and random layer codes are partially self-correcting; their maximum memory times are
t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}=\exp(\exp(\Omega(\beta)))
with cutoff sizes
n^{*}=\exp(\beta/2)
.

Conventionally, a quantum memory requires the existence of a computationally efficient decoder

;
Bre 16
;
BLP+ 16
.
Thus, we distinguish between (partially) self-correcting systems and (partially) self-correcting
memories
by the efficiency of the decoding algorithm.
In the proof of Theorem
IV.1
, we used the energy barrier decoder, which is not necessarily efficient.
By using the concatenated decoder (or its modified version), we obtain the following stronger result.

Theorem IV.2

(informal)
.

Quantum Tanner layer codes and random layer codes are partially SCQM with respect to the concatenated decoder.

For a precise statement and proof of the fact that quantum Tanner layer codes are partially SCQM, see Theorem
C.7
. For analogous statements regarding random layer codes, see Theorem

and Remark

VI
Conclusions

In our work, we show that layer codes provide a concrete example of partially SCQM, with code parameters and memory time better than for previous 3D constructions.
We also argue that partial self-correction is more common than expected—it arises from a diverging energy barrier and its interplay with code parameters, as exemplified by Theorem
IV.1
.

Fundamentally, layer codes are notable because they saturate the BPTH bounds with an optimal energy barrier.
Practically, though, layer codes constructed from generic families of qLDPC codes are prohibitively qubit-intensive.
To mitigate this issue, we introduce random layer codes and prove their code parameters and energy barrier.
We thus view random layer codes as a step toward more resource-efficient constructions.

Looking ahead, several key challenges include identifying specific families of layer codes, developing better decoders and designing fault-tolerant logical gates.
Progress on these fronts would transform layer codes from a theoretical construct into a practical quantum architecture.

Appendix A
Layer Codes

In this appendix, we provide a short overview of the layer code construction and associated background. The layer code construction is based on coupling different surface code patches in three-dimensional space. For us, a surface code is defined by a two-dimensional square grid, where qubits are placed on edges,
X
checks associated with vertices, and
Z
checks associated with plaquettes. The boundaries of a surface code patch may be rough or smooth, allowing
Z
or
X
strings, respectively, to terminate without causing non-trivial syndromes.

A CSS code
C
is a pair of parity-check matrices
H_{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{X}\times n}
and
H_{Z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{Z}\times n}
satisfying the orthogonality condition
H_{X}H_{Z}^{\mathrm{T}}=0
. The rows of
H_{X}
(resp.
H_{Z}
) define the
X
-type (resp.
Z
-type) stabilizer generators, or checks, of the code. In particular, we will always regard a CSS code as coming equipped with a particular choice of stabilizer generators, and we will generally denote a CSS code explicitly by its parity-check matrices, e.g.,
C=(H_{X},H_{Z})
. The
sparsity

w

Given an arbitrary CSS code

1.

5.

The arrangement of

•

Each

•

Likewise, each

•

The intersection of

The line (segments) defined by non-trivial intersections are called

6.

The physical qubits of the layer code are defined as the union of all physical qubits in the surface codes defining the

The exact form of the stabilizers themselves will not be of great importance to us; we will interface with the stabilizers primarily through the fusion rules that they define (see section

). We refer to the original paper

It will often be convenient to think of the layer code as a map

Note that different choices of parity-check matrices defining the same codespace define distinct layer codes, so the input is really the pair of matrices

A.1
Fusion Rules

We will work with the layer code primarily through its fusion (or condensation) rules. Similar to the surface code, the layer code supports topological excitations, which are also called anyons. A violation of an

Fusion rules characterize the local structure of the layer code by identifying the types of excitations that can be locally annihilated – or

– at a given location. Fig.

The regions away from defect lines, which include trivial intersections between layers, support the usual fusion rules

for the surface code. The fusion rules at the boundaries of layers are also the same as for the surface code, i.e., rough boundaries condense

Appendix B
Decoders for Layer Codes

In this appendix, we define and prove properties of two decoders for the layer code construction. The first, called the cluster decoder, is a renormalization-group (RG) based decoder which has provable threshold against stochastic noise. The second, called the concatenated decoder, is suitable for adversarial noise when the layer code is defined with a qLDPC code as input. We will also use the concatenated decoder as a key ingredient in the proof that layer codes are partially self-correcting.
For the cluster decoder, we consider
X
errors as the description is cleaner compared to that with
Z
errors. For the concatenated decoder we analyze
Z
errors. The cases for the opposite bases are mostly symmetric. Since layer codes are CSS codes, we can correct
X
and
Z
errors independently. Consequently, our threshold result is applicable to the setting of the depolarizing noise too.

B.1

Lemma B.5

The cluster

Proof.

B.2
Concatenated Decoder

The concatenated decoder proceeds by first decoding “low-level” syndromes on the surface code patches in order to obtain a “high-level” syndrome which is a valid syndrome of the input code. We then apply a decoder of the input code to find a high-level correction, which, when lifted to a corresponding correction on the surface code layers, annihilates all syndromes on the layer code. We prove that the concatenated decoder succeeds against adversarial errors of weight at most
O(d)
, where
d
is the distance of the layer code defined with a qLDPC input code (cf. Theorem
B.20
). When the layer code is instantiated with an asymptotically good qLDPC code, the concatenated decoder also succeeds against errors with sufficiently small energy barrier, which is the key property required for partial self-correction (cf. Appendix
C
).

Let
e_{0}
be a
Z

\mathscr{L}(C)
, and let
\sigma_{0}
be its syndrome (decoding
X

, and

to be the

-, and

The decoder operates by applying minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM) to these different layers and using a decoder
\operatorname{Dec}_{C}^{Z}
for
Z
errors of the input code when it gets stuck.
There are four main steps to the decoding procedure:

1.

Considering only the
Z
-layers, apply MWPM on
R_{Z}
to eliminate all excitations on those layers. Because the boundaries of the
Z
-layers are all rough, we may match to any of the four boundaries. Note that we ignore defect lines with the other layers when performing the matching. In particular, this step may create additional excitations on the
Q
- and
X
-layers.

2.

Considering only the
Q
-layers, apply MWPM on
R_{Q}
to eliminate all excitations on those layers (both the original excitations from
\sigma_{0}
and the new ones from the correction in Step 1). We may match to the top and bottom boundaries, which condense
e
-excitations. We again ignore defect lines when performing the matching.

3.

Let
\sigma_{C}
be the indices of all
X
-layers that now contain an odd number of excitations. Viewing
\sigma_{C}
as a syndrome of the input code, we apply the decoder
\operatorname{Dec}_{C}^{Z}
to find a
Z

with that syndrome. For every qubit in

, apply a vertical string operator from the top to the bottom of the corresponding
Q

X
-layers.

4.

Apply MWPM on

to eliminate the remaining excitations on the
X
-layers (from
\sigma_{0}
and the three previous steps). We may not match to any of the boundaries because
X
-layer boundaries do not condense
e
-excitations.

The first step eliminates all excitations on
R_{Z}
. The second eliminates all excitations on
R_{Q}
. Since the second and third steps only apply corrections within

, the fusion rules imply that no new excitations are created in
R_{Z}
. Finally, the fourth step eliminates all excitations in

while not creating additional excitations in

or

. Therefore, as long as all the steps are achievable, the decoder will output a correction that returns the state back to the codespace. The first and second steps are always achievable because we may match to a boundary. The non-trivial result is to show that the input decoder

can correct the syndrome
\sigma_{C}
in the third step and that the resulting number of excitations on every
X
-layer is even so that MWPM can be applied in the fourth step.

Appendix C
Partial Self-Correction of Layer Codes with Quantum Tanner Code Input

C.1
Definition of Partial Self-Correction

Unlike active memories, which rely on frequent feedback-based error-correction loops, passive (self-correcting) quantum systems preserve information through the intrinsic dynamics of the physical system and its coupling to a thermal bath at sufficiently low temperature
T
. The joint unitary evolution of such a system with a thermal bath is generated by a Hamiltonian acting on the composite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm mem}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\rm bath}}
of the form

\displaystyle H=H_{{\rm mem}}\otimes I_{{\rm bath}}+I_{{\rm mem}}\otimes H_{{\rm bath}}+H_{{\rm int}},

(20)

where
H_{{\rm mem}}
is the memory Hamiltonian,
H_{{\rm bath}}
is a heat bath Hamiltonian, and
H_{{\rm int}}
is a memory-bath interaction term.

We will consider memory Hamiltonians which are constructed from the checks of a stabilizer code.
For a CSS code
C=(H_{X},H_{Z})
, let
S_{X}
be the set of
X
-type stabilizer generators and
S_{Z}
the
Z
-type stabilizer generators. Then the associated memory Hamiltonian is defined by

\displaystyle H_{{\rm mem}}=-\sum_{g_{x}\in S_{X}}g_{x}-\sum_{g_{z}\in S_{Z}}g_{z}.

(21)

This Hamiltonian is designed so that the ground space is exactly the codespace of
C
.

At time
t=0
, we initialize the state of the memory in a ground state
\rho(0)
of the Hamiltonian
H_{{\rm mem}}
. The thermal evolution generated by the joint Hamiltonian
H
in Eq. (
20
) drives the quantum state of the memory subsystem towards the canonical Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}=e^{-\beta H_{{\rm mem}}}/{\rm Tr}(e^{-\beta H_{{\rm mem}}})
, where
\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature (we set
k_{B}=1
).

For the study of self-correcting quantum memories, the dynamics generated by
H
is typically modeled using the Davies weak-coupling limit
Dav 74
, which makes a few natural assumptions about the bath and the coupling term. The bath is assumed to be Markovian, and the interaction Hamiltonian is weakly-coupled
\|H_{{\rm int}}\|\ll\|H_{{\rm mem}}\|
and of the form

\displaystyle H_{{\rm int}}=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\otimes B_{\alpha},

(22)

where the jump operators
A_{\alpha}
are
O(1)
-local, meaning they act on a constant number of qubits of the memory system. We assume without loss of generality that the jump operators
A_{\alpha}
are Hermitian operators with operator norm
\|A_{\alpha}\|\leq 1
. The Davies weak-coupling limit
Dav 74
leads to system dynamics governed by the Lindblad equation

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(t)=-i[H_{{\rm mem}},\rho(t)]+\sum_{\alpha,\omega}h(\alpha,\omega)\left(A_{\alpha}(\omega)\rho A^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho,A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{\alpha}(\omega)\}\right),

(23)

where
\omega
are the Bohr frequencies and
A_{\alpha}(\omega)
are Fourier components of
A_{\alpha}
. The coefficients
h(\alpha,\omega)
capture the rates of these jumps and satisfy
\max_{\alpha,\omega}h(\alpha,\omega)=O(1)
. The rate function
h(\alpha,\omega)
satisfies the detailed balance condition

\displaystyle h(\alpha,-\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}h(\alpha,\omega),

(24)

which ensures that the canonical Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
is a fixed-point of the dynamics. If, additionally, the Lindbladian satisfies certain ergodicity conditions,
\rho_{\beta}
is the unique fix point of the dynamics
Spo 77
and the system thermalizes
\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho(t)=\rho_{\beta}
.

Under this setup, we show that layer codes are partially self-correcting quantum memories (SCQM).

Remark C.1
.

While the scaling of the memory time with system size for a genuine self-correcting quantum system is well defined asymptotically, the breakdown of a partially self-correcting system at finite system size means that there is no precise way to talk about the functional scaling of the memory time; it is meaningless to say, for example, that a function “scales exponentially” when restricted to a finite interval. Due to this fact, we adopt a more technical definition of partial self-correction, as explained in the main text.

Definition C.2

(Partially Self-Correcting Quantum System and Memory)
.

Fix a constant
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
\{C_{n}\}
be a family of quantum codes. We define the memory time
t_{\mathrm{mem}}(n,\beta)
as the maximum time such that, for all
t<t_{\mathrm{mem}}
, there exists some decoder
\Phi
satisfying

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi\left(\rho^{(n)}(t)\right)-\rho^{(n)}(0)\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon

(25)

for all initial states
\rho^{(n)}(0)
in the codespace
C_{n}
, where
\rho^{(n)}(t)
is the time-evolved state under the Lindblad equation (
23
) with bath temperature
T=1/\beta
. Let
t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\beta)
denote the maximum value of
t_{\mathrm{mem}}(n,\beta)
over
n
. Then we say that
\{C_{n}\}
is a
partially self-correcting quantum system
if
t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\beta)
scales superexponentially with
\beta
, i.e.,
t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\beta)=\exp(\omega(\beta))
. Furthermore, we say that
\{C_{n}\}
is a partially self-correcting quantum
memory
(SCQM) if there exists an efficient, i.e.,
\mathrm{poly}(n)
runtime, decoder
\Phi
with respect to which the system is partially self-correcting.

The definition of partial self-correction is therefore associated with the scaling behavior of the optimal memory time as a function of
\beta
, which we require to grow sufficiently quickly to rule out trivial systems.
For the purposes of proving partial self-correction, we often consider a cutoff system size
n^{*}(\beta)
, which provides a lower bound
t^{*}_{\mathrm{mem}}(\beta)\geq t_{\mathrm{mem}}(n^{*}(\beta),\beta)
.
Note that we also distinguish partially self-correcting
systems
and partially self-correcting
memories
. The former is an intrinsic statement about the memory time scaling of a quantum system, whereas the latter has the additional requirement of admitting an efficient decoder with respect to which it is self-correcting.

In the remainder of this appendix, we prove that layer codes instantiated with quantum Tanner codes form a partially self-correcting memory.

In Appendix
D.3
we prove an analogous result for random layer codes.

Appendix D

Theorem

Definition D.1

(Random CSS Code)
.

A random CSS code with block length

\rho_{X},\rho_{Z}\in(0,1/2)

for which this condition fails to hold is

Note that a random CSS code is equivalently defined by choosing, uniformly at random, a pair of binary matrices

\displaystyle Y_{C}^{i}\coloneq\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\mid\exists s\in S_{Z}:\ (\forall j\leq i:\ y_{j}=s_{j}\ \text{ and }\ \forall j>i:\ y_{j}=0)\}.

Lemma D.4
.

Let
C\sim\mathrm{CSS}_{n}(\rho_{X},\rho_{Z})
be a random CSS code. For any constant
f\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{C}\left(B_{C}(f)\cap L_{Z}(C)\neq\emptyset\right)\leq 2^{-n(\rho_{X}-6f)}

(33)

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Proof.

First, consider a fixed
H_{Z}
defining the
Z
-checks. There are a total of
\rho_{Z}n
stabilizer generators, so the size of
Y_{H_{Z}}
is bounded above by
\rho_{Z}n^{2}
. The size of
B_{H_{Z}}(f)
is therefore bounded above by

\displaystyle|B_{H_{Z}}(f)|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{fn/\log n}\binom{\rho_{Z}n^{2}}{k}\leq 2^{\rho_{Z}n^{2}H_{2}\left(\frac{f}{\rho_{Z}n\log n}\right)},

(34)

where
H_{2}(p)=-p\log_{2}(p)-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)
is the binary entropy function. We can further bound the binary entropy function by

\displaystyle H_{2}\left(\frac{f}{\rho_{Z}n\log n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f}{\rho_{Z}n\log(2)\log n}\left(1-\log\left(\frac{f}{\rho_{Z}n\log n}\right)\right)

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f}{\rho_{Z}n\log(2)\log n}\log\left(\frac{e\rho_{Z}n\log n}{f}\right)

(36)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6f}{\rho_{Z}n},

(37)

where the last inequality holds for
n\log n\geq e\rho_{Z}/f
. Therefore it follows that

\displaystyle|B_{H_{Z}}(f)|\leq 2^{\rho_{Z}n^{2}H_{2}\left(\frac{f}{\rho_{Z}n\log n}\right)}\leq 2^{6fn}

(38)

for sufficiently large
n
.

Now let us sample
H_{X}
uniformly at random from the space of matrices orthogonal to
H_{Z}
and let
C=(H_{X},H_{Z})
denote the resulting CSS code. Then
\ker(H_{X})
is uniformly random among the subspaces of dimension
(1-\rho_{X})n
containing
\operatorname{row}(H_{Z})
. It follows that for any fixed vector
z\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, we either have
z\in L_{Z}(C)
with probability zero if
z\in\operatorname{row}(H_{Z})
, or else

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X}}(z\in L_{Z}(C)\mid H_{Z})

\displaystyle=\frac{|\text{\lx@text@lbrace subspaces of dim }(1-\rho_{X})n\text{ containing }z\text{ and }\operatorname{row}(H_{Z})\}|}{|\text{\lx@text@lbrace subspaces of dim }(1-\rho_{X})n\text{ containing }\operatorname{row}(H_{Z})\}|}

(39)

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{(1-\rho_{Z})n-1}{(1-\rho_{X}-\rho_{Z})n-1}_{2}}{\binom{(1-\rho_{Z})n}{(1-\rho_{X}-\rho_{X})n}_{2}}

(40)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{(1-\rho_{X}-\rho_{Z})n}-1}{2^{(1-\rho_{Z})n}-1}

(41)

for
z\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\backslash\mathrm{row}(H_{Z})
, where the second equality is the
2
-binomial coefficient. Taking a union bound, we find

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X}}(B_{H_{Z}}(f)\cap L_{Z}(C)\neq\emptyset\mid H_{Z})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z\in B_{H_{Z}}(f)}\Pr_{H_{X}}(z\in L_{Z}(C)\mid H_{Z})

(42)

\displaystyle\leq|B_{H_{Z}}(f)|\cdot\frac{2^{(1-\rho_{X}-\rho_{Z})n}-1}{2^{(1-\rho_{Z})n}-1}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{6fn}\cdot\frac{2^{(1-\rho_{X}-\rho_{Z})n}-1}{2^{(1-\rho_{Z})n}-1}

(44)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-n(\rho_{X}-6f)}.

(45)

Since this bounds holds independently of the choice of
H_{Z}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\Pr_{C}(B_{C}(f)\cap L_{Z}(C)\neq\emptyset)\leq 2^{-n(\rho_{X}-6f)}.

(46)

∎

Theorem D.5

(Random Layer Code Distance)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}(C)
be a layer code with random CSS input
C\sim\mathrm{CSS}_{n}(\rho_{X},\rho_{Z})
. There exists a constant
c_{0}>0
such that
\mathscr{L}(C)
has distance
d\geq c_{0}n^{2}/\log n
with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

We will prove that the
Z
-distance of
\mathscr{L}(C)
satisfies
d_{Z}\geq c_{0}n^{2}/\log n
with high probability. A completely analogous argument will then imply that the
X
-distance satisfies the same bound with high probability, and the full result then follows by the union bound.

First, consider a fixed input code
C
of length
n
. Let
\overline{Z}\in L_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
be a minimal weight logical
Z
operator for
\mathscr{L}(C)
. For any slab
A_{i}
, let
\overline{Z}_{i}
denote the restriction of
\overline{Z}
to
A_{i}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left|\overline{Z}_{i}\right|\geq\left|E_{i}(\overline{Z})\right|.

(47)

Applying the fusion rules to convert any excitation on a
Q
-layer into two excitations on the adjacent
Z
-layer segments for an intersecting
Z
-layer, it follows that there exists
E^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{Z}
such that
E_{i}(\overline{Z})\approx E^{\prime}
and
|E^{\prime}|\leq 2|E_{i}(\overline{Z})|
. Applying the fusion rules one more time to move any excitations on a
Z
-layer segment onto all intersecting
Q
-layers to its left, it follows from Lemma
A.7
and Fig.
7
that there exists some
E^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{Q}
such that:

1.

P(E^{\prime\prime})\in L_{Z}(C)
,

2.

P(E^{\prime\prime})
is a linear combination of at most
|E^{\prime}|
elements of
Y_{C}
, i.e.,
P(E^{\prime\prime})\in B_{C}(|E^{\prime}|\log n/n)
.

In particular, it follows that

\displaystyle B_{C}(|E^{\prime}|\log n/n)\cap L_{Z}(C)\neq\emptyset.

(48)

Let us now bound the probability over
C
that there exists some
\overline{Z}\in L_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
such that
|\overline{Z}_{i}|\leq fn/\log n
for some constant
f
and index
i\in[\rho_{X}n]
. Since
|\overline{Z}_{i}|\leq fn/\log n
implies that
|E^{\prime}|\leq 2fn/\log n
, it follows from the discussion above and Lemma
D.4
that

\displaystyle\Pr_{C}(\exists\overline{Z}:\ |\overline{Z}_{i}|\leq fn/\log n\text{ for some }i)\leq\Pr_{C}(B_{C}(2f)\cap L_{Z}(C)\neq\emptyset)\leq 2^{-n(\rho_{X}-12f)}

(49)

for sufficiently large
n
. For any fixed
\rho_{X}
, we may choose
f
such that
12f<\rho_{X}
. For such a choice of
f
, it follows that we will have
|\overline{Z}_{i}|>fn/\log n
for all
i\in[\rho_{X}n]
with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle|\overline{Z}|=\sum_{i=1}^{\rho_{X}n}|\overline{Z}_{i}|>\rho_{X}fn^{2}/\log n

(50)

for all
\overline{Z}\in L_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
. Taking
c_{0}=\rho_{X}f
, it follows that
d_{Z}>c_{0}n^{2}/\log n
with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
.
∎

D.2
Energy Barrier of Random Layer Codes

In this section, we prove that random layer codes have energy barrier (see Definition
A.1
)
\Delta_{\mathscr{L}(C)}=\Omega(n/\log n)
with high probability.

It will be convenient throughout this section and the next to work with
Z
-type Pauli operators that have been put into a canonical form. Let
P\in P_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
be a Pauli-
Z
operator on the layer code
\mathscr{L}(C)
. By applying string operators, we will define an associated operator
\tilde{P}\in P_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
. The precise definition of
\tilde{P}
and its properties are stated in the following lemma and accompanying figures.

Lemma D.6

(Mapping Lemma)
.

Let
C=(H_{X},H_{Z})
be a CSS code and let
\mathscr{L}(C)
be its layer code. By an application of string operators, we can associate to each
P\in P_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
another operator
\tilde{P}\in P_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
such that:

1.

\tilde{P}
is supported entirely on
X
and
Z
layers,

2.

\tilde{P}
has the same excitations as
P
on
X
layers,

3.

Restricted to the
Z
layers,
\tilde{P}
is a union of vertical strings located half-way between successive
Q
-layers. Each region between successive
Q
-layers contains at most one excitation—located at its center—and the vertical strings connect successive excitations. More explicitly, in each region, the vertical strings either:

(a)

connects the top
X
-layer of the region to the bottom
X
-layer,

(b)

connects the top
X
-layer to an excitation located at the center of the region, or

(c)

connects the bottom
X
-layer to an excitation located at the center of the region.

4.

The restriction of
\tilde{P}
to
Z
-layers is a function of only
P
and
H_{Z}
. In particular, it is independent of
H_{X}
, i.e., as long as the number of
X
layers (
\rho_{X}n
) is the same, the matrix
H_{X}
does not matter. This will be important in the argument to follow. Note that the parts of
\tilde{P}
on the
X
layers, including its set of excitations,
will
generally depend on
H_{X}
.

5.

The syndrome weights are related by
|\sigma(\tilde{P})|\leq 2|\sigma(P)|
.

6.

If
P
is a logical operator, then
\tilde{P}
is stabilizer-equivalent to
P
.

7.

If
|P_{0}P_{1}|=1
, then the
e
-configurations defined by
\tilde{P}_{0}
and
\tilde{P}_{1}
differ on at most a single slab boundary.

Proof.

Definition of
\tilde{P}
.

For each
Q
-layer, fix some
Z
-layer which has non-trivial intersection with
Q
. We construct
\tilde{P}
in three stages, which we call cleaning, branching, and merging:

1.

In the cleaning stage (Fig.
8
), we use string operators and stabilizers to move all excitations on each
Q
-layer horizontally to the defect line defined by the chosen
Z
-layer. This process does not modify the excitations on the
X
-layers, although the support of the operator on the
X
-layers may be modified by the addition of stabilizers. Any string operators remaining on the
Q
-layers can be cleaned away by stabilizers so as to be supported solely on the chosen
QZ
line defect. This leaves us with an operator whose
Q
-support consists entirely of vertical string segments supported on the chosen
QZ
line defects. This process may merge some excitations on the
Q
-layers but will not increase the number.

In the branching stage (Fig.

Proof of the required properties.

1.

By the end of the cleaning and branching stages, the operator is already supported solely on
X
and
Z

2.

The cleaning and branching stages can potentially change the existing string operators on the
X
-layers. The merging stage does not touch the
X
-layers at all. Note that
\tilde{P}
and

will differ on the

-layers in general; only the excitations (the endpoints of the string operators) remain unchanged.

3.

This is essentially by definition of the mapping

.

4.

The support on the

. Note however, the mapping itself, and in particular the strings on the

. The set of excitations on the

-layers will also depend on the choice of

.

5.

The cleaning and merging stages can only decrease the number of excitations. The branching stage at most doubles the number of excitations. Therefore
|\sigma(\tilde{P})|\leq 2|\sigma(P)|
.

6.

The cleaning and branching steps are accomplished solely using horizontal strings to move excitations and stabilizers. If

is a logical operator, then it has no excitations, and hence these two steps are accomplished solely using stabilizers. Since there are no excitations, the merging step is trivial. Therefore
\tilde{P}
is stabilizer-equivalent to

is a logical operator.

7.

Finally, suppose
P_{0}
differs from

on a single edge. If this edge is on an

\tilde{P}_{0}
vs.

at all. If the edge is on a

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{2fn/\log n}\binom{\rho_{Z}n(n+1)(\rho_{X}n+1)}{\ell}\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{2fn/\log n}\binom{2\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{3}}{\ell}\leq 2^{2\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{3}H_{2}\left(\frac{f}{\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}\log n}\right)},

(53)

where the first inequality above hold for all
n
sufficiently large. The binary entropy function satisfies

\displaystyle H_{2}\left(\frac{f}{\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}\log n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f}{\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}\log(2)\log n}\left(1-\log\left(\frac{f}{2\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}\log n}\right)\right)

(54)

\displaystyle=\frac{f}{\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}\log(2)\log n}\log\left(\frac{2e\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}\log n}{f}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f}{\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}\log(2)\log n}\log\left(n^{3}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3f}{\log(2)\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}},

(57)

where the penultimate inequality holds for all
fn/\log n\geq 2e\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}
. It follows that we have

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{B}_{f}|\leq 2^{2n^{3}\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}H_{2}\left(\frac{f}{\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}\log n}\right)}\leq 2^{2n^{3}\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}\frac{3f}{\log(2)\rho_{X}\rho_{Z}n^{2}}}\leq 2^{10fn}

(58)

for all sufficiently large
n
.
∎

Lemma D.13

(Configuration)
.

Let
C=(H_{X},H_{Z})
be a CSS code and let
\mathcal{P}=\{P(t)\}_{t=0}^{T}
be a Pauli
Z
path on the layer code
\mathscr{L}(C)
ending on a non-trivial logical operator
P(T)
. If
\Delta_{Z}(\mathcal{P})\leq fn/\log n
, then there exists
t^{\prime}\in[T]
such that
\partial c(\tilde{P}(t^{\prime}))\in\mathfrak{B}_{f}
.

Proof.

We will write
c(t)=c(\tilde{P}(t))
and
c^{\underline{k}}(t)
for the
e
-configurations defined by
\tilde{P}(t)
. At
t=0
we begin with identity operator
P(0)=I
, so
c^{\underline{k}}(0)
is trivial for all
k\in[\rho_{X}n]
. At the last step
t=T
, we have a non-trivial logical operator
P(T)
, and so
\tilde{P}(T)
is a stabilizer-equivalent logical operator by Property
6
of Lemma
D.6
. It follows from Lemma
A.6
that
c^{\underline{k}}(T)
are non-trivial and boundary-equivalent to each other for all
k
.

At each step,
P(t)
and

differ by weight
1
, and so by Property
7
of Lemma
D.6
, the operators
\tilde{P}(t)
and
\tilde{P}(t+1)
can define boundary inequivalent

can differ on at most a single value of

starts trivial for all

\left\lfloor{\frac{\rho_{X}-1}{2}}\right\rfloor

Finally, since

\displaystyle|\partial c(t^{\prime})|\leq|\sigma(\tilde{P}(t^{\prime}))|\leq 2|\sigma(P(t^{\prime}))|\leq 2\Delta_{Z}(\mathcal{P})\leq 2fn/\log n,

where the first inequality follows from the fact that

restricted to the

.
∎

Lemma D.14
.

Suppose that
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\rho_{Z}n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\rho_{X}n+2}
are two string indicators that share a common boundary
\partial c_{1}=\partial c_{2}=b
. If

, then

are boundary-equivalent configurations for all
k\in[\rho_{X}n]
.

Proof.

If
\partial(c_{1}-c_{2})=0
, then the strings defined by
c_{1}-c_{2}
contain no excitations in the bulk. It follows that they must stretch the entire height of the layer code. This implies that the

-configurations defined by
c_{1}-c_{2}
are
k
-independent. Since

, both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
define precisely
\left\lfloor{(\rho_{X}n-1)/2}\right\rfloor
non-trivial configurations. Since the sum of trivial configurations remain trivial, it follows that

can define at most
2\left\lfloor{(\rho_{X}n-1)/2}\right\rfloor\leq\rho_{X}n-1<\rho_{X}n

must be trivial for some

, and since its

-configurations are

-independent, it follows that the

-configurations defined by

must be identically trivial. Hence

for all

.
∎

Lemma D.15
.

The syndrome of an operator
P\in P_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
on the
X
-layers has weight

\displaystyle\bigg|\sigma|_{X}(P,H_{X})\bigg|=\bigg|\sigma|_{X}(\tilde{P},H_{X})\bigg|=\bigg|\sigma|_{X}(\hat{P},H_{X})\bigg|\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}a_{j}^{\ k}(\hat{P})\right|.

(61)

Proof.

The mapping
P\mapsto\tilde{P}
preserves the excitations on
X
-layers by Property
2
of Lemma
D.6
, so
\sigma|_{X}(P,H_{X})=\sigma|_{X}(\tilde{P},H_{X})
. Likewise, the map
\tilde{P}\mapsto\hat{P}
also preserves the excitations on the
X
-layers, so
\sigma|_{X}(\tilde{P},H_{X})=\sigma|_{X}(\hat{P},H_{X})
.

Now, fix an
X
-layer
\ell
(say the
k
-th layer) and consider the excitations of
\hat{P}
on this layer. Every vertical string that crosses
\ell
on a
Q
-layer that intersects
\ell
non-trivially will create a potential excitation in the bulk which can only be condensed by another such crossing. The parity of these crossings, given by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}a_{j}^{\ k}(\hat{P}),

(62)

therefore lower bounds the number of excitations on
\ell
. The syndrome weight on all
X
-layers is therefore lower bounded by the sum of the parities above over all
X
-layers, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bigg|\sigma|_{X}(\hat{P},H_{X})\bigg|\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}a_{j}^{\ k}(\hat{P})\right|.

(63)

Note that the absolute values indicate that the outer sum is not over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
since distinct
X
-layers are independent.
∎

Remark D.16
.

Since
c^{\underline{k}}\approx a^{\underline{k}}
, it follows from Lemma
A.7
that
c^{\underline{k}}
is trivial if and only if
a^{\underline{k}}\in\mathrm{row}(H_{Z})
.

Lemma D.17
.

Fix a
\rho_{Z}n\times n
matrix
H_{Z}
and some
b\in\mathfrak{B}_{f}
. Let
\mathfrak{C}(b)=\partial^{-1}(b)
be the space of all string indicators
c
with boundary
b=\partial c
. For a given
H_{X}
, let
\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})
be the space of all
c
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}a_{j}^{\ k}(c)\right|\leq\frac{\rho_{X}n}{8}.

(64)

Then we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\mathfrak{C}(b)\cap\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})\neq\emptyset\mid H_{Z}\right)\leq 2^{-\rho_{X}n/15}

(65)

for all sufficiently large
n
, where the probability is over all
\rho_{X}n\times n
matrices
H_{X}
orthogonal to
H_{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathfrak{C}(b)
. By Lemma
D.14
, it follows that
c_{1}^{\underline{k}}\approx c_{2}^{\underline{k}}
for all
k
. Consequently,
(a_{1}-a_{2})^{\underline{k}}\in\mathrm{row}(H_{Z})
for all
k
by Remark
D.16
. Since the rows of
H_{Z}
are orthogonal to the rows of
H_{X}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}(a_{1}-a_{2})_{j}^{\ k}=0

(66)

for all
k
. This implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}(a_{1})_{j}^{\ k}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}(a_{2})_{j}^{\ k}\right|

(67)

It follows that if
\mathfrak{C}(b)\cap\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})\neq\emptyset
, then we actually have
\mathfrak{C}(b)\subseteq\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})
.

Now, fix an arbitrary
c\in\mathfrak{C}(b)
and consider the quantities

\displaystyle\alpha^{k}(H_{X})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}a_{j}^{\ k}(c)

(68)

as we vary over random
H_{X}
. Since
\mathfrak{C}(b)\cap\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
c\in\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})
, if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}|\alpha^{k}(H_{X})|=\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}a_{j}^{\ k}(c)\right|\leq\frac{\rho_{X}n}{8},

(69)

it follows that we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\mathfrak{C}(b)\cap\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})\neq\emptyset\mid H_{Z}\right)=\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}|\alpha^{k}(H_{X})|\leq\frac{\rho_{X}n}{8}\ \bigg|\ H_{Z}\right).

(70)

Since
H_{X}
is uniformly random in the orthogonal complement of
H_{Z}
, each row is also i.i.d. uniformly random in the orthogonal complement. It follows that the
\alpha^{k}
’s, each being a linear functional of the
k
-th row of
H_{X}
, are also independent. Each
\alpha^{k}
is either identically zero if
a^{\underline{k}}\in\mathrm{row}(H_{Z})
, or else it is uniformly random on
\{0,1\}
if
a^{\underline{k}}\notin\mathrm{row}(H_{Z})
. From Lemma
D.14
and the fact that
b\in\mathfrak{B}_{f}
, it follows that
c^{\underline{k}}
, and hence
a^{\underline{k}}
, defines a non-trivial configuration for exactly
\left\lfloor{(\rho_{X}n-1)/2}\right\rfloor
values of
k
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}|\alpha^{k}(H_{X})|=\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}a_{j}^{\ k}(c)\right|

(71)

is a sum of
\left\lfloor{(\rho_{X}n-1)/2}\right\rfloor
independent Bernoulli random variables. The probability in Eq. (
70
) can therefore be bounded by above by Hoeffding’s inequality as

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}|\alpha^{k}(H_{X})|\leq\frac{\rho_{X}n}{8}\ \bigg|\ H_{Z}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}|\alpha^{k}(H_{X})|-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor{\frac{\rho_{X}n-1}{2}}\right\rfloor\leq\frac{\rho_{X}n}{8}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor{\frac{\rho_{X}n-1}{2}}\right\rfloor\ \bigg|\ H_{Z}\right)

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}|\alpha^{k}(H_{X})|-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor{\frac{\rho_{X}n-1}{2}}\right\rfloor\leq-\frac{\rho_{X}n}{9}\ \bigg|\ H_{Z}\right)

(73)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2(\rho_{X}n/9)^{2}}{\left\lfloor{(\rho_{X}n-1)/2}\right\rfloor}\right)

(74)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{4\rho_{X}n}{81}\right)

(75)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\rho_{X}n/15},

(76)

where we subtract off the mean
\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor{\frac{\rho_{X}n-1}{2}}\right\rfloor
in the first line and apply Hoeffding’s inequality in the third.
∎

Now we combine everything to get the main theorem for the energy barrier.

Theorem D.18

(Layer Code Energy Barrier)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}(C)
be a layer code with random CSS input
C\sim\mathrm{CSS}_{n}(\rho_{X},\rho_{Z})
. Then there exists a constant
f>0
such that

\displaystyle\Pr_{C}\left(\Delta_{\mathscr{L}(C)}\leq\frac{fn}{\log n}\right)\leq 2^{-\Omega(n)}.

(77)

In particular,
\mathscr{L}(C)
has energy barrier
\Delta_{\mathscr{L}(C)}>fn/\log n
with high probability as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for the
Z
energy barrier. A symmetric argument will then imply that the
X
energy barrier satisfies the same bound with high probability, and the full result follows by the union bound. We adopt the same notation used in the Lemma
D.17
.

First, let us fix some
H_{Z}
. Let
H_{X}
be a random matrix orthogonal to
H_{Z}
. Let
C=(H_{X},H_{Z})
. We will write
\Delta_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))
for the
Z
energy barrier associated with
\mathscr{L}(C)
. Suppose that
\Delta_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))\leq fn/\log n
for some constant
f
. Then there exists some Pauli path
\mathcal{P}=\{P(t)\}_{t=0}^{T}
, ending on a non-trivial logical operator
P(T)
, such that
\Delta_{Z}(\mathcal{P})\leq fn/\log n
. It follows by Lemma
D.13
that there exists some
t^{\prime}\in[T]
such that
c=c(\tilde{P}(t^{\prime}))
satisfies
b=\partial c\in\mathfrak{B}_{f}
. It also follows from Lemma
D.15
that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\rho_{X}n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(H_{X})_{kj}a_{j}^{\ k}(c)\right|\leq\left|\sigma|_{X}(P(t^{\prime}),H_{X})\right|\leq\Delta_{Z}(\mathcal{P})\leq\frac{fn}{\log n}.

(78)

In particular, for
n
sufficiently large, we have
fn/\log n<\rho_{X}n/8
, so that
\Delta_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))\leq fn/\log n
implies
\mathfrak{C}(b)\cap\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})\neq\emptyset
. It follows that

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\Delta_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))\leq\frac{fn}{\log n}\bigg|\ H_{Z}\right)\leq\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\exists b\in\mathfrak{B}_{f}:\ \mathfrak{C}(b)\cap\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})\neq\emptyset\mid H_{Z}\right)

(79)

for sufficiently large
n
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\Delta_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))\leq\frac{fn}{\log n}\bigg|\ H_{Z}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\exists b\in\mathfrak{B}_{f}:\ \mathfrak{C}(b)\cap\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})\neq\emptyset\mid H_{Z}\right)

(80)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b\in\mathfrak{B}_{f}}\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\mathfrak{C}(b)\cap\mathfrak{C}(H_{X})\neq\emptyset\mid H_{Z}\right)

(81)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathfrak{B}_{f}|\cdot 2^{-\rho_{X}n/15}

(82)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{10fn}2^{-\rho_{X}n/15},

(83)

where we apply a union bound on the second line, Lemma
D.17
on the third line, and Lemma
D.12
on the last line. It follows that if we choose
f
sufficiently small so that
150f<\rho_{X}
, then

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X}}\left(\Delta_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))\leq\frac{fn}{\log n}\bigg|\ H_{Z}\right)=2^{-\Omega(n)}.

(84)

This result holds for any choice of
H_{Z}
, so randomizing over
H_{Z}
gives

\displaystyle\Pr_{H_{X},H_{Z}}\left(\Delta_{Z}(\mathscr{L}(C))\leq\frac{fn}{\log n}\right)=2^{-\Omega(n)},

(85)

which is our desired result.
∎

D.3
Partial Self-Correction of Random Layer Codes

We show that layer codes with random input codes are partially self-correcting with high probability (taken over the choice of input code). To do so, we first define a variant of the concatenated decoder (cf. section
B.2
) that is suitable for correcting against errors with low energy barrier which can then be used in Lemma
C.5
(Lemma 1 of Ref.
BH11a
) to infer partial self-correction.

We define the decoder for
Z
errors (with
X
errors treated analogously). Let
\mathscr{L}(C)
be a random layer code with input code
C\sim\mathrm{CSS}_{n}(\rho_{X},\rho_{Z})
. Let
R_{Z}
,
R_{Q}
, and
R_{X}
denote the
Z
-,
Q
-, and
X
-layers of code
\mathscr{L}(C)
, respectively. Let
e_{0}
be a
Z
-error on
\mathscr{L}(C)
and
\sigma(e_{0})=\sigma_{0}
be its syndrome. The modified concatenated decoder then proceeds as follows:

1.

Match all
e
-excitations on
R_{Z}
to the top boundary of each
Z
-layer (which condenses
e
-anyons). These matchings are done with straight vertical strings operators. This step may create additional excitations on
R_{X}
.

2.

Next, we match all
e
-excitations on
R_{Q}
to the top boundary (which is also
e
-condensing) by straight vertical string operators. Again, this step might create new excitations on
R_{X}
, but none on
R_{Z}
.

3.

Let
\sigma_{C}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{X}}
be the indicator vector of all
X
-layers that now contain an odd number of
e
-excitations. Notice that
\sigma_{C}
can be viewed as a syndrome to the input code
C
. Let
\operatorname{Dec}_{C,Y}^{Z}
be a decoder of input code
C
that outputs a correction
\hat{f}_{C}
of minimal
Y
-weight (see Definition
D.3
) such that
\sigma(\hat{f}_{C})=\sigma_{C}
. For every qubit in
\hat{f}_{C}
, we apply a straight vertical string operator from the top to the bottom of the corresponding
Q
-layer of the layer code. As before, additional
e
-excitations may appear on
R_{X}
, but none on
R_{Z}
.

4.

Finally, apply MWPM on each
X
-layer to eliminate the remaining excitations.

We now prove the correctness of the modified concatenated decoder for errors of sufficiently low energy barrier (cf. Lemma
D.21
). We begin with a preliminary result on the correctness of the input decoder
\operatorname{Dec}_{C,Y}^{Z}
.

Lemma D.19
.

Let
C\sim\mathrm{CSS}_{n}(\rho_{X},\rho_{Z})
be a random CSS code. There exists a constant
c_{0}>0
such that with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
, there exists a decoder
\operatorname{Dec}_{C,Y}^{Z}
that corrects all
Z
-error
e
of
Y
-weight
|e|_{Y}\leq c_{0}n/\log n
.

Proof.

This is an immediate corollary of Lemma
D.4
and the fact that the minimum
Y
-weight decoder corrects errors of
Y
-weight at most
(d_{Y}-1)/2
, where
d_{Y}=\min\{fn/\log n:B_{C}(f)\cap L_{Z}(C)\neq\emptyset\}
is the
Y
-distance of the code.
∎

Next, we establish an analog of Lemma
D.13
for errors of sufficiently low energy barrier.

Lemma D.20
.

Let
\mathscr{L}(C)
be a random layer code. Let
f>0
be the energy barrier constant from Theorem
D.18
. Then with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
, all Pauli-
Z
operators with energy barrier
\Delta_{Z}(P)\leq fn/\log n
satisfies the property that
a^{\underline{k}}(\hat{P})\in\operatorname{row}(H_{Z})
for at least half of the values

.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{P}
denote the set of all Pauli

.

Let
\mathscr{L}(C)
be a random layer code with input code

, such that with probability

, where

and

are the constants appearing in Lemmas

and

, respectively. Let

, where

is defined as in Fig.

(88)

where

is some vector which depends on the location of excitation. Note that the vertical string will spawn additional excitations on the

i
-th

Remark D.23
.

Note that the decoder
\operatorname{Dec}_{C}^{Z}
of the random input code is not necessarily an
efficient

E.1
Estimating the Memory Time

The Hamiltonian of a CSS code consists of pairwise commuting terms that decompose as
H_{\rm mem}=H_{X,\rm mem}+H_{Z,\rm mem}
, where
H_{X,\rm mem}
and
H_{Z,\rm mem}
comprise terms
corresponding to the
X
- and
Z
-type stabilizer generators, respectively (cf. Eq.
21
).
The interaction of a CSS code with a heat bath of finite temperature can be studied for both
X
- and
Z
-type stabilizer generators separately. This allows us to use standard tools from statistical mechanics
BLP+ 16
;
BH11a
. In particular, the memory time can be simulated by a Monte Carlo algorithm with Glauber
Gla 63
dynamics for
X
- and
Z
-type errors separately. Since the layer code construction treats Pauli
X
and
Z
errors symmetrically, it suffices to consider just
X
-type errors. We consider only
X
-type errors in the remainder of this appendix.

Consider the set
S_{Z}
of
Z
-type stabilizer generators of a CSS code of block length
N
. We can associate a spin Hamiltonian
H:\{\pm 1\}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
to
S_{Z}
, defined as

\displaystyle H(\sigma)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in S_{Z}}\prod_{i\in\operatorname{supp}(s)}\sigma_{i},

(91)

which assigns energy to each spin configuration
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
.
The set of ground states for
H
corresponds to the codewords of the classical code
C_{Z}=\ker H_{Z}
, where
H_{Z}
is the parity check matrix associated with
S_{Z}
.

At time
t=0
the state
\sigma
is initialized in a ground state of the Hamiltonian
H
. The Monte Carlo simulation with Glauber dynamics models thermal noise due to the memory-bath interaction as a sequence of spin-flips, which corresponds to applications of the respective Pauli-
X
errors. For each time step, a spin index
i\sim\mathrm{Uniform}([N])
is drawn uniformly and independently at random. The spin
i
is then flipped with probability depending on the energy change it causes, i.e.,
\Delta E_{i}(\sigma)=H(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{i-1},-\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1},\ldots,\sigma_{N})-H(\sigma)
. We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\mapsto\begin{cases}-\sigma_{i},&\quad\text{ with probability }\frac{1}{1+\exp(\beta\Delta E_{i}(\sigma))},\\
\sigma_{i},&\quad\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(92)

where
\beta
is the inverse temperature. This evolution then drives the state of the system to the Gibbs distribution.

Given a syndrome decoder for the CSS code, the memory time
t_{\text{fail}}
is the first time at which the memory becomes corrupted, meaning that the encoded logical information cannot be recovered using the decoder. To determine
t_{\text{fail}}
, in each time step, we obtain the error syndrome from the spin configuration, attempt to decode it, and verify if the decoder introduces a logical error.

At low temperatures, the probability of accepting a spin flip in each round is small, cf. Eq. (
92
), which slows down the standard Monte Carlo approach. To address this, we employ the kinetic or
n
-fold-way Monte Carlo method
YE 66
;
BKL 75
. This is a rejection-free algorithm which samples a spin
i
with probability proportional to
(1+\exp(\beta\Delta E_{i}(\sigma)))^{-1}
, flips the resulting spin, and then advances the simulation time by a stochastic amount proportional to the waiting time in configuration
\sigma
. Here, the waiting time is the average time elapsed before a spin is flipped in the regular Monte Carlo method. This provides an effective algorithm which avoids numerous rejected moves due the low acceptance probability in Eq. (
92
), thereby speeding up the simulation. A pseudocode for this approach is given in Algorithm
3
.

Since the decoder runtime is significantly slower than updating individual spins and we have to probe dynamics over exponentially-long periods of times, we perform decoding at geometrically-increasing time intervals. Specifically, we run the decoder if the simulation time has increased by at least
10\%
relative to the time of the previous decoder run.
