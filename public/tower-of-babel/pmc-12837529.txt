Oxidoreductase delivery by cell therapies in cyclophosphamide-induced premature ovarian insufficiency: a mechanistic insight into ovarian rejuvenation

Published: 2025

Background

The declining global female fertility rate has emerged as a significant public health concern with profound socioeconomic implications. With reproductive health increasingly recognized as vital for population sustainability, ovarian dysfunction-particularly diminished ovarian reserve (DOR), premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), and premature ovarian failure (POF)-has been identified as major contributing factors [1]. The ovary, as the central female reproductive organ, plays dual roles in oocyte production and endocrine regulation, making its functional integrity essential for fertility maintenance.

The etiology of ovarian dysfunction is complex and multifactorial, encompassing genetic, environmental, iatrogenic, and immune-inflammatory factors. Genetic factors, including chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., X-chromosome abnormalities) and gene mutations (e.g., BMP15, FMR1, FOXL2, NOBOX), play a crucial role in POI/POF [2]. Similarly, iatrogenic factors such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy also represent major causes of ovarian damage, leading to rapid ovarian function decline in young women [3]. While chemotherapy remaining indispensable in oncology, its gonadotoxic effects present a clinical dilemma. Alkylating drugs like cyclophosphamide (CTX) exemplify this paradox-while indispensable for saving lives, it inflicts significant gonad toxicity. CTX induces oxidative damage, follicular depletion, and stromal fibrosis, leading to ovarian dysfunction and even premature failure in women of childbearing age [4]. Its mechanisms of ovarian damage include DNA damage, activating oocyte apoptosis pathways, impairing follicular development, and directly depleting primordial follicles, thereby reducing ovarian reserve. Furthermore, granulosa cell apoptosis accelerates follicular atresia and ovarian function decline. CTX may also damage ovarian vascular endothelial cells, disrupt the ovarian vascular niche and microenvironment, trigger inflammation, and induce fibrosis, contributing to its long-term adverse effects on ovarian health [5, 6]. The clinical consequences extend beyond reproductive failure to encompass a spectrum of symptoms including vasomotor disturbances, genitourinary syndrome, and psychological comorbidities, all of which substantially deteriorate quality of life.

Despite considerable progress in reproductive medicine, current therapeutic interventions largely fail to restore ovarian function. While hormone replacement therapy and assisted reproductive technologies (ART) are valuable for symptom management and fertility preservation, they do not address the fundamental need for ovarian tissue regeneration. This therapeutic gap has driven exploration of regenerative medicine approaches, with mesenchymal stem cell therapy emerging as a highly promising strategy [7]. MSCs, a type of multipotent stem cell derived from the mesoderm, are widely distributed in various tissues such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and placenta [8, 9]. They are highly accessible, exhibit robust proliferative capacity and low immunogenicity, and have shown significant therapeutic effects in various diseases, including cardiovascular, neurological, autoimmune, and reproductive disorders [10–12].

Human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (HucMSCs) are particularly appealing due to their abundant availability from newborn umbilical cords, coupled with low immunogenicity, strong proliferative ability, and multi-differentiation potential. In ovarian function repair, HucMSCs secrete factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and insulin like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) to improve the ovarian microenvironment, promote ovarian angiogenesis, and reduce granulosa cell and oocyte apoptosis [13]. Research indicates that HucMSCs may enhance cellular energy metabolism and promote cell repair and functional recovery by transferring healthy mitochondria to damaged cells [14]. However, as allogeneic stem cells, their safety remains a concern and warrants further investigation. Human adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), obtained from autologous adipose tissue, primarily from subcutaneous fat-rich areas such as the abdomen and buttocks, exhibit strong proliferative capacity and low immunogenicity. Compared to HucMSCs, ADSCs offer better clinical acceptability by avoiding allogeneic immunogenicity through autologous transplantation [15]. ADSCs can also secrete multiple growth factors to promote ovarian angiogenesis, enhance local blood flow, and improve the ovarian microenvironment. In addition, ADSCs can promote granulosa cell proliferation and improve endocrine function. Stromal vascular fraction (SVF), a heterogeneous cell population derived from autologous adipose tissue, comprises ADSCs, endothelial progenitor cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, and immunomodulatory cells [16, 17]. SVF exhibits potent immunomodulatory and tissue-repair capabilities and is considered a safer option due to its direct clinical applicability without in vitro culture and expansion [18]. The endothelial progenitor cells in SVF can differentiate into vascular endothelial cells, thereby promoting ovarian angiogenesis and improving tissue blood supply [19]. HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF have all demonstrated therapeutic potential for ovarian function restoration, primarily through paracrine signaling or immunomodulatory capabilities [19, 20]. While each cell source presents distinct advantages in terms of availability, proliferative capacity, and clinical applicability, yet systematic comparisons of their efficacy and safety profiles remain notably lacking.

The present study was designed to address these critical gaps by comprehensive evaluation of clinical-grade HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF therapies in a well- established murine model of chemotherapy-induced ovarian failure. This investigation places particular emphasis on elucidating conserved mechanisms. Thorough a comparative experimental design that incorporating both functional and molecular endpoints, this work aims to provide an evidence-based framework for selecting optimal cell sources for further clinical applications. It aims to enhance the understanding of stem cell-mediated ovarian regeneration, optimize treatment therapeutic strategies, and advance the clinical application of stem cell in the field of reproductive health.

Methods

Cell acquisition and culture

HucMSCs were obtained from Gens Stem Cell Biotech Co., Ltd (Huzhou, China). ADSCs and SVF were obtained from Re-Stem Biotech Co., Ltd (Suzhou, China). The characteristics and multipotent differentiation potential of HusMSCs and ADSCs had been illustrated previously [21, 22]. The SVF utilized in this study were freshly isolated from adipose tissue of screened healthy donors by Re-Stem Biotech Co., Ltd, and each batch of cells is accompanied by a comprehensive quality-control report confirming >90% cell viability prior to usage. HucMSCs and ADSCs at Passage 3–5 was delivered from mentioned companies and cultured up to two passages for experiments, while SVF was immediately used when it arrived. HucMSCs were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/F-12 (DMEM/F-12, Meilunbo, MA021) with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cellmax, SA112.02), 1% penicillin and streptomycin (P/S, Biosharp, BL505A). ADSCs were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco, C11875500BT,) with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% P/S. KGN cell line was cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium with 10% FBS and 1% P/S at 37℃ with 5% CO2, and passaged at a ratio of 1:3 − 1:4.

Establishment of mouse POI model and cell in situ ovarian injection

The mice used in the study were procured from the Animal Center of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital and housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility maintained at 23–25℃, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and 40–70% humidity. All experimental procedures were carried out with the approval of the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (NO. ZJU20220410). The sample sizes for our studies were determined based on established conventions in the field rather than a priori statistical calculations.

To establish the premature ovarian failure model, 6–8-week-old female ICR mice in the experimental group were injected intraperitoneally with cyclophosphamide (CTX, 150 mg/kg, dissolved in normal saline), while mice in the Control group (n = 20) were injected with equal volumes of DMSO and normal saline.

POI mice were anesthetized with 200 mg/kg avertin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) via intraperitoneal injection. A dorsal incision was made to access the ovarian tissue, and 29G-insulin syringes were used for bilateral intraovarian injections. POI mice were randomly divided into four groups: the CTX-con group received saline injections (25µL per ovary); the CTX-HucMSC, CTX-ADSC, and CTX-SVF groups received injections of HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF, respectively (25µL per ovary containing 1 × 106 cells). Post-surgery, mice were returned to their original environment. Experimental layout is depicted in Fig. 1A. In brief, POI was induced with a single CTX injection; seven days later mice received an intra-ovarian administration of the assigned treatment (designated day 0). Body weight was recorded on days 0, 10 and 20. On day 21 (3 weeks post-surgery) animals were allocated to distinct end-points: serum and ovarian harvest for molecular/histological analyses (n = 15), long-term fertility tracking (n = 4), superovulation to collect MII oocytes for quality assessment (n = 8), or short-term mating to retrieve 2-cell embryos for in-vitro culture (n = 3).

For the in-vivo antioxidant rescue experiment, mice received a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) dose of 150 mg/kg CTX to induce ovarian injury. Beginning 24 h post-CTX, animals were injected i.p. daily for five consecutive days with either resveratrol (RSV, 50 mg/kg, HY-16561, MCE, USA) or N-acetylcysteine (NAC, 150 mg/kg, 50303ES05, Yeasen, China). After which mice were euthanized; ovaries were immediately harvested for histological and molecular analyses to evaluate whether RSV or NAC protects against CTX-induced ovarian toxicity.

Western blot analysis

The protocol for Western blot analysis was adapted from prior studies [23]. In brief, protein extraction was performed using RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340, Sigma-Aldrich, United States), after which protein concentrations were assessed using the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer. The extracted proteins were then diluted using loading protein sample buffer (P1040, Solarbio, China). Following denaturation at 95℃ for 10 min, equal quantities of protein (approximately 10 µg per sample) were resolved by electrophoresis on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (SDS-PAGE, PG112, Epizyme, China) and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked in TBST buffer with 5% skimmed milk for a minimum of 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used and their corresponding target proteins are listed in Supplementary Table 1. After three washes in TBST buffer, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies and the protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (WBKLS0500, Millipore, USA) in conjunction with a ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).

Hormone assays

Mouse blood was swiftly collected into serum separator tubes (yellow-top) (BD Vacutainer, USA). After which serum was harvested by centrifugation at 3000 g for 10 min at 4℃. The concentrations of estradiol and progesterone in the serum were measured using the Access Immunoassay System (UniCel DxI 800, Beckman Coulter, USA) by the Clinical Laboratory Department at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University. In addition, the level of anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) was assayed following the manufacturer’s instructions for the ELISA kit (Elabscience, China).

Ovary histological analysis and follicle counting

Tissues were fixed with 4% formaldehyde (PFA, MA0192, Meilunbio, China) for 24 h, embedded in paraffin and then serially sectioned (3-µm thick) using a microtome. The sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate the intraovarian cellular morphology. These procedures were conducted by the Department of Pathology at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University. Follicle counting was performed manually, and only follicles containing the nucleus of an oocyte were counted to avoid any duplicate counting. Follicular stages were classified as follows: primordial follicles surrounded by a single layer of flattened squamous antral granulosa cells; primary follicles surrounded by a single layer of cuboidal granulosa cells; secondary follicles with two or more layers of cuboidal granulosa cells and no visible lumen; and antral follicles with a sinusoidal space with follicular fluid. Follicles exhibiting poor morphology, oocytes nearing apoptosis, or those with thin granulosa cell layers are regarded as atretic follicles.

Oocyte collection and embryo incubation

The female mice were injected with 7.5 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG, Ningbo San Sheng Biotech, Ningbo, China), and 44–48 h later injected with 7.5 IU human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG, Ningbo San Sheng Biotech, Ningbo, China). For collection of MII oocytes in vivo, each mouse was humanely killed after 14–16 h. The oocyte and cumulus cell complexes were digested with 3 mg/ml hyaluronidase (H4272, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) dissolved in M2 medium. To harvest 2-cell embryos, each female mouse was injected with hCG and paired with a vigorous 10-week-old male ICR mouse. After 14 h, the vagina was inspected for plugs, and mice with vaginal plugs were regarded as successfully mated. Subsequently, at 34 h post-mating (i.e., 48 h post-hCG injection), the mice were euthanized, and their fallopian tubes were flushed to collect the 2-cell embryos, which were then cultured in KSOM medium for blastocyst formation counting.

Fertility test

For the mating experiment, twenty mice (n = 4 per group) were randomly chosen. Three weeks following cell transplantation, female mice were bred with sexually mature male mice at a 2:1 ratio for a period of 1–2 months. Subsequently, after each pregnancy, male mice were permuted between cages in a random manner, and the litter size per female mouse was meticulously documented.

Collection and of primary granulosa cells (pGCs) and co-culture with cells in vitro

To isolate pGCs, young mice at 21 days of age were euthanized 44 h after intraperitoneal injection of 5IU PMSG. Antral follicles were punctured using a 29-G needle to release pGCs. The cells were then mixed thoroughly and passed through a 40-µm filter to remove impurities. The resulting pGCs were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

To induce aging in pGCs, cells were treated with 10µM 4-Hydroperoxy cyclophosphamide (4-HC, HY-117433, MCE, USA)) for 4 h and then the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF cells were seeded at a density of 1× 10⁵ cells per well in transwell inserts (3401, Corning, USA). These inserts were then co-cultured with the senescent pGCs for 24 h at 37℃ and 5% CO₂. After co-culture, cells were collected and lysed for subsequent experimental analysis.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy analysis

Ovarian tissues were fixed in 4% PFA and underwent gradient dehydration using 10% and 30% sucrose solutions. The tissues were then embedded in OCT compound (Tissue-Tek, Sakura) for cryo-sectioning. 8-µm-sections and cultured cell slides were permeabilized and blocked at room temperature for 1 h using a solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 0.3% Triton X-100. The sections were incubated overnight at 4℃ with the primary antibody diluted at a 1:200 ratio in the blocking solution. The primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with conjugated fluorescent secondary antibodies and DAPI at room temperature for 30 min. Following an additional three washes, the samples were mounted using an antifade mounting medium (Beyotime, China), and fluorescence signals were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM800, Zeiss, Germany). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Senescence β-Galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining

To detect SA-β-Gal activity, we utilized a Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime, China). Cultured cells were fixed using the fixation solution provided in the kit for 15 min. Following fixation, the samples were incubated with the staining solution, prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, at 37℃. The SA-β-Gal activity was evaluated after 48 h of incubation.

Apoptosis detection of cells

After co-culture with HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF cells, KGN cells were collected and digested, then gently re-suspended in a staining buffer composed of 5 µl of Annexin V-FITC solution and 10 µl of propidium iodide solution. This staining buffer was prepared according to the instructions provided with the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1062S, Beyotime, China). The cells were then incubated for 20 min in the dark at room temperature. After the incubation period, the cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur, USA).

Quantitative real‑time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‑PCR)

Total RNA was harvested and processed from cells using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (RN001, ESScience, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR was carried out in accordance with the guidelines for SYBR Green Mix (Vazyme, China) on a CFX-Connect platform (BioRad, USA). The expression level of each mRNA was normalized to the human-GAPDH or mouse-Actin transcript. The transcriptional levels of the target genes were compared to those in the control group, and the results were presented as fold changes. The sequences of the primers used for the target genes are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

RNA sequencing (RNA‑seq) and data analysis

Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (#E7530L, NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, with index codes added to distinguish sequences from different samples. Briefly, total RNA was extracted and enriched for mRNA using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by mRNA fragmentation. First-strand cDNA was synthesized with random hexamer primers, and the second strand was synthesized using buffer, dNTPs, DNA polymerase I, and RNase H. After purification, the double-stranded cDNA underwent end repair, had a single ‘A’ base added, and was ligated with sequencing adapters. Fragments around 350 bp were selected and enriched via PCR to create cDNA libraries. Library quality was assessed using Qubit 3.0 and Agilent 2100 systems, with effective concentration quantified by Bio-RAD CFX 96. Libraries were pooled and sequenced to produce 150 bp paired-end reads via Sequencing by Synthesis. Raw data underwent quality control to remove adapter sequences, N-containing reads, and low-quality reads. These clean reads were precisely mapped to the mouse reference genome (mm10) utilizing the STAR aligner (version 2.5.2a). RNA-seq based transcriptome analysis was conducted by Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd.

For the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we employed the DESeq2 bioinformatics package to analyze the raw transcriptomic dataset. A stringent statistical threshold was applied, requiring a P-value of less than 0.05 and a Log2 fold change (FC) of either greater than 0.5 or less than − 0.5 to deem a gene as differentially expressed.

Protein preparation and data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (DIA-MS) conduction

For the HPLC analysis, an Agilent 1100 HPLC system with a Nano Chrom-C18 column was used. Peptides were separated using a gradient of acetonitrile in HPLC water at a flow rate of 250µL/min, with samples collected between 10 and 50 min. The eluent was lyophilized for subsequent mass spectrometry. For DIA-MS, a nanoElute liquid chromatography system was employed with a 25 cm × 75 μm column. Peptides were separated over 90 min at a flow rate of 300nl/min. DIA was performed using Bruker of Control software with specific parameters for quadrupole isolation windows, capillary voltage, dry gas temperature, and flow rate. The mass spectrometry data were analyzed using Spectronaut software against a sample protein database, considering trypsin specificity and cysteine alkylation, with FDR settings for protein, peptide, and PSM at 0.01 and quantification FDR at 0.05. DIA-MS based proteomics analysis was conducted by OE-Bio Technology Co., Ltd.

ROS and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP, ΔΨm) detection of oocytes and cells

To detect reactive oxygen species (ROS), oocytes were exposed to 10µM 2’,7’-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, S0033S, Beyotime, China) in M2 medium at 37℃ in the absence of light for 30 min. After incubation, the oocytes were thoroughly rinsed three times in fresh M2 medium. To assess mitochondrial membrane potential, a JC-1 kit (C2006, Beyotime Biotech) was used as per the manufacturer’s protocol. MII oocytes or KGN cells were placed in medium containing JC-1 (1:200) and incubated for 30 min at 37℃ with 5% CO₂. Subsequently, the oocytes were washed multiple times in fresh M2 medium, and cells were washed by PBS. Fluorescence images of ROS or JC-1 aggregates and monomers were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). The washing intensities and exposure times were strictly controlled across all groups. The fluorescence intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and the ratio of aggregates to monomers was calculated to reflect the mitochondrial membrane potential.

In vitro culture of preantral follicles

Preantral follicle isolation and culture were performed as previously described [23]. Briefly, ovaries from 2-week-old female mice were dissected in Leibovitz-15 medium; intact follicles were mechanically released and individually cultured in 10µL growth medium droplet at 37℃, 5% CO₂. Four experimental groups were established: (1) Control (complete medium), (2) 4HC-con (15µM 4-HC), (3) 4HC-RSV (15µM 4-HC + 10µM RSV), and (4) 4HC-NAC (15 µM 4-HC + 2 mM NAC). During the 5-day culture to follicle development observation, medium was changed and bright-field image acquisition were carried out every other day. On day 3, follicles (n = 6–10 per group) were fixed in 4% PFA for immunofluorescence, and additional follicles were denuded with 29G insulin needles to separate granulosa cells and oocytes for micro-scale RNA extraction. Briefly, after mechanical dissociation, oocytes and granulosa cells were snap-frozen in 4µL ice-cold lysis solution (0.2% Triton X-100, 2 IU µL⁻¹ RNase inhibitor, 10mM dNTPs and 10µM oligo-dT) [23]. First-strand cDNA was generated with SuperScript II (Invitrogen, USA) and pre-amplified using KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KK2602, Roche, Switzerland) following the Smart-seq2 workflow [24]. Subsequent qRT-PCR analysis was performed as above. The primer sequences of the targeted genes are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad 9.0 software. Quantitative results are presented as means ± SEMs. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was applied. When comparing three or more groups, one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test were used. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed by the robust DAVID online bioinformatics tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov), which serves as a comprehensive resource for functional annotation and bioinformatics analysis. The data analysis results, encompassing volcano plots, heatmaps, and scatter plots, were generated through an online visualization platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn), which offers interactive capabilities for the visualization of complex omics data.

Results

Efficient recovery of HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF on the ovarian function in CTX‑induced POI mice

The experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Longitudinal monitoring of body weight at day 0, day 10, and day 20 revealed CTX-induced systemic toxicity in mice. Although intraovarian administration of HucMSCs, ADSCs, or SVF did not maintained physiological weight trajectories, they didn’t worsen the slower growth induced by CTX, indicting the biosafety of ovarian in situ cell therapy (Fig. 1B). After 3 weeks post-operation, ovaries from all five groups were harvested and weighed. Ovarian morphology in the CTX-con group showed marked atrophy than the Control group, with partial restoration of ovarian weight and volume in all cell-treated groups (Fig. 1C-D). The western blot analysis showed enhanced follicle-stimulating hormone receptor (FSHR) expression and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) production, revealing consistent upregulation of follicular development regulators and rejuvenated granulosa cell functionality (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1). Hormone assay was also conducted, interindividual variability limited statistical significance. Nevertheless, the average level of estrogen, progestogen, and AMH was elevated to vary extents after cell therapy compared to CTX-con group (Fig. 1F-H). Furthermore, histological examination of ovary following H&E staining revealed a significant increase in the total number of follicles across all therapeutic cohorts, particularly primordial follicles and primary follicles (Fig. 1I-L). Interestingly, unlike the other two groups, ADSC treatment showed no improvement in secondary follicles compared to CTX-con (Fig. 1M), while antral follicle counts remained comparable across groups (Fig. 1N). Significantly, the number of atretic follicles was markedly reduced, which indicated that HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF effectively attenuate follicular atresia, resisting direct damage to follicles caused by chemotherapy drugs, and conferred protective functions on follicle development (Fig. 1O).

Restoration of fertility potential in POI mice through cell therapies

Comprehensive evaluation of reproductive competence revealed differential therapeutic efficacy among cell populations. Total ovulated oocyte counts showed inter-individual variability across groups and showed no significance (Fig. 2A-B). Despite CTX-induced profound oocyte quality deterioration, all three cell therapeutic modalities significantly decreased oocyte morphological abnormalities, with HucMSC-therapy demonstrating the most marked reduction (Fig. 2C). To further validate these findings, we performed western blot analysis, which demonstrated that the ovarian DEAD-Box Helicase 4 (DDX4) expression was significantly higher in the HucMSC and SVF groups than in the CTX-con group (Fig. 2D-E). No significant increase was observed in the CTX-ADSC group, possibly correlating the lower total oocyte number (Fig. 2B). Following exposure to CTX, the capacity for embryonic development was markedly diminished, as evidenced by a significant reduction in blastocyst formation [25]. Overall, cell therapy notably enhanced blastocyst formation in POI mice compared to the CTX-con group, indicating improved embryo quality following cell-based protection (Fig. 2F-G). To explore the final fertility outcomes, female mice were mated with 10-week-old male ICR mice at a 2:1 ratio for 3 weeks after ovarian surgery. In the first month post-mating, only 1 of 4 mice in the CTX-con group became pregnant and delivered 9 pups, whereas all 4 mice in other treatment groups produced healthy offspring (Fig. 2H-I). Additionally, the time interval from mating to the first birth was significantly longer in the CTX-con group compared to the other groups, suggesting the recovery of healthy reproductive cycling by cell therapies (Fig. 2J).

HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF promoted proliferation, inhibited apoptosis, and attenuated senescence of impaired granulosa cells in vitro and in vivo

As granulosa cells play an essential role in follicular development and are likely the major target of stem cells, we investigated the effects of HucMSCs, ADSCs and SVF. The proportion of cleaved-Caspase 3-positive cells [26], was significantly downregulated in the follicles of cell therapy groups (Fig. 3A-B). Conversely, the proportion of Ki-67 positive granulosa cells within ovarian follicles was markedly increased (Fig. 3C-D). These suggested that HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF enhance granulosa cell survival by modulating the balance between proliferation and apoptosis.

To further explore these effects in vitro, we utilized the KGN cell line, which is widely recognized as an optimal model for studying granulosa cell function. KGN cells were incubated with 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide (4-HC), the active metabolite of cyclophosphamide and then co-cultured with HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF. Collectively, results from Western blot analysis (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. 2 A), gene expression analysis (Fig. 3G), EdU and cleaved-caspase 3 staining assays (Figs. 3H-J) demonstrated a significant enhancement of KGN cells proliferation and a corresponding decrease in apoptosis. As expected, the 4-HC control group exhibited a much lower ratio of healthy cells, while co-culture with these three types of cells protected KGN cells from apoptosis, as indicated by Annexin V/PI staining (Fig. 3L-M).

Subsequently, we assessed DNA damage and cellular senescence in granulosa cells. The tumor suppressor factor P53 protein serves as a major participant in the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway and functions as a key regulator of the cell cycle [27], was significantly downregulated in cell therapy groups, indicative of reduced cellular senescence (Fig. 3K). The SA-β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) assay further revealed a lower proportion of senescent cells in the treated groups (Fig. 3N-O). Immunofluorescence staining and western blot analysis of γ-H2AX expression consistently indicated that HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF significantly alleviated 4-HC–induced DNA damage in both KGN cells in vitro and granulosa cells in vivo (Fig. 3P-R). The up-regulation of the pro-senescent factor p16(INK4a) and the down-regulation of the anti-senescent factor Cyclin B1 are both pivotal in cellular senescence. Co-culture with HucMSCs, ADSCs, or SVF effectively alleviated 4HC-induced damage and aging by down-regulating p16 and restoring Cyclin B1 expression (Fig. 3R and Supplementary Fig. 2B). Collectively, these comprehensive results demonstrated that HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF effectively promote the proliferation and survival of granulosa cells and mitigate the adverse effects of chemotherapy, thereby providing a promising therapeutic strategy for preserving ovarian function.

Transcriptional alterations of granulosa cells after co-culture with HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF in vitro by RNA-seq analysis

To elucidate the effects and potential mechanisms of cell therapy on granulosa cells, we performed RNA-seq analysis on primary granulosa cells following co-culture with HucMSC, ADSC, and SVF in vitro. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed transcriptional similarity between the Control group and the 4HC-HucMSC group, while co-culture with ADSC and SVF resulted in distinct transcriptional profiles (Fig. 4A). To delineate the common changes across the cell therapy groups, we employed Venn plots, which revealed 32 consistently upregulated transcripts and 42 consistently downregulated when compared to the 4HC-control group (Fig. 4B-D). Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and discovered upregulated genes are highly enriched in terms related to “Positive regulation of cell proliferation” and “Response to estradiol and progesterone”. Conversely, downregulated genes are enriched associated with “Negative regulation of cell proliferation” (Fig. 4E-F). These findings corroborate the proliferative effects of cell therapy on granulosa cells observed in vivo (Fig. 3).

Notably, comparative functional analysis revealed that HucMSC co-culture exerted more pronounced regulatory effects on granulosa cells compared to ADSC and SVF (Fig. 4D). This differential potency may, in part, be attributed to inherent donor-dependent biological heterogeneity in ADSC and SVF preparations [28]. Consequently, we directed our focus toward transcripts exhibiting significant differential expression between the 4HC-HucMSC and 4HC-con groups (Fig. 4G). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated significant enrichment of HucMSC-modulated pathways, including “Cell population proliferation” (NES = 1.537), “Positive regulation of developmental process” (NES = 1.506), “Cytokine- cytokine receptor interaction” (NES = 1.946), and “Steroid biosynthetic pathways” (NES = 1.824) (Figs. 4 H-K). These pathways collectively orchestrate critical aspects of granulosa cell physiology during folliculogenesis, particularly regarding paracrine signaling and steroidogenic capacity. Furthermore, we observed the upregulation of “peroxisome”-related genes (Fig. 4L), suggesting that enhanced ROS metabolic regulation may represent a previously unrecognized mechanisms mediating HucMSCs’ therapeutic effects on ovarian function.

Proteomic profiling of HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF reveals potential effectors of ovarian function restoration

To elucidate the proteomic determinants underlying stem cell-mediated ovarian recovery, we performed data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry profiling of HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF, with human embryonic kidney cell lines (HEK-293T) as somatic control. Previous studies have demonstrated that extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from HucMSCs exhibit potent anti-apoptotic effects on KGN cells and activate PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, while EVs from HEK-293T cells show no significant biological activity in this context [29]. PCA analysis revealed that HucMSCs and ADSCs displayed similar protein expression patterns, consistent with their shared mesenchymal stem cell characteristics, whereas 293T and SVF exhibited distinct profiles (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, SVF showed intragroup heterogeneity, as evidenced by correlation heatmap and relative standard deviation (RSD) analysis (Fig. 5B-C), suggesting potential implications for the poor repeatability of SVF cell samples.

HucMSC, ADSC, and SVF treatments collectively improved ovarian function; however, the magnitude of the therapeutic effect varied among the three groups. We conducted a comparative analysis of the top 1,000 abundant proteins and identified 574 conserved proteins across all therapeutic cell types (Fig. 5D). Functional annotation using the DAVID online database revealed that the molecular functions of these 574 proteins were highly enriched in terms such as “Oxidoreductase,” “Antioxidant,” and “Peroxidase,” all associated with redox regulation processes (Fig. 5E). To focus on functionally relevant proteins rather than housekeeping proteins, we individually compared each cell type with 293T. Volcano plots revealed the significantly expressed proteins. The results showed that compared with 293 T cell, HucMSC had 1,455 upregulated proteins and 1,464 downregulated proteins (Fig. 5F), while ADSC had 1,587 upregulated proteins and 1,668 downregulated proteins (Fig. 5G). SVF showed more extensive differential protein expression, with 3,030 upregulated and 3,563 downregulated proteins (Fig. 5H). GO and KEGG analyses were conducted on these differentially expressed proteins separately (Supplementary Fig. 3). We identified 861 proteins that were consistently upregulated in the three cell types compared to 293 T (Fig. 5I). Critically, these proteins showed significant enrichment in oxidoreductase pathways (Fig. 5J), consistent with prior findings. Collectively, these proteomic findings suggest that redox homeostasis modulation might serve as a conserved therapeutic mechanism across diverse stem cell populations, contributing to their observed efficacy in ovarian function recovery.

The antioxidant effect of cell therapies on the oocytes and KGN cell lines

Building upon multi-omics evidence from RNA sequencing and proteomic profiling, we hypothesized that antioxidant restoration constitutes a principal mechanism underlying stem cell therapy in ovarian injury. To validate this hypothesis, we systematically evaluated mitochondrial function and ROS levels in both oocytes and granulosa cells.

In vivo functional validation revealed that CTX treatment significantly compromised oocyte mitochondrial function, as indicated by a reduction in membrane potential (ΔΨm). However, all three stem cell types (HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF) effectively restored ΔΨm in these injured oocytes (Figs. 6A-B). Consistent with these findings, CTX-induced ROS accumulation in oocytes from our POI mouse model was substantially attenuated following cell therapy (Figs. 6C-D).

Complementary in vitro experiments using 4-hydroperoxycyclophosphamide (4HC)-treated KGN cells demonstrated similar therapeutic effects. Co-culture with HucMSC, ADSC, or SVF significantly improved ΔΨm (Figs. 6E-F) and reduced ROS levels (Fig. 6G) in these granulosa cells. Mechanistic investigations revealed that stem cell co-culture upregulated key mitochondrial functional proteins (MFN2, TOMM20, TIM23) and antioxidant enzymes (SOD1, GPX4, SOD2) to varying extents (Fig. 6H). At the transcriptional level, HucMSC and ADSC consistently restored expression of most mitochondrial and antioxidant-related genes that were suppressed by 4HC treatment, while SVF showed more variable effects (Figs. 6I-N).

The reversible effect of antioxidant administration to CTX or 4-HC-induced injury to granulosa cells and oocytes

Given that the stem cell contains a relatively high proportion of other components, to further verify that our hypothesis operates through antioxidants, we conducted in vivo and in vitro antioxidant rescue experiments to determine whether the widely used antioxidants N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and resveratrol (RSV) could counteract cyclophosphamide (CTX)-induced ovarian injury.

We first investigated whether NAC and RSV could protect against CTX-induced ovarian injury in vivo. After administering RSV and NAC for five consecutive days to mice treated with intraperitoneal CTX, we collected ovarian tissues from each group for morphological and molecular marker analysis. H&E staining confirmed that CTX indeed caused ovarian damage, consistent with previous findings, as evidenced by a reduced follicle count and the presence of atretic follicles (Fig. 7A). In contrast, the RSV and NAC groups retained morphologically normal follicles and showed an increase in follicle number (Fig. 7B). Subsequent Ki67 staining of ovarian sections revealed that the CTX-control group exhibited nearly no Ki67-positive follicles, with a sharp decline in granulosa cells showing proliferative activity (Fig. 7C). In comparison, the RSV and NAC groups displayed significantly higher Ki67 positivity (Fig. 7D). These results demonstrate that in vivo supplementation with antioxidants effectively mitigates CTX-induced targeted damage to the ovary.

We subsequently employed an in vitro follicle culture system. Over the 5-day culture period, we documented the morphology of each follicle and measured their diameters. Due to the significant differences in growth rates among follicles at different stages, we categorized them into two groups: small-sized follicles (less than 90 μm) and large-sized follicles (greater than 90 μm). We observed that after the addition of 4-HC to the follicle culture medium, follicular growth was nearly arrested, with granulosa cells ceasing proliferation and even approaching death (Fig. 7E). In contrast, the addition of RSV and NAC exhibited a marked rescue effect, allowing follicles to grow almost as normally as those in the control group (Fig. 7F-G). On the third day of drug treatment, we performed immunofluorescence staining on follicles from each group and found that the DNA damage marker γ-H2AX was barely detectable in the control group, while its signal was significantly enhanced in the nuclei of oocytes in the 4HC group (Fig. 7H). In contrast, the RSV and NAC treatment groups showed a notable reduction in γ-H2AX signal (Fig. 7I). Subsequently, we isolated oocytes and granulosa cells from follicles of each group and conducted micro-scale RNA extraction. We detected a significant decrease in the expression levels of oocyte quality markers, including Nobox, Zp3, and Zar1, in the 4HC-con group, whereas the other three groups exhibited statistically significant increases (Fig. 7J). In granulosa cells, the RSV and NAC groups showed significant upregulation of the proliferation gene Pcna and functional genes Cyp19a1, as well as mitochondrial function-related genes Sirt1, Mfn1, and Ndufc1, which displayed similar trends (Fig. 7K-L). These results demonstrate that antioxidants can directly promote follicular development in vitro and rescue 4HC-induced damage in both granulosa cells and oocytes.

Discussion

Stem cell-based regenerative therapy has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for POI and POF. Owing to their robust paracrine activity, anti-inflammatory properties, and inherent capacity for tissue repair, MSCs have garnered attention for ovarian function restoration. Among these, HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF have been extensively utilized in murine models of POI/POF. For instance, in murine models of chemotherapy-induced POF, HucMSC therapy has been shown to partially restore ovarian weight, replenish the follicular reserve, elevate levels of gonadotropins and sex hormones, and re-establish a regular estrous cycle [30]. Similarly, SVF treatment demonstrated comparable therapeutic efficacy in a CTX-induced rat model of POI/POF. Following SVF therapy, a significant increase in follicle numbers, a decrease in atretic follicles, and an elevation in AMH levels were observed, collectively indicating that SVF treatment can enhance ovarian reserve and promote overall ovarian function recovery [19]. Beyond these specific observations, MSCs are widely recognized for their capacity to reduce apoptosis in ovarian granulosa cells and enhance oocyte quality [31]. Current evidence from the literature further indicates that stem cell transplantation promotes comprehensive ovarian tissue regeneration, as evidenced by increased ovarian volume, restored follicular architecture, and normalized developmental progression. The therapeutic efficacy is primarily mediated through paracrine secretion of bioactive factors including VEGF, IGF-1 and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), which collectively enhance angiogenesis and tissue repair [13]. Our findings corroborate these established phenotypes, demonstrating that stem cell intervention significantly increases follicular count, improves endocrine parameters, and enhances fertility outcomes.

However, despite promising advances, the therapeutic efficacy of distinct stem cell populations in ovarian regeneration exhibits marked heterogeneity. Critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the comparative regenerative potential among various stem cell sources, their mechanistic divergence in modes of action, and optimization clinical translation paradigms. Addressing these unresolved questions necessitate systematic investigation to establish evidence-based selection criteria and refined treatment protocols. In response to these challenges, our study systematically investigated HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF both in vitro and in vivo experimental models. We found that all three cell types can repair damaged ovarian function by inhibiting granulosa cell apoptosis, promoting cell proliferation, reducing cellular senescence, improving mitochondrial function, and exerting significant antioxidant effects. Leveraging a multi-omics approach combining by combining RNA sequencing and proteomic analysis, we comprehensively evaluated the therapeutic effects and intricate molecular mechanisms by which these three different sources of stem cells and components in rescuing ovarian function. Our findings not only provide a robust understanding of their respective contributions but also establish a novel theoretical basis new theoretical basis for their clinical application in ovarian regenerative medicine.

Mitochondrial homeostasis and oxidative stress regulation emerge as central determinants in the pathogenesis of ovarian dysfunction [32]. The characteristic ovarian functional decline strongly correlates with redox imbalance and mitochondrial compromise [33]. Specifically, excessive ROS impairs mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) function, ultimately leading to reduced ovarian reserve. MSCs effectively counter these pathological processes through multi-modal antioxidant interventions. Following stem cell in vitro coculture, the levels of various oxidoreductase proteins in granulosa cells (KGN cells) significantly increased, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), catalase (CAT), and other antioxidant-related proteins. These proteins collectively neutralize ROS, mitigate lipid peroxidation, and reduce DNA damage, thereby safeguarding oocytes and granulosa cells from oxidative stress-induced damage [34]. Human placenta-derived MSCs can modulate the HO-1/HO-2 ratio in the ovaries to alleviate oxidative stress-induced damage [35]. Moreover, HucMSCs overexpressing the HO-1 gene have also been shown to exhibit enhanced ovarian function recovery in POF models, underscoring the significance of HO-1-mediated antioxidant effects [36]. The restoration of mitochondrial function is crucial for oocyte quality and follicular development [37]. In terms of improving mitochondrial function, stem cell therapy not only enhances the expression of antioxidant enzymes that directly scavenge oxygen free radicals but also modulates the expression of mitochondrial metabolism-related proteins [38, 39]. Research confirms that the subunits of OXPHOS chain-related enzyme complexes are restored following stem cell therapy, indicating recovered electron transport chain efficiency and improved mitochondrial energy metabolism function [40]. Furthermore, stem cells may also enhance the energy metabolism of damaged cells through mitochondrial transfer mechanisms [41]. For instance, in an experiment on elderly mice with ovarian dysfunction, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) transplantation increased the mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial DNA copy number of ovarian cells, improved mitochondrial cristae structure, and regulated the expression of mitochondrial motility-related proteins [14]. These findings align with the results of our study, which demonstrated that three types of cell therapies significantly increased mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced ROS levels in oocytes and granulosa cells, in both in vivo models and in vitro cell cultures.

N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is a well-established antioxidant that has been shown to improve mitochondrial functions in granulosa cells and oocytes, thus enhance embryo quality [42, 43]. Multiple clinical trials have demonstrated its efficacy in ameliorating endocrine dysfunction in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and in improving oocyte and embryonic outcomes [44–46]. To counteract the deleterious effects of exogenous insults—such as chemotherapeutic agents, ultraviolet radiation, and ionizing radiation—as well as endogenous reactive oxygen species (ROS), organisms have evolved a sophisticated oxidative-stress response network that mitigates cellular damage [47]. Central to this defense is the transcription factor Nrf2, which orchestrates the antioxidant response by up-regulating cytoprotective genes [48, 49]. Resveratrol (RSV), a natural polyphenol with potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, functions as a Nrf2 activator [50]. Accumulating evidence indicates that RSV alleviates oocyte aging and enhances mitochondrial biogenesis [51, 52]. Clinical studies have further reported that RSV supplementation improves mitochondrial function and reproductive outcomes in PCOS patients undergoing assisted reproductive technology [53]. In the present study, we employed two antioxidants, NAC and RSV, to systematically investigate the role of antioxidant interventions in counteracting CTX-induced ovarian injury. This approach was designed to further test the hypothesis that the therapeutic benefits of stem cell therapy in POI may be attributed, at least partially, to the delivery of antioxidant proteins and the resultant modulation of redox homeostasis. Our findings from both in vitro and in vivo experiments consistently demonstrate that NAC and RSV administration effectively mitigates oxidative damage, preserves follicular integrity, and restores cellular functions in the ovary, thereby supporting the proposed mechanism. However, it is important to note that this study did not isolate or identify specific antioxidant proteins or molecules responsible for the observed protective effects. Instead, a broad-spectrum assessment of antioxidant capacity and redox balance was conducted. While this approach provides valuable evidence for the overall involvement of antioxidant pathways, it does not delineate the precise molecular contributors or their specific mechanisms of action. Future investigations should therefore prioritize the elucidation of the exact antioxidant components and signaling pathways involved.

Current evidence highlights significant heterogeneity in extracellular vesicle (EV) proteomic and miRNA cargo profiles across MSCs derived from distinct tissue sources and cultured under varying passage conditions [54, 55]. Despite this, comparative studies indicate comparable therapeutic efficacy between bone marrow-derived and umbilical cord-derived MSC-EVs in restoring ovarian function within POI murine models [56]. However, existing research predominantly focuses on singular stem cell types or isolated molecular pathways, often overlooking the holistic tissue restoration capacity. The robust tissue-repair effects of stem cells are not attributable to any single influence but rather to the systematic improvement of the local tissue microenvironment. While different types of stem cells share therapeutic similarities, they also exhibit distinct differences, warranting further comparative insights. Our investigation directly addresses this knowledge gap by systematically evaluating and revealing conserved antioxidant mechanisms across multiple MSC sources. Proteomic profiling demonstrated a marked upregulation of antioxidant-associated proteins (including oxidoreductases and peroxidases) relative to somatic controls, suggesting MSC-mediated oxidative stress mitigation via targeted protein delivery. These findings are further supported by mitochondrial functional improvements, collectively substantiating redox homeostasis restoration as a fundamental therapeutic mechanism. While RNA sequencing analysis did not to reveal pathway-level enrichment in co-regulated genes, this could potentially attributable to extended in vitro culture durations and may amplify downstream proliferative signaling over redox regulatory effects. Future optimization strategies for MSC-based ovarian regenerative approaches may involve culture condition modulation or genetic engineering to enhance antioxidant potency.

In our study, clinical-grade HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF were procured from commercial companies for each batch of modeling mice or in vitro-cultured granulosa cells. We observed that the therapeutic effects produced by these distinct cells were not entirely consistent, highlighting inherent variability. Comparative analysis revealed distinct therapeutic profiles: HucMSC exhibited superior functional outcomes across experimental models, whereas ADSC demonstrated comparatively reduced efficacy in follicular augmentation. SVF preparations showed diminished capacity to modulate antioxidant-related transcriptional programs in granulosa cells. Complementing this, proteomic profiling identified notable inter-group heterogeneity within SVF cohorts, indicating notable batch-to-batch differences. Transcriptomic data revealed that HucMSCs were more effective in promoting granulosa cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis. HucMSC treatment eliciting substantially more differentially expressed genes between 4HC-HucMSC and control groups compared to ADSC or SVF interventions. This observation suggests that while ADSC/SVF-mediated granulosa cell proliferation primarily manifests at the proteomic level, their transcriptional regulatory effects remain comparatively attenuated. Previous studies have demonstrated that the health and age status of donors significantly influence the MSCs quality [57]. The secretome of MSCs, a key mediator of their therapeutic effects, is affected by the in vitro culture environment and the physiological and pathological conditions of the source tissue [58, 59]. Mesenchymal stem cells from adipose tissue are prone to losing their stem cell-related markers under obesity-related stress conditions [60]. Since both ADSCs and SVF are derived from adipose tissue, and although donors are generally selected from healthy individuals with normal physical examination indicators, their unknown lifestyle habits and sub-health status are often difficult to identify. In contrast, HucMSCs’ neonatal tissue origin, coupled with rigorous maternal-fetal health screening protocols, correlated with enhanced batch-to-batch consistency. Due to the timeliness required for the use of cell preparations, we couldn’t directly compare the in vitro and in vivo experimental effects of different batches of cells within the same experiment. Therefore, our conclusion regarding the high stability of HucMSCs is drawn from the consistency across various experimental results. Technical limitations precluded direct inter-batch comparisons within unified experimental frameworks, future experiments should aim to overcome this limitation and incorporating standardized validation paradigms to comprehensively assess therapeutic reproducibility.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that HucMSCs, ADSCs, and SVF exert potent therapeutic effects, effectively mitigating CTX- and 4-HC-induced ovarian damage. Mechanistically, these cell-based therapies primarily operate by attenuating mitochondrial dysfunction, reducing ROS accumulation, and alleviating oxidative stress through the delivery of key antioxidant proteins. This restorative process enhances granulosa cell viability by promoting proliferation and suppressing apoptosis, ultimately leading to improved follicular integrity and oocyte quality. Our findings underscore the pivotal role of redox homeostasis in ovarian protection and suggest that stem cell-derived antioxidant modulation represents a promising therapeutic strategy for chemotherapy-induced ovarian dysfunction, which provides valuable insights for optimizing regenerative approaches in clinical fertility restoration.
