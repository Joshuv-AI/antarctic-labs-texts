Changes in oral health-related quality of life and self-esteem after fixed orthodontic treatment
Authors: Jung-Sub An, Hye-Young Sim, Min-Ho Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine the associations of fixed orthodontic treatment with oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) and self-esteem (SE) in relation to sex and premolar extraction status.MethodsAdult patients undergoing fixed orthodontic treatment at a private orthodontic practice from 2010 to 2021 were included. Pre- and posttreatment OHRQoL and SE were evaluated using the Orthognathic Quality of Life Questionnaire (OQLQ) and Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSE), respectively. Outcomes were compared by sex and premolar extraction status.ResultsA total of 358 patients (mean age: 26.98 years), including 250 female patients (69.8%) and 206 patients (57.5%) who underwent premolar extraction, were evaluated in this study. Before treatment, male patients had higher OHRQoL and SE than female patients (OQLQ score: 20.25 vs. 25.77, P < 0.001; RSE score: 31.99 vs. 30.06, P < 0.001). After treatment, OHRQoL improved in both sexes (P < 0.001) and SE improved in female patients (P < 0.001), although the between-sex difference was not significant. Male patients maintained higher OHRQoL and SE than female patients posttreatment (OQLQ score: 12.56 vs. 16.79, P < 0.001; RSE score: 32.44 vs. 30.94, P = 0.001). Pretreatment OHRQoL and SE in the extraction group were lower than those in the non-extraction group (OQLQ score: 25.82 vs. 21.78, P = 0.005; RSE score: 30.21 vs. 31.23, P = 0.018), but these differences became non-significant posttreatment (P > 0.05).ConclusionsAfter orthodontic treatment, OHRQoL improved in both sexes and SE improved in female patients, although the clinical significance of these changes was uncertain. In the extraction group, OHRQoL and SE were initially lower than those in the non-extraction group but showed no significant intergroup differences after treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Studies on the effectiveness of orthodontic treatment have mainly focused on clinician-based outcome measures, such as dentoskeletal changes. However, the effects of orthodontic treatment on patient-based outcomes, including social and psychological well-being, have attracted growing interest.1,2 Accordingly, a growing number of studies have evaluated the psychosocial effects of malocclusion or orthodontic treatment on patients.3

Improving quality of life (QoL) is one of the main goals of healthcare systems,4 and the effects of various diseases and interventions have been assessed in terms of QoL. In dentistry, several indices have been developed to assess oral health-related QoL (OHRQoL), which is defined by the absence of negative effects of oral conditions on social life and a positive sense of dentofacial self-confidence.5

Malocclusions may affect OHRQoL, but previous studies have reported inconsistencies between the severity of malocclusion and the reported OHRQoL or patient’s perception of their occlusion.6 This is because patients have a higher level of concern for noticeable malocclusions, such as crowding, overjet, or lip profile, but are tolerant of less noticeable problems, such as Class II molar relationship.7 Since several patients value esthetic impairment more than the malocclusion itself, and malocclusion is rarely associated with pain or noticeable functional problems, a greater focus on psychosocial problems is essential to evaluate the effects of malocclusion or orthodontic treatment on patients. The Orthognathic Quality of Life Questionnaire (OQLQ) has been developed to assess the QoL of patients with dentofacial deformities and places more importance on esthetic problems in the dentofacial area.8 Although the OQLQ was developed for patients undergoing orthognathic treatment, it focuses more on esthetic impairment than other OHRQoL measures and has been applied to nonsurgical orthodontic patients.9

Self-esteem (SE) refers to an individual’s overall subjective sense of personal worth or value.10 Individuals with low SE tend to be less sure of their abilities and may doubt their decision-making process.11 Various factors can influence SE, including socioeconomic status,12 physical health,13 facial esthetics, and malocclusion.9,14 Malocclusions such as crowding, protrusive profile, or orthognathic problems can affect SE.9,15 A long-term study of 1,018 participants showed that adults who had notable malocclusion in childhood but did not undergo orthodontic treatment exhibited lower SE than those without malocclusion who received no treatment or those with malocclusion who received treatment.16 Additionally, SE is known to increase during adolescence and shows little change after 30 years of age, since social roles are generally established and career decisions are typically made after this age.17 Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSE)18 is one of the frequently used questionnaires to measure SE.11

Patients with malocclusion and an unesthetic appearance have low OHRQoL or SE,9,19 but previous studies have not definitively clarified whether these characteristics can be improved through orthodontic treatment.3,20,21 In particular, studies on changes in OHRQoL and SE after orthodontic treatment in relation to sex or premolar extraction status in adult patients are lacking.

This study aimed to assess the associations of fixed orthodontic treatment with OHRQoL and SE, focusing on differences based on sex and premolar extraction. The null hypothesis was that the OHRQoL and SE changes following fixed orthodontic treatment would not differ by sex or premolar extraction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

Adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) who started and completed orthodontic treatment in a single private practice with one clinician (MHJ) between January 2010 and January 2021 were included. Patients who received full-arch fixed orthodontic treatment without orthognathic surgery and agreed to participate in this study were included. The need for premolar extraction was initially assessed by the clinician. Extraction was generally considered in patients with severe crowding (arch length discrepancy ≥ 10 mm), lip protrusion exceeding 4 mm relative to the esthetic line in both lips, or severe anteroposterior jaw discrepancy. In such cases, the clinician explained the rationale for extraction and began treatment after obtaining the patient’s informed consent. When both extraction and non-extraction treatments were clinically feasible, the advantages and disadvantages of each option were explained, and the final plan was determined on the basis of the patient’s preference. Patients with craniofacial deformities, including cleft lip and/or palate, a history of previous orthodontic treatment, and chronic medical problems or temporomandibular joint (TMJ) pain were excluded.

During the pre- and posttreatment clinical examination visits, patients were asked to complete the OQLQ and RSE questionnaires using paper forms. This step was performed in a private room after providing the patients with an explanation of the questionnaires. The OQLQ consists of 22 items evaluated using a four-point scale; the items are grouped into four components: social aspects of deformity (eight items), facial esthetics (five items), function (five items), and awareness of facial deformity (four items). Lower OQLQ scores indicate better OHRQoL, and total scores range between 0 and 88.8 The RSE consists of 10 questions and was scored as a four-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree.” The score for the five questions decreased from 4 to 1 (from strongly agree to strongly disagree), and the score for the remaining five questions increased from 1 to 4. The minimum and maximum scores were 10 and 40, respectively. Higher RSE scores indicate better SE.18

The design of this study was approved by the Public Institutional Review Board Designated by Ministry of Health and Welfare (2021-3379-001).

Statistical analysis

Patients were grouped by sex and premolar extraction status. The extraction group included patients who underwent maxillary and/or mandibular premolar extraction, whereas the non-extraction group consisted of patients who did not undergo extraction of any premolars. Third molar extraction was not considered in the grouping. Differences in pretreatment age and treatment duration between the groups were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test, and differences in premolar extraction and use of orthodontic mini-implants (OMIs) in relation to sex were analyzed using the Pearson chi-squared test.

The patients were grouped according to sex or premolar extraction; multiple linear regression analysis was performed to compare OQLQ and RSE scores before and after treatment between the groups; and adjusted P values were calculated. Differences in the OQLQ and RSE score changes after orthodontic treatment in relation to sex or premolar extraction were analyzed using a linear mixed model. To calculate the adjusted P values, sex-classified groups were adjusted for premolar extraction, pretreatment age, OMI use, and treatment duration, and groups divided according to premolar extraction were adjusted for sex, pretreatment age, OMI use, and treatment duration.

Pearson correlation coefficients between total OQLQ and RSE scores were calculated both before and after treatment to examine the relationship between OHRQoL and SE. Because the two correlations were obtained from the same patients, Steiger’s method (a modification of Dunn and Clark’s z) for dependent correlations was used to determine whether they differed significantly between the two time points.22

The level of significance was set at α = 0.05 for all tests, except for the OQLQ component-level analyses, where a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.0125 (0.05/4) was applied to account for multiple comparisons among the four components, which were regarded as secondary outcomes.

RESULTS

Demographic characteristics

In total, 467 adult patients agreed to participate at the start of treatment. Thirty-three patients refused to complete the questionnaire after orthodontic treatment, and 19 patients were excluded because of unanswered questionnaire items. Six patients had previously received orthodontic treatment, and two patients presented with TMJ pain. Forty-nine patients required surgical orthodontic treatment. Finally, data from 358 patients were analyzed.

The demographic characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. In total, 108 male (30.2%) and 250 female (69.8%) patients were included in this study. The mean pretreatment age was 26.98 years, and the mean pretreatment age in the female group (27.86 years) was significantly greater than that in the male group (24.95 years; P < 0.001). The mean treatment duration was 31.56 months, and the treatment duration showed no significant difference in relation to sex. Premolars were extracted for orthodontic treatment in 206 (57.5%) patients, and the extraction rate was significantly higher in female patients (62.4%) than in male patients (46.3%) (P = 0.005). OMI placement was performed in 251 (70.1%) patients, with no significant sex-related difference in OMI placement. Pretreatment age did not differ significantly between the extraction and non-extraction groups (26.45 ± 6.11 years vs. 27.70 ± 7.57 years; P = 0.303). Treatment duration also did not differ significantly between the groups. However, the rate of OMI placement was significantly higher in the non-extraction group (127 of 152, 83.6%) than in the extraction group (124 of 206, 60.2%) (P < 0.001).

Changes in the OQLQ and RSE scores in relation to sex

Differences in OHRQoL and SE in relation to sex are presented in Table 2. The OQLQ and RSE scores significantly differed in relation to sex before and after treatment. Both before and after treatment, male patients had significantly lower total and component OQLQ scores, except for function, and significantly higher RSE scores than female patients (P < 0.05). However, the function component scores showed no significant sex-related differences before and after treatment (Table 2).

Table 3 shows the changes in OHRQoL and SE after orthodontic treatment. Both sexes showed a significant decrease in the total OQLQ score (P < 0.05) and in most OQLQ component scores (P < 0.0125), except for awareness of facial deformity (Table 3). Additionally, the social aspects of the deformity score decreased more in female patients than in male patients (P = 0.007), whereas the function component score decreased more in male patients than in female patients (P = 0.012). After orthodontic treatment, the RSE score increased significantly in female patients (P < 0.001) but not in male patients; however, the between-sex difference in the change in the RSE score was not statistically significant (Table 3).

Changes in the OQLQ and RSE scores in relation to premolar extraction

The patients were divided into the premolar extraction and non-extraction groups, and the differences in OHRQoL and SE between the groups were analyzed. Before treatment, the total OQLQ (P < 0.05) and facial esthetics scores (P < 0.0125) were significantly higher in the extraction group than in the non-extraction group. The RSE score was higher in the non-extraction group than in the extraction group (P = 0.018). However, these differences disappeared after treatment (Table 4).

After orthodontic treatment, the total OQLQ score improved significantly in both groups (P < 0.05), as did the scores for most OQLQ components (P < 0.0125), except for the awareness of facial deformity in the non-extraction group. The total OQLQ, social aspects of deformity, and facial esthetics scores decreased more in the extraction group (total, P < 0.05; social aspects and facial esthetics, P < 0.0125), indicating greater OHRQoL improvement. The RSE score significantly increased in both the extraction and non-extraction groups (P < 0.05) without showing intergroup differences (Table 5).

Correlation between OQLQ and RSE scores

A significant correlation was found between the total OQLQ and RSE scores. The pretreatment correlation was –0.410 (P < 0.001), and the posttreatment correlation was –0.387 (P < 0.001), indicating a moderate negative correlation at both time points. However, the difference between the two correlations was not statistically significant (z = –0.426, P = 0.670; 95% confidence interval for the difference, –0.13 to 0.08) (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

The chief complaints of patients considering orthodontic treatment are mainly focused on esthetic problems rather than functional problems, such as chewing or pronunciation. Several OHRQoL measures, such as the Child Perceptions Questionnaire23 and Oral Health Impact Profile,19 mainly evaluate pain, pronunciation, or chewing abilities, which may not be highly related to the subjective problems attributable to malocclusion. Therefore, in this study, we used the OQLQ, which focuses on dentofacial esthetics and its psychosocial effect, to evaluate the association of malocclusion or orthodontic treatment with OHRQoL from the patient’s perspective.

Previous studies on the effect of malocclusion on SE have shown varying results depending on the study design.9,14,21,24 This is because facial esthetics or malocclusion is not the single determinant of SE. A recent systematic review concluded that definitive determination of the influence of orthodontic treatment on SE was difficult.25 In this study, factors such as pretreatment age, OMI use, and treatment duration were adjusted using statistical methods to exclude psychosocial influences caused by other factors, except group-dividing factors.

The patients included in this study received orthodontic treatment for an average of approximately 31.6 months, which was slightly longer than the previously reported treatment period (Table 1).26 The premolar extraction rate of the patients was approximately 57.5%, and OMI was used in approximately 70.1% of the patients, particularly in the majority (83.6%) of cases in the non-extraction group (Table 1). The need for OMI may be influenced by multiple factors, including case complexity, biomechanics, and anchorage strategy. Since the use of OMI reflects the adoption of less conventional biomechanics, it may have contributed to the longer treatment period.

Patients’ data were divided into two groups on the basis of sex or premolar extraction, and the differences and changes in OHRQoL and SE were compared. Pretreatment age and premolar extraction rates were significantly higher in female patients than in male patients (Table 1). Severe crowding, lip protrusion, and anteroposterior jaw discrepancy may warrant consideration of premolar extraction.27 Therefore, patients undergoing extraction may differ from those treated without extraction for several reasons. Cases requiring extraction may involve greater complexity, whereas in borderline cases amenable to either extraction or non-extraction treatment, the treatment decision may depend on clinical feasibility and patient preference.

OMIs were used in the non-extraction group to gain space for tooth alignment through the posterior movement of molars, although the amount of space obtained was usually less than that achieved through premolar extraction. In the extraction group, OMIs were used to reinforce anchorage, limiting the forward movement of posterior teeth during space closure and achieving greater retraction of anterior teeth in comparison with that in cases without OMI use. Cases requiring such additional anchorage strategies and biomechanics may differ from those that do not require them. The frequency of OMI use was compared to examine whether treatment approaches differed by sex, and no significant difference was observed between the two groups (Table 1). However, the ratio of OMI use in the non-extraction group was significantly higher than that in the extraction group. To exclude the effects of confounding variables on OHRQoL and SE, P values were adjusted for pretreatment age, sex, premolar extraction, OMI use, and treatment duration.

OHRQoL and SE differences according to sex

The pretreatment overall OQLQ scores, excluding the function component, were significantly higher in female patients than in male patients, indicating that female patients have lower OHRQoL than male patients (Table 2). After orthodontic treatment, both sexes showed significant reductions in OQLQ scores. The social aspects of the deformity component improved more in female patients and the function component improved more in male patients, indicating that female patients may be more sensitive to changes in appearance than male patients, whereas male patients may be more interested in changes in function than female patients (Table 3). Owing to the similar changes in the total OQLQ score for both sexes after orthodontic treatment, the total OQLQ score was still lower in male patients than in female patients even after treatment (Table 2). These results indicate that even after a significant improvement in OHRQoL was observed in both sexes, female patients still had relatively lower OHRQoL than male patients after treatment. In the adult population, the OHRQoL of female patients has been reported to be lower than that of male patients,28 and since this study showed similar results in patients after orthodontic treatment, this difference may be attributable to the characteristics of the OQLQ.

Although the RSE score increased significantly within the female group but not within the male group, the between-sex difference in the change in the RSE score was not statistically significant (Table 3). Nonetheless, in line with the greater improvement in the social aspects of the deformity component among female patients, female patients may generally place greater importance on facial esthetics than male patients. This tendency may also be related to the higher extraction rate in female patients (62.4%) than in male patients (46.3%). In cases where both extraction and non-extraction treatment were clinically feasible, female patients may have been more likely to select extraction, possibly reflecting greater concern about facial esthetics.

The ability of orthodontic treatment to improve SE is a topic of debate,29 and in this study, the mean RSE scores of both sexes exceeded 30 before treatment (Table 2), showing a high level of SE.30 Thus, the SE improvement observed after treatment may not have been clinically significant.

OHRQoL and SE differences in relation to premolar extraction

The pretreatment total OQLQ and facial esthetics component scores were significantly higher in the extraction group than in the non-extraction group (Table 4). Because premolar extraction is usually planned when a large amount of space is required to correct severe crowding or lip protrusion,27 patients who underwent premolar extraction likely had more significant esthetic concerns than those who did not.

After orthodontic treatment, OQLQ component scores decreased significantly in both groups, except for awareness of facial deformity in the non-extraction group (Table 5). The awareness of the facial deformity component, which requires awareness of one’s own and others’ faces and teeth, did not show a significant difference between the two groups before treatment (Table 4), and the component is thought to be relatively less sensitive to esthetic concerns than the other components.

The social aspects of deformity and facial esthetics components and total OQLQ scores decreased more in the extraction group than in the non-extraction group (Table 5). Consequently, the total OQLQ and all component scores no longer showed significant differences between the two groups after treatment (Table 4); thus, the OHRQoL difference was no longer apparent following orthodontic treatment, possibly in association with improvements in facial esthetics.

Before orthodontic treatment, the RSE score of the extraction group was significantly lower than that of the non-extraction group (Table 4), which is also thought to be attributable to the presence of more esthetic concerns in the extraction group. After orthodontic treatment, the RSE scores significantly improved in both groups (Table 5), with no significant difference in the RSE scores between the two groups posttreatment (Table 4). Similar to the changes in the OQLQ, the difference in SE between the two groups appeared to diminish after orthodontic treatment, possibly in association with changes in appearance. However, since both groups had high SE scores before and after treatment,30 the SE improvement observed after treatment may not be clinically meaningful.

Correlation between OHRQoL and SE

The total OQLQ and RSE scores demonstrated a moderate negative correlation in both pretreatment (–0.410, P < 0.001) and posttreatment (–0.387, P < 0.001) assessments. Because lower OQLQ scores indicate better OHRQoL, the negative correlation between the OQLQ and RSE scores indicates that better OHRQoL was associated with higher SE. Moreover, the correlations observed pre- and posttreatment did not differ significantly (P = 0.670). This moderate correlation, consistent across time points, may reflect the distinct characteristics of the two questionnaires. Our findings are consistent with the results of previous studies reporting a negative correlation between the Oral Health Impact Profile–14 and RSE scores.31

Limitations

This study included only adults, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other age groups. In addition, 52 patients who did not complete the questionnaires were excluded, raising the possibility of attrition bias. Furthermore, this study evaluated differences on the basis of the premolar extraction status but did not fully assess the specific extraction indications or detailed malocclusion characteristics. Similarly, although differences in treatment approach in relation to OMI use were statistically controlled, the clinical indications for OMI use were not assessed. Measurement of malocclusion severity using indices such as Peer Assessment Rating (PAR) would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between clinical and patient-reported outcomes. Previous studies have reported a moderate positive correlation between changes in PAR and Psychosocial Impact of Dental Aesthetics Questionnaire scores following fixed orthodontic treatment,32 and demonstrated that greater PAR improvement was independently associated with greater OHRQoL improvement.33 These findings imply that the OHRQoL and SE improvements observed in the present study are likely reflected by occlusal improvements. Data for socioeconomic status, of which occupation is a core component, were not collected in this study, but socioeconomic status has been shown to influence both OHRQoL and SE, with lower status consistently associated with poorer OHRQoL34 and lower SE.12 Further studies including detailed malocclusion characteristics and socioeconomic factors across diverse age groups are warranted.

CONCLUSIONS

After nonsurgical fixed orthodontic treatment, OHRQoL improved significantly in adults of both sexes, while SE improved significantly in female patients, although the difference between sexes was not significant. Female patients showed lower OHRQoL and SE than male patients both before and after treatment. Pretreatment differences in OHRQoL and SE between extraction and non-extraction groups became non-significant after treatment. However, the improvement in SE may not be clinically significant.
