Assessing the effectiveness of decentralised antenatal ultrasound in a primary health care clinic compared with a hospital-based service in rural South Africa: an interrupted time series analysis
Authors: Christopher James Westwood, Andrew Charles Miller, James Dudley Porter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal ultrasound is recommended as a routine part of care in all pregnancies by the World Health Organisation and South African National Department of Health—ideally before 24 weeks’ gestation. Despite these recommendations, access to timely antenatal ultrasound is still limited in many low-resource settings, including South Africa. Decentralisation of services away from the hospital setting has been identified as a possible solution to access-related challenges. This study assessed the effect of a decentralised, primary health care based antenatal ultrasound service compared to an existing hospital-based service in a rural sub-district of the Eastern Cape of South Africa.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, quasi-experimental, interrupted time series analysis comparing the periods before and after the implementation of a new, twice-monthly ultrasound service at a Community Health Centre. The effect of the service on timing of antenatal ultrasounds, overall uptake of ultrasounds, and antenatal care engagement was analysed.ResultsA total of 1239 women were included in the study period from January 2017 to July 2020 with 693 and 546 in the pre- and post-intervention periods, respectively. After the introduction of the decentralised ultrasound service, there was a significant slope change with consistent improvement over time across multiple outcomes. The proportion of women receiving ultrasounds before 24-weeks’ gestation increased from 52% to 66% within 18 months of the start of the intervention, while overall uptake of antenatal ultrasounds improved similarly with monthly rates consistently above 90% at the end of the study period. Women completing four or more antenatal appointments also increased from 52% to 82%. The timing of the initial booking visit did not show any significant change.ConclusionDecentralisation of an antenatal ultrasound service to a primary health care facility significantly improves timing of ultrasounds and overall engagement with antenatal care. These findings underscore the potential role of decentralised antenatal services in enhancing access to ultrasound and engagement with care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08419-w.Keywords: Ultrasound, Antenatal, Decentralised /Decentralized, Primary Health Care

Background

Over the last three decades, the role of ultrasound in antenatal care has developed from use in a minority of select cases to being recommended as a routine part of care in all pregnancies. This change is reflected by the inclusion of antenatal ultrasound in the World Health Organisation (WHO) antenatal care recommendations [1], South African Department of Health (NDOH) [2], as well as other international guidelines [3–5]. Despite these recommendations, access to antenatal ultrasound is still limited in many parts of the world-particularly in rural settings [1].

The benefits of ultrasound in antenatal care include documented clinical benefits such as accurate estimation of gestational age, pregnancy location, fetal and placental position, diagnosis of multiple pregnancy, and identification of fetal anomalies [2, 6]. In addition, antenatal ultrasounds are viewed in an overwhelmingly positive light and as an essential part of antenatal care by mothers and partners-particularly in legitimising the pregnancy, framing the fetus as a person, and as a bonding opportunity for couples [1, 7, 8]. Access to ultrasound has also been associated with improved uptake of antenatal care more broadly [9–16].

The barriers to traditional antenatal ultrasound are well documented and predominantly related to cost and access, with ultrasound machines both expensive and frequently only available at referral centres [1]. This requires time, money, and reliable transportation for women to access services. In places where ultrasound machines are available in the periphery, a lack of trained personnel and supporting infrastructure-such as reliable electricity-can prevent a functional service from being offered [1, 17–19]. These barriers are felt most acutely in poorer, rural areas—particularly South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa. In these areas, most pregnant women do not have access to ultrasound services, and perinatal mortality accounts for 95% of the global burden [20, 21]. South Africa’s rural areas follow the global pattern with poorer maternal and perinatal outcomes than their urban counterparts [22–25], and more difficulty accessing obstetric care [26].

In South Africa, the “National Integrated Maternal and Perinatal Care Guidelines For South Africa”-in line with the WHO-recommend a minimum of one antenatal ultrasound for all pregnant women before 24 weeks of gestation [2]. According to these guidelines, ultrasounds can be performed by ultrasonographers, obstetricians, medical officers, or trained midwives. However, neither the South African Nursing Council (SANC) nor the South African government explicitly include ultrasound as part of the scope of practice for midwives or midwife specialists [27, 28]. South Africa has a two-tiered health system comprising a large public sector, which serves a large majority (84%) of the population, and a smaller, better-resourced private sector (16%) [29]. Public health care is structured across primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with community health centres (CHCs) and primary health care clinics (PHCs) forming the foundation of primary care and the antenatal service [30].

Studies from multiple sub-Saharan African countries have demonstrated improvements in antenatal care engagement, institutional deliveries, and identification of high-risk pregnancies following the decentralisation of ultrasound services [11–14, 16]. In Nigeria, Luntsi et al. [14] reported an increase of 50% in the rates of antenatal visits, a 30% increase in institutional deliveries, and an 11% increase in the number of high risk pregnancies identified after a decentralised antenatal ultrasound service began. Similarly in Uganda, Kawooya et al. [11] showed a 47% increase in the number of women attending four or more antenatal visits, and a 32% increase in the number of women attending the antenatal clinic during their pregnancies after the introduction of decentralised ultrasound. However, there is limited evidence from South Africa-particularly regarding the impact of decentralised ultrasound on the timing of antenatal ultrasounds.

This study evaluates whether decentralising antenatal ultrasound to a primary health care facility increases the proportion of pregnant women who receive a scan before 24 weeks’ gestation, in accordance with the National Department of Health (NDOH) guidelines. Secondary objectives were to assess the impact of decentralised ultrasound on: (i) the number of women who received at least one antenatal scan, (ii) the timing of booking visits, and (iii) the total number of antenatal visits. The WHO monitoring framework for antenatal care guided the choice of objectives [31].

Research methods and design

Study setting

The study was conducted at Madwaleni Hospital and Xhora CHC in the Mbhashe Subdistrict of the Eastern Cape of South Africa. Madwaleni Hospital is a rural district hospital in the Amathole District of the Eastern Cape of South Africa, and the only hospital in the Mbhashe Subdistrict. The Mbhashe subdistrict is one of the poorest in South Africa with more than half of households living in poverty [32], and three-quarters not having access to piped water at home [33].

In November 2018, an outreach program was initiated from Madwaleni Hospital to Xhora CHC, offering basic antenatal ultrasounds on a regular, twice-monthly basis. Prior to this, antenatal ultrasounds had only been available at Madwaleni Hospital. This outreach was added onto an existing regular outreach that had previously focused on support for the HIV and TB programs at the clinic. Ultrasounds were performed by the visiting medical officer, and all pregnant women who attended Xhora CHC for their antenatal care were offered ultrasounds there. The ultrasound machine used was permanently located at the clinic. The hospital-based service continued as before as a free, weekly, walk-in service. Madwaleni Hospital is 22 km (13.6 mi) away from Xhora CHC, with a daytime round trip on public transport costing R70.

Study design

We conducted a quasi-experimental interrupted time series (ITS) analysis without a control group, comparing the periods before and after the implementation of the decentralised ultrasound service at Xhora CHC. ITS designs are particularly suited to evaluating interventions introduced at the population level over a clearly defined period and targeting population-level outcomes [34]. A control group was not included due to the lack of a comparable clinic in the study setting. Other primary health care clinics in the subdistrict differ substantially in infrastructure, service capacity, and patient volume, and are even more remotely located. These structural and contextual differences would introduce substantial bias and limit the validity of comparisons.

Sample size

The sample size required was estimated based on the effect sizes seen in two studies in Ethiopia [15] and Nigeria [14] using modelling of ITS power levels by Hawley et al. and Liu et al. [35, 36] Based on these studies, a sample size of at least 36 time-points with 30 cases per time-point was estimated to reach a power of 80%. Significance was set at an alpha value of 0.05.

Study period

The study period comprised 43 months from 01 January 2017 until 31 July 2020, with 23 months in the pre-intervention arm and 20 months in the post-intervention arm. The post-intervention arm was truncated due to the coronavirus disease 2019 (Covid-19) pandemic and associated lockdowns that were identified as potential confounders that would be difficult to control for.

Study population

All the women who booked their pregnancies at Xhora CHC and delivered at Madwaleni Hospital or Xhora CHC during the study period were included. On average, Madwaleni performs 1500 deliveries per year, while Xhora performs 160 deliveries per year. Their records were accessed retrospectively via the Maternal Care Records (MCRs) stored at each facility. There were no exclusion criteria.

Data collection

Data were collected from the individual MCRs that are issued at the first antenatal visit and used as the primary record during the antenatal, labour, delivery, and postpartum periods. These records are stored at the delivery facility after delivery. The data were entered and cleaned using Microsoft Excel [37], before being exported to R version 4.4.3 for statistical analysis [38]. Factors such as age, HIV status, and high-risk pregnancy status were captured at the time of booking. High-risk pregnancies were defined according to the Basic Antenatal Clinic checklist in the NDOH guidelines [2].

Data analysis

The individual data were aggregated by month and divided into pre-intervention and post-intervention cohorts. Continuous data were aggregated into medians or means based on their distribution patterns, while categorical data were aggregated into proportions. Baseline descriptive statistics for each group were calculated to assess for differences between the two groups.

For binary outcomes, including the primary outcome of whether the first antenatal ultrasound occurred before 24 weeks’ gestation, we used Generalised Estimating Equations (GEE) to account for autocorrelation within repeated monthly observations over time. A logit link function was specified for the binomial outcome, and an autoregressive (AR1) working correlation structure was chosen to reflect the expected temporal dependence between adjacent months. Time was modelled as a continuous variable, with an indicator variable included to represent the intervention.

Models were fitted to the pre-intervention period to establish underlying trends, and counterfactual predictions were generated by extrapolating these trends into the post-intervention period. The analysis assessed for both a change in the intercept at the time of the intervention (“step change”), and a change in the gradient of the trend (“slope change”) in the post-intervention period. Robustness of the effect estimates was tested through the inclusion of covariates such as maternal age, HIV status, and high-risk pregnancy status. Further sensitivity analysis was conducted using varying correlation structures, varying the intervention time points, and, to assess for bias related to the Covid-19 truncation, varying the endpoint of the data set. The estimated effects were expressed as Odds Ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

Ethics

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from Walter Sisulu University Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (024/2024) and the Eastern Cape Health Research Committee (EC_202406_003).

Results

Baseline

A total of 1239 cases were identified across the 43 monthly time points of the study period, with 697 (30.3 per month) in the pre-intervention period, and 542 (27.1 per month) in the post-intervention period. The median age of the combined cohort was 25 years (IQR 21–31), with an HIV prevalence of 37%. Teenage pregnancies comprised 18% of the total sample, while 33% were nulliparous. The median estimated gestational age at booking was 19 weeks and zero days (IQR 13 + 3–24 + 4), while the median delivery occurred at 39 weeks and two days (IQR 37 + 0–40 + 4). The baseline characteristics were similar between the two groups (Table 1).

ITS analysis

Primary outcomes

For the primary outcome of the proportion of ultrasounds done before 24 weeks of gestation, the pre-intervention median across the time-points was 52% (IQR 44–60). There was no pre-intervention trend of an increase or decrease in this rate. GEE analysis revealed no significant step change in the odds of receiving an early ultrasound (before 24 weeks of gestation) immediately following the intervention (OR = 1.021, 95% CI: 0.734–1.410). However, there was a statistically significant positive deflection in the slope of the trend after the intervention (Fig. 1a), indicating that the odds of receiving an early ultrasound increased over time post-intervention compared to the pre-intervention period (OR for slope change = 1.052 per month, 95% CI: 1.017–1.081). Adding covariates to the model (Fig. 1b) decreased the effect size (Table 2), but the change remained statistically significant (OR 1.034, 95% CI: 1.004–1.070). In real-world terms, this odds ratio equates to an increase in the proportion of ultrasounds before 24 weeks from a baseline of 52%, to 56% by six months, 61% at 12 months, and 66% at 18 months post-intervention. Of the 19 post-intervention months studied, only two had proportions of early ultrasounds lower than the pre-intervention months, and both occurred in the first four months following the intervention.

Secondary outcomes

While the primary outcome focused on the proportion of early ultrasounds, we also assessed the proportion of women who had at least one ultrasound during their antenatal period, regardless of when in the pregnancy it was performed. Pre-intervention, the median rate was 82% without an increasing or decreasing trend. As with the primary outcome, the post-intervention trend showed a statistically significant slope change without a step change in the post-intervention period (OR 1.131, 95% CI 1.062–1.192) equating to incrementally higher rates over time (Table 3). In the post-intervention period, four of the monthly time-points had 100% uptake rates, and 13 of the 19 had rates above 90% (Fig. 2a).

Discussion

This study assessed the effect of a decentralised antenatal ultrasound service on the timing of antenatal ultrasounds in rural South Africa. The demographic composition of the study population is consistent with other studies in antenatal populations in South Africa in terms of age, teenage pregnancy rates, HIV positivity, and obstetric history [39–41]. The initial, pre-intervention statistics showed that, while most women (82%) accessed ultrasounds via the centralised, hospital-based service, nearly half accessed the service late into their pregnancies. There was also evidence of poor engagement in the available antenatal services, with 45% of the pre-intervention cohort not completing at least four antenatal visits.

The results across the different outcomes in this study show a consistent pattern of slope change in the post-intervention period. Our initial hypothesis predicted a step change as the new service began, and access became easier. However, none of the outcome categories exhibited this behaviour, but rather an incremental improvement in ultrasound timing, overall uptake of ultrasound, and engagement with antenatal care over time in the post-intervention period. The consistent nature of this change across various outcomes along with the results of the sensitivity analysis adds robustness to these findings.

In addition to the overall trend, the month-to-month improved consistency in the proportion of early ultrasound in the post-intervention period is also a notable improvement from the pre-intervention period, where the percentage of early ultrasounds ranged from the mid-20% to the early 80% without a discernible seasonal or other pattern. While official data on where the ultrasounds were performed (Hospital or Clinic) are unavailable, routine service-level data from Madwaleni suggest that, by July 2020, 79% of women from Xhora CHC were receiving their ultrasounds at their clinic [42]. This suggests that the implementation of the decentralised service allowed women to access early ultrasounds more consistently in their pregnancies.

A commonly used framework for investigating and describing barriers to care in lower-middle-income countries is the “Three-Delays model” proposed by Thaddeus and Maine in 1994 [43]. This framework identifies three critical phases that might delay access to care: phase one, a delay in the decision to seek care; phase two, a delay in reaching the health care facility; and phase three, a delay in receiving appropriate treatment at the facility. Conceptually, the intervention in this study aimed to affect the phase two delay by decreasing the financial and time costs associated with accessing antenatal ultrasounds. However, it has been documented that factors beyond time and money affect behaviour when seeking health care including the perceived quality of the care received, and a variety of individual and sociocultural factors [43–46]. This might explain the steady improvement over time post-intervention while the new service developed the community’s trust as an acceptable alternative to the existing hospital-based service rather than an immediate step in outcomes as was initially hypothesised.

There was no significant change in the rates of early booking despite an underlying increase in early booking over time, suggesting that access to ultrasound is not the driving factor behind the decision to attend the initial antenatal booking visit. Studies examining factors that influence the timing of antenatal booking in South Africa have highlighted the roles of individual beliefs and knowledge, sociocultural norms, health system factors—such as health care worker attitudes and waiting times—and financial constraints [16, 46–48]. However, the increased engagement in antenatal care post-intervention is a positive outcome with an increase in antenatal clinic visits associated with improved perinatal outcomes [49]. The mechanism of this improvement is unclear, however it is consistent with previous research in multiple other African countries [9, 11–16].

One potential confounder in this study is the addition of a doctor alongside the decentralised ultrasound service. In South Africa, basic antenatal care is typically delivered by nurses without routine involvement of doctors. The inclusion of a doctor-who was also responsible for conducting the ultrasounds-may have enhanced the acceptability of the service, introducing a change beyond the ultrasound component itself and differing from standard practice. However, this is unlikely to explain the entire effect seen in this study as other studies with midwife and radiographer run services have shown similar outcomes [9, 11, 16]. In addition, it may have had effects outside of the provision of ultrasound such as upskilling of the nurses working alongside them, streamlining referral pathways, and increased identification of high-risk cases.

While in this study an ultrasound machine was already present at the facility, access to ultrasound machines has been a major barrier to decentralisation [18, 50]. However, the rise of handheld, highly portable ultrasound machines have made ultrasound more affordable and accessible in low-resource and rural settings [51]. As access to machines improves, it is likely that access to trained personnel in peripheral facilities will become the next major barrier to implementation of a decentralised service with nurse- and midwife-run services a possible solution [52–54]. In South Africa, one barrier to a midwife-led service is related to the described competencies and scope of practice of midwives and registered nurses. Neither the scope of practice published by the South African Government [27], nor the “Competencies for Midwife Specialities” [28] or the “Education and Training Guidelines for Advanced Diploma in Midwifery” [55] published by SANC include any mention of ultrasound as being either within or outside of the scope of nursing practice despite midwives being listed in the NDOH guidelines as one of the cadre of staff who are allowed to perform basic antenatal ultrasound [2]. Resolving this discrepancy will be a necessary step if decentralised antenatal ultrasound services are to be rolled out more broadly in the South African setting.

The strengths of this study are the quasi-experimental design which allows for a stronger causal inference between the intervention and outcome to be made in comparison to other non-experimental designs. Despite the truncation of the post-intervention arm due to Covid-19, the number of time-points sampled were sufficient to reach the required power level. The findings are also consistent with previous literature [9, 11–16], and consistent across multiple outcome variables and analysis techniques.

Limitations of this study include the lack of a control group, single centre of study with population-level data, the rural setting, retrospective design, and was underpowered to conduct any meaningful subgroup analyses. These increase the risk of confounders, misclassification bias, and decrease the generalisability to individuals and contexts. This study also did not include any direct maternal or neonatal outcomes as outcome measures with access to ultrasound and antenatal attendance presumed to be positive outcomes in and of themselves.

Conclusion

The decentralisation of antenatal ultrasound services to a primary health care clinic was associated with an increased proportion of early ultrasounds aligned with local and international guidelines, improved overall uptake of antenatal ultrasound, and greater engagement in antenatal care. These findings underscore the potential role of decentralised antenatal services in enhancing access to and engagement with care, in line with WHO and NDOH recommendations. Further research in diverse settings is needed to assess the generalisability of these findings, including evaluations of nurse-led services and the equipment, personnel, and training costs required for broader implementation of primary health care based ultrasound services. In addition to this, clarity on the scope of practice of midwives in South Africa is needed before any broader programmes can be implemented.
