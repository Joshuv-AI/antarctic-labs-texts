Analysis of clinical characteristics and risk factors for severe EBV infection in children—a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xifeng Tang, Ting Sun, Guangyao Zhu, Yiping Zhou, Yucai Zhang, Rongxin Chen, Yun Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the clinical features and risk factors of severe Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection in children.MethodsA retrospective analysis of the medical records of 144 cases admitted to the PICU (Pediatric Intensive Care Unit) and pediatric general wards of Shanghai Children’s Hospital from January 2019 to March 2024 for Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection was conducted. The cases were categorized into severe and IM (Infectious Mononucleosis) groups according to the presence or absence of organ dysfunction.ResultsThe children in the severe group exhibited elevated incidences of gastrointestinal symptoms, cervical lymph node enlargement, hepatomegaly, and gallbladder and bile duct involvement (P ≤ 0.05). The plasma EBV DNA copy number of children in the severe group was higher than that in the IM group. Additionally, hemoglobin levels, D-dimer levels, lymphocyte subsets, and cytokines IL (Interleukin)-10 and IFN (Interferon)-γ were statistically different between the two groups (all P ≤ 0.05). Independent risk factors for severe EBV infection were identified as hemoglobin, IFN-γ and D-dimer. The predicted AUC (Area Under Curve) for hemoglobin was 0.83, with a critical value of 11.1 g/dL (P = 0.02); for D-dimer, the AUC was 0.93, with a critical value of 2.4 mg/L (P = 0.04); and for IFN-γ, the AUC was 0.80, with a critical value of 243.7 pg/ml (P = 0.04).ConclusionThis study suggests that hemoglobin, IFN-γ and D-dimer may are risk factors for progression of EBV infection to severe disease.

Background

Discovered in 1964, EBV is one of the most common herpes viruses, infecting approximately 90% of adults worldwide. A national study reported that the seroprevalence of EBV in Chinese children exceeds 50% by the age of 3 and rises to over 90% after the age of 8 [1]. EBV is primarily transmitted through saliva but can also be spread via blood transfusion and sexual contact. While some individuals infected with EBV remain asymptomatic, the majority present with IM. IM is a self-limiting illness characterized by sore throat, enlarged cervical lymph nodes, fatigue, and fever lasting several weeks [2]. Although most cases have a favorable prognosis, EBV-associated infections can manifest with a wide range of clinical presentations, and some children may develop severe EBV infection. Severe EBV infection can impair the function of multiple organ systems, including the central nervous system, cardiovascular system, respiratory system, renal system, gastrointestinal system, eyes, and hematopoietic system [3]. Without timely intervention, severe EBV infection can result in serious complications and even death. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the risk factors for progression of EBV infection to severe disease in order to help clinicians to recognize and intervene early.

Materials and methods

Research subjects and diagnostic criteria

The study consists of children who were hospitalized for the first time for EBV infection. They should meet the diagnostic criteria for either severe EBV infection or IM. Patients with a prior diagnosis of malignancy, immunodeficiency and/or previous immunosuppressive therapy or other predominantly viral infections were excluded from the study. The diagnostic criteria for severe EBV infection included cases with dysfunction of one or more organs caused by EBV infection or those that had progressed to EBV-associated hemophagocytic syndrome (HLH) [3, 4]. A “Pediatric Sequential Organ Failure Assessment Score” (pSOFA) ≥ 2 was used to define organ dysfunction [5]. The specific definitions are as follows: scores ranging from 0 to 4 are assigned according to the following organ system criteria: Respiratory System: (1) PaO₂/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) ratio: ≥400 (0), 300–399 (1), 200–299 (2), 100–199 with respiratory support (3), < 100 with respiratory support (4); (2) peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂)/FiO₂ ratio: ≥292 (0), 264–291 (1), 221–263 (2), 148–220 with respiratory support (3), < 148 with respiratory support (4);Coagulation: Platelet count (×10³/µL): ≥150 (0), 100–149 (1), 50–99 (2), 20–49 (3), < 20 (4);Hepatic Function: Total bilirubin (mg/dL): <1.2 (0), 1.2–1.9 (1), 2.0–5.9 (2), 6.0–11.9 (3), > 12.0 (4);Cardiovascular System: Mean arterial pressure (MAP, mmHg) stratified by age: <1 month: ≥46 (0), < 46 (1); 1–11 months: ≥55 (0), < 55 (1); 12–23 months: ≥60 (0), < 60 (1); 24–59 months: ≥62 (0), < 62 (1); 60–143 months: ≥65 (0), < 65 (1); 144–216 months: ≥67 (0), < 67 (1); >216 months: ≥70 (0), < 70 (1). Vasoactive agent use in all age groups: dopamine ≤ 5 µg/kg/min or any dose of dobutamine (2), dopamine > 5 µg/kg/min or epinephrine/norepinephrine ≤ 0.1 µg/kg/min (3), dopamine > 15 µg/kg/min or epinephrine/norepinephrine > 0.1 µg/kg/min (4);Neurologic Status: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score: 15 (0), 13–14 (1), 10–12 (2), 6–9 (3), < 6 (4);Renal Function: Serum creatinine (mg/dL) by age category: <1 month: <0.8 (0), 0.8–0.9 (1), 1.0–1.1 (2), 1.2–1.5 (3), ≥ 1.6 (4); 1–11 months: <0.3 (0), 0.3–0.4 (1), 0.5–0.7 (2), 0.8–1.1 (3), ≥ 1.2 (4); 12–23 months: <0.4 (0), 0.4–0.5 (1), 0.6–1.0 (2), 1.1–1.4 (3), ≥ 1.5 (4); 24–59 months: <0.6 (0), 0.6–0.8 (1), 0.9–1.5 (2), 1.6–2.2 (3), ≥ 2.3 (4); 60–143 months: <0.7 (0), 0.7–1.0 (1), 1.1–1.7 (2), 1.8–2.5 (3), ≥ 2.6 (4); 144–216 months: <1.0 (0), 1.0–1.6 (1), 1.7–2.8 (2), 2.9–4.1 (3), ≥ 4.2 (4); >216 months: <1.2 (0), 1.2–1.9 (1), 2.0–3.4 (2), 3.5–4.9 (3), ≥ 5.0 (4). The diagnostic criteria for EBV infection are as follows: serologic antibody positivity (any one of items 1 to 3 under “laboratory indicators” below) or EBV-DNA positivity (considered positive based on the hospital’s reference values: plasma > 10⁴ copies/mL or whole blood > 5 × 10³ copies/mL). For IM, the indications for admission are as follows: firstly, the clinical presentation must be consistent with typical symptoms, and secondly, there must be persistent fever with organ injury (e.g. the liver, the coagulation system, etc.). The diagnosis of IM is mainly based on clinical symptoms and laboratory findings. Clinically diagnosed cases: fulfilling any three of the following clinical indicators and the fourth of the laboratory indicators. Laboratory-confirmed cases: any three of the following clinical indicators and any one of items 1 to 3 of the laboratory indicators are met. Clinical indicator: (1) fever; (2) pharyngotonsillitis; (3) enlarged cervical lymph nodes; (4) enlarged spleen; (5) enlarged liver; (6) eyelid edema. Laboratory indicators: (1) Positive anti-EBV-VCA (Viral Capsid Antigen) -IgM and anti-EBV-VCA-IgG antibodies and negative anti-EBV-NA (Nuclear Antigen)-IgG; (2) Negative anti-EBV-VCA-IgM but positive anti-EBV-VCA-IgG antibody with low affinity; (3) Double serum anti-EBV-VCA-IgG antibody titers were elevated 4-fold or more; (4) Peripheral blood heterogeneous lymphocyte ratio ≥ 0.10 and/or lymphocytosis ≥ 5.0 × 109/L [6].

Methods

The cases in this study were divided into two groups: The first group consisted of patients with IM due to EBV infection, who had mild disease without organ dysfunction during hospitalization. The second group included patients with dysfunction of one or more organs caused by EBV infection. Gender, age, duration of illness before admission, fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, enlarged cervical lymph nodes and so on were collected. Laboratory findings were also collected, including plasma EBV DNA copy number, lymphocyte subpopulation distribution, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, glutaminase, triglycerides, ferritin, coagulation profiles, cytokine levels, and imaging results. These laboratory results were the worst values within 48 h of admission to the hospital. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 26.0 software program. Data are expressed as percentages, means and standard deviations, or medians and interquartile ranges. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using a t-test, and non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical data were analyzed by the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Multiple comparisons were made to identify sources of significant differences, and the results were adjusted using the Bonferroni method. Composited ROC curves were derived from multifactor binary logistic regression analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Of the 144 cases included in this study, 29 developed organ dysfunction on admission or during hospitalization and were admitted to the PICU. The remaining 115 cases were patients with IM without organ dysfunction. In the severe group, there were 18 cases of EBV-associated HLH, 2 cases of severe encephalitis, 2 cases of severe pneumonia and 7 cases of severe IM. The age of the severe group ranged from 8 months to 127 months, with a median age of 37 months, which was significantly younger than that of the IM group (P < 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference in the gender composition of the two groups (P = 0.59).

Clinical presentation

Children in the severe group had a longer pre-hospital illness duration compared to the IM group (P < 0.05). And children in both groups presented with fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, hepatosplenomegaly, and enlarged lymph nodes on admission. Among the severe group, 26 (89.6%) cases had fever on admission, 17 (58.6%) had enlarged lymph nodes and 7 (24.1%) had eyelid oedema, all of which were significantly lower than those in the IM group (P < 0.05). The proportions of children with gastrointestinal symptoms and hepatomegaly on admission were significantly higher in the severe group (P < 0.05). The percentage of children with splenomegaly was 79.3% in the severe group, vs. 72.2% in the IM group (P = 0.44) (Table 1).

Ancillary examinations

Patients in the severe group had lower blood cell counts, with a median white blood cell count of 3.8 × 109 /L, median hemoglobin count of 10.1 g/dL, and median platelet count of 127 × 109/ml (P < 0.05). However, total bilirubin, triglycerides, ferritin, and D-dimer were significantly higher (P < 0.05). As for lymphocyte subsets and cytokines, CD8 + and NK cell counts were significantly lower in the severe group and IFN-γ measured values were higher on admission (P < 0.05). The median plasma EBV copy number of children in the severe EBV infection group was significantly higher than that of the IM group (P < 0.05). All 29 cases in the severe group underwent abdominal imaging early in the admission process, whereas 114 cases in the IM group underwent abdominal imaging. In this study, it’s worth noting that the gallbladder or biliary system was more likely to be involved in children in the severe infection group early in the course of the disease (Table 1).

Analysis of risk factors for the occurrence of severe EBV infection

We used the indicators that were statistically different in the univariate analysis: age, hemoglobin, total bilirubin, triglycerides, LDH, D-dimer, INR and IFN-γ as dependent variables, and the occurrence of a severe EBV infection as an independent variable using a multifactorial logistic regression analysis. The results suggest that hemoglobin, D-dimer and IFN-γ are independent risk factors for the development of severe EBV infection (Table 2). Using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, we observed that these three indicators may be good predictors of severe EBV infection. It was more likely to occur when hemoglobin ≤ 11.1 g/dL (AUC = 0.83, specificity at 0.82 and sensitivity at 0.76), D-dimer ≥ 2.4 mg/L (AUC = 0.93, specificity at 0.91 and sensitivity at 0.72) and IFN-γ ≥ 243.7 pg/mL(AUC = 0.80, specificity at 1 and sensitivity at 0.66) (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 2).

Discussion

EBV has a high seroprevalence rate in all age groups, and EBV infection can involve a wide range of organ functions, leading to serious complications. Furthermore, EBV has been associated with the development of many tumors and autoimmune diseases [7, 8], imposing a greater economic burden on society. Severe EBV infection leads to a mortality rate of more than 50% [9]. Therefore, early recognition of severe EBV infection is particularly important.

The ages of the enrolled children were predominantly within the early childhood and preschool years. Previous studies have shown that HLH is prevalent in children aged 3 to 4 years, with an age of onset younger than the IM group, similar to our study [10]. In our study, children with severe EBV infection presented with fever, enlarged cervical lymph nodes, hepatosplenomegaly, and gastrointestinal symptoms with no specific clinical manifestations. Notably, hepatomegaly and elevated total bilirubin and liver enzymes were significantly higher in the severe group, suggesting that liver dysfunction plays an important role in severe EBV infection. Previous studies have shown that EBV itself does not infect hepatocytes and bile duct epithelial cells, but causes liver injury by activating the immune response, inducing cell cycle abnormalities, and promoting proliferation. Pathologic findings suggested that a large number of lymphocytes were aggregated in the liver, and the infiltrating cells mainly included CD8 + T cells and NK cells [11]. In the severe infection group, 62.1% of the patients presented with gallbladder lesions, which were mainly characterized by edema, thickening of the gallbladder wall or signs of non-calculous cholecystitis, while approximately 10.3% of patients present with bile duct dilatation. It’s rare in IM groups. There is no evidence that EBV can directly invade gallbladder epithelial cells and some researchers believe that EBV-associated cholestatic hepatitis is the primary etiology [12, 13]. Most patients with EBV infection present with mild or moderate liver function abnormalities, while cholestatic hepatitis is rare. Therefore, when cholecystitis or bile duct dilatation occurs early in the course of the disease in a child with EBV infection, there is a risk of the disease progressing to a more severe form [14, 15].

CD8 + T cells are primarily comprised of cytotoxic T cells and suppressor T cells. The primary functions of these cells are to specifically destroy target cells through cytotoxic effects and to secrete suppressor factors that weaken or inhibit the immune response. EBV has been observed to imitate the function of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2, thereby preventing the apoptosis of EBV-infected T lymphocytes, particularly CD8 + T cells [16]. This results in the abnormal proliferation of these cells and the subsequent release of substantial quantities of cytokines. The results of this study showed a significant decrease in the number of CD8 + T cells in the severe group. Bakul I Dalal et al. observed that a decrease in the number of CD8 + T cells may be associated with poor prognosis [17]. This may be associated with the depletion of T-lymphocyte functionality following a severe EBV infection [18].Thus, dynamic monitoring of lymphocyte subsets may be useful for early diagnosis of severe EBV infection and assessment of treatment efficacy.

Elevated triglyceride and ferritin levels serve not only as diagnostic criteria for HLH, but also as risk factors for the prognosis of HLH [19]. In the study, a small number of children with IM also had elevated triglycerides. Infection and inflammation result in a multitude of alterations to lipid and lipoprotein metabolism. It has been demonstrated that elevated triglyceride levels are positively correlated with serum concentrations of inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein and interleukin-1b [20]. Lisha Cai et al. showed that ferritin is one of the biomarkers for early differentiation between IM and HLH [21]. Smits et al. found a minimal set of parameters, including elevated ferritin and triglycerides, predicted HLH [22], and Li et al. developed an EBV-HLH score using 5 parameters including hemoglobin, platelets, neutrophils, albumin and LDH to predict the occurrence [23]. More and more researchers are focusing on the importance of early detection of HLH, and this study discusses an earlier point in time.

Our research observed that hemoglobin, IFN-γ, and D-dimer were risk factors for the occurrence of progression of EBV infection to severe disease. Reduced hemoglobin is associated with phagocytosis, DIC and high inflammatory factor level [23]. In addition to hemoglobin, platelets were likewise lower in the children in the severe group in this study compared to the IM group, suggesting that severe EBV infection may have affected bone marrow hematopoiesis. This may be related to the fact that most of the cases in this study were HLH. It is difficult to observe the sequential relationship between hemoglobin and platelet decline in this study, and more sample size is needed for subsequent studies. Plasma levels of D-dimer, a marker of endogenous fibrinolysis, is used to assess hypercoagulability, which is associated with poor prognosis in a variety of malignancies, including lung-pancreatic, prostate, gastric, colorectal and EBV associated nasopharyngeal cancers [24]. Similarly, in patients with infection or sepsis, high D-dimer levels were strongly associated with 28-day mortality [25]. In this study, it was observed that D-dimer ≥ 2.4 mg/L may be a good marker with an AUC of 0.93. It is an easily accessible test that may help us to quickly identify severe EBV infections.

In contrast to sepsis, IFN-γ and IL-10 were significantly increased, while IL-6 was slightly increased in children with severe EBV infection [26]. Thus, analyzing the characteristics of cytokine profiles may help to distinguish EBV infection from other infectious diseases. Following EBV infection, T cells and macrophages become activated and produce a large number of cytokines, leading to defective T cell defense and over-activation of Th1 cells, which produce a large number of cytokines such as IFN ⁃γ. IFN-γ promotes uncontrolled proliferation and differentiation of T lymphocytes and macrophages, sustained release of large amounts of cytokines, depletion of T lymphocytes and reduced killing function. IFN-γ may therefore be a good biomarker for diagnosing severe EBV infection and assessing treatment response.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the majority of severe EBV infections were cases of HLH and if subgroup analysis was performed again, then the statistical reliability was limited, the factor may have influenced the study results. In a subsequent study, we intend to undertake a multicentre investigation with the objective of examining the clinical characteristics and genotyping differences between children with severe EBV infection in HLH and non-HLH. Second, the treatment received by the enrolled children prior to admission was not evaluated, which may have affected our laboratory findings at the time of admission as well as the assessment of organ function. Third, this study did not continuously monitor organ function indicators and did not assess and analyze possible factors affecting prognosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, serum levels of IL-10 and IFN-γ were higher in children with severe EBV infection early in the course of the disease. Hemoglobin, IFN-γ, and D-dimer were risk factors for the development of severe EBV infection.
