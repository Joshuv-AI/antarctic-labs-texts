Plasma and colostrum concentrations of fentanyl in clinical practice: Intravenous versus epidural administration for labor analgesia
Authors: Benedicte Sandhaug Nygaard, Ragnhild Bergene Skråstad, Olav Spigset, Tonje Gottenberg Skaalvik, Are Hugo Pripp, Janne Rossen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPain relief during labor is essential. Systemic opioids for labor analgesia have been used for decades as a less invasive alternative to epidural analgesia (EA). Although their analgesic efficacy is generally considered inferior to EA, concerns regarding potential maternal and neonatal adverse effects have likely limited their wider adoption. Measurements of fentanyl concentration may provide an objective assessment of systemic exposure and potential transfer to the fetus and colostrum. The aim of the study was to compare fentanyl concentrations in maternal plasma, umbilical cord blood, and colostrum following intravenous (midwife‐administered) versus epidural administration for labor analgesia. We also included a group of women, who initially received intravenous fentanyl but were subsequently converted to EA due to insufficient pain relief.Material and MethodsThis prospective study included three groups: women who received fentanyl epidurally only (EA), intravenously only (IV), or intravenously followed by epidural administration (IV + EA). Samples of maternal plasma, arterial and venous umbilical cord plasma, and two colostrum samples were collected for analysis of fentanyl concentration.ResultsA total of 98 women were included: 34 in the EA group, 38 in the IV group and 26 in the IV + EA group. Fentanyl concentrations were significantly higher in the IV group compared to the EA group in maternal plasma (0.25 ng/mL vs. 0.11 ng/mL, p < 0.001) and umbilical vein plasma (0.084 ng/mL vs. 0.054 ng/mL, p < 0.001). However, within the first 24 h postpartum, no significant differences in fentanyl concentrations were observed in colostrum samples between the IV and EA groups (0.020 ng/mL vs. 0.021 ng/mL, p = 0.93). The estimated theoretical infant dose through breastfeeding in the total study population was very low (0.0079 μg). All measured fentanyl concentrations were low and were accompanied by normal early warning scores, with no requirement for naloxone administration or ventilator support in any group.ConclusionsIntravenous fentanyl for labor analgesia was associated with low fentanyl concentrations in maternal plasma, umbilical cord plasma, and colostrum. These findings suggest that intravenous fentanyl may be considered as an additional analgesic option for laboring women.

INTRODUCTION

Pain relief during labor is essential and epidural analgesia (EA) is considered the “gold standard”. 1 , 2 , 3 However, a regional technique is resource‐intensive for the anesthesia department and may not be available for women in resource‐limited settings. In addition, EA has limitations, including personal preferences, the potential for technical failure, and it may be contraindicated in cases of coagulopathy or bleeding disorders. 2 , 4 , 5 , 6

Systemic opioids for labor analgesia have been used for decades as a less invasive alternative due to easy administration, flexibility in usage, and ability to provide rapid pain relief. 7 , 8 Intravenous fentanyl is often preferred over other opioids because of the fast onset and predictable duration, fewer sedative effects, minimal hemodynamic disturbance, and no harmful active metabolites. 9

Nevertheless, the administration of intravenous fentanyl for laboring women has not yet been implemented as a standard clinical practice in most countries. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 The analgesic efficacy is shown to be moderate and generally inferior to EA administration, but the concern about maternal and fetal side effects is probably the main factor contributing to its limited adoption. 3 , 8 , 11 , 14 , 15 After intravenous administration of fentanyl, the onset of analgesia occurs within minutes, with the peak effect typically reached after about 3–5 min. The drug has a short distribution half‐life, but the terminal elimination half‐life is usually around 3–12 h, depending on the dose, patient factors, and duration of infusion. Fentanyl is extensively metabolized in the liver, primarily via CYP3A4‐mediated N‐dealkylation to inactive metabolites. 16 , 17

There are few studies on the use of intravenous fentanyl during labor with associated fentanyl concentrations in blood and colostrum. Reported pharmacokinetic evaluations indicate very low or often undetectable umbilical cord concentrations, and transient, low colostrum levels after maternal dosing. 18 , 19 , 20 Serious neonatal respiratory events are rare, limiting the ability to draw firm conclusions about safety due to the small sample sizes of existing studies. 18 , 21 , 22

The administration regimen in previous studies has mostly been a patient‐controlled analgesia (PCA) pump with no upper limit of maximum overall dosage or timing before delivery. 23 In our labor ward, intravenous fentanyl has been an option to selected women since 2005. 24 , 25 The administration is performed by the midwives supported by an established local clinical guideline. Our clinical experience aligns with the existing literature, which indicates a minimal risk related to the primary concern of neonates requiring respiratory support, 19 , 22 in addition to a preference from the laboring women. 26

In this study, we aimed to compare fentanyl concentrations in maternal plasma, umbilical cord blood, and colostrum following intravenous versus epidural administration for labor analgesia. We also included a group who initially received intravenous fentanyl but were subsequently converted to EA.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Design and setting

A prospective observational study was conducted from June 18 to September 12, 2024, at Sørlandet Hospital Kristiansand, Norway.

Inclusion criteria were an uncomplicated singleton pregnancy with gestational age ≥ 36 + 0 weeks and with a normal cardiotocography (CTG) at arrival at the hospital. Acute cesarean sections were excluded from the study after enrollment due to logistical limitations related to the sampling. The allocation of women to either intravenous fentanyl or EA was determine d by the midwife based on the woman's assessed need or preferences for pain relief.

Participants were allocated into three distinct groups according to their individual preferences, intravenously (i.e., availability of anesthesiologists) and in alignment with prevailing local clinical guidelines 25 : those only receiving fentanyl epidurally (EA), those only receiving fentanyl intravenously (IV), and those receiving fentanyl intravenously initially but who were due to insufficient analgesia subsequently switched to epidural fentanyl (IV + EA). We attempted to include a total of 100 women in the final study population, with the aim of having approximately the same numbers in the three groups. These numbers were not based on a formal power analysis due to limited prior data on variability in fentanyl concentrations in these matrices. The sample size was based on feasibility and previous pharmacokinetic studies. As we sought to create three groups of comparable sizes, not all eligible patients were approached for participation; recruitment was adjusted according to which groups’ required additional participants. Consequently, the study period was prolonged due to delays in achieving complete enrollment in the final group of IV + EA.

Local clinical guideline

Timing and dosage of fentanyl were administered in accordance with the local clinical guideline. 25 Intravenous fentanyl is preferably offered to multiparous women with an expected shorter labor duration and lower anticipated need for epidural analgesia (EA). It was also administered when EA was requested but temporarily unavailable due to limited anesthesia resources. Intravenous fentanyl was administered by the midwives, initially with four bolus doses of 25 μg within the first 20 min (25 μg × 4, total 100 μg). This was followed by a maintenance dose of 25 μg intravenously every 15 min based on the mother's need for pain relief, with a maximum dose of 100 μg per hour. Intravenous fentanyl should not be administered the last 20 min before delivery; concurrent administration of other analgesic drugs was not recommended and the maximum total recommended dose in the IV group was 500 μg.

The EA consisted of a 50 mL syringe containing a standard mixture of ropivacaine (1 mg/mL) and fentanyl (2 μg/mL). The maximum accumulated recommended dose of fentanyl was 300 μg for women receiving EA only (EA group) corresponding to three 50 mL syringes. In women who initially received intravenous fentanyl, but subsequently necessitated an EA, the maximum intravenous fentanyl dose should not exceed 300 μg (not 500 μg). This implied a maximum total dose of 600 μg fentanyl (IV + EA group) among these women. The differing maximum fentanyl doses between the groups reflect established local clinical guidelines and routine clinical practice at our institution, rather than protocol‐driven study design.

Plasma and colostrum samples

Samples from maternal plasma, arterial, and venous umbilical cord plasma were obtained directly or shortly after birth for analyses of fentanyl concentration. A midwife collected the maternal blood sample after delivery, using either a pre‐inserted intravenous cannula or a needle, and transferred it into designated collection tubes. Blood samples from the umbilical artery and vein were collected as part of routine care shortly after delivery by a pediatric nurse for blood gas analyses. The remaining excess blood from these syringes was transferred into the appropriate collection tubes. The blood samples were delivered to the Department of Medical Biochemistry at Sørlandet Hospital and centrifuged at 2200 G for 10 min (within 1.5 h) to allow the plasma to be pipetted into polypropylene tubes. The plasma samples were subsequently frozen at −80°C.

The first colostrum sample was collected within the first 2 h postpartum at the labor ward, preferable within 30–60 min after delivery. The second colostrum sample was collected at the maternity ward approximately 24 h after birth. We focused on not disturbing the first interaction between mother and child and respected the decision to refrain from conducting a sample if the timing was not appropriate. We aimed to collect at least 0.1 mL of colostrum in each colostrum sample. The first colostrum sample was stored in a refrigerator until the second sample was obtained. Both samples were then frozen in polypropylene tubes at −80°C for preservation.

All biological samples were transported frozen to the Department of Pharmacology at St. Olavs Hospital, Trondheim, Norway for the analysis of fentanyl.

Laboratory analysis

Samples were analyzed using semi‐automatic sample preparation and ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS). Samples were mixed with an isotopically labeled internal standard (fentanyl‐d5) and prepared by liquid–liquid extraction (colostrum) or protein precipitation and phospholipid removal (plasma). The sample volume needed for analysis was 80 μL colostrum and 100 μL plasma. When the required sample volume was unavailable, samples were diluted to the target volume prior to analysis. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was 0.0067 ng/mL. Concentrations below the LLOQ were set to the half of the LLOQ, that is, 0.0034 ng/mL. For further explanation, see Appendix S1.

Collection of clinical data

Clinical observations were performed by the midwife during labor and after delivery and recorded continuously. CTG monitoring was applied 30 min before administration of intravenous fentanyl or EA and continued for a total of 120 min. During this period blood pressure was measured every 30 min. Timing and dosage of intravenous fentanyl and timing, length, and dosage of EA were also registered. Additional variables such as parity, maternal age, pregestational body mass index (BMI), gestational age, induction of labor, length of active labor, mode of delivery, use of oxytocin, and pudendal block were routinely registered. The timing of all biological sample collections was also recorded.

The newborn was assessed using the Apgar score 1, 5, and 10 min postpartum. A pediatric nurse performed an Early Warning Score 2 h after birth. Other routinely registered variables were birthweight, placental weight, pH in the artery umbilical cord, and admission to the neonatal intensive care unit.

All routine registered data was extracted from the hospital's structured electronic birth journal.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported using means, medians, standard deviations (SDs), interquartile ranges (IQRs), minimums, and maximums concentrations, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐squared test. Continuous variables were compared using Mann–Whitney's U test, as the data were not normally distributed according to the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences SPSS 30.0 (IBM, SPSS, Armonk, NY). All significance tests were two‐tailed and conducted at the 5% significance level. Fentanyl elimination half‐lives in colostrum were calculated from the two concentrations available and the time interval between them, assuming linear pharmacokinetics, using the formula t 1/2 = (ln 2 Δt)/(ln C1 − ln C2), where t1/2 is the elimination half‐life, Δt is the time interval between the two samples, C1 is the initial concentration, C2 is the second concentration and ln denotes the natural logarithm.

The average fentanyl concentration in colostrum over the 24‐h period after delivery was calculated by dividing the sum of the initial concentration and concentration 24 h later by 2. If the second sample was not obtained exactly 24 h after the first, we calculated an estimated 24‐h concentration by means of the elimination half‐life in the specific case and used this concentration in the calculation. Based on the data from the literature, the estimated volume of colostrum during the first day after delivery is 5 mL per sitting, irrespective of infant body weight. 27 We presupposed that the baby was breastfed every 2 h during the first day, giving a total volume of 60 mL to estimate a theoretical infant dose the first 24 h. Thus, the infant dose was calculated as the product of the mean 24‐h concentration and this volume.

The relationship between fentanyl concentration in plasma or colostrum and the administered fentanyl dose from EA or IV was assessed using linear regression analysis and described using marginal plots with a 95% confidence interval. The analysis was conducted in Stata/SE 18.0.

DISCUSSION

Fentanyl concentrations in maternal plasma and umbilical cord plasma were significantly higher in women receiving intravenous fentanyl for labor analgesia compared to women receiving EA only. However, within the first 24 h postpartum, no significant differences in fentanyl concentrations were observed in colostrum samples between the EA and the IV group, and the estimated theoretical infant dose through breastfeeding was very low. Importantly, all fentanyl concentrations were evaluated as low, which were accompanied by normal early warning scores of all neonates and no use of naloxone or ventilator support.

This study has certain limitations, but it also possesses important strengths that should be acknowledged. The quantification of fentanyl concentration is regarded as a surrogate endpoint, providing strength as an objective measure. The prospective design, without randomization, and adherence to an implemented standardized local clinical guideline enhance the reliability of our findings. Importantly, the study was conducted in a real‐world clinical setting, which increases the external validity for everyday obstetric practice.

However, the relatively small cohort of the three subgroups, in a single center, limits the ability to detect rare adverse events such as low Apgar score and respiratory depression. Potential long‐term consequences, including breastfeeding success, were not assessed. Correlating fentanyl concentrations with actual breastfeeding rates would be of clinical relevance and should ideally be addressed in future studies. Parity and labor onset differed between the groups due to non‐randomized group allocation, which was influenced by both maternal preference and midwife assessment. However, as the primary outcomes were objective pharmacokinetic measures, the impact on the main findings is considered negligible. Furthermore, we did not investigate the potential impact of maternal and fetal physiology, nor did we consider variations in sampling timing and the measured concentrations. The differing maximum fentanyl doses administered in the IV and EA groups may represent a potential confounder, in addition to possible mediating effects related to pharmacokinetic differences. Maternal satisfaction with intravenous fentanyl was also not within the scope of the present study.

Reviews on the use of opioids for labor pain management provide limited evidence for evaluating the possible side effects associated with intravenous fentanyl. 2 , 3 , 7 , 21 , 28 , 29 One of the most cited studies highlighting awareness and skepticism on its use is a retrospective study examining the analgesic efficacy and effects on neonatal outcome after intravenous fentanyl in 51 women. 23 That study observed that 59% of neonates demonstrated signs consistent with respiratory depression, requiring some degree of supportive measures immediately after birth. However, fentanyl was administered as PCA and there is no information on maximum fentanyl doses or concentrations. Mean fentanyl dose administered was 1216 μg, compared to 125 μg in our study (IV group). In addition, 90% of the women continued to use PCA until delivery, compared to our clinical guideline of avoiding intravenous fentanyl administration the last 20 min before delivery. 23 , 26 Awareness toward the importance of a clinical guideline with safety margins seems important and may explain the difference in neonatal observations in our study using a different protocol.

A literature search examining the use of intravenous fentanyl for labor analgesia, specifically assessing the concentrations of fentanyl in maternal plasma, umbilical cord, and/or colostrum after vaginal delivery, identified only a limited number of studies. 7 , 21 , 28 , 30

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) of 20 women evaluated intravenous PCA fentanyl as an alternative to EA and suggested that the newborns should be monitored after delivery due to lower SpO2 in the intravenous fentanyl group. 21 They reported a wide variability in the total intravenous fentanyl dose during labor (190–885 μg, mean 447 μg), also notably higher than in our IV group (min–max 25–375 μg, median 125 μg). Consistent with this dosing difference, they detected umbilical concentrations from 0.10 to 0.66 ng/mL (compared to our maximum umbilical vein concentration of 0.25 ng/mL). Fentanyl was detected in only 5/10 of their umbilical samples (unknown limit of detection) and no correlation between the reported low concentrations of fentanyl and the status of the fetuses was observed. In contrast, our quantification limit was 0.0067 ng/mL, which is considered very low. Neonatal side effects of fentanyl obviously depend on the cumulative maternal dose, but may also result from other factors such as gestational age, duration of labor, and maternal characteristics. Importantly, no differences in SpO2 were observed in our study between any groups which is supported by a 60% lower maximum fentanyl concentration in the umbilical cord in our study than in the RCT. The interval between the last intravenous fentanyl dose and delivery varied substantially, reflecting the unpredictable nature of labor and the challenges of timing systemic opioid administration, yet no difference in adverse neonatal outcomes was observed between shorter intervals (down to 14 min) as all neonatal parameters remained within normal ranges. However, the study was not powered to assess dose timing/outcome relationships.

Another RCT 20 compared PCA and nurse‐administered intravenous fentanyl during labor. Analgesic efficacy was similar between the groups, and total fentanyl consumption did not differ significantly. In the nurse‐administered group, dosing, and protocol were comparable to our midwife‐administered clinical guideline, with two women receiving a total maximum of 500 μg fentanyl. Umbilical cord fentanyl concentrations were comparable in the two study groups (0.84 and 0.95 ng/mL, respectively), which are approximately 10 times higher than in our sample (0.09 ng/mL). Naloxone was administered in 3 cases, and the timing of the last fentanyl dose was not reported. Nevertheless, that study suggests that intravenous fentanyl may be considered a potential alternative for labor analgesia when appropriate monitoring is ensured. We support their conclusion based on our present findings of very low fentanyl concentrations in all our samples, in addition to 20 years of clinical experience. 26

While Fleet et al. 18 report cord fentanyl levels safely below the threshold for respiratory depression after intranasal and subcutaneous administration, our study – the first to investigate fentanyl concentrations following a midwife‐administered IV dosing – reports even lower concentrations.

During cesarean section, the use of intravenous fentanyl and the subsequent neonatal exposure via colostrum has previously been considered safe, with minimal clinical impact on neonatal feeding behavior. 30 , 31 This aligns with our findings, as colostrum fentanyl concentrations in the IV group were comparable to those observed in the EA group (0.23 vs. 0.19 ng/mL in the first sample and 0.02 vs. 0.02 ng/mL in the second sample respectively) (Table 2). A previous study conducted at our hospital reported a significant reduction in spontaneous suckling and an increased incidence of breastfeeding difficulties among women who received IV fentanyl doses above 200 μg, EA, or IV + EA fentanyl, compared with women who received no labor analgesia. 26 The combined IV + EA group experienced most difficulties and IV fentanyl lowest. These results suggest that confounding factors may play a role, highlighting the value of using objective measures like fentanyl concentrations to evaluate potential side effects (Table 4).

There is no universally accepted threshold dose for respiratory depression when fentanyl is used therapeutically in neonates, but the risk increases with higher and/or rapidly administered doses. Some reviews report respiratory depression at doses above 5 μg/kg, 22 and serious adverse events (including chest wall rigidity) have been observed even within therapeutic ranges, underscoring the need for continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring. In our IV group, median early colostrum levels were 0.23 ng/mL, declining to 0.02 ng/mL by 24 h. The cumulative theoretical neonatal dose over 24 h was 0.0078 μg (Table 4). This dose is more than 2000‐fold lower than the putative respiratory depression threshold dose (5 μg/kg of a 3.5 kg infant vs. 0.002 μg/kg via colostrum) in our study. However, it should be considered that when fentanyl is administered to the mother during labor, infant exposure via transplacental passage comes in addition to the dose via colostrum.

Studies have shown minimal plasma concentrations levels of fentanyl when EA is used for labor analgesia. 32 , 33 Desprats et al. 34 examined epidural administration of 100 μg fentanyl for elective cesarean section and reported mean umbilical arterial and venous concentrations of 0.06 ± 0.03 ng/mL and 0.13 ± 0.04 ng/mL, respectively, which is comparable to the median concentrations of approximately 0.09 ng/mL in both the umbilical artery and the vein in our IV group (Table 2). This similarity likely reflects the relatively low total fentanyl doses used in our study, the short maternal–fetal equilibration time of fentanyl, and its high lipophilicity, which allows rapid placental distribution.

In summary, we encourage intravenous fentanyl administered by midwives who closely monitor labor to be an available option to epidural administration for laboring women. 35 This midwife‐led model allows individualized titration and continuous assessment within established safety limits for the mother and newborn in addition to increasing the possibility of positive childbirth experience. 36 Fentanyl for labor pain management is found to be effective even at low doses and when administered through different routes such as intravenous and subcutaneous administration. 35 , 37 Importantly, we acknowledge the need for larger and preferably randomized studies to evaluate potential long‐term neonatal outcomes and to more precisely assess maternal satisfaction regarding the analgesic efficacy of intravenous fentanyl during labor.

CONCLUSION

Intravenous fentanyl for labor analgesia was associated with low to negligible levels of fentanyl concentrations in maternal plasma, umbilical cord plasma and colostrum. A strict clinical guideline appears to be a reassuring alternative for the mother and neonate. We advocate for changes in clinical practice to ensure that more laboring women could have access to intravenous fentanyl as an additional analgesic option, thereby expanding the range of pain relief methods available during childbirth.
