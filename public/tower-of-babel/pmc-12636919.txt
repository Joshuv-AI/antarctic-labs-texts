Feasibility of an AI-assisted transcranial duplex sonography protocol for early detection of intracerebral haemorrhage: the HYPER-AI-SCAN single-centre prospective study
Authors: Renato Simonetti, Pere Canals, Jesus David Gonzalez Riveros, Manuel Alanís-Bernal, Olalla Pancorbo, David Rodriguez-Luna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) is associated with high early mortality and morbidity. Early clinical deterioration is common and influenced by haematoma expansion, which can occur within the first hours after symptom onset. Transcranial duplex sonography (TCD) is a rapid, non-invasive tool that may aid in early ICH detection but is highly operator-dependent. Introduction

Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) represents one of the most severe forms of stroke, with a 30-day mortality rate exceeding 40% and limited effective therapeutic options.1 One key challenge in ICH management is the dynamic nature of haematoma expansion, which often occurs within the first hours of symptom onset. Approximately 20% of patients experience clinical deterioration between prehospital and emergency department evaluations, with another 20% worsening in the early in-hospital phase.1

Timely blood pressure control and anticoagulation reversal remain the cornerstone of medical interventions in the hyperacute phase of ICH. The INTERACT-4 trial recently investigated the impact of early blood pressure management in patients with suspected stroke in the prehospital setting. In this multicentre, randomised study, patients were eligible if they presented within 2 hours of symptom onset with a motor deficit and systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 150 mm Hg. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either immediate SBP-lowering treatment (target: 130–140 mm Hg) or usual-care management, with intervention initiated directly by paramedics in the ambulance.

While the trial did not show overall benefit in the full cohort of suspected stroke patients, a pre-specified subgroup analysis demonstrated that early blood pressure reduction was significantly associated with improved outcomes in patients with confirmed ICH (common OR 0.75; 95% CI 0.60 to 0.92).2 3 These findings underscore the importance of identifying ICH as early as possible—ideally in the prehospital phase—to tailor timely therapeutic interventions. Yet, in the absence of neuroimaging, clinicians currently cannot distinguish ICH from ischaemic stroke or stroke mimics before hospital arrival.

At present, diagnosis of ICH relies almost exclusively on CT, which is often unavailable in prehospital settings or in many resource-limited environments. Delays in hospital arrival are common worldwide and are influenced by modifiable factors such as limited symptom awareness, delayed activation of emergency services or initial contact with general practitioners. These elements collectively prolong time to diagnosis and reduce the likelihood of early therapeutic interventions.4 5 Additionally, in stroke systems using the ‘mothership’ model, where patients from geographically remote areas are directly transferred to comprehensive stroke centres in the presence of severe symptoms possibly related to large vessel occlusion (LVO) ischaemic stroke, onset-to-CT times may be significantly prolonged, adding further delays to critical ICH early management.5 6

Transcranial duplex sonography (TCD) has demonstrated the ability to detect supratentorial haematomas—particularly in deep brain regions such as the basal ganglia and thalamus.7 These locations are especially relevant as they are both frequently affected in hypertensive ICH and better visualised through the transtemporal acoustic window.8 Chronic hypertension predisposes to rupture of small perforating arteries supplying these regions, making deep bleeds not only common but also pathophysiologically predictable in the acute setting.

Despite its diagnostic potential, TCD has historically seen limited clinical adoption due to high operator dependency and variability in acquisition and interpretation.9 However, recent technological innovations have led to the development of portable and pocket-sized ultrasound devices, which are compact, battery-powered and capable of producing high-resolution images. These systems can now be deployed in diverse environments, including ambulances, rural clinics and emergency departments without CT access. Their increasing affordability and user-friendliness expand the potential for point-of-care neuroimaging, particularly when paired with AI-based support systems. This could enable early haemorrhage detection even by non-specialist personnel, thereby accelerating time-critical interventions such as blood pressure control or anticoagulant reversal.

Furthermore, recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI)—particularly deep learning applied to medical imaging—have shown strong diagnostic performance in ultrasound across multiple domains such as breast, prostate and cardiac imaging.10,12

To date, no prospective study has evaluated an AI-driven TCD approach for the early detection of ICH. The integration of AI with portable TCD devices may offer a scalable and accessible tool for hyperacute haemorrhage triage and possibly treatment in settings lacking immediate neuroimaging access.

Objectives

The primary objective of the HYPER-AI-SCAN study is to evaluate the feasibility of a standardised, AI-assisted TCD protocol for the early identification of ICH in patients with suspected acute stroke.

This initial phase of the project focuses on verifying whether high-quality TCD images can be systematically acquired across a representative patient sample using a portable ultrasound device, within timeframes compatible with hyperacute stroke care. The study also aims to assess whether these images can be successfully annotated and processed for subsequent AI model development.

The long-term goal of the HYPER-AI-SCAN project is to develop and validate an AI-powered bedside tool capable of detecting ICH in the prehospital or emergency setting.

By improving early ICH recognition at the bedside, this protocol aims to (1) Reduce the time from symptom onset to initiation of blood pressure lowering therapy and (2) Enable earlier administration of anticoagulant reversal agents when appropriate, and ultimately improve outcomes by minimising haematoma expansion and preventing early neurological deterioration.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

HYPER-AI-SCAN is a single-centre, prospective, observational feasibility study conducted at the Stroke Unit of Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron, a tertiary care academic hospital and comprehensive stroke centre in Barcelona, Spain.

Participants and eligibility criteria

A total of 500 patients will be prospectively enrolled, maintaining a 1:4 haemorrhagic-to-ischaemic stroke ratio, reflecting epidemiological prevalence in the general population. Inclusion criteria include adult patients (≥18 years) presenting with acute stroke confirmed by CT within 48 hours of symptom onset. Informed consent will be obtained from the patient or legal representative. Exclusion criteria include infratentorial haemorrhage and clinical instability precluding safe TCD examination.

Pilot phase (n=100 patients)

The pilot phase (September 2025 to January 2026) will include 100 consecutive patients meeting the inclusion criteria. The primary objective of this phase is to evaluate the feasibility and reproducibility of the standardised TCD acquisition protocol in the hyperacute stroke setting. Specific aims include verifying systematic image acquisition across a representative patient cohort, refining preprocessing pipelines and curating a high-quality dataset for subsequent AI model development. To ensure methodological rigour, all pilot scans will undergo blinded review by an independent expert neurosonologist. The haemorrhagic-to-ischaemic stroke ratio will be preserved at approximately 1:4, consistent with the epidemiological prevalence and the overall study design. No diagnostic performance metrics will be reported at this stage, as the emphasis is on technical validation and workflow optimisation.

Definitive phase (n=400 patients)

Following the pilot phase, an additional 400 patients will be enrolled in a planned period of 16–18 months, for a total planned cohort of 500 participants. The definitive phase aims to develop and validate the AI model for early ICH detection using the quality-assured dataset generated in the pilot phase. Methodological consistency will be maintained by adhering to the same standardised acquisition protocol and operator training procedures. The AI pipeline will be applied to this expanded cohort to evaluate diagnostic performance, including sensitivity, specificity, Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) and F1-score. Subgroup analyses will further examine the robustness of the model across different haemorrhage locations, haematoma volumes and operator variability.

Data collection and variables

Clinical, haemodynamic and imaging data will be prospectively collected for each patient, including demographic characteristics, stroke risk factors and baseline neurological status. Particular emphasis will be placed on temporal parameters relevant to the feasibility of the workflow in acute care settings. Specifically, two key intervals will be recorded: (1) The time from symptom onset to TCD acquisition and (2) The duration of the TCD examination itself, measured in seconds, to reflect real-world procedural speed and practicality in time-sensitive environments such as prehospital care or emergency triage.

Given that blood remains hyperechoic for up to 48 hours following ICH onset, with gradual loss of echogenicity thereafter,7 all ICH cases included in the study will fall within this window. For additional granularity, each TCD acquisition will be categorised based on whether it occurred within 24 hours or between 24 and 48 hours from symptom onset. This temporal stratification will allow for the assessment of potential subtle differences in image quality or haemorrhage detectability across early and late hyperacute phases.

All ICH patients will undergo standard neuroimaging with non-contrast CT and CT angiography, which will serve as the diagnostic gold standard for the identification, localisation and volumetric characterisation of ICH. These imaging modalities will also be used to label TCD images for training and validation of the AI model. All clinical and imaging data will be securely stored in anonymised, coded form and managed in accordance with applicable data protection regulations.

TCDs will be performed using a Clarius PA HD3 portable ultrasound device (frequency range: 1–5 MHz), following a standardised acquisition protocol.13 The examination will begin at the mesencephalic plane, obtained through the transtemporal acoustic window. As part of ongoing quality control, in the pilot phase (n=100 patients), all scans will undergo blinded review by an independent expert neurosonologist (DRL) to ensure methodological rigour and dataset reliability. For the definitive phase, a random sample (~15%) will continue to be reviewed to monitor quality consistency across the study.

AI model development

The central objective of this study is to train an AI model capable of identifying ICH from TCD images.

To ensure a robust and scalable approach, we will use a model-agnostic pipeline with fixed data handling and flexible backbones. All TCD sweeps are de-identified; on-screen overlays are removed by cropping, followed by intensity normalisation and resizing to each encoder’s native input. Considering video AI models require frame sampling rather than using the complete sequences according to the applications, and to standardise visual content and align with acquisition, we will try different frame sampling strategies, avoiding duplicated views, matching the way the scan is acquired, and making the method easier to understand and reproduce.

On the modelling side, we will fine-tune ultrasound foundation models already trained on large ultrasound collections and also evaluate general video encoders (eg, transformer-based or 3D-CNN–based) as frozen feature extractors with a lightweight classifier; if sample size permits, we will explore compact 3D models trained from scratch. Class imbalance will be handled with weighted binary cross-entropy (BCE) and/or minority oversampling.

Training and validation are patient-level using fivefold Stratified Group Fold, stratified by ICH/no-ICH, with all hyperparameter tuning confined to the training folds. The primary endpoint is per-patient sensitivity at a prespecified operating point; we will also report specificity, AUROC and F1-score. Since this is a feasibility study, the acquisition protocol is fixed, while the choice of backbone remains flexible within this pre-specified pipeline so that methodological rigour is preserved even as architectures evolve.

Sample size justification

To define an appropriate sample size, we reviewed comparable deep learning studies in ultrasound imaging, which suggest that reliable performance can often be achieved with 300 to 2000 annotated cases.14,16 Based on this and the expected 1:4 prevalence of haemorrhagic versus ischaemic strokes, we aim to include 500 patients, providing approximately 100 ICH cases. This will allow for a balanced training-validation strategy and support the evaluation of the model’s feasibility in a real-world setting.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis will serve two primary purposes: to describe and characterise the clinical cohort, and to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the AI model in identifying ICH from TCD images.

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise demographic, clinical, haemodynamic and imaging variables. Model performance will be assessed using a fivefold cross-validation approach and independent test set evaluation. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, F1-score and AUC-ROC will be reported, and CIs calculated when appropriate. Analyses will be conducted using known and validated statistics elaboration software (such as R 4.5.2, SPSS 30.0.0).

The design, conduct and reporting of this study will adhere to established methodological standards to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Specifically, the study will follow the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies guidelines, given its primary aim of evaluating the diagnostic performance of an AI-assisted protocol for ICH detection. In addition, general principles from the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines will be applied, as the project follows a prospective observational design.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has received approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron (code: PR(AG)015/2025). All participants, or their legal representatives when applicable, will provide written informed consent prior to enrolment.

Patient data will be collected and stored using a coded data structure, ensuring that no identifiable information is accessible to the investigators. The coding process will be carried out by members of the research team in accordance with local data protection policies. All data will be stored on secure hospital servers with restricted access and used exclusively for research purposes within this project.

As this is a non-interventional study based on standard diagnostic procedures, no modifications to clinical care will occur. TCD will be performed only after CT confirmation of diagnosis, and the acquisition will not interfere with emergency workflows.

Findings from this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed scientific journals and presented at relevant national and international conferences. In the longer term, the results may contribute to the development of real-time AI-assisted diagnostic tools for use in prehospital or bedside stroke assessment.

Patient and public Involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. The study focuses on technical feasibility and data-driven model development in the acute care setting.

Trial registration

This study is not a randomised clinical trial and does not involve the administration of therapeutic interventions. Therefore, trial registration is not mandatory.
