Effects of size and atomic defects on the buckling of single-layer and double-layer graphene nanoplates using AFEM
Authors: Karim Aliakbari, Akbar Jafari, Mina Amiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effect of single atomic vacancy defects with different random distributions on the buckling behavior of single- and double-layer graphene nanoplates. A parametric analysis was conducted to assess the influence of nanoplate dimensions, aspect ratio, defect concentration, number of layers, and boundary conditions on the critical buckling load (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{cr}$$\end{document}). To capture the discrete nature of graphene, the nanoplates were modeled using a space frame approach, where covalent bonds between carbon atoms were represented as beam elements and interlayer van der Waals forces were simulated using linear elastic springs. The atomic positions were generated using MATLAB and imported into ANSYS, where neighboring atoms were connected based on an energy-equivalent mapping between interatomic and elastic beam energies. Springs were added between atoms located within a defined cutoff distance to represent long-range interlayer interactions. A linear finite element buckling analysis was performed by solving an eigenvalue problem using beam and spring elements with linear elastic behavior. Model validation was achieved by comparing simulation results to data available in the literature. The results show that vacancy defects significantly reduce \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{cr}$$\end{document}—up to 40% at 10% defect concentration. Furthermore, three distinct distributions of defects were examined for each concentration to explore spatial effects on buckling resistance.

Introduction

Recent advances in the study of nanoplates, which feature two-dimensional structures at the nanoscale, have attracted growing interest. Single-layer and multilayer graphene sheets are among the most important nanostructures due to their extraordinary properties and broad range of potential applications1–4. Graphene, recognized as the thinnest and strongest material known, has become a focal point due to its outstanding mechanical, electrical and thermal characteristics. The unique properties of graphene positions it as promising candidate for numerous applications in fields such as electromechanical devices5, catalytic oxidation6, nanosensors7–10, nanoactuators11, coatings and shields with electromagnetic microwave absorption12,13, adsorptive materials for environmental remediation14, transistors15, solar energy systems16, lithium-ion batteries17, and biomedical technologies18. As another application case, graphene has been incorporated to reinforce magnetorheological elastomer nanocomposite19–22. Owing to its transformative potential across the electronics, energy storage, and healthcare sectors, this nanostructure has become the subject of rigorous theoretical and experimental investigation.

In the above, some applications of the graphene have been reviewed, and to have a better view on the literature review of the current research, the rest of the reviewed references are categorized and presented here in terms of their methodology including: Molecular/atomic-based methods such as molecular dynamics (MD), Continuum-based methods and atomic finite element method (AFEM).

The second category refers to molecular dynamics (MD) as an approach that has been employed by many researchers to analyze small scale behavior of different materials including graphene sheets23. Recently, Wang and Zhang used MD simulations to evaluate the Young’s modulus and fracture toughness of bilayer graphene, showing that temperature has a minimal effect on the elastic modulus but plays a key role in determining fracture toughness24. In another study, Wang et al. investigated the failure of imperfect graphene with two types of defects: Stone-Wales (S-W) defects and vacancies25. Fadaei Heydari et al.26 explored the impact of graphene oxide nanoparticles (GO-NP) on the mechanical and thermal properties of PU/PCL nanocomposites using MD simulations. They found that a 2% GO-NP concentration provided the best balance, enhancing thermal conductivity, heat flux, and mechanical strength without adverse effects on the other material properties.

Another category of research pertains to the continuum-based approaches in which the elasticity theories incorporated with the beam or plate theories. Behfar and Naghdabadi utilized this method to examine the vibration characteristics of multilayer graphene sheets embedded in an elastic medium27. As another study, they applied an analytical approach to predict the flexural modulus of multilayer graphene sheets28. Kitipornchai et al.29 developed a continuum-plate model to analyze multilayer graphene sheet vibrations. Jafari et al.30 explored the free vibration behavior of nanoplates using classical and various non-classical theories, including first-order strain gradient, second-order strain gradient, non-local elasticity, and implicit gradient. They employed the Navier and Galerkin methods to numerically solve the governing partial differential equations and obtained parametric responses for natural frequencies, assessing the impact of parameters such as classical and nonlocal properties, boundary conditions, and plate dimensions. Namin and Pilafkan31 applied nonlocal elasticity theory to analyze the vibrational behavior of defective graphene sheets, demonstrating that defects within the atomic structure reduce the natural frequencies of these sheets. Pradhan32 employed nonlocal elasticity theory to examine the influence of nanoscale effects on the critical buckling load of single-layer graphene, revealing that buckling capacity increased with nonlocal parameter values and decreased with increasing graphene sheet length. Samaei et al.33 utilized nonlocal Mindlin plate theory to model single-layer graphene sheets, assessing their buckling behavior in an elastic medium. Zhang et al.34 applied the element-free Kp-Ritz method and non-local elasticity theory to buckling analysis of single-layer graphene sheet, emphasizing the influence of nonlocal parameters and surrounding elastic support on critical buckling capacity. Omar et al.35 investigated the static stability and buckling behavior of functionally graded porous nanoplates embedded in elastic media, incorporating nonlocal strain gradient theory for size effects. Based on that study, the nanoplate’s critical buckling load is influenced by parameters such as porosity distribution, boundary conditions and the stiffness of the elastic environment, offering valuable insights into nanoplate stability under different loading conditions. Arpanahi et al.36 studied the buckling behavior of a nanoplate subjected to fluid flow, using Navier-Stokes equations and surface effects theory. Their findings highlighted how fluid viscosity, flow velocity, and surface energy influence the critical buckling load of nanoplates. Farajpour et al.37 studied the buckling behavior of graphene sheets with various thicknesses, showing that the buckling characteristics of a single-layer graphene sheet are highly sensitive to the nonlocal parameter.

Understanding probable effects of atomic scale issues such as defects is necessary in applied viewpoint as allows prediction of their behavior and proper designing based on the realistic conditions. Regarding theoretical studying of such matters, molecular dynamics methods is a good approach however, it is computationally expensive and takes long time for simulations. On the other hand, continuum-based approaches cannot fully capture atomic-scale phenomena, such as atomic vacancies. Therefore, a methodology that has advantages of atomic-based approaches but less computational cost and time can be a favorable tactic. In this regard, a combination of atomic-based approaches and FEM has been developed that is called atomic finite element method (AFEM) in the open literature16. In this strategy, the potential functions defined in molecular mechanics are employed to obtain the physical characteristics of the elements required in FEM. Related references are put in another category and as will be reviewed hereafter, has been applied in different aspects such as elastic deformation, buckling, free and forced vibration. Liu et al.39 applied AFEM to analyze the mechanical behavior of single-walled carbon nanotubes40. The authors of the current work have previously utilized this approach to study the elastic response behavior of graphene sheets and determination of its elastic coefficients41. Gu et al. employed AFEM to investigate local buckling in bent single-walled carbon nanotube42. Sakhaee-Pour43 conducted elastic buckling analysis and found a nonlinear relationship between buckling force and sheet width while observing no significant effect from the aspect ratio. Rouhi and Ansari, using AFEM, studied the influence of boundary conditions and the aspect ratio on buckling of single-layer graphene sheets. That study revealed that increasing the aspect ratio causes reduction of the buckling load44. Giannopoulos45 examined the critical buckling load of graphene nanoribbons, revealing that chirality generally affects the critical load, which increases almost linearly. Additionally, as the length of the nanoribbon increases, the critical buckling load decreases significantly in a nonlinear trend. Firouz abadi et al.46 explored the critical buckling load of graphene sheets through molecular structural mechanics, molecular dynamics, and finite element models under different boundary conditions. Jafari et al.47 studied the mechanical and piezoelectric properties of boron nitride nanotubes via refined AFEM as incorporating piezoelectric effect and concentrated spring elements. They observed that as the nanotube diameter increased, mechanical properties stabilized, with Young’s modulus converging from approximately 0.7 TPa at smaller diameters to about 1.1 TPa at larger diameters.

In summary, research into graphene buckling behavior remains essential, particularly regarding the impact of defects on structural stability. While various studies have examined single-layer and multi-layer graphene sheets under different conditions, limited work has comprehensively addressed impact of atomic defects on the buckling performance. This study aims to develop an enhanced AFEM model to analyze single-layer and double-layer graphene buckling in both defect-free and defective forms. The key innovation of this research is its exploration of how parameters such as the number of layers, size, aspect ratio, defect concentration and boundary conditions influence the buckling behavior—factors that have received limited attention in previous studies.

Governing equations and buckling analysis

Graphene, a two-dimensional nanomaterial, consists of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice via covalent bonding. These bonds exhibit specific lengths and angles in three-dimensional space. When an external force is applied to a graphene nanoplate, atomic displacements are governed by these bonds, leading to an overall deformation dictated by interatomic interactions. Within this modeling framework, interatomic forces are represented as load-bearing beam elements, while atoms serve as the nodes of the structural system. (Fig. 1) illustrates the unit cell of graphene nanoplates, where each covalent bond is modeled as a structural component within the space frame, and single vacancy defects are introduced by removing one atom from selected lattice sites.Fig. 1Typical space-frame model of graphene nanoplates with single vacancy defects.

Typical space-frame model of graphene nanoplates with single vacancy defects.

Beam elements can effectively represent covalent bonds between carbon atoms. By making an analogy between beams in structural mechanics and atomic bonds in molecular mechanics, a relationship is formulated between beam stiffness parameters and potential function constants. This correlation enables the derivation of beam element properties, allowing for an accurate simulation of the mechanical behavior of graphene and other nanostructures.

The potential energy of a carbon nanostructure is mainly determined by strong covalent bonds within each individual layer and weak van der Waals forces acting between layers. The effect of long-range electrostatic forces is negligible. In this study, beam-link elements are used to represent the covalent bonds between carbon atoms (Fig. 1), while mechanical springs are employed to model the interlayer van der Waals interactions, as shown in (Fig. 2). It is important to mention that the range of long-range forces follows a finite potential function and is taken as 2.5σ, based on the findings in Ref48. As depicted in (Fig. 2), although an atom is located near vacancies, it remains constrained through connections to several atoms of the adjacent layer.Fig. 2Mechanical springs as equivalent of the out-of-plane van der Waals bonds between atoms of the layers of the two‑layer graphene nanoplate, with limitation of cutoff distance.

Conducting FEM simulations

As aforementioned, the covalent bonds within the graphene structure were modeled using the three-dimensional beam elastic element. Each node in this model possesses three translational degrees of freedom along x, y, and z axes, as well as three rotational degrees of freedom around these axes. Additionally, interlayer springs were incorporated to represent van der Waals interactions between adjacent layers. These interactions were considered only when the interatomic distance was equal to or less than the cutoff radius, denoted by σ (the Lennard-Jones parameter)44,48. Beyond this threshold, no interaction was assumed between atoms. Once the springs were defined between atomic sites, their stiffness values were assigned based on an equivalence principle linking the elastic energy of the springs to the chemical bond energy.

To conduct the numerical simulations, a combination of ANSYS and MATLAB is employed. Regarding FEM model generation, BEAM4 as a 2-node 3D beam element is used between the atoms of each layer52. On the other hand, spring element COMBIN14 was used to create the van der Waals interaction between the layers52. To facilitate the modeling process, required MATLAB codes were developed to create the model geometry and the data then imported into ANSYS for applying boundary conditions and performing the solution. Moreover, a MATLAB script was developed to generate a command code, that is subsequently executed within ANSYS. The model represents a two-dimensional structure with length a and width b and a given thickness t, as shown in (Fig. 4) along with the coordinate system used. The modeling process begins by defining points that correspond to carbon atoms, followed by connecting these points with lines representing covalent bonds. To construct the planar structure, a single unit cell is generated and replicated along X- and Y- global axes, forming a complete two-dimensional lattice.Fig. 4Applying fixed constrains under simply supported and clamped boundary conditions in buckling analysis pf the graphene using FEM.

Applying fixed constrains under simply supported and clamped boundary conditions in buckling analysis pf the graphene using FEM.

Defective nanoplate

To investigate the impact of defects on the buckling behavior of the nanoplate, adequate simulations were conducted with different concentrations of vacancies defects. It should be noted that the studies were done on single- and double-layer graphene with b=30 Å and b=70 Å with various values of the length, a, meaning various aspect ratios, a/b under different BCs. For each case, three different random distributions of vacancies were simulated and their average values were also calculated and reported as the result of that case.

Effects of the defect’s distribution

As a matter of fact, the vacancies can be placed at different positions and probably affecting the buckling load. The numerical results revealed that for a given percentage of atomic vacancy defects, the critical buckling load exhibits a certain degree of sensitivity to the random distribution of these defects. As examples, for two cases, ranges of the buckling load obtained for different random defects distributions are given in (Fig. 8). It is seen that the results belong to different defects distributions are not identical as some variances are exhibited between them. It seems that the variances range for the smaller graphene (b=30Å) is greater than the variance range happened for the wider graphene (b=70Å) however, no meaningful pattern was observed for the range of the variances belonged to different sizes and boundary conditions.Fig. 8The average and variances of the buckling load obtained for different defect random distribution for two specific problem cases.

The average and variances of the buckling load obtained for different defect random distribution for two specific problem cases.

It should be emphasized that the positions of the atomic vacancies are distributed randomly. The program selects defect positions at random, ensuring no two defects are immediate neighbors. If a randomly picked atom has adjacent neighbors that were already selected, the choice is rejected and replaced by a new random selection. Effects of the defect’s concentration

The most important factor that is under study in the current research is the impact of the defect’s concentration on the buckling capacity of the nanoplate. To have a clear sense about this matter, the following dimensionless parameter, called buckling degradation factor, BDF, is defined. In this expression, the numerator represents the reduction in the critical buckling load due to the presence of the defects, relative to the ideal (perfect) nanoplate, while the denominator denotes the critical buckling load of the perfect type. Conclusion

One of the strengths and novelties of the present study is that the effects of various parameters on the buckling behavior of graphene have been investigated simultaneously. This approach makes it possible to examine the interaction effects among different factors. Such a comprehensive study has not been observed in previously published research. In this regard, buckling behavior of single- and double-layer rectangular nanoplates was analyzed under different types of boundary conditions, different sizes and different aspect ratios. In addition, effect of the vacancy defects was also studied.
