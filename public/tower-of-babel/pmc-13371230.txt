Ignavigranum ruoffiae isolated from a scrotal abscess: a case report and literature review
Authors: Kaixuan Zhang, Sudi Zhu, Mengyu Zhang, Huihui Li, Yuanyuan Xu, Pingping Zhao, Zhikang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIgnavigranum ruoffiae is a rare catalase-negative, facultatively anaerobic Gram-positive coccus. Published human infections remain exceptionally uncommon and have mainly involved localized suppurative disease outside the genitourinary tract. Scrotal involvement has not, to our knowledge, been described previously in the available literature.Case reportA 63-year-old man presented with a 2-day history of progressive painful swelling of the left scrotum and inguinal region. Systemic inflammatory findings were not striking on admission, with a white blood cell count of 9.27 × 10^9/L and a procalcitonin level of 0.08 ng/mL. Urinalysis was unremarkable, and urine culture showed no bacterial growth. Scrotal ultrasonography demonstrated a 21 × 12 × 18 mm heterogeneous lesion in the left inguinal-scrotal region, with peripheral strip-like blood-flow signals and no documented internal vascularity. Empirical intravenous levofloxacin was started after admission. On hospital day 2, the patient developed a transient fever of 38.0 °C, and the lesion ruptured spontaneously. Needle aspiration drainage yielded approximately 5 mL of gray-white pus. Culture of aspirated pus showed pure growth of small gray-white colonies after 48 h under aerobic conditions with 5% CO2; anaerobic culture recovered no additional anaerobic organisms. The isolate was identified as I. ruoffiae by MALDI-TOF MS using a Bruker microflex LT/SH system with Biotyper software version 4.1.100 and database Revision H2022, which had been locally expanded in the laboratory; the identification score was 2.40. The isolate was further confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. MICs were generated using the VITEK 2 system with an AST639 card. Quality control was performed using Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213. On hospital day 5, treatment was changed to intravenous amoxicillin/clavulanate (1.2 g every 8 h). The patient improved steadily thereafter. By discharge on hospital day 13, scrotal swelling had resolved, the wound bed showed healthy granulation tissue, and no purulent discharge remained. At 1-month and 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no clinical evidence of recurrence.ConclusionsThis case documents the isolation of I. ruoffiae from a scrotal abscess, an anatomical site not previously reported in the available literature. Because the specimen was obtained from a single post-rupture abscess after empirical antimicrobial exposure, the organism should be interpreted as a putative pathogen rather than as definitive proof of sole causation. The case also shows that localized suppurative infection may occur despite minimal systemic inflammatory response. Early clinical evolution before drainage should not be interpreted as definitive antimicrobial failure, and the clinical course emphasizes the importance of source control and careful microbiological recognition.

Introduction

Ignavigranum ruoffiae (I. ruoffiae) is a rare catalase-negative, facultatively anaerobic Gram-positive coccus within the family Aerococcaceae. The species was first described in 1999 from human clinical specimens, and only a small number of clinically relevant human isolates have since been reported [1, 2]. Most published infections have involved localized suppurative disease, including wound infection, ear abscess, skin abscess, and an infected breast cyst [3].

Recognition of this organism in routine practice remains limited. Rare catalase-negative Gram-positive cocci, particularly aerococci-like organisms, may be misidentified when laboratories rely only on conventional phenotypic methods [4]. MALDI-TOF MS has improved the recognition of unusual clinical isolates, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing remains valuable when uncommon pathogens are recovered from clinically significant specimens [5–7].

A focused literature search was performed in PubMed and Google Scholar up to March 2026 using the terms “Ignavigranum ruoffiae” and “Ignavigranum”. Scrotal involvement associated with I. ruoffiae has not, to our knowledge, been reported previously in the available literature [2]. This report describes a 16S rRNA-confirmed scrotal abscess from which I. ruoffiae was isolated and places the case in the context of the limited published literature.

Case report

A 63-year-old man was admitted to the Department of Urology on 26 December 2025 with a 2-day history of progressive pain and swelling involving the left scrotum and inguinal region. He denied preceding trauma. His medical history included hypertension for more than 10 years, severe traumatic brain injury in September 2024, decompressive craniectomy, and subsequent cranioplasty in August 2025. He had no known diabetes mellitus, malignancy, chemotherapy, long-term corticosteroid use, or other immunosuppressive therapy, and HIV testing was negative. The patient reported oral-genital sexual exposure before symptom onset and had poor oral condition, including tooth loss, yellowish dental discoloration, and recurrent gingival bleeding; no recent dental procedure, recent intubation, or oral swab culture was documented.

At presentation, physical examination showed a tender erythematous swelling extending from the left scrotum toward the inguinal region. The overlying skin was intact, and fluctuation was equivocal, suggesting either a tense abscess cavity or an inflammatory phlegmon-like stage. The patient was afebrile on admission.

Initial laboratory testing showed a white blood cell count of 9.27 × 10^9/L and a procalcitonin level of 0.08 ng/mL. These findings did not indicate a marked systemic inflammatory response. Urinalysis was unremarkable, and urine culture showed no bacterial growth. Blood cultures were not obtained. Scrotal ultrasonography on admission showed that both testes were normal in size and morphology, with preserved intratesticular blood flow. The left testis showed focal calcification. Both epididymides appeared enlarged and heterogeneous, with multiple punctate echoes, while epididymal blood flow was not markedly increased. No hydrocele or spermatic cord abnormality was detected. An irregular hypoechoic lesion measuring approximately 21 × 12 × 18 mm was identified in the relatively deep left inguinal-scrotal region, with a clear boundary and peripheral strip-like blood-flow signals on color Doppler imaging. Definite internal vascularity within the lesion was not documented. These findings were considered compatible with localized inflammatory change in the left inguinal-scrotal region (Fig. 1).

Empirical intravenous levofloxacin (0.5 g once daily) was initiated after admission. On hospital day 2, the patient developed a transient fever of 38.0 °C, and the lesion ruptured spontaneously with discharge of gray-white purulent material. Needle aspiration drainage was then performed through the rupture site, yielding approximately 5 mL of gray-white pus. Local pain improved after drainage, and the patient became afebrile thereafter.

The aspirated pus was inoculated onto 5% sheep blood agar and incubated at 35 °C in an aerobic atmosphere with 5% CO2. Small gray-white colonies became visible after 48 h, and the culture showed pure growth. Anaerobic culture was performed under routine anaerobic conditions at 35 °C, and no additional anaerobic organisms were recovered. Colony Gram staining demonstrated Gram-positive coccoid organisms arranged in pairs and short chains (Fig. 2). Species identification was performed from 48-h colonies using a Bruker Daltonics microflex LT/SH MALDI-TOF MS system with Biotyper software version 4.1.100 and database Revision H2022, which had been locally expanded in the laboratory; the identification score was 2.40. Species identification was further confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing using GenBank reference sequence CP096206.2. Detailed microbiological identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing results are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

MICs were generated using the VITEK 2 system with an AST639 card according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and disk diffusion zone diameters for selected agents were obtained using the Kirby–Bauer method. Quality control was performed using Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213. EUCAST was considered; however, species-specific EUCAST breakpoints for I. ruoffiae are not available. Given the taxonomic placement of I. ruoffiae within Aerococcaceae, CLSI M45 breakpoints for Aerococcus spp. were used as the closest available surrogate criteria where applicable. Because VITEK 2-derived MICs have not been specifically validated for this rare organism, the results were interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with the clinical context. The isolate showed low MICs for penicillin G, ampicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, and ceftriaxone. Levofloxacin also demonstrated in vitro activity.

Although levofloxacin showed in vitro activity against the isolate, fever and spontaneous rupture occurred during the early treatment phase before effective source control had been established. This should not be interpreted as definitive antibiotic failure, because rupture may have reflected the natural evolution of a mature abscess. On hospital day 5, antimicrobial therapy was changed to intravenous amoxicillin/clavulanate (1.2 g every 8 h). This change was a pragmatic clinical adjustment based on the susceptibility profile, the beta-lactam activity observed in vitro, and the clinical preference for beta-lactam therapy in a localized suppurative infection after drainage, rather than proof of fluoroquinolone inferiority. The patient improved steadily after drainage and beta-lactam therapy. By discharge on hospital day 13, the scrotal swelling had resolved, the wound bed showed healthy granulation tissue, and no purulent discharge remained. Oral amoxicillin/clavulanate (875 mg/125 mg, twice daily) was prescribed at discharge.

At the 1-month and 3-month outpatient follow-up visits, the patient remained asymptomatic, and no clinical evidence of recurrence was observed. The overall clinical course is summarized in Fig. 3.

Discussion

This case extends the limited clinical spectrum of I. ruoffiae by documenting its recovery from a scrotal abscess. Previously published human reports have involved wound infection, ear abscess, skin abscess, and an infected breast cyst [1–4]. Based on the focused search described above, scrotal involvement has not been described in the available literature. This observation is clinically relevant because rare catalase-negative Gram-positive cocci recovered from non-sterile or superficial sites can be overlooked or dismissed as contaminants. In the present case, the organism was recovered as pure growth from purulent material and was identified by MALDI-TOF MS and confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Even so, the finding should remain framed cautiously as isolation of a putative pathogen from a clinically significant abscess rather than definitive proof that I. ruoffiae was the sole causative agent.

Comparison with previous reports helps place the present case in context (Table 3). Despite differences in anatomical site, the reported cases share several features: localized purulent disease, the need for careful laboratory identification, and favorable outcomes when antimicrobial therapy was combined with drainage or other source-control measures when required [8]. The present lesion was located relatively deeply in the inguinal-scrotal region, which made early bedside recognition less straightforward than in superficial abscesses. The absence of marked leukocytosis or procalcitonin elevation also shows that limited systemic inflammatory abnormalities do not exclude clinically relevant localized suppuration.

Antimicrobial susceptibility findings should be interpreted descriptively. Organism-specific susceptibility data for I. ruoffiae remain scarce, and standardized species-specific breakpoints are unavailable. The present isolate showed low MICs for several beta-lactams and susceptibility to levofloxacin in vitro, broadly consistent with the limited published experience [2, 3]. However, these results cannot define an optimal treatment regimen for this species. The use of surrogate criteria and an automated system that has not been validated specifically for this rare organism means that categorical interpretations should remain cautious and clinically contextualized.

The microbiology workflow is central to the interpretation of this case. Recovery of an unusual catalase-negative Gram-positive coccus from purulent material should prompt full identification when the specimen is clinically meaningful, especially when growth is pure or predominant. MALDI-TOF MS can support rapid recognition of uncommon organisms, but database coverage and local validation remain important [9, 10]. Confirmatory 16S rRNA sequencing is therefore useful when a rare species is recovered from an abscess or another clinically significant specimen [5, 11]. This approach reduces the risk that uncommon pathogens are misclassified as contaminants or grouped under imprecise phenotypic labels.

The treatment course should also be interpreted cautiously. Although the isolate was susceptible to levofloxacin in vitro, fever and spontaneous rupture occurred before effective drainage had been established. This sequence does not prove fluoroquinolone failure. It more likely reflects the natural evolution of an abscess before adequate source control. Limited antimicrobial penetration into an undrained cavity, high local bacterial burden, inoculum effects, and altered drug activity within acidic or protein-rich purulent material may all weaken the relationship between in vitro susceptibility and early bedside response [12–14]. The subsequent change to amoxicillin/clavulanate was a pragmatic clinical adjustment after drainage and susceptibility review, not evidence that beta-lactam therapy is superior for I. ruoffiae.

The route of acquisition remains unresolved. A hypothetical illustration summarizing possible routes and the interpretation of clinical response is shown in Fig. 4. The history of oral-genital sexual exposure and poor oral condition makes an oral-associated route biologically plausible, either through direct mucocutaneous transfer or transient bacteremia. However, the absence of visible skin disruption and the relatively deep inguinoscrotal location weaken any confident claim of direct inoculation. Hematogenous seeding of a locally susceptible site is another possible explanation [15]. The ultrasonographic findings of left testicular calcification and bilateral epididymal echogenic heterogeneity may indicate pre-existing local abnormalities, but their relationship to abscess formation cannot be established. Because blood cultures, oral swabs, partner samples, and molecular linkage analysis were not available, the acquisition route should be considered speculative [16].

This report has several limitations. It describes a single patient and cannot establish the prevalence, virulence, transmission route, or optimal treatment of I. ruoffiae infection. The specimen was obtained after spontaneous rupture through the rupture site and after empirical levofloxacin had been started. This sequence introduces the possibility of contamination, selective survival, or suppression of other organisms. Although the culture showed pure growth and anaerobic culture recovered no additional anaerobic organisms, I. ruoffiae should be regarded as a putative pathogen isolated from a clinically significant abscess rather than as definitively proven to be the sole cause. Blood cultures, oral swabs, partner samples, and molecular linkage data were unavailable, so the route of infection cannot be determined. Species identification was strengthened by MALDI-TOF MS and 16S rRNA sequencing, but susceptibility interpretation remains imperfect because species-specific breakpoints are unavailable and VITEK 2-derived MICs have not been validated specifically for this rare organism [17]. The value of the report is therefore descriptive: it documents a rare isolate at an unreported anatomical site and highlights the need for careful laboratory recognition, cautious interpretation of susceptibility data, and source control in localized suppurative infection.

Conclusions

This case describes a previously unreported scrotal abscess from which I. ruoffiae was isolated as a putative pathogen. The finding expands the reported anatomical context of this uncommon organism but should not be interpreted as establishing the organism as the definitive sole cause beyond this individual case. Minimal systemic inflammatory abnormalities did not rule out clinically significant localized suppuration. The clinical course supports careful microbiological identification and timely source control, while antimicrobial susceptibility results should be interpreted cautiously because species-specific breakpoints are unavailable.
