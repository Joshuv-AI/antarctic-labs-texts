An Expressive Trace Logic for Recursive Programs
Dilian Gurov, Reiner Hähnle

Abstract
We present an expressive logic over trace formulas, based on binary state predicates, chop, and least fixed-points, for precise specification of programs with recursive procedures. Both, programs and trace formulas, are equipped with a direct-style, fully compositional, denotational semantics that on programs coincides with the standard SOS of recursive programs. We design a compositional proof calculus for proving finite-trace program properties, and prove soundness as well as (relative) completeness. We show that each program can be mapped to a semantics-preserving trace formula and, vice versa, each trace formula can be mapped to a canonical program over slightly extended programs, resulting in a Galois connection between programs and formulas. Our results shed light on the correspondence between programming constructs and logical connectives.

1
Introduction

It is uncommon that specification languages used in program
verification are as expressive as the programs they are intended to
specify:
In model checking
[
7
]
one typically abstracts away from data
and unwinds unbounded control structures. Specification of unbounded
computations is achieved by recursively defined temporal operators.
In deductive verification
[
14
]
, first-order
Hoare-style contracts
[
17
]
are the basis of widely used
specification languages
[
3
,
22
]
. The latter
specify computation by taking symbolic memory snapshots in terms of
first-order formulas, typically at the beginning and at the end of
unbounded control structures, such as procedure call and return, or
upon loop entry and exit. Contracts permit to approximate the effect
of unbounded computation as a finite number of first-order formulas
against which a program’s behavior can be verified.

Imagine, in contrast, a logic for program specification that is

at least as expressive
as the programs it is supposed to
describe. Formulas
\phi
of such a logic characterize a generally
infinite set of program computation traces
\sigma
. One can then form

judgments
of program statements
S
of the form

S:\phi
with the natural semantics that any possible trace

\sigma
of
S
is one of the traces described by
\phi
.

Two arguments against such a
trace logic
are easily raised:
first, one expects a specification language to be capable of

abstraction
from implementation details; second, its
computational complexity. Regarding the first, we note that any
desired degree of abstraction can be achieved by
definable

constructs, i.e.
syntactic sugar
, in a sufficiently rich trace
logic. Regarding the second, it is far from obvious whether the
computational worst-case tends to manifest itself in
practical

verification
[
14
]
. First experiments seem to
indicate this is not the case.
On the other hand, a rich, trace-based specification logic bears many
advantages:

1.

On the practical side, trace-based specification permits to
express, for example, that an event or a call happened (exactly
once, at least once) or that did not happen. Likewise, one can
specify without reference to the target code (via assertions) that a
condition holds at some point which is not necessarily an endpoint
of a procedure call. It is also possible to specify
inter-procedural properties, such as call sequences, absence of
callbacks, etc. Such properties are important in security analysis
and they are difficult or impossible to express with Hoare-style
contracts or in temporal logic.

2.

The semantics of trace formulas in terms of trace sets can be
closely aligned with a suitable program semantics, which greatly
simplifies soundness and completeness proofs.

3.

An expressive trace logic makes for simple and natural
completeness proofs for judgments
S:\phi
, because it is
unnecessary to encode the program semantics in order to construct
sufficiently strong first-order conditions.

4.

In a calculus for judgments of the form
S:\phi
one
one can design
inference
rules that directly decompose
judgments into simpler ones, but also
algebraic
rules that
simplify and transform
\phi
, and one may mix both reasoning
styles.

5.

The semantics of programs, of trace formulas, and the rules of
the calculus can be formulated in a
fully compositional

manner: definitions and inference rules involve no intermediate
state or context.

6.

In consequence, we are able to establish a close
correspondence between programs and formulas, which sheds light on
the exact relation between each program construct and its
corresponding logical connective. We formulate and prove a Galois
connection that formalizes this fact.

Our main contribution is a trace logic for imperative programs with recursive procedures where
we formalize and prove the claims above.
We restrict attention to the
terminating executions
of
programs, i.e. to their
finite trace semantics
. This is not a
fundamental limitation, but the desire not to obscure the construction
with complications that may be added later.
Still, adaptating the theory to maximal traces (including infinite runs)
is not trivial, and working out the details is left as future work
(see Section
8.3
).

Our paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
we define
the programming language
Rec
used throughout this paper and we give
it a standard SOS semantics
[
29
]
. In
Section
3
we define a denotational semantics for

Rec
in fully compositional style and prove it equivalent to the SOS
semantics. In Section
4
we introduce our trace
logic and map programs to formulas by the concept of a
strongest
trace formula
, which is shown to fully preserve program
semantics. In Section
5
we define a proof
calculus for judgments and show it to be sound and complete. In
Section
6
, using the concept of a

canonical program
, we map trace formulas back to programs in
the slightly extended language
\mbox{\bf Rec}^{\ast}
with non-deterministic
guards. We prove that
\mbox{\bf Rec}^{\ast}
and trace formulas form a Galois
connection via strongest trace formulas and canonical programs. In
Section
7
we discuss related work, in
Section
8
we sketch some extensions, including
options to render specifications more abstract and how to prove
consequence among trace formulas. In Section
9
we
conclude.

Definition 6

3
A Denotational Finite-Trace Semantics for
Rec

We define the semantic function

\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}:\mbox{\bf Stm}\rightarrow 2^{\mbox{\bf State}^{+}}
with the
intention that
\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right]\>=\>\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{sos}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right]
.
Unlike
\mathcal{S}_{sos}
, however,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}
is
defined directly, without referring to other semantic rules as SOS
does (hence the term “direct-style”).
We define
\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right]
in the style of

, compositionally, by induction on the structure of
S
,
and through defining equations.

Let us define a unary

for any trace set

\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\,}c@{\,}l@{}}A\!\!\mid_{b}&\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\left\{s\cdot\sigma\in A\>\middle|\>\mathcal{B}\!\left[\!\left[b\right]\!\right](s)=\mbox{\bf tt}\right\}\end{array}
.
It filters out all traces in

Another unary operator on trace sets is defined as

\sharp A\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\>\left\{s\cdot s\cdot\sigma\>\middle|\>s\cdot\sigma\in A\right\}

which duplicates the first state in each trace in
A
.
Finally, let us define the binary operator on trace sets:

A\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}B\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\>\left\{\sigma_{A}\cdot s\cdot\sigma_{B}\>\middle|\>\sigma_{A}\cdot s\in A\>\wedge\>s\cdot\sigma_{B}\in B\right\}

which concatenates traces from
A
with traces from
B
that agree on
the last and first state, respectively, but without duplicating that
state, see also
[
15
]
.

\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{\qquad}r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{}}\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[\mbox{\bf skip}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\left\{s\cdot s\>\middle|\>s\in\mbox{\bf State}\right\}&\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[x:=a\right]\!\right]_{\rho}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\left\{s\cdot s[x\mapsto\mathcal{A}\!\left[\!\left[a\right]\!\right](s)]\>\middle|\>s\in\mbox{\bf State}\right\}\\

4
A Logic over Finite Traces

Our trace logic can be seen as an Interval Temporal Logic

[
15
]
with
\mu
-recursion
[
28
]
, or
alternatively, as a temporal
\mu
-calculus with a binary temporal
operator corresponding to the chop operation over sets of traces (see,
e.g.,
[
31
]
for a general introduction to

\mu
-calculus).

4.1
Syntax and Semantics of the Logic

The philosophy behind our logic is to have logical counterparts to
the statements of the programming language in terms of their
finite-trace semantics.
For instance, we use binary relation symbols that correspond to the
atomic statements, and a chop operator corresponding to sequential
composition.
This design choice helps to simplify proofs of the properties of the
logic and the calculus.

Definition 9

(Logic Syntax)
.

The
syntax
of the logic of
trace formulas
is defined by
the following grammar:

\phi\>::=\>p\mid R\mid X\mid\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2}\mid\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}\mid\phi_{1}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\phi_{2}\mid\mu X.\phi

where
p
ranges over state formulas not further specified here, but
assumed to contain at least the Boolean expressions
BExp
,

R
ranges over binary relation symbols over states, and
X
over a
set
\mathsf{RVar}
of
recursion variables
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl@{\qquad}rcl}\left|\!\left|p\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\left\{s\cdot\sigma\>\middle|\>s\models p\right\}&\left|\!\left|R\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\left\{s\cdot s^{\prime}\>\middle|\>R(s,s^{\prime})\right\}\\
\left|\!\left|X\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\mathcal{V}(X)&\left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}\cap\left|\!\left|\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}\\
\left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}\cup\left|\!\left|\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}&\left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}&\left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\left|\!\left|\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}\\
\lx@intercol\hfil\left|\!\left|\mu X.\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\ {\displaystyle\bigcap}\left\{\gamma\subseteq\mbox{\bf State}^{+}\>\middle|\>\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}[X\mapsto\gamma]}\subseteq\gamma\right\}\hfil\lx@intercol\end{array}

Definition 10

(Logic Semantics)
.

The
finite-trace semantics
of a formula
\phi
is defined as
its denotation
\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq\mbox{\bf State}^{+}
, relativized on
a
valuation

\mathcal{V}:\mathsf{RVar}\rightarrow 2^{\mbox{\bf State}^{+}}
of the
recursion variables, inductively by the equations given in
Figure
3
, where in the last clause

\mathcal{V}[X\mapsto\gamma]
denotes the updated valuation.

One can show that the transformers

\lambda\gamma.\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}[X\mapsto\gamma]}
are monotonic
functions in the complete lattice
(2^{\mbox{\bf State}^{+}},\subseteq)
and
hence, by Tarski’s fixed-point theorem for complete
lattices
[
32
]
, they have least and greatest fixed-points.
In particular, the least fixed point is simultaneously also the least
pre-fixed point, hence the defining equation for
\mu X.\phi
.
And because it is a fixed point, we have the following result for
unfolding fixed-point formulas.

Proposition 11

(Fixed-Point Unfolding)
.

Let
\mu X.\phi
be a formula and
\mathcal{V}
a valuation. Then:

\left|\!\left|\mu X.\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}=\left|\!\left|\phi[\mu X.\phi/X]\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}
.

Our calculus is based on closed formulas of the logic. Observe that
fixed-point unfolding preserves closedness. For closed formulas the
valuation
\mathcal{V}
is immaterial to the semantics

\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}
. In this case, we often omit the subscript and
simply write
\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|
.

4.2
Binary Relations

We instantiate the set
\mathsf{Rel}
of binary relation symbols with
two specific relations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathit{Id}(s,s^{\prime})&\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}&s^{\prime}=s\\
\mathit{Sb}^{a}_{x}(s,s^{\prime})&\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}&s^{\prime}=s[x\mapsto\mathcal{A}\!\left[\!\left[a\right]\!\right](s)]\end{array}

These two relations are used to model
skip
and assignment
statements, respectively.
The
transitive closure

R^{+}
of a binary relation
R
over
states is easily defined as a recursive formula in our logic as

R^{+}\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\>\mu X.\ (R\vee R\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}X)
.

Example 12
.

For any arithmetic expression
a
let
\mathit{Dec}_{a}
be a binary
relation symbol interpreted as follows:

\mathit{Dec}_{a}(s,s^{\prime})\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\>\mathcal{A}\!\left[\!\left[a\right]\!\right](s^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{A}\!\left[\!\left[a\right]\!\right](s)
.
That is, the value of
a
does not increase between two consecutive
states.
With this symbol, the formula
\mathit{Dec}_{a}^{+}
expresses the
property that the value of
a
does not increase throughout the
whole execution of a program.

Remark 13
.

It is conceivable to define arbitrary binary relations
(p,q)
over
state formulas
p

5.2

Our proof system is

, in the sense that it can only
derive valid sequents.

Theorem 23

(Soundness)
.

The proof system is sound: every derivable sequent is valid.

Our proof system is

to an oracle, used by rule

By Theorem

We next show that all such judgments are derivable in our proof system.
Together with Corollary

with the help of the rule (

By definition:

,

is the body of

. We abbreviate

and in the following use

(Existence of Canonical Proof)
.

Let
\langle S,T\rangle
be a

(Relative Completeness)
.

The proof system is relatively complete: for every
Rec

S

without statement variables and every closed formula
\phi
, any

By Theorem

Compared to a typical completeness proof of first-order Dynamic Logic,
where the invariant is constructed as equations over the Gödelized
program in the loop, the argument is much simpler, because the
inductive specification logic is sufficiently expressive to
characterize recursive programs (and loops as a special case).
First-order quantifiers are not even necessary, so our logic
is
not
first-order, even though it is obviously Turing-hard and
thus undecidable.

In general, this is not possible, as the following example shows:

7
Related Work

We do not discuss higher-order logical frameworks

[
5
,
27
]
. Even though these are expressive, in program
verification they are not used to specify trace-based properties of
programs, but rather to mechanize conventional contract-based
deductive verification
[
33
]
.

Stirling
[
31
, p. 528, footnote 2]
suggests
that the
\mu
-calculus can be generalized to non-unary predicates,
but does not develop this possibility further.
In
[
24
]
, Müller-Olm proposes a modal fixed-point logic with chop,
which can characterize any context-free process up to bisimulation or
simulation. The logic is shown to be strictly more expressive than the
modal
\mu
-calculus.
Lange & Somla
[
21
]
relate propositional dynamic logic
over context-free programs with Müller-Olm’s logic and show the former
to be equi-expressive with a fragment of the latter.
Fredlund et al.
[
13
]
presented a verification tool for the

Erlang
language based on first-order
\mu
-calculus with
actions
[
9
]
.

In contrast to these papers, we separate programs from fixed-point
formulas and relate them in the form of judgments. Our logic has only
a single binary operator
\mathit{Sb}_{x}^{a}
over arithmetic
expressions
a
and program variables
x
, together with the chop
operator
\frown
. The latter models composition of binary relations
in the denotational semantics. Our logic is sufficient to characterize
any
Rec
program. Specifically,
\mu
-formulas can serve as
contracts of recursive procedures. More importantly, our approach
leads to a
compositional
calculus, where all rules but the
consequence rule are analytic.

Kleene algebra with tests (KAT)
[
18
]
are an equational
algebraic theory that has been shown to be as expressive as
propositional while programs. They have been mechanized in an
interactive theorem prover
[
30
]
and are able to express at
least Hoare-style judgments
[
19
]
. The research around
focuses around
propositional while
programs: we are not aware
of results that relate KAT with recursive stateful
programs. Specifically, our result that procedure contracts can
expressed purely in terms of trace formulas
(Theorem
17
) has not been obtained by
algebraic approaches.

Expressive trace-based specification languages are relatively rare in
program verification.
The trace logic of Barthe et al.
[
2
]
actually is a
many-sorted
first-order logic
, equipped with an arithmetic
theory of explicit trace positions to define program semantics. It is
intended to model program verification in first-order logic for
processing in automated theorem provers. Like
\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[\right]\!\right]
, their
program semantics is compositional; however, it uses explicit time
points instead of algebraic operators.
An extension of Hoare logic with trace specifications is presented by
Ernst et al.
[
11
]
. The standard Hoare-style pre- and
postcondition for a statement is extended with regular expressions
recording events emitted before the execution of the command and the
events emitted by its execution. Our trace logic is more expressive.
Also the first-order
temporal logic of nested words
(NWTL) of
Alur et al.
[
1
]
permits to specify certain execution
patterns within symbolic traces. It is orthogonal to our approach,
being based on nested event pairs and temporal operators, instead of
least fixed points and chop. NWTL is equally expressive over nested
words as first-order logic. The intended computational model is not

Rec
, but the more abstract non-deterministic Büchi automata over
nested words for which it is complete.
The
temporal stream logic
of Finkbeiner et al.
[
12
]
,
like our trace logic, has state updates
\mathit{Sb}_{x}^{t}
(with a
different syntax) and state predicates, but it is based on linear
temporal logic and has no least fixed point or chop operator. Again,
the intended computational model is an extension of Büchi automata,
the verification target are FPGA programs.

Cousot & Cousot
[
8
]
define a trace-based semantics
for modal logics where (infinite) traces are equipped with past,
present, and future. Their main focus is to relate model checking and
static analysis to abstract interpretation—the trace-based semantics
is the basis for it. In contrast, our paper relates a computation
model to a logic. Like our semantics, theirs is compositional and the
operators mentioned in their paper could inspire abstractions of our
trace logic, cf. Section
8.1
below.

Nakata & Uustalu
[
25
]
present a trace-based co-inductive
operational semantics with chop for an imperative programming language
with loops. Following up on this work,
[
10
]
extended the
approach to an asynchronous concurrent language, but neither of these
uses fixed-points, so that the specification language is
incomplete. Also the calculus is not compositional.

Closest to our work is trace-based deductive
verification
[
6
]
, using a similar trace logic as in
the present paper, but neither their semantics nor the calculus are
compositional. Also they prove soundness, but not completeness which
is left as an open question.

Future Work

8.1
Abstract Specifications and Extension of Recursive
Programs

It is desirable to formulate specifications in a more abstract manner
than the programs whose behavior they are intended to capture. For
example, if we reinstate the atomic trace formula
p
in our logic, we
can easily express the set of all finite traces as

\left|\!\left|\mathit{true}\right|\!\right|=\mbox{\bf State}^{+}
. Then we can define a binary
connective as

\phi\cdot\cdot\,\psi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\phi\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathit{true}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\psi
as “any
finite (possibly, empty) computation may occur between
\phi
and

\psi
”. For example, the trace formula
\mu X.(X\cdot\cdot\,X)

expresses that a procedure
m_{X}
calls itself at least twice
recursively, at the beginning and at the end of its body,
respectively.

A more general approach to introduce non-determinism to the logic is
to define for a Boolean expression
b
a
binary relation

R_{b}

with semantics
R_{b}(s,s^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}\mathcal{B}\!\left[\!\left[b\right]\!\right](s)=\mbox{\bf tt}
. The atomic
formula
p
is then
definable
as:

p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}R_{p}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}R_{\mathit{true}}^{+}
.
The advantage of basing
p
on a binary relation is that it is more
easily represented as a canonical program. To this end, we introduce
an atomic statement
havoc
with the trace semantics

\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[\mbox{\bf havoc}\right]\!\right]=\{s\cdot s^{\prime}\mid s,s^{\prime}\in\mbox{\bf State}\}
, i.e. in any given
state
s
, executing
havoc
results in an arbitrary successor state.
The proof rule for
havoc
is the axiom

\Gamma\>\vdash\>\mbox{\bf havoc}:R_{\mathit{true}}
and obviously

\mathsf{stf}(\mbox{\bf havoc})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}R_{\mathit{true}}
. Then
p
is characterized by

\mathsf{can}(p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\langle\mbox{\bf if}\ p\ \mbox{\bf then}\ \mathit{havoc()}\ \mbox{\bf else}\ \mbox{\bf diverge},T\rangle
, where
T
contains the declaration

\mathit{havoc}\ \{\mbox{\bf if}\ \ast\ \mbox{\bf then}\ \mbox{\bf havoc}\ \mbox{\bf else}\ \mbox{\bf skip};\mathit{havoc()}\}.

Interestingly,
R_{\mathit{true}}
(and, therefore,
havoc
on the side
of programs) permits to define
concatenation
of trace formulas

\phi\cdot\psi
with the obvious semantics:

\left|\!\left|\phi\cdot\psi\right|\!\right|\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\,\{s_{0}\cdots s_{n}\cdot s^{\prime}_{0}\cdots s^{\prime}_{m}\mid s_{0}\cdots s_{n}\in\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|,\,s^{\prime}_{0},\cdots s^{\prime}_{m}\in\left|\!\left|\psi\right|\!\right|\}\,=\,\left|\!\left|\phi\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}R_{\mathit{true}}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\psi\right|\!\right|

8.2
Proving Consequence of Trace Formulas

In general this is a difficult problem that requires fixed-point
induction, but the derivations needed in practice might be relatively
simple, as the following example shows.

Example 34
.

Consider the two trace formulas:

\displaystyle\mathsf{stf}(\mathit{down()})

\displaystyle=\,\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mu X_{\mathit{down}}.\left((x>0\land\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathit{Sb}_{x}^{x-2}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}X_{\mathit{down}})\lor(x\leq 0\land\mathit{Id\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}}\mathit{Id})\right)

\displaystyle\mathit{Dec}_{x}^{+}

\displaystyle=\,\mu X_{\mathit{dec}}.(\mathit{Dec}_{x}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}X_{\mathit{dec}}\lor\mathit{Dec}_{x})

from Examples
3

and
16
, respectively. We expect the trace
formula implication

\mathsf{stf}(\mathit{down()})\Rightarrow\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathit{Dec}_{x}^{+}

.

It turns out that the following fixed-point induction rule and
consequence rule, combined with straightforward
first-order

consequence and logic rules, are sufficient to prove the claim:

FP-Ind

A proof system for trace formula implication that can prove the above

in

.

8.3
Non-terminating Programs

Our results so far are limited to
terminating
programs, i.e. to
sets of finite traces. To extend the calculus with a termination
measure, such that a proof of
S:\phi
not only shows
correctness of
S
relative to
\phi
, but also ensures it produces
only finite traces, is easy.

However, the trace-based setup permits, in principle, also to prove
properties of
non-terminating
programs. To this end, it is
necessary to extend the logic with operators whose semantics contains
infinite traces. One obvious candidate are
greatest
fixed-points

[
31
]
. The downside to this
approach is that nested fixed points of opposite polarity are
difficult to understand, as is well known from
\mu
-calculus. Is
there a restriction of mixed fixed-point formulas that

naturally
corresponds to a certain class of programs? The
theory of strongest trace formulas and canonical programs might guide
the search for such fragments.

9
Conclusion

We presented a fixed-point logic that characterizes recursive programs
with non-deterministic guards. Both, programs and formulas have the
same kind of trace semantics, which, like the calculus for proving
judgments, is fully compositional in the sense that the definitions
and rules embody no context.
The faithful embedding of programs into a logic seems to suggest that
we merely replace one execution model (programs) with another (trace
formulas). So why is it worth having such an expressive specification
logic? We can see four reasons:

First, the logic renders itself naturally to extension and abstraction
that cannot be easily mimicked by programs or that are much less
natural for programs.
This is corroborated by the discussion in
Section
8.1
, but also by the case of conjunction: It
is trivial to add conjunction
\phi\land\psi
to the trace logic and
to a calculus for trace formulas, but conjunction has no natural
program counter-part. Yet it permits to specify certain
hyper-properties, i.e., properties relating sets of traces.

Second, the logic offers reasoning patterns that are easily justified
algebraically, such as projection, replacement of equivalents,
strengthening, distribution, etc., that are not obvious in the realm
of programs.

Third, the concept of
strongest trace formula
leads to a
characterization of valid judgments and, thereby, enables a simple
completeness proof.

And finally, the duality between programs and formulas permits to
prove judgments by freely mixing two styles of reasoning: with the
rules of the calculus in Figure
5
, or using a calculus
for the consequence of trace formulas.
For example, a judgment such as

\mathit{down()}:\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathit{Dec}_{x}^{+}
can be
proved as in Example
34
or directly with the rules in
Figure
5
, but also by
mixing
both styles.

A perhaps surprising feature of our trace logic is the fact that no
explicit notion of
procedure contract
is required to achieve
procedure-modular verification: Instead, strongest trace formulas and
statement variables are employed.
This results in a novel (
Call
) rule that works with

symbolic continuations
realized by statement variables.

Fixed-Point Induction and Bekič’s Principle.

It can be shown that

\rho_{0}=\bigsqcup_{k\geq 0}H^{k}(\varnothing^{n})
, where
\sqcup
is
point-wise set union, a result originally due to Kleene, but popularly
known as the Knaster-Tarski Fixed-Point Theorem.
The interpretations
\rho^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}H^{k}(\varnothing^{n})
are referred
to as
fixed-point approximants
of
H
.

Then, for a given transformer
H
and set
A
, a result of the type

\mathit{LFP}\,H\sqsubseteq A
can be established by proving

H^{k}(\varnothing)\sqsubseteq A
for all
k
, by mathematical
induction on
k
. Since
\bigsqcup_{k\geq 0}H^{k}(\varnothing^{n})
is
the least upper bound of the set of approximants, we obtain

\mathit{LFP}\,H\sqsubseteq A
.
But sometimes one can prove a stronger result, namely that

\gamma\sqsubseteq A
implies
H(\gamma)\sqsubseteq A
for
all
\gamma
. This immediately entails the above proof by mathematical
induction—a proof schema commonly referred to as
Fixed-Point
Induction
.
We make use of these proof principles below.

We will also make use of another principle, known as
Bekič’s
Principle
, which allows a simultaneous fixed-point, such
as
\rho_{0}
, to be expressed as a series of individual least
fixed-points
[
4
]
.
Using this principle, we can apply the above Fixed-Point Induction
Principle, but on one procedure name at a time.

Fixed-Point Induction.

As in the domain of finite traces,
one can also apply
fixed-point induction in proofs about recursive formulas.
In particular, a result of shape
\left|\!\left|\mu X.\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq A

can be established by proving that
\gamma\subseteq A
entails

\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}[X\mapsto\gamma]}\subseteq A
.

Proposition 39

(Restricted Traces and State Formulas)
.

Let
b
be a Boolean expression, and
\phi
a trace formula. Then

(\sharp\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|)\!\!\mid_{b}\,=\,\left|\!\left|b\land\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\phi\right|\!\right|
.

Proof.

We have:

\begin{array}[]{cl}&(\sharp\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|)\!\!\mid_{b}\\
=&\left\{s\cdot\sigma\in\sharp\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|\>\middle|\>\mathcal{B}\!\left[\!\left[b\right]\!\right](s)=\mbox{\bf tt}\right\}\\
=&\left\{s\cdot\sigma\>\middle|\>\mathcal{B}\!\left[\!\left[b\right]\!\right](s)=\mbox{\bf tt}\right\}\cap\left\{s\cdot s\cdot\sigma\>\middle|\>s\cdot\sigma\in\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|\right\}\\
=&\left\{s\cdot\sigma\>\middle|\>s\models b\right\}\cap\left\{s\cdot s\>\middle|\>s\in\mbox{\bf State}\right\}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\left\{s\cdot\sigma\>\middle|\>s\cdot\sigma\in\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|\right\}\\
=&\left|\!\left|b\right|\!\right|\cap(\left|\!\left|\mathit{Id}\right|\!\right|\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|)\\
=&\left|\!\left|b\land\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\phi\right|\!\right|\end{array}

∎

A.3
Soundness of the Calculus

For the proof we need to relate traces restricted by a condition
b

to trace formulas. In the following proposition, the intuition for

\left|\!\left|\lnot b\lor\phi\right|\!\right|
is that it ignores any trace, that is, it
is trivially true for any trace, where
b
does not hold in the
beginning.

Proposition 43

(State formulas in judgments)
.

Let
b
be a Boolean expression,
\phi
a trace formula. Then

(\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right])\!\!\mid_{b}\;\subseteq\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right]\subseteq\left|\!\left|\lnot b\lor\phi\right|\!\right|.

Proof.

“Only If” direction:
Assume
(\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right])\!\!\mid_{b}\;\subseteq\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|
and there
is a trace
s\cdot\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right]
that is not in

\left|\!\left|\lnot b\lor\phi\right|\!\right|=\left|\!\left|\lnot b\right|\!\right|\cup\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|
, hence,

s\cdot\sigma\not\in\left|\!\left|\lnot b\right|\!\right|
and

s\cdot\sigma\not\in\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|
. But

s\cdot\sigma\not\in\left|\!\left|\lnot b\right|\!\right|
implies

s\cdot\sigma\in(\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right])\!\!\mid_{b}\;\subseteq\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|
:
contradiction.

“If” direction:
Assume
\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right]\subseteq\left|\!\left|\lnot b\lor\phi\right|\!\right|
and there
is a trace
s\cdot\sigma\in(\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right])\!\!\mid_{b}
that is not in

\left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|
. From the assumption and

(\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right])\!\!\mid_{b}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S\right]\!\right]
we obtain

\mathcal{B}\!\left[\!\left[b\right]\!\right](s)=\mbox{\bf tt}

\>\vdash\>S_{1}:\mathsf{stf}(S_{1})
and

\>\vdash\>S_{2}:\mathsf{stf}(S_{2})
can be proven.
We also use that
\phi\models(p\lor(\lnot p\land\phi))
is a valid
consequence.
We have:

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\>\vdash\>\mbox{\bf if}\ b\ \mbox{\bf then}\ S_{1}\ \mbox{\bf else}\ S_{2}:\mathsf{stf}(\mbox{\bf if}\ b\ \mbox{\bf then}\ S_{1}\ \mbox{\bf else}\ S_{2})\\
\Leftrightarrow&\>\vdash\>\mbox{\bf if}\ b\ \mbox{\bf then}\ S_{1}\ \mbox{\bf else}\ S_{2}:(b\land\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathsf{stf}(S_{1}))\lor\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(\lnot b\land\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathsf{stf}(S_{2}))\\
\Leftarrow&\mbox{\lx@text@lbrace By rule ({If}) combined with rule ({Or})\lx@text@rbrace}\\
&\>\vdash\>\mbox{\bf skip};S_{1}:\lnot b\lor(b\land\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathsf{stf}(S_{1}))~\mbox{and}\\
&\>\vdash\>\mbox{\bf skip};S_{2}:b\lor(\lnot b\land\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathsf{stf}(S_{2}))\\
\Leftarrow&\mbox{\lx@text@lbrace By rule ({Cons})\lx@text@rbrace}\\
&\>\vdash\>\mbox{\bf skip};S_{1}:\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathsf{stf}(S_{1})~\mbox{and}\>\vdash\>\mbox{\bf skip};S_{2}:\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\mathsf{stf}(S_{2})\\
\Leftrightarrow&\mbox{\lx@text@lbrace By rule ({Seq}) combined with rule ({Skip})
and}\\
&\mbox{the induction hypothesis\lx@text@rbrace}\\
&\mathsf{true}\end{array}

Case
S=m()
.

There are two subcases: either
m\in\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\}
or not.
In the first case,

S[\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m_{1}}/m_{1}(),\ldots,\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m_{n}}/m_{n}()]=\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m}

In the second case,

S[\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m_{1}}/m_{1}(),\ldots,\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m_{n}}/m_{n}()]=m()
.
In both cases, we have

\mathsf{stf}(m())=\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\phi_{m}
.

Subcase
\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m}

We have to prove the judgment

\Gamma\>\vdash\>\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m}:\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\phi_{m}
.
Using rules (
Seq
) and (
Skip
), this reduces to

\Gamma\>\vdash\>Y_{m}:\phi_{m}
. Because of the assumption

m\in\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\}
we have
Y_{m}:\phi_{m}\in\Gamma
, so
the proof is finished.

Subcase
m()

We have to prove the judgment

\Gamma\>\vdash\>m():\mathit{Id}\raisebox{4.30554pt}{$\frown$}\phi_{m}
. Rule
(
Call
) is applicable due to assumption

m\not\in\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\}
.
Let

S_{m}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}S_{m}[\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m}/m(),\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m_{1}}/m_{1}(),\ldots,\mbox{\bf skip};Y_{m_{n}}/m_{n}()]

The obtained premise yields the new claim to prove:

\Gamma\cup\{Y_{m}:\phi_{m}\}\>\vdash\>S_{m}^{\prime}:\mu X_{m}.\,\mathsf{stf}(\{m\},S_{m})

We apply rule (
Unfold
) on the right and obtain:

\Gamma\cup\{Y_{m}:\phi_{m}\}\>\vdash\>S_{m}^{\prime}:\mathsf{stf}(\{m\},S_{m})[\phi_{m}/X_{m}]

By induction and by the definition of
\mathsf{stf}(S)
we finish the proof
up to subgoals of the form (for some
m^{\prime}\neq m
):

\Gamma\cup\{Y_{m}:\phi_{m}\}\>\vdash\>m^{\prime}():\mathsf{stf}(\{m\},m^{\prime})[\phi_{m}/X_{m}]

Unfortunately, the structure of the formula on the right does not
conform to
\mathsf{stf}(m^{\prime})
as required.
But, observing that

\left|\!\left|\mathsf{stf}(S)\right|\!\right|=\left|\!\left|\mathsf{stf}(\overline{X},S)\right|\!\right|
,
as well as soundness of unfolding, we can use
Cons
to
obtain:

\Gamma\cup\{Y_{m}:\phi_{m}\}\>\vdash\>m^{\prime}():\mathsf{stf}(m^{\prime})

This follows from the induction hypothesis, because of

N-\left|\Gamma\right|>N-\left|\Gamma\cup\{Y_{m}:\phi_{m}\}\right|
.

∎

A.5
Canonical Programs

Proof of Proposition
30
.

We need to show that for any call
m_{X}()
in
\mathsf{can}(\phi)
there is
exactly one declaration of
m_{X}
in
T_{\phi}
. This is a
straightforward structural induction. ∎

Lemma 44
.

Let
\phi
be an open trace formula not containing fixed-point
binders
(\mu X.)
, and let

\mathsf{can}(\phi)=\langle S_{\phi},T_{\phi}\rangle
.
Then, we have:

\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\phi}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}\,\widetilde{=}\ \left|\!\left|\phi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}

for all interpretations
\rho:M\rightarrow 2^{\mbox{\bf State}^{+}}

of the procedures
M
declared in
T_{\phi}
, and (induced) valuations

\mathcal{V}_{\rho}:\mathsf{RVar}\rightarrow 2^{\mbox{\bf State}^{+}}

defined by

\mathcal{V}_{\rho}(X_{m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\rho(m)
.

Proof.

We proceed by structural induction on
\phi
.

Case
\phi=p\land\psi

By the induction hypothesis,

\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\psi}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}\widetilde{=}\,\left|\!\left|\psi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[\mbox{\bf if}\ p\ \mbox{\bf then}\ S_{\psi}\ \mbox{\bf else}\ \mbox{\bf diverge}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}

\displaystyle\qquad=(\sharp\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\psi}\right]\!\right]_{\rho})\!\!\mid_{p}\cup\;(\sharp\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[\mbox{\bf diverge}\right]\!\right]_{\rho})\!\!\mid_{\neg p}

\displaystyle\qquad=(\sharp\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\psi}\right]\!\right]_{\rho})\!\!\mid_{p}\quad\quad\text{(Proposition~\ref{prop:any})}

\displaystyle\qquad=\mbox{\bf State}^{+}\!\!\mid_{p}\cap\;\sharp\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\psi}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}\widetilde{=}\ \ \mbox{\bf State}^{+}\!\!\mid_{p}\cap\;\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\psi}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}

\displaystyle\qquad\;\widetilde{=}\ \mbox{\bf State}^{+}\!\!\mid_{p}\cap\,\left|\!\left|\psi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}\quad\text{(Induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left|\!\left|p\land\psi\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}

Case
\phi=\phi_{1}\lor\phi_{2}

By the induction hypothesis, we have that

\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\phi_{1}}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}\,\widetilde{=}\,\left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}

and

\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\phi_{2}}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}\,\widetilde{=}\,\left|\!\left|\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[\mbox{\bf if}\ \ast\ \mbox{\bf then}\ S_{\phi_{1}}\ \mbox{\bf else}\ S_{\phi_{2}}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\,\sharp\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\phi_{1}}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}\cup\;\sharp\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\phi_{2}}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}

\displaystyle\;\widetilde{=}\ \ \mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\phi_{1}}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}\cup\;\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[S_{\phi_{2}}\right]\!\right]_{\rho}

\displaystyle\;\widetilde{=}\ \left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}\cup\ \left|\!\left|\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}\quad\text{(Ind.\ hyp.)}

\displaystyle=\,\left|\!\left|\phi_{1}\lor\phi_{2}\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}

Case
\phi=X

We have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mathit{tr}}\!\left[\!\left[m_{X}()\right]\!\right]_{\rho}=\rho(m_{X})=\mathcal{V}_{\rho}(X)=\left|\!\left|X\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{V}_{\rho}}

∎

Proof of Theorem

. We only sketch the proof idea
