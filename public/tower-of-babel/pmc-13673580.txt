Situated vulnerabilities and resiliencies associated with lifetime and past 6-month suicide attempt among transgender and gender diverse primary care patients
Authors: David R Pletta, Meg Quint, Alex S Keuroghlian, Asa E Radix, Madeline B Deutsch, Kenneth H Mayer, Jennifer Potter, Dana J Pardee, Juwan Campbell, Jaclyn MW Hughto, Sari L Reisner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransgender, nonbinary, and gender diverse (TGD) adults experience elevated suicide attempt (SA) risk relative to cisgender adults, yet modifiable vulnerabilities and resiliencies remain understudied. This cross-sectional study examined correlates of lifetime and past 6-month SA among TGD adults receiving primary care. Electronic surveys were completed by 2131 TGD patients at two urban health centers, assessing demographics, gendered contexts, social determinants, interpersonal violence, mental health, and protective factors. Bivariate and multivariable log-Poisson generalized estimating equation models estimated associations with lifetime and past 6-month SA risk. The median age was 28 years; 29.2% had a non-White racial identity and 30.8% were nonbinary. Overall, 30.8% reported a lifetime SA and 2.5% a past 6-month SA. Higher gender dysphoria, childhood physical and sexual violence, lifetime intimate partner violence, and mild or severe depression/anxiety symptoms were associated with increased lifetime SA risk (adjusted risk ratios [aRRs]=1.19–2.12, p < 0.05) Past 6-month SA was associated with high gender dysphoria, lack of health insurance, recent homelessness, childhood sexual violence, and severe depression/anxiety symptoms (aRRs=2.12–4.22, p < 0.05). Ages 31–40 years and college degree attainment were protective against lifetime SA. Findings underscore the importance of addressing early-life trauma and current structural vulnerabilities to reduce suicide risk among TGD adults.Keywords: Transgender, Suicide attempt, Risk factors, Protective factors, Clinical sample

Introduction

In the United States (US), an estimated 1.5 million adults had a suicide attempt (SA) in 2023, and approximately 49,000 died by suicide—an average of one death every 11 min (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). With suicide being a leading cause of death in the US (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025), public health surveillance systems must be able to identify communities at elevated risk for suicide as well as multilevel factors contributing to observed disparities. However, these systems often fail to collect data pertinent to identifying these vulnerable populations, including the collection of robust gender identity data to monitor whether inequities in suicide attempts and deaths exist between transgender, nonbinary, and gender diverse (TGD) adults—those with a gender identity different from their assigned sex at birth—and cisgender adults (Coleman et al., 2022). An estimated 1.6 million TGD adults reside in the US, and prior national studies have reported high rates of suicidality in this population (Herman et al., 2022; James et al., 2016). For instance, the 2015 US Transgender Survey (USTS)—a survey of >27,000 TGD adults—found that 41% had attempted suicide in their lifetime (James et al., 2016). Furthermore, one national study of inpatient encounters reported that TGD patients had greater than 13 times the prevalence of self-harm and death by suicide relative to cisgender patients (Hanna et al., 2019). Suicide remains a public health crisis among TGD adults, and clinical research—serving as a necessary supplement to lacking surveillance data—is needed to investigate both risk and protective factors associated with SA for the purpose of informing intervention development.

The Situated Vulnerabilities and Resiliencies Framework (Reisner et al., 2016) can be applied to conceptualize and understand the high rates of SA among TGD adults. This framework views TGD mental health as shaped by the distribution of power along lines of gender due to cissexism and cisnormativity, as well as other intersecting axes of oppression, such as classism and racism. The health-related vulnerabilities TGD people face are related to challenging gendered relations of power and the policing of gender by social structures. These are situated because TGD health risks and resiliencies cannot be understood without the multilevel gendered contexts that shape them. Factors which contribute to elevated SA prevalence for TGD people can be conceptually grouped into multiple domains: demographic characteristics (e.g., age as a proxy for ageism, race as a proxy for racism), gendered context (e.g., gender identity diversity, gender affirmation), social determinants of health (e.g., health insurance status, homelessness), lifetime interpersonal violence (e.g., childhood violence, intimate partner violence), mental health (e.g., depression and anxiety symptom levels), and protective health-promoting factors (e.g., social support, psychological resilience) (see Fig. 1). Further, this framework posits that while shared risk and protective factors exist for both the general and TGD populations, TGD people also experience unique vulnerabilities and resiliencies that may be leveraged for future interventions.

Situated Vulnerabilities and Resiliencies Framework as applied to suicide attempts in TGD adults.

Existing research supports the situated risks associated with suicide-related self-harm behaviors and mental health morbidity in TGD adults, including SAs (Bird et al., 2024; Kohnepoushi et al., 2023; Pellicane and Ciesla, 2022). For example, SA risk is typically highest in younger age groups, demonstrating that age is an important demographic characteristic reflecting developmental processes (Ivey-Stephenson et al., 2022). The gendered context includes gender identity diversity and how transgression of gendered norms and expectations differs for those who identify as transgender or nonbinary, or for those assigned male or female at birth given cisnormativity is more permissive of gender diversity for individuals assigned a female sex at birth than for those assigned a male sex at birth (Lipson et al., 2019; Schilt and Westbrook, 2009). Several studies have identified differences in SA prevalence by gender identity, including for nonbinary and assigned female sex at birth individuals (Adams et al., 2017; Tebbe and Budge, 2022), while others have not, which highlights the need to investigate gendered contexts further.

Social determinants of health, such as health insurance status (a proxy for socioeconomic status) and homelessness, are associated with mental health morbidity in the general population (Allen et al., 2014). However, the higher prevalence of many adverse social determinants in TGD people, relative to cisgender people, may fuel SAs and contribute to population-level inequities in suicide vulnerability (Adams and Vincent, 2019). For instance, TGD adults without health insurance who seek medical or behavioral healthcare may be at greater risk for having services denied, a risk factor correlated with higher rates of SAs among TGD help-seekers (Romanelli et al., 2018). TGD people can also experience intersecting forms of stigma and interpersonal rejection, leading to housing instability and subsequent psychological stress (Glick et al., 2020; James et al., 2016), thus increasing their vulnerability to suicide attempts.

Experiences of lifetime interpersonal violence are widespread among TGD people, largely the result of stigma (Valentine and Shipherd, 2018). Of USTS respondents who reported four or more instances of discrimination and violence in the past year, 98% thought about suicide and 51% attempted suicide (Herman et al., 2019). In a cohort study using the electronic health records of TGD and cisgender individuals, identifying as TGD relative to cisgender and violence victimization were found to be independent predictors of SA (Progovac et al., 2021). TGD adults subjected to physical or sexual violence or abuse in childhood have been found to have increased risk for non-suicidal self-injury behaviors (Cao et al., 2023). These findings are concerning because TGD adults are frequently exposed to discrimination, violence, and other forms of social stress associated with suicide attempts across the life course (Cogan et al., 2021), including physical, sexual, and intimate partner violence related to acting outside of expected or anticipated cisnormativity (Peitzmeier et al., 2020; Stotzer, 2009).

Relative to cisgender people, TGD people experience mental health inequities, including elevated levels of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms (Pinna et al., 2022). One national study reported that the strongest predictors for those who attempted suicide within 90 days of a primary care visit included having a prior SA, mental health diagnoses, poor responses to items on the Patient Health Questionnaire (9-item) (PHQ-9), and prior inpatient or emergency mental health care (Simon et al., 2018). Prior research has examined the association between depression symptom levels and PTSD symptoms in suicidal ideation among TGD people and found that depression symptom levels, but not PTSD symptoms, were predictive of suicide ideation (Wolford-Clevenger, 2021); however, research has not, to our knowledge, examined both conditions in relation to lifetime or past 6-month SA in TGD adults.

Research has also identified protective factors buffering SA risk for TGD people, including having a supportive family or peers (Bird et al., 2024; Kia et al., 2021), accessing desired gender-affirming medical care (Almazan and Keuroghlian, 2021), living in places with gender identity nondiscrimination statutes (Herman et al., 2019), and psychological resilience (McDowell et al., 2019). In many populations, including TGD communities, social support has been a protective factor against the likelihood of a lifetime SA (Kleiman and Liu, 2013; Pardue-Bourgeois et al., 2023; Trujillo et al., 2017), underscoring the need for interventions that enhance social support.

Applying a Situated Vulnerabilities and Resiliencies Framework, the current study explores vulnerabilities and protective factors associated with risk for lifetime and past 6-month SA in a clinical cohort of TGD primary care patients receiving care at two urban community health centers.

Methods

Study sample

Data for this analysis originated from the LEGACY Project (Reisner et al., 2021), a longitudinal cohort study assessing the relationship between gender-affirming medical care and the sexual and mental health of TGD adults. Data were drawn from electronic surveys administered between February 2019 and March 2021 (i.e., the baseline wave of the study) to 2192 TGD adults receiving medical care at either Fenway Community Health Center (Boston, Massachusetts) or Callen-Lorde Community Health Center (New York City, New York) (Reisner et al., 2015). TGD patients who provided data regarding their lifetime suicide history were included in the current analysis (N = 2131). Patients were considered eligible for LEGACY if they met the following criteria: (1) were aged ≥ 18 years; (2) had a gender identity differing from their sex assigned at birth (verified via two-step method cross-categorizing assigned birth sex and gender identity reported on patient registration, and/or ICD-10 code of F64.0–9); (3) were a primary care patient (defined as having a medical visit in the past 12 months); (4) had a signed patient consent form on file and no research exclusion documented in their patient chart. Additional information on eligibility criteria and recruitment methods for LEGACY can be found in its published protocol (Reisner et al., 2021).

Both Fenway Health and Callen-Lorde Community Health Center are community health centers nationally recognized for delivering comprehensive, culturally responsive primary care to sexual and gender minority populations through an integrated, gender-affirming model of care (Reisner et al., 2015). At both sites, primary care is co-located with on-site behavioral health services, including individual and group psychotherapy, psychiatric evaluation and medication management, and substance use treatment, alongside case management and navigation support for gender-affirming medical and surgical care. Patients identified as being at elevated risk during a primary care encounter can therefore be referred to collaborative, gender-affirming behavioral health and psychiatric care within the same health center.

Measures

Outcome variables for the current analysis were whether a patient had a lifetime history of self-reported SA and whether a patient had SA in the past 6 months. Using an adapted version of validated items from the US Army’s Study to Assess Risk and Resilience in Service Members (STARRS) Instrument (Ursano et al., 2025), patients were asked “Have you ever attempted suicide?” with response options: “No”; “Yes, once”; “Yes, more than once;” and “Choose not to answer.” Patient responses were recoded as “Yes, any lifetime suicide attempt,” “No prior suicide attempt,” and “Missing” for those who chose not to answer the question. Patients who reported a lifetime SA (either “Yes, once” or “Yes, more than once” on the lifetime SA question) were subsequently asked if they had attempted suicide within the past 6 months (yes, no).

Data were collected about patients’ demographics, gendered context, social determinants of health, lifetime interpersonal violence, mental health, and protective factors. Demographic variables included patient age in years, racial identity (American Indian/Alaskan Native, Asian, Black/African American, Latine/Hispanic, Multiracial, Native Hawaiian/other Pacific Islander, White, or another racial identity), and Latine/Hispanic ethnicity (yes, no). Due to sample size considerations, racial identity categories were collapsed to Black/African American, Latine/Hispanic, Multiracial, White, and another racial identity for statistical modeling.

Gendered context variables included patients’ gender identities (trans man, trans woman, nonbinary assigned male at birth [AMAB], nonbinary assigned female at birth [AFAB]), legal gender affirmation history (i.e., had their gender marker changed on either a passport, driver’s license or state identification card, insurance card, birth certificate, or social security card) (yes, no), medical gender affirmation history (i.e., none, hormones only, surgery only, hormones and surgery), and whether their gender dysphoria score was above the sample median. Gender dysphoria was measured using an adapted version of the Utrecht Gender Dysphoria Scale (UGDS) (Cohen-Kettenis and Van Goozen, 1997). The original UGDS comprises separate 12-item forms for individuals assigned male and assigned female at birth; for the present study, the instrument was adapted into a single 12-item gender-neutral form administered to all participants regardless of gender identity or sex assigned at birth, so that trans, nonbinary, and gender diverse patients could be assessed on a common metric. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree); one positively worded item was reverse-scored so that higher values denoted greater dysphoria, and the 12 items were summed to a total score (possible range 12–60, with higher scores indicating greater gender dysphoria). The adapted scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the current sample (standardized Cronbach’s α = 0.81). In the absence of validated clinical cut-points for the adapted measure, patients were classified as having substantial gender dysphoria if their total score exceeded the sample median of 49 (yes, no).

Social determinants of health variables included health insurance status (no insurance, public insurance, private insurance), homelessness in the past 6 months (yes, no), geographic location (site of study participation, New York City, NY or Boston, MA), and educational attainment (high school diploma or less; associate degree, technical school, or some college; four-year degree; graduate degree; or another level of education). Educational attainment was recoded to college degree (four-year degree or graduate degree) (yes, no) for statistical modeling.

Lifetime interpersonal violence variables included childhood violence before the age of 18 years (no violence, both physical and sexual violence, physical violence exclusively, sexual violence exclusively, unsure) and experiences with any intimate partner violence (yes, no). Lifetime intimate partner violence experiences were measured via an 11-item scale representing various forms of physical and sexual violence (e.g., “Did a partner choke or burn you on purpose?”, “Did a partner threaten or blackmail you to have sexual intercourse when you did not want to?”) derived from prior research on intimate partner violence (Garcia-Moreno et al., 2006; Peitzmeier et al., 2021, 2019) (standardized Cronbach’s α = 0.81).

Mental health variables included depression and anxiety symptom levels and whether a patient had clinically elevated PTSD symptoms. Depression and anxiety symptom levels were measured via the validated Patient Health Questionnaire (4-item) (PHQ-4) with continuous scores categorized into four symptom levels: normal (summed score 0–2), mild (3–5), moderate (6–8), and severe (9–12) (Kroenke et al., 2009) (standardized Cronbach’s α = 0.87). Patients were screened for clinically significant PTSD symptoms using the validated Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5 (PC-PTSD-5) using the validated cutoff score of > 3 points (yes, no) (Prins et al., 2016) (standardized Cronbach’s α = 0.81).

Measured protective factors included perceived social support and psychological resilience levels. Perceived social support was measured using the validated Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS); the 12 items were summed and coded as low (0–35), medium (36–60), and high (61–84) support (Zimet et al., 1990, 1988) (standardized Cronbach’s α = 0.92). Psychological resilience was assessed using an adapted version of the validated Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) (Smith et al., 2008). The BRS comprises six items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree): three positively worded and three negatively worded; the three negatively worded items were reverse-scored per the standard protocol. Rather than averaging the items as in the original scoring, the six items were summed to a total score (possible range 6–30), with higher scores indicating greater resilience. The adapted scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the current sample (standardized Cronbach’s α = 0.89). In the absence of validated clinical cut-points, patients were classified as having a substantial level of psychological resilience if their total score exceeded the sample median of 18 (yes, no).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS OnDemand for Academics (SAS Institute Inc, 2026). Descriptive statistics, stratified by past 6-month SA, were used to summarize patients’ suicide histories, demographics, gendered context, social determinants of health, lifetime interpersonal violence, mental health, and protective factors. Assuming data were missing at random, multiple imputation with fully conditional specification was used to impute 20 analytic datasets. Bivariate and multivariable log-Poisson generalized estimating equation (GEE) models, with sandwich estimator-corrected standard errors, were fit across datasets for two outcomes: 1) lifetime SA, and 2) past 6-month SA. Parameter estimates and standard errors were pooled using Rubin’s Rules (Rubin, 1987). Candidate covariates were specified a priori to operationalize each domain of the Situated Vulnerabilities and Resiliencies Framework, including demographics, gendered context, social determinants of health, lifetime interpersonal violence, mental health, and protective factors (Fig. 1). This ensured that model specification was theory-driven rather than purely data-driven. Because the past 6-month SA outcome was rare, and to retain parsimonious, well-identified models, we screened these prespecified domain indicators by their bivariate association with each outcome and retained those with a statistically significant independent association (p < 0.05) in the corresponding multivariable model. Risk ratios (RRs), adjusted risk ratios (aRRs), and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were estimated, and statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

To evaluate the robustness of these modeling decisions, we conducted two prespecified sets of sensitivity analyses using the same multiple imputation and log-Poisson GEE approach (20 imputations; implemented in R version 4.6.0 with the mice, sandwich, and lmtest packages): first, both multivariable models were re-estimated using the continuous UGDS and BRS scores in place of their median-dichotomized indicators; second, both models were re-estimated using a fully prespecified specification that retained one a priori indicator from each framework domain regardless of bivariate significance. Results of these sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Results

Sample characteristics

Descriptive statistics for the sample are presented in Table 1. Of the 2131 TGD patients, 30.8% (n = 657) reported a lifetime SA, and 2.5% (n = 53) reported a past 6-month SA (8.1% of those reporting a lifetime SA). Patients had a median age of 28 years (interquartile range [IQR]= 10; range=18–71 years). The sample was predominantly White (70.1%), followed by Multiracial (14.5%) and Black/African American (4.6%); 10.9% reported a Latine/Hispanic ethnicity. Roughly half (53.8%) of the sample reported having a four-year college degree. The sample was diverse in gender identities (39.7% trans men, 28.8% trans women, and 30.8% nonbinary). Approximately half (46.3%) reported legal gender affirmation. Most patients reported a lifetime history of both hormone use and surgery (41.4%), followed by hormone use only (39.6%) and no hormones or surgery (16.6%). Approximately half (48.2%) had a gender dysphoria score above the sample median. Patients predominantly reported private health insurance (67.4%), followed by public health insurance (26.6%) and no health insurance (3.1%); 6.4% reported homelessness in the past 6 months. Most patients were receiving primary care at Fenway Health in Boston, MA (63.0%).

Lifetime suicide attempt

Bivariate and multivariable model results for lifetime SA are presented in Table 2. Variables demonstrating a statistically significant bivariate association with lifetime SA included age, high gender dysphoria, health insurance status, homelessness in the past 6 months, having a college degree, childhood violence, lifetime intimate partner violence, PHQ-4 depression and anxiety symptom level, clinically significant PTSD, perceived social support, and high psychological resilience. In the final adjusted model, factors associated with a significantly lower risk of lifetime SA were ages 31–40 vs 18–24 years (aRR=0.74, 95% CI=0.59–0.93) and a four-year college degree (aRR=0.74, 95% CI=0.62–0.89). Variables associated with significantly higher SA risk were: a gender dysphoria score above the sample median vs. below (aRR=1.38; 95% CI=1.17–1.62), all forms of child violence victimization before age 18 (aRRs ranged from 1.44 to 2.12), lifetime intimate partner violence vs. not (aRR=1.19; 95% CI=1.01–1.42), and mild (aRR=1.28, 95% CI=1.01–1.61) or severe (aRR=1.45, 95% CI=1.11–1.90) depression and anxiety symptom levels vs. none.

Sensitivity analyses

Results of the two prespecified sets of sensitivity analyses are presented in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. When the continuous UGDS and BRS scores were modeled in place of their median-dichotomized indicators (Supplementary Table S1), inferences were materially unchanged. Higher gender dysphoria remained associated with greater risk of lifetime SA (aRR per 1-SD increase=1.16, 95% CI=1.07–1.25); for past 6-month SA, the association was of similar direction and magnitude but no longer reached statistical significance in the smaller event sample (aRR per 1-SD increase=1.48, 95% CI=0.91–2.40). Higher psychological resilience remained weakly and inversely associated with both outcomes (lifetime aRR per 1-SD increase=0.93, 95% CI=0.87–1.00; past 6-month aRR per 1-SD increase=0.82, 95% CI=0.60–1.12), consistent with the dichotomized models.

In the fully prespecified models, which retained one a priori indicator from each framework domain regardless of bivariate significance (Supplementary Table S2), the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of the principal associations were preserved. This included childhood violence victimization, past 6-month homelessness, lack of health insurance, lower educational attainment, gender dysphoria (lifetime SA aRR=1.42, 95% CI=1.23–1.63), and, for past 6-month SA, having no history of medical gender affirmation (aRR=0.21, 95% CI=0.07–0.65), suggesting our bivariate screening step did not materially distort model inference. As anticipated, given only 53 past 6-month SA events, the fully saturated past 6-month model was estimated with reduced precision, and the gender dysphoria association attenuated to statistical non-significance (aRR=1.88, 95% CI=0.90–3.94) while retaining a consistent direction and relative magnitude.

Discussion

The current study examined vulnerabilities and protective factors for lifetime and past 6-month SA in a sample of TGD adult patients receiving primary care at two urban health centers. Nearly one in three (30.8%) reported a lifetime SA and 2.5% a past 6-month SA. These SA prevalences are slightly lower than those observed in some other samples, such as the USTS (James et al., 2016), likely because ours was a clinical sample of patients engaged in primary care at community health centers experienced in providing affirming care to TGD people. We identified multiple correlates of SA representing vulnerabilities that are modifiable intervention targets, including gendered context (e.g., gender dysphoria), social determinants (e.g., health insurance, homelessness), childhood adversity (e.g., childhood violence victimization), adulthood violence (e.g., intimate partner violence), and mental health (e.g., depression and anxiety symptom levels). Routinely screening for these factors in clinical care settings may aid in identifying TGD patients in need of additional support and mental health referrals to prevent future SA risk.

Results from our multivariable models are largely aligned with findings from prior research on suicide risk factors in TGD adults. TGD adult patients with high gender dysphoria were at elevated risk for both lifetime and past 6-month SAs, a finding aligned with a recent systematic review of mental health outcomes reporting that young adults with gender dysphoria had a higher prevalence of suicidal ideation than those without (Marconi et al., 2023). Additional research is needed on the relationship between gender dysphoria, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts among TGD adults, given that a different study found no association between gender dysphoria levels and same-day or next-day suicidal intent among TGD adults (Botelho et al., 2023).

For social determinants of health, not having health insurance and past 6-month homelessness were associated with TGD patients’ risk for past 6-month SA. These findings resonate with prior research on the potential adverse mental health impact of barriers to medical and behavioral healthcare for TGD people, including that being denied services due to not having health insurance may affect TGD patients’ risk for SA (Learmonth et al., 2018; Romanelli et al., 2018). Additionally, our finding that past 6-month homelessness was significantly associated with past 6-month SA corroborates prior research which has found an association between housing instability and suicidality among TGD adults (Glick et al., 2020). Social determinants of health were found to be associated with past 6-month SA but not with lifetime SA, suggesting the potential importance of temporal proximity for contemporaneous social determinants and SA risk.

Lifetime experiences of interpersonal violence were pervasive in our sample and were associated with SA, even when controlling for presenting PTSD symptom levels. Childhood violence victimization experienced before age 18 was associated with increased risk for both lifetime SA and past 6-month SA among TGD patients. More specifically, all reported forms of childhood violence victimization – including instances where patients were unsure of the type of abuse they had endured – were associated with elevated risk for having a lifetime SA, and experiencing sexual violence exclusively was associated with higher past 6-month SA risk. Prior research has found childhood violence victimization to be associated with non-suicidal self-injury among TGD adults (Cao et al., 2023), and one study with transgender women found childhood sexual abuse to be associated with higher risk for suicidal ideation and suicide attempts (Xavier Hall et al., 2021). We also identified an association between lifetime intimate partner violence and elevated lifetime SA risk, a finding aligned with the results of one study on lifetime exposure to intimate partner violence and mental health outcomes in TGD adults over the age of 50 years (Hillman, 2022). Additional research is needed on the relationship between childhood violence victimization, violence experienced in adulthood, and SA risk across the life course for diverse populations of TGD youth and adults, including development and dissemination of interventions designed to prevent childhood violence targeting TGD people and to address the SA-related sequelae of interpersonal violence victimization for survivors of abuse.

Elevated levels of depression and anxiety symptoms were associated with higher risk for both lifetime and past 6-month SA. While our study used the PHQ-4, our results coincide with prior research which has found that poor responses to the PHQ-9 are a strong predictor of patients attempting suicide within 90 days of a primary care visit (Simon et al., 2018). TGD patients who reported mild or severe depression and anxiety symptom levels were at elevated risk for having a lifetime SA, and TGD patients with severe depression and anxiety symptom levels had higher risk for past 6-month SA. Corroborating and extending prior research on suicidal ideation examining both depression symptom levels and PTSD symptoms (Wolford-Clevenger, 2021), we found that depression and anxiety symptom levels were a robust mental health correlate of SA, whereas PTSD symptoms were not. Future research may benefit from longitudinally measuring the association between levels of depression and anxiety symptoms, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts in diverse populations of TGD adults, such as studies utilizing ecological momentary assessments to dynamically capture the timing of patients’ symptom onset and treatment response. Additionally, depression and anxiety are prevalent mental health conditions often screened for and treated through primary care alone, which can pose challenges for longitudinally monitoring patients’ symptoms and treatment response. Collaborative care models, in which primary care and behavioral health work together to facilitate a patient’s treatment, have been shown to improve treatment outcomes for depression and anxiety relative to usual primary care alone (Archer et al., 2012). Ensuring patients have consistent access to such collaborative care may also serve as a concrete, modifiable, and systems-level intervention for mitigating suicide risk in TGD primary care patients.

Three variables were protective in the SA modeling. For lifetime SA, protective factors included older age (31–40 years vs. 18–24 years) and higher educational attainment (a four-year college degree vs. no college degree). Our findings are consistent with SA research in the general US population, which shows a socioeconomic gradient in health with inverse associations between higher educational attainment and adverse mental health (Allen et al., 2014). Our findings on age differences in suicide risk are consistent with national surveillance data in the US, wherein suicidality is highest among those ages 18–39 years (Ivey-Stephenson et al., 2022), and the suicide attempt rate is highest for those in the 18–25 year age group (National Institute of Mental Health, 2024). Our findings suggest that TGD young people ages 18–24 years are an important group for suicide prevention efforts.

With respect to past 6-month SA, having no history of medical gender affirmation, relative to a history of gender-affirming hormones only, was associated with lower risk; however, this counter-intuitive finding warrants cautious interpretation. Only three past 6-month SAs occurred among the 353 patients with no medical gender affirmation history. Critically, the observed association is unlikely to reflect a protective effect of forgoing medical gender affirmation: a robust body of evidence links access to desired gender-affirming medical care, including hormones and surgery, to better mental health and reduced suicidality (Almazan and Keuroghlian, 2021). The finding is more plausibly attributable to selection effects and unmet need. Because the majority of patients in this clinical sample were engaged in some form of gender-affirming care (82.9%; n = 1767/2131), those with no medical gender affirmation history may disproportionately comprise those with lower gender dysphoria or who did not desire hormonal or surgical interventions, whereas patients who had accessed hormones alone may include a subset with unmet desire or need for gender-affirming surgery—an unmet need that may itself elevate distress and SA risk. While nondefinitive, this hypothesis is supported by a prior study conducted with the LEGACY cohort that reported a significant unmet need for gender-affirming surgery among patients (Pletta et al., 2025). Because the current study did not assess desire for, or met and unmet need for, medical gender affirmation, these explanations cannot be disentangled.

The current study is subject to several limitations. First, by using lifetime and past 6-month SA as outcomes, and both lifetime and contemporaneous characteristics of TGD adults as statistical predictors, our study cannot ensure temporal ordering between exposures and outcomes. This suggests the potential presence of reverse causality, in which our exposures (e.g., social support levels) follow the occurrence of our outcome (e.g., a past 6-month SA). Readers are cautioned against drawing causal inferences. Second, our sample was predominantly White, highly educated, and recruited from two urban health centers in the Northeastern US specializing in affirming care for sexual and gender minority populations, thereby limiting the generalizability of our results to racially, socioeconomically, and geographically diverse TGD adult populations or other clinical populations. Third, regarding measurement, some of our validated scales lack cutoffs for clinical significance (e.g., gender dysphoria and psychological resilience); thus, we coded these variables relative to the distribution in the current sample, which may limit generalizability to other TGD populations.

Other limitations pertain to the omission of other suicide-related vulnerabilities or potential confounders from the current analysis, including substance use. Additionally, patient responses of “Choose not to answer” were coded as missing and subsequently imputed for the current analysis; consequently, we may be misrepresenting the nuanced identities of TGD adults who do not wish to label their gender identity, racial identity, or other demographic factors. Finally, given that our data were self-reported and our outcome of interest was SA, the current study is prone to survival bias: TGD adults who died by suicide were not represented in our data. Consequently, we may be underestimating the true prevalence of lifetime and past 6-month SA among TGD adults. Limitations notwithstanding, the current study has notable strengths, including a clinical sample of patients with diverse gender identities, assessment of multiple TGD-specific (e.g., gender dysphoria) situated risks and resiliencies, consideration of multiple types of violence both in childhood and adulthood, and identification of modifiable targets for future interventions (e.g., depression and anxiety symptom levels).

Suicide in TGD adults is a public health crisis in the US. The current study explored vulnerabilities and protective factors associated with lifetime and past 6-month SA among TGD adult patients. Study findings suggest both lifetime (e.g., childhood violence victimization) and contemporaneous (e.g., depression and anxiety symptom levels) vulnerabilities are associated with TGD patients’ risk for having lifetime and past 6-month SA. Future research may benefit from pragmatically applying theories of suicidal behavior, such as the ideation-to-action framework, to identify additional risk and protective factors for SA for TGD adults (Wolford-Clevenger et al., 2018). Our findings underscore the importance of developing interventions to mitigate suicide risk factors across the life course for TGD adults, including the provision of health insurance, secure housing, ready access to evidence-based treatment for depression and anxiety within primary care, early identification and prevention of childhood violence victimization and abuse, and screening and prevention for intimate partner violence. By addressing suicide-related vulnerabilities earlier in the life course and known structural social determinants of suicide, public health professionals may be able to mitigate risk for future SA among TGD adults.
