Counterion-enabled divergent reactivity in dinuclear gold photoredox catalysis
Authors: Mahesh Bhagwan Thoke, Anupam Das, Deep S. Kulkarni, Manoj V. Mane, Nitin T. Patil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tuning the electronics of photocatalysts is crucial for controlling the reactivity pathways in photoredox catalysis. While the key approaches like ligand engineering and donor-acceptor modulation are crucial, they invariably require multistep synthesis for the generation of photocatalyst library. Counterions are inherent to all cationic photocatalysts but interestingly their potential to influence reactivity has often been overlooked. Herein, we demonstrate an unforeseen reactivity control that uses counterions to tune electronics in dinuclear-gold-photoredox catalysis. The photoexcited [AuI–AuI]2+ complex selectively reduces S(VI)–Cl bonds; whereas, inner sphere halide coordination generates a potent reductant [Au1/2–Au1/2]+, capable of reducing otherwise unreactive S(VI)–F bonds. A successful application of this concept is demonstrated in the alkene functionalization using sulfonyl chlorides and sulfonyl fluorides. Detailed mechanistic studies provide experimental evidence for the [Au1/2–Au1/2]+ species, which has been proposed earlier but has not been experimentally validated.

Introduction

Photoredox catalysis has emerged as a powerful strategy to enable previously elusive transformations which have high kinetic or thermodynamic barriers for selective activation of chemical bonds1–7. A central design element in these transformations is the redox tuning of the photocatalyst, which upon photoexcitation generates highly reactive oxidant or reductant from stable ground-state precursors8,9. The redox properties of transition metal photocatalysts, such as Ir- and Ru-polypyridyl complexes are primarily governed by the metal–ligand interactions which modulate the HOMO–LUMO energies10,11. In the case of organic photocatalysts, donor–acceptor moieties primarily dictate the redox window by modulating the HOMO–LUMO gap and conjugation length further influences excited-state stability and charge-transfer efficiency (Fig. 1A)12,13. Typically, photocatalysts are salts and studies highlighting the influence of counterion on their electronics are rare15,16. It is known that bulky and non-coordinating counterions enhance the oxidative power of triplet excited states, improving both the rate and selectivity of photocatalytic transformations via single-electron transfer (SET)16,17 or energy transfer (EnT)18 mechanisms. These studies indicate that non-coordinating counterions can promote photoinduced outer-sphere electron transfer (OSET); whereas, small coordinating counterions impede the process by shielding the metal center17. Hu and Gautier recently showed that ion-pairing between an Ir(III) photosensitizer and trifluoroacetate enables efficient CF₃• generation, which is otherwise challenging due to the high oxidation potential (∼2.3 V vs NHE)19. Meyer and co-workers showed that the size and charge of counterions influence the ion-pairing strength, which in turn affects the electrochemical behavior of the photosensitizer20–23. Clearly, understanding the effect of coordinating counterions would unlock new avenues for modulating redox events to access reactive intermediates that are otherwise inaccessible with non-coordinating counterion19.

In recent years, dinuclear gold photocatalysis has emerged as a powerful tool for single-electron reduction of non-activated substrates including organic halides24–27. Unlike Ir- and Ru-based polypyridyl complexes, dinuclear gold complexes display distinct photophysical behavior28. For instance, Ir- and Ru-based photocatalysts heavily rely on ligand-centered transitions29. Contrarily, dimeric gold photocatalysts feature metal-centered excited states30,31, where 5dσ* to 6pσ excitation converts the [Au⋯Au] interaction into a covalent [Au–Au]* bond, thereby increasing electron density between the two Au atoms. Further, in dinuclear gold complexes, both HOMO and LUMO are localized on the gold centers32, offering a unique platform for tuning their electronics on the gold center itself. The pioneering work by Che33, and the recent contributions from the groups of Barriault34, Hashmi35,36, Xie37–40, Fensterbank41, and Bao42 have demonstrated the distinct reactivities of dinuclear gold complexes43. To the best of our knowledge, all these reports are confined to the generation of carbon-centered radicals24–27, and there exists no report on the generation of heteroatom-centered radicals (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, detailed computational investigations by the Bao group propose the formation of [Au1/2–Au1/2]⁺ species, arising from single-electron reduction of a [Auᴵ–Auᴵ] dimer42. Electronic-structure analysis indicates pronounced electron delocalization across the Au–Au bond, which endows the species with enhanced reducing power. Although these calculations provide a compelling mechanistic hypothesis, direct experimental evidence for the [Au1/2–Au1/2]⁺ intermediate has not yet been reported.

The photochemical methods for the generation of high-valent sulfur-centered (S(VI)) radicals have largely been limited to the use of sulfonyl chlorides (Ered ≳ −1.3 V vs SCE)44,45. In contrast, sulfonyl fluorides are difficult to reduce due to the strong S(VI)-F bond and high reduction potential (Ered ≳−1.87 V vs SCE)46, thus requiring either a powerful excited-state reductant or pre-activation technique47,48. Sulfonyl chlorides are highly reactive but often unstable, which limits their use in multistep synthesis. The unique balance of stability and reactivity makes sulfonyl fluorides indispensable in the field of bioconjugation, material functionalization, and late-stage drug modification, where controlled SO2 transfer is required49–51.

Herein, we demonstrate an unforeseen reactivity-control mechanism that uses counterions to tune electronic properties in dinuclear-gold-photoredox catalysis. The photoexcited [Auᴵ–Auᴵ]²⁺ complex selectively reduces S(VI)–Cl bonds; whereas, halide addition generates a potent reductant [Au1/2–Au1/2]⁺ which reduces the otherwise unreactive S(VI)–F bonds (Fig. 1C). A successful demonstration of this concept is shown for the alkene functionalization to synthesize alkenyl sulfones from sulfonyl chlorides as well as sulfonyl fluorides.

Results and discussion

Reaction development

Inspired by the pioneering work of Che52 and Mason32 on Au-anion interactions in polynuclear gold complexes, we sought to exploit these features to tune mechanisms in organic synthesis. In particular, we noted that the photoexcitation of such polynuclear gold complexes perturbs their excited states due to exciplex formation with anions or solvents52. Building on this, we envisioned that strategically selecting counterions could tune these interactions, thereby modulating the photocatalytic properties of gold complexes.

Considering the lack of reports on the generation of heteroatom-centered radicals in dinuclear photocatalysis24–27, we sought to utilize sulfonyl chlorides/fluorides to generate S(VI)-centered radicals and their applications in alkene functionalization. Be noted that the S(VI)-centered radicals are commonly generated through single-electron transfer (SET) from sulfonyl chlorides; however, the direct SET reduction of sulfonyl fluorides is known to be highly challenging48. Initial exploration of photoinduced digold-catalyzed generation of S(VI) radical began by using sulfonyl chlorides with [Au2(dppm)2]Cl2 as a photocatalyst in dry CH3CN under 365 nm light irradiation (Fig. 2A). The reaction of 4-toluenesulfonyl chloride 1a with alkene 3a in the presence of K3PO4 as base afforded the desired product 4a in 90% yield (Fig. 2A, Condition A, entry 1). A series of photocatalyst screenings revealed [Au2(dppm)2]Cl2 as the optimal catalyst for this transformation (Condition A, entries 2-5). When K3PO4 was replaced with Na2CO3, a decrease in yield (68%) was observed (Condition A, entry 6). Next, we turned our attention to the single-electron reduction of sulfonyl fluoride. When 4-toluenesulfonyl fluoride was subjected to the optimized reaction condition A, the desired product was not obtained (Fig. 2A). This opted to investigate the effect of various counterions to tune the reactivity of digold photocatalysts. At first, we examined the effect of non-coordinating counterions such as trifluoroacetate and triflate. Interestingly, the sodium trifluoroacetate provided a trace amount of product, whereas the sodium triflate afforded 4a in 12% yield (condition B, entries 5 and 6). Next, we studied the effect of coordinating counterions by screening halide ions. Gratifyingly, the desired product was obtained in 45% yield using NaCl (condition B, entry 7). Furthermore, the addition of NaBr improved the yield of the product 4a up to 72% (condition B, entry 1). However, NaI afforded the product in 51% yield (condition B, entry 8). To rationalize the trend observed with halides, we consider the electrophilicity of halogen radicals, which increases in the order I• <Br• <Cl• <F•. As electrophilicity increases, the rate of back electron transfer (BET) between the electron-rich [Au1/2–Au1/2]⁺ species and oxidized halide becomes increasingly favorable. This accounts for the diminished reactivity with Cl⁻ and the complete absence of reaction with F⁻. Bromide ion thus strikes an ideal balance: it coordinates sufficiently to enable halide binding without over-stabilization, and its optimal Au–Br distance provides appropriate σ-donating ability. For non-coordinating counterions such as OTf ‾, SCN‾, and CF₃CO₂‾, the poor reactivity stems from their inability to coordinate to the gold center, precluding formation of the exciplex intermediate required for inner-sphere electron transfer. Consequently, the reaction reverts to an inefficient outer-sphere pathway. Overall, these results indicate that the rate of product formation is influenced by both the size and coordinating nature of the counterion. Notably, the reaction did not work with well-established Ir(III), Ru(II), and 4-CzIPN photocatalysts, underscoring the distinct reactivity of the counterion-assisted digold photocatalysis (condition B, entries 1–4). Control experiments confirmed that the digold photocatalyst, NaBr and light irradiation are essential as no product was detected in their absence (condition B, entry 9). Furthermore, no conversion was observed under thermal conditions (80 °C), underscoring the photochemical nature of the transformation (see ESI, Section 7.1).

Mechanistic investigations

At first, we started with photophysical studies by recording the UV-Vis absorption spectra of the photocatalyst [Au2(dppm)2]Cl2 along with other starting materials Ts–Cl, Ts–F, and alkene (Fig. 2B). The PC1 catalyst displays a strong absorption peak at 319 nm (λ max) with a tail extending to the 365-370 nm region. In contrast, Ts–Cl, Ts–F, and alkene do not exhibit any absorption peaks near the 365 nm region, suggesting that only PC1 exhibits photoinduced activity under the reaction condition. Furthermore, when PC1 was mixed with either of the reaction components (Ts–Cl, Ts–F, or alkene) in a 1:1 (V/V) ratio, no new absorption peaks or shifts were observed. This suggests that there is no aggregation of PC1 in the ground state when combined with other starting materials. To identify which reagent engages in the initial SET with the excited-state PC1 under both the reaction conditions, steady-state fluorescence quenching experiments with individual reaction components were conducted (see ESI, Section 7.3). It was found that Ts–Cl and NaBr were involved in SET under conditions A and B, respectively. Interestingly, a notable blue shift in the fluorescence intensity of PC1 was observed in the presence of Ts–Cl, indicating the formation of an exciplex species (Fig. 2C(a)). However, in the case of NaBr, significant fluorescence quenching of PC1 was detected with a slight red shift (Fig. 2C(b)). The blue shift with Ts–Cl and the red shift with NaBr clearly indicate the altered electronic behavior of the excited-state digold species, implying that the excited-state digold serves as both donor and acceptor depending upon the nature of the exciplex. These results are consistent with simulated electronic spectrum analysis via frontier molecular orbitals analysis (Fig. S39) and TD-DFT analysis (Fig. S40).

Mechanistic studies were conducted to get deeper insight into the role of counterions in understanding the origin of selectivity. To rule out the formation of sulfonyl bromide in the presence of NaBr, the reaction was monitored by HPLC. No detectable peak corresponding to sulfonyl bromide (Fig. S24) was observed even after 20-36 hrs (see ESI, Section 7.6). Furthermore, we performed a control experiment in which sulfonyl fluoride was stirred with NaBr in the dark (see ESI, Section 7.6.2). Reaction monitoring confirmed that the sulfonyl fluoride remained intact with no detectable conversion to sulfonyl bromide. These results confirm that halogen exchange does not occur. Having recognized the role of halide anion, we plan to investigate its effect on ground-state interactions. With increasing NaBr concentration, the UV-Visible spectrum of PC1 exhibited a red shift (325 nm to 360 nm) that can be attributed to enhanced LMCT facilitated by bromide coordination (Fig. 3A). These spectral changes could be explained by the migration of the Br‾ ion to the inner-sphere of the ground state gold complex as [Au2(dppm)2]2+ + Br‾ ⇌ [Au2(dppm)2Br]+52. Comparative UV-Vis spectra with all three halides reveal distinct ground-state interactions depending on the nature of the halide (see ESI, Figs. S8–S10). To further understand the effect of Br⁻ concentration on the excited-state behavior of PC1, time-resolved photoluminescence measurements were conducted. (Fig. 3B). In DMSO, PC1 exhibited a significant excited-state lifetime (0.68 μs). Although polynuclear gold complexes are known to facilitate ISET for halide reduction27, direct evidence for exciplex formation, to the best of our knowledge, has not been reported. Interestingly, our emission quenching studies (Fig. 2C(a)) revealed the formation of an exciplex, [Au–Au–Cl–Ts]* (see ESI, S13). Next, we conducted Circularly Polarized Luminescence (CPL) studies to shed light on the formation of an exciplex in a chiral environment. Accordingly, CPL titration experiment was performed with [Au2(dppm)2]Cl2 and (1S)-(+)−10-camphorsulfonyl chloride 1q. The observed CPL signal (Fig. 3C(i), green) at ~450 nm matches with the emission observed in fluorescence quenching studies of [Au2(dppm)2]Cl2 with 1q (Fig. 3C(ii)), indicating the formation of exciplex.

The excess of halide ion (X‾) concentration facilitates their migration into the inner coordination sphere53 of the digold complex [AuI–AuI]2+ to form [AuI–AuI–X]+ which possesses distinct photocatalytic behavior32,52. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the [Auᴵ–Auᴵ–X]⁺ complex upon photoexcitation would generate the reduced [Au1/2–Au1/2]⁺ species and an X• radical via ISET pathway (Fig. 5A). Notably, the reduction of excited-state digold with halide via OSET is thermodynamically unfavourable (Ered* of [Au2(dppm)2]Cl2* = 0.60 V, Eₒₓ of Br‾ = 1.18 V, Eₒₓ of Cl‾ = 2.03 V vs SCE)54,55. To validate our hypothesis, we attempted to trap the X• radical with diphenyl alkene 3a. Moreover, we have computed the Mulliken charges and spin densities for key intermediates in the reaction to elucidate the charge transfer process occurring during the reaction (Fig. 6B). The photoexcited state, [AuI–AuI]²⁺* exhibits an even distribution of spin density (0.50 on each Au), characteristic of a delocalized mixed-valent excited state. The coordination of Br‾ to form the [AuI–AuI–Br]⁺* exciplex fundamentally disrupts this symmetry, as demonstrated by the Mulliken charges, which indicate a notable accumulation of electron density on the proximal Au2 (−0.72) compared to distal Au1 (−0.43). Notably, the accompanying spin density becomes heavily localized on Au2 (0.607), identifying this center as the site of radical character and confirming an inner-sphere single-electron transfer from the bromide ion. This SET event oxidizes Br‾ to a bromine radical and results in the highly reduced, mixed-valent [Au1/2–Au1/2]+ species. The formation of this potent reductant is unequivocally substantiated by the final computed electronic structure, which reveals complete delocalization of the unpaired electron across both gold centers (spin density of ~0.205 on each) and near-equivalent, significantly negative Mulliken charges (ca. −0.51 on each Au). In the case of digold exciplex with TsCl, [AuI–AuI–Cl-Ts]⁺*, the Mulliken charges indicate a shift of electron density towards Au2 (−0.774), while spin density further corroborates the radical character on Au2 (0.44). After reduction with TsCl, the oxidized digold showed equal Mulliken charges and charge density at both gold centers, with a clear decrease in spin character due to the loss of an electron.

Based on the above mechanistic experiments, a plausible mechanism has been proposed (Fig. 5C). In the case of sulfonyl fluorides, the addition of NaBr promotes inner-sphere coordination of Br‾ to the ground-state gold complex, forming [Au2(dppm)2Br]⁺ (B). Upon photoexcitation, increased Au–Au covalency and coordination number at the Au(I) center strengthen the Au–Br interaction in the excited-state B* (Path A). Additionally, a minor path B may involve post-excitation ligand association to generate [AuI–AuI–Br]* species B*. Subsequently, bromide ion from the inner coordination sphere [AuI–AuI–Br]* could facilitate the inner-sphere single-electron reduction of digold to generate [Au1/2–Au1/2]+ species C along with the Br•. The [Au1/2–Au1/2]+ species C is a potent reductant, which facilitates the single electron reduction of sulfonyl fluoride to produce the corresponding sulfur (VI) radical (cf. D) along with the regeneration of the dinuclear gold complex A. Furthermore, the resulting sulfonyl radical is prone to undergo a Giese-type addition with alkene 3, producing the alkyl radical species G, which ultimately leads to the vinyl sulfone product.

In the case of sulfonyl chlorides, upon light irradiation, an electron transition within the excited-state species A* would enhance the aurophilic interaction. This in turn creates vacant coordination sites on the gold center, resulting in exciplex E. Owing to the reducing ability of the photoexcited Au–Au, an inner-sphere SET occurs within exciplex E, resulting in the formation of the S(VI) radical and the [AuI–AuII]3+ species F. The resulting sulfonyl radical is prone to undergo a Giese-type addition with alkene, forming the alkyl radical species G, which subsequently oxidizes to the carbocation intermediate H via reduction of the [AuI–AuII]3+ complex F to regenerate the dinuclear gold complex A. Finally, deprotonation of the carbocation intermediate H leads to vinyl sulfone 4.

Application

We then turned our attention to evaluating the scope of the photoinduced digold-catalyzed alkene functionalization with sulfonyl chlorides and sulfonyl fluorides (Fig. 7). To our delight, a wide variety of sulfonyl chlorides and sulfonyl fluorides reacted well with styrene derivatives, providing products in moderate to excellent yields. The reaction was compatible with ortho-, meta-, and para-substituted sulfonyl halides (4a–4e). Sulfonyl halides bearing electron-rich substituents performed well, affording products 4a–4c in 71–89% yield; however, 4-methoxy sulfonyl fluoride failed to deliver the desired product. Importantly, sulfonyl chlorides and sulfonyl fluorides bearing halides on aromatic rings are well tolerated (4f–4i, 50–70% yield). Substrates bearing electron-withdrawing groups such as cyano and trifluoromethyl were also found to be compatible under the reaction conditions (4k and 4 l, 48–73% yield). However, diminished yield (4j, 35% yield) was observed with para-nitro sulfonyl chloride substrate, whereas para-nitro sulfonyl fluoride failed to give the desired product. We attribute the low yield to uncontrolled side reactions and photodegradation of the nitro-substrate under irradiation. Next, in the case of electronically neutral sulfonyl halides, the corresponding products 4 m and 4n were obtained in good to excellent yields (66–84%). Be noted that the desulfonylation is the major obstacle when aliphatic sulfonyl chlorides are exposed to SET reduction57. Interestingly, we found that aliphatic sulfonyl chlorides are well tolerated and generate sulfonated products under our conditions (4o, and 4q, 68–78% yield). However, camphor sulfonyl fluoride shows a diminished yield (4q, 28% yield), likely due to slower radical generation from the sulfonyl fluoride, enabling faster desulfonylation to outcompete efficient product formation. The reaction also demonstrated a broad scope with alkenes, as a wide variety of alkene derivatives proved compatible and successfully coupled with sulfonyl fluorides and sulfonyl chlorides. For example, alkenes bearing electron-rich groups reacted well to afford desired products in good to excellent yield (4r and 4s, 65–89% yield). Halogen-containing alkenes worked equally well to afford corresponding products in good to excellent yields (4t–4v, 57–84% yield). In addition, an alkene derived from menthol was also tolerated to provide 4w in moderate yields (30–41% yield). Next, 4-methoxy styrene worked well with condition A (4y, 68% yield, E/Z = 5/1); however, it failed to give the desired product under condition B. According to literature precedence57 and our observation, the sulfonylated product derived from methoxystyrenes appears to be photosensitive. Under the prolonged irradiation required for Condition B, this product is likely to undergo rapid photodegradation. Interestingly, the isomerized product 4x was obtained when the exocyclic alkene was subjected to both reaction conditions (84% yield with sulfonyl chloride and 20% with sulfonyl fluoride).

In summary, we demonstrated that counterions play a critical role in modulating both the ground-state electronic properties and the excited-state reactivity of dinuclear gold photocatalysts. Specifically, we demonstrated that the in situ generated [Au1/2–Au1/2]⁺ species, upon halide ion addition, enables the reductive activation of inert S(VI)–F bonds, whereas the photoexcited [Auᴵ–Auᴵ]²⁺ complex selectively reduces S(VI)–Cl bonds. This concept was successfully applied to sulfonylation of alkene using both sulfonyl chlorides and sulfonyl fluorides, addressing a longstanding challenge in SuFEx reagents by enabling robust photocatalytic activation of S(VI)–F bonds49,50. Detailed mechanistic studies provide experimental evidence for the [Au1/2–Au1/2]⁺ species - a potent reductant that has been proposed earlier42 but has not been experimentally validated. We anticipate that these findings will not only spur the development in the field of polynuclear gold photocatalysis but also open new avenues in photoredox catalysis, establishing counterions as a previously overlooked design element for the development of novel reactions.

Methods

General procedures used for digold-catalyzed reduction of sulfonyl chlorides and sulfonyl fluorides are as follows. More details can be found in the Supplementary Information.

General procedure for photoinduced digold-catalyzed sulfonylation of alkenes using sulfonyl chlorides

To an oven-dried Schlenk tube equipped with a stirring bar was charged with sulfonyl chloride, (0.4 mmol, 2 equiv), K3PO4 (0.4 mmol, 2 equiv), and [Au2(dppm)2]Cl2 (0.008 mmol, 4 mol%). The Schlenk tube was capped with a rubber septum, evacuated, and refilled with nitrogen (3×), then alkene (0.2 mmol, 1 equiv), and degassed MeCN (2 mL, 0.1 M) was added. The mixture was then sparged with nitrogen for 2 min and capped with a glass stopper under nitrogen flow. The reaction tube was sealed with parafilm, and the reaction was placed in the EvoluChem photoreactor (equipped with a fan, 365 nm light source). The reaction mixture was irradiated for 14 h with stirring at 700 to 800 rpm. The tube was opened, and the reaction mixture was diluted with ethyl acetate and filtered through a short pad of Celite. The filtrate was collected in vacuo, and the resultant residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography to afford the desired product 4.

General procedure for photoinduced digold-catalyzed sulfonylation of alkenes using sulfonyl fluorides

An oven-dried Schlenk tube equipped with a stirring bar was charged with sulfonyl fluoride (0.8mmol, 4 equiv), Cs2CO3 (0.4mmol, 2 equiv), NaBr (0.8mmol, 4 equiv), and [Au2(dppm)2]Cl2 (0.008mmol, 4mol%). The Schlenk tube was capped with a rubber septum, evacuated, refilled with nitrogen (3×), then alkene (0.2 mmol, 1 equiv), and degassed DMSO (2mL, 0.1 M) was added. The mixture was then sparged with nitrogen for 2 min and capped with a glass stopper under nitrogen flow. The reaction tube was sealed with parafilm, and the reaction was placed in an EvoluChem photoreactor (equipped with a fan, 365 nm light source). The reaction mixture was irradiated for 36 h with stirring at 700 to 800 rpm. The 18 tube was opened, and the reaction mixture was diluted with ethyl acetate and ice-cold brine. The layers were separated, and the aqueous layer was extracted with EtOAc (2 × 5 mL). The combined organic layers were dried (Na2SO4), filtered, and evaporated. Further, the resultant residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography to afford the desired product 4.
