Integrated assessment of drinking water quality and child nitrate health risk using indices and Monte Carlo simulation
Authors: Mohammad Naghiloo, Jalil Nassiri, Adel Mirza Alizadeh, Esrafil Asgari, Ghasem Mohammadi, Shahram Nazari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents an integrated assessment of the physicochemical quality and nitrate-related health risks associated with drinking water in Zanjan City, northwestern Iran. In 2024, fifty-six water samples were collected from groundwater wells, treatment plant outlets, and distribution networks, and analyzed for key parameters (pH, turbidity, EC, TDS, major ions, hardness, nitrate) following APHA (2017) standards. Water quality was evaluated using the water quality index and the Schoeller classification scheme to concurrently assess regulatory compliance and usability. WQI results indicated that 46.4% of samples were “Excellent,” 44.6% “Good,” and 5.4% “Moderate,” reflecting generally acceptable water quality from a health-based perspective. In contrast, the Schoeller classification revealed widespread usability concerns, with many samples classified as “Unsuitable” to “Non-potable” due to elevated total hardness, bicarbonate, and sodium of predominantly geogenic origin. Nitrate concentrations (5.3–45 mg/L) remained below WHO and national guidelines. However, probabilistic health risk assessment using Chronic Daily Intake, Target Hazard Quotient, and Monte Carlo simulation indicated a potential non-carcinogenic risk for children, whose mean THQ (1.04) slightly exceeded the safety threshold, while adults remained at low risk (THQ = 0.304). Monte Carlo analysis confirmed a significant probability of exceedance for children, attributable to higher water intake relative to body weight. The study concludes that although Zanjan’s drinking water largely meets health-based standards, its geogenically influenced mineral content impairs usability and poses a measurable nitrate risk to children, underscoring the need for continuous monitoring, targeted management, and preventive public health measures to ensure sustainable water resource safety.Keywords: Children’s health, Groundwater quality, Health risk assessment, Hydrogeochemistry, Nitrate exposure, Water safetySubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Risk factors

Introduction

Safe drinking water must meet appropriate quality standards, including both physical and chemical characteristics1. Among the key indicators of water quality is the measurement of major ion concentrations and other chemical parameters, which can provide a clear picture of the overall status of water resources. In recent years, climate change, the expansion of human activities, and the increased exploitation of groundwater resources have significantly impacted water quality in many regions worldwide2. One of the most pressing challenges, particularly in developing areas, is the rise in salinity and the alteration of the chemical composition of groundwater a phenomenon recognized as the most common form of groundwater contamination2,3. Traditional methods of water quality assessment, which rely on the direct comparison of measured values with national and international standards, are practical and straightforward but often fail to provide a comprehensive and actionable framework for water resource management4. To address this gap, composite indices such as the water quality index (WQI) and the Schoeller diagram have been developed to enable a more accurate and simplified evaluation of water quality5. The WQI, introduced in the 1970s, was designed to facilitate integrated and comprehensive water quality monitoring6. By combining multiple key parameters, this index generates a unified and easily interpretable representation of water quality, making it a powerful tool for assessing temporal and spatial changes in water resources. Similarly, the Schoeller diagram analyzes the ionic composition of water, offering valuable insights into its hardness, salinity, and overall quality. It has become widely used in hydrogeochemical studies and water quality monitoring5. Numerous studies around the world have demonstrated that the combined use of the WQI and Schoeller diagram can provide a more precise evaluation of water resources. For instance, Shil et al., in a study conducted in India, assessed water quality using the WQI and found that two out of fourteen sampling stations fell into the “very poor” category, while two others were classified as “poor”7. Likewise, Babiker et al., in Japan reported that groundwater quality in their study area was generally excellent, with WQI values exceeding 908. Concurrent analysis using the Schoeller diagram in the same region confirmed that the ionic composition of the water was stable and suitable for drinking. These findings highlight the potential of composite indices to deliver a more holistic understanding of the condition of water resources8.

In Iran, similar investigations have been conducted to evaluate groundwater quality. Rezvani et al., in a study on the quality of groundwater used for drinking purposes in Kashan County, employed water quality indices to assess chemical composition and suitability. Their findings revealed that ions such as sodium, sulfate, and chloride were directly associated with the presence of saline soils in certain areas, contributing to elevated levels of total dissolved solids (TDS) and electrical conductivity (EC), as well as disrupting the cation-anion balance in aquatic environments. Despite these localized challenges, the study indicated that more than half of the wells exhibited excellent water quality, confirming that groundwater in Kashan is largely suitable for drinking purposes9. Similarly, Karimi et al., examined the physical and chemical quality of drinking water in Ahvaz and compared the results with both national and World Health Organization (WHO) drinking water standards. They concluded that, aside from parameters such as EC, hardness, sodium, and chloride, all measured indicators met the national standard (ISIRI 1053) and WHO guidelines, suggesting that the overall sanitary quality of the city’s drinking water posed no significant health risks10. Conversely, studies conducted in arid and semi-arid regions of Iran, such as Rafsanjan, have highlighted that although a substantial portion of water resources maintain acceptable quality, certain areas face challenges related to high salinity or the presence of specific ions, necessitating more precise and localized management strategies11. Zanjan, located in northwestern Iran, has a cold, mountainous climate with seasonal rainfall that shapes its unique hydrogeological conditions and, consequently, the quality of its groundwater resources. A significant portion of the city’s drinking water supply comes from these underground sources12. However, rapid population growth, agricultural expansion, and the extensive use of chemical fertilizers in recent decades have raised concerns regarding potential changes in groundwater quality13. Under these circumstances, comprehensive monitoring of physical, chemical, and biological parameters particularly through composite indices such as WQI and the Schoeller diagram is essential to provide decision-makers and experts with an accurate understanding of current conditions and ongoing trends14. Beyond general water quality assessment, evaluating health risks associated with nitrate contamination holds particular importance. Due to its high stability and mobility in aquatic environments, nitrate is considered one of the most common pollutants in groundwater. Elevated nitrate concentrations in drinking water can lead to serious health issues, including methemoglobinemia, commonly known as “blue baby syndrome” in infants, and an increased risk of gastrointestinal and thyroid cancers. Consequently, the WHO and the Iranian national standard have set the maximum permissible nitrate concentration in drinking water at 50 mg/L15,16. Both domestic and international studies consistently demonstrate that nitrate contamination can become a major public health threat in communities heavily dependent on groundwater. For example, Aghapour et al., in a study conducted in Isfahan, reported that the non-carcinogenic health risk index, or target hazard quotient (THQ), exceeded safe levels for infants in certain regions17. Similarly, research by Golaki et al., in Kazerun revealed that nitrate posed a significant health risk to vulnerable age groups, emphasizing the urgent need for remedial measures to control and mitigate sources of contamination18. In Zanjan, scattered studies have indicated that, although the overall quality of water resources remains within acceptable standards, the rising concentration of nitrate in certain urban and rural areas is a growing concern. This issue underscores the need for a more precise assessment of health risks associated with nitrate exposure, particularly for vulnerable groups such as infants, children, and pregnant women. The calculation of parameters such as Chronic Daily Intake (CDI) and the non-carcinogenic HQ can provide a comprehensive perspective on potential health risks, offering valuable insights for decision-makers19. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to conduct a comprehensive, integrated evaluation of Zanjan’s drinking water quality and associated health risks. This is achieved by concurrently applying the WQI for regulatory compliance assessment, the Schoeller diagram for usability and palatability classification, and a probabilistic Monte Carlo simulation for a child-specific health risk assessment of nitrate exposure. This combined multi-method and spatial approach is designed to bridge the identified research gaps, providing a nuanced understanding that distinguishes predominant geogenic influences from potential anthropogenic pressures. The findings are expected to establish a scientific and practical foundation for the optimal management of Zanjan’s water resources. Specifically, the results will inform the design of targeted monitoring programs, support evidence-based strategies for pollution control and resource protection, and guide public health interventions, particularly for vulnerable sub-populations such as children. Ultimately, this work aims to contribute directly to safeguarding public health and advancing sustainable water resource management goals in Zanjan and similar hydrogeological settings.

Materials and methods

Study area

Zanjan city, the capital of Zanjan Province, is located in northwestern Iran (geographical coordinates: 36°40′–36°70′ N latitude and 47°30′–48°10′ E longitude). Situated in a mountainous region at an average elevation of approximately 1,638 m above sea level, the city experiences a cold, semi-arid climate. The average annual temperature is around 11°C, while annual precipitation ranges between 300 and 400 millimeters, with the majority falling during winter in the form of rain and snow. The geographical and climatic characteristics of Zanjan, including its mountainous terrain and cold climate, play a significant role in shaping the quality of its drinking water resources. The city’s water supply is derived primarily from groundwater sources (approximately 85%), with surface water contributing about 15%. As water percolates through soil and rock layers, it is influenced by both natural processes and human activities. Assessing water quality in this region is therefore of high importance, particularly as seasonal variations, the intensification of agricultural and urban activities, and the widespread use of chemical fertilizers have the potential to elevate nitrate concentrations in local water resources20. Given the critical importance of water as a vital resource and the associated implications for public health, evaluating the physical and chemical quality of drinking water, as well as analyzing nitrate-related health risks in Zanjan’s cold, mountainous environment, is essential. Sampling and sample preparation

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024 across various locations in Zanjan city. Water samples were collected as grab samples from different sources, including groundwater wells, treated water at the outlet of the treatment plant, and multiple points within the water distribution network. The sampling campaign comprised a total of 56 grab samples, distributed as follows: 40 samples from groundwater production wells, 4 samples from the outlet of the central treatment plant, and 12 samples from various points within the municipal distribution network. This distribution reflects the approximate supply composition of Zanjan’s drinking water, which is sourced from approximately 90% groundwater and 10% treated surface water. To enhance accuracy, each parameter was analyzed in triplicate, yielding a total of 168 samples, and the mean values were reported as the final data. Prior to sampling from groundwater wells, each well was pumped for 10 to 15 min to flush out stagnant water and ensure a representative sample. Immediately after collection, samples were transported under controlled conditions to the Zanjan Water and Wastewater Laboratory, where they underwent physical and chemical analyses. Clean 1.0 L polyethylene bottles, prewashed with distilled water and thoroughly dried, were used for collecting samples to prevent any potential contamination. Prior to analysis for major ions, all samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate membrane filter to remove suspended particulates. Samples were then preserved and analyzed according to Standard Methods (APHA, 2017)21. The analytical techniques for all reported physicochemical parameters were as follows: (a) pH and residual chlorine: Measured in situ using a calibrated portable digital pH meter and a DPD (N, N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine) reagent-based test kit, respectively, (b) Turbidity: Determined turbidimetrically, 3) EC and TDS: Measured using a WTW MultiLine multiparameter instrument; TDS was also verified gravimetrically, 4) Major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+): Total hardness, calcium, and magnesium were determined by complexometric titration with EDTA using Eriochrome Black T indicator. Sodium and potassium concentrations were measured by flame photometry22, 5) Major anions (Cl−, SO42−, NO3−, HCO3−): Chloride was analyzed by argentometric titration (Mohr method). Sulfate was determined by the turbidimetric method. Nitrate concentration was quantified using a HACH DR 5000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer via the cadmium reduction method (SM 4500-NO3). Bicarbonate was calculated from alkalinity titrations22. All analytical procedures, including instrument calibration and quality control (using blanks, duplicates, and certified reference materials), followed established protocols to ensure data accuracy and precision. The comprehensive approach to quality assurance and control in water analysis, as outlined in contemporary guidelines, was adhered to throughout the study.

Monte Carlo simulation of nitrate

Recent investigations underscore the significance of groundwater nitrate contamination and associated human health risks, with hydrogeochemical characterization and health risk estimation revealing that even moderate nitrate levels can contribute to non-carcinogenic hazards when exposure parameters are considered in risk models24. Complementary synthesis of exposure and toxicity factors highlights the need for standardized ingestion rates, body weight, and exposure duration parameters to improve the comparability and accuracy of water quality health risk assessments across studies25. Together, these studies provide a rigorous framework for quantifying human exposure pathways and assessing risk thresholds for contaminants in groundwater. Integrating such detailed exposure metrics strengthens the methodological basis for assessing nitrate-related health outcomes in drinking water risk assessments. Several quantitative approaches have been developed to assess the potential health risks associated with nitrate exposure through drinking water. In this study, a non-carcinogenic risk assessment was conducted for children and adults based on nitrate concentrations in drinking water, following the criteria outlined in Table 2. Health risks were evaluated using multiple internationally recognized metrics, including chronic daily intake (CDI) and target hazard quotient (THQ), providing a robust assessment of long-term, non-carcinogenic effects of nitrate exposure. This integrated approach enables a more comprehensive characterization of health risks, aligning with best practices in environmental and public health risk assessment. These indices were employed to evaluate the potential adverse health effects of nitrate consumption in both children and adults, based on established exposure assessment equations. Monte Carlo simulation (MCS) is a powerful stochastic modeling technique that allows for the quantification of variability and uncertainty in exposure assessments by repeatedly sampling input parameters according to their respective probability distributions. In the present study, empirical nitrate exposure data were fitted to an appropriate Beta distribution, which effectively captured the skewness and bounded nature of the observed data. The MCS model was executed with 100,000 iterations, providing a highly robust and stable estimate of the probabilistic distribution of the Target Hazard Quotient (THQ). From this distribution, the 5th percentile, mean, and 95th percentile were extracted to represent low-end, central, and high-end exposure scenarios, respectively. These percentiles enable a nuanced characterization of non-carcinogenic health risks, reflecting both typical consumption patterns and extreme exposures that may occur among the top 2.5% of the population. By incorporating the full range of variability in key input parameters, including nitrate concentrations, water ingestion rates, and body weight, MCS allows for the estimation of the probability that children’s nitrate exposure exceeds established safety thresholds, providing a scientifically rigorous basis for risk management decisions. Furthermore, this approach facilitates sensitivity analysis, identifying the most influential variables that drive risk and highlighting priority areas for mitigation or further research, thereby supporting a comprehensive and evidence-based assessment of public health risks associated with nitrate exposure.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means, and standard deviations, and analyzed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 17.0. Differences among groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. To account for variability and uncertainty in exposure parameters, a probabilistic risk assessment was conducted using Monte Carlo simulation (MCS), implemented with Crystal Ball software, version 11.1.2.3 (Oracle Corporation, Redwood Shores, CA, USA).

The pH values of water samples ranged from 6.3 to 7.8, indicating neutral to slightly alkaline conditions, which align with WHO and ISIRI standards. Such pH levels are suitable for drinking purposes and help prevent corrosion in the distribution network. The low turbidity levels (0.18–1.34 NTU) reflect the effective performance of water treatment processes and indicate minimal suspended solids and low risk of severe microbial contamination. The mean TDS was approximately 367 mg/L, suggesting an overall acceptable taste quality. However, the maximum recorded value (1000 mg/L) approached the permissible limit, potentially indicating localized sources with elevated mineral content.

The drinking water in Zanjan exhibits high TH, ranging from 124.7 to 863.5 mg/L. While the average hardness (266 mg/L) is relatively acceptable, the extremely high maximum values are atypical and may cause operational issues such as scale formation in heating systems and reduced effectiveness of detergents. The primary source of this hardness is the dissolution of limestone and dolomitic formations in the region. Additionally, bicarbonate concentrations ranged from 372 to 3,538 mg/L, indicating strong alkalinity, which contributes to the water’s slightly bitter and unpleasant taste. Elevated levels of calcium and magnesium in certain samples were also observed, consistent with high hardness levels, and could result in minor laxative effects in sensitive individuals.

In some samples, sodium levels exceeded the permissible limit, reaching a maximum of 386.8 mg/L, posing potential health risks for individuals with hypertension or kidney disorders. In contrast, chloride and sulfate concentrations remained within safe ranges, indicating a relatively low risk of contamination from industrial pollutants. Nitrate levels showed a mean of 19.9 mg/L and a maximum of 45 mg/L, remaining below the WHO guideline limit. While concentrations above 25 mg/L may indicate agricultural runoff infiltration, this study did not detect nitrate levels posing significant health risks. Fluoride concentrations in some samples were found to be below the optimal threshold, suggesting a need for supplementary fluoride sources to help prevent dental caries. Therefore, the drinking water quality in Zanjan is acceptable with respect to major parameters and key ions; however, high hardness, elevated bicarbonate levels, and excessive sodium concentrations in certain areas highlight the need for continuous monitoring and targeted management. These findings are consistent with observations in other regions of Iran, such as Divandarreh in Kurdistan and Kashan9,36. For areas where hardness and sodium levels are elevated, the use of household reverse osmosis (RO) systems is recommended to improve water quality. Furthermore, the low fluoride levels could negatively affect dental health, making public health awareness campaigns essential, especially for sensitive groups such as cardiac and renal patients, as well as promoting the use of fluoridated salt to compensate for fluoride deficiency. Although nitrate concentrations are within safe limits and pose no significant health risks, periodic monitoring is advised given the region’s agricultural activities. In general, maintaining and improving the drinking water quality in Zanjan requires: (a) effective management of hardness-causing sources, (b) continuous monitoring and control of contaminants, and (c) strategic supplementation of essential elements like fluoride to protect public health and enhance consumer satisfaction14. Additionally, more detailed hydrogeochemical studies are recommended to accurately identify the sources contributing to elevated concentrations. Where feasible, blending high-concentration sources with higher-quality water could serve as a practical approach for optimized water resource management.

The physicochemical assessment of Zanjan’s drinking water reveals a profile largely compliant with regulatory standards, yet defined by significant geogenic influences. The predominance of neutral to slightly alkaline pH (6.3–7.8) and low turbidity (< 1.34 NTU) indicates effective treatment and corrosion-stable distribution, which are fundamental for microbial safety and infrastructure integrity37. However, the primary water quality challenge stems from the region’s carbonate-rich geology. The elevated total hardness (up to 863.5 mg/L as CaCO3) and exceptionally high bicarbonate concentrations (372–3538 mg/L) are directly attributable to the weathering and dissolution of limestone and dolomitic formations, a common phenomenon in similar aquifer systems globally12,14. This results in water with strong temporary hardness and alkalinity, which can impair palatability, promote scaling, and reduce detergent efficiency. Furthermore, sodium levels exceeding the 200 mg/L guideline in some samples, concurrent with high bicarbonate, suggest natural ion exchange processes or weathering of sodium-rich minerals, posing a consideration for individuals on sodium-restricted diets (WHO, 2017). Interestingly, while nitrate concentrations remain below the 50 mg/L limit, the mean value (19.9 mg/L) and instances above 25 mg/L warrant vigilant monitoring given the region’s agricultural activity, as such levels can be a precursor to more significant contamination17. The sub-optimal fluoride levels observed align with findings in other semi-arid regions of Iran and highlight a potential public health gap for dental caries prevention, suggesting the need for structured supplementation programs10. This geogenically driven quality profile, characterized by high mineral content but low anthropogenic pollution (evidenced by low chloride/sulfate), underscores that management strategies should prioritize aesthetic and operational treatment (e.g., softening, blending) and targeted public health measures over broad pollution remediation.

Areas classified as Good include most central city samples (e.g., hospitals, Kochmeshki, Karmandan town, Amirkabir) and certain Sayan wells (wells 36 and 37). WQI values in these areas generally range from 55 to 85, indicating acceptable water quality suitable for drinking and hygiene purposes, though not ideal. Some industrial regions, such as the Eshragh industrial town (WQI between 59.3 and 90.7), also fall into this category, reflecting proper water resource management and pollution control in these areas. Areas with Moderate water quality include Hosseiniyeh (WQI = 156.2), Honarestan (WQI = 124.3), and Ghods town (WQI = 193.2). This indicates a decline in water quality, which may result from specific geological conditions (e.g., dissolution of gypsum or salt formations), human-induced pollution, inadequate drainage, or the infiltration of contaminants (domestic or industrial wastewater) into groundwater, as well as local hydrogeochemical characteristics. A more detailed investigation of chemical and microbial parameters in these areas can help identify the underlying causes of reduced water quality. High WQI values in these critical samples (Ghods town, Hosseiniyeh, Honarestan) primarily correspond with elevated concentrations of sodium, bicarbonate, TH, and TDS, as previously identified. This confirms that the main water quality issue in Zanjan is geogenic in nature rather than anthropogenic, although human activities may contribute in certain cases. Therefore, the data suggest that most areas exhibit desirable water quality, with only a limited number of locations requiring further investigation and management measures to enhance water quality. The primary source of quality issues is the regional geology, although the impact of human activities in some areas cannot be ignored. The observed differences between urban, industrial, and groundwater well areas emphasize the importance of continuous monitoring and strategic planning of water resources. For optimal water management, it is recommended that: (a) continuous monitoring of Moderate quality sites (Ghods town, Hosseiniyeh, Honarestan) be implemented; (b) water from lower-quality sources be blended with higher-quality sources; and (c) advanced treatment systems (e.g., reverse osmosis) be employed in critical areas38. When compared with these previous investigations, the findings of our study suggest that groundwater resources in Zanjan are generally of acceptable quality according to the WQI. In a nationwide study conducted by Mohebbi et al., the applicability of the WQI for evaluating groundwater resources across Iran was examined. Their findings indicated that the modified WQI classified approximately 85% of the groundwater sources as having good quality, which is consistent with the results of the present study39. Similarly, Khosravi et al. assessed groundwater quality in Birjand for drinking purposes using the WQI. Their results revealed that 10.5% of the sources were categorized as excellent, 31.57% as good, 36.84% as poor, 15.7% as very poor, and 5% as unsuitable for drinking40. In another study, Al-Hadithi et al. evaluated groundwater quality in the Haridwar region of India, reporting that 48% of the sources fell within the “very good” range, another 48% in the “good” range, and 4% in the “very poor” category41. By contrast, Ketata-Rokbani et al. investigated groundwater in the El Khairat aquifer of northeastern Tunisia and reported that more than 82% of the sources were classified as poor to very poor in quality42. However, it is important to acknowledge that the WQI does not encompass all relevant water quality parameters, particularly toxic heavy metals, and therefore cannot provide a definitive conclusion regarding overall water safety. To safeguard water quality in the long term, sustainable groundwater management and the prevention of overextraction should remain a priority. The most significant limitation of this study is the lack of data on heavy metal concentrations in the analyzed samples. Future research should therefore incorporate additional parameters, such as heavy metals and emerging contaminants, into the WQI framework to provide a more comprehensive assessment of groundwater quality. In this study, the geographical pattern of water quality was described based on sampling point locations. For a more comprehensive visual representation of the spatial distribution of the Water Quality Index across the city, the creation of interpolation maps is recommended for future studies. The water quality challenges identified in Zanjan particularly elevated hardness, bicarbonate, sodium, and nitrate are not isolated but resonate with hydrogeochemical patterns documented in similar semi-arid regions globally. For instance, in northern India, integrated assessments combining Water Quality Indexing (WQI) with geochemical modelling and machine learning have revealed comparable issues of high TDS, fluoride, and nitrate, often linked to natural weathering of carbonate and fluoride-bearing minerals43,44. These studies underscore the importance of distinguishing between geogenically derived constituents, such as hardness and bicarbonate in Zanjan, and anthropogenic inputs like nitrate, which may signal increasing agricultural pressure. The application of advanced predictive modelling, including Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and Random Forest (RF), in studies, demonstrates the growing role of data-driven approaches in identifying contamination patterns and forecasting water quality degradation45. In Zanjan, where geology plays a dominant role, such methodologies could help refine source apportionment and anticipate temporal changes in water chemistry, especially under growing extraction and land-use pressures.

Classification of drinking water quality based on the Schoeller diagram

Evaluating the quality of drinking water is a critical component of water resource management and public health protection. The Schoeller diagram is a well-established tool that allows qualitative classification of water based on dissolved mineral concentrations and TH. In this study, water samples from different regions were assessed using this method to determine their hygienic status and suitability for drinking (Fig. 2). The results were evaluated based on various chemical parameters, providing a comprehensive understanding of the water’s appropriateness for consumption. The water quality was interpreted across six categories: Good, Acceptable, Moderate, Unsuitable, Emergency Non-Potable, and Completely Non-Potable. A comparison of the studied samples with the Schoeller standard, including the parameter ranges and the number of samples in each category, is presented in Table 6. The analysis reveals clear patterns of regional water quality challenges. According to the Schoeller standard, the drinking water quality in Zanjan can be summarized as follows: (a) Water hardness is extremely high across most parts of the city. Among the 56 samples, 17 samples (e.g., samples 44, 51, 42, 33, 34, 35, 46, 54) fall into the “Unsuitable” category (above 1000 mg/L). This high hardness, primarily caused by calcium and magnesium, can lead to scaling in pipes and heating equipment and renders the water unsuitable for industrial and domestic use; (b) In terms of TDS, nineteen samples (e.g., samples 44, 51, 42, 33, 34, 35, 46) fall within the moderate range (1000–2000 mg/L). While this TDS level is not immediately hazardous for drinking, it indicates a high mineral content that may adversely affect taste. Sample 44 (Hosseiniyeh) is on the boundary between the moderate and unsuitable range (TDS = 1000 mg/L); (c) Bicarbonate parameter shows the most critical condition. All 56 samples have very high bicarbonate concentrations (above 600 mg/L), and 27 samples (48%) exceed the non-potable threshold (> 1200 mg/L). Such elevated bicarbonate significantly increases water alkalinity, imparting a bitter and unpleasant taste; (d) In terms of sodium, sixteen samples (29%) including samples 44, 51, 42, 49, 40, and 33 have sodium concentrations in the non-potable range (> 920 mg/L). Long-term consumption of high-sodium water may pose health risks, particularly for individuals with hypertension or kidney disorders. The worst-quality samples were identified as 44 (Hosseiniyeh), 51 (Honarestan), 42 (Shohada town), 33 (Eshragh industrial town 2), 46 (Gavazang), and 54 (Fatemieh district). These samples simultaneously exhibit critical levels of hardness, TDS, bicarbonate, and sodium, representing the most unfavorable water quality scenarios. Conversely, wells in the Bonab area (particularly wells 9.0–20) and Pounak town (sample 55) show relatively better conditions, representing areas with comparatively higher water quality. However, even in these locations, bicarbonate and hardness remain in the undesirable range. The analysis of different parameters indicates that bicarbonate and sodium are the most critical, chloride and sulfate are generally within acceptable ranges, and TH and TDS exceed permissible limits in many samples. Therefore, based on the Schoeller standard, drinking water in Zanjan city falls within the “Unsuitable” to “Non-Potable” range for key parameters such as TH, TDS, and major ion concentrations. Although toxic contaminants (e.g., sulfate and chloride) are within permissible limits, the extremely high concentrations of minerals particularly bicarbonate, hardness, and sodium significantly reduce sensory quality (taste, odor, and palatability) and public acceptability46. Therefore, based on the Schoeller-based qualitative classification scheme (Table 6), drinking water in Zanjan city falls within the ‘Unsuitable’ to ‘Non-Potable’ range for key parameters such as TH, TDS, and major ion concentrations. Also, water is unsuitable for use in boilers or heating systems without proper softening treatment.

Health risk assessment of nitrate

Health risk assessment is a scientific approach that is widely used in food safety appraisals. CDI is a central concept in health risk assessment, reflecting the average daily amount of a chemical contaminant consumed by an individual over a prolonged exposure period. This parameter forms the foundation for evaluating non-carcinogenic risks, which are typically determined by comparing the estimated CDI with a reference dose (RfD) to calculate the hazard quotient. The CDI is derived using Eq. (4), where C represents contaminant concentration (mg/L), IR the ingestion rate (L/day), EF the exposure frequency (days/year), ED the exposure duration (years), BW the individual’s body weight (kg), and AT the averaging time (days). For non-carcinogenic assessments, AT corresponds to the product of EF and ED. Using nitrate levels measured in drinking water (C = 19.94 mg/L), CDI values were estimated for different age categories. The results indicated that children had a CDI of 1.662 mg/kg bw/day, while adults exhibited a value of 0.486 mg/kg bw/day. Thus, children experienced an intake approximately 3.4 times greater than that of adults, largely attributable to their smaller body weight combined with a higher relative water consumption rate. These calculated CDI values are critical for subsequent HQ estimation and provide an essential quantitative basis for determining the likelihood of adverse non-carcinogenic health outcomes linked to nitrate exposure through drinking water. The THQ is a widely used metric in health risk assessment, representing the ratio of an individual’s CDI of a contaminant to its established RfD (1.6 mg/kg bw/day for nitrate) (Eq. (5))47. This parameter helps determine the likelihood of non-carcinogenic health effects. A THQ equal to or greater than one generally signals a possible health concern, whereas a value below one indicates that significant risk is unlikely. Using the previously calculated CDI values, the THQ for children was estimated at 1.04, marginally surpassing the safety threshold and suggesting a potential non-carcinogenic risk for this group. For adults, the THQ was calculated as 0.304, a value well under one, pointing to a minimal probability of adverse health outcomes. These findings underscore the heightened susceptibility of children to nitrate exposure compared to adults, a difference largely driven by physiological and behavioral factors such as body weight and intake rates. Consequently, ongoing surveillance and targeted risk management strategies are particularly important to safeguard children’s health under current exposure conditions.

MCS results assessing the THQ from nitrate exposure through drinking water consumption among Iranian children and adults indicate significant health concerns especially in the children’s group (Fig. 3). For children, the distribution shows a mean THQ of 1.038, with the 5th and 95th percentiles ranging from 0.333 to 1.869. This indicates that while some children fall below the threshold of 1.0, a substantial proportion of the population may exceed it, suggesting a potential non-carcinogenic risk for this age group under current exposure conditions. In contrast, adults exhibit a markedly lower risk profile, with a mean THQ of 0.303 and a 5th–95th percentile range of 0.095 to 0.548. These values remain consistently below the critical value of 1.0, confirming that nitrate exposure at the measured concentrations is unlikely to pose significant health risks to the adult population. Comparing the two groups highlights a clear difference in vulnerability: children not only have higher average THQs but also a broader upper range that surpasses the threshold of concern. Adults, by contrast, remain well within the safe margin. This disparity can be attributed to children’s higher water intake relative to body weight, which amplifies their exposure. Therefore, the findings emphasize that adults are at minimal risk, whereas children may face potential health concerns, particularly those at the higher end of the exposure distribution. Continuous monitoring and preventive measures are therefore crucial, with particular attention directed toward safeguarding children’s health.

The magnitude of non-carcinogenic health risk from nitrate exposure varies significantly between children and adults. Using the CDI approach, children were estimated to have a CDI of 1.662 mg/kg bw/day, which is approximately 3.4 times higher than adults (0.486 mg/kg bw/day), mainly due to their lower body weight and higher relative water consumption. The THQ further quantifies this risk: children had a mean THQ of 1.04, slightly exceeding the safety threshold of 1, indicating a potential non-carcinogenic risk. Adults, in contrast, had a THQ of 0.304, well below the threshold, suggesting minimal health concern. MCS results reinforced these findings by capturing population variability: for children, the THQ ranged from 0.333 (5th percentile) to 1.869 (95th percentile), highlighting that a substantial fraction of children may exceed the safe limit. Adults exhibited a narrower and lower range (0.095–0.548), remaining consistently below the threshold. Therefore, these results indicate that children are considerably more susceptible to nitrate-related non-carcinogenic health risks than adults, emphasizing the need for focused risk management and protective measures for this vulnerable group.

The present study’s finding that children are at higher non-carcinogenic risk from nitrate exposure aligns consistently with health risk assessments conducted in fluoride- and nitrate-affected regions of India, where probabilistic methods such as Monte Carlo simulation have been widely applied. Research in Agra, Uttar Pradesh, repeatedly identified children as the most vulnerable subgroup due to lower body weight and higher water intake per kilogram, resulting in HQ values often exceeding safe thresholds45,48. These studies emphasize that even when average contaminant concentrations remain below regulatory limits, variability in exposure parameters can lead to significant risk for sensitive populations. The use of sensitivity analysis within probabilistic frameworks further clarifies which factors most often contaminant concentration and ingestion rate drive health risks, offering clear targets for intervention44. In Zanjan, although nitrate levels are currently within WHO guidelines, the elevated risk for children highlighted by Monte Carlo simulation calls for a similarly precautionary approach: continuous monitoring, targeted risk communication, and consideration of remedial options such as blending or point-of-use treatment in vulnerable zones.

Suggested integrated management and surveillance interventions

Based on the observed water quality profile characterized by geogenically elevated hardness, bicarbonate, sodium, and nitrate levels approaching concern for children the following integrated management and monitoring interventions are recommended to ensure long-term public health protection and sustainable resource use in Zanjan City:

Implement managed blending of groundwater from high-hardness/high-sodium sources with lower-mineral water from better-quality wells or treated surface water. In critical zones (e.g., areas identified with “Moderate” WQI or “Unsuitable” Schoeller classification), centralized or community-scale softening (e.g., lime softening) or reverse osmosis units could be considered to improve palatability and reduce scaling.

Conclusion

This study conducted an integrated evaluation of the physicochemical quality and associated nitrate health risks of drinking water resources in Zanjan City, northwestern Iran. The findings reveal a dual-character water profile: from a regulatory health perspective, the WQI classifies the majority of samples as “Excellent” to “Good,” confirming general compliance with WHO and national standards. However, a more nuanced assessment using the Schoeller classification scheme exposes significant usability limitations, with substantial portions of the water supply rated “Unsuitable” to “Non-potable” due to geogenically elevated total hardness, bicarbonate, and sodium. This divergence underscores the critical need to complement standard health-based indices with usability-focused tools for a complete resource assessment. Although nitrate concentrations remained below guideline values, a probabilistic health risk assessment using Monte Carlo simulation identified children as a vulnerable subgroup, with their THQ marginally exceeding the safety threshold, while adult risk remained low. Consequently, sustainable water management in Zanjan must adopt a two-pronged strategy: first, implementing continuous monitoring and source-specific interventions such as blending or targeted treatment to address aesthetic and operational challenges posed by high mineral content; and second, instituting protective public health measures focused on children, including enhanced surveillance of nitrate trends in vulnerable zones and targeted risk communication. The integrated methodology and findings presented here provide a replicable framework and actionable insights for evidence-based water resource management and public health protection in Zanjan and other semi-arid regions with similar hydrogeological characteristics.
