Racial trends in outcomes and access to treatment for mitral regurgitation: a South London population cohort study
Authors: Omar Chehab, Sudhamshu Gummadavelly, Vitaliy Androshchuk, Edouard Long, Harminder S Gill, Natalie Lina Montarello, Can Zhou, Camelia Demetrescu, Sahrai Saeed, Tiffany Patterson, Bernard Prendergast, Simon Redwood, Ronak Rajani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRacial disparities in valvular heart disease are well-documented, yet evidence specific to mitral regurgitation (MR) remains fragmented. This study examined racial differences in mortality and treatment access among patients with moderate-severe MR within a universal healthcare system.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilised digital health records from a South London tertiary centre using natural language processing (NLP), including demographic, clinical, echocardiographic and socioeconomic data. Adult patients with moderate or greater MR diagnosed between 2010 and 2023 were stratified by ethnicity (White, Black, Asian). Primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and surgical/transcatheter intervention rates. Multivariable Cox regression and competing risk analyses adjusted for demographics, MR characteristics, comorbidities and socioeconomic deprivation.ResultsThe cohort (n=6665) comprised 5331 (80.0%) White, 958 (14.4%) Black and 376 (5.6%) Asian patients with under-represented minority proportions relative to local demographics. Black patients presented younger (65.4 vs 72.2 years; p <0.001) with greater socio-economic deprivation (median IMD decile 3 vs 5), higher comorbidity burden and predominantly secondary MR (91% vs 85%; p <0.001). Crude mortality rates were 103.8, 88.1 and 80.4 per 1000 person-years for White, Black and Asian patients respectively. After adjustment, ethnicity was not independently associated with mortality (Black vs White: HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.80 to 1.00; p=0.06) or intervention (OR 0.81; 95% CI 0.59 to 1.11; p=0.18). Socioeconomic deprivation was independently associated with mortality (HR 0.97/IMD decile increase; p <0.001) and intervention rates. Crude intervention rates were lower in Black than in White patients (6.9% vs 12.7%), this difference attenuated after adjustment for MR aetiology, comorbidities and socioeconomic deprivation and did not persist as an independent association (OR 0.81; 95% CI 0.59 to 1.11; p=0.18).ConclusionMR appears under-detected among minority patients relative to local demographics and occurs at a younger age and with a greater prevalence of secondary MR and comorbidity. Once identified, clinical factors and socioeconomic deprivation rather than ethnicity itself drive outcomes and treatment access, with no statistically independent association between ethnicity and either mortality or intervention, although modest but clinically relevant differences cannot be excluded given the cohort sizes. These findings highlight the need for enhanced valve disease detection in diverse communities alongside strategies addressing socioeconomic determinants of cardiovascular health.

Introduction

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is the most prevalent valvular heart disease in developed countries,1 2 and among those aged ≥75 years, moderate or severe MR affects 10%–16% of the population.3 This demographic shift is further compounded by the increasing incidence of secondary MR due to heart failure, with overall prevalence expected to double before 2050.3 As such, the burden of MR will continue to increase, placing escalating demands on global healthcare systems.4

Racial and ethnic disparities in cardiovascular disease outcomes are well-documented across multiple domains, although the terminology applied across the literature has not always been consistent. In valvular heart disease, large-scale population studies from the United States have demonstrated marked disparities in diagnosis, treatment access, and outcomes.5 The National Inpatient Sample and Society of Thoracic Surgeons databases reveal that Black and Hispanic patients with aortic stenosis (AS) undergo valve replacement at rates 2–3-fold lower than White patients, despite presenting at a younger age with more advanced disease.6 7 Similar patterns emerge in structural heart interventions, where minorities comprise only 4%–8% of patients undergoing transcatheter procedures despite them accounting for substantially larger proportions of the elderly population.6 Similar work on AS in South London demonstrates that Black patients present later with more advanced disease and have lower treatment rates and higher mortality.8

Evidence of racial disparities for MR outcomes and treatment remains sparse and incomplete. Recent studies demonstrate that Black patients undergoing mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) present 10–15 years younger and with higher comorbidity burdens than White patients, experience similar short-term outcomes, but face worse long-term mortality and higher rates of re-admission.9 10 However, most evidence focuses on procedural outcomes rather than upstream access barriers and inadequately examines the intersection of race and socioeconomic factors that influence treatment access and outcomes.

The principal aims of the present study were to characterise racial and ethnic disparities in MR outcomes and treatment access in a large south London population cohort. Secondary aims were to examine all-cause mortality (ACM) trends, valve intervention rates across a diverse population while accounting for socioeconomic deprivation (SED) and disease severity.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted under the RAIDER Research Database ethics approval from the Health Research Authority (REC 22/NW/0298) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective observational design.

Patient and public involvement

This is a retrospective observational study, and as such patients were not directly involved in the investigational design. The findings of this research are highly relevant to local and wider patient communities and will be disseminated as part of an effort to improve the detection of valvular heart disease.

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study utilised electronic health records (EHRs) from Guy’s and St Thomas’ NHS Foundation Trust, a multi-site tertiary academic medical centre serving as the regional referral centre for structural heart disease in South London. Adult patients (≥18 years) with at least moderate native MR (grade ≥2+) diagnosed on transthoracic echocardiography between January 2010 and November 2023 were eligible for inclusion. Severity of MR was graded according to contemporary guideline recommendations.1113 Patients with a history of prior MV surgery were excluded.

Patients were stratified by self-reported ethnicity into three groups: White, Black and Asian. Those with ethnicity recorded as ‘other’, ‘not specified’ or mixed ethnic background (n=1860) and those with missing Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) data (n=70) were excluded. Follow-up continued until death or censoring at the study end date (10 April 2025), and zero follow-up was retained as a valid baseline event. The study flow is summarised in online supplemental figure 1.

Terminology

The ethnicity variable used in this study reflects self-reported categorical information recorded within the NHS EHR at Guy’s and St Thomas’, corresponding to what is captured as race in other healthcare systems. We use ‘ethnicity’ when referring to our cohort and ‘racial and ethnic’ when describing the wider literature, with no intended distinction between the two terms.

Data extraction and processing

Echocardiographic data were extracted from TOMCAT Systems, the institutional reporting platform. Patients with moderate or greater MR were identified using an artificial intelligence-based natural language processing (NLP) pipeline (CogStack) applied to free-text echocardiography reports. MR severity was graded using conventional semi-quantitative classification (2+, 3+, 4+) based on integrated assessment of qualitative and quantitative parameters.

MR aetiology was classified as primary or secondary based on valve morphology described in the echocardiographic reports. Primary MR was identified using predefined search terms, including ‘prolapse’, ‘flail’, ‘Barlow’s’, ‘chordal rupture’, ‘ruptured chord’, and ‘perforation’. Secondary MR was defined by the absence of primary valve pathology in the context of ventricular or atrial dilatation and dysfunction.

Demographic data, including self-reported ethnicity, age and sex, were extracted from the structured EHR. Comorbidity data were ascertained from coded diagnoses using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) criteria. Socioeconomic status was determined using the IMD, derived from patient residential postcodes and linked to the English Indices of Deprivation 2019 dataset.14 The IMD is the UK government’s official measure of relative deprivation at small-area level, integrating 39 indicators across seven domains including income, employment, education, health, crime, housing and living environment. Patients were assigned to IMD deciles, with decile 1 representing the most deprived 10% of areas nationally.14

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was ACM. Secondary outcomes included mitral valve intervention (surgical repair or replacement or M-TEER) and time to and intervention rates. Mortality data were ascertained through linkage with national death registration records, supplemented by hospital mortality data. Interventions were identified using OPCS-4 procedural codes and validated against departmental procedural databases.

Statistical methods

Baseline comparators

Baseline characteristics were summarised by ethnicity using means with SD or medians with IQRs for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons used analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, and χ2 tests for categorical variables. All tests were two-sided with p <0.05 denoting statistical significance.

Survival analysis

Crude mortality rates were expressed per 1000 person-years. Kaplan-Meier curves estimated cumulative survival, compared using the log-rank test, with rates reported at 1, 3, 5 and 10 years.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression identified factors associated with mortality. A staged modelling approach evaluated potential mediators of ethnic disparities: Model 1 adjusted for demographics (age, sex); Model 2 additionally for MR characteristics (type and severity); Model 3 for comorbidities; and Model 4 for socioeconomic status (IMD decile). Comorbidities were modelled as time-varying covariates using the counting process formulation to avoid immortal time bias.

Kaplan-Meier plots for all-cause mortality stratified according to ethnicity.

Intervention analysis

For mitral valve intervention, cause-specific Cox regression was performed with death as a competing risk; prior myocardial infarction was replaced by coronary artery disease to ensure model stability. Multivariable logistic regression was also performed for this outcome.

Scaled Schoenfeld residuals verified the proportional hazards assumption. Primary exposures satisfied the assumption with stable trajectories over 11 years. Clinical covariates demonstrated time-dependency; sensitivity analyses confirmed primary coefficients were unaltered, and average HRs are reported.

Adjusted curves were generated for a reference patient (mean cohort age, female, primary MR, no comorbidities) stratified by ethnicity, IMD, income and education. HRs and ORs are presented with 95% CIs. All analyses used R V.4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

From 8595 patients with at least moderate MR diagnosed between January 2010 and November 2023, we excluded 1860 with ethnicity recorded as ‘other’, ‘not specified’ or mixed backgrounds and 70 for missing IMD data (online supplemental figure 1). The final cohort therefore comprised 6665 patients: 5331 (80.0%) White, 958 (14.4%) Black and 376 (5.6%) Asian.

Baseline characteristics differed substantially between groups (table 1). White patients were oldest at diagnosis (mean 72.2±14.4 years), followed by Asian (67.3±15.9 years) and Black patients (65.4±16.2 years; p <0.001). Women comprised 57% of Black patients compared with 45% in both White and Asian groups (p <0.001). Black patients resided in more deprived areas, with a median IMD decile of 3 (IQR 2–4) versus 4 (IQR 3–6) for Asian and 5 (IQR 3–7) for White patients (p<0.001).

Secondary MR predominated across all groups (86% overall) but was more prevalent in Black (91%) than White (85%) or Asian patients (87%; p <0.001). The distribution of MR severity did not differ significantly between ethnic groups (p=0.23), but comorbidity profile varied markedly: Black patients had higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, dialysis dependence and prior stroke, while White patients more frequently had atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease and chronic obstructive airways disease (all p <0.001).

ACM

During follow-up, 3207 deaths (48.1%) occurred. Crude mortality rates were 103.8, 88.1 and 80.4 per 1000 person-years in White, Black and Asian patients, respectively. Mean age at death was 79.8±11.7 years for White patients, 73.7±15.3 years for Black patients and 76.2±11.6 years for Asian patients (table 2).

Multivariable analysis of mortality

Sequential Cox regression models revealed progressive attenuation of ethnic associations with mortality (table 3). In the age- and sex-adjusted model (Model 1), Black ethnicity was associated with higher mortality compared with White patients (HR 1.15; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.28; p=0.011). This association was attenuated following adjustment for MR characteristics (Model 2: HR 1.08; 95% CI 0.97 to 1.20; p=0.146), with secondary MR conferring a more than two-fold increase in mortality risk (HR 2.19; 95% CI 1.92 to 2.49; p<0.001). Further adjustment for comorbidities (Model 3) rendered the association non-significant (HR 0.95; 95% CI 0.85 to 1.05; p=0.31).

In the fully adjusted model incorporating socio-economic status (Model 4), neither Black (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.80 to 1.00; p=0.06) nor Asian ethnicity (HR 0.91; 95% CI 0.78 to 1.07; p=0.27) were independently associated with mortality. The strongest predictors of mortality were dialysis (HR 3.63; 95% CI 3.24 to 4.07; p<0.001), chronic obstructive airways disease (HR 1.62; 95% CI 1.48 to 1.77; p<0.001), secondary MR (HR 1.98; 95% CI 1.74 to 2.26; p<0.001) and MR severity (HR 1.10 per grade; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.16; p<0.001).

Socioeconomic associations with mortality

Each decile increase in IMD (representing less deprivation) was associated with a 3% reduction in mortality in the overall cohort (HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.95 to 0.98; p<0.001). Ethnicity-stratified analyses demonstrated that this association was significant in White patients (HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.95 to 0.98; p<0.001) but not in Black (HR 1.01; 95% CI 0.96 to 1.07; p=0.76) or Asian patients (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.87 to 1.00; p=0.05). Adjusted survival estimates stratified according to deprivation level are presented in figure 2 and online supplemental table 2, with corresponding analyses for income in online supplemental figure 2 and online supplemental table 3. Education was not significantly associated with ACM (online supplemental table 4).

Mitral valve intervention

Overall, 840 patients (12.6%) underwent mitral valve intervention, comprising 789 surgical (11.8%) and 51 M-TEER procedures (0.8%). The low rate of M-TEER reflects the relatively recent adoption in the UK and evolution of guideline recommendations.11 Crude intervention rates were 13.6% in White, 7.4% in Black and 11.7% in Asian patients (online supplemental table 5). Cumulative incidence analysis (accounting for the competing risk of death) demonstrated that intervention rates at 5 years were 12.7% (95% CI 11.8 to 13.7%) in White patients and 6.9% (95% CI 5.2 to 8.6%) in Black patients (Grey’s test p<0.001; figure 3, online supplemental table 6).

Cumulative incidence of mitral regurgitation intervention stratified according to ethnicity. Cumulative incidence function accounting for death as a competing risk. Black patients had a 5-year cumulative incidence of 6.9% compared with 12.7% for White patients and approximately 10% for Asian patients (Gray’s test p<0.001). The disparity in intervention was evident from baseline and persisted throughout the study period.

Predictors of intervention

In multivariable cause-specific Cox regression (table 4), Black ethnicity was associated with a lower hazard of intervention (HR 0.74; 95% CI 0.57 to 0.96; p=0.02), with no significant difference between Asian and White patients (HR 1.00; 95% CI 0.73 to 1.36; p=0.99). MR severity was the strongest predictor of intervention (HR 2.3 per grade; 95% CI 2.12 to 2.48; p<0.001), while secondary MR was associated with markedly lower intervention rates compared with primary MR (HR 0.12; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.14; p<0.001).

Socio-economic associations with intervention

Each decile increase in IMD was associated with 9% higher odds of intervention in the overall cohort (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.13; p<0.001). This association was present in White patients (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.14; p<0.001) and more pronounced in Asian patients (OR 1.25; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.50; p=0.02), but absent in Black patients (OR 0.97; 95% CI 0.83 to 1.14; p=0.75). Discussion

This study examined racial and ethnic disparities in outcomes and treatment access among patients with at least moderate native MR in a large South London population. Guy’s and St Thomas’ NHS Foundation Trust serves an area where approximately 44% of residents identify as ethnic minorities.15 The principal finding is that Black and Asian patients comprise a substantially smaller proportion of the cohort with diagnosed MR (14.4% and 5.6% respectively) than would be anticipated from the corresponding population demographics,15 suggesting a detection gap in these communities. When MR was identified, ethnic minority patients presented at a younger age with a greater burden of comorbidities and predominantly secondary MR. After adjustment for clinical characteristics and SED, ethnicity was not independently associated with mortality or rates of intervention. Although the borderline adjusted mortality HR for Black versus White patients (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.80 to 1.00; p=0.06), considered alongside the substantial imbalance in cohort sizes, means that small but clinically relevant differences cannot be excluded. Meanwhile, SED remained independently associated with both outcomes across the overall cohort.

The disparity between the ethnic composition of our MR cohort and the corresponding South London population merits careful consideration. Similar patterns have been observed in a similar study within the same healthcare setting, where the prevalence of AS among patients aged ≥65 years was 20.3% in White patients compared with 10.9% in Black and 13.7% in Asian patients.8 Furthermore, clinical diagnosis of AS was less likely in Black patients, who also experienced lower rates of intervention and higher mortality. The present findings in MR extend this evidence and indicate a broader pattern of under-detection of valvular heart disease in ethnic minority populations, even within a universal healthcare system.

Baseline characteristics and disease presentation

Black patients presented nearly 7 years younger than White patients and with a substantially higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, heart failure and dialysis dependence. This earlier and more symptomatic presentation is likely to reflect the fact that ethnic minority patients come to medical attention when MR becomes clinically apparent through associated comorbidities or symptoms, rather than through routine surveillance or incidental detection. The predominance of secondary MR in Black patients (91% vs 85% White) aligns with London-based echocardiographic data demonstrating lower rates of mitral valve prolapse in Black patients (3.1% vs 12.5% White) and correspondingly higher prevalence of hypertension and non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy-related MR.16 These aetiological differences carry prognostic and therapeutic implications, since secondary MR is associated with poor survival and more circumscribed indications for surgical intervention compared with primary degenerative disease.11

Specific contemporary data on MR prevalence in Black and Asian populations remain sparse. The Bronx-Valve Registry reported MR prevalence across ethnic groups in a highly diverse US population and demonstrated that overall prevalence of MR was highest in non-Hispanic White individuals (but highest in non-Hispanic Black individuals in those aged <65 years).17 However, European data examining MR detection rates and treatment outcomes in ethnic minorities are notably absent. This gap in the evidence base underscores the need for population-level studies examining the true burden of valvular heart disease in diverse communities.

ACM

Our staged modelling approach provides insight into factors associated with mortality. In unadjusted analyses, White patients demonstrated lower survival, although this pattern was reversed after adjustment for age and sex, with Black ethnicity associated with increased mortality (potentially as a consequence of the confounding effect of younger age at presentation). On further adjustment for MR characteristics, this association was further attenuated, likely as a reflection of the negative prognostic impact of secondary MR and consistent with MR aetiology acting as a mechanistic mediator rather than as a simple confounder. In fully adjusted models, neither Black nor Asian ethnicity were independently associated with mortality.

These observations require cautious interpretation given the substantial disparity in cohort sizes (5331 White, 958 Black, 376 Asian patients). The apparent absence of mortality disadvantage in ethnic minority patients after adjustment for clinical factors should not be misconstrued as evidence of equivalent outcomes across populations. Rather, the data suggest that clinical characteristics drive prognosis when MR is identified, rather than ethnicity per se. The younger age and lower prevalence of coronary artery disease in Black patients (19% vs 36% White) may contribute to comparable adjusted survival, though these observations require validation in larger, more balanced cohorts.

SED was consistently associated with mortality in the overall cohort, such that each decile increase in IMD was associated with a 3% reduction in mortality. This association is concordant with established relationships between area-level deprivation and cardiovascular outcomes in UK populations18 and reached statistical significance in White (but not Black or Asian) patients. The IMD distribution within Black patients was substantially narrower than within White patients (median decile 3 (IQR 2–4) vs 5 (IQR 3–7)), reducing the variance available to detect a gradient. The pronounced collinearity between ethnicity and deprivation in South London further attenuates the independent signal once ethnicity is stratified. Area-level deprivation indices may also imperfectly capture individual socioeconomic circumstances in heterogeneous urban populations.

Mitral valve intervention

Unadjusted intervention rates were substantially lower in Black patients, with 5-year cumulative incidence approximately half that of White patients (6.9% vs 12.7%). After adjustment for demographics, MR characteristics, comorbidities and socioeconomic status, ethnicity was not independently associated with intervention. The marked influence of secondary MR aetiology (HRs 0.12–0.15 compared with primary MR across all ethnic groups) underscores the fact that disease phenotype determines eligibility for intervention, rather than ethnicity. These observations are consistent with guideline recommendations and the more limited evidence base for intervention in secondary MR, where outcomes are influenced by underlying ventricular function and potential for recurrent MR.11

Similar conclusions have emerged from US surgical registries, where racial differences in mitral valve repair rates were substantially explained by disease aetiology and pre-operative clinical factors.19 SED was independently associated with rates of intervention, each decile increase in IMD being associated with 9% higher odds of intervention in the overall cohort. These findings align with UK national data demonstrating lower rates of cardiac surgery in deprived populations.20

Clinical implications and the case for enhanced detection

The central finding of this study is the apparent under-detection of MR in ethnic minority populations relative to the demographic composition of South London, consistent with similar findings for AS in the same region8 and suggesting a substantial gap in the detection of valvular heart disease in diverse communities. The fact that minority patients who are detected present younger with higher burden of comorbidities suggests that these individuals only access care once symptomatic and that opportunities for earlier identification and intervention are missed.

These observations support the need for enhanced programmes of valvular heart disease detection. Community-based screening initiatives, care delivered closer to home and education campaigns targeting both patients and healthcare providers may help to address this gap. The NHS Long Term Plan emphasises cardiovascular disease prevention and extension of this focus to include valvular heart disease screening in under-served communities. Potential strategies include opportunistic echocardiography in primary care, integration of valve assessment into existing heart failure pathways and targeted outreach to communities with lower detection rates.

The earlier presentation and higher burden of comorbidities in ethnic minority patients with established MR underscores the importance of pro-active cardiovascular risk factor management and optimisation of guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure. Predominance of secondary MR in these populations reinforces the need for timely referral to specialist valve clinics, where multidisciplinary assessment can appraise suitability for intervention, including transcatheter approaches that have expanded options for patients previously deemed high-risk for surgery.

Study limitations

The substantially unequal cohort sizes between ethnic groups constrain the precision of ethnicity-stratified estimates and limits statistical power to detect modest differences within minority subcohorts. The observational design limits causal inference and residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Data concerning patient preferences or reasons for withholding intervention were not captured, limiting our assessment of whether observed patterns reflect patient choice, clinical unsuitability or other factors. Furthermore, the classification of MR aetiology using the text from echocardiographic reports may result in some errors, though the proportion of secondary MR in our cohort is similar to other population studies.

The IMD is an area-level measure, in combination with the narrow IMD distribution within minority sub-cohorts and its collinearity with ethnicity, this limits the ability of current socioeconomic metrics to capture the social determinants of cardiovascular outcomes in ethnically diverse populations. The study spans a period when transcatheter options expanded and guidelines evolved, but temporal trends in disparities were not examined. The small M-TEER subcohort precluded stratified analysis of transcatheter versus surgical intervention. Generalisation to other UK regions requires validation since this was a single-centre study. Importantly, our analysis was restricted to those patients who underwent echocardiography and is therefore unable to assess potential disparities in access to diagnostic imaging that may have contributed to the observed detection gap.

Conclusion

The proportion of ethnic minority patients with MR in this diverse South London population is lower than expected from local demographics, suggesting that valvular heart disease is under-detected in Black and Asian communities. Ethnic minority patients with MR present at younger age with predominantly secondary MR and a higher burden of comorbidities. After adjustment, ethnicity was not independently associated with ACM or rates of intervention, indicating that disease phenotype and clinical characteristics drive outcomes rather than ethnicity per se, modest effects in minority subcohorts cannot be excluded. SED was independently associated with both mortality and rates of intervention in the overall cohort, supporting the need for enhanced programmes of valvular heart disease detection in ethnically diverse communities, alongside strategies to address socio-economic determinants of cardiovascular health.
