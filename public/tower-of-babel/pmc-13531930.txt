The growing need for heart failure specific risk prediction models and standardized care pathways in patients with cancer
Authors: Arianne Clare Agdamag, Wilson Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among cancer survivors. Multiple cardiovascular risk prediction models exist to provide risk stratification for patients who are about to undergo cancer treatment. Current risk models have limited guidance on how to prevent, modify, and monitor for heart failure (HF) particularly heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Cancer survivors are at an elevated risk for HFpEF due to the development of cardiometabolic syndrome associated with aging and long-term survival. Future efforts should focus on novel biomarkers in assessing HF, development of large, multicenter databases that can be used to validate models across diverse populations, and defining clear treatment pathways once risk is identified. A more comprehensive and inclusive risk stratification model with pragmatic approaches to clinical care will allow for more widespread use among cardiologists and oncologists.Keywords: Heart failure, Cardio oncology, Risk prediction, Risk score, Survivorship, Cancer

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among cancer survivors. Depending on the underlying malignancy, patients may have up to a threefold higher risk of developing heart failure (HF) than the general population, a burden that will continue to grow as advances in cancer therapy improve long term survival [1, 2]. Consequently, survivorship care has become an increasingly important component of cancer care, creating a need for tools that proactively identify patients at risk and guide cardiovascular (CV) management throughout the cancer care continuum [3]. Existing HF risk prediction models have not fulfilled this role. Most estimate risk only before cancer therapy begins, rely on endpoints’ that do not reflect survivors’ evolving cardiovascular profile, and provide minimal guidance once elevated risk is identified. As a result, these models have seen limited clinical adoption despite their promise. To become clinically meaningful, HF risk prediction models must move beyond static risk classification to incorporate dynamic, treatment-specific risk, distinguish evolving HF phenotypes, and directly link risk estimates to evidence-based interventions across the cancer care continuum.

Risk prediction models stratify patients by their likelihood of developing heart failure, guiding clinicans toward targeted mitigation before complications arise. For cancer patients, general CV risk and complications have seen notable advances in both regression-based and machine-learning approaches [4]. However, HF-specific risk prediction presents unique unmet challenges and is the focus of this correspondence. Before treatment, risk prediction models must assess baseline CV and HF risk to guide treatment planning and facilitate completion of cancer therapy while minimizing short- and long-term CV complications. During survivorship, these should continue to characterize evolving HF risk throughout long-term follow up. Throughout cancer care, these models must account for the unique CV and competing risks conferred by both the underlying malignancy and its treatment. With respect to these goals, current risk prediction models fall short in three main areas: (a) timing of risk assessment, (b) lack of actionable guidance, and (c) limited external validation and generalizability (Table 1).

The timing for risk assessment remains focused on baseline and pre-treatment evaluation, yet models should track how risk changes as treatment progresses and evolves over long term survivorship. Cardiotoxic risk is dynamic and evolves as cancer therapies are initiated and age-related comorbidities emerge over long-term survival. During active treatment, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) is the primary concern. Across long-term survivorship the risk evolves toward failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). However, existing risk calculators provide mainly generalized CV outcomes or all comer HF endpoints and fail to capture this important shift. With prolonged survival, cancer patients frequently develop hypertension, diabetes, elevated body mass index, and hyperlipidemia leading to cardiometabolic risk that drives HFpEF. Yet, these phenotypes of heart failure are not well evaluated in current risk stratification models. Fundamentally, the field must reassess whether the endpoints addressed by current risk prediction models are relevant to the associated CV diseases that develop with cancer treatment. There should be a balance on assessing not just rare but highly fatal outcomes such as ICI myocarditis but also more chronic conditions such as HFpEF, and transient self-resolving conditions such as mild pericarditis [5].

Actionable guidance remains overlooked in current risk prediction models, which primarily focus on categorizing patients into low-, intermediate- or high-risk tiers. However, the role of HF risk prediction is not simply to estimate but to contextualize that risk in relation to the anticipated cancer treatment benefit, available treatment alternatives, and patient preferences to support shared clinical decision making. Once risk is identified, cardiologists and oncologists often lack standardized and evidence-based mitigation strategies. Existing studies supporting the preemptive use of guideline directed medical therapy (GDMT) remain limited and have largely reported neutral findings. Protocols regarding frequency of biomarker reassessment, optimal interval for cardiac imaging, and specific thresholds for adjusting oncologic regimen also remain poorly defined. To increase these models’ clinical relevance, the output must be linked to a prescriptive care pathway, such as guidance on when to modify anthracycline administration, when to initiate cardioprotective agents such as dexrazoxane, or consider preemptive SGLT2 inhibitor therapy.

External validation and generalizability are lacking in current risk models. The majority of existing models have been derived from single-center studies and are either based on specific drug classes or a solitary intervention (Table 2). The widely used HFA-ICOS risk assessment tool, while helpful for general CV stratification, lacks HF- specific prediction and validation for HF endpoints. The simplicity of generalized risk models comes at the expense of HF specific predictive accuracy. This lack of granularity is particularly significant for complex oncology patients who have histories of multiple cancers or are treated with multiple drug classes [6, 7].

Heart failure risk prediction models in cardio oncology

To address these gaps, the field of cardio-oncology should focus on risk prediction models that provide more precise timelines on risk assessment, prescriptive clinical action, and external validation and generalizability. First, there must be a shift from static scoring to a dynamic risk model that aligns specific tools in relation to the patient’s timeline in the cancer care continuum. Pre-treatment models should focus on acute HFrEF risks, while survivorship models must adapt to capture long-term HFpEF risk.

Second, risk stratification tools should provide more guidance to users on what to do once risk is identified through prescriptive risk-linked treatment pathways. These may include tools that recommend clinical interventions at predefined milestones including cancer regimen adjustment, modifying anthracycline infusion rate, altering dosing intervals, utilizing liposomal formulations and determining timing of HF medication initiation. During treatment, the baseline risk score should be used to determine biomarker and imaging surveillance. Incorporating biomarkers such as NT pro BNP, circulating microRNA, growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15) can help provide more precise recommendations on initiation of cardioprotective strategies such as RAS inhibitors or beta blockers [8]. The role of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP1 agonist should also be integrated in these pathways given their role in decreasing cardio-kidney- metabolic risk and reported benefit in preventing CTRCD in preliminary studies. During survivorship, the combination of clinical symptoms, volume status, NT pro BNP, co morbid conditions should be used to determine long-term surveillance frequency and need for advanced HF therapies referral.

Third, incorporating multi-center international databases such as the Global Cardio Oncology Registry is one avenue to expand generalizability. Expanding participation in these registries will provide diverse and multi-ethnic cohorts needed to ensure that these models have external validity. Future clinical trials must incorporate these risk profiles directly in their inclusion and exclusion criteria to expand these models’ clinical utility. Determining which interventions within specific tiers provide benefit will guide the transition from generalized care to an individualized, precision-medicine approach. Diagnosing heart failure is challenging in this population due to overlapping symptoms. This highlights the need for future studies to use multi-parametric criteria for heart failure that integrate patient symptoms, biomarkers, imaging, comorbidities, and specific cancer therapy to generate results that are accurate, reproducible, and clinically actionable.

The cardio-oncology community should also reevaluate current society guidelines regarding the frequency and duration of imaging modalities during therapy. With the declining incidence of acute cancer therapy related cardiac dysfunction in certain patients, revising guidelines to safely increase the interval of diagnostic testing for low-risk patients could optimize limited resources without compromising patient safety.

As the scope of cardio-oncology expands beyond preventing acute cardiotoxicity to managing lifelong cardiovascular risk, increased clinical adoption of HF risk prediction models will require moving beyond static risk classification toward dynamic risk assessment, clinically relevant HF phenotyping, prescriptive management pathways, and rigorous external validation. These principles should guide the development, validation, and implementation of the next generation of HF risk prediction models. Importantly, these advances build upon existing progress in cardio-oncology, making this a realistic and achievable path toward improving the clinical utility of HF risk prediction. Collectively, these improvements will enable broader adoption of HF risk prediction models among cardiologists and oncologists and help realize their potential to play an integral role in precision cancer care and survivorship.
