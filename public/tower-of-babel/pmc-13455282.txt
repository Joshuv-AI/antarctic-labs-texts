Breastfeeding support experiences of mothers of preterm infants in China: a qualitative study
Authors: Shui-xiu Huang, Yu Zhao, Xiao-juan Wang, Long Huang, Wei Zhang, Ying Lin, Gui-hua Liu, Li Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMothers of preterm infants in China face unique breastfeeding challenges due to neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) hospitalization and restrictive visiting policies enforcing mother-infant separation. While breastfeeding is often assumed instinctive, little is known about the support needs of Chinese mothers from NICU admission to 6 months post-discharge, a critical period when breastfeeding cessation is common.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted using semi-structured video interviews with 20 mothers of preterm infants (8 during NICU hospitalization, 5 preparing for discharge, and 7 post-discharge) at a maternal and child health center in Fuzhou, China. Data were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using qualitative content analysis, following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist.ResultsThree themes emerged, reflecting breastfeeding support needs: (1) Family support, including assistance with milk pumping and storage; (2) Professional support, encompassing dietary guidance, lactation initiation, and breastfeeding technique feedback; (3) Social and peer support, emphasizing guidance from experienced mothers and workplace accommodations. Subthemes highlighted needs for continuous professional consultation across the care continuum and standardized education for family caregivers as essential strategies to sustain breastfeeding.ConclusionsMothers of preterm infants expressed distinct, interconnected breastfeeding support needs across stages. These needs, when acknowledged and met, support successful breastfeeding. Findings can inform targeted interventions to enhance breastfeeding support, emphasizing continuous, comprehensive, and individualized care to promote breastfeeding among preterm infants.

Background

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation [1], affects approximately 9.9% of live births globally [2], with China contributing 7.5% of these cases [3]. Preterm birth is a leading cause of neonatal mortality, accounting for 35% of newborn deaths [4]. In China, the first month after childbirth, known as postpartum confinement, involves traditional rest at home, limiting mothers’ mobility and access to professional support. Breast milk is critical for preterm infants, offering immunological protection, promoting gut maturation, reducing complications like necrotizing enterocolitis and sepsis, and supporting cognitive development, which are vital for infants vulnerable to infections and developmental delays [5]. However, breastfeeding preterm infants is challenging due to physiological immaturity, weak sucking reflexes, low birth weight, respiratory issues requiring tube feeding, and prolonged mother-infant separation in NICUs, particularly in China, where restrictive visiting policies limit maternal involvement [6]. Additional barriers include maternal postpartum recovery challenges, such as pain from cesarean sections or insufficient milk supply [7]. In China’s NICU settings, mothers are often instructed to pump breast milk, which is delivered to the hospital by family members, but standardized guidance on pumping techniques, storage, and transportation is rarely provided, leading to issues like improper handling or reduced milk production [8, 9]. Healthcare strategies, such as lactation counseling, peer support groups, skin-to-skin contact initiatives, and telehealth follow-ups, have shown promise in promoting breastfeeding among preterm mothers [10, 11]. For example, structured lactation programs in NICUs can increase exclusive breastfeeding rates by providing hands-on guidance and emotional support [10]. However, these interventions are often inconsistent, and mothers report receiving haphazard support dependent on healthcare providers’ expertise [12].

Qualitative studies highlight that mothers seek recognition of their unique breastfeeding needs, yet these remain poorly understood, particularly in the Chinese context [13]. This study aims to explore the breastfeeding support needs and experiences of Chinese mothers of preterm infants during NICU hospitalization, 2–3 days before discharge, and up to 6 months post-discharge, to inform targeted interventions for improving breastfeeding rates.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative descriptive study used semi-structured video interviews to explore the breastfeeding support needs and experiences of mothers of preterm infants across three stages: NICU hospitalization, 2–3 days before discharge, and up to 6 months post-discharge. Video interviews were chosen due to postpartum confinement practices and COVID-19 restrictions, which limited face-to-face interactions.

Setting and recruitment

The study was conducted in the NICU of a tertiary hospital in southeast China with 70 infant beds. The head nurse facilitated recruitment by introducing eligible mothers to the first author, who provided study information and obtained informed consent.

Purposive sampling with maximum variation ensured diversity in maternal age, occupation, and infant birth weight. Selection by the head nurse may introduce bias, which is acknowledged in the limitations.

Inclusion and/or exclusion criteria

Eligible participants included mothers of infants born before 37 weeks of gestation, hospitalized in the NICU for over 3 days, and at home for up to 6 months post-discharge at the time of the interview. Mothers required sufficient Mandarin proficiency to participate in interviews, assessed by their ability to engage in clear verbal communication during initial contact. Mothers participating in other studies that could influence results were excluded.

Data collection

Participants were recruited from November 2022 to February 2023. Video interviews were conducted via Tencent Conference due to postpartum confinement and COVID-19 restrictions. The first author ensured network stability 15 min prior to interviews to facilitate clear communication. Participants were asked to enable cameras to allow observation of gestures and facial expressions, which were noted in memos to enrich data interpretation. Interviews lasted 30–45 min (mean: 39 min). The sample size was determined by data saturation, achieved when no new meaningful data emerged [14].

Interview guide

The guide, developed by two doctoral researchers and refined by the NICU head nurse, included open-ended questions like: “What challenges do you face in breastfeeding?” and “What support would you find helpful from family or professionals?” Exploratory prompts encouraged detailed responses. Simulated interviews minimized deviations.

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim within 24 h. NVivo 20.0 facilitated data management. Descriptive content analysis was used [15]: two researchers independently coded transcripts, extracting similar statements into meaning units, which were categorized into themes and subthemes. A third researcher reviewed codes for consistency, resolving discrepancies through consensus. No inter-rater agreement statistics (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) were used; reliability was achieved through iterative discussion. Saturation was confirmed when no new themes emerged in additional interviews.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist and was approved by the Fujian Maternal and Child Health Hospital Ethical Review Committee (NO. 2022YJ092). Written informed consent was obtained, emphasizing voluntary participation and the right to withdraw.

Rigor and reflexivity

Credibility was ensured through member checking with mothers. Confirmability was established by the NICU head nurse reviewing the process. Reliability was maintained via detailed audit trails. For transferability, findings were shared with an independent senior researcher, whose feedback clarified theme definitions but did not alter them. The interviewer built trust by participating in NICU nursing activities. Data were stored securely on an encrypted drive.

Themes identified

Three themes emerged, organized by types of support rather than time-based stages, reflecting interconnected needs: (1) Family support, (2) Professional support, and (3) Social and peer support.

Family support

Mothers emphasized the role of family in breastfeeding success across all stages.

During NICU hospitalization, mothers relied on family to deliver pumped milk to the hospital:

“After my cesarean, I couldn’t move much. My mother-in-law and husband helped pump and deliver milk to the hospital. Without them, my baby wouldn’t get breast milk.” (A16); Post-discharge, family members, including “Yue Sao” (maternity matrons), were crucial for breast milk management:“My mother-in-law and Yue Sao store and heat milk. Last week, my baby had diarrhea due to improper storage. They need guidance to do it right.” (A18).

Professional support

Mothers sought professional guidance to initiate and maintain lactation. During NICU hospitalization, they desired dietary advice: “I’m told to drink fish soup for milk production, but I don’t know if it’s right. I need scientific diet advice.” (A1) In most Chinese NICUs, mother-infant separation policies are enforced, limiting opportunities for direct breastfeeding. Pre-discharge, mothers needed help transitioning to direct breastfeeding: “My baby’s been bottle-fed in the NICU. I’m scared he won’t latch. I need guidance to succeed.” (A17) Post-discharge, mothers requested ongoing professional contact: “After discharge, I didn’t know how much milk my baby needed. He vomited from overfeeding. I wish I could consult a doctor.” (A12) Feedback on breastfeeding techniques was also critical: “At a clinic, I learned my breastfeeding posture was wrong. I need professionals to correct me.” (A9).

Social and peer support

Mothers valued peer support, particularly from experienced preterm mothers:

“Another mother told me the right breastfeeding posture. It’s like having a guide when I’m lost.” (A15) Post-discharge, workplace support was a concern: “At work, there’s no place to pump. Colleagues dislike me using the office fridge. I need society to support breastfeeding.” (A7).

Discussion

This qualitative study provides a comprehensive exploration of the breastfeeding support needs of Chinese mothers of preterm infants, revealing three interconnected pillars: family, professional, and social/peer support. Our findings underscore that successful breastfeeding is a collective effort requiring a synergistic support system.

The indispensable role of family

Our results highlight that family members, particularly husbands and mothers-in-law, are the backbone of practical support, especially during the demanding NICU phase. Their involvement in tasks like milk expression, transportation, and later, storage and heating, is often the only way mothers can provide breast milk under restrictive visiting policies. This finding aligns with broader research on the importance of familial support in collectivist cultures like China [10]. However, our study also reveals a critical gap: family members often lack the necessary knowledge to perform these tasks correctly, as evidenced by incidents of milk spoilage. This indicates that support interventions must extend beyond the mother to include education and guidance for the entire family unit, particularly for ‘Yue Sao’ and other caregivers involved in post-discharge care.

The critical need for continuous professional guidance

Mothers in our study consistently reported a profound need for professional support that is not only evidence-based but also continuous and accessible. The initial need for dietary and lactation initiation guidance in the NICU evolves into a need for skilled coaching on direct breastfeeding techniques as discharge approaches, and finally into a need for accessible post-discharge consultation to troubleshoot feeding problems.

The current model, where professional support often ends at discharge, creates a dangerous vacuum. This is consistent with international findings that identify the post-discharge period as a high-risk time for breastfeeding cessation [16], Our study strongly advocates for the development of integrated care models that bridge the hospital-to-home transition, such as telehealth consultations or dedicated community lactation clinics, to provide mothers with the ongoing professional backing they need.

The power of community and peer networks

The emotional and practical value of peer support cannot be overstated. Mothers described experienced peers as “guides” who helped them navigate uncertainty, a finding that resonates with the principles of the WHO’s Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative, which promotes mother-to-mother support [17, 18]. Furthermore, the challenge of balancing breastfeeding with work is a universal issue, but our study highlights the specific lack of infrastructure (e.g., lactation rooms) and societal understanding in the Chinese context. This calls for policy-level interventions, such as mandating workplace accommodations and public awareness campaigns, to foster a truly breastfeeding-friendly society [19].

Implications for policy and practice

The core message of this study is that breastfeeding support for mothers of preterm infants must be continuous, comprehensive, and individualised. It must move beyond the outdated notion that breastfeeding is an innate, solitary act. Instead, healthcare systems and policymakers must recognize it as a complex process requiring coordinated efforts from families, healthcare professionals, and society at large. Interventions should be designed to strengthen all three pillars of support simultaneously. For instance, a hospital program could include: (1) family education sessions on milk handling, (2) mandatory skin-to-skin contact and lactation consultation during NICU stays, and (3) connection to peer support groups and information on workplace rights before discharge. By adopting this holistic, support-centric (rather than stage-centric) approach, we can create an environment where mothers feel empowered, informed, and genuinely supported, ultimately leading to higher breastfeeding rates and improved health outcomes for preterm infants in China.

Limitations

The study has two primary limitations. First, video interviews were conducted instead of face-to-face interviews due to the cultural tradition of ‘postpartum confinement’, which restricts mothers’ mobility for at least one month after birth. While video calls allowed for observation of non-verbal cues, they may have limited the depth of rapport that can be built in person. Second, most participants were recruited from an urban tertiary hospital. Consequently, the findings may not fully represent the experiences of mothers from rural areas or those with lower socioeconomic status. Future research should include a more diverse socio-demographic sample to confirm and expand upon these findings.

Conclusions

The preterm mothers expressed specific breastfeeding support needs at different stages: preterm infants during the NICU stay, 2-3days before discharge, and 6 months after discharge. These support needs were interconnected. The breastfeeding support needs of premature mothers during a specific stage are not only a phased expression throughout the entire breastfeeding journey but also a key connection to opening up the next stage of breastfeeding support needs. When discussing breastfeeding support with the mothers, they actively expressed their needs and feelings. Mothers desire for their breastfeeding support needs to be acknowledged, valued, and met to smoothly start and sustain breastfeeding. In the future, it is recommended to further develop intervention studies based on the support needs of mothers’ breastfeeding to promote breastfeeding in premature infants. Moreover, healthcare providers should provide continuous, comprehensive, and individualised breastfeeding support to avoid the issue of ‘support overload’. The findings of this study provide expanded options and broader coverage for future phased integrated breastfeeding support policies.
