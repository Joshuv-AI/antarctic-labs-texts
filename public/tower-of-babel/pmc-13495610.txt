Feasibility, acceptability, and usability of a patient decision aid for participation in the DAHANCA 35 proton radiotherapy trial: A qualitative study☆
Authors: Anne Wilhøft Kristensen, Annesofie Lunde Jensen, Kenneth Jensen, Susanne Oksbjerg Dalton, Jeppe Friborg, Hilary L Bekker, Cai Grau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical trial participation in oncology remains low, despite its importance for advancing treatment. Patients are often asked to consider trial participation in emotionally and cognitively demanding situations, which may limit their ability to engage with complex information and relate decisions to their values and life context. Patient decision aids (PtDAs) may support informed, value-based decision-making, but their feasibility in clinical trial settings warrants further exploration.ObjectiveTo qualitatively assess the feasibility of integrating a clinical trial PtDA into routine trial communication and to explore its acceptability and usability in practice.MethodsA qualitative feasibility study using interpretive description methodology was conducted. Semi-structured telephone interviews were performed with 11 patients and 14 healthcare professionals involved in a Danish randomised controlled trial comparing photon and proton radiotherapy. Data were analysed inductively.ResultsIntegration of the PtDA into clinical workflows was feasible. However, its acceptability and usability varied depending on how it was introduced and embedded in clinical interactions. Patients engaged with the PtDA in different ways; some used it independently or with family members, while others required clinician support. Participants described the PtDA as supporting understanding and dialogue and as being versatile in different settings, although some needed additional interaction with a clinician to use it meaningfully.ConclusionA clinical trial PtDA can be integrated into routine practice, but its usefulness depends on active implementation and clinician engagement. PtDAs should be implemented as part of broader interventions to support person-centred decision-making and care in clinical trial contexts.

Introduction

Cancer clinical trials are essential for advancing oncology. However, participation requires patients to make complex decisions about whether to take part in a clinical trial, often in situations characterised by substantial informational and emotional burden. Patients are frequently introduced to trial participation alongside discussions of diagnosis and standard care, which can make it cognitively challenging to process information and engage in decision-making [1]. Physicians also face challenges, including time constraints and limited familiarity with clinical trials, which may affect how trial information is communicated [2], [3].

Patient information leaflets (PILs) are developed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and aim to support informed consent in clinical trials [4], [5], [6]. However, they primarily provide information rather than supporting decision-making. They do not support comparison with standard care and are often written in complex language, which may limit understanding [7], [8], [9]. Thus, patients may lack the support needed to compare options, weigh benefits and risks, and align decisions with their personal values, highlighting the need for additional decision support in clinical trial communication and decision-making.

Patient decision aids (PtDAs) are designed to support informed decision-making when patients must choose between two or more healthcare options. By providing clear, structured, and balanced information, PtDAs help patients compare options and clarify personal values [10], [11]. In clinical trial contexts, PtDAs have been shown to improve patients' understanding, confidence, and involvement in decision-making [8], [9], [12], [13], [14]. However, evidence remains limited and inconsistent, and little is known about how PtDAs are integrated and used in routine clinical trial communication.

Before large-scale implementation or evaluation of effectiveness, it is essential to assess whether such interventions can be integrated into clinical practice. Feasibility studies examine whether an intervention can be delivered as intended, is acceptable to users, and functions within clinical contexts [15], [16].

In this study, feasibility is conceptualised as the extent to which a PtDA can be integrated into clinical workflows for trial communication and meaningfully used by patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs).

The PtDA feasibility tested in this study was developed using a user-centred, iterative design process, described in detail elsewhere [17].

This study aims to qualitatively assess the feasibility of integrating a clinical trial PtDA into radiotherapy workflows and to explore its acceptability and usability among patients and HCPs.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative feasibility study was informed by interpretive description methodology to generate clinically relevant insights and support iterative, inductive analysis [18]. Data consisted of semi-structured interviews conducted between September and December 2024.

Context

The Danish Head and Neck Cancer Study Group (DAHANCA) is conducting DAHANCA 35, a national randomised controlled trial (RCT) comparing photon and proton radiotherapy in patients with pharyngeal or laryngeal cancer [19]. Proton therapy allows more precise dose distribution than photon therapy and may reduce radiation exposure to surrounding tissues, thereby reducing the risk of late effects [20]. The trial is conducted across six Danish cancer clinics in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [21]. Recruitment began in 2020 and is expected to conclude in 2027.

Eligible patients receive verbal trial information during a hospital consultation and are provided with both the PIL and the PtDA (Supplementary material 1). Patients take the materials home and return one to two days later for a follow-up consultation, during which trial participation is discussed and a decision is made.

Photon radiotherapy is delivered at regional hospitals, whereas proton therapy is centralised at a single national facility. For some patients, proton treatment may require temporary accommodation during the six-week treatment period.

Participants

Danish-speaking patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with pharyngeal or laryngeal cancer and eligible for curative-intent radiotherapy were invited to participate in the feasibility study if they had used the PtDA when considering participation in the RCT.

Dedicated nurses at the six radiotherapy clinics involved in DAHANCA 35 approached eligible patients during routine clinical encounters and invited those reporting PtDA use to participate. Convenience sampling with consecutive recruitment was used to capture experiences of PtDA use during the defined data collection period. No formal maximum variation purposive sampling strategy or a priori definition of sufficient variation was applied. Consistent with interpretive description, the aim was not to achieve data saturation, but to generate clinically meaningful insights from the experiences represented in the sample [22].

Physicians and nurses from all six radiotherapy clinics involved in trial enrolment were invited to participate in telephone interviews. They were informed about the feasibility study during PtDA introductory meetings and received written information and a consent form. Those who agreed to participate were contacted by email approximately two months after the PtDA had been implemented in their clinic. Eligibility required experience with using or distributing the PtDA in clinical practice.

Intervention

The clinical trial PtDA was developed to help patients decide whether to participate in the DAHANCA 35 trial or receive standard care. It was used as a supplement to the PIL and routine verbal trial information. The PtDA evaluated in this study was developed through a user-centred, iterative process informed by the International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS) [23]; the full development process and the additional frameworks applied are described in detail elsewhere [17].

The PtDA presents two options: [1] standard photon radiotherapy and [2] participation in the RCT comparing photon and proton radiotherapy. It compares the two treatment modalities and pathways and supports deliberation through practical prompts, a tool for weighing advantages and disadvantages, the SURE test, and space for questions to discuss with HCPs [24], [25].

Before implementation, the first author (AWK) conducted one-hour introductory meetings with oncology teams at all six participating clinics, including physicians and nurses. The purpose and use of the PtDA were presented. HCPs were instructed to provide the PtDA alongside the PIL when informing patients about DAHANCA 35, briefly explain its purpose, and encourage patients to bring it to the subsequent consultation one to two days later, where it could support discussion and decision-making regarding trial participation.

Procedures and data collection

Interviews were conducted using semi-structured interview guides to explore experiences of integrating the PtDA into clinical trial communication and decision-making, with a focus on feasibility, acceptability, and usability (Supplementary material 2).

Interviews were conducted by telephone by an experienced interviewer (AWK) with clinical and research experience within the field. This background informed both data collection and interpretation [22].

Interviews were audio-recorded. Transcripts were imported into NVivo 15 to support data management and analysis [26].

Analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using an inductive interpretive descriptive approach [22]. Transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data, followed by systematic coding and grouping of codes into broader categories. Interpretive themes were then developed to capture patterns related to the feasibility, acceptability, and usability of the PtDA.

The analysis moved from a descriptive account of patterns in the data to an interpretive understanding of their clinical meaning, focusing on what the findings indicate for patient decision-making and the use of the PtDA in practice. In this subsequent interpretive phase, the Making Informed Decisions Individually and Together (MIND-IT) framework was applied as a sensitising analytical lens (Supplementary material 3). The framework conceptualises decision-making as shaped by the reasoning, knowledge, values, emotions, experiences, and circumstances that both patients and HCPs bring to the encounter, together with influences from relatives, other professionals, information sources, and the wider clinical and social context. It was used to interpret how these factors interacted within the clinical encounter and influenced engagement with the PtDA and decision-making [27], [28].

The analysis was conducted collaboratively by the first author (AWK) and co-authors (ALJ and HB). Rigour and reflexivity were supported through collaborative analysis and ongoing discussions of coding, categorisation, and interpretation within the multidisciplinary author group [22].

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [21]. An amendment to the DAHANCA 35 trial, introducing the PtDA as a supplement to the PIL, was approved by the relevant ethics committee (109830). The study was also approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (record no. 1–10–72–181-20) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06291610).

Participants received written and verbal information about the present feasibility study and provided informed consent before participation. Data were handled confidentially, and all quotations were anonymised using participant identification numbers.

Results

The interviews were conducted between September and December 2024 and lasted 10–20 min.

Patient recruitment was uneven across the six participating clinics: three clinics recruited no patients, while the remaining three recruited nine, one, and one patient, respectively. Of the 12 patients invited, 11 participated, and one declined participation in the feasibility study. Information about potentially eligible patients who were not invited was not documented.

Feasibility of integration into the trial communication process

Participants generally perceived integration of the PtDA into clinical workflows as feasible.

The tool was placed in consultation rooms alongside the PIL to support routine distribution.

“The PtDA is routinely distributed to patients during the consultation, so the procedure runs smoothly.” (ID20, physician).

However, feasibility depended on how physicians integrated the PtDA into their trial communication practices. Variation was observed in both whether the PtDA was offered and how it was introduced, reflecting differences in local workflows and clinical approaches.

Patients often expressed immediate interest or declined participation following the initial trial information, making rapid decisions without further reflection. This was often driven by emotional overwhelm.

“I said no right away because I couldn't cope with it.” (ID31, patient).

This created an ethical dilemma for physicians regarding whether to introduce the PtDA after an initial refusal. Some chose not to provide further information:

“I think it's rude to provide further information about the trial if the patient has just said they don't want to participate.” (ID34, physician).

Others adopted a more exploratory approach:

“If the patient says they don't want to participate, I ask if they would still like to read about the trial.” (ID21, physician).

Variation appeared in how the PtDA was introduced. Some physicians actively explained its purpose and use:

“I introduce the PtDA and explain how they can use it at home.” (ID17, physician).

Others distributed it with minimal explanation due to time constraints:

“I inform as usual and hand out the PtDA. We don't have much time.” (ID2, physician).

Patients who did not receive a clear introduction sometimes experienced confusion and were less likely to use the PtDA:

“I was a bit confused about why they gave it to me.” (ID22, patient).

The PtDA also supported shared understanding in consultations by providing a common point of reference, particularly benefiting less experienced clinicians.

Thus, while integration was feasible, effective use depended on how the PtDA was introduced and embedded in clinical communication.

Acceptability and usability of the clinical trial patient decision aid

The PtDA was well accepted by patients and HCPs and described as structured, accessible, and easier to understand than the traditional PIL.

“Providing patients with this well-designed, easy-to-navigate PtDA feels more professional and respectful.” (ID14, physician).

Patients highlighted its value in enhancing understanding by making complex information more concrete:

“When you're told you have cancer, everything is a mess, but this tool was a great help in considering my options.” (ID1, patient).

Usability was linked to clear language, visual elements, and structured comparison of options, supporting understanding and reflection. The PtDA was often used to support reflection and dialogue.

Timing influenced engagement. Receiving the PtDA during the consultation was described as overwhelming:

“It felt overwhelming, and I couldn't take it all in.” (ID22, patient).

In contrast, reviewing the PtDA at home was valued, allowing patients to process information more calmly:

“I felt much calmer when I read it at home.” (ID16, patient).

The home thus functioned as a calm decision-making space.

The PtDA also facilitated family involvement by supporting shared understanding and discussion:

“By reviewing the PtDA together, my family understood the options better” (ID38, patient).

Thus, usability extended beyond individual comprehension to relational aspects of decision-making.

Perceived usefulness

When actively used, the PtDA supported understanding, deliberation, and clarification of personal values. HCPs observed that patients appeared better prepared, more involved, and more confident in their decisions, and asked more relevant, well-informed questions about the available options.

“It's as if we now have a shared language about the RCT.” (ID3, nurse).

Patients described how the PtDA enabled more focused dialogue and active engagement:

“I asked the doctor if we could review it together.” (ID30, patient).

The PtDA supported comparison of options, value clarification, and articulation of reasoning, contributing to a more transparent and value-based decision-making process.

Usefulness varied across patients, with the greatest benefit among those experiencing uncertainty, whereas patients with an immediate decision demonstrated limited engagement.

Overall, participants perceived the PtDA as supporting informed, value-based, and confident decision-making.

Discussion

This study suggests that integrating a clinical trial PtDA into trial communication was feasible. At the same time, its acceptability, usability, and perceived usefulness depended on how it was introduced and embedded in clinical interactions. Some patients used the PtDA independently or with family members, whereas others required HCP support. Engagement was more limited when the PtDA was not actively introduced or when patients were emotionally overwhelmed.

Engagement appeared to depend on the perspectives and resources both patients and HCPs brought into the clinical encounter. Patients contributed prior experience, coping strategies, and situational context, while HCPs brought their communication approaches, clinical experience, and attitudes towards trial participation. Decision-making was thus shaped by the interaction between patients and HCPs, in which the PtDA served as a shared point of reference that supported dialogue, consistent with the MIND-IT framework, in which individual and interpersonal factors jointly influence decision-making [27], [28].

Previous studies indicate that PtDAs can support informed decision-making in clinical trial contexts [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]. This is consistent with the present findings, in which patients described improved understanding and an increased ability to ask focused questions and engage in dialogue with HCPs. However, the present findings suggest that availability alone does not ensure meaningful use. This aligns with implementation research showing that PtDAs require active integration into clinical workflows and communication practices [35]. Although evidence of patient benefit may motivate HCP use, uptake also appeared to depend on clinicians' attitudes, perceived time constraints, and opportunities to adapt established routines. Thus, feasibility, acceptability, and usability were shaped not only by the PtDA itself, but also by how it was introduced and used to support patients in relating information to their values and circumstances [1], [2], [36].

The study also identified an ethical tension in clinical trial communication. HCPs balanced respect for autonomy with concerns about burdening emotionally overwhelmed patients, which sometimes led to the PtDA not being introduced after an initial refusal. This reflects known challenges in trial recruitment, where perceptions of patient vulnerability and trial burden shape how options are presented [3], [37], [38], [39]. Although well-intentioned, this may limit patients' opportunity to engage with information and reconsider their decision in a less acute context.

These findings suggest that PtDAs should be implemented as part of a broader intervention supporting HCP use in practice [35]. Such interventions could combine PtDAs with training in shared decision-making and decision coaching [40], [41], alongside implementation strategies such as train-the-trainer models to support integration into local workflows [42].

A limitation is that most patient interviews were conducted at a single centre because several clinics had limited capacity to recruit patients during the data collection period. Furthermore, consecutive convenience sampling was used without predefined clinical or sociodemographic criteria for variation, and the sample included only two women. Consequently, some patient perspectives may be underrepresented, limiting the breadth and transferability of the findings. The PtDA was used across all centres, and HCPs from five of six clinics were included, providing insight into variation in PtDA use.

As the PtDA was provided alongside the mandatory PIL and routine verbal information, its specific contribution could not be isolated. The findings therefore reflect participants' experiences of the PtDA within the broader trial communication process.

A strength of the study is the inclusion of 11 patient interviews and 14 HCP interviews, providing complementary perspectives, together with a structured, iterative analysis supported by ongoing team-based interpretation [22].

This study did not include quantitative measures of decisional outcomes, as the aim was to assess qualitative feasibility. Future research should evaluate a multicomponent intervention combining the PIL and PtDA with structured HCP support, compared with usual trial communication, in a randomised or cluster-randomised study.

Conclusion

This study suggests that integrating a clinical trial PtDA into routine trial communication is feasible. However, its acceptability and usability are shaped by how it is introduced and embedded in clinical interactions, as well as patients' ability to engage with the tool.

The findings indicate that PtDAs should not be implemented as stand-alone tools but as part of a broader intervention that supports HCPs in introducing and using them. Combining PtDAs with training in decision coaching may strengthen their ability to support value-based decision-making in clinical trial contexts.
