Quantum propagation scheme using Hagedorn wave packets : numerical developments Preprint: APS/123-QED
Rabiou Issa, Kokou Mawulonmi Robert Afansounoudji, Komi Sodoga, David Lauvergnat

Abstract
In this study, we provide a novel wave packet propagation method that generalizes the Hagedorn approach by introducing alternative primitive basis sets that are better suited to describe different physical processes. More precisely, in our propagation scheme, we can mix basis sets with time-dependent parameters (the Hagedorn basis set) and time-independent ones, such as Fourier series, particle-in-a-box, or harmonic oscillator basis sets. Furthermore, our implementation can handle models with several electronic states, so that non-adiabatic processes can be studied. Instead of the time-dependent variational principle, our propagation scheme uses a three-step procedure (standard propagation, time-dependent parameter evaluation, and projection). It relies on multidimensional integrations, which are performed numerically with Gaussian quadrature, so that we have no constraints on the for of the Hamiltonian operator. This numerical algorithm has been implemented in a modern Fortran code available on GitHub ( https://github.com/asodoga/TD_Schrod_Rabiou ). The code has been tested and validated by comparisons with standard propagation schemes on 2D-models with harmonic and anharmonic potentials (modified Hénon-Heiles). More precisely, our benchmark tests show that the wave packets obtained with our propagation scheme are able to converge to exact wave packets (obtained from standard propagation techniques). Finally, we have applied our method to compute the vibrational spectrum of the 6D-modified Hénon-Heiles model and we show that our scheme reproduces well the results obtained with the standard approach and with smaller basis functions. As a perspective, we show that with the generalized Hagedorn wave packet method, we are able to study the non-adiabtic dynamics of the cis-trans retinal isomerization in a reduced 2D-model with two coupled electronic surfaces.



I
Introduction

Understanding the behavior of molecular systems requires quantum mechanics, which is essential for describing both their electronic structures and their nuclear motions. For example, the simulation of the dynamics of certain fundamental processes, such as multiphoton excitations
Walker et al. 1994
;
Rudenko et al. 2004
, photodissociation
Engel et al. 2005
;
Pedersen et al. 2007
;
Sodoga et al. 2009
, photo-isomerization
Hahn and Stock 2000a
;
Hahn and Stock 2000b
and charge transfer
Kimura and Lin 1986
;
Jain et al. 1987
;
Loreau et al. 2010
requires to solve numerically the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE). In particular, these photoinduced processes involve non-adiabatic transitions between different electronic states, which then requires the quantum treatment of the system. This is the case, for example, with the photoisomerization of retinal
Hahn and Stock 2000a
;
Hahn and Stock 2000b
;
Marsili et al. 2020
;
Manathunga et al. 2017
;
Pereira et al. 2023
, which can only be studied in the context of non-adiabatic quantum dynamics.

To solve the TDSE equation, we need to determine the time evolution of a wave packet
\Psi
. A formal solution of TDSE as a function of the evolution operator is known if the Hamiltonian operator is independent of time:

\ket{\Psi\left( t\right) }=\exp[-\imath/\hbar\cdot\hat{H}(t-t_{0})\big]\ket{\Psi\left( t_0\right) }.

(1)

However, this requires explicit knowledge of the evolution operator
\exp[-\imath/\hbar\cdot\hat{H}\left(t-t_{0}\right)\big]
, which is generally not straightforward unless we know a basis in which
\hat{H}
is diagonal. This is called propagation in the spectral representation. In the general case, propagation is usually divided into small constant time intervals,
\Delta t
, and:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi\left( t + \Delta t\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp[-\imath/\hbar\cdot\hat{H}\Delta t\big]\ket{\Psi\left( t\right)}.

(2)

To solve the TDSE, one can numerically express the action of the evolution operator,
\exp[-\imath/\hbar\cdot\hat{H}\Delta t\big]
, on a wave packet using various techniques
Leforestier et al. 1991
: (i) split operator
Feit et al. 1982
, (ii) decomposition on polynomials (usually Chebychev)
Tal-Ezer and Kosloff 1984
, (iii) Taylor expansion
Mercouris et al. 1994
;
Lauvergnat et al. 2007
.
However, the above-mentioned methods have drawbacks related to the number of degrees of freedom in the system. Indeed, the size of the basis (or grid) scales exponentially with the number of degrees of freedom. In addition, the basis must accurately describe the evolution of the wave packet over time. Thus, if the wave packet delocalizes, the basis describing it should be very large. Similarly, if the center of the wave packet evolves strongly while remaining localized, the basis must also be very large.
Furthermore, non-adiabatic processes present significant challenges to simulations. These transitions between electronic states often involve a topological feature known as a conical intersection.
At this intersection, nuclear and electronic motions become intertwined by so-called non-adiabatic coupling, where quantum effects play a crucial role in the dynamics of the nuclear motion.
For these reasons, quantum dynamics and especially non-adiabatic quantum dynamics using multidimensional grids have been essentially limited to 4-atom or 5-atom systems for several decades, despite all the progress that has been made.

To solve the problem when the center of the wave packet evolves strongly, it is possible to represent the wave packet in a basis set (and grid) that evolves over time. More precisely, some basis set parameters change variationnally during the propagation. Several wave packet propagation techniques have been developed, based on this idea of a time-evolving basis set (or grid). Without being exhaustive, one can mention:
(i) The semiclassical approach introduced by Heller
Heller 1976
;
Huber and Heller 1987
;
Drolshagen and Heller 1983
;
Vaníček and Begušić 2021
in which a single Gaussian evolves over time.
(ii) The approaches in which the wave packet is described by a sum of Gaussians evolving over time
Worth et al. 2004
;
Martinez et al. 1996
;
Curchod and Martinez 2018
.
(iii) In the references
Sielk et al. 2009
;
Choi and Vaníček 2019
, the wave packet is represented
on a grid that changes over time according to the evolution of the wave packet.
(iv) George Hagedorn introduced the approach now known as Hagedorn wave packets
Hagedorn 1981
;
Hagedorn et al. 2006
;
Hagedorn 1998
;
Faou et al. 2009
;
Lasser and Lubich 2020
;
Zhang and Vaníček 2024
, i.e. products of complex Gaussians with polynomials that form an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}
space for which certain parameters (center, width and momentum) adapt variationnally during the propagation.
(v) A widely adopted solution in this context is the multi-configuration time-dependent Hartree (MCTDH) method
Meyer et al. 1990
;
Hammerich et al. 1990
;
Meyer and Worth 2003
. This approach represents the wavefunction as a small direct product of time-dependent basis sets (known as single-particle functions). This method allows the propagation of quantum wave packets with a few dozen of degrees of freedom, or even larger with the multilayer version of MCTDH
Wang and Thoss 2003
;
Manthe 2008
;
Vendrell and Meyer 2011
. However, this approach has its limitations. Both the kinetic and the potential components of the Hamiltonian must be expressed as sums of products of one-dimensional terms, which is not always an easy task.
It is therefore still essential to develop wave packet propagation methods that are well-suited for handling a large number of strongly coupled degrees of freedom without limitation in terms of the form of the Hamitonian.

For a molecular system composed of
nc
coordinates,
\mathbf{Q}\left(\mathbf{Q}=\left\{q_{1},q_{2}\cdots q_{nc}\right\}\right)
, the total wave packet,
\ket{\Psi\left( t\right) }
, is expanded, as usual, on
ne
electronic diabatic states,
\ket{e}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi\left( t\right) }

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{ne}\ket{\psi^{(e)}\left( t\right) }\ket{e}

(3)

where,
\ket{\psi^{(e)}\left( t\right) }
, represents the nuclear wave packet,
\psi^{(e)}\left(\mathbf{Q},t\right)
, on the
e^{th}
electronic diabatic state. This electronic component can be expanded on a multidimensional basis and written as:

\Psi^{(e)}\left({\bf Q},t\right)=\sum_{I}^{NB}\tilde{C}_{I}^{t}\cdot B_{I}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\Lambda}\right),

(4)

where
B_{I}\left(\mathbf{Q,\Lambda}\right)
is the
I^{th}
mutidimensional basis function depending on a set of parameters
\mathbf{\Lambda}\equiv\left\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k},\\
\ldots,\lambda_{NB}\right\}
,
\tilde{C}_{I}^{t}
the corresponding time dependent coefficient, and
NB
the size of the basis set.
When studying the dynamics of a molecular system, the representation of the wave function
\psi^{e}\left({\bf Q},t\right)
can be developed on either a time-independent or time-dependent basis. The choice between these two types of basis defines the nature of the numerical methods used to solve the TDSE. A basis
\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\Lambda}\right)
is time-independent, when the elements
B_{I}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\Lambda}\right)
do not change with time (
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is time-independent). In this case, only the coefficients
\tilde{C}_{I}^{t}
of the wave function expansion on the basis vary with time. Methods that use these basis set types are known as standard methods
Leforestier et al. 1991
;
Feit et al. 1982
;
Gharibnejad et al. 2020
;
Tal-Ezer and Kosloff 1984
;
Lauvergnat et al. 2007
. The basis is time-dependent when its elements, now denoted
B_{I}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\right)
, vary over time. In this case, the coefficients, now denoted
C_{I}^{t}
, of the wave function expansion, as well as the basis set parameters,
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}
, vary with time. This is the case with the Hagedorn wave packet approach
Hagedorn 1998
and also with the approach proposed by Choi et al.
Choi and Vaníček 2019
. Usually, wave packet propagation methods with time-dependent basis sets are based on the time-dependent variationnal principle
McLachlan and Ball 1964
;
Heller 1976
;
Lasorne et al. 2006
;
Lasorne et al. 2007
.
The aim of this work is to present a method to solve numerically the TDSE using the wave packet approach expanded on a time-dependent basis using primitive Hagedorn basis sets.
The method takes into account non-adiabatic coupling processes and is compared with other standard approaches using time-independent basis sets, in particular the Taylor expansion technique, although other approaches could have been used.

The method is based on the use of Hagedorn functions, introduced by George Hagedorn
Hagedorn 1981
;
Hagedorn et al. 2006
, which are obtained by multiplying a complex Gaussian by a so-called Hagedorn polynomial, also known as a prefactor polynomial
Dietert et al. 2017
;
Vaníček and Zhang 2025
;
Ohsawa 2019
. Any wave function developed on this basis is called a Hagedorn wave packet and is an exact solution of the TDSE when the potential is harmonic. A detailed presentation of these functions can be found in the literature
Hagedorn 2013
;
Ohsawa and Leok 2013
;
Gradinaru and Hagedorn 2014
;
Lasser and Troppmann 2014
;
Li and Xiao 2014
;
Vaníček and Zhang 2025
;
Ohsawa 2015
;
Punoševac and Robinson 2016
. However, the standard Hagedorn scheme is not general as it does not allow the use of other types of primitive basis sets, such as the particle-in-a-box basis or the Fourier basis, which are often used to model torsional phenomena in molecules and it is restricted to a single electronic state.

The new scheme developed in this work is a generalization of the Hagedorn basis, allowing the use of alternative basis sets. In the case of systems with several degrees of freedom, the wave function is expanded on the direct product of time-dependent primitive basis sets.
Instead of the well-established use of wave packet propagation from time
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
, using the time-dependent variational principle, we propose a numerical strategy based on three steps, where the wave packet in
t_{1}
is propagated to
t_{2}
through an intermediate wave packet assuming that the basis set is time-independent (using a standard approach), then the time-dependent basis parameters at
t_{2}
are computed, and finally the intermediate wave packet at
t_{2}
is projected onto the new basis set.
More precisely, the present work consists in implementing this three-step propagation technique in a Fortran code (freely available on Github
Issa 2025
), that generalizes the multidimensional Hagedorn basis and works with other types of primitive basis.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section II provides a detailed presentation of the relevant theoretical methods. In Section III, we present the results of tests conducted on 2D models with a single electronic surface. Section IV focuses on applications, including the 6D Hénon-Heiles model. Finally, Section V summarizes the key points and discusses the results of this work. Furthermore, as perspective to non-adiabatic dynamics, the isomerization of retinal is considered as a 2D model with two electronic surfaces.

II
Methods

As mentioned in the introduction, the aim of the present study is to propose a propagation strategy with a time-dependent basis set, closely related to the Hagedorn wave packet propagation scheme. However, we will also compare this new approach against a standard approach with a time-independent basis set.

The expression of the wave packet is given by Eqs.
3
,
4
where the
I^{th}
coefficient,
\tilde{C}_{I}^{t}
, is associated with the
time-independent
basis function,
B_{I}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\Lambda}\right)
and the
\mathbf{\Lambda}
are time-independent basis parameters (such as, the center and the scaling factor of the Harmonic Oscillator, HO, basis set). In the present study, the standard propagation is performed with a simple but efficient Taylor expansion
Lauvergnat et al. 2007
;
Mercouris et al. 1994
of the wave packet at order
N_{Tayl}
around
t
. The wave packet at
t+\Delta t
is obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(t+\Delta t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{max}}\frac{\Delta t^{\ell}}{\ell!}\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}\left( t\right) }.

(5)

In the previous equation (Eq
5
),
\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}\left( t\right) }
is the
\ell^{th}
derivatives of the wave packet at
t
. It can be obtained recursively from the time-dependent Schrödinger equation:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{(\ell+1)}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\imath/\hbar\cdot\hat{H}\ket{\Psi^{(\ell)}\left( t\right) }.

(6)

Remarks:
\ket{\Psi^{(0)}\left( t\right) }
(
\ell=0
) is simply the wave packet at time
t
. The previous relation (Eq.
6
) is correct only when the Hamiltonian is time-independent. When it is not the case, time derivatives of the Hamiltonian must be taken into account
Lauvergnat et al. 2007
;
Mercouris et al. 1994
.
Furthermore, the order,
\ell_{max}
, of the Taylor series is obtained when the norm of the
\ell_{max}{}^{th}
term,
\frac{\Delta t^{\ell_{max}}}{\ell_{max}!}\ket{\Psi^{(\ell_{max})}(t)}
, is smaller than a threshold (here

). This guarantees that the wave packet is propagated with high accuracy so that the wave packet norm and energy are conserved over time. For most of our calculations with a time step of 0.1 au, the Taylor series order varies from 11 for the 2D applications up to 25 for the 6D one.

II.1
Wave packet with time-dependent basis set

When the basis set is time-dependent, the wave packet expansion is similar to Eq.
4
, except that the basis parameters,
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}
, are time-dependent. Then this expansion becomes:

\psi^{(e)}\left(\mathbf{Q},t\right)=\sum_{I=1}^{NB}C_{I}^{t}\cdot B_{I}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\right).

(7)

In the previous equation, (Eq. (
7
)),
B_{I}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\right)
is the
I^{th}

time-dependent
multidimensional basis function, the
C_{I}^{t}
is its corresponding coefficient. The
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}
are the time-dependent parameters of the basis set common to all electronic states, where
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}=\left\{\lambda^{(t)}_{1},\cdots,\lambda^{(t)}_{k},\cdots\lambda^{(t)}_{nc}\right\}
. Each basis function is expressed as a product of 1D-primitive basis functions,
b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},\lambda^{(t)}_{k}\right)
, as follows:

\displaystyle B_{I}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{nc}b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},\lambda^{(t)}_{k}\right),

(8)

where,
I
corresponds to a multidimensional index,
\left\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k},\cdots,i_{nc}\right\}
, associated with the 1D-primitive basis functions. The total number of functions,
NB
, is expressed as
\prod\limits_{k=1}^{nc}nb_{k}
for a direct-product basis set, where
nb_{k}
is the number of functions of the
k^{th}
primitive basis set.
The
\lambda^{(t)}_{k}
are the time-dependent parameters of the
k^{th}
primitive basis set and are particularized according to its type:
(i) for the
Fourier basis set
there are no time-dependent parameters
(ii) for the
sine
or the
particle-in-box basis set
, the parameters (the domain range),
\lambda_{k}=\left\{A_{k},B_{k}\right\}
, are time-independent. Although, they can also be implemented as time-independent parameters.
(iii) for the Hagedorn primitive basis set, the time-dependent parameters are particularized in
\lambda^{(t)}_{k}=\left\{\alpha^{(t)}_{k},q^{(t)}_{k},p^{(t)}_{k}\right\}
where
\alpha^{(t)}_{k}
is related to the basis set width and
q^{(t)}_{k}
,
p^{(t)}_{k}
represent the center, and the momentum of the Hagedorn basis set, respectively.
More precisely, the expression of the Hagedorn primitive basis function,
b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},\lambda^{(t)}_{k}\right)
(or more concisely
b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},t\right)
), is given by:

\displaystyle b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},t\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},\lambda^{(t)}_{k}\right)=N\left(\lambda_{k}^{(t)}\right)\cdot H_{i_{k}-1}\left(x_{k}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{(t)}_{k}}{2}\Delta q_{k}^{2}+\imath\cdot p^{(t)}_{k}\Delta q_{k}\right)

where
\alpha^{(t)}_{k}
is a complex number (
\alpha^{(t)}_{k}=a^{(t)}_{k}+\imath\cdot b^{(t)}_{k}
) where
a^{(t)}_{k}
and
b^{(t)}_{k}
are its real and imaginary parts, respectively.
In Eq (
10
),
\Delta q_{k}=q_{k}-q^{(t)}_{k}
and
x_{k}=\sqrt{a^{(t)}_{k}}\Delta q_{k}
. The square root of
a^{(t)}_{k}
is a scaling factor and it is related to the width of the Gaussian part of the basis set and it is therefore positive. Furthermore,
N\left(\lambda_{k}^{(t)}\right)
is a normalization parameter that depends only on
a^{(t)}_{k}
and
H_{i_{k}}\left(x_{k}\right)
is the usual normalized Hermite polynomial of degree
i_{k}
.
It is worth noting that: (i) The Hagedorn and Harmonic Oscillator (HO) basis sets are similar, except that the imaginary contributions of the Hagedorn basis set are absent in the HO ones. (ii)
Some parameters can be excluded for the time-dependent basis. For instance, the imaginary part
b^{(t)}_{k}
of
\alpha^{(t)}_{k}
or
p^{(t)}_{k}
. This does not prevent an exact propagation, since the missing contributions are transferred to the
C_{I}^{t}
coefficients if the basis set is large enough. (iii) The Heller approach
Heller 1976
;
Heller 1981
;
Heller 1975
can be cast in the Hagedorn scheme where only one basis set is present (
nb_{k}=1
and hence,
NB=1
). (iv) In the standard Hagedorn or Heller schemes, the time-dependent parameter
\bm{\alpha}^{(t)}
is a full complex
nc\times nc
matrix. In our scheme, this matrix is diagonal and the diagonal elements are the
\alpha^{(t)}_{k}
. This approximation does not prevent an exact propagation (the missing coupling contributions are transferred to the
C_{I}^{t}
coefficients) and was made to facilitate the standard grid-to-base transformation required in our scheme. (v) With our scheme, we can mix time-dependent and time-independent basis sets, and it also works with several electronic states.

II.2
Propagation strategy

The key point of our Hagedorn approach lies in the way we propagate the time-dependent parameters
\left\{\mathbf{C}^{t},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\right\}
, where
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}
are the basis set parameters and
\mathbf{C}^{t}=\left\{C_{1}^{t},\cdots C_{NB}^{t}\right\}
are the wave packet coefficients.
Indeed, the usual way to propagate the Hagedorn wave packet or other approaches (Heller
Huber and Heller 1987
;
Drolshagen and Heller 1983
;
Moghaddasi Fereidani and Vaníček 2023
, VMCG(Variational Multi-Configuration Gaussian)
Lasorne et al. 2011
;
Lasorne et al. 2006
;
Lasorne et al. 2007
;
Lasorne et al. 2013
;
Mendive-Tapia et al. 2012
;
Allan et al. 2010
;
Araújo et al. 2009
;
Lasorne et al. 2008
;
Araujo et al. 2010
;
Richings et al. 2015
, MCTDH
Beck et al. 2000
;
Meyer et al. 2023
) from time
t_{1}
to time
t_{2}
is to use the time-dependent variational principle. However, we use a different strategy with three steps (fig.
1
):

1.

Standard propagation
: In this step (See fig.
1
), we perform a standard propagation technique (here Taylor propagation scheme) assuming a time-independent basis,
\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{1}}\right)
, to obtain an intermediate wave packet at
t_{2}
(see Eq
11
). We obtain a new set of
\left\{\mathbf{\tilde{C}}^{t_{2}},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{1}}\right\}
. It is important to note that
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{1}}
is still at time
t_{1}
and the wave packet coefficients,
\mathbf{\tilde{C}}^{t_{2}}
, are not the final ones. Furthermore, and depending on the propagation technique used, the time step (
\Delta t=t_{2}-t_{1}
) can be relatively large.

\Psi\left(\bm{Q},t_{2}\right)=\sum_{I=1}^{NB}\tilde{C}^{t_{2}}_{I}\cdot B_{I}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{\Lambda}_{t_{1}}\right)

(11)

2.

New basis set
: Then we build the new basis set,
\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{2}}\right)
, by computing the parameters
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{2}}
, at time
t_{2}
(See fig.
1
). More precisely, the parameters,
q^{(t)}_{k}
,
p^{(t)}_{k}
and
\alpha^{(t)}_{k}
(for
k=1,\cdots nc
), are computed from expectation values of operators assuming the wave packet has a Gaussian shape (Heller-like wave packet). Of course, these time-dependent parameters are not optimal (except for the Heller wave packet), but this does not prevent accurate propagation. With this assumption, it is relatively easy to show that they are given by the following expressions:

\displaystyle q^{(t)}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{q_{k}}{\Psi\left( t\right) }

(12)

\displaystyle p^{(t)}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\imath\expectationvalue{\frac{\partial}{\partial{q_{k}}}}{\Psi\left( t\right) }

(13)

\displaystyle a^{(t)}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[2\left(\expectationvalue{q_{k}^2}{\Psi\left( t\right) }-\left(q^{(t)}_{k}\right)^{2}\right)\right]^{-1}

(14)

\displaystyle b^{(t)}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{(t)}_{k}\left[2p^{(t)}_{k}q^{(t)}_{k}+\imath\left(1+2\expectationvalue{q_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial{q_{k}}}}{\Psi\left( t\right) }\right)\right].

(15)

3.

Projection
: Finally, the wave packet obtained in the first step,
\left\{\mathbf{\tilde{C}}^{t_{2}},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{1}}\right\}
, is projected onto the newly constructed basis,
\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{2}}\right)
, to obtain the new wave packet
\left\{\mathbf{C^{t_{2}}},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{2}}\right\}
at time
t_{2}
(See fig.
1
).
Once the new basis is constructed (previous step), we can proceed with the projection.
The wave packet at
t_{2}
can be expanded on both basis sets: the one at
t_{1}
,
\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{1}}\right)
(see Eq
11
) and the new one at
t_{2}
,
\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{2}}\right)
(see Eq
16
) :

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{Q},t_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{I=1}^{NB}C^{t_{2}}_{I}\cdot B_{I}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{\Lambda}_{t_{2}}\right),

(16)

\displaystyle C^{t_{2}}_{J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\innerproduct{B_{J}\left( \bm{Q}, \bm{\Lambda}_{t_2}\right) }{\Psi\left( t_2\right) }.

(17)

Then from Eqs
16
and
11
, the new coefficients
\mathbf{C^{t_{2}}}
can be evaluated from the coefficients
\mathbf{\tilde{C}}^{t_{2}}
as follows:

\displaystyle C^{t_{2}}_{J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{I=1}^{NB}\innerproduct{B_{J}\left( \bm{Q}, \bm{\Lambda}_{t_2}\right) }{B_{I}\left( \bm{Q}, \bm{\Lambda}_{t_1}\right) }\tilde{C}^{t_{2}}_{I}.

(18)

A

Some comments are in order: in the first step, the wave packet norm and energy can be conserved with some high numerical accuracy, and with some propagation techniques (such as Lanczos) they are rigorously conserved. However, during the third step, the wave packet norm may not be conserved if the basis set size,
NB

Furthermore, in our implementation, we want to test several options regarding the number of time-dependent basis parameters. More precisely,

(the imaginary part of

III.3
Comparative Analysis of Energy Spectrum

In this section, we also use the modified Hénon-Heiles potential with two degrees of freedom. However, the aim is the computation of the spectrum with the Hagedorn scheme and compare it with a reference calculation obtained from a standard calculation. For both approaches, the spectra are computed from the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function,
a(t)
:

a(t)=\innerproduct{\Psi(t_0)}{\Psi(t)}

(24)

In addition, and to attenuate the oscillations of each band due to the Gibbs phenomenon, a filter (
\cos^{3}\left(\frac{\pi(t-t_{0})}{t_{f}-t_{0}}\right)

t_{f}
is the final propagation time) is added to the Fourier transform.
For the standard approach, the autocorrelation function calculation is just a scalar product between the wave packet coefficients,
\tilde{C}_{I}
, at
t
and
t_{0}
. However, for the Hagedorn scheme, the wave packets at
t
and
t_{0}
do not share the same basis set. Therefore, before performing the scalar product, the wave packet at
t
developed on the basis set

t_{0}
,
\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}\right)
using the technique of the third step of the Hagedorn scheme.
In figure

, three spectra are shown, one from the standard propagation with 20 HO basis functions in each dimension, labeled (STD20) and two from the Hagedorn scheme with 5 basis functions in each dimension with different options. For both propagation approaches, the time step is
0.1\,au
and the propagation time is
60\,au
.
The first spectrum (blue solid line, positive spectrum, STD20) is our reference spectrum and it shows 6-7 peaks more or less equally spaced by
1.0\,au

and
p^{(t)}_{k}
and without renormalization (labeled HAG5TTF). The position and width of the peaks are very well reproduced. However, the peaks around

and

The second Hagedorn spectrum is obtained with the renormalization after the third step (green dashed line, negative spectrum, labeled HAG5TTT) and in this case, the peak positions and intensities are in good agreement with those of the reference spectrum. V
Conclusion and perspectives

In this study, we present a numerical implementation of the Hagedorn wave packet with the possibility to use a combination of time-dependent basis sets (Hagedorn ones) and time-independent basis sets (standard ones) with one or several diabatic electronic states. This implementation relies on several numerical features:

•

The propagation is performed in three steps: (i) standard propagation (ii) parameter computation of the time-dependent basis set, assuming the wave packet remains Gaussian (iii) projection of the wave packet onto the new basis set. On the one hand, this strategy does not rely on the time-dependent variational principle, so we do not need to compute an inverse of the overlap matrix, which may be singular. On the other hand, this scheme is not optimal because the time-dependent parameters are computed assuming that the wave packet remains Gaussian, which is not the case for realistic systems. Therefore, we are planning to implement the time-dependent variational principle
McLachlan and Ball 1964
to propagate the time-dependent parameters.

•

Since we are using grid, we are not formally restricted in the choice of the Hamiltonian form. Indeed, we can use simple analytical models (as we did in the present study), but also more complex ones for which the potentials do not have simple expressions. Furthermore, we can easily extend our implementation so that complex curvilinear coordinates can be used with analytical kinectic energy operator (KEO) expressions
Wilson et al. 1955
;
Meyer and Günthard 1968
;
Chapuisat et al. 1991
;
Chapuisat and Iung 1992
;
Wang and Carrington 2000
;
Gatti et al. 2001
;
Ndong et al. 2012
;
Ndong et al. 2013
or with a KEO obtained numerically
Meyer 1979
;
Harthcock and Laane 1982
;
Senent et al. 1995
;
Luckhaus 2000
;
Lauvergnat and Nauts 2002
;
Mátyus et al. 2009
;
Marsili et al. 2022
.
Indeed, the first step of our propagation scheme can be used with any Hamiltonian form (as it is done in discrete variable representation or pseudo-spectral approaches
Gottlieb and Orszag 1977
;
Light and Carrington 2000
;
Friesner 1986
;
Szalay and Ádám 2012
;
Yu 2005
;
Gatti et al. 1999
) and the other two steps are independent of the Hamiltonian.

This implementation has been tested on several models. In particular, it was able to reproduce the Heller scheme for the quadratic model. This test was important to check the numerical stability of our three-step scheme. The tests on anharmonic systems (2D and 6D) show that it was able to reproduce a standard propagation scheme with high accuracy. Furthermore, the spectra, computed from the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation fonction, are also well reproduced, but with a smaller basis set than the one required for the standard propagation scheme.

As mentioned above, our implementation allows the study of models with several electronic states. To show this feature, we test our implementation with the 2D-model of retinal photoisomerization developed by Susanne Hahn and Gerhard Stock
Hahn and Stock 2000b
(see the appendix
B
). This model has played a key role in understanding ultrafast photochemical processes in biological systems and was later extended to a 2+23D model that includes additional vibrational modes, providing a more comprehensive description of the system
Hahn and Stock 2000a
. Over the years, this model has been extensively studied and refined, contributing significantly to the field of photoinduced molecular dynamics
Pereira et al. 2023
;
Johnson et al. 2017
;
Seidner and Domcke 1994
;
Domcke and Stock 1997
;
Balzer et al. 2003
.

This model is described by a
2\times 2
Hamiltonian in the diabatic basis with two coordinates,
\Phi
associated with the torsion (it describes the cis-trans isomerization) and
Q
associated with the active vibronic coupling mode. For this system, we use a Fourier basis set for the
\Phi
coordinate (with
nb_{\Phi}=150
) for both the standard and Hagedorn propagation approaches. For the coordinate
Q
, we use the HO basis set and the Hagedorn one for the standard and Hagedorn propagation schemes, respectively. The size of the basis set is denoted by
nb_{Q}
. Initially, the system is assumed to have already absorbed light from the cis isomer. Therefore, the initial wave packet is expressed as a 2D-Gaussian on the second diabatic electronic state (
e_{0}=2
). The basis set and initial wave packet parameters are given in the appendix
B
.

For the comparison between the standard and the Hagedorn propagation scheme, we evaluate the populations,
p_{e}
, along time for each diabatic state
e
(
p_{e}=\innerproduct{\psi^{(e)}(t)}{\psi^{(e)}(t)}
).
The numerical convergence is reached with
nb_{Q}=20
and
nb_{Q}=50
for the standard and Hagedorn propagation schemes, respectively (see the first diabatic state population in the Fig.
17
). The fact that more basis functions are needed for the Hagedorn scheme seems surprising. However, since we have a common basis set for both diabatic states (equivalent to the single-set formalism in MCTDH), the Hagedorn basis parameters are better adapted to the state with a large population (here the second one). To overcome this difficulty, we are planing to describe the wave packet with different basis sets for the different electronic states (equivalent to the multi-set formalism in MCTDH).

Figure 17:
Time evolution of the ground state population during retinal isomerization obtained from two computational approaches: (i) the standard method (STD20, blue dashed line) and (ii) the Hagedorn approach with two variants (HAG20TTT, orange solid line with solid dot; HAG50TTT, green solid line with squares).

As mentioned above, the actual implementation relies on a direct-product of the basis sets and grids. Therefore, it is limited to small system sizes since the total number of basis functions,
NB
, or grid points,
NQ
, grows exponentially with the number of degrees of freedom,
nc
, although this is somehow attenuated since a small basis set size could be used when spectra are computed.
To overcome this curse of dimensionality, a basis function selection scheme has already been implemented for Hagedorn wave packets using algebraic approaches
Vaníček and Zhang 2025
. However, in our implementation this will not be sufficient since a multidimensional grid is required. Therefore, a Smolyak scheme
Smolyak 1963
could be well adapted since it avoids exponential scaling for the basis set and also for the grid. For vibrational spectroscopy, this technique has been applied first by Avila and Carrington and then by several groups
Avila and Carrington 2009
;
Avila and Carrington 2017
;
Lauvergnat and Nauts 2014
;
Avila and Carrington 2017
;
Avila and Mátyus 2019
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Lauvergnat and Nauts 2023
. Furthermore, the Smolyak scheme has been applied to wave packet propagation with or without non-adaibatic couplings
Gradinaru 2008
;
Lasser and Lubich 2020
;
Pereira et al. 2023
;
Xie et al. 2024
. Therefore, we are also planning to combine the Smolyak scheme with the Hagedorn propagation technique.

Appendix: Projection

Once the new basis is constructed, we can proceed with the projection. From Eqs
11
,
16
,
18
, we recall that, the
C^{t_{2}}_{I}
are given by:

C^{t_{2}}_{J}=\innerproduct{B_{J}\left(\bm{\Lambda}_{t_2}\right) }{\Psi(t_2)}=\sum_{I=1}^{NB}\tilde{C}^{t_{2}}_{I}\innerproduct{B_{J}\left(\bm{\Lambda}_{t_2}\right) }{B_{I}\left(\bm{\Lambda}_{t_1}\right) },

(25)

where,
\tilde{C}^{t_{2}}_{I}
and
C^{t_{2}}_{J}
are the wave packet coefficients at time
t_{2}
associated, respectively, to the
I^{th}
basis function at time
t_{1}
and
J^{th}
basis function at time
t_{2}
.
This equation shows that the projection is performed on a multidimensional basis. The key elements are the overlaps between the basis functions at different times,
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. However, numerically, it can be achieved sequentially using partial projections (one coordinate after another). To perform the sequential transformations, the coefficients
\tilde{C}^{t_{2}}_{I}
and
C^{t_{2}}_{I}
notations need to be re-expressed as :

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tilde{C}^{t_{2}}_{I}&\equiv C_{i_{1},\cdots i_{k},\cdots,i_{nc}}^{\bm{b}^{1}_{t_{1}}\cdots\bm{b}^{k}_{t_{1}},\cdots,\bm{b}^{nc}_{t_{1}}}(t_{2}),\\
C^{t_{2}}_{I}&\equiv C_{i_{1},\cdots i_{k},\cdots,i_{nc}}^{\bm{b}^{1}_{t_{2}}\cdots\bm{b}^{k}_{t_{2}},\cdots,\bm{b}^{nc}_{t_{2}}}(t_{2}).\end{aligned}

(26)

This notation enables to show that the coefficients come from primitive basis sets at different times
t_{1}
or
t_{2}
.Furthermore, we recall that:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} B_{I}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{\Lambda}_{t_{1}}\right)&\equiv b^{1}_{i_{1}}\left(q_{1},t_{1}\right)\cdots b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},t_{1}\right)\cdots b^{nc}_{i_{nc}}\left(q_{nc},t_{1}\right),\\
B_{I}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{\Lambda}_{t_{2}}\right)&\equiv b^{1}_{i_{1}}\left(q_{1},t_{2}\right)\cdots b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},t_{2}\right)\cdots b^{nc}_{i_{nc}}\left(q_{nc},t_{2}\right).\end{aligned}

(27)

The functions
b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},t_{1}\right)\equiv b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},\lambda_{k}^{(t_{1})}\right)
and
b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},t_{2}\right)\equiv b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k},\lambda_{k}^{(t_{2})}\right)
represent the
i_{k}^{\text{th}}
function of the primitive basis
k
at time
t_{1}
and time
t_{2}
, respectively.
With these notations, the only difference between the
\tilde{C}^{t_{2}}_{I}
and
C^{t_{2}}_{I}
or between the
B_{I}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{\Lambda}_{t_{1}}\right)
and
B_{I}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{\Lambda}_{t_{2}}\right)
are the time indices (
t_{1}
or
t_{2}
) of the
\lambda_{k}^{t}
time dependent basis parameters (
k
).

The partial projection for the coordinate
k
reads:

\displaystyle C_{j_{1},\cdots j_{k},i_{k+1}\cdots,i_{nc}}^{\bm{b}^{1}_{t_{2}}\cdots\bm{b}^{k}_{t_{2}},\bm{b}^{k+1}_{t_{1}},\cdots,\bm{b}^{nc}_{t_{1}}}\left(t_{2}\right)=\sum_{i_{k}}\innerproduct{\bm{b}^k_{j_k}\left(t_2\right) }{\bm{b}^k_{i_k}\left(t_1\right) }

(28)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times C_{j_{1},\cdots j_{k-1},i_{k}\cdots,i_{nc}}^{\bm{b}^{1}_{t_{2}}\cdots\bm{b}^{k-1}_{t_{2}},\bm{b}^{k}_{t_{1}}\cdots,\bm{b}^{nc}_{t_{1}}}\left(t_{2}\right)

(29)

For this projection, some remarks are in order:

1.

The partial projection starts with
k=1
and ends at
k=nc
.

2.

For a given
k
, the partial projection has to be applied for all values of the indices
\left\{j_{1},\cdots j_{k},i_{k+1}\cdots,i_{nc}\right\}
.

3.

The overlap elements obtained by
n
D-multidimensional integrals,
\innerproduct{B_{J}\left(\bm{\Lambda}_{t_2}\right) }{B_{I}\left(\bm{\Lambda}_{t_1}\right) }
, are substituted by several
1
D-integrals,

\innerproduct{\bm{b}^k_{j_k}\left(t_2\right) }{\bm{b}^k_{i_k}\left(t_1\right) }\equiv\innerproduct{\bm{b}^k_{j_k}\left(\lambda_k^{(t_2)}\right) }{\bm{b}^k_{i_k}\left(\lambda_k^{(t_1)}\right) }
.

4.

These
1
D-integrals are just the overlaps,
\mathcal{S}^{k}\left(j_{k},i_{k}\right)
, between the
k^{th}

1
D-basis sets at different time.

The calculation of the
\mathcal{S}^{k}\left(j_{k},i_{k}\right)
overlap matrix elements are performed numerically using Gauss-Hermite quadratures:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{k}(j_{k},i_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\bm{b}^k_{j_k}\left(t_2\right) }{\bm{b}^k_{i_k}\left(t_1\right) }

(30)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{u=1}^{nq_{k}}b^{k}_{j_{k}}\left(q_{k}^{u},t_{2}\right)\cdot b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k}^{u},t_{1}\right)\cdot w_{k}^{u}

In Eq.
30
, the
q_{k}^{u}
and the
w_{k}^{u}\left(u\in\{1\cdots nq_{k}\}\right)
represent the Gauss quadrature,
\bm{g}^{k}
, for the shifted and scaled Harmonic Oscillator basis set. In term of standard Harmonic Oscillator grid (
x_{u}
and
w_{u}
),
q_{k}^{u}
and
w_{k}^{u}
are expressed as follows:

\displaystyle q_{k}^{u}=q^{(t)}_{k}+x_{u}/\sqrt{a^{(t)}_{k}},\quad w_{k}^{u}=w_{u}/\sqrt{a^{(t)}_{k}}

(31)

It is important to note that the grid used to compute
\mathcal{S}^{k}\left(j_{k},i_{k}\right)
is neither the grid associated with time
t_{1}
nor the one associated with time
t_{2}
. Indeed, since the product of two Gaussians is also a Gaussian, this grid is an intermediate grid constructed from the time-dependent basis set parameters at times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. More precisely, it can be shown that the intermediate parameters,
a^{Int}_{k}
and
q^{Int}
are:

\displaystyle a_{k}^{Int}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{(t_{1})}_{k}+a^{(t_{2})}_{k}}{2}

(32)

\displaystyle q_{k}^{Int}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{(t_{1})}_{k}\cdot q_{k}^{(t_{1})}+a^{(t_{2})}_{k}\cdot q_{k}^{(t_{2})}}{a^{(t_{1})}_{k}+a^{(t_{2})}_{k}}

(33)

Remarks: (i) we do not transform the basis sets
\bm{b}^{k}\left(q_{k},\lambda_{k}^{(t_{1})}\right)
and
\bm{b}^{k}\left(q_{k},\lambda_{k}^{(t_{2})}\right)
to a new basis set with the intermediate parameters, but we just compute
b^{k}_{j_{k}}\left(q_{k}^{u},\lambda_{k}^{(t_{2})}\right)
and
b^{k}_{i_{k}}\left(q_{k}^{u},\lambda_{k}^{(t_{1})}\right)
on the new grid points. (ii) When,
b^{(t)}_{k}
(imaginary part of
\alpha^{(t)}_{k}
) and
p^{(t)}_{k}
are not taken into account, the computation of
\mathcal{S}^{k}\left(j_{k},i_{k}\right)
are numerically exact as soon as
nq_{k}=nb_{k}
. (iii) To reach a good accuracy, we chose
nq_{k}=nb_{k}+5
.

Appendix: 2D-retinal model

The 2D-retinal model is described by a
2\times 2
Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}^{d}
, in the diabatic basis, expressed as:

\hat{H}^{d}\left(\bm{Q}\right)=\hat{T}\left(\bm{Q}\right)+\begin{bmatrix}V^{d}_{11}\left(\Phi,Q\right)&V^{d}_{12}\left(Q\right)\\
V^{d}_{21}\left(Q\right)&V^{d}_{22}\left(\Phi,Q\right)\end{bmatrix},

(34)

where the potentials for the two diabatic electronic states and their coupling are defined by:

\displaystyle V^{d}_{11}(\Phi,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}W_{1}(1-\cos\Phi)+\frac{1}{2}\omega Q^{2},

(35)

\displaystyle V^{d}_{22}(\Phi,Q)

\displaystyle=E-\frac{1}{2}W_{2}(1-\cos\Phi)+\frac{1}{2}\omega Q^{2}+\kappa Q,

(36)

\displaystyle V^{d}_{12}(Q)

\displaystyle=V_{21}(Q)=\lambda Q.

(37)

Here,
m
represents the effective mass of the reaction coordinate while
\omega
denotes the frequency of the coupling mode. The parameter
\lambda
corresponds to the inter-state coupling, and
W_{0}
,
W_{1}
, and
E
are adjusted to reproduce the properties of the electronic states
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. The coordinate
\Phi
represents the mode along which the molecule undergoes isomerization, and
Q
corresponds to the active vibronic mode coupling the two electronic states. The kinetic energy operator, assumed to be the same for both diabatic electronic states, is given by:

\hat{T}\left(\bm{Q}\right)=-\frac{1}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\Phi^{2}}-\frac{\omega}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial Q^{2}}.

(38)

The values of the model parameters, determined through fitting to experimental data and theoretical calculations, are:

•

the inverse of the effective mass,
m^{-1}=$4.84\text{\times}{10}^{-4}\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$

•

the torsional barriers
W_{0}=$3.6\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$
and
W_{1}=$1.09\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$

•

the linear coupling
\kappa=$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$

•

the vibronic frequency
\omega=$0.19\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$

•

the inter-state coupling
\lambda=$0.19\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$
,

•

the offset energy
E=$2.48\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$
.
