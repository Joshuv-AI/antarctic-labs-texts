Beyond the extra 5 min: Methodological considerations on optimizing breast milk expression
Authors: Faizan Ahmed, Maryam Bibi, Shahzadi Gulfishan, Ahmed Raza, Muhammad Hassan Imtiaz
Published: 2026

Sir,

We read with great interest the study by Manshanden et al., which reports that adding 5 min of nutritive suction increased expressed milk volume during the first four postpartum days. 1 The findings are clinically intriguing; however, several important methodological considerations deserve attention before the intervention is translated into routine practice.

First, the absence of randomization and a concurrent control condition make it difficult to attribute the additional 1.44 mL of milk specifically to the modified suction pattern. The breast received five additional minutes of stimulation, so increased duration alone could explain part or all of the observed effect. A randomized trial comparing equal duration sessions with different suction patterns, alongside a standard 15‐min control, would isolate the pattern‐specific effect and provide a more reliable estimate. 2

Second, the cohort was clinically heterogeneous, encompassing ineffective feeding, supplementation, prematurity, parent–infant separation, and other indications for pumping. These conditions may independently influence lactogenesis and pumping response, potentially producing effect modification or residual confounding. Future studies should stratify or prespecify clinically relevant subgroups and use multivariable models incorporating indication, lactation stage, and baseline milk production. 3

Third, the single‐center design limits external validity. The study was conducted in a Baby‐Friendly hospital with established lactation support practices, which may not reflect settings with different staffing, breastfeeding support, pump availability, or postpartum discharge pathways. Multicenter trials across diverse healthcare systems and populations would establish whether the observed benefit is reproducible. 4

Finally, the intervention depends on a proprietary suction program delivered using a Medela Symphony pump, and one author is employed by the manufacturer. Although the conflict is transparently disclosed, device‐specific effects may limit reproducibility and generalizability to other pumps. Independent replication using different commercially available devices and standardized, publicly described suction parameters would strengthen confidence in the biological rather than device‐specific nature of the finding. 5

In conclusion, the study addresses an important clinical question and offers a potentially simple strategy for improving early colostrum extraction. Addressing these limitations in future trials could transform an interesting physiological observation into robust, generalizable clinical evidence.
