Cross-sectional trends in healthy lifestyles among the oldest-old in China, 1998–2018
Authors: Ruixue Bai, Hua Zhang, Xiaoya Qi, Tao Jiang, Yueling Deng, Xiangyang Gao, Jing Deng, Yao Yao, Ying Mei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthy lifestyle is vital for active aging and increasing life expectancy of older adults. This study aimed to examine a cross-sectional trend in the lifestyles of the oldest-old in China over the past two decades and to identify the major influencing factors.MethodsAfter excluding participants with missing data, 19,736 oldest-old (≥80 years old) were analyzed across 1998, 2008, and 2018 three cross-sectional waves of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS).Healthy lifestyle factors were defined based on the “four cornerstones of health” proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO), including balanced diet, moderate exercise, former/never tobacco and alcohol behavior, and good mental health. Logistic regression models were used to assess associations between sociodemographic factors and healthy lifestyles across different years.ResultsThe study results revealed that the prevalence of healthy lifestyle factors among the oldest-old Chinese increased from 1998 to 2018 (8.1%, 13.7%, 21.2% in 1998, 2008, 2018, respectively). Trend analyses also showed that the prevalence of balanced diet, former/never smoking and alcohol drinking behavior, and mental health increased from 1998 to 2018 (p trend <0.05), but no significant change in regular exercise. Improved education and non-manual occupation before 60 years were associated with a higher chance of having healthy lifestyles in all three study years, whereas rural residence was associated with a lower chance of having healthy lifestyles. The gap between rural–urban trend has been narrowing from 1998 to 2018, yet a significant disparity remains.ConclusionThe findings suggest that targeted interventions are needed to promote physical activity and rural-urban disparities in healthy lifestyles should be addressed among the oldest-old Chinese.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06747-2.

Introduction

Population aging has emerged as a critical public health concern worldwide in the 21 st century [20, 54]. Among middle-income countries, China is experiencing an accelerated pace of aging. According to the Seventh National Census of China, in 2020, the population aged 65 years and above reached 190 million, accounting for 13.5% of the country’s total population and surpassing the United Nations’ threshold for aging countries, where the proportion of the population aged 65 years and above is 7% [55]. The aging population is projected to further increase to 28% by 2040, cementing China’s status as an aging society country [47]. Life expectancy at birth in China has increased significantly, reaching 75.3 years in 2015 and is expected to reach around 80 years by 2050. Meanwhile, the prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease (CVD), has risen with aging [1, 24, 47, 49, 50, 52].

Lifestyle factors such as diet, smoking, drinking, and exercise are widely recognized to be closely linked with the occurrence of NCDs [41]. High-quality diets, regular exercise, less alcohol consumption, and non-smoking are significantly linked to lower risks of all-cause mortality and major chronic diseases, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, CVD, and cancer [6, 33, 39, 56]. Furthermore, as the number of healthy lifestyle factors increases, chronic disease morbidity declines [57]. Fortunately, these lifestyle factors are modifiable, therefore, promoting healthy lifestyle-oriented strategies may be the most cost-effective way to prevent NCDs [2, 8]. In recent years, growing evidence has shown that psychological health can also positively or negatively affect human health and serves as a vital predictor of longevity, with decreased all-cause mortality risk [16, 36, 43]. Mental health significantly impacts overall health and longevity, making it a critical factor in healthy aging. In 1992, the World Health Organization (WHO) first introduced the concept of the “four cornerstones of health” in the Victorian Declaration, which includes balanced diet, moderate exercise, former/never tobacco and alcohol use, and mental health. If the elderly follow these lifestyle touchpoints, then it can help prevent diseases, improve their quality of life, and promote health and longevity.

Existing studies have mainly focused on the national trends of combined healthy lifestyle factors among adults in Western countries [15, 58]. Most studies have investigated healthy lifestyle factors among the oldest-old in China, and revealed that the oldest-old population with a healthy lifestyle (higher dietary diversity, never smoking, never drinking, doing exercise) was associated with lower all-cause mortality, in addition, these factors have been linked to aging-related outcomes, including lower cognitive impairment, frailty, and sarcopenia [5, 12, 31, 40]. As the number of healthy lifestyle behaviors increased, the survival rate of the oldest-old improved gradually [4]. Recent data emphasized that maintaining a healthy lifestyle can yield comparable mortality benefits for older individuals, irrespective of their level of multimorbidity [73]. These studies have confirmed the importance of a healthy lifestyle for healthy aging from different perspectives among the oldest-old. Consequently, it is crucial to understand the trends of healthy lifestyle behaviors of the oldest-old to enhance the prevention of NCDs, reduce mortality risk and promote longevity. Few studies have assessed the combination of trends regarding healthy lifestyle factors among the oldest-old population in China, prior published data on the Chinese elderly has demonstrated that the evolution results of healthy behaviors generally have three trends: better, unchanged, and worse from 2005 to 2014 by using latent class analysis (LCA) [14]. However, the current study has primarily focused on older adults aged 65 years and over, not all oldest-old population, and the time span was only nine years and the study data were collected until 2014. Besides, lifestyle behaviors according to data characteristics were classified using the LCA method, it indicated the evolving trend of lifestyle behaviors in Chinese elderly, but it did not describe the detailed trend of a particular healthy lifestyle behavior, neglecting to consider the mental health whthin healthy lifestyle behavior. According to the framework of WHO Decade of Healthy Aging in recent years, in order to achieve healthy aging, older people need to have the functional capacity to experience health (i.e. absence of disease or frailty) and well-being (i.e. the state or quality of being and feeling healthy) in their daily lives [60]. Mental health can either enable (or disable) functional ability [21]. Subjective well-being (SWB) is a major psychological construct in the research tradition, which comprises three components: life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect, referring to individuals’ self-reported assessments of their own well-being, which is crucial for achieving successful aging and has become increasingly critical in the elderly population [3, 53]. Studying how the healthy lifestyles of the oldest-old change provides a basis for targeted preventive measures, especially for those who are not yet in their senior years. Also, to develop targeted recommendations and reduce health inequalities, exploring sociodemographic effects is crucial [49, 50]

In this context, to examine temporal changes in healthy lifestyles among the oldest-old Chinese population in the past two decades, we used three cross-sectional population-based epidemiological surveys from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) conducted among participants aged 80 years and above in 1998, 2008, and 2018. Healthy lifestyle dimensions were constructed based on the WHO’s four cornerstones of healthy lifestyle, with SWB used to evaluate the mental health of the oldest-old in the context of healthy aging, and we investigated the influencing factors of healthy lifestyles among the oldest-old population to explore effective health intervention strategies to promote health management among the elderly population.

Methods

Study setting and population

The Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) is a nationwide survey that uses a multistage, stratified cluster sampling design. It was conducted in 631 randomly selected cities and counties across 22 of China’s 31 provinces, covering approximately 85% of the country’s total population, including eastern, central, western, and northeastern regions. The baseline survey was conducted in 1998, dataset contains 9093 respondents, of whom more than 8805 were aged 80 and above. Participants were followed up in 1998, 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008-09, 2011-12, 2013-14, and 2018. In the first four nationwide follow-up surveys (2000, 2002, 2005, and 2008), deceased respondents and those lost to follow-up were replaced by recruiting new participants of the same sex and age. However, no such new recruits were included as replacements in the 2011 and 2014 CLHLS nationwide follow-up surveys, primarily due to budget constraints. Exceptions were made only in eight selected longevity areas, where the density of centenarians remained exceptionally high in each follow-up wave. As reported by the 2018 wave cross-sectional dataset, only 10 of participants were interviewed in all seven waves (1998, 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008, 2011/2012, 2014), and 12,411 of them were newly interviewed in 2018. The CLHLS is the most representative large-scale population cohort survey of the oldest-old in China [19, 67]. Consequently, the CLHLS participants of the oldest-old interviewed in 1998, 2008, and 2018 were nationally representative samples, and adequate for the analysis of healthy lifestyle changes over 20 years in ten years apart. Details of the sampling strategy of CLHLS have been described in previous studies [65, 68]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-1307). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To observe changes in healthy lifestyle behavior over a long period of time, three cross-sectional population-based surveys in 1998, 2008, and 2018 were selected, with individuals aged over 80 years (8959, 12277, 10427 in 1998, 2008, and 2018, respectively) were included in the analysis. After excluding11927 participants with missing data on healthy lifestyle behavior, demographic data, knee height, hypertension disease history, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and activity of daily living (ADL), a total of 6034, 8638, and 5064 oldest-old Chinese were included in the final analyses of 1998, 2008, and 2018 wave, respectively. Excluded participants had a higher mean age compared to the final analytical sample (mean age: 94.6±7.6 yearsvs. 91.3±7.3). They also had a lower educational level, were more likely to have unmarried marital status, were more prone to functional limitations, and were more likely to have a manual occupation before age 60. Consequently, our final analytical sample is subject to potential selection bias. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to the more frail and socioeconomically disadvantaged elderly subgroups. Thus, our results are likely more applicable to a relatively healthier and more educated sub-population. Construction of Healthy lifestyle score(HLS)

We selected five healthy behavior factors to construct a four-dimensional healthy lifestyle score (HLS) according to the four cornerstones of health proposed by the WHO, including a balanced diet, moderate exercise, former/never smoking and alcohol drinking behavior, and mental health.

Balanced diet was assessed by dietary diversity scores (DDS), which were based on a food frequency questionnaire associated with eight major food groups: fresh fruits, fresh vegetables, meat, fish, eggs, beans, tea, and garlic. The study participants were asked how often they ate or drank the above foods at present. They were given a score of 1 point if they answered “almost every day” and 0 otherwise. The scores for these food groups ranged from 0 to 8, with higher dietary scores indicating more adequate food intake and the greater healthiness of the diet [27]. The maximum total score for DDS is 8 points. Thus, balanced diet was defined as DDS score≥5 points, as previously reported [72].

Moderate exercise was assessed using a single self-report question: “Do you do exercises regularly at present? (This refers to purposeful physical activities such as walking, playing sports, running, qigong, etc.)” rather than detailed metrics (e.g., intensity, duration, or metabolic equivalents). Participants who answered “yes” received a score of 1, whereas those who answered “no” received 0. This lack of granularity may limit the nuance of our physical activity analysis, potentially contributing to the nonsignificant trend.

Based on the theoretical framework proposed by the WHO for the four cornerstones of health, we combined smoking and alcohol drinking behavior into one dimension “Former/never smoking and alcohol drinking behavior”. Former/never smoking and drinking behavior was assessed by questions about tobacco smoking and alcohol drinking. Tobacco smoking was verified by asking the participants through two questions, “Do you smoke at the present time?” and “Did you smoke in the past?” Based on their answers to these questions, we recorded the tobacco smoking status as “never smoking,” “former smoker,” and “current smoker.” A similar approach was taken to define alcohol drinking as “never drinking,” “former drinker,” and “current drinker” through two questions, “Do you drink alcohol at the present time?” and “Did you drink alcohol in the past?”. Former/never smoking and drinking behavior was defined as participants who were “never smoking or former smoker” and “never drinking or former drinker,” and we recorded as “1” point, and “0” if otherwise.

Mental health was evaluated using subjective well-being (SWB). SWB refers to a broad category of phenomena encompassing emotional responses, overall life satisfaction, and affective states (positive or negative) [11]. Life satisfaction was assessed using the five-point Likert scale item “How do you rate your life at present?”. Positive affect was measured with four items that represented optimism, conscientiousness, personal control, and happiness, each scored on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”). Negative affect was assessed using three items that measured anxiety, loneliness, and uselessness, and was also scored on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (“always”) to 5 (“never”) [18]. SWB consisted of the sum of eight items. Scores ranged from 8 to 40, with a higher score indicating better SWB. Mental health was defined by an SWB total score of ≥33, as previous studies reported good construct validity for this measure in assessing the SWB of the oldest old [10, 70]. A score of 1 was assigned to individuals with SWB ≥33, and 0 otherwise.

Each positive answer to the four lifestyle factors was given a score of 1.Generally, a healthy lifestyle score is defined as the sum of all four items and ranging from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating a healthier lifestyle, in this study, we defined a healthy lifestyle as a total HLS of ≥3.

Sociodemographic variables

Sociodemographic variables were also assessed for their influence on healthy lifestyle. Participants’ ages were classified into three groups (80–90 years, 90–100 years, and ≥100 years). Current residence was categorized as urban or rural. Education was grouped into three duration categories (0 years, 1–5 years, and ≥6 years). Occupation before age 60 years was classified as manual or non-manual, in which professional or administrative occupations were recorded as non-manual. Marital status was divided into unmarried (married but not living with spouse, never married, divorced, or widowed) and married (married and living with spouse).

Other variables included hypertension, defined as having a history of hypertension or a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure(DBP)≥90mmHg ("National guideline for hypertension management in China [45])," 2020). Height was estimated using knee height, as it was not recorded in the 1998 wave [61, 69]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and then divided into four groups based on the recommendation of the Working Group on Obesity in China (WGOC) criteria recommendation: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–23.9.5.9 kg/m2), overweight (24–27.9.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥28 kg/m2) [9]. Disability was assessed using the Katz Activities of Daily Living (ADL) Scale, with difficulty performing any one or more of the ADL tasks (bathing, dressing, toileting, transfers, continence, and eating) defined as having an ADL disability [26].

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of participants were summarized based on the different years (wave 1998, 2008, and 2018 years). We presented data as frequencies and percentages [n (%)] for categorical variables. Chi-square tests were used to test differences of categorical variables by different years. Survey year was considered as a continuous variable in a Cochran-Armitage test to examine the statistical significance of trends in healthy lifestyle (HLS and each dimension of HLS) over time. The health inequality resulting from social stratification is an important perspective for exploring the health inequity [42]. Social stratification for the purpose of this study was defined as education level, current residence, and main occupation before age 60. Logistic regressions were used to assess the associations between sociodemographic variables and healthy lifestyles (HLS ≥3), overall and stratified by social stratification, eg education level (0 years, 1–5 years, above 6 years), current residence (urban, rural) and main occupation before age 60 (manual, non-manual). Interactions were conducted by incorporating multiplicative terms of the survey cycle with each stratified variable to assess the heterogeneity of trends over time among subgroups. Sensitivity analysis of the main outcome without the mental health indicator to see if the high proportion of missing affect results. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using statistical software Stata 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

Multivariate analysis of factors associated with healthy lifestyle

Logistic regression results showed that those with higher education level, and had non-manual occupation before 60 years had a higher chance of having healthy lifestyles (total score ≥ 3) in all three study years. Similarly, those with rural residence had a lower chance of having healthy lifestyles in all three study years. Participants in women (OR: 1.22, CI: 1.03–1.44.03.44) were more likely to have healthy lifestyle behavior (HLS ≥ 3) that compared with men in 2018 years. Participants living in rural areas, having non-married status were less likely to have healthy lifestyle (HLS ≥ 3) than those living in urban areas and having married status. Associated factors for healthy lifestyle among oldest-old Chinese, by years of education, occupation, and residence

For the education level subgroups, the participants, especially those who had 0 years of education in 2018, had lower chance of having healthy lifestyle(HLS≥3) than those who were 90-100years old(OR:0.76, CI: 0.59–0.97.59.97) and ≥100 years old (OR: 0.52, CI: 0.39–0.70.39.70), compared with those who were 80–90 years old. Those with high education level (years of education for above 6 years), particularly in women, had a higher chance of having healthy lifestyle (HLS≥3) than men in 2018 (OR: 1.42, CI: 1.05–1.92.05.92)).

For residence subgroups, there was a 1.28-fold higher observation of having healthy lifestyle (HLS≥3) in women than men living in urban regions in 2018. Furthermore, in the occupation stratified analysis, older age (OR: 0.79, CI: 0.66–0.95.66.95 for those 90–100 years old and OR: 0.57, CI: 0.45–0.72.45.72, ≥100 years old) decreased the odds of having healthy lifestyle (HLS≥3) among participants with manual occupation in 2018. However, for those with non-manual occupation, women(OR: 1.55, CI: 1.04–2.32.04.32) had increased the odds of having healthy lifestyle (HLS≥3) in 2018 (Supplementary table 1).

Sensitivity analysis after excluded mental health indicator for basic characteristics and healthy lifestyles of the oldest-old by different years

Results of sensitivity analysis of the main outcome without the mental health indicator (SWB) for basic characteristics and healthy lifestyles of the oldest-old was showed in Supplementary table 2, and Supplementary table 3. It indicated that after excluded mental health indicator, similar results were obtained in dietary behavior, smoking and alcohol drinking behavior. Engagement in regular exercise declined from 1998 to 2018 (from 28.2% to 25.7%, P for trend <0.001), whereas the P trend for regular exercise showed no statistically significant difference from 1998 to 2018 after including the mental health indicators (prevalence of regular exercise was reduced from 30.7% to 29.7%, P for trend =0.119). We found that after excluding the mental health indicators the prevalence of having healthy lifestyle also showed an increasing trend.

Discussion

In this first study on the healthy lifestyle over the past two decades in China focusing on the oldest-old, we found that there was an increasing trend in healthy lifestyle among oldest-old Chinese in the past two decades (from 8.1% in 1998 to 21.2% in 2018). The increase in healthy lifestyle in this study was mainly attributed to balanced diet, reasonable tobacco and alcohol behavior, and mental health. However, we did not find the same trend in the proportion of regular exercise from 1998 to 2018.

The healthy behavior of former/never smoking and drinking behavior increased in recent 20 years among Chinese oldest-old. The vast majority of oldest-old have former/never smoked (82.2% to 87.1%, from 1998 to 2018), which is in line with declines in current smoking reported in previous studies [51, 59, 71]. The overall self-reported smoking prevalence in China decreased moderately from 1984 to 2018 [51]. Nevertheless, the prevalence of never smoking and never drinking in older adults is much higher than results from other studies, whose participants are younger than 69 years old [71]. This finding reveals that participants of the studies may not be comparable in age or other important factors with the oldest-old population. In addition, these improvements may be attributable to the implementation of a series of actions since 2005, when the Chinese government ratified the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC), as a result of which a comprehensive array of tobacco control measures has been progressively implemented across the country, marking an important milestone in the implementation of China’s tobacco control policies [7]. For trends in drinking behavior, our results are inconsistent with previous studies. One study conducted in Jiangsu Province China, reported that the drinking rates increased from 13.58% to 28.77% between 2008 to 2018 [34]. While another study reported that the proportion of current drinkers is rising in China between 2010 to 2020, yet the incidence of alcohol drinking decreased gradually with age [64]. With the development of society and economy, the prevalent drinking behavior during rituals, festivals, and social gatherings may have changed for oldest-old, and the elderly may pay more attention to other leisure ways, which necessitates further research to corroborate.

The proportion of balanced diet improved in our study, which is consistent with prior reports that observed improvements in the Healthy Eating Index (HEI)–2015 from 1999 to 2020 and the American Heart Association(AHA) diet score during 2001 to 2018 in US adults [32, 37]. However, only the prevalence of fresh vegetable intake and tea intake have declined in the last 20 years in oldest-old Chinese, while all other food (fruit, meat, fish and sea food, egg, bean, and garlic) intakes was elevated. This divergence—declining vegetables alongside rising meat, egg, and bean consumption suggests that the oldest-old’s dietary preferences shifted toward more diverse, protein-rich foods as 1998-2018 socioeconomic development and urbanization improved food accessibility and quality of life. With greater availability of animal products and processed options, older adults may have prioritized these over fresh vegetables, reflecting a change in “food choice” driven by improved living standards. The prevalence of almost everyday have vegetable intake was contrary to the results from the national estimates of chronic diseases and risk factor surveillance with inadequate vegetable/fruit intake decreased during 2013 to 2018 [49, 50, 59], though our participants (oldest-old) and longer timeframe (1998-2018) highlight unique trends for this vulnerable group. Monitoring future changes in these foods will be crucial. Further research is needed to explain the causes of this phenomenon.

Evidence has shown that tea consumption is associated with beneficial effects on different health outcomes including weight, blood pressure, blood glucose, lipids and frailty status [22, 46], our study revealed that the tea intake is declined in recent 20 years, the exact reasons were unclear. For tea, two interconnected factors likely play a role: first, China’s booming food processing industry expanded beverage options (e.g., sweetened juices, functional drinks) [63], giving older adults more palatable alternatives to traditional tea. Second, the rise of social media/self-media increased public discussion about tea’s health effects, potentially leading some elders to abandon tea for “trendier” or more personalized options that better suit their tastes [29, 44]. Future study is needed to explain the underlying reasons for the declining trend in vegetable and tea consumption among oldest-old population, which would provide insights into the development of public health and nutrition policies, as well as interventions for promoting healthy eating.

However, given that only 30% of oldest-old reported engaging in regular exercise in the last 2018 survey, there is still much room for improvement. Previous study highlighted that the oldest-old in China have become more sedentary and solitary over the past 20 years [13]. Sensitivity analysis showed that after excluded mental health indicator, the engagement in regular exercise was declined over past two decades. Declining in physical activity levels in oldest-old adults requires urgent concerns. Numerous studies have shown that maintained regular exercise habits have various aspects of health for the elderly, such as maintaining muscle content, improving cardiopulmonary function and cognitive function, preventing sarcopenia, falls and so on [28, 30, 35, 48]. At the same time, in order to increase the exercise participation rate of the elderly, it is very important to develop habits of regular exercise during young and middle ages. Health promotion interventions can be implemented when individuals are as young as possible to increase their engagement in physical activity in old age. Furthermore, it is a slight regret that this exercise question design did not include about the frequency, intensity, and specific types of exercise, which would have helped us better understand the exercise patterns among the elderly population. Further research is needed to explore this aspect so as to better develop targeted policies for improving this behavior among the oldest-old.

The mental health of the elderly in China has shown a trend of improvement. The proportion of participants answered with “always be happy as younger”, “always can make own decisions”, “never feel nervous and scared”, “never feel lonely and isolated”, and “never feel useless with age” in 2018 was significantly higher than that in 1998. In the past 20 years, with the rapid development of economic level, and the improvement of living standards, the life expectancy was extended, older people are living more independently than ever before, also our data showed that the marital status of married was higher in 2018 than in 1998, research declared that marriage can be predictive of higher SWB, these may make older people feel less depression and loneliness [25]. And, the support system for the mental health of the elderly in society is gradually being established and improved, including psychological counseling, community activities, etc., which may provide more mental health support for the elderly.

Logistic regression models showed a significant association of having healthy lifestyles with increasing years of education and the non-manual occupation before 60 years, and oldest-old adults with rural residence had a lower chance of having healthy lifestyle, in agreement with previous studies [17, 23, 38].

Stratified analyses indicated that particularly those who are nonagenarian and centenarian with lower education levels, had manual occupation before 60 years are more likely to have less healthy lifestyle. For these groups, policymakers could consider optimizing the health intervention processes and methods. To address these barriers, we can develop visually oriented, colloquial health education, for example pictograms illustrating exercise routines and simplified dietary guides using familiar local foods to bypass literacy barriers. Community health workers (CHWs) could then deliver these materials door-to-door, leveraging trust within community networks. Residence-related differences that were observed with participants living in rural area had a lower healthy lifestyle more often than urban area persons attenuated over time across 20 years. It mirrored the enhancement in equality of health behavior between urban and rural areas. From 1980 s to 2010, a national system of social security in China was gradually established, all older people (including rural residence) had basic medical insurance and pensions. The coverage rate of social health insurance increased from 20.5% in 1998 to 97.1% in 2018 in China, the disparities in health care utilization between urban and rural areas have been narrowed over the past 25 years [17]. The proportion of people without health insurance coverage in 2018 was only 2.9% and 2.0% for urban and rural areas respectively. As well as, the gap between urban and rural life expectancy has been reduced [62], these suggested that societies can expect significant improvements in health equality. China has simultaneously issued a series of development plans for elderly population. For China’s Equalization of Basic Public Health Services (EBPHS) policy have implemented since 2009, which included chronic disease management, health education, etc [66]. More older people can acquire more knowledge about health care, and disease prevention to improve their health awareness and lead them to adopt healthier lifestyle behaviors. Though these policies may promote health equity, they do not eliminate it entirely. The study data showed that the odds of having a healthy lifestyle have improved for rural residents over time (OR from 0.38 in 1998 to 0.57 in 2018), yet a significant disparity remains. The gap between urban and rural areas has been narrowing, but it remains high. Therefore, it is necessary to focus more on rural areas when formulating public policies and allocating public services to eliminate disparities in health care.

This study has several strengths: CLHLS is a nationally representative surveys only for Chinese elderly (oldest-old mostly), with standardized data collection and rigorous quality control, therefore, the results can be generalized to the Chinese oldest-old population. In addition, based on the framework of four cornerstones of healthy lifestyle that proposed by WHO, we describe the healthy lifestyle of Chinese oldest-old, reflects the changes in healthy lifestyle of Chinese oldest-old over the past 20 years, which can provide reference for interventions towards healthy aging. Our study had certain limitations: Firstly, participants all self-reported their healthy behaviors, may lead to recall bias. Secondly, we conducted a correlation analysis but were unable to draw any causal conclusions from the data, the C-statistics of the models is mainly at a moderate level, indicating that there are still residual confounding factors. And a key limitation of our serial cross-sectional design is the inability to disentangle age, period, and cohort effects. For example, the health behaviors of an 85-year-old in 2018 (born c. 1933) may differ from an 85-year-old in 1998 (born c. 1913) not only because of period effects like economic development and public health policies, but also due to cohort effects, such as different levels of education, nutritional exposures in early life, and cultural norms experienced throughout their lives. Therefore, the observed trends likely represent a complex interplay of these factors, and our findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Thirdly, due to the design factors of the questionnaire, such as the crude measures for exercise, our understanding of the details of physical activity among the oldest-old populations was limited. Further study is needed to explore this issue. Also, for the evaluation of the disability of the elderly, our analysis was limited to ADLs and income data due to the absence of instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) data, and income information in the 1998 cross-sectional survey. In future research, we could further explore other important factors affecting health outcomes through further analysis. Since some data were not collected (missing data), our study may be subject to selection bias.

Conclusion

The current study provides the recent trends in lifestyle factors for oldest-old over the past 20 years based on the WHO’s four cornerstones of health lifestyles, the overall trend of health lifestyles among oldest old Chinese has increased, but moderate exercise behavior needs improvement. The healthy lifestyle behavior with urban-rural difference has been narrowing in the past two decades, but it has not yet been eliminated. Similarly, future developments rooted in these trends should be considered. At the same time, our findings reinforce the need for targeted interventions to change unhealthy lifestyles for healthy aging. Health promotion strategies should be implemented to provide social support (including financial assistance, and health education et) for the oldest-old. This will continuously improve their healthy lifestyles, particularly for older adults from lower social strata. Policy efforts are needed to eliminate the urban-rural disparities in health inequalities.
