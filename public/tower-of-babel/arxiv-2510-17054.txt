Note on Jackson’s formalism of gauge transformation
V. Hnizdo

Abstract
An outline is given of the derivation of two inhomogeneous wave equations in an influential 2002 AJP paper of Jackson on the transformation from the Lorenz gauge to other electromagnetic gauges. The derivation shows that, contrary to a statement in the paper, the function satisfying one of the equations is not associated with the Coulomb-gauge vector potential but is associated with the Lorenz-gauge vector potential instead.



In his influential 2002 AJP paper on the transformation from the Lorenz gauge to other electromagnetic gauges [1], Jackson does not derive the inhomogeneous wave equations (2.10) satisfied by the auxiliary functions
\Psi
and
\bf V
of the formalism he develops for the transformation from the Lorenz gauge to the Coulomb gauge. In this note, we outline how these equations can be derived. The derivation shows that, contrary to a statement in the paper, the function
\Psi
is not associated with the Coulomb-gauge vector potential but is associated with the Lorenz-gauge vector potential instead.

The well-known inhomogeneous wave equation for the Lorenz-gauge vector potential
{\bf A}_{L}

reads

\displaystyle\Box\,{\bf A}_{L}=-(4\pi/c)\,{\bf J},

(1)

where
{\bf J}={\bf J}_{l}+{\bf J}_{t}
is the full current, decomposed into its longitudinal and transverse components
{\bf J}_{l}
and
{\bf J}_{t}
, respectively. Decomposing similarly also the vector potential

{\bf A}_{L}
itself, Eq. (
1
) will read

\displaystyle\Box\,({\bf A}_{L\,l}+{\bf A}_{L\,t})=-(4\pi/c)\,({\bf J}_{l}+{\bf J}_{t}),

(2)

where
{\bf A}_{L\,l}
and
{\bf A}_{L\,t}
are the longitudinal and transverse components of the Lorenz-gauge vector potential
{\bf A}_{L}
, respectively. Since the Coulomb-gauge vector potential
{\bf A}_{C}
, which is satisfied by the inhomogeneous wave equation

\displaystyle\Box\,{\bf A}_{C}=-(4\pi/c)\,{\bf J}_{t},

(3)

is the transverse component of the vector potential in any gauge
[
2
]
,
{\bf A}_{L\,t}={\bf A}_{C}
and so Eq. (
2
) reduces to

\displaystyle\Box\,{\bf A}_{L\,l}=-(4\pi/c)\,{\bf J}_{l}.

(4)

With
{\bf A}_{L\,l}
written as
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Psi
, using the fact that a longitudinal vector component can be expressed as the gradient of a scalar, and with
{\bf J}_{l}
given by the expression for the longitudinal component of a current
\bf J
in terms of the pertinent current density
\rho
, Eq. (4)
can be written as

\displaystyle\Box\,(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Psi)=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\,(\Box\Psi)=-\frac{1}{c}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\int\frac{d^{3}x^{\prime}}{R}\frac{\partial\rho({\bf x}^{\prime},t)}{\partial t},

(5)

where
R=|{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}|
. Finally, this equation is taken to imply that

\displaystyle\Box\,\Psi=-\frac{1}{c}\,\int\frac{d^{3}x^{\prime}}{R}\frac{\partial\rho({\bf x}^{\prime},t)}{\partial t},

(6)

which is the first of Jackson’s equations (2.10).

Expressing the transverse component
{\bf J}_{t}
of
\bf J
by

\displaystyle{\bf J}_{t}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\int\frac{d^{3}x^{\prime}}{R}\,{\bf J}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)\right),

(7)

and writing the transverse vector
{\bf A}_{C}
as the curl of a vector,
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bf V}
, the inhomogeneous wave equation (3) for the Coulomb-gauge vector potential will read

\displaystyle\Box\,(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bf V})=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(\Box{\bf V})=-\frac{1}{c}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\int\frac{d^{3}x^{\prime}}{R}\,{\bf J}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)\right),

(8)

which is taken to imply that

\displaystyle\Box\,{\bf V}=-\frac{1}{c}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\int\frac{d^{3}x^{\prime}}{R}\,{\bf J}({\bf x}^{\prime},t),

(9)

and that is the second of Jackson’s equations (2.10).

These derivations show that Jackson’s sentence just above his equations (2.10), “For the Coulomb-gauge vector potential, the auxiliary functions
\Psi
and
\bf V
satisfy inhomogeneous equations”, is an unfortunate misstatement. It should read something along these lines: “For the Lorenz-gauge (Coulomb-gauge) vector potential, the auxiliary function
\Psi
(
\bf V
) satisfies the first (second) of the following inhomogeneous equations”.

Deservedly, Jackson’s paper has been cited many times since its publication in 2002 – still, to the present auhtor’s knowledge, nobody has pointed out this misstatement publicly yet. It may have confused some readers, but it should not have those who are well-versed in the subject, since equations (2.10) and the auxiliary functions
\Psi
and
\bf V
are used in the paper in accordance with their true meaning.

The auxiliary function
\Psi
plays an important role in Jackson’s paper since the inhomogeneous wave equation (
6
) it satisfies is, except for the different sign of its RHS, the same as
the inhomogeneous equation for the gauge function
\chi_{C}
(see [1], Eq. (3.8)) introduced by Jackson for the transformation of Lorenz-gauge potentials to those in the Coulomb gauge. The retarded solutions of these inhomogeneous equations are thus the same except for their signs.
