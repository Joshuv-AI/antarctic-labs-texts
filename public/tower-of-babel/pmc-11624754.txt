Analysing the effects of National Centralised Drug Procurement and Price Negotiation Policies on novel hypoglycaemic drug usage and costs in Shanghai, China: an interrupted time series analysis
Authors: Jie Song, Wei Guo, Chunlin Jin, Yuan Xu, Xiaojing Hu, Zheng Zhang, Hai Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo analyse the impacts of multiple rounds of National Centralised Drug Procurement Policy (NCDP) and National Drug Price Negotiation Policy (NDPN) on the utilisation, expenditure and daily cost of novel hypoglycaemic drugs.DesignThe drug procurement data were obtained from the Shanghai Medical Procurement Agency between January 2016 and December 2022. We examined changes in volume, expenditure of overall hypoglycaemic drugs by descriptive analysis. For novel hypoglycaemic drugs, we employed interrupted time series analysis with segmented regression to investigate the change in usage, expenditure and daily cost of specific drug affected by related policies.SettingAll the public and private hospitals in Shanghai, China.Primary outcome measuresVolume, expenditure and daily cost of novel hypoglycaemic drugs.ResultsFrom 2016 to 2022, the overall usage and expenditure of hypoglycaemic drugs in Shanghai have been gradually increasing. Consumption of novel hypoglycaemic drugs had shown a rapid increase, which was significantly increased by 212.28 defined daily doses (95% CI 188.25, 236.31, p<0.001) after policy implementation. For each specific drug, daily cost was significantly decreased immediately after policy implementation (p<0.001). Dulaglutide, loxenatide, liraglutide, semaglutide and dapagliflozin had all seen significant increases in consumption and expenditure after NDPN implementation (p<0.001). Vildagliptin and saxagliptin, which were affected by the NCDP, experienced a significant decrease in usage. Empagliflozin and canagliflozin, which were regulated by both NCDP and NDPN, experienced an initial increase, followed by a significant decline. Consumption of beinaglutide and exenatide was not upregulated by NDPN.ConclusionImplementation of NDPN and NCDP had successfully cut-off the price of novel hypoglycaemic drugs and significantly increased their utilisation. In the future, the government needs to control pharmaceutical expenses more strictly while ensuring the rational use of the drugs.Keywords: Drug Utilization, Health policy, DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY

Introduction

Diabetes is a complex chronic condition, which requires continuous medical care beyond glucose management.1 Complications caused by diabetes severely affect the survival rate and quality of life of patients. Diabetes has become one of the chronic diseases with the highest mortality rate after cardiovascular diseases, tumours and chronic respiratory diseases.2 The global prevalence of diabetes has been rapidly increasing. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the global diabetes prevalence among people aged 20–79 will reach 12.2% (783.2 million), and diabetes-related healthcare expenditure will reach US$1054 billion by the year 2045.3 As the population in China continues to age, coupled with changes in lifestyles and dietary habits, an increasing number of Chinese are facing the threat of diabetes. Currently, there are approximately 110 million diabetes patients in China, making it the country with the highest number of patients with diabetes in the world with escalation.3,5

The use of hypoglycaemic drugs is an important clinical intervention for controlling blood glucose. In the past decade, with the continuous introduction of novel hypoglycaemic drugs, the usage pattern of hypoglycaemic drugs has changed. In many developed countries, novel hypoglycaemic drugs such as dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) and sodium-glucose transporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) have rapidly replaced some traditional ones due to their improved glycaemic control and better cardiovascular outcome.6,9 At the same time, these novel drugs are usually priced higher, which may bring considerable economic burden to patients and society even after being included in medical insurance, and thus may affect patients’ medication compliance and willingness to use.10 11

To provide patients with affordable and innovative drugs, the National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA) has explored two important drug policies since its establishment: The National Centralised Drug Procurement Policy (NCDP) and the National Drug Price Negotiation Policy (NDPN). The National Centralised Drug Procurement (NCDP) policy operates on the principle of ‘quantity for price’. Initiated in 2018 with the ‘4+7’ city-based volume purchasing pilot work, NCDP aims to conduct centralised bulk procurement for drugs with high consumption, significant procurement amount and sufficient market competition within the medical insurance drug list. The NHSA organises this process, gradually covering various clinically necessary and quality-reliable drugs in batches. The basic principles followed by NCDP include prioritising clinical needs while ensuring drug quality, and integrating bidding and procurement by linking quantity with price. The policy’s expected outcomes include significantly reducing drug prices, alleviating patients’ medication burden, improving the pharmaceutical industry’s ecosystem and supporting public medical institution reform.12 The NDPN policy was piloted by the former National Health and Family Planning Commission in 2015 and later taken over by the NHSA. The policy primarily targets drugs with limited competition (mostly exclusive), high unit prices and innovative varieties with limited clinical application experience. During the negotiation process, the NHSA and pharmaceutical companies consider factors such as drug cost-effectiveness, clinical alternatives and medical insurance fund capacity. Successfully negotiated drugs are incorporated into the medical insurance catalogue with a specified reimbursement ratio. The policy aims to rationalise drug prices, improve patient access to medications and ensure the sustainable operation of the medical insurance fund.13 Both two policies have saved billions of yuan in drug costs and significantly reduced the burden of medication for society.14 15

By the end of December 2022, the Chinese pharmaceutical market had undergone 7 rounds of NCDP and 6 rounds of NDPN, involving 11 insulin and analogues, 26 non-insulin hypoglycaemic drugs, which includes 13 novel hypoglycaemic drugs (see online supplemental appendix table S1). Have the multiple rounds of NCDP and NDPN policy implementations achieved their intended effects? What impacts have they had on the overall usage and structural changes of hypoglycaemic drugs? What are the effects of multiple rounds of policy regulation on novel hypoglycaemic drugs? To our knowledge, there is no literature analysing the long-term impact and comprehensive effects of multirounds of drug policies on the utilisation and cost of novel hypoglycaemic drugs. The purpose of this study is to analyse the impact of the series of drug procurement and negotiation policies on the utilisation, expenditure and daily cost of novel hypoglycaemic drugs, and thus provide a basis for further optimising the structure of drug use and improving relevant policies.

Methods

Study design

We extracted all procurement records for hypoglycaemic drugs from January 2016 to December 2022 from the Shanghai Medical Procurement Agency (SMPA). Each purchasing record includes information on the year, month, unified coding, quantity, purchase amount, drug generic name, dosage form, packaging specification and manufacturing enterprise. First, we performed a descriptive analysis of the overall usage, expenditure and consumption proportions of insulin and non-insulin hypoglycaemic drugs in Shanghai from 2016 to 2022. Second, we analysed the changing trends in the usage and expenditure of each subcategory of non-insulin hypoglycaemic drugs from 2016 to 2022. We also compared recent consumption and expenditure between novel hypoglycaemic drugs (DPP-4i, GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i) and traditional non-insulin hypoglycaemic drugs (including biguanides, sulfonylureas, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, thiazolidinediones and other blood glucose-lowering drugs, excluding insulins). Third, we used interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) to examine the impact of multiple rounds of drug procurement and negotiation policies on the usage, expenditure and daily cost of novel hypoglycaemic drugs. At the overall level, we took the earliest policy implementation time point, December 2017, as the starting point of policy intervention, and analysed the level and trend changes in the usage and expenditure of novel hypoglycaemic drugs before and after the policy intervention. Each novel hypoglycaemic drug that was subject to policy regulation may have been affected by one or two rounds of policy interventions, and thus we conducted ITSA on each of them.

Data sources and outcome measures

Our data are entirely sourced from the SMPA, which has collected drug procurement data from all the public and private hospitals in Shanghai. In this study, the three main outcome indicators we evaluated are monthly procurement volume, monthly purchasing amount and daily cost. The monthly procurement volume was measured by defined daily doses (DDDs), which were developed by the WHO to compare drug consumption.16 The DDD value of each generic drug can be found in the guidelines for Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification and DDD assignment 2024.17 The monthly purchasing amount is the actual procurement amount of the generic name for 1 month. Daily cost was assessed by defined daily drug cost (DDDc), which was calculated by the ratio of expenditures and DDDs, as a surrogate measure of the actual medication price paid. The annual data in the descriptive analysis are the sum of the monthly data.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we employed ITSA with segmented regression to investigate the change in usage, expenditures and daily cost of novel hypoglycaemic drugs affected by related policies. The choice of this statistical method was motivated by its ability to effectively capture both immediate and longitudinal effects of interventions occurring at a fixed time point, offering a robust quasi-experimental design of inferring causality.18

Two types of ITSA model were constructed in our study: (1) two segments with one interruption point, one is the preintervention period and another is the postintervention period and (2) three segments with two interruption points, the first one is the period before the first intervention, the intermediate one is the period between two interventions, and the most recent period is the one after the second intervention.

The equation listed below summarises the specifications of our first ITSA model:

Yt=β0+β1Tt+β2Xt+β3XtTt+εt

where Yt is the monthly aggregated outcome variable (DDDs, expenditures or DDDc) measured in month t, Tt is the time since the start of study period (in months) by the time of month t, Xt is a dummy (indicator) variable representing the intervention (preintervention periods 0, otherwise 1), and XtTt is an interaction term. β0 represents the intercept or starting level of the outcome variable. β1 is the trajectory (slope) of the outcome variable until the start of the intervention. The model allows to test for both a one-time immediate level change at the start of the intervention (intercept; β2) and the difference in the preintervention and postintervention trends in the outcomes (slope; β3). εt is the estimate of random error. We set the time point immediately following the implementation of the policy.

The specifications of our second ITSA model are as the following equation:

Yt=β0+β1Tt+β2X1t+β3X1tTt+β4X2t+β5X2tTt +εt

where the interpretation of Yt, Tt, β0, β1, β2, β3, εt is the same as model (1). X1t is a dummy (indicator) variable representing the first intervention (preintervention periods 0, otherwise 1), and X2t is a dummy variable representing another intervention at a later point in time (preintervention periods 0, otherwise 1). X1tTt, X2tTt both are interaction terms. β4 represents the immediate change in the level of the outcome and β5 estimates the change in trend in the outcome following the second intervention.

To ensure that our model accounts for the correct autocorrelation structure, we used Cumby-Huizinga test to test for serial autocorrelation.19 Further, the Newey-West SEs of regression coefficients were calculated to account for autocorrelation in addition to possible heteroskedasticity.20 Data management and descriptive analyses were performed by using SAS, V.9.4 (SAS Institute). The interrupted time-series analyses were performed by using STATA, V.18.0 (Stata). Estimated effects with 95% CIs were calculated. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive analysis of volume, structures and expenditures of overall hypoglycaemic drugs

From 2016 to 2022, the overall procurement volume and expenditure of hypoglycaemic drugs in Shanghai have been gradually increasing. The proportion of insulins (including its analogue) and non-insulin hypoglycaemic drug usage remained stable at 1/6 (see online supplemental appendix figure S1).

Descriptive analysis of volume and expenditures of different categories of non-insulin hypoglycaemic drugs

Sulfonylureas, biguanides and α-glucosidase inhibitors rank as the top three classes in terms of highest average annual usage and expenditure. Regarding usage growth rates, SGLT-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, combinations of oral blood glucose-lowering drugs and DPP-4 inhibitors have demonstrated rapid increases (see online supplemental appendix figure S2).

Novel hypoglycaemic drugs have received attention and favour from many countries since their market introduction due to their minimal side effects and high safety. With the continuous accumulation of clinical experience, guidelines in many countries are being updated successively, and novel hypoglycaemic drugs are gradually replacing traditional ones as important second-line or even first-line treatments. The novel hypoglycaemic drugs referred to in this study mainly include DPP-4i, GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i classes. Although combinations of oral blood glucose-lowering drugs (A10BD) include compounds of traditional and novel hypoglycaemic drugs (such as metformin and empagliflozin), due to the very small component of novel antidiabetic drugs in the compound preparation, A10BD is classified as a traditional hypoglycaemic drug in this study. Both the usage and purchase amounts of novel hypoglycaemic drugs are rapidly increasing (see online supplemental appendix figure S3).

Impacts of mutirounds of NCDP and NDPN on volume, expenditures of overall novel hypoglycemic drugs

We have set December 2017 as the policy intervention time point for the overall novel hypoglycaemic drugs. Before this time point, the novel hypoglycaemic drugs were not directly affected by relevant policies. From December 2017 to the end of the observation period, the novel hypoglycaemic drugs were affected by three rounds of NCDP and four rounds of NDPN (see online supplemental appendix figure S4).

Our results presented the impacts of the overall usage and expenditure before and after policy execution. The DDDs and expenditures had experienced a relatively stable baseline trend before the policy intervention. After the beginning of the first round of NDPN on December 2017, both the DDDs and expenditures decreased significantly at the level change, which was reflected in the coefficient β2. However, the trend coefficient β3 represented a rapid increase in DDDs and expenditure, which implied a robust positive effect of the series of policies (p<0.001) (see onlinesupplemental appendix table S3 figure S5).

Impacts of NCDP and NDPN on volume, expenditures and daily costs of individual medication of novel hypoglycaemic drugs

For each specific drug, the intervention type and implementation time point were different (see onlinesupplemental table S1 figure S4). Thus, we analysed the changes in the volume, expenditure and daily cost before and after policy implementation for each drug. We used ITSA to analyse the following 11 drugs affected by the NCDP and/or NDPN policies, including saxagliptin (A10BH03), vildagliptin (A10BH02), exenatide (A10BJ01), liraglutide (A10BJ02), dulaglutide (A10BJ05), semaglutide (A10BJ06), beinaglutide (A10BJ07), loxenatide (A10BJ10), dapagliflozin (A10BK01), canagliflozin (A10BK02), empagliflozin (A10BK03). Since lixisenatide (A10BJ03) and ertugliflozin (A10BK04) had no purchase records before policy intervention, they were not analysed here.

Dulaglutide (A10BJ05), beinaglutide (A10BJ07) and loxenatide (A10BJ10) were successfully negotiated in December 2020 and were officially included in the medical insurance reimbursement list, with related reimbursement policies being implemented in Shanghai in March 2021. However, we observed different performances in terms of usage and expenditure for these three drugs before and after the implementation of the policy. Dulaglutide (A10BJ05) had a continuously growing demand for medication even before the policy was implemented, with positive baseline trends (β1) in DDDs and expenditures that are statistically significant (p<0.001). After policy implementation, there was a further significant increase in medication demand and expenditure, as indicated by β2 and β3 both being positive with significant p values (online supplemental appendix table S2, figure 1A,B). Following the policy implementation, loxenatide (A10BJ10) also showed a similarly significant increasing trend in usage and costs (online supplemental appendix table S2, figure 1E,F). However, beinaglutide (A10BJ07) showed a slight decreasing trend at the baseline level before the policy was implemented, and NDPN did not stimulate an increase in its usage and costs (online supplemental appendix table S2, figure 1C,D). With the implementation of NDPN, daily cost of dulaglutide (A10BJ05), beinaglutide (A10BJ07) and loxenatide (A10BJ10) had dropped by CNY39.01 (P < 0.01), CNY24.58 (p<0.001) and CNY1.46.

Semaglutide (A10BJ06), liraglutide (A10BJ02) and dapagliflozin (A10BK01) were affected by NDPN at different time points, but all of them showed significant growth in usage before policy intervention (positive β1, P is significant). After policy intervention, the demand for these three drugs continued to increase, as indicated by positive β3 (p<0.001). The daily cost of semaglutide (A10BJ06), liraglutide (A10BJ02) and dapagliflozin (A10BK01) has dropped by CNY 35.15, CNY20.20, CNY11.67, respectively (p<0.001) after policy implementation (onlinesupplemental table S2 and appendix figures S68).

Vildagliptin (A10BH02) and saxagliptin (A10BH03) were affected by NCDP in November 2020 and October 2021, respectively. We can observe both drugs exhibited significant growth in usage and expenditures before the implementation of the centralised procurement policy (positive β1, p<0.001). However, after the implementation of the policy, there was a significant decrease in both usage and expenditures (negative β2, with significant p values). Daily cost of both drugs dropped significantly after NCDP execution (p<0.001) (online supplemental appendix table S2, figure 2).

Exenatide (A10BJ01) was successfully negotiated in January 2020 and was included in the national medical insurance reimbursement list. It is evident that inclusion in the medical insurance reimbursement list significantly reversed the declining trend of exenatide’s usage and expenditure before policy implementation (β3 equals 2.30, p<0.001). However, around March 2021, we observed an obvious decline in its usage and expenditure. Therefore, we artificially set March 2021 as an intervention time point (the reason for which will be elaborated in the discussion section) and proved that exenatide showed a significant decrease in level and trend at the breakpoint time. Daily cost of exenatide (A10BJ01) was significantly decreased after NDPN execution (online supplemental appendix table S2, figure 3).

Both canagliflozin (A10BK02) and empagliflozin (A10BK03), two SGLT-2 inhibitors, were included in the national medical insurance reimbursement list following successful negotiations in January 2020 and underwent centralised procurement in May 2021. ITS analysis indicated that after their inclusion in the medical insurance list, both drugs experienced an increased trend in usage and expenditures (positive β3, p<0.001). However, after centralised procurement, both the level change and trend change decreased significantly (p<0.001). The daily cost of canagliflozin (A10BK02) and empagliflozin (A10BK03) dropped by CNY4.13, CNY6.93 after NDPN, and further decreased by CNY2.48, CNY3.46 affected by NCDP (online supplemental appendix table S2, figure 4).

Discussion

Using descriptive analysis and ITSA, we found that the multirounds of NDPN and NCDP have significantly boosted the overall use of novel hypoglycaemic drugs, while usage of each specific drug that was affected by the policies varies a lot.

With the acceleration of urbanisation and the deepening of the ageing population, by 2045, the number of diabetes patients in China will exceed 174 million.3 Correspondingly, there is a continuous increase in the demand for hypoglycaemic drugs. Our research showed that from 2016 to 2022, the usage of hypoglycaemic drugs in Shanghai showed a year-by-year growth trend. Similar to other countries,21 22 sulfonylureas and biguanides are still the most used classes. The dietary structure of Asians with high carbohydrates makes α-glucosidase inhibitors also have a higher proportion in Asian countries such as China and Japan.23 DPP-4i, GLP-1RA, SGLT-2i and other novel hypoglycaemic drugs are the fastest-growing classes in recent years in many countries.6 7 Our results also showed that from the beginning to the end of the observation, the overall usage of novel hypoglycaemic drugs has increased by 50 times.

Tendering and negotiation are common methods used by some countries to determine the price of anticancer drugs.24 In China, centralised procurement and negotiation policies have been routinely promoted and widely applied to different types of drugs. While controlling pharmaceutical expenditure, it greatly reduces the burden of medication for patients and increases the accessibility of drugs. Most of the existing literature discusses the impact of a single round of drug procurement or negotiation policy on the related drug usage, expenditure and daily cost. In terms of the NDPN effect, most of the existing literature focuses on anticancer drugs. Huang et al evaluated the implementation of the first-round NDPN on targeted anticancer medicines (TAMs), which found a significant decrease in the daily cost and hospital purchasing spending but a robust increase in the purchasing volume of the related TAMs.24 Zhang et al assessed the effects of the second-round NDPN on hospital procurement prices, volumes and spending on anticancer drugs, which also witnessed reduced medication prices, increased usage volume and decreased hospital spending.25 Other previous studies also showed similar results.26,28 In terms of the effectiveness of NCDP, the types of drugs evaluated in existing research are more extensive. Wen et al showed a significant decrease in daily cost and expenditure, but a statistical increase in the purchasing volume of policy-related drugs after the ‘4+7’ pilot policy implementation.29 Chen et al also depicted similar changing trends caused by NCDP.16 30

Our results revealed that the implementation of multiple rounds of NCDP and NDPN led to a significant increase in the overall use of novel hypoglycaemic drugs and reduced drug prices. However, it also resulted in a substantial increase in drug expenditure. Similar findings have been reported in other countries. Kwon et al observed that drastic drug price reductions have only a temporary and unsustainable effect on controlling drug expenditure.31 Looking at the impact of policies on individual drugs, several novel hypoglycaemic drugs, including dulaglutide (A10BJ05), loxenatide (A10BJ10), liraglutide (A10BJ02), semaglutide (A10BJ06) and dapagliflozin (A10BK01), have all seen significant increases in usage and expenditure after NDPN implementation. Whereas vildagliptin (A10BH02) and saxagliptin (A10BH03), affected by the NCDP, experienced a significant decrease in usage and expenditure. Both empagliflozin (A10BK03) and canagliflozin (A10BK02) were successively influenced by NDPN and NCDP, with their usage and expenditure going through a process of initial increase, followed by a significant decline. We assumed usage decrease caused by NCDP may be associated with the ‘bypass’ effect, which was also noted by other studies.31 32

It is interesting to note the performance of beinaglutide (A10BJ07) and exenatide (A10BJ01) after policy intervention. Unlike dulaglutide (A10BJ05) and loxenatide (A10BJ10), beinaglutide (A10BJ07) did not experience the expected increase in usage following negotiation policy intervention. We believe this may be related to the high frequency of medication required for beinaglutide (A10BJ07). Compared with other GLP-1RA drugs, beinaglutide (A10BJ07) needs to be injected three times a day, which may reduce patient compliance and lead them to choose dulaglutide (A10BJ05) and loxenatide (A10BJ10), which were included in the same NDPN round and require injection only once a week. Exenatide (A10BJ01) is one of the earliest novel hypoglycaemic drugs to be included in the medical insurance negotiation. After being successfully negotiated and included in the medical insurance catalogue, both its usage and expenditure significantly increased. However, around March 2021, its usage and expenditure plummeted. We took this time as a breakpoint and found a significant decline in the level change and trend change of exenatide’s usage and expenditure. We believe this phenomenon is related to the negotiation price reduction and inclusion in the medical insurance catalogue of the once-weekly GLP-1RA drugs (dulaglutide and loxenatide) at that time point. Although lixisenatide (A10BJ03) and ertugliflozin (A10BK04) had no procurement records before policy intervention, they experienced rapid growth in both usage and expenditure after the implementation of the NDPN policy.

Novel hypoglycaemic drugs are gradually replacing traditional ones in many developed countries, becoming the mainstream first and second-line treatments for diabetes. In the future, more promising novel antidiabetic drugs are expected to enter the Chinese market.33 34 benefiting more Chinese patients. Undoubtedly, the implementation of the NCDP and NDPN has significantly improved patient access and affordability of medication while reducing drug prices and controlling pharmaceutical expenditures.

However, from a long-term perspective, there are still some issues that deserve attention and need to be improved and optimised. Regarding the implementation of the NCDP policy, delayed supply of winning bid drugs may lead to clinical shortages. The government should ensure a stable supply of these drugs. To prevent abnormal growth in the usage of non-winning products, authorities should strengthen monitoring of centralised procurement winners, non-winners and alternative varieties. While lowering drug prices, it is crucial to consider enterprise research and development (R&D) costs and innovation vitality, striking a balance between price reduction and protecting innovation. As patients are guided to adjust their medication habits, their personalised needs should be guaranteed. For the NDPN policy, it is essential to bolster the organisational system for drug negotiations, continually refine negotiation standards and methods, ensure rational allocation and standardised use of negotiated drugs. While enhancing medical insurance fund efficiency, efforts should focus on promoting high-quality, sustainable development of the funds. In the future, both NCDP and NDPN will maintain a value-driven approach, continuously refining policies to ensure seamless integration with other policies while balancing the interests of all stakeholders.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this study was the first to analyse the long-term impact and comprehensive effects of multiple rounds of policy on hypoglycaemic drugs. Second, using continuous and high-quality administrative data, this study fully reflected the demand and changes in hypoglycaemic drug use in Shanghai, a megacity with a population of more than 24 million, which had good representativeness. Third, this study reported some results that were not anticipated at the beginning of policy design, providing reference suggestions for the optimisation and adjustment of policies in later stages.

However, our study also had several limitations. First, our procurement data only included hospital purchasing records. Retail pharmacy consumption was not covered by our research. Although all the novel hypoglycaemic drugs require a doctor’s prescription to dispense, with the continuous advancement of related policies such as internet hospitals and prescription outsourcing in recent years, the proportion of novel hypoglycaemic drugs at retail terminals will gradually increase.35 36 Using only hospital data may underestimate the usage of novel hypoglycaemic drugs. Second, we use hospital procurement data instead of usage data to reflect drug consumption, which may be limited to some extent. Third, data used in the ITSA model in our analyses were at the aggregated level and the inability to incorporate individual-level confounders may lead to biased estimates of the intervention effect. Besides, the ITSA was conducted in Shanghai, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other regions or populations of China.

Conclusions

From an overall perspective, the implementation of NDPN and NCDP has successfully reduced the price of novel hypoglycaemic drugs and significantly increased their utilisation. The performance of each drug subject to policy regulation varies. In the future, with the rapidly increased use of novel hypoglycaemic drugs, the Chinese government needs to control pharmaceutical expenses more strictly while ensuring the rational use of the drugs.
