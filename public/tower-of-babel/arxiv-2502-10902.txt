Yuto Nakajima and Hiroki Takahasi

Abstract
. We build a bridge from density combinatorics to dimension theory of continued fractions. We establish a fractal transference principle that transfers common properties of subsets of ℕ \mathbb{N} with positive upper density to properties of subsets of irrationals in ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) for which the set { a n ​ ( x ) : n ∈ ℕ } \{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\} of partial quotients induces an injection n ∈ ℕ ↦ a n ​ ( x ) ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N}\mapsto a_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{N} . Let ( ∗ ) (*) be a certain property that holds for any subset of ℕ \mathbb{N} with positive upper density. The principle asserts that for any subset S S of ℕ \mathbb{N} with positive upper density, there exists a set E S E_{S} of Hausdorff dimension 1 / 2 1/2 such that the set ⋃ n ∈ ℕ ⋂ x ∈ E S { a n ​ ( x ) } ∩ S \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{x\in E_{S}}\{a_{n}(x)\}\cap S has the same upper density as that of S S , and thus inherits property ( ∗ ) (*) . Examples of ( ∗ ) (*) include the existence of arithmetic progressions of arbitrary lengths and the existence of arbitrary polynomial progressions, known as Szemerédi’s and Bergelson-Leibman’s theorems respectively. In the same spirit, we establish a relativized version of the principle applicable to the primes, to the primes of the form y 2 + z 2 + 1 y^{2}+z^{2}+1 , to the sets given by the Piatetski-Shapiro sequences.

1.
Introduction

Problems on the existence of arithmetic progressions lie at the interface of a number of fields, including combinatorics, number theory, ergodic theory and dynamical systems.
Van der Waerden’s theorem

[
39
]

states that if the set
\mathbb{N}
of positive integers is partitioned into finitely many sets, then one of these sets contains
an
\ell
-term arithmetic progression for every
\ell\geq 3
.
A significant strengthening of Van der Waerden’s theorem was obtained by
Szemerédi
[
35
]
who proved
that any subset of
\mathbb{N}
with positive upper density contains
an
\ell
-term arithmetic progression for every
\ell\geq 3
.
For a set
S\subset\mathbb{N}
, define its

upper density
by

\overline{d}(S)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#({S\cap[1,N]}),

and its

upper Banach density

by

d_{\rm B}(S)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sup_{M\in\mathbb{N}}\#({S\cap[M,M+N]}).

Note that
d_{\rm B}(S)\geq\overline{d}(S)
.
The positive upper density assumption in Szemerédi’s theorem can actually be weakened to the positivity of upper Banach density.

Furstenberg
[
10
,
11
]
established the multiple recurrence theorem and the correspondence principle, linking the field of density combinatorics to recurrence in ergodic theory.
Using this principle he obtained a new proof of Szemerédi’s theorem. This development laid a foundation for further extensions of
Szemeredi’s theorem to several different directions, see
[
2
,
12
,
14
,
34
,
36
]

for example.
The aim of this paper is to provide counterparts of these
density combinatorics theorems
in dimension theory of continued fractions.

Each
x\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}

has the unique infinite

regular continued fraction expansion

x=\frac{\displaystyle{\hfill{1}\hfill\;\vrule}}{\displaystyle{\vrule\;\hfill{a_{1}(x)}\hfill}}+\frac{\displaystyle{\hfill{1}\hfill\;\vrule}}{\displaystyle{\vrule\;\hfill{a_{2}(x)}\hfill}}+\frac{\displaystyle{\hfill{1}\hfill\;\vrule}}{\displaystyle{\vrule\;\hfill{a_{3}(x)}\hfill}}+\cdots

(1.1)

where each positive integer
a_{n}(x)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
is called a partial quotient or simply
a
digit
of
x
.
We analyze the structure of the set of digits
as an infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
. For this purpose, overlapping digits do not count and we may restrict to the set of points whose digits are all different:

E=\{x\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\colon a_{m}(x)\neq a_{n}(x)\ \text{\rm for all }m,n\in\mathbb{N},\ m\neq n\}.

An investigation of this set was suggested by Erdős in the early stage of dimension theory of continued fractions.
Ramharter
[
31
]
proved that
E
is of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
. Ramharter’s result is a refinement of Good’s
landmark theorem
[
13
]
which states that the set
\{x\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\colon a_{n}(x)\to\infty\text{ as }n\to\infty\}
is of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
.

1.1.
Statements of results and their consequences

We now state our two main results (Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.4
), along with five consequential theorems which are manifestations of density combinatorics theorems in the regular continued fraction.

1.1.1.
First main result

Let

\dim_{\rm H}
denote the Hausdorff dimension on
[0,1]
.

Theorem 1.1

(fractal transference principle)
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}
.

(a)

If
\overline{d}(S)>0
, then there exists
E_{S}\subset E

such that

\dim_{\rm H}E_{S}=\dim_{\rm H}E=\frac{1}{2}\ \text{ and }\ \overline{d}\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{x\in E_{S}}\{a_{n}(x)\}\cap S\right)=\overline{d}(S).

(1.2)

In particular,

\dim_{\rm H}\left\{x\in E\colon\overline{d}(\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cap S)=\overline{d}(S)\right\}=\dim_{\rm H}E.

(1.3)

(b)

If
d_{\rm B}(S)>0
, then
there exists
F_{S}\subset E

such that

\dim_{\rm H}F_{S}=\dim_{\rm H}E\ \text{ and }\ d_{\rm B}\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{x\in F_{S}}\{a_{n}(x)\}\cap S\right)=d_{\rm B}(S).

(1.4)

In particular,

\dim_{\rm H}\left\{x\in E\colon d_{\rm B}(\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cap S)=d_{\rm B}(S)\right\}=\dim_{\rm H}E.

(1.5)

Equation (
1.3
) is a
‘pointwise version’ of the second equation in (
1.2
).
Equation (
1.3
) asserts that the condition

\overline{d}(\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cap S)=\overline{d}(S)
does not cause a dimension drop in
E
. More importantly,
it allows us to
immediately transfer statements on subsets of
\mathbb{N}
with positive upper density to statements on continued fractions of points in sets of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
. For example, from (
1.3
)
and Szemerédi’s theorem it follows that if
S\subset\mathbb{N}
has positive upper density then for any
x\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
in a set of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
and for every integer
\ell\geq 3
, there exist
k(x)\in\mathbb{N}
,
m(x)\in\mathbb{N}
,

n_{1}(x),n_{2}(x),\ldots,n_{\ell}(x)\in\mathbb{N}

such that

k(x)+m(x),k(x)+2m(x),\ldots,k(x)+\ell m(x)\in S
and
a_{n_{1}}(x)=k(x)+m(x),a_{n_{2}}(x)=k(x)+2m(x),\ldots,a_{n_{\ell}}(x)=k(x)+\ell m(x)
.
Equations (
1.2
)
ensure that all these integers are taken uniformly on the set
E_{S}
of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
.
Moreover, from the uniformity in multiple recurrence (see
[
1
, Theorem F2]
) and Furstenberg’s correspondence principle, the common difference
m(x)

(x\in E_{S})
of the
\ell
-term arithmetic progression can be taken to be bounded from above by a universal constant that depends only on
\ell
and
\overline{d}(S)
.
Even if the upper density of
S
is zero, similar statements follow from (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) provided
S
has positive upper Banach density.

1.1.3.
Second main result

Theorem
1.3
does not apply to subsets of
\mathbb{N}
whose upper Banach density is zero.
To accommodate such sets, notably the set of primes,
we develop a relativized version of Theorem
1.1
.
Given two infinite sets
A\subset S\subset\mathbb{N}
,
the upper density of
A
relative to
S
is defined by

\overline{d}(A|S)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#(A\cap[1,N])}{\#(S\cap[1,N])}.

We say
S
has
polynomial density
if there exist constants

C_{1}>0
,
C_{2}>0
and
\alpha\geq 1
,
\beta\geq 0
such that
for all sufficiently large
N>1
,

\frac{C_{1}N^{1/\alpha}}{(\log N)^{\beta}}\leq\#(S\cap[1,N])\leq\frac{C_{2}N^{1/\alpha}}{(\log N)^{\beta}}.

In this case we also say
S
has
polynomial density with exponent

\alpha
.
Our second main result is
a relativized version of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.4

(relative fractal transference principle)
.

Let
S
be an infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
that has polynomial density with exponent
\alpha
.
For any
A\subset S
with
\overline{d}(A|S)>0
,
there exists a subset
E_{S,A}
of
\{x\in E\colon\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S\}
such that

\dim_{\rm H}E_{S,A}=\dim_{\rm H}\{x\in E\colon\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S\}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}

(1.6)

and

\overline{d}\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{x\in E_{S,A}}\{a_{n}(x)\}\cap A|S\right)=\overline{d}(A|S).

(1.7)

In particular,

\dim_{\rm H}\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{l}\!\!\!$x\in E\colon$\\
\!\!\!$\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S$,\\
\!\!\!$\overline{d}(\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cap A|S)$\\
\!\!\!$=\overline{d}(A|S)$\end{tabular}\right\}=\dim_{\rm H}\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{l}\!\!\!$x\in E\colon$\\
\!\!\!$\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S$\end{tabular}\right\}.

(1.8)

Since
\mathbb{N}
has polynomial density with
\alpha=1
,
\beta=0
, Theorem
1.4
includes Theorem
1.1
(a) as a special case.
Equation (
1.8
) is a
‘pointwise version’ of the first equality in (
1.6
), and
allows us to
immediately transfer density combinatorics statements on subsets of
S
to statements in dimension theory of continued fractions.
This transfer can be done uniformly on
E_{S,A}
by virtue of (
1.7
),
as developed below.

1.1.4.
Polynomial progressions in primes

The first application of Theorem
1.4
is to the set
\mathbb{P}
of prime numbers. It is well-known (see e.g.,
[
23
, Corollary 3.4]
) that the Banach density of
\mathbb{P}
is zero.
Green and Tao
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
proved that
any subset of
\mathbb{P}
with positive upper relative density contains an
\ell
-term arithmetic progression for every
\ell\geq 3
, which they call ‘Szemerédi’s theorem in prime numbers’. Green-Tao’s theorem is a corollary of the wide open conjecture of Erdős and Turán
[
7
]
which states that any subset of
\mathbb{N}
with divergent reciprocal sum contains arithmetic progressions of arbitrary lengths. Subsequently, Tao and Ziegler
[
36
]
extended Green-Tao’s theorem to polynomial progressions as follows.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
36
]
Theorem 1.3)
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{P}
and
\overline{d}(A|\mathbb{P})>0
, then
for any finite collection
h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{\ell}
of elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{0}[X]

there exist
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

k+h_{1}(m),k+h_{2}(m),\ldots,k+h_{\ell}(m)\in A.

From the prime number theorem it follows that
\mathbb{P}
has polynomial density with
\alpha=\beta=1
. Transferring Theorem
1.5
to the continued fraction via Theorem
1.4
we obtain the following statement.

Theorem 1.6
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{P}

and
\overline{d}(A|\mathbb{P})>0
, then
for any finite collection
h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{\ell}
of elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{0}[X]
there exist

k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k+h_{1}(m),k+h_{2}(m),\ldots,k+h_{\ell}(m)\in A
and
a_{n_{1}}(x)=k+h_{1}(m),a_{n_{2}}(x)=k+h_{2}(m),\ldots,a_{n_{\ell}}(x)=k+h_{\ell}(m)
for any
x\in E_{\mathbb{P},A}.

Taking
A=\mathbb{P}
in Theorem
1.6
yields the following statement.

Corollary 1.7
.

We have

\dim_{\rm H}\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{l}\!\!\!$x\in E\colon\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ is contained in $\mathbb{P}$ and contains\\
\!\!\!\! an $\ell$-term arithmetic progression for every $\ell\geq 3$\end{tabular}\right\}=\frac{1}{2}.

Surprisingly, there are few literature on continued fractions with prime digits. Schindler and Zweimüller
[
33
]
investigated probabilistic laws governing the appearance of prime regular continued fraction digits for irrationals which are typical in the sense of the Lebesgue measure on
(0,1)
.
Das and Simmons
[
4
]
investigated the set of irrationals in
(0,1)
all whose regular continued fraction digits are prime numbers.

1.1.5.
Arithmetic progressions in primes of quadratic form

We give three more examples of application of Theorem
1.4
, one of which is still related to prime numbers.
Let
\mathbb{P}(1)
denote the set of prime numbers represented by a quadratic form
x^{2}+y^{2}+1
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(1)=\{p\in\mathbb{P}\colon p=x^{2}+y^{2}+1\ \text{\rm for some }x,y\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Linnik
[
20
]
proved that
\mathbb{P}(1)
contains infinitely many primes. Motohashi
[
25
]
proved an upper bound

\#(\mathbb{P}(1)\cap[1,N])\leq CN/(\log N)^{\frac{3}{2}}
, and Iwaniec
[
17
, Theorem 1]
obtained a lower bound of the same order,
namely

\mathbb{P}(1)
has polynomial density with
\alpha=1
,
\beta=3/2
.
For more information on
\mathbb{P}(1)
, see
[
21
,
41
]
for example.

Teräväinen
[
37
]
proved that ‘almost every’ even positive integer
n\not\equiv 5,8
mod
9
can be written as the sum of two elements of
\mathbb{P}(1)
. As a by-product of a proof of this, he also proved that any subset of

\{p\in\mathbb{P}\colon p=x^{2}+y^{2}+1\ \text{\rm for some coprime }x,y\in\mathbb{N}\}

with positive relative upper density contains infinitely many non-trivial
3
-term arithmetic progressions.

[
34
]
extended Green-Tao’s theorem to

Theorem 1.8

(
[
34
]
Theorem 1.1)
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{P}(1)

and
\overline{d}(A|\mathbb{P}(1))>0
,
then
A
contains an
\ell
-term arithmetic progression for every

.

Transferring Theorem
1.8
to the continued fraction via Theorem
1.4
we obtain the following statement.

.

If

and

1.1.6.
Arithmetic progressions in sets given by Piatetski-Shapiro sequences

For
x\geq 0
let
\lfloor x\rfloor
denote the largest integer not exceeding
x
.
For
\alpha>1
we consider the set

{\rm PS}(\alpha)=\{\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

For
\alpha>1
and
\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
(\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor)_{n=1}^{\infty}

is called
Piatetski-Shapiro’s sequence

[
29
]
,
after Piatetski-Shapiro
[
29
]
who proved that for all
1<\alpha<12/11
the sequences contain infinitely many prime numbers. In spite of a vast amount of literature,
prime numbers in Piatetski-Shapiro’s sequences are not a topic of this paper.
There is no integer
\alpha>1
such that
{\rm PS}(\alpha)
contains arithmetic progressions of arbitrary lengths
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
.
Since
{\rm PS}(\alpha)
has a convergent reciprocal sum, it is independent of the conjecture of Erdős and Turán.

Piatetski-Shapiro’s sequence is
obtained by curving with bounded errors
the sequence
(n^{\alpha})_{n=1}^{\infty}
whose graph
\{(n,n^{\alpha})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is contained in the graph of the smooth function
x\in[1,\infty)\mapsto x^{\alpha}
.
Positive integer sequences with this kind of properties are called
slightly curved sequences

[
32
]
.
Saito and Yoshida
[
32
, Theorem 4]
extended Szemerédi’s theorem to graphs of a class of slightly curved sequences, and as a corollary proved that for every
1<\alpha<2
, the graph
\{(n,\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
contains an
\ell
-term arithmetic progression for every
\ell\geq 3
(see
[
32
, Corollary 6]
). From this it follows that

{\rm PS}(\alpha)
contains an
\ell
-term arithmetic progression for every
\ell\geq 3
.
In fact,
the next theorem follows from combining the results in
[
32
]
.
For completeness we include a proof in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
1<\alpha<2
. If
A\subset{\rm PS}(\alpha)

and
\overline{d}(A|{\rm PS}(\alpha))>0
,
then
A
contains an
\ell
-term arithmetic progression for every
\ell\geq 3
.

Clearly
{\rm PS}(\alpha)
has polynomial density with exponent
\alpha
and
\beta=0
.
Transferring Theorem
1.10
to the continued fraction via Theorem
1.4

we obtain the following statement.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
1<\alpha<2
. If
A\subset{\rm PS}(\alpha)

and
\overline{d}(A|{\rm PS}(\alpha))>0
, then for any integer
\ell\geq 3
there exist
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

such that

and

1.2.
Essential role of non-monotonicity of digits

Arithmetic progressions in the continued fraction was first studied by Tong and Wang
[
38
]
, who considered the smaller set

J=\{x\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\colon a_{n}(x)<a_{n+1}(x)\ \text{ for every }n\geq 1\}\subset E,

and proved that the set
of
x\in J
whose digit set
\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
contains an
\ell
-term arithmetic progression with a common difference
m

for every
\ell\geq 3
and every
m\geq 1

is of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
.
The argument in
[
38
]
is constructive and does not rely on Szemerédi’s theorem.
Zhang and Cao
[
42
]
proved that
the set of points in
J
whose digit sets have upper Banach density
1
is of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
, a statement comparable to (
1.5
) in the special case
S=\mathbb{N}
.

Since the map
x\in J\mapsto\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}
induces a bijection between
J
and the collection of infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
,
J
is indeed a natural set to investigate. However, the next theorem of independent interest, to be proved in
\lx@sectionsign
4.3
, suggests that
E
rather than
J
is the right choice for our purpose of building a bridge from density combinatorics to dimension theory of continued fractions.

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}
.

(a)

If
\overline{d}(S)>0
,
then we have

\dim_{\rm H}\left\{x\in J\colon\overline{d}(\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cap S)=\overline{d}(S)\right\}=0<\dim_{\rm H}J.

(b)

If
S
has polynomial density with exponent
\alpha<2
, then for any

\overline{d}(A|S)>0
we have

\dim_{\rm H}\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{l}\!\!\!$x\in J\colon$\\
\!\!\!$\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S$,\\
\!\!\!$\overline{d}(\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cap A|S)$\\
\!\!\!$=\overline{d}(A|S)$\end{tabular}\right\}\leq\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}<\dim_{\rm H}\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{l}\!\!\!$x\in J\colon$\\
\!\!\!$\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S$\end{tabular}\right\}.

In sharp contrast to (

1.3.
Outline of proofs of the main results

Notice that Theorem
1.1
(a) follows from Theorem
1.4

since
\overline{d}(S|\mathbb{N})=\overline{d}(S)
holds for any
S\subset\mathbb{N}
.
Moreover, a proof of Theorem
1.1
(b) and that of Theorem
1.4
rely on the same set of ideas. Hence, we only outline the proof of Theorem
1.4
below.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}

have polynomial density with exponent
\alpha\geq 1
, and let
A\subset S
have positive upper density relative to
S
.
The construction of the set
E_{S,A}
in Theorem
1.4
breaks into two main steps. The central idea is to use Moran-like fractals.
A Moran fractal is a Cantor-like set
with a tractable structure, and
has been used extensively for estimating Hausdorff dimension, see
[
22
,
24
,
27
,
28
]
for example.
In the first step we choose
an infinite subset
S_{*}
of
S
, and construct a Moran-like fractal of Hausdorff dimension
1/(2\alpha)
consisting of points whose digit sets are contained in
S_{*}
.
This set is our seed set, with no relation to
A
. The second step is to
modify the seed set into another Moran-like fractal with exactly the same Hausdorff dimension, by
adding elements of

A
into the digit sequences of points in the seed set. The resultant Moran-like fractal is our target set
E_{S,A}
.

The two-step construction of
E_{S,A}
has been inspired by the afore-mentioned papers
[
38
,
42
]
that construct subsets of
J
of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
corresponding to arithmetic progressions of arbitrary lengths.
As already illustrated in
\lx@sectionsign
1.2
, an introduction of new ideas is necessary
to prove our main results.
Since the goal is to construct a subset of
E
, overlapping digits must be avoided. This means that elements of
A
already appearing in the digit sequences of points in the seed set cannot be added. In order to avoid overlaps of digits, what we actually do in the second step is to add elements of
A\setminus S_{*}
.
According to (
1.8
), the elements of
A
must appear in the digit set of any point in
E_{S,A}
with positive relative upper density equal to
\overline{d}(A|S)
.
Hence, the upper density of
S_{*}
relative to
S
must be zero.
This restriction requires an elaboration on the choice of
S_{*}
in the first step. As such,
the seed set we construct in
\lx@sectionsign
3.1
is of maximal Hausdorff dimension and least relative density of digits,
called an
extreme seed set
.

Our main task in the second step is to add sufficiently many elements of
A\setminus S_{*}
in appropriate positions to achieve the positive density
\overline{d}(A|S)
without losing Hausdorff dimension of the seed set at all. This seemingly contradictory task can be accomplished by
a sharp estimate of Hölder exponent of the associated
elimination map
, which essentially relies on
the non-monotonicity of continued fraction digits for points in
E\setminus J
.
See
\lx@sectionsign
3.2
for a general setup of the second step and relevant definitions, and

\lx@sectionsign
3.3
for the key estimate on the elimination map.

2.1.
Basic properties of continued fraction digits

For each
n\in\mathbb{N},
define non-negative integers

p_{n}
and
q_{n}
by the recursion formulas

\begin{split}p_{-1}=1,\ p_{0}=0,\ p_{i}&=a_{i}p_{i-1}+p_{i-2}\ \text{ for }1\leq i\leq n,\\
q_{-1}=0,\ q_{0}=1,\ q_{i}&=a_{i}q_{i-1}+q_{i-2}\ \text{ for }1\leq i\leq n.\end{split}

(2.1)

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we call the interval

I(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\begin{cases}\vskip 2.84526pt\left[\displaystyle{\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}},\frac{p_{n}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n}+q_{n-1}}\right)&\text{if}\ n\ \text{is even,}\\
\displaystyle{\left(\frac{p_{n}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n}+q_{n-1}},\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right]}&\text{if}\ n\ \text{is odd,}\end{cases}

an
n
-th fundamental interval
.
This interval represents the set of elements of
(0,1]
which have the finite or infinite regular continued fraction expansion beginning by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
, i.e.,

I(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\{x\in(0,1]\colon a_{i}(x)=a_{i}\ \text{ for }1\leq i\leq n\}.

The next lemma estimates the length of each fundamental interval in terms of the set of digits prescribing it. Let
|\cdot|
denote the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
we have

\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(a_{i}+1)^{2}}\leq|I(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{a_{i}^{2}}.

Proof.

By
[
16
,
19
]
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
we have

|I(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|=\frac{1}{q_{n}(q_{n}+q_{n-1})}\ \text{ and }\ \prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq q_{n}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+1),

(2.2)

where
q_{n-1}
,
q_{n}
are given by (
2.1
).
Since
q_{n-1}<q_{n}
we have

\frac{1}{2q_{n}^{2}}\leq|I(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|\leq\frac{1}{q_{n}^{2}}.

Combining these with the double inequalities in (
2.2
) yields the desired ones.
∎

2.2.
Lower bounds of Hausdorff dimension

Let
F=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{n}
be a set in
[0,1]
such that the following holds for every
n\geq 1
:

•

E_{n-1}
is the union of finitely many closed intervals of positive length, called
(n-1)
-th level intervals
;

•

for each
(n-1)
-th level interval
I
,
the number of
n
-th intervals contained in
E_{n}\cap I
is at least
r_{n}
, and all of these
n
-th intervals are separated by gaps of length at least
\delta_{n}
, where
r_{n}\geq 2
and
0<\delta_{n+1}<\delta_{n}
.

We call
F

a Moran fractal with profile

(r_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
(\delta_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. The Hausdorff dimension of a Moral fractal is estimated from below with its profile by the next lemma.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
8
]
pp.71–72)
.

Let
F\subset[0,1]
be a Moran fractal with profile

(r_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
(\delta_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
Then

\dim_{\rm H}F\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(r_{1}r_{2}\cdots r_{n-1})}{-\log(r_{n}\delta_{n})}.

Let
F\subset[0,1]
be a set. We say
f\colon F\to[0,1]
is Hölder continuous with exponent
\gamma\in(0,1]
if there exists
C>0
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C|x-y|^{\gamma}\ \text{ for all }x,y\in F.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
8
]
Proposition 3.3)
.

Let
F\subset[0,1]
and let
f\colon F\to[0,1]
be Hölder continuous with exponent
\gamma\in(0,1]
.
Then
\dim_{\rm H}F\geq\gamma\cdot\dim_{\rm H}f(F).

We say
f\colon F\to[0,1]
is
almost Lipschitz
if
for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

f
is Hölder continuous with exponent
\gamma
.
If
f
is not Lipschitz continuous and almost Lipschitz, then the multiplicative constant
C
grows to infinity as
\gamma
increases to
1
, but this growth is
inconsequential in the
lower bound of Hausdorff dimension.
Increasing
\gamma
to
1
in Lemma
2.3
we obtain the next lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
F\subset[0,1]
and let
f\colon F\to[0,1]
be almost Lipschitz. Then

\dim_{\rm H}F\geq\dim_{\rm H}f(F).

For an infinite subset
S
of
\mathbb{N}

\tau(S)=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\colon\sum_{a\in S}a^{-t}<\infty\right\}.

We call

of

Note

2

to

If we write

The next lemma links the convergence exponent to relative upper density.
The case
A\neq S
will only be used in the proof of Theorem
1.13
.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
S\subset\mathbb{N}
has polynomial density with exponent
\alpha
, then for any
A\subset S
with
\overline{d}(A|S)>0
we have

\tau(A)=\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Proof.

Write
A=\{a_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
,
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots
. Since
S
has polynomial density with exponent
\alpha
, there exist
C_{2}>0
and
\beta\geq 0
such that for all sufficiently large
n
we have

\frac{\log n}{\log a_{n}}=\frac{\log\#(A\cap[1,a_{n}])}{\log a_{n}}\leq\frac{\log(C_{2}/(\log a_{n})^{\beta})}{\log a_{n}}+\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Letting
n\to\infty
and combining the result with (
2.3
) we obtain
\tau(A)\leq 1/\alpha.

In the case
A=S
, the same argument as above yields the reverse inequality
\tau(A)\geq 1/\alpha.
In the case
A\neq S
,
the same argument does not work
since the positivity of
\overline{d}(A|S)
does not imply that of
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\#(A\cap[1,a_{n}])/\#(S\cap[1,a_{n}])
. Hence we argue as follows.
Take a strictly increasing sequence
(N_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
of positive integers such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#(A\cap[1,N_{k}])}{\#(S\cap[1,N_{k}])}=\overline{d}(A|S)>0.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/\alpha)
. Since
S
has polynomial density with exponent
\alpha
, there exist
C_{1}>0
and
\beta\geq 0
such for all sufficiently large
k
we have

\begin{split}\frac{\#(A\cap[N_{k}^{\varepsilon},N_{k}])}{\#(S\cap[1,N_{k}])}&\geq\frac{\#(A\cap[1,N_{k}])}{\#(S\cap[1,N_{k}])}-\frac{\#(A\cap[1,N_{k}^{\varepsilon}])}{\#(S\cap[1,N_{k}])}\\
&\geq\frac{\overline{d}(A|S)}{2}-\frac{(\log N_{k})^{\beta}}{C_{1}N_{k}^{1/\alpha-\varepsilon}}\geq\frac{\overline{d}(A|S)}{3}.\end{split}

Rearranging this gives

\#(A\cap[N_{k}^{\varepsilon},N_{k}])\geq\frac{\overline{d}(A|S)}{3}\#(S\cap[1,N_{k}])\geq\frac{\overline{d}(A|S)}{3}\frac{C_{1}N_{k}^{1/\alpha}}{(\log N_{k})^{\beta}}.

Let
0<t<1/\alpha
. For all sufficiently large
k\geq 1
we have

\sum_{a\in A\cap[N_{k}^{\varepsilon},N_{k}]}a^{-t}>N_{k}^{-t}\#(A\cap[N_{k}^{\varepsilon},N_{k}])\geq\frac{\overline{d}(A|S)}{3}\frac{C_{1}N_{k}^{1/\alpha-t}}{(\log N_{k})^{\beta}},

Letting
k\to\infty
we conclude that
\sum_{a\in A}a^{-t}=\infty
, namely
\tau(A)\geq t
. Increasing
t
to
1/\alpha
we obtain
\tau(A)\geq 1/\alpha
.∎

Lemma 2.8

(
[

.

We have

\dim_{\rm H}\{x\in J\colon\tau(\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\})=c\}=\frac{1-c}{2},\ 0\leq c\leq 1.

3.1.
Construction of extreme seed sets

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}
have polynomial density. For an infinite subset
S_{*}
of
S
and positive reals
t,L
satisfying
t>L>1
, define

R_{t,L}(S_{*})=\{x\in E\colon a_{n}(x)\in S_{*}\cap[t^{n},Lt^{n}]\ \text{ for every}\ n\geq 1\}.

Non-empty sets of this form are called
seed sets
. Seed sets are Moran-like fractals,
and their Hausdorff dimensions do not exceed
1/(2\alpha)
by Lemmas
2.5
and
2.7
.
In fact, one can decrease the relative upper density of
S_{*}
without decreasing the Hausdorff dimension of the corresponding seed set.
We say the seed set
R_{t,L}(S_{*})
is an
extreme seed set
if
S_{*}
does not contain consecutive integers, and

\overline{d}(S_{*}|S)=0\ \text{ and }\ \dim_{\rm H}R_{t,L}(S_{*})=\frac{1}{2\alpha}.

Extreme seed sets are of maximal Hausdorff dimension and least relative density of digits. The assumption of not containing consecutive integers is not essential, but a little facilitates
the proof of Proposition
3.5
in
\lx@sectionsign
3.2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
S\subset\mathbb{N}
has polynomial density, then there exist
S_{*}\subset S
and positive reals
t,L

such that
t>L>1
,
t>2
and

R_{t,L}(S_{*})
is an extreme seed set.

For a proof of this proposition we need two preliminary lemmas.
First, define a function
\nu\colon[1,\infty)\to\mathbb{N}
by

\nu(\xi)=k\ \text{ for }\xi\in[k!,(k+1)!),\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.1)

This is a slow increasing function that has the following upper bound.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
t>e
there exists
\lambda>1
such that

\nu(t^{n})\leq(n\log t)^{\lambda}\ \text{ for every }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

We claim that
\nu(t^{n})\leq n\log t
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
but finitely many ones.
For otherwise the definition of
\nu
in (
3.1
) would yield
t^{n}>\lfloor n\log t\rfloor!
for infinitely many
n
, which is a contradiction.
There exists a constant
c(t)\geq 1
such that

\nu(t^{n})\leq c(t)n\log t
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Take
\lambda>1

such that
c(t)\leq(\log t)^{\lambda-1}
. ∎

The next general lemma allows us to extract
from an arbitrary infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}

a set of zero relative density with definite growth bounds.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S
be an infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
and write
S=\{a_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
,
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots
. There exists
S_{*}\subset S
that does not contain consecutive integers and satisfies

\frac{1}{2\nu(\Delta)}\leq\frac{\#\{1\leq n\leq\Delta\colon a_{n}\in S_{*}\}}{\Delta}\leq\frac{3}{\nu(\Delta)}

(3.2)

for all sufficiently large
\Delta\geq 1
. In particular,
\overline{d}(S_{*}|S)=0
.

Proof.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, taking every
k
elements of
\mathbb{N}
with respect to the natural order one can construct a subset of
\mathbb{N}
with density
1/(k+1)
. Our construction here relies on this simple observation.
Let

Q=\bigcup_{k=2}^{\infty}\{k!+ik\colon i=0,\ldots,k!-1\}\subset\mathbb{N}.

The following interpretation of
Q
should be useful along the way. Split
[2,\infty)=\bigcup_{k=2}^{\infty}[k!,(k+1)!)
. For each integer
k\geq 2
, further split
[k!,(k+1)!)
into consecutive left-closed, right-open subintervals containing exactly
k
integers:

[k!,(k+1)!)=[k!,k!+k)\cup[k!+k,k!+2k)\cup\cdots\cup[k!+(k!-1)k,(k+1)!).

Then
Q
consists of the left endpoints of all these subintervals.
Note that
Q
does not contain consecutive integers.
Set

S_{*}=\{a_{n}\in S\colon n\in Q\}.

Then
S_{*}
does not contain consecutive integers.

For every
\Delta\geq 1
we have

\#\{1\leq n\leq\Delta\colon a_{n}\in S_{*}\}=\#(Q\cap[1,\Delta]).

If
\Delta\geq 1
is sufficiently then we have

\begin{split}\#(Q\cap[1,\Delta])&=\#(Q\cap[1,(k-1)!))+\#(Q\cap[(k-1)!,k!))+\#(Q\cap[k!,\Delta])\\
&\leq\#(Q\cap[1,(k-1)!))+\frac{\#([(k-1)!,k!)\cap\mathbb{N})}{k-1}+\frac{\#([k!,\Delta]\cap\mathbb{N})}{k}\\
&\leq(k-1)!+(k-1)!+\frac{\#([k!,\Delta]\cap\mathbb{N})}{k}\leq\frac{3\Delta}{\nu(\Delta)},\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\#(Q\cap[1,\Delta])&\geq\sum_{i=2}^{k-1}\frac{\#([i!,(i+1)!)\cap\mathbb{N})}{i}+\frac{\#([k!,\Delta]\cap\mathbb{N})}{k}-1\\
&\geq\sum_{i=2}^{k-1}\frac{\#([i!,(i+1)!)\cap\mathbb{N})}{k}+\frac{\#([k!,\Delta]\cap\mathbb{N})}{k}-1\\
&=\frac{\#([2,\Delta]\cap\mathbb{N})}{k}-1\geq\frac{\Delta}{2\nu(\Delta)},\end{split}

where
k\geq 1
is the integer with
\Delta\in[k!,(k+1)!)
.
This verifies (
3.2
).

The ordering of elements of
S
implies
\#(S_{*}\cap[1,N])=\#\{1\leq n\leq\#(S\cap[1,N])\colon a_{n}\in S_{*}\}
for every
N\geq 1
with

S\cap[1,N]\neq\emptyset
.
By the second inequality in (
3.2
), for all sufficiently large
N\geq 1
we have

\frac{\#(S_{*}\cap[1,N])}{\#(S\cap[1,N])}=\frac{\#\{1\leq n\leq\#(S\cap[1,N])\colon a_{n}\in S_{*}\}}{\#(S\cap[1,N])}\leq\frac{3}{\nu(\#(S\cap[1,N]))}.

Letting
N\to\infty
we obtain

\overline{d}(S_{*}|S)=0
as required.
∎

we have

C_{1}C_{2}^{-1}2^{-\beta}L^{-1/\alpha}\leq\frac{\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])}{\#(S\cap[1,Lt^{n}])}\leq C_{1}^{-1}C_{2}2^{\beta}L^{-1/\alpha}.

(3.6)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, fix

such that

If

is sufficiently large so that

, then

k!\leq\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])\leq\#(S\cap[1,Lt^{n}])\leq\frac{\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])}{C_{1}C_{2}^{-1}2^{-\beta}L^{-1/\alpha}}<(k+2)!.

By the definition of
\nu
in (
3.1
), this estimate implies

0\leq\nu(\#(S\cap[1,Lt^{n}]))-\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}]))\leq 1.

(3.7)

By
t>e
and Lemma
3.2
, there exists
\lambda>1
such that
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}]))\leq\nu(t^{n})\leq C_{1}(n\log t)^{\lambda}.

(3.8)

Using (
3.3
), (
3.5
) and then (
3.6
), (
3.7
),
for each integer

we have

\begin{split}\#(S_{*}\cap[t^{n},Lt^{n}])&\geq\#(S_{*}\cap[1,Lt^{n}])-\#(S_{*}\cap[1,t^{n}])\\
&\geq\frac{\#(S\cap[1,Lt^{n}])}{2\nu(\#(S\cap[1,Lt^{n}]))}-\frac{3\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])}{\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}]))}\\
&\geq\frac{C_{1}C_{2}^{-1}2^{-\beta}L^{1/\alpha}\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])}{2(\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])+1)}-\frac{3\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])}{\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}]))}.\end{split}

By (
3.5
), the last expression is bounded from below by

\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])\left(\frac{C_{1}C_{2}^{-1}2^{-\beta}L^{1/\alpha}}{3\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}]))}-\frac{3}{\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}]))}\right),

which equals
\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])/\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}]))
by (
3.4
). Set

.
Using (
3.8
) and the assumption that
S
has polynomial density, we obtain

\begin{split}\#(S_{*}\cap[t^{n},Lt^{n}])\geq\frac{\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}])}{\nu(\#(S\cap[1,t^{n}]))}&\geq C_{1}\frac{t^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}}{(n\log t)^{\beta}}\cdot\frac{1}{C_{1}(n\log t)^{\lambda}}\\
&=\frac{t^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}}{(n\log t)^{\rho}}.\end{split}

(3.9)

We now set

F=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{i}\in{S_{*}\cap[t^{i},Lt^{i}]}\\
1\leq i\leq n\end{subarray}}\overline{I(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})},

where the bar denotes the closure.
Since
t>L
, the intervals
[t^{n},Lt^{n}]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
are pairwise disjoint.
The Moran fractal
F
is contained in
R_{t,L}(S_{*})

up to countable sets consisting of the boundary points of the level intervals.
For every
n\geq 2
and every
(n-1)
-th level interval of
F
, the number of
n
-th level intervals contained in that interval is at least
{\lfloor t^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}/(n\log t)^{\rho}\rfloor}
by (
3.9
).
Since

does not contain consecutive integers, these

-th level intervals are separated at least by some
n
-th fundamental intervals. Hence,

-th level intervals is at least

\min\{|I(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|\colon t^{i}\leq a_{i}\leq Lt^{i},1\leq i\leq n\}.

By Lemma
2.1
and
t>L
, we have

|I(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(a_{i}+1)^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(Lt^{i}+1)^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(Lt^{i+1})^{2}}.

So,

is a Moran fractal with profile

({\lfloor t^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}/(n\log t)^{\rho}\rfloor})_{n=1}^{\infty}
,

((1/2)\prod_{i=1}^{n}(Lt^{i+1})^{-2})_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
Lemma
2.2
gives

\dim_{\rm H}F\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(2\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}{\lfloor t^{\frac{i}{\alpha}}/(i\log t)^{\rho}\rfloor})}{-\log\left((\lfloor t^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}\rfloor/(n\log t)^{\rho})\prod_{i=1}^{n}(Lt^{i+1})^{-2}\right)}.

Let us agree that
for two eventually positive functions
f
,
g
on
\mathbb{N}
, the expression

f(n)\sim g(n)
indicates that
f(n)/g(n)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
Direct calculations show

\displaystyle\log\left(2\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{t^{\frac{i}{\alpha}}}{(i\log t)^{\rho}}\right\rfloor}\right)

\displaystyle=\log 2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\log\left\lfloor\frac{t^{\frac{i}{\alpha}}}{(i\log t)^{\rho}}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\sim\log 2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\log t^{\frac{i}{\alpha}}-\log(i\log t)^{\rho}\right)\sim\frac{1}{2\alpha}n^{2}\log t,

and

\begin{split}-\log\left(\frac{\lfloor t^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}\rfloor}{(n\log t)^{\rho}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(Lt^{i+1})^{2}}\right)&{\sim}-\frac{n}{\alpha}\log t+\rho\log(n\log t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\log(Lt^{i+1})\\
&\sim n^{2}\log t.\end{split}

Combining these two expressions yields
\dim_{\rm H}R_{t,L}(S_{*})\geq\dim_{\rm H}F\geq 1/(2\alpha)
. This completes the proof of Proposition
3.1
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

If
S\subset\mathbb{N}
has polynomial density with exponent
\alpha
, then

\dim_{\rm H}\{x\in J\colon\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S\}=\dim_{\rm H}\{x\in E\colon\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S\}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}.

Proof.

On the one hand, by Lemmas
2.5
and
2.7
we have

\dim_{\rm H}\{x\in E\colon\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S\}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}.

On the other hand,
Proposition
3.1
implies

\dim_{\rm H}\{x\in J\colon\{a_{n}(x)\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset S\}\geq\frac{1}{2\alpha}.

Since

, these two inequalities yield the desired equalities. ∎

Appendix A
Bergelson-Leibman’s theorem in
\mathbb{N}

This appendix gives the proof of Theorem
1.2
. It relies on Furstenberg’s correspondence principle and the following multiple recurrence theorem with polynomial times.
Recall that
\mathbb{Z}_{0}[X]
denotes the set of polynomials with integer coefficients that vanish at
X=0
.

Theorem A.1

(
[
2
]
Theorem
{\rm A}_{0}
)
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
be a probability space, let
T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell}
be a finite collection of commuting invertible measure preserving transformations on
X
, let
h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}[X]
, and let
A\in\mathcal{B}
satisfy
\mu(A)>0
. Then

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\mu(T_{1}^{-h_{1}(m)}(A)\cap T_{2}^{-h_{2}(m)}(A)\cap\cdots\cap T_{\ell}^{-h_{\ell}(m)}(A))>0.

A.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}
satisfy
d_{\rm B}(S)>0
.
Let
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the two-sided Cartesian product space endowed with the product topology of the discrete topology on
\{0,1\}
.
Define
x^{*}=(x_{i}^{*})_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
by

x_{i}^{*}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i\geq 1$ and $i\in S$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
\sigma
denote the left shift acting on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and let

\Sigma=\overline{\{\sigma^{i}(x^{*})\colon i\in\mathbb{Z}\}}
. Then
\Sigma
is a compact metrizable space and
\sigma(\Sigma)=\Sigma
. Let

A=\{(y_{i})_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\in\Sigma\colon y_{0}=1\}
.
Let
(I_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of compact intervals in
[1,\infty)
such that
\#(\mathbb{N}\cap I_{k})\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
and

d_{\rm B}(S)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#(S\cap I_{k})}{\#(\mathbb{N}\cap I_{k})}.

For each
k\geq 1
define a Borel probability measure
\mu_{k}
on
\Sigma
by

\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{\#(\mathbb{N}\cap I_{k})}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}\cap I_{k}}\delta_{\sigma^{i}(x^{*})},

where
\delta_{\sigma^{i}(x^{*})}
denotes the unit point mass at
\sigma^{i}(x^{*})
.
Note that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mu_{k}(A)=d_{\rm B}(S)
.
Pick a limit point of the sequence
(\mu_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
in the weak* topology and denote it by
\mu
. Then

\mu
is
\sigma|_{\Sigma}
-invariant.
Since
A
is a clopen set, we have

\mu(A)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mu_{k}(A)
and so
\mu(A)=d_{\rm B}(S)>0
.

For each
m\in\mathbb{Z}
let
S-m=\{i\in\mathbb{N}\colon i+m\in S\}.

Let
k\geq 1
and
m_{1},\ldots,m_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}
. For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, note that

i\in I_{k}\cap S\cap(S-m_{1})\cap\cdots\cap(S-m_{\ell})
if and only if

\sigma^{i}(x^{*})\in A\cap\sigma^{-m_{1}}(A)\cap\cdots\cap\sigma^{-m_{\ell}}(A)
. This correspondence yields

\begin{split}\frac{\#(I_{k}\cap S\cap(S-m_{1})\cap\cdots\cap(S-m_{\ell}))}{\#(\mathbb{N}\cap I_{k})}=\mu_{k}(A\cap\sigma^{-m_{1}}(A)\cap\cdots\cap\sigma^{-m_{\ell}}(A)).\end{split}

Hence

\begin{split}d_{\rm B}(S\cap(S-m_{1})\cap\cdots\cap(S-m_{\ell}))&\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#(I_{k}\cap S\cap(S-m_{1})\cap\cdots\cap(S-m_{\ell}))}{\#(\mathbb{N}\cap I_{k})}\\
&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mu_{k}(A\cap\sigma^{-m_{1}}(A)\cap\cdots\cap\sigma^{-m_{\ell}}(A))\\
&=\mu(A\cap\sigma^{-m_{1}}(A)\cap\cdots\cap\sigma^{-m_{\ell}}(A)).\end{split}

The first inequality follows from the definition of upper Banach density. The second equality holds because
A\cap\sigma^{-m_{1}}(A)\cap\cdots\cap\sigma^{-m_{\ell}}(A)
is a clopen set.
Let

. Replacing
m_{j}
by
h_{j}(m)
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
for

we have

d_{\rm B}(S\cap(S-h_{1}(m))\cap\cdots\cap(S-h_{\ell}(m)))\geq\mu(A\cap\sigma^{-h_{1}(m)}(A)\cap\cdots\cap\sigma^{-h_{\ell}(m)}(A)).

Applying Theorem
A.1
to the invertible
\mu
-preserving system

we obtain

d_{\rm B}(S\cap(S-h_{1}(m))\cap\cdots\cap(S-h_{\ell}(m)))>0
for infinitely many
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
This yields the desired statement.∎

Appendix C
Generalizations to Iterated Function Systems

A countable collection of self-maps is called an
Iterated Function System (IFS)
. It is well-known that the regular continued fraction (
1.1
) is generated by an IFS on
[0,1]
.
Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
can be generalized to a wide class of

that are generated by IFSs.

Let

to itself and let

We say

is a

-decaying IFS

[

]
if the following conditions hold:

(i)

there exists

and
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that for every
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
and every

we have

0<|(f_{a_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{a_{m}})^{\prime}(x)|\leq\gamma;

(ii)

for all distinct

f_{i}((0,1))\cap f_{j}((0,1))=\emptyset;

(iii)

for any
\varepsilon>0,
there exist

c_{2}(\varepsilon)
with
0<c_{1}(\varepsilon)\leq 1\leq c_{2}(\varepsilon)
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every

we have

\frac{c_{1}(\varepsilon)}{k^{d(1+\varepsilon)}}\leq|f_{k}^{\prime}(x)|\leq\frac{c_{2}(\varepsilon)}{k^{d(1-\varepsilon)}};

(iv)

\overline{\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}([0,1])}=[0,1]

i<j
then
f_{i}(x)>f_{j}(x)
for all

If
\{f_{k}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a
d
-decaying IFS, then
by (i) one can define a natural projection
\Pi\colon\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow[0,1]
by

\Pi((a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{a_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{a_{n}}(1).

For any
x\in[0,1],
there exists a sequence
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of positive integers such that

x=\Pi((a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}).

(C.1)

By (ii), this correspondence is one-to-one
up to a countable number of points, and
(
C.1
) gives an expansion of

into an infinite series.
The sequence
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in (

) may be viewed as a digit sequence of

.

The regular continued fraction is generated by the
2
-decaying IFS
\{f_{k}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}\}
defined by

f_{k}(x)=1/(x+k)
with
m=2
.
Our setup and main results can be generalized to number expansions generated by arbitrary

-decaying IFSs.
The constant
1/2
in Theorem
1.1
should be replaced by
1/d
,
and the constant
1/(2\alpha)
in Theorem
1.4
should be replaced by
1/(d\alpha)
.
Lemma
2.1
is the only place where the specific formulas for the regular continued fraction were used.
In this generalization, (iii) can be used as a substitute for Lemma
2.1
.

Let us stick to Billingsley’s notation for continued fractions.
Each
