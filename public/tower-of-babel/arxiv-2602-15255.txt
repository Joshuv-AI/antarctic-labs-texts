Helium superluminous SN 2021bnw : an explosion of a massive star with a pre-outburst
Alexandra Kozyreva, Matteo Bugli, Alexey Mironov, Petr Baklanov

Abstract
Superluminous supernovae (SLSNe) remain an intriguing topic in supernova (SN) transient astronomy. While the majority of SLSNe are shown to be explained by energy streaming from the newly born magnetar, there are others which are powered by different mechanisms. We analyse the pseudo-bolometric light curve of the nearby helium-rich SLSN 2021bnw. We built models and run hydrodynamics radiative-transfer simulations with STELLA . Our best-fit models include 15–22.5 M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} of ejecta enriched with 1.7 M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} of 56 Ni and carrying energy of 4 foe 1 1 1 1 ​ foe ≡ 10 51 ​ erg 1\,\mathrm{foe}\equiv 10^{51}\,\mathrm{erg} , and colliding with 7 M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} of circumstellar matter which match the observed light curve very well. The early data can be explained as cooling of an expanding shell with the mass of 0.5 M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} and kinetic energy of 0.7 foe. We tend to exclude a pulsational pair-instability (PPISN) origin for SLSN 2021bnw. Instead we conclude that SLSN 2021bnw was preferably a core-collapse explosion of a star with the initial mass of not less than 61 M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} aided by magnetorotational effects.

I
Introduction

Superluminous supernovae (SLSNe) have become more and more popular over the last decade and remain intriguing subject in transient astronomy. By definition, their high peak luminosity exceeds absolute magnitude of
-20
mag
(
A. Gal-Yam 2012
)
.
Mechanisms suggested to be responsible for their light curves (LCs) include primarily radioactive nickel
56
Ni and cobalt
56
Co decay, interaction between supernova (SN) ejecta and circumstellar matter (CSM), extraction of rotational energy from a central magnetar and other
(
T. J. Moriya et al. 2018
)
.
Moreover, a number of SLSNe (together with other types of SNe) display double-peak or bumpy LCs, therefore suggesting that combinations of different mechanisms are involved in a single event.

In the family of SLSNe there are those SNe which show identified helium lines in their spectra
(
L. Yan et al. 2020
)
.
Among those few He-SLSNe there is a recently discovered SN 2021bnw
(
F. Poidevin et al. 2023
)
, to which helium lines are clearly seen over photospheric phase
(
A. Fiore et al. 2026
)
.
The SN reaches a peak magnitude of
-20.5
mags in
g
,
r
,
i
, and
o
-bands and has a broad LC spanning about 140 days.

Assuming a pure radioactive powered LC, the maximum luminosity of SN 2021bnw of
\log L=43.6
[erg s
{}^{\,-1}
] requires
\sim
14
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of radioactive nickel, which can be produced only in a pair-instability explosion of a very massive star with helium core of 110
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
.
However, a pair-instability SN (PISN) LC is generally too slow-evolving and with a relatively high-luminosity tail, since it rises to the peak during
\sim\,100
days and lasts more than 500 days
(
D. Kasen et al. 2011
)

Instead, the LC of SN 2021bnw drops 100 days after the peak, and its tail luminosity is in fact relatively low, although there is a lack of data at late epochs. Hence,
56
Ni –
56
Co radioactive decay cannot be the main powering mechanism for SN 2021bnw. We note, that the magnetar spindown can mimic the
56

(
T. J. Moriya et al. 2017
)
, and this powering mechanism was explored for SN 2021bnw by
F. Poidevin et al. 2023

Kinetic energy coming from interaction of SN ejecta with the surrounding matter may complement
56
Ni radioactive decay energy supply (“CSM+Ni”) and amplify the luminosity of a SN.
This combination was proposed, for instance, for SLSNe 2006gy and PTF12dam by
T. J. Moriya et al. 2013
and
A. Tolstov et al. 2017
, and other SNe
(
T. Moriya et al. 2011
;
U. M. Noebauer et al. 2016
;
S.-C. Leung et al. 2021
)
.
Therefore, we choose to focus on CSM+Ni as possible powering mechanism for SN 2021bnw in the present study.

In Section

we describe the CSM+Ni models, and in Section
III

We connect the derived ejecta and explosion characteristics to the progenitor scenario in Section

II
Light curve modeling: two main powering contributors

To build the input model for the radiative transfer simulations, we consider the SN ejecta which is unbound from the central compact object and expanding, and a CSM appended to the ejecta.

As a template for the freely expanding ejecta we use the hydrodynamical profile of a 90
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
He-model
(
A. Heger & S. E. Woosley 2002
)
, and construct the density-scaled profiles with different total masses.
The hydrodynamical quantities of the ejecta are taken at day 5 after the explosion of the original model He90.
The ejecta consist mainly of oxygen, helium, carbon, silicon, and sulfur.
For instance, the density profile for the case of ejecta of 22.5
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
is acquired while scaling the original He90 profile by a factor of 4.
The ejecta in this case contain: 11
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, 3.8
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, 3.4
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, 2.6
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, 1.6
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, of O, He, C, Si, S, respectively, and 0.33
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of
56
Ni.
The explosion energy, which is stored in the ejecta in form of kinetic energy, is scaled with the ejecta mass accordingly, thereby corresponding to an energy of 5.9 foe.
We do not inject any additional energy during the simulations except that produced in
56
Ni and
56
Co decay.

We surrounded the newly constructed ejecta with a wind-like CSM (
\rho\sim r^{\,-2}
), whose inner boundary is the outer edge of the SN ejecta.
The chemical composition of the CSM corresponds to the surface composition of the SN ejecta, namely, with the ratio of
\mathrm{He}:\mathrm{C}:\mathrm{O}=5:3:2
(where numbers are rounded for simplicity).
The choice of a He-rich CSM is motivated by the observed He I
feature in the NIR spectrum of SN 2021bnw at day 87 after the peak
(
A. Fiore et al. 2026
)
.

Radiative-transfer simulations for the present study are carried out with the hydrodynamics radiative-transfer code
STELLA

(
S. I. Blinnikov et al. 1998
;
S. Blinnikov et al. 2000
)
, which is able to follow both the radiative field and hydrodynamical evolution of the ejecta interacting with the CSM.
The expansion of the ejecta and its interaction with the CSM obeys the standard set of non-relativistic hydrodynamic equations.
We refer a reader to the extensive description of the code and comparison to other sophisticated radiative-transfer codes presented in
S. Blondin et al. 2022
.

We searched for ejecta and CSM masses suitable to reproduce the pseudo-bolometric LC of SN 2021bnw within the ranges of 5–90
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and 1–22.2
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, respectively. We also varied the mass of
56
Ni and radius of the CSM. Note that we do not carry out full parameter study.

IV
Evolutionary path to the eruption and explosion that led to SN 2021bnw

IV.1
The initial mass of a progenitor of SN 2021bnw

According to our LC modelling, SN 2021bnw progenitor requires not less than 22–30
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, with the average between models of 26
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
H-deficient stellar matter before the pre-SN outburst.
This means that the original star had to develop a helium core not less than 26
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
during earlier evolution, although this estimate excludes an unknown mass of a compact object formed during the core collapse.

A. Heger & S. E. Woosley 2002
derived an empirical relation between the initial zero-age main sequence (ZAMS) mass (
\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{ZAMS}}
) and a corresponding He-core mass (
\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{He}}
):

\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{ZAMS}}=\nicefrac{{24}}{{13}}\>\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{He}}+20
.
This relation is valid for progenitor ZAMS masses
140-260

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and corresponding He-cores of
64-133

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, i.e. higher than 26
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
inferred from the LC modelling.
Assuming this relation might be applicable for lower mass stellar models, the progenitor in our case would have 60.8–74.8
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
at ZAMS

.
Mass-losses during the stage of H-deficient evolution should also be taken into account, however, this cannot be estimated straight-forward, since wind mass-losses are unpredictable for most massive stars
(
J. S. Vink 2015
)
.
Therefore, 74.8
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
serves as a rough low limit for the initial mass of SN 2021bnw progenitor.

N. Yusof et al. 2013
calculated a grid of very massive stellar models, and found that a star with a ZAMS mass of 120
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
at solar metallicity results in a progenitor of 30.81
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
at the end of oxygen burning, close to 26
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
related to the case of SN 2021bnw. The environment of SN 2021bnw has the lower metallicity (
Z_{\odot}/3
), i.e. 120
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
is an overestimated value for an initial mass of the progenitor, because of the lower expected wind mass-losses. Their 120
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
rotating model at LMC (
Z_{\odot}/2.3
) metallicity ended up as 39.2
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
progenitor, which is higher than needed for our best-fit models and means that the SN 2021bnw progenitor had a ZAMS mass lower than 120
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. Hence, we deduce that the progenitor of SN 2021bnw was a massive star whose ZAMS mass lies above 61
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
but below 120
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, if a single stellar evolution scenario is considered. Refining the prediction of the progenitor mass range requires detailed stellar evolution calculations, which are beyond the scope of the current study.

The terminal explosion of such progenitor should be an abnormally high (5 foe) energetic event, similar to hypernovae
(
P. A. Mazzali et al. 2006
;
K. Iwamoto et al. 1994
)
, during which a high mass of
56
Ni (1.7
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) is produced, as inferred from our LC modelling. These numbers lie beyond the current limits of physically-consistent neutrino-driven core-collapse simulations
(
T. Ertl et al. 2020
)
but are hypothetically possible, especially if magnetic fields are involved, and the core retains its angular momentum. This phenomenon is represented by a collapsar or a magnetorotational core collapse
(
G. S. Bisnovatyi-Kogan et al. 1976
;
A. I. MacFadyen & S. E. Woosley 1999
;
M. Bugli et al. 2021
;
M. Obergaulinger & M. Á. Aloy 2021
;
B. Müller 2024
)
.

IV.2
Can SN 2021bnw be a PPISN?

The average sum of the ejecta mass and helium-rich CSM between best-fit models equals 26
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, i.e. suspiciously close to the range of PPISN progenitors
(
S. E. Woosley 2017
;
P. Marchant et al. 2019
;
M. Renzo et al. 2020
)
. A general consensus is that the lowest mass of a helium core which can finalise its life as a PPISN ranges between 34
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
for evolution without mass-loss, and 48.5
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
for evolution which accounts for mass-loss, rotation, and magnetic field. We note that in any reliable PPISN scenario, the final mass of the progenitor preceding the terminal core collapse, i.e. after a sequence of pair-instability pulses, is 33.87–43.06
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, which entirely collapses into a black hole. This terminal event occurs without mass ejection, ejected radioactive nickel, consequently, without a SN-like transient.

Other studies
(
T. Yoshida et al. 2016
, see, e.g.,)
explore PPISN models which end up as core-collapse explosions with very high explosion energies, up to 10 foe, small black hole masses of 2
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, final ejecta of 50
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, and relatively large amount of
56
Ni, 1.2-2
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
.

A. Tolstov et al. 2017
,
S.-C. Leung et al. 2019
, and
T. J. Moriya et al. 2020
use massive stellar models, including PPISNe, with the final progenitor mass of about 40
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, which by default should collapse into a black hole
(
N. Rahman et al. 2022
)
.
Being blown up by an artificial injection of high energy (5–20 foe) and with artificially set large mass of
56
Ni, these models explain several luminous transients (PTF12dam, SN 2011kl, AT2018cow, SN 2018gep), in turn, interpreted as PPISNe
(
S.-C. Leung et al. 2021
)
.

Our best-fit models have about 20
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of expanding ejecta interacting with 7
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of CSM, and we do not include a central compact object in our simulations. It means that we cannot decide what was the mass of the final pre-collapse core before the terminal explosion. Therefore, to be on the safe site, we propose that the progenitor in the case of SN 2021bnw was well below the low-limit of the PPISN carbon-oxygen core mass of 34
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, and it is fair to conclude that SN 2021bnw is most likely not a PPISN
(
R. Hirschi et al. 2025
, see also)
.

IV.3
The origin of CSM and the pre-SN outburst in SN 2021bnw

As discussed above, we decline a possibility of SN 2021bnw to be a PPISN, as the estimated ejecta+CSM mass lies below the low limit for a PPISN progenitor, therefore, the progenitor is presumably a massive star which ends its life as a core-collapse SN. In this view the progenitor at the moment of pre-outburst is at the stellar evolution stage of core oxygen burning, which lasts about a month and is followed by several hours of core silicon burning
(
S. E. Woosley et al. 2002
;
R. Hirschi et al. 2004
)
. The star consists mainly of a burning core with a helium-enriched outer layer which expected to be ejected and shape a CSM. Assuming a velocity for the CSM of 100 km s
{}^{\,-1}
, it requires 2 000 yrs to form a CSM extended to the radius of
7\times 10^{\,15}
cm, or 20 000 yrs, if the velocity equals 10 km s
{}^{\,-1}
. This means that the CSM has been formed since core helium burning, and the wind mass-loss can be responsible for the surrounding matter.

The origin of a possible mass ejection preceding the terminal explosion by 18 days is more speculative. There are different physical processes that can be responsible for mass ejection in massive stars. Firstly, convection driven gravity waves generate a non-linear velocity field and cause additional energy deposition into loosely bound envelope
(
E. Quataert & J. Shiode 2012
;
J. Fuller & S. Ro 2018
;
C. D. Matzner & S. Ro 2021
)
.
Secondly, as a star has a set of nuclear burning shells, instabilities arise from turbulent convective motions coupled with shell burning
(
N. Yadav et al. 2020
;
V. Varma et al. 2025
;
E. E. Whitehead et al. 2026
)
.
Additionally, pulsations can lead to pulsationally-driven mass-loss
(
Y. A. Fadeyev & M. F. Novikova 2004
;
S.-C. Yoon & M. Cantiello 2010
)
.

V
Conclusions

In this study, we built descriptive models which explain the pseudo-bolometric light curve of He–SLSN 2021bnw well. Our models combine interaction of the SN ejecta with the surrounding matter, and radioactive nickel
56
Ni power supply.
15-22.5

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of ejecta with kinetic energy of 4 foe smash into 7
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of helium-rich CSM and produce the amount of energy needed to power the first 80 days of the observed LC. To support the rest of SN 2021bnw LC until day 140, the ejecta should be enriched with 1.7
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of
56
Ni. The early data points in the observed LC cannot be explained by interaction of the ejecta which carry such high energy, and might represent the pre-outburst which occurred 18 days before the terminal explosion, i.e. during core oxygen burning. The expanding shell with the mass of half of solar and kinetic energy of 0.7 foe emits enough energy while cooling to match these data.

The sum of the ejecta and the CSM in our best-fit models ranges between 22
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
and 30
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. These numbers lie below the PPISN mass limit, therefore, we lean against a pulsational pair-instability origin for SLSN 2021bnw. Instead, we suggest that SN 2021bnw was preferably a core-collapse explosion of a massive star with initial mass not less than 61
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
that was driven by the magnetorotaitonal mechanism (rather than neutrino heating) and produced a large amount of
56
Ni.

Current nucleosynthesis calculations from state-of-the-art extreme core-collapse SN simulations demonstrate indeed their potential in producing high amounts of
56
Ni
(see
M. Obergaulinger & M. Reichert 2020
, for a recent review)
. There are, however, a few caveats that need to be considered. While the magnetorotational core-collapse SN scenario appears to be the most likely to have occurred in the case of SLSN 2021bnw, it remains still unclear whether the explosion dynamics of a high-mass progenitor such as the one required in our model could deliver the inferred 1.7
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
of
56
Ni.
Moreover, to achieve such high yields the intense magnetic field that extracts the central engine’s rotation would most likely need to be a large-scale dipolar field aligned with the rotational axis, since this appears to be the only configuration that ensures a sufficiently high efficiency in the explosion dynamics
(
M. Bugli et al. 2020
;
M. Bugli et al. 2023
)
and nucleosynthesis
(
M. Reichert et al. 2023
;
M. Reichert et al. 2024
)
.
Future work on explosive nucleosynthesis from magnetorotational core-collapse SNe could address this scenario more specifically and therefore put to the test the results of the current study.
