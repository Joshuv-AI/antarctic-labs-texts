Threshold-guided optimization of transcranial electrical stimulation parameters for motor evoked potential monitoring in pediatric spinal surgery
Authors: Vizmary J Montes Peña, Fayez AlEnazy, Abdullah AlBishi, Samir O AlSayegh, Sami AlEissa, Wael A AlShaya, Moutasem Azzubi, Faisal R Jahangiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTranscranial electrical stimulation motor evoked potential (TES-MEP) monitoring during pediatric spinal surgery is technically challenging due to developmental corticospinal physiology. We quantified the effects of electrode montage, stimulation pattern, and interstimulus interval (ISI) on motor threshold (MT) to define a practical optimization protocol for pediatric neuromonitoring.MethodsEighty-one pediatric patients (0.5–17 years) undergoing spinal or spinal cord surgery were analyzed using a prospective within-patient design. Effects of montage and stimulation pattern were assessed with repeated-measures ANOVA. ISI modulation (2, 3, 4 ms) was evaluated within the linked quadripolar montage with double-train stimulation (LQP-DTS) in patients without floor effect (n = 65). Multivariable regression identified determinants of baseline MT, ISI responsiveness, and pulse escalation.ResultsMontage and stimulation pattern exerted large independent effects on MT (η2p = 0.986 and 0.969; both p < 0.001). LQP-DTS reduced mean MT by 77.8 V (95% CI 76.1–79.6) compared with C1–C2 STS and produced the lowest threshold in all patients. ISI significantly modulated MT (η2p = 0.868, p < 0.001), with a 17.35 V increase at 2 ms and a 3.46 V decrease at 4 ms relative to 3 ms. Younger age and preoperative motor deficit independently predicted higher baseline MT (R2 = 0.699, p < 0.001). Baseline MT predicted ISI responsiveness and pulse escalation (AUC 0.917–0.918).ConclusionPediatric TES-MEP thresholds follow a reproducible physiological hierarchy. Optimization using LQP-DTS at ISI 3 ms with threshold-guided adjustment reduces stimulation requirements and supports reliable corticospinal monitoring during pediatric spinal surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00381-026-07339-0.Keywords: Intraoperative neuromonitoring, Motor evoked potentials, Linked quadripolar montage, Double-train stimulation, Interstimulus interval, Pediatric spinal surgery

Introduction

Intraoperative neuromonitoring is routinely used during pediatric spinal deformity correction, spinal cord tumor resection, and spinal dysraphism repair, where corticospinal tract integrity is at risk. Transcranial electrical stimulation motor-evoked potential (TES-MEP) monitoring enables real-time assessment of motor pathway function, but its reliability in children is influenced by developmental neurophysiology. Maturation of the corticospinal tract alters excitability and conduction, resulting in higher motor thresholds and greater variability in younger patients [2, 25], with reduced acquisition success compared with older cohorts [5, 13].

Anatomical and clinical factors further affect monitoring. Age-related anterior displacement of the motor cortex can influence electrode positioning [19], and preoperative motor deficits are associated with reduced MEP acquisition and increased stimulation requirements [3].

Stimulation parameters also play a critical role. Electrode montage influences current distribution and cortical recruitment [22], with linked quadripolar (LQP) configurations associated with lower thresholds than conventional bipolar montages [6]. Double-train stimulation enhances temporal summation [10], and interstimulus interval (ISI) modulates corticospinal recruitment [18, 24]. However, these parameters are typically evaluated in isolation, and integrated optimization strategies in pediatric populations remain limited [20].

We implemented a two-phase within-patient optimization protocol in a consecutive pediatric cohort undergoing spinal surgery. Phase 1 compared six montage–pattern configurations to identify the lowest-threshold condition, and phase 2 evaluated ISI-dependent modulation. Age and preoperative motor status were examined as determinants of baseline motor threshold (MT) and pulse-count escalation.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted using a predefined within-patient TES-MEP optimization protocol established prior to enrollment. Consecutive pediatric patients undergoing spinal or spinal cord surgery between January and November 2023 at a tertiary pediatric referral center were screened for inclusion. TES-MEP monitoring was performed as part of standard intraoperative care in accordance with established intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring standards [14]. Institutional review board approval was obtained before study initiation (King Abdullah International Medical Research Center, KAIMRC; approval number NRC23R/567/08).

Inclusion criteria comprised patients aged 0–18 years undergoing surgery requiring motor pathway monitoring. Exclusion criteria were limited to recognized contraindications to transcranial electrical stimulation, including implanted electrical stimulators capable of altering current distribution, skull defects significantly affecting transcranial conduction, and uncontrolled epilepsy, consistent with published safety recommendations for TES-MEP monitoring [14].

MT was defined as a reproducible compound muscle action potential ≥ 50 μV obtained in the predefined target muscle. A total of 83 consecutive patients met inclusion criteria during the study period; two remained non-elicitable despite stepwise stimulation up to the predefined institutional safety ceiling of 650 V and were excluded from MT analyses. Both demonstrated severe preoperative motor deficits (Medical Research Council (MRC) grade ≤ 2/5 in at least one limb), consistent with prior evidence linking severe baseline weakness to absent MEP responses [7]. These two cases are reported at the screened cohort level (2/83).

The final analytic cohort consisted of 81 children (age range 0.5–17 years; mean 7.6 ± 4.3 years; median 8.0 years), including 45 females (55.6%) and 36 males (44.4%). Surgical indications included spinal deformity (idiopathic, congenital, and neuromuscular scoliosis; n = 45, 55.6%), tethered cord spectrum disorders (n = 24, 29.6%), cervical spine pathology (n = 9, 11.1%), and other spinal conditions (n = 3, 3.7%). Preoperative motor status was categorized using the MRC 0–5 scale as normal (5/5 in all tested limbs) or impaired (< 5/5 in at least one limb). Age was analyzed as a continuous variable in regression models.

The study followed a two-phase optimization framework, schematically illustrated in Fig. 1. Phase 1 characterized MT across six stimulation configurations combining electrode montage and stimulation paradigm using a fixed ISI of 3 ms. Phase 2 evaluated ISI-dependent modulation (2 and 4 ms) relative to the patient-specific lowest-threshold configuration identified during phase 1, within the physiologic refractory window described for corticospinal activation [18].

Anesthesia

All procedures were performed under total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) using continuous propofol (6–12 mg/kg/h) and fentanyl (0.1–1.0 μg/kg/h) infusion. Volatile anesthetic agents were avoided after induction. Neuromuscular blockade was limited to tracheal intubation and was not maintained during MT determination. Absence of neuromuscular blockade was confirmed with train-of-four monitoring prior to baseline MT acquisition.

TES stimulation setup and MT determination

Transcranial electrical stimulation was delivered in constant-voltage mode using a Cadwell Cascade IOMAX system (Cadwell Industries, Kennewick, WA, USA) with monophasic rectangular pulses (75 μs). Stimulating electrode type was selected according to patient age and scalp characteristics: EEG cup electrodes in infants or patients with open fontanelles, subdermal needle electrodes in toddlers or those with ventriculoperitoneal shunts, and corkscrew electrodes in older children. Electrode impedance was verified prior to testing and maintained below 5 kΩ.

Three montages were evaluated in a fixed sequence: C1–C2, M3–M4, and LQP. The M3–M4 montage was selected instead of conventional C3–C4 positioning based on anterior displacement strategies described to optimize activation of the primary motor cortex hand representation [14]. In children, age-dependent anterior localization of the motor cortex further supports this strategy [19]. Accordingly, in patients younger than 6 years, electrodes were positioned slightly anterior to M3–M4 (FC3–FC4) when required to optimize cortical recruitment. In the LQP montage configuration, C1–M3 and C2–M4 were electrically linked to opposite stimulator outputs with polarity held constant, following the quadrupolar approach previously described in TES-MEP literature [21].

Single-train stimulation (STS) consisted of a 5-pulse train at ISI 3 ms. Double-train stimulation (DTS) consisted of a 2-pulse conditioning train followed by a 5-pulse test train separated by a 10-ms intertrain interval. This interval was selected based on Journée et al. [10, 11], who described an early S-ITI facilitation peak at ≈8–10 ms attributed to spinal EPSP summation under near-threshold conditions. The ITI was held constant and not systematically varied. An ISI of 3 ms was selected as reference based on prior evidence of optimal recruitment within the 3–4-ms range [18, 24]. Representative TES-MEP waveforms obtained under STS and DTS paradigms across montages are shown in Online Resource 1.

MT was determined sequentially across the six montage–stimulation combinations (three montages under STS followed by the same three under DTS). Stimulation began at 50 V and increased in fixed 3-V increments until MT criteria were met or the predefined safety ceiling of 650 V was reached.

MT was defined as the lowest stimulation intensity producing a reproducible compound muscle action potential ≥ 50 μV peak-to-peak in at least three consecutive trials recorded simultaneously from the right abductor pollicis brevis (APB) and tibialis anterior (TA). The first muscle meeting criteria was designated as the endpoint and fixed for that patient across all stimulation conditions to ensure a uniform within-patient threshold definition.

The configuration yielding the lowest MT at ISI 3 ms was designated as the patient-specific reference condition. MT was subsequently re-titrated at ISI 2 and 4 ms with all other parameters held constant to evaluate ISI-dependent modulation [18, 24].

Electromyography (EMG) recording and signal acquisition

Compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) were recorded using subdermal needle electrodes placed bilaterally in upper and lower extremity muscles. Primary recording muscles for threshold determination were the APB and TA.

Signals were amplified, band-pass filtered at 30–3000 Hz, digitized at 10 kHz, and displayed as single sweeps over a 100-ms analysis window without averaging using the Cadwell Cascade IOMAX system. Filter settings were within recommended ranges [14].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Jamovi (version 2.6.44). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (range), and categorical variables as counts and percentages.

For phase 1, motor thresholds were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA with two within-subject factors: electrode montage (C1–C2, M3–M4, LQP) and stimulation pattern (STS vs. DTS). Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction applied when violated. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared (η2p).

Directed post hoc comparisons were performed using one-sample t-tests on predefined within-subject difference variables (deltas), equivalent to paired comparisons between configuration pairs and tested against a null hypothesis of zero. Normality of difference scores was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Holm correction controlled the family-wise error rate across seven directed comparisons.

For phase 2, repeated-measures ANOVA evaluated the effect of ISI (2, 3, 4 ms) on MT in patients without a floor effect at the 3-ms reference ISI (LQP-DTS MT > 50 V). Post hoc comparisons were performed using one-sample t-tests on within-subject difference variables with Holm correction across three comparisons.

Determinants of baseline excitability were evaluated using multivariable linear regression with LQP-DTS MT as the dependent variable and age, preoperative neurological deficit, and endpoint muscle as predictors. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and residual normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

ISI-4 responsiveness (MT_ISI4 < MT_ISI3) was modeled using multivariable logistic regression including age and baseline LQP-DTS threshold as predictors. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and model fit using McFadden’s pseudo-R2 and the likelihood ratio test.

Pulse escalation (> 5 pulses required to reach MT under LQP-DTS) was analyzed using multivariable logistic regression including baseline threshold, age, and preoperative deficit as predictors. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Study population and descriptive motor thresholds

Eighty-one pediatric patients were included in the full cohort analysis. MT values across stimulation conditions are provided in Online Resource 2. LQP-DTS consistently yielded the lowest mean threshold (98.6 V), whereas C1–C2 STS produced the highest (194 V). No missing data were present for any MT condition.

Phase 1: effects of electrode montage and stimulation pattern

Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated large independent effects of both electrode montage and stimulation pattern on MT (Tables 1 and 2). Montage exerted a dominant effect (F[1.65, 132.37] = 5511.0, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.986), as did stimulation pattern (F[1, 80] = 2474.5, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.969). A significant montage × pattern interaction was also observed (F[1.98, 158.31] = 45.0, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.360), indicating non-uniform DTS effects across montages.

Directed within-subject contrasts (Tables 1 and 2) confirmed that DTS significantly reduced threshold relative to STS across all montages (all p < 0.001, Holm-corrected). The largest clinically relevant difference was observed between C1–C2 STS and LQP-DTS (mean difference 77.8 V; 95% CI 76.1–79.6), representing the contrast between the highest- and lowest-threshold configurations. Phase 2: effect of ISI

Sixteen patients (19.8%) demonstrated a floor-effect LQP-DTS threshold of 50 V at ISI 3 ms and were excluded from phase 2 analysis. In the remaining 65 patients, ISI significantly modulated MT (F[1.15, 73.66] = 419, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.868; Table 3). ISI of 2 ms was associated with a 17.35 V threshold increase relative to the 3 ms reference (95% CI 16.30–18.41; p < 0.001), whereas ISI of 4 ms produced a modest 3.46 V reduction relative to 3 ms (95% CI 2.19–4.73; p < 0.001). Estimated marginal means are shown in Fig. 3.

Determinants of baseline motor excitability

Multivariable linear regression (Table 4) identified age and preoperative neurological deficit as independent determinants of baseline LQP-DTS MT (R2 = 0.699, p < 0.001). Younger age was associated with higher thresholds (β = − 6.74 V/year; 95% CI − 7.85 to − 5.62; p < 0.001), and preoperative neurological deficit was associated with a 25.99 V increase in threshold (95% CI 16.41–35.57; p < 0.001). Endpoint muscle was not a significant predictor (p = 0.852). Model residuals showed mild non-normality (Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.025); regression estimates remain valid by the central limit theorem.

Predictors of ISI-4 responsiveness

In the phase 2 subset (n = 65), ISI-4 responsiveness was observed in 49/65 patients (75.4%). The multivariable model was significant (likelihood ratio χ2(2) = 29.4, p < 0.001; McFadden R2 = 0.405). Baseline LQP-DTS MT was the sole independent predictor (OR 1.09 per volt; 95% CI 1.035–1.144; p < 0.001; Tables 5 and 6), whereas age was not significant (p = 0.299). Discriminatory performance was strong (AUC = 0.917; Fig. 4).

Predictors of pulse escalation

Pulse escalation was required in 26/81 patients (32.1%). The multivariable model was significant (likelihood ratio χ2(3) = 47.1, p < 0.001; McFadden R2 = 0.463). Baseline LQP-DTS threshold was the sole independent predictor (OR 1.062 per volt; 95% CI 1.023–1.102; p = 0.002; Tables 5 and 6). Neither age nor preoperative neurological deficit reached significance. Discriminatory performance was high (AUC = 0.918).

Discussion

This prospective within-patient study identifies a reproducible motor threshold hierarchy during pediatric TES-MEP monitoring across spinal deformity correction, spinal dysraphism repair, and cervical spine surgery, with direct implications for corticospinal tract surveillance reliability. Electrode montage, stimulation pattern, and ISI exerted independent but interacting effects on motor threshold, supporting structured optimization over empirical escalation.

Electrode montage emerged as the principal determinant of threshold behavior. The marked advantage of LQP-DTS over conventional C1–C2 STS is consistent with finite element modelling demonstrating reduced scalp impedance and improved cortical current delivery with LQP configurations [6, 9]. Impedance-dependent threshold variation described by Journée et al. [9] is particularly relevant in infants and young children, in whom skull conductivity heterogeneity and open sutures amplify montage effects. The safety profile of LQP stimulation has been confirmed in large comparative series [4]. These findings favor spatial optimization before intensity escalation.

The magnitude of the montage effect warrants contextualization. The 77.8 V reduction from C1–C2 STS to LQP-DTS substantially exceeded the ISI modulation range observed between 2 and 4 ms (20.82 V), indicating that montage selection exerts a dominant influence on pediatric MT compared with millisecond-scale ISI adjustments. This suggests that LQP-DTS may partially offset developmental excitability constraints in younger patients.

The stimulation pattern effect reflects temporal summation mechanisms underlying double-train facilitation [10]. When STS produces marginal corticospinal recruitment, the additional conditioning train increases depolarization probability through cumulative activation. The observation that DTS benefit was greatest at lower baseline excitability, explaining the montage × pattern interaction, is consistent with prior double-train and multi-pulse optimization data [11, 12, 23]. In the pediatric context, maturational differences in cortical inhibitory circuitry [8] and feasibility observations in infants and young children [16] indicate that excitability dynamics differ from adults, providing a developmental basis for the facilitation effects observed here. The 10 ms ITI was selected as an early S-ITI facilitation condition based on Journée et al. [10, 11], who described spinal EPSP summation as the dominant mechanism at short intervals. Systematic comparison of ITI values in pediatric populations remains an open question.

ISI modulation exhibited a markedly asymmetric, non-linear profile. The 17.35 V threshold penalty at 2 ms relative to 3 ms contrasted with only a 3.46 V reduction at 4 ms, yielding an approximate 5:1 asymmetry across the 2–4 ms window. This pattern likely reflects incomplete recovery of fast-conducting corticospinal axons at shorter intervals under submaximal stimulation [18]. Although 4 ms produced the lowest thresholds, the modest improvement over 3 ms indicates diminishing returns. Consistent with prior pediatric intraoperative data showing 3 ms as the most frequently optimal interval [1], ISI of 3 ms represents a practical default in pediatric LQP-DTS monitoring, with selective extension to 4 ms in patients with elevated baseline thresholds.

Baseline LQP-DTS threshold independently predicted both ISI-4 responsiveness and pulse escalation requirements, indicating that initial excitability state conditions subsequent modulation behavior. Developmental trajectories of corticospinal myelination [2, 15] and feasibility data in very young children [5, 17] are consistent with systematic maturational variation in excitability characteristics. Baseline threshold thus integrates variance from age and preoperative motor status.

From a surgical neuromonitoring perspective, these findings support a threshold-guided optimization sequence implemented at monitoring onset to improve signal acquisition stability during pediatric spinal procedures. LQP-DTS at ISI 3 ms represents the optimal starting configuration, combining the lowest observed thresholds with near-complete corticospinal recovery. Patients with elevated baseline thresholds, typically younger children or those with preoperative motor deficit, form a prospectively identifiable subgroup in whom ISI extension to 4 ms and pulse escalation can be anticipated from the initial threshold measurement, with high discriminatory accuracy (AUC 0.917–0.918). This supports proactive stimulation management in pediatric spinal surgery.

Limitations

This single-center study used a fixed constant-voltage platform (Cadwell Cascade IOMAX, 75 μs pulse width), which may limit generalizability to constant-current systems or alternative pulse durations. The sequential, non-randomized configuration order cannot fully exclude residual order effects; however, LQP-DTS produced the lowest threshold in every patient, making a pure order artifact unlikely.

Phase 2 ISI analysis was restricted to the LQP-DTS configuration. Sixteen patients (19.8%) demonstrated a floor-effect threshold (MT = 50 V at initial stimulation) and were excluded from ISI analysis, limiting evaluation of modulation effects. Voltage does not directly correspond to cortical current density, and integration of current-controlled systems or computational modelling approaches [6] may further refine excitability quantification. Voltage thresholds are influenced by circuit impedance and do not directly reflect delivered current; power-based metrics may better characterize stimulation intensity across configurations in future studies. The ITI was fixed at 10 ms and not systematically varied; whether alternative values produce lower pediatric thresholds remains unknown and warrants future investigation. Threshold behavior was characterized at baseline only; intraoperative drift and adverse event rates across configurations were not systematically assessed.

Predictive models were internally derived and lack external validation; prospective validation in independent cohorts is required. The pulse escalation model included 26 events, yielding an event-per-variable ratio slightly below the traditional threshold of 10, which may affect coefficient stability. Diagnostic categories were strongly associated with age and included small subgroups, limiting the ability to model diagnosis as an independent predictor.

Conclusion

In pediatric patients undergoing spinal surgery, LQP-DTS at ISI 3 ms consistently produced the lowest stimulation requirements and may serve as a practical default starting configuration. Baseline threshold, shaped by age and preoperative motor status, predicted ISI responsiveness and pulse escalation (AUC 0.917–0.918), enabling identification of patients requiring protocol adjustment before critical surgical steps. These findings support a structured, threshold-guided approach to TES-MEP optimization in pediatric spinal surgery.
