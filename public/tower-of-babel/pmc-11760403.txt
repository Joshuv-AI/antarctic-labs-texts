Clinical and Radiographic Evaluation between Conventional Mineral Trioxide Aggregate and Gel-based Mineral Trioxide Aggregate in Indirect Pulp Therapy: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: B K Vanishree, Supriya S Dighe, Rooposhi Saha, Yusuf Chunawala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndirect pulp treatment (IPT) is often employed in dentistry as a valuable technique for preserving dental vitality. While mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) remains a popular choice, the need for materials with shorter setting times, lower costs, and minimized discoloration concerns has led to the exploration of alternative options.AimTo evaluate and compare the radiographic and clinical outcomes of gel-based MTA Kids e-MTA (Kids-e-Dental, Mumbai, India) with MTA (ProRoot MTA, Dentsply Tulsa, Johnson City, TN, USA).Materials and methodsA randomized clinical trial was conducted in which participants aged 6–12 years were selected according to inclusion criteria. A sample size of 32 primary molars and young permanent molars with deep caries were treated by IPT with 2 mm ProRoot MTA and e-MTA. The groups were divided into two: group I (ProRoot MTA) and group II (e-MTA), with 16 samples in each group. Clinical and radiographic success, in addition to newly formed dentin thickness, were evaluated regularly at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months.ResultsBoth groups showed a clinical and radiographic success rate of 100% throughout all follow-up intervals. Analysis of the mean thickness of newly formed dentin at various follow-up intervals revealed no statistically significant difference between the two groups at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, with p-values of 0.32, 0.33, 0.33, 0.33, and 0.33, respectively.How to cite this articleK VB, Dighe SS, Saha R, et al. Introduction

In pediatric dentistry, treatment of primary and young permanent teeth is done by pulp therapy followed by biocompatible medications beneficial to the pulpal tissue.1 The discourse on how extensively to excavate caries to halt the carious progression in a deep carious lesion is not a recent development. According to John Tomes, “it is always better if a layer of discolored dentine is left for the protection of the pulp than to risk the tooth itself.”2

Vital pulp therapy includes pulpotomy and pulp capping procedures (which depend on the extent of pulpal involvement). These procedures help preserve the vitality of a decayed tooth and allow it to remain in the arch for a longer period of time. Modern endodontics provides a range of pulp capping agents characterized by distinct properties, benefits, and limitations.3

Many materials such as glutaraldehyde, formocresol, ferric sulfate, mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), calcium hydroxide, etc., have been suggested. However, the use of calcium hydroxide pulp capping material in the past has demonstrated unpredictable outcomes. Literature shows evidence of gradual dissolution over time, resulting in a low sealing ability. Additionally, its promotion of dentin bridge formation with multiple tunnel defects has led to limited acceptance.4

Mineral trioxide aggregate has been marketed as gray- and white-colored. Conventional MTA is composed of 20% bismuth oxide, 5% gypsum, and 75% Portland cement by weight. MTA forms a colloidal gel after mixing with powder and liquid, within 4 hours, that solidifies into a hard cement.5

In the present study, vital pulp therapy was conducted using MTA-based liquid and MTA-based gel. Since its introduction in dentistry, MTA has tested as a one-of-a-kind material with widespread use in both primary and permanent dentitions, owing to its remarkable qualities. It is biocompatible, sets in the presence of body fluids, and induces repair and regeneration while providing a robust barrier.

However, some drawbacks of conventional MTA persist, including difficulties in mixing and handling, a prolonged setting time, and the high cost of the material. Despite MTA's excellent biocompatibility, antibacterial and antifungal efficacy, which position it as a material of high standards, it comes with some drawbacks, like delayed setting time, poor handling characteristics, and expensive material.6

To achieve better handling properties and significantly reduce the setting time, MTA has been mixed with various resins or gels to obtain a cohesive mass. When MTA is combined with a water-based gel for the same procedures, it exhibits reduced tackiness and a considerably shorter setting time, from 165 to 55 minutes.

A recent introduction in endodontic materials is Kids e-MTA bioactive cement, available as powder and liquid with mineral oxides of fine hydrophilic grains. Calcium chloride in a saturated suspension of polycarboxylate is the liquid component, and the setting time is 12 minutes.6

As per the manufacturer, e-MTA material has a fast setting time, good handling properties, good washout resistance, and high compressive strength. However, currently, there are no literature or clinical studies that validate these characteristics.6 The purpose of our study was to compare clinical and radiographic success between conventional MTA and gel-based MTA for young permanent and primary deep caries molars vital pulp therapy in 6- to 12-year-old children, with a follow-up period of 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted at M A Rangoonwala College of Dental Sciences and Research Centre, Pune, in the Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry department, which was approved by the Ethical Committee and Review Board, letter number–MCES\EC\839\2023.

The study was a simple randomized clinical trial. A total of 32 teeth from children aged 6–12 years with deep dental caries in molars were selected randomly. They were then divided into two groups: group I—ProRoot MTA and group II—e-MTA, with 16 samples in each group, which satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The survey design is given in Figure 1. Before the study, clinical and radiographic evaluations of the selected teeth were done with a brief history. Informed consent was obtained and the study was explained to the children and their parents or guardians. On a regular basis at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, the freshly established dentin thickness was evaluated clinically and radiographically.

Sample Size Calculation

A total of 32 teeth that fulfilled the inclusion criteria were selected for vital pulp therapy. To determine the success rate of the power analysis, the G*Power program (version 3.1.9.2 for Windows) was used.

Clinical Procedures

A standardized procedure, conducted by a single operator on the selected teeth in accordance with the revised the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) guidelines (2014), was implemented. Mechanical cleaning and disinfection of the teeth were performed before cavity preparation. Local anesthesia was administered, followed by rubber dam isolation. A No. 330 high-speed bur with water spray was used to remove overhanging enamel and dental caries. Subsequently, a No. 4 or 8 round slow-speed bur and spoon excavators were used to excavate soft caries near the pulp. The soft infected dentin layer was removed based on touch perception, visual criteria, and hardness. Hard affected dentin was left over the pulpal floor. When increased resistance of dentin was felt, the excavation was stopped, thereby preventing exposure of the pulp tissue. Cavity irrigation was done with 1% NaOCl solution, which was used to wash away dentinal debris over a 3-minute period. In cases where accidental exposure of the pulp occurred or profuse bleeding was observed, those specific teeth were excluded from the study. ProRoot MTA and Kids e-MTA (Figs 2AA and B) were used as per the manufacturer's instructions, and final rebuilding was done with resin-modified glass ionomer cement. The rubber dam was removed once the restoration was completed, followed by an occlusion check using articulating paper, and immediate postoperative intraoral periapical radiographs were taken.

Follow-up and Evaluation

At 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, patients were called for clinical and radiographic evaluation. Throughout the study period, assessment of pain and postoperative sensitivity was checked. To assess pulp health at every follow-up period, electric and thermal pulp sensitivity testing was performed. Postoperative radiographs were taken to detect dentin bridge formation. Absence of pain on percussion, postoperative pain, fistula, sinus, pathological mobility, and swelling were considered clinical success. No internal or external resorption, newly formed dentin, lack of integrity of the lamina dura and PDL widening, absence of interfurcal radiolucency or periapical changes, and no bone resorption were considered radiographic success.

Using Digora software (Digora for Windows 2.5), freshly established dentin thickness was calculated, which involves two irrelevant lines passing through constant points A and B. The deepest point of restoration is Point A, and the deepest point in dentin is Point B. A third line connecting these points was measured in millimeters, representing the remaining dentin thickness. Instant posttreatment and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, radiographic measurements were taken (Figs 3A to C). According to Sharma et al.,7 newly formed dentin thickness was determined by subtracting the instant remaining dentin thickness measurements from all investigated periods.

Statistical Analysis

Using Statistical Product and Service Solution (SPSS) version 25 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), statistical analysis was done. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for data normality. All the data were found to be parametric; therefore, all tests used were parametric tests. The p-value was set at p ≤ 0.05, and a 95% confidence interval was established. Quantitative data are presented as mean and standard deviation, respectively. An independent t-test was used for intergroup comparison between Pro-MTA and e-MTA. An ANOVA test was used for intragroup comparison between Pro-MTA and e-MTA at baseline, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months.

Results

Children aged 6–12 years old, a total of 32 (100%) teeth were included in this study, of which 17 (53.1%) second primary teeth were treated with ProRoot MTA and 15 (46.87%) first permanent teeth were treated with e-MTA.

Statistically, no significant difference was observed amongst material distribution in primary and permanent teeth (p = 0.654) (Table 1). The clinical outcome of both groups was 100% (Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference in the intragroup comparison of Pro-MTA and e-MTA (p = 0.99) (Table 3). The intergroup comparison of Pro-MTA and e-MTA showed no statistically significant difference (p = 0.31) in both groups at different time intervals (Table 4).

No unfavorable radiographic findings were observed in either group, and all investigations were considered as 100% radiographic success. Immediately after indirect pulp treatment (IPT), the mean remaining dentin thickness for group I was 0.096 (±0.1) mm and for group II was 0.092 (±0.1) mm, respectively. There was no statistically significant difference between either group, with a p-value = 0.31, and no statistically significant difference between either group at different time intervals, with p-values of 0.32, 0.33, 0.33, 0.33, and 0.33, respectively.

Discussion

According to AAPD, IPT is a procedure for preserving innermost caries close to the pulp to avoid pulp exposure. The caries-affected dentin is covered with a radiopaque liner such as resin-modified glass ionomer, dentin bonding agent, MTA, or calcium hydroxide to create a biological seal.8

Indirect pulp capping is considered a conservative approach to treating the dentin-pulp complex, particularly in primary dentition due to the life process of primary teeth in the oral cavity.9

Mineral trioxide aggregate has been reported as an effective pulp-capping substance due to its small particle size, providing a tight barrier against microorganism migration, and its high pH level, resulting in minimal inflammatory reactions during healing. It can stimulate cell differentiation and activation, contributing to hard tissue matrix formation/mineralization. In comparison with the gold standard material, calcium hydroxide, MTA exhibits superior clinical performance, including less pulpal inflammation and better dentin bridge formation.10,11

Adequate marginal seal and proper case selection result in effective and successful IPC treatment in primary molars, as per Rosenberg et al.12

Age-group of 6–12 years children, ProRoot MTA and e-MTA materials were selected in our study. This age range was chosen due to the presence of odontoblasts, excellent vascular supply, undifferentiated mesenchymal cells liable for dentinogenetic function, and which encourage tertiary dentin formation in deep carious lesions in young patients. The study opted for a single-visit IPT approach for both groups, aligning with previous research supporting the effectiveness of single-visit IPT without the need for reentering the tooth to remove residual caries. This decision was based on findings from various studies that advocated for single-visit IPT procedures.7,13,14

Cavity was closed with glass ionomer as the final restoration after the pulp capping procedure. To promote a strong peripheral seal, demineralized dentin was restored properly to achieve a successful seal, preventing the entry of microorganisms from the oral cavity and effectively preventing microleakage. Consequently, this approach led to a reduction in bacterial count and diversity, ultimately arresting the cavity's progression. For clinical and radiographic results, evaluations were carried out at every follow-up period of 3, 6, 9, and 12 months.

In terms of the study outcomes, both groups exhibited a 100% clinical and radiographic success throughout all follow-up periods. Statistically, there were no significant differences between ProRoot MTA and e-MTA success rates in our study.

Based on the absence of clinical and radiologic signs or symptoms in primary and permanent teeth among young patients, there was a high life expectancy of IPT in Gruythuysen et al. study.15 However, there was statistically no significant difference among groups at different time intervals (3, 6, 9, and 12 months) with p-values of 0.32, 0.33, 0.33, 0.33, and 0.33 in terms of mean thickness of freshly established dentin.

Our study findings align with the study conducted by Benoist et al., which showed a statistically significant success rate after 3 months in the MTA group relative to the Dycal group.13 However, no statistically significant difference was found in the dentin thickness among groups after 6 months. Whereas, in both groups, there was an increase in mean leftover dentin thickness at 3 and 6 months. Similarly, by de Assunção Pinheiro et al.,16 after 12 months, there was no significant difference in leftover dentin thickness when compared with the baseline.

Kids-e-MTA showed superior properties compared to ProRoot MTA, including quick setting time, good handling characteristics, good washout resistance, and high compressive strength. The variations in results were attributed to factors such as patient and tooth numbers, age range, restoration methods, follow-up period, and radiographic evaluation methods.

This discrepancy in results may be attributed to challenges associated with lesion depth measurement. Clinical carious lesions may extend deeper than their presumed radiographic appearance, posing difficulty in clinically and radiographically determining the thickness of remaining dentin. The potential constraints of this study encompass a limited sample size and a shortage of clinical and radiographic investigations on e-MTA.

Conclusion

Both pro-MTA and e-MTA were comparable in success for IPT of primary and young permanent molars, both clinically and radiographically. Even though pro-MTA and e-MTA have the same basic powder component and only differ in their liquid component, suggesting they are very similar, it's worth mentioning that Kids-e-MTA seemed a bit better, especially in terms of ease of use.
