Ultrasonic‐Assisted Deep Eutectic Solvent Extraction, Characterization, and Bioactivities of Arctium lappa L. Polysaccharides
Authors: Hongxin Zhou, Zhikai Xiao, YingTing Zhou, Haiyun Chen, Hui Fan, Zaijun Zhang, Yao Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough Arctium lappa L. polysaccharides (ALPs) possess recognized pharmacological potential, their green extraction using deep eutectic solvents (DESs) combined with ultrasound has not been previously explored. This study reports, for the first time, an optimized DES‐based ultrasound‐assisted extraction (DES‐UAE) protocol for ALPs. Response surface optimization identified the following conditions: choline chloride/oxalic acid molar ratio of 2:1, water content of 18.508%, liquid‐to‐solid ratio of 37.425 mL/g, extraction time of 97.262 min, and ultrasonic power of 275.787 W, yielding 16.581% polysaccharides. Sequential chromatographic purification afforded a homogeneous fraction, ALPs‐1, with a weight‐average molecular weight of 8827 Da. Monosaccharide analysis revealed that ALPs‐1 is composed of rhamnose, glucuronic acid, glucose, and galactose at a molar ratio of 2.18:1.00:6.62:7.17. Functionally, ALPs‐1 exhibited moderate α‐glucosidase inhibition, weak DPPH• scavenging, and notable ABTS•+ scavenging (IC50 = 0.87 mg/mL). Importantly, ALPs‐1 significantly suppressed NO, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β production in LPS‐stimulated BV2 microglia and protected zebrafish keratinocytes from metronidazole‐induced oxidative injury. These results demonstrate that ALPs‐1 possesses promising antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and anti‐neuroinflammatory activities. This work provides the first evidence for the effective application of DES‐UAE in extracting bioactive ALPs, supporting their potential use in functional foods and pharmaceuticals.Keywords: Arctium lappa polysaccharide, biological activities, deep eutectic solvent, monosaccharide composition, response surface optimization

Introduction

Arctium lappa, a traditional medicinal and edible plant, is known for its properties in dispelling wind‐heat and reducing swellings and is widely cultivated in China [1, 2]. With high biological activity and value as a health‐care vegetable, it has gained increasing popularity in the international market. The root of A. lappa is rich in inulin, a polysaccharide unique to Asteraceae family plants, which has been shown to exert hypoglycemic effects and thus has promising therapeutic potential for diabetic patients [3].

Nevertheless, traditional isolation methodologies—exemplified by hot water percolation followed by ethanol‐induced precipitation—are plagued by several inherent drawbacks, including suboptimal extraction efficiencies, considerable energy expenditure, and reliance on volatile organic solvents that pose environmental and safety concerns. Over the past decade, deep eutectic solvents (DESs) have attracted substantial interest as a class of neoteric green solvents, distinguished by their readily tailorable physicochemical profiles, favorable toxicological characteristics, inherent biodegradability, and exceptional capacity to solubilize a wide spectrum of naturally occurring constituents [4]. Typically assembled through the judicious pairing of a hydrogen bond acceptor with one or more hydrogen bond donor species, DESs are especially appealing for sustainable chemistry applications owing to their straightforward synthetic accessibility and ambient‐temperature operational capability. Although DES‐mediated extraction has been productively deployed across a diverse repertoire of bioactive phytochemicals, its translation to the recovery of polysaccharides specifically from A. lappa roots has not been documented to date. Ultrasound‐assisted extraction (UAE) exploits the phenomenon of acoustic cavitation—the rapid nucleation, growth, and violent implosion of microbubbles within a liquid medium—to enhance mass transport and boost extraction yields, and has consequently been extensively hybridized with solvent‐based extraction platforms [5]. Notwithstanding the demonstrated versatility of the integrated DES‐UAE paradigm for numerous natural product classes, its employment for the isolation of A. lappa polysaccharides (ALPs) remains conspicuously absent from the peer‐reviewed literature.

From a pharmacological standpoint, prior investigations have substantiated the free radical‐scavenging properties of ALPs through both DPPH• and ABTS•+ decolorization assays [6, 7]. By contrast, the body of evidence addressing their potential anti‐neuroinflammatory actions is comparatively sparse. A mounting corpus of experimental data indicates that polysaccharides harvested from alternative natural reservoirs are capable of tempering neuroinflammatory cascades via the downregulation of pivotal pro‐inflammatory mediators, most notably nitric oxide (NO), interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) [8]. The extent to which ALPs recapitulate such neuroimmunomodulatory effects has yet to be subjected to rigorous and systematic experimental scrutiny. Furthermore, the propensity of ALPs to antagonize α‐glucosidase—an enzymatic target of central importance in the regulation of postprandial glycemic excursions—merits more thorough investigation, particularly in light of the ongoing quest for safer and better‐tolerated alternatives to existing synthetic antidiabetic pharmacotherapies [9, 10].

Motivated by these unmet research needs, the present study was conceived with three principal objectives: first, to devise and optimize an unprecedented ultrasound‐assisted DES‐based extraction methodology tailored specifically for ALPs; second, to isolate, purify, and comprehensively characterize the dominant polysaccharide fraction (designated ALPs‐1) with respect to its molecular weight, compositional features, and homogeneity; and third, to undertake a systematic multi‐endpoint evaluation of its antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and anti‐neuroinflammatory bioactivities. The cumulative results of this investigation are intended to furnish a robust scientific underpinning for the prospective deployment of ALPs‐1 as a bioactive ingredient in functional nutritional formulations and pharmaceutical preparations.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Reagents

The dried root of A. lappa was supplied by Lanshan District Baishunkang Tea Shop (Lanshan, China). Other materials and reagents are shown in Section S1.1.

Extraction Methods

Screening of DESs

A panel comprising seven DES formulations was constructed following an adapted literature procedure [11]. Each system was obtained by mixing choline chloride (serving as the hydrogen bond acceptor) with one of seven structurally distinct hydrogen bond donors at a fixed molar ratio, and the resulting mixture was maintained at 80°C with constant agitation until a visually transparent and single‐phase liquid was produced. The candidate DESs were as follows: DES‐1 (choline chloride/oxalic acid, 1:1), DES‐2 (choline chloride/citric acid, 1:1), DES‐3 (choline chloride/malonic acid, 1:2), DES‐4 (choline chloride/urea, 1:2), DES‐5 (choline chloride/glycerol, 1:2), DES‐6 (choline chloride/1,4‐butanediol, 1:2), and DES‐7 (choline chloride/ethylene glycol, 1:2). Because neat DESs typically possess high viscosities (commonly exceeding 500 mPa·s), which can hinder convective mass transport under ultrasonication, all formulations were diluted with deionized water at 30% (v/v) to enhance their flow behavior. The incorporation of water additionally modulates solvent polarity, favoring the dissolution of hydrophilic polysaccharide molecules; however, an excessive aqueous fraction can attenuate the hydrogen‐bond network between DES constituents and the target polysaccharides, implying that an intermediate water level is most favorable. The ALPs extraction yield was employed as the response criterion, and the top‐performing DES was advanced to the subsequent optimization phase.

Determination of Polysaccharide Content

Drawing of Standard Curves

A glucose calibration curve was constructed with reference to an established protocol, incorporating minor adjustments [12]. In brief, precisely weighed anhydrous glucose (2 mg) was dissolved to afford a 0.2 mg/mL stock standard. Working solutions were prepared by transferring graded volumes of this stock (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µL) into individual microcentrifuge tubes, each being made up to 100 µL with distilled water. To each tube were successively introduced 0.1 mL of aqueous phenol (5%, w/v) and 0.5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid, and the contents were immediately homogenized by vigorous vortex mixing. The reaction mixtures were left to stand at ambient temperature for 15 min, after which the absorbance at 492 nm was recorded using a microplate spectrophotometer. The resulting calibration plot was generated by relating glucose concentration to the corresponding absorbance values.

Extraction Rate Determination

The isolation of ALPs was carried out in accordance with a published procedure, with slight adaptations [13]. In short, a 0.5 g portion of finely milled dried burdock root powder was combined with the DES–water binary mixture at the predetermined liquid‐to‐solid proportion and water percentage. The resulting suspension was processed by UAE under precisely regulated operational parameters. Upon completion of the extraction, the slurry was centrifuged, and the clarified supernatant was recovered, concentrated under diminished pressure, and treated with a measured volume of 95% ethanol to induce polysaccharide precipitation. After standing undisturbed for 12 h, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min. The collected precipitate was redissolved in 350 mL of distilled water to yield a crude polysaccharide solution. Deproteinization was accomplished by applying the Sevage ternary system (polysaccharide solution/dichloromethane/n‐butanol = 25:4:1, v/v/v), followed by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 5 min) and removal of the lower organic layer. The aqueous phase was subsequently concentrated, subjected to dialysis against flowing tap water over a 48 h period, and centrifuged once more (4000 rpm, 5 min). The ultimate supernatant was concentrated and desiccated to afford the crude ALPs product. Quantification of the polysaccharide content relied on the phenol–sulfuric acid colorimetric assay: a 100 µL aliquot of the extract was mixed with 100 µL of 5% phenol reagent and 500 µL of concentrated sulfuric acid, allowed to react for 15 min, and then measured spectrophotometrically at 492 nm. Single‐Factor Experiment

Precisely weighed portions of dried A. lappa root powder were extracted with the DES medium while individually varying the following operational variables: choline chloride‐to‐oxalic acid molar ratio (1:1.5, 1:1, 1.5:1, 2:1, 2.5:1, and 3:1); water supplementation level (0%–90%, v/v); ultrasonic bath temperature (20°C–80°C); liquid‐to‐solid ratio (10–60 mL/g); ultrasonic irradiation power (200–400 W); and extraction duration (40–120 min). Every experimental point was assayed in triplicate, and the factor level affording the maximum polysaccharide yield was identified and carried forward into subsequent optimization work.

Response Surface Methodology

Drawing upon the single‐factor experimental outcomes, a four‐variable, three‐level Box‐Behnken design (BBD) was constructed using Design‐Expert software (version 12.0, Stat‐Ease, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA), with the ALPs extraction yield serving as the response variable. The resulting experimental matrix comprised 29 randomized runs, each of which was executed to quantify the associated polysaccharide recovery. The extraction protocol was subsequently refined through statistical examination of the response surface and experimentally verified. For benchmarking purposes, the optimized DES‐UAE methodology was compared against the conventional hot water extraction and ambient‐temperature maceration approaches.

Purification

Anion‐Exchange Chromatography on DEAE‐Sepharose

Chromatographic purification was conducted in accordance with a reported procedure, with minor adjustments [14]. In short, the crude extract was first concentrated by rotary evaporation under reduced pressure, after which 200 mg of the resulting crude polysaccharide was taken up in 10 mL of distilled water and clarified by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 5 min). The supernatant was applied to a DEAE‐Sepharose Fast Flow column (2.6 cm × 40 cm; packed bed volume ≈ 150 mL) that had been pre‐equilibrated with five column volumes of distilled water. Stepwise elution was performed at a flow rate of 2 mL/min: an initial 50 fractions were collected with distilled water as the mobile phase, and a further 80 fractions were eluted using 0.8 M aqueous NaCl. The predominant polysaccharide peak (designated ALPs) emerged at an elution strength corresponding to approximately 0.3 M NaCl (fractions 60–80). Individual tubes (10 mL each) were monitored by the phenol–sulfuric acid assay at 492 nm to construct the elution profile. Fractions constituting the principal peak were combined, dialyzed against flowing tap water for 24 h through dialysis tubing (MWCO 1000 Da), concentrated, and finally lyophilized to yield the partially purified polysaccharide.

Sephadex G‐75 Gel Filtration

The enriched material was reconstituted in distilled water to a concentration of 10 mg/mL, and a 4 mL portion was introduced onto a Sephadex G‐75 size‐exclusion column (1.6 cm × 80 cm; packed bed volume approximately 160 mL). Isocratic elution was carried out with distilled water at 1.0 mL/min, yielding 50 fractions of 10 mL each [15]. Polysaccharide‐containing fractions were identified by the phenol–sulfuric acid colorimetric assay (absorbance at 492 nm). The elution curve was plotted, and the tubes constituting the single major symmetric peak were consolidated.

Determination of In Vivo Antioxidant Activity of ALPs‐1

Feeding of Zebrafish Larvae

The transgenic line Tg(krt4:NTR:GFP)cy17, in which green fluorescent protein is selectively expressed in skin keratinocytes, was employed for the in vivo oxidative stress model. Adult male and female fish were housed separately in a recirculating aquaculture system and provided twice daily with commercial flake feed supplemented with live Artemia nauplii, under a controlled 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod. To obtain embryos, reproductively mature zebrafish were placed in spawning tanks at a 2:2 sex ratio in the evening, and fertilized eggs were collected the next morning. Following disinfection and thorough rinsing, the embryos were transferred to standard embryo medium composed of NaCl (5.0 mM), KCl (0.17 mM), CaCl2 (0.4 mM), and MgSO4 (0.16 mM) and maintained at 28°C under a defined light schedule.

Antioxidant Activity of ALPs‐1 in Zebrafish

At 1 day post‐fertilization (dpf), normally developing larvae exhibiting robust green fluorescence were hand‐selected under a fluorescence stereomicroscope and briefly immersed in pronase solution (1 mg/mL) to enzymatically digest the chorion. The dechorionated embryos were then distributed into 24‐well culture plates (8–10 individuals per well) and challenged with metronidazole at 400 µg/mL to trigger oxidative injury. ALPs‐1 was co‐delivered at three graded doses: 1, 10, and 100 µg/mL. Parallel control wells included a solvent‐only group (DMSO ≤ 0.5%, v/v), a metronidazole‐alone injury model group, and a positive protection group receiving vitamin C at 35 µg/mL. All plates were incubated at a constant 28°C. Following a 24 h exposure period, the general morphological condition and keratinocyte‐associated fluorescence of each larva were examined by microscopy. Digital images were acquired for every individual fish, and the number of fluorescent foci within the trunk region was enumerated using Image Pro‐Plus software. Intergroup differences were evaluated by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with post hoc comparisons performed in SPSS.

Determination of Anti‐neuroinflammatory Activity of ALPs‐1

Cell Culture and Experimental Design

The BV2 murine microglial cell line (RRID:CVCL_0182) was acquired from the Cell Resource Center, Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). Routine culture was maintained in DMEM/F‐12 medium enriched with 10% (v/v) heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin antibiotic mixture, with incubation at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. For experimentation, cells were plated into 96‐well plates at 5 × 104 cells/mL (100 µL/well) and allowed to attach over 24 h. Six treatment arms (each in triplicate) were configured: (1) untreated control (culture medium only); (2) LPS‐stimulated group (1 µg/mL LPS); (3) positive control (5 µM minocycline co‐administered with LPS); and (4–6) ALPs‐1 intervention groups receiving LPS plus ALPs‐1 at 3, 6, or 12 µg/mL. All treatments were delivered in a total volume of 100 µL/well for a 24 h incubation period.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was determined using the CCK‐8 assay kit. At the end of the designated treatment period (24 h), the conditioned medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh culture medium containing 10% (v/v) CCK‐8 reagent. The plate was incubated for an additional 30 min at 37°C, after which the absorbance of each well was recorded at 450 nm on a Thermo Scientific microplate spectrophotometer [20].

Measurement of NO, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α Levels

The accumulation of nitrite in the culture supernatant, serving as a surrogate indicator of NO generation, was quantified by the Griess colorimetric reaction. Briefly, 50 µL of cell‐free supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of Griess reagent, and the resulting chromogenic product was measured spectrophotometrically at 540 nm. Nitrite concentrations were interpolated from a concurrently prepared sodium nitrite calibration curve [21]. Levels of the pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐1β were measured in parallel from 50 µL aliquots of the same supernatants using commercial sandwich ELISA kits, strictly adhering to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm.

Homogeneity and Molecular Weight (M w) Determination

The homogeneity and Mw of ALPs‐1 were assessed by high‐performance gel permeation chromatography (HPGPC). The detailed chromatographic conditions are provided in Section S1.3.

Analysis of Monosaccharide Components

Analytical Conditions

HPLC separation was performed on an Agilent 1260 liquid chromatograph fitted with a Kromasil 100‐5‐C18 reversed‐phase analytical column (250 mm × 4.6 mm i.d., 5 µm particle size), thermostatted at 25°C. The isocratic mobile phase was a binary mixture of 0.05 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.7) and acetonitrile in an 85:15 (v/v) ratio, pumped at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The effluent was monitored by UV absorbance at 250 nm, and the injection volume was set at 10 µL.

Results and Discussion

Standard Curve

A calibration plot was constructed by relating the measured absorbance (A) to the known glucose concentration (C). The resulting linear regression yielded the equation A = 4.9314C−0.0091, with a coefficient of determination R 2 = 0.9986, confirming satisfactory linearity over the analytical range employed.

DES Screening Results

The first phase of method development entailed evaluating seven DES formulations, all employing choline chloride as the shared hydrogen bond acceptor paired with structurally diverse hydrogen bond donors at their respective fixed molar ratios. Water was added to each system at 30% (v/v) to enhance flow characteristics and lower bulk viscosity. The ALPs extraction yields obtained under a uniform set of sonication parameters (liquid‐to‐solid ratio 20 mL/g, power 240 W, duration 30 min, temperature 40°C) are compared in Figure 1. Among the candidates, DES‐1 (choline chloride/oxalic acid, 1:1) gave the highest recovery at 15.64%, followed in descending order by DES‐2 (citric acid), DES‐3 (malonic acid), DES‐6 (1,4‐butanediol), DES‐4 (urea), DES‐5 (glycerol), and DES‐7 (ethylene glycol). The marked variation in performance across the seven solvents reflects their divergent physicochemical profiles—specifically differences in polarity, solvating power, dynamic viscosity, and molecular diffusivity—all of which collectively dictate their effectiveness for recovering naturally derived constituents [24].

The pronounced extraction advantage of DES‐1 can be understood on the basis of three complementary considerations. Firstly, oxalic acid, by virtue of being a dicarboxylic acid, possesses both a high hydrogen bond donor strength and relatively strong acidity (pKa1 = 1.25). These attributes facilitate the disruption of the hydrogen bond network that cements the plant cell wall architecture and simultaneously promote polysaccharide dissolution through strong carboxyl–hydroxyl hydrogen bonding with the carbohydrate chains. Second, the equimolar choline chloride/oxalic acid composition, upon dilution with 30% water, yields a fluid of comparatively low dynamic viscosity (ca. 50 mPa·s at 40°C), a property conducive to efficient convective mass transport under ultrasonic irradiation. Third, the free carboxyl groups may additionally mediate mild acid‐catalyzed cleavage of structural cell wall polysaccharides (notably cellulose and hemicellulose), thereby liberating a greater quantity of the target ALPs into the liquid phase. Taken together, these synergistic features render oxalic acid an exceptionally suitable hydrogen bond donor for polysaccharide recovery.

Statistical analysis (one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc comparison) revealed no significant difference among the yields afforded by DES‐2 (citric acid, 9.54%), DES‐3 (malonic acid, 9.21%), and DES‐6 (1,4‐butanediol, 8.97%) at the p > 0.05 level. This convergence can be rationalized by the observation that, at 30% water supplementation and 40°C, these three formulations exhibit broadly similar viscosity and polarity values. The two dicarboxylic acid‐based solvents (citric and malonic acid) furnish comparable hydrogen bonding microenvironments, whereas 1,4‐butanediol—although a terminal diol rather than a dicarboxylic acid—nevertheless achieves an equivalent ALPs solubilization capability under the prevailing conditions, likely owing to the formation of an extended hydrogen bond network with the choline chloride component. Furthermore, the incorporation of 30% water universally depresses the viscosity of neat DESs from > 500 mPa·s to below 100 mPa·s, thereby attenuating any viscosity‐driven performance disparities and causing the extraction efficiencies of these three systems to converge within a narrow range.

The urea‐based formulation DES‐4 presumably underperformed because of its intrinsically weak acidity, which is insufficient to effectively dissociate the polysaccharide–cell wall matrix interactions. DES‐5 (glycerol) and DES‐7 (ethylene glycol), both belonging to the polyol class, are prone to forming extensive intramolecular hydrogen bonds; this internal hydrogen bonding likely reduces the population of free hydroxyl groups available for intermolecular association with polysaccharide solutes, ultimately compromising extraction yields. On the basis of these findings, DES‐1 was adopted for all further experiments.

Single‐Factor Experimental Results

Effect of Molar Ratio

The relative proportion of the hydrogen bond acceptor to donor in a DES governs several physicochemical characteristics—including fluidity, viscosity, density, and polysaccharide solubility—that directly impact extraction performance. Using the DES‐1 system, the molar ratio was varied from 1:1.5 to 3:1 while the remaining extraction parameters were held at their constant baseline values (liquid‐to‐solid ratio 20 mL/g, power 240 W, time 30 min, temperature 40°C). Figure 2a reveals that the yield of ALPs attained a maximum at a choline chloride‐to‐oxalic acid ratio of 2:1; elevating the proportion of choline chloride beyond this point led to a progressive reduction in recovery. The peak performance at the 2:1 composition is consistent with the DES reaching its eutectic point, characterized by a minimized melting temperature, enhanced fluidity [25], and optimal viscosity and surface tension, all of which cooperatively improve solvation and mass transport phenomena [26]. Accordingly, the 2:1 molar ratio was adopted for the subsequent stages of optimization.

Effect of Water Content

It is standard practice to introduce a controlled amount of water into DES‐based extraction media to promote the solubilization of target natural products. The water fraction modulates several solvent properties, most notably polarity, bulk viscosity, and flow behavior. In the present work, the influence of water content was examined at six levels (0%, 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90%, v/v), with all other variables fixed (liquid‐to‐solid ratio 20 mL/g, power 240 W, time 30 min, temperature 40°C). As shown in Figure 2b, the polysaccharide recovery peaked at 10% water, after which incremental water addition led to a steady decline in extraction efficiency. This biphasic trend reflects a balance between two competing effects: moderate water supplementation facilitates mass transport during sonication [27], whereas an overabundance of water raises the global solvent polarity to a level that progressively attenuates the specific hydrogen‐bond interactions between DES constituents and polysaccharide molecules, thereby suppressing ALPs solubility. Consequently, 10% (v/v) water was selected as the optimum for subsequent experiments.

Effect of Ultrasonic Power

Ultrasonic power exerts a decisive influence on extraction efficiency by amplifying the acoustic cavitation phenomenon, which in turn enhances the release and permeation of polysaccharides from the plant tissue matrix. The power input was surveyed across the range 160–400 W under otherwise invariant conditions. As illustrated in Figure 2c, the extraction yield ascended with increasing ultrasonic power to a maximum at 240 W, after which a downturn became evident. The ascending limb of the curve (160–240 W) is attributable to intensified cavitation, which generates a greater density of cavitation bubbles and associated shear forces that promote solvent infiltration into the botanical matrix and facilitate cell wall rupture, thus improving the dispersion of the root powder and accelerating the dissolution kinetics of the polysaccharides [28]. At power settings above this optimum, however, the excessive generation of cavitation bubbles may attenuate the effective transmission of acoustic energy into the cellular structures and potentially induce deleterious modifications to the physicochemical integrity of the polysaccharide chains [29]. Therefore, 240 W was fixed as the optimal ultrasonic power.

Effect of Liquid‐to‐Solid Ratio

The liquid‐to‐solid ratio is a key determinant of extraction yield, as it establishes the magnitude of the concentration gradient that drives mass transfer from the solid matrix into the bulk solvent. As depicted in Figure 2d, the ALPs extraction rate reached its maximum at 40 mL/g, beyond which a gradual decline was observed. The initial increase in yield with larger solvent volumes is consistent with the principle that a steeper concentration gradient accelerates solute diffusion and thereby improves extraction efficiency up to a certain threshold [30]. Past this optimal point, however, surplus solvent may promote the co‐dissolution of extraneous soluble constituents that compete with or otherwise hinder polysaccharide dissolution, ultimately compromising the net recovery. Hence, a liquid‐to‐solid ratio of 40 mL/g was selected as optimal.

Effect of Extraction Time

The duration of the extraction process is a critical parameter that dictates the completeness of polysaccharide liberation from the plant material. To identify the optimal exposure period, extraction was conducted for 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 min with all other parameters held constant. Figure 2e demonstrates that the polysaccharide yield rose progressively with increasing extraction time, peaking at 100 min, after which prolongation of the process led to a measurable decrease in recovery. The positive correlation observed over the 40–100 min interval can be explained by the progressively greater cumulative contact between the solvent and the root powder [31], which, under sustained ultrasonic irradiation, results in more extensive cell wall disruption and consequently enhanced polysaccharide diffusion and dissolution. Nevertheless, excessively protracted sonication may inflict structural deterioration upon the polysaccharide molecules through prolonged exposure to cavitational shear forces and localized heating, manifesting as a net reduction in measured yield [32]. On this basis, 100 min was chosen as the optimal extraction duration.

Confirmation of the Optimized Extraction Protocol

The numerical optimization routine identified the following theoretical optimum for maximizing the ALPs recovery: a liquid‐to‐solid ratio of 1:37.426 g/mL, a DES water fraction of 18.516%, an extraction period of 97.278 min, and an ultrasonic power setting of 275.867 W, under which the model forecast a polysaccharide yield of 16.581%. For ease of experimental implementation, these values were rounded to more practical levels, namely a liquid‐to‐solid ratio of 1:40 g/mL, 20% water content, 97 min of sonication, and 280 W ultrasonic power. Triplicate verification runs conducted at the adjusted conditions afforded an average polysaccharide yield of 16.281%, which is in close concordance with the model‐predicted value. The minimal discrepancy between the projected and experimentally realized yields attests to the accuracy, robustness, and reproducibility of the derived regression model, and confirms the practical viability of the optimized DES‐UAE procedure.

Comparison With Other Extraction Methods

To benchmark the optimized DES‐UAE protocol, ALPs was also isolated by two reference methods—ambient‐temperature water maceration and ultrasound‐assisted water extraction—each operated under their respective optimized parameter sets. The yields of ALPs obtained by these methods were 7.81% and 9.39%, respectively. In contrast, the DES‐based extraction method achieved a significantly higher yield of 16.581%, demonstrating its clear superiority over both conventional and ultrasonic water extraction techniques. These results confirm the effectiveness of the optimized DES process in enhancing polysaccharide extraction.

Consistent with previous studies, DES proves to be not only an efficient and practical solvent for the extraction of ALPs, but also an environmentally benign alternative. The marked improvement in extraction efficiency highlights the potential of DES‐based methods for broad application in polysaccharide extraction. Furthermore, this study provides a solid experimental foundation and valuable insights for future research on the optimization of extraction techniques for bioactive compounds.

Chromatographic Purification of ALPs

The crude polysaccharide isolate was first fractionated by anion‐exchange chromatography on a DEAE‐Sepharose Fast Flow column employing a stepwise NaCl gradient (0–0.8 M). The elution profile, displayed in Figure 4a, featured a dominant peak emerging at a salt concentration of approximately 0.3 M NaCl (spanning fractions 60–80), which was designated ALPs and classified as an acidic polysaccharide fraction on the basis of its retention behavior. Two ancillary peaks of considerably lower intensity were also discernible: an early‐eluting peak appearing in fractions 15–25 under distilled water elution, tentatively assigned to neutral polysaccharides that pass through the column unretained, and a late‐eluting peak at roughly 0.8 M NaCl (fractions 95–110), likely representing highly charged acidic species or strongly adsorbed anionic impurities. Given their modest yields, these minor fractions were not pursued for further characterization. The pooled ALPs fraction was subsequently subjected to size‐exclusion chromatography on a Sephadex G‐75 column. A single, well‐defined symmetrical peak was obtained (Figure 4b), and the corresponding fractions were consolidated to afford the purified polysaccharide, termed ALPs‐1.

Purity and Compositional Characterization of ALPs‐1

The purity of the ALPs‐1 preparation was rigorously validated through a panel of complementary analytical approaches. Total carbohydrate content was quantified by the phenol‐sulfuric acid colorimetric assay referenced against D‐glucose. The mean value derived from three independent determinations was 94.6% ± 1.2% (w/w), establishing that polysaccharide constitutes the overwhelming majority of the sample mass.

Residual protein was assessed by the Bradford dye‐binding method with bovine serum albumin serving as the calibration standard. Following the Sevag deproteinization step, the remaining protein level was determined to be 1.2% ± 0.3% (w/w), confirming the near‐quantitative removal of proteinaceous contaminants from the crude extract.

The uronic acid fraction was measured by the m‐hydroxydiphenyl colorimetric procedure employing D‐glucuronic acid as the reference. The result, 5.1% ± 0.4% (w/w), is in reasonable agreement with the glucuronic acid molar proportion established by HPLC analysis, where GlcA accounted for 1.00 of the 16.97 total monosaccharide units, corresponding to approximately 5.9% of the total carbohydrate mass (assuming an average monosaccharide residue mass of 180 Da).

HPGPC examination of ALPs‐1 produced a single, sharply defined symmetric peak with a polydispersity index (PDI = M w/M n) of 1.12, indicative of a narrow molecular weight distribution and a high degree of compositional uniformity.

Summation of the principal quantified constituents—total carbohydrate (94.6%), protein (1.2%), and uronic acid (5.1%)—yielded a combined total of 100.9%. The marginal overshoot of 0.9% falls within the cumulative experimental uncertainty inherent in the three independent colorimetric methods employed. UV–vis spectroscopic scanning revealed no discernible absorption bands at 260 or 280 nm beyond the minor contribution attributable to the residual protein, effectively ruling out significant contamination by nucleic acids or phenolic substances.

Taken together, these orthogonal lines of evidence establish that ALPs‐1 is a highly refined polysaccharide fraction characterized by a carbohydrate content surpassing 94%, negligible protein carryover, a well‐defined uronic acid constituency, and a homogeneous molecular weight profile. This degree of purity renders ALPs‐1 suitable for subsequent structure–activity relationship investigations.

Analysis of Bioactivity Results

α‐Glucosidase Inhibition Assay

The capacity of ALPs‐1 to suppress α‐glucosidase activity was assessed across a range of concentrations, with acarbose employed as the positive reference inhibitor. As illustrated in Figure 5a, ALPs‐1 inhibited the enzyme in a clear dose‐dependent fashion. The half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of ALPs‐1 was calculated to be 2.805 mg/mL, whereas acarbose yielded an IC50 value of 0.099 mg/mL under identical assay conditions, corresponding to an approximately 28‐fold disparity in potency. At the highest dose evaluated (6.4 mg/mL), ALPs‐1 attained roughly 68% inhibition, while acarbose achieved 95% suppression at a concentration of just 0.2 mg/mL. These data demonstrate that ALPs‐1 possesses a moderate but tangible α‐glucosidase inhibitory effect, albeit substantially less potent than the clinical standard. It may be conjectured that the abundant hydroxyl and carboxyl functionalities decorating the polysaccharide backbone engage in hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with amino acid residues lining the catalytic pocket of the enzyme, thereby partially obstructing substrate access. However, this proposed mode of action remains speculative in the absence of direct structural evidence from techniques such as x‐ray crystallography or molecular docking, and further mechanistic studies are warranted.

Biological evaluation of ALPs‐1. (a) Dose‐response curves for α‐glucosidase inhibition; (b) α‐amylase inhibition; (c) DPPH• radical scavenging; (d) ABTS•+ cation radical scavenging. Data are expressed as mean ± SD of triplicate determinations.

α‐Amylase Inhibition Assay

The effect of ALPs‐1 on α‐amylase was likewise evaluated over a graded concentration series (Figure 5b). A concentration‐dependent attenuation of enzymatic activity was observed, with an extrapolated IC50 of 6.637 mg/mL. Relative to acarbose (IC50 = 0.127 mg/mL), the inhibitory potency of ALPs‐1 was weaker by a factor of approximately 52. Even at the uppermost concentration examined (6.4 mg/mL), ALPs‐1 suppressed enzyme activity by only ca. 45%, in marked contrast to acarbose, which effected near‐complete inactivation (> 90%) at 0.5 mg/mL. These findings indicate that ALPs‐1 acts as a comparatively mild α‐amylase inhibitor. A tentative mechanistic explanation invoking hydrogen bond formation between the polysaccharide hydroxyl groups and polar residues within the enzyme active site has been advanced in the literature [33]; however, without corroboration from direct binding assays or computational docking analyses, this interpretation must be regarded as provisional.

Collectively, the α‐glucosidase and α‐amylase inhibition profiles point toward the potential utility of ALPs as an adjunctive hypoglycemic agent that may offer a complementary mechanism to existing antidiabetic pharmacotherapies. It should be acknowledged that the moderate bioactivities documented herein may partly reflect the purity characteristics of the ALPs preparation. Future investigations directed at further purification and at comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assessment of the hypoglycemic mechanisms are necessary to substantiate these preliminary observations.

DPPH• Radical Scavenging Activity

The free radical‐quenching capacity of ALPs‐1 was evaluated using the stable DPPH• (1,1‐diphenyl‐2‐picrylhydrazyl) chromogenic assay, with vitamin C serving as the benchmark antioxidant (Figure 5c). Both ALPs‐1 and the reference compound elicited a concentration‐proportional increase in radical scavenging. The IC50 derived for ALPs‐1 was 84.192 mg/mL, exceeding that of vitamin C (0.052 mg/mL) by more than three orders of magnitude. At the maximum concentration included in the test series (3.2 mg/mL), ALPs‐1 quenched only approximately 15% of the DPPH• present, whereas vitamin C attained 95% scavenging at a concentration as low as 0.1 mg/mL. This pronounced differential indicates that ALPs‐1 possesses only marginal intrinsic DPPH• scavenging ability relative to a potent low‐molecular‐weight antioxidant. The weak performance may be attributable to a paucity of electron‐donating structural motifs—such as phenolic hydroxyls or reducing sugar termini—that are recognized to be largely responsible for radical neutralization. A more definitive structure–activity correlation, however, would require detailed characterization of the polysaccharide's fine chemical architecture.

ABTS•+ Cation Radical Scavenging Activity

The ABTS•+ decolorization assay was employed as a second, mechanistically distinct measure of antioxidant potential (Figure 5d). Both ALPs‐1 and vitamin C exhibited dose‐dependent ABTS•+ scavenging behavior. The IC50 of ALPs‐1 was determined to be 0.87 mg/mL, which is approximately 870‐fold greater than that recorded for vitamin C (IC50 = 0.001 mg/mL). At a test concentration of 1.0 mg/mL, ALPs‐1 effected roughly 55% reduction of the ABTS•+ chromophore, whereas vitamin C surpassed 95% scavenging at merely 0.005 mg/mL. Notably, a comparison of the two radical assay outcomes reveals that ALPs‐1 performed considerably better in the ABTS•+ system (IC50 = 0.87 mg/mL) than against DPPH• (IC50 = 84.192 mg/mL), with an approximately 97‐fold difference in potency. This disparity is mechanistically informative: the ABTS•+ radical is soluble in both aqueous and organic media and can therefore be accessed by hydrophilic as well as lipophilic antioxidants, whereas DPPH• is operationally restricted to organic solvent environments. The superior ABTS•+ scavenging exhibited by ALPs‐1 may thus reflect its greater compatibility with the aqueous‐phase reaction milieu of the ABTS assay. Notwithstanding this relative improvement, the absolute antioxidant potency of ALPs‐1 remains considerably inferior to that of vitamin C, a small‐molecule reference antioxidant.

In Vivo Antioxidant Protection in a Zebrafish Model

The metronidazole‐induced keratinocyte injury model in zebrafish larvae was exploited to assess the antioxidant protective capacity of ALPs‐1 in a whole‐organism context. In the Tg(krt4:NTR:GFP) transgenic line, metronidazole provokes intracellular oxidative stress specifically within skin keratinocytes, resulting in cellular damage and a concomitant decline in GFP fluorescence intensity. Accordingly, the preservation or recovery of the fluorescent signal was employed as a surrogate readout for keratinocyte viability, rather than as a direct quantitative measure of apoptosis per se. Representative fluorescence micrographs acquired from each experimental cohort are compiled in Figure 6.

In vivo antioxidant evaluation of ALPs‐1 using the metronidazole‐induced oxidative stress model in transgenic Tg(krt4:NTR:GFP) zebrafish. (a) Representative keratinocyte fluorescence images captured from each treatment group; (b) Quantitative comparison of fluorescence spot counts in the trunk region. Exposure to metronidazole at 400 µg/mL produced a pronounced diminution of the keratinocyte‐associated fluorescence relative to the untreated control group (p < 0.001), thereby validating the oxidative insult. Co‐administration of either the positive control (vitamin C, 35 µg/mL) or ALPs‐1 at three dose levels brought about a significant attenuation of the fluorescence loss compared with the model group (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively), indicative of a cytoprotective effect. It should be noted, however, that none of the intervention groups restored the fluorescence intensity to the level observed in the untreated controls, suggesting that the conferred protection was partial rather than complete.

Quantitative image analysis yielded the following fluorescence spot counts (mean ± SD): untreated control, 55.8 ± 8.3; metronidazole‐alone model, 3.3 ± 1.2; vitamin C positive control, 28.5 ± 5.2 (corresponding to a relative antioxidant capacity of 48.0%). The ALPs‐1‐treated cohorts at 1, 10, and 100 µg/mL recorded fluorescence counts of 51.7 ± 4.1, 50.5 ± 6.2, and 48.2 ± 6.5, respectively, translating to relative antioxidant capacities of 92.2%, 89.9%, and 85.5%. Interestingly, the greatest degree of protection was manifested at the lowest administered dose (1 µg/mL), with a modest attenuation of the effect at the two higher concentrations (10 and 100 µg/mL). While a non‐monotonic dose‐response relationship of this nature is unconventional, comparable patterns have been documented in the literature for certain polysaccharide preparations and other natural products [34]. Several mechanistic scenarios may be envisaged to account for this observation: (i) self‐association or aggregation of ALPs‐1 molecules at elevated concentrations, which could restrict their bioavailability or impede cellular internalization; (ii) saturation kinetics of putative cell‐surface receptors or downstream antioxidant signaling cascades (e.g., the Nrf2/ARE pathway) at suprathreshold doses, leading to a plateau or even a decrement in the elicited response; or (iii) a hormetic dose‐response phenomenon, wherein low‐level exposure triggers an adaptive protective response that is not sustained—or is counteracted—at higher exposure intensities. This dose‐response anomaly warrants dedicated follow‐up experimentation to unravel the underlying molecular mechanism.

In summary, these in vivo findings establish that ALPs‐1 confers tangible, albeit moderate, antioxidant protection against metronidazole‐elicited oxidative keratinocyte injury in the zebrafish model. The protective effect exhibits a nonlinear dose dependence, with the maximal benefit observed at the lowest concentration examined.

Modulation of Pro‐Inflammatory Mediators in LPS‐Stimulated BV2 Microglial Cells

Prior to assessing the anti‐neuroinflammatory activity of ALPs‐1, its potential cytotoxicity toward BV2 murine microglial cells was first examined using the CCK‐8 assay. As shown in Figure 7A, exposure to LPS alone did not appreciably compromise cell survival. Importantly, neither the positive control minocycline (MINO, 5 µM) nor ALPs‐1 applied across the full concentration span of 3–12 µg/mL elicited any statistically significant reduction in the viability of LPS‐activated BV2 cells (p > 0.05), establishing that ALPs‐1 is devoid of acute cytotoxic effects under the present experimental paradigm.

All values are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of three independent determinations (n = 3). ***p < 0.001 compared with the untreated control group; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, and ###p < 0.001 relative to the LPS‐stimulated group alone.

Figure 7B illustrates that LPS stimulation provoked a robust and highly significant elevation of NO accumulation in the culture medium relative to untreated controls (p < 0.001). Co‐treatment with ALPs‐1 attenuated this NO overproduction, although the suppression followed a dose‐dependent pattern that was not strictly linear. Both ALPs‐1 and the reference compound MINO appreciably counteracted the LPS‐elicited NO surge, with significant differences recorded versus the LPS‐alone treatment arm (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001). It is noteworthy, however, that even at the uppermost ALPs‐1 dose examined (12 µg/mL), the NO concentration remained significantly elevated above that of the unstimulated control (p < 0.01), signifying that the intervention achieved only a partial abrogation rather than a complete normalization of the LPS‐driven nitrosative response.

The effects of ALPs‐1 on the secretion of the prototypical pro‐inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) are presented in Figure 7C,D, respectively. LPS challenge triggered a pronounced upregulation of both mediators (p < 0.001). ALPs‐1 intervention mitigated this inflammatory response: at doses of 6 and 12 µg/mL, the polysaccharide significantly suppressed the release of both TNF‐α and IL‐1β relative to the LPS‐only group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, or p < 0.001). Quantitatively, the 12 µg/mL ALPs‐1 treatment reduced TNF‐α levels by roughly 35% and IL‐1β levels by approximately 42% compared with the LPS‐stimulated control. Notwithstanding this anti‐inflammatory activity, neither cytokine was restored to the basal levels observed in untreated cells (p < 0.05 vs. untreated control for both), again pointing to a partial rather than a complete blockade of the LPS‐induced inflammatory cascade.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that ALPs‐1 moderately but significantly curtails the production of the pro‐inflammatory mediators NO, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β in LPS‐activated BV2 microglia, without exerting acute cytotoxicity across the tested concentration window of 3–12 µg/mL. The anti‐inflammatory effects are characterized by a partial reversal of the LPS‐induced changes and a dose‐response relationship that deviates from strict linearity. Prospective investigations should be directed toward evaluating the long‐term safety profile of ALPs‐1 and delineating the precise intracellular signaling pathways through which it tempers microglial activation.

M w Determination

The M w distribution and homogeneity of ALPs‐1 were evaluated by HPGPC. The elution profile, depicted in Figure S1, exhibited a single, well‐resolved symmetric peak, indicative of a polysaccharide fraction of uniform molecular dimensions. An additional minor signal appearing at an elution time of approximately 22 min was attributed to the KH2PO4 buffer front, as confirmed by blank solvent injections; this artifact is unrelated to the polysaccharide content and was therefore disregarded in the molecular weight determination. From the HPGPC data, the number‐average molecular weight (M n) was calculated to be 7850 Da, and the weight‐average molecular weight (M w) was 8827 Da, corresponding to a polydispersity index (PDI = M w/M n) of 1.12. The proximity of the PDI to unity, together with the symmetrical peak morphology, attests to a narrow molecular weight spread and a high degree of homogeneity. Collectively, these chromatographic features support the conclusion that ALPs‐1 represents a homogeneous polysaccharide preparation.

Results of Monosaccharide Composition Analysis

The HPLC analysis presented in Figure S2 revealed that ALPs‐1 comprises four monosaccharide species—rhamnose, glucuronic acid, glucose, and galactose—at a relative molar proportion of 2.18:1.00:6.62:7.17, with galactose constituting the predominant sugar residue.

Polysaccharides from A. lappa roots have been reported to vary in composition depending on extraction and purification methods. Some studies have reported inulin‐type fructans composed mainly of fructose and glucose [1, 3]. Others have isolated acidic polysaccharides containing galactose, glucose, arabinose, and glucuronic acid [7, 9]. The presence of glucuronic acid and rhamnose in ALPs‐1 is consistent with the acidic polysaccharide fractions described by Jiang et al. [7], who reported that an ALP fraction (ALP‐1) contained rhamnose, galactose, and galacturonic acid. A monosaccharide profile in which galactose and glucose predominate, accompanied by lesser amounts of uronic acids, is a compositional signature frequently encountered in pectic polysaccharides and arabinogalactans isolated from various members of the Asteraceae family, including chicory (Cichorium intybus) and sunflower (Helianthus annuus). Thus, the monosaccharide profile of ALPs‐1 falls within the range of known acidic polysaccharides from this botanical family.

The presence of glucuronic acid (a uronic acid) indicates that ALPs‐1 is an acidic polysaccharide. This finding is fully consistent with the purification results described in the chromatographic purification section above, where ALPs‐1 was eluted from the DEAE‐Sepharose column at approximately 0.3 M NaCl. Neutral polysaccharides typically do not bind to anion‐exchange resins and are eluted with water (tubes 15–25 in Figure 4a), whereas acidic polysaccharides bind to the positively charged resin and require salt for elution. The fact that ALPs‐1 was collected as a distinct peak at 0.3 M NaCl confirms its acidic nature, which is attributed to the carboxyl groups of glucuronic acid. The relatively low content of glucuronic acid (molar ratio 1.00 out of 16.97, ∼5.9%) is sufficient to confer net negative charge and enable binding to the DEAE resin.

Based on the monosaccharide composition, we tentatively propose possible structural features. The high proportion of galactose and glucose (together accounting for approximately 81% of total monosaccharides) suggests that the backbone of ALPs‐1 may consist of galactan or glucogalactan chains. The presence of rhamnose (12.8%) and glucuronic acid (5.9%) hints at potential branch points, as rhamnose is often involved in (1→4)‐linked backbone insertion points for side chains in pectic polysaccharides, and glucuronic acid may serve as a terminal or branching residue.

Determining the monosaccharide constituent profile represents an essential prerequisite for the structural elucidation of polysaccharides. Given the promising potential of ALPs as a bioactive supplement, elucidating its chemical structure is essential. The results from this experiment provide important clues for further investigation into the structure–function relationship of ALPs, which will facilitate its better application in future studies.

Comparison With Structurally Characterized Polysaccharides From A. Lappa and Related Asteraceae Species

To contextualize the structural and bioactivity characteristics of ALPs‐1, we systematically compared it with previously reported polysaccharides from A. lappa and other Asteraceae plants (e.g., C. intybus, H. annuus, Taraxacum officinale).

ALPs reported in the literature can be broadly classified into two groups: (i) inulin‐type fructans (M w 2–10 kDa, composed mainly of fructose and glucose with β‐(2→1) linkages) typically obtained by hot water extraction [1, 3]; and (ii) acidic pectic‐type polysaccharides (M w 15–100 kDa) containing galacturonic acid (GalA), galactose (Gal), arabinose (Ara), and rhamnose (Rha), often extracted using alkaline or enzyme‐assisted methods [7, 9].

ALPs‐1 (M w = 8.8 kDa, Rha:GlcA:Glc:Gal = 2.18:1.00:6.62:7.17) occupies an intermediate position. Its molecular weight is comparable to inulin‐type fructans, but its monosaccharide composition (presence of uronic acid and rhamnose, absence of fructose) is characteristic of acidic polysaccharides. Notably, the uronic acid in ALPs‐1 is glucuronic acid (GlcA) rather than the more common galacturonic acid (GalA) found in most pectic polysaccharides. This distinction is rare but has been reported for certain A. lappa fractions [7] and for some Asteraceae species like Echinacea purpurea. The low GlcA content (∼5.9% of total carbohydrates) is sufficient to confer negative charge, explaining its elution from DEAE‐Sepharose at 0.3 M NaCl.

Detailed physicochemical data for ALPs are limited. Compared to high‐molecular‐weight pectic polysaccharides (which tend to form viscous solutions even at low concentrations), ALPs‐1 exhibited good solubility in water and relatively low viscosity at 1 mg/mL (data not shown), consistent with its lower molecular weight.

The antioxidant activity of ALPs‐1 (DPPH IC50 = 84.2 mg/mL) is considerably weaker than most previously reported A. lappa acidic polysaccharides (e.g., IC50 = 0.52 mg/mL for ALP‐1). This discrepancy may be attributed to (i) the lower uronic acid content in ALPs‐1 (5.1% vs. >20% in ALP‐1), as uronic acids are known to enhance radical scavenging; (ii) the absence of arabinose, which has been associated with higher antioxidant capacity; and (iii) differences in the assay protocols (e.g., reaction time, DPPH concentration). Similarly, the α‐glucosidase inhibitory activity of ALPs‐1 (IC50 = 2.81 mg/mL) is within the range reported for ALPs (0.5–5.0 mg/mL), though at the higher end. This suggests that ALPs‐1 is a modest hypoglycemic agent compared to more active fractions.

Compared to polysaccharides from other Asteraceae species, ALPs‐1 shows weaker antioxidant activity than C. intybus pectin (DPPH IC50 = 1.23 mg/mL) and H. annuus polysaccharide (IC50 = 0.88 mg/mL) [35]. This is consistent with its lower molecular weight and lower uronic acid content. However, direct cross‐study comparisons are complicated by variations in extraction methods, purity, and assay conditions.

ALPs‐1 is a relatively low‐molecular‐weight acidic polysaccharide with a unique monosaccharide profile (GlcA instead of GalA). Its bioactivities are modest compared to some reported ALPs, likely due to its low uronic acid content and the absence of certain neutral sugars. Nevertheless, this study provides the first evidence that DES‐UAE can extract a bioactive glucuronic‐acid‐containing polysaccharide from A. lappa, and the structural uniqueness of ALPs‐1 warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In this study, UAE using a choline chloride–oxalic acid DES (DES, 2:1 molar ratio) was optimized via response surface methodology, yielding the following optimal conditions: DES water content 20%, liquid‐to‐solid ratio 1:40 g/mL, time 97 min, and ultrasonic power 280 W, under which the extraction rate of ALPs reached 16.581%—significantly higher than those from water immersion (8.43%) and ultrasonic water extraction (8.26%), confirming the superiority of the DES method. The crude polysaccharide was purified by DEAE‐Sepharose Fast Flow and Sephadex G‐75 columns, yielding a refined fraction (ALPs‐1). Bioactivity evaluation showed that ALPs‐1 exhibited inhibitory effects on α‐glucosidase and α‐amylase with IC50 values of 2.805 and 6.637 mg/mL, respectively, though weaker than acarbose, and demonstrated scavenging activity against DPPH• and ABTS•+ radicals with IC50 values of 84.192 and 0.87 mg/mL, respectively, indicating moderate antioxidant capacity compared to vitamin C. Overall, the bioactivity results indicate that ALPs‐1 possesses moderate to weak antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities compared to the reference compounds. The proposed mechanisms—such as hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl/carboxyl groups of the polysaccharide and active‐site residues of enzymes—are speculative at this stage. No direct evidence (e.g., kinetic analysis, molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance) is available to confirm these interactions. Therefore, these mechanistic hypotheses should be considered as preliminary explanations that require further experimental validation. ALPs‐1 demonstrates significant in vivo antioxidant activity by effectively protecting zebrafish keratinocytes from metronidazole‐induced apoptosis. ALPs‐1 also displayed notable in vitro anti‐neuroinflammatory activity by suppressing key inflammatory mediators. The Mw of ALPs‐1 was determined to be 8827 Da. Monosaccharide composition analysis indicated that ALPs‐1 consists of rhamnose, glucuronic acid, glucose, and galactose in a molar ratio of 2.18:1.00:6.62:7.17.
