Uncertainty-Aware Hybrid Machine Learning in Virtual Sensors for Vehicle Sideslip Angle Estimation
Abinav Kalyanasundaram, Karthikeyan Chandra Sekaran, Philipp Stäuber, Michael Lange, Wolfgang Utschick, Michael Botsch

Abstract
Precise vehicle state estimation is crucial for safe and reliable autonomous driving. The number of measurable states and their precision offered by the onboard vehicle sensor system are often constrained by cost. For instance, measuring critical quantities such as the Vehicle Sideslip Angle (VSA) poses significant commercial challenges using current optical sensors. This paper addresses these limitations by focusing on the development of high-performance virtual sensors to enhance vehicle state estimation for active safety. The proposed Uncertainty-Aware Hybrid Learning (UAHL) architecture integrates a machine learning model with vehicle motion models to estimate VSA directly from onboard sensor data. A key aspect of the UAHL architecture is its focus on uncertainty quantification for individual model estimates and hybrid fusion. These mechanisms enable the dynamic weighting of uncertainty-aware predictions from machine learning and vehicle motion models to produce accurate and reliable hybrid VSA estimates. This work also presents a novel dataset named Real-world Vehicle State Estimation Dataset (ReV-StED) , comprising synchronized measurements from advanced vehicle dynamic sensors. The experimental results demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed method for VSA estimation, highlighting UAHL as a promising architecture for advancing virtual sensors and enhancing active safety in autonomous vehicles.

Introduction

Safe autonomous driving and

VSA

\beta
, is a key indicator of vehicle stability and rider comfort making it crucial for control strategies in active safety systems
[
1
]
. However, sensors that directly measure
VSA
for real-time monitoring are expensive to implement in production cars
[
2
]
. Alternatively, virtual sensors can estimate vehicle dynamic states by leveraging data from available sensors without space and cost constraints. The
VSA
is often estimated through model-based virtual sensors employing different versions of the
Kalman Filter (KF)

. The recent trend towards software-defined vehicles has paved way for the adoption of
Machine Learning (ML)
-based virtual sensors
[
3
]
. This work presents a virtual sensor designed for reliable
VSA

State-of-the-art (SOTA)

[
4
,
5
]
or

-based approaches

. While

-based approaches can handle non-linearities, they suffer from the “long-tail” problem, where more erroneous predictions occur in underrepresented scenarios.
They also typically lack the ability to quantify the uncertainty in their predictions

. In safety-critical applications, such as autonomous driving, understanding these uncertainties is essential for ensuring reliable estimates from virtual sensor outputs

II
Related works

Virtual sensors are an active area of research in intelligent vehicles, enabling advancements across a variety of domains, including vehicle dynamics
[
10
]
, battery thermal management
[
11
]
, electric drivetrain
[
12
]
, etc.
They have huge potential in
VSA
estimation, defined as the angle
\beta
between a vehicle’s longitudinal axis and the direction of its velocity vector at the
Center of Gravity (CoG)

[
2
]
. The existing research works on virtual estimation of the
VSA
can be categorized into observer-based approaches and data-driven
ML
methods
[
13
]
.

The observer-based approaches like the Luenberger observer, the sliding mode observer and the Kalman filter utilize vehicle models for state estimation
[
4
]
. They are then combined with measurements from
Global Positioning Systems (GPS)
or inertial measurement units for
VSA
estimation. The vehicle models can be either kinematic
[
14
,
15
]
or dynamic
[
16
]
. While kinematic models offer simplicity from complex parameters, they can suffer in low yaw rate conditions as the system becomes unobservable
[
17
]
. Dynamic models incorporate forces and moments acting on the vehicle and vary in complexity depending on the type of model used, such as single-track
[
16
]
, two-track, and tire models
[
18
]
. Although dynamic models can provide good
VSA
estimation, their accuracy heavily depends on reliable parameters identification, the tire model and the tire wear
[
19
]
. Recently
[
5
]
attempted to address these challenges using an interacting multiple model
KF
with
GPS
data. Many observer-based approaches depend on external correction data
[
4
]
, which can be challenging to acquire. Moreover, their high accuracy is generally limited to specific vehicle states where the models are most effective
[
5
]
.

The
ML
-based approaches use onboard
[
2
]
or external sensor data to directly estimate
VSA
. Dynamic Neural Networks (DNN)
[
1
]
, Recurrent Neural Networks
[
2
]
and
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
models
[
20
]
are some of the promising architectures. They exhibited similar performance levels as observer-based approaches in certain maneuvers
[
21
]
. The main challenge with
ML
-based estimation is their inability to quantify uncertainty and performance reliance on dataset quality. In this work, some limitations of both observer-based and
ML
-based approaches are addressed, while leveraging their individual strengths, through the proposed
UAHL
architecture for virtual sensors.

III-A

Problem Formulation

Let the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{({\bm{X}}^{1},{\bm{y}}^{1}),..,({\bm{X}}^{N},{\bm{y}}^{N})\}
represent
N

{\bm{X}}=\{{\bm{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times m}
denotes the history of measured vehicle dynamic states for
L

{\bm{x}}_{t}=\{x_{t}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes the set of measurements obtained from
m
onboard sensors at time step

. The data obtained from a ground truth

sensor is denoted by
{\bm{y}}=\{y_{t}\}_{t=L}^{L+F}\in\mathbb{R}^{F+1}
, where
F
represents future time steps. The main objective of this work is to develop a function
f_{\theta}
to learn the mapping from onboard sensor data
{\bm{X}}
towards a high performance
VSA
sensor,

{\bm{y}}=f_{\theta}({\bm{X}};L,F,m).

(1)

Here
f_{\theta}
acts as a virtual sensor by predicting the
VSA
values based on the history of onboard sensor data.

III-B

Preliminaries

The Transformer architecture’s
[
22
]
ability to capture long-range dependencies and interactions, has led to significant progress in time series applications
[
23
]
. However,
the vanilla Transformer
[
22
]
faced challenges in the time series domain due to the computational complexity of self-attention and its autoregressive decoder
[
9
]
. While various strategies have been proposed to modify the network for efficient time series Transformers, the Informer model introduced by Zhou et al.
[
9
]
has shown particularly good performance. It explored the low-rank property of the self-attention matrix in time series to introduce “ProbSparse Self-attention” as follows

\mathcal{A}_{s}(\overline{{\bm{Q}}},{\bm{K}},{\bm{V}})=\text{Softmax}\left(\frac{\overline{{\bm{Q}}}{\bm{K}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right){\bm{V}},

(2)

here
\overline{{\bm{Q}}},{\bm{K}},{\bm{V}}
represent a sparse query, key and value matrix respectively. The sparse
\overline{{\bm{Q}}}
matrix contains only the top
u
queries based on sparsity measurement
M({\bm{q}}_{i},{\bm{K}})
defined as

M({\bm{q}}_{i},{\bm{K}})=\max_{j}\left\{\frac{{\bm{q}}_{i}{\bm{k}}_{j}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right\}-\frac{1}{L_{K}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{K}}\frac{{\bm{q}}_{i}{\bm{k}}_{j}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}.

(3)

The main intuition behind Eq. (
3
) is that dominant queries will have corresponding query’s attention probability distribution far away from the uniform distribution
[
9
]
.
The attention for non-dominant queries is taken as mean over all values
\mathbf{V}
. This sparse attention mechanism reduces the time and memory complexity of canonical attention from
\mathcal{O}(L_{Q}L_{K})
to
\mathcal{O}(\log L_{Q}L_{K})
, where
L_{K}
and
L_{Q}
represents the number of rows in matrix
{\bm{K}}
and
{\bm{Q}}
. The proposed
ML
model is based on Informer
[
9
]
.

UAHL
architecture consists of three main components: A
ML
model,

,
and an uncertainty-aware hybrid fusion module. The onboard sensor data
{\bm{x}}_{t}
is the input for both
ML
and

. The

\beta_{\text{ml}}
with model uncertainty
\delta_{\text{ml}}
using the Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) block. The

output their predictions,
\beta_{\text{vm1}}
and
\beta_{\text{vm2}}
with their respective uncertainties,
\delta_{\text{vm1}}
and
\delta_{\text{vm2}}
. Finally, the hybrid fusion module leverages uncertainty-aware inputs to estimate the
VSA
for the virtual sensor.

III-C

Machine Learning Model

The proposed
ML
model aims to learn the mapping function
f_{\theta}
as shown in Eq. (
1
). Each time step
t
in the input space
{\bm{X}}
, represented as
{\bm{x}}_{t}
, is transformed to a
d
-dimensional space using an up-projection layer
\Phi

and added with the positional encoding
[
22
]
to produce the encoder’s input embeddings
{\bm{U}}_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
.
The encoder contains two sparse self-attention
\mathcal{A}_{s}
blocks with
H
heads each and a distillation operation
[
9
]
between them.
This distillation operation reduces the temporal dimension
L
by half. Finally, the encoder outputs a feature map denoted as
{\bm{F}}_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{L}{2}\times{d}}
as shown in Fig.
2
.

The decoder takes as input the last
B
observed time steps along with
F
future steps, following the approach in
[
9
]
. The decoder’s input sequence
{\bm{X}}_{\text{dec}}
is defined as
\{{\bm{x}}_{t}\}_{t=L-B}^{L+F}\in\mathbb{R}^{(B+F+1)\times m}
. Since the
ML
model does not have access to future vehicle states, they are initialized as
\{{\bm{x}}_{t}\}_{t=L+1}^{L+F}=0
. Similar to the encoder,
{\bm{X}}_{\text{dec}}
is transformed to a
d
-dimensional space with the up-projection layer
\Phi
and added with the positional encoding to form the decoder’s input embeddings,
{\bm{U}}_{\text{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(B+F+1)\times d}
. A sparse self-attention followed by a normal cross-attention
[
22
]
block is utilized in the decoder to make predictions. The output from the decoder at time steps
t\in[L,L+F]
corresponds to
VSA
estimates as illustrated in Fig.
2
.

Two major network modifications were made to the Informer
[
9
]
to adapt it from time series forecasting to the virtual sensor setting, referred to as Informer*. First, unlike in multivariate time series forecasting, the virtual sensor should not have access to the history of
VSA
values in
{\bm{X}}
. Therefore, measurements
{\bm{y}}
from the ground truth
VSA
sensor were used only during loss computation. Second, uncertainty estimation
\delta_{\text{ml}}
was incorporated into the model to make reliable
ML
predictions.

Uncertainty Quantification in
ML
(Informer*)

It was assumed that the
VSA
estimates from the
ML
model follow a Student’s t-distribution. Its ability to model heavier tails allows it to handle outliers and variability more effectively than the traditional Gaussian distribution
[
24
]
. The final fully connected layer in the decoder is designed to predict the mean
\mu_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
, the variance
\sigma_{t}
, and the degrees of freedom
\nu_{t}
of the underlying distribution in order to compute the posterior density for
VSA

(\beta_{\text{ml}}[t])
at time step
t
.
To ensure numerical stability during training, these parameters are clipped accordingly to
\sigma_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\nu_{t}\in[3,\infty]
.
Correspondingly the loss function for one data sample
({\bm{X}},{\bm{y}})
is formulated as a negative log-likelihood loss

\!\mathcal{L}_{\text{ml}}\!=\!-\!\sum_{t=L}^{L+F}\!\ln\!\left(\!\frac{\Gamma\left(\!\frac{\nu_{t}+1}{2}\right)}{\sigma_{t}\sqrt{\nu_{t}\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu_{t}}{2}\right)}\!\left(1\!+\!\frac{({y}_{t}\!-\!\mu_{t})\!^{2}}{\nu_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}}\!\right)^{\!\frac{-\nu_{t}-1}{2}}\!\right)

(4)

The underlying principle of this loss formulation is that it encourages the
ML
model to output higher variance for more erroneous predictions.
During testing, the prediction
\beta_{\text{ml}}[t]
and uncertainty
\delta_{\text{ml}}[t]
for virtual
VSA
estimates are computed as follows

\beta_{\text{ml}}[t]=\mu_{t}\;,\;\delta_{\text{ml}}[t]=\max\left(\frac{\nu_{t}}{\nu_{t}-2}\times\sigma_{t}^{2},1\right).

(5)

Unlike previous works in
VSA
, the proposed
ML
model is formulated as a time series predictor, utilizing a large observation window
L
to capture the vehicle’s dynamic state within the
UAHL
architecture. It is also designed with a rolling forecasting setting, enabling it to compensate for computational latencies during
ML
model deployment on the vehicle.

III-D

Vehicle Motion Models (VMMs)

The
VMMs
are mathematical models that strive to capture the relationship between the vehicle inputs, geometry and dynamic parameters towards its motion while accounting for underlying non-holonomic constraints. The
VMMs
act as
a priori
knowledge for virtual sensors to estimate their target quantity from onboard sensor data. In this work, only simple
VMMs
for
VSA
estimation are utilized as more complex models require several original equipment manufacturer parameters such as tire-parameters, engine torque, etc.

III-D
1
Vehicle Motion Model - 1 (VMM-1)

It is a kinematic model designed for flat vehicle movement, assuming negligible tire slip angles. In
VMM-1
, the velocity vectors at the “virtual” wheels are aligned with the rolling direction of the tires
[
25
]
. This model assumption is valid under the conditions of slow speed cornering, constant steering ratio and absence of rear axle steering. The
VSA
and yaw rate at time step
t
for
VMM-1
are derived from geometric parameters as follows

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{vm1}}[t]

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\ell_{\text{r}}}{\ell}\tan(\theta_{\text{A}}[t])\right),

(6)

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}_{\mathrm{vm1}}[t]

\displaystyle=\frac{v[t]\tan(\theta_{\text{A}}[t])}{\ell}\;,\;\theta_{\text{A}}[t]=\frac{\theta_{\text{sw}}}{r_{\text{s}}}.

here
\ell,\ell_{\text{r}},\theta_{\text{A}},v,\theta_{\text{sw}},r_{\text{s}}
represent the wheelbase, the distance from the rear axle to the
CoG
, the Ackermann steering angle, the velocity, the steering wheel angle, and the steering ratio respectively. This
VMM-1
is a simple motion model based on geometric constraints.

III-D
2
Vehicle Motion Model - 2 (VMM-2)

utilizes the individual wheel speed data obtained from onboard sensors defined in Eq.
16
to compute the
VSA
. During cornering the speed difference between the inside and outside wheels can be used to give a rough estimate for the lateral velocity
v_{\text{y}}
at the
CoG

[
26
]
. The longitudinal velocity
v_{\text{x}}
is approximated as the mean speed of all wheels at time
t
. The
VSA
can then be computed as

\displaystyle v_{\text{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{\text{fl}}+v_{\text{fr}}+v_{\text{rl}}+v_{\text{rr}}}{4},

(7)

\displaystyle v_{\text{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{\text{fr}}-v_{\text{fl}}+v_{\text{rr}}-v_{\text{rl}}}{2},\;\;\tilde{\beta}_{\text{vm2}}[t]=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{\text{y}}}{v_{\text{x}}}\right)

This
\tilde{\beta}_{\text{vm2}}[t]
suffers heavily due to wheel speed noise and the absence of vehicle dynamics during cornering. To improve the rough
VSA
estimates in Eq. (
7
), a
Kalman Filter (KF)

[
27
]
is implemented for
VMM-2
, based on the assumption of a Gaussian-Markovian process for vehicle motion. Vehicle dynamic knowledge is incorporated into
VMM-2
by using a linear single-track (st) model for system representation
[
25
]
. The state
({\bm{s}})
and measurement vector
({\bm{z}})
for the
KF
is defined as:

{\bm{s}}[t]=\big[\beta_{\text{st}},\dot{\psi_{\text{st}}}\big]^{\top}\;,\;\bm{z}[t]=\big[\tilde{\beta}_{\text{vm2}},\dot{\psi}_{\text{obd}}\big]^{\top}.

(8)

Here, the measurement vector
{\bm{z}}
consists of the
VSA
estimated in Eq. (
7
) and yaw rate
\dot{\psi}_{obd}
from onboard sensors. The noise covariance matrices required for the
KF
can be estimated from the dataset. The
KF
for
VMM-2
is implemented recursively at the frequency of onboard sensor data rate from the vehicle. The final a-posteriori estimates and their covariance
{\bm{P}}
from
KF
, serve as the outputs of
VMM-2

{\bm{s}}[t|t]=\big[\beta_{\text{vm2}},\dot{\psi}_{\text{vm2}}\big]^{\top}\;,\;{\bm{P}}[t|t]=\text{diag}\left[\sigma_{\beta}^{\text{vm2}},\sigma_{\dot{\psi}}^{\text{vm2}}\right]^{\top}.

(9)

Previous works in
VSA
estimation often relied on complex vehicle dynamic models and filtering algorithms that required external correction data
[
13
]
. In contrast, the proposed
UAHL
architecture utilizes simple
VMMs
to compute the
VSA
from onboard sensor data, without the need for extensive parameter identification. Although simple, the
VMMs
can effectively integrate vehicle dynamics knowledge into the
UAHL
architecture.

III-E

Uncertainty Quantification in
VMMs

The uncertainty in a
Vehicle Motion Model (VMM)
arises from the inherent assumptions made to simplify the model. For instance,
VMM-1
performs poorly in
VSA
estimation during high-speed cornering, where tire slip angles cannot be neglected. However, numerically quantifying this uncertainty for the
VMMs
during inference is challenging due to the absence of a ground truth. Isermann et al.
[
28
]
proposed a fault diagnosis procedure for onboard sensors based on residuals and fuzzy logic. This foundation is extended by proposing a hypothesis for uncertainty estimation in
VMMs
.

Let
{\bm{x}}=\{{x}^{1},..,{x}^{p}\}
denote the vehicle’s ground truth state, and
\hat{{\bm{x}}}=\{\hat{x}^{1},..,\hat{x}^{p}\}
denote the estimates from a
VMM
for
p
different state quantities. Then the residual vector
{{\bm{e}}}=\{{{e}}^{1},..,{{e}}^{p}\}
is defined as the error in
VMM
estimates such that
{{e}}^{i}=\left|\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{i}-{{\bm{x}}}^{i}\right|
.

Hypothesis 1

The uncertainty
\delta_{\text{vm}}
of a
VMM
arising from model simplifications propagates to the residual vector
{\bm{e}}
. This makes the individual residuals correlated to each other such that
{e}^{i}\not\perp{e}^{j}\;\forall i,j\in p
.

The Hypothesis 1 is empirically validated in section
V-B
and Fig.
3
by showing that
{e}^{\beta}\propto{e}^{\dot{\psi}}
. In this work, Hypothesis 1 is used to quantify the uncertainty in
VMM-1
predictions for the
VSA
by leveraging the residual yaw rate

\delta_{\text{vm1}}(\beta_{\text{vm1}}[t])\approx\left({\dot{\psi}_{\text{vm1}}[t]-\dot{\psi}_{\text{obd}}[t]}\right).

(10)

For
VMM-2
, innovation residual from
KF
in Eq. (
8
) can directly used as an uncertainty such that,
\delta_{\text{vm2}}[t]=\sigma_{{\beta}}^{\text{vm2}}
.

The uncertainty quantification for
VMMs
within the
UAHL
architecture incurs minimal computational overhead, making it well-suited for real-time monitoring of virtual sensors. While the
VMMs
are relatively simple, their ability to produce uncertainty-aware estimates significantly enhances their effectiveness.

III-F

Uncertainty-Aware Hybrid Fusion

In safety-critical applications like autonomous driving, a false sense of certainty for virtual sensor estimates can become hazardous. Hence, identifying model limitations through uncertainty evaluation is one of the key aspects of this work.
The complementary strengths of data-driven and model-based methods developed for virtual sensors can be effectively integrated through hybrid fusion as shown in Fig.
2
.
The input to the fusion architecture consists of
{\bm{h}}[t]=[\beta_{\text{ml}},\delta_{\text{ml}},\beta_{\text{vm1}},\delta_{\text{vm1}},\beta_{\text{vm2}},\delta_{\text{vm2}}]
at time step
t
defined in
Eqs. (
5
),
(
6
), (
8
), and (
10
)
. Three different
UAHL
fusion strategies are proposed varying on the level of interpretability and complexity.

III-F
1
Expert Fusion (EF)

The
ML
(Informer*) model generally performs well; however, its performance decreases in highly uncertain regions. This decline in performance is mitigated by incorporating the estimates from the
VMM
s. The vehicle dynamics domain knowledge is leveraged to select the most suitable
VMM
for fusion. At lower speeds,
VMM-1
performs well due to the validity of its model assumptions within this region. Conversely, at higher speeds,
VMM-2
exhibits superior performance. The proposed expert fusion method is described mathematically by

\beta_{\text{EF}}=\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{ll}\beta_{\text{vm1}}\delta_{\text{ml}}\!+\!\beta_{\text{ml}}(1-\delta_{\text{ml}})&\!\delta_{\text{ml}}\!>\!\delta_{\text{th}}\!\land\!v_{\text{s}}\!\leq\!v_{\text{th}},\\
\beta_{\text{vm2}}\delta_{\text{ml}}\!+\!\beta_{\text{ml}}(1-\delta_{\text{ml}})&\!\delta_{\text{ml}}\!>\!\delta_{\text{th}}\!\land\!v_{\text{s}}\!>\!v_{\text{th}},\\
\beta_{\text{ml}}&\delta_{\text{ml}}\leq\delta_{\text{th}},\end{array}\right.

(11)

where
\delta_{\text{th}}
denotes the uncertainty threshold for the
ML
model and
v_{\text{th}}
denotes the velocity threshold for choosing between the
VMM
s.

III-F
2
Deep Fusion (DF)

Both
ML
and
VMMs
produce uncertainty estimates that differ in scale and characteristics. For example, the
ML
model provides probabilistic estimates that may be prone to overconfidence or underconfidence in certain scenarios. In contrast, the
VMMs
generate uncertainty estimates rooted in physical principles, making them more interpretable but less adaptable to complex patterns. To account for these differences, a non-linear mapping is learned through a dedicated module, referred to as the deep fusion network (
f_{\text{DF}}
). This hybrid architecture captures complex relationships and interactions between the outputs of the
ML
and
VMMs
to generate an estimate for
VSA

\beta_{\text{DF}}=f_{\text{DF}}({\bm{h}}).

(12)

After training the
ML
model, a fusion dataset
\mathcal{D}_{f}
is created with
{\bm{h}}
as inputs and
y
as targets. Each time step forms a data sample in this dataset and the predictions are made for each time step independently. However, the dataset splits are not generated by sampling individual time instances but by sampling entire scenarios. This prevents overly optimistic results that could arise from the high correlation between consecutive time steps in the time series. An L2 loss function is utilized to train the model

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DF}}=\|f_{\text{DF}}({\bm{h}})-y\|_{2}^{2}.

(13)

III-F
3
Gaussian Regression Fusion (GF)

A
Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)
is used to learn the joint distribution
p({\bm{h}},y)
between the fusion input and
VSA
. Expectation-maximization algorithm is used to determine the parameters of the
GMM
.
During inference, a
Gaussian Mixture Regression (GMR)

[
29
]
is performed by predicting the conditional distribution
p(y\mid{\bm{h}})
using the learnt joint distribution
p({\bm{h}},y)
. The estimate with the maximum
a-posteriori
probability is assigned as

\beta_{\text{GF}}=\argmax_{y}(p(y\mid{\bm{h}})).

(14)

IV
Dataset

The proposed
UAHL
framework for virtual sensors requires a dataset for training and evaluation. Most existing works on
VSA
estimation rely on data collected through onboard sensors in Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) simulations
[
20
]
or from external retrofitted sensors
[
30
]
. However, these datasets are often proprietary, limiting their accessibility. To address this limitation, a publicly available, large-scale dataset is essential for advancing virtual sensors.

This work introduces
ReV-StED
, a 5-hour driving dataset comprising space-time synchronized measurements from a test vehicle (a Smart Fortwo from the CARISSMA outdoor test track
[
31
]
), equipped with several vehicle dynamic measurement sensors. The primary sensor, the GeneSys ADMA-G-PRO+ GNSS/Inertial System provides precise accelerometer and gyroscope measurements along with accurate vehicle dynamic state estimates at 100 Hz with 1 ms latency
[
32
]
. Complementing this, a Kistler Correvit S-Motion sensor enables slip-free measurement of longitudinal and transverse speeds, as well as
VSA
, making it ideal for driving dynamics tests. This sensor operates at 500 Hz, with an accuracy of
\pm 0.1^{\circ}
and a latency of 6 ms
[
33
]
. Furthermore, the dataset includes onboard sensor data from the test vehicle via a dedicated
Controller Area Network (CAN)
connection. The process of data extraction and synchronization is described below.

IV-
1
Data Extraction

The dataset was collected by driving the test vehicle in the test track
[
31
]
by five test drivers. The drivers performed a variety of driving maneuvers, including slalom, constant-radius turns, step steer, sine with dwell, and double-lane changes, to capture diverse vehicle dynamics and sensor responses. The majority of the data was recorded under bright weather conditions, while some recordings were taken during rainy conditions to add variability. The test conditions were designed to provide a comprehensive dataset, enabling research to improve
ADAS
functionalities through virtual sensing for vehicle state estimation.

IV-
2
Sensor Synchronization

The data obtained from various sensors must be synchronized to achieve a high-quality dataset. Both the ADMA and Correvit sensors obtain timestamps using
GPS
data. The latency for onboard sensor data is assumed to be negligible, with timestamps provided upon reception through a high-precision PTP service available through ADMA.
To ensure consistency across all sensor data, the sensor messages are down sampled to match the frame rate of the slowest sensor, which in this case is the onboard sensor data. For this, each onboard sensor data point is matched to the closest preceding ADMA and Correvit measurements, ensuring a synchronized and accurate dataset for further analysis.
The Correvit sensor measures velocity at the
Point of Installation (PoI)
. This velocity vector
{\bm{v}}
is transformed to the
CoG
through rigid-body transformations to achieve spatial synchronization

{\bm{v}}_{\text{CoG}}={\bm{v}}_{\text{PoI}}+\bm{\omega}\times{\bm{r}}\;,\;\beta_{\text{CoG}}=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{\text{CoG}}^{\text{y}}}{v_{\text{CoG}}^{\text{x}}}\right),

(15)

where
\bm{\omega}
denotes the angular velocity of the vehicle obtained from ADMA and
{\bm{r}}
the lever arm connecting the
CoG
and the Correvit sensor. State estimates for the ADMA data at
CoG
are precomputed and directly obtained from the sensor. In total, approximately 0.9 million data samples were collected at a rate of
50\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
.

The methodologies developed in this work are evaluated using the
ReV-StED
dataset. Inputs to the proposed methodology are obtained through onboard sensor data

{\bm{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{\text{s}},\delta_{\text{sw}},\dot{\psi},a_{\text{y}},p_{\text{br}},v_{\text{fl}},v_{\text{fr}},v_{\text{rl}},v_{\text{rr}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}},

(16)

where
v_{\textbf{s}}
denotes speedometer reading,
\delta_{\text{sw}}
the steering wheel position,
v_{\text{fl}},v_{\text{fr}},v_{\text{rl}},v_{\text{rr}}
the speeds of each wheel,
p_{\text{br}}
the brake pressure and
a_{\text{y}}
the lateral acceleration. The ground truth output for the
UAHL
framework consists of
VSA
measurements
{\bm{y}}
, from the Correvit sensor.
The data is divided based on a scenario level into training, validation, and test sets, with a split ratio of 70:10:20 continuous across the time domain.

V
Experiments and results

In this section, the experimental setup, implementation details, evaluations and ablation studies are discussed. The following questions are answered: (1) How to validate the proposed Hypothesis 1? (2) What is the performance improvement achieved by the
UAHL
architecture for virtual sensors? (3) Do hybrid machine learning architectures enhance reliability in estimation? (4) How does the virtual sensor perform during vehicle dynamics testing maneuvers?

V-A

Experimental Setup

V-A
1
Baselines

Three recent data-driven
VSA
estimation methods - Dynamic neural Networks
[
1
]
, ANN-SSE
[
20
]
, and RANN
[
2
]
were selected as baselines. To effectively compare the modified
ML
(Informer*) architecture, a vanilla Informer
[
9
]
model was also used for comparison. The models were implemented based on the descriptions in the corresponding papers, wherever the code was not publicly available.

V-A
2
Implementation details

The
ML
model has an embedding dimension of
d=512
and utilizes
H=8
sparse-attention heads. The encoder processes an observation window of
L=100
, while the decoder operates with a context window of
B=25
and a forecast window of
F=5
steps. The
ML
model was trained with a batch size of
b_{1}=128
and a learning rate of
lr_{1}=10^{-4}
using the Adam optimizer for a total number of
e_{1}=20
epochs. For the expert fusion,
\delta_{\text{th}}
was set at the 90th percentile of
\delta_{\text{ml}}
observed in the validation dataset, and
v_{\text{th}}
was set to
20\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{h}}^{-1}
.
The deep fusion network was trained with a batch size
b_{2}=1000
, layer dimensions
(6,20,10,1)
employing ReLU activation functions and a learning rate of
lr_{2}=10^{-3}
for
e_{2}=100
epochs.
The Gaussian fusion was trained based on the
GMR

[
29
]
. All models were trained on a single NVIDIA Quadro RTX 5000 GPU with 16 GB VRAM.

V-A
3
Evaluation metrics

Popular metrics such as
Mean Squared Error (MSE)
and
Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

[
4
]
are used to evaluate the performance of the proposed methods. Additionally,
Max Error (MaxE)

=\max_{i\in\{1,..,N\}}|y^{i}-\hat{y}^{i}|
metric is used to evaluate the potential worst-case scenarios for virtual sensor models. Here,
\hat{y}^{i}
and
y^{i}
represent the
VSA
prediction and ground truth for the
i
-th sample, respectively. Since some baselines and
VMMs
cannot forecast future estimates, all evaluations are conducted exclusively for
t=L
to ensure fair comparison. A large dataset, which includes numerous vehicle maneuvers with high lateral accelerations and diverse weather conditions, is expected to capture the main factors influencing
VSA
estimation. Thus, the effectiveness of the proposed uncertainty-aware hybrid architecture is evaluated using the
ReV-StED
dataset, which will be made publicly available.

V-B

To validate the proposed Hypothesis 1, the dependency between yaw rate residual
{e}^{\dot{\psi}}
and
VSA
residual

{e}^{\beta}

VMM-1

. The Pearson correlation coefficient
r
quantifying the linear relationship between two random variables and t-test statistic

was computed for these residuals. The
VMM-1
had

and
t^{*}=453
for the test dataset. The null hypothesis, which states that no correlation exists between the residuals, was rejected at a 99% confidence level, providing strong empirical evidence of dependency between the residuals. The residual plot in Fig.
3

VMM-1
has a linear relationship between yaw rate and
VSA
residual. This empirical validation of Hypothesis 1 supports Eq. (
10
) and demonstrates that in simple

, uncertainty estimation for

VI
Conclusion

The proposed
UAHL
architecture enables the design of a virtual sensor to estimate
VSA
with high precision while relying only on onboard sensor data. Our
ML
model employs a sparse-attention mechanism to effectively capture the vehicle’s state over a long history of measurements. Unlike data-driven
ML
methods,
UAHL
incorporates vehicle dynamics knowledge through
VMMs
, enhancing performance in “long-tail” scenarios. The uncertainty quantification for individual model estimates within the
UAHL
architecture enhances its reliability. The introduced hybrid fusion strategies efficiently exploit the uncertainty-aware estimates from the
VMMs
and
ML
model to outperform the state-of-the-art approaches in
VSA
estimation. The published
ReV-StED
dataset will also support future works in virtual sensors for vehicle state estimation.

The
UAHL
architecture currently limits the virtual sensor applicability to the test vehicle in the dataset. Future works will focus on understanding the transfer learning to different vehicles.
