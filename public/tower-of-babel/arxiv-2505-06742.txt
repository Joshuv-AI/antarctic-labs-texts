Ciliberto–Di Gennaro conjecture for sextic hypersurfaces
Ksenia Kvitko

Abstract
. The Ciliberto–Di Gennaro conjecture addresses the factoriality of three-dimensional nodal hypersurfaces, and their geometric properties. We prove this conjecture for hypersurfaces of degree 6 by adapting a recent technique due to R. Kloosterman.



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
of degree
d
that has only ordinary double points (nodes) as singularities. Such a hypersurface is called
nodal
. One could ask whether it is factorial or equivalently
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial. This property has important implications for the study of rationality (see
[
Che05
]
).
C. Ciliberto
and V. Di Gennaro formulated in
[
CDG04
]
the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
be a nodal hypersurface of degree
d
with at most
2(d-2)(d-1)
singular points.
Then one of the following holds:

(1)

X
is factorial;

(2)

X
contains a plane and has at least
(d-1)^{2}
nodes;

(3)

X
contains a quadric surface and has exactly
2(d-2)(d-1)
nodes.

Conjecture
1.1
was proved for
d=3
by H. Finkelnberg and J. Werner
[
FW89
]
, for
d=4
by
I. Cheltsov
and C. Shramov
[
Che06
,
Shr07
]
, and for
d\geqslant 7
by R. Kloosterman in
[
Klo22
]
. In addition, it was shown in
[
Che10a
,
Che10b
]
that a non-factorial nodal threefold
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
of degree
d
must have at least
(d-1)^{2}
nodes; moreover, if equality holds then
X
contains a plane.

In this paper we present a proof of the Ciliberto–Di Gennaro conjecture for sextic hypersurfaces using the method from
[
Klo22
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Conjecture
1.1
holds for
d=6
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
recalls basic results on the Hilbert function of
a graded ring
, including Macaulay’s and Gotzmann’s Theorems. Section
3
treats artinian Gorenstein rings and describes results on unimodality, which concerns the behavior of the Hilbert function. In Section
4
, we consider a homogeneous ideal giving artinian Gorenstein quotient ring and analyze the degrees of its generators. Here, the estimates on the values of the Hilbert function are given.
In Section
5
,
we follow an approach from
[
Klo22
]
to prove Theorem
1.2
. We give a notion of defect used to catch factoriality property and deeply connected to the Hilbert function of an ideal of
singularities
\text{Sing}(X)
.
Switching to algebraic methods, we split Kloosterman’s proof into several parts and fix the part that fails in our case. Specifically, let
X
be a three-dimensional nodal hypersurface of degree
d\geqslant 6
in
\text{Proj}(R)\simeq\mathbb{P}^{4}
and let
J\subset R
be a homogeneous ideal of
\text{Sing}(X)
.
Let
\{\ell=0\}
be a general hyperplane not passing through
\text{Sing}(X)
. By taking an ideal
\overline{J}
corresponding to this hyperplane section and possibly adding other homogeneous polynomials
f_{j}
of degree
d_{j}
,
where
d-1\leqslant d_{j}\leqslant 2d-4
, we can construct such an ideal
I\supset\overline{J}
in
S=R/(\ell)
that the corresponding quotient ring
S/I
will be artinian Gorenstein of socle degree
2d-4
. In other words, we obtain some local ring having symmetric Hilbert function
h_{I}(k)
for
k\leqslant 2d-4
and
h_{I}(k)=0
for
k\geqslant 2d-3
. Then a lower bound on
the number
\#\text{Sing}(X)
can be found in terms of the values
h_{I}(k)
. It turns out that
\text{Sing}(X)
contains a complete intersection of multidegree either
(1,1,d-1,d-1)
or
(1,2,d-2,d-1)
if
h_{I}(d-4)
equals at most
2d-7
. Otherwise, Kloosterman showed that
X
of degree
d\geqslant 7
has more nodes than it was supposed in Conjecture
1.1
. The consequent estimation on
\#\text{Sing}(X)
through the values of
h_{I}
and then through the Hilbert function of complete intersection ideal
I_{CI}
of multidegree
(1,2,d-2,d-1)
works perfectly in the case
d>7
. For
d=7
this lower bound is exactly
2(d-2)(d-1)
. So, there is only one bad option for values
h_{I}
(i.e. when all inequalities on
h_{I}(k)
become equalities). As the geometric analysis carried out in
[
Klo22
]
shows, this option does not realize. However, this analysis is not applicable for sextic hypersurfaces with more than one options giving
\#\text{Sing}(X)
less than
2(d-2)(d-1)+1
. According to the theorems on unimodality for such an ideal
I
as we work with, if
h_{I}(k)\leqslant h_{I}(k+1)
for some
k\leqslant d-4
, then
h_{I}(k+1)\leqslant h_{I}(k+2)
. Hence, we limit all possible bad sequences of
h_{I}(k)
to 2 cases and exclude them too by analyzing the dimension of an intersection of hypersurfaces of fixed degree
t
passing through the subscheme defined by the
t
-graded part of
I
.

2.
Preliminaries

Given the polynomial ring
S=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
and a homogeneous ideal
I\subset S
, the Hilbert function
h_{I}(k)=\dim(S/I)_{k}
measures the dimension of the
k
-th graded component of
S/I
. The Hilbert polynomial
p_{I}(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
asymptotically agrees with the Hilbert function for large values of
k
.

To understand the Hilbert function, we use a notion of so-called
Macaulay expansion
. Let
d\geqslant 1
be an integer, and let
h_{I}(d)=C
represent the value of the Hilbert function at
d
. The Macaulay
d
-expansion provides a unique representation of
C
in base
d

C=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\binom{i+\epsilon_{i}}{i},

where the coefficients
\epsilon_{i}
satisfy
\epsilon_{d}\geqslant\epsilon_{d-1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\epsilon_{1}\geqslant-1
. This expansion can be obtained inductively as follows. The number
\epsilon_{d}
is the largest integer such that
\binom{d+\epsilon_{d}}{d}\leqslant C
. The numbers
\epsilon_{i}
for
i<d
are the coefficients in the expansion of
C-\binom{d+\epsilon_{d}}{d}
in base
d-1
.

Using the Macaulay expansion of
C
for any positive integers
d
we define the functions
{}^{\langle d\rangle}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
and
{}_{\ast d}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
:

C^{\langle d\rangle}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\binom{i+\epsilon_{i}+1}{i+1},\hskip 10.0ptC_{\ast d}=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{d}\binom{i+\epsilon_{i}-1}{i-1}.

Note that
C\mapsto C_{\ast d}
and
C\mapsto C^{\langle d\rangle}
are increasing functions in
c
.

Definition 2.1
.

A sequence
\{h_{k}\,\mid\,k\geqslant 0\}
of nonnegative integers is called an
O
-sequence
if

h_{0}=1,\hskip 5.0pt\text{and}\hskip 5.0pth_{k+1}\leqslant h_{k}^{\langle k\rangle}\hskip 5.0pt\text{for all }k.

Theorem 2.2

(Macaulay
[
Mac27
]
)
.

The following are equivalent:

(1)

a sequence
\{h_{k}\,:\,k\geqslant 0\}
of nonnegative integers is an
O
-sequence;

(2)

there exists a polynomial ring
S
and a homogeneous ideal
I\subset S
such that
h_{I}(k)=h_{k}

for all
k
.

Corollary 2.3
.

If
h_{I}(N)=0
for some positive integer
N
then
h_{I}(k)=0
for all
k\geqslant N
.

Proof.

Indeed, by Theorem one has
2.2

h_{I}(N+1)\leqslant 0^{\langle N\rangle}=0
. Hence,
h_{I}(N+1)=0
and similarly
h_{I}(k)=0
for all
k>N+1
.
∎

Having that
h_{I}(d)<h_{I}(d-1)^{\langle d-1\rangle}
for the values of the Hilbert function, we can also obtain information on
h_{I}(d-1)
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
I\subset S
be a homogeneous ideal,
d\geqslant 2
an integer and
C=h_{I}(d)
. Then

h_{I}(d-1)\geqslant C_{\ast d}.

Moreover, if
\epsilon_{1}
is nonnegative then
h_{I}(d-1)>h_{\ast d}
holds.

Proof.

We have

h_{I}(d)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\binom{i+\epsilon_{i}}{i}

and
h_{I}(d)\leqslant h_{I}(d-1)^{\langle d-1\rangle}
from Theorem
2.2
. So, in terms of the Macaulay expansions the following inequality holds

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\binom{i+\epsilon_{i}}{i}\leqslant\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\binom{i+\epsilon_{i}^{\prime}+1}{i+1}.

After replacing
i+1
with
i
, the required condition is obtained

h_{I}(d)_{\ast d}=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{d}\binom{i+\epsilon_{i}-1}{i-1}\leqslant\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\binom{i+\epsilon_{i}^{\prime}}{i}=h_{I}(d-1).

∎

Remark 2.5
.

For small
h
we have the following Macaulay expansions in base
d
.

•

For
h\leqslant d
we have
\epsilon_{d}=\dots=\epsilon_{d-h+1}=0
and
\epsilon_{d-h}=\dots=\epsilon_{1}=-1
. Hence
h^{\langle d\rangle}=h
.

•

For
d+1\leqslant h\leqslant 2d
we have
\epsilon_{d}=1
,
\epsilon_{d-1}=\dots=\epsilon_{d-a}=0
,
\epsilon_{d-a-1}=\dots=\epsilon_{1}=-1
, where
a=h-d-1
. Hence
h^{\langle d\rangle}=h+1
.

•

For
h=2d+1
we have
\epsilon_{d}=\epsilon_{d-1}=1
and all other
\epsilon_{i}
equal
-1
. Hence
h^{\langle d\rangle}=2d+3=h+2
.

Applying Corollary
2.4
repeatedly yields

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
I\subset S
be a homogeneous ideal,
d\geqslant 2
an integer and
h=h_{I}(d)
. For
0\leqslant k\leqslant d
we have

h_{I}(k)\geqslant\begin{cases}\min(h,k+1)\hskip 65.0pt\text{if }h\leqslant d;\\
\min(k+(h-d),2k+1)\hskip 12.0pt\text{if }d+1\leqslant h\leqslant 2d;\\
2k+1\hskip 95.0pt\text{if }h=2d+1.\end{cases}

The following result will be used to detect the Hilbert polynomial of the ideal generated by
I_{d}
.

Theorem 2.7

(Gotzmann
[
Got78
]
)
.

Let
V\subset S_{d}
be a subspace in the
d
-graded part of
S
and
J\subset S
be the ideal generated by
V
. Set
C=h_{J}(t)
. If
h_{J}(d+1)=C^{\langle d\rangle}
then we have
h_{J}(k+1)=h_{J}(k)^{\langle k\rangle}
for all
k\geqslant d
. In particular, the Hilbert polynomial
p_{J}(t)
of
J
is given by

p_{J}(t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\binom{t+\epsilon_{i}}{t},

and the dimension of
V(J)
equals
\epsilon_{d}
.

3.
Structure of artinian Gorenstein rings

Here, we provide the theory for computations in artinian rings (see
[
Vas04
, Chapter 4]
for details).

Let
S
be the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
and let
I
be a homogeneous ideal of
S
such that
A=S/I
is an artinian ring. Then there is a decomposition of
A
into a product of local rings

A=A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{r},

and the Jacobson radical
\mathfrak{J}(A)
of
A
can be written as

\mathfrak{J}(A)=\mathfrak{m}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathfrak{m}_{r},

where
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
is the maximal ideal of
A_{i}
. In the lattice of ideals of
A
, sitting at the opposite end of
\mathfrak{J}(A)
there lies another distinguished ideal, the socle of
A
:

Definition 3.1
.

The
socle
of the artinian ring
A
is the annihilator of its Jacobson radical
\mathfrak{J}(A)
. It will be denoted by
\text{Socle}(A)
.

The socle of
A
can also be presented as a product

\text{Socle}(A)=(0:\mathfrak{m}_{1})\times\dots\times(0:\mathfrak{m}_{r}).

Definition 3.2

(
[
Eis13
, Proposition 21.5]
)
.

An artinian ring
A
over
\mathbb{C}
is called
Gorenstein
if one of the following equivalent conditions holds:

(1)

each
A
-module
(0:\mathfrak{m}_{i})
is generated by one element
s_{i}
;

(2)

each
A_{i}
-module
(0:\mathfrak{m}_{i})
is simple.

Remark 3.3
.

Simpleness of
M=(0:\mathfrak{m}_{i})
implies
M\simeq A_{i}/\mathfrak{m}_{i}
(see
[
Eis13
, Theorem 2.13]
). Hence, each annihilator of
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
is a 1-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{C}
.

Artinian Gorenstein rings have a wonderful property which we will refer to as Gorenstein duality.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
Hun99
, Theorem 9.1]
)
.

Let
A
be a
0
-dimensional Gorenstein ring
S/I
. Suppose that

A=A_{0}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{e},

where
A_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,e
, is graded component,
A_{0}=\mathbb{C}
, and
A_{e}\neq 0
. Then the ideal
I
has symmetric Hilbert function

h_{I}(k)=h_{I}(e-k)\hskip 15.0pt\text{for all }k=0,\dots,e.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
A
be a local artinian Gorenstein ring and
s
be a polynomial that generates its socle. Then the degree of the polynomial
s
is called the
socle degree
.

There are special cases when the socle and the socle degree can be found explicitly. We describe below rings arising from complete intersections of
n+1
hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. A homogeneous
ideal
I\subset S
is called a
complete intersection ideal
if it is generated by a regular sequence
(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
of homogeneous polynomials
f_{i}
. Let
d_{i}=\deg f_{i}
. Then the multidegree of complete intersection ideal is the sequence
(d_{0},\dots,d_{n})
. The quotient
ring
A=S/I
is called a
complete intersection ring
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})\subset S
be a homogeneous complete intersection ideal. Then the complete intersection ring
A=S/(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
satisfies the following properties.

(1)

The ring
A
is local.

(2)

The maximal ideal of
A
is generated by the images of
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
in
A
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
I_{CI}=(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
. Then the codimension of
A
equals
n+1
. So, the ideal
I_{CI}
defines an empty set in projective space or, equivalently, the origin in
\mathbb{A}^{n+1}
. For any ideal
I
in
S
, there is a natural bijection between the maximal ideals in
A=S/I
and the points
of
V(I)\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
.
Hence, there is only one maximal ideal in
A
.

(2)

Recall that a maximal ideal in
S
corresponding to a point
(p_{0},\dots,p_{n})
in
\mathbb{A}^{n+1}
can be written as
(x_{0}-p_{0},\dots,x_{n}-p_{n})
. Moreover, the image of
(x_{0}-p_{0},\dots,x_{n}-p_{n})
in
S
is again maximal ideal. Then the image of
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is among maximal ideals of
S
. Since
S
is local, it is the only maximal ideal.

∎

Proposition 3.7

(
[
BH98
, Proposition 3.1.20]
)
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian local ring.
If
A
is complete intersection ring then it is Gorenstein.

We use a result of Tate
[
MR70
, Theorem A.3(4)]
(see also
[
SS75
]
) that gives a concrete description of the socle of
A
.

Theorem 3.8

(Tate)
.

Let
I=(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})\subset S
be a homogeneous ideal such that
A=S/I
is both local and a complete intersection ring. Then
\text{\emph{Socle}}(A)
is generated by the determinant
det(Jac)
of the Jacobian matrix of polynomials
f_{0},\dots,f_{n}
.

Proof.

First, we show that
det(Jac)
annihilates the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
. For each
f_{i}
we have

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\cdot x_{j}=(\text{deg }f_{i})\cdot f_{i}.

Then we check that
det(Jac)
annihilates
x_{k}
for all
k
:

\text{det}\bigg(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\bigg)\cdot x_{k}=\text{det}\bigg(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{1}}\hskip 10.0pt\dots\hskip 10.0pt\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{k}}\cdot x_{k}\hskip 10.0pt\dots\hskip 10.0pt\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{n}}\bigg)=\\
=\text{det}\bigg(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{1}}\hskip 10.0pt\dots\hskip 10.0pt(\text{deg }f_{i})\cdot f_{i}-\sum\limits_{j\neq k}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\cdot x_{j}\hskip 10.0pt\dots\hskip 10.0pt\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{n}}\bigg)=0.

It remains to show that
det(Jac)
is the generator of the socle. If
\text{det(Jac)}=h\cdot\varphi
where
\varphi
is the generator and
h
is not a unit, then
h
lies in the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
. Therefore,
det(Jac)
should be equal to 0. Contradiction.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
I=(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})\subset S
be a complete intersection ideal, and let
d_{i}=\deg f_{i}
. Then the socle degree of
S/I
equals

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}(d_{i}-1).

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
I\subset S
be a proper homogeneous ideal and let
I\supset I_{CI}=(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
be a complete intersection ideal. Suppose both rings
S/I
and
S/I_{CI}
are artinian Gorenstein and of the same socle degree. Then
I=I_{CI}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.6
, the ring
S/I_{CI}
is local. Since
I\supset I_{CI}
, we have inclusion of the corresponding affine algebraic sets

V(I)\subset V(I_{CI}).

Here, the left hand side is not empty due to homogeneity of
I
and the right hand side is just one point. So,
S/I
is local.
Let
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{m}_{CI}
denote the maximal ideals of local rings
S/I
and
S/I_{CI}
, respectively. From Lemma
3.6
the generators of these ideals are
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
. Pick a nonzero element
G\in\text{Socle}(S/I)
. Since
G
annihilates all
x_{i}
in both rings, it is also an element of
\text{Socle}(S/I_{CI})
. Thus, the socles have the same generator which is the determinant of the Jacobian. The ideal
I
defines an empty set in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, so we need at least
n+1
linearly independent polynomials
g_{0},\dots,g_{3},\dots,g_{m}
,
m\geqslant n
, to generate
I
.

If
m>n
, then due to Theorem
3.8
all
(n+1)\times(n+1)
minors of the Jacobian matrix define elements of the socle which is a 1-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{C}
by Remark
3.3
. Therefore, these elements must be proportional. However, in this case all
g_{i}
,
i>n
, are linear combinations of
g_{0},\dots,g_{n}
. This contradicts our assumption that
m>n
, so we must have
m=n
.

Let us arrange the generators of ideals in ascending order of degree. Note that from the inclusion
(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})=I_{CI}\subset I=(g_{0},\dots,g_{n})
and Corollary
3.9
it follows that
\text{deg}\,g_{i}=\text{deg}\,f_{i}
. Indeed, since
f_{0}
is an element of
I
, we have

\deg f_{0}\geqslant\min\{\deg g_{0},\dots,\deg g_{n}\},

which is just
\deg f_{0}\geqslant\deg g_{0}
. If
\deg f_{1}<\deg g_{1}
, then there exist polynomials
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}
such that
f_{1}=\alpha_{1}g_{0}
and
f_{0}=\alpha_{0}g_{0}
. However, in this case
(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
is not a regular sequence. Hence,
\deg f_{1}\geqslant\deg g_{1}
. Similarly, if
\deg f_{2}<\deg g_{2}
, then
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
are linear combinations of
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
, and
(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
is not regular. Arguing in the same way, one can show that
\deg f_{i}\geqslant\deg g_{i}
. Then Corollary
3.9
implies
\deg f_{i}=\deg g_{i}
. Consequently,
I
equals
I_{CI}
.
∎

Now, consider the sequence of non-zero values of the Hilbert function
h_{I}(k)=\dim(S/I)_{k}
, where
S/I
is artinian Gorenstein of socle degree
e
. It is also called an
h
-vector
and denoted by
(h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{e})
, where
h_{0}=1
and
h_{e}=1
.

Definition 3.11
.

The
h
-vector
(h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{e})
is said to be
unimodal
if it is never strictly increasing after a strict decrease, i.e., the following condition is satisfied for some
1\leqslant k\leqslant e

h_{0}\leqslant h_{1}\leqslant\dots\leqslant h_{k-1}\leqslant h_{k}\leqslant h_{k+1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant h_{e}.

It is known that all
h
-vectors with
h_{1}\leqslant 4
and
h_{4}\leqslant 33
are unimodal (see
[
MNZ08
, Theorem 3.1]
). However, in case of
h_{1}\leqslant 3
there is even stronger restriction.

Theorem 3.12

(
[
Sta78
, Theorem 4.2]
)
.

Let
(h_{0},h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{e})
be a sequence of nonnegative integers with
h_{1}\leqslant 3
and
h_{e}\neq 0
. Then it is a
h
-vector of a Gorenstein ring if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

h_{i}=h_{e-i}
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant e
;

(2)

(h_{0},h_{1}-h_{0},h_{2}-h_{1},\dots,h_{t}-h_{t-1})
is an
O
-sequence, where
t=[\frac{e}{2}]
.

Corollary 3.13
.

There is no artinian Gorenstein ring with
h
-vector

(1,3,6,6,7,6,6,3,1)\hskip 10.0pt\text{or}\hskip 10.0pt(1,3,6,6,8,6,6,3,1).

Proof.

The sequence of the differences
(h_{0},h_{1}-h_{0},h_{2}-h_{1},h_{3}-h_{2},h_{4}-h_{3})
is
(1,2,3,0,1)
or
(1,2,3,0,2)
. Note that

2>1>0^{\langle 4\rangle}=0.

Therefore,
(1,2,3,0,1)
and
(1,2,3,0,2)
are not
O
-sequences by Theorem
2.2
. Consequently, there is no Gorenstein ring with
h
-vector

(1,3,6,6,7,6,6,3,1)\hskip 10.0pt\text{or}\hskip 10.0pt(1,3,6,6,8,6,6,3,1)

by Theorem
3.12
.
∎

4.
Estimates for values of Hilbert function

Recall that the
t
-graded piece of
S=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
corresponds to a set of hypersurfaces of
degree
t

in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Similarly, given a homogeneous ideal
I=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
, one could think of a set
|I_{t}|
of hypersurfaces of degree
t
passing through the subscheme
Z
defined by the generators
g_{i}
with
\deg g_{i}\leqslant t
. We will call such sets

Proposition 4.3
.

Let

be a homogeneous ideal in

\text{\emph{Bs}}|I_{k}|=\varnothing

\text{\emph{Bs}}|I_{k-1}|=\varnothing

Proof.

Suppose that

5.
Nodal hypersurfaces of degree 6

Kloosterman’s recent work
[
Klo22
]
significantly advanced our understanding of the Ciliberto–Di Gennaro conjecture for hypersurfaces of high degree (
d\geqslant 7
). In this section, we build upon his approach and extend these results to the case of degree 6.

We use the notion of defect to detect the factoriality property.

Definition 5.1
.

The
defect

\delta(X)
of a nodal hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
is a number defined by the formula

h^{4}(X,\mathbb{Q})-h^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q}).

Remark 5.2

(
[
Klo22
, Remark 3.5]
)
.

Any nodal hypersurface
X
satisfies the Poincaré duality property. In particular, there is an isomorphism
H^{4}(X,\mathbb{Q})\simeq H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
and therefore the Weil divisor class group
\text{Cl}(X)
has rank equal to
h^{4}(X,\mathbb{Q})
. On the other hand, the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem implies that
the Picard group

\text{Pic}(X)
is isomorphic to
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
. So, the defect
\delta(X)
measures
\text{rk}\,\text{Cl}(X)/\text{Pic}(X)
and it is zero only for factorial varieties.

The defect of a nodal hypersurface
X
is deeply connected to the number of singularities.

Definition 5.3
.

Let

X=\{F(x_{0},\dots,x_{4})=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}=\text{Proj}\,\mathbb{C}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}]

be a hypersurface. Then one can define the
Jacobian ideal
of
X
as

\text{Jac}=\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}F,\dots,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{4}}F\big)\subset\mathbb{C}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}].

Let
J
be the ideal of nodes, that is,

J=I(\text{Sing}(X))=I(V(\text{Jac})).

Then
J
is the saturation of
Jac
(see
[
EH06
, Exercise III-16]
for definition).

Proposition 5.4

(
[
Dim90
, Proposition 3.3]
)
.

Assume
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
is a nodal hypersurface of degree
d
. Let
J
be the ideal of nodes. Then

\delta(X)=\#\text{\emph{Sing}}(X)-h_{J}(2d-5),

where
\#\text{\emph{Sing}}(X)
is the number of singular points of
X
.

Now, studying factoriality of nodal hypersurfaces we can switch from a geometric point of view to an algebraic one and investigate the behavior of Hilbert functions of zero-dimensional subschemes
in
\mathbb{P}^{4}=\text{Proj}(R)
.

If
X
is not factorial, then by Remark
5.2
it has a positive defect, i.e.

due to Proposition
5.4
. Moreover, we have
h_{J}(2d-4)>0
. Otherwise,
h_{J}(k)=0
for all

due to Corollary
2.3
and so
p_{J}(k)=0
. By taking a general hyperplane section
of
\text{Sing}(X)
, one obtains an empty set in
\mathbb{P}^{3}=\text{Proj}(S)
. Assume the hyperplane is given by an equation
\{\ell=0\}
. Without loss of generality, we can choose

. So, we get an artinian coordinate ring

, where

is

of

. In particular, the ring

is isomorphic
to

, where

and

is the image of

in

.
Then we construct an ideal

Let

.
Set

S_{k},\hskip 123.0ptk>2d-4.\end{cases}

Note that the socle of

(1,4,6,6,6,6,6,4,1)
. Proposition
4.3

\text{Bs}\,|I_{3}|

d_{2}\leqslant d_{1}\leqslant d_{0}\leqslant d_{-1}\leqslant 3
. Then we compute the socle degree of
I_{CI}
which is

\sum\limits_{i=-1}^{2}(d_{i}-1)\leqslant 8<2d

and get a contradiction.

∎

Proposition 5.8
.

If
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a nodal hypersurface, then the scheme
\text{\emph{Sing}}(X)
is reduced.

Proof.

Let
U=\mathbb{A}^{n}
be an affine chart containing
P\in\text{Sing}(X)
. Then in this chart the
hypersurface
X
is given by an equation
\bar{F}=0
, where
\bar{F}\in\mathbb{C}[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
. Since
P
is a node, we can find an analytic neighborhood of
P
and local coordinates
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,n}
such that the equation
\bar{F}=0
is

(5.3)

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{2}=0.

By considering equation (
5.3
), we find all derivatives

\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}\big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{2}\big)=2z_{j}

and conclude the Hessian matrix

\bigg(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{j}z_{k}}\big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{2}\big)\bigg)

is invertible at
P
.

Now, it remains to show that an intersection

\{\frac{\partial\bar{F}}{\partial y_{1}}=\dots=\frac{\partial\bar{F}}{\partial y_{n}}=0\}

is transversal at
P
, i.e. the gradient vectors

(\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{F}}{\partial y_{1}\partial y_{i}},\dots,\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{F}}{\partial y_{n}\partial y_{i}})

are linearly independent or, equivalently, the Hessian matrix
(\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{F}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}})
is non-degenerate at
P
. Note that

\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{F}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\big(\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{F}}{\partial z_{l}\partial z_{k}}\frac{\partial z_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial z_{k}}{\partial y_{j}}+\frac{\partial\bar{F}}{\partial z_{k}}\frac{\partial^{2}z_{k}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}\big).

Since
P
is singular, all
\frac{\partial\bar{F}}{\partial z_{k}}(P)
vanish and we have

\bigg(\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{F}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(P)\bigg)=\bigg(\frac{\partial z_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}(P)\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{F}}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}}(P)\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\partial z_{k}}{\partial y_{j}}(P)\bigg).

Thus, the left hand side is a non-degenerate matrix as a product of invertible ones. So, each point
P\in\text{Sing}(X)
is a transversal intersection of hypersurfaces and therefore
\text{Sing}(X)
is reduced.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
X
be a nodal hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
which is given by an equation
F=0
. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
be a regular sequence. Suppose the scheme
\text{\emph{Sing}}(X)
contains a complete intersection

\Sigma=\{f_{1}=f_{2}=\dots=f_{n}=0\}

such that

F=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}f_{i}

for some homogeneous polynomials
\varphi_{i}
. Then
\varphi_{i}(P)=0
for all
P\in\Sigma
and all
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

Consider an affine chart
U
containing
P\in\Sigma
. Without loss of generality, we can assume
that
U=\{x_{0}\neq 0\}
.
Denote by

\bar{f_{1}},\dots,\bar{f_{n}}

the polynomials
in
\mathbb{C}[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
defined as

\bar{f_{i}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=f_{i}(1,y_{1},\dots,y_{n}),

where
i=1,\dots,n
and
y_{1}=\frac{x_{1}}{x_{0}},\dots,y_{n}=\frac{x_{n}}{x_{0}}
. Note that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a 0-dimentional subscheme due to the regularity of the sequence
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
. Thus, it remains 0-dimensional in the affine chart
U
.

First, we show that
\bar{f_{1}},\dots,\bar{f_{n}}
form a local system of coordinates at some neighborhood
of
P\in\Sigma
.
It suffices
to show that gradient vectors

(5.4)

(\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{1}}\bar{f}_{i},\hskip 5.0pt\dots,\hskip 5.0pt\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{n}}\bar{f}_{i}),\hskip 50.0pti=1,\dots,n,

are linearly independent at
P
. Suppose the contrary. On the one hand, a matrix made up of
vectors (
5.4
)
has rank less than
n
at the point
P
. On the other hand, this matrix is the Jacobi matrix of the 0-dimensional subscheme
\bar{\Sigma}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
given by
\bar{f_{1}},\dots,\bar{f_{n}}
. So, the point
P
must be singular on
\bar{\Sigma}
. However,
\bar{\Sigma}
is reduced as a subscheme of
\text{Sing}(X)
by Proposition
5.8
and therefore it must be smooth. Thus we get a contradiction. Then
\bar{f_{1}},\dots,\bar{f_{n}}
are local coordinates.

Now, let
\bar{F}
and
\bar{\varphi_{i}}
the polynomials corresponding to
f
and
\varphi_{i}

in
U
.
Then the equation of
X
in the chart
U
can be written as

\bar{F}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\varphi_{i}}\bar{f_{i}}=0.

Moreover, for its singular point
P\in\Sigma
and local coordinates
\bar{f_{1}},\dots,\bar{f_{n}}
at
P
the following condition is satisfied

(5.5)

\dfrac{\partial\bar{F}}{\partial\bar{f_{1}}}(P)=\dots=\dfrac{\partial\bar{F}}{\partial\bar{f_{n}}}(P)=0.

For every
i=1,\dots,n
, the calculations show

\dfrac{\partial\bar{F}}{\partial\bar{f_{i}}}(P)=\bar{\varphi_{i}}(P)+\dfrac{\partial\bar{\varphi_{i}}}{\partial\bar{f_{i}}}(P)\bar{f_{i}}(P).

However,
\bar{f_{i}}(P)=0
because
P\in X
is a point where all
f_{j}
vanish simultaneously. Then
\bar{\varphi_{i}}(P)
must be equal to
0
due to condition (
5.5
) implying that
\varphi_{i}(P)=0
for all
i
and all
P\in\Sigma
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

If
X
is not factorial, then due to Lemma
5.5
the subscheme
\text{Sing}(X)
contains a complete intersection
\Sigma
of multidegree either
(1,1,d-1,d-1)
or
(1,2,d-2,d-1)
. Consequently, we have
\#\text{Sing}(X)\geqslant(d-1)^{2}
if
\Sigma
has multidegree
(1,1,d-1,d-1)
or
\#\text{Sing}(X)=2(d-2)(d-1)
if
\Sigma
has multidegree
(1,2,d-2,d-1)
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{4}
be a regular sequence which generates an ideal
I(\Sigma)
defining
\Sigma
. Thus it is generated by all homogeneous polynomials vanishing on
\Sigma
. Let
F=0
be the equation of
X
in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
. Therefore, there is an inclusion
F\in I(\Sigma)
and, in particular, for some polynomials
\varphi_{i}
one has

F=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{4}\varphi_{i}f_{i}.

By Proposition
5.9
, each
\varphi_{i}
vanishes on
\Sigma
. Hence, all
\varphi_{i}
are also elements of
I(\Sigma)
, i.e.

\varphi_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}g_{ij}f_{j}

for some polynomials
g_{ij}
.

Now, suppose that

\deg f_{1}\leqslant\dots\leqslant\deg f_{4},

implying
\deg f_{j}>2
for
j>2
and
d-2\leqslant\deg f_{3}\leqslant\deg f_{4}
. To express
F
of degree
d
as

F=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{4}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{i}g_{ij}f_{i}f_{j}

requires that

d=\deg F\geqslant\deg f_{i}+\deg f_{j}\geqslant 2\deg f_{j}.

Comparing with

2\deg f_{3}\geqslant 2d-4>d=\deg F,

we conclude that
\deg f_{j}\leqslant 2
and
j\leqslant 2
.

Therefore,
F\in(f_{1},f_{2})
, and there are two possibilities
for
X
.
If
\deg f_{1}=\deg f_{2}=1
,
then
X
contains a plane. If
\deg f_{1}=1
and
\deg f_{2}=2
, then
X
contains a quadric surface.
∎
