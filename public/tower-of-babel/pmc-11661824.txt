Applied deaf aesthetics toward transforming deaf higher education
Authors: Joanne Weber, Denyse Hayward, Michael Skyer, Sarah Snively
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeaf aesthetics is a theoretical framework we actualized to enhance interactions in deaf education, particularly via multimodal pedagogy and curricular experiences. Prior research illustrates that deaf aesthetics are desired by deaf teachers and students who are deaf; however, most instructional-delivery formats lack these supports. The present mixed-methodology, multi-method case study is an empirical evaluation of how deaf aesthetics contributed to the process of redesigning a course, including major revisions to instructional slide decks (e.g., PowerPoint, Google Slides, Prezi). The research question we examined is: How can instructional designers and university educators effectively design and use deaf aesthetics and multimodal curricula and pedagogies to prompt and sustain educational interactions with deaf or deafblind learners and teachers?Keywords: deaf, deafblind, access, Instructional Technology

Our study examined curriculum materials for a course about educational inclusion at the University of Alberta. The course involved one deaf faculty member, one deafblind student, and 120 other nondeaf, nondisabled students. Data-collection methods included: (a) novel surveys delivered to all class members about the redesigned materials, (b) in-depth interviews with the deafblind student, and (c) analytic reflections written by the deaf instructor, and more. Data were analyzed by a research team including three deaf members. Our findings support the assertion that deaf aesthetics in curriculum benefits teaching and learning for all involved personnel: The deafblind student reported enhanced learning and reduced cognitive load. The deaf faculty member enjoyed the process. Likewise, most nondeaf students reported learning gains. Overall, we posit that deaf aesthetics are useful guidelines for improving multimodal educational interactions.

Curricula are usually designed for students who can hear and speak and see; however, these materials are seldom compatible with the learning needs of deaf and deafblind students (Freeman, 2001; Lang & Meath-Lang, 1995). This leads to unsatisfying outcomes. By unsatisfying, we mean subpar learning and cumbersome teaching (Cherryholmes, 1999). From this premise, we investigated deaf aesthetics, a data-grounded theoretical framework that claims to mitigate dilemmas associated with incompatible curriculum in deaf education (Skyer, 2012, 2021).

Our study operationalized deaf aesthetics to empirically document how changes in curriculum affect learning and teaching in one deaf higher education context. The present case study is interventionary. It used mixed methodology, including qualitative methods focused on individual experiences (e.g., in-depth interviews and reflective writing) and quantitative measurements with broader impacts, samples, and vantages (e.g., course surveys and the analyses derived from them). In short, we found that deaf aesthetics resolved the dilemma of incompatible curricula that frustrate deaf students and burden deaf instructors; furthermore, data show that redesigned curricula were appreciated by and beneficial for nondeaf and nondisabled students.

First, we review the literature review and reveal the scarcity of research on this topic, then, to address the lack of data, later in this manuscript we offer a comprehensive theoretical and methodological framework about these and related subjects. We also explore empirical data and interpret key findings about deaf aesthetics in action. Presently, we pause to address varying nomenclature about disability-forward design principles in pedagogy and curriculum, describe affordances and limitations of each, and show why deaf aesthetics was appropriate for our case.

Deaf aesthetics is a flexible suite of design principles intended to enhance educational processes in places where deaf students and faculty interact (Skyer, 2012, 2021), which is distinct from Universal Design for Learning (UDL). Deaf aesthetics theory explicitly addresses insufficiencies of UDL, because UDL is seldom universal and frequently problematic for deaf learners (Burgstahler, 2015; Kourbetis et al., 2016). UDL inadequately incorporates the situated needs and desires of deaf and deafblind people in education; in particular, despite lofty aspirations, UDL has not led to universal accessibility or the elimination of barriers and reduction of biases against deaf, deafblind, and deaf and disabled students in collegiate settings (Weber & Skyer, 2022; Skyer, 2023).

We started our study by asking how and why faculty and instructional designers in higher education could design curricula and pedagogy to be situated for deaf and deafblind learners (Gee, 2004). The literature we reviewed (see below) suggested that deaf aesthetics is a pragmatic approach to building deaf education curriculum. It centers educational ethics and seeks to redress historical dilemmas in deaf education. The theory finds roots in Vygotsky et al.’s (1993) assertion that deafness is fundamentally biosocial and interactionary. Deaf education results in positive adaptations when pedagogy and curriculum explicitly uplift deaf people and are oriented toward the inherent positives of deafness (Bauman & Murray, 2014; Skyer, 2023). Deaf aesthetics recognizes the fundamental importance of Deaf Cultures and sign language, but transcends the sociocultural paradigm’s focus on language.

Deaf aesthetics centralizes multimodality, including its basic concern for redressing historical ills in education (Kress, 2010), such as pedagogies and curricula that disempower deaf learners and decenter expert deaf instructors (Ladd, 2022). While multimodal overall, deaf aesthetics asserts that visual modes are primary for all sighted deaf learners as tactile modes are for all deafblind learners. As a situated theory, deaf aesthetics aligns with neuroaesthetics theory, the latter of which prioritizes how the designs of materials have observable, measurable effects on psychological cognition and neurological processing, including how perception, emotion, and memory combine to produce learning in real social contexts (Gee, 2004; Meng & Liang, 2022).

Before we settled on deaf aesthetics, we evaluated three other approaches: Universal Design for Learning (UDL), Disability Accommodations (DA), and Deaf Friendly Design (DFD). We summarize them and then describe why we rejected them. First, UDL focuses on accessibility, which, in education sciences, generally refers to how well or not learners can directly perceive curriculum or pedagogy using their intact senses (Gargiulo & Bouck, 2021). We emphasize that deaf aesthetics does not interpret accessibility as an end-goal, which UDL does. Differently from UDL, we see access as a fundamental precursor of educational interactions and learning experiences (Skyer, 2021). Deaf education, we propose, must include but supersede basic access. While we return to issues of UDL throughout this manuscript, here we illustrate two examples that show why basic access is not enough to transform deaf educational inequities. First, while sighted deaf students can physically see any image in an instructional slide deck, we think that a well slide with careful attention paid to formatting may increase emotional resonances and enhance fine-grained dimensions of learning. Second, while deafblind students may have basic access materials presented in large type, we think that the content of body copy, font choices, layout, and spacing all contribute to learning in ways that current UDL deafblind research has not examined in depth (Nelson et al., 2022).

Another approach to basic access are Disability Accommodations (DAs). Very often in K-12 or higher education, deaf, deaf and disabled, and deafblind people are offered DAs, which comprise small changes that make curriculum and pedagogy accessible. DAs are usually determined in concert, including documented rationales from medical professionals that are certified and implemented by learning institutions. DAs requested are not always granted. DAs granted are not always what students require to thrive. DAs are generally minor and may not align with or foster social justice. For example, imagine a deaf student working in a group project with all nondeaf colleagues who has a sign language interpreter DA. This mediated experience can hardly replicate an immersive project with all deaf, signing peers who share comparable tacit and experiential needs and traits. In the disability design paradigm, Hamraie (2017) shows that DAs may counterintuitively reinforce political oppression or marginalization. While we often find accommodations helpful, they are seldom transformative.

These instructions, infantilize deaf learners, are biased to favor the hearing-speaking teacher, and position spoken and written language as authoritative knowledge modes. The DFD guidelines show that deaf student knowledge is subjugated below the knowledge of the hearing teacher. They entirely lack sociopolitical context and neglect the profound power differentials and ethical quandaries extant in deaf classrooms. DFDs, like “chaining” (Humphries & McDougall, 2000) and “translanguaging” (Skyer, 2023), are deaf pedagogies that position multimodal, visual, and tactile materials as subordinate to spoken or written language modes. Lastly, we are not aware of any empirical research on DFDs or research about DFD that is transformative in orientation to social justice.

As our work progressed, it became increasingly clear that deaf aesthetics offered a productive starting point for curriculum design. Throughout this article, we focus on identifying and resolving the dilemmas and limits of UDL, DAs, and DFD. As a theoretical orientation, deaf aesthetics impels us toward the uncontroversial and ethically imperative goal: providing an equitable and socially-just curriculum across deaf higher education (Kress, 2010; Skyer, 2021). Simply put, we cannot merely tweak an oppressive system. We must transform it.

Materials and methods

The following summary of research is purposefully narrow. We focus only on studies prompted by the COVID-19 pandemic (2019–2024), during which a flurry of research was disseminated about online learning for deaf students. These yielded valuable insights. Below, we offer five concise themes.

First, online learning designs for deaf students must build on the unique ways that deaf students learn (Graham et al., 2023; Alcazar et al., 2016). Sighted deaf learners are predisposed toward visual learning processes, which radically reorganize cognition, information processing, retrieval from memory, which generalize to learning (Graham et al., 2023; Mohammed, 2021). Many online courses are not designed effectively for deaf learners and underdeliver on visual linguistic inputs, such as sign languages and captions (McKeown & McKeown, 2019; WFD, 2020). Likewise, online learning courses seldom accommodate deaf learners who sign, read, write, and sometimes speak numerous languages (Karampidis et al., 2021; Ngubane & Adigun, 2024).

Secondly, social–emotional aspects of deaf education are critical to the success of online learning (Graham et al., 2023; Probert et al., 2023). The literature also reports complex challenges consisting of heightened potential for reduced interactions with peers, instructional staff for deaf online learners (Bowman & Crowe, 2023; Baroni & Lazzari, 2020; Graham et al., 2023; Alshawabkeh et al., 2021; McKeown & McKeown, 2019; Alsadoon & Turkestani, 2020; Taylor & Yuknis, 2023; Aljedaani et al., 2023; Aljedaani et al., 2021; Yuwono et al., 2022). Snoddon and Madaparthi (2023) found that social mediation, which involves dynamic meaning-making processes, sustains interpersonal connections and plays a vital role in an online teaching involving sign languages. The widespread presence of sign language deprivation coincides with socio-affective learning; this challenges deaf students who are learning a spoken/written second language (i.e., English). Language deprivation compounds the difficulties of relaying content, even when sign language interpreters are available on the screen (Aljedaani et al., 2023; Mallory et al., 2023).

Third, deaf educational Learning Management Systems (LMS) must be flexible and customizable to improve their applicability for deaf learners (Aljedaani et al., 2023). For instance, if sign language interpreters are shown on a tile that is too small, signed videos may be inaccessible; likewise, videos with inaccurate auto-generated captioning disadvantage deaf learners, as do technologies that rely on sonic cues to track speakers, but not signers (; Aljedaani et al., 2023; Alsadoon & Turkestani, 2020; Alshawabkeh et al., 2021; Graham et al., 2023; Mallory et al., 2023; McKeown & McKeown, 2019; Yuwono et al., 2022). These and a range of other technical difficulties result in extended wait times, missed connections, and reduced interactions between instructors and deaf students (Taylor & Yuknis, 2023). Thus, rigid teacher-centric LMS structures may not work well for all deaf students.

A fourth theme is attentional fatigue in online learning. Small screens are often densely packed with images and unrelated decorative elements in online environments; these increase visual “noise” and fatigue for deaf students; likewise, unnecessary background music or actual noise generates listening fatigue (Baroni & Lazzari, 2020; Luft & Brochu, 2023; Probert et al., 2023). Negative effects for deaf learning correspond with split attention (Aljedaani et al., 2023; Luft & Brochu, 2023). When attention is split, for example, between two areas on the screen, the deaf student may have difficulty integrating multiple sources of information, which negatively affects content uptake and increases cognitive overload (Luft & Brochu, 2023). Complex images, such as instructional diagrams, require further study and often require concentration. If a student is concentrating on decoding an image, they may miss interpreted or direct instructional content in sign language (Behm et al., 2015; Luft & Brochu, 2023). Without careful design and attention to details, deaf learners will be fatigued if they must simultaneously navigate multiple windows, videos, interpreted content, and captions within online class gatherings (Luft & Brochu, 2023; Taylor & Yuknis, 2023; Hayward & Rohatyn-Martin, 2016).

Not all is lost. Our final theme shows that useful multimodal strategies positively impact deaf learning, especially when LMS designs are configured to the needs of deaf and deafblind students (Astuti et al., 2023; Batanero et al., 2019). Skyer (2023) reports that multimodal content delivery is enhanced when both teachers and students are deaf. Specific video strategies, such as posting professionally captioned videos or superimposing just-in-time comments or questions in video format lectures, support educational, emotional, and social interactions for deaf learners. Moreover, “flipped” or asynchronous presentations allow students to preview and review course content (Graham et al., 2023; Taylor & Yuknis, 2023). Pre-recorded lectures with bilingual-bimodal minimums allow deaf students to pause and take notes as opposed to experiencing negative effects of split attention (Graham et al., 2023). Curated LMS platforms enhance digital navigation and allow deaf students in finding materials quickly and easily (Graham et al., 2023). Overall, while many challenges exist in deaf online learning, research is uncovering strategies that benefit deaf learners and deaf instructors; to this theme, we now turn our full attention.

Research context: research question, purpose, and positionality

Our study reports findings from a project involving extensive reformatting of instructional material for a teacher-education course about disability inclusion called EDPY 301: Other Ways of Seeing Inclusive Education. The purpose of the present research was to inaugurate and then evaluate extensive revisions to the initial curriculum, which operationalized the theory of deaf aesthetics. These changes were intended to enhance overall accessibility and to enable the deaf faculty member to introduce a deaf aesthetic infused multimodal approach to teaching the deaf/blind student and their nondeaf peers.

During the Winter term of 2022, completely revised materials for the same course were presented by a deaf faculty member (Weber) to 116 students, including one deafblind student (Snively). Typical participants of EDPY 301 are youthful undergraduates who can reliably hear, speak, see, move, and think. They are literate and experienced adequate language acquisition opportunities in a socioculturally-dominant language (e.g., English). People who are deaf or deafblind cannot reliably hear, speak, or see, and may have complex mobility or healthcare needs. Likewise, deaf and deafblind people are often raised without robust language acquisition opportunities and whose language learning experiences may be cumbersome due to the pervasive effects of language deprivation (Glickman & Hall, 2019).

Said differently, EDPY 301’s initial curricula didn’t match the characteristics of all the people who used it. Beyond our case, it is important to resolve similar dilemmas, including necessary basic research about how learning happens in deaf online higher education with sign languages or tactile sign languages. For example, sign languages are spatial and embodied, while digital contexts offer visual benefits for deaf learners, these parameters may disadvantage deafblind learners, whose tactile needs may be excluded. Likewise, as a spatial language, online learning “flattens” sign languages into 2D representations. In consequence, available LMS technologies that house deaf pedagogies and curricula may not adequately draw on the intact abilities and biosocial experiences that are endogenous to deaf and deafblind people (Skyer, 2021).

Here, we pause to reflect on how our positionalities affected this work. Each author of this paper contributed specific knowledge and experiences to the project. Hayward was the original creator of the curriculum for the course: EDPY 301 (Hayward, 2019). Hayward is hearing (nondeaf) and has extensive experience in research about language and communication disorders, including clinical and experimental research about deafness and online learning. In response to the dilemma that course materials were incompatible for deaf and deafblind people, Weber became project’s leader and primary curriculum (re)designer. Weber contributed data sources in the study’s qualitative arm. Weber is deaf herself and a veteran teacher across primary, secondary, tertiary, and graduate deaf education. Skyer originally conceived deaf aesthetics in 2012, and continues to examine its theoretical and empirical implications in pedagogy and curriculum studies in deaf education. Skyer has taught for almost 20 years, across primary, secondary, tertiary, and graduate deaf education. Skyer contributed to data analysis and interpretation and report writing. Snively is a deafblind undergraduate student who enrolled in EDPY 301. Snively contributed data in the qualitative arm of this study and offered valuable insight assisted curriculum analyses and designs under the direct supervision of Weber and Hayward.

Principally, we evaluated how and why education materials designed for the nondeaf were unsuitable for deaf and deafblind people. Based on our literature review, prior research, and teaching experiences, we determined that while these groups share similarities, the differences are profound and include diverging needs of deaf people from deafblind people (Garciá-Fernandès, 2020; Nelson et al., 2022), divergences between the educational needs and desires at the population and individual level of deaf and disabled learners (Gargiulo & Bouck, 2021), and divergences of subjectivity and positionality (e.g., students vs. faculty; Graham & Horejes, 2017). Given this complexity, our research aimed to unify heterogeneity to determine, in broad strokes, the kinds of representations that superseded minimal accessibility/inclusivity for deaf and deafblind students and instead support sustained educational interactions by deaf and deafblind people in university coursework.

Theoretical framework—four themes of deaf aesthetics

Research about deaf aesthetics is situated for higher education (Raike et al., 2014; Skyer, 2023). Here, we present a synthesis of findings about deaf aesthetics (Boote & Beile, 2005) based on previous research, which we used to understand our data and findings. Weber and Skyer (2022) developed a novel theoretical framework comprising seven categories of empirical and theoretical studies about digital curriculum in deaf higher education. Here, we extract four considerations most applicable to the present study.

Deaf axiology amplifies deaf aesthetics and deaf ethical values in education

Deaf axiology describes the impacts of values about deafness on teaching and learning. It contains both deaf ethics and deaf aesthetics. Deaf axiology interrelates aesthetic choices made by teachers and considers these as inputs for educational interactions that may enhance ethical outcomes for deaf learners, such as learning (Skyer, 2021). Visuality and multimodality are primary conduits for deaf aesthetics. As such, they are an ethical approach to adapting and constructing the aesthetics underlying deaf pedagogical praxis (Kress, 2010; Skyer, 2021). Deaf axiology values the unique multimodal discourse ideologies (MDIs) and artistic preferences of deaf teachers and learners, who often prefer signed languages and visual communication. MDIs in deaf education support biosocial adaptive learning (Kusters & O’Brien, 2017; Skyer, 2012, 2021; Thoutenhoofd, 2010; Vygotsky et al., 1993); meaning, that linguistic, cultural, and social benefits in deaf students’ development emanate from positively valuing deafness in teaching and curriculum (Bauman & Murray, 2014; Hauser et al., 2010; Skyer, 2021).

Deaf MDIs provide accessible curricular pathways (Skyer, 2021). Researchers from Scandinavia (Raike et al., 2014) and Greece (Karipi & Kourbetis, 2021) empirically substantiate benefits of applying deaf educational aesthetics and MDIs. Recent innovations supersede basic accessibility to include those that are fundamentally interactive and which enhance visual learning processes. For instance, images or videos can be superimposed with subtitles, icons, transcripts, symbols, pictograms, and text descriptions that can also be manipulated by deaf end-users to customize their learning experiences (Boukouras & Kourbetis, 2014; Raike et al., 2014). Deaf aesthetics includes racial, gendered, and dis/ability representations in deaf curriculum (Skyer & Cochell, 2020). Deaf aesthetic design projects in educational spheres, we think, are effective because they’re often led by teams of deaf pedagogy experts and artists who are deaf themselves. In this way, deaf novices are taught by deaf exemplars using aesthetic principles in deaf-affirmative, culturally appropriate pedagogies.

Deaf educational aesthetics differently affect learners, teachers, and curriculum designers (Easterbrooks & Stoner, 2006; Raike et al., 2014; Skyer, 2012). Deaf pedagogues and students prefer sign languages to be centralized, inclusion of graphic icons for curricular navigation, and when equal epistemic “weight” is afforded to text, sign, and other imagery (Skyer, 2021). Deaf students prefer interactive sign language functions and minimalist, multimodal designs (Raike et al., 2014). Instructional designers can increase clarity, construct visual metaphors, explicate implied elements of academic texts, and enhance the overall utility of visual or graphic tools (Skyer, 2023). These functions clarify relationships and demonstrate unity among parts. In curricula, deaf aesthetics support efficacious information exchange and facilitate interactions to be more beautiful and ethical (Cherryholmes, 1999).

Universal design for learning is inadequate for deaf learners

Universal Design (UD) purports common solutions for diverse people; its goal is full disability accessibility (North Carolina State University, 1997). UD standards for accessibility emerged alongside the US Americans with Disabilities Act (US Department of Justice, 1990), which aimed to address inaccessible employment, housing, and public spaces (Hamraie, 2017). UD focused on tangible architecture and industrial designs, but has since expanded to include educational web design (Hamraie, 2017). UDL is a subset formed on the supposition that everybody requires adaptation to learn. UDL aims to meet all educational access needs for all people. UDL research includes cognitive psychology and neuroscience and has affected how designs are implemented and under whose direction (CAST, 2021). The National Deaf Center (NDC, 2021) emphasizes that deaf students use UDL to capitalize on strengths including visual perception, sign language, and multimodal/multimedia supports. To maximize benefits of UDL, NDC recommends using:

(1) Multiple Means of Representation include images and texts to illustrate ideas, including multiple, interactive digital formats, that allow learners to customize video captions, which, NDC suggests, enhance perception, symbolic-linguistic expressivity, and comprehension.

(2) Multiple Means of Action and Expression require multimedia formats, including paper and electronic formats, and multimodal expression (writing, sign, verbal), which, NDC states, support embodied learning, instructional scaffolding, advanced cognition, discursive expressivity, and executive function.

UDL has seen wide uptake in deaf education. The Canadian Association of the Deaf/Association des Sourds du Canada wrote guidelines for deaf educational UDL. Summarizing the current state and future of deafblind education, Nelson et al. (2022) posit that deafblind students require UDL. Ideally, UDL should minimize physical effort and fatigue and support simple and intuitive designs, as cognitive overload is associated with the excessive labor deaf students exert when wrangling with inaccessible content (Skyer, 2021).

UD began in the 1940s, reached maturity in the 1970s, and hit a high-water mark in the 1990s. If UDL was truly universal and truly fostered equitable designs for all disabilities and combinations thereof, the world would have been transformed in these 80 intervening years. Seeing as how injustices remain and endure demonstrates that the UDL paradigm is insufficient. UDL is built on Western medical, scientific, civil rights, and consumer ideologies; which have been contested by disability activists who argue that disability is social, relational, and spatial (Hamraie, 2017). Concluding this section we are emphatic: total UDL may prove impossible (Shakespeare, 2014).

UDL digital accessibility levels identify but may not resolve inequities

McKeown and McKeown (2019) propose three levels of accessibility for digital deaf curricula. Level-One Access (L1A) comprises how deaf learners access LMS courseware shells where curricula are housed. L1A includes internet stability, high-resolution video and images, good lighting, and reduced visual noise. Level-Two Access (L2A) presidents content in multiple formats including audiovisual slide decks and machine-readable files with archival storage. The McKeowns place language in Level-Three Access. L3A focuses on language needs and abilities of deaf learners, including which modes are included and how they are used. For instance, visual aids, plain language, self-study activities, and videos should be shown in local sign languages. For McKeown and McKeown sign language is a final consideration. In opposition, Weber and Skyer (2022) have argued for the reverse; that sign language access is a minimum foundation (e.g., Level-Zero Accessibility), atop which other levels are built.

Applying the McKeown framework, Mohammed (2021) found that deaf learners experienced inaccessibility at all levels. Deaf students struggling with language deprivation, including those originating from the Global South in Mohammed’s study, found digital content problematic (L1A). Despite high-impact potential, sign language was not included. Other opportunities appeared inclusive (e.g., captioned videos with transcripts) but were inaccessible for deaf students without well-developed reading skills (L3A). Some digital materials lacked visual inputs (L2A), and digital textbooks were dominated by spoken/written modes that curtailed natural sign languages—the McKeown’s L3A and Weber and Skyer’s L0A were both missing. Despite positive potential, deaf learners in Mohammed’s study were explicitly barred from using sign language receptive online environments and were frustrated that environments were also devoid of social or expressive sign language.

Multimodality, multimedia, and new literacy change what is considered learning

Deaf aesthetics in curriculum design is resonant with theories of New Literacy, visual literacy, and multimedia instruction (Jenkins et al., 2013; Kress, 2010). Building on social literacy theory, the New London Group (1996) shows that literacy and learning are trans-modal. Literacy is a participatory activity set within communities of agents who use and re/mix media to creatively explore multiple modes to express and consume knowledge. Visual literacy is valued by deaf researchers and deaf teachers of deaf students (Kuntze & Golos, 2021; Skyer, 2021). Curricula for deaf/deafblind learners of all ages should have unique design properties, including multilingualism and multimodality, and should support visual literacy and bimodal-bilingual language acquisition (Kuntze & Golos, 2021).

Studies of multimodality in deaf education suggest that artistic exercises using visual media support online learning (Bauman & Murray, 2014; Hunter, 2015; Karipi & Kourbetis, 2021; Kourbetis et al., 2016). However, research also shows that a lack of coordinated involvement from deaf educators and deaf social organizations results in insufficient cohesion in the digital environments in deaf education (Skyer, 2023). Skyer (2021) compares modes to atoms (e.g., color, gesture, shape) and multimodal assemblages to molecules (e.g., memes, website layouts). Skyer (2022) writes:

In addition to a common respect for visual curricular and pedagogic design, multimodal theorists and deaf educators share a focus on the ethics of increasing access and empowerment and the reduction of exclusion or elimination of oppression. Multimodality illuminates the ethics of pedagogical choices about modes relative to [deaf] students and their situated learning needs (p. 353).

Deaf learners with other disabilities benefit when digital curricula use multimodal elements including audio, visual, tactile, braille code, simplified text, and described images. Text and sign language that coexist alongside images and videos positively support deaf children’s enjoyment of reading (Gentry et al., 2004). Research about deaf students’ motivation shows that learning is enhanced in multimodal environments which allow deaf students to primarily access content in sign language alongside illustrated conceptual dictionaries (Karipi & Kourbetis, 2021). Hladík and Gůra (2012) recommend hybrid digital documents with multimedia content (text, audio recording, sign language videos) as effective for teaching deaf students’ foreign languages.

Multimodality increases access to and interaction with educational texts in deaf curriculum. Kourbetis (2013) developed standards for accessible digital learning, later implemented by Kourbetis et al. (2016) who built e-books combining translations of written text using sign language, subtitles of the original text on the sign language videos, videos with navigational aids, and audio recordings for students with residual hearing. Mich et al. (2013) studied how deaf children use animated web pages; they found visual aids and text simplification facilitated deaf children’s reading. To enhance comprehension, they use “intelligent dynamic feedback” (p. 35). Video-based sign language navigation supports deaf signers when searching the web and using hyperlinks (Fajardo et al., 2010). Multimodal approaches are interactive, a digital extension of the shared reading project (Delk & Weidekamp, 2001), a commonly cited evidence-based “best practice” (Moore & Mertens, 2015).

Summary

These four themes suggest a primary need for multimodal discourse ideologies (MDIs) to guide deaf curriculum using deaf aesthetics. MDIs support a range of language modes alongside a comprehensive suite of communication modes (e.g., color, font, layout) (Skyer, 2023; Weber & Skyer, 2022). Including multiple modes is not enough; there must be a purposeful alignment and interaction of modes for deaf and deafblind people, including signed and tactile languages (e.g., American Sign Language, ProTactile, etc.) (National Deaf Center on Postsecondary Outcomes, 2021; Skyer, 2021). While prior studies mainly examine the learning needs of deaf and deafblind youngsters, there is a dearth of comparable empirical research in higher education (McKeown & McKeown, 2019; Mohammed, 2021; Raike et al., 2014). Scant research has analyzed how deafblind people explore multimodality in advanced coursework. In contrast to the literature, we found evidence that deafblind students are creative users of multimodal materials, including visual media, provided they are designed well. These are gaps we aim to address empirically.

Methodology

Mixed methodology and research questions

Our multimethod, mixed-methodology study is pragmatic and innovative in orientation to deaf education (Mertens, 2010). First, we summarize the pragmatics of mixed methodologies, then we examine the methods we combined (multimethod design). Mixed-methods research characterizes studies where quantitative and qualitative methods are employed together for specific purposes (Creswell et al., 2011). We juxtaposed quantitative and qualitative data because both provide insights into operationalizing deaf aesthetics but at different levels and scales. Overall, we worked to avoid a shallow form of mixed-methods research (Mertens, 2010). It is important to note that a rigorous research ethics review was conducted and permission was obtained for all aspects of the research study.

Mainly, we wanted to know how deaf university faculty as instructional designers create, develop, or revise curricula to enhance equity for people who are deaf and deafblind. As discussed, we determined that deaf aesthetics theory was a probable approach to our goals, including increasing beauty and decreasing harm via curricula (Cherryholmes, 1999). Multimodality, in turn, helped us focus on educational ethics and the politics of transformation (Kress, 2010). Methodologically, deaf aesthetics oriented our choices and aligned our inquiry, methods, and theories; this is because it interrelated sub-components, such as interactions between teachers, learners, and course materials (Skyer, 2021). To answer the following research question, we identified a few large and several subordinate dilemmas that emerged when the course materials were reformatted. Because of the array of dilemmas, we opted for a mixed-methodology approach using a multimethod design, which is recommended for deaf education research projects examining teaching and learning concurrently (Easterbrooks & Stoner, 2006).

In combination, these subjects surfaced the following research question, which we explore empirically in subsequent sections.

How can instructional designers and university educators effectively design and use deaf aesthetics and multimodal curricula and pedagogies, to prompt and sustain educational interactions with deaf or deafblind learners and teachers?

The study consisted of four components. Component A was a comparative qualitative analysis of Hayward’s original materials and Weber’s redesigned slide decks. Component A focused on Weber’s reflections about the design process. Component B included an in-depth qualitative interview with Snively, who was also a primary participant. It focused on the needs of the deafblind undergraduate preservice teacher about the reformatted materials. Component C involved both a quantitative survey with all class members and an additional qualitative midpoint evaluation by Snively who conducted a formal analysis of the curriculum roll-out and discussed additional changes with Weber. Lastly, Component D comprised a large-scale quantitative survey given to all participants, who were undergraduate preservice teachers enrolled in the course. On successful completion, all students reflected on the new curriculum and pedagogy. The surveys in Components C and D leaned toward quantitative methods and required the construction, testing, distribution, and subsequent analysis of data from online surveys. These surveys were an attempt to add a “Large N” feature to an otherwise “Small N” study, represented best by Components A and B. In combination, each dataset allowed us to triangulate our claims and corroborate our analyses (Marshall & Rossman, 2016).

Multimethod design

Beyond mixing methodologies, we also used a multimethod design. It was centered on the following qualitative case study elements: Weber’s direct participants’ observations of class events, Weber’s in-depth interviews with Snively, and Weber’s analytic memo-writing about every stage and step (Creswell et al., 2011; Lindlof & Taylor, 2011; Marshall & Rossman, 2016). These methods combined created abundant data. We used them to empirically explore our theoretical hunches and to revise our approaches when our hunches were not evidenced in the data. Through them we gained a deeper, broader understanding of Snively’s experiences in higher education courses and the impact of the reformatted curricular materials to her learning and engagement.

Case study research methods

We digress for a word on the merits and requirements of case study methods in deaf education, as they relate to our study design. Easterbrooks (2006) supports the use of case studies in deaf education to examine in-depth dilemmas from small samples that can offer insight into larger dilemmas. Easterbrooks’ core recommendations include that they (a) triangulate data using multiple sources (See: Components A,B,C,D above), (b explain the context in which the case occurred (See: Research Context, above), (c) overtly analyze how the researcher and the research subject align (See: Theoretical Frame and Positionality, above), and (d) most importantly, researchers must offer a compelling answer to Stake’s (2005) classic question: “What is this a case of?”.

Our case is defined as follows: one example of how deaf aesthetics is operationalized in a higher education context in a bid for educational equity. Our case study describes the processes we used in sufficient detail so as to support others attempting similar work. We also provide outcomes about the end results for three classifications of people—deaf faculty researcher, deafblind student, and a broader nondeaf undergraduate student population.

Case studies in deaf education are increasing in frequency and rigor (Enns, 2017). A benefit of deaf case studies is the particularization of deaf-centric knowledge at local sites and specific, often idiosyncratic contexts (Skyer, 2021). The authors of this study have a wealth of direct experiences (as students and faculty and researchers) to support the assertion that current online tools in higher learning can be positively impacted by the inclusion of deaf aesthetics, but these supports are also limited and in need of additional research. The section later in the paper called “Leveraging our Experiences” therefore draws on the personal, qualitative experiences of the three deaf authors of this study. This concluding accounting aims for particularization our our site, participants, and local contexts (Enns, 2017); however, if readers deem our analyses sufficient, they may find that our recommendations are transferable to other nonlocal sites and populations (Marshall & Rossman, 2016).

EDPY 301 is a required undergraduate-level course on inclusive education in the teacher education program. Class enrollment was 116, all of whom had access to reformatted materials. Of them, 21 students signed up to participate in the study. One primary participant was the deafblind student (Snively), and the remaining 20 participants had no hearing or vision issues. Additional characteristics are shown below.

Component A’s qualitative understanding of design required in-depth analysis about Weber’s individualistic understanding of how and why the slide decks needed to be redesigned. Weber used her own experiential knowledge of deafness and higher education, alongside the theories and bodies of evidence we have summarized. Components C and D were the most “mixed” of the three methods. They required both an in-depth, individualized vantage point about this particular deafblind student’s understanding of the course and materials in conjunction with a wide-angle analysis of the course revision by all members of the class.

Snively contributed points of data to both the larger quantitative datasets and smaller qualitative datasets. Procedures and data analysis

Reformatting materials—Components A and B

Weber was assigned to teach EDPY 301 for a class including Snively. Weber immediately recognized that the original course materials were not deafblind accessible. Digital slide-deck presentations were then reformatted using Skyer’s (2012) work about deaf aesthetics as a guide for pedagogical and curricular design. Since Weber is deaf, a veteran teacher, and an artist, she relied on all aspects of these experiences to guide her work. Using both formal and intuitive design experiments, she developed a set of tentative rules to update slide aesthetics. For instance, the text on each slide was to be enlarged to a 30-point font, adjusted to one concept per slide, and each slide included an image to reinforce the concept. The reformatting of the original materials required a team effort consisting of graduate students, sign language interpreters, a technology coach, and an undergraduate student who did the captioning of the ASL videos. Throughout the process, Weber wrote extensive analytic memos to describe the main and subordinate processes of design.

The new slides were then used in elicitation tasks involving the deafblind student, who provided feedback through surveys and interviews about the practical instructional efficacy of the theoretical deaf aesthetic principles. After course closure, Weber and Snively discussed how the revised course materials impacted Snively’s learning. This interview was video recorded and transcribed (from ASL to English). Weber selectively transcribed key passages, which were verified by Snively for accuracy.

Survey instruments—Components C and D

We developed, tested, refined, distributed, and analyzed data from two novel surveys. The questions corresponded to Skyer (2012) propositions about the existence of six deaf aesthetic categories: (a) multiple languages and modes, (b) use of still imagery, (c) format and layout, (d) kinetic imagery, (e) font and typography, and (f) user interface and interactivity. These are outlined in Appendix A. The survey questions focused primarily on language modes, formatting and layout, still imagery, and font and typography. Two surveys were provided at different times.

Mid-course survey with primary research participant

The deafblind preservice teacher (Snively) responded to a survey as the primary participant in this case study. A follow up interview between Weber and 4 investigated survey responses in depth. The Snively evaluated the extent to which the reformatted presentations conformed with the deaf aesthetic design principles. The survey (see Appendix B) had 15 questions. The first question established that the respondent was deaf and blind. The next 13 items were administered using a 5 point Likert scale with 1 = strongly disagree, and 5 = strongly agree. The remaining question (Q 14) invited qualitative comments and observations.

End-of-Course Survey with Deafblind and Nondeaf Class Participants—The end-of-course survey questions were broad in scope and intended to determine if and how the new slides affected learning interactions for nondeaf students. The survey (see Appendix C) had 16 questions. The first four addressed demographics: enrollment in course, hearing and sighted status, language usage, and indication of having requested accommodations for other disabilities during the course delivery. The next 11 used a 5-point Likert scale with 1 = strongly disagree and 5 = strongly agree. The last question (Q 16) invited written comments and observations.

All students completed the end-of-course survey that sought to capture their experiences with the new presentation materials, which were intentionally bilingual (American Sign Language and spoken English), and multimodal, using visual images, videos, and other multimodal stimuli. Beyond the deaf aesthetics angle, we wished to see if these inclusions had a positive effect on nondeaf student interactions, as prior research shows that multimodality encourages participants to examine and reflect on realistic artifacts of teaching and learning in inclusive education environments (Barton, 2014). Findings are discussed below.

Results

Our case study findings begin with an analysis about the reformatted materials. The COVID-19 pandemic was contemporaneous with our study. It affected the research in the sense that all coursework was not only redesigned, but also then separately adapted to suit the online modality. For instance, reformatted materials were presented to students as integrated recordings, including the use of Prezi software and hosting on YouTube. All records were archived in the university LMS called eClass, which is a Moodle-based platform. The initial design approach was minimalist, featuring a video of Weber signing in ASL alongside still imagery that supported the concepts being taught.

Approximately halfway through the course, students requested that additional materials be made available, such as PDF files, copies of other presentation materials, and in alternative formats. After further discussion with the technical and production team consisting of an educational technology consultant, the editor of the videos, sign language interpreter, captioner, it was determined that the use of Prezi did not support the additional requests and concerns voiced by many nondeaf students in the class. Thus, midway through the semester, the course material delivery shifted from Prezi. Subsequent recordings were developed in YuJa, another video recording management system that better addressed the additional concerns expressed by the students in the class.

Interview with the primary study participant to review mid-course surveys

Weber, as course instructor and research coordinator, conducted an open-ended 1-hr interview with Snively to discuss her responses to the survey designed to address the use of the deaf aesthetics. Snively elaborated on her responses, which would be otherwise constrained by the Likert scale items. Her qualitative description of personal experiences in response to the reformatted materials allows us to gain a situated understanding of her personal perspectives and experiences with the reformatted materials.

The survey provided to the deafblind student was administered midway through the course as a checkpoint to see if the formatted materials supported her learning, and determine what other changes could be beneficial. Since she was the sole respondent in that survey, only her responses were noted and further elaborated in an interview after the course marks were submitted. The end-of-course survey was provided to the deafblind and nondeaf students. Likert scale items were totaled and converted into percentages indicating responses from the participant pool.

Snively’s responses about deaf aesthetics

Overall, the deafblind student responded “strongly agree” (the highest ranking) to 10 of the 13 Likert items. (Appendix B shows the full survey.) For the remaining three items, the student selected agree (2) or somewhat agree (1). Likert items were grouped by category (see Appendix A). Some intriguing analytical themes are analyzed below. These are organized using the quantitative survey categories, but also include data from qualitative interviews to expand on key themes.

Language modes (Items 2–6)

Evidence from our case study supports Weber and Skyer’s (2022) claims about language being Level-Zero Access. Survey items focused on the use of American Sign Language (ASL) on the screen in juxtaposition with captions, text, and audio language modes. The deafblind student strongly agreed that the reformatted materials presented sign language and spoken language equally, and that such equality was pedagogically effective. This meant that she did not have to resize windows or move windows around to position the interpreter on top of text and images in order to access the content. This reduced cognitive load. Snively could press play and the signing space was available without any additional adjustments. This experience is equitable, as for nondeaf students, they, too, must simply press “play” on audio/video files, such as YouTube, to simultaneously access multimedia files, with minimal effort. This design is in accord with Skyer and Cochell’s (2020) finding about the equipotentiality of ASL and English.

The deafblind student strongly agreed that the signing space was enlarged adequately. Another deaf aesthetic principle expounds on the importance of featuring a competent signer, capable of faithfully rendering the content. While the deafblind preservice teacher strongly agreed that the deaf instructor was a competent signer, however, sign language content was not always available through the LMS. Snively responded that they “somewhat agree” in response to Item 5. When asked to elaborate as to why this issue was later amplified in the interview where Snively illustrated that the aesthetic design feature of sign language was important, however, that it was inadequately embedded in all digital formats and contexts, including LMS navigation. Nevertheless, the deafblind subject strongly agreed that sign language modes, alongside other multimodal language representations (e.g., text, captions, print, voice), were essential for her learning.

Layout and design (Items 7–10)

These items focused on not the inclusion of text but the positioning of texts, in relation to images and the video recordings of the deaf instructor. The deafblind student indicated learning benefits emanated from screen spaces were designed to be sparse, clean, and uncluttered. In line with Raike et al. (2014), visual minimalist designs were helpful for the deafblind learner. The size of the signing screen was a unique feature of the reformatted presentation, as, in contrast to the original materials that featured no signing, the deaf instructor occupied half of the screen and was positioned beside the text and image, appearing in the same window (Figure 1).

Text (Items 10–12)

Text items focused on the size of fonts and placement of text (above the image in most cases). The deafblind preservice teacher strongly agreed that enlarged fonts was a semiotic resource; furthermore, that typographic considerations, such as color, size, and typeface (bold, italic, etc.), were educational. Some indicated hierarchies of texts and others highlighted various meanings in the content. The deafblind participant strongly agreed that customizing font size, shape, spacing, and other elements were advantageous. When jargon was rewritten in plain language, Snively found some texts reductive. When asked to clarify, she explained that “Print language [should be] written plainly but not reductively so.” Snively also strongly agreed that the reformatted materials should avoid hard-to-navigate long quotations or dense paragraphs on slides.

Visual aids (Item 14)

The survey item about Visual Aids item focused on the suitability of images as correlated with text and signing. The availability for multimodal access is a critical feature of deaf aesthetics, which suggests that visuals are not ancillary but central to deaf student learning. We wanted to know if the same was true for our deafblind participant. The participant strongly agreed that visual aids in the form of images, infographics, diagrams, lists, and tables supported her comprehension. The deafblind participant indicated that salient educational features were presented for the most part. In the interview, she indicated that images did not always reflect the text or what was presented in the context. For this reason, they selected the Likert item “agree” for Item 14; future research should examine this in more detail: what kinds of images are beneficial (and which are not) to support deafblind students’ learning in higher education?

Interviews with the deafblind student

Snively described the issues they had encountered in previous online classes provided throughout their undergraduate studies. The student spoke at length about the value of pre-recorded lectures. The selection taken from the transcript of her interview with the deaf instructor is provided below:

In a virtual class, I’d have to attend the lecture given in a synchronous learning environment. At that time, the class management system (for instance, Zoom) would provide a recording of the instructor and their slides. Then, I’d have to wait until the class was over and for the recording to be posted three or four days later. Then the sign language interpreters would create a recording as an overlay to the original recording. They would increase their screen size when adding their interpretation as a layer to the recorded presentations. Then I’d have to download the final recordings and enlarge everything so I could see the interpreter even more. Then during the viewing of the post recorded material, I would decipher the images, and follow the interpreter and view the slides at the same time. I would have to stop frequently during the viewing of the recording and rewind and go forward in order to make notes and to ensure my own understanding. A one hour lecture easily became a five to six hour viewing when it was finally in my hands near the end of the week. This meant I was nearly a week behind my classmates in accessing and learning the material. The preformatted materials in the form of pre-recorded lectures saved me hours of time.

Juxtaposing deafblind and nondeaf student responses

The end-of-course survey involved nondeaf students and the deafblind student. Its purpose was to find out how all students experienced learning interactions with the reformatted curriculum. Given the visual-forward designs of deaf aesthetics, we assumed that fully sighted, fully hearing students would experience curricula differently than deaf people (such as Weber), the deafblind student (Skyer). Interestingly, we found a large degree of concordance; that is, the majority of the nondeaf students responded similarly and to the deafblind student, which is to say they largely responded favorably to the new curricula. We do so by illustrating a key topic and by indicating data from the survey that resonates with the theme and the survey prompts we designed.

A closer look at the data revealed elements of concordance as well as discordance. While some data suggest a high degree of agreement between the deafblind student and the hearing and sighted students, there were also elements denoting a wider variance. In essence, while some deaf aesthetics designs were widely beneficial, others were only benefits for deaf and deafblind people. While our sample is small, varied responses were common. Attention is therefore given to two data-based juxtapositions: 1) data that present the least variance (high agreement) between the deafblind student and the nondeaf/nonblind survey respondents and 2) data that present the greatest variance (low agreement) between the deafblind student and the other (nondeaf/nonblind, or hearing/sighted) survey respondents. Discussion

We discuss two major data-based findings, presented in conjunction with references to the literature. Finding one focuses on applying our ideas to general deaf/deafblind education and reflects on the human labor involved and places our research into a practical context. The second describes particularities of our experiences as teachers and designers and researchers during the COVID context in which this study took place. All themes address the need for a transformative approach to deaf education using mixed-methodology data and multiple case study methods.

Deaf aesthetics and learning

Traditionally, deaf and deafblind students are not well served in digital learning environments (McKeown & McKeown, 2019; Mohammed, 2021). Deaf aesthetics are theorized as having the potential to increase educational equity and create a space for inclusion of deaf and deafblind students. This study highlighted a common experience of disabled people in having to expend more time and more effort and tolerate more dilemmas, such as schedule disruptions, learning fatigue, and increased administrative tasks, in order to complete the same tasks required for other students or professors in terms of study, work, and recreation (Hamraie, 2017).

In colloquial deaf studies, this is often expressed colloquially as the “deaf tax.” Similar “disability taxes” affect other communities of disabled people and generally represent additional labor or cognitive resources that are required for disabled people and deaf people to access what others take for granted. In our study, the deafblind student noted special “deafblind taxes” that commonly required additional labor, for example securing interpreters or disability accommodations that were not always provided, as our data show. These “taxes” bring on more fatigue and require additional time or labor (Hayward & Rohatyn-Martin, 2016; Luft & Brochu, 2023). Following the redesigned course materials, the deafblind student found significantly reduced time that was required to engage in construct-irrelevant tasks (e.g., “taxes”) in order to make way for construct-relevant tasks associated with the course material.

The consistent indication that around 50% of other students responded positively to deaf aesthetics suggests that there may be a wide-ranging, perhaps, visceral overall impact of multimodality on all forms of self-directed learning, regardless of hearing/vision/dis/ability statuses. Notable differences exist between ocularcentrism and audiocentrism, and multimodal discourse ideologies, yet a consistent and marked support for multimodality from survey data, indicates that perhaps most post-secondary students require self-directed multimodal learning (Olivier, 2020). Multiple modes such as text, fonts, images, sign language, and captioning all allow for transformation (changes within a mode), and transduction in which “semiotic material is moved across modes, from one mode (or set of modes) to another mode (or set of modes)” (Kress, 2010, pp. 125, 139).

Skyer (2023) explores the effectiveness of multimodal transductions created by post-secondary instructors using sign language, image, video, and text with deaf students. Similarly, Tytler and Prain (2022) have suggested that multimodal transduction, that is the ability to “link, confirm and expand similar and different meanings across modes” (p. 1), is a fundamental component for all student learning. In our study, a positive response (66%) to the captioning feature alongside ASL videos and spoken voice-overs supports multimodal language learning. In a related sense, the extremely high positive response (90%) to whether pre-recording “saved” time suggests that while this is true for the deafblind student, this may also be true for all students who are engaged in self-directed learning.

Olivier’s (2020) model of multimodal learning includes considerations for delivery (institutional multimodality), mode of cognition (individual multimodality), mode of the environment (instructional multimodality), and mode of communication (interactional multimodality). While this study focused exclusively on instructional multimodality, nearly all participant responses indicated a higher degree of comfort and ease in using deaf aesthetic designed materials. Finally, the focus on instructional modality only might explain why the positive responses (agree/fully agree) to the items related to student engagement (55%) and enhancement of learning (47%) were mixed for nondeaf students. More research is needed to consider how the deaf aesthetic can be applied to the other levels of multimodality within multimodal learning, in particular interactional multimodality (Olivier, 2020).

Lack of agreement about reduced text on slides, relationship between image and text, enlarged space dedicated to sign language users suggest access friction between audiocentric (nondeaf) and ocularcentric (deaf) people. Access friction implies favoring one set of access needs over another set (Hofmann et al., 2020). The presence of access friction is not surprising as deaf aesthetics are built on multimodal discourse ideologies and ocularcentrism, which centrally position sign languages and visual designs. Furthermore, the principles associated with deaf aesthetics suggest that UDL conventions, DA modifications, and DFDs may not always apply in every case. In short, the claim to design that is universal for all may be an ideological construct rather than a universally applicable reality (Hamraie, 2017). Such ideological constructs are informed by language ideologies, cultural and social expectations, and oppressive constructs of deaf (audism) and disabled persons (ableism) (Skyer, 2021).

Pre-recorded lectures required careful attention to and coordination of deaf aesthetic principles that can easily involve a large team including the deaf instructor, ASL interpreter, captioning assistant, and technology courseware. What we suggest here, along with Table 4 and 5, noted elsewhere, is that the deaf aesthetics re-design process is rather involved and should not be mistaken with a veneer of adaptation. Our process entailed a complete transformation of our pedagogy and curricula; further, that the depth of our revision work was commensurate with the depth of need for revision. It is interesting to note that Snively largely supported improved visual design, which, while intended mainly for deaf people, appears to also benefit this deafblind student. This is notable, since it is in contradiction to the literature, which suggests that visual materials are of secondary or minimal importance to blind and deafblind people (Gargiulo & Bouck, 2021; Nelson et al., 2022).

Leveraging our experiences within the COVID-19 context

Platforms like Zoom, Canvas, and Blackboard offer many useful tools for teachers and students; however, they are also limited in deaf education in important ways. Here, we focus on Zoom—the tool that was the de-facto standard for most video-enabled coursework during the initial onset of the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., 2020–2023). For deaf end-users, Zoom centralizes visual accessibility, which is a major asset, and can be used creatively. But Zoom also defaults to assumptions of an able-bodied, hearing-speaking end-user who has adequate cognitive and attentional and language resources, and adequate internet access and hardware, as well as all needed fine motor movements to deftly apply its tools—these presumptions are not universal among college students and are not constant for all deaf people, and this creates precise dilemmas for sub-groups.

Zoom uses small tile-like frames that can feature signers or speakers (SorS), such as teachers or students doing classroom presentations. Concurrently, Zoom shows a bias for large frames featuring audiovisual (AV) presentation materials, which is especially true in default recording settings, which further shrink tiles where signers may appear. In higher educational settings, AV presentations usually consist of slide decks (e.g., PowerPoint, Google Slides), dynamic screen-shares (such as YouTube or TikTok video content or desktop projections), alongside image documents (such as PDFs or static images), all of which can be concurrently leveraged as learning objects. Meanwhile, there is always the necessary labor of “working the chat box,” which includes different language modalities and directionalities. While these options offer access for some sighted deaf people, it also makes accessing sign language content difficult for students who are deaf with low vision, and it can prove impossible to navigate all content concurrently for deafblind persons.

Zoom is customizable but within precise parameters that, again, are designed with hearing/speaking able-bodied norms in mind. For example, Zoom’s designers prefer to have a single “slider” that is used to reduce or increase the relative proportionality of AV and SorS content. This customizability is helpful in that end-users can resize content to their preferences (i.e., end-users may employ the “slider” to enlarge AV materials); however, doing so concurrently reduces the proportionate space for SorS content. There is also the dilemma of large patches of empty black space, which may be a poor use of limited screen resources.

In our teaching (and for our learning), we have found that while Zoom is customizable in many ways, it does not currently support changing how these major tiles are configured on the screen. The limitations of Zoom are even more profound for people with complex disabilities and can inhibit accessibility or offer a constrained, ableist learning experience for deafblind people. Another major design issue that deaf people face using Zoom involves adequately capturing the nuances available in the facial grammar associated with sign languages. Deaf end-users require a larger space for a signer—which is often a sign language interpreter, who must share the already-crowded screen space layout. While the “Pin” feature for Zoom allows deaf end-users to enlarge the interpreter screen, it concomitantly results in reducing the proportionality available to the instructor or other students. Likewise, “Multi-Pin” is not a default option—but it is often desired by deaf students who wish to have their instructors and interpreters on equal “visual” footing.

Prior research about deaf education suggests that “divided visual attention” creates new learning demands and requires more cognitive load (Mather & Clark, 2012). Therefore, deaf end-users of online courses most likely have even more domains to juggle, such as visually attending to multiple, simultaneous presentations, personnel, and formatting, meanwhile trying to learn the content that is shown in curriculum and other learning objects. The dilemma may be mitigated by having more than one computer monitor or a tablet with a monitor at the same time; but not all deaf or deafblind people possess two computers or multiple monitors. Furthermore, deafblind people in Zoom environments, we surmise, have even more complex cognitive demands placed on them when learning in a Zoom-based context.

The result is that the onus for this additional educational labor is placed entirely on the deaf or deafblind end-user. Sighted deaf persons must visually “zigzag” back and forth between the interpreter tile, the instructor tile, and the multimedia learning content, as well as juggle the tools for the chat box, reactions, captions (which may or may not be accurate), and a myriad of other customization functions—which all co-occur with the practical demand of learning the content and maintaining focus, when computer alerts to email or other programs may fight for dominance of attention.

Very often, as teachers and learners and researchers, we have experienced feeling lost in place during the lecture, or fear missing valuable information while making minor adjustments. Increased visual and cognitive fatigue is also common. Also, the constant requirement of movement between the sign language interpreter, text and image, and so on may contribute to cognitive overload and fatigue, thereby necessitating hours of additional review on the part of the deaf or deafblind student. Listening and visual fatigue in deaf students adversely impacts the ability to learn, engage, and participate in classroom settings (Hayward & Rohatyn-Martin, 2016; Luft & Brochu, 2023). Despite these challenges, we have also been able to decouple our initial apprehension of new tools and have seen how powerful and helpful they can be—when well designed, that is. We are hopeful that more empirical research will increase the benefits and decrease potential harms, as Zoom (and similar) formats appear to be here to stay.

Limitations of the study

At the outset, we recognize that our (case) study by itself is not final evidence that deaf aesthetics is a superior theory or final framework for all deaf education, but we do find sufficient measures of evidence to support the claim that additional empirical study about deaf aesthetics is warranted. Our study confirms many aspects of the theoretical model and moves toward an increasingly-refined empirical examination of its efficacy in varied contexts and with diverse personnel. Further study is warranted with other sites, personnel, and methods for research, including other data sources and analytic frameworks.

More granularly, the survey instruments need further development and testing. The redesigned materials could also be taught by different deaf faculty and presented to more and different deaf and deafblind students, likewise other students with different disabilities or students with no disabilities. Other design iterations by other deaf faculty would be beneficial. Even as a case study, our sample size of one deaf instructor and one deafblind student is small. Additional iterations, even several iterations of the same or similar studies in other sites and with different personnel, might yield significantly different results. Moreover, more explicit connections between deaf aesthetics and multimodal discourse ideologies and their impacts on learning, particularly multimodal transduction, could guide future research.

Lastly, we feel confident that multimodality is vital in deaf education, but its benefits can be undermined if educational designers lack technical knowledge, time, or skills to implement it well (Skyer, 2019). In connection, it is possible that UDL’s shortcomings are not due to an inadequate theory, but inadequate implementation. This demonstrates a significant need to develop and disseminate better resources including those about deaf aesthetics for use by deaf education professionals and teachers in inclusive education settings.

Conclusion

Deaf curriculum and pedagogy fundamentally require that the balance of power be tipped toward deaf people (Skyer, 2021); however, transformation is seldom the reality. Our project identified the insufficiency of curricula in deaf education that are designed for and normed on able-bodied, hearing-speaking people. Researchers sometimes call ableist biases in deaf curriculum audionorms, audiocentricity, or phonocentricity. We also identified how deaf aesthetics, for students and faculty alike, may be oriented toward the visually-dominant learning needs of most sighted deaf people. These situated discourse frameworks are sometimes called visu-centrism, ocularcentrism, or ocularcentricity. In contrast to many theoretical studies, we have proffered compelling evidence that gives us reason to think deaf aesthetics uplifts and centers multimodal discourse ideologies, including both the visual-dominant needs of sighted deaf people and the tactile-dominant learning needs of most deafblind people.

While nominally “adapted,” or “inclusive,” or even “universal” designs tout high-minded goals, we find that many are problematic for deaf learners and deaf teachers, especially those who use sign languages and tactile languages to learn and access pedagogy and curriculum. While Universal Design for Learning (UDL), Disability Accommodations (DA), and Deaf Friendly Designs (DFD) claim to be resolutions, we find them problematic. It is not enough to merely include images, text, and learning objects, if little attention is paid to how students and faculty interact with these multimodal designs. Deaf aesthetics as we have posed it and explored it here have the potential to reshape the learning landscape for deaf curriculum including the learning management systems that store and deliver our curriculum, such as LMS and Zoom. Our study is small, but it is an important step toward transforming deaf education to be equitable and socially just.

We conclude with three theoretical considerations that sum up our points. These axioms synthesize unique principles about the situated needs of deaf learners in online curriculum interactions. First, UDL accessibility is relevant to deaf education but lacks specificity for transforming the deaf educational uses of inclusive or accessible digital curricula. Next, deaf aesthetics affects deaf pedagogy, curriculum, and learning, with a net-positive influence that can be explored and expanded in accessible digital curricula. Finally, multimodality and visuality are important for deaf learners and their teachers and positively affect digital curriculum interactions.

We posit that multimodality and deaf aesthetics are situated theories that honor and centralize the inborn learning and teaching habits of deaf people. Professionals, researchers, teachers, and students should understand that UDLs, DAs, and DFDs are fundamentally limited. They promise what they can’t deliver. Some even lack empirical components. Seldom are they transformative with respect to social justice. Our study used deaf aesthetics as an alternate framework that we find to be better situated for the specific needs of deaf and deafblind teachers and students. Through our mixed-methodology, multi-method case study, we have shown that there is sufficient empirical evidence to continue exploring deaf aesthetics as a means to transform unjust, unequal deaf educational systems.

Anything less just tinkers around the edges.
