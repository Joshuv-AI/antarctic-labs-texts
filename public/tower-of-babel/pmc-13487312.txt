Envisioning a more equitable and economical future for genomic testing
Authors: Laura M. Amendola, Robert Pilarski, Jessica Grzybowski, Marcy Richardson, Kelly D. Farwell Hagman
Published: 2026

Genetic testing is becoming an integral part of clinical medicine as genomic information increasingly informs patient care across the lifespan for both healthy individuals and those with disease. The value of genetic testing in the care of patients who may have inherited conditions, such as cancer1 and cardiac disease,2 is well established. Carrier screening is recommended for all individuals who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, and prenatal cell-free DNA screening has become a standard genetic screening option during pregnancy.3 In pediatrics, genomic sequencing to identify molecular diagnoses is increasingly standard practice for common neurological phenotypes4,5 and has demonstrated clear clinical utility in critical care settings.6,7 Research initiatives are underway to evaluate the integration of genomic testing into newborn screening and large-scale population screening programs for medically actionable inherited conditions have demonstrated both feasibility and impact on patient management.8,9

In parallel with the expansion of indications for genetic testing, technological advancements and reduced costs have improved access to large-scale sequencing tests. Standard practice has shifted from focused single-gene testing to gene panels, and now, increasingly, to diagnostic exome and genome sequencing. Current approaches depend on targeted enrichment strategies for specific conditions. As the field advances, genome sequencing (GS) is poised to become the foundational platform for clinical genetic testing across all likely inherited diseases,10 consolidating today’s fragmented approaches into a single platform that spans applications from newborn screening11 to carrier screening to inherited cardiac disease.12

Patients will likely soon benefit from multiple genetic tests throughout their lifetime. The National Human Genome Research Institute’s bold predictions for 2030 anticipate the regular use of genomic information in mainstream clinical settings and personalized access to genome sequence data on smart devices.13 To prepare for this future, the field of genomic medicine must begin laying the foundations now for a genetic testing approach that is both more equitable and economical than current models.

The “test once, evaluate often” Approach

Here, we describe the concept and potential benefits of a “test once, evaluate often” approach; a forward-thinking model that leverages a single, high-quality GS test to serve as a lifelong dataset for future evaluations. In this model, an individual’s GS data can be reevaluated to address future clinical questions unrelated to the original testing indication without the need for repeated laboratory analysis.

This reevaluation contrasts with “reanalysis,” in which re-review of prior sequencing data is done for the same testing indication (typically driven by either the development of new clinical features associated with the condition originally assessed or by the discovery of genes newly associated with that condition) (Box 1). Reanalysis is a common practice in genomic medicine and is widely performed across clinical laboratories to improve diagnostic rates for rare disease patients.14,15 The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics recommends periodic reanalysis of genomic data for undiagnosed patients, driven by evolving phenotypes, advancements in bioinformatics, or new scientific knowledge, with the aim of reclassifying variants of uncertain significance and/or identifying new disease-causing variants.14Box 1Reanalysis vs Reevaluation Reanalysis is the re-review of sequencing data for the same indication for testing.Reevaluation is the review of sequencing data for new indications for testing.The purpose of reanalysis is to solve a diagnostic odyssey, and it is fueled by the development of new clinical features or the identification of new genes associated with a condition.The purpose of reevaluation is to analyze genetic risk in a new clinical scenario, and it is fueled by the development of new clinical testing indications.

Reevaluation, in contrast, is not standard practice. There is currently no clinical guidance for when a reevaluation is appropriate, ordering processes that support laboratories to perform a reevaluation are not in place, and mechanisms for reimbursement do not exist. Patients may enter the “test once, evaluate often” approach at any point in their life via panel testing on a genome backbone or by undergoing GS with analysis for a rare disease diagnosis. Because new clinical symptoms or family history emerge, the laboratory can reevaluate the existing dataset, avoiding the need for additional sample collection and resequencing (Figure 1). Improved bioinformatic processing pipelines, complementary analyses (eg, RNA sequencing, epigenetic methylation, and other omics analyses), and future technologies will undoubtedly provide additional diagnostic value over time. The standard of care for clinical genetic testing will also evolve. It is unrealistic to imagine that genomic data created today will be sufficient to support standard clinical testing in the future. In conceptualizing this approach, we are prioritizing developing a practical, accessible model over a perfect solution and propose reevaluation as the first and most easily accessible step, one element of a future testing event. Reevaluation can be performed before or alongside analyses using more advanced or different technologies as they become available. The scope of findings reported from these reevaluations will need to expand to include analyses that align with future practice standards for the testing indication. For example, reevaluations could include the return of polygenic risk scores and/or pharmacogenomic findings generated from the individual’s original GS dataset if these analyses become standard of care testing.15

Decreasing Costs for Payors and Patients

In the current genetic testing paradigm, payors and patients often incur costs associated with wet-lab sequencing multiple times. Bioinformatic processing of genomic sequencing data remains resource-intensive and costly and represents an increasingly larger share of the total test cost. The “test once, evaluate often” approach eliminates the steps before evaluating annotated genomic data. Steps such as sample collection and transportation, DNA extraction and quality checks, genetic sequencing, and data processing and annotation are eliminated for each reevaluation, thereby reducing associated costs (Figure 2). Addressing Barriers to Test Access

Patient access to genetic testing faces persistent challenges rooted in financial constraints, geographic limitations, deficits in provider knowledge, and biases in ordering practices.17 Genetic testing models that rely on multiple independent tests require patients to repeatedly navigate these access barriers, which disproportionately impact individuals from underserved populations and exacerbate existing inequities in genomic medicine. Removing the need for repeated blood draws and the costs associated with sample collection, wet-lab testing, data processing, and annotation removes logistical barriers. Sample collection via buccal swab or saliva addresses barriers related to accessing and performing blood draws; these approaches are more convenient and less invasive. Nevertheless, buccal and saliva samples also require logistical coordination to acquire and return to the laboratory and can have issues with DNA quality and quantity because of sample collection variability.

The “test once, evaluate often” approach also leverages the established advantages of telemedicine-based care, including improved patient satisfaction, improved health outcomes, and the ability to deliver asynchronous services that are especially critical for patients who struggle to access care during restrictive, standard health care provider hours.18,19 Reducing critical access barriers decreases the likelihood for patients to delay or skip retesting altogether and miss out on potentially critical, life-saving information.

Building for the Future

Storing GS data for an individual’s lifespan raises cost, access, and privacy considerations. Although current data storage costs are manageable and likely to decrease, thoughtful decisions about the long-term storage of sequencing data that balance file size and associated costs are needed to ensure alignment with community standards. Equitable implementation necessitates consideration of changes in patients’ providers, health systems, and contact information. Solutions that support ongoing patient contact and access are especially important to ensure that inequities that exist in genomic medicine are not exacerbated. Genomic data privacy concerns are valid and are not unique to this context. Questions about data ownership, portability, and ensuring secure storage will need to be addressed and are comparable to patient portal privacy concerns. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated action among genomic medicine stakeholders, including patients, payors, hospital systems, and laboratories.

A “test once, evaluate often” model operationalizes how evolving technologies can be applied over time. This model prioritizes practicality and accessibility over an idealized solution. To address gaps that arise as new sequencing technologies emerge, data from future technologies will need to be integrated with a patient’s baseline GS data. Reevaluation of existing GS data can serve as a first test or may occur in parallel with novel technologies when new clinical indications arise.

Necessary Enablers

Several factors are essential for the broad implementation and acceptance of this model. First, reliable frameworks for the long-term storage and retrieval of raw genomic data must be established. Second, patients will require counseling at the time of their initial sequencing test that their genomic data will remain available for future clinical needs. And most critically, health insurers must develop reimbursement pathways for evaluation-only services. As noted above, although insurance reimbursement exists for reanalysis, reimbursement for reevaluation is currently lacking. Establishing reimbursement would offer clear benefits to payors by reducing the costs of repeated sequencing over a patient’s lifetime.
