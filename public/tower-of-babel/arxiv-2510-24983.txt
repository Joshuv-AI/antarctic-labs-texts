LRT-Diffusion: Calibrated Risk-Aware Guidance for Diffusion Policies
Ximan Sun, Xiang Cheng

Abstract
Diffusion policies are competitive for offline RL but are typically guided at sampling time by heuristics that lack a statistical notion of risk. We introduce LRT - Diffusion, a risk - aware sampling rule that treats each denoising step as a sequential hypothesis test between the unconditional prior and the state - conditional policy head. Concretely, we accumulate a log - likelihood ratio and gate the conditional mean with a logistic controller whose threshold τ \tau is calibrated once under H 0 H_{0} to meet a user - specified Type - I level α \alpha . This turns guidance from a fixed push into an evidence-driven adjustment with a user-interpretable risk budget. Importantly, we deliberately leave training vanilla (two heads with standard ϵ \epsilon -prediction) under the structure of DDPM. LRT guidance composes naturally with Q - gradients: critic - gradient updates can be taken at the unconditional mean, at the LRT - gated mean, or a blend, exposing a continuum from exploitation to conservatism. We standardize states/actions consistently at train and test time and report a state - conditional OOD metric alongside return. On D4RL MuJoCo tasks, LRT - Diffusion improves the return–OOD trade - off over strong Q - guided baselines in our implementation while honoring the desired α \alpha . Theoretically, we establish level - α \alpha calibration, concise stability bounds, and a return comparison showing when LRT surpasses Q - guidance—especially when off - support errors dominate. Overall, LRT - Diffusion is a drop - in, inference - time method that adds principled, calibrated risk control to diffusion policies for offline RL.

Introduction

Offline reinforcement learning (RL) aims to learn high
-
performing policies from fixed datasets without further environment interaction. A central difficulty is
distributional shift
: actions proposed by the learned policy can drift away from the behavioral support where value estimates are reliable. Diffusion policies have recently emerged as strong generative decision
-
makers for offline RL

: by learning a conditional diffusion model over actions given state, they produce smooth, high
-
fidelity samples that respect support better than direct regression. However,
how
these policies are guided at sampling time remains largely heuristic. Common practices—injecting
Q
-
gradients with hand
-
tuned schedules and ad
-
hoc clipping—lack a statistical notion of risk and offer limited control of the return–shift trade
-
off. Unlike prior works, our approach keeps training strictly vanilla (no critic-guided losses) and moves all risk control to inference via a calibrated likelihood-ratio gate (LRT), yielding an interpretable, reproducible risk knob.

Intuition.

Rather than always pulling samples toward the conditional head, we ask at every denoising step:
is there enough evidence to move toward the “good
-
action” direction for this state?
Concretely, we split offline actions into a
good

background

-

conditional
head on good data) with class
-

-

calibrated
likelihood ratio. The gate is motivated by the Neyman–Pearson test

-
I rate
\alpha

H_{0}
(“background is correct”); while the hard LRT is UMP at level
\alpha
under equal covariances, we use a smooth gate in practice for numerical stability and keep
\alpha
interpretable via calibration on held
-

Method overview.

We introduce
LRT
-
Diffusion
, a risk
-

-

At each denoising step we make a binary decision between a background prediction and a data

We accumulate evidence during sampling and open the gate only when the evidence is strong, interpolating between the two predictions with a data

A single threshold is calibrated once so that the empirical false

Composition with value guidance.

We optionally combine LRT gating with a small critic step: at each denoising update, take a capped step that increases the learned critic at the current proposal (standard decreasing schedule). The gradient can be evaluated near the background proposal, the LRT-gated proposal, or an interpolation; the threshold is calibrated with the same choice, so the Type-I semantics are preserved.

Under equal

The gate exposes monotone, interpretable knobs:

Related Work

Distribution shift in offline RL and conservative learning.

A central obstacle in offline RL is that policy actions may fall outside the behavior support, where critic estimates are brittle. Conservative algorithms explicitly counter this by penalizing or constraining OOD actions, e.g., BCQ
Fujimoto2019BCQ
, BEAR
Kumar2019BEAR
, CQL
Kumar2020CQL
, and TD3+BC
Fujimoto2021TD3BC
. IQL
Kostrikov2022IQL
learns values via expectile regression and performs advantage-weighted improvement without explicit behavior cloning. Our work is complementary: we keep training unchanged (e.g., use IQL advantages only to label/weight “good” actions) and control OOD at
sampling time
via a calibrated gate.

Diffusion policies for decision-making and their guidance.

Diffusion models have been adopted for control and planning (e.g., Diffuser
Janner2022Diffuser
). To improve returns, many systems apply
Q-guidance
—adding an action-space step along
\nabla_{a}\hat{Q}(s,a)
with hand-tuned schedules and clips—conceptually analogous to guidance in image diffusion (e.g., classifier-based guidance
Dhariwal2021GuidedDiffusion
and classifier-free guidance
Ho2022CFG
). However, such heuristics lack a statistical notion of risk: there is no global control on the probability of “falsely” pulling away from the background prior.

Risk-aware control and calibration.

Risk-sensitive and conservative offline RL methods (e.g., behavior-regularized or pessimistic objectives)
encode risk
in the training loss
by penalizing value estimates or constraining policy deviation from the dataset

(
Fujimoto2019BCQ
;
Wu2019BRAC
;
Kumar2020CQL
;
Nair2020AWAC
;
Peng2019AWR
;
Kostrikov2022IQL
)
.
A complementary line uses
distribution-free calibration
to turn data-driven thresholds into finite-sample guarantees
(e.g., conformal prediction)
(
Vovk2005ALRW
;
Angelopoulos2023ConformalSurvey
)
.
In diffusion-based decision making, sampling is typically guided heuristically (e.g.,
Q
-gradient pushes with hand-tuned schedules/clipping) without an explicit statistical notion of risk.
Our approach bridges these threads: we cast each reverse step as a simple-vs-simple test between an unconditional (background) head and a conditional (good) head, accumulate a log-likelihood ratio (LLR), and
calibrate one threshold
on held-out states so that the empirical Type-I rate under
H_{0}
does not exceed a user-chosen
\alpha
.
Under equal covariances—satisfied by our two-head design—the hard likelihood-ratio test is uniformly most powerful (Neyman–Pearson)
(
NeymanPearson1933
)
, and we use a smooth gate for numerical stability while preserving the same
\alpha
-semantics via calibration.
This gives a statistically grounded alternative to heuristic mixing: guidance becomes evidence-driven with an explicit level-
\alpha
risk budget at inference time, without changing training.

Hypothesis testing and likelihood ratios in RL

Statistical testing has been used in RL primarily for off-policy evaluation and safe policy improvement, not for stepwise guidance inside a generative sampler. High-confidence OPE
Thomas2015HCOPE
derives concentration-based bounds to accept/reject policy deployment with user-specified risk, while SPIBB
Laroche2019SPIBB
ensures safe improvement over a baseline via count-based constraints rather than explicit tests. In safety-constrained RL, trust-region and constraint-violation controls (e.g., CPO
Achiam2017CPO
) target reliable deployment but do not implement likelihood-ratio gating during action generation. Classical sequential tests such as Wald’s SPRT
Wald1945SPRT
have inspired sequential decision criteria, yet we are not aware of prior work that uses a calibrated likelihood-ratio test as an inference-time gate to steer diffusion-policy denoising steps. Our method fills this gap by turning denoising guidance into an evidence-controlled, level-
\alpha
process.

Advantage-based labeling and weighting.

For higher return actions, advantage-weighted and critic-regularized schemes (RWR
Peters2007RWR
, AWR
Peng2019AWR
, AWAC
Nair2020AWAC
) are proposed. We use IQL advantages to (i) define a top-
p
“good” subset for the conditional head and (ii) optionally apply a temperature-controlled soft weight on positives, while keeping a background head trained on all data. This two-head setup strengthens the conditional signal without altering the base training pipeline, and remains compatible with our calibrated LRT at inference. Our two-head design enforces equal covariances by construction, which simplifies the LLR and aligns with the NP test.

Background

Offline RL is given a fixed dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(s^{(i)},a^{(i)},r^{(i)},s^{(i)^{\prime}})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, collected by an unknown behavior policy
\pi
, and aims to learn a high-return policy while controlling out-of-distribution risk. In the dataset,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
is the current state,
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
is the action,
r\in\mathbb{R}
is the reward,
s^{\prime}
is the next state. We consider the process as a discounted Markov Decision Process with continuous state/action spaces.

3.1
IQL Advantages and “Good vs. Background” Labels

For each pair of state and action
(s,a)\in\mathcal{D}
, we train an IQL-style critic
Kostrikov2022IQL

(\hat{Q}(s,a),\hat{V}(s))
on standardized inputs and define an
advantage

A(s,a)
as:

A(s,a)\;=\;\hat{Q}(s,a)-\hat{V}(s),

which quantifies the value contributed by action
a
at state
s
relative to the state’s baseline.

Let
\kappa
be the
(1-p)
-quantile of
\{A(s_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
We label each pair of state-action data by

c\;=\;\mathbf{1}\{A(s,a)\geq\kappa\}\in\{0,1\}.

Pairs with
c{=}1
are considered
good
, as their advantages fall within the top
(1-p)
fraction of the dataset, while those with
c{=}0
form the non-advantage subset. In practice, we set
p=0.2
, allocating approximately
20\%
of the data as
good
examples.

3.2
Diffusion Policies with Two Heads

We train a diffusion policy with two heads: an
unconditional
head
trained on all state–action pairs
\mathcal{D}
, and a
conditional

head trained only on the high-advantage subset (
c{=}1

Formally, let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{all}}:=\mathcal{D}
and

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{good}}:=\{(s,a,r,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}:c=1\}
.
The unconditional head learns broad dataset coverage from

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{all}}

high-advantage behavior using
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{good}}

3.2.2

We adopt the predict-

3.4
Likelihood-Ratio View of a Reverse Step

Unknown labels
\Rightarrow
a trajectory-level test.

At inference the label
c
is unknown. Given two simple trajectory models

p_{u}(a_{T:1}\mid s)
(background) and
p_{c}(a_{T:1}\mid s)
(good), the
Neyman–Pearson (NP) lemma implies that the level-
\alpha
test that rejects

H_{0}
when the cumulative log–likelihood ratio (LLR) exceeds a threshold
is uniformly most powerful (UMP). We therefore base our gate on the
trajectory statistic

\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}(a_{T:1})\;:=\;\log\frac{p_{c}(a_{T:1}\mid s)}{p_{u}(a_{T:1}\mid s)}.

How to compute
\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}
: step-wise LLRs.

With the reverse chain factorization in DDPM, for
i\in\{u,c\}

p_{i}(a_{T:1}\mid s)=q(a_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{i}(a_{t-1}\mid a_{t},s,t),

so the trajectory LLR decomposes into a sum of step-wise terms:

\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}(a_{T:1})~=~\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t},\qquad\ell_{t}~:=~\log\frac{p_{c}(a_{t-1}\mid a_{t},s,t)}{p_{u}(a_{t-1}\mid a_{t},s,t)}.

(4)

Head-induced one-step densities and quadratic form.

From Sec.
3.3
, each head induces a Gaussian kernel with
shared covariance. Hence the step-wise LLR admits the shared-variance simplification

\ell_{t}~=~\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Big(\|a_{t-1}-\mu_{u}(t,s,a_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\|a_{t-1}-\mu_{c}(t,s,a_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\Big),

(5)

i.e., a linear discriminant in
a_{t-1}
and cheap to evaluate.

Decision rule for assigning labels.

The NP test uses
\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}
with a calibrated threshold
\tau
to control
the level-
\alpha
Type-I rate. We implement a smooth gate that monotonically
approximates the NP decision and calibrate
\tau
under
H_{0}
; details are given in
Sec.
4
.

Under the two-head diffusion policy in Sec.

\mathcal{L}=\tfrac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\tilde{w}_{i}\,\big\|\hat{\epsilon}_{\phi}(a_{t,i},t,\tilde{s}_{i})-\epsilon_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2},\quad\tilde{w}_{i}=\frac{w^{\text{cb}}(c_{i};\hat{\rho})\cdot u_{i}}{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}w^{\text{cb}}(c_{j};\hat{\rho})\cdot u_{j}},

4.3
Evidence-gated Sampler

4.3.1
Motivation

1D evidence direction.

From Eq.
5
, under equal covariances the one-step LLR has
constant gradient
\nabla_{a_{t-1}}\ell_{t}=\Sigma_{t}^{-1}(\mu_{c}-\mu_{u})
; among unit directions

u

\langle u,\nabla\ell_{t}\rangle
is maximized by

u\parallel\Sigma_{t}^{-1}(\mu_{c}-\mu_{u})
(isotropic:
u\parallel\mu_{c}-\mu_{u}
).
We therefore restrict the reverse-step mean to the 1D ray

\mu_{u}+\beta(\mu_{c}-\mu_{u}),
and let the scalar gate
\beta
depend monotonically on the cumulative evidence
\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}
.

Why not always use
\mu_{c}
?

Always setting
a_{t-1}{=}\mu_{c}{+}\sigma_{t}z_{t}
is equivalent to
always accepting
H_{1}

(i.e.,
\alpha{=}1
), which (i) removes any control on false activations of the conditional head
and thus increases off-support mass in offline RL, and (ii) couples performance to critic/label
errors where the model is most brittle. Our gate opens
only when there is sufficient
evidence
(large
\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}
), yielding a calibrated budget on false activations
(Prop.
2
and Thm.
2
).
Section
5.3
further shows that, when off-support errors dominate
(
\varepsilon_{\rm out}\!\gg\!\varepsilon_{\rm in}
), reducing the state-conditional OOD rate via

\alpha

4.3.2

Let

4.6
Composition with Value Gradients

To cleanly separate
risk control
(via the LRT gate) from
return seeking
, we optionally add a small critic step that mirrors common practice in diffusion-policy samplers. This step uses the
action-space
gradient of a learned critic (not the diffusion score) with a simple schedule and clipping:

a_{t-1}\ \leftarrow\ a_{t-1}\ +\ \lambda_{t}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}\,\nabla_{a}\hat{Q}_{\theta}(s,a)\big|_{a=a_{c}},

(11)

where
a_{c}\in\{\mu_{u},\ \mu_{\text{LRT},t},\ (1-\rho)\mu_{u}+\rho\,\mu_{\text{LRT},t}\},
and
\mu_{\text{LRT},t}=\mu_{u}+\beta_{t}(\mu_{c}-\mu_{u})
is the LRT-gated mean. We use a light, hand-tuned schedule (e.g.,
\lambda_{t}\propto\sigma_{t}
) and gradient clipping to keep updates stable.

We use the evaluation center
a_{c}=\mu_{\text{LRT},t}
as evidence opens the gate, suggesting the gradient is closer to the good direction. For more evaluation center choice discussion, refer Appendix
D.1

5.2
Finite-sample Calibration and Stability

Let
F_{0}
be the CDF of the cumulative LLR

for the

(soft gate and, if present, the fixed

5.2.1
Finite-sample Guarantee

Theorem 2

(Calibration accuracy via DKW
Dvoretzky1956DKW
;
Massart1990
)
.

For any
\zeta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\zeta
over the calibration sample,

\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}^{\text{(sampler)}}\!\big(\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}\geq\hat{\tau}\big)~\leq~\alpha+\varepsilon_{n},\qquad\varepsilon_{n}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2n}\log\tfrac{2}{\zeta}}.

Calibration budget.
Rearranging the bound gives a one-line rule: to guarantee

\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}^{(\text{sampler})}(\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}\!\geq\!\hat{\tau})\leq\alpha+\varepsilon

with confidence at least
1-\zeta

See Appendix
B.1
for proof.

5.2.2
Stability

Lemma 3

(Deterministic displacement bound)
.

At reverse step
t
, write
\Delta\mu_{t}:=\mu_{c,t}-\mu_{u,t}
and let
g_{t}
be the
clipped

critic gradient with
\|g_{t}\|\leq G
.
Our update has deterministic mean

m_{t}\;=\;\mu_{u,t}\;+\;\beta_{t}\,\Delta\mu_{t}\;+\;\lambda_{t}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}\,g_{t},\qquad 0\leq\beta_{t}\leq\beta_{\max},\ \ 0\leq\lambda_{t}\leq\lambda_{\max}.

Hence the per-step
deterministic displacement
from the background anchor is bounded by

\|m_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|~\leq~\beta_{\max}\,\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\;+\;\lambda_{\max}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}\,G\;=:\;B_{t}.

(12)

If, additionally,
\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\leq D
(e.g. clamp on

\Delta\mu_{t}
) and
\sigma_{t}^{2}\leq S^{2}
, then

\|m_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|\leq\beta_{\max}D+\lambda_{\max}S^{2}G\;=:\;B_{\text{step}}

for all
t
,
and the cumulative
deterministic
deviation from the background chain across
T
steps is at most

\sum_{t=1}^{T}B_{t}\leq T\,B_{\text{step}}
.

Thus, the deterministic component of the sampler admits a uniform, hyperparameter
-
controlled drift bound both per step and over the full reverse trajectory; see Appendix
B.2
for the detailed proof.

Implication for one-step LLR fluctuations.

Under equal covariances, the one-step LLR increment admits the identity

\displaystyle\Delta\ell_{t}~=~

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{\Delta\mu_{t}^{\top}\big(m_{t}-\tfrac{\mu_{c,t}+\mu_{u,t}}{2}\big)}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}}_{\text{deterministic part}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{\Delta\mu_{t}^{\top}z_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}}_{\text{zero-mean Gaussian}},

where
z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. Therefore, conditionally on
(m_{t},\mu_{u,t},\mu_{c,t})
,

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[\Delta\ell_{t}]\big|

\displaystyle~\leq~\frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\,\Big(B_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\Big),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\ell_{t})

\displaystyle=~\frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

In particular, if
\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\leq D
and
\sigma_{t}\geq\sigma_{\min}>0
, then

\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\ell_{t})\leq D^{2}/\sigma_{\min}^{2}
and

\big|\mathbb{E}[\Delta\ell_{t}]\big|\leq\frac{D}{\sigma_{\min}^{2}}\big(B_{\text{step}}+\tfrac{D}{2}\big)
, so

\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}=\sum_{t}\Delta\ell_{t}
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy

\sum_{t}\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|^{2}/\sigma_{t}^{2}\leq T\,D^{2}/\sigma_{\min}^{2}
.
This quantifies that our gate and gradient clipping keep both the
magnitude
of mean shifts
and the
variance
of the accumulated evidence controlled. See proofs on Appendix
B.3
and
B.4
.

5.3
Distributional Control and Return Bounds

Let
\mathcal{S}(s)
denote the dataset action support at state
s

\pi
by

\eta(\pi\,|\,s)=\Pr_{a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)}[a\notin\mathcal{S}(s)],\quad\eta(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\text{eval}}}\![\eta(\pi\,|\,s)].

Let
\hat{Q}
be a learned critic and
Q^{\text{true}}
the environment action-value.
2
2

2

Any fixed evaluation state distribution
d_{\text{eval}}
may be used; in our experiments it is the dataset state marginal.
Assume the standard offline error split

\varepsilon_{\rm in}:=\sup_{a\in\mathcal{S}(s)}|\hat{Q}(s,a)-Q^{\text{true}}(s,a)|,\quad\varepsilon_{\rm out}:=\sup_{a\notin\mathcal{S}(s)}|\hat{Q}(s,a)-Q^{\text{true}}(s,a)|,

and that
Q^{\text{true}}
is
L
-Lipschitz in
a
. Intuitively,
\nu:=\varepsilon_{\rm out}-\varepsilon_{\rm in}\geq 0
in offline RL due to extrapolation error. Additionally, write
\pi_{\rm LRT}
for the LRT-gated policy and
\pi_{Q}
for pure
Q
-guided sampling, with
a_{\rm LRT}\!\sim\!\pi_{\rm LRT}(\cdot|s)
and

.

Proposition 3

(Return comparison under offline errors)
.

Under the assumption above, let

\displaystyle\Delta_{Q^{true}}:

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}[Q^{true}(s,a_{LRT})-Q^{true}(s,a_{Q})],

\displaystyle\Delta_{\hat{Q}}:

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}[\hat{Q}(s,a_{LRT})-\hat{Q}(s,a_{Q})],

the following inequality holds:

\boxed{\Delta_{Q^{true}}\geq\Delta_{\hat{Q}}-2\varepsilon_{\rm in}-\nu\,\left(\eta(\pi_{Q})+\eta(\pi_{\rm LRT})\right).}

Sketch.

Decompose expectations into on-support and OOD parts. On-support deviations are bounded by
\varepsilon_{\rm in}
, off-support by
\varepsilon_{\rm out}
. Refer to Appendix
C.1
for complete proof.
∎

Assumption (Monotone support w.r.t. gating).
To relate
\eta(\pi_{\rm LRT})
to the calibrated level
\alpha
, we use a mild monotonicity assumption. With the background head fixed, opening an LRT gate at any step does not decrease the probability that the final
a_{0}
is outside
\mathcal{S}(s)
; if all
T
gates reject, the final
a_{0}
lies in
\mathcal{S}(s)
with high probability.

Proposition 4

(From level-
\alpha
to an OOD bound)
.

Under the assumption above, if each step’s gate has size at most
\alpha
under
H_{0}
and the sampler uses
T

\eta(\pi_{\rm LRT})~\leq~\Pr(A)~\leq~1-(1-\alpha)^{T}~\approx~\alpha T\quad(\alpha T\ll 1).

A is when at least one gate opens under
H_{0}
.

Remarks.

The bound follows from a union/Bonferroni argument (or Ville’s inequality
Ville1939
) for the
activation
events. It formalizes the role of
\alpha
as a risk knob: smaller
\alpha

\Rightarrow
fewer/softer activations

lower OOD rate, which—via Prop.

Q

\varepsilon_{\rm out}\!\geq\!\varepsilon_{\rm in}
.

5.4
A sufficient condition for LRT to dominate Q

Combining Prop.
3
and Prop.
4
yields

\Delta_{Q^{\rm true}}\ \geq\ \Delta_{\hat{Q}}\ -\ 2\varepsilon_{\rm in}\ -\ \nu\big(\alpha T+\eta(\pi_{Q})\big).

Hence, if

\boxed{\alpha\ \leq\ \alpha_{\max}\ :=\ \frac{\Delta_{\hat{Q}}\ -\ 2\varepsilon_{\rm in}\ -\ \nu\,\eta(\pi_{Q})}{\nu\,T}\,,}

then
\mathbb{E}_{s}[Q^{\rm true}(s,a_{\rm LRT})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{s}[Q^{\rm true}(s,a_{Q})]
. Moreover,
\alpha_{\max}>0
if and only if

\Delta_{\hat{Q}}>2\varepsilon_{\rm in}+\nu\,\eta(\pi_{Q})
.

The bound is sufficient (and conservative): it uses the union proxy
\eta(\pi_{\rm LRT})\!\leq\!\alpha T
and the critic gap
\Delta_{\hat{Q}}
, which may be biased off–support. Nevertheless it yields actionable levers:
(i) decreasing

(late–step gating) or
\beta_{\max}
reduces
\nu

(ii) making

(iii) evidence–tied guidance near

can raise
\Delta_{\hat{Q}}
.
In the favorable regime where

is small,
\alpha_{\max}
grows as

\alpha_{\max}=(\Delta_{\hat{Q}}-2\varepsilon_{\rm in}-\nu\,\eta(\pi_{Q}))/(\nu T)
, so moderate
\alpha
can still be certified.
In practice we treat
\widehat{\alpha}_{\max}
as a diagnostic and sweep
\alpha
on a log grid; the selected
\alpha
is the knee of the return–risk curve (Fig.
1

C.5

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We presented
LRT-Diffusion
, a calibrated, inference-only guidance rule that turns diffusion-policy denoising into an evidence-gated process governed by a single, interpretable risk knob
\alpha
.
Under equal covariances, the hard likelihood-ratio test (LRT) is uniformly most powerful at level
\alpha
; our smooth gate retains the same statistical semantics through a simple Monte-Carlo calibration matched to the deployed sampler.
Empirically, LRT delivers calibrated control of Type-I risk and consistently improves the return–OOD trade-off over standard
Q
-guided sampling, while integrating seamlessly with a small critic step for additional exploitation.
Together, these results demonstrate that
risk-aware diffusion guidance
can be achieved entirely at inference without modifying training objectives.

Several extensions naturally follow.

(1) Learned or adaptive gating.
Instead of a fixed calibrated threshold, the gate could be learned or amortized across tasks, or adapt a target
\alpha
online under deployment constraints while preserving calibration.

(2) Richer statistical models.
Relaxing the equal-covariance assumption with heteroscedastic or learned head variances may yield stronger likelihood ratios and sharper decisions.

(3) Sequential and early-stopping tests.
Connecting the cumulative LLR to sequential or martingale tests (e.g., SPRT
Wald1945SPRT
) would enable any-time risk control and early termination of denoising when the evidence is decisive, reducing compute without violating the level-
\alpha
guarantee.

(4) Broader integration and evaluation.
Composing LRT with model-based planners or diffusion world models could link evidence-gated actions to trajectory-level reasoning, potentially higher returns on broader datasets.

A.2
Soft to Hard Limit: Proof of Lemma
1

Proof.

Recall from Sec.
4.3.2
the logistic gate

\beta_{t}(\ell_{\mathrm{cum}};\tau,\delta,\beta_{\max})~=~\beta_{\max}\,\sigma\!\Big(\tfrac{\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}-\tau}{\delta}\Big),\qquad\sigma(x)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}},

and the proposal mean at step
t
,

\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}=\mu_{u}+\beta_{t}(\mu_{c}-\mu_{u})
,
with reverse variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}I
.
We first establish the pointwise limit of
\beta_{t}
and hence of
\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}
,
then show weak convergence of the trajectory law by a coupling argument.

Step 1: Pointwise limit of the gate and the mean.

Fix
(\tau,\beta_{\max})
.
For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
x\neq\tau
,

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\ \sigma\!\Big(\tfrac{x-\tau}{\delta}\Big)~=~\mathbf{1}\{x\geq\tau\}.

Hence, for any realization of the running evidence
\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}
at step
t
such that

\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}\neq\tau
,

\beta_{t}(\ell_{\mathrm{cum}};\tau,\delta,\beta_{\max})~\xrightarrow[\delta\downarrow 0]{}~\beta_{\max}\,\mathbf{1}\{\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}\geq\tau\}.

Therefore

\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}~=~\mu_{u}+\beta_{t}(\mu_{c}-\mu_{u})~\xrightarrow[\delta\downarrow 0]{}~\mu_{u}+\beta_{\max}\,\mathbf{1}\{\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}\geq\tau\}\,(\mu_{c}-\mu_{u})~=:\ \mu_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}.

It remains to note that, under our reverse kernels with Gaussian noise,
the scalar
\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}
is a continuous function of the Gaussian seeds,
so
\mathbb{P}(\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}=\tau)=0
; hence the convergence above holds almost surely.

Step 2: Weak convergence of the trajectory law.

Couple all samplers (for each
\delta\geq 0
) by using the
same
initial latent

a_{T}\sim q(\cdot)
and the
same
Gaussian noise draws
z_{T},\ldots,z_{1}
:

\displaystyle a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{soft}}\;=\;

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}\big(t,s,a_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}\big)\;+\;\sigma_{t}z_{t},

\displaystyle a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{hard}}\;=\;

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{{\mathrm{hard}}}\big(t,s,a_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}\big)\;+\;\sigma_{t}z_{t}.

We prove by backward induction on
t=T,\ldots,1
that

a_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}\to a_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}
and the running evidence prefixes

\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{soft}}\to\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{hard}}
almost surely as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Base case
t=T
:
By construction
a_{T}^{\mathrm{soft}}=a_{T}^{\mathrm{hard}}
almost surely.

Inductive step:

Assume
a_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}\to a_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}
and

\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{soft}}\to\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{hard}}
almost surely.
The maps
(t,s,a)\mapsto\mu_{u}(t,s,a)
and
\mu_{c}(t,s,a)
are continuous (shared backbone + MLP heads with standard activations), hence

\mu_{u}\big(t,s,a_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}\big)\to\mu_{u}\big(t,s,a_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}\big),\quad\mu_{c}\big(t,s,a_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}\big)\to\mu_{c}\big(t,s,a_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}\big)\quad\text{a.s.}

By Step 1 and the a.s. convergence of
\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{soft}}
,

\beta_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}=\beta_{t}(\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{soft}};\tau,\delta,\beta_{\max})\to\beta_{\max}\mathbf{1}\{\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{hard}}\geq\tau\}

almost surely (the boundary has probability zero). Therefore

\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{soft},(\delta)}~\longrightarrow~\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}\quad\text{a.s.}

With the coupled noise
z_{t}
, we obtain

a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{soft}}=\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}+\sigma_{t}z_{t}~\longrightarrow~\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}+\sigma_{t}z_{t}=a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{hard}}\quad\text{a.s.}

Finally, the one-step LLR increment
\ell_{t}
is a continuous function of

(a_{t},a_{t-1})
under equal covariances, hence

\ell_{t}^{\mathrm{soft}}\to\ell_{t}^{\mathrm{hard}}
and the updated evidence

\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{soft}}\to\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}^{\mathrm{hard}}
almost surely,
closing the induction.

Thus
a_{0:T}^{\mathrm{soft}}\to a_{0:T}^{\mathrm{hard}}
almost surely under the coupling.
Almost-sure convergence under a coupling implies weak convergence of the induced
trajectory laws, so the sampler with soft gate converges (in distribution) to the
sampler with the hard gate as
\delta\downarrow 0
.
∎

B.1
Finite-sample Calibration: Proof of Theorem

Proof.

Let
F_{0}
be the CDF of the cumulative LLR
\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}
under
H_{0}
for the
frozen
sampler
(soft gate and, if present, the fixed
Q

\ell^{(1)},\dots,\ell^{(n)}

Hence,

\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}\geq\hat{\tau})\leq\alpha+\varepsilon_{n}
. Moreover, when

B.2
Deterministic Displacement bound: Proof for Lemma
3

Proof.

By definition of the (deterministic) update at step
t
,

a_{t}-\mu_{u,t}=\beta_{t}\,\Delta\mu_{t}\;+\;\lambda_{t}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}\,g_{t}.

Taking Euclidean norms and applying the triangle inequality,

\|a_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|\;\leq\;|\beta_{t}|\,\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\;+\;|\lambda_{t}|\,\sigma_{t}^{2}\,\|g_{t}\|.

Using the bounds
0\leq\beta_{t}\leq\beta_{\max}
,
0\leq\lambda_{t}\leq\lambda_{\max}
and

\|g_{t}\|\leq G
, we obtain

\|a_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|\;\leq\;\beta_{\max}\,\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\;+\;\lambda_{\max}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}\,G\;=:\;B_{t},

which is (
12
).

If, in addition,
\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\leq D
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}\leq S^{2}
, then

\|a_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|\;\leq\;\beta_{\max}D\;+\;\lambda_{\max}S^{2}G\;=:\;B_{\text{step}}\quad\text{for all }t.

Finally, summing the per-step bounds yields the stated cumulative bound:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|a_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|\;\leq\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}B_{t}\;\leq\;T\,B_{\text{step}}.

∎

B.3
One-step LLR Decomposition and Moment Bounds

Under equal covariances
\sigma_{t}^{2}I
, the one-step LLR admits the exact identity

\Delta\ell_{t}=-\frac{\|a_{t-1}-\mu_{c,t}\|^{2}-\|a_{t-1}-\mu_{u,t}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}=\frac{\Delta\mu_{t}^{\top}\!\left(a_{t-1}-\tfrac{\mu_{c,t}+\mu_{u,t}}{2}\right)}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

(15)

Write the stochastic update as
a_{t-1}=m_{t}+\sigma_{t}z_{t}
, where
z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
is independent of
z_{t^{\prime}}
for
t^{\prime}\neq t
and independent of the past given the sampler’s history (the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
generated by
(a_{T},\ldots,a_{t})
and the gating/gradient choices). Substituting into (
15
) yields the
deterministic + Gaussian
decomposition

\Delta\ell_{t}=\underbrace{\frac{\Delta\mu_{t}^{\top}\!\left(m_{t}-\tfrac{\mu_{c,t}+\mu_{u,t}}{2}\right)}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}}_{=:~A_{t}\text{ (deterministic given }\mathcal{F}_{t})}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{\Delta\mu_{t}^{\top}z_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}}_{=:~B_{t}\text{ (zero-mean Gaussian)}}.

(i) Conditional mean bound.)
Since

m_{t}-\tfrac{\mu_{c,t}+\mu_{u,t}}{2}=(m_{t}-\mu_{u,t})-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\mu_{t}
,
by Cauchy–Schwarz,

|A_{t}|\;=\;\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\,\big|\langle\Delta\mu_{t},\,(m_{t}-\mu_{u,t})-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\mu_{t}\rangle\big|\ \leq\ \frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Big(\|m_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\Big).

Taking expectation conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
gives

\big|\mathbb{E}[\Delta\ell_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\big|\ \leq\ \frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Big(\|m_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\Big)\ \leq\ \frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Big(B_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\Big),

where in the last inequality we used the displacement bound
\|m_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|\leq B_{t}
from §
B.2
.

(ii) Conditional variance.)
Because
B_{t}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}\Delta\mu_{t}^{\top}z_{t}
with
z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
,

\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\ell_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})=\mathrm{Var}(B_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})=\frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

In particular, if
\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|\leq D
and
\sigma_{t}\geq\sigma_{\min}>0
, then

\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\ell_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})\leq D^{2}/\sigma_{\min}^{2}

and

\big|\mathbb{E}[\Delta\ell_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\big|\ \leq\ \frac{D}{\sigma_{\min}^{2}}\Big(B_{\text{step}}+\tfrac{D}{2}\Big)\qquad\text{(using }\|m_{t}-\mu_{u,t}\|\leq B_{\text{step}}\text{).}

∎

B.4
Sub-Gaussian concentration for the cumulative LLR

Define the variance proxy

V\;:=\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the sampler’s filtration up to step
t
. As above, given
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we can write

\Delta\ell_{t}=A_{t}+B_{t}
with
B_{t}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}\Delta\mu_{t}^{\top}z_{t}
, where
z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and of
z_{t^{\prime}}
for
t^{\prime}\neq t
. Hence, conditionally,

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda B_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\big]=\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right).

Iterating conditional expectations (tower property) and using independence of the
z_{t}
across
t
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}(B_{t}-\mathbb{E}[B_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}])\right)\right]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)=\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}V\right).

Therefore
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(B_{t}-\mathbb{E}[B_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\big)
is
sub-Gaussian
with proxy
V
.
Adding the deterministic (given history) terms
A_{t}-\mathbb{E}[A_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
only shifts the mean and does not affect the sub-Gaussian proxy. Consequently,

\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(\Delta\ell_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\Delta\ell_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\big),

is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy V,
and for any
x>0
,

\Pr\!\left[\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}-\mathbb{E}\ell_{\mathrm{cum}}\geq x\right]\ \leq\ \exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2V}\right).

Under the scale controls in §
B.2
,

V\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}D^{2}/\sigma_{\min}^{2}=T\,D^{2}/\sigma_{\min}^{2}

.
∎

Summary of implications.

The cap
\beta_{\max}
and gradient clipping (via
G,\lambda_{\max}
) yield a per-step deterministic displacement
B_{t}
and a uniform bound
B_{\text{step}}
; together with the equal-covariance identity, these give (i) explicit bounds on the conditional mean and variance of each LLR increment, and (ii) a sub-Gaussian concentration for the cumulative evidence with proxy
V=\sum_{t}\|\Delta\mu_{t}\|^{2}/\sigma_{t}^{2}
. These stability controls make the calibration reproducible (Theorem
2
) and the gate numerically well-conditioned.

Proof.

C.5
Further Discussion for LRT dominate Q

Combining Prop.
3
and Prop.
4
yields the sufficient condition

\displaystyle\Delta_{Q^{\rm true}}~\geq~\Delta_{\hat{Q}}~-~2\varepsilon_{\rm in}~-~\kappa\big(\alpha T+\eta(\pi_{Q})\big),

\displaystyle\alpha_{\max}~=~\frac{\Delta_{\hat{Q}}-2\varepsilon_{\rm in}-\kappa\,\eta(\pi_{Q})}{\kappa\,T},

where

\Delta_{Q^{\rm true}}:=\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\left[Q^{\rm true}(s,a_{\rm LRT})-Q^{\rm true}(s,a_{Q})\right],

and

\Delta_{\hat{Q}}:=\mathbb{E}_{s}\!\left[\hat{Q}(s,a_{\rm LRT})-\hat{Q}(s,a_{Q})\right].

The bound decomposes into three interpretable pieces:

•

Predicted–value gap
\Delta_{\hat{Q}}
.
How much the critic
\hat{Q}
prefers LRT samples over Q–guided samples on average. If the critic systematically overestimates off–support actions,
\Delta_{\hat{Q}}
can be small or negative; the condition is
sufficient only
and may fail even when LRT is better in the environment.

•

On–support error
2\varepsilon_{\rm in}
.
A safety margin independent of
\alpha
.
Better–fit (or pessimistic/trimmed) critics reduce this term and enlarge the feasible range.

•

OOD penalty
\kappa(\alpha T+\eta(\pi_{Q}))
.
The LRT part scales with
\alpha
and with the number of gated steps
T
via the union proxy
\eta(\pi_{\rm LRT})\leq\alpha T
; the Q–guided baseline pays
\kappa\,\eta(\pi_{Q})
.
Smaller
\kappa
—e.g., via a smaller
\beta_{\max}
or late–step gating when
\sigma_{t}
is small—makes the condition easier to satisfy.

C.5.1
When does LRT “dominate” Q–guidance?

If
\alpha\leq\alpha_{\max}
, then

\mathbb{E}_{s}[Q^{\rm true}(s,a_{\rm LRT})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{s}[Q^{\rm true}(s,a_{Q})]
is certified.
Two practical corollaries are useful:

•

A small–OOD Q baseline helps the certificate.

If
\eta(\pi_{Q})
is small (Q–guidance rarely leaves support), the denominator in
\alpha_{\max}
is fixed while the numerator increases, so
\alpha_{\max}
grows:

\eta(\pi_{Q})\!\downarrow\Rightarrow\alpha_{\max}\!\uparrow
.
In the extreme
\eta(\pi_{Q}){=}0
, the feasible range reads

\alpha\leq(\Delta_{\hat{Q}}-2\varepsilon_{\rm in})/(\kappa T)
.
Intuitively, when Q–guidance already behaves conservatively, we can open the LRT gate more (choose a larger
\alpha
) and still retain a certificate.

•

Levers that enlarge
\alpha_{\max}
.

(i)
Reduce
T
by gating only in late steps (then
\alpha_{\max}\propto 1/T
);
(ii)
Lower
\kappa
(e.g., smaller
\beta_{\max}
or restricting gating to small–variance steps; anchoring gradients at
\mu_{u}
also lowers off–support effects);
(iii)
Increase
\Delta_{\hat{Q}}
by taking a modest Q–step near
\mu_{\rm LRT}
when the gate opens or by using evidence–tied/annealed guidance.

C.5.2
Sweeping
\alpha
in practice.

The certificate is conservative for two reasons:
(i) it uses the union proxy
\eta(\pi_{\rm LRT})\!\leq\!\alpha T
;
(ii)
\Delta_{\hat{Q}}
can be biased when
\hat{Q}
overestimates OOD.
Thus even when
\alpha>\alpha_{\max}
and the inequality cannot be certified, LRT may still outperform Q–guidance empirically.
In experiments we therefore
sweep
\alpha
on a logarithmic grid and select the knee of the return–risk curve (Fig.
1
); the bound serves as a sanity upper bound for
\alpha
, not as a hard constraint.

A practical process.

1.

On a small held–out state batch, estimate
\widehat{\Delta}_{\hat{Q}}
and
\widehat{\eta}(\pi_{Q})
by generating actions from both samplers and evaluating
\hat{Q}
and the state–conditional OOD rate.

2.

Form the diagnostic

\widehat{\alpha}_{\max}~=~\frac{\widehat{\Delta}_{\hat{Q}}-2\varepsilon_{\rm in}-\kappa\,\widehat{\eta}(\pi_{Q})}{\kappa\,T}\quad

(use a conservative constant if
\varepsilon_{\rm in}
is unknown).

3.

Sweep
\alpha
on a log grid in

[\,\min(0.5\,\widehat{\alpha}_{\max},\,0.2),\,0.2\,]
,
and select the knee on the return–risk curves using the
calibrated
LRT sampler.

Why
T
matters.

For
T{=}50
and
\kappa{\approx}1
, even
\Delta_{\hat{Q}}-2\varepsilon_{\rm in}\approx 0.4
gives

\alpha_{\max}\approx 0.008
(quite small). Gating only in the last
10
steps effectively sets
T{=}10
, yielding
\alpha_{\max}\approx 0.04
.
This explains why late–step gating / smaller
\beta_{\max}
are both stable and easier to certify in practice.

Appendix D
Appending with Q-guidance

D.1
Choice of the evaluation center
a_{c}
.

•

a_{c}=\mu_{u}
(conservative).
Evaluate
\nabla_{a}\hat{Q}
near the behavior-supported mean; most stable and robust off-support.

•

a_{c}=\mu_{\text{LRT},t}
(exploitative).
When evidence opens the gate (
\beta_{t}
large), probe gradients closer to the “good” direction.

•

a_{c}=(1-\rho)\mu_{u}+\rho\,\mu_{\text{LRT},t}
(interpolate).

\rho\!\in[0,1]
trades conservatism for exploitation; a simple adaptive choice is
\rho=\beta_{t}/\beta_{\max}
.
