Risk factors for a full stomach assessed by gastric ultrasound in patients undergoing elective caesarean section: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yue Wei, Kunpeng Liu, Xi Lu, Jing Wang, Lan Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine the prevalence and risk factors for full stomachs as assessed by gastric ultrasound in patients scheduled for elective caesarean section (CS).DesignCase-control study.SettingOne tertiary general hospital in Beijing.ParticipantsA total of 140 non-labouring singleton pregnant women scheduled for elective CS between March 2024 and November 2024 at Peking University International Hospital were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: ≥18 years of age, ≥37 weeks of gestational age, an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status of II to III, and the ability to understand the rationale of the study assessments. The exclusion criteria included multiple pregnancy, obstetric urgency, an antenatal history of upper gastrointestinal surgery or upper gastrointestinal tract disease, the use of medications that may influence gastrointestinal motility and refusal to participate in the study.InterventionsBased on quantitative and qualitative ultrasound assessments of the gastric antrum, patients were divided into two groups: a full stomach group (defined by a Perlas A score of grade 2 or the presence of solid contents or a gastric volume (GV)/weight >1.5 mL/kg) and an empty stomach group (defined by a Perlas A score <grade 2 and a GV/weight ≤1.5 mL/kg).Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe prevalence and risk factors for full stomachs in patients scheduled for elective CS.ResultsA total of 134 parturients were analysed. Overall, 48 parturients (35.8%) were identified as having a full stomach despite adherence to standard fasting rules for elective CS. According to multifactorial logistic regression analysis, gastro-esophageal reflux disease (GERD) during pregnancy (adjusted OR: 3.90; 95% CI 1.15 to 13.23; p=0.029) was identified as an independent risk factor for full stomachs in patients scheduled for elective CS.ConclusionsIn this study, we found that GERD during pregnancy is an independent risk factor for full stomachs as assessed by gastric ultrasound in patients scheduled for elective CS. Our results suggest that if a parturient has GERD during pregnancy, it would be desirable to perform gastric ultrasound to evaluate gastric emptying status prior to anaesthesia.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trials Registry at http://www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2400082033).

Introduction

Pulmonary aspiration of gastric contents remains one of the most feared and potentially catastrophic complications in obstetric anaesthesia, with significant implications for maternal morbidity and mortality.1 Pregnant women are particularly vulnerable due to pregnancy-related physiological changes, including increased intragastric pressure from the gravid uterus and progesterone-mediated reductions in gastrointestinal motility.2 Gastric volume (GV) is a key determinant of both the risk and severity of aspiration events.3 4 As such, preoperative fasting has long been a fundamental component of anaesthetic practice, aimed at minimising GV and mitigating aspiration risk.

Nevertheless, despite adherence to established fasting guidelines, a considerable proportion of parturients presenting for elective caesarean section (CS) continue to demonstrate a high-risk gastric state. Previous studies employing gastric point-of-care ultrasound have reported a wide range of prevalence estimates from 9% to over 40%, suggesting that fasting duration alone is an unreliable predictor of gastric content status.57 This variability highlights the influence of patient-specific factors beyond fasting time, which remain poorly characterised.

The introduction of gastric ultrasound has provided a valuable, non-invasive tool for bedside assessment of gastric contents and volume. Its utility has been validated in both general adult8 9 and obstetric populations.5710 This technological advancement has enabled a transition from descriptive prevalence studies to more focused investigations into the determinants of delayed gastric emptying in pregnancy. While obesity has been implicated in some reports,7 11 12 a consistent and clinically actionable risk profile has not yet been identified.

Consequently, a significant knowledge gap persists in determining which parturients are at greatest risk of retaining substantial gastric contents despite appropriate fasting. Addressing this gap is essential for improving preoperative risk assessment and individualising perioperative management strategies. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of full stomachs in patients scheduled for elective CS and, more importantly, to identify independent risk factors associated with full stomachs using gastric ultrasound.

Methods

Participants

This prospective and observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University International Hospital, Beijing, China (Approval number: 2023⁃KY⁃0014⁃02) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry at http://www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2400082033). Written informed consent was obtained from all enrolled participants. We included consecutive non-labouring singleton pregnant women scheduled for elective CS between March 2024 and November 2024 in Peking University International Hospital, Beijing, China. All subjects routinely followed fasting guidelines (at least 6 hours for solid foods and 2 hours for clear liquids). The inclusion criteria were as follows: ≥18 years of age, ≥37 weeks of gestational age, an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of II to III, and the ability to understand the rationale of the study assessments. The exclusion criteria included multiple pregnancy, obstetric urgency, an antenatal history of upper gastrointestinal surgery or upper gastrointestinal tract disease, the use of medications that can influence gastrointestinal motility and refusal to participate in the study.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Data acquisition

Ultrasound examination was performed by a single anaesthesiologist with prior experience in the technique prior to anaesthesia. All subjects were first scanned in a semi-recumbent (45° head up) supine position, followed by a semirecumbent (45° head up) right lateral position (RLP). Gastric ultrasound was performed using a curvilinear low-frequency (2–5 MHz) transducer and a Philips CX50 ultrasound machine (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA, USA), following a standardised scanning protocol described in previous studies.13 On a sagittal scanning plane in the epigastrium, the gastric antrum was located in front of the left hepatic lobe and superficially behind the pancreas. Important landmarks of the standard gastric antrum cross-section include the abdominal aorta, inferior vena cava and superior mesenteric artery. A clear image was obtained by rotating or tilting the probe slightly, and the patient was instructed to breathe evenly, freezing the image at the end of expiration when the gastric sinus was in the contractile interval. The cross-sectional area (CSA) of the entire antral wall was then calculated using a free-tracing tool on the ultrasound machine. The average of three measurements was computed in each position.

Ultrasonographic assessment consisted of a qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the gastric antrum. Qualitative analysis of the nature of the gastric content (empty, fluid or solid content) at the antrum was performed with a grading score described by Perlas et al,14 which has been validated for general population and is increasingly being used in the obstetric population.6 7 15 16 Grade 0 was defined as the failure to visualise fluid in both supine and RLP; grade 1 was defined as the visualisation of fluid in RLP only; grade 2 was defined as the visualisation of fluid in both supine and RLP. The visualisation of solid, or of solid and liquid contents, in the antrum was recorded solid, whatever the position. Quantitative assessment was performed using the following mathematical model to calculate the volume of gastric contents in women in the third trimester of pregnancy, based on the antral area measured in both the supine and right lateral decubitus positions10: GV (ml)=0.18×R-CSA (cross-sectional area in RLP; mm2) ＋0.11×S-CSA (cross-sectional area in supine position; mm2)−62.4.

According to the qualitative and quantitative evaluation of gastric contents, the patients were divided into two groups: a full stomach group, in which subjects had a Perlas A grade of 2 (or had solids or had a GV/weight>1.5 mL/kg), and an empty stomach group, in which patients had a Perlas A grade <2 and a GV/weight≤1.5 mL/kg.8 17 18 According to previous literature, a GV/weight ratio of ≤1.5 mL/kg is considered to be within the normal range for gastric fluid volume.19

The patients’ age, gestational age, body mass index (BMI), fasting duration, parity, as well as gestational comorbidities, including gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) or pre-GDM, hypothyroidism, pre-eclampsia, polyhydramnios and macrosomia were recorded. Obesity was defined as a BMI≥30 kg∕m2, and advanced maternal age was defined as patients’ age≥35 years. Pregestational diabetes was diagnosed before pregnancy or in early pregnancy, and included type 1 and type 2 diabetes. GDM was diagnosed for the first time in mid-pregnancy (usually 24–28 weeks) by failing an Oral Glucose Tolerance Test. Gastro-esophageal reflux disease (GERD) during pregnancy was assessed by another anaesthesiologist, using the Chinese version of the Reflux Disease Questionnaire (RDQ), who was not involved in the gastric ultrasound evaluation. The RDQ comprises eight questions assessing the frequency and severity of burning and pain behind the breastbone, an acid taste in the mouth, the movement of materials upwards from the stomach, and burning and pain in the upper stomach, and complaints over the last 4 weeks which were scored on a six-point Likert scale. The frequency of symptoms was defined as follows: 0 points for no symptoms; 1 point for <1 day/week; 2 points for 1 day/week; 3 points for 2–3 days/week; 4 points for 4–5 days/week and 5 points for 6–7 days/week. The severity of symptoms was defined as follows: 0 points for no symptoms; one point for no obvious symptoms, as detected by medical staff; three points for obvious symptoms that affected daily life; and five points for very obvious symptoms that affected daily life and required medication. A symptom severity of between 1 and 3 was graded as two points, while a severity between 3 and 5 was graded as four points. The total of the symptom frequency and symptom intensity scores was graded out of a maximum of 40 points; a score of 12 was considered as the critical RDQ score for GERD.20

After ultrasound examination and RDQ scoring, the subjects received combined spinal-epidural anaesthesia at the L3-4 intervertebral space in the RLP. We injected 2.5 mL of 0.5% hyperbaric ropivacaine (1.5 mL of 1% ropivacaine +1.5 mL of 10% glucose) over 15 s with the spinal needle orifice facing in a cephalad direction. Once the subarachnoid block was complete, the epidural catheter was inserted 3–4 cm in the cephalad direction. The parturients were then returned to the supine position and given oxygen through a face mask at a rate of 5 L/min. The sensory block level was controlled at T6–T8. Ephedrine, phenylephrine and atropine were used to maintain the blood pressure and heart rate of the patient within 20% of baseline values. If general anaesthesia was required for patients with high-risk gastric contents, as assessed by ultrasound (eg, due to ineffective intravertebral anaesthesia), a nasogastric tube was placed to suction the gastric contents prior to induction and the volume and nature of the gastric contents were recorded.

Statistical analysis

According to a previous study,7 the proportion of parturients with a full stomach, despite a routine fasting period undergoing elective CS, was 37.5%, and the sample size was calculated by referring to the 10 events per variable (EPV) principle. Four influencing factors were proposed to be included in this study, so that the study required a minimum of 120 patients when the EPV was 10, and 140 patients were included in the study, taking into account the 15% dropout rate.

SPSS V.19.0 software was used to analyse the data. Normally distributed measures are expressed as mean±standard deviation (x±s), and comparisons between groups were performed using the t-test for two independent samples. The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact probability method was used to compare count data. Variables with p<0.05 or clinical relevance factors (eg, advanced maternal age, parity) were entered into a multifactor logistic regression to analyse and stratify risk factors for parturients with a full stomach scheduled for elective CS. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In total, 140 pregnant women were enrolled in this study. Due to an antenatal history of gastric surgery, gastric ultrasound was not performed for one parturient, and the other three parturients declined to take part in the study. The gastric antrum was not adequately visualised in two participants and 1.5% of inconclusive exams were excluded from the analysis. The analysis was therefore performed on 134 pregnant women in total (figure 1).

A flowchart depicting the study process.

None of the women had solid food visible. Of these, 41 patients (31%) were Perlas A grade 0, 74 patients (55%) were grade 1, and 19 patients (14%) were grade 2. In total, 86 patients (64.2%) had a GV/weight≤1.5 mL/kg, and 48 patients (35.8%) had a GV/weight>1.5 mL/kg. A full stomach occurred in 48 patients (35.8%) despite a routine fasting period undergoing elective CS. No intraoperative vomiting was observed in all the patients.

There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of age, gestational age, fasting time, BMI, parity, combined GDM, type 2 diabetes, hypothyroidism, polyhydramnios rates (p>0.05). Compared with the empty stomach group, the ASA classification ratio, the rate of obesity, the rate of combined GERD during pregnancy and the rate of combined fetal macrosomia were all significantly higher in the group with a full stomach (p<0.05); see table 1.

Discussion

In this prospective observational study using point-of-care gastric ultrasound, we found that over one-third of parturients presenting for elective CS had evidence of a full stomach despite adherence to standard preoperative fasting guidelines, which is consistent with two previous studies.6 7 The key finding of our multivariable analysis is that GERD during pregnancy emerged as the sole independent predictor of this high-risk gastric state. While obesity, higher ASA physical status and fetal macrosomia were associated with increased gastric content volume on univariate analysis, none remained significant after adjusting for GERD.

GERD is highly prevalent in pregnancy, largely attributable to hormonal and mechanical changes. Using the validated RDQ,20 we established that symptomatic GERD is strongly linked to incomplete gastric emptying. We propose that the underlying gastrointestinal dysmotility characteristic of GERD extends beyond the oesophagus to delay gastric clearance, a view supported by existing literature.21 22 Consequently, even in the absence of other comorbidities, parturients with GERD should be considered at elevated risk of a full stomach. Our findings reinforce the value of point-of-care gastric ultrasound in this subgroup to guide anaesthetic management, particularly when general anaesthesia is planned. Prophylactic use of prokinetics may also be considered, although further studies are required to confirm their efficacy.

Notably, obesity was not an independent predictor in our regression model, contrasting with some earlier studies.7 12 This discrepancy suggests that the effect of obesity may be mediated through its close association with GERD.23 24 Thus, in obese parturients without symptomatic GERD, the risk of a full stomach may not be substantially elevated, representing a nuanced insight with direct clinical relevance. Similarly, although fetal macrosomia and higher ASA status showed univariate associations, these likely reflect confounding by comorbidities such as GERD rather than representing direct causal pathways.

Contrary to expectations derived from studies on diabetic gastropathy,25 26 we observed no association between GDM and a full stomach. This distinction is clinically meaningful, as the transient metabolic disturbance of GDM may not induce the autonomic dysfunction required to delay gastric emptying. Therefore, routine gastric ultrasound may not be warranted in this group, provided standard fasting is observed.

A key strength of our study lies in its prospective design and standardised gastric ultrasound protocol performed by a single operator, minimising interobserver variability. The use of a validated tool (RDQ) for GERD diagnosis further enhances data reliability. Nevertheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Our definition of a full stomach relied on a volume threshold rather than clinical aspiration events. Given the rarity of aspiration,27 our study was not powered to assess this outcome. Future large-scale multicentre studies incorporating emergency caesarean deliveries are warranted to correlate sonographic findings directly with pulmonary aspiration risk.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides robust and clinically applicable evidence that GERD during pregnancy is a pivotal risk factor for a full stomach. Integrating preoperative GERD screening and targeted gastric ultrasound into anaesthetic workflows could enhance maternal safety by individualising risk stratification and management. Additionally, further research is needed on fasting guidelines for these specific populations.
