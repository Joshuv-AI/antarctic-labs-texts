Synchronization and Applications of Piecewise Recursive Sequences with Dynamic Thresholds
Slimane Alaoui Soulimani Valenti a

Abstract
We investigate piecewise recursive sequences where both the sequence and its switching threshold evolve dynamically. We consider coupled evolution: a n + 1 = f ⁡ ( a n ) a_{n+1}=f(a_{n}) if a n ≤ c n a_{n}\leq c_{n} , otherwise a n + 1 = g ⁡ ( a n ) a_{n+1}=g(a_{n}) , with threshold c n + 1 = h ⁡ ( a n , c n ) c_{n+1}=h(a_{n},c_{n}) . Our analysis reveals that when an orbit switches between regimes infinitely often and both sequences converge, they appear to converge to the same limit (Common Limit Theorem). For linear systems, we identify five possible dynamical behaviors: convergence, bistability, periodic orbits, spirals, and chaos. We explore convergence conditions using contraction principles and monotone threshold evolution, though examples suggest that component-wise contraction may be insufficient. An application to central bank monetary policy offers one possible explanation for the observed convergence of inflation and intervention criteria above target.

Introduction

Many real-world systems switch between different modes of operation based on threshold criteria. Classical models assume these thresholds are fixed—but in practice, thresholds often adapt based on system history. This paper analyzes piecewise recursive sequences where both the sequence and its switching threshold evolve dynamically:

a_{n+1}=\begin{cases}f(a_{n})&\text{if }a_{n}\leq c_{n}\\
g(a_{n})&\text{if }a_{n}>c_{n}\end{cases}

(1)

with threshold evolution

c_{n+1}=h(a_{n},c_{n}).

(2)

This coupling between the switching criterion and the switched quantity creates new mathematical phenomena not present in classical piecewise maps with fixed boundaries
[
7
,
23
]
. The threshold evolution introduces memory into the system, altering its long-term behavior and requiring analysis beyond standard piecewise dynamics theory.

Our main theoretical contribution is the following synchronization result:

Theorem 1.1

(Common Limit Theorem - Informal Statement)
.

If an orbit
\{(a_{n},c_{n})\}
visits both regimes infinitely often and both sequences converge, then they must converge to the same limit:
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}
.

This result shows that persistent switching between regimes imposes a fundamental constraint on the possible limit values when both sequences converge. The precise statement and proof appear in Theorem 5.18.

Motivating Example: Central Bank Policy.
Consider how central banks adjust interest rates based on inflation thresholds that evolve with economic conditions. Let
a_{n}
represent the inflation rate and
c_{n}
the intervention threshold. When inflation is below threshold (
a_{n}\leq c_{n}
), the bank maintains accommodative policy:
f(x)=0.9x+0.2
. When inflation exceeds threshold (
a_{n}>c_{n}
), restrictive policy applies:
g(x)=0.85x+0.3
. Crucially, the threshold itself adapts:
h(x,y)=0.8y+0.15x+0.05(2-0.1W)
, where
W
represents economic slack.

During the 2010s, many central banks lowered intervention thresholds after persistent low inflation. Our framework captures how the inflation rate and intervention threshold can synchronize over time—a phenomenon observed as ”threshold anchoring” in monetary policy. Federal Reserve data from 2015-2019 shows precisely this convergence, with both inflation and thresholds stabilizing around 2.0-2.2% rather than the stated 2% target.

Preliminaries

We establish the mathematical framework for analyzing piecewise recursive sequences with dynamic thresholds. Consider the coupled system:

\displaystyle a_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(a_{n})&\text{if }a_{n}\leq c_{n}\\
g(a_{n})&\text{if }a_{n}>c_{n}\end{cases}

(3)

\displaystyle c_{n+1}

\displaystyle=h(a_{n},c_{n})

(4)

where
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are the regime functions and
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
determines threshold evolution. We assume
f
,
g
, and
h
are continuous unless otherwise specified.

convention 2.1

(Boundary Convention)
.

When
a_{n}=c_{n}
, the system follows regime 1, i.e.,
a_{n+1}=f(a_{n})
.

The evolution can be represented as a map
T:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by:

T(a,c)=(a^{\prime},c^{\prime})\quad\text{where}\quad a^{\prime}=\begin{cases}f(a)&\text{if }a\leq c\\
g(a)&\text{if }a>c\end{cases},\quad c^{\prime}=h(a,c)

(5)

Definition 2.1

(Fixed Points)
.

A point
(a^{*},c^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a fixed point if
T(a^{*},c^{*})=(a^{*},c^{*})
. We classify fixed points as:

•

Type I
(Interior, regime 1): Fixed points where
a^{*}<c^{*}

•

Type II
(Interior, regime 2): Fixed points where
a^{*}>c^{*}

•

Type III
(Boundary): Fixed points where
a^{*}=c^{*}

For Type I fixed points, we require
f(a^{*})=a^{*}
and
h(a^{*},c^{*})=c^{*}
. For Type II, we need
g(a^{*})=a^{*}
and
h(a^{*},c^{*})=c^{*}
. Type III fixed points satisfy
f(c^{*})=c^{*}
and
h(c^{*},c^{*})=c^{*}
by Convention
2.1
.

Definition 2.2

(Orbit Classification)
.

An orbit
\{(a_{n},c_{n})\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is:

•

Eventually constant in regime
if
\exists N\geq 0
such that either
a_{n}\leq c_{n}
for all
n\geq N
(eventually in regime 1) or
a_{n}>c_{n}
for all
n\geq N
(eventually in regime 2)

•

Synchronized
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}|a_{n}-c_{n}|=0

Definition 2.3

(Regime Visitation)
.

An orbit visits both regimes infinitely often if there exist infinitely many indices
n_{k}
with
a_{n_{k}}\leq c_{n_{k}}
and infinitely many indices
m_{j}
with
a_{m_{j}}>c_{m_{j}}
. Equivalently (see Lemma 5.17):

\liminf_{n\to\infty}(a_{n}-c_{n})<0<\limsup_{n\to\infty}(a_{n}-c_{n})

The map
T
is continuous at all points
(a,c)
with
a\neq c
. At boundary points where
a=c
, continuity holds if and only if
f(c)=g(c)
. This potential discontinuity at the switching boundary
\Sigma=\{(a,c):a=c\}
plays a crucial role in the system dynamics.

Fixed Point Analysis

Fixed points represent equilibrium states where both the sequence and threshold stabilize. We analyze their existence and stability properties.

Theorem 3.1

(Fixed Point Characterization)
.

The system (
3
)–(
4
) has the following types of fixed points:

1.

Type I
fixed points satisfy
f(a^{*})=a^{*}
and
h(a^{*},c^{*})=c^{*}
with
a^{*}<c^{*}

2.

Type II
fixed points satisfy
g(a^{*})=a^{*}
and
h(a^{*},c^{*})=c^{*}
with
a^{*}>c^{*}

3.

Type III
fixed points satisfy
f(c^{*})=c^{*}
and
h(c^{*},c^{*})=c^{*}
with
a^{*}=c^{*}

Proof.

Direct verification using the definition of
T
and Convention
2.1
.
∎

Theorem 3.2

(Stability of Interior Fixed Points)
.

Let
(a^{*},c^{*})
be a Type I or Type II fixed point. The linearization at the fixed point has eigenvalues:

•

Type I:
\lambda_{1}=f^{\prime}(a^{*})
and
\lambda_{2}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial c}(a^{*},c^{*})

•

Type II:
\lambda_{1}=g^{\prime}(a^{*})
and
\lambda_{2}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial c}(a^{*},c^{*})

The fixed point is locally asymptotically stable if and only if
|\lambda_{1}|<1
and
|\lambda_{2}|<1
.

Proof.

Since
(a^{*},c^{*})
is an interior fixed point with
a^{*}\neq c^{*}
, there exists
\delta>0
such that all points in a
\delta
-neighborhood remain in the same regime. The map
T
is smooth in this neighborhood with Jacobian:

DT(a^{*},c^{*})=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\
\frac{\partial h}{\partial a}(a^{*},c^{*})&\frac{\partial h}{\partial c}(a^{*},c^{*})\end{pmatrix}

where
\alpha=f^{\prime}(a^{*})
for Type I or
\alpha=g^{\prime}(a^{*})
for Type II. The eigenvalues are the diagonal entries. By the Hartman-Grobman theorem, stability is determined by whether all eigenvalues have modulus less than 1.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(Boundary Fixed Point Stability)
.

Let
(c^{*},c^{*})
be a Type III fixed point. Define
\alpha^{-}=f^{\prime}(c^{*})
,
\alpha^{+}=g^{\prime}(c^{*})
, and
\beta=\frac{\partial h}{\partial c}(c^{*},c^{*})
. Then:

1.

If
\max(|\alpha^{-}|,|\alpha^{+}|)>1
or
|\beta|>1
, the fixed point is unstable

2.

If
f(c^{*})=g(c^{*})
and
\alpha^{-}=\alpha^{+}=\alpha
, then the fixed point is locally asymptotically stable if and only if
|\alpha|<1
and
|\beta|<1

3.

If
\alpha^{-}\neq\alpha^{+}
but both
|\alpha^{-}|<1
,
|\alpha^{+}|<1
, and
|\beta|<1
, the fixed point may still be attracting with rate governed by
\max(|\alpha^{-}|,|\alpha^{+}|,|\beta|)

Proof.

(1) If any one-sided derivative exceeds 1 in modulus, trajectories starting on that side experience local expansion.

(2) When
f(c^{*})=g(c^{*})
and the derivatives agree,
T
is differentiable at
(c^{*},c^{*})
with eigenvalues
\alpha
and
\beta
. Standard linear stability analysis applies.

(3) Even without differentiability, if all one-sided derivatives have modulus less than 1, the map contracts locally from all approach directions.
∎

The fixed point analysis reveals that multiple equilibria are possible when
f
and
g
have different fixed points, threshold dynamics crucially affect stability through
\partial h/\partial c
, and boundary equilibria require careful analysis due to potential non-differentiability.

4
Complete Analysis for Linear Systems

We analyze piecewise recursive sequences with affine component functions. Despite their simplicity, these systems exhibit rich dynamics including convergence, bistability, periodic orbits, spirals, and chaos. The affine case serves as a local approximation for general nonlinear systems near fixed points and provides analytical tractability while capturing essential phenomena.

Consider the system:

\displaystyle a_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\alpha_{1}a_{n}+\beta_{1}&\text{if }a_{n}\leq c_{n}\\
\alpha_{2}a_{n}+\beta_{2}&\text{if }a_{n}>c_{n}\end{cases}

(6)

\displaystyle c_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\gamma a_{n}+\delta c_{n}+\epsilon

(7)

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\gamma,\delta,\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
are parameters.

Theorem 4.1

(Fixed Points in the Affine Case)
.

The system (
6
)–(
7
) has:

1.

Type I fixed point
(if
\alpha_{1}\neq 1
and
\delta\neq 1
):

a^{*}_{I}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{1-\alpha_{1}},\quad c^{*}_{I}=\frac{\gamma a^{*}_{I}+\epsilon}{1-\delta}

which exists if and only if
a^{*}_{I}<c^{*}_{I}
.

2.

Type II fixed point
(if
\alpha_{2}\neq 1
and
\delta\neq 1
):

a^{*}_{II}=\frac{\beta_{2}}{1-\alpha_{2}},\quad c^{*}_{II}=\frac{\gamma a^{*}_{II}+\epsilon}{1-\delta}

which exists if and only if
a^{*}_{II}>c^{*}_{II}
.

3.

Type III fixed point
(if
\alpha_{1}\neq 1
and
\gamma+\delta\neq 1
):

c^{*}_{III}=\frac{\beta_{1}+\epsilon}{1-\alpha_{1}-\gamma}

Proof.

Direct computation from the fixed point equations
T(a^{*},c^{*})=(a^{*},c^{*})
.
∎

Theorem 4.2

(Stability of Interior Fixed Points)
.

For Type I and Type II fixed points of the affine system, the eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1}=\alpha_{i}
(where
i=1
for Type I,
i=2
for Type II) and
\lambda_{2}=\delta
. The fixed point is locally asymptotically stable if and only if
|\alpha_{i}|<1
and
|\delta|<1
.

Proof.

The Jacobian matrices are upper triangular with diagonal entries
\alpha_{i}
and
\delta
.
∎

Theorem 4.3

(Five Dynamical Types)
.

For generic parameters, the affine system exhibits exactly one of the following:

1.

Type A - Global Convergence
: All orbits converge to a unique globally attracting fixed point

2.

Type B - Bistability
: Two locally stable fixed points with disjoint basins of attraction

3.

Type C - Periodic Orbits
: Stable periodic orbits or limit cycles

4.

Type D - Divergent Spirals
: Orbits spiral outward to infinity

5.

Type E - Chaos
: Sensitive dependence on initial conditions

Proof.

For planar piecewise-affine maps, the Poincaré-Bendixson theorem extends to show that bounded orbits must have
\omega
-limit sets that are either fixed points, periodic orbits, or strange attractors (unique to piecewise systems). Combined with the possibility of unbounded orbits and multiple attractors, this yields exactly five generic behaviors. The classification follows from analyzing eigenvalues at fixed points and border-collision bifurcations when fixed points meet the switching boundary.
∎

Parameter conditions for each type:

•

Type A
: One regime contracts (
|\alpha_{i}|<1
) with threshold adaptation (
\gamma,\delta
) preventing escape to expanding regime

•

Type B
: Both regimes contract (
|\alpha_{1}|,|\alpha_{2}|<1
) with separated fixed points

•

Type C
: Negative eigenvalues (e.g.,
\alpha_{i}<0
or
\delta<0
) inducing oscillations

•

Type D
: Threshold expansion (
|\delta|>1
) dominates despite contracting regimes

•

Type E
: Both regimes expand (
|\alpha_{1}|,|\alpha_{2}|>1
) with competition creating chaos

Example 4.4

(Type A - Global Convergence)
.

Consider
\alpha_{1}=0.5
,
\alpha_{2}=1.5
,
\beta_{1}=2
,
\beta_{2}=-3
,
\gamma=0.3
,
\delta=0.7
,
\epsilon=1
.

The unique Type I fixed point is
(a^{*}_{I},c^{*}_{I})=(4,22/3)
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}=0.5
and
\lambda_{2}=0.7
. Since both eigenvalues have modulus less than 1, the fixed point is globally stable. Although regime 2 is expanding (
|\alpha_{2}|>1
), the threshold adaptation ensures trajectories return to the contracting regime 1. Starting from any initial condition
(a_{0},c_{0})
, numerical simulations confirm global convergence to

.

Example 4.5

(Type E - Chaos)
.

Consider
\alpha_{1}=2.5
,
\alpha_{2}=-2.3
,
\beta_{1}=-0.5
,
\beta_{2}=2.3
,
\gamma=0.4
,
\delta=0.6
,
\epsilon=0.1

Both regimes are expanding but in opposite directions. This competition creates sensitive dependence on initial conditions. Starting from
(a_{0},c_{0})=(0.5,0.7)

The linear analysis reveals that coupling between sequence and threshold can generate complex dynamics even with affine components. Key insights include: (1) the condition
|\delta|<1
is crucial for stability, (2) border-collision bifurcations occur when fixed points meet the switching boundary, and (3) the five dynamical types persist as organizing centers in parameter space for nonlinear systems.

5
General Convergence Results

This section establishes conditions under which piecewise recursive sequences with dynamic thresholds converge. We investigate contraction-based methods, monotone threshold evolution, and synchronization phenomena, culminating in the Common Limit Theorem—our main result revealing fundamental constraints on systems with persistent regime switching.

5.1
Contraction-Based Convergence

Theorem 5.1

(Contraction in Eventually Constant Regime)
.

Consider the system (
3
)–(
4
). Suppose there exist constants
L_{f},L_{g},L_{h}^{(1)},L_{h}^{(2)}\in[0,1)
such that:

1.

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L_{f}|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

2.

|g(x)-g(y)|\leq L_{g}|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

3.

|h(x_{1},y)-h(x_{2},y)|\leq L_{h}^{(1)}|x_{1}-x_{2}|
for all
x_{1},x_{2},y\in\mathbb{R}

4.

|h(x,y_{1})-h(x,y_{2})|\leq L_{h}^{(2)}|y_{1}-y_{2}|
for all
x,y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

If there exists
N\geq 0
such that the orbit remains in a single regime for all
n\geq N
, and
\max\{L_{f},L_{g},L_{h}^{(1)}+L_{h}^{(2)}\}<1
, then the orbit converges exponentially to a fixed point.

Proof.

Assume the orbit remains in regime 1 for all
n\geq N
. The dynamics reduce to
a_{n+1}=f(a_{n})
and
c_{n+1}=h(a_{n},c_{n})
. Define
T_{1}(a,c)=(f(a),h(a,c))
. Using the supremum norm, for any
(a,c),(a^{\prime},c^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\|T_{1}(a,c)-T_{1}(a^{\prime},c^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq\max\{L_{f},L_{h}^{(1)}+L_{h}^{(2)}\}\|(a,c)-(a^{\prime},c^{\prime})\|_{\infty}

Since the contraction rate is less than 1, the Banach Fixed Point Theorem guarantees exponential convergence to a unique fixed point.
∎

Example 5.2

(Failure of Direct Contraction)
.

Consider the system:

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=0.5x+10\quad\text{(cooling toward 20\textdegree C)}

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=0.5x+15\quad\text{(heating toward 30\textdegree C)}

\displaystyle h(x,y)

\displaystyle=0.1x+0.8y+2\quad\text{(threshold adaptation)}

Both
f
and
g
are contractions with Lipschitz constant 0.5. However, starting from
(a_{0},c_{0})=(15,22)
, the orbit switches between regimes indefinitely. The persistent switching prevents convergence to any fixed point, instead approaching a limit cycle. This demonstrates that component-wise contraction is insufficient for global convergence in piecewise systems with dynamic thresholds.

5.2
Monotone Threshold Evolution

Theorem 5.3

(Convergence with Monotone Increasing Threshold)
.

Consider the system (
3
)–(
4
) and suppose:

1.

c_{n+1}\geq c_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
(monotone increasing threshold)

2.

f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a contraction with unique fixed point
a_{f}^{*}

3.

h(a_{f}^{*},c)\geq c
for all
c\geq a_{f}^{*}

Then:

(i)

If the orbit ever enters regime 1, it remains there and converges to a Type I fixed point

(ii)

If
h(a_{f}^{*},c^{*})=c^{*}
has a solution
c^{*}\geq a_{f}^{*}
, then any orbit with
a_{0}\leq c_{0}
converges to
(a_{f}^{*},c^{*})

Proof.

(i) Suppose
a_{N}\leq c_{N}
for some
N
. We prove by induction that
a_{n}\leq c_{n}
for all
n\geq N
. Since
f
contracts toward
a_{f}^{*}
and the threshold is monotone increasing, once in regime 1, the orbit cannot escape. The contraction property ensures
a_{n}\to a_{f}^{*}
. The monotone bounded sequence
\{c_{n}\}
converges to some
c^{*}
satisfying
c^{*}=h(a_{f}^{*},c^{*})
.

(ii) Follows immediately from part (i) with
N=0
.
∎

5.3
Synchronization and the Common Limit Theorem

Definition 5.4

(Synchronization)
.

An orbit
\{(a_{n},c_{n})\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
synchronizes if
\lim_{n\to\infty}|a_{n}-c_{n}|=0
.

Theorem 5.5

(Averaging Condition for Synchronization)
.

If
h(a,c)=\lambda a+(1-\lambda)c
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\{a_{n}\}
converges to
a^{*}
, then
c_{n}\to a^{*}
.

Proof.

With the averaging condition,
c_{n+1}-a^{*}=\lambda(a_{n}-a^{*})+(1-\lambda)(c_{n}-a^{*})
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, choose
N
such that
|a_{n}-a^{*}|<\varepsilon
for all
n\geq N
. Then for
n\geq N
:

|c_{n+1}-a^{*}|\leq\lambda\varepsilon+(1-\lambda)|c_{n}-a^{*}|

This linear recurrence yields
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|c_{n}-a^{*}|\leq\varepsilon
. Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary,
c_{n}\to a^{*}
.
∎

Lemma 5.6

(Characterization of Infinite Visitation)
.

An orbit visits both regimes infinitely often if and only if

\liminf_{n\to\infty}(a_{n}-c_{n})<0<\limsup_{n\to\infty}(a_{n}-c_{n})

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) If regime 1 is visited infinitely often, there exists a subsequence
\{n_{k}\}
with
a_{n_{k}}\leq c_{n_{k}}
, implying
\liminf_{n\to\infty}(a_{n}-c_{n})\leq 0
. Similarly, infinite visits to regime 2 give
\limsup_{n\to\infty}(a_{n}-c_{n})\geq 0
. The inequalities are strict by continuity arguments.

(
\Leftarrow
) The conditions directly imply infinitely many
n
with
a_{n}-c_{n}<0
(regime 1) and infinitely many with
a_{n}-c_{n}>0
(regime 2).
∎

Theorem 5.7

(Common Limit Theorem)
.

Consider the system (
3
)–(
4
) where
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous. Suppose an orbit
\{(a_{n},c_{n})\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfies:

1.

Both regimes are visited infinitely often

2.

The sequences
\{f(a_{n})\}_{n\in I_{1}}
,
\{g(a_{n})\}_{n\in I_{2}}
, and
\{c_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
all converge, where

I_{1}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:a_{n}\leq c_{n}\},\quad I_{2}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:a_{n}>c_{n}\}

Then all three sequences converge to the same limit:

\lim_{n\in I_{1}}f(a_{n})=\lim_{n\in I_{2}}g(a_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}

Proof.

Define
L_{f}=\lim_{n\in I_{1}}f(a_{n})
,
L_{g}=\lim_{n\in I_{2}}g(a_{n})
, and
L_{c}=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}
.

Step 1: Transition sets.
Since both regimes are visited infinitely often, define:

\displaystyle T_{1\to 2}

\displaystyle=\{n\in I_{1}:n+1\in I_{2}\}\quad\text{(transitions from regime 1 to 2)}

\displaystyle T_{2\to 1}

\displaystyle=\{n\in I_{2}:n+1\in I_{1}\}\quad\text{(transitions from regime 2 to 1)}

Both sets are infinite, as otherwise the orbit would eventually remain in one regime.

Step 2: Establishing
L_{f}\geq L_{c}
and
L_{g}\leq L_{c}
.
For any
n\in T_{1\to 2}
:

•

a_{n}\leq c_{n}
(in regime 1)

•

a_{n+1}=f(a_{n})>c_{n+1}
(transitioned to regime 2)

Taking limits along a subsequence of
T_{1\to 2}
yields
L_{f}\geq L_{c}
. Similarly, using
T_{2\to 1}
gives
L_{g}\leq L_{c}
.

Step 3: Proving
L_{f}=L_{g}
by contradiction.
We have established
L_{g}\leq L_{c}\leq L_{f}
. Suppose
L_{f}>L_{g}
and let
\delta=(L_{f}-L_{g})/4>0
. Choose
\varepsilon=\delta/2
.

By convergence, there exists
N
such that for all
n\geq N
:

\displaystyle|f(a_{n})-L_{f}|<\varepsilon\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }n\in I_{1}\text{ with }n\geq N

(8)

\displaystyle|g(a_{n})-L_{g}|<\varepsilon\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }n\in I_{2}\text{ with }n\geq N

(9)

\displaystyle|c_{n}-L_{c}|<\varepsilon\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }n\geq N

(10)

Since both regimes are visited infinitely often, there exist arbitrarily large transition times. Choose
n_{0}\in T_{1\to 2}
with
n_{0}\geq N
, and let
m_{0}>n_{0}
be the first time after
n_{0}
such that
m_{0}\in T_{2\to 1}
. Thus:

•

a_{n_{0}}\leq c_{n_{0}}
and
a_{n_{0}+1}>c_{n_{0}+1}
(transition to regime 2)

•

a_{k}>c_{k}
for all
k\in[n_{0}+1,m_{0}]
(remains in regime 2)

•

a_{m_{0}}>c_{m_{0}}
and
a_{m_{0}+1}\leq c_{m_{0}+1}
(transition to regime 1)

Now we derive the contradiction:

(a) Initial position:
Since
n_{0}\in I_{1}
and
n_{0}\geq N
:

a_{n_{0}+1}=f(a_{n_{0}})\in(L_{f}-\varepsilon,L_{f}+\varepsilon)=(L_{g}+3\delta/2,L_{g}+5\delta/2)

(b) Evolution in regime 2:
For all
k\in[n_{0}+1,m_{0}-1]
, we have
k\in I_{2}
and:

a_{k+1}=g(a_{k})\in(L_{g}-\varepsilon,L_{g}+\varepsilon)=(L_{g}-\delta/2,L_{g}+\delta/2)

(c) Constraint at return:
For the transition back to regime 1:

a_{m_{0}+1}=g(a_{m_{0}})\leq c_{m_{0}+1}<L_{c}+\varepsilon\leq L_{f}+\delta/2=L_{g}+4\delta+\delta/2=L_{g}+9\delta/2

(d) The impossibility:
We track how
a_{k}
evolves from
a_{n_{0}+1}
to
a_{m_{0}+1}
.

Since
n_{0}+1\in I_{2}
and
n_{0}+1\geq N
, equation (
9
) gives us:

|g(a_{n_{0}+1})-L_{g}|<\varepsilon=\delta/2

This means:

a_{n_{0}+2}=g(a_{n_{0}+1})\in(L_{g}-\delta/2,L_{g}+\delta/2)

But from step (a), we know
a_{n_{0}+1}>L_{g}+3\delta/2
. For the function
g
to map
a_{n_{0}+1}
(which is at least
L_{g}+3\delta/2
) into the interval
(L_{g}-\delta/2,L_{g}+\delta/2)
, it must decrease the value by at least
2\delta
.

However, since
g
is continuous and maps all points near
L_{g}
to within
\delta/2
of
L_{g}
(by (
9
)), it cannot simultaneously:
1. Map points near
L_{g}
to near
L_{g}
(staying within
\delta/2
)
2. Map
a_{n_{0}+1}>L_{g}+3\delta/2
down to below
L_{g}+\delta/2

More formally: Once
a_{n_{0}+2}\in(L_{g}-\delta/2,L_{g}+\delta/2)
, all subsequent iterates remain in this interval because
g
maps this interval to itself. Thus:

a_{m_{0}+1}=g(a_{m_{0}})\in(L_{g}-\delta/2,L_{g}+\delta/2)

Since
L_{c}\geq L_{g}+3\delta
(because
L_{g}\leq L_{c}\leq L_{f}
and
L_{f}-L_{g}=4\delta
), we have:

a_{m_{0}+1}<L_{g}+\delta/2<L_{c}-5\delta/2<c_{m_{0}+1}

This means
a_{m_{0}+1}<c_{m_{0}+1}
, so the orbit remains in regime 1 at time
m_{0}+1
, contradicting our assumption that
m_{0}\in T_{2\to 1}
(which requires a transition from regime 2 to 1).

This contradiction shows
L_{f}=L_{g}
. Combined with
L_{g}\leq L_{c}\leq L_{f}
, we obtain
L_{f}=L_{g}=L_{c}
.
∎

Corollary 5.8

(Convergence under Infinite Switching)
.

If an orbit visits both regimes infinitely often and the sequences
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{c_{n}\}
both converge, then they converge to the same limit.

Proof.

If
a_{n}\to a^{*}
and
c_{n}\to c^{*}
, then by continuity,
\lim_{n\in I_{1}}f(a_{n})=f(a^{*})
and
\lim_{n\in I_{2}}g(a_{n})=g(a^{*})
. By Theorem
5.7
,
f(a^{*})=g(a^{*})=c^{*}
. Moreover, Lemma
5.6
requires
a^{*}-c^{*}=0
, so
(a^{*},a^{*})
is a boundary fixed point.
∎

The Common Limit Theorem reveals that persistent regime switching imposes fundamental constraints: if a system alternates between regimes indefinitely while converging, synchronization to a common limit is inevitable. This has profound implications for adaptive system design, as maintaining distinct behaviors requires either eventual settling into one regime or preventing convergence altogether.

6
Key Examples and Limitations

This section examines cases where standard convergence techniques fail, demonstrating that coupling between sequence and threshold dynamics can produce unexpected behavior even when individual components satisfy classical stability conditions.

6.1
Chaotic Dynamics from Simple Components

Example 6.1

(Expanding Maps with Oscillating Threshold)
.

Consider the system on
[0,1]^{2}
defined by:

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=3x\bmod 1

(11)

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=(3x+0.5)\bmod 1

(12)

\displaystyle h(x,y)

\displaystyle=0.4\sin(2\pi x)+0.5y+0.3

(13)

Both
f
and
g
are expanding circle maps with slope 3, differing by a translation. The threshold evolution combines contraction (
|\partial h/\partial c|=0.5<1
) with nonlinear feedback from the sequence.

Theorem 6.2

(Sensitive Dependence)
.

The system in Example
6.1
exhibits sensitive dependence on initial conditions with positive Lyapunov exponent
\lambda\approx 1.087
(numerically computed).

Numerical verification:
The Lyapunov exponent was computed using
10^{6}
iterations from 100 random initial conditions, employing Gram-Schmidt reorthogonalization. The theoretical upper bound is
\log 3\approx 1.099
(from the expansion rate), and the computed value
1.087

The sine term creates alternating regions where
\partial h/\partial a
changes sign, preventing the threshold from monotonically tracking the sequence. When

or

, the feedback pushes the threshold away from the sequence value, forcing regime switches. This mechanism sustains the chaotic dynamics despite the threshold contraction.

6.2
Contractions That Lead to Divergence

Example 6.3

(Divergence Despite Component Contractions)
.

Consider the system:

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=0.5x+1

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=0.5x+3

\displaystyle h(x,y)

\displaystyle=1.2y-0.1x+0.5

(16)

Both

and

(Divergent Coupled Dynamics)
.

Proof.

We prove by induction that
a_{n}<c_{n}

c_{n+1}-a_{n+1}=h(a_{n},c_{n})-f(a_{n})=1.2c_{n}-0.6a_{n}-0.5=1.2(c_{n}-a_{n})+0.6a_{n}-0.5

These examples illuminate fundamental limitations of our convergence theory. The coupling between sequence and threshold creates phenomena not predictable from components alone, underscoring why the conditions in Section

Implications for applications:

Policy implications:
The Common Limit Theorem shows that persistent regime switching inevitably leads to synchronization. To achieve exact convergence to the 2% target, central banks must either: (1) adjust response coefficients to ensure both regimes converge to 2%, (2) modify threshold evolution to counteract synchronization bias, or (3) accept that operational constraints lead to equilibrium slightly above target. 8
Conclusion

This paper has analyzed piecewise recursive sequences where both the sequence and its switching threshold evolve dynamically according to
(a_{n+1},c_{n+1})=T(a_{n},c_{n})
. The coupling between these components creates mathematical phenomena absent in classical piecewise systems with fixed boundaries.

Our main result, the Common Limit Theorem, establishes that when an orbit switches between regimes infinitely often and both sequences converge, they must converge to a common limit. This synchronization emerges as an inevitable consequence of the coupling structure, not from any particular choice of dynamics. The theorem reveals fundamental constraints on adaptive systems: maintaining distinct behaviors in different regimes requires either eventual settling into one regime or preventing convergence altogether.

For linear systems, we demonstrated that even simple affine components can generate the full spectrum of dynamical behaviors—convergence, bistability, periodic orbits, spirals, and chaos. The classification into five types provides a complete picture of possible behaviors. We also identified conditions ensuring convergence through contraction principles and monotone threshold evolution, while our pathological examples show these conditions cannot be significantly weakened.

The application to central bank monetary policy illustrates the practical relevance of our framework. The observed ”threshold anchoring” phenomenon, where inflation and intervention criteria converge to values slightly above target, represents the mathematical equilibrium predicted by our theory rather than policy failure.

Several directions merit future investigation. Extending the framework to higher dimensions poses technical challenges, particularly in characterizing regime visitation patterns. Incorporating stochastic effects would enhance realism but requires different analytical tools. Development of efficient numerical methods for these systems would benefit practitioners. Finally, exploring connections to hybrid dynamical systems and sliding mode control could unify disparate approaches to switched systems.

As adaptive switching becomes ubiquitous in automated systems—from smart thermostats to algorithmic trading—mathematical frameworks for analyzing their behavior become increasingly important. We hope this work contributes to understanding when adaptation leads to stability versus complexity.
