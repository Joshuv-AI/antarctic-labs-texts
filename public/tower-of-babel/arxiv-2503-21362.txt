Stokes’ phenomenon in continuous limits of discrete Painlevé I
Christopher J. Lustri, John R. King

Abstract
We use exponential asymptotic analysis to identify the relevance of Stokes’ phenomenon to integrability in discrete systems. We study Stokes’ phenomenon in two discrete problems with the same (leading-order) continuous limit, a finite-difference discretisation of the first continuous Painlevé equation and the first discrete Painlevé equation, as well as a family of differential equation associated with each discrete problem. This analysis reveals two important observations. Firstly, the orderly behaviour that characterises Stokes’ phenomenon in discrete equations emerges naturally from corresponding continuous differential equations as the order of the latter increases, although this is not apparent at low orders. Secondly, Stokes’ phenomenon vanishes in the continuum limit of the integrable discrete equation, but not the non-integrable discrete equation. This means that subdominant exponentials do not appear in the integrable equation, and therefore do not cause moveable singularities to form in the solution. The results are clarified further by consideration of one-parameter family of difference equations that interpolates between the two considered in detail.



1
Introduction

We consider two discrete variants of a scaled version of the first Painlevé equation,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}y}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}+3y^{2}=-2z.

(1)

The first discrete equation is obtained by approximating the differential term using a second-order central derivative. The second equation is the first discrete Painlevé equation (dPI), which is integrable
[
1
,
37
]
. Both of these equations tend to (
1
) in the appropriate continuum limit
[
15
]
when represented as an infinite-order differential equation.

This purpose of this study is to answer two questions:

1.

How is Stokes’ phenomenon seen in discrete equations consistent with that seen in continuous differential equations with finite order?

2.

What are the differences in Stokes’ phenomena between integrable and non-integrable discrete equations that have the same continuum limit?

We will resolve the first question by studying Stokes’ phenomenon in the infinite-order equation for the finite-difference discretisation of (
1
) and comparing it with the finite-order differential equations obtained by truncating the finite-difference expansions. We will resolve the second question by comparing Stokes’ phenomenon from the finite-difference discretisation with the corresponding behaviour in the integrable first discrete Painlevé equation.

Recent studies have shown that discretisation of continuous systems causes asymptotic effects to appear in the solution that depend on the choice of discretisation. In
[
25
]
, the authors observed that discretising the KdV (Korteweg-de Vries) equation using a finite-difference scheme causes exponentially small ripples to appear around the classical soliton solution. The authors of
[
30
]
studied this phenomenon in a discretised nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation. They identified the same behaviour, and found that the choice of discretisation, including properties such as the discretisation order and the relative step size in space and time, can cause the ripples to change in form or even vanish entirely.

This asymptotic behaviour is a consequence of Stokes’ phenomenon. Stokes’ phenomenon describes behaviour seen in asymptotic solutions to singularly-perturbed equations, whereby terms that are exponentially small in the asymptotic limit appear and disappear as important curves in the complex plane, known as Stokes lines, are crossed
[
9
,
11
]
. The solutions also contain anti-Stokes lines, across which exponentially small terms grow to become non-negligible.

To calculate the effects of Stokes’ phenomenon, we utilise asymptotic techniques that can capture behaviour that is exponentially small in the asymptotic limit, known as exponential asymptotics. If a divergent asymptotic series, such as those typically obtained from singularly perturbed equations, is truncated optimally, the remainder term is generally exponentially small in the asymptotic limit
[
9
,
11
]
. This remainder term may be studied directly, and describes an exponentially small asymptotic contributions to the solution. This idea was introduced by Berry
[
7
,
8
]
and Berry & Howls
[
6
]
, who used such methods to determine the behaviour of special functions such as the Airy function.

In this study, we will apply an exponential asymptotic method for studying discrete equations in their continuum limit developed by King & Chapman
[
27
]
, which relies on writing the discrete equation as an infinite-order ordinary differential equation. This method built on the techniques developed by Olde Daalhuis et al.
[
34
]
for linear differential equations, and by Chapman et al.
[
13
]
for nonlinear differential equations. We will use this method to locate Stokes lines in the two discrete equations and to calculate the exponentially small terms that appear as they are crossed. These methods were used in
[
27
]
to study dislocations in Frenkel-Kontorova chains, in
[
24
,
22
,
23
]
to study the validity of asymptotic solutions to discrete Painlevé I and II, and
q
-Painlevé I, and in
[
2
,
25
,
30
]
to study exponentially small waves in discretised KdV and NLS equations.

In these analyses, Stokes’ phenomenon led to the appearance of an infinite number of exponential terms with exponents
-\chi/\epsilon
, where the values of
\chi^{\prime}
were evenly spaced along the imaginary axis, typically occurring at
\chi^{\prime}=2M\pi\mathrm{i}
for
M\in\mathbb{Z}
. This behaviour is not seen in corresponding continuous equations
[
25
,
30
]
. In this study, we will clarify how the behaviour of exponential terms in discrete systems emerges from the associated infinite-dimensional differential equations.

An important class of discrete equations is integrable nonlinear difference equations, such as the discrete Painlevé equations. Integrability in discrete equations is typically associated with conditions such as singularity confinement and algebraic entropy (see
[
1
]
for a detailed discussion of indicators for discrete integrability). We will not concentrate on discrete integrability conditions, but rather the emergence of continuous markers of integrability when taking appropriate continuum limits; however, we will discuss singularity confinement as a marker of discrete integrability for two significant discrete equations in Appendix
E
.

Joshi & Lustri
[
24
]
and Joshi, Luu & Lustri
[
22
]
used exponential asymptotic techniques to calculate the effect of Stokes’ phenomenon in the far-field solutions to discrete Painlevé equations I and II. Other previous studies
[
5
,
26
,
41
]
determined special solutions that are asymptotically pole free in sectors of the complex plane, and used transseries to study matrix models associated with discrete Painlevé equations
[
4
]
.

We will explore the asymptotic behaviour of solutions to discrete Painlevé I in an important continuum limit which connects dPI with the continuous first Painlevé equation
[
15
]
, in order to explain how the integrability of the system is linked to Stokes’ phenomenon in this system. Our analysis will show that the solution for this special case does not contain Stokes switching, as the Stokes multipliers are equal to zero. As we shall see, this means that the solution to the corresponding infinite-order differential equation does not contain any moveable singularities, which is a marker of integrability in continuous problems.

Finally, we note that other exponential asymptotic methods, such as Borel transform analysis, have been used to study exponentially small terms in discrete equations, such as the hyperasymptotic analysis of second-order linear difference equations in
[
35
]
. It is likely that such methods could also be applied to the discrete problems considered in this study.

2.2
Finite-Difference Discretisation

We can apply a straightforward finite-difference discretisation to (
1
). Define the finite-difference step to be
\delta z=\epsilon
. Using a second-order central difference stencil to approximate the second derivative in (
1
) gives

\frac{y(z-\epsilon)-2y(z)+y(z+\epsilon)}{\epsilon^{2}}+3y(z)^{2}=-2z.

(7)

Formally expanding this expression around
\epsilon=0
gives

2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\epsilon^{2m-2}}{(2m)!}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2m}y(z)}{\mathrm{d}z^{2m}}+3y(z)^{2}=-2z.

(8)

This provides a continuous interpolation
y(z)
between the discrete solution points. This interpolation is not unique; for example, it is always possible to add multiples of
\sin(2\pi z/\epsilon)
to
y(z)
to produce a new function that takes the same value at each finite difference step
z=z_{0}+n\epsilon
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. These terms oscillate rapidly in the limit that
\epsilon\to 0
, meaning that an interpolation containing these terms is likely to be highly singular in the asymptotic limit. The interpolation generated by (
7
) does not contain such highly singular oscillations.

Motivated by the form of the tritronquée solution, we would like to pose the condition that on the positive real axis,
y(z)=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{z})
as
z\to\infty
. In fact, this condition does not uniquely define the solution, as there is an exponentially-small term with an arbitrary constant present in the solution on the positive real axis. Instead, we prescribe the condition that
y(z)=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{z})
as
|z|\to\infty
for
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
. This forces the arbitrary constant to be zero in a region containing the positive real axis and therefore does uniquely specify the solution to the differential equation.

We define the asymptotic power series

y(z)\sim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2j}y_{j}(z)\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad\epsilon\to 0.

(9)

The far-field condition gives
y_{0}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{z})
as
|z|\to\infty
in the right half-plane. The leading-order behaviour
y_{0}
satisfies the differential equation (
1
). We select
y_{0}
to be the tritronquée solution, as the asymptotic behaviour as
|z|\to\infty
is given by (
5
), which is consistent with the far-field condition for
y_{0}
. This solution for
y_{0}
contains double poles at the locations in Table
1
, the asymptotic behaviour near these poles being given by (
6
).

2.3
Discrete Painlevé I

An important discrete equation is the discrete first Painlevé equation, or discrete Painlevé I, given by

w_{n+1}+w_{n}+w_{n-1}=\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}n}{w_{n}}+c_{3},

(10)

where

are constants for

. We consider a distinguished continuum limit from

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon^{2}y(z))(3+\epsilon^{2}y(z+\epsilon)

\displaystyle+\epsilon^{2}y(z)+\epsilon^{2}y(z-\epsilon))

\displaystyle=3+r_{2}\epsilon^{4}z+6\epsilon^{2}y(z)-r_{1}\epsilon^{4}y(z)-r_{3}\epsilon^{4}(1+y(z)).

Formally expanding about
\epsilon=0

This equation can always be transformed into the Painlevé I equation (

satisfies (

) is not well-ordered in the asymptotic limit if

. It therefore ceases to be valid in the regime where
\epsilon(z-z_{p})^{-1}
is not small in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, i.e. when
z-z_{p}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. This motivates the rescaling of (
17
) in terms of the rescaled variables

z-z_{p}=\epsilon\eta,\qquad y(z)=\frac{v(\eta)}{\epsilon^{2}}.

(31)

This change of variables gives

{2}\sum_{m=1}^{K}\frac{1}{(2m)!}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2m}v(\eta)}{\mathrm{d}\eta^{2m}}+{3v(\eta)^{2}}=-2{\epsilon^{4}}(z_{p}+\epsilon\eta).

(32)

To describe the behaviour of the solution near the singularity
z_{p}
, we require only the leading-order terms as
\epsilon\to 0
, and therefore neglect the right-hand side. We expand the leading-order inner solution as

v(\eta)\sim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{v_{j}}{\eta^{2j+2}}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad|\eta|\to\infty,

(33)

where
v_{0}=-2
is required to be consistent with the asymptotic behaviour (
6
). Substituting this expression into (
32
) and neglecting the subdominant terms in the limit that
\epsilon\to 0
gives

2\sum_{m=1}^{K}\frac{1}{(2m)!}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2j+2m+1)!}{(2j+1)!}\frac{v_{j}}{\eta^{2j+2m+2}}+3\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{v_{\ell}v_{j}}{\eta^{2j+2\ell+4}}=0.

(34)

Balancing this expression at
\mathcal{O}(\eta^{-2k-4})
, we obtain

2\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{min}(K,k+1)}\binom{2k+3}{2m}v_{k-m+1}+3\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}v_{\ell}v_{k-\ell}=0.

(35)

This gives the recurrence relation for
v_{k}

2(k+3)(2k-1)\,v_{k}=2\sum_{m=2}^{\mathrm{min}(K,k+1)}\binom{2k+3}{2m}v_{k-m+1}+3\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1}v_{\ell}v_{k-\ell}.

(36)

Using this recurrence relation, values of
v_{j}
may be calculated to arbitrarily large order. Note that the number of terms in the first sum in the recurrence relation increases until
j>K-1
, beyond which the recurrence relation does not change. This will not be true for the discrete equation (i.e.
K\to\infty
), in which the summation term grows indefinitely.

The final step is to match the inner series (
33
) with the late-order ansatz (
28
). For simplicity, we will demonstrate the matching explicitly for
K=2
in Section
3.2.1
, and will later contrast the process for obtaining this result with the equivalent analysis for the discrete problem (i.e.
K\to\infty
) in both the finite-difference and integrable cases.

The contribution to
y_{k}
as
k\to\infty
from the pole at
z=z_{p}
is given by a sum of terms with the form

y_{k}\sim\sum_{\ell=1}^{2K-2}\frac{\Lambda_{\ell}\Gamma(2k+2)}{\chi_{\ell}^{2k+2}}.

(37)

From this, we are able to determine the Stokes structure of the solution caused by the pole at
z=z_{p}
.

3.2.1
K=2

For the case
K=2
, the singulant equation becomes

\frac{\chi^{\prime}(z)^{2}}{12}+1=0,\qquad\chi(z_{p})=0.

(38)

Each pole produces two singulants,

\chi_{1}=2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}(z-z_{p}),\qquad\chi_{2}=-2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}(z-z_{p}).

(39)

Note that
\chi^{\prime}
is imaginary here. In any case where
K
is even, we will obtain at least one pair of imaginary values for
\chi^{\prime}
, due to the symmetry of solutions to (

\Lambda_{\ell}
for

. By expressing the terms of the inner series in terms of the outer variable

, we find that setting
j=2k
gives the matching condition

\frac{v_{k}}{(z-z_{p})^{{2k}+2}}\sim\frac{(\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2})\Gamma(2k+2)}{[2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}(z-z_{p})]^{2k+2}}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad k\to\infty.

(40)

The ansatz (
28
) is chosen under the assumption that there are no terms in the expansion with odd powers of
\epsilon
. This condition requires that
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
be identical; if this were not the case, we would have to include terms with odd powers of
\epsilon
in (
33
). For simplicity, we subsequently denote both
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
as
\Lambda
. The matching condition (

2\Lambda=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{v_{k}(2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3})^{2k+2}}{\Gamma(2k+2)}.

(41)

The value of the prefactor constants can be approximated by calculating
v_{2k}
up to some large value of

, and evaluating the right-hand side of this expression. In Figure

obtained by evaluating this expression for various

y_{k}\sim\frac{\Lambda\Gamma(2k+2)}{[2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}(z-z_{p})]^{2k+2}}+\frac{\Lambda\Gamma(2k+2)}{[-2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}(z-z_{p})]^{2k+2}},\qquad\Lambda\approx 119.635.

(42)

3.3
Stokes’ Phenomenon

Once we have the form of the late-order terms (
37
), we may determine the Stokes structure of the solutions. Stokes lines are curves across which exponentially small terms experience rapid jumps, often switching on or off entirely. From
[
17
]
, we know that Stokes lines corresponding to exponentially small terms that are switched by an algebraic power series satisfy the condition

\mathrm{Im}(\chi)=0,\qquad\mathrm{Re}(\chi)>0.

(46)

Anti-Stokes lines are curves across which exponential terms change between being exponentially small to exponentially large in the asymptotic limit. These curves satisfy the condition

\mathrm{Re}(\chi)=0.

(47)

It is important to note that, given we are dealing with nonlinear problems, in the presence of exponentially large terms we can no longer describe the solution behaviour using the power series (
9
) beyond these curves. The exponential terms in the solution will instead grow, and the dominant asymptotic balance in the equation will change.

3.3.1
K=2

If
K=2
we find that there are two exponential contributions associated with each leading-order pole, given by
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
in (

and

Stokes lines originate at leading-order singularities. We consider the Stokes lines originating at

\chi_{1}

If we return to the expression obtained by substituting the late-order term ansatz (

This expression is almost identical to (

Conclusions

This study used exponential asymptotics to resolve two questions about Stokes’ phenomenon. The first question was how Stokes’ phenomenon in discrete systems emerged from the solution of continuous systems as the order tends to infinity. The second question was how Stokes’ phenomenon differs between integrable and non-integrable discrete systems, even when they tend to the same continuum limit.

In the first section of this paper, we studied Stokes’ phenomenon present in a family of equations obtained by applying finite-difference discretisation to the Painlevé I equation, expanding this as a power series in the discretisation parameter, and truncating after a fixed number of terms,
K
. Our analysis showed that increasing
K
caused Stokes’ phenomenon in the solution to become more complicated, due to the increasing number of complex-valued solutions for the quantity
\chi^{\prime}
.

We then calculated Stokes’ phenomenon present in the solution to the full, non-truncated equation, which only has imaginary-valued solutions for
\chi^{\prime}
, producing a much simpler Stokes structure. By studying the behaviour of solutions for
\chi^{\prime}
as
K
increases, we showed that as
K\to\infty
, the solutions for
\chi^{\prime}
either tend to the imaginary axis, or vanish. This explains the emergence of the simple Stokes structure in discrete problems that was observed in
[
25
,
30
]
.

In the second section of this paper, we performed a similar analysis for the continuum limit of discrete Painlevé I. We found that for finite
K
, Stokes’ phenemonon present in the truncated differential equation family was nearly identical to that seen in the finite-difference equations, with the exponentially small terms differing only by a nonzero constant factor.

When we repeated the exponential asymptotic analysis for the infinite-order differential equation generated from discrete Painlevé I, we determined that, unlike the finite-difference case, the asymptotic series for the solution does not diverge. In fact, the local solution near each pole is convergent, meaning that Stokes’ phenomenon is not present in the solution. Hence, no subdominant exponentials appear; in the analysis of the finite-
K
family, we showed numerical evidence that the exponentials would grow across anti-Stokes lines and generate moveable singular points in the asymptotic solution. The absence of Stokes’ phenomenon in the solution to the the infinite-order differential equation means that these moveable singular points instead do not appear. This demonstrates that the asymptotic behaviour of the integrable discrete system in the continuum limit is consistent with continuous markers of integrability; in this case, the asymptotic solution does not contain moveable singular points.

The analysis in Section
6
predicted that Stokes’ phenomenon is not present in the
\Delta=1
case, among others. It should be noted that the
\Delta=1
case is different to the
\Delta=0
case in that the coefficients of the inner problem,
v_{k}
, do not decay as
k\to\infty
. For
\Delta=0
the coefficients were found to be
v_{k}=-2^{1-2k}
, while for
\Delta=1
the coefficients were
v_{k}=-2
. This means that the late-order terms do not vanish, but neither do they demonstrate factorial over power divergence. We did not pursue this question in the present study; however, it would be instructive to determine whether difference equations associated with
\Delta=1
, such as (
20
) are also integrable.

In each problem, determining the Stokes switching behaviour required us to rescale the problem to study the local behaviour of the solution near singular points. It is notable that for both the finite-difference and discrete Painlevé I equation, this inner problem recovered the leading-order discrete equation (see (
63
) and (
78
)). In discrete problems, we typically find that
\chi^{\prime}
is a constant, and therefore that
\chi
is a linear function of
z
. If
\chi\propto(z-z_{s})
, the asymptotic series (
9
) must cease to be asymptotic if
z-z_{s}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. From this, we conclude that the inner scaling should be
\eta=\epsilon z
, which is precisely the original discrete scaling. We therefore expect that in discrete problems, Stokes switching in the corresponding infinite-order differential equation (eg. (
14
)) is connected to the large-
|n|
asymptotic behaviour of the original discrete equation (eg. (
10
)).

Noting this connection, it would be worthwhile in future studies to investigate whether there is a direct connection between discrete markers of integrability, such as singularity confinement, and continuous markers of integrability, such as the Painlevé property, in systems obtained by taking continuum limits of discrete integrable equations.

Appendix B
Approximating solutions to the singulant equation for
\chi^{\prime}

We wish to approximate solutions to the partial sums of
\cosh(\chi^{\prime})-1
, or solutions to

\sum_{n=1}^{K}\frac{2(\chi^{\prime})^{2n-2}}{(2n)!}=0,

(115)

in the asymptotic limit
K\to\infty
. To locate these zeroes, we will first consider the partial sums of the exponential function,

\phi^{+}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

(116)

in the limit that
N\to\infty
. We can rewrite this expression as

\phi^{+}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{z}-\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

(117)

Locating zeroes of the partial sums of
\mathrm{e}^{z}
is a classical problem was first studied by Szegő
[
39
]
and later by others
[
12
,
32
,
28
]
; this can be directly extended to locate zeroes of related functions such as
\cos(z)

[
40
]
. Early direct analytic estimates showed that the solutions in the limit
K\to\infty
lie either on the imaginary axis or on curves in the complex plane known as Szegő curves.

The integral analysis in
[
28
]
reveals that the zeros of the partial sums of the exponential function can be located by performing steepest-descents analysis on an integral expression, and therefore that they can be understood by studying the Stokes’ structure of the solution. In this section, we present a derivation of this Stokes structure, and the associated zero locations, using differential equation methods; this approach can be conveniently generalised to other more complicated discrete singulant equations. Where appropriate, we will note the different regimes identified in
[
28
]
and how they relate to the regimes found in the present analysis.

B.1
z\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

In this section, we first utilise results from the NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions
[
18
]
and the references therein, as well as contributions from
[
31
]
, which contains a comprehensive history of the relevant asymptotic approximations. We use

\phi^{+}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{z}Q(N+1,z),

(118)

where
Q(a,z)=\Gamma(a,z)/\Gamma(a)

[
18
,
(8.4.10)
]
. We then set
z=N\zeta
and make use of known expansions. The asymptotic behaviour of
Q(a,b)
as
a\to\infty
is catalogued in
[
18
,
§8.12
]
, and predicts three relevant regions to consider: in the rescaling, these are
0<\zeta<1
,
\zeta>1
, and a transition region near
\zeta=1
.

In the first two regimes, the asymptotic behaviour of
\phi^{+}
is given by the expansion for
\Gamma(a)-\Gamma(a,z)
in
[
18
,
(8.11.6)
]
and the expansion for
\Gamma(a,z)
in
[
18
,
(8.11.7)
]
respectively. This gives

\displaystyle\phi^{+}\sim\begin{cases}{\mathrm{e}^{N\zeta}}&\mathrm{if}\quad 0<\zeta<1,\\
\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{N}\zeta^{N}}{\sqrt{2\pi N}}\dfrac{\zeta}{\zeta-1}&\mathrm{if}\quad 1<\zeta<\infty,\end{cases}

(119)

as
N\to\infty
. Note that the asymptotic behaviour of
\phi^{+}
if
\zeta<1
is singular in the limit
\zeta\to 1
, therefore predicting a transition regime near this point.

The asymptotic behaviour in the transition regime is more complicated, and was rigorously established in
[
31
]
, in which the authors studied the asymptotic behaviour of
Q(a,z)
in the transition regime. This work found that the transition regime occurs (in our notation) at
\zeta=1+N^{-1/2}\xi
with
\xi=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and the asymptotic behaviour as
N\to\infty
, catalogued in
[
18
,
(8.12.4)
]
, is

\phi^{+}\sim\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{2}}\right){\mathrm{e}^{N+N^{1/2}\xi}}.

(120)

This correctly matches to the first and second regime as
\xi\to-\infty
and
\xi\to+\infty
, respectively.

Note that in
[
28
]
, the integral expression for the partial sum
\phi^{+}
predicts a solution that jumps on either side of a critical point at
z=1
; this corresponds to the point
\zeta=1
in our notation. The location of this transition region is equivalent to the effect of the critical point in the Riemann-Hilbert integral formulation.

We next present an alternative derivation the above results in a way that we hope provides additional intuition as well as allowing for a self-contained analysis.

B.1.1
First Regime:
0<\zeta<1

In this region, the behaviour of
\phi^{+}
is dominated by a saddle point associated with
n=N\zeta+N^{1/2}\sigma
, where
\sigma=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
N\to\infty
. The saddle point can be located by writing

\phi^{+}(\zeta)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{g(n)},\qquad g(n)=n\log(N\zeta)-\log(n!).

(121)

and solving for
g^{\prime}(n)=0
. Expanding the solution for
N\to\infty
gives the required result, including the value of
\sigma
, which is not significant to the present analysis. Since
N^{1/2}\gg 1
, we may apply a continuum limit approximation to (
117
) and integrate over
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. We know that the saddle point will lie within the range of integration, so this will be the dominant contribution to the asymptotic behaviour. We use the result

g(n)\sim n\log(N)+n\log(\zeta)-n\log(n)+n\sim N\zeta-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\zeta}-\log(\sqrt{2\pi N\zeta})

(122)

to obtain

\phi^{+}(\zeta)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi N\zeta}}\mathrm{e}^{N\zeta}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma^{2}/2\zeta}.

(123)

We can then apply a continuum limit, and integrate over
\mathbb{R}
to find, as
N\to\infty
,

\phi^{+}\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{N\zeta}}{\sqrt{2\pi\zeta}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma^{2}/2\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\mathrm{e}^{N\zeta}=\mathrm{e}^{z}.

(124)

B.1.2
Second Regime

If
\zeta>1
, the saddle point lies outside of the range of integration. In this case, the dominant asymptotic contribution must arise due to the end point of the sum. It is straightforward to see that it must come from the upper end point, as the summand increases until the saddle is reached. We shift the summation by writing it in terms of
m
, where
n=N-m
and
m=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

We now have

\phi^{+}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{z^{N-m}}{(N-m)!}=\frac{z^{N}}{N!}\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{N!z^{-m}}{(N-m)!}.

(125)

The dominant contribution now comes from
m\ll N
, so we can extend the summation to infinity without changing the asymptotic behaviour. We again use
z=N\zeta
and find

\frac{z^{N}}{N!}\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{N!z^{-m}}{(N-m)!}\sim\frac{z^{N}}{N!}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{N!z^{-m}}{(N-m)!}\sim\frac{{(N\zeta)}^{N}}{N!}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\zeta^{-m}.

(126)

Evaluating the sum exactly gives

\phi^{+}(\zeta)\sim\frac{{(N\zeta)}^{N}}{N!}\frac{\zeta}{\zeta-1}\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{N}\zeta^{N}}{\sqrt{2\pi N}}\frac{\zeta}{\zeta-1}.

(127)

This has no poles in the region
\zeta>1
, but does predict one at
\zeta=1
, which indicates that the important intermediate region, corresponding to the critical point in
[
28
]
, lies near this point.

B.1.3
Distinguished Regime

From the variation we see in the first regime, we know that the transition region between the two regimes must occur at
\zeta=1+N^{-1/2}\xi
with
\xi=\mathcal{O}(1)
. This translates to
n=N-N^{1/2}\nu
with
\nu=\mathcal{O}(1)
, or
m=N^{1/2}\nu
. We will use this scaling so that we can again take advantage of the continuum limit. We have

\phi^{+}(\xi)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{g(n)},\qquad g(n)=(N-m)\log(N+N^{1/2}\xi)-\log((N-m)!).

(128)

Using a similar argument to before, we find that the saddle point occurs at
m=N^{1/2}\nu
, where
\nu=\mathcal{O}(1)
. While we can calculate the value of
\nu
, it is not needed here. Using similar reasoning, we are able to expand
g(n)
in the limit that
N\to\infty
to find that

g(n)\sim N+N^{1/2}\xi-\log(\sqrt{2\pi N})+\frac{(\xi+\nu)^{2}}{2}.

(129)

This allows us to write (
125
) as an integral, and hence find as
N\to\infty
that

\phi^{+}\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{N+N^{1/2}\xi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(\xi+\nu)^{2}/2\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{2}}\right){\mathrm{e}^{N+N^{1/2}\xi}}.

(130)

This has the correct asymptotic behaviour to match to the first and second regime as
\xi\to-\infty
and
\xi\to+\infty
, respectively as expected.

B.2
z\notin\mathbb{R}^{+}

We now wish to consider values of
z
that do not lie on the real axis. The key difference between this and the analysis above is that the saddle point no longer lies in the range of integration, and therefore analytic continuation is required to approximate the behaviour. In this section we again identify the critical point at
\zeta=1
, and determine the associated Stokes structure, analytically continuing behaviour off the real axis using an ordinary differential equation approach. The use of a differential equation approach is different to preceding analyses of this problem, such as
[
12
,
32
,
28
,
39
]
.

B.2.1
Governing Differential Equation

Let us consider

\phi^{+}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{z}-\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}

(131)

We begin by differentiating (
131
) with respect to
z
, to obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi^{+}}{\mathrm{d}z}=\mathrm{e}^{z}-\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\phi^{+}-\frac{z^{N}}{N!}.

(132)

As before, we work in terms of
\zeta
, where
z=N\zeta
. This will ensure that the distinguished region still corresponds to
\zeta
near 1. We apply the change of variables
\phi^{+}(z)=z^{N}\Phi(\zeta)/N!
to obtain

\frac{(N\zeta)^{N}}{(\zeta N)!}\Phi+\frac{z^{N}}{N!}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{\mathrm{d}z}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}\zeta}=\frac{z^{N}\Phi}{N!}-\frac{z^{N}}{N!},

(133)

which simplifies to give

\frac{1}{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}\zeta}+\frac{1}{\zeta}\Phi=\Phi-1.

(134)

Note that for
N=0
we obtain
\phi^{+}=1
. This gives the boundary condition
\Phi\to 1
as
\zeta\to\infty
.

B.2.2
Asymptotic Contributions

We write the solution as

\Phi(\zeta)\sim\Phi_{0}(\zeta)+\frac{1}{N}\Phi_{1}(\zeta)+\ldots

(135)

as
N\to\infty
. The first few orders of the solution are given by

\Phi\sim\frac{\zeta}{\zeta-1}+\frac{1}{N}\frac{\zeta}{(\zeta-1)^{3}}\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad N\to\infty.

(136)

This is the same result to leading order as the analysis from the second regime, given in (
119
). Furthermore, it is clear that the asymptotic analysis breaks down if
\zeta=1+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
, suggesting the appropriate distinguished scaling
\zeta=1+N^{-1/2}\xi
, where
\xi=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and
\Phi(\zeta)=\Psi(\xi)/N^{1/2}
. The rescaled differential equation becomes

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Psi}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=\frac{\xi}{1+N^{-1/2}\xi}\Psi-1.

(137)

The boundary condition is obtained by matching the behaviour in the limit that
\xi\to\infty
with (
136
) as
\zeta\to 1
. The solution to this equation has leading-order behaviour as
N\to\infty
, denoted
\Psi_{0}(\xi)
, that satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Psi_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=\xi\Psi_{0}-1,

(138)

giving (cf. (
130
))

\Psi_{0}(\xi)=C\mathrm{e}^{\xi^{2}/2}+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\xi^{2}/2}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

(139)

where
C
is a parameter determined by the asymptotic matching condition, which gives
C=0
. Hence,

\Psi_{0}(\xi)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\xi^{2}/2}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

(140)

or, in the original coordinates,

\Phi(\zeta)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi N}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{N(\zeta-1)^{2}/2}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{N(\zeta-1)}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(141)

As
N\to\infty
for
\zeta<1
, the asymptotic behaviour of this expression is

\Phi(\zeta)\sim\sqrt{2\pi N}\mathrm{e}^{N(\zeta-1)^{2}/2}\qquad\mathrm{as}\qquad N\to\infty.

(142)

To confirm that this expression has the appropriate behaviour for
\zeta<1
, we note that
\phi\sim\mathrm{e}^{z}
as
N\to\infty
in this region. This means that

\Phi\sim\frac{N!\mathrm{e}^{z}}{z^{N}}=\frac{N!\mathrm{e}^{N\zeta}}{(N\zeta)^{N}}\sim\sqrt{2\pi N}\mathrm{e}^{N(\zeta-1-\log(\zeta))}.

(143)

As
\zeta\to 1
, this behaviour is consistent with the intermediate region (
130
).

For most of the complex plane, there are two significant asymptotic contributions to the solution, given in (
136
) and (
143
). We will subsequently denote these expressions as
\Phi^{(1)}
and
\Phi^{(2)}
respectively. The expression (
141
) describing the behaviour in the neighbourhood of the turning point at
\zeta=1
; we will denote this as
\Phi^{(3)}
. Note that neither
\Phi^{(1)}
nor
\Phi^{(2)}
predict zeroes in the solution, and any zeroes must arise due to interactions between these contributions.
\Phi^{(3)}
does, however, have zeroes, a number of these being reported in
[
18
]
.

To determine the location of the zeroes we require, we need to study the Stokes switching behaviour that occurs between
\Phi^{(1)}
and
\Phi^{(2)}
. We will briefly note the impact on the solution behaviour from
\Phi^{(3)}
at the conclusion of the analysis.

B.2.3
Stokes Structure

From (
143
), we see that the solution contains a Stokes line that satisfies

\mathrm{Im}(\zeta-1-\log(\zeta))=0,\qquad\mathrm{Re}(\zeta-1-\log(\zeta))>0,

(144)

and an anti-Stokes line that satisfies

\mathrm{Re}(\zeta-1-\log(\zeta))=0.

(145)

These curves are presented in Figure
13
. Note that this is precisely the condition that would be obtained using a steepest-descents approach to the the integral equation from
[
28
]
to determine whether the saddle point contributes to the approximation, and confirming the methods agree.

We see that the contribution
\Phi^{(2)}
cannot be present on the right of the Stokes line, because it would dominate the behaviour on the real axis for
\zeta>1
if it were present. Hence, it appears as the Stokes line is crossed, and is asymptotically subdominant compared to the dominant contribution
\Phi^{(1)}
when it appears. Crossing the anti-Stokes line towards the origin causes the relative dominance of the two contributions to change, which means that
\Phi^{(2)}
is the dominant contribution inside the anti-Stokes line, in the region that includes the origin and the real axis for
0<\zeta<1
. This This behaviour is consistent with the results we obtained for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
in Section
B.1
.

B.2.4
Zero Locations

In the vicinity of the anti-Stokes lines,
\Phi^{(1)}
and
\Phi^{(2)}
are comparable in asymptotic size as
N\to\infty
. The asymptotic leading-order behaviour here is therefore given by
\Phi\sim\Phi^{(1)}+\Phi^{(2)}
. This means that zeroes of
\Phi
can only exist near the anti-Stokes line (
145
), occurring due to cancellation between
\Phi^{(1)}
and
\Phi^{(2)}
.

If we let
\zeta=\zeta_{r}+\mathrm{i}\zeta_{i}
, we can express the anti-Stokes line as

\zeta_{r}-1-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(\zeta_{r}^{2}+\zeta_{i}^{2})=0.

(146)

Solving this and setting
\zeta_{r}=x/N
and
\zeta_{i}=y/N
gives

y=\pm\sqrt{N^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2x/N-2}-x^{2}},

(147)

where
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
. We will use this result to approximate the curves on which zeroes are found for the more complicated expression (
115
).

To approximate the actual solutions to
\phi^{+}(z)=0
for large
|z|
, we set
\zeta=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
and look for solutions to
\Phi^{(1)}(\zeta)=-\Phi^{(2)}(\zeta)
, or

\frac{r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}{r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}-1}=-\sqrt{2\pi N}\mathrm{e}^{N(r\exp(\mathrm{i}\theta)-1-\log(r)-\mathrm{i}\theta)}.

(148)

Some algebra gives

r\exp(\mathrm{i}\theta)-1-\log(r)-\mathrm{i}\theta=\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi N}}\frac{\zeta}{\zeta-1}\right)+\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}M}{N},

(149)

where
M\in\mathbb{Z}
. Further algebraic manipulation gives

r\exp(\mathrm{i}\theta)-1-\log(r)-\mathrm{i}\theta=-\frac{\log(2\pi N)}{2N}+\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}M}{2N}+\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\frac{\zeta}{\zeta-1}\right).

(150)

Let let
\log(\zeta/(\zeta-1))=\log(\kappa)+\mathrm{i}\sigma
, where
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\in(-\pi,\pi]
. Matching real and imaginary parts of this equation gives a system of equations for
\kappa
and
\sigma
,

\displaystyle r\cos(\theta)-\log(r)-1=-\frac{\log(2\pi N)}{2N}+\frac{\log(\kappa)}{N},\qquad r\sin(\theta)-\theta=\frac{2\pi M}{2N}+\frac{\sigma}{N},

(151)

and therefore that

\displaystyle\kappa\cos(\sigma)+\frac{r^{2}-r\cos(\theta)}{r^{2}-2r\cos(\theta)+1}=0,\qquad\kappa\sin(\sigma)-\frac{r\sin(\theta)}{r^{2}-2r\cos(\theta)+1}.

(152)

We are interested in the case where
M=\mathcal{O}(N)
as
N\to\infty
, which means that we can balance (
151
) at leading-order to obtain the system

\displaystyle r\cos(\theta)-\log(r)-1=0,\qquad r\sin(\theta)-\theta=\frac{2\pi M}{N},

(153)

which we can solve to determine the leading-order value of
\zeta
along which the expressions are balanced, and hence approximate locations of solutions to
\phi^{+}(z)=0
.

It is useful to set
\theta=\pi
, for which (
153
) gives
M=-N/2
and
r=\tau
where
\tau
solves
1+\tau-\log\tau)
; this is the point where the anti-Stokes line in (
13
) intersects with the real axis, so all zeroes must be to the right of this point. As we expect the zeroes to appear in complex conjugate pairs, this is consistent with the polynomial (
116
) having
N
zeroes.

This choice of
\theta
and
r
may be substituted into (
152
), which gives
\sigma=\pi
and
\kappa=\tau/(\tau+1)
. Substituting these results into (
151
) gives
M=-(N+1)/2
. This means that a real zero is present in the solution only if
N
is odd, whereas if
N
is even then there are no zeroes on the real axis, and the zeroes appear solely in complex conjugate pairs.

By setting
\theta=\pi/2
, we follow a similar approach and show that
r=\mathrm{e}^{-1}
and
M=-N/4+Nr/(2\pi)
. This indicates that we expect to find
N/4-N/(2\pi\mathrm{e})
zeroes in the first quadrant of the
\zeta
-plane, and hence
\lfloor N/2-N/(\pi\mathrm{e})\rfloor
zeroes in the right half plane.

The zeroes of (
148
) for
N=40
are presented in Figure
14
. This figure contains 40 zeroes, which appear in complex conjugate pairs follow the anti-Stokes line more closely on the left-hand side, and diverge from the curve as they approach the transition region near
z=1
. There are no real-valued zeroes, as
N
is even. The previous analysis predicts that there are approximately
\lfloor 7.658\rfloor
, or 7 zeroes in the first quadrant, which is equal to the value shown in the figure.

Note that the anti-Stokes lines in Figure
15
reach the points
z=\pm N
, where we expect that the solution to instead be described by
\Phi^{(3)}
. This explains why the numerical solutions in Figure
14
diverge from the anti-Stokes line as these points are approached. The behaviour of the zeroes as the intermediate regime is approached can be calculated from
\Phi^{(3)}
in (
130
), which governs the solution near the turning point. Zeroes of
\Phi^{(3)}
must be calculated numerically; however, numerical zeroes of the complementary error function nearest to the origin are documented, and can be found in
[
18
]
.

B.3
Zeroes of singulant equation

In addition to (
131
), we define

\phi^{-}(z)=\phi^{+}(-z)=\mathrm{e}^{-z}-\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{n}}{n!}.

(154)

This function has the same Stokes structure shown in (
13
), reflected across the vertical axis, using the mapping
z\mapsto-z
. Hence, the zeroes of
\phi^{-}
must lie on the curve

y=\pm\sqrt{N^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2x/N-2}-x^{2}}.

(155)

We now write

\psi(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi^{+}+\phi^{-}\right)=\cosh(z)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{z^{2n}}{(2n)!}.

(156)

If
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
and
|z|\gg 0
, then
\psi\sim\tfrac{1}{2}\phi^{+}
as
N\to\infty
. The anti-Stokes lines in (
147
) take are located at
|z|=\mathcal{O}(N)
as
N\to\infty
, and are therefore present in the Stokes structure of
\psi(z)
for
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
. This implies that solutions of
\phi^{+}(z)=0
for
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
must also be approximate solutions to
\psi(z)=0
.

A similar argument can be made for
\mathrm{Re}(z)<0
and
|z|\gg 0
, where
\psi\sim\tfrac{1}{2}\phi^{-}
as
N\to\infty
. In this region, the anti-Stokes lines of
\phi^{-}(z)
, and hence solutions of
\phi^{-}(z)=0
must also be approximate solutions to
\psi(z)=0
. We therefore predict that solutions for
|z|\gg 0
are found along the curves (
147
) if
\mathrm{Re}(z)>0
and (
155
) if
\mathrm{Re}(z)<0
. Combining these relations shows that zeroes of
\psi(z)
for large
|z|
approximately lie on

y=\pm\sqrt{N^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2|x|/N-2}-x^{2}}.

(157)

This behaviour is shown in Figure
15
. Note that neither approximation is valid if
|\mathrm{Im}(z)|=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
N\to\infty
.

Anti-Stokes lines of
\psi(z)
in the limit that

, where

. Solutions to (

) for large

are expected to be located approximately on the anti-Stokes lines, shown in red. The Stokes lines always have a distance from the origin that is proportional to

.

Replacing
N
with
2K
gives, after some algebra,

\displaystyle\psi(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{z^{2n}}{(2n)!}=\frac{z^{2}}{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{K}\frac{2z^{2n-2}}{(2n)!}+\frac{2}{z^{2}}\right).

(158)

For
z
which is large in the limit that
K\to\infty
, the final term is
\mathcal{O}(K^{-2})
, and hence can be neglected. This means that solutions of
\psi(z)
for large
|z|
approximately satisfy

\sum_{n=1}^{K}\frac{2z^{2n-2}}{(2n)!}=0,

(159)

which is precisely the expression from (
115
). Hence, applying
N=2K
to (
157
) shows that solutions to (
115
) for large
|z|
must asymptotically approach the curve

y=\pm\sqrt{4K^{2}\mathrm{e}^{|x|/K-2}-x^{2}}

(160)

in the limit
K\to\infty
.

Finally, recall that the number of zeros
\phi^{+}(z)
possesses
\sim N/2-N/(\pi\mathrm{e})
in the right-half plane. Therefore
\psi(z)
contains
\sim N-2N/(\pi\mathrm{e})
zeroes away from the imaginary axis. Given that we expect the solution to contain
N
zeroes in total, we expect that it will contain
\sim 2N/(\pi\mathrm{e})
zeroes below the anti-Stokes line near the imaginary axis. This is consistent with the expectation that the anti-Stokes lines intersect the imaginary axis at
\mathrm{Im}(z)=N/\mathrm{e}
and that there are two zeroes in the vicinity of each multiple of
2\pi\mathrm{i}
, whereby
\cosh(z)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
, within
|\mathrm{Im}(z)|<N/\mathrm{e}
.

These results could be obtained using the analytic estimates from
[
40
]
or the steepest-descents analysis from
[
28
]
. Nonetheless, we believe that it is instructive to see directly how the zeroes relate to the Stokes structure found from the differential equation solved by the partial sums. This method is straightforward to generalise to other partial sums that typically appear in singulant equations to discrete problems.

Appendix C
Transasymptotic Analysis for
K=3

C.1
Finite-Difference Family

We begin with the differential equation from (
23
). The Stokes structure for this equation is presented in Figure
4
. The purpose of this analysis is to construct a transseries approximation for the behaviour in the blue-shaded region of Figure
4
(c). We will then use transasymptotic resummation to continue this approximation into the red-shaded region of the plane, and finally use Padé approximation to provide numerical evidence that the red-shaded region contains moveable branch points.

C.1.1
Transseries

In the blue-shaded region of Figure
4
(c), the solution contains subdominant exponential contributions as
\epsilon\to 0
. We will study the exponentials that grow as we cross the anti-Stokes line in the positive angular direction from the real axis, crossing into the red-shaded region. We write the solution in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
in the form of a transseries as

y\sim\Phi_{0}(z;\epsilon)+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{\beta_{q}}\mathrm{e}^{-q\chi(z)/\epsilon}\Phi_{q}(z;\epsilon)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0,

(161)

where

\Phi_{q}(z;\epsilon)\sim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2m}\Phi_{q}^{(m)}(z),

(162)

and
\Phi_{q}^{(m)}\neq 0
. This transseries captures not just the largest exponentially small term, but also the smaller exponentials associated with this value of
\chi
; in nonlinear problems, the transseries should contain exponential terms with exponents that are integer multiples of the original exponential term
[
3
]
. The term
\Phi_{0}(z;\epsilon)
corresponds to the leading-order power series solution, and the exponentially small remainder term (
100
) gives the leading-order behaviour of
\Phi_{1}(z;\epsilon)
.

Recall that
\chi^{\prime}(z)
is constant; for convenience, we let
\chi^{\prime}=\alpha
. The first anti-Stokes line we encounter if we move in the positive angular direction is the anti-Stokes line associated with
\alpha=\sqrt{-15+3\mathrm{i}\sqrt{15}}
, or
\chi_{3}
from (
45
).

We substitute the transseries (
161
) into the governing equation (
23
) to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{360}\left[\epsilon^{4}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{6}\Phi_{0}}{\mathrm{d}z^{6}}+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{\beta_{q}-2}(q\chi^{\prime})^{6}\mathrm{e}^{-q\chi/\epsilon}\Phi_{q}+\ldots\right]+\frac{1}{12}\left[\epsilon^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}\Phi_{0}}{\mathrm{d}z^{4}}+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{\beta_{q}-2}(q\chi^{\prime})^{4}\mathrm{e}^{-q\chi/\epsilon}\Phi_{q}+\ldots\right]

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\Phi_{0}}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{\beta_{q}-2}(q\chi^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-q\chi/\epsilon}\Phi_{q}+\ldots\right]+\left[\Phi_{0}+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{\beta_{q}}\mathrm{e}^{-q\chi/\epsilon}\Phi_{q}\right]\left[\Phi_{0}+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{\beta_{q}}\mathrm{e}^{-q\chi/\epsilon}\Phi_{q}\right]

\displaystyle=-2z,

(163)

where the terms denoted by the ellipsis in each set of braces are smaller than those retained in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
. By balancing the dominant behaviour of the linear terms and the nonlinear term at each exponential order, we obtain a relation for
\beta_{q}
, where

\beta_{q}-2=\beta_{1}+{\beta_{q-1}}.

(164)

We know from the form of the remainder term in (
100
) that
\beta_{1}=-2
. This recurrence relation is therefore solved by
\beta_{q}=-2
for
q\geq 1
, giving the algebraic scaling for each transseries term.

We next define a transseries parameter
\xi
, where

\xi=\sigma\mathrm{e}^{-\chi/\epsilon},

(165)

where
\sigma
is a scaling constant to be determined. Using (
165
), we can write (
161
) as

y\sim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\xi^{q}\Phi_{q}(z;\epsilon)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

(166)

This has the effect of scaling each term by a factor of
\sigma^{q}
, but we can absorb this scaling into the
\Phi_{q}
term. In the unshaded regions in Figure
4
, there are no exponential terms; we therefore set
\sigma=0
.

If we continue in the positive angular direction across the Stokes line into the blue-shaded region of Figure
4
, the remainder term (
100
) appears, which is the leading-order asymptotic behaviour of the series with
q=1
. Matching this leading-order behaviour with the remainder term at leading-order gives

\frac{\xi}{\epsilon^{2}}{\Phi_{1}^{(0)}(z)}\sim\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\Lambda}{\mu\epsilon^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\chi/\epsilon}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0,

(167)

which, using (
165
), gives

\sigma\Phi_{1}^{(0)}(z)=\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\Lambda}{\mu}.

(168)

We are free to pick
\sigma
, and this expression suggests choosing
\sigma=2\pi\mathrm{i}\Lambda/\mu
, which gives
\Phi_{1}^{(0)}(z)=1
.

The transseries (
166
) is valid in regions where the exponential term
\xi
is asymptotically small as
\xi\to 0
(or zero), corresponding to the unshaded and blue regions shown in Figure
4
(b). In order to determine the behaviour in the red region, we write the double expansion for
y
in the blue region of Figure
4
(b),

y\sim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\xi^{q}\epsilon^{2m}\Phi_{q}^{(m)}(z)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0,\,\xi\to 0,

(169)

where we note that
\Phi_{0}^{(0)}=0
so that the
q=0
series is
\mathcal{O}(1)
in the limit that
\epsilon\to 0
. This is a valid asymptotic expansion in the region where
\xi
is small in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
.

C.1.2
Resummation

Now that we have the transseries (
161
) in the region where
\xi
is small in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, we may reverse the order of summation to obtain

y\sim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2m}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\xi^{q}\Phi_{q}^{(m)}(z)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0,\,\xi\to 0.

(170)

We may now evaluate the inner summation term, to obtain

y\sim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2m}\Phi^{(m)}(z;\xi),\qquad\Phi^{(m)}=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\xi^{q}{\Phi}_{q}^{(m)}(z).

(171)

Evaluating the inner sum permits us to continue the transseries expression analytically into regions where
\xi
is not small in the limit that
\epsilon\to 0
. Note that this is an established procedure which can alternatively be replicated by using traditional matched asymptotic analysis to continue to the solution between regions as different contributions contribute to the dominant asymptotic behaviour. The outer sum remains asymptotically valid outside of regions where
\xi
is small, and therefore allows us to determine the asymptotic solution behaviour as
\epsilon\to 0
even after the anti-Stokes line is crossed. In this region of the plane, the solution behaviour is

y\sim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2m}\Phi^{(m)}(z;\xi)\quad\mathrm{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0,

(172)

with no asymptotic dependence on
\xi
. Now we can substitute (
172
) into the governing equation and obtain an equation for
\Phi^{(0)}
(as well as
\Phi^{(m)}
). This is the only term that generates new singularities, as equations for
\Phi^{(m)}
are linear.

Note that
z
and
\xi
are not independent, and we can use (
165
) to write all derivatives in terms of
\xi
. Applying the chain rule gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}z}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{\mathrm{d}z}\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=-\frac{\alpha\xi}{\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\xi}.

(173)

We can substitute (
172
) into (
23
) and use this expression to write the resultant equation in terms of
\xi
. Balancing this expression at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-4})
gives the equation for
{\Phi}^{(0)}

Appendix E
The inner difference equation for
\Delta=0
and
\Delta=1

Here we consider the homogeneous difference equation

\left(1+\left(1+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\right)w_{n}\right)(w_{n+1}-2w_{n}+w_{n-1})+3w_{n}^{2}=0,

(182)

where we identify
w_{n}
with
v(\eta)
in the difference equation (
84
). We focus on exceptional properties of the special cases
\Delta=0
and
\Delta=1
, with potential implications for their integrability properties.

To do this, we will identify polynomial conserved quantities that are symemtric properties of
w_{n}
and
w_{n+1}
and contain no higher powers of
w_{n+1}
than squares. The reasons for these constraints readily become apparent over the course of the calculation. This approach proves effective in precisely two cases,
\Delta=0
and
\Delta=1
, as follows.

E.1
Case 1:
\Delta=0

The case
\Delta=0
is the discrete Painlevé I equation, and therefore contains known conserved quantities. One of these can be constructed by defining

F_{0}(w_{n},w_{n+1})=w_{n}w_{n+1}(w_{n}+w_{n+1})+(w_{n}-w_{n+1})^{2}.

(183)

It can be shown that

F_{0}(w_{n},w_{n+1})-F_{0}(w_{n-1},w_{n})=(w_{n+1}-w_{n})\left[\left(1+w_{n}\right)(w_{n+1}-2w_{n}+w_{n-1})+3w_{n}^{2}\right].

(184)

By comparing the right-hand side with (
182
), we see that it is equal to zero. Consequently

F_{0}(w_{n},w_{n+1})=E

(185)

for constant
E
.

E.2
Case 1:
\Delta=1

We can perform a similar analysis for the case
\Delta=1
. In this case we can derive

F_{1}(w_{n},w_{n+1})=-\tfrac{3}{4}w_{n}^{2}w_{n+1}^{2}+w_{n}w_{n+1}(w_{n}+w_{n+1})+(w_{n}-w_{n+1})^{2}.

(186)

In the same fashion as previously, we calculate

\displaystyle F_{1}(w_{n},w_{n+1})-F_{1}(w_{n-1},w_{n})

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}(w_{n+1}-w_{n})(w_{n}-2)\left[\left(1+\tfrac{3}{2}w_{n}\right)(w_{n+1}+w_{n-1})-2w_{n}\right],

(187)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}(w_{n+1}-w_{n})(w_{n}-2)\left[\left(1+\tfrac{3}{2}w_{n}\right)(w_{n+1}-2w_{n}+w_{n-1})+3w_{n}^{2}\right].

(188)

Again, comparing this with (
182

F_{1}(w_{n},w_{n+1})=E

(189)

for constant
E
.
