Best evidence summary for post-discharge follow-up of patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy
Authors: Xiaoyan Zeng, Yibing Jiang, Xiaoxuan Ji, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo summarize the best available evidence on post-discharge follow-up of patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy.MethodsFollowing the 6S evidence hierarchy, a systematic search was performed from July 1, 2015, to January 1, 2026, covering major academic databases, professional society websites, and clinical decision support tools. Two reviewers independently evaluated the quality of the included studies and extracted the relevant evidence, respectively.ResultsA total of 15 articles were included, comprising 4 guidelines, 2 systematic reviews, 2 meta-analyses, 1 clinical decision tool, 1 expert consensus, 2 expert opinions, and 3 evidence summaries. The overall methodological quality of the included literature was high. A total of 12 best-evidence recommendations were identified and synthesized across five key domains: follow-up providers, follow-up methods, follow-up frequency, follow-up content, and complication prevention and management. Among these, the majority of recommendations were supported by Level 1 or Level 2 evidence, with 8 recommendations rated as Grade A and 4 as Grade B based on the JBI evidence pre-grading system.ConclusionsThis review identified 12 evidence-based recommendations for post-discharge follow-up of patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy. The findings suggest that health care providers should develop appropriate follow-up approaches based on evidence translation while considering individual patient characteristics, with the ultimate goal of enhancing the quality of patient care.Systematic review registrationThis study was registered in the Fudan University Centre for Evidence-based Nursing (Registration No. ES20231600).Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Enterostomy, Follow-up, Evidence summary, Evidence-based nursing

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide. According to global statistics, there were approximately 1.93 million new cases of CRC in 2022, ranking third in global cancer incidence rates.1 In China, both the incidence and mortality rates of CRC continue to rise,2 accounting for 28.2% of the global burden.3 Enterostomy is a common surgical treatment for CRC, involves creating an opening in the abdominal wall to exteriorize a segment of the intestine, thereby replacing the natural excretory function of the anus.4

Despite the importance of ostomy in prolonging the survival of patients, it has a serious negative impact on their psychological, physiological, and social functioning due to the alteration of their normal physiological structure and appearance.5 Relevant studies have shown that over 90% of stoma CRC survivors will experience severe psychological distress in the first three months after stoma surgery.6 In particular, patients who require long-term or permanent stoma use are particularly prone to anxiety and emotional distress due to perceived differences from others.7 Common postoperative complications include peristomal skin irritation, surgical site infection, and dehydration, which are mainly caused by improper stoma care, such as problems with the stoma appliance not fitting or sealing properly. In severe cases, it may even lead to rehospitalization.8 Odor management is also a major concern, causing embarrassment and lowering patients’ self-esteem and overall well-being.9

With the rapid development and widespread implementation of the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocol, hospital stays have been significantly shortened.10 Consequently, most patients fail to adequately master stoma self-care skills during hospitalization, leading to a range of clinical challenges and complications. In the early stages of discharge, patients are often stressed by surgical trauma, insufficient information, and inadequate stoma care skills. Over time, problems such as changes in external appearance, uncontrollable defecation, restrictions on somatic activities, and increased financial burden lead to difficulties in assuming their roles and participating in social activities, thus affecting their quality of life (QOL).11 Therefore, providing necessary follow-up to patients is crucial for enhancing their self-care capacity and QOL.

Follow-up refers to the provision of professional medical advice by health care providers. It involves monitoring the health status of discharged patients through various means. The goal is to enhance patients’ self-management and support their recovery.12 Although some guidelines or recommendations have addressed prehospital and posthospital care for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy, relevant evidence is often embedded within broader perioperative care frameworks. Consequently, systematic discussions and specialized evidence specifically targeting follow-up care for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy remain relatively limited. Additionally, there are a growing number of international studies on discharge follow-up management of patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy. Most Chinese studies in this field rely on cross-sectional surveys and analysis of influencing factors, and the interventions are often based on expert opinion rather than robust evidence, resulting in a lack of standardized, evidence-based protocols.13

The modalities include telephone follow-up, outpatient visits, home visits, and web-based remote consultation that have emerged in recent years. Follow-up not only improves the quality of medical services, but also facilitates health care professionals to continuously monitor the patient's condition and provide relevant professional guidance. Additionally, the clinical data and experience accumulated during the follow-up process are of great value in guiding clinical practice and conducting relevant research, which can enhance patient satisfaction and promote the optimization of health care services at the same time. For patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy, follow-up is particularly important in the postoperative recovery process. Studies have demonstrated that follow-up services significantly improve patients' QOL, self-care ability, and self-efficacy,14,15 while reducing the incidence of complications and unplanned readmissions.16,17 Although the importance of follow-up has been widely recognized, its implementation standards and effectiveness still vary greatly between different countries and regions. Few studies have explored the follow-up protocols for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy.

To address the existing research gaps, this study aims to synthesize evidence for clinical nursing staff to develop scientific, standardized, and feasible discharge follow-up pathways, thereby improving recovery and QOL for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy.

Methods

Clinical question formulation

The evidence-based question was constructed using the PIPOST model developed by the Fudan University Center for Evidence-Based Nursing, covering aspects such as problem formulation, evidence retrieval, inclusion and exclusion criteria of evidence, literature screening, literature quality evaluation, evidence extraction and summary.18 It is based on the methodological process for the summary of the evidence produced by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI).19 This study was enrolled in the Fudan University Centre for Evidence-Based Nursing (Registration No. ES20231600).

The components include: P (the targeted population) referred to patients undergoing colorectal cancer ostomy surgery; I (intervention): Structured post-discharge follow-up interventions (including assessment, guidance, monitoring, complication prevention, and support) for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy; P (the professionals who apply evidence) included clinical health care providers; Outcomes covered the quality of discharge education, self-care ability, QOL, complication rates, unplanned readmission rates, and satisfaction levels among colorectal cancer ostomy patients; S (the evidence-application setting) included the colorectal surgery wards, outpatient clinics, and community settings; T (type of evidence) consisted of guidelines, expert consensus or recommendations, systematic reviews, evidence summaries, and clinical decision support.

Sources of evidence and search strategy

Literature was searched according to the “6S” evidence resource pyramid model, and the search was conducted in order from top to bottom based on the level of evidence. The searched clinical decision support systems included BMJ Best Practice Website, UpToDate, Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, Registered Nurses Association of Ontario (RNAO), Wound, Ostomy and Continence Nurses Society (WOCN), Guidelines International Network (GIN), World Council of Enterostomal Therapists (WCET), The guideline websites and databases included National Guideline Clearinghouse (NGC), Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Database. A combined search strategy utilizing both subject headings and free-text terms was employed, with PubMed serving as the representative database. The search timeframe spanned from July 1, 2015, to January 1, 2026. The specific search strategies for PubMed are detailed in Table 1, and strategies for other databases were adapted accordingly.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of evidence

The inclusion criteria were (1) study subjects were patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy; (2) study content pertained to discharge follow-up management of patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy; (3) study types included guidelines, expert consensuses or recommendations, expert opinions, meta-analyses, evidence summaries, systematic reviews, and clinical decision support; (4) publications in either Chinese or English.

Exclusion criteria were (1) incomplete information or unavailable full texts; (2) translated versions or updated publications; (3) study protocols, reports, or abstract-only publications; (4) publications rated as low quality after methodological appraisal.

Literature quality evaluation

Two qualified researchers independently evaluated the literature according to the respective quality assessment criteria and cross-checked their findings. In cases of disagreement, they engaged in discussion to reach consensus. If consensus could not be achieved, a third reviewer (a nursing faculty member with doctoral qualifications) was consulted for final determination. When conflicting evidence emerged from different sources, this study adhered to the principle of prioritizing higher-level, higher-quality, and more recently published authoritative literature.20

The guidelines were evaluated using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation Instrument II (AGREE II).21 Meta-analyses and systematic reviews were assessed according to the Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) criteria from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence-Based Healthcare Center.22 Expert opinions, consensus statements, or recommendations were appraised using the JBI Evidence-Based Healthcare Center's quality assessment tool for expert consensus (2016 version).18 For evidence summaries and clinical decision support resources, we traced back to their original source literature and applied appropriate evaluation criteria based on the document type.23 Evidence from UpToDate or the JBI Evidence - Based Database was directly included and classified as high - quality. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values ≥ 0.75 indicating good agreement and ≥ 0.90 indicating excellent agreement.24

Evidence extraction and recommendation level

Two researchers independently extracted and synthesized post-discharge follow-up related content for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy from the included literature, and a third researcher subsequently verified and organized the findings. Evidence quality was assessed concurrently. The extracted evidence was then organized into thematic categories based on content. The synthesized evidence was uniformly classified using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence-Based Healthcare Center's Evidence Pre-grading and Recommendation Level System (2014 version),25 which categorizes evidence from Level 1 (highest) to Level 5 (lowest). The evidence synthesis followed these principles26: (1) for consistent evidence, priority was given to logically clear and concisely presented evidence; (2) for complementary evidence, corresponding inductive synthesis was performed; (3) for conflicting evidence, higher-quality evidence and the most recently published authoritative literature were prioritized. The recommendation level of the evidence was classified as Grade A or Grade B using the feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and effectiveness (FAME) framework.27

Quality evaluation of literature

Quality evaluation results of guidelines

This study ultimately included 4 clinical guidelines,28, 29, 30, 31 which were assessed using the AGREE II instrument. As shown in Table 3, WOCN scored over 60% in all six domains and was rated Grade A. Similarly, RNAO achieved a score above 60% in five domains and was also rated Grade A. The ETN guideline scored 50.0% in the Editorial independence domain but exceeded 60% in the remaining five domains, resulting in a Grade B rating. The guideline by Prinz et al. scored 57.3% in the Rigor of development domain but exceeded 60% in the remaining five domains, and was also rated as Grade B. All intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) values for guideline ratings were ≥ 0.75, indicating good inter-rater reliability.

Quality evaluation results of systematic reviews and meta-analyses

Two systematic reviews17,32 and two meta-analyses33,34 were included, with all quality assessment items rated as "Yes," indicating high methodological quality and warranting inclusion.

Quality evaluation results of expert consensus and expert opinion

One expert consensus36 and two expert opinion papers37,38 were included; while item 6 ("Are there any inconsistencies between the presented viewpoints and previous literature?") was rated as "Unclear," all other items received "Yes" ratings, justifying their inclusion based on overall quality.

Quality evaluation results of clinical decisions

One clinical decision support document35 from UpToDate was automatically classified as high-quality evidence and included.

Quality evaluation results of evidence summaries

The three evidence summaries39, 40, 41 yielded 7 distinct evidence statements, which were traced to their original sources: one guideline,31 one JBI publication,42 and previously included references (ETN,31 Alper et al.,35 Trindade et al.,32 WOCN,30 and RNAO29). These sources all met high quality standards upon evaluation and were approved for inclusion.

Description and summary of evidence

Evidence on discharge follow-up for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy was extracted from the 15 included studies. Through systematic synthesis, 12 evidence-based recommendations were ultimately developed across five key domains: follow-up team, follow-up methods, follow-up time and frequency, follow-up content, and complication prevention and management. Each of the 12 final recommendations was directly derived from the synthesized evidence, with a clear mapping between the evidence sources and the corresponding recommendations. To ensure an explicit and reproducible evidence-to-recommendation pathway, Table 4 presents the evidence level, supporting literature, and key basis for each recommendation. Discussion

Main findings

This evidence summary synthesized 12 best evidence-based recommendations for post-discharge follow-up care among patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy, covering five core domains: follow-up providers, methods, timing and frequency, content, and complication prevention and management. Most recommendations were supported by Level 1 evidence and graded as Grade A, indicating high clinical validity and applicability. These findings address the current lack of standardized, evidence-informed follow-up protocols for this population, especially in Chinese clinical settings where practice often relies on expert opinion rather than robust evidence.

Multidisciplinary team - based follow-up: the cornerstone of continuity care

Evidence 1 shows that forming a multidisciplinary team is the key to ensuring high-quality postoperative rehabilitation, which is consistent with international guidelines, including those from RNAO and WOCN. This team-based approach addresses the limits of a single professional view and meets the diverse needs of stoma patients after rectal cancer surgery. Previous studies have shown that access to specialist stoma care can improve health outcomes, lower complication rates, and boost patients’ self-care confidence. Such an integrated model addresses the complex physical, psychological, and social needs of ostomy patients. Achieving this requires strong interprofessional collaboration, supported by standardised workflows and care pathways.29 However, the current multidisciplinary care system in China is still limited and not fully developed.43 To bridge this gap, hospitals and communities should prioritize training certified ostomy therapists, standardize interprofessional workflows, and establish clear referral and communication mechanisms to ensure sustainable, coordinated follow-up care.

Integrating multiple follow-up approaches: enhancing service accessibility and patient experience

Our evidence synthesis confirms that integrated follow-up approaches, including telephone, outpatient, home visit, and digital remote monitoring, yield superior health outcomes compared with reliance on a single modality. Telephone follow-up remains the most widely utilized method due to its low cost, broad coverage, and ease of implementation; however, it lacks visual assessment and direct physical evaluation, which may restrict accurate appraisal of stoma condition and peristomal skin complications.44 Home visits enable face-to-face assessment, hands-on instruction, and enhanced emotional support, thereby improving patient satisfaction.45 In many countries, home visits serve as an effective strategy for post-discharge transitional care, typically delivered by community nurses with specialized ostomy training and certification, who can provide tailored home guidance and complication management.12 Nevertheless, in China, the shortage of qualified nursing personnel and the limited number of community nurses meeting ostomy therapist certification requirements impede the broad implementation of home visits.46

With the widespread adoption of mobile internet technology, mobile health (mHealth) applications allow patients to conveniently access ostomy care knowledge and support postoperative self-management. Yet elderly patients, those with low educational attainment, or individuals with limited digital literacy often struggle with device operation and app utilization, potentially leading to fragmented follow-up or ineffective communication. Existing evidence demonstrates that combined follow-up modalities are more effective than single approaches in enhancing health outcomes among patients with stomas.17 Accordingly, future follow-up models should be patient-centered, taking full account of age, cognitive ability, cultural background, and regional disparities. Such an approach will facilitate the development of a diversified, precise, and efficient follow-up system, ultimately maximizing the continuity and accessibility of postoperative rehabilitation care.

Optimized timing and frequency: ensuring phased and continuous care

This review identified evidence-based critical time points for follow-up: initial follow-up within 2 weeks after discharge to evaluate stoma morphology, peristomal skin integrity, and ostomy appliance fit; reassessment within 2 weeks after changing ostomy products to ensure compatibility and prevent leakage; and continuous follow-up for at least 12 months postoperatively to support long-term adaptation and early identification of complications.36 Specifically, the first telephone follow-up is recommended at 7–10 days after discharge, with a second telephone contact in the second week to monitor patients’ stoma adaptation and home self-care performance. Home visits are suggested at 2, 4, and 6 weeks after discharge, with a minimum of six home visits throughout the early recovery phase.31,39 Outpatient follow-up should be scheduled flexibly based on individual recovery progress, providing comprehensive assessment and timely adjustment of care plans.39

These time-specific recommendations correspond well with the early postoperative recovery trajectory, when patients are most susceptible to self-care deficits, appliance-related problems, and acute complications. In Chinese clinical settings, the widespread implementation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols has significantly shortened hospital stays, leaving patients inadequate time to master stoma self-care skills during hospitalization. This gap further underscores the importance of early and structured post-discharge follow-up.

Notably, follow-up intensity should be risk-stratified: patients with obesity, emergency surgery, or limited exposure to WOC nurse guidance require more frequent monitoring to reduce the risk of leakage and peristomal skin damage. Follow-up approaches should be selected and combined dynamically according to the recovery stage and individual needs. However, in rural and remote areas of China, insufficient community health resources limit the broad implementation of intensive home visit protocols.47 Therefore, future efforts should focus on strengthening the stoma care competency of community nurses and expanding health care resources in rural regions to improve the quality and accessibility of home visit services.

Comprehensive follow-up content: holistic assessment and multidimensional support

Current evidence underscores that effective follow-up must integrate physical, functional, and psychosocial dimensions to achieve holistic care.35,38,41 Core assessment domains include stoma appearance, peristomal skin integrity (erythema, erosion, exudation), ostomy pouch seal and fit, self-care proficiency (pouch changing, emptying, abnormality recognition), dietary compliance, and psychosocial adjustment (anxiety, depression, social withdrawal). Systematic psychological evaluation should be embedded in routine follow-up, with timely referral to mental health and social support resources as needed.

Beyond clinical monitoring, health care providers should offer comprehensive support services such as 24-h consultation hotlines, community nursing teams, online education platforms, and peer-support programs. These services help improve patients’ health literacy and confidence in self-care.28,30,31,33,40,41 Traditional follow-up often overemphasizes physical complications while neglecting psychological distress and social isolation, both of which profoundly impair long-term QOL.

Comprehensive, person-centered follow-up reduces unplanned readmissions, enhances self-efficacy, and fosters sustainable self-management. With advances in artificial intelligence, intelligent monitoring tools may further enable dynamic assessment and optimize recovery quality and long-term satisfaction for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy.

Complication prevention and management: enhancing nursing response and patient self-management

Currently, there is increasing international attention to stoma-related complications.48 Stoma-related complications, including leakage, peristomal skin injury, parastomal hernia, and dehydration, are highly prevalent and frequently associated with insufficient self-care knowledge and skills. One study has shown that the better patients are at identifying early signs of complications, the lower their risk of developing such complications and associated discomfort in the future.49 Nevertheless, many patients lack adequate stoma care knowledge at discharge and cannot recognize early complications in clinical practice.50 This review summarizes evidence-based warning signs and preventive strategies: patients should be instructed to promptly report absent ileostomy output for more than 8 hours, darkening of the stoma, or persistent severe pain; maintain a BMI of 20–25 kg/m2 and avoid heavy lifting to lower hernia risk; apply skin barriers and ostomy powder for moisture-associated skin damage; and monitor fluid balance to prevent dehydration, particularly within the first 2 postoperative weeks. Therefore, during follow-up, health care providers should strengthen health education on complication prevention and management.

However, evidence also identifies significant knowledge gaps among clinical nurses regarding complication identification and management, with high error rates in competency assessments,51 which directly weakens the quality of patient education. To address this, it is essential to improve stoma nurses’ knowledge and competence in recognizing and managing stoma-related complications. More training should be provided to enhance the overall stoma care capacity of the nursing team.48 At the same time, it is also important to educate patients and their families about stoma complications (Evidence 12).33 Meanwhile, structured, individualized health education should be delivered to patients and caregivers using pamphlets, digital tools, and personalized instruction (Evidence 12). Equipping patients to recognize early warning signs and respond appropriately can enhance self-management, reduce complication rates, and improve long-term QOL.

Implications for nursing practice and research

This study systematically summarized the best available evidence on post-discharge follow-up of patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy. The 12 evidence-based recommendations synthesized in this study are highly applicable to clinical settings, including colorectal surgery wards, stoma clinics, and community nursing institutions. The multidisciplinary team-led, mixed-mode follow-up model is suitable for most Chinese tertiary hospitals and developed urban areas with adequate nursing staffing and specialized stoma nurses. For rural or remote regions with limited community nursing resources, simplified protocols can be adopted, such as intensified telephone follow-up combined with regular outpatient visits, instead of intensive home visits. For elderly patients or those with poor digital literacy, traditional face-to-face guidance and telephone follow-up should be prioritized over mobile health applications. These recommendations can be flexibly adjusted according to hospital grade, staffing, economic level, and patient needs to support safe, feasible, and patient-centered post-discharge follow-up care for patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, only studies published in English or Chinese were included, which may have excluded relevant high-quality evidence published in other languages and thus limited the global generalizability of the findings. Second, the search was restricted to literature published from July 2015 to January 2026, and older guidelines or consensus documents were excluded, which may introduce selection bias and omit some historically important clinical recommendations. Third, most included evidence originated from high-income countries or tertiary medical centers; few studies focused on low-resource, rural, or community settings, which may restrict the direct applicability to under-resourced areas. Finally, the long-term effects of the proposed follow-up model were not fully addressed in the included literature. Therefore, when translating these evidence-based recommendations into clinical practice, health care providers should fully consider regional medical resources, local staffing conditions, patient individual characteristics, and cultural background to achieve adaptive and feasible application.

Conclusions

This evidence-based study systematically synthesized 12 pieces of best evidence for discharge follow-up of patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy, covering five core dimensions: follow-up providers, methods, frequency, content, and complication prevention and management. The finding provides an evidence base to inform future protocol development. Future research should prioritize original studies and implementation science to promote the translation and application of these evidence-based recommendations into routine care, thereby further optimizing patient outcomes. These evidence-based recommendations are clinically applicable with appropriate adaptation to local health care contexts and patient characteristics. Future implementation research is needed to verify the effectiveness, feasibility, and cost-effectiveness of these recommendations in real-world clinical settings, especially in low-resource and rural areas.
