Visceral Leishmaniasis–Associated Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis in a Patient With Crohn’s Disease in Therapy With Anti‐TNFα: A Challenging Diagnostic Case Report
Authors: Caterina Pelligra, Lorenzo Bolis, Andrea Costantino, Maurizio Vecchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare yet life‐threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome, increasingly reported in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) receiving biologic therapy. While Epstein–Barr virus and cytomegalovirus represent the most frequent infectious triggers, visceral leishmaniasis (VL) is extremely uncommon and may be easily ignored outside endemic areas.Case PresentationWe report the case of a 31‐year‐old man with Crohn’s disease treated with escalated anti‐TNFα therapy who presented with persistent fever, pancytopenia, hyperferritinemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and progressive systemic inflammation. Both infectious and oncologic workups were initially inconclusive. PET/CT showed splenomegaly, diffuse splenic, and bone marrow hypermetabolism, raising suspicion for a lymphoproliferative disorder; however, bone marrow aspirate revealed systemic inflammation and hemophagocytosis, suggesting HLH and detected intracellular amastigotes consistent with Leishmania spp., subsequently confirmed by serology. Liposomal amphotericin B prompted rapid clinical and biochemical improvement, with complete resolution of HLH. Anti‐TNFα was discontinued and replaced with anti–IL‐12/23 therapy to maintain IBD control while minimizing infectious risk.ConclusionIn patients receiving anti‐TNF therapy who present with persistent fever, cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, and unexplained systemic inflammation, HLH should be promptly investigated. Early bone marrow examination may facilitate diagnosis by revealing both hemophagocytosis and Leishmania amastigotes, enabling timely treatment. Following resolution of infection, transition to a biologic with a different mechanism of action, such as anti–IL‐12/23 therapy, may represent a reasonable strategy for long‐term IBD management.

1. Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare and life‐threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome caused by uncontrolled activation of cytotoxic T cells and macrophages, leading to massive cytokine release and multiorgan dysfunction [1, 2]. Secondary HLH is most commonly associated with viral infections, malignancies, autoimmune diseases, and iatrogenic immunosuppression [3]. Within the field of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), HLH is considered an uncommon but increasingly recognized complication in patients exposed to biologic agents, particularly anti‐TNFα therapies [4, 5]. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) and cytomegalovirus (CMV) represent the predominant infectious etiologies [6, 7]; however, atypical pathogens may act as precipitants in severely immunosuppressed patients. Visceral leishmaniasis (VL) is one of the rarest reported triggers and may be easily overlooked in geographical areas of low prevalence [8, 9]. Although HLH is treatable when recognized early, its clinical manifestations overlap substantially with infectious and oncologic conditions, often resulting in diagnostic delays and therapeutic uncertainty [10]. We describe a case of VL‐induced HLH in a patient with Crohn’s disease receiving anti‐TNFα therapy, illustrating the step‐by‐step diagnostic reasoning and the complexity of disentangling coexisting inflammatory, infectious, and hematologic features.

2. Case Presentation

A 31‐year‐old Sicilian man was referred to gastroenterology consultation for persistent fever (> 38°C), severe asthenia, and diffuse arthromyalgia ongoing for nearly one month. He had no history of travel to other endemic areas. His past medical history was remarkable for ileocolic Crohn’s disease diagnosed in 2012, treated with right hemicolectomy in 2013, followed by the initiation of anti‐TNFα therapy with adalimumab. In 2022, due to recurrent intestinal inflammation associated with extraintestinal manifestations (tibiotarsal arthritis and erythema nodosum), the biologic dose was escalated from 40 mg every two weeks to 40 mg once weekly. The patient had been on the escalated adalimumab dose for approximately 3 years before the onset of fever and systemic symptoms. He also had congenital deafness and a history of latent tuberculosis (TBC) successfully treated in 2016. At the time of presentation, the patient was not taking azathioprine, methotrexate, corticosteroids, or any other concomitant medications.

Upon admission, vital signs showed only low‐grade fever (37.5°C) and hemodynamic stability. Physical examination was otherwise unremarkable apart from persistent cough. Laboratory investigations revealed microcytic anemia (Hb 8–9 g/dL), leukopenia with predominant neutropenia, markedly elevated C‐reactive protein (> 100 mg/L), hyperferritinemia (4159 μg/L), hypertriglyceridemia (316 mg/dL), hypofibrinogenemia (2.1 g/L), and mild elevation of transaminases. Despite the systemic inflammatory burden, multiple blood cultures obtained during fever spikes yielded no microbial growth. Given his immunosuppressed status, an extensive infectious workup was undertaken, including SARS‐CoV‐2, EBV, CMV, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), Legionella, Brucella, stool cultures, and TBC QuantiFERON. All results were negative except for the expected persistent TBC immunological memory. Colonoscopy was performed to evaluate possible Crohn’s flare as fever source and demonstrated anastomotic recurrence (Rutgeerts i2) with inflamed polypoid mucosa; histology corroborated chronic inflammation without dysplasia or granulomas. Because fever (up to 39.1°C) persisted throughout hospitalization and ferritin levels continued to increase (up to 9165 μg/L), a PET/CT was requested to exclude occult malignancy or deep‐seated infection. Imaging demonstrated splenomegaly (14 × 8 cm) associated with diffuse hypermetabolism of the spleen and bone marrow without focal lesions (Figure 1). Although these findings initially raised suspicion for an underlying hematologic malignancy, they were nonspecific and not entirely consistent with the overall clinical and laboratory picture. Therefore, a broad differential diagnosis was considered, including lymphoma, opportunistic infection, and immune flare. Bone marrow aspirate was therefore performed and revealed hemophagocytosis together with a reactive inflammatory lymphoid component, favoring systemic immune activation; moreover, intracellular amastigotes consistent with Leishmania spp. were identified, redirecting the diagnostic and therapeutic trajectory (Figures 2 and 3). At this point of the diagnostic pathway, the patient fulfilled 6 of the 8 HLH‐2004 diagnostic criteria: fever, splenomegaly documented on PET/CT, cytopenias involving at least two lineages (anemia and neutropenia), hypertriglyceridemia, hyperferritinemia, and hemophagocytosis on bone marrow aspirate. The peak ferritin value was 9165 μg/L, triglycerides were 316 mg/dL, and fibrinogen was 2.1 g/L. Soluble CD25 and NK‐cell activity were not measured (Table 1). Serology and PCR testing on both peripheral blood and bone marrow samples confirmed Leishmania antibody positivity, consolidating the diagnosis of VL as the triggering infection. Liposomal amphotericin B (4 mg/kg) was commenced the same day. Fever resolved within 48 h; blood counts and ferritin levels showed progressive improvement during follow‐up and returned to normal values by approximately 2 months after treatment initiation. Anti‐TNFα was discontinued and replaced with anti–interleukin‐12/23 agents to reduce the risk of infectious relapse while maintaining Crohn’s disease control. The patient was discharged in good general condition and remained under infectious disease follow‐up until discontinuation of antimicrobial therapy, approximately 2 months after hospital discharge. After resolution of the infectious episode, the patient was referred to the gastroenterologists and resumed regular follow‐up every 4–6 months. Following the switch to anti–interleukin‐12/23 agents, the patient remained in clinical remission, with no evidence of Crohn’s disease flare and no need for corticosteroids. No endoscopic reassessment was performed until last follow‐up visit. Since the discharge, the patient remained clinically well, with no relapse of HLH or leishmaniasis and stable values of blood counts and ferritin levels.

3. Discussion

Our case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of VL‐induced HLH in patients with IBD receiving biologic therapy. HLH is a rare but life‐threatening syndrome caused by uncontrolled immune activation and macrophage hyperinflammation, and without rapid recognition and appropriate therapy, the condition is fatal (up to 60%) [4]. Most cases of secondary HLH are associated with an identifiable trigger, typically an infectious agent or malignancy, and occur predominantly in patients exposed to immunomodulating therapies. EBV and CMV infections are considered the most frequent trigger, while younger age, male sex, Crohn’s disease, and the induction phase of biologic treatment have been recognized as additional risk factors [5, 6] In contrast, VL is an exceptionally uncommon cause of HLH in IBD and is poorly described in literature [11].

From an immunopathological point, Leishmania represents an ideal trigger for HLH in patients exposed to anti‐TNFα [12, 13]. The parasite survives and replicates within macrophages, promoting sustained release of IFN‐γ, IL‐10, and IL‐18 and amplifying macrophage activation. At the same time, anti‐TNFα therapy weakens granuloma formation and pathogen containment, reducing NK‐ and CD8‐mediated cytotoxicity. This convergence of intracellular parasitic persistence and suppression of antigen‐directed immune surveillance creates an environment in which cytokine signaling becomes self‐amplifying and uncontrollable, explaining the fulminant inflammatory escalation observed in VL‐associated HLH [14, 15].

The diagnostic pathway in this case reflects the evolving clinical reasoning and the difficulty of establishing a differential diagnosis between inflammatory, infectious, and hematologic features. Similar diagnostic challenges have been reported in recent cases of VL‐associated HLH, where the clinical presentation initially mimicked alternative inflammatory or hematologic disorders, resulting in delayed recognition of the underlying parasitic infection, particularly in low‐endemic settings [16, 17]. As a result, many patients undergo an extensive series of diagnostic evaluations, often with inconclusive results delaying the establishment of a definitive diagnosis.

Persistent fever and elevated inflammatory markers led us to attribute at first to IBD reactivation, directing investigations toward the intestinal tract, but colonoscopy was negative for reactivation. The several negative blood cultures despite daily fever spikes were discordant with bacterial sepsis, supporting our idea to shift away from empirical antibiotic therapy. Nevertheless, no infectious triggers were identified despite extensive virological, microbiological, and autoimmune screening (SARS‐CoV‐2, CMV, EBV, HIV, HBV, HCV, Legionella, Brucella, and TBC). At the same time, the progressive rise in ferritin suggested an escalating hyperinflammatory state, and cytopenias on two lines (white and blood cells) raised the suspicion of a hematologic malignancy, reinforced by the PET/CT results showing diffuse splenic and bone marrow hypermetabolism as in a lymphoproliferative disorder. However, the bone marrow aspirate contradicted this hypothesis and, instead, documented hemophagocytosis and a reactive inflammatory lymphoid component, redirecting our focus toward HLH; the presence of intracellular amastigotes allows us the identification of Leishmania spp. as the underlying cause. Only after uncovering VL was it possible to rationalize the overall clinical picture and start with treatment (liposomal amphotericin B) that induced rapid and striking improvement, with fever resolution within 48 h and progressive normalization of laboratory parameters. This response strongly corroborated the causal relationship between VL and HLH. Beyond the acute phase, the case also underscored the therapeutic challenges required by immunosuppression management after HLH. While withdrawal of anti‐TNFα was essential to lower infectious risk, complete discontinuation of biologic therapy would have exposed the patient to a high probability of severe Crohn’s flare. Switching to anti–IL‐12/23 enabled balanced long‐term disease control while minimizing the risk of reexposing the patient to the same infectious susceptibility profile.

This case contributes to the scarce literature reporting VL‐induced HLH in patients with IBD and give practical clinical messages. First, in patients on biologics, the triad of persistent fever, rapidly rising ferritin, and cytopenias should trigger early consideration of HLH even in the absence of classical infectious or oncologic clues. Second, bone marrow examination should be considered when clinical, biochemical, and radiological findings remain discordant. Third, post‐HLH management requires careful reevaluation of the immunosuppressive regimen to guarantee both infectious safety and control of underlying IBD. Finally, multidisciplinary collaboration between gastroenterologists, hematologists, and infectious disease specialists played a crucial role in converging toward the diagnosis and coordinating treatment without elapse Crohn’s disease control.

4. Conclusions

VL‐induced HLH represents a rare but potentially fatal complication in immunosuppressed patients with IBD. Because its clinical and laboratory manifestations overlap with infectious and malignant disorders, diagnosis is frequently delayed. In patients receiving anti‐TNF therapy presenting with disproportionate systemic inflammation, rapidly rising ferritin, and cytopenias, HLH should be actively investigated, even in the absence of classical epidemiologic risk factors, and VL should remain in the differential diagnosis even in low‐prevalence settings. Early bone marrow evaluation is particularly valuable, as it may simultaneously demonstrate hemophagocytosis and identify Leishmania amastigotes, allowing prompt initiation of targeted therapy. Finally, careful reassessment of immunosuppressive treatment is essential after recovery, and switching to a biologic with a different mechanism of action, such as anti–IL‐12/23 therapy, may represent a reasonable long‐term strategy to maintain disease control while minimizing infectious risk.
