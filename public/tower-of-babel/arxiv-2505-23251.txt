Achieving Equitability with Subsidy
Yuanyuan Wang Tianze Wei

Abstract
We study the fair allocation problem of indivisible items with subsidies. In this paper, we mainly consider the notion of fairness - equitability (EQ), which requires that items be allocated such that all agents value the bundle they receive equally. Firstly, we study the upper bounds of the required subsidy to achieve EQ in different settings of items and provide the corresponding lower bounds. Secondly, we consider the bounded subsidy for achieving EQ and another popular notion of fairness - envy-freeness (EF) and give a characterization of the allocations that can achieve both EQ and EF. Finally, we analyze the bounds of subsidy of the allocations achieving fairness and efficiency (utilitarian social welfare or Nash welfare), and design several polynomial-time algorithms to compute the desired allocation.

Introduction

The fair division involves
fairly
distributing a set of items among a group of agents with individual preferences.
This problem has received considerable interest in diverse research communities, such as algorithmic game theory and artificial intelligence, and its relevant theory already has practical applications like the popular fair division platform spliddit.org
Goldman and Procaccia 2015
.
From different perspectives, there are various definitions of fairness,
such as
envy-freeness
(EF), where each agent prefers her own bundle of items to any other agent’s bundle, and
equitability
(EQ), which requires that each agent’s bundle of items has the same value.
In our paper, we focus on the fairness criterion - EQ, whose practical importance has been highlighted in the experimental study, such as the free-form bargaining experiments
Herreiner and Puppe 2009
.
Intuitively, EQ tries to minimize the bundle’s value gap between the highest and lowest one, and EQ corresponds to EF when agents have the same valuation.

If the items are divisible, like cakes, an EQ allocation always exists
Dubins and Spanier 1961
. However, in the setting of indivisible items, where each item must be allocated integrally to an agent, EQ allocations cannot be guaranteed.
For example, assume that there are two agents and a piece of jewelry, and both agents believe that the piece of jewelry is very valuable. It is trivial that no allocation can be EQ.
Accordingly, much of the recent research has focused on the relaxation of equitability, such as equitability up to one item (EQ1)
Freeman et al. 2019
;
Hosseini and Sethia 2025
and equitability up to any item (EQX)
Gourvès et al. 2014
;
Freeman et al. 2019
;
Barman et al. 2024
, which is a feasible way to circumvent this issue.
Another doable way is that we have enough money at our disposal, which may help alleviate inequality.
Consider the same example of two agents and a piece of jewelry.
EQ can be achieved by giving agent 1 this jewelry and giving agent 2 the corresponding amount of money that agent 1 values this jewelry.
In this paper, we study whether EQ could be achieved by the addition of a small quantity of money, namely
subsidy
, and then proceed to consider whether both EQ and EF can be guaranteed with the same limited subsidy.
In the literature on fair division, a significant amount of work focused on achieving precise fairness with subsidy, and more details can be found in the related work
Halpern and Shah 2019
;
Brustle et al. 2020
;
Barman et al. 2022
;
Goko et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023
;
Wu and Zhou 2024
.

The problem of fair division involves not only ensuring fairness but also allocating items efficiently to maximize the collective welfare of the agents.
In our paper, we focus on utilitarian social welfare and Nash welfare as key efficiency measures for allocations.
The formal definitions of these measures can be found in the preliminaries section.
A natural question related to subsidy is whether it is possible for an allocation to achieve the goal that its subsidy for fairness is optimally bounded and the efficiency can be guaranteed simultaneously.
Therefore, going one step further,
we study the compatibility between the optimally bounded subsidy and efficiency.
To summarize, our paper aims to study the fair allocation of
m
indivisible items among a set of
n
agents with subsidy and mainly answer the following questions:

How much money is required to achieve EQ?
What is the requirement if an allocation can be both EQ and EF with the same subsidy?
What efficiency can an allocation guarantee when its minimum required subsidy is optimally bounded?

1.2
Related Work

In the fair allocation of indivisible items, there is a line of work about the relaxation of EQ.

Gourvès et al. 2014
show that EQX allocation always exists for additive valuations and can be computed in polynomial time.

Barman et al. 2024
study the existence of EQX allocations in the goods setting when agents have beyond additive valuations,
where the existence algorithm is not executed in polynomial time.
Hosseini and Sethia 2025
study EQ1 allocations in the setting of mixed goods and chores when agents have additive valuations.
In our paper, we study the exact EQ fairness with the subsidy, where one side result is the polynomial time computation of EQ1 allocations for the goods or chores setting beyond additive valuations.

Moreover, money compensation has been prevalent in classic economic literature
Maskin 1987
;
Alkan et al. 1991
.
When agents have additive valuations,

Halpern and Shah 2019

characterize
an allocation that can be
envy-free with some payments, which is called envy-freeable allocation,
and show that an envy-freeable allocation requires a subsidy of
at most
(n-1)m
.
Brustle et al.
Brustle et al. 2020
propose a polynomial time algorithm to compute an envy-freeable allocation where each agent’s subsidy is at most one.
Later,
Caragiannis and Ioannidis 2021
study the optimization of computing the minimum subsidy and showed the additive approximation algorithm for bounded subsidy.
Caragiannis et al. 2021
initiate the study of interim envy-freeness (iEF) allocation with subsidy and focused on several different payments.
When agents have monotone valuations,
Brustle et al. 2020
prove that there exists an envy-freeable allocation where the subsidy to each agent is at most
2(n-1)
.
Consequently,
Kawase et al. 2024
improve the upper bound of each agent’s subsidy to at most
(n-\frac{3}{2})
.
In the restricted valuation domain,
Barman et al. 2022
study the general valuations with binary marginal and showed the optimal upper bound
(n-1)
of the total subsidy.

Goko et al. 2024
show that when agents have matroid valuations, there is a truthful allocation mechanism that achieves envy-freeness and utilitarian optimality by subsidizing each agent with at most one.
Money compensation is also studied in other fairness notions, such as proportional allocations with subsidy
Wu et al. 2023
;
Wu and Zhou 2024
, EF and EQ allocations with money transfer for agents with superadditive valuations
Aziz 2021
, where the negative payment is allowed, and the total subsidy is zero, which is different from our setting.
In our paper, we fill the gap in the literature on fair allocation with subsidy, where we consider EQ fairness.

For the fair division problem that satisfies both fairness and efficiency,
Freeman et al. 2019
;
Freeman et al. 2020
explore the compatibility between EQX (EQ1) and Pareto optimality,
and

Sun et al. 2023
study the equitability and utilitarian social welfare in allocating indivisible items when agents have additive valuations.
In addition,

Narayan et al. 2021
show that for general monotone valuations, there always exists an envy-free allocation with transfer payment, which guarantees a high fraction of optimal Nash welfare, and they also study the compatibility with utilitarian social welfare.

Preliminaries

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Let
N=[n]
denote the set of agents and let
M=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
denote the set of
m
indivisible items.
Each agent
i\in N
is endowed with a valuation function
v_{i}:2^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
We assume that
v_{i}(\emptyset)=0
.
For any valuation function
v_{i}
, we define the marginal value of an item
e\in M
with respect to a bundle
S\subseteq M
,
as
\Delta_{i}(S,e)=v_{i}(S\cup e)-v_{i}(S)
.
If
\Delta_{i}(S,e)\geq 0
for any
S\subseteq M
, we say an item
e\in M
is a
good
for agent
i\in N
.
Additionally, if
\Delta_{i}(S,e)\leq 0
for any
S\subseteq M
, with at least one of inequalities being strict, we say that an item
e\in M
is a
chore
for agent
i\in N
.
We consider three different settings of items: (1) all items are goods, (2) all items are chores, and (3) mixtures of goods and chores,
where an agent may derive positive, negative, or zero marginal valuation from an item.

A valuation function
v_{i}
is additive if
v_{i}(S)=\sum_{e\in S}v_{i}(e)
for any
S\subseteq M
.
A valuation
v_{i}
is submodular if
v_{i}(S)+v_{i}(T)\geq v_{i}(S\cup T)+v_{i}(S\cap T)
for any
S,T\subseteq M
.
A valuation
v_{i}
is called a matroid rank function if
v_{i}
is submodular and the marginal value of each item is zero or one.
A valuation
v_{i}
is subadditive if
v_{i}(S\cup T)\leq v_{i}(S)+v_{i}(T)
for any
S,T\subseteq M
.
Without loss of generality, we assume a standard value oracle access to valuations, that given any agent
i\in N
and any subset
S\subseteq M
, returns
v_{i}(S)\in\mathbb{R}
in
O(1)
time.
Unless specified otherwise, we assume that each agent’s valuation function
v_{i}
is scaled so that the maximum marginal value of each item is at most one, i.e.,
\lvert\Delta_{i}(S,e)\rvert\leq 1
for any
i\in N
,
e\in M
and
S\subseteq M\setminus\{e\}
.
For a singleton set
\{e\}
, we will use
v_{i}(x)
as a shorthand for
v_{i}(\{x\})
.
A fair allocation instance is denoted as
\mathcal{I}=\langle M,N,\boldsymbol{v}\rangle
, where
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
.
An allocation
{\bf A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is a
n
-partition of
M
, where
A_{i}
is the bundle allocated to agent
i
,
\bigcup_{i\in N}A_{i}=M
and
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
for any two agents
i\neq j
.
Let
\Pi_{n}(M)
denote the set of all
n
-partitions of the items.

2.1
Fairness and Efficiency Notions

Definition 1

(Equitability)
.

An allocation
{\bf A}
is equitable (EQ) if for any two agents
i,j\in N
,
v_{i}(A_{i})=v_{j}(A_{j})
.

Definition 2

(Equitability up to One Item)
.

An allocation
{\bf A}
is equitable up to one item (EQ1), if for any two agents
i,j\in N
with
A_{i},A_{j}\neq\emptyset
,
v_{i}(A_{i}\setminus\{e\})\geq v_{j}(A_{j}\setminus\{e\})
for some item
e\in A_{i}\cup A_{j}
.

Definition 3

(Envy-freeness)
.

An allocation
{\bf A}
is envy-free (EF) if for any two agents
i,j\in N
,
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq v_{i}(A_{j})
.

Specifically, Let
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
be the payment vector, where each agent
i
receives a payment
p_{i}\geq 0
.

Definition 4

(Equitablility with Payment)
.

An allocation with payment
({\bf A},\boldsymbol{p})
is said to be equitable if for any two agents
i,j\in N
,
v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}=v_{j}(A_{j})+p_{j}
.

Definition 5

(Envy-freeness with Payment)
.

An allocation with payment
({\bf A},\boldsymbol{p})
is said to be envy-free if for any two agents
i,j\in N
,
v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}\geq v_{i}(A_{j})+p_{j}
.

Definition 6

(Equitable-convertible)
.

An allocation
{\bf A}
is said to be equitable-convertible if there exists a payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}
such that
({\bf A},\boldsymbol{p})
is EQ.

Definition 7

(Envy-freeable)
.

An allocation
{\bf A}
is called envy-freeable if there exists a payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}
such that
({\bf A},\boldsymbol{p})
is EF.

Definition 8

(Utilitarian Social Welfare)
.

Given an allocation
{\bf A}
, its utilitarian social welfare
USW
is defined as the sum of value that the agent derives from the allocation, i.e.,
USW({\bf A})=\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i})
.
For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we say that an allocation
A
is
\alpha
-max-USW if it holds that
USW(\textbf{A})\geq\alpha\cdot\max_{\textbf{B}\in\Pi_{n}(M)}USW(\textbf{B})
.

Definition 9

(Nash Welfare)
.

Given an allocation
{\bf A}
in the goods setting, its Nash welfare
NSW
is defined as the geometric mean of value that the agent derives from the allocation, i.e.,
NSW({\bf A})=(\prod_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i}))^{\frac{1}{n}}
.
For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we say that an allocation
A
is
\alpha
-max-NSW if it holds that
NSW(\textbf{A})\geq\alpha\cdot\max_{\textbf{B}\in\Pi_{n}(M)}NSW(\textbf{B})
.

3
Bounding Subsidy for Equitable-Convertible Allocations

In this section, we study the minimum subsidy required to achieve equitability in the goods, chores, and mixed items settings.
We are interested in two cases where an (equitable-convertible) allocation is given to us, and where we can choose such an allocation to minimize the subsidy.

3.1
When the Allocation Is Given

First, we note that every allocation is equitable-convertible.
i.e., for an arbitrary allocation, we can always find a feasible payment vector, which enables this allocation to be EQ.
We provide the following lemma to illustrate what the optimal payment vector is when an allocation is given.

Lemma 1
.

For any allocation
{\bf A}
, consider a payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
, such that
p_{i}^{*}=\max_{j\in N}v_{j}(A_{j})-v_{i}(A_{i})
, we have

(1)

({\bf A},\boldsymbol{p}^{*})
is EQ.

(2)

With this payment vector, the total subsidy for achieving EQ is minimized.

Proof.

For the first claim, let
j^{max}\in\arg\max_{j\in N}v_{j}(A_{j})
.
For any agent
i\in N
, we have
p_{i}^{*}=v_{j^{max}}(A_{j^{max}})-v_{i}(A_{i})
. Rearranging this, we get
v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}^{*}=v_{j^{max}}(A_{j^{max}})
, which means that this allocation
({\bf A},\boldsymbol{p}^{*})
is EQ.

For the second claim, it suffices to show that for any feasible payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}
, we have
p_{i}^{*}\leq p_{i}
for every agent
i\in N
.
Suppose, for the contradiction, that there exists a feasible payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
such that
p_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}<p_{i^{\prime}}^{*}=v_{j^{max}}(A_{j^{max}})-v_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i^{\prime}})
for some agent
i^{\prime}\in N
.
Then, we have
v_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i^{\prime}})+p_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}<v_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i^{\prime}})+v_{j^{max}}(A_{j^{max}})-v_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i^{\prime}})=v_{j^{max}}(A_{j^{max}})\leq v_{j^{max}}(A_{j^{max}})+p_{j^{max}}^{\prime}
, which contradicts that
\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
is a feasible payment vector.
∎

Next, we have the following proposition to demonstrate the minimum subsidy required for a specific allocation in the worst case when items are goods or chores.

Proposition 1
.

Given an allocation
{\bf A}
, in the worst case, the minimum required subsidy is
(n-1)m
for the goods setting or
m
for
the chores setting to achieve EQ.

Proof.

First, let us consider the goods setting.
For the lower bound, consider an instance with
N=[n]
agents who have identical additive valuations and
M=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
items,
where for any
e\in M
, the value of item
e
for each agent
i\in N
is exactly 1.
Then, consider an allocation
{\bf A}
where one agent
i^{*}
picks all items.
By Lemma
1
, we can derive the optimal payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}
, where
p_{i^{*}}=0
and
p_{i}=m
for any
i\neq i^{*}
.
Therefore, we need at least
(n-1)m
subsidy.
Next, we show the upper bound. Note that for any allocation
{\bf A}
, we have
\max_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i})-\min_{j\in N}v_{j}(A_{j})\leq m
since there are at most
m
items whose total value is at most
m
in one bundle.
By Lemma
1
and the fact that the agent whose bundle has the highest value must receive zero payment, the minimum subsidy is at most
(n-1)m
.

Next, let us consider the chores setting.
For the lower bound, consider an instance with
N=[n]
agents who have identical additive valuations and
M=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
items, where for any
e\in M
, the value of item
e
for each agent
i
is exactly
-1
.
Then, consider an allocation
{\bf A}
where one agent
i^{*}
picks all items.
By Lemma
1
, we can get the optimal payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}
, where
p_{i^{*}}=m
and
p_{i}=0
for any
i\neq i^{*}
.
Therefore, we need at least
m

As for the upper bound,
for any allocation
\bf{A}
, we have
\max_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i})\leq 0
since all items are chores.
In this case, except for the agent whose bundle has the highest value, we need to pay money for the remaining agents, and the minimum subsidy required is at most
m

\max_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i})
.
∎

Squeeze Algorithm

We design the following algorithm, named Squeeze Algorithm, and the details of our algorithm can be found in Algorithm

The high-level idea of our algorithm is that in each iteration, we first try to allocate the item with the highest negative marginal value to the agent whose bundle has the highest value since, by Lemma

, for agent
i

p_{i}=\max_{j\in N}v_{j}(A_{j})-v_{i}(A_{i})

v_{i}(A_{i})
can also save money that is paid for her.
At last, if the above cases fail, we directly allocate the remaining unallocated items to the agent whose bundle has the lowest value to ensure that no item is left.
For obtaining efficient algorithms despite the exponential size of the input, the valuations are presented to the algorithm as “valuation oracles” – black boxes that can be queried for the valuation of a set

Bounding Subsidy for Both Equitable-Convertible and Envy-Freeable Allocations

In this section, we study whether an allocation can satisfy EQ and EF with the same payment vector.
Recall that there are some characterizations for an allocation that is envy-freeable
Halpern and Shah 2019
.
Because we consider two criteria, it is clear that there exist some allocations, such that they cannot be EQ and EF simultaneously, even if we allocate some money to agents.
First, we characterize an allocation that is both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable with the same payment vector and give the following sufficient and necessary conditions.

Theorem 2
.

For an allocation
{\bf A}
, the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

{\bf A}
is both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable with the same payment vector.

(2)

v_{j}(A_{j})\geq v_{i}(A_{j})
for any two agents
i,j\in N
.

Proof.

First, we show that
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Suppose an allocation
{\bf A}
is equitable-convertible and envy-freeable. In that case, it means that there exists a payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}
, such that for any two agents
i,j\in N
, we have
v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}=v_{j}(A_{j})+p_{j}
and
v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}\geq v_{i}(A_{j})+p_{j}
.
Combining the above two inequalities together, we get
v_{j}(A_{j})\geq v_{i}(A_{j})
.

Next, we show that
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. When the claim (2) is satisfied, it suffices to show that there exists a payment vector
\boldsymbol{p}
to enable
{\bf A}
to be both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable.
Let
p_{i}=\max_{k\in N}v_{k}(A_{k})-v_{i}(A_{i})
for any agent
i\in N
.
Note that for any agent
i\in N
, we have
v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}=\max_{k\in N}v_{k}(A_{k})
, which means that
{\bf A}
is equitable.
Then, fix any two agents

v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}=\max_{k\in N}v_{k}(A_{k})\geq\max_{k\in N}v_{k}(A_{k})+v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{j}(A_{j})=v_{i}(A_{j})+p_{j}

v_{i}(S_{j})\leq v_{i}(S_{j}\setminus\{e_{\ell}\})+v_{i}(e_{\ell})<\frac{2}{3n-3}
.
Consequently, for agent
i
, there are two cases.

Case 1:
If
N^{+}\neq\emptyset
, we have

\displaystyle v_{i}(M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=v_{i}(M\setminus\bigcup_{j\in N^{+}}B_{j}\setminus\bigcup_{j\in N\setminus(N^{+}\cup N^{-})}S_{j})

\displaystyle\geq v_{i}(M)-v_{i}(\bigcup_{j\in N^{+}}B_{j})-v_{i}(\bigcup_{j\in N\setminus(N^{+}\cup N^{-})}S_{j})

\displaystyle\geq v_{i}(M)-\sum_{j\in N^{+}}v_{i}(B_{j})-\sum_{j\in N\setminus(N^{+}\cup N^{-})}v_{i}(S_{j})

\displaystyle>1-\frac{n}{3n-3}-\frac{2}{3n-3}(n-\lvert N^{+}\rvert-\lvert N^{-}\rvert)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{3n-3}.

N^{+}=\emptyset

\displaystyle v_{i}(M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=v_{i}(M\setminus\bigcup_{j\in N\setminus N^{-}}S_{j})

\frac{n}{3n-3}
.
In the following steps, the utilitarian social welfare cannot decrease, so the utilitarian social welfare of output allocation must be at least
\frac{n}{3n-3}
.
Given an instance with normalized subadditive valuations,

is a trivial upper bound on the maximum utilitarian social welfare.
Therefore, the returned allocation has an

Next, we prove that the output allocation is EQ1.
Let
\bf{B}

B_{i}=\emptyset
for any agent
i\in N^{-}
.
Thus, that partial allocation
\bf{B}

If

2
.
If not, the algorithm proceeds to allocate items to agents in
N^{-}
.

N^{-}=\emptyset

For any agent
i\in N

\lvert C_{i}\rvert=1

If

If not, the algorithm follows Algorithm

By the proof of Theorem

∎

Algorithm 4

Nash Welfare

Next, we consider the Nash welfare for two agents.
In contrast to the study of envy-based notions for two agents, some classic techniques like divide and choose cannot work for EQ1.
Here, we need a subtle case analysis to allocate the items.
The intuition of our algorithm follows a similar idea of Algorithm
2
.
In the first step, we try to identify the items that have high marginal values.
If such an item exists, we allocate it first.
If not, we use the bag-filling algorithm to iteratively add items in an empty bag until one agent thinks its value is good enough and allocates it to her.
At last, we execute Algorithm
1
to extend the partial allocation to a complete one.
The details of our algorithm can be found in Algorithm
3
.

Theorem 5
.

For two agents with normalized subadditive valuations in the goods setting, Algorithm
3
computes an EQ1 allocation that has a
\frac{1}{3}
-max-NSW in polynomial time.

Proof.

The algorithm is executed in two phases.
In the first phase, we get a partial EQ1 allocation with some guarantees of Nash welfare.
In the second phase, the partial EQ1 allocation is extended to a complete EQ1 allocation by Algorithm

\frac{1}{3}

Then, we further study the normalized additive valuation setting for Nash welfare when there are two agents and present an algorithm to return an EQ1 allocation with a better fraction of maximum Nash welfare compared to the setting of subadditive valuations.
The difference between this algorithm and the above one is that we start with a maximizing Nash welfare allocation and use the fact that with additive valuations, a maximizing Nash welfare allocation is always envy-free up to one item (EF1), which does not hold with the subadditive valuations.
Like the above algorithm, we also need a detailed case analysis, and the intuition is similar, i.e., we first allocate the high-valued item and then the remaining items.
The details of our algorithm are shown in Algorithm
4
.

It is noteworthy that when dealing with additive valuations, given any instance, there exists an EF1 allocation that maximizes Nash welfare
Caragiannis et al. 2019
, meaning that envy can be eliminated by hypothetically removing the item with the highest value from the envied bundle in the maximizing Nash welfare allocation.
Surprisingly, based on the constructed instance in the proof of Theorem
3
, it can be seen that any EQ1 allocation guarantees an arbitrarily bad fraction of maximum Nash welfare, which is in sharp contrast to EF1.

5.2
Equitability and Envy-Freeness

In this part, we consider the combination of two fairness notions, i.e., equitability and envy-freeness.
In Section
4
, we have seen that as long as one allocation satisfies the conditions in Theorem
2
, the minimum subsidy required for both equitability and envy-freeness is optimally bounded.
Therefore, we only need to pick one from all allocations that are both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable and make sure that it can guarantee some efficiency.
First, we present a negative result where there is no compatibility between the property of equitable-convertible and envy-freeable and Nash welfare.

Proposition 6
.

There exists a goods instance where no allocation that is both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable can guarantee a non-zero fraction of maximum Nash welfare, even for additive valuations.

Proof.

Given any instance, consider a maximizing utilitarian social welfare allocation
{\bf A}^{*}
.
Now pick an arbitrary agent
i\in N
.
For any item
e\in A_{i}^{*}
, it must hold that
v_{i}(e)\geq v_{j}(e)
for any agent
j\neq i
.
Otherwise, we could find another allocation that has a higher utilitarian social welfare.
Thus, we get
v_{i}(A_{i}^{*})=\sum_{e\in A_{i}^{*}}v_{i}(e)\geq\sum_{e\in A_{i}^{*}}v_{j}(e)=v_{j}(A_{i}^{*})
for any agent
j\neq i
.
By Theorem
2
, it is clear that
{\bf A}^{*}
is both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable.
∎

For additive valuations, although we may not find any equitable-convertible and envy-freeable allocation to guarantee any non-zero fraction of maximum Nash welfare in some instances, we find a maximizing utilitarian social allocation is always equitable-convertible and envy-freeable.

Theorem 7
.

For additive valuations in the goods setting, maximizing utilitarian social welfare allocations are both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable.

Proof.

Consider an instance with
n

m
items.
For agent
1
, the value of each item is
1
.
For the remaining agent
i\neq 1
, the value of each item is
(1-\epsilon)

(0<\epsilon\leq 1)
.
By Theorem
2
, in this instance, the only equitable-convertible and envy-freeable allocation
{\bf A}
is that agent

picks all items since for any item

v_{1}(e)>v_{i}(e)
for any

.
Then, we can derive that the Nash welfare of allocation
\bf{A}
is 0, but the maximum Nash welfare of this instance is trivially non-zero.
∎

Then, we complement the above result by showing that if agents have beyond additive valuations like submodular valuations, the above theorem may not hold.

Proposition 7
.

For submodular valuations in the goods setting, there exists an instance where maximizing utilitarian social welfare allocations are not both equitable-convertible and envy-freeable.

Proof.

Consider an instance with two agents who have submodular valuations and four items
M=\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}

The valuation of each agent is given as follows.
For any subset

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we focus on the fairness notion of equitability and study the subsidy for achieving equitability.
First, we delve into the analysis of the amount of payment required for an equitable allocation beyond additive valuations.
Next, we study the allocation that achieves both equitability and envy-freeness with the same subsidy, and we provide the characterization.
At last, we study the subsidy for achieving equitability or both equitability and envy-freeness with efficiency, and present different results for two efficiency notions, including utilitarian social welfare and Nash welfare.

As for future work, one area of focus is bridging the gap between the lower and upper bounds of subsidy for achieving equitability in the context of non-monotone additive valuations.
Another interesting direction is the study of what Nash welfare can be guaranteed for an EQ1 allocation when there are more than three agents with normalized subadditive or additive valuations.
