Gene dependency-informed inference of response to targeted cancer therapies
Authors: Nilabja Bhattacharjee, Sreeram Chandra Murthy Peela, Abhishek Halder, Bernadette Mathew, Swarnava Samanta, Sakshi Gujral, Stuti Kumari, Smruti Panda, Ritwik Ganguly, Gaurav Ahuja, Subhajyoti De, Angshul Majumdar, Debarka Sengupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeted therapies such as small-molecule inhibitors act by blocking proteins essential for cancer cell survival, yet omics-based modeling of drug sensitivity often lacks mechanistic grounding. We present FORGE (Factorization Of Response and Gene Essentiality), a joint matrix factorization framework that co-models drug response and target gene dependency to enable biologically informed stratification of treatment groups. FORGE derives a Benefit Score from basal gene expression to estimate therapeutic potential. In unseen cell lines treated with erlotinib, FORGE achieves high concordance for dependency (0.69) and IC50 (0.62), with benefit score stratification showing increased dependency and decreased IC50 across quartiles. Joint modeling improves predictive performance over single-task approaches and enhances agreement between gene-level effects (p = 0.039). Validation across independent datasets shows that higher benefit scores associate with tumor regression in patient-derived xenografts and with predicted drug susceptibility in the Tahoe-100M dataset. Mechanistic analyses further identify gene programs underlying drug susceptibility.

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of mortality globally and is a complex disease affecting multiple organ systems1. Anti-cancer therapies typically target genes and pathways deemed essential for cancer cell survival, proliferation, or metastasis. However, gene essentiality varies significantly among patients and cancer types, limiting the efficacy of single-agent or combinatorial therapies2,3. This lack of efficacy is further intensified by dose-limiting toxicities arising from on-target effects in normal tissues (e.g., EGFR inhibitors inducing dermatological and gastrointestinal toxicity), which narrow therapeutic windows and negatively impact long-term outcomes4,5. Personalized medicine aims to circumvent these challenges by designing therapies using patient-specific omics profiles. This paradigm shift in cancer therapeutics has been facilitated by the availability of large-scale omics and high-throughput drug screens. Numerous databases like the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap)6, CREAMMIST7, the Cancer cell-line Encyclopedia (CCLE)8, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)9, and the NCI’s Patient Derived Models Repository (PDMR) (https://pdmdb.cancer.gov/web/apex/f?p=101:41) provide comprehensive genomic and functional profiling data for multiple cancer types. These resources were successfully employed by researchers globally in studying cancer mechanisms and identifying new biomarkers for diagnosis and/or therapy.

Predictive machine learning (ML) or deep learning (DL) models utilize large-scale omics data as input features for predicting drug responses or identifying potential biomarkers for drug susceptibility. Numerous DL-based models, such as CDRscan10, DeepTTA11, and DeepDR12 utilize neural network architectures for predicting cancer drug responses using molecular and/or drug-related data. Similarly, models like DeepHE13 and XGEP14 use the gene expression profiles for predicting gene essentiality, achieving accuracies in the range of 94–96%. We previously developed and validated Precily, a deep neural network for inferring treatment responses using drug descriptors and gene expression profiles along with the pathway enrichment scores (PES)15. PES aids in representing cells or samples as summarized values combining pathway activities and gene set enrichment scores16. In Precily, we discussed the issue of train–test data leakage, wherein reported accuracies of many DL models are artificially inflated due to the lack of proper cell-line and drug blinding. Randomly splitting drug–cell-line pairs across training and testing leads to the reuse of cell-line expression profiles, enabling models to “memorize” baseline sensitivities and mimic the expected IC50 values for unseen drugs. To mitigate this, Precily enforced cell-line disjoint splits, ensuring that no cell-line appeared in more than one dataset partition, thereby providing a more rigorous and clinically realistic evaluation of model performance.

While the above-discussed ML/DL models are gaining popularity, the practical applications of many ML/DL models are hindered by complexities in data integration, training, and model transferability to newer datasets17. These nonlinear approaches are particularly prone to overfitting, especially when trained on limited biological datasets. Deep neural networks, with their high parameter count, tend to “memorize” training data rather than learn generalizable patterns, resulting in poor performance on unseen samples18–20. This issue undermines model transferability and reduces clinical utility. Evidence suggests that pan-cancer, pan-drug ML models may not provide meaningful advantages over naive baselines, as a mean-baseline model that simply predicts the average IC50. This often yields predictive performance comparable to complex ML models, particularly in the challenging context of z-scored IC50 predictions where pan-drug ML models largely fail21.

On the other hand, the dimensionality problem in traditional ML (N ≪ P) plagues numerous omics databases as the number of input features (genes, P) is substantially larger than the number of samples (patients or cell lines, N). Matrix factorization (MF) techniques, on the other hand, are resilient in handling such high-dimensional omics datasets and play a critical role in omics integration by linking latent embeddings to hidden biological states22. Techniques like Similarity-regularized MF (SRMF)23, weighted graph regularized matrix factorization (WGRMF)24, and Drug Sensitivity Prediction using Logistic Matrix Factorization (DSPLMF)25 identify groups of cell lines that are susceptible to specific drugs. These methods learn embeddings from drug chemistry and cell-line similarities, and can predict the drug response values accurately. Compared with DL and transformer-based models (as in DSPLMF evaluation) or 10-fold cross-validation (in SMRF and WGRMF), these models demonstrated higher accuracy23–25. Matrix factorization models, therefore, provide a robust and interpretable alternative to the black-box nature of DL approaches.

Predictive accuracy alone is not enough to design effective therapies. A mechanistically grounded and generalizable understanding of why a tumor is vulnerable is needed. In computational oncology, an important but underused idea is that drug sensitivity should biologically align with gene dependency. Targeted therapies work best when drug inhibition mimics impairment in the function of the target gene26. Given that drug discovery in cancer is dependent on gene essentiality identified among the cancer cells, it makes it biologically meaningful to combine these two features in predictive modeling. Such models can capture the drug inhibitory effects towards specific targets, and potentially ignore any off-target effects. When a drug’s inhibitory effects match the effect of knocking out a gene across many cancer cell-lines, it is more likely to act on its true target. For example, in the Cancer Dependency Map, inhibitors of PI3K show cell-line sensitivity patterns that closely mirror CRISPR knockouts of PI3K genes, directly linking drug response to genetic dependency and on-target action27. In pancreatic cancer, CRISPR screens identified genes like CENPE and RRM1 as essential only when the MEK pathway was inhibited. Knocking out these genes made cancer cells much more sensitive to MEK inhibitors, and such synergistic effects were reported among the cell-lines and in animal models28. Unlike many of the black-box ML/DL models that may often learn drug toxicity or shared response patterns21, matrix-factorization based joint modeling of drug response and gene dependency should give predictions inline with underlying biological mechanisms, improving interpretability while separating pathway-specific effects from nonspecific cytotoxicity.

In this work, we develop a matrix factorization-based algorithm called FORGE as an alternative approach to existing DL methods. FORGE relies on gene expression profiles–consistently shown to be the most predictive omics layer for drug response29–thereby reducing complexity while retaining predictive power. We leverage publicly available large-scale resources, including DepMap (gene dependency and bulk RNAseq profiles) and CREAMMIST (drug response profiles), to design FORGE as a joint-matrix factorization method, learning sample representations as a combination of latent weight matrices. Using the embeddings, we compute gene influence scores as contributions of each gene in predicting either dependency or IC50, and identify a high correlation of these influence scores when using the joint-modeling framework. We successfully show that the learned embeddings are transferable, enabling accurate prediction of drug responses across both single-cell datasets and patient-derived xenografts. We further extend FORGE to derive a biomarker, the Benefit Score, which integrates the combined effect of drug response and target gene essentiality, thereby stratifying samples according to their likelihood of exhibiting favorable responses to targeted therapies. Furthermore, FORGE can detect key genes that impact therapy outcomes, highlighting its ability to not only predict drug response but also generate biologically grounded hypotheses with translational potential for personalized cancer therapy.

Results

Overview of FORGE

Targeted therapy works by inhibiting key molecular vulnerabilities involved in cancer cell growth and survival. The overlap of datasets like the DepMap and CREAMMIST databases in measuring gene dependency and drug IC50 presents a unique opportunity to model drug response together with genetic dependency, so that drug predictions are grounded in mechanistic evidence of gene essentiality. Matrix factorization-based frameworks, which decompose large data matrices into low-dimensional representations of features and samples, are well established in computational biology because they are simple, interpretable, and computationally efficient. We asked whether it is possible to jointly model target gene dependency and drug IC50 within such a framework, capturing shared biological structure while also preserving task-specific signals, and developed a joint matrix factorization model called FORGE that maps gene expression into a shared latent space and uses task-specific heads to independently predict gene dependency and IC50.

Biologically, these latent factors may reflect underlying regulatory or signaling mechanisms linking gene expression to phenotypic outcomes. The latent vectors hD and hI represent complementary axes of drug susceptibility: hD captures gene essentiality and potential druggability, while hI models additional system-level adaptations and residual effects that influence drug response robustness, reflected in IC50 (Eq. This complexity motivates our hypothesis that joint modeling better captures variability in cellular drug responses.

FORGE successfully models EGFR targeted therapy

We focused on applying FORGE to stratify cell lines shared among the DepMap and the CREAMMIST databases for Erlotinib that targets EGFR and blocks PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, suppressing cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis (Fig. 2a). Erlotinib is clinically approved in non-small cell lung cancer and has also been used in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, making it a well-characterized and clinically relevant test case30,31. Based on our observation that EGFR expression and Erlotinib IC50 were negatively correlated (Supplementary Fig. 2d), we hypothesized that joint modeling of gene dependency and IC50 would outperform independent models by learning a shared biological representation rather than disconnected feature spaces. To test these hypotheses, FORGE was evaluated on 686 cell lines common among the DepMap and CREAMMIST databases, by dividing them into train (N=549) and test (N=137) sets using an 80/20 split (Fig. 2b). Hyperparameter optimization was performed using Optuna with five-fold cross-validation using the 549 cell-lines in the training set, where each fold used 80% of the training data for fitting and 20% for validation. The per-fold best hyperparameter set was used for training with the entire training dataset. The final tuned models were analyzed through Pearson correlations along with an array of metrics computed between predicted and observed dependency and IC50 values. For the test set, Pearson r was 0.68 and 0.31 for the dependency and IC50 values, respectively, while the concordance index was 0.62 (for IC50) and 0.69 (for gene dependency) (Fig. 2h–k). Consistent with biological expectations, Benefit Scores were higher in sensitive quartiles, confirming that the score captures treatment-relevant information (Fig. 2l–o). While joint and independent models showed comparable correlations with outputs (Fig. 2c), correlations between task-specific heads were substantially reduced in independent models, highlighting the advantage of joint learning.

We next benchmarked FORGE against Precily, a neural network-based pan-cancer drug response model. Precily was retrained using updated MSigDB pathways and drug descriptors, and evaluated for erlotinib IC50 predictions. We observed a strong linear relationship (Pearson r = 0.69, p = 1.02 × 10−20; Fig. 2q) between Precily and FORGE predictions. Across nine additional drug-target pairs, FORGE achieved slightly variable IC50 correlations, although differences were not statistically significant (two-sided paired t-test, p = 0.22; Fig. 2r). Finally, we extended FORGE to a multi-drug setting using drug-specific IC50 heads while retaining a shared embedding for gene essentiality. On the test set, this achieved correlations of r = 0.36 for erlotinib and r = 0.21 for gefitinib, both targeting EGFR, with dependency correlation of 0.47 (Fig. 2p and Supplementary Figs. 3b–h). Differences in predictive performance likely reflect dataset artifacts and dosing effects rather than biological divergence. Erlotinib is often administered at its maximum tolerated dose (MTD), potentially yielding stronger and more consistent measurements, whereas gefitinib is typically dosed below its MTD and may exhibit greater variability in response32,33. These results demonstrate that FORGE can be extended to multiple inhibitors of the same target, supporting its scalability and broader applicability.

FORGE meaningfully clusters cancer cell-lines based on their treatment sensitivity

FORGE projects gene expression into a shared latent embedding from which both dependency and IC50 are predicted. Integrating these outputs yields a Benefit Score that reflects the potential therapeutic advantage of targeting a gene in a given sample. Correlating the latent space with the Benefit Score enables translating model embeddings into biologically and clinically meaningful groups. We asked whether samples with shared biological features cluster in the FORGE latent space and whether such clusters show high EGFR dependency, low erlotinib IC50, and elevated Benefit Scores, thereby linking latent structure to drug response and underlying biology.

Hierarchical clustering of the latent representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{Z}}}}$$\end{document}Z revealed multiple subgroups, with clusters 13, 24, and 15 emerging as the most sensitive (Fig. 3a, b). Box plots confirmed their clear separation from the broader distribution (Fig. 3c, d), with significant differences in the EGFR dependency (Mann-Whitney statistic = 6373, p = 1.35E-13, rank biserial correlation = 0.61) and Erlotinib IC50 (Mann-Whitney statistic = 25,452, p = 6.69E-11, rank biserial correlation = -0.54) among the key (N=60) and other (626) clusters. Among the 60 cell-lines in these clusters, most were derived from head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), while lung cancers predominated in cluster 25, ranked fourth in the overall sensitivity (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Similarly, these highly susceptible clusters were observed across both primary and metastatic tumors, with comparable representation in each group (Supplementary Table 1). This aligns with evidence that EGFR is a key oncogenic driver in HNSCC and ESCC, where tumors depend on EGFR signaling for proliferation and survival34,35. Differential expression analysis revealed robust shared signatures across sensitive clusters (3 e). Up-regulated genes included PTPN6, SYK, SYTL1, FGD3, DAPP1, BCL11A, CSTA, IRF6, and BSPRY, which are linked to epithelial differentiation and keratinocyte barrier formation (Fig. 3e, f). PTPN6 promotes cancer progression by sustaining MAPK/ERK-driven EMT programs and engaging SYK/SRC-family and JAK-STAT pathways36,37. Low SYK combined with high TGF-β1 marks an epithelial state driving EMT-mediated resistance to EGFR-TKIs38. The coordinated PTPN6-SYK axis fine-tunes EGFR phosphorylation and integrates epithelial and immune signaling39. BCL11A is a key determinant of prognosis and therapeutic response through SOX2-dependent transcriptional programs40. Among downregulated genes, AXL and NNMT are known mediators of EGFR-TKI resistance, and their inhibition restores drug sensitivity41–44. Joint modeling of essentiality and response set a common ground for model explanation

FORGE decomposes gene-level contributions and predicts cell-line-level outcomes, yielding task-specific effect sizes for both genetic dependency and drug sensitivity. For the dependency task, the Dependency-Effect is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{W}}}}\,{{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{D}$$\end{document}WhD, where positive values indicate increased vulnerability to CRISPR knockout. For drug response, lower IC50 values indicate greater sensitivity. However, the drug-specific head weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{W}}}}\,{{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{I}$$\end{document}WhI associate positive values with resistance. To align the biological interpretation across tasks, we define the IC50-Effect as -\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{W}}}}\,{{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{I}$$\end{document}WhI, such that positive values consistently reflect genes that promote drug sensitivity. By mapping these task-specific heads back into gene space via the shared projection matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{W}}}}$$\end{document}W, FORGE yields per-gene effect sizes for both dependency and drug response. This unified representation enables direct comparison of genetic dependency and drug sensitivity effects, facilitating the identification of shared biological drivers underlying therapeutic vulnerability. We hypothesized that genes increasing dependency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{W}}}}\,{{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{D}$$\end{document}WhD) would also promote drug sensitivity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{W}}}}\,{{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{I}$$\end{document}WhI), reflecting shared mechanisms, while discordant effects could indicate resistance or pathway divergence. We further tested whether joint modeling improves this concordance compared to independent models.

To quantify gene-level effects, we ℓ2-normalized the task-specific heads (hD and hI) to obtain unit-norm vectors (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{D,{{{{\rm{norm}}}}}}={{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{D}/\parallel {{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{D}\parallel,\,{{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{I,{{{{\rm{norm}}}}}}={{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{I}/\parallel {{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{I}\parallel$$\end{document}hD,norm=hD/∥hD∥,hI,norm=hI/∥hI∥), removing scale differences while preserving directional information. Gene-level Dependency-Effect and IC50-Effect were then computed using the normalized heads. Plotting these values allowed assessment of concordance using Pearson’s r. For interpretability, we extracted the top-20 and bottom-20 genes ranked by total influence and performed pathway enrichment using Enrichr KG. This pipeline was applied to both joint and independent FORGE models and repeated across multiple drug-target pairs.

Overall, for the nine drug-target pairs evaluated, the joint modeling achieved significantly higher concordance than independent models (paired t-test, p = 3.9 × 10−2; (Fig. 4c)), supporting a shared biological basis linking genetic dependency and pharmacologic response. For the Dabrafenib–BRAF model, joint modeling produced strong alignment between gene-level dependency and drug sensitivity effects (Pearson’s r = 0.89, p = 9.2 × 10−69; Fig. 4a), whereas independently trained models showed much weaker concordance (Pearson’s r = 0.13, p = 7.7 × 10−2; Fig. 4b). This improvement reflects the shared embedding learned in the joint framework, which reduces task-specific noise while preserving biologically meaningful signal. The top 20 highly influential genes consistently showed reported associations with key pathways that may favor drug susceptibility or gene essentiality. The EMT transition is a known mechanism of tyrosine-kinase inhibitor (TKI) (like Erlotinib) resistance59. Following this evidence, we analyzed the expression levels of key EMT mediators. Our results point to a combined genetic signature characterized by the upregulation of epithelial markers EPCAM and CDH1 (log2FC > 1.2) and the repression of mesenchymal markers, specifically DAB2 and the COL6A family (log2FC ranging from -1.5 to -2.9) within the ’susceptible’ cell-lines. EPCAM is a known ligand for EGFR, and when bound, it induces cell proliferation but not EMT60, and high CDH1 expression maintains the epithelial state requisite for EGFR-TKI sensitivity61,62. Conversely, DAB2 repression leads to EMT differentiation in breast cancer63, while the collagen-family proteins (COL6A) are involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and their upregulation leads to increased collagen deposition around tumor cells, ultimately influencing the cellular proliferation, migration and survival64. Together, this evidence points to a complex interplay of genes among various cancer types, and FORGE was able to recover these signatures by mapping them with highly influential genes.

FORGE performs consistently across other drug-target pairs

We hypothesized that the Benefit Score would align with tissue lineage, such that model-derived scores recover tissue-specific drug sensitivities consistent with known biology and clinical evidence. This would demonstrate that the score not only predicts response but also reflects established treatment patterns. In addition, we tested whether FORGE maintains predictive accuracy on both dependency and IC50 tasks across multiple drug-target pairs, to evaluate the robustness of its multitask design. Finally, we examined whether FORGE can reproduce mechanistic insights for targets beyond EGFR–erlotinib, since this would show that the framework generalizes to uncover interpretable biology across diverse contexts.

We first compared the benefit scores derived from the ten drug-target pairs, revealing lineage-specific patterns (Fig. 7a). These observed enrichments are best interpreted as potential repositioning hypotheses, suggesting lineage-specific contexts that warrant further preclinical or clinical investigation. By integrating Benefit Score stratification with differential expression and enrichment analysis, FORGE provides not only statistical robustness but also biologically grounded interpretability, positioning it as a framework for biomarker discovery and the rational exploration of targeted treatment strategies.

Discussion

Our study describes an implementation of joint-matrix factorization called FORGE, and its critical evaluation by validating on independent datasets. We also demonstrated the applicability of FORGE in understanding therapeutic responses of the EGFR-erlotinib pair, and in modeling multiple other drug-target pairs. Our implementation merits in using a simple but effective matrix factorization method to jointly learn two related biological variables - the gene’s dependency and the drug’s IC50. Using these jointly learned embeddings, we derived a quantifiable score, the Benefit Score, which can be used in predicting prognosis for targeted therapies in cancer. Through this approach, we successfully bridged pharmacogenomics and functional genomics in a single framework that can be used for personalized therapy design. Our joint model, in particular, identified key genetic signatures that can drive therapy responses. Our model formulation involved using numerous publicly available resources - the DepMap and the CREAMMIST databases, which provide high-quality cell-line level influences of gene knockouts and the pharmacodynamics of numerous drugs, either approved or under active research.

Classifying patients into high and low responders remains a key goal in cancer research and facilitates the design of personalized therapies. Earlier methods predominantly relied on multiple neural network architectures, such as graph neural networks (CDRscan)10 and autoencoders (DeepDR)12 to predict drug responses using various DL architectures. These models often integrated various omics datasets, and even included drug structure information in training78. However, very few approaches utilized transcriptomic data78, and DL-based models are generally compute-intensive and generate black-box predictions that rarely help in understanding the biological mechanisms driving model outputs. On the other hand, simple linear models are effective in interpreting perturbation effects in single-cell data79,80. In direct comparison with Precily, a deep neural network-based model, FORGE demonstrated comparable performance while providing substantially greater explainability, enabling biological insight beyond predictive accuracy.

FORGE’s validation using multiple independent datasets shows the model both stratifies samples meaningfully and offers interpretable mechanistic signals. In the DepMap-CREAMMIST cohort, FORGE identified compact clusters of sensitive cell lines (notably HNSCC and ESCC) that align with established EGFR biology. The sparsity-inducer in the W matrix concentrates influence on a small set of genes, producing gene-influence maps that reduce noise and are straightforward to interpret. Put simply, FORGE keeps a balance between accuracy and transparency: it achieves competitive prediction while giving clear, biologically sensible explanations for those predictions. We further demonstrated portability by testing FORGE’s benefit scores on two different biological models - the Tahoe-100M data representing cell-line perturbation effects at the single-cell level, while the PDX dataset contains baseline measurements of tumors in mouse models. Applying the learned gene weights to these external datasets showed a biologically meaningful signal. In Tahoe-100M single-cell perturbations, higher Benefit Scores were inversely associated with IC50 values, while in PDX xenografts, elevated scores corresponded to larger treatment-induced tumor regression. Our main objective for validating with the PDX and the Tahoe-100M datasets was to demonstrate the generalizability of FORGE to multiple variants of transcriptomes - from bulk expression profiles of cell lines to xenograft models and finally pseudo-bulk aggregates. Our results clearly indicate that FORGE is robust to various models of bulk expression datasets. While we did not apply FORGE in analyzing single-cell data, in any future studies, it would be appropriate to aggregate such datasets as pseudo-bulk profiles at the clonal (‘cluster’) level or cell type level. Transforming sparse single-cell data into pseudo-bulk profiles has the added advantage of improving downstream analysis81. In a recent study, we demonstrated that such workflows can be used to rank clonal aggressiveness in tumors by employing ‘phenotype algebra’82. This approach also remains practical as routine high-throughput cancer drug screening datasets are generated from bulk assays, and models are consequently trained on bulk expression data. These cross-system validations show that FORGE’s embeddings and Benefit Score are not idiosyncratic to a single dataset but capture reproducible, treatment-relevant biology across cell lines, single-cell perturbations, and in vivo models. We even demonstrated that the Benefit Scores are not meaningful if an unrelated gene (A1CF) has been paired with a drug (see Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. 9).

Biologically, FORGE identifies sensitizers and resistance factors that are consistent with known EGFR mechanisms (for example, PTPN6 and SYK as sensitizers; AXL and NNMT as resistance-associated). Unlike a simple differential expression analysis, FORGE aligns gene effects across dependency and drug response simultaneously, so the prioritized genes are more likely to be mechanistic drivers of sensitivity or resistance rather than context-specific expression changes. Protein-protein interaction analysis further supported these findings, showing that highly influential FORGE genes are centrally positioned within known signaling networks, such as EGFR. This suggests that the model captures coordinated biological programs, rather than isolated gene effects. By a detailed comparison of the model’s gene influence scores with gene expression changes and known biological pathways, FORGE helped in uncovering significant biological signals that drive tumor susceptibility to a particular drug. FORGE also recovers lineage-specific vulnerabilities (e.g., NAMPT in hematologic contexts, BRAF in skin) and flags unexpected enrichments (such as strong Uprosertib/AKT1 signal in prostate cell lines) that serve as testable repositioning hypotheses. In short, FORGE is both a discovery engine and a hypothesis generator for translational follow-up. Conceptually, the benefit of the joint embedding is straightforward: by forcing the same latent factors to explain both dependency and IC50, the model reduces task-specific noise and aligns pharmacologic readouts with functional genetic evidence. That alignment yields coherent molecular drivers that support clinically meaningful stratification strategies — i.e., groups defined by shared latent programs that map to pathway biology and likely treatment benefit.

Limitations remain. The linear factorization used in FORGE may miss highly nonlinear gene–drug interactions, and the current design requires training a model per drug–target pair (a trade-off we accept because per-target models are more useful for personalized therapy). External validations, such as the PDX set, were hampered by missing genes and small sample sizes, reducing statistical power. The Benefit Score depends on accurate baseline expression and can be affected by residual batch effects if not handled during the preprocessing steps. Finally, the present implementation targets single drugs or small drug sets; extending FORGE to poly-therapy or to incorporate other omics or drug-structure features is a promising direction but raises the question of whether the core linear framework should be modified or the input matrix G simply augmented.

In summary, FORGE is a simple yet effective framework that provides a tractable approach to jointly link CRISPR-based gene dependency and IC50 drug response. It combines predictive performance with mechanistic interpretability, producing Benefit Scores that function as practical, transferable biomarkers and highlighting both established biological mechanisms and potential repositioning hypotheses. FORGE therefore offers a generalizable, mechanism-aware tool for biomarker discovery and the rational stratification of targeted therapies.

Methods

Overview of the datasets

Below, we list key datasets used in our study. The DepMap and the CREAMMIST datasets were utilized for model training, while model validation was performed using Tahoe-100M and PDX datasets.

DepMap datasets

We utilized the ’Public 24Q4’ version of the DepMap (https://depmap.org/portal/) database, which contains gene expression (’omicsExpressionRNASeQCGeneCountProfile.csv’) profiles of 53,970 genes among 1690 cancer cell lines. Of these, 1178 cell lines were subjected to CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockouts targeting 17,917 genes. The effects of gene perturbation are estimated as gene dependency values (’CRISPRGeneDependency.csv’) that range between 0 (no effect) and 1 (highly dependent).

CREAMMIST database

The CREAMMIST (https://creammist.mtms.dev/drug/select/) database contains log10-scaled half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (hereafter referred as IC50) of 1,132 drugs tested in 1,325 cancer cell lines belonging to 31 different cancer types. From this list of drugs, we included the drugs annotated in both OpenTargets (https://www.opentargets.org/) and TTD (https://idrblab.net/ttd/) databases, and have at least one known molecular target annotated in the CREAMMIST database, leaving a final count of 184 drugs for subsequent analysis.

Tahoe 100M scRNAseq

The Arc Institute (https://arcinstitute.org/news/arc-vevo) recently published a large-scale single-cell perturbation dataset comprising of 100M cells belonging to 49 cancer and one normal (HERT-hPNE) cell-lines83. The atlas was derived through their Mosaic platform and measured the effects of 1100 small molecule perturbations at varying concentrations. Each plate (the batch variable) included a specific concentration of the perturbagen and DMSO (as a control), and was sequenced to measure the expression levels of 62,710 transcripts.

Patient-derived Xenografts (PDX)

This dataset is composed of baseline gene expression (FPKM) measurements of ~1000 PDX samples belonging to six cancer types, including the non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)65. The tumors are then treated using a variety of anti-cancer drugs, among which 25 samples were treated with erlotinib. Treatment responses were recorded as percentage change in tumor volumes (%TVChange) when compared with baseline (T = 0) across different time points.

Preprocessing

Preprocessing was carried out separately for training and each of the validation datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1) and included voom normalization of raw gene counts and subsequent scaling using Z-scores. The DepMap gene expression and dependency data, and the IC50 data from CREAMMIST databases, together form the training datasets, while model validation was performed using the PDX and the Tahoe-100M datasets. We initially focused on the EGFR-erlotinib pair for FORGE implementation, and the pre-processing steps described below were specific to this pair. We then expanded FORGE on other drug-target pairs using the same pre-processing schema.

We initially filtered the common cell lines in all three training datasets, leaving a total count of 686 cell-lines for downstream analysis. The cancer types and tissue sources for these cell lines are presented in the (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). The gene expression dataset is further filtered to include genes (N=16,289) present in Enrichr-KG (https://maayanlab.cloud/enrichr-kg), a knowledge-graph-based gene set enrichment tool. Subsequently, the gene expression was normalized using Limma’s Voom normalization with only the intercept in the design formula, and using default parameters, yielding log-normalized count matrices.

Feature selection and generating input matrices

From the above datasets, the data was split into train-test fractions in an 80:20 ratio. Given the large number of input features, we identified the top 200 genes (hereafter referred to as highly-correlated genes or HCGs) with the highest Spearman correlation with either of the output variables in the training data alone. We also included the target gene if it is not within the HCG set. This subset of gene expression values scaled through Z-transformations constituted the training feature set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{G}}}}$$\end{document}G. Both gene dependency and drug IC 50 values were centered using the respective means of the training data and represented the output variables (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{D}}}}$$\end{document}D and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{I}}}}$$\end{document}I, respectively) for model training.

Tahoe 100M dataset

From the Tahoe-100M single-cell perturbation dataset, we selected plates 7, 8, and 9, corresponding to Erlotinib treatment at low (0.05 nM), medium (0.5 nM), and high (5.0 nM) concentrations, respectively. For each plate, cells treated with Erlotinib and matched DMSO controls were extracted and aggregated at the cell-line level to generate pseudo-bulk gene expression profiles. Only cell lines with paired DMSO- and drug-treated cells within the same plate were retained. Pseudo-bulk raw count matrices were normalized using the limma–voom, wherein counts were first scaled using TMM normalization, followed by voom transformation to obtain log2-CPM values with precision weights. A cell-means design matrix (~0 + Group) was constructed using a treatment–plate grouping factor (DMSO or Erlotinib, stratified by plate), with no additional covariates included, ensuring that treatment effects were not explicitly modeled during normalization. No batch correction was applied, as multidimensional scaling analyses of DMSO samples across plates revealed no detectable plate-specific batch effects. FORGE-derived gene weights were applied to baseline (DMSO-treated) pseudo-bulk expression profiles from plates 7, 8, and 9 to compute Benefit Scores for each cell-line. Scores were calculated independently for each plate and subsequently averaged across plates to obtain a per–cell-line estimate. These averaged Benefit Scores were then aligned with Erlotinib IC50 values from the CREAMMIST dataset for matched cell-lines (N = 686).

FORGE: Individual models

In the individual modeling framework, we considered a single prediction task at a time, either gene dependency (D) or drug response (I). Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\bf{G}}}}}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{n\times p}$$\end{document}G∈Rn×p denote the gene expression matrix, where n is the number of samples (cell-lines or PDX models) and p is the number of genes. The aim is to learn a mapping from the high-dimensional input space (p genes) into a latent embedding that explains either dependency profiles or drug sensitivity values.

For the gene dependency prediction task, the model takes the form 17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{{{{{\bf{D}}}}}}={{{{\bf{G}}}}}{{{{{\bf{W}}}}}}_{D}{{{{{\bf{h}}}}}}_{D},\,{{{{{\bf{W}}}}}}_{D}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{p\times k},\,{{{{{\bf{h}}}}}}_{D}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{k\times 1},$$\end{document}D^=GWDhD,WD∈Rp×k,hD∈Rk×1, where k is the latent dimensionality, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\boldsymbol{W}}}}}_{D}$$\end{document}WD represents the projection of gene expression features into the latent space, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}}_{D}$$\end{document}hD denotes the task-specific weights that combine these embeddings into the prediction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{{{{{\bf{D}}}}}}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{n\times 1}$$\end{document}D^∈Rn×1.

Loss function

The optimization objective for each individual model balances fidelity to the observed data with a sparsity-inducing regularizer on the weight matrix: 19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{{\mathcal{L}}}}}}_{D}({{{{{\bf{W}}}}}}_{D},{{{{{\bf{h}}}}}}_{D})\,=\,\parallel {{{{\bf{D}}}}}-{{{{\bf{G}}}}}{{{{{\bf{W}}}}}}_{D}{{{{{\bf{h}}}}}}_{D}{\parallel }_{F}^{2}\,+\,\lambda \parallel {{{{{\bf{W}}}}}}_{D}{\parallel }_{1},$$\end{document}LD(WD,hD)=∥D−GWDhD∥F2+λ∥WD∥1,20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{{\mathcal{L}}}}}}_{I}({{{{{\bf{W}}}}}}_{I},{{{{{\bf{h}}}}}}_{I})\,=\,\parallel {{{{\bf{I}}}}}-{{{{\bf{G}}}}}{{{{{\bf{W}}}}}}_{I}{{{{{\bf{h}}}}}}_{I}{\parallel }_{F}^{2}\,+\,\lambda \parallel {{{{{\bf{W}}}}}}_{I}{\parallel }_{1},$$\end{document}LI(WI,hI)=∥I−GWIhI∥F2+λ∥WI∥1, where ∥ ⋅ ∥F denotes the Frobenius norm, and ∥ ⋅ ∥1 denotes the element-wise ℓ1 norm. The first term enforces reconstruction accuracy (minimizing the squared error between observed and predicted values), while the second term encourages sparsity in the gene-to-latent projection matrix, thereby identifying a compact set of informative features. The regularization coefficient λ > 0 controls the trade-off between reconstruction accuracy and sparsity.

Interpretation of parameters

WD and WI capture the mapping from gene expression profiles to latent factors relevant to each task. - hD and hI represent task-specific linear coefficients that weight the latent factors for predicting dependency or IC50, respectively. - The sparsity regularizer λ∥W∥1 ensures interpretability by shrinking irrelevant gene weights toward zero.

Optimization

Each loss function (19) and (20) was minimized independently using stochastic gradient descent over a maximum of 500 epochs. Five independent runs with different random initializations were performed to account for stochastic variability. The latent dimensionality k was fixed to the value optimized in the joint model setting.

Evaluation metrics

The performance of the trained models was quantified by computing Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) to measure monotonic agreement, and mean squared error (MSE) to assess average predictive deviation. These metrics were averaged across the ten replicate runs, and the resulting distributions were reported for each learning task.

Multi-drug FORGE

To accommodate multiple drugs targeting the same molecular target, we extended the FORGE framework into a multi-output setting. To illustrate this framework, we considered two EGFR inhibitors, Erlotinib and Gefitinib, which exhibit substantial heterogeneity in IC50 distributions across cell lines. Ten replicate runs with different random initializations were conducted.

Evaluation

Model performance was evaluated on the DepMap dataset by computing (i) Spearman rank correlation coefficient (ρ) between predicted and observed IC50 values for each drug separately, and (ii) mean absolute error (MAE) across the n × d prediction matrix. This allowed us to quantify the ability of the shared latent structure to jointly explain drug responses that target the same pathway.

Multi-omics FORGE

For the multi-omics FORGE, model optimization was performed using an alternating minimization scheme. At each iteration, the task-specific heads were updated in closed form using ridge regression in the latent space, while the embedding matrices were optimized using stochastic gradient descent. To map the modality importance landscape, a systematic grid search was conducted over (α, β), with γ defined as 1 − (α + β). For each grid point, an inner hyperparameter optimization loop using Optuna (100 trials) was employed to identify the optimal learning rate, latent dimension, and regularization coefficients that minimized the weighted validation loss. The resulting relationship between modality weights and validation performance was used to determine gene expression as the optimal data configuration adopted in the final FORGE architecture (Supplementary note 1).

Benchmarking drug response predictions using Precily

Precily is a deep neural network-based pan-drug model that predicts drug response using baseline gene expression data and drug descriptors. Originally, two model frameworks were described for Precily - the gene expression profiles (the ’gene’ models) and the pathway enrichment scores (the ’PES’ models) computed from the expression profiles. The PES models highlighted the biological mechanisms influencing drug responses, and the cell fates were inferred successfully when tested on single-cell transcriptomic datasets. Our model, FORGE, too, is involved in learning drug responses based on plausible biological mechanisms, and therefore, the PES Precily (hereafter referred to as the ’Precily’) models were used as the benchmarks for comparing FORGE results. The initial published version of Precily used MSigDB.CP.v.6.1 for computing the pathway enrichment scores, and SMILESVec Python tool (https://github.com/hkmztrk/SMILESVecProteinRepresentation) for generating drug embeddings. Due to the non-availability of learned embeddings for the drugs and the MSigDB version updates, we recreated the input matrices (drug embeddings for the 186 drugs and the enrichment scores using the DepMap gene expression data), retrained Precily in a five-fold framework, and compared FORGE predictions against this retrained Precily. We also prevented any data leakage by excluding any cell line that was included in the test set during FORGE evaluations.

(a) Pathway enrichment score computations. Precily uses pathway enrichment scores as a proxy for the high-dimensional gene expression data. The voom-normalized gene expression data from the DepMap portal were used as the input matrix for the PES computations. These expression profiles are essentially log2(CPM + 1) scaled counts, and follow similar distributions of log2(TPM + 1) counts originally used in Precily training. We selected the C2 canonical pathways (total number of gene sets = 4023) from the Human MSigDB v2025.1.Hs (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) (accessed on 10 September 2025), and computed the pathway enrichment scores using the GSVA package in R (Bioconductor v3.22) (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/GSVA.html). From these 4023 pathway activity scores, we computed mean absolute deviation (MAD) for each gene set across all the cell lines, which were then log-transformed and standardized, followed by fitting a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM). The mean log-MAD of each component is inverse-transformed and ranked, and the component(s) with the highest mean log-MAD (N=1238, 30.8%) were retained as the model’s PES input features.

(b) Generating drug embeddings from the drug SMILES. From the 186 drugs that were common between the DepMap dependency assays and the CREAMMIST database, we retrieved the SMILES representations, and using the pretrained transformer model ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1 (seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1) in evaluation mode, we generated embeddings of size 768 by extracting the [CLS] token of the final hidden layer. We also included the 13 molecular descriptors from RDKit (Supplementary table 3) that include key physico-chemical features like molecular weight, structural complexity descriptors, etc. The final set of 781 (768+13) drug embeddings was then included as drug descriptors for the Precily model training.

The final input matrices were of dimensions 2019 (1238 + 781) features, and 95,407 rows, where each row corresponded to a unique drug-cell-line pair. This massive input dataset was split based on the train and test cell lines of the EGFR-Erlotinib’s FORGE model, and predictions for all the drugs were performed over the 137 test cell lines. This ensured strict separation between training and evaluation data and prevented information leakage across datasets.

Precily model architecture and hyper-parameter tuning

We implemented Precily in TensorFlow v2.20.0, a popular choice for deep-learning architectures in Python. Similarly, we used KerasTuner v1.4.8 for tuning the hyperparameters. We tuned for the number of hidden layers, neurons within each hidden layer, dropout rate, and learning rate (ranges described in Supplementary table 4) in a five-fold cross-validation strategy run for 30 epochs. We then inferred the best trial by minimizing the validation losses per fold. For each of the folds and their corresponding best hyper-parameter set, we retrained Precily by using the entire train set for 50 epochs.

Comparing Precily and FORGE performances

Once Precily training per fold has been completed for all the ten drugs, we evaluated the performance by computing the metrics used for FORGE evaluation (refer FORGE performance evaluations). For the comparisons, we aggregated the per-fold Precily IC50 predictions for each drug, followed by computing the Pearson correlations among predicted and actual IC50 values. These correlations were then compared with those generated during FORGE evaluations on the test data.

Differential gene expression analysis

For the differential expression analyses for the DepMap and the Tahoe 100M datasets, the samples were categorized into two groups (high-score and low-score) using the median Benefit Score of each dataset as cutoffs. This variable was the used as the main contrast, and we included cancer type or tissue type (whenever applicable) as one of the co-variates. For the DepMap dataset, differential expression analyses were carried out using ebayesR (from the limma R package) using the existing normalized counts, while for the Tahoe-100M pseudo-bulk counts, we used DESeq2 and specified the counts as ’poscounts’ in the estimateSizeFactors() step accounting for sparsity observed in the single-cell transcriptomes. The top 200 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on abs(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\log }_{2}(Foldchange)$$\end{document}log2(Foldchange) > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05.

Alternatively, for the DepMap dataset with 1034 cell lines that have both EGFR dependency and gene expression data, we performed additional DEG analysis. Based on the learned gene expression embeddings (Z = G W), we clustered the cell-lines using hierarchical clustering using pairwise Euclidean distances and Ward’s method, which minimizes within-cluster variance. Subsequently, we resolved clusters using cutreeHybrid from the dynamicTreeCut library in Python by setting a minClusterSize of 13 (minimum number of samples per cluster) and deepSplit of four (maximize the cluster split, ranges from 1 to 5). From the resulting clusters, we aggregated samples from the top three clusters with high benefit scores and EGFR dependency and low erlotinib IC50, and compared differential expression profiles with other samples.

The DEGs from each analysis were then used as separate input lists for the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using Enrichr-KG (https://maayanlab.cloud/enrichr-kg). We selected four databases to infer the gene functions in metabolism and disease - the GO Biological Process v2021, the WikiPathways v2021, the KEGG Pathway v2021 (for human), and the Reactome pathways v2022 databases. We limited the results to gene sets of sizes ranging between ten and 500 genes, at least ten genes from the input list were mapped to each set, and a gene was mapped to at least three different gene sets. All the enriched terms were filtered based on FDR q-value < 0.05, and we validated our findings through an extensive literature search.

Computing Steiner tree

From the STRINGdb v12.0 (https://string-db.org/), we retrieved the full human proteome interaction network (accessed on 21 January 2025), which consisted of 19622 nodes and around 6.86 M edges. From the total 201 genes identified as HCGs for either of the models, and 86 genes involved in the ERBB pathway (38 genes in case of Daporinad-NAMPT pair), we mapped 265 (and 424 genes for the Daporinad-NAMPT pair) genes using the STRINGdb’s aliases (available via the STRINGdb v12.0). Subsequently, we queried the STRINGdb via their API (method = ’interaction-partners’) to infer the interacting proteins. We ensured high-quality interactions by setting a minimum confidence score of 700 (high confidence interactions), and limited the results to 10 interacting neighbors. From this gene set, we computed the Steiner tree by randomly selecting 80% of the genes per iteration for 50 iterations, merging the subgraphs generated in each iteration. The resulting Steiner trees were analyzed using AnalyzeNetwork and STRING apps in Cytoscape v3.10.3.

Inferring key nodes using RWR algorithm

The Random-Walk-with-Restart (RWR) algorithm is a network propagation algorithm that ranks nodes by the number of times a node is visited when traversing a path from a source node. For the EGFR-Erlotinib pair, we considered EGFR as the source node, using a restart probability of 0.4 for 1 × 106 iterations and including edge weights during the path traversal. From the resulting RWR probabilities, we extracted the top 20 ranked genes and inferred their gene influence scores and/or log2-fold changes. We then analyzed these key genes in the context of their known biological functions and roles in cancer or drug responses using published literature. We followed a similar approach for the Daporinad-NAMPT pair by considering the NAMPT gene as the source node. Networks were visualized using Gephi v0.10 after computing smaller Steiner trees for the EGFR (Fig. 5b) and NAMPT (Fig. 5c) with the top 20 genes ranked by their RWR probabilities and top 40 genes ranked by their gene influence scores as terminal nodes.

Statistical analysis

We used the Benefit Scores for comparing therapy outcome variables by categorizing samples into high and low scoring samples. The final outcome variables used for assessing Benefit Scores are dataset-specific – correlation of IC50 values in the Tahoe-100M dataset and %TVChange in PDX datasets. For all the above analyses, P<0.05 was considered as the level of statistical significance, and the following hypotheses were tested using one-sided Kolmogrov-Smirnov or Mann-Whitney tests:

H0 (the null hypothesis): There is no difference of response variable among the two groups.

H1 (the alternate hypothesis): The %TVChange was higher among the samples with low Benefit Scores (based upon the dataset tested).

Statistical significance of performance differences between multi-task and single-task models for multiple drug-target pairs were assessed using a paired t-test on correlation between Dependency-Effect and IC50-Effect. Similarly, paired t-test was used to compare the Pearson correlations of nine drugs tested by FORGE and Precily.
