A systematic review of the impact of food and nutrition programs on child nutrition and household food security in Ethiopia
Authors: Kassahun Ayele, Meron Demisew, Habtamu Fekadu Gemede
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite Ethiopia’s agricultural potential and policy efforts, food insecurity and malnutrition remain major challenges, particularly among children. The Ethiopian government has launched programs to improve food security and nutrition in the country. However, the effectiveness of these initiatives in addressing child malnutrition and food insecurity has not been comprehensively reviewed yet.MethodsA systematic review following the PRISMA guidelines was conducted to evaluate the impact of food and nutrition programs on child nutrition and household food security in Ethiopia. A comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar identified relevant studies published between 2015 and March 2025. Fourteen studies focusing on various program types in the country were included.ResultsStudies have reported inconsistent results regarding the effectiveness of food and nutrition programs in improving child anthropometry and household food security outcomes. Combined programs positively affected child nutrition, including reduced stunting and wasting, whereas single programs had limited impact. Program effectiveness varies by geography, seasonality, and socioeconomic conditions. Multicomponent programs, women’s empowerment, and community engagement were critical to effectiveness.ConclusionThis review highlights the importance of multi-component interventions in enhancing child nutrition and food security. Policymakers should design context-specific interventions that address geographical, seasonal, and socioeconomic variation. Future programs should focus on long-term sustainability, maternal and child health education, improved access to nutritious foods, strengthening social safety nets, and integrating programs. Enhanced community engagement and further investigation into the long-term effects of these programs are crucial for their efficacy and resilience against climate change and economic challenges.Keywords: Child, Ethiopia, Food, Nutrition, Programs, Household, Food security

Background

Despite Ethiopia’s considerable agricultural potential and supportive policies, food and nutrition security remains a critical concern [1, 2]. Malnutrition and food insecurity primarily affect children, negatively impacting their growth and health [3, 4]. According to the Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey (2019), 75.8% of Ethiopian children live in households with insufficient food and 48.5% suffer from malnutrition. A significant number of children under five years are affected by stunting (37%), wasting (7%), and being underweight (21%) [5–7].

The factors contributing to childhood malnutrition and food insecurity include droughts, outdated agricultural practices, economic constraints, and conflict [8, 9]. In Ethiopia, recurring conflicts have caused displacement, disrupted food supply chains, and limited access to essential health and nutrition services, further worsening malnutrition and food insecurity [10]. This crisis results in limited access to diverse diets, inadequate maternal and child healthcare, and insufficient clean water and sanitation services [11].

Addressing these challenges requires comprehensive evidence-based interventions that integrate nutrition-sensitive and nutrition-specific approaches [12]. This research supports achieving sustainable development goals, specifically SDG 2, which aims to end hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture [6, 13, 14]. By evaluating food and nutrition programs in Ethiopia, this study identifies strategies to ensure that vulnerable populations have access to adequate nutrition. Given the high malnutrition rates despite policy initiatives, these findings can help strengthen existing programs.

This study addresses evidence gaps regarding intervention sustainability and supports the development of integrated approaches crucial for long-term food security. To address food insecurity and malnutrition, the Government of Ethiopia, in collaboration with international partners, has implemented multi-sectoral programs, including the National Nutrition Program, the Productive Safety Net Program (PSNP), and the Seqota Declaration [15].

The PSNP provides predictable food or cash transfers (often via public works or direct support) to vulnerable households to protect their consumption and build resilience [16]. Evidence shows that PSNP participation reduces food insecurity, shortening the hungry season, and can support asset accumulation, including livestock holdings, especially when linked with complementary livelihood programs [17]. The National Nutrition Program (NNP) aims to improve maternal and child nutrition through community-based services, dietary diversification, and micronutrient supplementation across the first 1,000 days and beyond [18]. The Seqota Declaration commits Ethiopia to ending child undernutrition by 2030 through coordinated multisectoral actions spanning agriculture, WASH, health, and social protection [19]. Additionally, the National Food and Nutrition Policy (NFNP) provides strategic guidelines to coordinate efforts across sectors to ensure sustainable improvements in food security and nutrition [20–22].

Collectively, these programs are designed to improve food availability, accessibility, and utilization, especially for vulnerable populations within a multisectoral food-systems framework [17]. However, despite these initiatives, malnutrition rates remain high, highlighting the need for a thorough evaluation of their effectiveness. Assessing the impact of food and nutrition programs is crucial for understanding their success in addressing malnutrition and food insecurity in children. Such evaluations offer insights into program achievements, limitations, and areas that need improvement.

The purpose of this review includes measuring program effectiveness, identifying barriers to successful implementation, informing policymakers about best practices, and promoting sustainable solutions by examining long-term sustainability and scalability in the face of climate change, economic instability and population growth. Despite ongoing research on food security and nutrition in Ethiopia, there are still significant gaps. Many studies have focused on individual interventions rather than assessing their combined effects on the same outcome.

There is a lack of longitudinal research on the sustainability of these programs and their resilience to external shocks such as climate variability and economic fluctuations. However, many evaluations emphasize broad food security measures and consumption smoothing, with comparatively less analysis of dietary diversity or micronutrient intake, and limited attention to marginalized groups, particularly rural households in conflict-affected areas. Addressing these research gaps through a systematic review is essential for generating comprehensive evidence-based recommendations to enhance food and nutrition interventions.

This review synthesizes evidence from multi-component interventions and longitudinal outcomes, identifies best practices across various programs, and highlights key areas for policy and program improvements. It aims to assess the effectiveness of key food and nutrition programs in reducing malnutrition and enhancing food security in Ethiopia. Additionally, the review identifies gaps in the existing research and provides policy recommendations to improve program implementation and impact. This study plays a crucial role in guiding policymakers, researchers, and development partners, thereby contributing to the formulation of more effective strategies for combating malnutrition and food insecurity in Ethiopia.

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies from multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The search was designed to capture a wide range of studies that examined the impact of food and nutrition programs on child nutrition and household food security in Ethiopia. A combination of keywords was used, including “food and nutrition programs,” “child nutrition,” “household food security,” “Ethiopia,” “food insecurity,” “nutrition interventions,” “malnutrition,” “food security programs,” and “nutrition education.” Both published peer-reviewed articles and gray literature were considered for inclusion. Studies published between 2015 and March 2025 were considered for inclusion. The latest searches were conducted on March 15, 2025, to ensure that no further relevant studies were overlooked. This review was conducted in accordance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [23].

Study selection

Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies based on predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles that met the initial inclusion criteria were assessed for relevance [24]. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved by a third independent reviewer. The study selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram to ensure transparency and replicability. A total of 511 records were identified through database searches and other sources. After removing duplicates, 126 records remained for further screening. Of these, 83 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Finally, 14 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Data extraction and sensitivity analysis

Data were extracted using a standardized extraction form to capture the key study characteristics, intervention details, outcome measures, and results. Two reviewers independently extracted the data, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The extracted data were systematically organized to facilitate the synthesis and analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings by excluding studies with high risk of bias. Subgroup analyses were performed based on geographical location, program type, and socioeconomic status to explore potential variations in program effectiveness.

Quality assessment and risk of bias evaluation

The quality of the included studies was assessed using appropriate tools based on the study design. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool was used for randomized controlled trials, whereas the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was used for observational studies [25]. The studies were categorized as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias, and the findings were interpreted accordingly to ensure reliability. Owing to the narrative synthesis approach, a formal publication bias assessment using funnel plots or Egger’s test was not conducted. Efforts were made to incorporate a diverse array of studies, including gray literature, to reduce selection bias.

Data synthesis and analysis

A narrative synthesis was conducted for all the included studies, summarizing the key findings related to the program’s effectiveness. Data were systematically compared across studies to identify common themes, patterns and variations in intervention outcomes. The quantitative results were presented descriptively, without statistical pooling or meta-analysis.

Results

Characteristics of included studies on impacts of food and nutrition programs on child nutrition and household food security

Fourteen studies were included in this systematic review, comprising five quasi-experimental studies, four cluster randomized trials, three observational studies, one systematic review, and one randomized controlled trial. These studies assessed various food and nutrition programs implemented across different regions of Ethiopia. The study population included children under the age of five years, mothers, pregnant women, and households. Programs combining components, such as BCC with food vouchers, showed consistent positive effects on child nutrition and household food security. Research focusing solely on the PSNP has yielded mixed outcomes; while some studies indicate improvements in food security, the impact on child nutrition remains unclear. Incorporating WASH-focused interventions leads to greater dietary diversity and reduced child malnutrition. There are inconsistent results on the effectiveness of programs: some of the interventions are effective in improving children’s and women’s dietary diversity, but some studies are insignificant in terms of nutritional and dietary outcomes (Table 1).

Discussion

The impact of food and nutrition interventions on child nutrition and household food security has been widely studied, with varying outcomes. Although many programs have demonstrated positive effects, some have reported mixed or insignificant results. These variations highlight the complexity of nutrition-sensitive interventions and the numerous factors that influence their success.

The impact of interventions on child nutrition, particularly stunting, wasting, and dietary diversity, has shown mixed but largely positive results [38, 39]. Five studies reported a reduction in stunting after implementation, suggesting that long-term interventions addressing multiple determinants of malnutrition can be effective. However, two studies found no significant improvements, indicating that some programs may not have been implemented effectively or may have lacked essential components, such as maternal education, sustained follow-up, or sufficient dietary support. Wasting, an indicator of acute malnutrition, was reduced in three of the studies. This suggests that targeted interventions, such as those incorporating supplementary feeding programs, can effectively address short-term nutritional deficiencies [40]. However, the fact that wasting is not universally reduced underscores the need for interventions that combine preventive and therapeutic strategies.

One of the most consistently observed improvements was in the dietary diversity. Improved dietary diversity has significant implications for child health, as it reflects access to a variety of micronutrient-rich foods. For instance, a review indicated a substantial increase in dietary diversity among children (from 12% to 34%) and women (from 2% to 16%) following the implementation of livelihood support and a WASH-focused social and behavior change communication (SBCC) program [26]. This indicates that integrating food and nutrition programs with broader health and hygiene initiatives can yield tangible improvements [41]. The sustainable undernutrition reduction (SUR) program contributes to better dietary diversity, further emphasizing the importance of community-based nutrition education and livelihood programs [37, 42].

Household food security interventions have produced mixed results, highlighting the variability in program effectiveness based on implementation strategies, geographical factors, and household characteristics. Four studies reported significant improvements with positive changes in meal frequency and dietary diversity. This suggests that programs focusing on both food availability and accessibility can enhance household food security [36, 43]. The Productive Safety Net Program (PSNP), one of Ethiopia’s largest social protection initiatives, has been a key intervention in addressing food insecurity. The review found that PSNP implementation led to significant improvements in household food security [34, 37, 44]. These improvements were attributed to increased access to food resources, enhanced income-generating opportunities, and strengthened resilience to seasonal food shortages. However, one study reported no significant improvements in food security following program implementation [29]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in program design, local socioeconomic conditions, or external factors such as climate variability and fluctuating markets. This highlights the need for context-specific adaptations and complementary interventions to maximize the effectiveness of the program.

The effectiveness of food and nutrition programs is influenced by several factors, including the nature of the intervention, program integration, community involvement, and external conditions [45–47]. Single interventions, such as the PSNP, have shown inconsistent results. In this review, improvements in weight-for-height Z-scores after the PSNP showed no significant changes [28, 30]. This suggests that a single intervention may not be sufficient to address the multifaceted causes of malnutrition. In contrast, combined interventions demonstrated more consistent positive effects. Integrating behavior change communication (BCC) with food vouchers has been shown to improve child feeding practices and reduce chronic undernutrition [31, 35]. This indicates that comprehensive multisectoral approaches are more effective than standalone interventions [48, 49]. These approaches involve collaboration among multiple sectors, including the government, private sector, civil society, and community organizations, to create integrated solutions that consider the interconnectedness of different issues. For instance, in public health, tackling obesity might require not only healthcare interventions but also collaboration with the food industry to improve nutritional standards, urban planning to create walkable environments, and educational campaigns to raise awareness of healthy lifestyles.

Water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions play a crucial role in enhancing nutritional outcomes [50, 51]. Programs incorporating WASH elements have led to better nutritional improvements [26, 27]. This is likely due to reduced exposure to infections, improved food safety, and enhanced absorption of nutrients among children. Similarly, the role of women in nutritional interventions cannot be overlooked. Maternal education and labor supply have been linked to better program outcomes [28]. Women empowered by knowledge and economic opportunities are more likely to make informed nutritional choices for their children, leading to better health and food security.

High levels of community engagement have also been associated with improved outcomes. Communities actively participate in program implementation and decision-making, and the effectiveness of nutritional interventions has improved [33]. Community buy-ins ensure program sustainability, better adherence to interventions, and increased knowledge transfer [52]. Similarly, programs that align with ongoing national or local initiatives tend to have higher participation rates and yield cumulative benefits. Integrated programs can leverage existing infrastructure, reduce duplication of efforts, and enhance the efficiency of resource allocation. Seasonal fluctuations in food availability have been shown to significantly affect the success of interventions [53, 54]. Despite ongoing interventions, food insecurity worsens during the lean season, affecting nutritional outcomes [32]. Programs must incorporate seasonal considerations, such as food storage solutions and income diversification strategies, to mitigate these challenges.

Despite the valuable insights provided by these studies, several limitations must be acknowledged. The diverse nature of these interventions makes direct comparisons difficult. Variability in program design, implementation strategies, and targeted populations has contributed to these inconsistent findings. Many studies lack long-term follow-up, making it challenging to assess the sustained impact of interventions. Nutritional improvements require continuous monitoring to determine whether the benefits persist beyond the program period. The effectiveness of the interventions varied according to geographic location, socioeconomic conditions, and local cultural practices, highlighting the need for regionally tailored approaches rather than one-size-fits-all solutions. Additionally, some studies failed to provide detailed assessments of nutritional outcomes, thereby reducing the comprehensiveness of their findings. Improved data collection and standardized reporting methods are essential for a more accurate evaluation of the program’s impact.

Food and nutrition programs impact child nutrition and household food security depending on program design, integration with other interventions, and socioeconomic conditions. Although many interventions have shown positive effects, inconsistencies highlight the need for multisectoral approaches and context-specific adaptations. Future research should address program sustainability, implementation strategies and nutrition-sensitive approaches. This systematic review had several strengths, including a comprehensive and systematic search strategy across multiple databases, clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and adherence to the PRISMA guidelines to ensure methodological rigor. This review also critically appraises and synthesizes recent relevant studies, providing a thorough understanding of the evidence on the topic. Furthermore, efforts were made by multiple reviewers attempted to minimize bias through independent screening and data extraction.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, although protocol registration is crucial for enhancing transparency and reducing bias in systematic reviews, the protocol for this study was not registered because of time constraints. Nevertheless, the review was conducted with meticulous adherence to the PRISMA guidelines, with all methods clearly delineated prior to data extraction and analysis to ensure methodological rigor. Second, a meta-analysis was not feasible because of the substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. Variations in study design, population characteristics, intervention types, and outcome measures rendered statistical pooling inappropriate for this analysis. A narrative synthesis was employed to summarize and interpret the findings. Third, reliance on published literature may have introduced publication bias, as studies with positive or significant results are more likely to be published. Furthermore, most studies focused on short-term evaluations, limiting the ability to draw conclusions regarding the long-term sustainability and impact of the programs reviewed. Finally, regional differences and variations in program implementation may affect the generalizability of the findings across Ethiopia. While the review provides valuable insights into national program effectiveness, local-level dynamics and outcomes may vary significantly and warrant further investigation in future studies.

Conclusion

In Ethiopia, food and nutrition programs have demonstrated varying effectiveness. Integrated approaches combining behavior change communication with food assistance or WASH components have improved children’s nutrition and household food security. Although the Productive Safety Net Program remains pivotal, its impact varies across studies, requiring context-specific adaptations. Programs should adopt multicomponent strategies and consider geographical, seasonal, and socioeconomic variations. Future interventions should focus on sustainability, continuous monitoring, maternal and child health education, access to nutritious food and stronger social safety nets. Women’s empowerment and community engagement significantly enhanced nutritional outcomes. Longitudinal and context-specific research is needed to understand the long-term effects, especially in rural and conflict-affected areas. This will ensure that interventions remain resilient to climate change, economic instability, and evolving nutritional needs, ultimately improving Ethiopia’s progress in addressing malnutrition and food insecurity in the long term.
