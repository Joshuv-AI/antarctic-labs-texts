rDNAcaller: a fast and robust pipeline to call ribosomal DNA variants
Authors: Jose Miguel Ramirez, Winona Oliveros, Raquel García-Pérez, Alba Jimenez-Lupiañez, Aisha Shah, Pablo Perez-Cano, Fairlie Reese, Miguel Vazquez, Marta Melé
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRibosomal DNA (rDNA) is arranged in tandem repeats in the human genome. Extensive genetic and copy number variation in rDNA has been reported both within and between individuals, contributing to phenotypic diversity. However, traditional variant callers, designed for diploid regions, have not been systematically benchmarked for analyzing rDNA. We developed a customized simulator that replicates real intra- and inter-individual rDNA variation to benchmark the performance of commonly used variant callers, including GATK, Mutect2, and Lofreq*. Additionally, we optimized the preprocessing and mapping steps to significantly improve accuracy. Based on these optimizations, we introduce rDNAcaller, a novel pipeline for accurate rDNA variant detection using short-read whole-genome sequencing that achieves 94% precision on experimental data. Applying our pipeline to data from the 1000 Genomes Project, we identify 5607 novel rDNA variant positions across human populations, with African individuals showing the highest number of variants. Overall, rDNAcaller is a robust and versatile tool for analyzing rDNA variation, addressing the limitations of existing methods in handling high ploidies. By enabling accurate detection of rDNA variants, it facilitates deeper exploration of rDNA’s role in phenotypic diversity, supporting future genomic studies and broadening our understanding of rDNA biology in health and disease.

Introduction

The ribosome is the cellular machinery responsible for protein synthesis. It is composed of ribosomal proteins and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs). In humans, the 5S rRNA is encoded in chromosome 1. The 18S, 5.8S, and 28S rRNAs are encoded together by the 45S ribosomal DNA (rDNA), which is organized in tandem repeats on the short arms of human acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, and 22) (Fig. 1A) [1, 2]. The total number of these rDNA repeats, known as rDNA copy number (CN), varies widely among individuals, ranging from 100 to 600 copies [3, 4].

Notably, the rDNA copies are not identical, resulting in substantial inter- and intra-individual genetic variation [8, 5]. Different rDNA variants have been shown to have tissue-specific expression patterns [4, 9] and to be associated with specific phenotypes, such as body size [7]. However, previous efforts to call rDNA variants have lacked comprehensive benchmarking [4, 5, 9, 6] and therefore focus mostly on common variants. This means that the effect of population on rDNA variation and the association between rDNA CN and rDNA genetic variation remain underexplored [10]. This limitation is primarily due to the absence of datasets with known rDNA sequences that could be used as gold standards, since genome assemblies have historically excluded highly repetitive regions such as rDNA. The advent of long-read sequencing technologies has recently enabled the assembly of the first complete telomere-to-telomere human genome [8]. This assembly includes the rDNA regions, allowing for the first time the benchmark of a pipeline designed to call rDNA variants.

Here, we present rDNAcaller, a pipeline designed for accurate rDNA variant calling from short-read sequencing data. We first create a custom simulator to generate rDNA reads from samples with diverse rDNA CNs and genetic variants. We use this simulated dataset to benchmark three variant callers and optimize our pipeline. rDNAcaller integrates fast preprocessing and mapping strategies that exclude rDNA pseudogenes and employs the top-performing rDNA variant caller, achieving an F1 score of 96%. Additionally, we evaluate the pipeline’s performance using WGS from the same cell line of the T2T assembly, for which the rDNA sequences are assembled and achieve 94% accuracy. Finally, we apply rDNAcaller on the 1000 Genomes Project dataset, identifying thousands of novel rDNA variants across human populations.

Materials and methods

Preprocessing and mapping in rDNAcaller

The first step of the rDNAcaller pipeline is to extract candidate rDNA reads from the FASTQ files of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data using the function nucmer from MUMmer version 4.0.0 [11] (Fig. 1B). This function parses a FASTQ file and selects the reads that match a reference sequence contiguously for a minimum nucleotide length. The rDNAcaller pipeline selects only paired-end reads that both match at least 30 consecutive nucleotides to the rDNA reference sequence using the parameters “-maxmatch -l 30.” The rDNA reference sequence, named chromosome R (chrR), has a total length of ~45 kb, including the 13.4 kb transcribed region, the 45S rRNA [12]. This MUMmer step speeds up the mapping and variant calling while removing reads likely coming from rDNA pseudogenes (see the “Results” section).

We then map the selected candidate rDNA reads to a modified version of the CHM13-T2T genome [8], specifically designed to analyze rDNA [12]. This is a publicly available modified CHM13-T2T genome reference that contains all rDNA-like regions masked, and a single rDNA reference sequence appended as a separate chromosome, the chrR [12]. We map the candidate rDNA reads using bwa mem version 0.7.17 [13] and we use samtools version 1.19.2 [14] to keep the uniquely mapped and properly paired reads to the chrR rDNA reference sequence.

When analyzing whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS), we used bismark version 0.23.1 [15] instead of bwa mem, as bismark deals with the conversion of unmethylated Cs into Ts that WGBS performs. Similarly, to correctly deal with these conversions, instead of MUMmer, we kept the reads that contain at least 30 consecutive matches to the reference using the CIGAR information from the bam files.

rDNA sequence simulator

To benchmark available variant callers and include the top-performing one in rDNAcaller, we created an rDNA sequence simulator capable of closely replicating true rDNA variation (Fig. 1C). In each run, the simulator first creates a catalog of random variants on the transcribed unit of the chrR reference sequence [12]. The number of variants, along with all other parameters, can be specified by the user. Specifically, we generate 300 single nucleotide variants (SNVs), 50 insertions and 50 deletions of size ranging from 2 to 7 base pairs. Next, the simulator creates 50 distinct rDNA sequences, each containing 50 random variants from the catalog. From this catalog, it then generates 100 samples, each containing between 5 and 20 distinct rDNA sequences, with CNs ranging between 1 and 100 copies per distinct sequence. The total CN per sample is constrained between 100 and 600 copies. The output of this step is a FASTA file for each sample containing all generated rDNA sequences. In addition, the simulator adds all rDNA-like regions of the hg38 genome reference, which include pseudogenes described in [12] to each generated FASTA file. This step ensures that both rDNA copies and rDNA pseudogenes are simulated, reflecting real genomic conditions where the presence of pseudogenes may lead to increased false positives. Finally, we use NEAT [16] to simulate Illumina next-generation sequencing reads from the generated FASTA files, incorporating sequencing errors. Specifically, we simulate 151 bp paired-end reads at 30X coverage.

We ran 50 simulations with the parameters described above, selected to reflect intra-individual diversity observed in the CHM13 cell line. The intra-genomic frequencies (IGFs) of the simulated variants showed a mean of 0.16 (SD = 0.11), with values ranging from a minimum of 0.0017 to a maximum of 1. Then, we ran 10 simulations with the same parameters but changing two to create more diverse samples. In this second set of simulations we fixed the number of distinct rDNA sequences to 40, with CNs ranging between 1 and 15 copies per distinct sequence. The IGFs of the simulated variants showed a mean of 0.13 (SD = 0.06), with values ranging from a minimum of 0.0016 to a maximum of 0.52.

Benchmarking variant callers using simulated data

None of the publicly available variant callers is optimized for calling rDNA variants. This is due to rDNA having effectively high and individually variable CNs (high ploidies).

Using our simulations, we benchmarked three publicly available and widely used variant callers: HaplotypeCaller, Mutect2, and LoFreq*. HaplotypeCaller and Mutect2 are part of the GATK workflow version 4.5.0.0 [17] but HaplotypeCaller is optimized for germline mutations and allows the analysis of different ploidies, whereas Mutect2 is optimized for somatic mutations. LoFreq version 2.1.5 (Lofreq*) is a sensitive variant caller [18] used in previous studies to analyze rDNA variation [4, 6].

We run HaplotypeCaller from GATK [17] with different --sample-ploidy (2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40) and hard filtering of quality >1000 on our simulated data. The --sample-ploidy parameter in GATK refers to the number of chromosome copies expected in an individual sample, which is usually 2 in diploid loci but in rDNA, the true ploidy is highly variable between samples.

In addition, while changing the parameter --sample-ploidy, we also change --max-genotype-count, an internal parameter, accordingly. The default internal parameter in GATK for --max-alternate-alleles is 6 and for --max-genotype-count, 1024. The total number of potential genotypes that can be evaluated per position is defined by the unordered combination of alternative alleles. To determine the number of potential genotypes, we need to consider the sample ploidy (ploidy) and the number of alternative alleles (num_alleles). This counting problem is equivalent to enumerating the multisets of size ploidy that can be drawn from the different num_alleles. This is a “combinations with repetition” problem, whose solution is given by the binomial coefficient:

F(num_alleles, ploidy) = (num_alleles + ploidy -1)! / ((num_alleles -1)!*ploidy!)

When ploidy is 2, and num_alleles is 2, the number of potential genotypes is 3: (AA, Aa, aa). When ploidy is 3 and num_alleles is 2, the number of genotypes is 4: (AAA, AAa, Aaa, aaa). However, when ploidy is 20, the number of potential genotypes increases to 53130.

If --max-genotype-count is not updated when using ploidy 20, fewer alternative alleles will be evaluated. When ploidy is 20, and num_alleles is 3, the number of genotypes would be 231, and if num_alleles is 4, the number of genotypes is 1771. Since the default maximum is 1024, only a maximum of 3 alleles would be considered per position. Hence, we used as --max-genotype-count the result of the formula to keep constant num_alleles = 6, the default value. Importantly, if >6 alleles could have been evaluated, GATK provides a warning, a situation not observed in our data. When using ploidy 20, we executed HaplotypeCaller using the following parameters: “gatk --java-options "-Xmx115g -Xms100g" HaplotypeCaller --sample-ploidy 20 --maxgenotype-count 53130 --max-reads-per-alignment-start 0 --dont-use-soft-clippedbasestrue --min-base-quality-score 0” and “gatk --java-options "-Xmx3g -Xms3g" VariantFiltration --filter-expression "QUAL < 1000.0" --filter-name "QUAL1000"”.

Since the output vcf files from Haplotypecaller contain different alternative alleles per position, we splitted them to report one alternative allele per row using bcftools version 1.19 [19] with bcftools view --trim-alt-alleles and bcftools norm -m -any --old-rec-tag INFO --fasta-ref $chrR.

Next, we run joint genotyping with GATK, which leverages population-wide information when multiple samples are available. After executing HaplotypeCaller, instead of applying a hard filter, we use GenomicsDBImport and GenotypeGVCFs with the recommended hard filters according to the GATK best practices: for SNVs we use “QD < 2.0, QUAL < 30.0, SOR > 3.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < -12.5, ReadPosRankSum < -8.0″, and for insertions/deletions (INDELs), “QD < 2.0, QUAL < 30.0, FS > 200.0, ReadPosRankSum < -20.0″.

We then used Mutect2, a variant caller within GATK version 4.5.0.0 initially optimized for somatic mutations [17]. We used two different sets of parameters. The first is its default execution, which we call “Mutect2–default”, and the other one uses the parameter “--mitochondria-mode,” which is optimized for variant calling in mitochondrial DNA, a scenario with ploidies similar to rDNA, that we call “Mutect2–mitoch”.

Finally, we used LoFreq* version 2.1.5 [18] with two different sets of parameters. We first used its default parameters, which consist of running “lofreq indelqual --dindel” to add base- and INDEL-alignment qualities, and “lofreq call --call-indels” to include the calling of INDELs. We named this combination of parameters “Lofreq*–default”. We then ran “lofreq filter -v 2000″ on top of the variant calling to filter by coverage, a combination we named “Lofreq*–filtered”.

To compare the different variant callers we computed the following statistics:

Precision: True positives / (true positives + false positives)

Recall: True positives / (true positives + false negatives)

F1 score: 2 * Precision * recall / (precision + recall).

We included the novel preprocessing steps and the best-performing variant caller, HaplotypeCaller with ploidy 20, inside a pipeline named rDNAcaller, which is publicly available on github: https://github.com/Mele-Lab/rDNAcaller.

Since variant calling with short-read sequencing has been associated with low precision in highly repetitive regions [20], we analyzed the performance of HaplotypeCaller in rDNA repetitive regions and compared it to the rest. To identify repetitive regions, we used the union of Repeatmasker version 4.1.7 [21] with parameters “-species human -norna,” Tandem Repeat Finder version 4.09.1 [22] with parameters “2 7 7 80 10 50 500 -f -d -m,” and the homopolymers longer than 3 bases identified with homopolymerFinder from sarlacc version 1.0.0 [23].

rDNA variant extraction in the human CHM13-T2T assembly

The CHM13-T2T genome assembly contains all rDNA copies present in the CHM13 cell line. To evaluate the performance of rDNAcaller in experimental data from the CHM13 cell line, we needed to extract the rDNA variants from the genome assembly. We obtained the genomic coordinates of the rDNA copies from the genome annotation file and extracted them using the function getfasta from bedtools version 2.31.1 [24]. To obtain the number of unique rDNA copies, we performed a multiple sequence alignment using clustalo version 1.2.4 [25] with parameters “--full --percent-id --iterations 50.” We retained a single unit of each distinct morph (i.e. we kept one representative of every cluster of identical copies) based on the distance measures obtained in the multiple sequence alignment. To get the list of variant positions indexed according to chrR, we performed a multiple sequence alignment for the 24 unique rDNA copies and the 13 kb of the 45S in chrR with the same parameters as before. We extracted the rDNA variants (.vcf file format) from the multiple sequence alignment (.aln file format) using msa2vcf from https://github.com/pinbo/msa2snp. Since the INDELs are reported differently by msa2vcf and GATK, we only kept SNVs when comparing the performance of different preprocessing steps on samples from the CHM13 cell line.

rDNA variant calling in the 1000G

We used rDNAcaller in the 1000G project dataset [26]. We downloaded the CRAM files from 2504 individuals included in Phase 3 from their portal.

When alignment files (cram/bam/sam) with reads mapped to hg19 (GRCh37), hg38 (GRCh38), or hs1 (T2T-CHM13) are available, we can directly extract into a FASTQ file the reads mapped to regions of the reference genomes annotated as rDNA-like regions. These are all the regions of the reference genome where an rDNA read could map: 361 kb, 881 kb, and 12 mb for hg19, hg38, and hs1, respectively [12]. This is much faster than converting the full cram/bam/sam files into FASTQ.

After remapping to our reference, we excluded 10 individuals with <300 rDNA reads mapped to chrR.

Results

HaplotypeCaller with high ploidy is the best-performing rDNA variant caller

To build a pipeline for rDNA variant calling, we benchmarked different variant caller workflows using data generated with our rDNA simulator (Fig. 1C). None of the standard variant caller workflows are optimized to call variants in genes with very high CNs, let alone to do so in samples with variable CNs as is the case for rDNA genes. We benchmarked three variant callers: GATK’s HaplotypeCaller, a tool optimized for germline variants that has a ploidy parameter; Mutect2, a tool optimized for somatic and mitochondrial variants; and LoFreq*, a sensitive variant caller used previously to analyze rDNA variation. We also evaluated whether running Joint-Genotyping after running HaplotypeCaller improves performance, as GATK recommends to leverage population-wide information from multiple samples [17].

We benchmarked all three variant callers against 50 different sets of simulations. HaplotypeCaller has the highest precision (Fig. 2A) whereas LoFreq* and Mutect2 have the highest recall (Fig. 2B). Based on F1 score, HaplotypeCaller is the best-performing variant caller (Fig. 2C). Although GATK recommends Joint-Genotyping when having multiple samples, in this context, it achieves the lowest performance of all rDNA variant calling approaches. Increasing the ploidy parameter in HaplotypeCaller reaches a plateau at ploidy 20 (mean F1 score = 0.96). Thus, increasing the ploidy parameter further only increases computing power with no accuracy benefit. When comparing the estimated IGF with the simulated one, all approaches perform well, with Joint-Genotyping showing the highest root mean squared errors (Fig. 2D). Therefore, we considered GATK’s HaplotypeCaller with ploidy 20 as the best-performing rDNA variant caller. Notably, HaplotypeCaller outperforms previously used tools. Joint-Genotyping with ploidy 2, the tool used by Fan et al. [5] has a mean F1 score of 0.74, and Lofreq*–default, the tool used by Park et al. [4] has a mean F1 score of 0.83 (Fig. 2C).

A common practice in previous studies without benchmarking was to filter variants based on intragenomic frequency [7, 6]. We calculated the same metrics as before considering only the subset of variants with intragenomic frequency higher than 0.05. The IGF distribution for this subset of variants shows a mean of 0.18 (SD = 0.1), with values ranging from a minimum of 0.05 to a maximum of 1. To compute recall, now the false negatives correspond to the variants that show IGF >0.05 in the simulated data, but have not been found with IGF >0.05 in the variant calling. The precision is very high with all methods (Fig. 2D), while the recall is only high in HaplotypeCaller, Mutect–default, and Lofreq*–filtered (Fig. 2E). Importantly, HaplotypeCaller remains among the top-performing variant callers based on the highest F1 scores (Fig. 2F) and the lowest root mean squared errors (Fig. 2H).

We then performed another set of simulations with 40 as the number of distinct rDNA copies in all samples (see the “Materials and methods” section). Mutect2 reaches the best F1 scores but the worst root mean squared errors (Fig. 3). Increasing the ploidy from 20 to 40 in HaplotypeCaller does not significantly improve HaplotypeCaller performance but rather it reaches a plateau where only the computational burden increases. This is because despite setting ploidy 20, the minimum IGF that HaplotypeCaller with ploidy 20 can detect is lower than 0.05 (1/20). This is because the ploidy value in HaplotypeCaller is used as a prior, but variants with a lower frequency than 0.05 can be -and are- called. Specifically, HaplotypeCaller computes a likelihood value for all potential combinations of genotypes. Thus, a variant with frequency 0.01, even at ploidy 20, can be called because, given the error model, it is more likely that the variant is true than that it is an error.

Benchmark of variant callers with simulated data (ploidy 40). (A) Precision, (B) recall, (C) F1 score, and (D) root mean squared error of the different variant callers and parameters benchmarked using 10 simulations. HC = GATK’s HaplotypeCaller with changing ploidy parameter.

Variant calling using short-read sequencing has been associated with low precision in highly repetitive regions [20]. Since rDNA contains highly repetitive regions, we evaluated the performance of HaplotypeCaller in the subset of positions that fall in repetitive regions (Fig. 4A). The F1 score in repetitive regions (mean F1 score = 0.93; Fig. 4B) is slightly lower than when considering all positions (mean F1 score = 0.96; Fig. 4C). However, this decrease is mostly driven by a decrease in recall, and not a decrease in precision (Fig. 4B and C). This suggests that while the number of false negatives might be slightly higher in the rDNA repetitive regions, the number of false positives is not higher. Thus, we can confidently trust the variants called by HaplotypeCaller across all the rDNA positions.

The rDNAcaller preprocessing step speeds up the processing

We named our pipeline rDNAcaller, which includes selecting candidate rDNA reads, mapping to a customized reference genome, and performing rDNA variant calling with HaplotypeCaller ploidy 20 (Fig. 1B). We tested the performance of rDNAcaller on experimental data. Specifically, we used short-read WGS from the CHM13 human cell line for which the rDNA copies are present in the T2T genome assembly [8]. This cell line contains 219 rDNA copies, from which there are 24 unique rDNA sequences. These unique sequences are extremely similar to one another (Fig. 5).

Human T2T unique rDNA copies. (A) Percentage of similarity between the 24 unique rDNA copies in the CHM13 cell line.

We evaluated the speed and accuracy of our preprocessing steps relative to previously used approaches in the literature. We specifically evaluated two tasks: (i) the inclusion of the MUMmer step, which retrieves candidate rDNA reads with a 30 nucleotide exact match to chrR before performing variant calling; and (ii) the effect of mapping either to chrR alone or the whole genome with chrR. We tested all four different combinations of these tasks and found that incorporating MUMmer improves both runtime efficiency and variant calling performance (F1 score) regardless of whether the analysis begins from raw FASTQ files (Fig. 6A) or BAM files (Fig. 6C). Of note, while the precision when starting from FASTQ files or bam files is similar, the recall is lower when using bam files. This is because the average number of rDNA reads used for the variant calling when starting from FASTQ files is 197 105, but when starting from bam files is 88 124. This means that more than half of the rDNA reads are lost when starting from an already mapped bam file to the hg38 human reference, highlighting the importance of using FASTQ files when available. We also observe a worse performance in estimating IGF when starting from bam files (Fig. 6D) instead of FASTQ files (Fig. 6B).

In addition, we observed that when the MUMmer step is included, the choice between mapping to just chrR or to the whole reference genome has minimal impact on performance. However, omitting MUMmer, led to an increase of 15 false positives, all mapping to the 18S rRNA gene. These false positives potentially originate from pseudogene-derived reads, as 18S pseudogenes are known to be present outside the acrocentric chromosomes [3, 12]. This highlights the critical role of the MUMmer-based read retrieval step, which not only accelerates the pipeline runtime but also enhances performance by excluding reads likely originating from rDNA pseudogenes that result in false positives. The MUMmer step removes reads with <30 nucleotides matching to the reference. If instead, we removed only reads with <15 nucleotides matching, we would still retrieve 7 extra false positives, all coming from the 18S, highlighting the importance of using 30 as the minimum required matching length.

Notably, while the precision of rDNAcaller is still high in real data, the recall is lower than the one observed in simulations. This is due to ground truth CHM13 variants with IGF of 0.0045 in the CHM13 cell line that are missed by the pipeline. We executed Mutect2 with default parameters, as it was the tool yielding the second-best F1 score after HaplotypeCaller in the set of simulations closer to the cell line (Fig. 2C), to explore whether these variants would also be missed. All evaluated metrics show worse performance when using Mutect2 (Fig. 7A) instead of HaplotypeCaller (Fig. 6A). Mutect2 also misses the real variants with 0.0045 IGF while also detecting many false positives that decrease the precision. Notably, even when using tools like Mutect2, our preprocessing step incorporated in rDNAcaller that runs MUMmer is key, since the F1 score is increased from around 0.2 to 0.6 (Fig. 7A). Even running Mutect2 and only keeping called variants with an IGF of >0.01 does not outperform the F1 scores achieved by HaplotypeCaller (Fig. 7B). Of note, the only scenario where running MUMmer becomes optional is when considering only the variants with IGF higher than 0.05 (Fig. 7C).

rDNAcaller finds thousands of novel rDNA variants across human populations

A previous work found that rDNA variants were positioned nonrandomly in samples from the 1000 Genomes Project [26], with 28S rRNA harboring more variants than the 18S and the 5.8S rRNAs [5]. Since rDNAcaller has higher sensitivity than their method (JG-2, Fig. 2), we expect to find more variants in the same data. Our analysis identified a catalog of 7640 variant positions, comprising 12 520 variant alleles (Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 8A). We recover almost all variant positions previously detected using the same dataset [5]. However, as expected, we retrieved 6822 additional variant positions that were not reported in the previous study, representing an increase of one order of magnitude. Nearly half of our detected variant alleles (~46%) are individual-specific, whereas 3201 are shared across five or more individuals.

From the 12 520 variants identified in the 1000 Genomes Project dataset, 28% of them correspond to INDELs. While this percentage is higher than the 2.7% [4], 19.2%, [5] and 23% [9] previously reported using short reads, it is a lower number than the 87%–96% reported using long reads [9]. Importantly, the CHM13-T2T genome assembly [8] that used both short and long-reads reports a 56% of INDELs, suggesting that short-read-only analysis might underestimate the proportion, while long-read-only analysis might overestimate it. Overall, these numbers suggest that rDNAcaller might be the best method using short reads for estimating the correct percentage of INDELs, but the availability of more T2T assemblies will help to uncover the real proportion of INDELs.

We then explored the distribution of the rDNA variants across the different regions of the locus. The 5′ external transcribed spacer (5′ETS) has the highest proportion of variant positions, while the 18S has the lowest (Fig. 8A). We also computed the average nucleotide diversity score (see the “Materials and methods” section), which accounts for both the proportion of variant positions and their allele frequency. We found that the 18S and the 5.8S had the lowest nucleotide diversity values, consistent with these regions being more evolutionarily conserved than the spacer regions (Fig. 8C). However, the 28S showed similar values to the spacers.

Expansion segments are structurally flexible regions of rRNAs that extend beyond the ribosomal core in three-dimensional space. These regions are less conserved across species, and despite interacting with ribosome-associated proteins, their functional roles still remain unclear [27]. We found that the nucleotide diversity scores are significantly higher in expansion segments, consistent with previous studies [9], suggesting that these regions experience weaker evolutionary constraints compared to the core ribosomal regions (Fig. 8D). Importantly, the expansion segments with the highest mean nucleotide diversity scores are the ones from the 28S, driving the similar scores between the whole 28S and the spacers observed previously (Fig. 8C)

African individuals have more rDNA variants

We then wanted to characterize rDNA genetic diversity between human populations. African individuals showed the highest number of rDNA variants among all populations (Fig. 9A). Importantly, African individuals also show a higher rDNA CN (Fig. 9B), a variable with a positive correlation to the number of total rDNA variants across individuals (Fig. 9C).

We wondered whether the higher number of variants in Africans is only due to higher CN, or whether this variability is still higher when accounting for CN. To check this, we run a linear model adjusting for CN (see the “Materials and methods” section), and we see that African individuals still show a significantly higher number of rDNA variants (Fig. 9D). To further confirm this, we explored the association between population and nucleotide diversity score, a variable less associated with CN (Fig. 9E). In this case, after adjusting for CN, there is also a significant difference between populations, with African individuals showing the highest nucleotide diversity scores (Fig. 9F). These results show that the higher number of variants in African individuals is not only driven by the higher CN, but African individuals also show greater underlying sequence diversity at rDNA, consistent with their higher effective population sizes and recent evolutionary history [26, 29].

Finally, we tested whether rDNAcaller could be applied to methylation data. We evaluated the pipeline on 48 African WGBS samples from the 1000 Genomes Project [6]. Since WGBS converts unmethylated Cs into Ts, we used bismark version 0.23.1 [15] for mapping and retained only reads matching at least 30 consecutive nucleotides from the bam using in-house scripts. To avoid spurious signals, C to T and G to A SNVs, along with INDELs affected by these substitutions, were excluded from the comparison. We found a total of 37 variants that were called in both the WGS and WGBS, with a median root mean squared error of 0.15 between the IGF called in both datasets. Importantly, we detected the A-to-G substitution in position 60 of the 28S (position 7980 from the TSS), a variant previously linked to differences in rDNA methylation [6], highlighting the potential of rDNAcaller in WGBS for specific examples. However, while the median number of total rDNA reads mapped to chrR was high in both WGS (713 236) and WGBS (513 274), the median coverage per position used to call variants differed sharply between WGS (5945) and WGBS (150). This is because HaplotypeCaller performs local realignments but, unlike bismark, does not account for C to T conversions, discarding many reads. Consequently, quality scores are reduced, so many real variants might have been missed. For such cases, we provide an option in rDNAcaller to supply a VCF of known variants, allowing users to “force call” alleles when WGS and WGBS data from the same donor are available.

Discussion

We developed rDNAcaller, a fast and highly accurate pipeline for detecting rDNA variants in both simulated and experimental datasets. Unlike previous methods, rDNAcaller reduces the impact of pseudogenes and outperforms them in both accuracy and computational efficiency.

Applying rDNAcaller on the 1000 Genomes Project dataset, we successfully replicated previously identified rDNA variants while also expanding the variant catalog by an order of magnitude. This increase enhances our understanding of human genetic variation within rDNA regions, providing a more comprehensive and accurate representation of these loci. Additionally, we reveal a correlation between higher rDNA CN and increased number of rDNA variants, with African individuals showing the highest values for both. Importantly, after controlling for CN, African individuals still exhibit more rDNA variants and higher nucleotide diversity scores than other populations, consistent with the patterns observed in the rest of the genome, where African populations show greater sequence diversity [26, 29]. The high similarity among rDNA copies has been attributed to concerted evolution [30], where multiple copies of a repeat family are homogenized over time to maintain high sequence similarity within a species. However, the persistence of rDNA variants indicates an incomplete concerted evolution [10]. Our findings suggest that rDNA variants escaping homogenization reflect the same population-level diversity seen across the genome, with African populations showing the greatest variation. Importantly, a previous study [4] reporting fewer rDNA variants did not detect this trend, highlighting the value of the increased sensitivity of rDNAcaller.

We anticipate rDNAcaller to be useful in many biological contexts. In genome-wide association studies, rDNA variants have traditionally been excluded [2]. However, a recent study focused on a very small subset of rDNA variants has already shown important associations with body size [7]. Our pipeline will greatly increase the number of rDNA variants to be tested for associations with human traits in large biobanks. Additionally, rDNA CN is extremely affected by cancer [31], potentially giving rise to novel rDNA variants or altering the frequency of existing variants. By integrating rDNAcaller into cancer genomics, we might uncover new insights into the role of rDNA variation in tumorigenesis. Beyond cancer, our pipeline holds promise for studying a plethora of diseases caused by ribosomal dysfunction, named ribosomopathies [32]. Some of these conditions may originate from specific rDNA variants, and rDNAcaller provides a powerful tool for identifying such pathogenic variants. Notably, some of these variants may arise somatically, since rDNA might be a hotspot for somatic mosaicism, and rDNAcaller could be instrumental in identifying such variation across tissues and disease states. Finally, our pipeline could be adapted for use in other species, such as mice, and other repetitive genomic elements with architectures similar to rDNA, such as SINEs and LINEs, which also show intra- and inter-individual variation.

Overall, rDNAcaller represents a significant advancement in the study of human genetic diversity. By enabling more precise analysis of rDNA variants, this pipeline has the potential to enhance our understanding of genetic variation in rDNA and its implications for health and disease, ultimately bridging the gap between rDNA diversity and phenotypic traits in biomedical research.
