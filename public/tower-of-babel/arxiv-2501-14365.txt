Open quantum dynamics of Josephson charge pumps
Ankit Kumar, Luis Cort, Marcin Łobejko, Alejandro Jenkins, Michał Horodecki

Abstract
We investigate the macroscopic dynamics of Josephson charge pumps in the light of Alicki et al.’s theoretical description of the Josephson junction as an open quantum system described by a Markovian master equation. Once the electrostatic interaction between the terminals is taken into account via nonlinear capacitive terms in the Hamiltonian, we find that the resulting description of pumping is physically reasonable and in good qualitative agreement with experimental observations. We comment on how this approach relates to other theoretical treatments of quantum pumps based on time-dependent potentials or scattering amplitudes. We also highlight the significance of our results in the broader context of the dynamics of charge pumping by active systems.

.
The setup realized in

The authors reported a picoampere current through an unbiased nanowire embedded in a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID).
Due to an increasing ability to manipulate charges at the nanoscale, quantum pumps find numerous applications in the development of precision quantum standards

,
21
,
22

23

.
It is therefore necessary to improve our understanding of the dynamics of such devices that will underpin future quantum technologies

In this work we study such pump-type devices by adapting the theoretical treatment of the JJ as an open quantum system that was recently proposed by Alicki et al.
[
25
]

In particular, the Cooper pairs in the terminals of JJ are treated as a (macroscopic) bosonic open quantum system coupled to external electronic baths, with their dynamics governed by a Markovian Master Equation (MME)
[
25
]
.
These tools are utilized to investigate two different designs of the Josephson pump consisting of four terminals, each in contact with an external electronic bath.
A disequilibrium between two of the baths imposes a bias, and the magnetic field is a control parameter that triggers a SQUID effect, eventually leading to a charge pumping between the other two identical baths.
We also discuss the symmetries of the pumped current under the reversal of the imposed bias and the control magnetic field.
The subtle role of the nonlinear Coulomb repulsion between different terminals is also demonstrated.

II
Macroscopic model of Josephson junction

The two best known phenomenological descriptions of macroscopic Cooper pair dynamics in a JJ are those based on Feynman’s
macroscopic wave function
(MWF),
and the phenomenological
resistively and capacitively shunted junction
(RCSJ) model.

In Feynman’s approach, the Cooper-pair condensates in the terminals of JJ are described by a two-component MWF
[
26
, Secs. 21-29]
:

\displaystyle\vec{\psi}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{A}\\
\psi_{B}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{n_{A}}\,e^{i\phi_{A}}\\
\sqrt{n_{B}}\,e^{i\phi_{B}}\end{pmatrix},

(1)

where
n_{A}
and
n_{B}
are the Cooper pair populations in the two electrodes with phases
\phi_{A}
and
\phi_{B}
, respectively.
The dynamics is governed by the Schrödinger equation:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{t}\vec{\psi}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}\vec{\psi},\quad\hat{H}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
+e\Delta V & \hbar K \\
\hbar K & -e\Delta V
\endlx@physics@matrix),

(2)

where
\Delta V
is the voltage applied across the JJ, and
K
is the tunneling amplitude. The current flow due to the tunneling of Cooper pairs is governed by a unitary evolution, but its impact on the charges of two electrodes is neglected.
This is because the terminals of the JJ are connected to an external battery that can provide or consume electrons in order to keep the local density of the Cooper pairs approximately constant, such that the net charge density of the corresponding material (including the fixed positive charge
density of the underlying atomic lattice) remains close to zero
[
26
, Secs. 21-29]
.
As pointed out in
[
25
]
, this makes the JJ an open system. Accordingly, any model based on the unitary Schrödinger equation leaves out a key part of the dynamics, and cannot explain how the JJ acts as engine, capable of outputting work in the form of a self-oscillation that causes the emission of non-thermal phonons and photons
[
27
]
.

On the other hand, the RCSJ model accounts for the JJ characteristics in terms of an idealized junction described by a “tilted washboard” potential unbounded from below, in parallel with a resistor and a capacitor, see
[
28
, Section 6.3]
and
[
29
, Secs. 4.6, 8.5]
. It was shown by Alicki et al.
[
25
]
that an open-system description of the JJ can explain the main features of the characteristic without invoking any washboard potential and with the resistive and capacitive effects emerging from the physical parameters describing the coupling between the electrodes and the external electronic baths. This is done in terms of the dissipation rates in the MME.

II.1
The open system approach

The open system model it is a natural extension of the model proposed by Feynman, where we replace the macroscopic wave function with a
macroscopic density matrix
(MDM) whose evolution in time is described by a MME
[
25
]
. The terminals of the battery are modeled as external electron baths that are coupled to the JJ electrodes through the dissipator part of the master equation.
The condensate of Cooper pairs in a superconducting electrode occupies the ground state of a particular effective Hamiltonian, which can be represented by a single quantum harmonic oscillator. If
\hat{a}
denotes the annihilation operator, the number of Cooper pairs is given by
\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\rangle
, which can be increased or decreased by exchanging an electron pair with the external bath.
For a single electrode this situation is represented by an effective Hamiltonian
\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{\text{bath}}+\hat{V}_{\text{int}}
, where
\hat{H}
describes the system,
\hat{H}_{\text{bath}}=\sum_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k}\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{k}
describes the electron bath that acts as the reservoir of Cooper pairs, and
\hat{V}_{\text{int}}
is the system-bath coupling:

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\text{int}}=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\quantity( g_{kk'} \, \ha\, \h b_k^\dagger\h b_{k'}^\dagger+ \bar{g}_{kk'} \, \ha^\dagger\, \h b_k \h b_{k'} ).

(3)

The form factors
\quantity{g,\bar{g}}
contain the spectral densities of the electron bath. Note that the results of this work do not have any explicit dependence on their functional forms
[
25
]
.
When restricted to on-shell processes, this interaction corresponds to the Andreev scattering between the Cooper pairs in the superconductor and the electrons and holes in the normal metal
[
30
,
31
]
.
The reduced density matrix of the system is governed by a master equation:

\displaystyle\derivative{\h\rho}{t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]+\mathcal{D}(\hat{\rho}),

(4)

where
\mathcal{D}(\hat{\rho})
is the dissipator due to coupling with the external electron bath.
In common experimental setups the relevant timescales for Cooper pairs (Josephson frequency, tunneling amplitudes, damping rates) are of order
10^{-12}
s
[
32
]
.
For the electrons in metallic baths this timescale, as estimated with the Drude model, is usually of order
10^{-14}
to
10^{-15}
s.
Such large separation of time scales implies that the bath refreshes much faster than the JJ, so that the open system dynamics is very well described in the Markovian regime.
It was argued in Ref.
[
25
]

that in Josephson junctions the onsite energies are proportional to the (large) number of Cooper pairs in the electrodes, where the so-called local master equation is justified.
For multiple electrodes the local form of the dissipator is written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\hat{\rho})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\gamma^{\downarrow}_{j}\quantity(
[ \ha_j , \h\rho\ha^{\dagger}_j ]
+ [ \ha_j \h\rho, \ha^{\dagger}_j ]
)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\gamma^{\uparrow}_{j}\quantity(
[ \ha^{\dagger}_j , \h\rho\ha_j ] + [ \ha^{\dagger}_j \h\rho, \ha_j ]
),

(5)

where
\hat{a}_{j}
and
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}
are the creation and annihilation operators for the Cooper pair condensate in
j^{\text{th}}
electrode, which is put in contact with an electron bath having annihilation and creation rates
\gamma^{\downarrow}_{j}>\gamma^{\uparrow}_{j}
[see Section 1.3 in
[
33
]
for their explicit forms].

\displaystyle\int_{L}^{D}\!\mathbf{A}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{dl}=\int_{D}^{R}\!\mathbf{A}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{dl}=\int_{R}^{U}\!\mathbf{A}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{dl}=\int_{U}^{L}\!\mathbf{A}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{dl}=\frac{\Phi}{4}.

\displaystyle t_{jk}=\hbar K\exp(-i \frac{\delta\varphi}{(\cdot)}),

The electrostatic effects are captured through the quartic terms, as we explain below. A population disequilibrium between two modes leads to the development of an electrical double layer across the junction.
The corresponding voltage due to separated charges is proportional to the charge difference,
V_{jk}\propto\Delta n_{jk}

U_{jk}\propto V_{jk}\Delta n_{jk}=E_{C}\Delta n_{jk}^{2}

z_{jk}=\langle\hat{z}_{jk}\rangle=\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\rangle

III.1
Steady state of a general JJ network

While in this work we focus on the pump setups shown in
Figure

1
, the theoretical description shall be kept generic in order to facilitate its extension to other Josephson junction based nanodevices in future research. Hence, we start a Hamiltonian for a system composed of an arbitrary number of terminals, where all components are interacting with each other:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{j}\epsilon_{j}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}+\sum_{j,k\neq j}t_{kj}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}+\sum_{j,k>j}c_{jk}(\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}-\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k})^{2}.

(12)

The
\epsilon
’s and
c
’s are, respectively, onsite energies and inverse capacitances matrix, while the coefficients
t_{kj}=t_{jk}^{*}
are the tunneling amplitudes between the respective sites.

Let us now introduce the aforementioned macroscopic density matrix, MDM (also known as one-body reduced density matrix, or the two-point correlation matrix). For a system composed of
J
modes the MDM is a
J\!\crossproduct\!J
positive definite matrix comprising all two-point correlations:
\sigma=[\sigma_{jk}]=[\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}_{k}\rangle]
.
The diagonal elements are the populations:
n_{j}
, and the off-diagonal elements represent coherences between different modes:
z_{jk}
:

\displaystyle\sigma=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
n_{1} & z_{12} & z_{13} & \dots& \dots& z_{1J}
\\
& n_{2} & z_{23} & \dots& \dots& z_{2J}
\\
& & \ddots& \ddots& \ddots& \vdots\\
& & & \ddots& \ddots& \vdots\\
& & & & \ddots& \vdots\\
& & & & & n_{J}
\endlx@physics@matrix),

(13)

where we wrote out explicitly only the upper-triangular part because the MDM is hermitian, i.e.,
z_{kj}=z_{jk}^{*}
.

The Coulomb part of the Hamiltonian has a higher-than-quadratic dependence on canonical operators, which implies that the dynamics is governed by an infinite set of coupled nonlinear equations involving ever-increasing statistical moments.
To make the MME description tractable we will work in a mean-field approximation, where we assume that the higher order correlations are negligible:

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k},\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n})=\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}\rangle-\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\rangle\langle\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}\rangle\approx 0.

(14)

Thus, all four-point correlations are thus replaced by products of second-order ones,
\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}\rangle=\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\rangle\langle\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}\rangle=\sigma_{jk}\cdot\sigma_{mn}
, essentially giving a closed set of non-linearly coupled equations
[see
Appendix

A
for an explicit derivation].

The non-equilibrium steady-state populations and coherences are then given by

\displaystyle n_{j}=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow}}{\gamma}+\frac{2}{\hbar\gamma}\sum_{m\neq j}\imaginary(t_{mj}z_{jm});

(15)

\displaystyle z_{jk}=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{jk}\Delta n_{jk}+\sum_{m\neq j,k}\quantity( t_{mk} z_{jm} - t_{jm} z_{mk} )}{i\hbar\gamma+\Delta\epsilon_{jk}+2\sum_{m}\quantity( c_{jm} \Delta n_{jm} + c_{mk} \Delta n_{mk} )},

where
\Delta n_{jk}=n_{j}-n_{k}
, and for simplicity we assumed that the difference between pumping and damping rates to be the same for all baths, so that we may work with a single parameter:

\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma_{j}^{\downarrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow},\hskip 14.22636pt\forall j.

(16)

The steady state conditions in
Equation

15
are numerically solved through a self-consistent fixed-point iteration for the populations and the coherences.
The initial guesses are generated randomly, and we find for a wide range of parameters that the iterative algorithm converges to the same final fixed-points irrespective of these random inputs.
In this work we set the acceptable error tolerance as
\sum_{j}|n_{j}^{(1)}-n_{j}^{(0)}|<10^{-8}
, where
n^{(1)}
is the next iterative guess based on the current solution
n^{(0)}
.

Pumping in the two geometries

We now use

\displaystyle I_{j}=\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_j \mathcal{D}(\h\rho) )=-\gamma n_{j}+\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow}.

\displaystyle I_{\text{pump}}=I_{D}-I_{U}=-\gamma\Delta n_{DU}.

For representative purposes, in the top panels we have turned off the Coulomb interaction.
It can be seen in panel (a) that the amplitude of

III.3
Hidden cycles

A charge pump is an
engine
, i.e., an open system that can perform work cyclically, at the expense of an external disequilibrium (in this case, the DC bias applied along the
L
-
R
branch in
Figure

2
). For a general discussion of this point of view, see
[
34
]
.
Figure

5
shows a general scheme for two types of irreversible processes. Panel (a) represents a passive process that consumes free energy from an external source. Such a process cannot maintain any circulation of flow. The panel (b) represents an active process that uses part of the free energy consumed to perform a sustained work, represented there by the turning of a turbine in order to pump the active flow in the circuit on the right. In an electrical device, the integral per unit charge of the active, non-conservative force that drives this circulation is called the
electromotive force
(emf). Such an active process corresponds to an engine in the sense defined above.

In the theoretical treatments of adiabatic quantum pumps of
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
, pumping is obtained from the time dependence of a potential or scattering amplitude. Ref.
[
36
]
considers a quantum pump in which the time dependence is generated endogenously (i.e., as a
self-oscillation
) as conceptualized in Ref.
[
27
]
, rather than imposed as an external periodic driving. In both cases the pumping can be interpreted in terms of an engine
cycle
. In Giazzotto et al.’s modeling of their Josephson pump, this cycle appears as the dependence upon the JJ phase (
\dot{\phi}=2eV/\hbar
) of the amplitude for Andreev scattering
[
13
, Supplementary Information]
. Thus, in terms of the scheme of
Figure

5
(b), we may say that the scheme of
[
13
]
uses the JJ phase as the “turbine” that drives the pump current.

We have argued that our description, which is based on a master equation for the JJ treated as an open quantum system, can clarify the thermodynamic analysis of the Josephson pump. But it also raises a puzzle: Where are the engine cycle and the associated “turbine” in this description? This is an important point, because at the first glance it could appear from our own treatment that the process described is analogous to the Hall effect, in which a voltage applied longitudinally along a conductor produces, by the action on an external magnetic field, a voltage in the transverse direction. However, in the scheme of
Figure

5
the Hall effect is not an instance of charge pumping, since there the generation of a transverse voltage results from a classical Lorentz force, which does no work on the charges. Indeed, it has been shown that most of the power that drives the lateral current between the edges of the Hall bar comes directly from the applied longitudinal current, rather than from the perpendicular current associated with the Hall voltage
[
37
]
. Moreover, the transverse voltage in a Hall bar is not an emf in the sense of
Figure

5
(b), since it cannot drive charges along a closed path.

It is therefore clear that the setup of
Figure

1
is qualitatively different from the Hall effect, as here the charges experience no classical Lorentz force, and the role of the magnetic field is only to break the
D
-
U
symmetry by introducing a relative phase in the amplitudes of quantum tunneling between different terminals. But it may still seem puzzling that we should have been able to describe the active pumping of
[
13
]
in terms of equations with no explicit time dependence or cycles.

In the model of the JJ presented in
[
25
]
, the external bias sustains the coherences
[z_{jk}]
against decay from the interaction with the baths. One of the key results of
[
25
]
is that the equations of motion for the JJ engine have “hidden attractor” cycles around fixed points with non-zero coherence. It is these hidden cycles that explain how the JJ acts as an engine, generating work in the form of nonthermal sound and electromagnetic waves at the Josephson frequency (
\Omega=2eV/\hbar
) and its lower harmonics. In the model of the Josephson pump presented in this article, it is the sustained coherences
[z_{jk}]
that act as cyclical “turbine” variables, which drive the pumped current.

The MME formalism is averaged over the period of these hidden attractors, so that the time dependence associated with the pumping cycle does not appear explicitly in our equations. We expect, however, that the active pumping of charge from
D
to
U
that we obtained in our calculations reflects the modulation of the tunneling rates by the coherences
[z_{jk}]
along the hidden attractors identified in
[
25
]
. The details of this, as well as the broader question of how non-equilibrium steady states more generally could be understood as time averages over hidden cycles, call for further investigation. We regard this as one of the most interesting and pressing questions raised by this work.

IV
Discussion

By treating the Cooper pairs in the JJ as a bosonic open quantum system, governed by a MME, we developed a simple and robust model for studying the dynamics of nanodevices based on the JJ. Using these theoretical tools we explored charge pumpings similar to the one realized in
[
13
]
. In the setup of
[
13
]
the current is pumped through a regular nanowire, which leads to resistive heat losses.
In the current proposal this is avoided by using only superconducting leads. The suppression of electrical resistance gives negligible heat losses, which could allow for a coherent operation over longer runtimes. We found that if the Coulomb interaction between the terminals is included in the model, via nonlinear terms in the Hamiltonian, the results obtained are in good qualitative agreement with available experimental data.

Although the steady state that we obtained from the MME has no explicit time dependence, the presence of “hidden attractor” cycles in the JJ equations of motion obtained in
[
25
]
suggests that our results are consistent with the presence of an engine cycle like that seen in other active systems. This may be of broader interest in light of the fact that major theoretical questions still surround the dynamics responsible for the generation of an emf in batteries, solar cells, thermoelectric generators, fuel cells, and other active devices of great practical importance. It has been proposed that these devices have hidden cycles associated with internal mechanical degrees of freedom; see, e.g.,
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
. By explicitly showing how the JJ can pump a steady current and how this is connected with a hidden internal cycle, this work could help to shed light on the broader problem of the dynamical generation of an emf in active systems.

We regard it as promising that a simple open-system model of the Josephson quantum pump, as the one presented here, is sufficient to qualitatively account for all the features reported by experimentalists. We hope that this will help to clarify the sense in which the AC Josephson effect should be understood as a non-unitary, thermodynamically irreversible dynamics, as previously advocated in
[
25
]
. Although still rather phenomenological, the model developed in this work promises to expand the footprint of MME to the dynamics of superconducting nanodevices.
In view of the fact that the simple model presented here succeeds in capturing the basic mechanism of Josephson pumps, we consider it imperative to explore how it may be adapted and extended to other devices, and also to better ground such a model in terms of the relevant microphysics. One broad problem in which this modeling can be relevant concerns the relation between non-equilibrium steady states and the time-averaging over hidden cycles in active systems such as the Josephson pump.

Appendix A
Markovian dynamics for a JJ network

The Hamiltonian considered in this work is of the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{j}\epsilon_{j}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}+\sum_{j,k\neq j}t_{kj}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}+\sum_{j,k>j}c_{jk}(\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}-\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k})^{2},

(19)

where
c_{jk}=c_{kj}
are elements of the inverse capacitance matrix, and
t_{kj}=t_{jk}^{*}
are tunneling amplitudes between modes
k
and
j
. The Cooper pair condensate in a superconducting electrode is modelled as a single quantum harmonic oscillator satisfying the following bosonic commutation relations:

\displaystyle\big[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{k}\big]=0,\quad\big[\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger},\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\big]=0,\quad\big[\hat{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\big]=\delta_{jk}.

(20)

The open dynamics of the system is governed by the local MME:

\displaystyle\derivative{\h\rho}{t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\commutator{\h H}{\h\rho}+\mathcal{D}(\hat{\rho}),\quad:\quantity{
\mathcal{D}(\h\rho) =
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}^{\da}
\qty(
[ \ha_{j} , \h\rho\ha_{j}^{\dagger} ]
+ [ \ha_{j} \h\rho, \ha_{j}^{\dagger} ]
)
+ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}^{\ua}
\qty(
[ \ha_{j}^{\dagger} , \h\rho\ha_{j} ] + [ \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \h\rho, \ha_{j} ]
)
},

(21)

where
\hat{H}
is the system Hamiltonian and
\mathcal{D}(\hat{\rho})
is the dissipator due to the external bath.
Accordingly, the time evolution of the MDM is given by:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\sigma_{jk}=

\displaystyle\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \dv{\h\rho}{t} )

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k [\h H,\h\rho] )+\Tr\quantity( a^\dagger_j \ha_k \mathcal{D}(\h\rho) )

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \h H \h\rho- \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \h\rho\h H )+\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \mathcal{D}(\h\rho) )

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \h H \h\rho- \h H \h a^\dagger_j \ha_k \h\rho)+\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \mathcal{D}( \h\rho) )

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\expectationvalue{ [\ha^\dagger_j \ha_k, \h H] }+\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \mathcal{D}(\h\rho) ).

(22)

Going forward, the following identity will be quite useful:

\displaystyle\quantity[ \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} , \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} ]

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}-\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\delta_{km}+\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k})\hat{a}_{m}-\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}(\delta_{jm}+\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m})\hat{a}_{k}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}\delta_{km}-\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\delta_{jm}

\displaystyle\equiv(\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm})\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}.

(23)

Due to the onsite energy part we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\epsilon_{m}\expectationvalue{
[ \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k , \ha_m^\dagger\ha_m ]
}=\sum_{m}\epsilon_{m}\expectationvalue{ \qty( \delta_{km} - \delta_{jm} ) \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k }=-\Delta\epsilon_{jk}\sigma_{jk},

(24)

where
\Delta(\cdot)_{jk}=(\cdot)_{j}-(\cdot)_{k}
.
The tunnelings between different modes contribute to:

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}t_{nm}\expectationvalue{
[ \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k , \ha_m^\dagger\ha_n ]
}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}t_{nm}\expectationvalue{
\ha_j^\dagger\ha_k \ha_m^\dagger\ha_n - \ha_m^\dagger\ha_n \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k
}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}t_{nm}\expectationvalue{
\ha_j^\dagger( \delta_{km} + \ha_m^\dagger\ha_k ) \ha_n - \ha_m^\dagger( \delta_{jn} + \ha_j^\dagger\ha_n) \ha_k
}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}t_{nk}\sigma_{jn}-\sum_{m}t_{jm}\sigma_{mk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\quantity( t_{mk} \sigma_{jm} - t_{jm} \sigma_{mk} ).

(25)

Note that
c_{mm}=0
, and hence for the electrostatic part we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n>m}c_{mn}\expectationvalue{
[ \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k ,
(\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n)^2 ]
}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,n}c_{mn}\expectationvalue{
[ \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k ,
(\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n)^2 ]
}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,n}c_{mn}\bigg(\expectationvalue{
[ \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k ,
(\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n )] (\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n) }+\expectationvalue{
(\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n)
[ \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k,
(\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n) ]
}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,n}c_{mn}\quantity( \delta_{km} - \delta_{jm} - \delta_{kn} + \delta_{jn} )\bigg(\expectationvalue{
\ha_j^\dagger\ha_k (\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n) }+\expectationvalue{
(\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n) \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k
}\bigg).

(26)

This involves fourth-order moments, and their dynamics will involve sixth-order moments, and so on, making the set of equations open. In order to close the set we shall work in a mean-field approximation (MFA) that neglects the higher-order correlations by assuming

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k},\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n})=\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}\rangle-\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\rangle\langle\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}\rangle\approx 0.

(27)

Accordingly, all fourth-order moments are replaced by a product of second-order ones:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}\rangle=\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\rangle\langle\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}\rangle=\sigma_{jk}\cdot\sigma_{mn}.

(28)

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n>m}c_{mn}\expectationvalue{
[ \ha_j^\dagger\ha_k ,
(\ha_m^\dagger\ha_m - \ha_n^\dagger\ha_n)^2 ]
}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,n}c_{mn}(\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}-\delta_{kn}+\delta_{jn})\Big(\sigma_{jk}(n_{m}-n_{n})+(n_{m}-n_{n})\sigma_{jk}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{jk}\sum_{m,n}c_{mn}(\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}-\delta_{kn}+\delta_{jn})\Delta n_{mn}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{jk}\quantity(
\sum_{n} c_{kn} \Delta n_{kn}
- \sum_{n} c_{jn} \Delta n_{jn}
- \sum_{m} c_{mk} \Delta n_{mk}
+ \sum_{m} c_{mj} \Delta n_{mj}
)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{jk}\quantity(
- \sum_{m} c_{mk} \Delta n_{mk}
- \sum_{m} c_{jm} \Delta n_{jm}
- \sum_{m} c_{mk} \Delta n_{mk}
- \sum_{m} c_{jm} \Delta n_{jm}
)

\displaystyle=-2\sigma_{jk}\sum_{m}\quantity( c_{jm} \Delta n_{jm} + c_{mk} \Delta n_{mk}
).

(29)

We now calculate the dissipator part. In the Markovian local master equation, its contribution to evolution of the two-point correlation matrix is:

\displaystyle\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \mathcal{D}(\h\rho) )=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\downarrow}\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k
[\ha_{m},\h\rho\ha_{m}^{\dagger}] + \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k[\ha_{m}\h\rho,\ha_{m}^{\dagger}]
)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\uparrow}\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k
[\ha_{m}^{\dagger},\h\rho\ha_{m}] + \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k[\ha_{m}^{\dagger}\h\rho,\ha_{m}]
).

(30)

The decomposition part is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\downarrow}\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k
[ \ha_{m}, \h\rho\ha_{m}^{\dagger}] + \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k[ \ha_{m} \h\rho, \ha_{m}^{\dagger}]
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\downarrow}\Tr\quantity(2 \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m} \h\rho\ha_{m}^{\dagger}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \h\rho\ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} \h\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\downarrow}\Tr\quantity(2 \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m} \h\rho- \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \h\rho- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} \h\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\downarrow}\expectationvalue{2 \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}- \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} }

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\downarrow}\expectationvalue{ 2 \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m}+(\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}) \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} }

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}^{\downarrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\downarrow})\sigma_{jk}+\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\downarrow}\expectationvalue{ \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}^{\downarrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\downarrow})\sigma_{jk}+\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\downarrow}\expectationvalue{ \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} \ha_{k}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger}(\delta_{km}+ \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}) \ha_{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}^{\downarrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\downarrow})\sigma_{jk}-\gamma_{k}^{\downarrow}\sigma_{jk}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{j}^{\downarrow}+\gamma_{k}^{\downarrow})\sigma_{jk},

(31)

and the creation part is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\uparrow}\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k
[\ha_{m}^{\dagger},\h\rho\ha_{m}] + \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k[\ha_{m}^{\dagger}\h\rho,\ha_{m}]
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\uparrow}\Tr\quantity(2\ha_{j}^{\dagger}\ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \h\rho\ha_{m}-\ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \h\rho\ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \h\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\uparrow}\Tr\quantity(2 \ha_{m} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \h\rho- \ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \h\rho- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \h\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\uparrow}\expectationvalue{2 \ha_{m} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger}- \ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}- \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\uparrow}\expectationvalue{2 \ha_{m} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger}- \ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}- \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{m} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}-(\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}) \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow})\sigma_{jk}+\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\uparrow}\expectationvalue{ \ha_{m} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} \ha_{m}^{\dagger}- \ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow})\sigma_{jk}+\sum_{m}\gamma_{m}^{\uparrow}\expectationvalue{ \ha_{m} \ha_{j}^{\dagger}(\delta_{km} + \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{k})- \ha_{m} \ha_{m}^{\dagger} \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow})\sigma_{jk}+\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}\expectationvalue{ \ha_{k} \ha_{j}^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow})\sigma_{jk}+\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}\expectationvalue{ \delta_{jk} + \ha_{j}^{\dagger} \ha_{k} }

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow}+\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow})\sigma_{jk}+\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}\delta_{jk}.

(32)

This implies that total dissipation is

\displaystyle\Tr\quantity( \ha^\dagger_j \ha_k \mathcal{D}(\h\rho) )

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}\delta_{jk}-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{j}^{\downarrow}+\gamma_{k}^{\downarrow})\sigma_{jk}+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow}+\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow})\sigma_{jk}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}\delta_{jk}-\frac{1}{2}\quantity( (\gamma_{j}^{\da}-\gamma_{j}^{\ua})
+
(\gamma_{k}^{\da}-\gamma_{k}^{\ua})
)\sigma_{jk}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}\delta_{jk}-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{j}+\gamma_{k})\sigma_{jk},

(33)

where
\gamma_{j}=\gamma_{j}^{\downarrow}-\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow}
can be loosely referred to as the relaxation rate
[
25
]
.
Summing up everything, the dynamics of all of the MDM is governed by:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\sigma_{jk}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\bigg[\frac{i\hbar}{2}(\gamma_{j}+\gamma_{k})+\Delta\epsilon_{jk}+\sum_{m}\quantity( c_{jm} \Delta n_{jm} + c_{mk} \Delta n_{mk} )\bigg]\sigma_{jk}+\gamma_{k}^{\uparrow}\delta_{jk}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{m}\quantity( t_{mk} \sigma_{jm} - t_{jm} \sigma_{mk} ).

(34)

For later convenience, the evolutions of the populations and coherences can be isolated:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}n_{j}=

\displaystyle-\gamma_{j}n_{j}+\gamma_{j}^{\uparrow}+\frac{2}{\hbar}\sum_{m\neq j}\imaginary(t_{mj}z_{jm});

(35)

\displaystyle\derivative{t}z_{jk}=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}\bigg[\frac{i\hbar}{2}(\gamma_{j}+\gamma_{k})+\Delta\epsilon_{jk}+2\sum_{m}\quantity( c_{jm} \Delta n_{jm} + c_{mk} \Delta n_{mk} )\bigg]z_{jk}-\frac{i}{\hbar}t_{jk}\Delta n_{jk}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{m\neq j,k}\quantity( t_{mk} z_{jm} - t_{jm} z_{mk} ).

The transient behavior of a device with any desired initial state is revealed through an integration of above set of differential equations, and the steady state is arrived when all the derivatives reach zero.
Alternatively, for directly revealing the steady state one could manually put the derivatives to zero and solve the resultant set of nonlinearly coupled equations [see
Equation

15
in the main text].
