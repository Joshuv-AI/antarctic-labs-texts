Permethrin induces epileptic susceptibility via activation of Na+ channels and rise in glutamate
Authors: Ming Su, Yuansong Zhang, Zhi Feng, Wei Li, Yuan Liu, Cenglin Xu, Congjian Zhao, Patrick Kwan, Xin Tian, Li Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPermethrin, a common pyrethroid insecticide, is extensively used to control insect pests and has been widely detected in the environment. A growing body of epidemiological studies has revealed a substantial increase in the prevalence of epilepsy among individuals highly exposed to pesticides. However, the mechanism of action of permethrin in epilepsy remains elusive. In this study, we investigate the effects of permethrin on zebrafish and mice exposed to environmentally relevant concentrations. Acute exposure to permethrin induces dose-dependent epileptiform symptoms in zebrafish, including whirlpool-like movement, clonus-like convulsions, nystagmus and neuronal hyperactivity. Extended exposure results in neuronal hyperexcitation, impaired neurogenesis and enhanced glial cell hyperplasia in zebrafish. Additionally, pre-exposure to permethrin significantly increases susceptibility to seizures in mice. Mechanistically, the effects of permethrin are conserved across different species, promoting the activation of sodium ion channels, particularly Nav1.6. This activation increases glutamate levels, which play a critical role in epileptiform symptoms. Our findings suggest that permethrin exposure leads to the activation of sodium ion channels and disrupts the balance between excitatory versus inhibitory neurotransmitters, resulting in seizure-like symptoms and increased susceptibility to epilepsy. Sodium channels and neurotransmitter balance may be protective targets against permethrin exposure.Subject terms: Epilepsy, Neurotoxicity syndromes, Ion channels in the nervous system, Mechanisms of disease

Introduction

Permethrin (PM), a common pyrethroid insecticide, is a highly efficient and broad-spectrum chemical that is artificially derived from natural pyrethrins1. It is extensively used to control insect pests in agriculture, forestry, public health sectors, and residential areas2. Currently, permethrin is used to treat lice and scabies, prevent malaria, and as an ingredient in pet shampoos, as well as to purge clothing to prevent mosquitoes and tick bites3. Permethrin is widely present in the environment, including in crops, surface water, soil, and air2. The general human population, particularly those in occupational settings, is exposed to permethrin through contaminated water, food, and air via dermal absorption and inadvertent ingestion4. Permethrin permeates the body through the skin as well the respiratory and digestive tracts5. 3-PBA, a common metabolite of various pyrethroids including permethrin, has been consistently detected in urine samples from the general population, including those of pregnant women and children6.

Concern is thus growing regarding the potential impact of permethrin on human health7. Experimentally, permethrin exposure leads to increased α-synuclein and decreased striatal dopamine levels, which induce oxidative stress and inhibit mitochondrial complex Ⅰ of the electron transport chain8. These alterations in the striatum are hallmarks of Parkinson’s disease8. Moreover, acute exposure to lethal or near-lethal doses of permethrin cause tremors, salivation, and peripheral nerve damage3. Chronic exposure to permethrin in neonatal mice resulted in long-term cognitive dysfunction and synaptic morphological changes during adulthood3. Similarly, zebrafish embryos subjected to high permethrin doses showed tremors and uncontrolled body movements9. Prolonged exposure to permethrin led to anxiety and cognitive deficits in adult fish10.

Epidemiological investigations have revealed a substantial increase in the prevalence of epilepsy among individuals highly occupationally exposed to pesticides11,12. Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by abnormal diffuse neuronal discharges13. Clinically, epilepsy manifests as recurrent unprovoked seizures with severe cognitive, social, and psychological implications for the person14. Epilepsy is among the most prevalent and serious brain disorders worldwide, affecting an estimated 65 million individuals14–16. The incidence and prevalence of epilepsy are higher in children (41–187/100,000) and the elderly (5.4/100)17,18, particularly in infants under 1 year old and in individuals over 50 years old15. Epilepsy rarely occurs independently: more than 50% of patients with epilepsy experience comorbidities such as depression, anxiety, and autism14. In clinical settings, the prevalence of anxiety in those with epilepsy is ~30–35% in children and 11%–50% in adults19,20. Pathophysiologically, epilepsy is a consequence of an imbalance in the excitatory and inhibitory neuronal activities within neural circuits, typically stemming from heightened excitability or diminished inhibition21,22. Epilepsy can arise from various pathologies, including developmental malformations, trauma, tumors, and infections23. Many genes encoding neuronal ion channels that regulate neuronal excitability have been proposed in the etiology of epilepsy24. Mutations in sodium channel genes, including SCN1A, SCN1B, SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A, are the primary causes of genetic epilepsy syndromes25,26. However, the etiology of ~60% of epilepsy cases remains unknown24, which has led to the consideration of environmental factors.

Zebrafish and mice are frequently used to evaluate the potential hazards of pollutants in the environment27. In this study, acute permethrin exposure induces epileptic seizures in zebrafish embryos, while chronic permethrin exposure causes anxiety-like symptoms in adult zebrafish, resembling the psychiatric comorbidities associated with epilepsy. Additionally, mice exposed to permethrin exhibit a substantial increase in the frequency of neuronal cell action potentials and heightened susceptibility to epilepsy. Mechanistically, permethrin activates sodium ion channels, particularly Nav1.6, which leads to the excessive production of glutamate.

Results

Permethrin leads to whirlpool-like swimming, clonus-like convulsions, and neuronal hyperactivity in embryonic zebrafish

Fertilized zebrafish embryos were exposed to 20, 50, 100, and 500 μg L−1 permethrin to evaluate the outcomes. We chose these permethrin concentrations to reflect those found in water in the environment (0.05–811 μg L−1)1,10. We initially evaluated the reactions of the 3 days post-fertilization (dpf) embryos after 70 h exposure. The majority of 3 dpf embryos in the control groups were immobile. In contrast, the permethrin-treated embryos, especially in the 500 μg L−1 group, displayed sudden increases in swimming, accompanied by a loss of direction and balance, resulting in rapid whirlpool-like swimming (Supplementary Movie 1). These embryos often fell to one side, collapsed, and remained immobile for 1–3 s (Supplementary Movie 1). They extensively coiled their tails during the bursts of imbalanced swimming (Supplementary Movies 1 and 2); this action was similar to the clonus-like convulsions induced by pentylenetetrazol (PTZ) treatment28 (Supplementary Movie 1). We further staged these behaviors based on the findings of previous studies28,29. Majority (95.65%) of the control embryos presented limited swimming activity at stage 0 (S0), and only 4.35% showed slight movement at stage S1. However, more than 12% embryos treated with 20 μg L−1 of permethrin exhibited swimming activity during stage S1. This swimming activity increased in a permethrin concentration dependent manner. Notably, more than 93% of the embryos in the 500 μg L−1 treatment group were swimming at either stage S1 (23.26%) or S2 (69.77%) (Fig. 1a, b). We designed an assay to ascertain the frequency and amplitude of tail convulsions by embedding the upper half of the embryonic body (including the head and yolk sac) in 1% agarose gel while the lower body segment remained free in water (Fig. 1c). An enhanced symmetrical swing at a frequency of 10.60 ± 0.40 times/min, with intervals lasting 8 to 20 s, was observed in 3 dpf embryos upon 500 μg L−1 permethrin treatment (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Movie 3). The maximum tail swing amplitude during twitching was 103.30 ± 10.27°; the tail sometimes even curled to touch the yolk sac (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Movie 3). However, this action was rarely observed in the controls or in those in the 20, 50, or 100 μg L−1 permethrin-treated groups.

The abnormally enhanced movement following permethrin exposure prompted us to examine the neuronal activity by imaging calcium sparks in Tg(HuC:GCaMP6s) embryos30,31. The GCaMP6s signals in the optic tectum were recorded for 9 min (300 cycles) (Supplementary Movie 4). Consistent low-frequency spontaneous calcium activity was observed in the HuC+ cells in the control group. However, the frequency and amplitude of the signal fluctuations gradually increased with increasing permethrin concentration in the treatment as demonstrated by the fluorescent images and ΔF/F0 traces32 (Fig. 1f, g, Supplementary Movie 4). The fluctuations in the amplitude of the GCaMP6s+ signals upon 20, 50, 100, and 500 μg L−1 permethrin exposure were 1.62, 2.48, 2.80, and 4.52 times larger than those of the controls, respectively (Fig. 1h). These findings implied that permethrin exposure dose-dependently induced neuronal excitability.

Extended permethrin exposure causes additional nystagmus and impairs sensory response in larval zebrafish

Zebrafish develop additional actions as embryos develop into larvae33. We thus further investigated the effects of permethrin treatment on zebrafish larvae 5 days after exposure. Movie recordings revealed that the control larvae swam smoothly and continuously at this stage. However, the larvae exposed to 500 μg L−1 of permethrin fell and lost their posture after convulsions, were unable to control their direction, and exhibited substantially reduced average speed and moving range (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c, Supplementary Movie 2). More frequent convulsions (16.00 ± 0.98 times/min) and enhanced tail swing amplitude (119.10 ± 5.58°) were observed in 5 dpf larvae than in 3 dpf embryos (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e, Supplementary Movie 5). Interestingly, we detected rapid repetitive rounds of eye movements in the larvae exposed to 50 μg L−1 of permethrin (Supplementary Movie 6). Among one round of movements, both eyes of the larvae turned in unison and stared in one direction for a duration of 6–10 s before turning to the other direction in the 50 μg L−1 permethrin-treated group. The repetitive eye movement intensities increased with increasing permethrin concentration (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Movie 6). The control larvae exhibited occasional eye rotation with an average frequency of 0.43 ± 0.30 times/min at 5 dpf. However, the eye rotation frequency increased to 1.33 ± 0.47, 1.50 ± 0.45, 3.38 ± 0.38, and 9.89 ± 0.68 times/min in the groups treated with 20, 50, 100, and 500 μg L−1 of permethrin, respectively (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Movie 6). This phenomenon reflected the nystagmus (jerky, horizontal, and conjugate34) observed in clinical cases of epilepsy but was not observed in larvae treated with 1 mg mL−1 PTZ (Supplementary Movie 6). Simultaneously, subtle twitching in the trunk of the bodies occurred in the larvae exposed to 20 μg L−1 of permethrin. Body twitching became more pronounced as permethrin concentration increased (Supplementary Movie 6). However, this phenotype was not detected in the control larvae.

Zebrafish larvae begin to perceive environmental alterations, including light, at 5 dpf33. As such, we conducted photoperiodic stimulation tests35. The control larvae exhibited increased locomotion in response to a light–dark transition, particularly during the dark period (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g). However, the movement distance and speed of the larvae exposed to 500 μg L−1 of permethrin considerably decreased in response to light–dark cycle stimulation compared with those of the control and other treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 1h, i). Notably, the movement distance and speed of larvae treated with 500 μg L−1 permethrin all decreased by 53.78% during the light cycle, whereas they decreased by 88.11% and 76.23% during the dark cycle compared with those of the control group, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1j, k). These results indicated similar but more deteriorated actions in the 5 dpf larvae than in the 3 dpf permethrin-treated larvae (Supplementary Table 1).

Reduced neurogenesis and glial cell hyperplasia in larval zebrafish upon extended permethrin exposure

Reduced neurogenesis and glial cell hyperplasia are prevalent characteristics of epilepsy models36. Various cell types, including neuronal and glial cells, were examined. The intensities of glial fibrillary acidic protein (Gfap)+ signals were increased notably after permethrin exposure (Fig. 1j, k), while the number of olig2-DsRed+ oligodendrocytes remained comparable to that of the controls (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). The number of neurod1-GFP+ and NeuN+ neuronal cells decreased in a dose-dependent manner upon exposure to permethrin (Fig. 1l, m, Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Among these, the signals of islet+ cranial motor neurons declined (Fig. 1n, o, Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). However, the number of vglut2a+ excitatory glutamatergic neurons, rather than gad1b-mCherry+ GABAergic neurons, increased after permethrin exposure compared to the controls (Fig. 1p, q, Supplementary Fig. 2g–j). Additionally, a significant reduction in Sox2-labeled neural stem and progenitor cells (NSPCs) was detected in the permethrin-treated group compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 2k, l). The number of proliferating neuronal cells, indicated by HuC+EdU+ staining in Tg(HuC:GFP) larvae, was markedly decreased following permethrin exposure than their controls (Supplementary Fig. 2m, n). But the counts of acridine orange+ (AO+) apoptotic cells remained unchanged under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2o, p). Collectively, these findings suggest that permethrin exposure induces gliosis but suppresses neurogenesis in larval zebrafish.

Chronic permethrin exposure leads to anxiety-like symptoms in adult zebrafish

When the larvae were continuously exposed to permethrin, those in the 100 and 500 μg L−1 groups exhibited a gradual increase in mortality from 8 dpf and all died before 12 dpf (Supplementary Fig. 3a). However, 22% of the samples exposed to permethrin at concentrations of 50 μg L−1 survived to adulthood, allowing us to explore the effects of chronic low-dose permethrin exposure. The spontaneous movements of the surviving zebrafish after 6 months of exposure (50 μg L−1 permethrin) were comparable to those of their controls, including in the open field test used to assess response to an unfamiliar environment37 (Supplementary Fig. 3b–g, Supplementary Table 1). However, the zebrafish in the permethrin-exposed group preferred staying close to the walls, away from the central area, compared with those in the control group (Fig. 2a, b). The distance parameters revealed a notable reduction in movement in the central region and an increase of 27.93% in movement in the peripheral region in the exposed fish compared with those of the control group (Fig. 2c). Increased thigmotaxis in the peripheral region suggested anxiety after chronic permethrin exposure.

The light/dark preference and social preference tests are validated assays for assessing anxiety-like behavior38. Adult zebrafish exhibit a natural preference for dark conditions (scototaxis) and light avoidance35. Indeed, chronic exposure to permethrin substantially increased the swimming distance and speed in the dark zone, but there was no significant difference in the time spent between the two areas (Fig. 2d–f, Supplementary Fig. 3h). Statistically, exposure to 50 μg L−1 of permethrin resulted in increases of 49.77% and 36.80% in movement distance and speed within the dark dwelling (an index of anxiety-like behavior39), respectively (Fig. 2e, f). The total distance traveled and average speed increased by 27.46% and 27.36%, respectively, in permethrin-treated zebrafish compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 3i, j). In the social preference test, zebrafish in the permethrin-treated group spent more time in region 2 than in region 1 compared with the control group (Fig. 2g, h), although the total movement distance and average speed of the zebrafish remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 3k, l). The residence time and movement distance in region 2 of the adult zebrafish exposed to permethrin were 16.32 and 19.35 times larger than those in the control group, respectively (Fig. 2h, i). We further examined the glial cells and neurons. The immunofluorescence staining results indicated a notable increase in Gfap+ signals and a considerable decrease in NeuN+ signals in the adult zebrafish brains after treatment with permethrin compared with those of the control group (Fig. 2j–m). These results were consistent with those observed in zebrafish larvae (Supplementary Table 1). Taken together, long-term permethrin exposure induces anxiety-like behavior by exacerbating both scototaxis and thigmotaxis, reducing exploratory ability, and impairing sociality37,40. These results are consistent with the anxiety comorbidities observed in adults with epilepsy (11–50%)41.

Permethrin induces the activation of sodium ion channels

The head tissues of 5 dpf zebrafish from the control as well as the 50 and 500 μg L−1 permethrin-treated groups were extracted to perform RNA-seq to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of the reactions following permethrin exposure. The results of principal component analysis (PCA) indicated that the nine samples could be categorized into three distinct groups (Supplementary Fig. 4a). We found 2338 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the samples from the 50 μg L−1 group (1469 up- and 869 down-regulated DEGs) and 5025 DEGs in the 500 μg L−1 group (2756 up- and 2269 down-regulated) in comparison with the controls (Supplementary Fig. 4b). The biological processes (BPs) and molecular functions (MFs) in the Gene Ontology (GO) database revealed that transmembrane transport (GO:0055085) was mostly enriched in both the 50 and 500 μg L−1 permethrin-treated groups compared with the control group (Fig. 3a). Among the transmembrane transport differences found, sodium ion transport (GO:0006814) and intracellular sodium ion homeostasis (GO:0006883) were notable (Fig. 3a). The downregulated GO terms were primarily related to cell division as well as brain and eye development (Supplementary Fig. 4c). The results of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed that the most upregulated terms were related to metabolic pathways, particularly amino acid metabolism (Fig. 3a).

The obtained RNA-seq data indicated the involvement of permethrin in sodium channels and glutamate transport. Accordingly, four types of clinical antiseizure medications (ASMs) validated in zebrafish and rodent epilepsy models were selected to seek the targets42,43: Topiramate (TPM), a glutamate receptor antagonist, Na+ channels inhibitor and GABAergic activity enhancer44; Sodium Valproate (VPA), a blocker of Na+ and Ca2+ and K+ channels in increasing GABA levels45; Carbamazepine (CBZ), a blocker of Na+ channels in decreasing glutamate levels46; Ethosuximide (ESM), a blocker of low threshold T-type Ca2+ channels47. We pretreated zebrafish embryos with 500 μg L−1 of permethrin, which was followed by treatment with these drugs according to the methods described in a previous study43. We evaluated embryonic movement at 3 dpf (Fig. 3b). The movement distances following the addition of TPM, VPA, and CBZ were reduced by 46.11%, 82.67%, and 90.89%, respectively, compared with those in the permethrin-only treatment (Fig. 3c). However, ESM had a limited effect. The CBZ treatment notably promoted the recovery to the normal phenotype in the permethrin-treated zebrafish, including larval convulsions (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Movie 7), eye movement (Fig. 3e), neuronal activity (Fig. 3f, g, Supplementary Movie 8), and neurod1+ cell count (Fig. 3h, i). The levels of some symptoms returned to those of the control group (Fig. 3d–i). The obvious effects of TPM, VPA, and in particular CBZ (rather than ESM) in alleviating the phenotypes caused by permethrin suggested that permethrin induced epileptoid symptoms in zebrafish by activating sodium ion channels and reducing glutamate levels.

Activation of sodium channels causes epileptoid symptoms

Fertilized zebrafish embryos were exposed to veratridine, a sodium channel activator, at concentrations of 6 and 12 μg mL−1 at 3 hpf to assess the activation of sodium channels in the epileptoid symptoms induced by permethrin48. At 3 dpf, treated embryos exhibited rapid whirlpool-like circular swimming and brief clonus-like convulsive behavior (Supplementary Movie 9). The distance the embryos moved significantly increased after treatment with veratridine (Fig. 4a). A total of 45.83% of the embryos in the 6 μg mL−1 veratridine group presented S2-stage movements, increasing to 91.67% when the dose was increased to 12 μg mL−1 (Fig. 4b). In contrast, limited movement was observed in the control group. Additionally, the amplitude of GCaMP6s+ cell fluctuations was notably higher in the veratridine-treated group than in the controls (Supplementary Fig. 4d, Supplementary Movie 10). The actions of the treated embryos resembled those cultured in permethrin (Supplementary Movies 1 and 9), suggesting a critical role of sodium ion channels in permethrin mediated seizure-like symptoms.

Targeting sodium channels mitigates the epileptoid symptoms caused by permethrin exposure

Voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs) serve as the principal conduits through which sodium ions permeate cells, thereby underpinning neuronal excitability49,50. In zebrafish, the VGSCs include scn1laa/scn1lab, scn4aa/scn4ab, scn5laa/scn5lab, and scn8aa/scn8ab, which encode the Nav1.1, Nav1.4, Nav1.5, and Nav1.6 channels, respectively51. The heatmap of the RNA-seq and qRT-PCR results indicated the up-regulation of scn4aa, scn4ab, scn5laa, scn5lab, scn8aa, and scn8ab in the zebrafish following permethrin treatment (Fig. 4c, d, Supplementary Fig. 4e). Therefore, we treated zebrafish embryos with methocarbamol, flecainide acetate, and SYM2206 from 3 hpf to 5 dpf, which target Nav1.4, Nav1.5, and Nav1.6, respectively52–54. The frequency of convulsions was reduced by 34.53%, 60.67%, and 51.14% following the simultaneous administration of permethrin with the inhibitors of Nav1.4, Nav1.5, and Nav1.6, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4f); the eye movement frequency decreased by 20.63%, 37.30%, and 27.59%, respectively, compared with those of the permethrin-only treatment group (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Similar decreases in the amplitude of the calcium signal fluctuations in the neurons were observed with the addition of these chemical inhibitors to the permethrin-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. 4h, Supplementary Movie 11). Moreover, the combination of different sodium channel inhibitors had a more pronounced effect than any single sodium channel inhibitor during permethrin exposure (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 4f). This indicated that permethrin simultaneously interacted with multiple sodium ion channels. Notably, when the Nav1.6 inhibitor was added to the combined treatment, the frequency of convulsions in the larvae was significantly lower than that observed with the combination of Nav1.4 and Nav1.5 inhibitors (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, administrations of methocarbamol and flecainide acetate did not significantly affect the neuronal cell counts (Supplementary Fig. 4i–l). In contrast, application of the Nav1.6 inhibitor (SYM2206) partially (11.73%) restored the number of neuronal cells in the permethrin-treated group (Fig. 4f, g). Collectively, these findings indicated that permethrin induced embryonic epileptoid symptoms through the activation of sodium ion channels, in which Nav1.6 probably played a major role.

Nav1.6 channels play a comprehensive role in the epileptoid symptoms induced by permethrin exposure in zebrafish

We then focused on Nav1.6. The expression levels of scn8aa and scn8ab were significantly elevated in permethrin-treated larvae compared with those in the controls (Fig. 4d). The results of immunofluorescence staining revealed that the expression level of Nav1.6 was significantly upregulated in zebrafish larvae acutely exposed to permethrin and in adult zebrafish chronically exposed to permethrin, compared with the controls (Fig. 4h–k). These findings suggest that permethrin exposure significantly induces Nav1.6 activation. As such, we used the crispant method to knockout scn8aa (encoding a subunit of Nav1.6 channels). The Western blotting results indicated that the expression level of Nav1.6 in scn8aacrispant was lower than that in the control group (Supplementary Fig. 4m). scn8aacrispant had no abnormalities in appearance compared with that in the control group, and no embryonic convulsions were observed (Supplementary Movie 12). In contrast, the frequency of convulsions and eye movements in the larvae treated with 500 µg L−1 of permethrin were lower by 75.50% and 55.90%, respectively, following the knockout of scn8aa in the scn8aacrispant (Fig. 4l, m, Supplementary Movie 12). Furthermore, the amplitude of the calcium signal fluctuations in the neurons was significantly lower in the permethrin-treated group after scn8aa knockout (Supplementary Fig. 4n, Supplementary Movie 13), with the movement distance and speed decreasing by 60.71% and 64.85%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4o–q). These findings indicate that Nav1.6 channels play a comprehensive role in the epileptoid symptoms induced by permethrin exposure.

Activation of sodium channels by permethrin plays an important role in the increased levels of glutamate

Epilepsy is characterized by an imbalance between excitation and inhibition within neural circuitry55. Glutamate and GABA are crucial excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters, respectively22. The number of vglut2a+ glutamatergic neurons increased compared with that in the controls after permethrin exposure (Fig. 1p, q, Supplementary Fig. 2g, h). The results of GO and KEGG analyses revealed that permethrin treatment up-regulated glutamate transport and metabolism (Fig. 3a). The heatmap and qRT-PCR data indicated that the expression levels of glutamate receptor and transporter genes, including grin1a, grin1b, gria4a, grinaa, grik1a, grid1a, vglut1, vglut2a, and vglut2b, significantly increased following permethrin exposure (Fig. 5a–i). Consistently, the glutamate concentration increased by 23.36% and 24.16% in both the larvae and adult zebrafish treated with permethrin, respectively, compared with that in the controls (Fig. 5j, k). However, when the embryos were exposed to a combination of permethrin and CBZ, significant reductions in the expression levels of glutamate receptor and transporter genes were observed compared with those in the permethrin treatment group (Fig. 5b–h). The application of the sodium channel activator veratridine increased the expressions of the glutamate receptor and transporter genes compared with those in the control group (Fig. 5l). These findings suggest that permethrin activates sodium ion channels and induces elevated glutamate level.

Glutamate plays a critical role in epileptoid symptoms in zebrafish

Zebrafish embryos were treated separately with the glutamate receptor inhibitor D-AP5 (NMDA receptor antagonist, 20 μg mL−1) and CNQX (AMPA and kainate receptor antagonist, 4 μg mL−1) from 3 hpf to 5 dpf to investigate the critical role of glutamate in the epileptoid symptoms induced by permethrin exposure28,56. The frequencies of tail convulsions and eye rotations were reduced by ~20–50% when D-AP5 and CNQX were administered alongside permethrin. This effect notably increased when these two inhibitors were used together compared with that in the permethrin treatment group (Fig. 5m, n), suggesting a critical role of glutamate overproduction induced by permethrin exposure in epileptic symptoms.

Epilepsy is characterized by an imbalance between excitability and inhibition within neural circuitry55. Therefore, we hypothesized that recovering this balance via increasing inhibitor neurotransmitter levels might mitigate this imbalance. As such, the GABA receptor was activated by baclofen28. The frequencies of tail twitching and eye movements were reduced by 59.60% and 51.17%, respectively, compared with those in the permethrin-only treatment group (Fig. 5o, p). Collectively, these results suggest that the seizure-like symptoms in permethrin-exposed zebrafish are caused by glutamate, and that restoring the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters can alleviate these effects.

Permethrin exacerbates susceptibility to epilepsy and seizure severity in mice

To evaluate the conservative outcomes of permethrin exposure across species, we employed both a PTZ-induced acute epilepsy model and a PTZ-induced chronic kindling model in mice to investigate the role of permethrin in the onset and progression of epilepsy57,58. In these experiments, 7-week-old mice were administered 30% and 50% of the semi-lethal dose (LD50) of permethrin (650 mg kg−1 59) for 10 days. In the acute epilepsy model, mice received intraperitoneal injections of PTZ at a dose of 12.5 mg kg−1 every 10 minutes, for a total of eight injections, following the 10-day permethrin treatment (Fig. 6a). Seizure activity was monitored and evaluated using the Racine scale58. The results showed that control mice experienced their first generalized seizure after the sixth PTZ injection, whereas those in the 30% and 50% LD50 permethrin treated groups exhibited generalized seizures after the fifth and fourth injections, respectively (Fig. 6b). The proportion of mice exhibiting generalized seizures was 62.50% in the control group, compared to 100% and 87.5% in the 30% and 50% LD50 permethrin-treated groups, respectively (Fig. 6c). The incidence was significantly higher in the treatment groups than in the control group. Furthermore, the latency to the first generalized seizure for the control group was 63.40 ± 4.34 min, which significantly reduced to 55.00 ± 2.55 and 50.71 ± 3.34 min for the 30% and 50% LD50 treatment groups, respectively (Fig. 6d).

For the chronic PTZ kindling assay, a permethrin solution was administered to the mice for 10 days, which was followed by an intraperitoneal injection of 35 mg kg−1 PTZ for an additional 15 days. The seizure grades were observed daily after each injection (Fig. 6e). The seizures in the control group were categorized as grade I after the first PTZ injection. However, the seizures for the 30% and 50% LD50 permethrin-treated groups increased to grade II. The seizure grades of the mice in the 50% LD50 treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group during the subsequent PTZ injections (Fig. 6e). These results suggested that the prior exposure to permethrin accelerated epileptogenesis and exacerbated seizure severity in both the PTZ-induced acute seizure model and the PTZ-induced chronic kindling model.

Kainic acid (KA), a potent glutamate receptor agonist, can induce status epilepticus (SE), which promotes chronic, spontaneous, and recurrent seizures60. To examine the impact of permethrin on epileptogenesis, after 10 days of continuous permethrin administration, 1.0 nmol of KA was injected into the right hippocampus of the mice to induce SE (Fig. 6f). The results showed that the average number of seizures per day in mice pre-exposed to 30% and 50% of the LD50 of permethrin was higher by 21% and 64.19%, respectively, than in the control group (Fig. 6g). These findings suggest that permethrin exposure exacerbates the persistent seizure state in epilepsy.

Epilepsy is characterized by recurrent spontaneous seizures resulting from the hyperexcitability and hypersynchrony of brain neurons61. To investigate the effects of permethrin on neural activity, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in primary mouse neurons (Fig. 6h). Following 24 h exposure to 5 μM permethrin, resting membrane potentials did not differ significantly between permethrin-treated and control groups (Fig. 6i). Furthermore, action potential (AP) properties, including threshold (Supplementary Fig. 5a), amplitude (Supplementary Fig. 5b), half-width (Supplementary Fig. 5c), and afterhyperpolarization (AHP) amplitude (Supplementary Fig. 5d), remained unchanged between the permethrin-treated and control groups. However, when depolarizing current pulses reached 30 pA, the AP firing frequency was significantly higher in permethrin-treated neurons compared to controls (Fig. 6j, k). This increased firing frequency indicates that permethrin exposure enhances neuronal excitability.

Permethrin potentiates epileptic susceptibility in mice via Nav1.6 activation

Dysfunction of voltage-gated sodium channels is a well-established pathogenic mechanism in epilepsy. Among these, Nav1.1, Nav1.2, Nav1.3, and Nav1.6 are the subtypes most frequently implicated in epileptogenesis25. To investigate whether the increased susceptibility to epilepsy induced by permethrin exposure in mice involves sodium channel dysregulation—as observed in zebrafish—we measured the expression levels of genes encoding Nav1.1 (Scn1a), Nav1.2 (Scn2a), Nav1.3 (Scn3a), and Nav1.6 (Scn8a) in the hippocampus, a brain region critically involved in seizure generation62. qRT-PCR results showed no significant difference in Scn1a expression between the permethrin-treated and control groups. However, Scn3a expression was decreased, while Scn2a and Scn8a expression levels were significantly upregulated, with Scn8a showing the most prominent increase in the hippocampus following permethrin exposure (Fig. 7a). Consistently, immunofluorescent results showed that Nav1.6 expression level in permethrin-treated mice hippocampus was significantly higher than that in the controls (Fig. 7b, c).

Previous studies have shown that ICA-121431 is a potent, broad-spectrum voltage-gated sodium channel blocker, with an IC50 > 10 μM for Nav1.663. To further examine the involvement of sodium channels in permethrin-induced epilepsy susceptibility, we administered intragastric doses of ICA-121431 (100 μM) and the Nav1.6 inhibitor SYM2206 (24 μM) to mice for five consecutive days before permethrin exposure. No visible abnormalities in appearance or behavior were observed following inhibitor treatment. qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significant reduction in Scn8a expression in the hippocampus of these mice (Fig. 7d). Behavioral assays demonstrated that pretreatment with ICA-121431 and SYM2206 significantly delayed the onset of seizures in the PTZ-induced acute epilepsy model, increasing generalized seizure latency by 30% and 35%, respectively, compared to the permethrin-only group (Fig. 7e, f). In addition, the incidence of generalized seizures was significantly reduced in permethrin-exposed mice treated with these inhibitors (Fig. 7g). Together, these results indicated that permethrin exposure increases susceptibility to epilepsy in mice by activating sodium channels, particularly Nav1.6.

Permethrin exposure induces premature activation and alters the activation kinetics of sodium channels

To further investigate the effects of permethrin exposure on sodium channel function, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings on pyramidal neurons in acute mouse brain slices to assess the impact on sodium current characteristics. The results showed that bath application of permethrin (5 μM) induced a significant hyperpolarizing shift in the current–voltage (I–V) relationship compared to the control group. The control group generated peak sodium currents at a membrane potential of −35 mV, while the permethrin-treated group showed peak current at −50 mV (Fig. 7h). Moreover, the peak sodium current density in the permethrin-treated group was −583.6 ± 48.44 pA, significantly higher than the control group’s −316.2 ± 71.99 pA (Fig. 7i). Further analysis revealed that the sodium channel activation curve in the permethrin-treated group shifted hyperpolarized, with a significant decrease in the half-activation voltage (Fig. 7j, k). These results suggest that permethrin exposure lowers the activation threshold of sodium channels, causing them to open at more negative membrane potentials. In contrast, analysis of sodium current inactivation showed no significant differences in the voltage-dependent steady-state inactivation between the control and permethrin-treated groups, with the half-inactivation voltage remaining unchanged (Fig. 7l, m). This indicates that permethrin exposure does not significantly affect the inactivation process of sodium channels.

To confirm the role of Nav1.6 channels in permethrin exposure, we continuously perfused the Nav1.6 inhibitor SYM2206 (12 μM) in acute mouse brain slices. The results showed that the addition of SYM2206 shifted the sodium current inactivation curve towards hyperpolarized potentials, with a significant reduction in the half-inactivation voltage, indicating that Nav1.6 channels became more prone to inactivation (Fig. 7l, m). When SYM2206 was applied to the permethrin-treated group, the peak sodium current density in the current–voltage relationship was significantly reduced compared to the permethrin-only group (Fig. 7h, i). Additionally, the activation curve shifted toward depolarized potentials, and the half-activation voltage increased (Fig. 7j, k). These results suggest that inhibiting Nav1.6 activity significantly alleviates the premature activation and altered activation kinetics of sodium channels induced by permethrin exposure, further validating the critical role of Nav1.6 channels in the effects of permethrin exposure.

Permethrin enhances presynaptic glutamate release in hippocampal neurons

To investigate the effects of permethrin exposure on synaptic transmission, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings of spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) in hippocampal pyramidal neurons of mice. Quantitative analysis revealed that permethrin treatment significantly increased the frequency of sEPSCs, which was 4.81 times higher than that observed in the control group (Fig. 8a, b). Additionally, the amplitude of sEPSCs in the permethrin-treated group was 1.30 times greater than that in the control group (Fig. 8a, c). Further analysis of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) showed that permethrin exposure led to a 3.13-fold increase in mEPSC frequency compared to the control group (Fig. 8d, e). However, there was no significant difference in mEPSC amplitude between the two groups (Fig. 8d, f). These findings suggest that permethrin exposure enhances excitatory synaptic transmission in mice, likely by facilitating presynaptic glutamate release.

To further validate this hypothesis, we examined whether permethrin exposure alters glutamate levels in the hippocampus. Glutamate content in the hippocampal region of mice was measured following treatment with 50% LD50 permethrin. The results showed a 6.10% increase in glutamate levels compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 5e). To more directly assess synaptic glutamate dynamics, we employed an intensity-based glutamate-sensing fluorescent reporter (iGluSnFR), which has been widely used to detect synaptic activity64. Primary mouse neurons were infected with the rAAV-hSyn-iGluSnFR (A184S) virus, and 24 h after permethrin treatment, time-lapse imaging revealed a marked increase in iGluSnFR fluorescence intensity in the permethrin-treated neurons relative to controls (Fig. 8g, h). Notably, a substantial accumulation of iGluSnFR+ signals was observed along the axons of the permethrin-treated neurons (Fig. 8g). Further quantitative analysis showed a significant increase in the amplitude of iGluSnFR signal fluctuations in permethrin-treated neurons (Supplementary Fig. 5f), indicating a higher frequency of synaptic glutamate release.

To investigate whether these observed changes in glutamate signaling were accompanied by molecular alterations, we performed qRT-PCR to assess the expression levels of glutamate receptor and transporter genes in the hippocampus of mice. Permethrin-treated mice showed significant upregulation of the glutamate receptor genes Gria1 and Grin1, as well as the glutamate transporter genes Vglut1 and Eaat1, relative to controls (Supplementary Fig. 5g). Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that VGLUT1 levels in the CA3 region of the hippocampus increased by 38.22% and 47.04% in the 30% and 50% LD50 permethrin groups, respectively, compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 5h, i). These results further confirmed that permethrin exposure enhances excitatory neurotransmission in the hippocampus.

Glutamate homeostasis in the synaptic cleft is regulated by both presynaptic release and glial reuptake mechanisms65,66. To determine whether the increase in glutamate following permethrin exposure resulted from impaired clearance, we evaluated the effect of permethrin on glutamate reuptake. Glutamate reuptake by excitatory amino acid transporters (EAATs) in astrocytes plays a critical role in maintaining extracellular glutamate levels65. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that EAAT1 expression in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus was increased by 35.28% in the 50% LD50 permethrin-treated group compared to controls (Fig. 8i, j). In addition, we observed a significant increase in the expression of the astrocyte marker GFAP in the hippocampus of permethrin-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 5j, k). These findings indicate that the elevated glutamate levels observed following permethrin treatment are primarily due to an enhanced probability of synaptic neurotransmitter release rather than impaired reuptake mechanisms.

In conclusion, exposure to permethrin increases susceptibility to epilepsy and exacerbates seizure severity in both zebrafish and mice. These alterations are mediated by the premature activation of sodium ion channels, particularly Nav1.6, due to permethrin exposure. This overactivation promotes the release of the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate, which disrupts the equilibrium between the excitation and inhibition within the neural circuits. Consequently, excessive neuronal excitability occurs, culminating in epilepsy-like symptoms (Fig. 8k).

Discussion

In this study, we observed that zebrafish and mice exposed to environmental relevant concentrations of permethrin acquired similar phenotypes or susceptibility resembling generalized seizures in mammals and almost fully demonstrated the cardinal features of epilepsy according to the results of behavioral, electrophysiological, and neuropathological assessments62. Therefore, our study provides strong evidence that permethrin exposure is involved in neurological disorders, particularly epilepsy, in humans, questioning the safety of permethrin as an insecticide. However, we also proposed that permethrin might be experimentally used to induce epilepsy in a model organism, including zebrafish and mice, to provide additional methods in the epilepsy research. Disturbances in neurotransmitters such as glutamate are linked to epilepsy, anxiety, and depression21,67, suggesting an association between epilepsy and anxiety. Therefore, the anxiety-like symptoms we observed in the adult zebrafish following prolonged permethrin exposure could also be attributed to the activation of sodium ion channels, leading to the excessive production of glutamate and disruption of the balance between excitation and inhibition. However, the relationship between anxiety and epilepsy is bidirectional, complex, and mutually influential20,41. The more precise interrelationship between the two and the underlying molecular mechanisms still require further investigation. A notable action of eyes, similar to nystagmus, was observed in the samples exposed to concentrations exceeded 50 μg L−1 of permethrin. However, this eye action was not found in the zebrafish treated with other chemicals, including PTZ. We think that nystagmus phenotype by permethrin exposure provides an additional indicator of neuronal disorders which deserve future investigations.

We selected four clinical antiseizure medications that have been validated in PTZ or allylglycine (AG) induced zebrafish and rodent epilepsy models42. The addition of TPM, VPA, and CBZ after permethrin exposure had a relieving effect on the convulsions of the embryos, but the addition of ESM had a limited effect. TPM, VPA, and CBZ all inhibit sodium ion channels45,46,53, while ESM inhibits calcium channels47. This result highlights the important role of sodium ion channels in permethrin exposure. The zebrafish sodium channel gene shares homology with its mammalian counterpart51. Nav1.1 and Nav1.6 are predominantly expressed in the central nervous system, with Nav1.1 primarily found in inhibitory neurons in mammals25. Nav1.4 and Nav1.5 are mainly localized in the skeletal muscle and cardiomyocytes, respectively49. The administration of specific inhibitors targeting Nav1.4 and Nav1.5 decreased both the tail convulsions and eye movement frequency in the zebrafish treated with permethrin, suggesting that permethrin exposure may affect the strength of muscle contractions, which is akin to the tonic and clonic seizures accompanied by muscle jerks13. However, the number of neurons partially augmented upon the inhibition of Nav1.6, rather than the inhibition of Nav1.4 or Nav1.5. These results imply that sodium ion channels have multiple functions in neurons. Nav1.6 probably serves as a major target of permethrin in the neutral composition in epilepsy.

No notable epileptic behavior was observed when mice were exposed to permethrin at 30% and 50% of the LD50 for 10 days; probably caused by the not enough time and/or dosages of exposure. However, pathological signs indicative of epileptic activity, such as gliosis and increased expression of the glutamate receptor VGLUT1, were detected in the hippocampus. Additionally, an increase in Nav1.6 expression was noted. These phenotypes were consistent with those observed in zebrafish. Moreover, pretreatment with permethrin substantially increased the incidence of PTZ- and KA-induced seizures, reduced seizure latency, and increased the susceptibility to epilepsy as well as the severity of seizures. These data suggest a conserved mechanism for the induction of epilepsy by permethrin in mice. Permethrin is extensively utilized as an active ingredient in mosquito repellents products and is the most frequently detected pyrethroid insecticide within indoor environments68. Its application spans residential and public health settings, as well as in the agricultural sector, where it is commonly employed to treat fruits, vegetables, and crops. Human exposure to permethrin can occur through various pathways, including inhalation, ingestion, and dermal contact, each of which may pose significant health risks5. Therefore, the necessity of ongoing environmental surveillance of pyrethroid residues is underscored, advocating for the judicious application of pyrethroids in public health contexts. People with a history of epilepsy or those at risk of developing epilepsy should endeavor to minimize their exposure to these pesticides. Permethrin exposure may serve as a viable model for epilepsy accompanied by nystagmus. Sodium ion channels and/or the neurotransmitter glutamate may function as crucial targets for the treatment or mitigation of the adverse effects following permethrin exposure.

Methods

Zebrafish and mice

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) were bred and maintained under standard laboratory conditions30. The wild-type (WT) AB strain and following transgenic lines were used: Tg(neurod1:eGFP)69, Tg(HuC:GCaMP6s)70, Tg(gad1b:mcherry)71. The transgenic line Tg(islet:GFP)72 and Tg(vglut2a:GUL4-UAS:GFP)71 were gifted from South China University of Technology. Mice (C57BL/6J) were maintained under specific pathogen free (SPF) conditions. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committees of the Chongqing Institute of Green and Intelligent Technology (Approval number: cigit202401, Chongqing, China). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Compound exposure in zebrafish

For acute zebrafish exposure, embryos at 3 h post-fertilization (hpf) were soaked in egg water containing chemical until 3 or 5 dpf, depending on experimental requirements. To inhibit the development of endogenous pigmentation, embryos were treated with 0.003% 1-phenyl-2-thiourea (PTU; Sigma, #103-85-5) starting from 12 hpf. For chronic exposure experiment, zebrafish were exposed to 50 μg L−1 of permethrin (Target Mol, #52645-53-1) beginning at 3 hpf. These solutions were renewed bi-weekly, and any deceased fish were promptly removed. The embryos were treated with the following compounds from 3 hpf to 5 dpf: carbamazepine (MCE, #298-46-4, 50 mg L−1), methocarbamol (Target Mol, #532-03-6, 25 μg mL−1), flecainide acetate (Target Mol, #54143-56-5, 10 μg mL−1), SYM2206 (Target Mol, #173952-44-8, 1 μg mL−1), D-AP5 (Target Mol, #79055-68-8, 20 μg mL−1), CNQX (Target Mol, #115066-14-3, 4 μg mL−1), and baclofen (Target Mol, #1134-47-0, 50 μg mL−1). The experiments were conducted at 5 dpf. Additionally, embryos were exposed from 3 hpf to 3 dpf to veratridine (Target Mol, #71-62-5) at concentrations of 6 and 12 μg mL−1, with experiments performed at 3 dpf. Embryos were exposed to topiramate (Target Mol, #97240-79-4, 50 μg mL−1), valproic acid sodium salt (Target Mol, #1069-66-5, 150 μg mL−1), and ethosuximide (Target Mol, #77-67-8, 1 mg mL−1) from 2 dpf to 3 dpf for 24 h. Control groups were treated with either water or 0.1% DMSO as the vehicle.

Compound exposure in mice

The oral semi-lethal dose (LD50) of permethrin for adult mice is 650 mg kg−1 59. In this study, seven-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to receive permethrin (dissolved in corn oil) via gastric feeding at doses corresponding to 30% LD50 (195 mg kg−1) and 50% LD50 (325 mg kg−1). The treatment was gavage administered daily for 10 days at the specified duration. To investigate the involvement of sodium channels in permethrin-induced epileptogenic susceptibility, the sodium channel blocker ICA-121431 (MCE, #HY-16787) and SYM2206 were administered intragastrically at doses of 100 and 24 μM, respectively52,63. These chemicals were applied once daily for five consecutive days. Starting on day 6, mice were administered the 50% LD50 dose of permethrin for an additional 10 days. Subsequently, acute epilepsy modeling was induced using pentylenetetrazol (PTZ). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sangon Biotech, #67-68-5, 0.1%), Corn oil (Beyotime, #8001-30-7), pentylenetetrazol (Target Mol, #54-95-5, 1 mg mL−1), kainic acid (Target Mol, #487-79-6, 100 μg mL−1) were used in this study.

Embryonic convulsion and nystagmus analysis

The larvae were embedded in a 1% (w/v) low gelling temperature agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #16520050) at 38 °C with dorsal surface facing upward. After the agarose gel cooled, the agarose beneath the yolk sac of the embryo was carefully excised using a scalpel to ensure that the embryos were not damaged. This allowed the upper portion of the yolk sac to remain anchored within the agarose gel, while the lower portion retained its mobility in water. Each embryo was videotaped under a microscope (Nikon, SMZ18) for a duration of 3 min. The frequencies of nystagmus and tail twitching were recorded manually. The tail swing was a reference to the central axis of the body. A swing was recorded when the tail leaves this central axis and subsequently returns to it. The maximum swing amplitude (°) was defined as the angle between the farthest position reached by the tail swing.

Zebrafish locomotor behavioral test

The locomotor behavioral test was performed according to previous reports39,73. All embryos and larvae locomotor behavior detection were performed in 24-well plates. For adult zebrafish, spontaneous behavior, open field test and light/dark preference test were assessed using a box measuring 17.5 cm × 10.5 cm × 9.5 cm (length × width × depth). The apparatus used for social preference detection is a transparent PP tank measuring 21 cm × 6 cm × 10 cm. This tank is divided into two compartments: a smaller one is 7 cm × 6 cm × 10 cm, and a larger chamber is 14 cm × 6 cm × 10 cm. The movement of the zebrafish was captured by the Danio Vision Observation Chamber System (Noldus IT, Netherlands) and the captured video was analyzed by EthoVision XT software.

Calcium imaging of neurons in vivo

In vivo time-lapse imaging was performed in Tg(HuC:GCaMP6s) following previously reported methods30,74. Briefly, the GCaMP6s fluorescence signal in the midbrain region was manually selected as the region of interest (ROI). Fluorescence intensity at each time points of each ROI was calculated by (F−F0)/F0. F0 was the average fluorescence intensity value in the control group across 50 ROIs (n = 5). The fluctuation amplitude of the GCaMP6s+ signal was defined as (ΔF/F0)max−(ΔF/F0)min.

RNA sequencing analysis

Brain tissues (50 fish/sample) from control and permethrin treated groups were collected at 5 dpf for RNA sequencing. Each group was replicated three times (n = 3). Transcriptomic sequencing was performed by annoroad Co. (Beijing, China). Libraries meeting quality standards were sequenced on the Illumina platform. The sequencing strategy employed was PE150. DESeq2 software provided statistical methods to analyze differential expression in digital gene expression data using a model based on the negative binomial distribution. A threshold of Padj (adjusted P value) < 0.05 and absolute Log2FC (fold change) ≥ 1 was established to determine significant differential expression.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted according to a previous method75. All the data were obtained using the Roche qRT-PCR machines (LightCycler® 96 System) and the expression level of β-actin was used as the reference control. The information regarding qRT-PCR primers were listed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Glutamate content detection

The glutamate (Glu) content was measured using the Glu Content Assay Kit (Sangon Biotech, #D799585-0050) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 500 larvae were collected from the control and permethrin treated groups at 5 dpf. For mice, hippocampal tissues were harvested from both control and permethrin-treated groups (n = 3) after 10 days of daily intragastric administration of permethrin. Glutamate concentrations were quantified using a standard calibration curve prepared according to the kit’s instructions.

scn8aa crispant preparation and injection

The construction of scn8aa crispants refers to previous studies76. Briefly, a two-part guide RNA comprises a trans-activated double-stranded CRISPR-RNA (tracrRNA) and a specifically targeted CRISPR-RNA (crRNA). The two crRNA sequences for scn8aa are: Target1: 5′-GGACGCACAGGCAAATGCAA-3′; Target2: 5′-ATTGCTGAGAGTGTTCAAGT-3′. These sequences were design and ordered through Integrated DNA Technologies (https://eu.idtdna.com.) The final gRNA duplexes were generated by combining 200 μM of each crRNA and tracrRNA (IDT, #1072532), heating to 95 °C for 5 min before storing them at −20 °C. Subsequently, the two gRNAs were mixed and ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) consisting of 800 pg of the gRNA double-stranded mix and 800 pg of Cas9 protein (IDT, #1081058), were micro-injected into one cell stage wild-type zebrafish embryos at a volume of 1 nL.

5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) labeling for detection of cell proliferation

Cell proliferation was assessed using the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, #C0075S). The protocol was adapted for zebrafish as follows: Tg(Huc-GFP) transgenic larvae77 were collected and exposed to permethrin from 3 hpf. At 5 dpf, 1 nL of EdU solution (10 mM) was microinjected into cardiac chamber of each larva. The larvae were then incubated at 28.5 °C for 2 h. Following incubation, the larvae were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 3 h at room temperature. After permeabilization, EdU detection was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Proliferating neurons were identified as cells co-expressing HuC-GFP and EdU.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted using a previous method75. Briefly, the brain tissues of the mice were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution. The tissue pieces were rinsed in PBST (PBS with 0.5% Triton X-100) and blocked with buffer (5% FBS and 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS) at RT for 1 h. Primary antibodies were utilized at 4 °C overnight: Mouse anti-PCNA (Abcam, #ab29, 1:400), rabbit anti-GFAP (GeneTex, #GTX128741, 1:400), rabbit anti-SCN8A (Proteintech, #28504-1-AP, 1:400), rabbit anti-SLC17A7/VGLUT1 (ABclonal, #A12879, 1:400), mouse anti-NeuN (Proteintech, #66836-1-Ig, 1:400), rabbit anti-EAAT1 (Proteintech, #20785-1-AP, 1:400). Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated anti-mouse (Invitrogen, #A31570, 1:800) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, #A31573, 1:800) were used as secondary antibodies treated for 2 h at room temperature. Immunofluorescence staining images were taken by Zeiss LSM900 confocal microscopes. The fluorescence images were processed by ZEN3.3 software, the number of cells and the average fluorescence intensity were counted by image J software.

Primary neuron isolation and culture

Mice primary neuron culture and whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were conducted using a previous method78. Briefly, the brains of C57BL/6 mice (P0–P1) were dissected, and the tissue was mechanically minced and treated with 0.25% trypsin for 10 min. After centrifugation at 1000 r/min for 5 min, the cells were re-suspended in DMEM basic medium (Gibco, #C11995500BT) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (ABW, #AB-FBS-1050) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, #C0222) and counted with a hemocytometer. The cells were plated in six-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/well. After incubation under 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 4 h, the medium was replaced by Neurobasal maintenance medium (Gbico, #21103049) supplemented with 2% B27 (Gbico, #17504044), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mmol L−1 glutamine (Beyotime, #C0212). Half of the medium was replaced with fresh medium every 2 days.

Pentylenetetrazol and kainic acid induce seizures in mice

As previously described, the convulsants pentylenetetrazol (PTZ) and kainic acid (KA) were administered to induce seizures in mice that had received permethrin (or corn oil)79. The acute seizure was triggered by repetitive intraperitoneal administration of PTZ every 10 min at a dose of 12.5 mg kg−1, until generalized seizures were observed, or the cumulative dose reached 100 mg kg−1 79. For the chronic PTZ kindling model, mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of PTZ at a dose of 35 mg kg−1 for 15 consecutive days. Following each injection, mice were monitored for 30 min, and seizure severity was evaluated using the Racine scale (0: no response; Ⅰ: ear and facial twitching; Ⅱ: myoclonic jerks without rearing; Ⅲ: myoclonic jerks, and rearing; Ⅳ: turning over onto its side, and clonic-tonic seizures; Ⅴ: turning over onto its back, and generalized tonic-clonic convulsions). The latency to the first generalized seizure (stages IV and V) and the incidence of PTZ-induced generalized seizure were recorded58,80.

The convulsant KA was administered to induce status epilepticus (SE) in mice that had received permethrin or corn oil79. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital and fixed on a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science Co. Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Subsequently, 1.0 nmol of kainic acid dissolved in 50 nL of saline was injected into the right hippocampus (anteroposterior, 1.5 mm; mediolateral, 1.5 mm; dorsoventral, 1.5 mm) to induce SE after the injection. Sham-control mice were treated identically but received the same volume of saline. For behavioral tests, mice in the control and permethrin-treated group were video-monitored for 30 consecutive days, and the number of spontaneous recurrent seizures (SRSs) during the final 7 days was quantified (Racine’s stages Ⅳ and V) by two independent observers, and one was blinded to the treatment.

Preparation of acute brain slices and patch-clamp recordings

Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital and transcardially perfused with ice-cold oxygenated slicing solution (95% O2, 5% CO2) containing 2.5 mM KCl, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 7 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 220 mM sucrose, 26 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM D-glucose. After decapitation, the brains were quickly removed and sectioned in the ice-cold slice solution using a vibratome (Leica VT1200S, Germany). For whole-cell patch-clamp recordings, 300 μm coronal hippocampal sections were prepared. Slices were incubated in oxygenated artificial CSF (aCSF) containing 119 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 26 mM NaHCO3, 2 mM MgCl2, 2.5 mM CaCl2, and 11 mM D-glucose (pH 7.4) at 32 °C for 30 min and then at room temperature (25 °C) for an additional 30 min before recording. Patch pipettes had resistance ranging from 3 to 6 MΩ.

For current-clamp recordings, the internal solution consisted of 60 mM K2SO4, 40 mM HEPES, 4 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 60 mM N-methyl-D-glucamine, 0.5 mM BAPTA, 12 mM phosphocreatine, 2 mM Na2ATP, and 0.2 mM Na3GTP. A currents step protocol was used to evoke action potentials by injecting depolarizing currents steps (1000 ms duration) of increasing amplitude from −30 to 140 pA in 10 pA increments. For voltage-clamp recordings, the internal solution for recording EPSCs contained 130 mM Cs-methanesulfonate, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM CsCl, 4 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 5 mM NMG, 5 mM MgATP, 0.5 mM Na3GTP, and 12 mM phosphocreatine (pH 7.25; 280–300 mOsm)81. Recordings were performed in the presence of 100 μM picrotoxin for sEPSCs, 500 nM TTX for mEPSCs. The control group was perfused with 0.1% DMSO, while the experimental group was perfused with 5 μM permethrin. To inhibit Nav1.6 channels, 12 μM SYM2206 was applied in the experimental group52. All compounds were diluted in external solution to achieve the desired concentrations. All recordings were conducted in whole-cell configuration using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Axon, USA) with 10 kHz digitization and a 2 kHz low-pass Bessel filter. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using pCLAMP 11.1 software (Molecular Devices Co., SanJose, CA, USA).

Sodium current recording

For sodium currents detection, the extracellular recording solution consisted of 140 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Glucose, 10 mM HEPES, 30 mM TEACl, 0.5 mM CdCl (310 mOsm L−1, pH 7.30)82. The cells were held at −90 mV, and the current–voltage (I–V) relationships were obtained by applying 50 ms voltage steps from −90 to 45 mV in 5 mV increments. Current density was assessed by normalizing the peak currents to the cell capacitance. The voltage dependence of activation was analyzed by fitting the normalized sodium current (I/Imax) with the Boltzmann function: I/Imax = (1 + exp [(V–V1/2)/k])−1, where V1/2 indicates the voltage at half-maximal activation, and k is the slope factor reflects the voltage sensitivity of the sodium channel. I was the peak current measured at test potential V. The voltage dependence of steady-state inactivation was analyzed by normalizing the current generated by a 20 mV post-pulse voltage step to the maximum current, and plotting this against the pre-pulse voltage, which ranged from −100 to 10 mV in 10 mV increments. The normalized current was then fitted with the Boltzmann function.

Glutamate imaging

The glutamate probe rAAV-hSyn-iGluSnFR(A184S) was purchased from BrainVTA (#PT-1140). On the 10th day of culturing primary mouse neurons, the virus was added at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Infection efficiency was confirmed by the expression of GFP under fluorescence microscopy. After 13 days of in vitro culture, DMSO or 5 μM permethrin was added to the cultures, and time-lapse glutamate imaging was performed 24 h later using a Zeiss LSM900 confocal microscope. Fluorescence images were processed using ZEN 3.3 software, and the average fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image J software.

Statistics and reproducibility

Positive signals were manually scored and independently confirmed in a blinded manner. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.3.1. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with sample sizes (n) provided in the text or figure legend for each experiment. The individual animal was defined as the experimental unit. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired one-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was employed to datasets with a single independent variable, while two-way ANOVA was used for analyses involving two independent variables. Repeated measures ANOVA was performed on the measurements obtained from the same samples at different time points. The probability value P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
