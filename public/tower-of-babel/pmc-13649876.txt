Evaluating AI-Enabled Voice to Text for Medical Affairs Documentation and Insight Capture: The RAPID Randomized Crossover Trial
Authors: Riaz Abbas, Jeongin Son, Marloes Oosterhof, Yosuke Amagai, Jackie Tang, Cheng Zhang, Bin Li, Caidan Luo, Seigo Susa, Ka Weng Mah, Helen Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesArtificial intelligence (AI) transcription systems using large language models (LLMs) and natural language processing (NLP) may reduce documentation burden. Medical science liaisons (MSLs) face challenges documenting scientific interactions with health care professionals (HCPs). This Amgen study evaluated whether AI-enabled transcription improved MSL documentation experience and insight documentation compared with existing practice (EP) of manual customer relationship management (CRM) entry.MethodsThis randomized crossover study enrolled 27 MSLs from six Amgen Asia-Pacific affiliates; 78% used non-English languages during V2T documentation. Participants were randomized 1:1 to voice-to-text (V2T) using Microsoft Copilot then EP, or EP then V2T (Copilot), with four-week study periods. The primary outcome was composite MSL Satisfaction Questionnaire (MSLSQ) score across efficiency, speed to CRM entry, cognitive load, ease of use, and insight structuring. Secondary assessment compared blinded therapeutic area (TA) expert ratings of insight quality. Exploratory analyses assessed language effects, interaction counts, and qualitative feedback.ResultsAll participants completed both periods. Composite MSLSQ scores were higher with V2T (Copilot) than EP (mean difference + 0.763; p = 0.0114, two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA)). Domain-level primary comparisons favored V2T (Copilot) across all five MSLSQ domains, while Wilcoxon sensitivity analyses most consistently supported improvements in speed to CRM entry, cognitive load, and insight structuring. TA expert-rated insight quality and documented interaction counts were similar. Satisfaction was lower among Japanese and Korean-speaking participants owing to lower transcription accuracy.ConclusionsV2T (Copilot) improved documentation experience without improving insight quality or field activity. Limitations related to scientific terminology recognition, multilingual transcription, highlight opportunities to optimize AI-supported documentation in Medical Affairs. V2T (Copilot) improved the composite MSL-reported documentation satisfaction score, with the greatest improvements observed in cognitive load, speed to CRM entry, and insight structuring. Performance strengths included reduced documentation burden and improved structuring, while practical limitations included recognition of scientific terminology, lower transcription accuracy in Japanese and Korean, and lack of native CRM integration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40290-026-00630-0.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) transcription systems leveraging large language models (LLMs) and natural language processing (NLP) have shown promise in reducing the cognitive and administrative burden associated with clinical documentation. Recent evaluations of ambient AI documentation platforms have reported significant decreases in documentation time, improved accuracy and completeness of clinical notes, and reduced clinician cognitive load and burnout, enabling more time for direct patient care [1, 2].

The Medical Affairs function has evolved from a primarily supportive role to a strategic partner within healthcare organizations, with increasing expectations around analytical rigor, compliance, and structured documentation. This evolution has expanded the scope and complexity of the MSL role, increasing the documentation demands placed on field medical professionals [3].

Although these findings demonstrate the potential of AI-assisted documentation in clinical practice, comparable evidence is lacking in nonclinical professional contexts. Within the biopharmaceutical industry, Medical science liaisons (MSLs) engage in nonpromotional, peer-to-peer scientific exchange with health care professionals (HCPs) and are responsible for documenting interactions to support scientific governance, insight capture, and organizational learning [4].

Manual documentation of these scientific exchanges in customer relationship management (CRM) systems remains cognitively demanding, time-intensive, and is often completed after hours under compliance constraints. In pharmaceutical organizations, documentation of scientific exchange serves not only as an operational record but as a foundational input into evidence generation, lifecycle strategy, governance, and compliance. Medical Affairs documentation informs internal decision-making, shapes local and global medical strategy, and contributes to the synthesis of real-world insights that may influence clinical development, regulatory engagement, and post-marketing evidence planning [4]. Variability or delays in documentation therefore have downstream implications beyond individual efficiency, affecting the quality, timeliness, and usability of insights within pharmaceutical systems.

Veeva CRM, launched in 2007, has become the de facto tool for compliant CRM in the life sciences industry, with a > 80% market share in 2023 [5]. As in most companies, Veeva is the CRM platform used at Amgen.

Although Veeva is developing integrated AI features, such as voice control, compliant free text, and the CRM Bot, these functions are not available yet [6]. The availability of Microsoft Copilot within the enterprise Microsoft 365 suite [7] provides an opportunity to evaluate AI-assisted documentation workflows outside the CRM platform.

There is no published evidence describing the use or user experience of AI-assisted transcription among field-based, HCP-facing medical teams in pharmaceutical organizations. Evidence also remains limited on AI transcription performance in non-English or mixed-language settings.

The RAPID (Real-time AI–Assisted Post-Interaction Documentation) study was designed to address this evidence gap. It evaluated two components of AI integration in the MSL workflow: (1) the use of voice to text (V2T) transcription in Microsoft Word and (2) subsequent use of Microsoft Copilot to structure the resulting summaries into potential insights, hereinafter referred to as V2T (Copilot). Other generative AI tools were excluded in accordance with Amgen’s transcription policy. Microsoft Copilot, deployed under Amgen’s enterprise Microsoft 365 license, was selected as a compliant, enterprise-approved solution for AI-assisted transcription.

The study compared V2T (Copilot) with the Existing Practice (EP; manual documentation) to determine whether V2T (Copilot) would enhance MSL satisfaction score while maintaining or improving the quality of insights captured in CRM.

Background and Significance

Documentation workflows represent a critical yet underexamined area of applied health informatics beyond direct clinical care. While AI-enabled transcription and ambient documentation tools have demonstrated benefits in clinical environments [2, 8] far less is known about their performance, usability, and limitations in regulated, non-HCP settings.

In Medical Affairs, documentation differs fundamentally from clinical notetaking. MSL documentation involves scientifically dense language, evolving evidence, contextual interpretation, and the capture of nuanced insights rather than structured clinical data. These characteristics place distinct demands on speech recognition and natural language processing systems and introduce additional governance considerations related to compliance, accuracy, and auditability.

Although Veeva CRM is the dominant platform for compliant documentation in the life sciences industry, it does not currently provide native voice transcription functionality [6]. As a result, organizations rely on manual entry or external tools to support documentation activities. Conversely, enterprise speech recognition capabilities are available within commonly used productivity platforms, such as Microsoft Word through Microsoft 365, creating a practical opportunity to evaluate transcription-first approaches in real-world workflows without direct CRM integration.

Recent publications of AI-assisted documentation tools have largely focused on English-language performance in US-based clinical settings [1, 2, 8]. There remains limited peer-reviewed evidence examining multilingual transcription performance, particularly in environments in which users routinely alternate between English scientific terminology and local languages. This gap is especially relevant in the Asia-Pacific region, where multilingual communication is common and transcription accuracy may vary substantially in bilingual settings [9].

The RAPID study addresses these gaps by providing a systematic evaluation of an AI-assisted, transcription-first documentation approach in a real-world Medical Affairs setting. By examining user satisfaction, cognitive load, insight structuring, language-dependent performance, and workflow implications, this study contributes real-world evidence on the use of enterprise AI tools within Medical Affairs documentation workflows.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This two-period, two-sequence randomized crossover study was conducted among 27 MSLs across Amgen’s Japan and Asia-Pacific (JAPAC) affiliates between July and September 2025.

Participants were randomized 1:1 to one of two sequences: V2T (Copilot) followed by EP, or EP followed by V2T (Copilot).

Each study period lasted 4 weeks and was separated by a 3-day washout interval to minimize potential carryover effects. All participants completed both study periods. The 4-week duration was considered adequate for participants to use and evaluate each documentation method, maintain protocol adherence, and support within-subject comparison of V2T (Copilot) and EP.

During the washout period, MSLs continued routine field interactions and CRM documentation using standard documentation practice. Participants entering the V2T (Copilot) study period received training and onboarding, while participants transitioning to EP had V2T (Copilot) use discontinued. The washout period was also used to reinforce study expectations, participants obligations, and responsibilities.

No formal a priori sample-size calculation or post hoc power estimation was performed. RAPID was designed as an internal randomized crossover pilot enrolling MSLs from the participating affiliates. The study was intended to estimate the direction and magnitude of within-participant differences in user experience and documentation outcomes rather than to serve as a fully powered confirmatory trial.

Inclusion criteria were active in an MSL role for > 1 year, regular use of Veeva CRM, willingness to participate, and a demonstrated record of sustained satisfactory performance during the preceding 12 months of employment at Amgen. Exclusion criteria included extended leave, probationary status, or lack of access to an Amgen-issued device with Microsoft 365.

Intervention and Comparator

V2T (Copilot) Intervention

MSLs captured post-HCP interaction summaries using the Dictate function in Microsoft Word on Amgen-issued devices. HCP conversations were not recorded or transcribed live. After generating each transcription, MSLs reviewed it for accuracy and then used Microsoft Copilot within Word to structure the content before manually entering it into Veeva CRM.

MSLs randomized to V2T (Copilot) received training on the compliant use of AI transcription at the start of the intervention period. Owing to the low awareness of the transcription function and the low utility of Copilot, this training included effective prompt engineering tips to assist with organizing interaction summaries into insights, as well as a Quick Reference Guide and access to technical support throughout the study period.

In accordance with Amgen’s compliance policy, Copilot was approved only for transcription structuring, not for generating an interaction summary. Other generative AI tools were prohibited.

Existing Practice Comparator

Participants completed documentation manually, entering interaction summaries directly into Veeva CRM without the use of any AI tool.

Outcome Measures

Primary Endpoint

The composite MSL Satisfaction Questionnaire (MSLSQ) score was calculated as the mean across five domains (efficiency, speed to CRM entry, cognitive load, ease of use, and insight structuring) rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied).

To our knowledge, there is currently no published or validated instrument designed to evaluate documentation experience for MSLs.

The MSLSQ was therefore developed specifically for this study to provide a pragmatic assessment across five domains considered relevant to operational efficiency and user experience with AI-enabled documentation: efficiency, cognitive load, speed to CRM entry, ease of use, and insight structuring.

Secondary Endpoint

The secondary endpoint was blinded therapeutic area (TA) expert assessment of insight quality using anonymized CRM summaries. Regional medical leads with more than 5 years of relevant TA experience independently assessed summaries using a predefined 5-point Likert framework.

This scale was developed specifically for the study and has not been externally validated. Ratings evaluated three predefined domains: clarity of “what,” depth of “why,” and strategic relevance of “so what.” For each domain, scores of 1, 3, and 5 were anchored to predefined descriptors ranging from poor or limited utility to highly relevant insights.

Medical leads remained blinded to documentation method and summary origin throughout the assessment process. Overall insight quality scores were compared between V2T (Copilot) and EP. (see supplementary information)

Exploratory Analyses

Exploratory analyses included overall user preference at study completion, total interaction counts, qualitative feedback on transcription accuracy and prompting effort, and evaluation of differences on the basis of baseline characteristics and time to CRM entry.

A post hoc review of CRM data was undertaken to explore the number of days between the recorded HCP interaction and entry of the interaction summary in the CRM.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.6.1.

The primary MSLSQ analysis was conducted at the participant level using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with documentation method (V2T [Copilot] vs EP) and MSLSQ domain as within-participant factors.

Domain-level comparisons between V2T (Copilot) and EP were performed using Holm–Sidak-adjusted post hoc tests (alpha = 0.05). Since MSLSQ responses used 5-point Likert-type scales and the sample size was modest, Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests were performed for all domains as nonparametric sensitivity analyses. These sensitivity analyses were used to assess robustness and were not selected post hoc on the basis of normality testing. Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared.

Secondary analyses of TA expert-rated insight quality scores treated deidentified summaries as the unit of analysis. Since V2T (Copilot) and EP summaries were not matched one-to-one by identical HCP target or interaction context, groups were compared using exact two-tailed Mann-Whitney tests. No data imputation was performed.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed as an exploratory descriptive analysis to summarize correlated language-performance measures. Metrics related to transcription accuracy, correction effort, and prompt load were standardized before PCA; variables reflecting burden were reverse-coded where needed so that higher values consistently represented better language-related performance. PCA was performed using the correlation matrix. The first principal component (PC1) was retained as a descriptive composite because it captured the largest proportion of shared variance across the language-performance measures. PC1 scores were used to visualize relative language-performance patterns only; no formal inferential testing was based on PCA because of the small subgroup sample sizes.

Ethics and Governance

The RAPID study was conducted as part of Amgen’s internal AI Accelerator initiative to evaluate emerging AI technologies in a controlled, real-world setting. The purpose of the initiative was to provide MSLs firsthand experience and generate much needed evidence on potential benefits and limitations of AI-assisted capture of interaction summaries and insights alleviating workload.

During the study, Microsoft Copilot was the only AI-enabled tool permitted under Amgen’s enterprise compliance framework. Copilot was implemented in a transcription-first workflow. MSLs dictated post-HCP summaries using the Dictate function in MS word, reviewed the transcript for accuracy, and then used Copilot to structure and format the verified content prior to entry into Veeva CRM.

All AI-assisted transcripts and structured summaries required review and editing by the MSLs prior to CRM entry. MSLs also remained responsible for adverse event identification and reporting in accordance with Amgen’s policies and processes. Final CRM records were maintained in line with existing audit-readiness requirements.

All analysis was conducted under Amgen’s policy for non-human-subjects research. Participants provided written acknowledgement, and all data handling complied with Amgen’s privacy and data-protection policies.

In the Efficiency domain, V2T (Copilot) yielded a higher mean satisfaction score (M = 3.30 ± 1.17) compared with EP entry (M = 2.74 ± 0.81), with a mean paired difference of + 0.56 (95% CI − 0.12 to 1.23). The Holm–Sidak-adjusted post hoc comparison favored V2T (Copilot) (p = 0.0131), while the Wilcoxon matched-pairs sensitivity analysis was directionally consistent but not statistically significant (p = 0.12).

Speed to CRM scores were higher under the V2T (Copilot) condition (M = 3.00 ± 1.39) than under EP (M = 2.26 ± 0.76), with a mean paired difference of + 0.74 (95% CI 0.08–1.40). The Holm–Sidak-adjusted post hoc comparison was significant (p = 0.0011), and the Wilcoxon matched-pairs sensitivity analysis supported this result (p = 0.047), indicating higher participant reported perceived speed to CRM entry.

In exploratory CRM timestamp review, mean time to CRM entry was 9.4 days with V2T (Copilot) and 7.5 days with EP. Results varied among participating countries and no formal statistical analysis was conducted owing to exploratory nature of this analysis.

Reducing Cognitive Load scores were higher with the V2T (Copilot) input (M = 3.41 ± 1.39) than with EP (M = 2.33 ± 0.83), with a mean paired difference of +1.07 (95% CI 0.42–1.73). The Holm–Sidak-adjusted post hoc comparison was significant (p < 0.0001), and the Wilcoxon matched-pairs sensitivity analysis supported this result (p = 0.003).

Ease of Use scores were higher with the V2T (Copilot) input mode (M = 3.41 ± 1.25) compared with EP entry (M = 2.96 ± 1.16), with a mean paired difference of + 0.44 (95% CI − 0.23 to 1.12). The Holm–Sidak-adjusted post hoc comparison favored V2T (Copilot) (p = 0.0287), while the Wilcoxon matched-pairs sensitivity analysis was directionally consistent but not statistically significant (p = 0.17).

Insight Structuring scores were higher with V2T (Copilot) (M = 3.41 ± 1.50) than with EP (M = 2.41 ± 1.12), with a mean paired difference of + 1.00 (95% CI 0.25–1.75). Both the Holm–Sidak-adjusted post hoc comparison (p < 0.0001) and the Wilcoxon matched-pairs sensitivity analysis (p = 0.011) supported improved insight structuring with V2T (Copilot).

No correlations were observed between satisfaction scores and baseline characteristics, including AI fluency or tenure at Amgen. The number of interactions recorded in Veeva CRM also did not differ between the V2T (Copilot) and EP study periods, indicating that use of AI did not affect the overall number of documented HCP interactions during the study period.

Language Effects

Post hoc analyses identified lower satisfaction scores among Japanese (n = 8) and Korean (n = 3) participants for V2T (Copilot), consistent with reduced speech recognition accuracy in those languages.

Transcription accuracy was particularly poor in mixed-language scenarios. Participants from Japan and Korea reported that in real-world discussions, they frequently alternated between English and their native language, especially when referencing clinical or scientific terms. Under these conditions, AI transcription accuracy declined substantially (< 50%), resulting in increased manual correction effort and reduced perceived efficiency.

User Preference and Qualitative Feedback

At study completion, 67% (17/27) of participants preferred V2T (Copilot) over EP, citing reduced cognitive burden and greater overall convenience. Preference was 100% (7/7) among participants from Australia (2/2), Taiwan (3/3), and Southeast Asia (2/2), but substantially less for those from Korea 33% (1/3) and Japan 25% (2/8), primarily owing to poor transcription accuracy.

Despite higher satisfaction scores, participants identified several limitations of the V2T (Copilot) method, the most cited being accuracy for scientific and technical terms, including drug names, protocol identifiers, and abbreviations such as “HCP,” which were often misrecognized or substituted with unrelated words, requiring editing efforts.

Microsoft Word Dictate supports multiple languages and separates them into “Supported Languages” and “Preview languages.” English, Mandarin, and Japanese are listed as Supported Languages, while Korean is listed as a Preview language [7]. This distinction does not correspond to any published accuracy data or benchmarking methodology. Microsoft provides no quantitative word error rate (WER) by language and merely cautions that “Preview languages may have lower accuracy or limited punctuation support” [10].

In this study, reported transcription accuracy varied by language. English and Mandarin showed higher reported accuracy (> 90%), whereas Japanese and Korean showed lower reported accuracy, at approximately 50%. Japanese therefore showed lower reported transcription accuracy than English and Mandarin despite being listed in the same Supported-language category, and performed similarly to Korean, which is listed as a Preview language.

Since V2T (Copilot) use was performed outside the CRM, some participants described this extra step as a gap and therefore a potential area for optimization to further reduce administrative burden.

Finally, several participants observed that although V2T (Copilot) reduced keystroke burden, it increased the time required for content reorganization and needed multiple prompt revisions, leading to a high prompt load in structuring and summarizing interaction into insightful information.

Some participants also commented that the 4-week exposure period may have been insufficient to build proficiency with the V2T (Copilot) system; while others suggested this had a snowball effect, with more MSLs using this strategy in their workflows.

Discussion

Principal Findings

This randomized crossover study demonstrates that V2T (Copilot) can enhance the documentation experience of MSLs in a diverse, real-world setting. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate an enterprise-level AI tool within the life sciences industry.

Across the study population, MSLs reported higher overall satisfaction with V2T (Copilot) compared with EP, driven by reduced cognitive load, improved structuring of interaction summaries, and faster perceived speed to CRM. These findings are consistent with reports from ambient AI scribe studies in clinical practice, which have shown reduced documentation burden, lower clinician burnout, and increased time for meaningful work [1, 2, 8].

Although participants reported significantly faster perceived “Speed to CRM” on the post period survey (V2T [Copilot] versus EP, mean = 3.00 versus 2.26; Holm–Sidak-adjusted post hoc p = 0.0011; Wilcoxon sensitivity p = 0.047), post hoc CRM timestamp analysis did not show a corresponding reduction in measured time to CRM entry. Mean time from HCP interaction to CRM entry was 9.4 days with V2T (Copilot) and 7.5 days with EP, although no formal statistical comparison was performed and results varied across participating countries.

This discrepancy indicates that while participants perceived documentation to be faster with V2T (Copilot) compared with EP, objective CRM data did not demonstrate a time-saving effect. This pattern likely reflects the absence of native voice transcription functionality within Veeva CRM, which required transcription and structuring to occur externally in Microsoft Word before being copied and pasted into the CRM system. This additional workflow step is not captured by self-reported measures and may have contributed to the observed perception–reality gap. Similar discrepancies between perceived and actual efficiency have been reported in other AI-enabled workflow evaluations, including studies showing overestimation of productivity gains despite longer task completion times. Becker et al. reported that software developers overestimated efficiency gains when using AI tools despite longer actual completion times [11].

This study provides real-world evaluation of AI-assisted documentation in a multilingual, technically complex real-world setting, highlighting both its benefits and its limitations.

The crossover design enabled within-participant comparisons across all five MSLSQ domains. In the primary repeated-measures ANOVA framework, V2T (Copilot) showed statistically significant domain-level improvements across the five MSLSQ domains; Wilcoxon sensitivity analyses most consistently supported reduced cognitive load, perceived speed to CRM entry, and insight structuring. Importantly, these improvements did not translate into higher expert-rated insight quality, suggesting that V2T (Copilot) supported how interaction summaries were captured and structured rather than improving the scientific quality of the documented insights. This finding further reinforces that communication skills, scientific expertise, and contextual understanding remain critical to the MSL role in the era of generative AI.

This distinction is important because high-quality insight documentation depends on more than the documentation tool. It is shaped by the quality of the scientific exchange itself, including the MSL’s scientific judgment, therapeutic area expertise, interpersonal skills, communication style, and questioning technique. This interpretation is consistent with Medical Affairs literature identifying strategic insight generation, communication skills, relationship building, and engagement with external experts as core Medical Affairs capabilities [3, 4]. Future studies should prospectively capture these human and contextual factors to better distinguish the contribution of AI-assisted documentation from the capabilities that shape the depth, relevance, and actionability of insights.

Language Performance and Technical Observations

Language-dependent variability was a major determinant of user experience. Microsoft’s conversational speech recognition research has reported a 5.1 % WER on the NIST 2000 Switchboard conversational speech benchmark, a recognition accuracy level that has been characterized as approaching human parity for conversational speech tasks [12].

Microsoft suggests their Supported and Preview language classifications correspond to differing levels of model maturity and reliability, with Supported languages generally demonstrating higher accuracy and Preview languages representing models still under development.

In this study, these classifications did not predict real-world performance. Although Japanese is listed as a Supported language and Korean as a Preview language, both indicated similarly low transcription accuracy (< 50%). By contrast, Mandarin and English achieved > 90% accuracy. These language-specific differences directly affected user satisfaction and perceived efficiency, with Korean and Japanese participants reporting the lowest satisfaction scores and higher correction effort.

Principal component analysis further demonstrated language dependence (Fig. 4). The first principal component (PC1) values varied by language, with Mandarin and English performing the best and Japanese and Korean exhibiting the lowest and most variable scores. Recent evidence from Korean-English bilingual clinical documentation similarly showed high number-only recognition accuracy (88.3–96.8%) but substantially lower complete bilingual phrase recognition (36.7–52.5%), supporting the view that mixed-language speech can reduce transcription performance in real-world documentation workflows [9].

These findings highlight that Microsoft’s Supported versus Preview language classifications may not fully align with real-world performance. Benchmark WER results from general conversational speech recognition and may not translate to documentation using scientific terminology.

These findings suggest that transcription in Microsoft Word may not be the optimal tool for all Medical Affairs documentation workflows or across all languages. Transcription tools trained on scientific terminology, medical dictation, and bilingual natural language processing may offer advantages and should be evaluated before broad-scale adoption.

Workflow and Considerations

Our study reflects the technological landscape at the time of execution. Although Veeva CRM is developing native AI capabilities, these features were not available at the time of the study. Consequently, transcription was conducted externally rather than within the CRM environment.

Future CRM embedded transcription functionality is expected to streamline workflow, remove manual transfer steps, and enable real-time entry, potentially amplifying the efficiency gains observed here.

Although V2T (Copilot) reduced keystroke burden, qualitative feedback from participants and affiliate study leads indicated that additional effort was sometimes required to re-prompt or correct outputs, particularly when handling scientific terminology, complex drug names, or mixed-language dialogue. The effectiveness of AI transcription depends on context, language capability, and workflow integration. Strategic Implications

Findings from this study are relevant to field of Pharmaceutical Medicine in several ways. First, the study provides real-world evidence on end-user experience with AI enabled documentation in field medical, an area where adoption is increasingly influenced by perceived benefits despite limited empirical evidence on actual benefits and limitations. Second, the study highlights implementation considerations that should influence successful AI adoption, including governance, workflow integration, native-language performance, and effective user training, before organizations can expect meaningful return on investment.

Reducing administrative workload through AI-assisted documentation may enable MSLs to devote more time to higher-value activities such as scientific exchange, evidence generation, and relationship building with HCPs.

The number of recorded HCP interactions did not differ between the V2T (Copilot) and EP study periods, suggesting that efficiency gains were primarily cognitive and experiential rather than operational. Importantly, absence of increased interaction volume should not be interpreted as absence of benefit. In high-workload Medical Affairs environments, reductions in documentation burden may instead enable MSLs to allocate more time and cognitive capacity toward higher-value activities, such as scientific preparation, evidence interpretation, strategic planning, stakeholder engagement, and cross-functional collaboration. Longer-term evaluations will be needed to determine whether sustained use of AI-assisted documentation tools translates into measurable improvements in productivity, quality of scientific exchange, and broader Medical Affairs impact.

Moreover, successful implementation of enterprise AI tools will require a comprehensive, human-centric strategy that considers local context, language, and workflow variability. Such an approach can ensure that AI technology complements rather than replaces the scientific expertise and relational skills that define the MSL role.

The RAPID study provides real-world evidence on the use of AI-enabled transcription with Microsoft Copilot in the highly regulated and operationally complex setting of Medical Affairs. The consistency between several quantitative and qualitative findings supports positioning enterprise AI tools as assistive technologies that may reduce documentation burden and enable MSLs to focus more on higher-value activities, including scientific exchange, relationship building, and insight generation.

Continued collaboration between industry and technology developers will be essential to close the gap between perceived and actual efficiency and to achieve equitable AI performance across languages and technical contexts.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The sample size was modest (n = 27), and no formal a priori sample-size calculation or post hoc power estimation was performed. RAPID was designed as an internal randomized crossover pilot to estimate the direction and magnitude of within-participant differences in user experience and documentation outcomes, rather than as a fully powered confirmatory trial. Nonsignificant secondary, exploratory, and subgroup findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

The primary outcome relied on the MSLSQ, which was developed specifically for this study and has not been externally validated. As a self-reported satisfaction measure, it may be vulnerable to expectancy, novelty, and social desirability bias. In addition, as with any behavioral crossover study, residual learning or carryover effects may have persisted despite the 3-day washout period.

The study was conducted within a single company using a specific governance framework, and tools that limits generalizability to other organizations, CRM systems, compliance environments, or AI models. Most participants were experienced MSLs with established documentation habits, and prior AI experience was reported but not examined as an effect modifier. Findings may differ among early career MSLs or those trained with AI enabled documentation tools from onboarding.

The CRM timestamp analysis was exploratory and underpowered, limiting conclusions regarding objective efficiency. Transcription accuracy was based on user-reported experience rather than formal word error rate testing or benchmarked accuracy assessment. Language heterogeneity, mixed-language dialogue, scientific terminology, accent variation, and environmental factors were not formally controlled and may have influenced transcription performance, particularly for Japanese and Korean.

Finally, the study evaluated a transcription-first workflow in which MSLs reviewed transcripts and used Copilot only to structure verified content before CRM entry. Findings should not be extrapolated to generative AI use or autonomous generation of interaction summaries. Future studies should include larger multilingual cohorts, validated outcome measures, formal transcription accuracy testing, and integrated CRM environments with robust telemetry.

Conclusions

AI-enabled V2T transcription using Microsoft Copilot was associated with higher overall MSL-reported documentation satisfaction compared with existing practice, particularly through reduced cognitive load, faster perceived speed to CRM entry, and improved structuring of interaction summaries. However, these benefits did not translate into improved expert-rated insight quality or increased documented field activity, indicating that AI-assisted transcription primarily supported the documentation process rather than the scientific quality of insights.

Real-world deployment revealed important implementation limitations, including recognition of scientific and technical terminology, lower transcription accuracy in Japanese and Korean, and additional correction effort with re-prompting.

These findings help inform future scale-up and adoption of AI-enabled documentation in Medical Affairs by identifying key implementation considerations prior to broad deployment. Future adoption should include assessment of multilingual and mixed-language transcription performance, pre-training of AI tools on scientific terminology glossaries, user training, and structured feedback loops for continued learning and improvement.
