Outcomes of micropulse transscleral cyclophotocoagulation using different sweep velocity in the treatment of refractory glaucoma
Authors: Anuwat Jiravarnsirikul, Natticha Jiamjira-Anon, Ngamkae Ruangvaravate, Darin Sakiyalak, Naris Kitnarong, Theerajate Phongsuphan, Wasawat Sermsripong, Sakaorat Petchyim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study compared the efficacy and safety of micropulse transscleral cyclophotocoagulation (MP-TSCPC) using different sweep velocities in refractory glaucoma. Twenty-eight eyes of 28 patients were treated with a slow-sweep protocol (20 s × 4 sweeps per hemisphere, fluence = 104.8 J/cm2), and 29 eyes of 26 patients were treated with a regular-sweep protocol (10 s × 8 sweeps per hemisphere, fluence = 52.4 J/cm2). Patients were evaluated at 1, 3, and 6 months to assess intraocular pressure (IOP), best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), number of medications, and complications. The regular-sweep group achieved significantly greater mean IOP reduction at 1 month (–12.1 vs. − 4.8 mmHg, P < 0.001) and 3 months (–10.5 vs. − 3.6 mmHg, P = 0.003). At 6 months, no significant change in BCVA from baseline was observed in either group (all P > 0.05), while both protocols significantly reduced topical IOP-lowering medication use (all P < 0.05). Complication rates did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.738). Multivariable analysis identified higher baseline IOP (P = 0.005) and the regular-sweep protocol (P < 0.001) as predictors of greater IOP reduction. Introduction

Glaucoma is the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, with approximately 64.3 million individuals affected in 2013 and more than 111 million projected to be affected by 20401. Progressive optic neuropathy in glaucoma is driven primarily by elevated intraocular pressure (IOP)2; therefore, all current therapies—including topical medications, laser therapy, and filtration surgery—aim to lower IOP. For patients with refractory or uncontrolled glaucoma—those with persistently elevated IOP despite maximal medication or prior surgery, or those unable to undergo further incisional procedures—transscleral cyclophotocoagulation (TSCPC) offers an effective, nonpenetrating alternative that ablates ciliary-body tissue, suppresses aqueous humour production, and lowers IOP when conventional therapies are insufficient3.

Traditional continuous-wave transscleral cyclophotocoagulation (CW-TSCPC) delivers continuous 810 nm diode energy to the pars plicata, irreversibly coagulating the pigmented ciliary epithelium, suppressing aqueous-humour production, and producing a marked reduction in IOP4,5. Its non-selective thermal profile, however, is associated with vision-threatening complications such as prolonged ocular inflammation, intraocular hemorrhage, cystoid macular edema, hypotony, visual loss, and phthisis bulbi4,5. As a result, CW-TSCPC is largely reserved for eyes with end-stage or otherwise refractory glaucoma4–6.

With the introduction of micropulse transscleral cyclophotocoagulation (MP-TSCPC; Iridex Corporation, Mountain View, California, United States of America), laser cyclodestruction shifted from a continuous burn to a precise, pulsed application7,8. MP-TSCPC uses an 810 nm diode laser in an on-off delivery mode to selectively ablate the ciliary body while sparing adjacent tissue7,8. Unlike CW-TSCPC, its short pulses with intervening “off” periods dissipate heat and reduce collateral damage7,8. Consequently, MP-TSCPC achieves IOP reductions comparable to CW-TSCPC but with far fewer complications, making it suitable for patients with useful vision rather than a procedure confined to end-stage disease7,9–11.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of MP-TSCPC in lowering IOP in patients with glaucoma. Dervos et al. reported a series of 138eyes in which mean IOP decreased from 24.1 mm Hg to 16.1 mm Hg at two years—an approximately 33% reduction12. Similarly, de Crom et al. observed a sustained mean IOP reduction of 32.5% in 165 eyes at five years13. However, one challenge in appraising MP-TSCPC outcomes is the variation in technique across studies and the lack of uniform reporting of laser settings in early reports14. Several studies have used a wide range of laser parameter combinations (different powers, exposure times, sweep counts, and sweep velocities) making direct comparisons difficult14,15. Optimizing these parameters has therefore become the focus of recent research and clinical consensus efforts in order to maximize IOP reduction while minimizing complications.

Recent evidence suggests that increasing treatment time or slowing sweep velocity tends to enhance the IOP-lowering effect of MP-TSCPC, as slower sweeps deliver higher laser fluence (energy per area) to the target tissue15. In a meta-analysis, Grippo et al. introduced fluence as a key dose metric that incorporates sweep velocity; eyes treated with higher fluence (for example, via slower sweeps) achieved an average IOP reduction of 53%, compared with 36% for lower-fluence (faster-sweep) protocols15. This finding emphasizes that standardizing the delivered fluence, rather than adjusting only exposure time or power, requires treating sweep velocity as an essential component of the dose.

Given the wide variation in laser parameter settings reported in earlier studies and the fact that many prior publications did not document treatment sweep velocity14,15, there are, to our knowledge, only a limited number of studies that have assessed the efficacy and safety of MP-TSCPC across different sweep velocities. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the efficacy and safety of MP-TSCPC delivered at two different sweep velocities in eyes with refractory glaucoma, and to investigate whether sweep velocity is associated with IOP reduction.

Materials and methods

Participants

A single-center, prospective study with historical controls of MP-TSCPC performed using the Cyclo G6 laser and the original MicroPulse P3 delivery device (Iridex Corporation, Mountain View, California, United States of America) was conducted. Participants were 18 years or older and diagnosed with refractory glaucoma, defined as IOP remaining above target despite maximally tolerated medical therapy and/or prior failed surgical intervention. Eyes treated with MP-TSCPC using the slow-sweep protocol (4 sweeps at 20-second sweep per hemisphere, sweep velocity 1.1 mm/second) were prospectively enrolled between April 2023 and May 2024, whereas eyes treated with the regular-sweep protocol (8 sweeps at 10-second sweep per hemisphere, sweep velocity 2.2 mm/second) between April 2021 and February 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. This study was approved by the Siriraj Instutitional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand (COA no. Si 244/2023) and the methods employed adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Eyes were classified as glaucomatous if they exhibited repeatable abnormal VF test results, accompanied by signs of glaucomatous optic neuropathy. This was defined as optic disc excavation, focal thinning, notching of the neuroretinal rim, or localized or diffuse retinal nerve fiber layer defects, as assessed through masked grading of optic disc photographs by two independent graders. An abnormal VF test result was defined as at least two consecutive tests showing a pattern standard deviation (PSD) outside the 95% normal confidence limits or a Glaucoma Hemifield Test result outside normal limits. Exclusion criteria included a history of any IOP-lowering intervention within three months before enrollment; ocular pathologies such as scleral thinning, or the presence of a glaucoma drainage device or trabeculectomy bleb that would preclude proper cyclodiode probe application over at least 180° of treatment; and inability to complete a minimum of six months of follow-up.

Baseline parameters and outcome measures

BCVA measured with the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart was converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) units. IOP was measured twice with Goldmann applanation tonometry; when corneal irregularity precluded this method, a Tono-Pen was used. Each measurement involved two examiners: an operator, who adjusted the tonometer, and a masked observer, who read and recorded the IOP. The mean of the two readings was used for analysis.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics included age, gender, ethnicity, glaucoma type and severity (defined by VF MD according to the Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson criteria16, prior glaucoma procedures, lens status, preoperative BCVA, IOP, and glaucoma medications.

Postoperative parameters were assessed at 1, 3, and 6 months after the initial MP-TSCPC treatment. The primary outcome measure was the change in IOP from baseline. Secondary outcomes included: (1) treatment success, defined as an IOP reduction of ≥ 20% from baseline or IOP less than 16 mm Hg with or without anti-glaucoma medications; (2) change in BCVA; (3) number of glaucoma medications; (4) need for additional IOP-lowering procedures; and (5) postoperative complications. Recorded complications included the following: visual loss, defined as a decrease of at least two ETDRS lines or a reduction to no light perception (NLP); hypotony, defined as an IOP ≤ 5 mm Hg; prolonged inflammation, defined as anterior-chamber inflammation exceeding Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN) grade 117 and persisting for more than two weeks despite steroid therapy; corneal edema; hyphema; phthisis bulbi; cystoid macular edema; retinal detachment; sympathetic ophthalmia; and endophthalmitis. Patients who underwent further IOP-lowering procedures after MP-TSCPC were excluded from subsequent outcome measurements to avoid confounding effects on IOP reduction attributable to additional interventions.

Laser procedure

Patients were placed in the supine position. Retrobulbar anaesthesia was administered with 5 mL of a 1:1 mixture of 2% xylocaine and 0.5% bupivacaine, after which a lid speculum was inserted and topical lidocaine eye drops were applied. All eyes were treated with 2,000 mW of 810 nm laser energy at a duty cycle of 31.3% using the Cyclo G6 trans-scleral diode laser and a sterile, single-use, original MicroPulse P3 (MP3) probe. The probe was swept continuously along the limbus, moving back and forth either four or eight times per hemisphere according to the treatment group. The treated arc ranged from 180° to 360°, with the 3- and 9-o’clock meridians avoided to protect the ciliary neurovascular bundle. The average arc length was 22 mm. All procedures were performed by glaucoma specialists.

Total energy was calculated as power × duty cycle × exposure duration, and fluence as power × duty cycle × dwell time ÷ area. Dwell time represents the equivalent stationary pulse duration during which equal energy is deposited per unit area per unit time as scanned delivery. The spot area of the 700 μm probe was 0.0038 cm². Both protocols delivered 50.1 joules (J) of total energy per hemisphere, while fluence was 52.4 J/cm² in the regular-sweep protocol and 104.8 J/cm² in the slow-sweep protocol (Table 1).

Postoperatively, patients were discharged on 1% prednisolone acetate four times daily for two weeks. Pre-operative glaucoma medications were not washed out, and patients were instructed to continue their usual regimen unless advised otherwise. Medications were adjusted at each visit according to the target IOP determined by the treating physician.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as means ± standard deviations with 95% CIs for continuous variables, and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Changes in primary and secondary outcomes were analysed with linear mixed-effects models for repeated measures using an unstructured variance–covariance matrix. The differences in outcomes between the two groups were compared by considering the correlated data of both eyes of patients. Between-cohort differences were evaluated with unpaired t-tests or χ² tests, as appropriate. Factors associated with the percentage reduction in IOP were examined with univariable and multivariable mixed-effects models. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE, version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA). P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Patient ages in both groups ranged from 21 to 87 years, with a mean of 61.2 ± 16.8 years in the regular-sweep protocol and 57.2 ± 19.4 years in the slow-sweep protocol (p = 0.402). In the regular-sweep protocol, glaucoma types were primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) in 5 eyes (17.2%), juvenile open-angle glaucoma (JOAG) in 1 eye (3.5%), primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) in 2 eyes (6.9%), and secondary glaucoma in 21 eyes (72.4%). In the slow-sweep protocol, they were POAG in 5 eyes (17.8%), JOAG in 3 eyes (10.7%), PACG in 1 eye (3.6%), and secondary glaucoma in 19 eyes (67.9%). No significant differences were observed between the two protocols in gender (p = 0.599), ethnicity (p = 1.000), baseline IOP (p = 0.994), baseline best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) (p = 0.309), baseline visual field (VF) mean deviation (MD) (p = 0.419), number of glaucoma medications (p = 0.219), lens status (all p ≥ 0.082), or degree of MP-TSCPC treated arc (p = 0.592). Significantly more eyes in the slow-sweep protocol had undergone MP-TSCPC (7 eyes, 25%) and shunt implantation (14 eyes, 50%) compared with the regular-sweep protocol (1 eye, 3.4% for MP-TSCPC; p = 0.021, and 6 eyes, 20.7% for shunt implantation; p = 0.028). Other prior glaucoma procedures did not differ significantly between the two groups (all p > 0.05). (Table 2).

In the regular-sweep protocol, 29, 29, and 26 subjects remained at months 1, 3, and 6, respectively. In the slow-sweep protocol, 28, 26, and 20 subjects remained at the same time points. In both protocols, subjects who subsequently underwent additional IOP-lowering procedures after month 1 had those subsequent visits excluded from analysis per protocol.

Table 4 presents treatment success, mean BCVA, and the number of topical and oral glaucoma medications at each follow-up time point, stratified by sweep protocol. Treatment success rates were higher with the regular-sweep protocol than with the slow-sweep protocol at 1 and 3 months postoperatively (p = 0.728 and p = 0.209, respectively). At 6 months, the slow-sweep protocol showed higher success rates; however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.729). Compared to baseline, there were no significant changes in mean BCVA for either the regular-sweep protocol (p = 0.238) or the slow‐sweep protocol (p = 0.194) at 6 months. Moreover, mean BCVA did not differ significantly between groups at any time point. Both the regular-sweep and slow-sweep protocols demonstrated significant reductions in topical IOP-lowering medication use at 6 months (P = 0.019 and P = 0.017, respectively). In contrast, the number of subjects requiring oral IOP-lowering medications at 6 months did not differ significantly from baseline in the regular-sweep (P = 0.392) or slow-sweep (P = 0.223) protocols. Although the mean number of topical IOP-lowering medications tended to be lower in the slow‐sweep group at months 1, 3, and 6, no significant between‐group differences were observed at any time point (all p ≥ 0.133) (Table 4).

When excluding participants who had previously undergone TSCPC from the analysis, the regular-sweep protocol still demonstrated a significantly lower mean IOP at month 1 (p = 0.032) and month 3 (p = 0.019) compared with the slow-sweep protocol (Supplemental Table 1). Treatment success rates, after excluding participants who had previously undergone TSCPC, remained higher with the regular-sweep protocol than with the slow-sweep protocol at all time points, though the differences did not reach statistical significance (Supplemental Table 2). Additionally, no statistically significant differences were observed in the mean BCVA, the mean number of topical IOP-lowering medications, and the number of subjects requiring oral IOP-lowering medications between groups across all follow-up visits (all p ≥ 0.054) (Supplemental Table 2).

Overall complication rates were similar between the two protocols: 17.2% (5 eyes) in the regular-sweep and 21.4% (6 eyes) in the slow-sweep protocol (p = 0.738). One eye in each protocol experienced hypotony. In the regular-sweep protocol, hypotony was transient and resolved within 3 months. In the slow-sweep protocol, hypotony was detected at month 6 in one eye; however, the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up. Four eyes in the slow-sweep protocol experienced a loss of vision of at least two lines; however, these decreases in visual acuity were attributable to other ocular pathologies rather than the laser treatment itself. Specifically, one eye developed diabetic macular edema during follow-up, one was diagnosed with a corneal ulcer, and two exhibited glaucoma progression. In contrast, visual loss in three eyes from the regular-sweep protocol was transient and resolved within 3 months after the MP-TSCPC procedure. Prolonged inflammation was detected in one eye from each protocol; both cases gradually improved with medication within 2 months. (Fig. 2) No participants reported ocular pain immediately after the procedure or at 1 month in either protocol.

Although no intraoperative or postoperative complications were reported, 3 eyes (10.3%) in the regular-sweep protocol and 8 eyes (28.6%) in the slow-sweep protocol required additional IOP-lowering procedures after MP-TSCPC to prevent glaucoma progression. In the regular-sweep group, two eyes underwent glaucoma shunt implantation, and one underwent CW‐TSCPC. In the slow‐sweep group, three eyes underwent CW‐TSCPC, three underwent repeat MP‐TSCPC, one underwent endocyclophotocoagulation (ECP), and one underwent ultrasound cycloplasty (UCP) (Fig. 3).

In univariable analysis, higher baseline IOP (coefficient [95% CI]: 0.98 [0.40, 1.57] per 1 mm Hg increase; p = 0.001) and use of the regular-sweep velocity protocol (coefficient [95% CI]: 19.63 [7.75, 31.52]; p = 0.001) were both significantly associated with greater percent IOP reduction (Table 5). After adjusting for potential confounders in multivariable models, baseline IOP (coefficient [95% CI]: 0.88 [0.32, 1.45] per 1 mm Hg increase; p = 0.005) and regular‐sweep velocity (coefficient [95% CI]: 21.35 [10.57, 32.13]; p < 0.001) remained statistically significant predictors of percent IOP reduction (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that MP-TSCPC significantly reduced IOP from baseline at all time points up to 6 months in eyes with refractory glaucoma, using either the regular- or slow-sweep velocity protocol. Notably, among eyes treated with the original probe in this study, the regular-sweep protocol consistently achieved greater IOP reduction than the slow-sweep protocol, with statistically significant differences observed at the earlier follow-up visits (months 1 and 3). While the regular-sweep protocol demonstrated higher treatment success (months 1 and 3), the slow-sweep protocol showed a trend toward greater reduction in the number of glaucoma medications, although this difference was not statistically significant. However, the complication rates were comparable between the two groups, with no significant change in BCVA from baseline at 6 months and no significant differences between groups at any time point. Importantly, no sight-threatening complications directly attributable to MP-TSCPC were observed. Multivariable analysis further revealed that both higher baseline IOP and use of the regular-sweep protocol were significantly associated with a greater percentage reduction in IOP. These findings underscore the efficacy and safety of MP-TSCPC across both sweep protocols, which together contribute to a better understanding of how to optimize treatment parameters, particularly sweep velocity, to maximize IOP reduction while minimizing adverse effects.

The efficacy of MP-TSCPC in lowering IOP in refractory glaucoma has been well documented in numerous prior studies14,18. While the present study demonstrated a 25.3% IOP reduction at 6 months with the regular-sweep velocity and 22.3% with the slow-sweep velocity, previous reports have shown IOP reductions typically ranging from approximately 10% to 40% of baseline, depending on variations in treatment parameters and patient characteristics14,18. For example, a study by Basto et al. demonstrated a 17.7% IOP reduction at final follow-up using the original probe with a total treatment duration of 80 s per hemisphere (8 sweeps at 10 s per sweep)19, which is comparable to the regular-sweep protocol used in our study. In contrast, Magacho et al. reported a markedly greater IOP reduction of 44.2%, achieved with a substantially longer mean treatment duration of 160–240 s per hemisphere20. Multivariable analysis in the current study revealed that higher baseline IOP was significantly associated with greater IOP reduction, consistent with findings from previous reports21–23. Moreover, the greatest IOP reductions were observed in the early post-MP-TSCPC period across both protocols, and the trend of IOP reduction over time also aligns with patterns demonstrated in prior studies22,24–27.

Fluence, defined as energy delivered per unit area, has recently been introduced as a key parameter that incorporates sweep velocity14,15. A meta-analysis by Grippo et al. demonstrated that eyes treated with higher fluence (e.g., via slower sweeps) achieved an average IOP reduction of approximately 53%, compared to about 36% with lower fluence (faster sweeps)15. An expert consensus panel of glaucoma specialists further suggested that increasing fluence by using slower sweep velocities (i.e., moving the probe more slowly) can enhance IOP-lowering outcomes14. Additionally, a recent study by Checo et al. supports this hypothesis through a multi-cohort analysis utilizing the revised MP3 probe to directly compare different sweep protocols18. The authors found that slower sweep settings were associated with higher treatment success rates and concluded that efficacy tends to improve with longer treatment durations and slower sweep velocities18. These findings indicate that reducing sweep velocity may ultimately lead to a greater reduction in IOP.

However, our study demonstrated an opposing result, showing that the regular-sweep protocol achieved greater IOP reduction. This finding was further supported by multivariable analysis, which identified regular sweep velocity as a significant predictor of a greater percentage reduction in IOP. Several hypotheses may explain this discrepancy. One possibility is the variation in technique across studies, including differences in treatment parameter combinations, such as power settings, duration, and sweep velocity, all of which directly influence MP-TSCPC outcomes and make direct comparisons challenging15. In contrast to the study by Grippo et al.15, in the present study, although the slow-sweep protocol delivered approximately twice the fluence compared with the regular-sweep protocol (104.8 vs. 52.4 J/cm², respectively), it still demonstrated a lower IOP reduction capacity. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors. It is well known that baseline IOP is an important determinant of the magnitude of IOP reduction following any intervention; thus, the lower baseline IOP in our study compared with that of the previous study15 may partially explain the smaller IOP reduction observed. In addition, differences in glaucoma type and disease severity between studies are also known to influence the response to laser treatment. An additional explanation for the discrepancy may relate to the type of probe used. In our study, both the regular- and slow-sweep protocols were performed using the original MP3 probe, whereas recent studies supporting the efficacy of slower sweep velocities primarily used the revised MP3 probe14,18.

The original MP3 probe featured a Gaussian ball fiber tip, which was more efficient in energy delivery28. However, it posed significant safety concerns during treatment, such as the risk of conjunctival damage when the device was swept across the ocular surface14. In contrast, the revised MP3 probe was designed to address these limitations. Although it sacrifices some energy delivery efficiency, the recessed laser tip and more posteriorly directed energy delivery design improve safety by minimizing the risk of conjunctival injury and reducing energy absorption by goblet cells and limbal vasculature14,18. To compensate for the reduced efficiency of the revised probe, higher laser power is typically required to achieve comparable treatment effects. For example, a consensus panel recommended standard initial settings for MP-TSCPC using the revised probe, including 2500 mW power, a 31.3% duty cycle, and four sweeps of 20 s each per hemisphere14, which differ from the parameters used in the present study, where a lower power setting of 2000 mW was applied. Moreover, the fluence calculated from the recommended standard initial settings for MP-TSCPC using the revised probe (approximately 131 J/cm²)14, which was higher than that used in the slow-sweep protocol in our study, together with anatomical variability in the location of target tissues associated with the use of different probes, particularly as the revised probe delivers energy more posteriorly, could also represent potential factors influencing the amount of energy absorbed by the target tissues and may thereby partly account for the inconsistent results observed across studies.

These findings suggest that adjusting sweep velocity may indeed offer a means to modulate delivered fluence and improve treatment success. However, even faster sweep protocols can be effective when compensated by other parameters, such as higher power, longer treatment duration, or the use of different probes. Nonetheless, the parameter settings used with the original probe in both protocols of the present study may not represent the most appropriate approach for achieving optimal IOP reduction. Therefore, further studies directly comparing different sweep velocities and energy settings in a controlled fashion, using both the original and revised probes, are warranted to optimize the treatment regimen for MP-TSCPC.

Although the slow-sweep group included a higher proportion of eyes with prior shunt or MP-TSCPC procedures than the regular-sweep group, previous studies indicate that these prior interventions do not compromise laser efficacy29–32. In addition, our analysis excluding those with previous TSCPC still favored the regular-sweep protocol, confirming that repeated TSCPC did not compromise the efficacy of subsequent laser treatment.

A major advantage of MP-TSCPC is its improved safety profile compared to traditional cyclophotocoagulation. Most studies report no cases of phthisis bulbi or significant persistent hypotony following MP-TSCPC, and vision loss directly attributable to the procedure is also considered rare13,18. Consistent with previous reports, the current study observed only mild and self-limiting complications with both the regular- and slow-sweep velocity protocols. Notably, no significant visual acuity loss could be directly linked to the laser procedure, with observed reductions instead attributed to underlying disease progression or unrelated ocular conditions.

Higher fluence from slower sweep velocities may raise concerns about an increased risk of complications. However, evidence from several studies suggests that the overall safety profile remains favorable18,33. Checo et al. reported no increase in complications in slower sweep groups, even when using the revised probe at relatively low total energy levels (under 80 J per eye)18. Similarly, Ramachandran et al. observed no adverse events in the revised-probe group, despite the use of higher fluence delivered through slower sweeps33. The observation that MP-TSCPC maintains a high safety margin across a range of sweep velocities, since slower movements do not appear to introduce additional treatment-related complications, aligns with the findings of our study. In our cohort, complication rates in the slow-sweep group were not increased compared to the regular-sweep group. This may be attributed to the use of similarly low total energy levels in both protocols, despite the difference in sweep velocity. However, these findings may not be directly comparable to studies employing the revised probe, due to differences in probe design and energy delivery characteristics. Therefore, while our results support the overall safety of slower sweep protocols under low-energy conditions, caution is still warranted when adjusting other parameters, such as increasing power or treatment duration, to avoid excessive total energy delivery.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design of the regular-sweep protocol limits causal inference and introduces potential selection bias and missing data. Second, the relatively short follow-up period may be insufficient to fully assess the long-term efficacy and safety of MP-TSCPC. Third, the anterior chamber reaction during the early postoperative period was not recorded, which may limit the assessment and comparison of early postoperative inflammation between the two protocols. Finally, the small sample size and inclusion of only Asian patients may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future prospective randomized controlled studies with larger, more diverse populations and longer follow-up, analyzing the effects of MP-TSCPC using both the original and revised probes with standardized settings, are needed to validate our findings and determine the optimal parameters for different glaucoma subtypes.

In conclusion, both regular- and slow-sweep velocity protocols of MP-TSCPC effectively reduced IOP and demonstrated comparable safety profiles in eyes with refractory glaucoma. Our findings further support the importance of exploring and standardizing optimized treatment parameters and techniques, with an emphasis on tailoring the “dose” through adjustments in sweep velocity and other settings to balance efficacy and safety for each patient.
