Higher characteristic classes of multisingularity loci
Jakub Koncki, Richárd Rimányi

Abstract
. A map between manifolds induces stratifications of both the source and the target according to the occurring multisingularities. In this paper, we study universal expressions—called higher Thom polynomials—that describe the Segre–Schwartz–MacPherson class of such multisingularity loci. We prove a Structure Theorem reducing these Thom polynomials to the data of a linear series associated with each multisingularity. The series corresponding to the empty multisingularity, referred to as the Master Series, plays a distinguished role. Motivated by connections with geometric representation theory, we further prove an Interpolation Theorem that allows Thom polynomials to be computed algorithmically within Mather’s range of nice dimensions. As an application, we derive an explicit formula for the image Milnor number of quasihomogeneous germs, providing one side of the celebrated Mond conjecture, computable up to the theoretical bound.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Multisingularity loci

A complex algebraic map
f:M\to N
between complex manifolds induces stratifications of both
M
and
N
by
multisingularities
. A multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}=\{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{r}\}
is a multiset of singularity types, and the corresponding
target locus

\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subset N

consists of points having exactly
r
preimages where
f
has singularities of types
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{r}
. The nonsingular behavior, denoted
A_{0}
, is regarded as a singularity type; for example,
\Sigma^{T}_{{A_{0},A_{0}}}(f)
is the locus of ordinary double points. With the notations
A_{2}=\mathbb{C}[t]/(t^{3})
and
I_{23}=\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(xy,x^{2}+y^{3})
, the locus
\Sigma^{T}_{{A_{0},A_{2},I_{23}}}(f)
consists of points of
N
having three preimages: one nonsingular and two with the indicated local algebras. The algebraic encoding of singular behavior will be recalled in Section
2
.

The superscript
T
indicates that
\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)
lies in the
target
; the corresponding
source locus

\Sigma^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subset M
is defined analogously for multisingularities with one distinguished component.

1.2.
Universal polynomials: the Thom principle

A central aim of global singularity theory is to express characteristic classes of the loci
\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)
and
\Sigma^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)
in terms of characteristic classes associated with the map
f
, defined by

1+c_{1}(f)+c_{2}(f)+\cdots=\frac{f^{*}(c(TN))}{c(TM)},\qquad s_{\lambda}(f)=f_{*}\left(\prod_{i}c_{\lambda_{i}}\right).

The guiding
Thom principle
(see Section
3
) — a theorem, conjecture, or heuristic depending on context — asserts that for any characteristic class theory
cl
applicable to subvarieties, there exist universal polynomials
P_{\underline{\eta}}
and
Q_{\underline{\eta}}
, depending only on the multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}
, such that for suitable maps
f
,

cl\left(\Sigma^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subset M\right)=P_{\underline{\eta}}\left(c_{i}(f),f^{*}(s_{\lambda}(f))\right),\qquad cl\left(\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subset N\right)=Q_{\underline{\eta}}\left(s_{\lambda}(f)\right).

The strength of this principle lies in the
universality
of
P_{\underline{\eta}}
and
Q_{\underline{\eta}}
: they are independent of the specific map
f
, depending only on
{\underline{\eta}}
. Since the characteristic classes
c_{i}(f)
and
s_{\lambda}(f)
are often readily computable (e.g. homotopy invariants of
f
), the Thom principle provides a powerful bridge from these accessible data to the intricate geometry of multisingularity loci.

A classical example of the Thom principle is the (target)
double point formula

[\overline{\Sigma^{T}_{{A_{0},A_{0}}}(f)}]=(s_{0}^{2}-s_{l})(f)\in H^{*}(N),

valid for sufficiently nice maps
f:M^{m}\to N^{m+l}
.
Here the fundamental cohomology class of the closure of the double point locus is expressed by the
target Thom polynomial

s_{0}^{2}-s_{l}
.

1.3.
Segre–Schwartz–MacPherson class

In this paper we consider a refinement of the notion of fundamental class: the
Segre–Schwartz–MacPherson
(or
\operatorname{ssm}
) class

\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma\subset N)=[\overline{\Sigma}]+\text{higher order terms}\quad\in H^{*}(N),

associated with a (possibly singular or non-closed) subvariety
\Sigma\subset N
of a smooth ambient space
N
.

The concept of
\operatorname{ssm}
classes has two origins.
First, up to normalization and suitable identifications, they can be viewed as motivic analogues of the total Chern class of the tangent bundle, extending this notion to singular varieties. This perspective traces back to the foundational works of Deligne, Grothendieck, MacPherson, and Schwartz; see the surveys in Chapters 5-7 of
[
CMTS22
]
.
Second, they are closely related to the stable envelope characteristic classes of Maulik and Okounkov
[
MO19
]
, central to geometric representation theory; the connection between stable envelopes and
\operatorname{ssm}

classes has been developed in
[
RV18
,
FR18
,
AMSS23
]
.

To illustrate the additional information carried by
\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma)
beyond the fundamental class, note that when
N
is a projective space, the class
[\overline{\Sigma}]
determines the degree of
\overline{\Sigma}
, which may be interpreted as the Euler characteristic of a general linear section of
\Sigma
of complementary dimension. In contrast,
\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma)
encodes the Euler characteristics of general linear sections of
\Sigma
of all dimensions
[
Ohm03
,
Alu13
]
.

The Thom principle for
\operatorname{ssm}
-classes of monosingularities — defining the first SSM-Thom polynomials — was introduced in
[
Ohm06
, Ch. 5]
; see also
[
Ohm16
]
. Real, mod 2, analogues of
\operatorname{ssm}
classes in singularity theory are explored in
[
FM25
]
.

1.4.
SSM–Thom polynomials for multisingularities

In what follows, we establish (in certain cases) and conjecture (in others) the existence of
SSM–Thom polynomials
of multisingularities, denoted

{\operatorname{Th}^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}}},\quad{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}.

These are universal power series depending only on the multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}
, not on the map
f
, and they express the
\operatorname{ssm}
classes of the source and target multisingularity loci in terms of the characteristic classes of
f
.

Our first main result is a
Structure Theorem
for
{\operatorname{Th}^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}
and
{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}
: Corollaries
6.6
,
6.7
.
The key observation is that the typically intricate high-degree expressions for
{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}
can be encoded compactly. Specifically, for every multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}
there exists a formal power series

S_{{\underline{\eta}}}\in\mathbb{Q}[[s_{\lambda}]],

linear in the variables
s_{\lambda}
, such that

(1)

\boxed{\sum_{{\underline{\eta}}}\frac{{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}}{|\operatorname{Aut}({\underline{\eta}})|}t^{\underline{\eta}}=\exp\bigg(\sum_{{\underline{\eta}}}\frac{S_{{\underline{\eta}}}}{|\operatorname{Aut}({\underline{\eta}})|}t^{\underline{\eta}}\bigg)\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{s}},{\underline{t}}]],}

(and see Corollary
6.11
for the source version).
Here
t^{\underline{\eta}}
denotes monomials in formal variables indexed by monosingularities; and the denominators are explicit factorials reflecting automorphism symmetries of
{\underline{\eta}}
. This structure of SSM-Thom polynomials for multisingularities was conjectured, based on a comment by M. Kazarian, in
[
Ohm16
, Sect. 6.4]
.

Equation (
1
) can also be rewritten as a
recursive
expression for the Thom polynomials
{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}
in terms of the fundamental building blocks
S_{{\underline{\eta}}}
. This structure — whether written in exponential or recursive form — is analogous to that established by Kazarian
[
Kaz03
]
and proved by Ohmoto
[
Ohm24
]
for the classical
fundamental class
Thom polynomials. However, even at the level of formulation, a crucial difference arises in the role of the empty multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}=\varnothing
. After all,

to understand something, the first step is to understand nothing
.

1.5.
The Master Series
S_{\varnothing}

For the classical (fundamental class) Thom polynomials one has
S_{\varnothing}=0
, for trivial reasons. In contrast, for the SSM-Thom polynomials, the non-trivial series
S_{\varnothing}
plays a central role; we therefore call it the
Master Series
.
As follows from (
1
), the Master Series is a fundamental ingredient in the construction of every
{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}
. Or,
to phrase it more philosophically:
emptiness is not void — it is the source of all things, the foundation of existence.

For every positive integer
l\geq 1
there is a Master Series corresponding to the empty multisingularity for maps
M^{*}\to N^{*+l}
. For
l=1
, it is

S_{\varnothing}=-s+\tfrac{1}{2}s_{1}+\tfrac{1}{6}(7s_{2}-2s_{11})+\tfrac{1}{4}(s_{3}-5s_{21}+s_{111})+\cdots,

see Example
8.3
and
[
TPP
]
for further terms and other
l
. Interestingly,
S_{\varnothing}
involves nontrivial denominators which, for
l=1
, appear to coincide with the denominators of the Cauchy numbers of the second kind:
1,2,6,4,30,12,84,24,\ldots
.
By contrast, all computed series
S_{{\underline{\eta}}}
for nonempty
{\underline{\eta}}
have integer coefficients; see e.g. Figure
2
.

1.6.
Computations

The theory of global singularities is inherently
computational
: explicit Thom polynomials often translate into concrete results in enumerative and algebraic geometry, as well as obstruction theory. In Section
8
we describe a method to compute the SSM-Thom polynomials in broad generality. Let us outline its main ingredients.

Our starting point is the
interpolation method
, introduced in
[
Rim01
]
.
Although alternative approaches exist — based on partial resolutions, iterated residues, or nonreductive quotients
[
BS12
,
FR12
,
Bér20
,
BS21
]
, — interpolation remains among the most effective ones for computing classical (fundamental class) Thom polynomials.
However, in its original form it does not extend directly to the
\operatorname{ssm}
setting.

An extension of this method was proposed by Ohmoto, and Ohmoto-Nekarda
[
Ohm16
,
NOa
,
NOb
]
, who reduced the computation of SSM-Thom polynomials to that of
\operatorname{ssm}
classes of certain singular affine varieties. While the latter computations are often difficult in themselves, the method proved efficient for some
{\underline{\eta}}
in low degree.

A further key input comes from
geometric representation theory
, specifically the
Maulik–Okounkov stable envelopes
. These are characteristic classes arising in quantum integrable systems, defined axiomatically. It is known that whenever both stable envelopes and
\operatorname{ssm}
classes are defined, they are closely related.
This suggests that suitable variants of the MO-axioms should characterize SSM-Thom polynomials — a fact established as part of our
Interpolation Theorem
8.2
.

One of the MO-axioms, the
support axiom
, is of geometric nature. Remarkably, it becomes purely
algebraic
in the SSM-Thom setting, manifesting as an interpolation constraint. This is another fact that is part of our Interpolation Theorem.
However, to emphasize the delicacy of the situation, let us note that we do not know of any counterpart of this phenomenon for stable envelopes.

Combining these ideas, our computation of SSM-Thom polynomials for multisingularities (including the Master Series) becomes entirely algorithmic, with no geometric input. The only ingredient needed is the list of monosingularities (55 of them) in the Mather range together with their symmetries.
The resulting formulas are implemented computationally; a selection of data is available at
[
TPP
]
.
For instance,

S_{A_{0}A_{0}}^{l=2}=-s_{2}+(2s_{21}+2s_{3})-(7s_{4}+7s_{31}+3s_{211})+(36s_{5}+37s_{41}+12s_{32}+15s_{311}+4s_{2111})-\cdots.

1.7.
Mond conjecture

We expect that the mentioned Structure Theorem and the Interpolation Theorem will have applications across several areas of geometry. In this paper, we present one such application: a contribution to Mond’s conjecture.

There are important theorems all around mathematics that link the interior features of a function (such as oscillatory behavior) to its exterior geometric or deformation-theoretic properties — that is, how it sits inside an ambient space of functions. Classic examples include the Sturm–Liouville theory and the inequality “Milnor number
\geq
Tjurina number” for complex analytic functions. Mond’s conjecture is an open problem of this same nature.

Let
f
be finite germ
f:(\mathbb{C}^{m},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{m+1},0)
(see Section
9
). In the space of germs, consider the subset consisting of those that become equivalent to
f
after reparametrizations of the source and target. The codimension of this subset is denoted by

\mathcal{A}_{e}\text{-codim}(f)
.

When
m\leq 14
, the germ
f
has a stable perturbation, and its image is homotopy equivalent to a bouquet of spheres. The number of spheres in this bouquet, denoted
\mu_{I}(f)
, is called the
image Milnor number
of
f
. Mond’s conjecture
[
MNB20
, Rem. 8.1]
asserts that

\mathcal{A}_{e}\text{-codim}(f)\leq\mu_{I}(f),

and that equality holds precisely when
f
is quasihomogeneous.

This conjecture remains open, although its analogue for function germs is known and forms a cornerstone of the theory of singularities of functions. At the current stage, even concrete examples supporting the conjecture are of interest — and this is precisely where Thom polynomials can make a contribution. Building on a result of Ohmoto
[
Ohm16
]
, see also
[
PPS21
]
, the information encoded in our Master Series translates directly into a formula for the image Milnor number
\mu_{I}(f)
of a quasihomogeneous map germ. We present this result in Section
9
; it provides an explicit expression for one side of Mond’s conjecture, valid up to the theoretical bound
M(1)=14
.

Remark 1.1
.

As already noted, the appearance of denominators in the Master Series — and consequently in Thom polynomials — remains somewhat mysterious. It appears that the theory of multisingularities may reveal new number-theoretic constraints on certain combinations of characteristic classes of maps. We do not pursue this direction in the present paper; however, we illustrate the nature of possible results in Example
9.6
.

1.8.
Conventions

Throughout the paper we work with even degree cohomology with rational coefficients, and will use the notation
\operatorname{H}^{*}(X)=H^{2*}(X;\mathbb{Q})
. For the Euler class of
\xi
we will write
\operatorname{eu}(\xi)
. In most of the paper we study maps from
m
dimensions to
m+l
dimensions with
l\geq 1
. We call
l
the relative dimension of such maps. At some places, but not in the main theorems, we permit
l=0
. The upper indices
S
,
T
always refer to source and target, that is, domain and codomain.

2.
Singularities

In this section we briefly recall some basic notions of singularity theory, for more details see
[
MNB20
]
.

2.1.
Contact monosingularities

For
m,l\geq 0
let
\E(m,m+l)
be the vector space of germs of holomorhic maps
(\mathbb{C}^{m},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{m+l},0)
. The group of complex holomorphic diffeomorphism germs of
(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{C}^{m+l},0)
of the form

\Phi(x,y)=(\phi(x),\psi(x,y))

where
\psi(x,0)=0
, is called the contact group
\K(m,m+l)
. The group
\K(m,m+l)
acts on
\E(m,m+l)
via its action on the graph. Orbits of the action are called
contact monosingularities
, or simply
singularities
.

The local algebra of a germ
f:(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\mapsto(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m+l})
is defined as

Q_{f}=\OO_{m}/f^{*}\Ideal_{m+l}
, where
\OO_{m}
is the ring of holomorphic function germs at
(\mathbb{C}^{m},0)
and
\Ideal_{m+l}
is the maximal ideal of
\OO_{m+l}
. We will be only interested in finite germs, that is, when this algebra is finite dimensional and can be presented as
Q_{f}=\mathbb{C}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]]/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m+l})
. It is a theorem of Mather
[
Mat69
]
that two germs in
\E(m,m+l)
are
\K(m,m+l)
-equivalent if and only if their local algebras are isomorphic. Hence a (commutative, finite dimensional, local) algebra
Q
as well as
m
and
l
determine a monosingularity
\eta(Q,m,l)
(unless this set is empty).

Remark 2.1
.

In practice, we can replace the vector space
\E(m,m+l)
of germs, and the group
\K(m,m+l)
of germs with their
N
-jets (
N\gg 0
) to obtain
\E_{N}(m,m+l)
and
\K_{N}(m,m+l)
. In this way we obtain an algebraic group acting on a finite dimensional vector space. Our constructions and results do not depend on
N
as long as
N
is large enough, hence by abuse of notation we will not write the subscript
N
.

In most of our considerations the
m
-dependence will be irrelevant, hence we identify singularities
\eta(Q,m,l)
for different
m
’s, and denote the obtained equivalence class by
\eta(Q,l)
, or simply by
\eta(Q)
if
l
is clear from the context. When no confusion arises, we can also simply write
Q
for the singularity
\eta(Q)
. The notation of algebras

A_{k}=\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^{k+1}),\qquad I_{a,b}=\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(xy,x^{a}+y^{b}),\qquad I\!I\!I_{a,b}=\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(x^{a},xy,y^{b})

(for
k\geq 0
,
a\geq b\geq 2
) is standard in singularity theory.
The codimension of
\eta(Q,m,l)
in
\E(m,m+l)
(when the former is not empty) is independent of
m
and is a linear function
\mu l+b
of
l
. Here
\mu
and
b
are numerical invariants of the algebra
Q
; in fact
\mu=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}Q-1
. For example

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\codim(A_{k}\subset\E(*,*+l))&=&kl+k&\text{for }l\geq 0,\\
\codim(I_{a,b}\subset\E(*,*+l))&=&(a+b-1)l+(a+b)&\text{for }l\geq 0,\\
\codim(I\!I\!I_{a,b}\subset\E(*,*+l))&=&(a+b-2)l+(a+b)&\text{for }l\geq 1.\end{array}

2.2.
Multisingularities

Consider singularities for a fix
l\geq 0
.

Definition 2.2
.

•

A T-multisingularity (target-multisingularity)
{\underline{\eta}}
is a finite multiset of singularities. We will use intuitive notation, for example, if
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
denote singularities, then
(\eta_{1}^{5},\eta_{2}^{2})
or simply
\eta_{1}^{5}\eta_{2}^{2}
will denote the multiset containing these two monosingularities with multiplicities 5 and 2.

•

An S-multisingularity (source-multisingularity) is a T-multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}
with a distinguished element, i.e. a pair
(\eta_{1},{\underline{\eta}})
, such that
\eta_{1}
is a singularity,
{\underline{\eta}}
is a T-multisingularity and
\eta_{1}\in{\underline{\eta}}
. We usually omit
\eta_{1}
in notation, when it is clear from the context.

The empty set is a T-multisingularity but not an S-multisingularity. A monosingularity can be regarded as both an S- and a T-multisingularity.

Definition 2.3
.

For a T-multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}=(\eta_{1}^{a_{1}},\dots,\eta_{k}^{a_{k}})
we define a number

|\operatorname{Aut}({\underline{\eta}})|=a_{1}!\cdot a_{2}!\cdot\ldots\cdot a_{k}!,\qquad|\operatorname{Aut}(\varnothing)|=1.

For an S-multisingularity
(\eta_{1},{\underline{\eta}})
we set
|\operatorname{Aut}(\eta_{1},{\underline{\eta}})|=|\operatorname{Aut}({\underline{\eta}}\setminus\eta_{1})|\,,
i.e. for
{\underline{\eta}}=(\eta_{1}^{a_{1}},\dots,\eta_{k}^{a_{k}})
we have

|\operatorname{Aut}(\eta_{1},{\underline{\eta}})|=(a_{1}-1)!\cdot a_{2}!\cdot\ldots\cdot a_{k}!\,.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
{\underline{\zeta}}
and
{\underline{\eta}}
be T-multisingularities.

•

We write
{\underline{\zeta}}\subset{\underline{\eta}}
when
{\underline{\zeta}}
is a submultiset of
{\underline{\eta}}
.

•

We write
{\underline{\eta}}+{\underline{\zeta}}
for the disjoint union of
{\underline{\eta}}
and
{\underline{\zeta}}
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
{\underline{\eta}}=A^{2}_{0}A_{1}
and
{\underline{\zeta}}=A_{0}^{2}
, then
{\underline{\eta}}+{\underline{\zeta}}=A^{4}_{0}A_{1}
.

Definition 2.6

(Multisingularity induced by subset)
.

Let
{\underline{\eta}}
be a T-multisingularity. Introduce an order on it
{\underline{\eta}}=\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{k}\}
, i.e. a bijection
{\underline{\eta}}\simeq[k]
. A subset
I\subset[k]
induces a T-multisingularity

{\underline{\eta}}_{I}=\{\eta_{i}\}_{i\in I}\,.

For an S-multisingularity we choose a bijection
{\underline{\eta}}\simeq[k]
, such that the distinguished element corresponds to 1. Then subsets containing 1 induce S-multisingularities.

Example 2.7
.

Consider the S-multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}=(A_{0},A_{0}^{3}A_{1}A_{2})
with an order
{\underline{\eta}}=\{A_{0},A_{1},A_{0},A_{2},A_{0}\}
. We have

{\underline{\eta}}_{\{2,3\}}={\underline{\eta}}_{\{2,5\}}=A_{0}A_{1},\qquad{\underline{\eta}}_{\{1,2,4\}}=(A_{0},A_{0}A_{1}A_{2})\ (\text{as an S-multisingularity}).

2.3.
Multisingularity loci

The contact group contains the complex holomorphic reparametrization group of the source and the target—the so called ‘right-left’ group. Hence, the following concepts are well defined.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
f:M\to N
be a map of relative dimension
l
.

•

For
x\in M
we write
\eta_{x}
for the singularity induced by the germ
f:(M,x)\to(N,f(x)).

•

Suppose that the preimage of
y\in N
is finite. We write
{\underline{\eta}}_{y}
for a T-multisingularity induced by germs of
f
on all preimages of
y
.

•

For a T-multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}
we consider a subset
\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subseteq N
(‘T-multisingularity locus’) defined by

\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)=\{y\in N|{\underline{\eta}}_{y}={\underline{\eta}}\}\,.

•

For an S-multisingularity
(\eta_{1},{\underline{\eta}})
we consider a subset
\Sigma^{S}_{(\eta_{1},{\underline{\eta}})}(f)=\Sigma^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subseteq M
(‘S-multisingularity locus’) defined by

\Sigma^{S}_{(\eta_{1},{\underline{\eta}})}(f)=\{x\in M|\eta_{x}=\eta_{1}\,,{\underline{\eta}}_{f(x)}={\underline{\eta}}\}\,.

2.4.
Prototypes of singularities, multisingularities

Let
Q=\mathbb{C}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{a}]]/(g_{1},\ldots,g_{a+l})
be a presentation of a finite dimensional algebra with minimal number
a
of generators, and exactly
l
more polynomial relations than generators. We call the germ

g:(\mathbb{C}^{a},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{a+l},0),\qquad(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a})\mapsto(g_{1},\ldots,g_{a+l})

the genotype for
Q
and
l
. The prototype germ for
Q
and
l
is defined as

p:(\mathbb{C}^{a}\times V,0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{a+l}\times V,0),\qquad(x,\phi)\mapsto(g(x)+\phi(x),\phi),

where

V
is a linear complement of

t_{g}(\theta_{a})+g^{*}(\Ideal_{a+l})\theta_{g}\quad\text{ in }\quad\Ideal_{a}\!\theta_{g}.

Here we used the following standard notions of singularity theory:

\theta_{a}=\E(a,1)\text{ (algebra)},\quad\Ideal_{a}=\text{its maximal ideal},\quad\theta_{g}=\text{space of vector fields along $g$},

t_{g}:\theta_{a}\to\theta_{g},\qquad t_{g}(h)=dg\circ h.

Example 2.9
.

Let
Q=\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(x^{2},y^{2})
(isomorphic to
I_{22}
) and
l=1
. The genotype is
g:(x,y)\mapsto(x^{2},y^{2},0)
. For a basis of
V
we can choose

u_{1}:(x,y)\mapsto(y,0,0),\quad u_{2}:(x,y)\mapsto(0,x,0),\\
u_{3}:(x,y)\mapsto(0,0,x),\quad u_{4}:(x,y)\mapsto(0,0,y),\quad u_{5}:(x,y)\mapsto(0,0,xy),

and for the prototype we obtain
p:(\mathbb{C}^{7},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{8},0)

p:(x,y,u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4},u_{5})\mapsto(x^{2}+u_{1}y,y^{2}+u_{2}x,u_{3}x+u_{4}y+u_{5}xy,u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4},u_{5}).

Let
{\underline{\eta}}=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k})
be a non-empty T-multisingularity.
Let
p_{i}:(\mathbb{C}^{b_{i}},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{b_{i}+l},0)
be the corresponding prototypes for
i=1,\ldots,k
. Let
b=\sum_{i}{b_{i}}+(k-1)l
, and define the

Definition 2.11

Nice dimensions, Mather singularities

2.6.
Important classes of maps

Universal counting formulas are expected to only be valid for maps that are stable under perturbations. Namely, define a map
f

stable
, if any closeby map
g
(in the appropriate topology)
f
and
g
are right-left equivalent:
g=\phi\circ f\circ\psi^{-1}
for diffeomophisms
\psi,\phi
of the domain and co-domain. This class of maps is reasonable over the reals, but not over the complexes (think of, for example, maps from a compact manifold to
\mathbb{C}^{n}
). Experience shows that for complex maps the ‘target-local version’ of perturbation and stability are the right substitutes:

Definition 2.12

(Def. 3.4 in
[
MNB20
]
)
.

An unfolding of a map germ
g:(\mathbb{C}^{m},S)\to(\mathbb{C}^{m+l},0)
is a germ
G:(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{C}^{d},S\times\{0\})\to(\mathbb{C}^{m+l}\times\mathbb{C}^{d},0)
of the form
(\tilde{g}(x,u),u)
with
\tilde{g}(x,0)=g
. The unfolding
G=g\times\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}
is called trivial. If all unfoldings of a germ are equivalent (via the natural equivalence on unfoldings) to a trivial unfolding, then it is called locally stable.

Definition 2.13

(Def. 4.4 in
[
MNB20
]
)
.

A map
f:M^{m}\to N^{m+l}
is locally stable if all its induced germs
f:(M,f^{-1}(y))\to(N,y)
are locally stable. The class of locally stable maps of relative dimension
l
will be called
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}
.

We will need some other classes of maps with more restrictions on the induced multigerms.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
0\leq k\leq M(l)
.
Consider maps
f\colon M^{m}\to N^{m+l}
such that, for every
y\in N
, the germ

f\colon(M,f^{-1}(y))\to(N,y)

is (right-left equivalent to) a trivial unfolding of the prototype of some multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}
with
\scodim({\underline{\eta}})\leq k
.
The class of such maps will be denoted by
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}^{M\!a,k}
.
In the case
k=M(l)
, that is, when all Mather multisingularities are allowed, we simply write

\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}^{M\!a}:=\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}^{M\!a,M(l)}.

Maps in
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}^{M\!a}
are more manageable: for example, it is clear that for
f\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}^{M\!a}
we have

\codim\left(\Sigma^{S}_{\zeta,{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subset M\right)=\scodim({\underline{\eta}}),\qquad\codim\left(\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subset N\right)=\tcodim({\underline{\eta}}),

independently of
\zeta\in{\underline{\eta}}
. For example, for
l=1
, we have

\codim\left(\Sigma^{S}_{\zeta,A_{0}^{2}A_{1}I_{22}}(f)\subset M\right)=12,\quad\codim\left(\Sigma^{T}_{A_{0}^{2}A_{1}I_{22}}(f)\subset N\right)=13,

for
\zeta
being either
A_{0}
,
A_{1}
or
I_{22}
.

The theory of locally stable maps outside of the nice dimension range is more subtle. However, it is a theorem of Mather that if
m\leq M(l)
then locally stable maps are exactly those in
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}^{M\!a}
.

In this paper, we aim to provide universal counting formulas for maps
f:M\to N
that involve the push-forward map
f_{*}
in cohomology. Hence, we restrict our map classes to those

\mathcal{C}_{l}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{l}^{M\!a,k}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}^{M\!a,k},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{l}^{M\!a}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{l}^{M\!a}

containing only finite morphisms. Then they are proper, hence
f_{*}
is defined.

Besides finite maps between compact manifolds, these classes include, for example, prototypes of Mather singularities even
considered equivariantly
—that we will explain now.

Let
G
be a complex algebraic group (typically a torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{r}
) with two representations
\rho^{S}
and
\rho^{T}
on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
\mathbb{C}^{m+l}
. We say that the group
G
(more precisely the triple
(G,\rho^{S},\rho^{T})
) is a symmetry of the germ
f:(\mathbb{C}^{m},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{m+l},0)
, if for a representative of
f
(that we also denote by
f
) we have

\rho^{T}(g)\circ f\circ\rho^{S}(g^{-1})=f\qquad\text{ for all }g\in G.

Applying the Borel construction (
X\mapsto B_{G}X:=EG\times_{G}X
for a contractible space
EG
with a free
G
-action) to this situation we obtain a map

(3)

B_{G}f:B_{G}\mathbb{C}^{m}\to B_{G}\mathbb{C}^{m+l}.

This map is a fibration over the classifying space
BG
of
G
with fiber
f:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m+l}
. In each fiber the map is right-left equivalent to
f
. In practice, when we are interested in a cohomological identity in a concrete degree, we can approximate the classifying space with a finite dimensional manifold (eg. for
G=\mathbb{C}^{*}
we can use
{\mathbb{C}}\!P^{N}
instead of
{\mathbb{C}}\!P^{\infty}
for large
N
). This way the map
B_{G}f
is a map between manifolds, and is locally just a trivial unfolding of
f
.

If
f\in\mathcal{C}_{l}
and the symmetry satisfies that the equivariant Euler class
\operatorname{eu}(\rho^{S})\in\operatorname{H}^{*}(BG)
is not 0, then the cohomology pushforward
(B_{G}f)_{*}:\operatorname{H}^{*}(B_{G}\mathbb{C}^{m})=\operatorname{H}^{*}(BG)\to\operatorname{H}^{*}(B_{G}\mathbb{C}^{m+l})=\operatorname{H}^{*}(BG)
is defined as multiplication by
\operatorname{eu}(\rho^{T})/\operatorname{eu}(\rho^{S})\in\operatorname{H}^{*}(BG)
, cf. Example
8.1
.

Maps of the type (
3
) have been a central tool in Thom polynomial theory since
[
Rim01
]
. For brevity, we often describe a map
B_{G}f
by saying that we consider
f
“equivariantly” or “in equivariant cohomology.”

3.
The Thom principle

Let
cl(\Sigma\subset M)\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(M)
be a cohomological invariant (a “characteristic class”) associated to (not necessarily closed) subvarieties
\Sigma
of smooth manifolds
M
. Suppose
cl
satisfies a suitable consistency condition with respect to pullbacks. The simplest example is
“fundamental class of the closure”
,
cl(\Sigma\subset M)=[\overline{\Sigma}]
.
The following intuitive statement we call the Thom principle for
cl
:

Thom Principle for Multisingularities.

For any S-multisingularity (respectively, T-multisingularity)
{\underline{\eta}}
, there exists a universal expression
P
—a multivariable polynomial or formal power series—such that, for every ‘suitably nice map’
f:M\to N
between manifolds, the class
cl(\Sigma^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subset M)
(respectively,
cl(\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f)\subset N)
is obtained by evaluating
P
on the characteristic classes associated with
f
.

For detailed discussions of various flavors of the Thom principle and its history, we refer the readers to the recent survey papers
[
Ohm25
,
Rim25b
]
and references therein. Here we just make a few remarks:

•

The Thom principle for monosingularities, and
cl
being the fundamental cohomology class of the closure of
\Sigma
, holds. The polynomial
P
in this case can be interpreted as the
\K(m,n)
-equivariant fundamental class of
\overline{\eta}\subset\E(m,n)
—after careful treatment of the infinite dimensional and non-compact groups and spaces. This is the field of the classical Thom polynomials.

•

The nature of the subjects is calculational. Often even the vague Thom principle is sufficient to calculate the universal polynomial
P
(assuming its existence), and then these universal formulas can be used in enumerative geometry—with ad hoc arguments about their validity.

•

In all instances of the Thom principle, the concept of “suitably nice map” is challenging.

•

There is an alternative approach to the Thom principle, that avoids (or relocates) the discussion on “suitably nice maps”. Namely, redefining the meaning of the class “
cl(\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f))
” in such a way that the Thom principle holds
for all maps
, and then discussing for which maps does the new definition agree with the geometric definition. This program is carried out for the fundamental classes of T-multisingularities in
[
Ohm24
]
.

In this paper we study the Thom principle for multisingularities, for the Segre-Schwartz-MacPherson characteristic class
cl(\Sigma\subset M)=\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma\subset M)
.

4.
Characteristic classes

4.1.
The
\operatorname{csm}
and
\operatorname{ssm}
classes

We present a brief introduction to the Chern–Schwartz–MacPherson (
\operatorname{csm}
) class and the Segre–Schwartz–MacPherson (
\operatorname{ssm}
) class. For a more extensive treatment as well as discussion of the broader topic of characteristic classes of singular varieties, see the survey
[
SY07
]
and monographs
[
Sch05
]
,
[
CMTS22
, Ch. 5-7]
.

For a variety
X
, let
F(X)
denote the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space of constructible functions. We may treat
F
as a functor from the category of varieties (with morphism being the proper maps) to the category of
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces. In
[
Mac74
]
MacPherson constructed a unique natural transformation from
F
to the Borel-Moore homology
c_{*}
,
such that for a compact smooth variety
M
we have

c_{*}(\operatorname{id}_{M})=PD(c_{\bullet}(TM))\in\operatorname{H}^{BM}_{\bullet}(M)\,.

Here
c_{\bullet}(TM)
denotes the total Chern class of the tangent bundle and
PD
is Poincairé duality.
The
\operatorname{csm}
and
\operatorname{ssm}
classes of a constructible subset
X
of a smooth variety
M
are defined as

\operatorname{csm}(X\subset M):=PD(c_{*}(1_{X}))\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(M)\,,\qquad\operatorname{ssm}(X\subset M):=\frac{\operatorname{ssm}(X\subset M)}{c_{\bullet}(TM)}\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(M)\,.\qquad

We omit
M
in the notation when it is obvious from the context. Here are some basic properties.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
M
and
N
be smooth varieties.

(1)

Let
X
and
Y
be disjoint constructible subsets of
M
. Then

\operatorname{ssm}(X\cup Y)=\operatorname{ssm}(X)+\operatorname{ssm}(Y)\,.

(2)

Let
X\subset M
and
Y\subset N
be constructible subsets. Then

\operatorname{ssm}(X\times Y\subset M\times N)=\operatorname{ssm}(X\subset M)\boxtimes\operatorname{ssm}(Y\subset N)\,.

(3)

Let
X\subset M
be a constructible subset and
U\subset M
an open subset. Then

\operatorname{ssm}(X\subset M)_{|U}=\operatorname{ssm}(X\cap U\subset U)\,.

(4)

Let
X\subset M
be an irreducible subvariety of codimension
a
. Then

\operatorname{ssm}(X)_{|r}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ for }r<a\,,\\
[X]&\text{ for }r=a\,.\end{cases}

Here
|_{r}
denotes the restriction to
\operatorname{H}^{r}(M)
and
[X]
denotes the fundamental class of
X
.

(5)

Let
X\subset M
be a constructible subset. Suppose that the variety
M
is projective. Then the
\operatorname{csm}
class of
X
determines the Euler characteristic of
X

\pi_{*}(\operatorname{ssm}(X)\cdot c_{\bullet}(TM))=\pi_{*}(\operatorname{csm}(X))=\chi(X)\,,

where
\pi
denotes the unique map from
M
to a point.

For a variety equipped with an action of an algebraic group
G
, the
G
-equivariant version of the MacPherson transformation and the
\operatorname{csm}
class were introduced by Ohmoto in
[
Ohm06
]
. The properties listed in Proposition
4.1
generalize to this equivariant setting.
In Section
8
we will need the following result.

Proposition 4.2

(Special case of
[
Web12
, Thm. 20]
)
.

Let
V
be a finite dimensional vector space equipped with a linear action of a torus
\mathbb{T}
. Suppose that
0
is an isolated fixed point. Let
X\subset V
be a constructible subset such that
0\notin X
. Then the top degree part of the class
\operatorname{csm}(X)
vanishes, i.e.

\operatorname{csm}(X)_{|\dim V}=0\in\operatorname{H}^{*}_{\mathbb{T}}(V)\,,

where
|_{\dim V}
denotes the component in
\operatorname{H}^{\dim V}_{\mathbb{T}}(V)
.

In certain situations, interpolation properties of this kind — together with normalization and support axioms — uniquely determine the
\operatorname{csm}
class. This viewpoint originates in
[
MO19
]
with the introduction of characteristic classes called
stable envelopes
and their relation to
\operatorname{csm}
classes established in
[
RV18
,
FR18
,
AMSS23
]
. In
[
Rim25a
]
, this stable–envelope–inspired framework was used to compute SSM–Thom polynomials of monosingularities. In Section
8
, we extend this approach to multisingularities.

Remark 4.3
.

Proposition
4.2
demonstrates how the hierarchy of strata, ordered by closure containment, can interact with characteristic classes. In the forthcoming work
[
KR25
]
, we will build on a similar such result from
[
FP09
]
, together with Thom polynomials of multisingularities, to establish a new and previously unknown hierarchy.

4.2.
Chern and Landweber-Novikov classes associated with maps

For partitions
\lambda
we use both the traditional notation

\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{r}),

and the ‘multiplicity’ notation

\lambda=(1^{a_{1}},2^{a_{2}},\dots)

meaning that
a_{i}
of the parts of
\lambda
are
i
. E.g.
\lambda=(4,3,3,2,1,1,1)=(1^{3}2^{1}3^{2}4^{1})
.

Definition 4.4
.

For a smooth map
f:M^{m}\to N^{m+l}
let
T_{f}=f^{*}TN-TM
be its relative tangent bundle.

(1)

Chern classes
c_{\bullet}(f):=c_{\bullet}(T_{f})
are defined by the formula

1+c_{1}(f)+\dots=\frac{1+f^{*}c_{1}(TN)+\dots+f^{*}c_{n}(TN)}{1+c_{1}(TM)+\dots c_{m}(TM)}\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(M)\,.

For a partition
\lambda
we consider the class
c_{\lambda}(f)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}c_{\lambda_{i}}(T_{f})
.

(2)

If
f_{*}
is defined in cohomology, the Landweber-Novikov classes
s_{\lambda}(f):=s_{\lambda}(T_{f})
are

s_{\lambda}(f):=f_{*}c_{\lambda}(f)\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(N).

Let
{\underline{c}}=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots)
denote a set of variables indexed by natural numbers and
{\underline{s}}=(s_{\lambda})
a set of variables indexed by partitions. We consider the rings of power series in these variables:
\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{c}},{\underline{s}}]]
and
\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{s}}]]
. There are two natural gradings on these rings. The cohomological grading is given by

\deg(c_{t})=t\,,\qquad\deg(s_{\lambda})=l+|\lambda|=l+a_{1}+2a_{2}+\dots\,,

where
\lambda=(1^{a_{1}},2^{a_{2}},\ldots)
.
When we say that an element is homogeneous we refer to this grading. The second grading is the
{\underline{s}}
-degree
\deg_{s}
—it is the degree in
{\underline{s}}
-variables, i.e.

\deg_{s}(c_{i})=0
,
\deg_{s}(s_{\lambda})=1
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
f:M\to N
be a map in
\mathcal{C}_{l}
.

(1)

For a power series
P\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{s}}]]
we define the class

P(f)\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(N)

by the substitution

s_{\lambda}\to s_{\lambda}(f)\,.

(2)

For a power series
P\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{c}},{\underline{s}}]]
we define the class

P(f)\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(M)

by the substitution

c_{t}\to c_{t}(f)\,,\qquad s_{\lambda}\to f^{*}s_{\lambda}(f)\,.

Both substitutions are homogeneous with respect to the cohomological grading.

Proposition 4.6
.

For every polynomial
A\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{c}}]]
there exists a map
f\in\mathcal{C}_{l}
such that
A(f)\neq 0
. The same holds for a polynomial
B\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{s}}]]
, or
C\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{c}},{\underline{s}}]]
.

Proof.

See Propositions
10.1
,
10.2
and
10.6
in Appendix
10
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

\mathcal{C}_{l}

\displaystyle\Delta^{*}\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f\,\dot{\sqcup}\,g))

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\underline{\eta}}_{1}+{\underline{\eta}}_{2}={\underline{\eta}}}\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}_{1}}(f))\cdot\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}_{2}}(g))\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{*}\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}}(f\,\dot{\sqcup}\,g))

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\underline{\eta}}_{1}+{\underline{\eta}}_{2}={\underline{\eta}}\,,\eta\in{\underline{\eta}}_{1}}\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma^{S}_{{\underline{\eta}}_{1}}(f))\cdot f^{*}\operatorname{ssm}(\Sigma^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}_{2}}(g))\,.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
f:M_{1}\to N_{1}
and
g:M_{2}\to N_{2}
be maps in
\mathcal{C}_{l}
. We have:

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}(f\,\dot{\sqcup}\,g)=\pi_{1}^{*}s_{\lambda}(f)+\pi_{2}^{*}s_{\lambda}(g)

\displaystyle c_{k}(f\,\dot{\sqcup}\,g)_{|M_{1}\times N_{2}}=\tilde{\pi}_{1}^{*}c_{k}(f)\,,

where

is the standard projection.

Proof.

The statement follows from isomorphism of relative tangent bundles
T_{f\,\dot{\sqcup}\,g}|_{M_{1}\times N_{2}}=\tilde{\pi}^{*}T_{f}

∎

5.
SSM-Thom polynomials for multisingularities

In the rest of the paper we assume that

Conjecture 5.1
.

The Thom principle holds for
\operatorname{ssm}
classes of multisingularities.

T:

For every T-multisingularity

{\underline{\eta}}

In Section

Remark 5.2

In Mather’s nice dimension range we consider a degree-cut version of Conjecture

In this section we assume Conjecture

Theorem 6.1
.

Thom polynomial
{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{\varnothing}}
is an invertible element of
\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{s}}]]
. There exists a linear power series

We call the series

Definition 6.2

For a nonempty T-multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}
we define a power series

as the
{\underline{s}}
-linear part of the quotient

Remark 6.3
.

For a nonempty multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}
the degree zero part of the Thom polynomial

is equal to zero. This has easy direct proof, or may be deduced from the Theorem

below. It follows that
S_{{\underline{\eta}}}
is also the
{\underline{s}}
-linear part of
{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
{\underline{\eta}}
be a nonempty T-multisingularity. Introduce an order on it, i.e.
{\underline{\eta}}=\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{k}\}
. We have

{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}=\sum_{1\in I\subset[k]}S_{{\underline{\eta}}_{I}}\cdot{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}_{I^{\prime}}}}\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\underline{s}}]]\,,

where
I^{\prime}
is the complement of
I
in
[k]
.

Example 6.5
.

For a monosingularity
{\underline{\eta}}=\{\eta\}
we have

{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{\eta}}=\exp(S_{\varnothing})\cdot S_{\eta}\,.

For
{\underline{\eta}}=A^{2}_{0}
:

{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{A^{2}_{0}}}=\exp(S_{\varnothing})\cdot\left(S_{A^{2}_{0}}+S_{A_{0}}^{2}\right)\,.

For
{\underline{\eta}}=A^{3}_{0}
:

{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{A^{3}_{0}}}=\exp(S_{\varnothing})\cdot\left(S_{A^{3}_{0}}+3S_{A^{2}_{0}}S_{A_{0}}+S_{A_{0}}^{3}\right)\,.

Corollary 6.6
.

Theorem
6.4
may be written in the equivalent forms:

Closed formula:

Fix a bijection
{\underline{\eta}}\simeq[k]
. Consider all decompositions of the set
[k]
into nonempty subsets, do not distinguish between decompositions which vary by a permutation of subsets. Then

{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{{\underline{\eta}}}}=\exp(S_{\varnothing})\cdot\sum_{[k]=I_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup I_{r}}S_{{\underline{\eta}}_{I_{1}}}\cdot S_{{\underline{\eta}}_{I_{2}}}\cdot\ldots\cdot S_{{\underline{\eta}}_{I_{r}}}\,.

Generating function:

To every singularity

Lemma 6.14

8.
Interpolation

In this section, we reformulate Conjecture
5.5
into a computationally testable statement, one that can be verified algorithmically. This approach, together with explicit computer calculations, yields the desired SSM-Thom polynomials of multisingularities, computed up to a prescribed degree at most the Mather bound.

8.1.
Prototypes of singularities with maximal symmetry

Let

Example 8.1

Let

•

9.
Application to Mond’s conjecture

9.1.
Mond’s conjecture

Let
f:(\mathbb{C}^{m},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{m+1},0)
be a map germ in the Mather region. Explicitly, this means that
m\leq M(1)=14
. The celebrated Mond conjecture (for a history see
[
MNB20
, Rem. 8.1]
) compares two invariants associated with such a germ: image Milnor number and
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension.

The
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension of the germ
f
, denoted
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-
\codim(f)
, is one of the standard notions of singularity theory. It is defined as a dimension of a certain vector space associated to the germ
f
, see
[
MNB20
, Def. 3.6 and Cor. 3.2]
.
A germ is
\mathcal{A}
-finite if it has a finite
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension. It is stable if and only if its
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension is zero.

Let
f_{t}
be a stable perturbation of
f
, see
[
MNB20
, Sect. 8.3]
for a precise definition. The image Milnor number
\mu_{I}(f)
describes the geometry of its image. This image has the homotopy type of a wedge of
m
-spheres
[
MNB20
, Prop. 8.3]
. The image Milnor number
\mu_{I}(f)
is the number of spheres, alternatively

(13)

\displaystyle\mu_{I}(f):=(-1)^{m}(\chi(\operatorname{im}f_{t})-1)\,.

The Mond conjecture states that for an
\mathcal{A}
-finite germ
f:(\mathbb{C}^{m},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{m+1},0)
in the Mather range we have

\mu_{I}(f)\geq\mathcal{A}_{e}\text{-}\codim(f)\,.

Moreover if
f
is quasihomogeneous, i.e. it is stabilized with a linear
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action with positive weights, then

\mu_{I}(f)=\mathcal{A}_{e}\text{-}\codim(f)\,.

9.2.
Formula for the image Milnor number

Let
f:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
be a quasihomogeneous
\mathcal{A}
-finite map in the Mather range. The image Milnor number
\mu_{I}(f)
may be computed from the torus weights on the source and target (usually called
weights
and
degrees
). Explicit formulas were obtained for
m=2
in
[
Mon91
]
,
m=3
in
[
Ohm16
]
, and
m=4,5
in
[
PPS21
]
. We generalize these formulas for all
m
up to the theoretical bound
m\leq 14
. Our approach follows that of Ohmoto
[
Ohm16
]

and depends on our calculation of the Master Series for
l=1
in Example
8.3
.

Ohmoto’s method
[
Ohm16
, Thm. 6.5]
provides a Thom polynomial computing the

\operatorname{ssm}
class of the image. This result was proved only for

m\leq 5
and for maps with Morin singularities. We present the following generalization.

Proposition 9.1
.

There is a polynomial

{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{im}}\in\mathbb{Q}[{\underline{s}}]

such that for any map
f:M\to N
in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
we have

{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{im}}(f)=\operatorname{ssm}(\operatorname{im}(f))|_{\leq 15}\in\operatorname{H}^{\leq 15}(N).

Proof.

This proposition is equivalent to Conjecture
5.5
[T] for the empty multisingularity
{\underline{\eta}}=\varnothing
and bound
k=15
. If the polynomial
\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{\varnothing,\leq 15}
exists, then

{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{\operatorname{im}}}=1-\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{\varnothing,\leq 15}=1-\exp(S_{\varnothing})|_{\leq 15}\,.

By Theorem
8.2
existence of
\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{\varnothing,\leq 15}
may be verified by an explicit computation. Computer algebra software shows that this polynomial exists and computes it. The resulting polynomial
S_{\varnothing}
is presented in Section
11
.
∎

Suppose that the variety
N
is compact of dimension at most
15
. Then the above theorem allows to compute the Euler characteristic of the image of
f
, cf. Proposition
4.1
[5]

\chi(\operatorname{im}(f))=\int_{N}c_{\bullet}(TN)\cdot{\operatorname{Th}^{T}_{\operatorname{im}}}(f)\,.

In our case, the target variety
N
is a vector space
\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
. It is not compact, so we cannot directly use the above formula. Fortunately, there is a generalization involving a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action. Suppose that
V
is a vector space equipped with a linear
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{C}^{\star}
action, with no zero weights. For an invariant subvariety
X\subset V
we have

\chi(X)=\frac{(c^{\mathbb{T}}_{\bullet}(V)\cdot\operatorname{ssm}_{\mathbb{T}}(X))|_{\dim V}}{\operatorname{eu}_{\mathbb{T}}(V)}\,.

For a quasihomogeneous stable map
f:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
, with
m\leq 14
we obtain

\chi(\operatorname{im}(f))=\frac{(c_{\bullet}(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})\cdot(1-\exp(S_{\varnothing})(f)))|_{m+1}}{\operatorname{eu}(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})}\,.

In fact both sides of the above equation are equal to
1
. The situation gets more interesting, when we relax the assumptions and consider a quasihomogeneous
\mathcal{A}
-finite map
f:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
, where
m\leq 14
. It is proved in
[
Ohm16
, Thm. 6.20]
that the same formula computes the Euler characteristic of the image of a stable perturbation

. Combining this result with formula (

(14)

\displaystyle(-1)^{m}\mu_{I}(f)+1=\chi(\operatorname{im}(f_{t}))=\frac{(c_{\bullet}(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})\cdot(1-\exp(S_{\varnothing})))|_{m+1}}{\operatorname{eu}(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})}\,.

10.3.
Landweber-Novikov variables

Proposition 10.2
.

Consider a polynomial
B\in\mathbb{Q}[{\underline{s}}]
. Suppose that for every
f\in\mathcal{C}_{l}
we have

B(s(f))=0\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(Y)\,.

Then
B=0
as a polynomial.

Fix
m>\deg B
. We consider two vector spaces over
\mathbb{Q}
:

•

V_{c}
with a dual basis
\{c_{1},...,c_{m}\}

•

V_{s}
with a dual basis
\{s_{\lambda}\}
where partitions
\lambda
correspond to variables occurring in
B
. We have
|\lambda|\leq m
.

We treat
B
as a polynomial function on the space
V_{s}
.
Let
S:V_{c}\to V_{s}
be a polynomial map defined by:

s_{\lambda}(S(x))=c_{\lambda}(x):=\prod c_{\lambda_{i}}(x)\,.

To a one parameter subgroup
\sigma:\mathbb{C}^{*}\to\mathbb{T}_{+}
and a sequence
{\underline{a}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
we associate a point
x({\underline{a}},\sigma)\in V_{c}
such that

c_{i}(T_{F_{{\underline{a}}}})=c_{i}(x({\underline{a}},\sigma))t^{i}\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}(\operatorname{pt})\simeq\mathbb{Q}[t]\,.

Consider the set

X=\{x({\underline{a}},\sigma)\in V_{c}|\operatorname{eu}_{\sigma}(F_{\underline{a}})\neq 0\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}(\operatorname{pt})\}\subset V_{c}\,.

Lemma 10.3
.

Suppose that a polynomial
A\in\mathbb{Q}[{\underline{c}}]
of degree at most
m
vanishes on
X
. Then
A=0
.

Proof.

For
{\underline{a}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
and a general one parameter subgroup of
\mathbb{T}_{+}
we have

A(c(F_{\underline{a}}))=0\in\operatorname{H}_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}^{\bullet}(\operatorname{pt})\,.

Therefore
A(c(F_{\underline{a}}))=0\in\operatorname{H}_{\mathbb{T}_{+}}^{\bullet}(\operatorname{pt})
. The proof of Proposition
10.1
implies that
A=0
.
∎

Lemma 10.4
.

The polynomial
B
vanishes on the linear span of the image
S(X)
.

Proof.

Choose arbitrary points
x({\underline{a}}_{1},\sigma_{1}),\dots,x({\underline{a}}_{k},\sigma_{k})\in S(X)
. Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
be nonzero numbers such that

\operatorname{eu}(F_{{\underline{a}}_{i}})=e_{i}t^{l}\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}_{\sigma_{i}}(\operatorname{pt})\simeq\mathbb{Q}[t]\,.

Pick any
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and consider a map

F=F^{(b_{1})}_{{\underline{a}}_{1}}\,\dot{\sqcup}\,\dots\,\dot{\sqcup}\,F^{(b_{k})}_{{\underline{a}}_{k}}

with the diagonal
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action. This map is in
\mathcal{C}_{l}
, therefore
B(s(F))=0\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}(\operatorname{pt})
. Proposition
4.15
implies that
s(F)
corresponds to a point

\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}e_{i}S(x({\underline{a}}_{i},\sigma_{i}))\in V_{s}

The set of all such points is Zariski dense in the linear span of points
S(x({\underline{a}}_{1},\sigma_{1})),\dots,S(x({\underline{a}}_{k},\sigma_{k}))
.
∎

Lemma 10.5
.

The image
S(X)
spans the space
V_{s}
.

Proof.

Suppose otherwise. Then the set
S(X)
is contained in a codimension one subspace given by some linear equation

\sum a_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(x)=0\,.

Therefore, the polynomial function
A=\sum a_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}
vanishes on the set
X
. It is of degree at most
m
. Lemma
10.3
implies that
A=0
.
∎

The lemmas above show that
B
vanishes on the whole space
V_{s}
. Therefore,
B=0
.

10.4.
Chern and Landweber-Novikov variables

Proposition 10.6
.

Consider a polynomial
C\in\mathbb{Q}[{\underline{c}},{\underline{s}}]
. Suppose that for every
f\in\mathcal{C}_{l}
we have

C(c(f),f^{*}s(f))=0\in\operatorname{H}^{\bullet}(X)\,.

Then
C=0
as a polynomial.

Fix
m>\deg C
. For a sequence
{\underline{a}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
consider the test map
F_{\underline{a}}
with the action of the torus
\mathbb{T}_{+}
and let
c_{\underline{a}}:=c(F_{\underline{a}})
. Consider a polynomial
B_{\underline{a}}(-)=C(c_{\underline{a}},-)\in\mathbb{Q}[{\underline{s}}]
. We have
\deg B_{\underline{a}}\leq\deg C<m
.

Lemma 10.7
.

For any
{\underline{a}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
we have
B_{\underline{a}}=0
.

Proof.

Consider a polynomial

B_{\underline{a}}^{\prime}(s):=B_{\underline{a}}(s+s(F_{\underline{a}}))\in\mathbb{Q}[{\underline{s}}]\,.

It is enough to show that
B_{\underline{a}}^{\prime}=0
.
Let
g
be a disjoint sum (
\,\dot{\sqcup}\,
) of test maps and
H=F_{\underline{a}}\,\dot{\sqcup}\,g
. Then

0=C(c(H),s(H))_{|1}=C(c(F_{\underline{a}}),s(F_{\underline{a}})+s(g))=B_{\underline{a}}(s(F_{\underline{a}})+s(g))=B_{\underline{a}}^{\prime}(s(g))\,,

where
|_{1}
denotes restriction to the component of the domain corresponding to
F_{\underline{a}}
. The proof of Proposition
10.2
uses only test maps and their disjoint sums, therefore
B_{\underline{a}}^{\prime}=0
.
∎

We use the isomorphism
\mathbb{Q}[{\underline{c}},{\underline{s}}]\simeq\mathbb{Q}[{\underline{c}}][{\underline{s}}]
and consider the coefficient of
C
corresponding to a given monomial in the
{\underline{s}}
-variables. It is a polynomial in
{\underline{c}}
-variables of degree at most
m
, denote it

. Due to the above lemma for any
{\underline{a}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
we have
A(c_{\underline{a}})
, where
c_{\underline{a}}
is defined in equation (
18
). The proof of Proposition
10.1
implies that

. All coefficients of

vanish, therefore

.
