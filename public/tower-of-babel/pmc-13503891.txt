Cancer-associated adipocytes: metabolic reprogramming, crosstalk and therapeutic implications in tumor progression
Authors: Jinmin Shi, Shaimaa Abdel-Ghany, Mariam Abdel-Fattah, Xuebo Hu, Al-Hassan Soliman Wadan, Hussein Sabit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdipose tissue, once considered a passive fuel store, is now recognized as a dynamic endocrine organ that shapes cancer behavior. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs) undergo marked reprogramming—losing large lipid droplets, adopting fibroblast-like features, and intensifying lipolysis—while releasing proinflammatory mediators that accelerate proliferation, invasion, and therapy resistance. This interaction is bidirectional: through cytokines, adipokines, and extracellular vesicles (including exosomal microRNAs), CAAs coordinate immune recruitment, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and angiogenesis. Mechanistically, several pathways converge at this interface. YAP/TAZ, STAT3, and PI3K/AKT integrate mechanical stress, inflammatory tone, and nutrient cues; metabolic symbiosis—enhanced fatty acid oxidation alongside glycolytic rewiring—supplies energy and redox support. CAAs also amplify metastasis and chemoresistance, particularly in triple-negative breast (TNBC) and pancreatic cancers, via effectors such as CXCL8, FAM3C, and SAA1. Systemic axes also matter in cancer cachexia, adipocyte-derived lipocalin-2 (LCN2) promotes tissue wasting and dampens thermogenesis, while obesity’s chronic inflammation further biases the TME toward tumor promotion. This review synthesizes how CAAs and adipose dynamics drive oncogenesis, progression and therapeutic failure and highlights actionable nodes within the adipose–tumor axis for precision oncology.Subject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Cancer therapy

Introduction

In recent years, growing evidence has revealed that adipose tissue is far more than a passive reservoir for energy storage depots; it is a dynamic endocrine organ that plays a critical role in various physiological and pathological processes, including cancer development. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), adipocytes located close to malignant cells often undergo phenotypic and functional transformation into cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs), which actively promote tumor progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy.1–3

CAAs display a dedifferentiated morphology, lipolytic phenotype, and altered secretome, supporting tumor cell proliferation and invasion.4,5 They secrete a broad spectrum of proinflammatory cytokines, adipokines, extracellular vesicles, and matrix-remodeling enzymes, establishing a protumorigenic niche that facilitates cancer progression.6,7 These adipocyte-derived factors, including IL-6, CXCL8, and leptin, modulate immune cell recruitment, promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and enhance angiogenesis.8

Mechanistically, the crosstalk between CAAs and cancer cells is orchestrated through reciprocal signaling pathways, including YAP/TAZ, STAT3, and PI3K/AKT, which reinforce the malignant phenotype of tumor cells while perpetuating the reprogramming of neighboring adipocytes.3,9 Furthermore, exosomal miRNAs derived from tumor cells or CAAs have been shown to mediate intercellular communication, reprogramming adipocytes to sustain cancer metabolism and immune evasion.9

Recent findings have also highlighted the metabolic plasticity of CAAs, particularly in the context of lipid mobilization and β-oxidation, which fuel tumor cell energetics and biosynthesis.10,11 In TNBC, CAA-derived CXCL8 and FUCA2 are implicated in promoting metastasis and resistance to immunotherapy, underscoring their therapeutic relevance.12,13 CAAs induce ECM remodeling through matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and collagenases, facilitating cancer cell migration.5

Emerging studies suggest that targeting CAAs or disrupting their bidirectional communication with tumor cells represents an intriguing therapeutic avenue. For instance, FAM3C expression in CAAs was recently identified as a key player enhancing breast cancer (BC) cell survival and metastasis. At the same time, interventions targeting the SAA1 and leptin pathways have shown preclinical efficacy in limiting CAA-induced tumor progression.2,7 Similarly, targeting exosomal pathways or metabolic reprogramming can potentially abrogate CAA-driven malignancy.4

This review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of recent advances in understanding the biological and molecular roles of CAAs in tumor progression. We explore the key mechanisms by which CAAs influence tumor cell behavior, dissect their contributions to immune modulation and therapeutic resistance, and discuss emerging strategies targeting the adipocyte–tumor axis. Through this lens, we underscore the clinical significance of CAAs as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets in the evolving oncology landscape.

Moreover, the current review unifies typically siloed domains—CAA identity and signaling, substrate-level metabolic exchange, and immunometabolic control—into a single, testable framework that maps actionable nodes (e.g., LCN2, KRAS-linked programs, FAM3C) onto JAK/STAT3, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, HIF-1, and redox–lipid circuits. It moves beyond description to nominate intervention strategies (CD36/FAO and SCD1/ferroptosis axes, brown-fat activation, GLP-1 receptor agonists) and aligns figures with explicit biomarkers and trial designs, converting mechanisms into clinically actionable hypotheses.

CAAs and the TME

The TME is a complex and interactive milieu that plays a pivotal role in all stages of cancer development, from initial growth to distant metastasis and therapeutic resistance.10,11 Far from being passive bystanders, the nonmalignant cells within the TME, including endothelial cells, ECM, immune cells, and stromal cells, actively communicate with cancer cells, shaping their behavior and ultimately influencing the disease trajectory.14 Among these stromal components, adipocytes, the primary cell type of adipose tissue, have emerged as significant contributors to the TME, particularly in cancers arising in or metastasizing to adipose-rich environments such as the breast and pancreas.2,7,15 The dynamic interplay between adipocytes and cancer cells leads to a unique cell population known as CAAs, which exhibit distinct characteristics and functions compared to their regular counterparts.

Defining CAAs and their niche in the TME

CAAs are terminally differentiated adipocytes that undergo a process of reprogramming or activation upon their interaction with cancer cells within the TME,16 as commented on in previous reports.2,3 This transformation is characterized by morphological, metabolic, and secretory changes that fundamentally alter their role from lipid storage to active support of tumor progression.17 Unlike mature adipocytes, which are typically large, lipid-laden cells with minimal secretory activity, CAAs often exhibit a dedifferentiated phenotype, characterized by reduced lipid droplet size and number and a more fibroblast-like appearance.3,7

Complex bidirectional crosstalk between adipocytes and cancer cells drives the formation of the CAA niche. Cancer cells secrete various soluble factors, including cytokines, including transforming growth factor-beta (TGFβ), which can directly influence the phenotype of adjacent adipocytes. For instance, TGFβ signaling from BC cells has been shown to drive the expression of FAM3C in CAAs.2 This paracrine signaling activates specific signaling pathways within adipocytes, triggering their reprogramming into CAAs. Conversely, CAAs secrete a range of bioactive molecules that can affect cancer cell behavior, creating a self-sustaining and tumor-supportive microenvironment (Table 1).

Furthermore, the preexisting conditions within the adipose tissue, such as the chronic inflammation observed in obese individuals, can further complicate the CAA niche. The presence of crown-like structures (CLS), indicative of macrophage infiltration and adipocyte death, contributes to a basal level of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines that can prime adipocytes for their transformation into CAAs and recruit other protumorigenic immune cells.12,13 This preexisting inflammatory state can amplify the effects of cancer cell-derived signals, leading to a more aggressive and metastasis-prone TME.

Metabolic and functional reprogramming of CAAs

A hallmark of CAA transformation is the significant metabolic and functional reprogramming these cells undergo to support tumor energetics and promote cancer cell survival and dissemination. One of the key metabolic changes in CAAs is the increased rate of lipolysis, resulting in the release of free fatty acids (FFAs) into the TME. These FFAs are readily available energy sources for cancer cells, fueling their rapid proliferation and survival, particularly under nutrient-stressed conditions.19,20 Cancer cells may upregulate the expression of fatty acid uptake transporters, such as CD36, to utilize these lipids efficiently.21–23 Conversely, CAAs may upregulate fatty acid oxidation (FAO) to meet their altered energy demands in this activated state.24–26

Beyond lipid metabolism, CAAs also exhibit alterations in glucose metabolism. While normal adipocytes primarily rely on glucose for lipid synthesis and energy storage, CAAs often show an upregulation of glycolysis, mirroring the Warburg effect observed in cancer cells.1,27,28 This shift toward glycolysis can provide metabolic intermediates for biosynthesis and contribute to the acidification of the TME, which can further influence the behavior of cancer cells and other stromal components.

Functionally, CAAs acquire the capacity to secrete a variety of adipokines, cytokines, and growth factors that directly impact cancer cell behavior. For instance, CAAs can secrete proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which, while having complex and context-dependent effects, can often promote cancer cell proliferation, survival, and invasion.29–31 Furthermore, CAAs secrete adipokines, including leptin and adiponectin, which may modulate insulin/IGF-1 signaling pathways in cancer cells, promoting their growth and survival.32–34

CAAs also actively remodeled the ECM within adipose-rich TMEs. These cells secrete MMPs, enzymes that breakdown ECM components, facilitating cancer cell invasion and metastasis.35 Additionally, CAAs can influence the deposition and organization of ECM proteins such as collagen, contributing to stromal stiffening that can promote cancer progression.36 For example, Runx1, a transcription factor upregulated in CAAs, was found to encourage their transformation by downregulating Plin1, ultimately enhancing BC cell migration and invasion37 (Table 2).

Molecular switches that drive adipocyte-to-CAA transformation

Adipocyte reprogramming into CAAs is governed by a compact set of stress- and mechanoresponsive transcriptional hubs that integrate cytokine, metabolic, and matrix cues. In this circuitry, TGF-β/SMAD3 operates as a principal switch: tumor-derived TGF-β1 activates SMAD3, repressing mature adipocyte identity while inducing fibroblast-like programs (for example, FN1, collagens, ACTA2) that facilitate CAA emergence and premetastatic niche formation.38–40 In parallel, hypoxia/HIF-1α stabilization under low oxygen and Krebs-cycle metabolite pressure (succinate/lactate) upregulates GLUT1, MCT4, CAIX, and VEGFA, thereby reinforcing glycolytic flux, acidosis, and angiogenesis that sustain CAAs at invasion fronts.41,42 Inflammatory inputs converge through IL-6–STAT3 and NF-κB: IL-6 from tumor and stromal sources activates STAT3 to maintain lipolysis and cytokine output, while TNF-α/IL-1β and saturated fatty acids (via TLR4–MyD88) engage NF-κB to drive IL-6/CCL2/CCL5 and matrix-remodeling enzymes, effectively locking CAAs into a protumorigenic, chemoattractant state.43–45 A complementary mechano-transcriptional axis, Hippo/YAP–TAZ, couples matrix stiffness and β-adrenergic/inflammatory tone to adipocyte plasticity; YAP/TAZ cooperate with SMADs to amplify fibrotic transcription and destabilize adipocyte identity, promoting dedifferentiation toward myofibroblast-like phenotypes.46–48 Finally, emerging evidence suggests RUNX1 and C/EBPβ as context-sensitive modulators: RUNX1 accelerates adipocyte-to-CAA switching and pro-invasive signaling in BC models, whereas C/EBPβ integrates hypoxic and inflammatory inputs to potentiate HIF-1–dependent invasive programs.49,50 Taken together, these nodes form an interconnected network rather than a linear cascade—one in which metabolic stress, cytokine tone, and matrix mechanics continually reinforce each other.

Do these switches act uniformly across cancers? Comparative data argue for cancer-type specificity. In PDAC, oncogenic KRAS and a desmoplastic, hypoxic stroma impose strong TGF-β/HIF-1 pressure and deliver exosome-borne cues that drive adipocyte lipolysis, ECM remodeling, and dedifferentiation.51,52 In contrast, in BC, the TGF-β/SMAD and IL-6–STAT3 axes are especially prominent in converting neighboring adipocytes to CAAs and in sustaining inflammatory crosstalk; these signals integrate with YAP/TAZ under obesity-related stiffness to consolidate the CAA phenotype.39,40,47 Similar TGF-β/SMAD–driven programs have been described in ovarian cancer, underscoring a conserved switch that accommodates organ-specific inputs and stromal contexts.39 This suggests a unifying framework: shared master regulators, tuned by tumor genotype and tissue biomechanics, yield distinct CAA settings across tumor types.

Single-cell and single-nucleus atlases now delineate CAA subclusters with discrete transcriptional states—lipolytic/inflammatory (IL-6, CCL2; ATGL/HSL-high), ECM remodeling (COL1A1, LUM, FN1; ACTA2), and metabolically rewired modules (glycolysis, one-carbon pathways, lipid desaturation)—that colocalize at tumor–adipose borders and vary with obesity and tumor subtype.53,54 Notably, adipocyte subsets defined in human WAT single-cell datasets are prospectively associated with BC outcomes and can be functionally validated as protumorigenic in vitro, suggesting that CAA heterogeneity is clinically consequential rather than merely taxonomic.53,55 Mechanistically, these states occupy a common landscape governed by TGF-β/SMAD–YAP/TAZ, HIF-1, and IL-6–STAT3/NF-κB; local gradients in stiffness, oxygen tension, and cytokine composition determine where an individual adipocyte settles on that landscape and hence which protumor functions it preferentially executes.

Clinical implications of CAAs

The growing understanding of the multifaceted roles of CAAs in the TME has significant clinical implications for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy.56 The presence and abundance of adipocytes within the TME have been correlated with disease progression and patient outcomes in various cancers. For instance, high adipogenesis in the TME of TNBC has been associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment and potentially poorer prognosis.57,58 The study by Kim et al.2 further highlights the clinical relevance of CAAs by demonstrating that high circulating levels of FAM3C, a factor produced by CAAs, are associated with metastatic BC. Similarly, in pancreatic cancer, CAAs have been shown to promote malignant characteristics and are associated with shorter overall survival.7,15 The study by Takehara et al.7 identified SAA1, a protein upregulated by CAAs, as a potential therapeutic target in pancreatic cancer.

However, the prognostic significance of CAAs is not always straightforward. In some contexts, a high adipocyte content might simply reflect a lower density of cancer cells, potentially indicating a less aggressive tumor.10 This complexity underscores the need for spatially resolved analyses and functional studies to truly understand the intricate interactions between adipocytes and cancer cells within the specific TME (Table 3).

Identifying key molecular players involved in CAA formation and function opens new avenues for therapeutic intervention. Targeting the pathways that drive CAA reprogramming, such as TGFβ signaling,59,60 or inhibiting the protumorigenic factors secreted by CAAs, such as FAM3C2 or SAA1,7 represent promising strategies. Furthermore, modulating the metabolic interactions between CAAs and cancer cells or disrupting the ECM remodeling orchestrated by CAAs could also offer novel therapeutic approaches to disrupt the tumor-supportive niche. The study by Luo et al.61 demonstrated that hesperidin nanoparticles could potentiate the efficacy of aPD-1 in TNBC by regulating CCL2 and ADPN expression in CAAs, highlighting the potential of targeting CAA-mediated immune modulation.

Adipocyte–tumor metabolic crosstalk in cancer cachexia

The inflammatory and endocrine rewiring of expanded adipose tissue is the upstream condition that hastens CAA emergence and sustains tumor-promoting crosstalk. The following sections integrate epidemiology with mechanism to show how systemic inflammation and adipokine imbalance set the stage for CAA-driven tumor behavior.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) provides a stringent test case for the adipose–tumor–host axis. Cachexia is not only common in PDAC but also unusually severe—frequently affecting 50–80% of patients across the disease course—and it exerts a disproportionate impact on survival and treatment tolerance. PDAC therefore serves as a mechanistic anchor for interrogating accelerated adipose wasting, pervasive inflammatory signaling and profound metabolic rewiring, examined under conditions in which these processes are most visible and clinically consequential. Importantly, the goal is generalizability rather than exclusivity: principles distilled from PDAC—adipocyte lipolysis, adipose browning, cytokine–myokine crosstalk and immune remodeling—illuminate cachexia biology across malignancies. Contemporary syntheses consistently place gastrointestinal cancers and PDAC at the top of the cachexia burden while still recognizing meaningful cachexia morbidity in other tumors.62,63

Although epidemiologic patterns differ by tumor type, BC—especially advanced or aggressive subtypes—also exhibits clinically relevant weight and skeletal muscle loss, with downstream implications for toxicity, functional status, and outcomes. Nevertheless, the peak prevalence and severity of cachexia remain concentrated in PDAC and other gastrointestinal malignancies. This contrast justifies a PDAC-centric framing while acknowledging that the CAA–cachexia axis extends beyond the pancreas and merits adaptation to BC–specific biology, therapies, and survivorship considerations.64

Cancer cachexia affects up to 80% of pancreatic cancer patients and contributes significantly to mortality.65,66 This systemic metabolic dysfunction disrupts interorgan communication, fostering a tumor-promoting microenvironment while destabilizing host energy homeostasis (Fig. 2). Adipocytes as metabolic collaborators in tumor progression

Adipocytes within the TME undergo phenotypic remodeling, transitioning into CAAs that secrete proinflammatory cytokines, free fatty acids (FFAs), and adipokines.70 In PDAC, this crosstalk is exemplified by the transfer of FFAs from adipocytes to cancer cells, which are repurposed for structural support and energy production via β-oxidation.71–73 Coculture experiments revealed that PDAC cells induce lipolysis in adipocytes, leading to FFA release and subsequent lipid droplet accumulation in tumor cells.74,75 Metabolic symbiosis not only fuels tumor development but also depletes host energy reserves, accelerating cachexia.

The role of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) is critical in this process. Tumor cells upregulate ATGL to hydrolyze stored triglycerides into FFAs, which are then oxidized to meet bioenergetic demands.76–78 Inhibition of ATGL in PDAC models reduces lipid mobilization and attenuates muscle wasting, underscoring its dual role in tumor progression and cachexia.79,80 Similarly, fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4), which is elevated at the adipocyte–tumor interface in ovarian cancer, facilitates FFA transport into cancer cells, promoting metastasis.81–83 Collectively, these findings position adipocyte-derived lipids as central mediators of tumor aggressiveness and systemic metabolic collapse.

The CAA–LCN2–tumor axis

Beyond lipid transfer, CAAs are now recognized as active participants in the pathogenesis of cancer cachexia through the secretion of LCN2, a secreted protein elevated in cachectic patients and mouse models.84 In PDAC, CAAs secrete LCN2 in response to tumor-derived signals.85 This establishes a triad of communication among adipose, muscle, and tumor tissues. Importantly, CAA-derived LCN2 is not just a biomarker but a causal factor in systemic energy dysregulation. For example, LCN2 deficiency in PDAC mouse models preserves body temperature and prevents fat and muscle loss.86

LCN2 also suppresses thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue (BAT), driving systemic hypothermia and rapid fat depletion.87 Elevated LCN2 levels correlate strongly with cachexia severity.88 Thus, the CAA–LCN2–tumor axis underscores a crucial regulatory mechanism in energy imbalance and highlights LCN2 as a promising therapeutic target.

Avoiding a siloed view of LCN2 requires cross-tumor synthesis. In PDAC, LCN2 has emerged as a mediator of adipose–muscle–tumor communication: it perturbs thermogenic programs and systemic metabolism, and circulating levels correlate with neutrophil activation as well as with concomitant losses of fat and lean mass. Mechanistically, LCN2 occupies a strategic interface linking adipose inflammation, innate immune activity, and tissue wasting.86 In BC, LCN2 promotes tumor aggressiveness in inflammatory models and has been associated with metastatic progression—including brain involvement—through effects on epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, and ECM remodeling, mirroring its role in PDAC and positioning LCN2 as a shared effector of adipose- and immune-driven adaptation rather than an organ-restricted factor.89 Finally, in lung cancer, neutrophil-derived LCN2 can trigger adipose and muscle ferroptosis, accelerating tissue wasting and reinforcing the concept that LCN2 links tumor-initiated inflammation to organ-level energy failure. This cross-disease convergence supports conceptualizing LCN2 as a pancancer node within the CAA–cachexia network.90

KRAS-driven metabolic reprogramming in CAAs

Oncogenic KRAS mutations, common in PDAC, further reinforce metabolic symbiosis with CAAs.91 KRAS-driven tumors secrete factors that induce lipolysis in CAAs, releasing FFAs that are subsequently shuttled into tumor cells via CD36 to fuel fatty acid oxidation (FAO).92 Beyond fueling growth, KRAS mutations activate PI3K signaling in both tumor cells and CAAs, promoting proinflammatory adipocytokine secretion and exacerbating cachexia.93–95 This creates a vicious cycle: KRAS mutations heighten FFA dependency, CAAs supply lipids, and inflammatory mediators worsen tissue wasting. Therefore, disrupting KRAS–CAA crosstalk, especially via PI3K inhibition, is a promising therapeutic avenue.96

Therapeutic targeting of adipocyte–tumor crosstalk

Therapeutic strategies to mitigate cachexia focus on disrupting adipocyte–tumor communication.97 For example, neutralizing LCN2 antibodies or CRISPR-mediated knockout preserve muscle and BAT function.98 Inhibition of ferroptosis with liproxstatin-1 reduces adipose wasting in lung cancer, although the applicability of this strategy in PDAC remains unclear.99

Other interventions include nutritional modulation: high-fat diets replenish lipid reserves but risk accelerating tumor growth,100 whereas cold exposure activates BAT, suppressing glycolysis in tumors and slowing growth in preclinical models.101 Combinatorial approaches—such as LCN2 blockade with ATGL inhibition—may offer synergistic benefits, although toxicity is a concern.102

The interplay of LCN2, KRAS, and ferroptosis reveals cachexia as a systemic metabolic network.103 A comprehensive approach integrating pharmacological, nutritional, and lifestyle interventions is needed to disrupt this cycle and improve patient outcomes.

Translational potential across cancers

Although most data come from BC, the principles of cold-induced BAT activation and metabolic modulation apply broadly.104 In PDAC, preclinical models suggest that cold-mediated metabolic shifts could disrupt tumor–CAA symbiosis.105–107 In colorectal cancer, BAT activation may alter TME metabolism and suppress growth.108 Cold exposure may also influence immune responses through IL-6 and MAPK modulation, with potential relevance in lung cancer, melanoma, and other malignancies.109–111 Expanding research beyond BC is crucial to define patient-specific protocols and therapeutic combinations.

The same adipose–tumor exchanges that precipitate cachexia—lipid shuttling, monocarboxylate cycling, and IL-6/LCN2 signaling—also license survival strategies of disseminated tumor cells. We therefore turn from systemic wasting to local fates of dissemination, examining how adipose-conditioned niches shape dormancy, reactivation, and metastatic outgrowth.

Adipose tissue crosstalk in cancer progression: beyond energy storage

Once considered a passive energy storage depot, adipose tissue is now recognized as a dynamic endocrine organ actively participating in various physiological and pathological processes, including cancer development and progression112–114 (Fig. 3). The intricate interactions between adipocytes and cancer cells within the TME are multifaceted, extending far beyond the simple provision of lipids as an energy source.10 This section delves into the emerging roles of adipose tissue and its cellular components, particularly adipocytes and adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs), in modulating cancer cell behavior, influencing dormancy, metastasis and chemoresistance through various mechanisms. These interactions highlight the importance of understanding the adipose tissue microenvironment, as it supports tumor growth and plays a critical role in the dormancy and reactivation of cancer cells.115

Adipocytes and cancer cell dormancy

Cancer dormancy, a state where disseminated tumor cells remain quiescent for prolonged periods before eventually relapsing, poses a significant challenge in cancer treatment.116 The TME, including adipocytes, plays a crucial role in inducing and maintaining this dormant state in various cancers.117

Studies have shown that the bone marrow, a niche rich in adipocytes, can imprint dormancy and chemoresistance on T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) cells.118,119 T-ALL cells in adipose-rich bone marrow sites exhibited reduced metabolic activity, slower cell cycle progression, and increased resistance to cell cycle-related drugs. This suggests that adipocytes within these niches contribute to establishing a dormant and drug-resistant phenotype.120 This highlights the potential of adipocytes to act as protective niches for cancer cells, shielding them from both the host immune system and chemotherapeutic agents.

Further supporting this notion, Mohd Ali et al.121 demonstrated that ASCs can induce dormancy in BC cells (MCF7 and MDA-MB-231) through the secretion of exosomal microRNAs (miRNAs). Coculture of BC cells with ASCs resulted in altered miRNA profiles within the exosomes, leading to the modulation of key signaling pathways involved in metastasis and EMT, ultimately pushing cancer cells toward a more dormant and epithelial-like state with increased chemoresistance. This suggests that ASCs, another crucial component of adipose tissue, can actively communicate with cancer cells to promote their dormancy.

The role of specific ECM components produced within the adipose-rich TME is also emerging as a factor in cancer dormancy. Pérez-Díaz et al.122 found that dormant BC cells exhibit elevated expression of collagen type VII (ColVII). In vitro, culturing BC cells on recombinant ColVII reduced their proliferation. Furthermore, in 3D spheroid cocultures of breast cancer cells and ASCs, a subpopulation of ASCs overexpressing ColVII was associated with reduced cancer cell proliferation. These findings suggest that ColVII, potentially secreted by adipocytes or ASCs within the TME, may contribute to maintaining cancer cell dormancy.

Interestingly, the interaction between adipocytes and cancer cells also influences the metabolic state of cancer cells, potentially contributing to dormancy.123 Kurihara et al.124 reported that Mycobacterium abscessus could reside within lipid droplets inside adipocytes, forming a dormancy-like state and causing chronic infections. This observation in the context of infection raises the possibility that cancer cells, through similar metabolic adaptations within adipocytes, might enter a state of quiescence characterized by altered nutrient utilization and reduced metabolic activity, mirroring some aspects of dormancy.

Conversely, Nyström et al.125 observed variable effects of ASCs on active BC cell growth and an inhibitory effect on dormant BC cell proliferation after removing the dormancy-inducing agent. Their study also indicated that coculture of ASCs with BC cells could produce cancer cell migration-promoting factor tenascin-C (TNC), suggesting a context-dependent role of ASCs that may shift from inducing dormancy to promoting migration under different conditions. This dynamic interplay between adipocytes, ASCs, and cancer cells highlights the complexity of the TME, where factors influencing dormancy can also shift toward promoting tumor progression depending on environmental cues (Fig. 4).

Adipose tissue and metastasis

Beyond facilitating local invasion, adipose tissue contributes decisively to metastatic dissemination, colonization, and outgrowth by shaping the premetastatic and metastatic niche. Adipose-resident cells (adipocytes, ASCs), their secretome (adipokines, cytokines, extracellular vesicles), and adipose-remodeled ECM collaborate to (i) prime distant organs, (ii) supply metabolic substrates that sustain survival under stress, and (iii) modulate immunity to favor metastatic seeding and regrowth.126,127

Adipose-driven remodeling of the premetastatic and metastatic niche

ASCs can convert into CAF-like phenotypes within TMEs and metastatic sites, depositing ECM and secreting pro-migratory factors that stiffen and rewire the niche.128 This ASC to CAF transition intensifies collagen/fibronectin deposition, alters mechanotransduction (YAP/TAZ), and enhances prometastatic signaling. TNC—a key ECM glycoprotein associated with invasion and immune modulation—emerges as a central node in this remodeling. TNC is enriched in metastatic contexts, correlates with poor survival, and can be induced by tumor–stroma crosstalk.129–131 Notably, TNC is upregulated in tumor-free draining lymph nodes of patients who later metastasize, supporting its role as a premetastatic niche marker and potential imaging/therapeutic target.132 Moreover, adipose–tumor signaling (e.g., HGF/MET) can drive tumor secretion of TNC and broader stromal rewiring in pancreatic cancer—illustrating how adipose-conditioned cues propagate TNC-rich niches that favor metastatic take.133 Together, these data support the contention that adipose-derived cells and signals contribute directly to niche construction rather than merely facilitating migration.

Metabolic symbiosis at distant sites: lipid transfer and FAO support survival/outgrowth

At metastatic destinations, adipocytes fuel colonizing tumor cells through lipid transfer that sustains β-oxidation (FAO) and stress tolerance. In ovarian cancer, the omentum provides an adipocyte-rich soil that promotes metastatic outgrowth via lipid uptake and FAO dependence; recent syntheses emphasize adipocyte-induced metabolic programs, including SIK2-driven FAO and mitochondrial respiration, that enhance motility and colonization.134–136 Mechanistically, adipocytes can reprogram cancer metabolism—even diverting glucose flux toward glycerol-3-phosphate and lipogenesis that favor dissemination and metastasis.137 Adipocyte pyroptosis in the omentum can further release FFAs and ATP, recruiting macrophages and linking adipocyte turnover to pro-metastatic inflammation and chemoresistance.138 These studies demonstrate that adipose depots at distant organs are not passive: they metabolically sustain disseminated tumor cells and accelerate metastatic outgrowth.

Exosomal and adipokine signaling to condition distant organs

Adipocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) prime metastatic behavior by delivering lipids, enzymes, and matrix-modulating proteins to tumor cells and stromal recipients in secondary organs. For instance, adipocyte EVs carrying MMP3 can increase MMP9 activity and promote lung metastasis; adipocyte EVs also drive melanoma invasion via FAO programming—mechanisms now broadly implicated in premetastatic niche (PMN) formation.139 In parallel, adipokines (e.g., leptin, IL-6) and complement–ECM crosstalk shape ECM stiffness and immune contexture, further conditioning distant tissues for tumor cell survival.127,140

ASC/CAA-mediated mechanobiology and immune modulation in metastasis

Adipose-rich niches undergo mechanical stiffening and ECM reorganization, which stabilize invadopodia, facilitate extravasation, and maintain dormancy/awakening programs in disseminated tumor cells.127 Adipocyte-conditioned matrices can activate YAP/TAZ-dependent programs and enhance metastatic potential; recent work also shows dysfunctional adipocytes at invasive fronts dynamically exchange cues with cancer cells to drive invasion and progression. Concomitantly, adipose-derived cytokines and EVs re-educate myeloid and lymphoid compartments, tilting immunity toward tolerance and supporting metastatic seeding.141 These immune-mechanical synergies clarify how adipose tissue promotes survival and growth in distant organs, not merely migration.3,142

While these tissue-scale programmes govern dormancy and metastatic outgrowth, their baseline is set systemically: obesity and chronic low-grade inflammation precondition adipose depots and immune tone, accelerating CAA formation and amplifying the very signals reviewed above. Adipose tissue and chemoresistance

The ability of cancer cells to evade the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy is a significant obstacle in cancer treatment, and the TME, particularly adipose tissue, has been implicated in promoting chemoresistance.143 Xie et al.144 found that mammary adipocytes protect TNBC cells from ferroptosis, a form of nonapoptotic cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation. Coculture with adipocytes reduced ferroptosis susceptibility in TNBC cells treated with ferroptosis inducers. Mechanistically, adipocytes secreted exosomes containing stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), which increased the levels of monounsaturated fatty acids in TNBC cells, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and conferring resistance to ferroptosis. This study highlights a novel mechanism by which adipocytes can contribute to chemoresistance by modulating lipid metabolism and cell death pathways in cancer cells.

Zhang et al.145 further demonstrated that adipocyte-derived exosomes promote chemoresistance in colorectal cancer (CRC) by suppressing ferroptosis. They identified microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP) as a key component of these exosomes. MTTP expression was elevated in plasma exosomes of CRC patients with high body fat ratios and served as an inhibitor of ferroptosis, reducing the sensitivity to oxaliplatin chemotherapy.146 Mechanistically, the MTTP/proline-rich acidic protein 1 (PRAP1) complex inhibited ZEB1 expression, leading to the upregulation of glutathione peroxidase 4 and xCT, ultimately decreasing lipid ROS levels and suppressing ferroptosis. These findings suggest that targeting secreted MTTP could reverse oxaliplatin resistance in CRC patients with high adiposity.

Metabolic modulation in adipose tissue and cancer

The metabolic crosstalk between adipose tissue and cancer cells plays a pivotal role in their bidirectional communication, with adipocytes providing crucial metabolic support to promote tumor growth and progression.147 It has been shown that adipose tissue enhances BC tumor growth by stimulating lipogenesis in tumor cells. Orthotopic BC tumors in direct contact with adipose tissue grew faster and exhibited increased de novo lipogenesis compared to ectopic tumors, as indicated by Micallef et al.147 Their findings revealed that adipocytes secrete substantial quantities of lactate, and both lactate pretreatment and adipose tissue coculture significantly enhanced de novo lipogenesis in breast cancer cells. These observations suggest that adipocyte-derived metabolites, such as lactate, serve as bioenergetic substrates that cancer cells exploit to sustain their proliferative and biosynthetic demands (Table 4).

Metabolic changes in adipocyte–tumor crosstalk

Furthermore, Cai et al.148 demonstrated that adipocytes enhanced pancreatic cancer migration and invasion through fatty acid metabolic reprogramming. Coculture with adipocytes activated hypoxia signaling in pancreatic cancer cells, increased the expression of glycolytic genes and enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation with increased lipid droplet accumulation in cancer cells. Conversely, lipid metabolism was downregulated in the cocultured adipocytes. This metabolic symbiosis, involving the transfer and utilization of fatty acids, contributed to the increased metastatic capacity of pancreatic cancer cells.

However, it is not solely the provision of energy that dictates the interaction Lecoutre et al.149 found that β-hydroxybutyrate, a ketone body produced during fatty acid oxidation, can dampen the profibrotic activation of adipose progenitors through both canonical Tgfβ signaling and noncanonical ZFP36-dependent mechanisms. This suggests that metabolites derived from adipose tissue can also exert regulatory effects on the tissue itself, potentially influencing the TME indirectly.

Dynamic effects of adipose tissue on tumor progression

Obesity-associated white adipose tissue (WAT) dysfunction drives systemic signals that support tumor initiation, progression and therapy resistance through intertwined inflammatory, endocrine, metabolic, extracellular vesicle (EV) and immune-mechanobiologic pathways. With WAT expansion, adipocyte hypertrophy and local hypoxia activate HIF-1α and stress pathways, triggering chemokine release, adipocyte death/pyroptosis, and recruitment of myeloid cells that remodel the microenvironment and spill protumor signals into the circulation.150,151 These processes convert metabolically healthy WAT into an inflamed endocrine organ that broadcasts tumor-promoting cues system-wide.152,153

Low-grade systemic inflammation and inflammasome signaling

Hypertrophic adipocytes and adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) adopt proinflammatory (M1-like) phenotypes, secreting IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2 and other mediators that enter the bloodstream and prime distant tissues for tumor growth, angiogenesis and immune tolerance.154,155 Concomitantly, NLRP3 inflammasome activation in metabolic tissues elevates IL-1β, linking obesity to tumor-promoting inflammation and therapy resistance.156,157 These circulating cytokines enhance STAT3/AKT signaling in tumors, support EMT, and expand suppressive myeloid populations.

Endocrine adipokines that reprogram tumor signaling

In obesity, adipokine balance shifts toward leptin/resistin (protumor) with relative loss of adiponectin (antitumor). Leptin activates JAK/STAT3, PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK cascades, augments EMT, and fosters stem-like traits, whereas adiponectin restrains growth via AMPK and anti-inflammatory effects.2 Emerging clinical data link leptin–adiponectin combinations with recurrence risk, underscoring translational potential for adipokine-based risk stratification.158

Whole-body metabolic rewiring and fuel delivery to tumors

Systemically, WAT inflammation drives insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, elevating circulating FFAs, glycerol and ketone bodies that tumors exploit for β-oxidation, lipogenesis and redox buffering. In parallel, adipocyte death/pyroptosis releases FFAs and ATP that recruit macrophages and create a prometastatic inflammatory loop, shown prominently in omental metastases.150 Conceptually, this establishes an adipose–tumor fuel axis: adipose lipolysis increases systemic substrate availability, while tumor FAO/oxidative programs enable stress survival and therapy tolerance.152,159,160

Extracellular vesicles as systemic carriers

Adipocyte- and ASC-derived EVs circulate system-wide, delivering lipids, FAO enzymes, proteases (e.g., MMPs), and miRNAs that enhance invasion, metabolic plasticity, and premetastatic niche (PMN) priming in distant organs. Recent syntheses highlight EV-mediated lipid transfer and stromal conditioning as key steps in metastasis.161,162 Experimental work further shows ASC exosomes reprogram breast tumor behavior and immunity.163

Immune remodeling and systemic tolerance

Obesity-conditioned adipose signals reshape systemic immunity: ATMs, neutrophils and MDSCs expand; T-cell metabolism is skewed toward exhaustion; and complement/ECM programs modulate trafficking. This immune spillover creates a permissive milieu that supports dissemination and outgrowth at distant sites.151,164 Mechanistically, ATM heterogeneity and plasticity underpin these effects, with shifts in M1/M2 balance and lipid-laden macrophage phenotypes that integrate metabolic and immune cues.154,165

Multiple recent studies implicate LCN2 as a causal mediator of adipose–muscle–tumor crosstalk in cachexia. In pancreatic cancer, Lcn2 deficiency preserved thermogenesis and reduced fat/muscle wasting in vivo, identifying LCN2 as a tractable target.86 In lung cancer cachexia, neutrophil-derived LCN2 drove tissue wasting and its inhibition reduced cachexia symptoms in vivo.90 In PDAC patients, circulating LCN2 is associated with neutrophil activation and poor nutritional status, supporting clinical relevance.166 Although oncology-specific anti-LCN2 antibodies are not yet in clinical trials, in vivo LCN2 neutralization has shown feasibility in other inflammatory contexts, arguing for translational potential.167

The fatty acid transporter CD36 is a key conduit for adipocyte-supplied lipids to tumors and stromal/immune cells. Recent overviews underline its role in metastasis, immune suppression and therapy resistance and highlight CD36 blockade as a rational strategy.168–170 A humanized anti-CD36 antibody (PLT012) has shown preclinical efficacy, supporting druggability.171 In parallel, curbing adipose lipolysis upstream—e.g., by targeting ATGL/HSL—has counteracted cancer-induced adipose loss and partially prevented muscle wasting in mouse models, directly addressing the systemic fuel supply into tumors.172

Emerging adipose-targeted strategies for cancer

The intertwined roles of obesity and inflammation in cancer development

Obesity, a global health concern characterized by excessive adipose tissue accumulation, is increasingly recognized as a significant risk factor for various cancers.174,175 The intricate relationship between obesity and cancer is multifaceted, with chronic low-grade inflammation emerging as a critical underlying mechanism.176,177 Beyond its role in energy storage, adipose tissue actively secretes various proinflammatory cytokines and adipokines, fostering a systemic inflammatory environment that can promote cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis.178 This section will explore the complex interplay between obesity-induced inflammation and various aspects of cancer development, drawing upon recent research highlighting key molecular mechanisms and clinical implications.

Numerous epidemiological studies have established a strong association between obesity, as measured by body mass index (BMI), and an increased risk of several cancers, including colorectal, breast (postmenopausal), endometrial, kidney, esophageal adenocarcinoma, and pancreatic cancers.179–182 Furthermore, the duration and timing of obesity throughout the lifespan may differentially impact cancer risk, with some evidence suggesting that age-related obesity presents a distinct paradigm.183 Interestingly, the concept of “normal weight obesity,” characterized by high visceral adiposity despite a normal BMI, has also been linked to elevated inflammatory markers and increased BC risk, underscoring the importance of body composition rather than solely BMI.180

The role of inflammation as a mediator in this association is supported by findings that elevated circulating levels of inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), are frequently observed in obese individuals and are associated with increased cancer risk and mortality.184–186 Moreover, genetic polymorphisms in genes related to inflammation and obesity have been implicated in cancer susceptibility.187,188

Epidemiologic integration of obesity, inflammation, and cancer risk

To explicitly address the interaction among adiposity, inflammation, and cancer, recent epidemiologic studies triangulate all three components. In a large, prospective cohort of postmenopausal women, the combination of higher BMI and elevated high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP ≥ 3 mg/L) conferred a significantly increased BC incidence compared with low BMI/low CRP, demonstrating that co-occurring adiposity and systemic inflammation synergize to elevate risk.189 Complementing incidence data, a nationwide cohort of women with early BC showed that higher CRP at diagnosis predicted worse disease-free and overall survival across BMI strata—i.e., systemic inflammatory tone adds prognostic information beyond body size alone.190

At the tissue level, CLS in breast adipose—macrophage aggregates encircling adipocytes—provide a histologic read-out of local adipose inflammation linking obesity to tumor biology. CLS correlates with higher BMI, adverse metabolic indices, and poorer outcomes in several cohorts, indicating that local adipose inflammation at the tumor border is clinically meaningful.191,192 Race-inclusive analyses also report strong associations between obesity and CLS detection and highlight how analytic choices can attenuate survival associations, helping explain variability across studies.193

Finally, genetic epidemiology strengthens causal inference: Mendelian randomization analyses of circulating inflammatory mediators identify specific inflammatory axes that appear causally linked to cancer risk (e.g., pro-adrenomedullin for BC), indicating that inflammation is not merely an epiphenomenon of adiposity but part of the causal pathway for certain cancers.194

Obesity and CAAs: local metabolic-inflammatory crosstalk

From homeostatic adipose to an obesity-primed reservoir for CAAs

In lean states, adipocytes store/release fuel and secrete hormones in concert with resident immune cells to maintain tissue homeostasis. With obesity, adipocyte hypertrophy and hypoxia ignite NF-κB/HIF-1α programs, macrophage recruitment, and crown-like structures (CLS), converting adipose tissue into a chronically inflamed endocrine organ. These obesity-conditioned cues (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, CCL2, leptin) “preprime” adipocytes and stromal cells, making adjacent adipose depots more prone to CAA formation once tumor signals arrive (e.g., IL-6/OSM/LIF). CLS in breast adipose tissue correlates with BMI, adverse metabolic indices, and worse outcomes, providing an anatomic bridge from obesity to tumor behavior.1,191,192,195,196

What makes an adipocyte a CAA?

Within the TME, tumor-derived factors and local hypoxia drive adipocyte dedifferentiation, lipolysis, loss of adipocyte markers, ECM remodeling traits, and heightened secretion of cytokines/adipokines. Recent reviews have synthesized CAA hallmarks across cancers (breast, colorectal, ovarian): proinflammatory outputs (IL-6/IL-8/CCL2/LIF/OSM), ECM remodeling, altered aromatase, and immune crosstalk that supports invasion, immune evasion, and therapy response.197–200

Inflammatory programming (local and systemic)

CAAs amplify IL-6 family signaling (IL-6, OSM, and LIF), sustaining STAT3, EMT, and stemness while recruiting and re-educating myeloid cells; this sits on top of obesity’s systemic inflammation. Contemporary analyses highlight IL-6–family cytokines as key coordinators of adipocyte–tumor immunopathology and detail adipocyte–macrophage reciprocity in the TME.5,201,202

Mechanistic activation of HIF-1, NF-κB and p38 MAPK at the adipose–tumor interface

Adipocyte–tumor crosstalk activates HIF-1 through convergent oxygen-dependent and oxygen-independent inputs. At hypoxic invasion fronts, prolyl hydroxylase (PHD) activity declines, stabilizing HIF-1α; in parallel, succinate and lactate exported by CAAs inhibit PHDs and acidify the pericellular space, further protecting HIF-1α from degradation.203–205 Stabilized HIF-1α induces GLUT1, MCT4, CAIX and VEGFA, increasing glucose influx, monocarboxylate shuttling, pH control and angiogenesis—features that amplify Warburg metabolism and invasiveness in adjacent cancer cells.41,206

NF-κB is engaged by TNF-α/TNFR1 and IL-1β/IL-1R emanating from CAAs and infiltrating myeloid cells, activating IKK complexes, releasing NF-κB from IκB, and driving transcription of CCL2/CCL5, IL-6 and MMPs that reinforce monocyte recruitment, macrophage polarization and ECM remodeling.207 A second NF-κB input is derived from saturated fatty acids released during CAA lipolysis; these ligate TLR4–MyD88, sustaining a lipotoxic inflammatory loop that heightens chemokine flux and immune suppression.44,208

p38 MAPK integrates β-adrenergic tone, IL-6 family cytokines and TNF-α; in adipocytes, it promotes HSL/ATGL-mediated lipolysis and exosome-linked signaling, whereas in tumor cells, it enhances motility, EMT-linked transcription and stress survival.209–212 These modules are not isolated: leptin–ObR and IGF-1–IGF1R feed PI3K/AKT/mTOR, which in turn increases HIF-1 output and cooperates with NF-κB at inflammatory enhancers, creating a self-amplifying circuit that couples substrate supply (lipids, ketones, alanine) to pro-invasive signaling outputs.213

Metabolic coupling: lipid transfer and FAO reliance

Obesity increases circulating FFAs; locally, CAAs lipolyze and donate fatty acids that tumor cells import (notably via CD36) to fuel β-oxidation and redox balance. Reviews and mechanistic work over the last 5 years show CD36-centric crosstalk in tumor/adipose/immune compartments and link CD36 to metastasis and therapy resistance.169,214

Extracellular vesicles

long-range messengers from adipose/CAAs. Adipose-derived EVs carry lipids, FAO enzymes, proteases, and miRNAs that reprogram cancer cells and condition stroma at distance; tumor–adipose EV exchange is an emerging axis in premetastatic niche priming. Several recent reviews (2024–2025) summarize EV cargo and functional consequences in adipose–tumor crosstalk.170,215,216

Microbiome-linked effects that prime CAAs

Obesity-associated dysbiosis drives metabolic endotoxemia (e.g., LPS), which heightens adipose IL-1β/TLR signaling and macrophage activation; this systemic–adipose inflammation preconditions depot adjacent to tumors, facilitating CAA formation and protumor functions. New work connects obese adipose IL-1β with colon cancer-relevant inflammation, consistent with the broader literature on obesity–microbiome–cancer risk217 with the broader mechanistic context above; see also CLS-oriented updates in breast adipose tissue.218

Adipose tissue as an active inflammatory organ in obesity

In obesity, the expansion of adipose tissue leads to adipocyte hypertrophy and hypoxia, triggering the activation of immune cells, particularly macrophages, within the adipose tissue.219,220 These ATMs undergo a phenotypic switch from an anti-inflammatory (M2) to a pro-inflammatory (M1) state, characterized by the increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines.221,222 This chronic low-grade inflammation in adipose tissue contributes to local metabolic dysfunction, such as insulin resistance. It spills into the systemic circulation, creating a fertile ground for cancer development.223,224

Recent research has highlighted specific molecular mechanisms driving this obesity-induced adipose tissue inflammation. For instance, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2M (UBE2M)-mediated neddylation of tripartite motif-containing 21 (TRIM21) has been shown to regulate obesity-induced inflammation and metabolic disorders.225 Similarly, fibroblast activation protein (FAP) expression in ATMs promotes obesity and metabolic inflammation, suggesting a role for stromal cell activation in the inflammatory cascade within adipose tissue.226 Furthermore, impaired efferocytosis, the clearance of dead cells by macrophages due to prolonged hypernutrition, can license chronic inflammation in tissues such as the liver, contributing to the development of nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), which is itself a risk factor for liver cancer.227

Conversely, some studies suggest potential protective mechanisms within adipose tissue. ASCs have been shown to control obesity-induced T-cell infiltration into adipose tissue, potentially modulating the inflammatory response.228 Moreover, adipocyte p53 has been identified as a coordinator of the response to intermittent fasting by regulating the adipose tissue immune cell landscape, suggesting that dietary interventions can influence adipose tissue inflammation.229

The role of inflammation in obesity-associated cancer progression

The chronic inflammatory milieu associated with obesity can promote cancer progression through various mechanisms, including angiogenesis, desmoplasia, and modulation of the immune microenvironment. In BC, obesity-associated inflammation has been shown to promote angiogenesis via angiopoietin-like 4.230 In pancreatic cancer, obesity-induced inflammation and desmoplasia, the excessive accumulation of ECM components, promote tumor progression and resistance to chemotherapy.231 Furthermore, the omental microenvironment in endometrial cancer is influenced by obesity and white adipose tissue inflammation, potentially impacting tumor behavior.232

The interplay between obesity, inflammation, and the immune system in the context of cancer is complex. While systemic inflammation associated with obesity can sometimes correlate with better responses to immunotherapy in certain cancers, such as melanoma,233 obesity has also been linked to an increased risk of immune-related adverse events during immunotherapy.234 Obesity-induced inflammation can also exacerbate clonal hematopoiesis, a preleukemic condition characterized by the expansion of somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem cells, potentially increasing the risk of hematological malignancies.235

Obesity-related metabolic dysregulation, including insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, is closely intertwined with chronic inflammation and contributes significantly to cancer risk.236,237 Hyperinsulinemia, often a consequence of insulin resistance, can act directly on cancer cells to promote proliferation and survival, and it has been shown to initiate pancreatic cancer by increasing digestive enzyme production and inflammation via acinar insulin receptors.238 Insulin resistance and inflammation have also been shown to mediate the association between abdominal obesity and colorectal cancer risk.239

Adipokines, such as leptin and adiponectin, secreted by adipose tissue play a crucial role in regulating energy metabolism and inflammation. In obesity, the dysregulation of adipokine production, with increased leptin and decreased adiponectin levels, contributes to insulin resistance and a pro-inflammatory state, thereby fostering cancer development.240

Furthermore, alterations in lipid metabolism and the production of lipid mediators, such as oxylipins, in obese adipose tissue can contribute to inflammation and potentially influence cancer risk.241,242 For instance, impaired phosphocreatine metabolism in white adipocytes has been shown to promote inflammation.242

The gut microbiota-inflammation axis in obesity and cancer

The gut microbiota, the complex community of microorganisms residing in the gastrointestinal tract, is increasingly recognized as a critical modulator of both obesity and inflammation and, consequently, cancer risk.224 Obesity-associated dysbiosis, characterized by alterations in gut microbial composition and function, can lead to increased gut permeability and the translocation of microbial products into the systemic circulation, triggering inflammation.243,244

Studies have shown that the gut microbiota can influence the development of obesity-associated cancers, such as colorectal cancer.245–247 Gut colonization with obesity-associated microbes can modulate premetastatic niches, promoting BC, lung, and liver metastasis.248 Conversely, interventions targeting the gut microbiota, such as prebiotic supplementation, can modulate inflammation and immune-regulating gut microbes and metabolites.249 Short-chain fatty acids, such as butyrate, produced by gut bacteria can also exert anti-inflammatory effects, potentially mitigating obesity-related inflammation and its consequences.246,250

Therapeutic implications: targeting obesity and inflammation in cancer prevention and treatment

Given the strong links between obesity, inflammation, and cancer, interventions aimed at reducing obesity and inflammation hold promise for cancer prevention and treatment. Lifestyle modifications, including dietary changes and increased physical activity, can reduce body weight and systemic inflammation, potentially lowering cancer risk.251,252 For example, a dietary inflammatory index has been associated with obesity and colorectal cancer incidence.253 Intermittent fasting has also shown promise in attenuating obesity-induced inflammation and cancer progression in preclinical models.229,254

Pharmacological interventions targeting specific inflammatory pathways or metabolic dysregulation associated with obesity are also being explored for their potential anticancer effects. For instance, fenugreek has been shown to attenuate obesity-induced inflammation and improve insulin resistance,255 while rabeprazole mitigated obesity-induced chronic inflammation and insulin resistance by modulating macrophage polarization.256 Targeting IL-23 has shown potential for intercepting obesity-associated colorectal cancer in preclinical studies.247

The intricate relationship between obesity and cancer is significantly driven by chronic low-grade inflammation arising from dysfunctional adipose tissue and altered metabolic homeostasis. This inflammatory environment can promote various stages of cancer development, including initiation, progression, and metastasis.235,257 The gut microbiota also emerges as a crucial player in modulating obesity-induced inflammation and its impact on cancer risk.245,258 Understanding the complex molecular mechanisms underlying these interactions is essential for developing effective strategies for cancer prevention and treatment in the context of the global obesity epidemic. Future research should focus on identifying specific therapeutic targets within the obesity-inflammation-cancer axis to improve patient outcomes.

GLP-1 receptor agonists: future directions

A promising, testable strategy is the deployment of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs; e.g., semaglutide, tirzepatide) to recalibrate the adipose–tumor interface in obesity-associated cancers. Beyond producing substantial and durable weight loss, GLP-1RAs ameliorate systemic metabolic stressors that mechanistically prime CAAs—notably insulin resistance, visceral adiposity, and chronic low-grade inflammation.259,260 At the population level, signals from a contemporary cohort of adults with overweight/obesity indicate lower overall cancer incidence among GLP-1RA users, with the largest reductions observed for endometrial and ovarian cancers; however, a small, nondefinitive excess for kidney cancer counsels longer follow-up and vigilant control for confounding.261 Mechanistically, GLP-1 signaling attenuates adipose inflammation—reducing macrophage infiltration and proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α)—while shifting adipokine tone toward a less protumorigenic milieu and depleting visceral/ectopic fat stores.260,262 Interestingly, tirzepatide further lowers high-sensitivity C-reactive protein over 24–52 weeks, suggesting a durable anti-inflammatory effect with potential downstream consequences for the TME.263 Taken together, these observations motivate a dual-role hypothesis: GLP-1RAs may (i) mitigate obesity-related cancer risk through weight loss-mediated biology and (ii) directly dampen CAA-driven crosstalk by curbing lipolysis-derived fatty acid flux, blunting CCL2/IL-6 signaling, and constraining proinflammatory extracellular vesicle trafficking—predictions now ready for prospective interrogation. Priority designs include window-of-opportunity trials with paired adipose and tumor biopsies; spatial transcriptomics to resolve CAA immune niches; integrated profiling of circulating EVs and adipokines; and alignment of metabolic response metrics (visceral fat, hs-CRP) with TME endpoints and oncologic outcomes. Definitive mapping of GLP-1 receptor expression and signaling competence in human CAAs in situ remains a critical knowledge gap.

Cold exposure and adipose–tumor interplay

Positioned alongside GLP-1RAs, thermogenic interventions provide a complementary host-directed strategy, leveraging BAT activation to lower glycemia/insulinemia and modulate cytokine tone, thereby countering the obesity-primed, CAA-dependent milieu.

Emerging evidence suggests that cold exposure can remodel systemic metabolism and influence the TME.10,264 Activation of the sympathetic nervous system stimulates BAT thermogenesis via norepinephrine, increasing systemic energy expenditure and glucose uptake.265–267 This glucose diversion antagonizes the Warburg effect in tumors.36

Cold exposure also induces lipolysis in white adipocytes, raising circulating FFAs.268,269 While BAT consumes FFAs, tumors reliant on FAO may exploit the increased systemic pool, creating a nuanced outcome.12,270 Additionally, cold stress activates p38 MAPK signaling, elevating IL-6 secretion, which may promote tumor growth and cachexia despite metabolic benefits.95,271,272 Thus, cold exposure simultaneously starves tumors of glucose while potentially supplying FFAs and proinflammatory cues. Its net therapeutic effect in cancer remains to be determined.273

Translational barriers and clinical status in targeting the adipose–tumor axis

On-target trade-offs and metabolic homeostasis

Perturbing protumorigenic adipocyte programs risks collateral disturbance of whole-body energy balance. A case in point is adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL/PNPLA2): while tumor-proximal lipolysis fuels β-oxidation and invasion programs, ATGL is indispensable for systemic triglyceride handling. Preclinical oncology studies now nominate ATGL as a driver of cancer metabolic plasticity, yet the best-known inhibitor, atglistatin, is murine-selective and not active on human ATGL, underscoring a translational gap (human-selective tool compounds such as NG497 are only beginning to delineate acute endocrine effects in human tissues).274–276 Moreover, human PNPLA2 loss of function causes neutral-lipid storage myopathy and cardiomyopathy, highlighting potential safety liabilities if systemic ATGL is inhibited.277 Pragmatically, CAA-selective delivery, intermittent dosing, or substrate-sparing combinations (e.g., FA uptake blockade + partial FAO inhibition) may be required to preserve metabolic homeostasis while suppressing tumor fueling.

Delivery barriers in adipose-rich and desmoplastic TMEs (PDAC as an example)

Dense ECM, elevated interstitial pressure, and poor perfusion constrain drug penetration in PDAC, where hyaluronan-rich stroma is a major determinant of vascular collapse and delivery failure. The phase III HALO-301 trial of pegvorhyaluronidase alfa (PEGPH20) did not improve outcomes when layered onto gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel, cautioning that “stroma depletion” alone is insufficient and may need better patient selection or orthogonal strategies.278,279 One promising alternative is tumor-penetrating peptides (iRGD/CEND-1), which transiently open a neuropilin-1–dependent transport route to enhance coadministered drugs; CEND-1 has completed early clinical evaluation with supportive human PK/tumor-selectivity data and ongoing trials in metastatic PDAC.280–282

Clinical status of selected targets

LCN2 blockade

Current evidence is preclinical; anti-LCN2 antibodies suppress tumor phenotypes in models, and multiple clinical studies use LCN2 as a biomarker, but no registered oncology trials have tested LCN2 neutralization as therapy to date, reflecting both biological complexity (context-dependent roles) and iron-handling safety considerations.283

ATGL inhibition

No human oncology trials are registered. Tool inhibitors demonstrate antitumor-relevant biology in vitro/in vivo and cardiometabolic effects in nononcology models, but species selectivity and systemic safety are key hurdles274,276

Penetration enhancers

CEND-1 (an iRGD derivative) has reached early-phase clinical testing in PDAC and remains the most clinically advanced approach to overcome stromal barriers.280 Collectively, these realities argue for staged translation: (i) prioritize delivery-enabling platforms (CEND-1, smart nanocarriers) to raise intratumoral drug exposure; (ii) pursue CAA-restricted target engagement (e.g., depot-targeted payloads, spatially constrained PROTACs); and (iii) embed mechanistic pharmacodynamics (adipose/tumor biopsies, spatial omics) into trials to confirm on-target effects without metabolic decompensation.

Bridging preclinical signals to clinical potential

Translating CAA-targeted concepts from mouse models to patients pivots on three practical requirements: (i) demonstrating on-target efficacy without provoking systemic metabolic harm, (ii) achieving reliable delivery into dense, poorly perfused, adipose-rich TMEs (notably PDAC), and (iii) establishing robust human pharmacology tied to clear, path-to-registration end points. These are not abstract hurdles; they determine whether a mechanistic idea survives first contact with clinical reality.

Inhibiting adipocyte lipolysis via ATGL is compelling preclinically—genetic knockdown and atglistatin both suppress lipolysis and tumor fueling—yet species selectivity and whole-body lipid homeostasis complicate translation. Atglistatin is murine-selective; only recently has a human-selective, reversible ATGL inhibitor (NG-497) been described, with attractive in vitro selectivity but no in-human efficacy signal to date.67,284,285 Moreover, clinical tumor data indicate that ATGL is a bona fide vulnerability in advanced prostate cancer;286 nonetheless, the systemic consequences of sustained lipolysis blockade—hepatic steatosis, myopathy, fatigue—must be anticipated and prospectively monitored in early trials. A similar caution applies to LCN2 neutralization: despite strong preclinical rationale, LCN2’s roles in iron handling and innate immunity argue for staged dose escalation with iron metrics and infection surveillance and for early pharmacodynamic (PD) readouts in adipose/tumor tissue rather than response alone.

Delivery is the second bottleneck. Desmoplasia and hypoperfusion in PDAC restrict intratumoral drug levels; prior efforts to “open” stroma with PEGPH20 failed at phase III, underscoring that stromal remodeling without dependable delivery and tumor control is insufficient.287,288 By contrast, tumor-penetrating peptides offer a pragmatic workaround. The αv-integrin/NRP-1–targeting peptide CEND-1 produced acceptable safety, drug-delivery biology, and encouraging activity in a first-in-human, open-label study with nab-paclitaxel/gemcitabine,289 and dedicated pharmacokinetic/disposition studies support its mechanism and scheduling.280 Platform advances—such as iRGD-based immune conjugates that couple delivery with T-cell engagement—further illustrate how ligand-guided vectors could be layered onto immunotherapy or metabolic agents to reach the adipose–tumor interface.290

Closing the translational gap will require window-of-opportunity and neoadjuvant designs with paired sampling (subcutaneous/visceral adipose and tumor) and mechanism-anchored PD endpoints: (i) lipolysis readouts (ATGL activity, glycerol/FFA flux), (ii) interface lipid trafficking (CD36 expression; ^18F-FTHA PET for fatty-acid uptake), (iii) inflammatory/EV signatures (IL-6, CCL2; EV lipid cargo), and (iv) spatial metrics of adipose–tumor juxtaposition. Delivery-enabling agents (e.g., CEND-1) should prospectively include coadministered drug intratumoral concentrations and stromal penetration as PD success criteria. For LCN2- or ATGL-directed strategies, early combination hypotheses (e.g., ATGL inhibition + glycolysis blockade;286) merit adaptive designs with prespecified metabolic toxicity stopping rules.

In short, preclinical wins around LCN2 and ATGL are biologically persuasive; clinically, success will likely depend on human-selective tools with careful metabolic safety management, validated delivery platforms in desmoplastic settings (CEND-1), and trials powered on mechanistic PD endpoints—rather than response alone—to derisk later-phase development.

Dynamic interplay between adipose tissue and the TME

Obesity’s influence on cancer extends far beyond systemic inflammation, profoundly impacting the TME – the complex cellular and extracellular network surrounding tumor cells that plays a crucial role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.291–293 With its intricate metabolic functions and secretory capacity, adipose tissue has emerged as a key player in shaping the TME, influencing various aspects of tumor biology. This section delves into the multifaceted relationship between adipose tissue and the TME, exploring the roles of adipocytes, adipokines and lipid metabolism in cancer development and treatment response.

Identification and characterization of CAAs

Rather than being merely “activated” adipocytes, CAAs represent a dynamic population of reprogrammed stromal cells that acquire distinctive morphological and molecular traits. Morphologically, these cells diverge strikingly from their mature adipocyte counterparts. While healthy adipocytes typically harbor large, unilocular lipid droplets, CAAs exhibit smaller lipid stores, a more elongated cytoplasmic shape, and an increased deposition of ECM, often assuming a fibroblast-like appearance.294 This structural remodeling reflects a fundamental reorientation of their cellular function.

At the molecular level, CAAs can be recognized by a constellation of myofibroblast- and fibrosis-associated markers that distinguish them from normal adipocytes. Proteins such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), fibroblast activation protein (FAP), integrin β1, and collagen I are consistently upregulated, indicating a shift toward a contractile and ECM-remodeling phenotype.295,296 Moreover, CAAs display elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, CCL2, and CCL5, which orchestrate immune cell recruitment and fuel local inflammation. Their lipid metabolism is also rewired: expression of FABP4, HSL, and ATGL underscores their role in mobilizing fatty acids and supplying metabolic substrates to neighboring tumor cells.297,298 Together, these changes highlight CAAs as both structural and metabolic accomplices in tumor progression.

Methodologically, identifying CAAs requires integrating complementary approaches. Histological staining remains a cornerstone, with immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence detecting markers such as α-SMA, FAP, and perilipin at tumor invasion fronts. More recently, single-cell RNA sequencing has transformed the resolution at which adipocyte heterogeneity can be studied, revealing discrete CAA clusters enriched in ECM remodeling and cytokine signatures.299 Lineage tracing studies provide another compelling dimension: experiments using Cre-Lox systems, including Adipoq-Cre and Pdgfra-Cre mouse lines, demonstrate that mature adipocytes can transdifferentiate into CAA-like fibroblasts under the influence of tumor-derived paracrine signals.300 Interestingly, this plasticity suggests that CAAs occupy a transitional state, bridging adipocytes and fibroblasts, rather than constituting a rigidly defined cell type.

Despite these advances, distinguishing CAAs from other stromal populations—particularly CAFs—remains problematic, largely owing to overlapping marker expression. This challenge underscores the importance of employing multimarker panels, ideally combined with spatial transcriptomic profiling, to capture not only marker expression but also the anatomical and contextual cues that confirm adipocyte origin. Emerging technologies such as multiplexed imaging and spatially resolved multiomics may soon refine this distinction further.

In summary, CAAs can be most reliably defined as adipocyte-derived stromal cells characterized by (i) morphological hallmarks—small lipid droplets and fibroblast-like morphology, (ii) molecular markers—α-SMA, FAP, integrin β1, FABP4, and IL-6, and (iii) functional validation—through lineage tracing or single-cell transcriptomic profiling. Framing CAAs in this manner not only enhances reproducibility across studies but also provides a necessary foundation for translating laboratory findings into clinically meaningful insights.

Adipocytes as active participants in the TME

Traditionally, adipocytes were primarily considered inert storage cells for triglycerides. However, it is now clear that they are dynamic contributors to the TME, actively shaping its composition and influencing cancer progression.17,301,302 Adipocytes interact directly and indirectly with tumor cells and other stromal components, significantly affecting tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.83,303,304 One key mechanism of this interaction involves adipocyte-derived EVs. These nanosized particles, secreted by adipocytes, carry a complex cargo of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, functioning as mediators of intercellular communication within the TME.305,306 Adipocyte-derived EVs can deliver bioactive molecules to tumor cells, potentially altering their signaling pathways and ultimately modifying their malignant behavior. While the precise molecular mechanisms and the full spectrum of functional consequences are still being actively investigated, the role of adipocyte EVs in mediating the complex crosstalk between adipose tissue and the TME is increasingly recognized as crucial.307 Researchers are developing advanced methodologies to isolate and analyze adipocyte-derived EVs, employing human differentiated adipocytes as surrogate mature adipocytes for in vitro studies to facilitate a deeper understanding of these intricate interactions.

Beyond EVs, adipocytes also play a critical role in modulating lipid metabolism within the TME. They represent a significant source of lipids, providing essential energy and building blocks for proliferating tumor cells.308–310 Cancer cells frequently exhibit altered lipid metabolism, characterized by increased uptake and utilization of fatty acids, to support their elevated energy demands and rapid growth.311–313 Dysregulation of lipid metabolism is particularly implicated in specific metastatic processes, notably in bone metastasis, where it significantly contributes to disease progression.83,314–316 Furthermore, identifying particular lipid biomarkers, such asLYPLA1 in intramuscular fat deposition, can provide novel insights into the complex metabolic interplay between adipocytes and tumor cells, potentially revealing new therapeutic targets.317–319

Although the role of CD36 in mediating fatty acid (FA) uptake and subsequent β-oxidation (FAO) is well established, recent studies emphasize that the type of fatty acids delivered by adipose depots is equally critical for metastatic behavior. For example, saturated palmitic acid (PA) selectively enriches CD36⁺ metastasis-initiating cells and enhances their dissemination capacity, while an abundance of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) reshapes membrane fluidity and redox tone, thereby altering the efficiency of metastatic seeding.320,321 Mechanistically, CD36 does not act in isolation but cooperates with intracellular chaperones such as FABP4, which direct exogenous FAs toward either β-oxidation or membrane remodeling, effectively coupling substrate availability to invasive programs.322,323 Notably, animal studies have revealed that high-fat or palmitate-rich diets increase metastatic frequency in a CD36-dependent manner, reinforcing the concept that dietary composition and depot-specific FA supply are active determinants of tumor dissemination.320,321

CAAs should not be viewed as a uniform population. Instead, single-cell and single-nucleus analyses of cancer-proximal adipose tissue demonstrate striking heterogeneity. Some CAA subsets exhibit a strong lipolytic bias, releasing FAs to fuel neighboring tumor cells, whereas others engage in de novo lipogenesis and desaturation, generating a lipid pool rich in MUFAs and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs).324,325 Enzymes such as SCD1 and FADS2 act as gatekeepers, refining the relative balance of saturated, mono-, and polyunsaturated species provided to cancer cells.326,327 This metabolic diversity may help explain why certain adipose-rich niches strongly support invasion and metastasis, while others appear more inert. It also raises intriguing questions: could mapping the “fatty-acid fingerprints” of CAAs predict metastatic potential?

Functionally, FAO does more than provide a backup fuel source. By generating ATP, NADPH, and acetyl-CoA, it enables tumor survival under therapeutic stress. Recent studies connect CPT1A-driven FAO to multiple resistance contexts: enhanced radioresistance through PGC-1α/CEBPB/CPT1A signaling, chemoresistance in colon and nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and impaired efficacy of targeted therapy in HER2⁺ BC, where FAO inhibition restores drug sensitivity.325,328,329 These findings suggest that FAO represents a modular resistance program, one that can be intercepted at several nodes—ranging from lipid uptake via CD36 to routing through FABP4/ACSLs, mitochondrial entry via CPT1A, and even downstream redox buffering mechanisms.

A deeper layer of complexity emerges at the intersection of lipid metabolism and ferroptosis regulation. Adipocyte-derived exosomes can remodel tumor lipid metabolism by suppressing miR-433-3p, thereby upregulating SCD1 in recipient cancer cells. This shift enriches MUFA pools, reduces phospholipid peroxidation, and ultimately supports tumor growth.330 Large oncologic datasets confirm that SCD1 not only enhances tumor proliferation but also confers ferroptosis resistance, while its inhibition sensitizes tumors to ferroptosis and even improves radiotherapy outcomes.321,331,332 Moreover, reviews of exosome–ferroptosis crosstalk describe additional adipocyte exosomal cargos, such as MTTP, that mitigate lipid peroxidation, underscoring the existence of a broad adipose-to-tumor protective axis.324,333 These data point to a coordinated circuit: CD36 defines the FA supply, FAO and MUFA synthesis preserve energetic and redox balance, and adipocyte exosomes reprogram desaturase activity to shield tumor cells from ferroptotic death.

These insights advance the field beyond the linear view of FFA–CD36–FAO toward a context-specific metabolic network with tangible therapeutic entry points. Potential interventions include CD36 blockade to restrict metastatic fueling, FAO inhibitors or CPT1A degradation to reverse therapy resistance, and SCD1/ACSL3 inhibition or ferroptosis-inducing combinations to overcome adipocyte-mediated redox protection.126,321,329 Looking ahead, stratifying patients according to their adipose FA fingerprint and CAA subtype could enable precision metabolic therapies tailored to the dominant adipose–tumor axis at play.

Adipocytes establish direct physical interactions with tumor cells and contribute to the remodeling of the ECM, further shaping the TME to favor tumor development.334 These physical interactions can create a supportive niche that promotes tumor growth by providing structural support and influencing signaling pathways. Adipocytes can also secrete ECM components and enzymes that modify the existing matrix, facilitating tumor cell invasion and migration and thereby promoting local invasion and distant metastasis.8,59,335,336 This multifaceted relationship underscores the importance of targeting adipocyte–tumor interactions in developing innovative cancer therapies. Targeting the metabolic and signaling pathways influenced by adipocytes may offer new avenues for therapeutic interventions in cancer treatment, particularly in obesity-related malignancies.

While the emerging understanding of adipocytes as active players in the TME presents intriguing possibilities, it is essential to critically evaluate the extent of their influence and the implications of this perspective. First, the traditional view of adipocytes as inert storage cells should not be entirely dismissed. There is a significant body of evidence suggesting that the primary role of adipocytes remains energy storage and metabolic regulation, which may not be directly linked to tumor progression in all contexts.337 The dynamic interactions proposed between adipocytes and tumor cells may be overstated, as the complexity of the TME involves numerous other factors, including immune cells, fibroblasts, and the ECM, which may play more pivotal roles in cancer dynamics.338,339 Moreover, while adipocyte-derived EVs are fascinating, their clinical relevance in cancer progression remains uncertain.340–343 The mechanisms by which EVs influence tumor behavior are not fully understood, and it is crucial to consider that the effects observed in vitro may not translate effectively to in vivo scenarios. Relying heavily on adipocyte-derived EVs as mediators of tumor communication risks oversimplifying the multifactorial nature of cancer biology. Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting CAAs

The role of adipokines in tumor–adipose tissue crosstalk

Adipokines, bioactive signaling molecules secreted by adipose tissue, are now recognized as critical mediators of the complex interactions between adipose tissue and the TME.344,345 These molecules influence tumor growth, progression, and response to therapy, highlighting the need for further research on their roles in cancer dynamics and potential therapeutic targeting. These signaling molecules exert direct and indirect effects on tumor cells, influencing various processes critical to cancer development and progression, including proliferation, survival, invasion, and metastasis.346,347 The dysregulation of adipokine secretion in obesity and metabolic disorders significantly contributes to the altered TME in these conditions, further highlighting their importance in cancer biology.348 Adiponectin, an abundant adipokine with predominantly anti-inflammatory properties, has generally been observed to exert protective effects against the development and progression of various cancers.349

Meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated that circulating adiponectin levels are frequently altered in numerous malignancies, often showing an inverse correlation with cancer risk and progression. Adiponectin exerts its anticancer effects through multiple mechanisms, including the modulation of insulin signaling, inhibition of angiogenesis, and regulation of inflammatory responses within the TME.350,351 However, it is essential to note that the role of adiponectin can be complex and context dependent.352–355 Beyond adiponectin, many other adipokines, including leptin, resistin, visfatin, and chemerin, play significant roles in modulating the TME. These adipokines often exhibit protumorigenic activities, promoting tumor growth and progression through diverse mechanisms. For instance, leptin, which is elevated in obesity, can stimulate cell proliferation, enhance angiogenesis, and promote invasion and metastasis.356,357 Resistin promotes inflammation and insulin resistance, which can contribute to a protumorigenic environment.358–360 Visfatin, also known as pre-B-cell colony-enhancing factor 1 (PBEF1), enhances cancer cell survival and migration.361–363

Furthermore, specific adipokines such as NOV/CCN3 have been identified as key links between obesity-associated insulin resistance and alterations in the TME, suggesting a crucial role in how metabolic dysfunction can fuel cancer development.364–366 Adipose tissue also influences the TME through its interactions with immune cells, particularly TAMs. A-FABP, a protein involved in intracellular fatty acid transport, is expressed in adipocytes and TAMs. Notably, the expression of A-FABP in TAMs has been shown to promote BC progression, highlighting the complex interplay between adipokines, lipid metabolism, immune cells, and tumor cells within the TME.366,367 TAMs are a major cellular component of the TME, and their polarization state and functional activity can be significantly modulated by factors derived from adipose tissue, including adipokines and lipids, ultimately influencing tumor behavior.368

Interestingly, CAAs—particularly those undergoing an adipocyte-to-myofibroblast transition—do not remain passive bystanders within the tumor milieu. Instead, they actively reshape the ECM in ways that facilitate tumor cell invasion. In this altered state, CAAs increase their secretion of structural ECM proteins, most notably collagen I and IV, while at the same time elevating the production of MMPs. The combination of augmented deposition and accelerated degradation creates a paradoxical environment: the ECM becomes less structured and mechanically weakened, thereby providing tumor cells with a more permissive pathway for migration and dissemination.369,370 This remodeling not only alters the physical architecture of the TME but also modulates biochemical signaling, further amplifying proinvasive cues.

Beyond these direct changes, transcriptional control has emerged as a critical layer of regulation. Among the key regulators, RUNX1 is a pivotal transcription factor orchestrating ECM remodeling. RUNX1 has been shown to directly govern the expression of ECM-related genes such as Fibronectin 1 (FN1), COL4A1, and Lumican (LUM) through interactions with the NPM1/FOSL2 complex, which enhances chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activation.371 This connection provides a tangible molecular bridge: RUNX1 is no longer an abstract player in adipocyte biology but a direct regulator of ECM reorganization in the tumor setting.

Moreover, the influence of RUNX1 extends beyond gene regulation. It is implicated in driving mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) proliferation and promoting myofibroblast differentiation, both of which contribute to ECM turnover and stromal evolution within the TME.372 Such findings suggest that RUNX1 acts as a master switch linking adipocyte lineage plasticity with broader stromal dynamics.

Now, a refined mechanistic framework appears. As adipocytes transdifferentiate into myofibroblast-like cells, they simultaneously enhance ECM deposition (e.g., collagens and fibronectin) and activate degradation pathways via MMPs. At the core of this transformation, RUNX1 emerges as a central modulator, coordinating transcriptional programmes that tie cellular plasticity directly to ECM remodeling. This dual function not only underscores the adaptability of CAAs but also highlights new avenues for therapeutic intervention aimed at disrupting stromal support for tumor progression.

Clinical and diagnostic implications

The intricate relationship between adipose tissue, the TME, and cancer carries significant clinical and diagnostic implications. Identifying specific biomarkers reflecting the complex interplay between adipose tissue and tumor cells holds promise for advancing cancer diagnosis and prognosis. For instance, machine-learning techniques have facilitated the identification of secretogranin V as a potential circulating diagnostic biomarker for PDAC specifically associated with adipopenia, highlighting the utility of considering adipose tissue-related factors in cancer detection.373 Furthermore, research has explored the potential of adipokines and lipid metabolites as biomarkers for various cancer types, which could provide valuable insights into disease progression and patient stratification. Recognizing the crucial role of the TME in cancer progression and therapeutic resistance has spurred the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting its components, including adipocytes and adipokines. This approach disrupts the supportive environment that fuels tumor growth and metastasis. Therapeutic interventions may involve disrupting the metabolic crosstalk between adipocytes and tumor cells, for example, by inhibiting lipid transfer or fatty acid oxidation. Modulating adipokine signaling pathways, either by blocking protumorigenic adipokines or enhancing the antitumorigenic effects, is another promising avenue.

CAAs are remodeled stromal adipocytes that arise at carcinoma invasion fronts; they are not neoplastic adipocytic tumors (e.g., ALT/WDLPS, myxoid or pleomorphic liposarcoma), which are defined by characteristic genetic alterations such as MDM2/CDK4 amplification or DDIT3 fusions and require distinct diagnostic workflows in surgical pathology.374 Refocusing on CAA–tumor crosstalk, several histological and molecular readouts now function as interface-level biomarkers. First, morphology-based features that quantify tumor–adipose juxtaposition—such as SARIFA (Stroma AReactive Invasion Front Areas)—carry independent prognostic value and have been validated across gastric cancer and PDAC, where SARIFA positivity tracks smaller adipocyte areas at the invasion front, higher FABP4 and CD68+ macrophages, and worse outcomes.375,376 Second, transcriptional signatures capturing adipocyte enrichment/rewiring—e.g., high adipocyte expression modules and lipid-handling genes such as FABP4—stratify prognosis in gastric cancer and may generalize to other adipose-rich tumor beds.377 Third, imaging biomarkers now quantify peritumoral adipose remodeling in vivo: radiomic texture features from peritumoral adipose tissue on FDG-PET/CT and MRI predict axillary nodal status, neoadjuvant response, and disease progression in BC, thereby noninvasively capturing CAA-linked inflammation and metabolic coupling.378–380 At the secretome level, endotrophin (the COL6A3-C5 fragment) is an adipocyte-derived matrix cue that drives fibrotic, inflammatory TME programs; its tumor-promoting activity and biomarker potential are supported by mechanistic and translational studies and warrant inclusion when discussing CAA-centric diagnostics.381 Finally, emerging circulating candidates include Secretogranin V (SCG5): machine-learning proteomics identified serum SCG5 as a diagnostic marker in PDAC associated with cancer-related adipopenia, and recent data suggest prognostic relevance in NSCLC—together indicating a systemic readout of tumor–adipose dysregulation that merits validation in CAA-rich settings.373,382 Consistent with the CAA literature, exosomal miR-155-driven CCL2/CCL5 signaling from the tumor–adipose niche also links interface activity to macrophage recruitment, providing an orthogonal, immunologic biomarker axis.383

Adipose tissue inflammation within the TME extends far beyond the classical depiction of CLS, in which macrophages encircle necrotic adipocytes. Although CLS remain a reliable histological hallmark of adipose dysfunction and correlate strongly with disease severity, they represent only one dimension of a far more intricate immunological landscape. Recent studies have revealed that the inflammatory profile of CAAs is shaped by a diverse constellation of immune mediators, each contributing to the evolving tumor milieu in distinct but interconnected ways.

Among these mediators, interleukin-6 (IL-6) has emerged as a particularly influential cytokine, serving as a molecular bridge between adipose inflammation and tumor progression. Persistent IL-6 secretion from CAAs not only sustains chronic systemic inflammation but also activates the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway, a well-recognized driver of cancer cachexia and tumor-promoting metabolic reprogramming.297 Interestingly, IL-6 may also interact with other adipocyte-derived signals to amplify paracrine crosstalk, further entrenching a protumorigenic niche. In parallel, osteopontin (SPP1)—a multifunctional matricellular protein enriched in inflamed adipose tissue—has gained prominence for its dual role in ECM remodeling and immune modulation. By recruiting macrophages into the tumor–adipose interface and cooperating with IL-4 to skew them toward an M2-like phenotype, osteopontin reinforces immunosuppressive circuits that blunt antitumor defenses.384,385 Its capacity to integrate structural and immune processes highlights the complexity of adipocyte-driven inflammatory signaling.

While macrophages dominate much of the discussion, T-cell subsets are now recognized as critical participants in the CAA inflammatory network. The enrichment of regulatory T cells (Tregs) within adipose depots often fosters an immunosuppressive environment that curtails antitumor responses. In contrast, the expansion of Th17 populations has been associated with intensified inflammatory signaling and, in some contexts, enhanced angiogenesis.386 This delicate equilibrium between effector and regulatory T cells suggests that even subtle shifts in T-cell composition could substantially influence tumor trajectory.

Equally noteworthy, although less extensively studied, are the contributions of dendritic cells (DCs). Adipocyte-derived free fatty acids and cytokines have been shown to disrupt DC maturation and antigen-presenting capacity, thereby skewing T-cell responses toward tolerance rather than immunity.387,388 Such findings indicate that even immune populations not traditionally associated with adipose tissue biology are functionally reprogrammed within the CAA niche. This observation raises an important question: might targeting adipocyte–DC crosstalk represent an underexplored therapeutic opportunity?

These insights illustrate that the inflammatory milieu of CAAs cannot be reduced to macrophage-centered pathology alone. Rather, it is orchestrated by a multilayered immune network, encompassing soluble mediators such as IL-6 and osteopontin, T-cell subsets with divergent immunologic roles, and dendritic cells with altered functional capacity. Acknowledging this complexity not only provides a more accurate understanding of how adipose inflammation sustains tumor growth but also suggests novel avenues for therapeutic intervention aimed at immunomodulating the adipose–tumor interface.

The proteomic landscape of adipose tissue and cancer

Understanding the proteomic changes in adipose tissue and the TME within the cancer context is paramount for identifying key molecular players and uncovering potential therapeutic targets. Succination, a posttranslational modification involving adding a succinyl group to lysine residues on proteins, can significantly alter protein function and activity, thereby contributing to metabolic adaptations in adipose tissue and cancer cells.389 This modification has been shown to affect enzymes involved in energy metabolism, signaling pathways, and antioxidant defense, potentially influencing cellular processes crucial for tumor growth and survival. Mass spectrometry-based approaches are indispensable for comprehensively characterizing the succinated proteome, mapping the sites of succination, and elucidating its functional significance in the complex interplay between adipose tissue and cancer.389 Further research is needed to fully understand how succination modulates the interactions between adipocytes, tumor cells, and other TME components.

Two related modifications predominate in the adipose-conditioned TME: lysine succinylation (addition of a succinyl group from succinyl-CoA, often reversed by SIRT5) and cysteine succination (irreversible addition of fumarate forming 2-succino-cysteine). Both track succinate/fumarate pools that rise in hypoxia or mitochondrial dysfunction and are influenced by CAA-derived metabolites. (i) Metabolic enzymes: succinylation of GLS and GDH/SHMT2 increases glutaminolysis/one-carbon flux, supporting nucleotide synthesis and redox balance in hypoxic regions; desuccinylation by SIRT5 restrains this pathway.390–392 Succinylated LDHA shows heightened catalytic activity and promotes lactate export, stabilizing HIF-1 and acidifying the niche.393,394 (ii) Chaperones/signaling adaptors: chaperones, including HSP90, are subject to succinylation in multiple succinylomes, and given HSP90’s role in stabilizing HIF-1α and oncogenic kinases, succinylation is poised to modulate client stability and signaling output.393–397 (iii) Chromatin: histone succinylation—exemplified by H3K122succ and regulated by acyl-transferases such as HAT1/KAT2A—loosens nucleosomes and favors transcription of glycolytic/angiogenic programs in hypoxia.398–401 (iv) Electrophile-stress axis: succination of KEAP1 cysteines by fumarate stabilizes NRF2, driving antioxidant and chemoresistance programs; KEAP1 is further sensitive to acyl modifications on lysine, underlining broad metabolite sensing in this pathway402–404 (Table 7).

Recent studies have extended this framework by demonstrating that succinylation of both histone and nonhistone proteins directly regulates oncogenic signaling and metabolic rewiring in tumors. Proteomic mapping coupled with functional validation has revealed succinylation events that stabilize oncogenic drivers or suppress tumor suppressor activity, highlighting potential therapeutic targets.405,406 These findings suggest that systematic succinylation profiling could uncover biomarkers of CAA–tumor metabolic coupling and identify points for therapeutic intervention.

Deconvoluting complex tissues, such as the TME, using expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL)-based analyses provides a powerful approach to identify genetic variants that influence gene expression in specific cell types, including adipocytes, immune cells, and cancer cells.407 eQTL mapping can reveal how genetic variation contributes to the heterogeneity of the TME and how it shapes the expression of genes encoding key signaling molecules, growth factors, and metabolic enzymes. This approach offers valuable insights into the genetic regulation of TME components. It can help to identify individuals at increased risk of developing cancer or who are more likely to respond to specific therapies.407

Building on this, integrative pipelines now combine eQTL maps with GWAS and single-cell transcriptomics to resolve cell-type–specific regulatory effects in the adipose–tumor interface. For instance, germline eQTL variants have been linked to the expression of metabolic enzymes and cytokines in stromal and adipose compartments, providing mechanistic insight into how inherited genetic variation can influence tumor progression.408,409 When coupled with machine learning, these methods can prioritize candidate biomarkers (e.g., lipid metabolism–related transcripts or cytokine regulators) for clinical validation.

These new approaches add functional depth: succination profiling links PTMs to altered protein behavior in adipocytes and tumor cells, while eQTL deconvolution connects genetic variation to gene expression programs within the TME. Integrated into a multiomics framework, they hold promise for identifying both molecular vulnerabilities and predictive biomarkers at the adipose–tumor interface.

Racial disparities and the adipose–tumor interaction

A growing body of evidence now highlights the presence of racial disparities in the intricate interactions between adipose tissue and tumors. These differences may significantly influence cancer outcomes across diverse populations. Specifically, investigations into BC among Black women have uncovered distinct characteristics within their TME when compared to non-Black Latinas and Caucasians.410 These variations might stem from differences in white adipose tissue inflammation, a factor known to influence tumor growth and progression.411 Consequently, a comprehensive understanding of these interactions is vital for developing targeted interventions that effectively address the unique needs of diverse racial groups, as underscored by recent studies.412

These investigations have consistently revealed a higher prevalence of immunosuppressive macrophages and crown-like structures within the breast tumors of Black women.413 Such disparities in the cellular composition of the TME, potentially influenced by variations in adipose tissue distribution, metabolism, and inflammatory profiles across racial groups, may contribute to the more aggressive clinical course and poorer prognosis often observed in BC among black women.411 The interplay between adipose tissue and the TME is, undeniably, a complex and dynamic process that profoundly impacts cancer development and its response to therapy. Beyond simply providing lipids and nutrients, adipocytes actively engage within the TME. They influence matrix remodeling through the secretion of ECM components and modifying enzymes and mediate intercellular communication via the release of EVs.414 Furthermore, adipokines, crucial signaling molecules derived from adipose tissue, play a pivotal role in this bidirectional crosstalk, influencing various aspects of tumor biology.415 Considering the inherent heterogeneity of the TME, both within and between tumors, which significantly contributes to therapeutic resistance, continuous and in-depth research into the specific molecular mechanisms governing these complex interactions becomes imperative. This is especially true when factoring in the influence of race and ethnicity on adipose tissue-tumor dynamics. Such research is crucial for developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies aimed at effectively targeting the adipose–tumor axis and, ultimately, improving cancer outcomes for all patients. Addressing these disparities will necessitate comprehensive research efforts that integrate molecular, genetic, and environmental factors influencing adipose–tumor interactions in different populations.

While the exploration of racial disparities in the adipose–tumor interaction presents a compelling narrative, it is essential to consider alternative perspectives that challenge an exclusive focus on race as the primary determinant of cancer outcomes. One argument posits that emphasizing racial differences may inadvertently oversimplify the intricate interplay of socioeconomic, environmental, and lifestyle factors that profoundly contribute to cancer disparities. For instance, disparities in access to healthcare, education, and nutrition can significantly affect cancer prevalence and treatment outcomes, often eclipsing biological differences solely attributed to race. Moreover, attributing distinct TMEs primarily to adipose tissue interactions may overlook other critical factors, such as genetic predispositions and hormonal influences that vary across populations. Research indicates that lifestyle choices, including diet and physical activity, can significantly impact tumor biology and adipose tissue characteristics. This suggests that interventions focused on these modifiable factors could prove more effective than those solely targeting racial differences.

CAA–immune crosstalk and immune-metabolic rewiring (Fig. 7)

The impact of obesity on the tumor microenvironment and the metabolic crosstalk between tumor cells and adipocytes. Adipose tissue from an obese host is composed of hypertrophied adipocytes, some of which undergo necrosis and form crown-like structures surrounded by macrophages. Adipose tissue inflammation is driven by M1-like and metabolically activated macrophages, which secrete inflammatory mediators. Tumors developing adjacent to obese adipose tissue are influenced by chronic immunometabolic dysregulation and receive numerous inflammatory and metabolic signals, leading to an immunosuppressed tumor microenvironment characterized by ineffective tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and immunosuppressive M2-like macrophage polarization. Adipose tissue further contributes to the tumor microenvironment through recruitment and trans differentiation of cancer-associated fibroblasts from mesenchymal stem cells, as well as adipocyte-derived fibroblasts from adipocytes. In parallel, aggressive tumor cells invade surrounding adipose tissue, inducing lipolysis in adipocytes and competing for lipids to fuel their rapid proliferation and survival under metabolic stress. The metabolic interface extends beyond the tumor microenvironment, as tumors also reprogram distant adipose tissues by suppressing major metabolic processes, a phenomenon partly counteracted by adipocyte p62. Both local and systemic alterations result in increased fatty acid release or circulating triglyceride/cholesterol-rich lipoproteins, which fulfill the high bioenergetic demands of tumor cells through fatty acid uptake and oxidation. CAAs sculpt antitumor immunity through coordinated cytokine programs and lipid transfer that rewire macrophage and T-cell function. At invasion fronts, CAAs release IL-6, TNF-α, leptin, CCL2, and CCL5, recruiting monocytes and biasing TAMs toward M2-like states via STAT3 and ERK/MEK signaling, with downstream VEGF-A production and angiogenesis in breast cancer models.416,417 In parallel, osteopontin (SPP1) acts as a matricellular amplifier: it attracts macrophages and synergizes with IL-4 to stabilize protumorigenic polarization, thereby reinforcing immunosuppression and increasing recurrence risk.418

Lipid flux from CAAs provides a second, orthogonal control layer. Free and oxidized fatty acids taken up via CD36 increase lipid peroxidation and blunt CD8⁺ T-cell effector function, fostering ferroptosis-prone, dysfunctional T cells within tumors.419,420 Within myeloid compartments, FAO-dependent programmes—driven in some contexts by CPT1A—promote M2 polarization and immune evasion, highlighting a therapeutically testable FAO–TAM axis.421,422 CAA-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) likely potentiate this reprogramming by shuttling lipids and metabolic enzymes that tune macrophage signaling and T-cell metabolism.423

Antigen-presenting cells are similarly affected. Lipid loading and oxidized-lipid adducts in dendritic cells impair cross-presentation and migration, diminishing cytotoxic T-cell priming; recent syntheses extend this paradigm across DC subsets and link it to cholesterol and phospholipid handling in the TME.424,425 Finally, adipokine signaling intersects with these metabolic circuits: leptin and IL-6 not only drive TAM recruitment and polarization but also modulate T-cell metabolism and checkpoint expression, supporting combinatorial strategies that pair CD36/FAO blockade with IL-6/leptin axis modulation to restore antitumor immunity.417,419

Conclusion

Adipose tissue plays a pivotal and multifaceted role in shaping the tumor microenvironment, driving cancer progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. The transformation of adipocytes into CAAs leads to profound alterations in lipid metabolism, immune signaling, and intercellular communication, promoting a protumorigenic niche. Beyond local interactions, systemic effects such as cachexia underscore the endocrine functions of adipose tissue in malignancy. Obesity-related inflammation, dysregulated adipokines, and metabolic reprogramming further contribute to the aggressive behavior of tumors in adipose-rich microenvironments. Emerging evidence highlights CAAs as potential therapeutic targets, and interventions aimed at disrupting their crosstalk with tumor cells offer promising avenues for cancer treatment. However, the complexity and heterogeneity of the adipose–tumor interface necessitate integrated, multidisciplinary approaches combining molecular insights with clinical strategies. Future efforts should prioritize translating these mechanistic understandings into precision therapies that target cancer cells and modulate their supportive adipose niches to enhance treatment efficacy and improve patient outcomes. In summary, this review contributes a unified, mechanism-driven framework that couples CAA biology to therapeutic resistance and identifies tractable intervention points (LCN2, FAM3C, SCD1, BAT activation, GLP-1RAs). By aligning figures with text and specifying biomarkers and study designs, it offers testable, clinically actionable hypotheses rather than a descriptive catalog, thereby charting a practical agenda for translational trials.
