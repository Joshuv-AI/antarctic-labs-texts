Evaluation of hydrogen and oxygen mixture addition in internal combustion engines under real driving conditions
Authors: František Synák
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe addition of a hydrogen and oxygen mixture (HHO) into internal combustion engines is often associated with expectations of oticeable increases in engine performance and substantial reductions in emissions and fuel consumption. This paper investigates the actual effects of introducing hydrogen and oxygen into combustion engines. A literature review was conducted, identifying several research gaps in this field. An improved experimental methodology was developed to quantify the effects of hydrogen and oxygen addition while minimizing the influence of external factors on the results. Measurements were carried out using three vehicles. Two of the vehicles were identical and operated for 67,000 km under the exact same conditions, with only one of them equipped with an HHO generator. The results of the measurements, discussed in the context of other scientific publications, indicate that HHO addition produced no measurable improvement in engine power or torque, and only minor changes in exhaust gas composition. These findings demonstrate that the benefits of HHO addition are significantly lower than those claimed by commercial providers. This study provides evidence-based insight into the limited practical impact of HHO on vehicle performance and emissions, helping to inform sustainable mobility solutions.Keywords: HHO, Real driving conditions, Emissions, Fuel consumption, Engine power, DecarbonisationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Vehicles powered by internal combustion engines are still a very significant part of the vehicle fleet. The average age of vehicles within the European Union or the United States exceeds 12 years1,2. With increasing vehicle age and mileage, there is a tendency for carbon deposits to accumulate within the engine, as well as in the intake and exhaust systems3. Carbon build-up in the engine can also lead to elevated concentrations of harmful components in exhaust gases, difficulties in passing mandatory emissions inspections, and deterioration in air quality, having an adverse impact on public health4. In the context of growing concerns over air quality and sustainable mobility, exploring methods to reduce vehicle emissions has become increasingly important. The potential contribution of HHO to lowering harmful exhaust components and improving engine efficiency could support cleaner urban transport and more sustainable vehicle operation. Hydrogen-oxygen (HHO) addition has been proposed as one such method, with the potential to influence combustion characteristics and lower harmful exhaust components. Additional consequences of engine fouling include increased fuel consumption and reduced engine performance5. The progressive decline in engine performance, increased fuel consumption, and the rise in harmful exhaust emissions with increasing vehicle age and mileage affect both spark-ignition and compression ignition engines. These factors have negative impacts on public health, the economy, and the depletion of non-renewable energy resources6.

Among the methods used to counteract the deterioration of the performance of vehicles with internal combustion engines, or to enhance their performance, is the addition of a hydrogen and oxygen mixture into the engine’s intake air7. This hydrogen-oxygen mixture, most commonly produced via water electrolysis, is referred to as HHO, or alternatively as oxy-hydrogen, stoichiometric hydrogen-oxygen mixture, stoichiometric H₂/O₂ mixture, electrolytic gas or Brown’s gas8–10. Commercial claims regarding HHO often report substantial increases in engine power, reductions in emissions, and improvements in fuel economy; however, these claims generally lack rigorous experimental verification11. In this study, HHO systems are evaluated specifically as commercially available retrofit devices, without optimization of dosing or control of the HHO-to-fuel ratio, in order to reflect their real-world application conditions. In addition to HHO generator manufacturers and providers of engine cleaning services using HHO, the use of HHO is sometimes recommended by technicians of emissions inspections12,13. There is also an ongoing effort to enable the installation of HHO generators into vehicles without requiring formal approval14,15. Despite the existence of a relatively large group of scientific publications dedicated to the issue of adding HHO into internal combustion engines, research gaps have been identified within this group of work. There is a lack of relevant scientific studies that would conclusively either confirm or refute the claims regarding the significant benefits of HHO. A discrepancy exists between the results reported by authors of scientific studies based on simulations and mathematical calculations, who claim a substantial reduction in nitrogen oxides (NOx) due to HHO, and those of researchers conducting experimental measurements, who, conversely, claim an increase in NOx. Most experimental scientific publications focus on research involving laboratory-scale engines with small displacements, typically up to 0.5 dm− 3. However, such laboratory engines do not account for real-world operating conditions, engine contamination, wear, and similar factors. There is very limited representation of experimental research conducted on real vehicles under actual driving conditions. Additionally, a gap in the literature has been identified concerning the impact of HHO on the results of regular emissions inspections. Likewise, with the exception of one16, studies on the immediate effects of engine cleaning using HHO are almost entirely lacking.

The aim of this publication is to propose a measurement methodology that takes practical conditions into account and subsequently assess the benefits of using HHO in internal combustion engines.

This publication pays attention to extensive experimental measurements performed with real vehicles. The issue researched is divided into two parts. The first part focuses on determining the immediate effects of cleaning the vehicle’s engine using HHO from an external source. The second part addresses the effects of both immediate and long-term addition of HHO into the engine from an HHO generator installed directly in the vehicle.

The investigation of the immediate effects of engine cleaning using HHO was performed with a vehicle with high mileage operating in urban traffic and equipped with exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) technology. The vehicle with EGR technology was selected due to the tendency of such vehicles to experience engine carbon fouling, which is associated with reduced engine power, increased fuel consumption, elevated emission production, diminished vehicle’s capability to pass regular emissions inspections, and decreased cylinder compression pressures. Thus, the research was focused specifically on these vehicle characteristics before and after cleaning using HHO.

The investigation of both long-term and immediate effects of HHO addition from onboard HHO generator was conducted using two vehicles. Both vehicles had been driven on identical routes under the same operating conditions since their manufacture, each accumulating approximately 67,000 km, with the only difference being that one vehicle was equipped with an HHO generator, while the other was not. The influence of both long-term and immediate HHO addition on vehicle performance and emissions was observed.

The measurement methodology was designed to reflect real-world driving conditions. There was emphasis placed on eliminating the influence of external factors that could distort the results. Such factor include, for example, the gradual increase in measured engine power during repeated runs on a cylinder dynamometer, which is often incorrectly attributed to the effect of HHO. The applicability and repeatability of the proposed methodology were verified.

The measurement results were compared and discussed in the context of claims made by providers of engine cleaning services using HHO, vendors of HHO generators, and findings reported in other scientific publications.

The novelty of this publication lies in several areas that are either scarcely represented or entirely absent in current scientific literature. The first novel aspect is the selection of vehicles from real-world operation, enabling the evaluation of HHO generator usage over several years and tens of thousands of kilometres driven since the production of identical vehicles. In contrast to theoretical studies or laboratory-based experiments with small-displacement engines, this work uses real vehicles with varying degrees of wear. Another novelty of this study is the proposed and validated methodology, which enables a realistic assessment of the contribution of HHO to mitigating the negative impacts of road transport. The scientific contribution of the publication is further enhanced by a discussion of results obtained from extensive experimental measurements, compared with findings from dozens of other scientific studies. On a practical level, the results can be used in the development and approval of legislation related to the installation of HHO generators, as well as in the elaboration of methodological procedures for performing vehicle emissions inspections. On a scientific level, the data obtained can serve as input data for mathematical simulations as well as for discussions of outcomes from experimental or computational approaches.

Given the increasing focus on reducing energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions in transportation, investigating the real-world effects of HHO on fuel efficiency and pollutant reduction is relevant for advancing sustainable mobility solutions. This study provides a controlled experimental evaluation of HHO effects using real vehicles operated under identical long-term driving conditions, addressing the lack of experimental studies performed under real-world conditions identified in the literature.

Literary review

In publication17, the authors determined the effects of hydrogen addition to the engine on ignition dynamics, flame propagation, and flame-wall interaction using Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) method. As this is a numerical simulation technique, several simplifications had to be applied, for example, that the engine geometry was modified by removing the long crevice volume and the injector cap, and no-slip inert walls are assumed to be isothermal17. In publication18, which also focuses on air-hydrogen mixtures, the authors introduced similar simplifications, such as neglecting the thermal conductivity of materials, assuming ideal gas behaviour, and other similar conditions. Simplifications were also considered in the numerical simulation performed using Diesel-RK system, as described in publication19. These included assuming a perfectly homogeneous mixture, ideal combustion, and neglecting engine wear, leakages, and other real-world operational factors.

Several publications have been paid attention to experimental measurements that account for all relevant factors influencing the effects of HHO addition into engines.

During the internal combustion engines’ operation, carbon fouling occurs within the engine components20,21. HHO is also used for decarbonisation of engines, including the intake and exhaust systems, as written in publication22. In publication23, the authors focused on the impact of spark-ignition engine decarbonisation on noise and vibration. They report that the application of HHO for decarbonisation resulted in an average reduction in engine noise by 3.28%, with a maximum reduction of 8.42%, and a reduction in vibrations by an average of 3.44%, with a maximum of 16.38%. In publication24, the authors also focused on the effect of cleaning a compression-ignition tractor engine using HHO on exhaust emissions. A review of the available scientific literature revealed that few relevant studies have addressed the direct effects of cleaning spark-ignition engines with HHO on vehicle dynamics and emissions.

The continuous addition of HHO into the engine’s intake manifold and its effects on performance, emissions, and fuel consumption are more extensively represented in the scientific literature25,26. Several studies have specifically paid attention to the impact of HHO on the engine performance19,27,28. Authors of publication27 conclude a maximum increase in engine power of 3.67% and torque of 3.40% due to HHO addition. Publication19 examined the effects of HHO as a function of its quantity, concluding that the HHO flow rate should relate to the engine speed, yielding a 14.2% increase in engine power and a 4.8% increase in torque. In publication29, a 4.2% rise in maximum engine power was observed, however, power measurements were taken when the accelerator pedal was applied at only 50%. In relation to the impact of HHO addition on the engine’s smoke opacity, publication30 reports a 3.1% reduction in smoke opacity when using diesel blended with 20% ethanol. A more substantial decrease of over 90% is observed in publication31, but this corresponds to a significantly higher proportion of HHO in the intake air than in30. In the study by Polverino et al.32, a theoretical energetic analysis of on-board HHO production was performed, showing that the potential fuel-saving benefits are strongly limited by the energy demand of electrolysis and depend on engine operating conditions.

An important aspect of HHO addition is its impact on exhaust gas emissions. Publication33 reports a reduction of CO by 66%, CO2 by 33%, HC by 38%, and NOx by 11%. However, it should be noted that the authors of publication33 obtained these results not through direct measurement, but by combining experimental research with modelling, conducted at engine idle speed. In publication34, where diesel fuel was blended with 10% biodiesel, the CO concentration in the exhaust gases decreased by a maximum of 14%. In publication35, the authors report several factors influencing both the increase and decrease in CO and CO₂ emissions. While the CO concentration decreased in nearly all cases following the addition of HHO, the CO₂ concentration decreased only at high engine speeds and with the lowest amount of HHO added, and thus by 18%. As engine speed decreased and the amount of HHO increased, the CO₂ concentration began to rise.

Relevant research overview.

The energy efficiency of HHO generators, i.e., the relationship between the energy supplied to the HHO generator and the energy that can be obtained from it, is relatively low, as reported in publication32, in which the efficiency of HHO generators used in the studies presented in42–45 was analyzed using theoretical calculations.

As shown in Table 1, despite the broader focus of scientific publications on the effects of HHO addition to engines, the majority of studies have been conducted using small-displacement or laboratory engines. In many cases, measurements are performed only at specific engine speeds or under idle conditions. Within the scope of engine cleaning using externally supplied HHO, only a single publication has been identified.

Overall, while numerous studies have examined the effects of HHO addition on engine performance, emissions, and smoke opacity, most experiments were conducted on small-displacement, laboratory, or simulated engines, often under simplified or idle conditions. Very few studies have directly evaluated HHO applications in real, in-use vehicles or assessed the combined effects on engine cleaning, fuel consumption, and emissions under practical driving conditions. This gap motivates the present study, which aims to experimentally investigate HHO effects in real vehicles under controlled, yet realistic, operational conditions.

Measurement methodology

Measurements of the impact of HHO use on selected vehicle characteristics were performed with two vehicles – the Škoda Fabia and the Kia Ceed. The study aims to provide an independent, long-term, real-world validation of the effect of HHO on engine performance, emissions, and fuel consumption.

This vehicle, having driven 230,000 km, represents a high-mileage engine, allowing assessment of HHO impact under long-term engine operation.

The engine control unit was checked prior to measurements using diagnostic software, and no stored faults or errors were detected.

Engine and exhaust system cleaning, including the catalytic converter, was carried out on the Škoda Fabia using HHO introduced upstream of the engine’s air intake filter. The cleaning process was performed with a Hydrogen Carbon Cleaning Machine (HCC-5000), a machine for production of Oxy-Hydrogen gas by electrolysis of distilled/demineralised water, intended for removing carbon deposits from internal parts in internal combustion engines47. HHO is produced through electrolysis using a 30% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution and demineralised water. The gas production rate of the machine is 30 l.min-1, or 1,800 l.h-148. At a laboratory temperature of 21 °C, the HHO gas density was estimated to be approximately 0.536 g.l-1. Based on this value, the volumetric flow rate of 1800 l.h-1 corresponds to a mass flow rate of approximately 16 g.min-1, or 0.96 kg.h-1. Considering the engine power of 74 kW, Table 2, this corresponds to a normalized HHO dosage of approximately 13 g.h-1.kW-1 of engine power. Since the cleaning procedure lasted one hour, the total supplied HHO dose was approximately 1 kg. This specific device was selected due to its widespread use in practical applications, as similar or identical machines are commonly employed for engine cleaning or decarbonisation49–51. The cleaning procedure, which included the engine and the exhaust system, lasted one hour, during which the engine alternated between idle and 3,000 revolutions per minute (rpm) operation, with each mode maintained for approximately equal durations.

The measurement of the course of engine power and torque of the Škoda Fabia

The measurement was performed with a MAHA MSR 1050 cylinder dynamometer. The measurement of engine power and torque on a chassis dynamometer represents a standard and commonly used methodology for evaluating the parameters of internal combustion engines. This procedure is considered reliable for comparative analyses, as it enables stable steady-state operating conditions and eliminates the influence of transient states. The measurement deviation is ± 2% from the measured value, as also claimed by the manufacturer52. Measurements were conducted at the engine’s operating temperature, ranging from 90 °C to 98 °C. Measurements were carried out under controlled laboratory conditions, at an ambient temperature of approximately 21 ± 2 °C and under standard atmospheric pressure. The vehicle was tested on a chassis dynamometer in an enclosed test facility with ensured ventilation, in order to minimize the influence of environmental conditions and ensure repeatability of the measurements. During measuring, the fourth gear was engaged, and the accelerator pedal was fully applied. To isolate the effect of HHO engine cleaning alone, all measurements were performed using the same vehicle components before and after the procedure. Moreover, measurements were carried out under identical laboratory conditions both prior to and following the cleaning process. To improve measurement accuracy, the power was measured at least five times, until the power and torque curves overlapped under identical conditions. This approach was adopted because, particularly in vehicles with higher mileage, engine power and torque may gradually increase across repeated measurements, even under the same conditions. Contributing factors include engine control unit adaptation and a self-cleaning effect, which may occur even without the addition of HHO. Any observed differences between pre- and post HHO measurements can be directly attributed to the HHO treatment, as vehicle components, fuel, and operating conditions were kept constant. To ensure objective results, repeated measurements were performed until the engine power and torque curves showed consistent values under repeated measurements, as also described in publications56–58 focused on repeatability of power measurements on cylinder dynamometers. HHO-based engine cleaning is often accompanied by replacement of spark plugs, engine oil, and the air filter. Following the cleaning process, drivers are typically advised to refuel with higher-octane gasoline and to operate the engine at higher RPMs with greater accelerator application. These behavioural changes can independently influence engine performance, even in the absence of HHO effects59. To isolate the effect of HHO engine cleaning alone, all measurements were performed using the same vehicle components before and after the procedure. Moreover, measurements were carried out under identical laboratory conditions both prior to and following the cleaning process.

Measurement of the composition of exhaust gases from the Škoda Fabia

The composition of exhaust gases was measured while vehicle driving at the speed of 50 km.h− 1 and 90 km.h− 1. When driving at the speed of 50 km.h− 1, the third gear was engaged and when driving at the speed of 90 km.h− 1, the fifth gear was engaged. Measurements were performed while driving on ground level, and while driving at 6% rising gradient. Each measurement was conducted over duration of 5 min to ensure stabilization of driving conditions. Values were recorded only after reaching a steady state, in order to maximize data relevance and reliability.

The driving simulation was performed using a MAHA MSR 1050 cylinder dynamometer. The driving resistance settings were configured in accordance with the conditions specified in EN 30 0556, ensuring that the simulated drive accurately reflected real-world on-road conditions, including driving resistances60. An initial coast-down test was performed on a flat road segment, during which the vehicle was accelerated to approximately 120 km/h, and then shifted into neutral gear. While the vehicle decelerated in neutral, vehicle speed and time from gear disengagement were monitored and recorded. The resulting coast-down data were subsequently entered into the dynamometer’s control system. A corresponding coast-down test was then conducted on the dynamometer. This process, including roller braking and inertia simulation, was repeated until the deceleration curve on the dynamometer matched that recorded during the on-road, thereby ensuring that the simulated driving resistances corresponded to real driving conditions.

To measure the composition of exhaust gases, MAHA MGT 5 analyser was used. The following exhaust gas components were measured: carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO₂), unburned hydrocarbons (HC), oxygen (O₂), lambda coefficient (λ), and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ). The measurement deviation of the MAHA MGT 5 analyser is 0.03 vol% for CO, 0.05 vol% for CO₂, 1 ppm vol. for HC, 0.1 vol% for O₂, and ± 2 ppm vol. for NOx61. The MAHA MGT 5 analyser operates on the non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) principle for CO, CO₂ and HC measurements, while O₂ and NOₓ are measured using electrochemical sensor cells. Measurements were conducted at the engine’s operating temperature, ranging from 89 °C to 96 °C.

Measurement of the fuel consumption with the Škoda Fabia

Fuel consumption was measured under the same conditions as the exhaust gas composition analysis, in accordance with the requirements specified in EN 30 055660. The amount of fuel consumed was determined using a volumetric flow meter AIC 1200, with a measurement deviation of ± 0.5% of the measured volume54. The measurement was conducted at the engine’s operating temperature, ranging from 89 °C to 96 °C.

Composition of exhaust gases during regular emissions inspection

The exhaust gas composition during the regular emissions inspection was measured using the MAHA MGT 5 device. The measurement was conducted at the engine’s operating temperature, ranging from 89 °C to 96 °C. The concentrations of CO, HC, CO₂, O₂, and the lambda coefficient were determined. Measurements were performed at elevated engine speeds (between 2500 min⁻¹ and 2600 min⁻¹) as well as at idle speed (between 700 min⁻¹ and 800 min⁻¹), in accordance with the methodological guideline for conducting regular emissions inspection62. Values were automatically recorded after 30 s of measurement and only once the readings had stabilized.

Measurement of the cranking current of the Škoda Fabia

Cranking current serves as an indirect indicator of cylinder pressure in the engine. The measurement of electric current in the cables leading to the starter motor during engine starting was carried out using the Bosch FSA 740 device in combination with current clamps. The measurement deviation for electric current is ± 1%63. The measurement was conducted at the engine’s operating temperature. This is an indirect diagnostic method based on the principle that increased cylinder sealing, i.e. reduced pressure leakage, leads to higher electrical current demand for starter operation, and vice versa. A higher compression pressure requires greater torque to rotate the engine, resulting in an increased current draw64. The objective of the cranking current measurement was not to determine the absolute value of cylinder pressure or the compression ratio. Instead, this method was employed as an indirect diagnostic approach to assess relative changes in cylinder conditions. The primary aim was to evaluate whether the engine cleaning process using one-hour HHO application affects the cylinder sealing condition, which may manifest as a change in mechanical resistance during engine cranking and consequently in the starter current draw. Therefore, the method was used exclusively for a comparative assessment of the engine condition before and after HHO application, with variations in cranking current interpreted as an indirect indicator of changes in cylinder sealing and related compression conditions.

Measurement methodology with the Kia Ceed vehicles

The measurements were carried out using two identical vehicles, with the same mileage and operated under the same conditions, as both vehicles had been driven along identical routes, at the same times, and under the same operating conditions since manufacture. All measurements presented for the Kia Ceed vehicles in this study were conducted specifically for this research and do not include previously published or reused experimental data. The use of vehicles operated under long-term real-world conditions enables the assessment of HHO effects beyond controlled laboratory environments. One of the vehicles was equipped with an HHO generator (HG80) immediately after production, while the other vehicle was not. The basic parameters of the Kia Ceed vehicles are presented in Table 3.

The HG80 hydrogen generator continuously produces 1.3 l of HHO per minute, corresponding to 78 l per hour. At a laboratory temperature of 21 °C, the HHO gas density was estimated to be approximately 0.536 g·l⁻¹. Based on this value, the volumetric flow rate of 78 l·h⁻¹ corresponds to a mass flow rate of approximately 0.70 g·min⁻¹, or 41.8 g·h⁻¹. Considering the engine power of 66 kW, Table 3, this corresponds to a normalized HHO dosage of approximately 0.63 g·h⁻¹·kW⁻¹. For a one-hour operation, the total supplied HHO dose was approximately 0.042 kg. The input power consumption is approximately 230 W. Considering the efficiency of electrical transmission and the alternator, the mechanical load on the engine can be estimated at approximately 350 W. As reported above, approximately 0.042 kg of HHO is supplied to the engine during one hour of operation. In the case of engine operation at idle conditions, the fuel consumption can be assumed to be approximately 0.6 l·h⁻¹. Assuming an excess air coefficient of λ = 3, the corresponding air mass flow rate is approximately 22 kg·h⁻¹. In mass terms, 0.042 kg of HHO thus represents approximately 0.19% of the air mass flow rate.

In terms of energy, 0.042 kg of HHO corresponds to approximately 2.5% of the energy content of 0.51 kg of diesel fuel. It should be noted that idle conditions represent a state with the highest relative proportion of HHO, since the fuel and air mass flow rates are minimal in this regime. With increasing engine speed, load, and fuel consumption, the mass flow rates of fuel and air increase, while the HHO production rate remains approximately constant; therefore, its relative proportion in the mixture decreases.

Mixing with intake air occurs within the engine’s intake manifold. The system operates independently of the engine management system; it does not interfere with sensor signals or the engine control unit software66.

Measurement of the smoke opacity

Smoke opacity was measured using an opacimeter (Maha MDO LON 2), with a measurement deviation of ± 1%67. The measurement was performed during engine power and torque measuring, i.e., under 100% engine load. The engine was maintained at its operating temperature, ranging from 89 °C to 95 °C.

Smoke opacity measurement was selected as it represents a standard diagnostic method used in periodic technical inspections for internal combustion engines in many EU countries, including Slovakia. While particulate matter (PM) emissions provide a more comprehensive description of exhaust aerosol mass, PM measurements are not routinely available in standard in-use vehicle testing for the investigated vehicle category; therefore, smoke opacity was used as a practical and widely accepted surrogate indicator within the scope of standard vehicle inspection and comparative engine testing.

Measurement of the composition of exhaust gases

Exhaust gas composition was measured during driving at 50 km/h and 90 km/h, using the same methodology as for the Škoda Fabia. Measurements were performed while driving on ground level, and while driving at 6% rising gradient. At 50 km/h, the third gear was engaged; at 90 km/h, the fifth gear was used. The driving simulation was performed using a MAHA MSR 1050 cylinder dynamometer. To measure exhaust gas composition, the MAHA MGT 5 analyser was used. The following emissions were measured: Carbon monoxide (CO), Carbon dioxide (CO₂), Unburned hydrocarbons (HC), Oxygen (O₂), Lambda coefficient (λ), and Nitrogen oxides (NOx) All measurements were performed at the engine’s operating temperature, between 89 °C and 95 °C.

Results and discussion

Measurement results with the Škoda Fabia vehicle

The Fig. 2 shows the course of the engine power and torque of the Škoda Fabia. The engine power and torque’s course prior to engine cleaning with HHO are shown in black, while the course after cleaning with HHO is displayed in red. Engine power is represented by solid lines, and engine torque by dashed lines.

Engine power and torque’s course of the Škoda Fabia vehicle before and after cleaning with HHO.

As follows from Fig. 2, intensive HHO addition into the engine for more than one hour, corresponding to over 1800 l of HHO, had virtually no measurable effect on the engine’s power and torque. The measured differences between the “before” and “after” can be attributed to the measurement uncertainty of the MAHA MSR 1050 dynamometer, ± 2% of measured power, and normal operational variability. Service providers and manufacturers of HHO-based engine cleaning systems commonly claim increases in engine power and torque as one of the primary benefits of HHO treatment68–70. These claims were not supported by the measurements in this study. These providers also strongly recommend post-cleaning driving at higher engine speeds and with greater accelerator pedal application71,72. Such driving behavior can create a subjective impression of improved engine performance73; however, no objective measurements confirming such improvements were found in the scientific literature. Figure 3 shows the specific fuel consumption measured during the measurement of the engine power and torque. The curve “before” is in black and the curve “after” is in red.

The course of specific fuel consumption of the Škoda Fabia vehicle before and after HHO cleaning.

There was no change in BSFC, i.e. the fuel efficiency of the engine, observed after HHO treatment (Fig. 3). The average BSFC value prior to HHO treatment was 287.2 g·kW⁻¹·h⁻¹, while after treatment it was 287.0 g·kW⁻¹·h⁻¹. The difference of 0.2 g·kW⁻¹·h⁻¹ represents a change of less than 0.07%, within the ± 0.5% measurement uncertainty of the AIC 1200 flowmeter. The graph in Fig. 3 was compiled from 420 BSFC data points recorded before the HHO cleaning and the same number of points after the treatment. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the two datasets is 0.987, indicating a very strong positive correlation, meaning the BSFC curve remained virtually unchanged. It can therefore be concluded that HHO treatment had no impact on engine fuel efficiency, which contradicts the claims made by providers of HHO engine cleaning services74,75.

When the accelerator pedal is fully applied, the maximum fuel injection quantity is delivered. In both cases, before and after HHO engine cleaning, the engine operated with nearly identical efficiency, as indicated by the virtually unchanged BSFC values shown in Fig. 3. Consequently, the engine also produced almost identical power and torque, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This is because the same amount of fuel, and thus the same amount of chemical energy, was converted into approximately the same amount of mechanical work, given the similar efficiency. The trends observed in Fig. 2 (engine power and torque’s course) and Fig. 3 (BSFC’s course) are therefore consistent and mutually supportive.

Using HHO had no impact on fuel consumption, as seen in Table 4. Similarly to the comparison of engine power before and after HHO-based cleaning (Fig. 2), no relevant scientific studies directly examining the impact of engine cleaning using HHO on fuel consumption were identified in the case of fuel consumption (Table 4) or BSFC (Fig. 3). However, providers of HHO engine cleaning services and manufacturers of the corresponding equipment frequently claim fuel consumption reductions exceeding one-third76,77, which was not confirmed in this specific case.

Composition of exhaust gases of the Škoda Fabia during driving before and after HHO cleaning.

As shown in Fig. 4; Table 5, the use of HHO resulted in a slight improvement in combustion quality and changes in exhaust gas composition, as indicated by a moderate increase in CO₂ levels and a concurrent decrease in CO, HC, and NOₓ emissions. However, the changes in emission values are minor and may not be solely attributed to the use of HHO. They could also be influenced by the one-hour engine operation, which increases the exhaust system temperature and consequently affects oxidation processes in the catalyst. In this case as well (Table 5), the claim made by the HHO engine cleaning service provider77, that CO emissions were reduced by 96.5% and CO₂ emissions by 26.6%, was not confirmed. Such a reduction would require a corresponding decrease in fuel consumption of approximately one-quarter, since, as previously explained, CO is gradually oxidized to CO₂ within the exhaust aftertreatment system under appropriate temperature conditions and sufficient oxygen availability78. Moreover, CO₂ is a product of complete fossil fuel combustion; therefore, its reduction is only possible through a decrease in the amount of fuel burned.

Table 6 shows the results of the regular emissions inspection of the Škoda Fabia vehicle before and after HHO cleaning. During the regular emissions inspection, the Škoda Fabia with a spark-ignition engine is evaluated based on the concentration of CO, HC, and the lambda (λ) coefficient.

Table 6Composition of exhaust gases from the Škoda Fabia during regular emissions inspection before and after engine cleaning using HHO.

Composition of exhaust gases from the Škoda Fabia during regular emissions inspection before and after engine cleaning using HHO.

The use of HHO had virtually no impact on the results of the regular emissions inspection (see Table 6). The vehicle would have been classified as unfit for operation on public roads both before and after the engine cleaning. The reason was the exceedance of the hydrocarbon (HC) concentration limit at the idling speed, where the maximum permissible value is 100 ppm. The measured value before cleaning was 114 ppm and after cleaning 101 ppm (Table 6). As observed during on-road testing, a slightly more favorable exhaust gas composition (lower CO and HC emissions) was recorded after cleaning. This change may be associated with enhanced oxidation processes in the exhaust aftertreatment system, as indicated by a reduction in CO, which is typically oxidized to CO₂ in the catalytic converter under appropriate operating conditions. However, no direct measurement of catalytic converter temperature was performed; therefore, no definitive conclusions regarding catalytic converter efficiency can be drawn. Nevertheless, the measured CO₂ concentrations remained below typical values reported for fully functioning catalytic converters (above 15%79), which may indicate suboptimal operating conditions of the exhaust aftertreatment system. A similar measurement was conducted in24, where the impact of HHO-based cleaning of a diesel engine on CO concentration in the exhaust gases was assessed. Over the course of one hour of HHO addition, the CO concentration decreased from 0.48% to 0.21%. This reduction in CO production was likely a result of engine cleaning, as reported by the authors of24: at the beginning of the measurements, 65% of the surface of the exhaust valve and its seat was covered with carbon deposits; by the end of the one-hour cleaning process, only 15% of the surface remained contaminated. In the case of the Škoda Fabia, however, HHO-based engine cleaning had a very limited effect on the composition of the exhaust gases.

To reduce CO and HC emissions, an additional catalytic converter was installed downstream of the vehicle’s original catalytic converter. Once the additional catalytic converter reached operating temperature, the HC concentration at idle decreased to 42 ppm, and to 27 ppm at elevated engine speed. The CO concentration was reduced by the additional catalytic converter to 0.10% at idling speed and 0.04% at elevated speed. Under these conditions, the vehicle would have passed the regular emissions inspection and been deemed roadworthy. Furthermore, an improvement in exhaust gas composition was observed, as indicated by a rise in CO₂ concentration to 15.24% at idling speed and 15.19% at elevated speed. The desired emission-reducing effect was therefore not achieved through the application of HHO, but only through the installation of the additional catalytic converter.

Table 7 presents the electric current values recorded during the starting of the Škoda Fabia, used as an indirect indicator of compression pressure in individual cylinders.

Table 7Starting current values of the Škoda Fabia before and after engine cleaning using HHO.

Starting current values of the Škoda Fabia before and after engine cleaning using HHO.

The application of HHO did not result in any measurable change in electric current values during engine starting, suggesting no significant change in compression conditions within the cylinders (Table 7). Cylinder no. 2 consistently exhibited the lowest compression pressure, which was reflected in the lowest electric current required to drive the starter motor during the compression lift. These results do not correspond with the claims made by manufacturers and providers of HHO-based engine cleaning systems, who state on their websites that the procedure leads to an overall improvement in engine operation80,81.

The results obtained from the Škoda Fabia in terms of engine power, fuel consumption, emissions, and compression pressures did not demonstrate a clear positive effect of HHO engine cleaning on improving performance parameters, as claimed by HHO service providers. It should be noted that the cranking current reflects changes in overall cranking resistance and provides only a comparative indicator of cylinder condition, rather than a direct measure of specific sealing components.

Measurement results with the Kia Ceed vehicles

Figure 5 shows the engine power and torque’s courses of the Kia Ceed vehicles. The curves of the vehicle without HHO are marked in black. The curves of the vehicle with the HHO system switched off are marked in green, and those with the HHO system switched on are marked in red.

Course of the engine power and torque’s curves of the Kia Ceed vehicles.

The power and torque curves of the vehicles equipped with and without the HHO generator were similar (Fig. 5). When evaluating the results, it is important to consider that the measurement deviation of engine power using the Maha MSR 1050 dynamometer is ± 2%, which is a typical margin of error for cylinder dynamometers82. In order to clearly demonstrate a positive effect of HHO addition on increasing engine power or torque in this case, the measured differences would need to exceed 4%, i.e. double the maximum deviation of the measuring device. For measurements with higher precision (± 0.1%), which would allow detection of power or torque changes greater than 0.2%, a dynamometer with a direct engine-to-dynamometer connection would be required83. However, this would necessitate engine removal from the vehicle, significantly increasing the complexity and labour intensity of the measurements. The objective of this section of the article is to determine whether the installation and use of an HHO generator has a significant and noticeable effect on the engine’s power and torque courses. Therefore, the measurement accuracy with a ± 2% margin of error can be considered sufficient for the intended purpose. The measured data (Fig. 5) in this case did not confirm a significant increase in engine power, exceeding 20%, as claimed by manufacturers and sellers of HHO generators84–86. The differences observed between the curves remain within the measurement uncertainty of the dynamometer and therefore cannot be interpreted as a measurable improvement in engine performance. On the contrary, the author of87 reports, based on measurements, a power decrease of 4 kW, or more than 6%. The author attributes the reduction in engine power following HHO addition to changes in the fuel mixture and combustion duration, which are not compatible with the engine control unit’s operating algorithm. In publication88, a substantial and noticeable increase in torque (up to 19%) was recorded. However, in addition to the introduction of HHO, an additional engine control unit, referred to by the abbreviation “HECU”, was also installed. Therefore, the observed effect may not have resulted solely from the addition of HHO, but also from the influence of the supplementary control unit.

Additional modules recommended by HHO generator manufacturers and vendors, such as those mentioned in89–91, modify and alter signals from various sensors, including the lambda sensor, manifold absolute pressure (MAP) sensor, mass air flow (MAF) sensor, intake air temperature (IAT) sensor, engine coolant temperature (ECT) sensor, as well as other engine sensors. An increase in torque may not be solely the result of HHO addition, but also a consequence of modified signals being sent to the engine control unit (ECU)92. However, even if engine power or torque increases, altering signals directed to the ECU may carry risks. These include the engine running on an overly lean mixture, increased thermal stress on engine valves, and overall accelerated engine wear93,94.

The effect of long-term HHO usage on vehicle smoke opacity is presented in Table 8.

Table 8Smoke opacity measurement results for two Kia Ceed vehicles.

Smoke opacity measurement results for two Kia Ceed vehicles.

The vehicle operated without an HHO generator exhibited a smoke opacity value of 0.2 [m⁻¹]. In contrast, the vehicle that had been operated long-term with HHO addition showed a smoke opacity value of 0.0 [m⁻¹]. A significant difference in smoke opacity was thus measured, even with the HHO generator switched off. Both vehicles were of the same model, engine type and model year, and had been operated under comparable conditions since production, each with approximately 67 000 km of accumulated mileage. Since both vehicles have identical engines, the same model year, and are operated on the same routes, the observed difference in smoke opacity may be associated with the long-term operation of the vehicle equipped with the HHO generator. However, the influence of other operational factors cannot be fully excluded. A reduction in smoke opacity due to the use of HHO by 12% at 100% engine load is also described in publication95. As with the data in Table 8, the values in95 were also measured at full engine load. However, in publication95, the 12% reduction was not due solely to HHO addition, but also to the addition of 20% biodiesel and 100 ppm of CeO2. When 20% biodiesel was added but no CeO2, smoke opacity was reduced by approximately 6% as a result of HHO addition96. Publication95 does not include a comparison between pure diesel fuel with and without HHO. In publication96, a 30% reduction in smoke opacity of a diesel engine was achieved through HHO addition when using pure diesel fuel. With the addition of 40% biodiesel, the smoke opacity was further reduced by approximately 40% due to HHO. The authors of96 also investigated the effects of adding H₂; with 40% biodiesel, HHO addition resulted in a 17.5% reduction in smoke opacity.

During driving under various conditions, the differences in emission composition between the vehicle without HHO and the vehicle equipped with an HHO generator, whether switched off or on, were relatively small. However, for a more accurate evaluation, it is essential to analyze the overall trends.

The CO concentration for the vehicle without HHO was 0.1% at a speed of 50 km·h⁻¹ on the ground level. In all other cases, the CO concentration was zero. In the case of CO₂, the highest concentrations were recorded when the HHO generator was switched on, while significantly lower values were measured in the vehicle without the HHO generator, and the lowest concentrations were observed in the vehicle with the generator installed but switched off (Table 9; Fig. 6). For compression ignition engine vehicles, such as the Kia Ceed, CO₂ concentration is also an indirect indicator of fuel consumption97,98. An increase in CO₂ concentration, together with a decrease in Lambda and O₂ values, may indicate a higher fuel injection rate under equivalent conditions, and thus increased fuel consumption, as also suggested by studies reported in99,100. This observation suggests that HHO operation does not necessarily lead to improved fuel efficiency under the tested conditions. A possible explanation may be the additional electrical power required for HHO generator operation, as well as altered combustion conditions resulting from HHO addition, which are not explicitly accounted for in the engine control unit calibration. In contrast, the authors of publication101 observed the opposite trend, a decrease in both CO₂ concentration and fuel consumption with the addition of HHO. Regarding HC, the same publication101 reports a reduction in HC concentrations resulting from HHO addition to a compression ignition engine during steady-speed driving. The vehicle without an HHO generator consistently exhibited the highest HC concentrations during both flat-road and uphill driving, while at the same time showing the lowest NOx concentrations. Similar results are reported in publication102, where the addition of HHO to vehicles with a compression ignition engine led to an increase in NOx concentration — by 10% in the first vehicle and by 100% in the second. An increase in NOx emissions due to HHO addition in compression ignition engines was also observed in publication101. A reduction in HC and an increase in NOx emissions are likewise reported by the authors of publications103–105. The increase in NOx concentration is primarily explained in publications106–109 by higher combustion temperatures, faster combustion, and increased cylinder pressures, all of which improve conditions for NOx production. To mitigate the increase in NOx emissions associated with HHO addition to compression ignition engines, the authors of publications110,111 recommend implementing exhaust gas recirculation (EGR), reducing the compression ratio, and water injection. Without such modifications, the operation of an HHO generator may contribute to increased NOx concentrations. It is important to note that NOx concentration is not monitored during regular emissions inspections for either diesel or gasoline vehicles. As a result, a vehicle equipped with an HHO generator and producing excessive NOx emissions may still be evaluated as roadworthy. Paradoxically, a vehicle equipped with an HHO generator may be perceived as contributing to cleaner mobility or more environmentally friendly operation, despite its increased NOx emissions112,113.

Conclusion

The first objective of this publication was to assess the current state of scientific literature focused on the use of HHO in internal combustion engines. Based on the conducted literature review, it can be concluded that there is a relatively large number of scientific publications evaluating the addition of HHO to combustion engines. However, several gaps have been identified. The first gap concerns the topic of engine cleaning using HHO and the subsequent evaluation of its effects, for which only one publication was found. The second gap is the lack of scientific studies involving real, in-use vehicles, as most existing research is based on laboratory engines or simulations. The present study contributes to addressing both of these gaps by evaluating HHO applications in real vehicles under controlled experimental conditions.

The second objective of this publication was to design and validate a measurement methodology that minimizes the risk of influence from other factors, i.e. effects often incorrectly attributed to HHO by sellers of HHO generators and providers of engine cleaning services using HHO. The proposed methodology aims to isolate the effect of HHO addition as much as possible under comparable operating conditions. The proposed methodology is also applicable for use by other researchers with different vehicle types.

The main objective was to verify whether the effects of HHO are as significant as claimed by providers of HHO-based engine cleaning services and HHO generator vendors. Based on the analysis of results obtained from extensive measurements, it can be concluded that the effects of HHO were not significant in the tested vehicles. The observed impacts of HHO usage were either minimal or negligible. The most noticeable benefit was a reduction in smoke opacity in the vehicle operated long-term with an HHO generator. However, none of the findings were substantial enough to conclusively confirm a significant contribution of HHO technology to reducing emissions, improving fuel economy, or increasing vehicle performance.

The results of this publication highlight the need for objective and experimentally verified examination of the claims made by providers of HHO technologies.

Although the tested HHO systems showed minimal impact, the study underlines the importance of evidence-based evaluation of technologies claiming environmental benefits in road transport. These findings highlight the importance of independent and experimentally verified evaluation of technologies claiming environmental or performance benefits in road transport. Systematic real-world studies, such as the one presented in this work, are essential for distinguishing between scientifically supported effects and unverified technological claims.
