Herpes zoster of undetermined strain in a 14-month-old girl presenting 65 days following live-attenuated varicella-zoster vaccination: A case report
Authors: Michiya Omi, Daisuke Watanabe, Takuya Takeichi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Very rarely, live attenuated varicella zoster virus (VZV) vaccine recipients develop herpes zoster (HZ) without a preceding history of clinical varicella. A 14-month-old girl without prior varicella developed vesicles on her right arm and back. She received zoster vaccine live (ZVL) on the right arm 65 days earlier. A rapid immunochromatographic VZV antigen detection kit (DermaQuick VZV, Maruho, Osaka, Japan) was positive. Because PCR strain typing was not performed, the causative agent could not be definitively identified as the vaccine strain; therefore, breakthrough varicella or HZ due to wild-type VZV could not be completely ruled out. Based on the clinical and laboratory findings, she was diagnosed with HZ. Weight-adjusted oral valacyclovir was initiated at 200 mg four times daily for seven days. At seven-day follow-up, the vesicles had resolved into erythema and erythematous papules accompanied by desquamation in some areas. Assuming the HZ in our current case originated from the ZVL strain, it is hypothesized that the administered virus replicated locally in the skin, traveled retrogradely via sensory nerves to establish latency, and subsequently reactivated to cause HZ in the dermatome that corresponded to the injection site. Clinicians should include HZ in the differential diagnosis when they encounter a unilateral vesicular eruption in an immunocompetent infant with no history of varicella. DermaQuick, a simple and minimally invasive method, is extremely useful for rapidly diagnosing such cases.

Introduction

Primary varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infection causes varicella (chickenpox) and establishes latency, with subsequent reactivation resulting in herpes zoster (HZ, shingles) [1]. Immunization against VZV with a live attenuated vaccine significantly lowers the risk of developing HZ, with a 78% decrease in incidence relative to unimmunized children [2]. Very rarely, zoster vaccine live (ZVL) recipients develop HZ without a preceding history of clinical varicella [3,4]. We present a pediatric case of HZ occurring 65 days post-vaccination without a history of varicella.

Case

A 14-month-old girl presented with erythematous vesicles and papules with an erythematous halo on her right palm, forearm and right back, in a distribution consistent with the C7, C8, and T1 dermatomes (Figs. 1a and 1b). Two days prior to presentation, the patient developed rhinorrhea without accompanying fever. One day prior to presentation, the patient's guardian noticed the almost simultaneous appearance of erythematous papules on the right forearm and the lateral aspect of the right elbow. On the day of presentation, the cutaneous lesions were seen to have spread, with new eruptions having appeared on the right upper arm and the right side of the back. Her family history was negative for HZ, including no maternal history of HZ during pregnancy, with no documented exposure to varicella in extrafamilial settings, such as daycare. She showed no signs of pain or itching at the wound. She had an unremarkable birth history and no significant past medical history, including of varicella or eczema. The papules lacked central umbilication. Her vital signs were normal. Her vaccination history showed that she had received her first shot of ZVL in the right upper arm 65 days before this presentation. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, serum biochemistry, and immunoglobulin levels, were all within normal limits, suggesting an immunocompetent status. Swab samples obtained from vesicles on the right back and a papule on the right upper arm tested positive for VZV by rapid immunochromatographic VZV antigen detection kit (DermaQuick VZV, Maruho, Osaka, Japan) [5]. Based on the clinical and laboratory findings, she was diagnosed with HZ. Weight-adjusted oral valacyclovir was initiated at 200 mg four times daily for seven days. At seven-day follow-up, the vesicles had resolved into erythema and erythematous papules accompanied by desquamation in some areas. Discussion

Although the specific viral strain could not be definitively identified, this case highlights a rare occurrence of HZ in an immunocompetent infant with no history of varicella shortly after ZVL vaccination.

The positive, negative, and overall agreement rates for DermaQuick compared to real-time PCR are 93.2% (69 out of 74 cases), 98.8% (83 out of 84 cases), and 96.2% (152 out of 158 cases), respectively [5]. In addition, Watanabe et al. have examined the cross-reactivity of DermaQuick, verifying no cross-reactivity against two viruses (human simplex virus-1 and −2; HSV-1 and HSV-2) and four bacteria (Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Propionibacterium acnes) [5].

The differential diagnosis included herpes simplex; impetigo contagiosa bullosa; allergic or irritant contact dermatitis; papular urticaria; eczema herpeticum; hand, foot, and mouth disease; breakthrough varicella; and wild-type VZV (Table 1). Based on the positive DermaQuick test, the clinical history, and the anatomical distribution of the lesions, we ruled out diseases except for breakthrough varicella and wild-type VZV. A limitation of this report is the absence of viral PCR testing to definitively identify the ZVL strain. By amplifying the open reading frame 62 region of the VZV via PCR, wild-type varicella strains can be distinguished from the ZVL strain [6]. Assuming the HZ in our current case originated from the ZVL strain, its onset 65 days after the first immunization in an immunocompetent infant is relatively rapid. Yoshikawa et al. analyzed adverse events from 9.47 million doses of the ZVL in Japan from 2005 to 2015 [7]. They identified 66 cases of HZ-like eruptions, with virological analysis detecting the vaccine strain in 10 cases and the wild-type in 25 cases. According to their analysis, the median interval from vaccination to the onset of vaccine-strain HZ was 167 days (range: 11–855 days) [7]. There is no description of the immune status of the patient who developed vaccine-strain HZ 11 days after vaccination [7]. A population-based study by Weinmann et al. that evaluated HZ cases among a pediatric population of 144,000 children under 18 years of age enrolled 322 children with a clinical diagnosis of HZ between May 2005 and September 2009 [8]. Vaccine-strain HZ was identified in 38 cases, with a median interval of 436 days (range: 78–4457 days) from vaccination to HZ rash onset [8]. Koide and Nishigaki reported a case of a 14-month-old immunocompetent girl who developed vaccine-strain HZ 62 days after receiving a first dose [3]. The authors highlighted this patient as the youngest immunocompetent child in the literature to develop vaccine-strain HZ following a single dose of the varicella vaccine [3].

It remains unclear whether the causative agent was the ZVL strain. Given the multiple reports of post-vaccination HZ matching the dermatome of the injection site, a similar mechanism could be responsible for the current case [3,4,7,8]. Epidemiological data indicate a tendency for vaccine-strain HZ to develop at the site of injection [7,8]. Yoshikawa et al. reported that half of the vaccine-strain HZ-like eruptions (5 out of 10) occurred at the vaccination site (upper arm), whereas all 25 wild-type cases spared the upper arm [7]. Similarly, Weinmann et al. reported distinct dermatomal distributions between vaccine-strain and wild-type HZ [8]. Vaccine-strain HZ predominantly occurred in the cervical (9/35 cases, 25.7%) and lumbar (13/35 cases, 37.1%) dermatomes, which correspond to common vaccination sites on the extremities. In contrast, wild-type HZ primarily involved the thoracic dermatomes (139/210 cases, 66.2%), followed by the lumbar dermatomes (31/210 cases, 14.8%) [8]. Multiple case reports have described ZVL-induced HZ presenting in dermatomes consistent with the injection site [3,4]. It is hypothesized that the administered virus replicated locally in the skin, traveled retrogradely via sensory nerves to establish latency, and subsequently reactivated to cause HZ in the dermatome that corresponded to the injection site in the present case and in most vaccine-strain HZ.

Importantly, vaccine-strain HZ does not always correspond to the dermatome of the injection site. Moodley et al. described a child vaccinated in the right upper arm at 20 months of age who developed PCR-confirmed vaccine-strain HZ on the left side of the spine (L4–S1) and on the left leg at 3 years and 3 months of age [4]. Ozaki et al. demonstrated transient viremia by performing PCR on peripheral blood mononuclear cells following vaccination in 20 healthy children with no history of varicella [9]. Thus, it is highly plausible that the ZVL strain can establish latency in the dorsal root ganglia distant from the injection site [4].

The clinical course and prognosis of vaccine-strain HZ in healthy, immunocompetent children are generally benign and self-limiting [3,4,8]. Symptoms such as pain, pruritus, tingling, or localized hypersensitivity may accompany the skin manifestation, but the overall severity does not significantly differ from or is milder than that of wild-type HZ [8]. Most cases resolve completely within a few weeks without permanent scarring or postherpetic neuralgia, with or without antiviral therapy [3,4]. Nevertheless, clinicians should be aware that the occurrence of vaccine-strain zoster in healthy infants can occasionally serve as an early prodromal indicator of a sentinel manifestation of a previously undiagnosed primary immunodeficiency or T-cell dysfunction, necessitating a thorough clinical and immunological history [3,4].

Clinicians should include HZ in the differential diagnosis when they encounter a unilateral vesicular eruption in an immunocompetent infant with no history of varicella. DermaQuick, a simple and minimally invasive method, is extremely useful for rapidly diagnosing such cases.
