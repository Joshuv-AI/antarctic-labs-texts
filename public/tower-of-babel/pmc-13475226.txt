Cost-effectiveness of secondary prevention of rheumatoid arthritis with methotrexate in the Netherlands: differences between developing ACPA-positive and ACPA-negative disease
Authors: Elise van Mulligen, Sterre S Bour, Quirine A Dumoulin, Maureen P M H Rutten-Van Mölken, Annette H M van der Helm-van Mil
Published: 2026

Since rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic disease with high disease burden, any intervention that can reduce or prevent disease burden is of great clinical and economic interest.1 The placebo-controlled TREAT EARLIER trial has investigated intervening in patients with arthralgia at risk for RA (ie, clinically suspect arthralgia (CSA)), which showed that a single corticosteroid injection and a time-limited intervention with 12 months of methotrexate did not prevent RA but did improve the inflammatory burden.2 Following this, a cost-effectiveness analysis in the total trial population over the course of 2 years showed a small improvement in quality of life and lower costs in the treatment arm, which was mainly driven by less work-related costs.3 Taking into account uncertainty, treatment of the total population was highly likely to be cost-effective.

Recent analyses showed that, in retrospect, the trial population was heterogeneous in pathophysiological subtype (anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA) positive/negative) and RA risk.4 Within the ACPA-negative group, patients could have low predicted risk; identification of this group is relevant since this group may not benefit from preventive treatment. Furthermore, methotrexate was only effective in preventing RA in the increased-risk ACPA-negative group and not in the ACPA-positive group. In both groups, disease burden improved over 2 years but was only durable within the ACPA-negative patients at increased risk.4 5 This subgroup analysis was prespecified in a statistical analysis plan.6 This population heterogeneity in efficacy may also influence cost-effectiveness results. Therefore, we evaluated the cost-effectiveness of a single intramuscular corticosteroid injection and a 1-year course of methotrexate compared with placebo in ACPA-positive and ACPA-negative arthralgia patients at increased risk of RA, excluding low-risk ACPA-negative patients. The latter were studied for comparison.

The TREAT EARLIER trial included 236 CSA patients with subclinical joint inflammation. According to an externally validated risk-stratification model,7 all 54 ACPA-positive patients and 66 ACPA-negative patients were at increased risk (>10%), while 116 ACPA-negative patients had low RA risk (<10%). Economic data were collected every 4 months until 2 years or until developing clinically apparent arthritis (online supplemental methods S1).3 Cost-effectiveness was analysed using a societal (work-productivity costs and healthcare costs) and a healthcare perspective (healthcare costs) within the Dutch socioeconomic setting (online supplemental methods S1).8

Baseline characteristics have been reported previously and are shown supplementary.4 Among ACPA-positive patients, treatment led to lower societal costs compared with placebo (€−5263 (95% CI −27 051 to 14 796)) and no improvement in quality of life (quality adjusted life years (QALYs) −0.0260 (95% CI −0.233 to 0.192), with wide CIs indicating substantial uncertainty). In contrast, treated ACPA-negative increased-risk patients had numerically higher savings in societal costs (€−9100 (95% CI −21 516 to 5283)) and numerically greater improvements in quality of life (QALYs 0.108 (95% CI –0.0275 to 0.244)) (figure 1 and online supplemental figure S1). As expected, treated ACPA-negative low risk patients did not benefit in societal costs or in quality of life (€202 (95% CI −690 to 1298) and QALYs −0.0496 (95% CI –0.143 to 0.0410) (figure 1 and online supplemental figure S1).

When considering healthcare cost only, treated ACPA-positive patients had slightly higher costs than placebo (€245 (95% CI −1251 to 1943)), whereas treated ACPA-negative increased-risk patients showed cost-savings (−€1778 (95% CI –3588 to –239)) (online supplemental figure S1). This indicates that the cost savings from the societal perspective were primarily driven by productivity costs.

At a willingness-to-pay threshold of €50 000, ACPA-positive patients had a 67% probability of being cost-effective, ACPA-negative with increased risk 95% and ACPA-negative with low risk 57%. From a healthcare perspective this was 38%, 97% and 16%, respectively (online supplemental figures S2 and S3).

To conclude, subgroup analysis of our previously published cost-effectiveness study seems to suggest that treatment of CSA patients with subclinical joint inflammation with one corticosteroid injection and 12 months of methotrexate has a high probability of being cost-effective for increased-risk ACPA-negative patients, but not for ACPA-positive patients. Hence, not only the efficacy but also the cost-effectiveness of secondary preventive efforts differ in ACPA-positive and ACPA-negative disease. Future evaluation of long-term (lifetime) cost-effectiveness outcomes in these subgroups is warranted to fully understand the societal and clinical impact of such interventions.
