From static guidelines to dynamic adaptation: a scoping review of maternal physical activity health services worldwide
Authors: Hao Xu, Wei Chen, Renyi Liu, Xiaoyu Cheng, Yi Liao, Yang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile the benefits of physical activity (PA) during pregnancy are well-established, PA levels remain low. Many countries have introduced maternal PA guidelines. This review synthesizes key recommendations, identifies gaps, and explores best practices for improving public health services and bridging the gap between evidence-based guidelines and real-world implementation.MethodsFollowing the Arksey and O’Malley scoping review framework and adhering to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, we systematically searched official sources and academic databases covering all 194 WHO-recognized countries from January 2024 to February 2025. National-level maternal PA guidelines and related implementation studies were identified and screened. Data extraction focused on guideline content, policy support models, implementation strategies, and service accessibility. Analytical frameworks included the Chief Medical Officers’ communication framework and the WHO health system assessment model to evaluate the translation of guidelines into practice. We registered a protocol in the Open Science Framework (OSF) ( 10.17605/OSF.IO/Z5SEY ).ResultsA total of 26 countries with eligible national maternal PA guidelines and 37 implementation studies published within the past decade were included. Core guideline recommendations were consistent across countries, emphasizing 150–300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, muscle strengthening, and sedentary behavior reduction. However, stage-specific and postpartum recovery guidance remain limited. Implementation models varied, including human-driven, digital, and hybrid approaches, with resource availability significantly influencing service delivery. Key barriers included insufficient cross-sector collaboration, delayed professional training, inadequate community mobilization, and limited use of digital health technologies, particularly affecting low- and middle-income countries’ ability to translate guidelines into effective health services.ConclusionGlobal maternal PA guidelines present coherent core principles but require enhancement with stage-specific and personalized recommendations and clearer delineation of professional roles. Dynamic adaptation between policies and practice through strengthened multisectoral collaboration, community engagement, and digital innovation is essential to bridge the implementation gap. Such multifaceted approaches will support sustainable, equitable promotion of maternal physical activity, advancing global health and gender equality goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25759-6.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) during pregnancy and postpartum is critical for maternal and child health. Its benefits include reducing the risks of excessive gestational weight gain, preterm birth, gestational diabetes mellitus, preeclampsia, and other delivery complications [1]. Furthermore, postpartum PA can significantly improve cardiovascular function, mental health, and overall quality of life. Despite these well-documented health benefits, global maternal PA participation rates remain alarmingly low [2]. Fewer than 30% of pregnant and postpartum women meet the internationally recommended PA guidelines [3], with high levels of sedentary behavior (SB) further exacerbating health risks.

The World Health Organization (WHO) and several national health agencies recommend that pregnant and postpartum women engage in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity weekly [4], supplemented by muscle-strengthening exercises which should involve a variety of types and can be combined with gentle stretching for additional benefits; those who habitually did vigorous-intensity aerobic activity before pregnancy can continue such activities during pregnancy and postpartum, while reducing sedentary behavior. However, numerous barriers, such as physiological changes, fatigue, cultural misconceptions, and lack of guidance, significantly hinder maternal PA participation [5]. While issuing PA guidelines represents a crucial first step in promoting PA, guidelines alone are insufficient to increase participation rates [6]. Their effectiveness largely depends on supporting policies and practical measures that dynamically adapt static guidelines into actionable, context-specific interventions within healthcare and community systems. For example, Australia’s “Maternity, Birth, and Baby” program [7] combines insurance reimbursement incentives with community exercise resources, providing strong support for increasing maternal PA participation. In contrast, countries lacking practical measures to promote maternal PA often experience a “floating guideline” phenomenon, where recommendations fail to translate into actionable outcomes.

Recognizing this challenge, this scoping review systematically evaluates not only the static maternal PA guidelines but also the dynamic adaptation processes through policy support models and public health service implementation. The review begins with comprehensive searches across all 194 WHO-recognized countries to identify relevant guidelines. For countries with eligible guidelines, further investigation of dynamic promotion measures and practical implementation actions is conducted. By analyzing commonalities and differences in guideline development, policy support, and health service accessibility, this review aims to bridge the gap between evidence-based recommendations and real-world practice.

Summarizing global policies and practices will help identify ways to optimize public health services to support maternal PA. Understanding the diverse strategies employed across nations can inform measures to close the gap between scientific recommendations and practical application, ultimately enhancing maternal health across varied cultural and healthcare contexts.

Methods

This review follows the scoping review framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [8, 9] and adheres to PRISMA-ScR checklist (Table S1). The objective was to identify official maternal PA guidelines issued by government authorities worldwide and relevant studies on practices promoting maternal PA. A comprehensive search was conducted across all 194 WHO-recognized United Nations member countries between January 2024 and February 2025. Maternal PA guidelines were primarily identified through structured Google searches of government websites and official health authority portals. Countries were not pre-selected based on language or region; instead, all eligible guidelines identified through the search were included. No language restrictions were applied, and non-English guidelines were translated when feasible. Ultimately, all national-level maternal PA guidelines that met the inclusion criteria were included in the review. All guidelines published up to February 2025 were considered.

For studies addressing practices to promote maternal PA, searches were conducted in PubMed, MEDLINE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, covering the last 10 years. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) with free-text terms. The analysis utilizes the Chief Medical Officers’ (CMO) communication framework [10] to identify commonalities in the content of PA guidelines. Additionally, the study examines implementation models (human-driven, hybrid, or digital), cross-sector collaboration, and service levels using WHO’s prevention-medical integration-community extension framework [11].This review has been registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF)(10.17605/OSF.IO/Z5SEY).

Stage 1: Identifying research questions

Following initial literature searches and discussions among the authors, two primary research questions were identified: What are the nature and scope of globally available guidelines on maternal PA? What targeted public health services are provided by the 26 countries that have issued maternal PA guidelines, and what is the level of these services?

Stage 3: Study selection

Three authors independently assessed the eligibility of all guidelines and studies identified through the search strategy. The exclusion criteria were as follows:

Maternal PA Guidelines: (1) Insufficient authority, including documents not published or officially reposted by national health authorities, government agencies, or recognized professional organizations; (2) Outdated content; (3) Incomplete or nonspecific recommendations; (4) Limited applicability to the general pregnant population;

Health Care Accessibility Studies/Policy Documents: (1) Irrelevant content focusing solely on individual PA behavior without addressing healthcare accessibility or public health services; (2) Research examples to be excluded: RCTs exploring yoga’s impact on postpartum recovery without discussing healthcare accessibility or policy factors.

Language restriction: No language restrictions applied. Any uncertainties regarding eligibility were resolved through discussions among three main authors (XH, LRY and CW). Stage 4: Charting and visualizing data

CW developed a standardized data extraction strategy, and XH extracted data from the guidelines using this approach. The extraction tool was created in Microsoft Excel, which was also used for coding, organizing extracted information, generating summary tables, and producing preliminary visual outputs. EndNote facilitated reference management and organization of retrieved literature, while Tableau Public 2024.3 was applied to create interactive and static visualizations of key findings. The process involved extracting and categorizing content from each country’s maternal PA guidelines, cross-checking for consistency, compiling guideline elements into a comprehensive summary list, evaluating healthcare services related to maternal PA based on WHO’s health system assessment framework [11] (focusing on prevention, medical integration, and community extension), and summarizing implementation models (human-driven, hybrid, or digital) in tabular format. All assessment results were visualized using Excel and Tableau. The final data extraction table is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Results

Through systematic searches of government websites, grey literature, and scientific databases conducted between January 2024 and February 2025, 22,244 records were initially identified. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 38 full-text guidelines were assessed for eligibility, and 26 guidelines met the inclusion criteria. These guidelines were from Austria [12], Australia [13], Belgium [14], Brazil [15], Brunei [16], Canada [17], Denmark [18], Estonia [19], Fiji [20], Finland [21], France [22], Iceland [23], Italy [24], Kenya [25], Latvia [26], Malaysia [27], New Zealand [28], Norway [29], Portugal [30], Singapore [31], Slovenia [32], Sri Lanka [33], Switzerland [34], the UK [35], Uruguay [36] and the USA [37]. For these countries, 37 studies [38–74] published within the last 10 years were included, focusing on initiatives and research related to promoting maternal physical activity levels.

Accessibility of Maternal PA Health Services.

Table 1 also presents the accessibility of maternal PA health services across countries, classified under the World Health Organization’s health service assessment framework covering three domains: prevention, medical integration, and community outreach. Each domain was rated as High, Medium, or Low based on service maturity, coverage, and systematization. “High” indicates well-developed, widely accessible, and systematically implemented services; “Medium” reflects partial coverage and ongoing development; “Low” denotes limited service availability and weak integration. These classifications reflect differences in countries’ economic development, healthcare structures, and technological infrastructure, and manifest in diverse maternal PA service delivery models.

Most countries employed hybrid (human-driven plus digital) or human-driven service models. Examples include Australia’s New South Wales “Get Healthy Service” and the UK’s NHS-led courses, which demonstrate high-level comprehensive services; some low- and middle-income countries rely heavily on community health workers for grassroots health promotion, reflecting relatively weaker service systems.

Summary of key recommendations in National-Level maternal physical activity guidelines

Regarding safety management, guidelines distinguish two key concepts: Warning Signs and Contraindications. Warning signs refer to abnormal symptoms occurring during exercise, such as vaginal bleeding or severe headache, serving as dynamic safety monitors that indicate the need to stop exercise immediately and seek medical advice. Contraindications are assessed prior to exercise initiation, classified as absolute or relative based on the woman’s medical condition, guiding whether exercise is appropriate or should be undertaken with caution. Additionally, some guidelines address health screening prior to exercise, pre-exercise assessments, physical fitness testing, and exercise adaptations for special populations such as disabled pregnant women and pregnant athletes. Although these aspects are included in fewer countries, they enrich the dimensions of safety assessment and personalized guidance. Together, these elements form the essential foundation of maternal PA safety management, ensuring exercise is both effective and safe.

Delivery models for maternal PA services

An evaluation of maternal PA service delivery models from health department websites (Table S5) and related studies identified three primary types of delivery [11]. (1) Human-driven: Reliance on health professionals (e.g., doctors, midwives, community health workers) for face-to-face consultations and guidance. Availability of in-person classes or group training sessions organized by hospitals, community centers, or public health institutions. Home-based health guidance provided by nurses or midwives during visits. In several countries, supervised exercise sessions conducted by exercise professionals (e.g., physiotherapists, exercise physiologists, certified fitness trainers) were also included as part of direct interventions. (2) Digital-based: Online health education platforms led by governments or healthcare institutions. Telehealth consultations through phone or video. Use of electronic health records (EHR) or mobile health (mHealth) tools. (3) Hybrid models: Combined online and offline services for health education and consultations. Digital health support offered by hospitals/government, supplemented by human-delivered services. Integration of private organizations, public health systems, and digital platforms, and supervised exercise programs led by exercise professionals for multi-channel interventions. High-income countries (e.g., Norway [61, 62], Australia [39, 40], Canada [44, 45], the UK [71–73]) achieve the highest levels in prevention, medical integration, and community outreach. Middle-income countries (e.g., Brazil [42, 43], Malaysia [57, 58], Portugal [63]) demonstrate strength in prevention and medical integration but face limitations in community outreach. Low-income countries (e.g., Kenya [55, 56], Sri Lanka [67, 68], Fiji) primarily rely on community health workers with lower levels of medical integration. Overall, the service delivery models for maternal PA across the 26 countries are diverse, but their prevalence varies based on national economic levels, healthcare systems, and technological development. Face-to-face consultations and offline courses are widely adopted in high-income countries (e.g., Nordic countries, Australia, Canada, the UK), but their coverage is limited in developing countries due to resource constraints. Home-based health guidance is implemented to some extent in countries like Canada and Nordic nations but is generally restricted by manpower and cost. Online health education and telemedicine consultations have rapidly developed in Western countries and some Asian nations (e.g., Singapore), becoming significant methods for promoting PA, while their application in low-income countries is limited by low internet penetration and inadequate technical support. The integration of EHR into maternal health management has been implemented in some developed countries (e.g., Finland, Denmark, the United States), but it still lacks systematic support in low- and middle-income countries.

Discussion

Global maternal physical activity guidelines consistently recommend core practices such as engaging in 150 to 300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise weekly, complemented by muscle-strengthening and pelvic floor training. These guidelines also emphasize the reduction of sedentary behavior (Table 2). However, these recommendations, while scientifically sound, reveal significant heterogeneity across countries and regions, especially regarding cultural adaptability and health system integration.

Beyond these variations, several critical gaps remain. Firstly, stage-specific guidance reflecting physiological changes during early, mid, and late pregnancy is provided by only a few countries, with late-pregnancy recommendations like avoiding the supine position often lacking strong evidence. This lack of trimester-specific adaptation may compromise safety and adherence, particularly in contexts where maternal health monitoring is limited. Secondly, postpartum recovery guidance is generally vague, missing detailed advice tailored to delivery methods and recovery phases. The adoption of a four-phase recovery model-acute, functional reconstruction, strength building, and long-term maintenance-could enhance postpartum exercise safety. Thirdly, many guidelines do not adequately address critical components such as health screening before exercise initiation, pre-exercise medical or fitness assessments, standardized physical fitness testing, and exercise adaptations for special populations, including disabled pregnant women and athletes. Our review highlights that only a minority of countries (e.g., Canada, UK) provide specific adaptations for high-risk or unique populations, leaving a critical evidence-to-practice gap. Moreover, the distinct roles of healthcare professionals in promoting PA and exercise specialists in delivering supervised interventions remain underdefined, impacting the implementation fidelity and safety of exercise recommendations.

Regional and economic disparities strongly influence PA implementation strategies. High-income countries (HICs) commonly employ digital health platforms and telehealth models to integrate exercise guidance into prenatal care, while low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) rely heavily on community-based, low-cost interventions due to resource limitations. These contextual differences emphasize the need for culturally sensitive and resource-adapted solution. Countries worldwide have implemented diverse and innovative strategies to promote maternal PA, ranging from digital platforms in high-income nations like Norway and Sweden to community-driven models in middle- and low-income countries such as Brazil and South Africa. The digital-based models leverage advanced public health infrastructures and technology to offer personalized, evidence-based exercise plans that integrate real-time updates and precision medicine approaches. For example, Norway’s Helsenorge platform customizes exercise recommendations for pregnant women with specific health conditions, while Sweden’s PAP-S project delivers stage-specific personalized guidance. Such “digital plus professional guidance” models demonstrate strong scalability in well-resourced health systems but remain challenging to implement in LMICs due to technological and financial constraints.

Cross-sector collaboration is another cornerstone of successful maternal PA promotion. The UK’s “This Mum Moves” program and Australia’s “Get Healthy Service” and “Mum-Alete” initiatives exemplify how integrating government, healthcare, community, and sports sectors can create comprehensive support networks. These programs combine policy, practice, and resources to expand coverage and improve intervention quality through standardized guidelines.

In contrast, human-driven, low-cost service innovations offer important lessons for resource-limited settings. Brazil’s Community Health Worker (CHW) program and South Africa’s “Baby Steps” initiative demonstrate effective integration of maternal PA into basic healthcare services by leveraging community trust and social capital. Such models highlight the importance of context-driven strategies, where cultural norms, gender roles, and social structures dictate feasibility and success [6].

Despite these advances, challenges persist. Practical implementation faces barriers including insufficient cross-sector collaboration, inadequate professional training, limited community mobilization, and underutilization of digital health technologies. These challenges disproportionately affect LMICs, where infrastructural gaps and health workforce shortages limit the reach of evidence-based PA. While Norway’s Helsenorge platform is technologically sophisticated, its replication in resource-constrained settings is limited by infrastructure and funding gaps. Nonetheless, it serves as an excellent model for other European countries to follow. At the same time, addressing the global gap—particularly in low- and middle-income countries—requires innovative, low-cost solutions such as mobile applications and simplified digital tools to achieve broader accessibility. For example, a recent randomized clinical trial [76] demonstrated that two low-cost smartphone-based physical activity applications developed for pregnant women significantly increased their exercise frequency and participation rates. The intervention incorporated daily exercise tasks, behavioral incentives, and real-time feedback, making the recommendations more personalized, evidence-based, and sustainable, thereby highlighting the feasibility of digital tools in diverse global contexts.

This situation underscores the critical role of dynamic adaptation—linking static guidelines with evolving policies and practical implementation. Continuous policy refinement and flexible implementation strategies are essential to translate evidence-based recommendations into effective, context-specific physical activity intervention strategies at the grassroots and clinical levels. Additionally, most current initiatives lack long-term follow-up and evaluation, particularly for postpartum interventions, leaving the sustainability and impact of these programs uncertain. Establishing robust data collection and monitoring systems is vital to optimize interventions, secure funding, and demonstrate value to stakeholders. In addition, differences in health system structures play a critical role in shaping maternal PA promotion. Using the WHO health system building blocks as an analytical framework, our review found that countries with strong governance and financing mechanisms (e.g., UK, Australia) implement integrated policies that align PA promotion with maternal health priorities, while LMICs struggle with fragmented service delivery and insufficient human resources. Addressing these systemic barriers is crucial for equitable implementation.

Aligning with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and 5 (Gender Equality), the promotion of maternal PA is both a vital health intervention and a means to advance health equity and gender equality. To bridge the gap between guideline development and real-world application, multi-level strategies are necessary: refining guidelines for greater scientific rigor and inclusivity; enhancing cross-sector collaboration; strengthening professional training; mobilizing community engagement; and integrating digital health technologies tailored to resource availability. Through these multi-level, dynamic adaptation strategies, maternal PA promotion can become more precise, effective, and sustainable, thereby contributing meaningfully to global health and equality objectives.

Limitations

This review is limited by the sample representativeness, focusing on 26 countries with published national-level maternal PA guidelines and analyzing their implementation pathways and public health service capacities. Countries without formal guidelines and unpublished local initiatives were not included, which may limit the generalizability of our findings and highlights the need for future research covering underrepresented regions and diverse healthcare systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, global maternal PA guidelines provide relatively consistent recommendations regarding exercise types, intensity, and safety. However, there is still significant room for improvement in terms of targeted guidance during different stages of pregnancy and specific recommendations for postpartum recovery. To address the gap between guideline recommendations and real-world implementation, future strategies should focus on refining guideline content to include stage-specific and personalized recommendations, clarifying professional roles, and integrating multidisciplinary resources. Moreover, enhancing training for healthcare and exercise professionals, mobilizing community-level stakeholders, and leveraging digital health innovations are key to improving service delivery and accessibility. These dynamic, multi-sectoral approaches will facilitate sustainable, equitable maternal PA promotion, contributing to the achievement of SDGs related to health and gender equality.
