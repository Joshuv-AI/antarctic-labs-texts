The expert consensus document on minimally invasive upper lobectomy for lung cancer in China
Authors: Licheng Wu, Longyong Mei, Jinbo Zhao, Cheng Shen, Wei Wu, Shiguang Xu, Yuchi Xiu, Wenzhou Liu, Guangqiang Zhao, Zhiqiang Xue, Dong Xie, Liang Xie, Haitao Huang, Naixin Liang, Lei Xian, Bo Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMinimally invasive upper lobectomy is recognized as a standard surgical intervention for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Nevertheless, there is a paucity of evidence-based technical standards regarding anatomical sequences, the scope of lymphadenectomy, and perioperative management in the context of upper lobectomy.MethodsA multidisciplinary team comprising thoracic surgeons from 12 tertiary centers across China was assembled. A systematic review of evidence from 33 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and 11 additional studies specific to the upper lobe [1996–2026] was conducted. Recommendations were developed in accordance with the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) criteria and the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) framework, employing a modified Delphi consensus process. The study protocol was prospectively registered under PREPARE-2026CN207.ResultsThe consensus resulted in the formulation of nine graded statements. Strong recommendations (grade A) advocate for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) or robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) as the standard surgical techniques, with the requirement for systematic lymphadenectomy. Moderate recommendations (grade B) delineate optimal airway management, enhanced perioperative care protocols, and customized nodal dissection strategies. These strategies specifically differentiate between early-stage ground-glass opacity (GGO) dominant tumors and invasive carcinoma to inform selective omission of lymph node stations. Expert consensus (grade C) defines surgical indications and establishes standardized anatomical dissection sequences for upper lobectomies.ConclusionsThis consensus provides a comprehensive, evidence-based framework for the standardization of minimally invasive upper lobectomy in NSCLC. Although core surgical approaches and systematic lymphadenectomy are supported by high-level evidence, tailored nodal management and anatomical sequencing remain dependent on expert consensus, underscoring essential directions for future clinical trials.

Highlight box

Key recommendations

• Minimally invasive upper lobectomy using video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery or robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery with systematic lymphadenectomy is the recommended standard for resectable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (grade A). Expert consensus suggests tailored anatomical sequencing and optimized perioperative care (grade B/C).

What was recommended and what is new?

• Systematic lymph node dissection should include at least six stations: stations 2R, 4R, and 7 for right upper lobe lobectomy, and 4L, 5, 6, and 7 for left upper lobe lobectomy.

• New guidelines allow skipping station 7 for early-stage subsolid tumors (≤2 cm, consolidation-to-tumor ratio <0.25) with negative nodes and omitting station 4L dissection for early-stage, ground-glass opacity (GGO)-dominant left-sided tumors to reduce left recurrent laryngeal nerve injury risk (grade B). Customized sequencing strategies are also proposed for managing incomplete fissures.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinical practice should move toward personalized surgical strategies for upper lobectomy. Thoracic surgeons should routinely use customized dissection techniques to reduce air leaks and bleeding, and adopt selective lymphadenectomy in early-stage GGO-dominant cases to balance cancer removal with nerve preservation.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most common cancer globally. Lobectomy with lymphadenectomy remains the primary treatment for resectable cases, with 5-year survival rates of 82–95% for stage IA and 74.5% for stage IB, decreasing significantly beyond stage II (1). Lung cancer often affects the upper lobes, especially the right upper lobe (RUL; 30–35% of cases) (2), a trend seen in lung adenocarcinoma where subsolid nodules are common in these areas (3). The pattern may be due to the upper lobes’ peripheral location and ventilation/perfusion characteristics, influencing lymphatic spread to superior mediastinal stations (3). Furthermore, upper lobe adenocarcinomas typically appear as peripheral subsolid nodules with lepidic growth, affecting surgical margins and lymph node sampling. In contrast, upper lobe squamous cell and small cell carcinomas are often centrally located, potentially necessitating sleeve lobectomy. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, common in Asian non-smoking adenocarcinoma patients, are linked to peripheral upper lobe locations and slow growth (4).

Upper lobectomy is distinct due to its unique anatomical characteristics. In 48% of cases, the RUL’s horizontal fissure is incomplete or absent, complicating dissection (5). Vascular anomalies, especially in the apical-anterior segmental artery, necessitate detailed preoperative angiographic evaluation to prevent bleeding (6). RUL lobectomy carries a 0.3–0.4% risk of middle lobe torsion, accounting for 74% of such cases post-lobectomy, particularly with a complete oblique fissure (7). Middle lobe counterclockwise rotation occurs in 11% of patients, increasing postoperative complications (8).

Minimally invasive lobectomy, including video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS), has been demonstrated to be safe and feasible, with no elevated risk of conversion, surgery-related intensive care unit (ICU) stay, or adverse postoperative outcomes (9,10). However, RUL lobectomy via minimally invasive resection has higher conversion rates to thoracotomy than lower lobectomies, mainly due to hilar lymphadenopathy, bleeding, and fused fissures (6). These measurable risks and limited space in the upper thoracic cage make upper lobectomy a unique procedure that requires specific technical guidelines beyond standard lobectomy protocols.

This consensus aims to offer guidance on minimally invasive upper lobectomy and lymphadenectomy, including sleeve lobectomy of the bronchus and/or pulmonary artery trunk. We present this article in accordance with the ACCORD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1410/rc).

Methods

Selection of expert panel

Sixteen thoracic surgeons were selected from 24 candidates of the First Youth Committee of the Thoracic Surgery Branch of the Chinese Medical Doctor Association. Selection criteria required having at least 10 years of experience in minimally invasive lung cancer surgery, authoring five relevant publications in the last 5 years, performing a minimum of 100 upper lobectomies annually, holding a title of attending physician or higher, and practicing in tertiary hospitals. The panel included representatives from 12 hospitals across nine cities: Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chongqing, Xi’an, Shenyang, Nanning, Kunming, and Suzhou. The 16 panelists were drawn from institutions comprising military medical centers [3], cancer centers [2], university hospitals [7], and regional centers [4]. This consensus focused solely on lung cancer surgical techniques and perioperative management, involving only thoracic surgeons. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Literature search and study selection

A systematic PubMed search [1996–2026] using medical subject headings (MeSH) terms for “pneumonectomy/methods” and “lung neoplasms” identified 220 articles on surgical techniques for lung cancer. Two reviewers screened these, identifying 33 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for primary evidence synthesis. These studies compared minimally invasive (VATS/RATS) and open thoracotomy, uniportal and multiportal VATS, dissection techniques, vascular control methods, fissure management, and lymph node dissection in upper lobectomy, focusing on operative parameters, complications, and oncologic effectiveness.

Additionally, 11 non-randomized studies on upper lobectomy techniques and 36 studies on general lobectomy techniques were included as supplementary evidence.

Among the 33 RCTs, five were high-quality level I, and 28 were smaller level II trials. The 11 upper-lobe-specific studies were level III evidence, while the 36 non-specific studies supported general technical recommendations when RCT evidence was lacking.

Of the remaining 140 articles, 58 covered sublobar resection, 28 addressed non-upper lobe resections, 22 focused on non-surgical interventions, 12 involved non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) histologies, and 20 were duplicates or unavailable.

Question generation strategy

Instead of relying on PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome) frameworks, this technical consensus identified questions from gaps in the literature and practice variations across 12 institutions. Six controversial areas were highlighted: patient selection, surgical approach, technical sequencing, fissure management, lymph node extent, and conversion criteria. The core research group initially drafted 16 statements to address these issues, which were then refined into nine final statements (the clinical decision pathway is illustrated in Figure 2).

Consensus development and Delphi process

Recommendations for upper lobectomy techniques and standards were developed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) framework. An expert panel of thoracic surgeons employed a modified Delphi method, involving two rounds of anonymous online voting on a 5-point Likert scale. A professional online survey platform, Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn), was used for conducting expert voting. This platform supports anonymized voting, restricted access, and real-time statistics, making it suitable for Delphi-based consensus procedures.

We conducted two rounds of anonymous electronic questionnaires and a final hybrid (on-site and on-line) meeting to reach consensus, with a threshold of ≥80% agreement for each recommendation. The final recommendations were categorized as grade A (strong recommendation based on consistent level I evidence), grade B (moderate recommendation), or grade C (weak recommendation or expert opinion).

Registration

This expert consensus, registered with PREPARE (PREPARE-2026CN207; https://www.guidelines-registry.cn), serves as a Clinical decision-making reference rather than a clinical guideline.

Surgical indications

The 2025 guidelines from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the Chinese Medical Association affirm that lobectomy remains the standard treatment (17,18). Sublobar resection, preferably segmentectomy, is a category 1 option only if: (I) the tumor is ≤2 cm and peripheral; (II) the consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR) is ≤0.5, or intraoperative analysis confirms adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), or lepidic-predominant adenocarcinoma; and (III) a surgical margin of ≥2 cm or greater than the nodule’s diameter is possible. Without these conditions, lobectomy is advised for complete tumor removal.

The 2025 JCOG0802 study shows that solid nodules ≤2 cm with high-risk subtypes (such as micropapillary or solid adenocarcinoma), or those with visceral pleural invasion, have a higher recurrence risk after sublobar resection (19).

While nodules ≤2 cm do not mandate lobectomy, it is still preferred if high-risk factors exist. Converting to lobectomy during surgery for safety or oncologic concerns follows current guidelines and helps prevent uncertain or incomplete resections.

Surgical approach

How do the perioperative safety and oncological effectiveness of VATS and RATS for upper lobectomy compare to open surgery? Additionally, does RATS offer clinical benefits over VATS in lymph node dissection, recovery, and complications?

Open surgery

The traditional posterolateral thoracotomy is the standard method for upper lobe resections, especially for complex cases such as T3/T4 tumors with chest wall invasion, extensive hilar lymphadenopathy, N2 disease with fixed mediastinal nodes, prior thoracic surgery with dense pleural adhesions, and complex reconstructive procedures such as sleeve lobectomies (20).

For sleeve lobectomies, the thoracotomy is performed through the 4th or 5th intercostal space, or via the costal bed after removing the 5th rib, to ensure optimal access to the hilar region and upper mediastinum (21). Complete mobilization of the pulmonary ligament and dissection of hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes are crucial for a tension-free bronchial anastomosis (21). Non-absorbable sutures such as 3-0 or 4-0 Prolene and V-Loc absorbable threads are used for either interrupted or continuous sutures. Traction sutures help reduce anastomotic tension (22).

VATS

VATS is generally recommended for patients with clinical stage I–IIA NSCLC in the upper lobe, provided they have adequate pulmonary reserve and the procedure is technically feasible for achieving an R0 resection (17). For patients with stage IIB or higher NSCLC, VATS may be a viable and safe option if a thorough MDT evaluation is conducted and experienced surgeons ensure complete R0 resection and systematic lymph node dissection. In well-chosen patients, VATS provides better short-term outcomes compared to thoracotomy, with similar long-term survival rates (23,24).

Surgeons must evaluate patient conditions and consider the advantages of each surgical method to ensure safety and effectiveness.

RATS

Over the past two decades, RATS has become popular for lung cancer lobectomy due to its three-dimensional (3D) high-definition visualization and precise manipulation, enhancing minimally invasive surgery in complex cases (29). The robotic arms’ flexible wrist allows for precise suturing with consistent tension. Although the learning curve is steep (32), RATS provides similar long-term survival rates for early-stage lung cancer as VATS lobectomy (33). Some pilot studies suggest RATS may offer shorter operative times, less blood loss, lower risk of conversions to thoracotomy, and more lymph node harvests (34-36). Furthermore, RATS is a viable alternative to VATS, with potential for remote-control surgery (37).

Growing randomized evidence compares RATS and VATS for upper lobectomy. The RVlob trial (n=320) found both methods had similar operative times and quality of life outcomes when performed by skilled surgeons (38). A smaller Italian RCT (n=75) reported less early postoperative pain and pleural effusion with RATS, despite longer surgery times (39). A systematic review and meta-analysis of four trials (total n=548) showed clinical equivalence between the approaches (40), with no significant differences in postoperative complications, conversion to thoracotomy, or mortality (40). RATS had a non-significant trend of longer operative time (+13.70 minutes), and lymph node yield was similar (40). Long-term disease-free and overall survival rates were also comparable (40).

Unlike smaller randomized trials, the large-scale PORTaL study (n=5,721) using real-world data and propensity-score matching showed that RATS had notable perioperative benefits over VATS (41), including shorter operative times, fewer thoracotomy conversions, shorter hospital stays, and lower blood transfusion rates, with comparable complication and mortality rates (41).

Therefore, we believe that the choice of surgical approach should ultimately consider institutional factors, cost-effectiveness, and surgeon expertise, rather than perceived technical superiority. These findings should be interpreted with caution until more robust randomized evidence is available.

The surgical procedures for upper lobectomy

Prolonged air leak (PAL) is mainly attributed to the integrity of interlobar fissures (fused or incomplete fissures, rather than suture line length) and parenchymal injury during dissection (42,43), particularly in the RUL due to its unique anatomy, where the horizontal fissure is absent or fused in up to 48% of cases (5). To mitigate this risk, a fissure-last (anterior-to-posterior) approach (42-45) is shown in Figures 3,4. These methods involve performing the lobectomy by first securing the hilar vessels and bronchus, and subsequently dividing the fused horizontal/oblique fissure last using endovascular staplers, without dissecting the parenchyma.

To date, very few RCTs have evaluated perioperative morbidity, bronchial stump complications, or long-term survival outcomes associated with these specific surgical variations.

Comparing the impact of unidirectional vs. traditional resection methods on surgery and complications in right upper lobectomy: is there a clear advantage?

Mei et al. (45) reported that a refined technique for right upper lobectomy offers better perioperative outcomes than the traditional method. It results in shorter operation time, less bleeding, fewer complications, shorter hospital stays, and lower costs. The procedural sequence is depicted in Figure 3. The procedure involves exposing and cutting the superior arterial trunk after pleural incision, stapling the upper lobe bronchus, and finally stapling the superior pulmonary vein, ascending A2 artery, and interlobar fissures simultaneously. Mei et al.’s (45) technique requires precise identification of the vascular space for A1+3 artery transection to avoid severe hemorrhage and risks missing station 10 lymph nodes, making it more suitable for early-stage lung cancer patients without N1 lymph node metastasis.

Lin et al. (51) and Zhai et al. (52) described procedures where the oblique fissures were divided to expose and transect the A2 artery and upper bronchus, followed by dividing the horizontal fissures and simultaneously transecting the right superior veins and arteries. This en-bloc approach can increase the risk of A1 vascular injury and bleeding.

The unidirectional resection method for right upper lobectomy, introduced by Liu et al. in 2010 (53), involves exposing the anterior right hilum, incising the pleura over the superior pulmonary vein with an electrocoagulation hook, and then dissecting and transecting the right superior pulmonary vein and the first branch of the right pulmonary artery trunk. The upper lobe bronchus is exposed and transected with a stapler, followed by the lung fissure using a stapler.

With the shift to uni-VATS, the traditional anterior-to-posterior approach faces ergonomic issues such as instrument interference. A trial explored a modified posterior-to-anterior approach for uni-VATS right upper lobectomy (54), using an aBVA sequence (apical branch of artery → bronchus → vein → ascending branch of artery). This method reduced operative time and blood loss compared to the traditional anterior-to-posterior VAB method (vein → artery → bronchus), without affecting lymph node clearance (54). This technique demands expert skill and a thorough grasp of anatomy to precisely locate nearby structures in the hilar region.

The LUL lobectomy is the most challenging among the five lobectomies (55). In the minimally invasive procedures, it often results in higher conversion rates and more frequent pulmonary artery injuries (56).

Upper pulmonary sleeve lobectomy methods: do perioperative outcomes and long-term survival differ between minimally invasive and open techniques?

Upper pulmonary sleeve lobectomy is a surgical technique for central lung cancer affecting the upper lobe bronchial orifice or main bronchus. It removes the affected bronchial segment and reconstructs the airway to preserve healthy lung tissue, aiming to avoid pneumonectomy while ensuring cancer safety.

Based on anatomical analysis, RUL sleeve lobectomy is less restricted by surrounding structures than LUL sleeve lobectomy, which is anatomically limited by the left main pulmonary artery. However, RUL sleeve lobectomy is a significant predictor of postoperative anastomotic complications [odds ratio (OR) =5.29; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.07–26.13] (57), likely due to bronchus caliber mismatches and localized ischemia. Therefore, the surgical strategy for RUL sleeve lobectomy focuses on caliber mismatch and complications, while LUL sleeve lobectomy emphasizes structural mobilization and tension reduction.

The RUL sleeve lobectomy requires precise margin assessment and careful transection of the right upper pulmonary artery branches while protecting the middle lobe artery. Tension reduction techniques include transecting the inferior pulmonary ligament, performing hilar pericardiotomy for mobility, and wrapping the anastomosis with adjacent tissues to prevent leakage risk (58,59). The LUL sleeve lobectomy is challenging due to the aortic arch and the left main pulmonary artery’s positions, causing high anastomotic tension. The bronchial anastomosis is performed behind the artery with limited visibility. To overcome these challenges, subaortic space dissection, distal mobilization of the lower lobe bronchus, traction sutures, and tilting the operative bed 45 degrees for better exposure are employed (60,61).

A meta-analysis of six studies with 655 patients found that VATS sleeve lobectomy had a longer operative time but less blood loss compared to open surgery, with no significant differences in other outcomes (62). An analysis of 1,652 patients from the ESTS database revealed that 90.2% underwent open thoracotomy and 9.8% had VATS, with a 21.1% conversion rate (63). Overall morbidity was 40.6%, and 30-day mortality was 2.2%. VATS was associated with lower morbidity and shorter hospital stays (63). Risk factors for complications included low body mass index (BMI) and bilobectomy, while age under 60 years, female sex, and VATS were protective (63). Neoadjuvant treatment did not increase complication risk (63).

Recent advancements now include multi-port/single-incision robotic assistance, with 10× high-definition 3D vision and 270° flexible instrument control for precise anastomosis (29,64). Prolene monofilament sutures or V-Loc absorbable threads enhance anastomotic stability and biocompatibility (65). The double sleeve lobectomies concept enables simultaneous bronchial and pulmonary artery reconstruction, supporting minimally invasive treatment and optimal pulmonary function preservation (66).

Consensus statement

Consensus 6

In upper pulmonary sleeve lobectomy, accurate margin assessment and careful vessel management are vital for safety. Tailoring specific tension-relief and exposure techniques to the unique anatomies of the left and right lungs reduces complications and improves anastomotic quality.

Voting: strongly agree: 100%; agree: 0%; neutral: 0%; disagree: 0%; strongly disagree: 0%.

Evidence: level III; grade: C.

What is the clinical relevance of the extent of lymph node dissection (selective vs. systematic) in upper lobe NSCLC?

Pathologic lymph node upstaging (cN0 to pN+) is crucial for assessing surgical quality in NSCLC (67). The 2020 American College of Surgeons Commission on Cancer (CoC) Operative Standard 5.8 requires examining at least one N1 station and three distinct N2 stations for accurate staging (68). The 2025 Chinese Medical Association Lung Cancer Guidelines advise specific lymph node dissection based on tumor location: stations 2R, 4R, and 7 for RUL tumors, and dissection stations 4L, 5, 6, and 7 for LUL tumors (18).

The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) defines uncertain resection (R-un) as a situation where nodal evaluation is inadequate or the highest mediastinal nodes are positive, preventing a clear assessment of N3 status (69,70). Patients with positive 2R/4L nodes have a high risk factor of N3 metastases, such as to contralateral mediastinal and supraclavicular lymph nodes, requiring further examination for confirmation such as PET-CT and neck/supraclavicular ultrasound or endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) (71,72).

The extent of lymphadenectomy should be tailored to tumor characteristics (67,73). In the case of ground-glass opacity (GGO) dominant lesions, nodal involvement is rare (74), and the ECTOP-1009 trial shows that skipping mediastinal lymph node dissection doesn’t affect survival and reduces complications (75). The JCOG0802/WJOG4607L trial supports limited nodal assessment for small peripheral NSCLC (3,19).

Routine dissection of subcarinal (station 7) lymph nodes may not be necessary for all upper lobe tumors due to their lower metastasis rates (0.2–1.3%) (73,76) compared to lower lobe tumors (76). For clinical T1 tumors with negative intraoperative findings at stations 2R/4R (RUL) or stations 5/6 (LUL), omitting station 7 dissection might be safe (76). For station 4L, surgeons must balance complete cancer removal with risk of left recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (77). Omitting 4L dissection is a reasonable approach for early-stage GGO tumors.

The panel recommends standard systematic dissection for station 7 (statement 7a, grade A) but allows selective omission for certain upper lobe tumors or situations (statement 7b), based on intraoperative evaluation and expertise, though this is not standard practice and has lower consensus strength.

Consensus statement

Consensus 7a (systemic lymph node dissection)

For invasive lung cancer patients, sample at least six lymph node stations. For RUL lobectomy, sample stations 2R, 4R, and 7; for LUL lobectomy, sample stations 4L, 5, 6, and 7. Also, dissect and assess station 11. Patients with positive nodes at 2R or 4L are classified as “uncertain resection (R-un)” and should be evaluated for N3 skip metastases.

Voting: strongly agree: 81.25%; agree: 18.75%; neutral: 0%; disagree: 0%; strongly disagree: 0%.

Evidence: level I; grade: A.

Consensus 7b (selective omission of stations 7 and 4)

In high-risk or elderly patients, omitting station 7 dissection can reduce operative time. For early-stage upper lobe tumors (≤2 cm, CTR <0.25), skipping station 7 is acceptable if intraoperative nodal evaluations are negative (stations 2R/4R for right tumors; stations 5/6 for left tumors). Furthermore, to avoid left recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, omitting station 4L dissection is advisable for early-stage, GGO-dominant LUL tumors with minimal metastasis risk.

Complications prevention and management

Proactive strategies to minimize upper lobe-specific issues involves: (I) Assessing incomplete fissures on preoperative CT can predict PAL risks; consider intraoperative fissureless techniques or sealant application like pleural tents or pneumoperitoneum (5); (II) right upper lobectomy risks middle lobe torsion (0.3–0.4% incidence); prophylactic fixation with pleural tenting or sutures is advised if residual space exceeds 1/3 hemithorax or the oblique fissure is developed completely (7); and (III) Using autologous pericardial fat or synthetic sealants on staple lines reduces bronchopleural fistula risk in sleeve lobectomy, especially for RUL short bronchus (29).

Currently, the ERAS Society/ESTS (27) and STS (78) guidelines recommend using a single chest tube instead of a double tube to reduce pain and drainage time without increasing reintervention risk. They suggest removing the chest tube when drainage is ≤450 mL/day, provided there is no hemorrhagic or chylous output. Recent findings suggest the absence of an air leak alone can be a safe removal criterion if the lung is fully expanded (79). PAL lasting over 5 days (80) occurs in 20–33% of upper lobectomies (81,82). This is usually managed conservatively with water seal drainage, but if needed, chemical pleurodesis with talc or a blood patch may be used (83). Early reoperation is essential if a “significant” air leak, defined as over 500 mL/min on digital drainage (84), or a grade 3 (continuous) to 4 (forced expiratory) on the Cerfolio scale (85), is detected within 24 hours post-resection, especially with a non-expanding lung or progressive subcutaneous emphysema (83). This can help rule out bronchopleural fistula, fix parenchymal injuries, or strengthen staple lines (83,86). No trials compare re-operation with other air leak treatments (86).

Bronchopleural fistulas are serious and managed based on timing: early (within a week) suggests technical failure, intermediate (8–30 days) indicates ischemia, and late (beyond 30 days) is usually due to infection or recurrence (87). Immediate treatment includes drainage, antibiotics, and nutritional support. For fistulas under 8mm or inoperable patients, bronchoscopic methods like endobronchial valves, covered stents, or sealants may be used, though success varies (88). Larger defects or failed endoscopic closures often require surgical repair with muscle flap transposition, typically using the latissimus dorsi muscle flap (89).

Postoperative atrial fibrillation occurs in 12–44% of lung resection patients, especially after upper lobectomy and right-sided surgery (90). The Society of Thoracic Surgeons and American Association for Thoracic Surgery recommend continuing beta-blockers perioperatively to prevent arrhythmia, with diltiazem advised for those not on beta-blockers (90). Amiodarone is effective but must be used carefully post- pneumonectomy due to potential pulmonary toxicity (90). Beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers help keep the heart rate under 110 bpm, and anticoagulation is advised if the arrhythmia lasts over 48 hours and bleeding risk is minimal (90).

Consensus statement

Consensus 8

In upper lobectomy, follow evidence-based perioperative care: manage bronchopleural fistulas under 8 mm with drainage and bronchoscopic closure or muscle flap surgery, and use beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers to prevent and control postoperative atrial fibrillation.

Voting: strongly agree: 43.75%; agree: 43.75%; neutral: 12.5%; disagree: 0%; strongly disagree: 0%.

Evidence: level II; grade: B.

Conclusions

Our expert consensus recommendations serve as practice standards and align with existing guidelines. With the NCCN, we agree on minimally invasive surgery and lung-sparing resection, adding technical details where NCCN is less specific. Our approach complements the IASLC’s standard of systematic nodal dissection. While statement 7a aligns with IASLC. Statement 7b refines the approach by suggesting the selective omission of station 7 in specific upper lobectomy, based on lymphatic drainage patterns.

To safely implement these recommendations, surgeons should perform at least 50 VATS lobectomies or 40 robotic lobectomies annually (91), supported by institutional resources like 24-hour thoracotomy backup and an experienced anesthesia team. An MDT team, including thoracic surgery, oncology, and radiology, should review cases. Best practices for upper lobectomy are evolving, requiring evidence-based approaches tailored to the patient’s anatomy and tumor. In areas lacking evidence, staying informed, following guidelines, and multidisciplinary collaboration are crucial.

Starting in 2028, reviews and updates will occur every 2 years or sooner if significant developments arise, such as new RCTs, major guideline changes, or advancements in surgical technology. Future updates will involve a multidisciplinary expert panel for a comprehensive perspective. The Delphi methodology will be used, and updates will remain registered with PREPARE. Additional considerations will include recovery metrics like return-to-work time, physical function scores, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) for pain and quality of life. Cost-effectiveness analysis will focus on quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios, especially given the balance between VATS and RATS to inform policy decisions.
