Vascular bundle cell-specific expression of a phosphate transporter improves phosphate use efficiency of transgenic Arabidopsis without detrimental effects
Authors: Yuichi Tada, Aoi Shimizu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractConstitutive overexpression of phosphate (Pi) transporter family 1 often results in the accumulation of toxic levels of Pi, which causes growth retardation in plants. In contrast, we had previously reported that root epidermis-specific overexpression of the phosphate transporter TaPT2 in Arabidopsis leads to improved growth and Pi use efficiency. In the present study, we used promoters AtHKT1;1 and SKOR, which are predominantly expressed in the vascular bundle tissues, to overexpress TaPT2. Transgenic lines exhibited increased shoot growth compared to wild type plants under normal- and low-Pi conditions, along with elevated root Pi and total P content, and higher xylem sap Pi concentration, specifically under low-Pi conditions. This was attributed to moderate Pi accumulation in the xylem parenchyma cells, enhancing the Pi uploading capacity to the xylem. SKOR-TaPT2, however, did not complement pho1 mutant, which was defective in uploading Pi to the xylem. The transcriptional levels of VPT1 and VPT3, which are responsible for transporting excess Pi into a vacuole, were upregulated in SKOR promoter lines under normal-Pi conditions. Our results suggested that root vascular bundle-specific expression of TaPT2 is another promising strategy for increasing biomass production, Pi uptake, and Pi use efficiency while preventing growth retardation in transgenic plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-78500-5.Keywords: Growth promotion, Phosphate transporter, Pi acquisition, Pi use efficiency, Vascular bundle cell-specific expressionSubject terms: Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Phosphorus (P) is one of the most important mineral elements for plant growth and development. Plants preferentially absorb P as orthophosphate (Pi). Pi can be a major limiting factor for plant growth owing to its low accessibility and availability in the soil, since it tends to form insoluble complexes in soil. Only 15–30% of the Pi from the applied fertilizer is taken up by crops in the year of its application1, the rest leaching into groundwater and causing environmental pollution2,3. Therefore, improving the efficiency of plant Pi acquisition and use is an important crop breeding objective for sustainable Pi use. Members of the phosphate transporter family 1 (PHT1) play direct roles in Pi uptake and translocation in plants4–7. Overexpression of PHT1s is an attractive strategy for generating transgenic plants with improved adaptability under low-Pi conditions8. Transgenic wheat plants overexpressing TaPHT1;4 or TaPT2 showed increased plant dry mass and total P content under low-Pi conditions9,10. The overexpression of OsPT4 resulted in an enhanced Pi uptake rate and increased xylem Pi, total P, and phytic acid concentrations in seeds8. Overexpression of PHT1s, however, have often been reported to resulted in the accumulation of toxic levels of Pi, leading to growth retardation11,12. For example, although transgenic plants overexpressing OsPHT1;8 (OsPT8), OsPHT1;9 (OsPT9), or OsPHT1;10 (OsPT10) had significantly higher Pi levels than WT plants, they did exhibit Pi toxicity symptoms13,14. Therefore, only overexpression of PHT1s may not improve the Pi-use efficiency of transgenic plants and is often detrimental to growth. The non-tissue-specific CaMV35S promoter was used to overexpress these PHT1s. In contrast, we previously reported that the root epidermal cell-specific overexpression of the phosphate transporter TaPT2 using the Arabidopsis Shaker family inward rectifying potassium channel 1 (AKT1) promoter improved Pi acquisition and utilization efficiency in transgenic Arabidopsis although the constitutive expression of TaPT2 using the actin 8 promoter in Arabidopsis resulted in reduced growth compared to WT plants15. Thus, even when the same gene is used, the phenotype differs depending on its expression characteristics.

Numerous quantitative trait loci (QTL) and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in both plants and animals have revealed that many of the genetic changes that drive evolution, domestication, and breeding occur in cis-regulatory regions16–18. Compared to mutations in coding sequences that alter protein structure, cis-regulatory variants often cause subtle phenotypic changes by altering the timing, pattern, or level of gene expression18. Although the genetic features that distinguish humans from chimpanzees are still of great interest, the differences have been suggested to be based on changes in the mechanisms that control the expression of genes rather than changes in the sequence of proteins, and that most structural gene-related changes between the human and chimpanzee genomes have occurred at the level of expression regulation19,20. Experimentally, mutations in the promoter sequences, rather than their coding sequences, have been shown to significantly alter plant phenotypes. CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing of promoter sequences have generated diverse cis-regulatory alleles of SlCLV3, resulting in locule number variations in tomatoes21. Similarly, genome editing of the promoter sequences of three major heading date genes in rice, namely Hd1, Ghd7, and DTH8, generated a series of germplasm with quantitative variation in heading date by adjusting the expression levels of these genes22,23. In transgenic research, appropriate temporal and spatial regulation of transgene expression is important to obtain transgenic plants with the desired traits. For example, while constitutive overexpression of the sodium transporter AtHKT1;1 failed to generate salt-tolerant plants, overexpression of AtHKT1;1 specifically in the root xylem parenchyma cells of Arabidopsis resulted in improved salinity tolerance24. Similarly, root cortical and epidermal cell-specific expression of AtHKT1;1 resulted in enhanced salinity tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis and rice plants25. Thus, altering the regulation of gene expression can significantly affect the phenotypes. In this study, we investigated the effects of vascular bundle cell-specific TaPT2 overexpression in Arabidopsis.

Results

Generation of transgenic Arabidopsis plants

We constructed the expression vector plasmids pAtHKT1;1-TaPT2 and pSKOR-TaPT2, and introduced them into Arabidopsis. TaPT2 was expected to be predominantly expressed in the vascular bundles of transgenic plants. Two independent single-transgene lines were randomly selected from the transgenic plants for each construct, namely AtHKT1-D and AtHKT1-G, and SKOR-F and SKOR-J. Plants with putative single transgenes were determined based on the segregation patterns of hygromycin-resistant and hygromycin-sensitive seedlings in the T1 generation. T2 homozygous lines and two AKT1 promoter lines, namely AKT1-C and AKT1-F15, were used for subsequent studies.

Growth and Pi and total P contents of transgenic plants

Fourteen-day-old transgenic and WT plants germinated on a 1/2 MS agar medium were transplanted into a hydroponic culture system with 1/2 Hoagland’s solution (normal-Pi condition, 500 µM PO43−) or modified 1/2 Hoagland’s solution (low-Pi condition, 10 µM PO43−) and grown for another 2 weeks. Their appearance was photographed, and shoot and root dry weights (DW) were determined (Fig. 1). AtHKT1 and SKOR promoter lines showed significantly higher shoot and root DW than the WT plants under both normal- and low-Pi conditions (Fig. 1). The increased shoot DW in AKT1 promoter lines under normal- and low-Pi conditions was consistent with the results of our previous study15.

Pi concentrations in xylem sap of hydroponically grown transgenic plants

Xylem sap was collected from transgenic and WT plants grown hydroponically under normal- or low-Pi conditions at the bolting stage, after which their Pi concentrations were determined (Fig. 5). The Pi concentrations in the xylem sap of each promoter line and the WT under normal-Pi conditions were equivalent (Fig. 5A). However, the Pi concentration in the xylem sap of the SKOR-J line was significantly higher than that in the WT, and the same in the other SKOR and AtHKT1;1 promoter lines were substantially higher than that in the WT under low-Pi conditions (Fig. 5B).

Expression of genes related to Pi transport and homeostasis

The transcript levels of the transgenes and genes related to Pi uptake, transport, and homeostasis in the transgenic lines were examined (Fig. 6). The transcription level of TaPT2 differed across the transgenic lines (Fig. 6A,B), and the expression level in the SKOR-J line was relatively low, although it had high Pi content in the shoot and xylem sap, high total P content in roots under low-Pi conditions, and high total P content in seeds under normal-Pi conditions.

Arabidopsis PHT1, which contributes to Pi transport in roots during growth under low-Pi conditions30, was upregulated in the roots of all transgenic lines, except for the AtHKT1-G line, which was similar to that of WT plants under low-Pi conditions (Fig. 6D). Vacuolar phosphate transporters (VPTs) maintain Pi homeostasis. We determined the transcriptional levels of Arabidopsis VPT1 and VPT3, which sequester excess cytoplasmic Pi in the vacuole to avoid the negative effects of high Pi accumulation (Fig. 6E–H). VPT1 was upregulated in the roots of the SKOR-J line and shoots of the AtHKT1;1 and SKOR promoter lines under normal-Pi conditions (Fig. 6E,F). VPT3 was upregulated in the roots of SKOR-J under normal-Pi conditions (Fig. 6G). The phosphate efflux transporter PHO1 (PHOSPHATE 1) and its homologue PHO1;H1 are expressed primarily in the vascular tissues of roots and are involved in the transfer of Pi from the roots to the shoots28. Expression of PHO1 and PHO1;H1 in the roots of the transgenic lines did not change significantly, although expression of PHO1 in the shoots was downregulated under low-Pi conditions and PHO1;H1 expression in the AtHKT1-G and SKOR-F lines was upregulated under normal-Pi conditions (Fig. 6I–L).

pSKOR-TaPT2 does not complement pho1 mutant Arabidopsis plants

The PHO1 phosphate transporter uploads Pi from xylem parenchyma cells to the xylem, and pho1 mutants show diminished growth compared to the WT due to severe leaf phosphate deficiency26. To investigate whether TaPT2-overexpression could complement the loss of PHO1 function, we transformed pho1 Arabidopsis mutant plants with pSKOR-TaPT2 and examined their growth on 1/2 MS agar medium (625 µM Pi). The transgenic lines showed diminished growth, similar to that of the pho1 mutant line that lacked the ability to transport Pi to the xylem, on 1/2 MS medium (Fig. 7A–C). This result indicated that TaPT2 does not complement the defective PHO1 function.

Discussion

Although research has been conducted to overexpress various PHT genes to improve plant Pi acquisition and use efficiency, the expected effects have not yet been achieved8–10,27. While the previous experiments used the CaMV35S promoter, which constitutively expresses PHT genes, we recently reported an improvement in Pi acquisition and use efficiency, leading to increased growth, by expressing TaPT2 under the control of the AKT1 promoter, which is specifically expressed in the root epidermis15. This improvement was attributed to the formation of a moderate Pi concentration gradient from the root epidermis towards the central cylinder, which enhanced the Pi uploading capacity to the xylem, followed by Pi transport to the shoot. In this study, we examined the effects of expressing TaPT2 using two promoters, AtHKT1;1 and SKOR, which are dominantly active in the vascular bundles, including the xylem parenchyma cells in the root.

Transgenic lines expressing TaPT2 with the AtHKT1;1 and SKOR promoters, like the AKT1 promoter lines15, showed better growth than the WT under both normal- and low-Pi conditions (Fig. 1). The Pi content in the roots or shoots of AtHKT1;1 and SKOR promoter lines was higher than that in the WT (Fig. 2B,E). Consequently, the Pi content per root of most AKT1, AtHKT1;1, and SKOR promoter lines was significantly higher than that of the WT under normal- and low-Pi conditions (Fig. 2F,H), and the Pi content per shoot of the AtHKT1-G and SKOR-J lines was significantly higher than that of the WT. These results indicated that more Pi was absorbed and utilized to develop shoots and roots in the transgenic lines, especially in the AtHKT1;1 and SKOR promoter lines. Similarly, under normal Pi conditions, the total P content in roots and total P content per shoot and root in most transgenic lines were higher than those in the WT (Fig. 3B,E,F), and the total P content per root was higher than that in the WT under low-Pi condition (Fig. 3H). However, the total P content per shoot was equivalent to that in the WT under low-Pi condition (Fig. 3G). Thus, excess Pi may be stored as organic P in the root rather than being transported to the shoot. In particular, the SKOR-J line showed higher total P content than the WT in the roots, even under low-Pi conditions (Fig. 3D). These data indicated that not only was more Pi absorbed and transported to the shoots, but excess Pi that could not be used was also stored in the roots of AKT1, AtHKT1;1, and SKOR promoter lines, especially in the SKOR-J line. Similar increases in Pi and/or total P content in transgenic plants overexpressing PHT1s via a constitutive promoter have been reported in several studies, although their growth was not enhanced or reduced13,14,27. In this study, the seed total P content of AKT1-F, SKOR-F, and SKOR-J lines was significantly higher than that of the WT under normal-Pi conditions (Fig. 4B), suggesting that excess Pi transported to the shoots of the transgenic lines was stored in seeds in the form of organic P. Such an increase in seed total P content due to overexpression of PHT1s is rare, and increased phytic acid concentrations have been reported in transgenic rice overexpressing OsPT48.

Among the transgenic lines with the three promoters, the Pi concentrations in xylem sap of the SKOR-J line were significantly higher than those in the WT under low-Pi conditions (Fig. 5B). The results suggested that Pi accumulation in xylem parenchyma cells in roots, generated by TaPT2 driven by a vascular bundle-specific promoter, could enhance Pi transport into the xylem mediated by PHO128,29, followed by increased Pi transport from the root to shoot and subsequent efficient Pi use for plant growth. PHO1 transporters contain an SPX domain at the N-terminus and an EXS domain at the C-terminus. The EXS domain of PHO1 transporters plays a role in the loading of Pi into the xylem (Hamburger et al., 2002; Wege et al., 2016). PHO1 and its homologue PHO1;H1 are expressed primarily in the vascular tissue of roots and are involved in the transport of Pi from roots to shoots28. PHO1 overexpression has deleterious effects and impairs cell growth29. In the present study, PHO1;H1 expression in AtHKT1-G and SKOR-F lines was upregulated in shoots under normal-Pi conditions (Fig. 6K,L). However, their expression was not significantly upregulated in the SKOR-J line, where the Pi concentration in xylem was elevated, suggesting that upregulation of PHO1/PHO1;H1 was not responsible for enhanced Pi uploading to the xylem in the SKOR promoter lines. Although the Pi concentration in the xylem sap of OsPT1-overexpressing plants was almost twofold higher than that in WT plants on normal-Pi soil27, the mechanism of increased xylem sap concentration in the transformants is still unknown. The results further suggested that xylem parenchyma cell-specific TaPT2 overexpression could form an effective Pi concentration gradient in the root to increase Pi uptake to the xylem. However, the Pi gradient formed by ectopic TaPT2 expression was not sufficient to rescue the Pi uploading-defective pho1 mutants (Fig. 7), indicating that PHO1 is indispensable for uploading Pi in xylem parenchyma cells to the xylem.

We examined the expression levels of genes related to Pi uptake, transport, and homeostasis in the transgenic lines (Fig. 6). PHT1 (AtPHT1;1) mediates P acquisition, and its transcript levels increase significantly in Arabidopsis roots following Pi starvation30. In all the transgenic lines, except for the AtHKT1-G line, endogenous PHT1 was upregulated in the roots, as in WT plants under low-Pi conditions (Fig. 6D). This result indicated that the increase in Pi uptake and transport by ectopic expression of TaPT2 had little effect on the expression of AtPHT1. The VPTs or SPX-MSF family maintain Pi homeostasis by importing Pi into and exporting Pi from vacuoles in Arabidopsis and rice31–35. Arabidopsis VPT1 and VPT3 sequester excess cytoplasmic Pi in the vacuoles to prevent the negative effects of high Pi accumulation. VPT1 was upregulated in the roots of the SKOR-J line and in the shoot of the AtHKT1;1 and SKOR promoter lines under normal-Pi conditions (Fig. 6E,F). VPT3 was upregulated in the roots of the SKOR-J line and shoots of the AtHKT1-D line under normal-Pi conditions (Fig. 6G,H). The results suggested excess Pi accumulation to be present in the cytosol of cells in the transgenic lines.

Our results suggested that root vascular bundle-specific expression of TaPT2, in addition to its root epidermis-specific expression, is another promising strategy for increasing biomass production, Pi acquisition, and Pi use efficiency. In these transgenic plants ectopically expressing TaPT2, the endogenous phosphate transport system was not significantly disrupted, and growth retardation was prevented. Thus, appropriate promoter selection is important for the overexpression of phosphate transporters, and SKOR promoter is the most suitable among the ones examined for regulating TaPT2 to improve Pi-use efficiency in Arabidopsis. In that case, plants with high Pi uptake and use efficiency can be bred by screening for natural variants or mutants with a high ability to upload Pi into the xylem. Furthermore, a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of SKOR promoter lines may provide novel insights into the mechanisms that maintain Pi homeostasis or improve Pi acquisition and use efficiency.

Methods

Production of transgenic Arabidopsis plants

PCR amplification of the cDNA for TaPT2 (AK331093) from a wheat variety Norin-6 (CAP corporation, Tokyo, Japan) had been reported previously15. In this study, we used transgenic Arabidopsis plants in which the TaPT2 gene was expressed using three types of promoters with different expression characteristics. Production and characterization of the transgenic lines AKT1-C and AKT1-F, in which TaPT2 is driven by the Arabidopsis AKT1 promoter (AT2G26650), had been reported previously15. The entry vector of TaPT2 was reacted with two other destination vectors, namely pAtHKT1 and pSKOR36, using an LR Clonase enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and converted into expression vectors. The plasmid pAtHKT1 contains the AtHKT1;1 promoter (AT4G10310), which is specifically expressed in the vascular bundle cells of Arabidopsis roots and shoots. The plasmid pSKOR contains the Shaker family potassium ion channel (SKOR) promoter (AT3G02850), which is specifically expressed in the stelar tissue of roots36,37. Arabidopsis WT plants (ecotype Columbia) (INPLANTA INNOVATIONS, INC., Yokohama, Japan) were transformed with expression vectors using the floral dipping method38. For the complementation test using an ethylmethane sulphonate-induced pho1 mutant line (ABRC Stock Number: CS850726, the mutant plants were transformed with the expression vector pSKOR-TaPT2. Agrobacterium strain GV3101 was used for the transformation.

Plant growth conditions

Plants were grown at 23 °C under a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle with approximately 60 µmole m−2 s−1 light intensity. Transgenic and WT Arabidopsis seeds were sown in 1/2 MS salt medium (containing 625 µM PO43−) supplemented with 1% sucrose and 0.8% agar (pH 5.7). At 14 d of age, the germinated seedlings were transplanted into a hydroponic culture system [the Home Hyponica Karen (Kyowa Co., LTD, Osaka, Japan)] containing 1/2 Hoagland’s salt solution (500 µM PO43−)39 or low-Pi solutions (modified 1/2 Hoagland’s solution containing 10 µM PO43−) supplemented with 0.2% MES. Shoots and roots were collected after another 14 days of incubation, washed with deionized water, blotted with a paper towel, and weighed to determine the FW. The DWs were finally determined after drying the roots and shoots at 60 °C for 24 h, followed by crushing the dried sample with a crusher mill (Tokken) in a 2 ml tube, incubation in 0.5 ml of deionized water at 60 °C overnight to extract soluble ions, and measurement of Pi concentrations in the extracts. For the complementation test of pho1 mutant line, pho1 transgenic lines, and the WT, 5-day-old seedlings were transferred to 1/2 MS medium and incubated for 14 d. Subsequently, fresh weights were determined.

Pi content measurements

Pi concentrations in the ion extracts were determined using the molybdate blue method. Briefly, the color-developing mixture consisted of 6 mL of 267 mM ascorbic acid, 10 mL of 2.5 M sulfuric acid, 1 mL of 4.19 mM bis[( +)-tartrato]diantimonate (III) dipotassium trihydrate, and 3 mL of 32.4 mM ammonium molybdate. One hundred and sixty microliters of the color-developing mixture was added to 840 µL of diluted sample solutions and a blank solution and the absorbance at 880 nm was measured. The concentration of Pi in the sample solution was determined by plotting the absorbance against a calibration curve prepared from the measurements of phosphate solutions of known concentrations. To analyze the total P content, the dried sample was digested with H2SO4-H2O2 at 250 °C for 1 h, neutralized, diluted to an appropriate concentration, and measured using the molybdenum blue method.

Collection of xylem sap

Transgenic and WT plants were incubated on a 1/2 MS agar medium for 14 d and then cultivated in 1/2 Hoagland’s solution (500 µM Pi) for another 2 or 3 weeks until they reached the bolting stage. Finally, xylem sap was collected as described previously40, after which the collected samples were used to measure Pi concentration.

Real-time qRT–PCR

To examine the expression levels of genes related to Pi transport and homeostasis in the transgenic lines, the shoots or roots of 14-d-old Arabidopsis plants grown on 1/2 MS agar medium were used for RNA extraction. After total RNA extraction from the plant samples, real-time qRT–PCR of the transgene was performed as described previously40. Transcriptional levels of the transgene TaPT2 and endogenous PHT1 (NM_123701.4), VPT1 (NM_001198368), VPT3 (NM_001203876), PHO1 (NM_113246), and PHO1;H1 (NM_105547) were determined. To examine the expression levels of TaPT2 in the pho1 transgenic lines, the 14-d-old plants grown on 1/2 MS agar medium were used for RNA extraction and semi-qRT-PCR was performed. AtUBQ5 gene was used as a reference gene. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.
