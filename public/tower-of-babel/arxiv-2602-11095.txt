Stochastic synthesis-degradation processes: first-passage properties and connections with resetting
Gabriel Mercado-Vásquez, Denis Boyer

Abstract
Processes controlled by stochastic synthesis and degradation (SSD) are widespread in biology but their reaction kinetics are not well understood. Using methods borrowed from the theory of resetting processes, we determine the first-passage properties of a collection of independent particles that are synthesized and degraded at constant rates, and follow an arbitrary diffusive process in space. At equal synthesis and degradation rates, the mean reaction time with a target site can be minimized as in stochastic resetting, and a C ​ V CV -criterion is derived. When the degradation rate is held fixed and the synthesis costs are taken into account, an optimal synthesis rate is obtained. In bounded domains, despite particle degradation, SSD improves the mean search time compared to a single non-degrading particle if the synthesis rate exceeds a critical value. The latter obeys a universal relation. We illustrate these findings with Brownian diffusion on the infinite line and in an interval.



The synthesis and degradation of proteins and other molecular structures is ubiquitous in cellular and intercellular processes. From the replication of simple viruses
Yin and McCaskill 1992
to quorum detection in bacteria communication
Waters and Bassler 2005
, or pattern formation in complex multi-cellular organisms
Gregor et al. 2007
;
Landge et al. 2020
, stochastic synthesis-degradation (SSD) processes are not only widespread but also crucial for robust biological function.

In the immune system, cell-to-cell communication is mediated primarily by secreted cytokines and chemokines that rapidly diffuse through the extracellular space, enabling propagation of immune signals
Su et al. 2024
;
Altan-Bonnet and Mukherjee 2019
. Upon signal detection, transcription factors are translocated to the nucleus to initiate RNA transcription, which is subsequently translated into thousands of functional protein copies
Friedman et al. 2006
;
Yu et al. 2006
. However, although protein secretion can be sustained for hours
Han et al. 2012
, due to biochemical denaturation or consumption by surrounding cells, the abundance of a protein is limited both spatially and temporally in the system
Altan-Bonnet and Mukherjee 2019
. This mechanism of synthesis and degradation of diffusing entities naturally produces concentration gradients and maintains non-equilibrium steady states of the protein profiles in the cell environment
Oyler-Yaniv et al. 2017
.
The efficient synthesis and detection of proteins is crucial for maintaining homeostasis and proper physiological function
Su et al. 2024
;
Mosser et al. 2021
, for example in the resolution of inflammation
Mosser and Edwards 2008
;
Murray and Wynn 2011
;
Akira et al. 2006
or for inhibiting hyperglycemia
Netea et al. 2017
.

In living organisms, however, stochasticity plays a fundamental role in the variability of RNA and protein expression
Elowitz et al. 2002
;
Friedman et al. 2006
. In this context, where the main functions of a system are controlled by the arrival of diffusive but degradative molecules at a specific location, understanding first-passage time (FPT) properties is crucial for quantifying reaction efficiency and signal fidelity
Kondev et al. 2026
.

A basic SSD model considers independent Brownian particles that are sequentially born at a constant rate
b
from a fixed position
x_{0}
. Each particle degrades at a rate
d
while diffusing, losing its ability to react with one or several target sites (Fig.
1
a). The density of viable particles evolves in the bulk according to a modified diffusion equation
Drocco et al. 2012
,

\frac{\partial\rho(x.t)}{\partial t}=D\Delta\rho(x,t)-d\rho(x,t)+b\delta(x-x_{0})\,,

(1)

where the last two terms describe degradation and synthesis, respectively. A key variable of interest here is the random time when a target site is reached by a non-degraded particle for the first time.

With
b=d=r
, Eq. (
1
) turns out to be identical to the forward Fokker-Planck equation describing diffusion with stochastic resetting (SR)
Evans and Majumdar 2011a
;
Evans and Majumdar 2011b
, a different kind of non-equilibrium process illustrated by Fig.
1
b. In SR, the motion of a single particle is interrupted at a rate
r
and restarts instantaneously from
x_{0}
. Resetting processes are efficient for completing search tasks and they have received a considerable attention in the past decade, with extensions well beyond simple diffusion. Their first-passage statistics are well-understood
Evans et al. 2020
;
Gupta and Jayannavar 2022
;
Kusmierz et al. 2014
;
Kusmierz and Gudowska-Nowak 2015
;
Montero et al. 2017
;
Evans and Majumdar 2018
;
Chechkin and Sokolov 2018
. Despite its proximity with SR, SSD has in principle its own reaction kinetics, that are not well-known. SR can actually be viewed as a “mean-field” SSD process, where death and birth events are perfectly correlated, so that the number of particles in the system is always unity (or some other number
Biroli et al. 2023
) instead of fluctuating in time.

In this Letter, we adapt methods from resetting theory to derive the first-passage properties of an arbitrary process under SSD. First-passage observables can be written in terms of the probability distribution function (PDF) of the FPT of a single non-degrading particle. We determine general conditions under which the mean first-passage time (MFPT) and related quantities can be optimized in open and bounded domains, similarly to SR. If synthesis exceeds a critical rate obeying a simple universal relation, SSD processes speed up the reaction compared to that of a single non-degrading particle. We illustrate the findings with a few Brownian examples

Survival probability.
In the setup depicted in Fig.
1
a, a quantity of special interest is the probability
Q^{(1)}(t)
that at time
t
no particle has yet reached a target site, given that one particle started from
x_{0}
at time
t=0
. Let
Q^{(0)}(t)
represent the same probability, but without a particle in the system at
t=0
, while
q_{d}(t)
is the probability that a single particle starting from
x_{0}
has not reached the target during
[0,t]
. The dimensionality, geometry, or type of diffusion is arbitrary. (And the dependence on
x_{0}
in all functions is implicit.) We have

\displaystyle q_{d}(t)=1-\int_{0}^{t}d\tau e^{-d\tau}P_{0}(\tau)\,,

(2)

where
P_{0}(t)
denotes the PDF of the FPT of the underlying process, i.e., a single diffusive particle with position
x_{0}
at
t=0
and non-degrading (
d=0
). Eq. (
52
) eliminates the trajectories that find the target in a time
\tau<t
, while
e^{-d\tau}
is the probability of not degrading in

[0,\tau]

Meerson and Redner 2015
.

The independence between particles implies

Q^{(1)}(t)=q_{d}(t)Q^{(0)}(t)\,.

(3)

In Eq. (
3
), the whole SSD process is viewed as composed of the trajectory of the first particle starting at
t=0
[associated to
q_{d}(t)
], superposed to the SSD process without an initial particle, representing all particles synthesized after
t=0
.
The quantity
Q^{(0)}(t)
obeys an integral, renewal-like equation,

Q^{(0)}(t)=e^{-bt}+b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,e^{-b(t-t^{\prime})}q_{d}(t^{\prime})Q^{(0)}(t^{\prime})\,.

(4)

The first term in the rhs of Eq. (
4
) is the probability that no particle is born during the time interval
[0,t]
. In such a case, the target survives with probability 1. The second term describes the probability that the first particle was born before
t
in the time interval
[t-t^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}+{\rm d}t^{\prime}]
, an event that occurs with probability
b{\rm d}t^{\prime}e^{-b(t-t^{\prime})}
. The probability that the process that starts with this particle has not yet reacted after the remaining time
t^{\prime}
is precisely
Q_{1}(x_{0},t^{\prime})
. Using Eq. (
3
), the second term of Eq. (
4
) is obtained.

Unlike in the renewal approach of stochastic resetting (SR)
Evans and Majumdar 2018
;
Chechkin and Sokolov 2018
, Eq. (
4
) does not have a convolution structure and the use of Laplace transform methods is not appropriate. However, as shown in the Supplemental Material (SM), it can be solved directly as

Q^{(0)}(t)=e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u[1-q_{d}(u)]}\,.

(5)

Using Eq. (
52
) and integrating by parts, this expression can also be written as

Q^{(0)}(t)=e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,e^{-du}P_{0}(u)(t-u)}\,.

(6)

The general expression for
Q^{(1)}
is thus

Q^{(1)}(t)=q_{d}(t)\,e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u[1-q_{d}(u)]}\,.

(7)

Notably, Eqs. (
5
) and (
7
) provide explicit survival probabilities at all times (if the FPT statistics of the underlying process are known), in contrast with SR, where the exact time dependence of this quantity is very rarely available, even in simple cases.

The late time behaviors of
Q^{(0)}(t)
and
Q^{(1)}(t)
are deduced by noticing that
q_{d}(t)
rapidly tends to a non-zero positive constant less than unity if
t\gg 1/d
. Introducing the Laplace transform
\widetilde{P}_{0}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-st}P_{0}(t)
, one obtains the exponential decay

Q^{(i)}(t)\simeq c_{i}e^{-b\widetilde{P}_{0}(d)t}\,,\quad i=0,1

(8)

with the prefactors
c_{0}=e^{b\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}u\,uP_{0}(u)e^{-du}}
and
c_{1}=c_{0}[1-\widetilde{P}_{0}(d)]
.
Eq. (
8
) yields the exact characteristic reaction time of the SSD process,

\tau_{b,d}=1/[b\widetilde{P}_{0}(d)]\,,

(9)

which is inversely proportional to
b
, while the effect of
d
depends on the process and geometry. Since
\tau_{b,d}<\infty
for any
b>0
, SSD shares with SR an advantageous feature: underlying processes having a fat-tailed survival probability,
q_{0}(t)\sim t^{-\theta}
with
0\leq\theta<1
(yielding an infinite MFPT), become completed in a finite mean time.

An example is the 1D Brownian particle on the semi-infinite line with an absorbing target at
x=0
(see Fig.
1
a), where
\theta=1/2
and
P_{0}(t)
is the Lévy-Smirnov distribution, given through
\widetilde{P}_{0}(d)=e^{-\sqrt{d/D}|x_{0}|}
. From Eq. (
9
),
\tau_{b,d}=b^{-1}e^{\sqrt{d/D}|x_{0}|}
, which diverges when
b\to 0
(slow synthesis) or
d\to\infty
(fast degradation).

To compare with the resetting phenomenology, let us set
b=d=r
and vary
r
. In the above example,
\tau_{r,r}=r^{-1}e^{\sqrt{r/D}|x_{0}|}
diverges at small and large
r
, with a global minimum at
r^{*}=4D/x_{0}^{2}
. Hence there is an optimal rate for which the no-reaction probability in SSD decays the fastest to 0. In SR, the asymptotic decay of the survival probability is also exponential and the associated time
\tau^{(SR)}_{r}
is solution of the general transcendental equation
1/\tau_{r}^{(SR)}=r\widetilde{P}_{0}(r-1/\tau_{r}^{(SR)})

Evans and Majumdar 2011a
.
While it cannot be obtained exactly in a close form,
\tau^{(SR)}_{r}
is also non-monotonous and becomes equal to
\tau_{r,r}
in the small and large
r
limits, where
\tau^{(SR)}_{r}\simeq r^{-1}
and
\tau^{(SR)}_{r}\simeq r^{-1}e^{\sqrt{r/D}|x_{0}|}
, respectively
Evans and Majumdar 2011a
.

Mean first-passage time and search costs.

The MFPT denoted as
T_{b,d}
is obtained from the general relation
T_{b,d}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,Q^{(1)}(t)
. One obtains (see SM),

T_{b,d}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,e^{-du}(t-u)P_{0}(u)}\,-\frac{1}{b}\,.

(10)

Contrary to its SR counterpart, given by
T_{r}^{(SR)}=[1-\widetilde{P}_{0}(r)]/[r\widetilde{P}_{0}(r)]

Evans and Majumdar 2018
;
Evans et al. 2020
,
T_{b,d}
above cannot be easily expressed in terms of elementary functions.

Fig.
2
a shows
T_{r,r}
, again in the case case
b=d=r
for Brownian particles on the 1D line. Similarly to
\tau_{r,r}
, the MFPT is non-monotonous with
r
and exhibits a minimum at
r_{\rm min}\simeq 3.25D/x_{0}^{2}
.
T_{r,r}
is distinct and larger than
T^{(SR)}_{r}
, also displayed, indicating that fluctuations in the number of particles around 1 prolong the reaction time.

Let us now consider the general case,
b\neq d
. Assuming that
d
is held constant, the minimization of
\tau_{b,d}
or
T_{b,d}
with respect to
b
yields the optimal value
b=\infty
. Clearly, this cannot be achieved in practice because of the costs associated with large synthesis rates. The costs incurred by SSD processes differ from their SR counterparts. In SSD, degradation is cost-free, unlike in resetting where the particle must be brought back from its current location to the starting point by a certain protocol
Pal et al. 2016
;
Pal et al. 2019
;
Tal-Friedman et al. 2020
;
Bodrova and Sokolov 2020
;
Olsen et al. 2024
;
Gupta and Cleuren 2025
. Synthesis however requires matter, energy and time, ideally the same amount at each event.

The cost of SSD processes can be quantified through the average total number of particles used until the FPT,
\langle n\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,P(t,n)
, where
P(t,n)
is the joint distribution of the reaction time (
t
) and of the total number of particles (
n
) used during
[0,t]
. As shown in the SM,

P(t,n)=\frac{\partial Y_{n}(t)}{\partial t}\frac{b^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-bt}\,.

(11)

with
Y_{n}(t)=[1-q_{d}(t)][\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)]^{n-1}
, leading to

\langle n\rangle=1+bT_{b,d}\,.

(12)

Eq. (
12
) has a simple interpretation. The particles created until the completion time
t
of the search are the initial one plus all the subsequent particles born during
(0,t]
, in number
bT_{b,d}
on average.
As shown by Fig.
2
a for the same example with
b=d=r
,
\langle n\rangle
increases monotonously with
r
and is about
6.94
at
r=r_{\rm min}
.

Synthesis costs can be added to the search cost, to build a total cost function,

\Theta_{b,d}=T_{b,d}+\lambda\langle n\rangle\,,

(13)

to be minimizes with respect to
b
, and where

\lambda
is a parameter. As shown in Fig
2
b, fixing
d
,
\Theta_{b,d}
is optimized at a certain
b_{\rm min}
for any value of
\lambda
. As the synthesis costs become larger, the overall optimization becomes more sensitive to the choice of
b
.

Small
b
and

expansion, CV-criterion.

We now assume that
P_{0}(t)
has a finite first moment
\langle T_{0}\rangle
, as observed in bounded domains, and wish to compare the MFPT of a SSD process to this bare search time
\langle T_{0}\rangle
(here,
\lambda=0
). To do so, it is instructive to analyze the behavior of
T_{b,d}
when both
b
and
d
are small. This strategy is well-known in resetting, where the study of
T_{r}^{(SR)}
at small
r
allows the derivation of a universal relation (the so-called CV-criterion) telling whether resetting expedites search or not for a given process
Reuveni 2016
;
Pal and Reuveni 2017
;
Pal et al. 2022
;
Pal et al. 2024
.

Let us assume quite generally that
P_{0}(t)
also has a finite variance
\langle T_{0}^{2}\rangle-\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}
and decays exponentially or faster at large
t
, over a time-scale
1/\mu
.
The expansion of
T_{b,d}
with
b,d\ll\mu
is somewhat subtle and presented in the SM. One obtains the expression,

T_{b,d}=\frac{b+d}{b}\langle T_{0}\rangle-\frac{(b+d)^{2}}{2b}\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}\left[CV^{2}-\frac{d^{2}}{(b+d)^{2}}\right]+{\rm h.o.t.}\,,

(14)

where
CV=\sqrt{\langle T_{0}^{2}\rangle-\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}}/\langle T_{0}\rangle
is the coefficient of variation of the FPT of the underlying process, while h.o.t. represents negligible terms of order
b^{2}
,
d^{2}
or
db
.

When
b=d=r
, the above relation becomes

T_{r,r}=2\langle T_{0}\rangle-2r\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}\left[CV^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right]+{\cal O}(r^{2})\,,

(15)

instead of

T_{r}^{(SR)}=\langle T_{0}\rangle-\frac{r}{2}\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}[CV^{2}-1]+{\cal O}(r^{2})\,,

(16)

for resetting processes
Pal et al. 2024
.
One can notice two fundamental differences between SSD and SR.

Firstly,
\lim_{r\to 0}T_{r,r}=2\langle T_{0}\rangle
and not
\langle T_{0}\rangle
, which may look surprising at first sight. The factor 2 (more generally
\frac{b+d}{b}
) can be explained qualitatively as follows. At small
r
, the first particle diffuses alone and reacts in an average time of
\langle T_{0}\rangle
, assuming that it has not degraded before. The probability of not degrading is roughly
e^{-r\langle T_{0}\rangle}
, close to 1. With the small complementary probability
1-e^{-r\langle T_{0}\rangle}
, the first particle dies before locating the target and one needs to wait for a very long time (
1/r
on average) before the second particle is created. From that instant, this particle will take a time
\langle T_{0}\rangle
to locate the target. Taking the average over these two search times gives

\langle T_{0}\rangle e^{-r\langle T_{0}\rangle}+\left(r^{-1}+\langle T_{0}\rangle\right)(1-e^{-r\langle T_{0}\rangle})\to 2\langle T_{0}\rangle\,,

as
r\to 0
. This limit (implying a discontinuity at
r=0
) is confirmed by Langevin dynamics simulations of 1D Brownian particles in the interval
[0,1]
with absorbing boundaries, see Fig.
3
a.

).

Secondly,
T_{r,r}
decreases with
r
at small
r
from the value
2\langle T_{0}\rangle
if

CV>\frac{1}{2}
, see Eq. (
15
).
In that case, there exists in principle an non-trivial rate
r_{\rm min}
where
T_{r,r}
has a local minimum, like in the semi-infinite domain. Conversely, if
CV<1/2
, the mean time
T_{r,r}
increases with
r
. The threshold value of
1/2
, instead of
1
for stochastic resetting in Eq. (
16
), implies that a much wider class of processes will exhibit a non-monotonous behavior when subject to SSD. Processes that exhibit a monotonous increase of the MFPT with
r
under SR may have a non-monotonous behavior under SSD.

In the above example of Brownian motion in the interval, the smallest
CV
is achieved by choosing the starting position at the middle of the domain (
x_{0}=1/2
), and the corresponding value is
\sqrt{2/3}
(see e.g.,
Dagdug et al. 2024
). As
\sqrt{2/3}<1
, resetting increases the exit time
Pal and Prasad 2019
. But since
\sqrt{2/3}>1/2
, the MFPT decreases with
r
at small
r
under SSD, see Fig.
3
a. Unlike in resetting
Pal and Prasad 2019
, a non-trivial
r_{\rm min}
locally minimizes
T_{r,r}
for any initial position
x_{0}
in the interval. Interestingly, as also shown by Fig.
3
a, the minimum of
T_{r,r}
at
r_{\rm min}
can be larger than
\langle T_{0}\rangle
(for
x_{0}=0.5
), or smaller (
x_{0}=0.13
). In the latter case, SSD with
b=d=r
turns out to be beneficial for search.

Critical synthesis rate.
The above findings lead us to a more general question in bounded domains. Given a process with a prescribed degradation rate
d
, what is the birth rate
b_{c}(d)
above which the MFPT
T_{b,d}
becomes
lower
than
\langle T_{0}\rangle
in the same setting? In other words, how the degradation of the individual searchers can be compensated through the synthesis of more searchers, in order to achieve identical or faster search times than a single particle with
d=0
?

In the case
d=0
, Eq. (
14
) gives

T_{b,0}=\langle T_{0}\rangle-\frac{b}{2}\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}CV^{2}+{\cal O}(b^{2})
. Clearly, any non-zero birth rate improves the MFPT of the first particle alone, or
b_{c}(d=0)=0
.
If
d
is non-zero, a minimal synthesis rate is necessary to improve
\langle T_{0}\rangle
. At small
d
, we can assume that
b_{c}(d)
is also small, so that Eq. (
14
) can be used. Solving
T_{b,d}=\langle T_{0}\rangle
we obtain the universal relation,

b_{c}(d)=\frac{1}{CV}\sqrt{\frac{2d}{\langle T_{0}\rangle}}+{\cal O}(d/\langle T_{0}\rangle)\,.

(17)

From Eq. (
17
), the critical synthesis rate is much larger than
d
in the small
d
limit. Both the square-root dependence and the prefactor are completely general, as Eq. (
14
) is independent of the geometry, spatial dimension or type of diffusion.
The scaling relation (
17
) agrees very well with the exact
b_{c}(d)
in the example of Brownian particles in an interval (see Fig.
3
b).

Conclusion.
Inspired from methods used in the study of resetting processes, we have analyzed the first-passage properties of stochastic synthesis-degradation (SSD) processes. The results are expressed in terms of the first-passage time PDF of the underlying process in the absence of SSD, and they are not limited to Brownian diffusion or even Markov processes. SSD can reduce the completion times of target search tasks in bounded and unbounded domains, and they can be optimized. The renewal-like approach employed here is flexible and could be adapted to non-Poisson synthesis-degradation kinetics, a common situation in cell biology
Harper et al. 2011
;
Kumar et al. 2015
.

Calculation of the survival probability
Q^{(0)}(t)

The survival probability
Q^{(0)}(t)
obeys the renewal-like relation

Q^{(0)}(t)=e^{-bt}+b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,e^{-b(t-t^{\prime})}q_{d}(t^{\prime})Q^{(0)}(t^{\prime})\,,

(18)

with

q_{d}(t)=1-\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,P_{0}(u)e^{-ru}.

(19)

Eq. (
18
) can be rewritten as

Q^{(0)}(t)=e^{-bt}+be^{-bt}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,e^{bu}Q^{(0)}(u)q_{d}(u)\,,

(20)

and can be solved directly for all
t
by defining

R^{(0)}(t)=e^{bt}Q^{(0)}(t)\,.

(21)

The integral equation for
R^{(0)}(t)
reads

R^{(0)}(t)=1+b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,R^{(0)}(u)q_{d}(u)\,.

(22)

Differentiating on both sides gives

\frac{{\rm d}R^{(0)}(t)}{R^{(0)}(t)}=bq_{d}(u){\rm d}t\,,

(23)

which is integrated as

R^{(0)}(t)=e^{b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)}\,,

(24)

after using the initial condition
R_{0}(0)=1
. Substituting in the definition (
21
), the identity
Q^{(0)}(t)=e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u[1-q_{d}(u)]}
of the main text is obtained.

Mean first passage time

The expression for the MFPT can be simplified to avoid dealing with three nested integrals,

\displaystyle T_{r,r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,q_{d}(t)e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u[1-q_{d}(u)]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}q_{d}(t)e^{b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)}

(26)

with
q_{d}(t)
given by Eq. (

). Integrating by parts, one has

\displaystyle T_{r,r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}e^{b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)}\,-\,\frac{1}{b}\,

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,[1-q_{d}(u)]}\,-\,\frac{1}{b}\,

Similarly the integral
\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,[1-q_{d}(u)]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,[1-q_{d}(u)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,\int_{0}^{u}{\rm d}y\,P_{0}(y)e^{-dy}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,e^{-du}(t-u)P_{0}(u)\,,

(30)

after integrating by parts and grouping the terms. Inserting this last expression into
T_{r,r}
leads to

T_{b,d}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,e^{-du}(t-u)P_{0}(u)}\,-\frac{1}{b}\,.

(31)

III
Derivation of the joint distribution
P(t,n)

When the synthesis process is Poisson, the probability that
n-1
particles have been created (after the initial one) during the time interval
[0,t]
and that none of these have crossed the origin at time
t
is

p_{n-1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}b{\rm d}t_{1}\,q_{d}(t-t_{1})\int_{t_{1}}^{t}b{\rm d}t_{2}\,q_{d}(t-t_{2})\ldots\int_{t_{n-2}}^{t}b{\rm d}t_{n-1}\,q_{d}(t-t_{n-1})\,e^{-bt}\,,

(32)

where
t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{n-1}
are the times of synthesis and the term
e^{-rt}
ensures that no more particles are created in the union of the intervals
(t_{i},t_{i+1})
. One can equivalently relax the order of the
t_{i}
’s and divide by the number of permutations,

p_{n-1}(t)=\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t_{1}\,q_{d}(t-t_{1})\right)^{n-1}\frac{b^{n-1}e^{-bt}}{(n-1)!}\,.

(33)

We define
P(n,t){\rm d}t
as the probability that
n
particles have been used up to time
t
(the first particle and the following
n-1
ones) and that the first passage at the origin by one of them occurs during the time interval
[t,t+{\rm d}t]
. There are two possibilities: either the first particle is the one that first crosses the origin, or one of the
n-1
following particles does so. Therefore,

P(n,t)=P_{0}(t)e^{-dt}p_{n-1}(t)+q_{d}(t)\left(\int_{0}^{t}b{\rm d}u\,P_{0}(t-u)e^{-d(t-u)}\right)p_{n-2}(t)\,.

(34)

In the first term of the rhs of Eq. (
34
), the factor
e^{-dt}
is the probability that the first searcher has not degraded before
t
.
The second term tells that the first particle does not find the target [
q_{d}(t)
], that a certain particle created at a time
u
finds it, and that the other
n-2
particles (which are exchangeable) do not find it. After making the variable change
t-u\to u
and using the relation (
52
),

P(n,t)=P_{0}(t)e^{-dt}p_{n-1}(t)+q_{d}(t)[1-q_{d}(t)]p_{n-2}(t)\,.

(35)

The above expression can be recast in a more compact form as,

P(n,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[[1-q_{d}(t)]\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)\right)^{n-1}\right]\frac{b^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-bt}\,.

(36)

which is presented in the main text.

One checks that the above expression is normalized. Integrating by part,

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,P(t,n)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,[1-q_{d}(t)]\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)\right)^{n-1}\frac{b^{n}}{(n-1)!}e^{-bt}\,.

(37)

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,P(t,n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,[1-q_{d}(t)]e^{b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)}e^{-bt}

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,[1-q_{d}(t)]e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u[1-q_{d}(u)]}

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\,,

(40)

after direct integration.
Similarly, the marginal distribution obtained by summing Eq. (
36
) over
n
coincides with the first passage time distribution, given by
-\partial Q^{(1)}(t)/\partial t
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(t,n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[[1-q_{d}(t)]e^{b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)}\right]e^{-bt}

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\partial q_{d}(t)}{\partial t}+b[1-q_{d}(t)]q_{d}(t)\right]e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u[1-\,q_{d}(u)]}

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[q_{d}(t)e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u[1-\,q_{d}(u)]}\right]

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial Q^{(1)}(t)}{\partial t}\,.

(44)

IV
Calculation of the search cost
\langle n\rangle

We recall that

Q^{(1)}(t)=q_{d}(t)\,e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u[1-q_{d}(u)]}\,.

(45)

Denoting
Y_{n}(t)\equiv[1-q_{d}(t)]\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)\right)^{n-1}
for brevity, the mean number of particles used until the reaction time is

\displaystyle\langle n\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,nP(t,n)

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,n\frac{\partial Y_{n}(t)}{\partial t}\frac{b^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-bt}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{1}}\left.\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,\frac{\partial Y_{n}(t)}{\partial t}\frac{b_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)!}e^{-bt}\right|_{b_{1}=b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{1}}\left.\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,Y_{n}(t)\frac{b_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)!}be^{-bt}\right|_{b_{1}=b}\,.

We further have

\displaystyle\langle n\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{1}}\left.\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,[1-q_{d}(t)]\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\left(b_{1}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)\right)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\right)b_{1}e^{-bt}\right|_{b_{1}=b}

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}[1-q_{d}(t)]\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{1}}\left.\left[b_{1}e^{b_{1}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)}\right]\right|_{b_{1}=b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,[1-q_{d}(t)]\left[1+b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)\right]e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,[1-q_{d}(u)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+b^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,[1-q_{d}(t)]\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)\right)e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,[1-q_{d}(u)]}

\displaystyle{\rm(after\ direct\ integration\ of\ the\ first\ term)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+b\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,q_{d}(t)e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,[1-q_{d}(u)]}\quad{\rm(after\ inegrating\ by\ parts)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+b\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,Q^{(1)}(t)\quad({\rm from\ Eq.\ (\ref{q1exact0A})})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+bT_{b,d}\,.

V
Expansion of the MFPT
T_{b,d}
at small
b
and
d

Instead of expanding the exact solution,

T_{b,d}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,e^{-du}P_{0}(u)(t-u)}\,-\frac{1}{b}\,,

(48)

at small
b
and
d
(both assumed to be of same order), we can start from the identity
Q^{(1)}(t)=q_{d}(t)Q^{(0)}(t)
and Eq. (
18
) to obtain the “renewal-like” equation for
Q^{(1)}(t)
,

Q^{(1)}(t)=q_{d}(t)e^{-bt}+bq_{d}(t)\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,e^{-b(t-t^{\prime})}Q^{(1)}(t^{\prime})\,.

(49)

When
b
is small, the second term of the rhs of Eq. (
49
) can be treated as a perturbation of the first one. Therefore one can substitute
Q^{(1)}(t^{\prime})
in the integral by its leading behavior

q_{d}(t^{\prime})e^{-bt^{\prime}}
,

Q^{(1)}(t)=q_{d}(t)e^{-bt}+bq_{d}(t)e^{-bt}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,q_{d}(t^{\prime})+{\rm h.o.t.}\,,

(50)

where h.o.t. represents higher order terms whose magnitude will be evaluated later. The MFPT is obtained from Eq. (
50
) by using the general relation
T_{b,d}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,Q^{(1)}(t)
,

T_{b,d}=\tilde{q}_{d}(b)+\frac{b^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,q_{d}(t^{\prime})\right)^{2}+{\rm h.o.t.}\,,

(51)

after integration by parts of the second term of the rhs. [The same expression can be obtained starting from the exact solution Eq. (
45
) and writing
e^{b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,q_{d}(u))^{n}}{n!}
and retaining the first 3 terms of the sum.]

Taking the Laplace transform of
q_{d}(t)
, which is given by

\displaystyle q_{d}(t)=1-\int_{0}^{t}d\tau e^{-d\tau}P_{0}(\tau)\,,

(52)

the first term of
Eq. (
51
) reads

\tilde{q}_{d}(b)=\frac{1-\tilde{P}_{0}(b+d)}{b}\,.

(53)

At small
b
and
d
,

\tilde{P}_{0}(b+d)=1-(b+d)\langle T_{0}\rangle+\frac{(b+d)^{2}}{2}\langle T_{0}^{2}\rangle+\ldots\,,

(54)

hence

\tilde{q}_{d}(b)=\frac{b+d}{b}\langle T_{0}\rangle-\frac{(b+d)^{2}}{2b}\langle T_{0}^{2}\rangle+\ldots\,,

(55)

where the neglected terms are quadratic, i.e., of order
b^{2}
,
d^{2}
and
bd
.

To evaluate the second term of the rhs of Eq. (
51
), let us first rewrite
q_{d}(t)
as

q_{d}(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}{\rm d}t^{\prime}P_{0}(t^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}(1-e^{-dt^{\prime}})P_{0}(t^{\prime})\,,

(56)

from the normalization of
P_{0}(t^{\prime})
to unity. Recall that
P_{0}(t)
is assumed to rapidly decay to
0
with
t
(say, exponentially) over a characteristic time-scale
1/\mu
. Since
d
is small, i.e.,
1/\mu\ll 1/d
, the factor
1-e^{-dt^{\prime}}
in Eq. (
56
) can be replaced by
dt^{\prime}
at first order, for all values of
t^{\prime}
. Hence,

\displaystyle q_{d}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}{\rm d}t^{\prime}P_{0}(t^{\prime})+d\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,t^{\prime}P_{0}(t^{\prime})+\ldots

(57)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{0}(t)+d\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,t^{\prime}P_{0}(t^{\prime})+\ldots\,,

where
q_{0}(t)
is identified with the survival probability of the underlying process with
d=0
. With Eq. (
57
), one deduces

\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}q_{d}(t^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}q_{0}(t^{\prime})+td\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}t^{\prime}P_{0}(t^{\prime})-d\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}t^{\prime 2}P_{0}(t^{\prime})+\ldots\,

(58)

after an integration by parts of the last term of Eq. (
57
).
The second term of the rhs of Eq.
(
51
)
can be decomposed into two parts. We define

I=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,q_{d}(t^{\prime})\right)^{2}=I_{1}+I_{2}\,,

(59)

by writing
\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{A/\mu}{\rm d}t+\int_{A/\mu}^{\infty}{\rm d}t
, with
A
a fixed constant much larger than 1.

In the integral
I_{2}
, defined as

I_{2}=\int_{A/\mu}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,q_{d}(t^{\prime})\right)^{2}\,,

(60)

t
is much larger than
1/\mu
. Consequently, in the expression (
58
) for the quantity
\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,q_{d}(t^{\prime})
, one can replace the upper integration bound
t
of the three integrals by
\infty
, which gives

\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}q_{d}(t^{\prime})=\langle T_{0}\rangle+td\langle T_{0}\rangle-d\langle T_{0}^{2}\rangle+\ldots\,,

(61)

where the neglected terms are
{\cal O}(d^{2})
. Substituting Eq. (
61
) into (
60
) and grouping the factors
e^{-bt}t^{0}
,
e^{-bt}t
and
e^{-bt}t^{2}
in the integrand, one obtains, respectively,

I_{2}=I_{2}^{(a)}+I_{2}^{(b)}+I_{2}^{(c)}\,.

(62)

with

\displaystyle I_{2}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\langle T_{0}\rangle-d\langle T_{0}^{2}\rangle\right)^{2}\int_{A/\mu}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}\simeq\frac{\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}}{b}(1-e^{-bA/\mu})\simeq\frac{\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}}{b}+{\cal O}(b^{0})

(63)

\displaystyle I_{2}^{(b)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2d\langle T_{0}\rangle\left(\langle T_{0}\rangle-d\langle T_{0}^{2}\rangle\right)\int_{A/\mu}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}t\simeq\frac{2d^{2}\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}}{b^{3}}+{\cal O}(b^{0})

(64)

\displaystyle I_{2}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d^{2}\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}\int_{A/\mu}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}t^{2}\simeq\frac{2d^{2}\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}}{b^{3}}+{\cal O}(b^{0})\,,

(65)

assuming that
b
and
d
are of the same order.

The other part
I_{1}
of the integral
I
is given by

I_{1}=\int_{0}^{A/\mu}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,q_{d}(t^{\prime})\right)^{2}\,.

(66)

Since
q_{d}(t)
is bounded by
1
,

I_{1}<\int_{0}^{A/\mu}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}t^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{A}{\mu}\right)^{3}+{\cal O}(b)\,,

(67)

therefore
I_{1}\ll I_{2}
as the latter is of order
1/b
at leading order, see Eqs. (
63
)-(
65
). Gathering Eqs. (
63
)-(
65
) one obtains the second contribution to
T_{b,d}
in Eq. (
51
),

\displaystyle\frac{b^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-bt}\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,q_{d}(t^{\prime})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}\left(\frac{b}{2}+\frac{d^{2}}{b}+d\right)+{\cal O}(b^{2})

(68)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}\frac{(b+d)^{2}+d^{2}}{2b}+{\cal O}(b^{2})\,.

Adding the two contributions (
55
) and (
68
) one obtains

T_{b,d}=\frac{b+d}{b}\langle T_{0}\rangle-\frac{(b+d)^{2}}{2b}\langle T_{0}^{2}\rangle+\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}\,\frac{(b+d)^{2}+d^{2}}{2b}+{\cal O}(b^{2})\,,

(69)

which is can be rewritten as

T_{b,d}=\frac{b+d}{b}\langle T_{0}\rangle-\frac{(b+d)^{2}}{2b}\langle T_{0}\rangle^{2}\left[CV^{2}-\frac{d^{2}}{(b+d)^{2}}\right]+{\rm h.o.t.}\,,

(70)

as presented in the main text.

VI
MFPT for Brownian particles under SSD in an interval

The probability density
\rho(x,t)
for a Brownian particle diffusing in a finite interval with absorbing boundaries at
x=0
and
x=L
is given by

\rho_{0}(x,t)=\frac{2}{L}\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}Dt}{L^{2}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x_{0}}{L}\right)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\,.

(71)

Therefore, the survival probability for this process will be given by

q_{0}(t)=\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}x\ \rho_{0}(x,t)=\frac{4}{\pi}\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}(2n-1)^{2}Dt}{L^{2}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi x_{0}}{L}\right)\frac{1}{2n-1}

(72)

The PDF of the exit time, or first passage at one of the two absorbing walls, is deduced

P_{0}(t)=-\frac{\partial q_{0}(t)}{\partial t}=\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}}\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(2n-1)\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}(2n-1)^{2}Dt}{L^{2}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi x_{0}}{L}\right).

(73)

We then plug this expression into the general formula for the MFPT of the SSD process,

T_{b,d}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\,e^{-b\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\,e^{-du}(t-u)P_{0}(u)}\,-\frac{1}{b}\,.

(74)

The integral
I(t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\ e^{-du}(t-u)P_{0}(u)
in Eq. (
74
) can be computed as

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}}\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(2n-1)\sin\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi x_{0}}{L}\right)\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}u\ (t-u)e^{-\sigma_{n}u}

(75)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}}\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(2n-1)\sin\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi x_{0}}{L}\right)\frac{e^{-\sigma_{n}t}+\sigma_{n}t-1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}

where we have defined
\sigma_{n}=d+\frac{\pi^{2}(2n-1)^{2}D}{L^{2}}
.
We can now write the mean first passage time as

\displaystyle T_{b,d}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\ \prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\frac{4b\pi D(2n-1)}{L^{2}}\sin\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi x_{0}}{L}\right)\frac{e^{-\sigma_{n}t}+\sigma_{n}t-1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right]-\frac{1}{b}\,,

(76)

which is evaluated numerically by the trapezium method. Taking the product with
n
up to
100
is sufficient for a good convergence.
