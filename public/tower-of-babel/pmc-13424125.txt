Emergence of Sprouty role from the signaling interplay of DUSP1 and DUSP4 interactions in NSCLC pathogenesis
Authors: Manasi Tukrul, Pooja Gulhane, Shailza Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the predominant subtype of lung cancer, remains a major global health challenge due to its complex etiology and resistance to therapy. Aberrant signaling through receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) drives tumor progression and immune modulation, underscoring the need for systems-level investigation. In this study, we employed mathematical modeling and experimental validation to dissect the dynamic interplay of EGFR, FGFR, and TNFR signaling in NSCLC cells and macrophages. Our flux-based sensitivity analysis revealed persistent pathway biases, highlighting DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 as critical high-flux regulators within the MAPK and PI3K/AKT cascades. Crosstalk analysis demonstrated that these proteins form an interconnected signaling axis influencing cytokine production (IL6, IL10, IL12, and TNF-α) and macrophage-mediated responses in the tumor microenvironment. Functional assays confirmed that silencing SPRY2 suppressed DUSP1 and DUSP4 expression, impaired cell migration, and induced G2/M arrest, establishing a coordinated regulatory network essential for NSCLC progression. Together, our findings integrate computational modeling with molecular validation to identify DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 as pivotal drivers of oncogenic signaling and immune regulation. These proteins represent promising therapeutic targets whose inhibition may disrupt tumor-promoting feedback loops and attenuate NSCLC growth and survival.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a leading cause of death worldwide. Despite advances in medical and surgical treatments, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) continues to account for 80–85% of all lung tumors and is the foremost cause of cancer-related mortality globally1. In recent advancements, treatment strategies and NSCLC diagnosis have improved radically with the development of molecular profiling and targeted therapeutics, such as the identification of genomic alterations (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, MET, RET, NTRK, and BRAF), along with the approval of increasingly effective systemic targeted therapies, has revolutionized the treatment of these specific NSCLC groups. Consequently, the initial lines of systemic treatment frequently consist of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)2–4. Several studies continue to investigate the molecular and biological aspects of lung cancer to advance targeted therapies5. However, despite recent progress, the inevitable development of systemic drug resistance limits durable remissions, even as response rates improve across certain NSCLC subtypes6. Biomarker-driven strategies and combination therapies that target multiple pathways or mechanisms hold promise for overcoming this resistance7.

Lung cancer arises from disruptions in normal cellular activity driven by multiple factors, including aberrant signaling pathways, oxidative stress, and genetic alterations. RTKs are the most commonly implicated signaling pathways in carcinogenesis and are mostly associated with lung cancer8. In NSCLC, the MAPK pathway, particularly the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK axis and PI3K-AKT, governs cell survival and proliferation through inducing the expression of critical regulatory genes. EGFR-induced ERK activation promotes chemotherapy resistance and inhibits apoptosis in various cancer1. The MAPKs, comprising p38, JNK, and ERK, are crucial modulators of cellular proliferation and growth. DUSPs are phosphatase that dephosphorylate them at the residues of phosphor-tyrosine and serine/threonine9, thus modulating cell growth. DUSP1 (MKP1) that is extensively expressed in various types of cancer, including NSCLC in its early stages. They not only activate MAPK signaling, but also deactivate it10. Study also reported DUSP4 overexpression in lung cancer cell lines with EGFR mutation, which also show constitutively active ERK1/211. Another key modulator in RTK signaling is the SPRY family of protein12. The hSpry2 promoter has been reported to exhibit preferential expression in lung epithelium and drives strong transcriptional activity in lung epithelial A549 cells13. Moreover, the absence of SPRY1/2 has been shown to enhance the generation of a greater number of memory CD8 + T cells14. DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 are critical negative regulators for EGFR and FGFR signaling axis (Fig. 1). These signaling axis also plays an important role in macrophages in Tumor Microenvironment (TME) (Fig. 2). The mechanistic roles of DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 in the NSCLC tumor microenvironment remain unexplored. To address this, we applied a systems biology approach, which emphasizes system structure, including gene networks, biochemical pathways, and the mechanisms that shape cellular organization15. Mathematical modeling is central to systems biology, as it reveals how system components interact to produce complex dynamics. Since intuitive reasoning alone cannot capture such complexity, computational simulations provide critical insights into the mechanisms driving biological behavior16. The Systems Biology Toolbox in MATLAB provides a user-friendly, extensible framework for mathematical analysis of biological systems17. It employs a core data structure to represent mathematical models18. Given the dual roles of DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 as downstream regulators of EGFR and FGFR signaling, examining their combined effects on signaling networks is essential for understanding NSCLC progression. Reconstructed mathematical model leads a pro-tumorigenic downstream signaling axis of DUSPs and SPRY2 driving NSCLC progression

The reconstructed mathematical model represents the NSCLC diseased state, comprising cancer and macrophage cells, each with three compartments, viz., plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus. The model includes 121 species, 101 reactions, and 211 parameters (Fig. 4). Prominent signaling pathways, EGFR, FGFR, and TNFα, were identified as key regulators of DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 expression, driving NSCLC progression. Simulations performed with the ODE15s solver generated nonlinear first-order kinetics, revealing that cytoplasmic DUSP4, DUSP1, activated SPRY2, and nuclear DUSP4, DUSP1, and ERK1/2 reached the highest concentrations in cancer cells (Fig. 5A). Inherent statistical tools reveal DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 as key regulators in NSCLC

PCA was computed by solving an m × n component adjacency matrix. In cancer cells, plasma membrane components identified as principal contributors included EGFR, FGF, and TNFα, along with downstream signaling molecules leading to cytoplasmic DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 expression. Within the nucleus, NF-κB, SPRY2, ERK1/2, YAP, and P53 exhibited high PCA scores. In macrophages, plasma membrane components included IL10 and LPS–TLR4 signaling, while cytoplasmic contributors comprised PI3K, AKT, STAT3 dimer, and the MYD88 complex. Nuclear components with high PCA included DUSP1, ERK1/2, AKT, JNK, CCL3, P38, STAT dimer, and IL1B. Identification of these principal components provides insight into the signaling network by eliminating transient intermediates and highlighting nodes that play significant roles in network output, particularly in cancer progression through the DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 pathway (Fig. 6A). The sensitivity matrix and principal components are provided in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Note 2.

By performing model reduction, we simplified the network while retaining essential components necessary to preserve model behavior. Model robustness was determined by key parameters, species, and their reactions. The model was reduced by 65% through the elimination of components with low flux, concentration, and sensitivity coefficients (Fig. 6B), leaving only 35% of reactions that significantly influenced the final phenotype. A quasi-potential landscape of the reduced model was generated after refining spurious parameters. The dome-shaped plot, with a color gradient from blue to red, highlights major components in the simplified model. At the peak of the dome, principal components with higher flux and concentrations were observed. The matrix used for construction is provided in Supplementary Table 3. Furthermore, responses to signals from multiple components may converge at common nodes, defining shortest paths in the biological network that influence system output. To identify these central nodes, we analyzed crosstalk within the model. Predicted protein structures and interaction studies reveal potential functional networks

Similarly, DUSP4 comprises 22 α-helices and 11 β-strands(Fig. 8B), while SPRY2 contains 20 α-helices and 6 β-sheets (Fig. 8c). Validation analyses of the predicted structures of DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 1–3. DUSP1 comprises the Rhodanese domain (nuclear targeting motif, KIM domain) and the catalytic active domain (phosphatase catalytic site, ERK1/2 phosphorylation site; Fig. 8D). Similarly, DUSP4 comprises the MAPK binding domain (nuclear localization sequence) and the catalytic phosphatase domain (Functional phosphatase pocket, catalytic active site; Fig. 8F). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed significant differences in DUSP1, DUSP4 and SPRY2 protein expression between NSCLC cell lines and normal lung cells (Fig. 10C, D), underscoring their crucial role in NSCLC pathogenesis.

After induction of human bFGF recombinant protein and EGF recombinant protein on NSCLC cell lines, we used an immunofluorescence study to measure DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 protein expression. WI 38 showed a significant increase in DUSP4 expression in response to bFGF and EGF recombinant protein induction, while SPRY2 showed a significant increase in response to recombinant Human EGF protein stimulation(Fig. 10E). In A549 cells, a significant elevation in DUSP1 and DUSP4 was observed in the presence of recombinant Human EGF protein (Fig. 10G). We also observed that bFGF and EGF induced H1299 and H1975 cells showed significantly higher expression of DUSP4 and SPRY2 expression (Fig. 10I, K). These findings suggest that human bFGF recombinant protein and EGF recombinant protein stimulate the signaling cascade, which results in higher expression of DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2, ultimately results into NSCLC progression. Knockdown of SPRY2 suppresses the NSCLC cells migration

The wound healing assay was employed to evaluate the effect of SPRY2 suppression on the proliferation of A549, H1299, and H1975 cells. In shSPRY2-transduced A549 cells, the percentage of wound closure was significantly reduced from 12 h (Fig. 12A), and H1299 cells showed significantly reduced wound closure area from 6 h post-scratch compared to control (Fig. 12B). Functional assays revealed that suppression of SPRY2 compromises the migratory and proliferative capacity of NSCLC cells. Specifically, H1975 cells displayed a pronounced reduction in wound-healed area at 24 h post-scratch (Fig. 12C), indicating impaired migration potential. Discussion

Lung cancer remains a major global health concern, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for 85% of cases. This type of cancer affects both smokers and non-smokers, highlighting the complexity of its risk factors. Treatment strategies vary depending on the tumor’s stage and location, pinpointing the need for personalized therapeutic approaches. Although treatments for cancer have been shown to lower disease severity, the increase in drug resistance has reduced cancer patients’ survival rates. In cells, signaling networks facilitate interaction between extracellular signals and intracellular mediators, hence controlling a variety of biological functions. Cancer is frequently caused by anomalies in these pathways, such as oncogene activation, overexpression, or the deletion of tumor suppressor genes. Such abnormalities in signal transduction result in unregulated cell growth and survival that exceed typical regulatory limitations. Genetic and epigenetic alterations allow cells to escape homeostatic systems, resulting in tumor development and progression. These changes promote cancer development through enabling cells to escape growth limitations and thrive in aberrant environments19.

Biological regulatory systems are inherently dynamic and interconnected, responding to perturbations through intricate control mechanisms. Their kinetic nature allows for continuous adaptation, ensuring stability while modulating interactions within complex networks. Research has revealed combinatorial gene regulation, complex dose-response relationships, dynamic behavior, feedback and forward-feedback loops, and gene activities that are redundant, competing, or synergistic. The most effective way to investigate the peculiarities of emergent systems is through kinetic mathematical models that employ ordinary differential equations (ODEs). These models may incorporate thermodynamic or stochastic formulas for molecular interactions. Modeling may then be used to determine robust/sensitive parameters or signaling behavior using parameter sensitivity analysis. The integration of discrete signals and the impact of the steady state on dynamic response to changes can both be investigated through modeling20. The models used to study signaling pathways can be broadly categorized into three groups. First, they may be continuous or discrete, depending on whether time and molecular abundance are treated as smooth variables (concentrations) or as discrete counts (molecule numbers). Second, they may be deterministic or probabilistic, i.e., deterministic models predict a defined future state from given initial conditions, whereas probabilistic models incorporate stochastic processes to account for randomness in molecular interactions. Third, models may be spatial or non-spatial, describing how processes unfold across cellular compartments or tissues, or treating them as uniform within a single compartment. The model is chosen based on the nature of the system being investigated, data availability, and the specific issues to be addressed. In numerous models, a deterministic approach using rate equations for substances and complex concentrations is utilized for biochemical reaction system and the mathematical representation consists of ordinary differential equations (ODEs)21. Additionally, George Box’s statement that “all models are wrong” highlights models’ inherent limits in adequately portraying complicated real-world occurrences. While no model can precisely capture the complexities of a system, simpler models, when used with caution, can yet provide significant insights and predictions22.

In this study, initially, the signaling appears to be a linear relationship between input components (receptors) and output components (gene expression regulators). A closer examination reveals that signaling pathways form an interconnected network involving EGFR, TNFα, and FGF, along with DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2, shaping the NSCLC tumor microenvironment within both cancer and macrophage cells. Upon binding of EGF, FGF, and TNFα to their respective receptors, viz., EGFR, FGFR, and TNFR, downstream signaling is initiated in cancer cells, leading to the activation of three key MAPKs, i.e., ERK1/2, P38, and JNK. Additionally, AKT activation occurs through its association with several adapter proteins, including FRS2, GRB2, SOS, PLC-gamma, and Dock1. Following the association of these adapter proteins, multiple downstream signaling axes are activated, including RAS/RAF/MEK1&2/ERK1&2, JNK, P38, and PI3K/AKT pathways. Once activated, MAPKs and AKT translocate to the nucleus, where they drive the activation of various transcription factors, such as AP1 (Activator Protein Family-1), ETS1, CREB, ATF1, NF-κB, SP1, AP2, STAT3, IL10, and HIF-1A, ultimately influencing tumor progression and immune regulation in NSCLC. Furthermore, these transcription factors are responsible for the synthesis of DUSP1 (Dual Specificity phosphatase-1), DUSP4 (Dual Specificity Phosphase-4), and SPRY2 (SPROUTY2), which results in tumor growth and survival of cancer cells. Our simulation findings highlight the critical role of DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 in NSCLC oncogenic signaling. The activation of ERK1/2 in cancer cells, along with increased levels of IL10, IL6, TNF-α, CCL3, and IL12 in macrophages, underscores the complex interplay between NSCLC cells and their microenvironment. This dynamic interaction, driven by both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, shapes tumor progression and immune response.

In mathematical modeling, local sensitivity analysis, where one input parameter is varied while all others are held constant, provides valuable insight into the overall behavior of the model. While using a steady-state flux assumption in the context of MAPK and RTK signaling is less about reproducing the true pulse-like phosphorylation dynamics and more about providing a tractable framework to study pathway utilization. These cascades are inherently transient, but in diseases like NSCLC, repeated or sustained receptor activation often creates a quasi-equilibrium where the average throughput of phosphorylation and dephosphorylation cycles can be approximated as steady. This helps to identify dominant signaling routes, bottlenecks, and consistently overactive nodes without requiring detailed kinetic parameters that are often unavailable. While this approach inevitably sacrifices temporal resolution and cannot capture feedback-driven oscillations or adaptive responses, it offers a coarse-grained yet useful snapshot of how signaling flux is redistributed in cancer cells23,24. In practice, steady-state flux analysis is best viewed as a complementary tool as it highlights persistent pathway biases in NSCLC pathogenesis, while dynamic models are needed to fully capture the rapid, transient signaling events that drive tumor biology. Our observations of DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 as high-flux reactions underscore their pivotal role in signaling pathways, highlighting their involvement in regulating immune responses and driving cancer progression. Principal component analysis plays a critical role in defining the model phenotype, as targeting key components could lead to its collapse. Based on flux, sensitivity, and component concentration, the model demonstrated a 65% reduction, effectively eliminating non-contributory elements while preserving accuracy and maintaining essential dynamics. To ensure stability, the model must prevent rapid transient changes, meaning its output should remain consistent and avoid unpredictable fluctuations over time, especially in dynamic systems. Crosstalk analysis of P38, DUSP4, and DUSP1 revealed an interconnected signaling axis that influences macrophage-mediated responses within the tumor microenvironment. These findings are supported by western blotting and immunofluorescence analyses, highlighting DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 as key regulators in NSCLC development. Stimulation of NSCLC cells with recombinant human bFGF and EGF proteins led to increased expression of DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2, suggesting their role in initiating signaling cascades in tumor cells. Consistent with predictions from our mathematical model, qRT-PCR analysis of Mo macrophages revealed upregulation of IL6, IL12, and DUSP1. Protein–protein interaction studies further indicated distinct interactions among DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2, pointing to a potential functional association and co-regulation. Functional validation was achieved through shRNA-mediated lentiviral silencing of SPRY2. Knockdown of SPRY2 resulted in suppression of DUSP1 and DUSP4, as confirmed by immunofluorescence and qRT-PCR analyses, highlighting a crucial regulatory network among these proteins. Moreover, wound-healing assays demonstrated a marked reduction in the migratory potential of transduced cells, while cell-cycle analysis revealed growth arrest at the G2/M phase, indicating impaired proliferation. Together, these findings suggest that DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 act in a coordinated regulatory network that promotes NSCLC progression. Their co-regulation and functional impact on cell migration and proliferation underscore their potential as novel therapeutic targets in lung cancer.

In conclusion, our study emphasizes the intricate signaling dynamics between DUSP1, DUSP4, and Sprouty proteins in NSCLC pathogenesis. The interplay among these regulators not only influences tumor progression but also modulates immune responses within the tumor microenvironment. Our findings highlight the pivotal role of Sprouty as an emergent factor in NSCLC, revealing its potential involvement in balancing oncogenic and tumor-suppressive signaling pathways. Building on these insights, it becomes essential to validate the model-derived predictions in clinically relevant contexts, thereby bridging computational findings with experimental and patient-derived evidence. Future work using patient-derived samples across diverse NSCLC subtypes will be essential to determine whether SPRY2 is a viable therapeutic target for inhibition, as predicted through systems biology models. We recognize that no single model can fully recapitulate the phenotypes and responses of patient tumors; however, the integration of multiple complementary approaches, applied with an awareness of their inherent limitations, continues to drive meaningful progress in understanding NSCLC pathogenesis and therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Methodology

Data collection

A comprehensive literature review, combined with publicly available databases such as KEGG, ConsensusPathDB, the Eukaryotic Promoter Database, and GO, was used to analyze the dynamic MAPK signaling axis and its downstream regulators DUSP1, DUSP4, and SPRY2 in NSCLC.

Reconstruction of the mathematical model

Mathematical modeling serves as a powerful approach for elucidating cancer mechanisms25, providing deeper insights and guiding the design of experiments tailored to the characteristics of specific biological systems26. In this study, key pathways of NSCLC were integrated into a mathematical framework (Fig. 12). The reconstructed model of the EGFR/FGFR/ERK/MAPK signaling axis indicated that NSCLC progression is driven by the expression of numerous cytokines in macrophages. Model construction was carried out using the SimBiology toolbox in MATLAB (version 7.11.1.866), where the diagram editor option was employed to define species, reactions, and compartments. The various reactions in the model were defined by different rate laws, as the Law of Mass action used for association, dissociation, and translocation, the Michaelis–Menten equation used for enzyme kinetics, and the Hill equation for gene expression. Parameters were selected based on reaction dynamics to mimic cancer cell behavior and enhance understanding of pathway modulation. Initial concentrations of model components were derived from literature, indicating that a single cell can secrete between 103 and 106 signaling molecules27.

Model behavior was analyzed using ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to evaluate how parameters and kinetics influence the system’s mathematical representation. These ODEs estimate species concentrations over time, defining the model’s state or phenotype and allowing discretization with experimental data28. All reactions were deterministically simulated using the ODE15s (stiff/NDF) solver for 100 time units. The methodology for mathematical model reconstruction is illustrated in Fig. 13.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity analysis evaluates how uncertainty in model input parameters influences output, thereby affecting pathway dynamics29. The sensitivity coefficient calculates time-dependent responses of species states relative to their initial concentrations and parameter values. For example, in a model with species x and parameters y and z, the sensitivities can be expressed as the derivatives dx/dy and dx/dz, where numerators represent sensitivity outputs, and denominators represent inputs30. This approach provides valuable insight into system behavior and model performance. In our study, we applied local sensitivity analysis31 using SUNDIALs as the default solver in MATLAB’s SimBiology toolbox (v7.11.1.866)19.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the numerous sensitivity coefficients obtained from sensitivity analysis. The PCA score reflects variation in sensitivity among system components32, allowing extraction of key information while minimizing data size33. A high PCA score indicates strong sensitivity, meaning that disruption of such a component could collapse system stability by interrupting biological information flow. In SimBiology, PCA was calculated using MATLAB’s princomp function, where the input matrix A (m × n) contained sensitivity scores for each component relative to others34. The resulting PCA scores were visualized in SigmaPlot to highlight components critical to specific disease phenotypes.

Flux analysis

Flux defines the productivity of each reaction and its contribution to the system. Quantitative and qualitative analysis of fluxes across metabolic networks and cellular compartments provides insight into metabolic regulation and highlights the relevance of metabolic changes in tumor development35. Since system outcomes depend on production rates, reactions with higher flux are particularly critical36. Comparative flux analysis is therefore a useful strategy for identifying high-productivity reactions that may drive disease progression32. In this study, the model was exported in SBML format and analyzed using COPASI (v4.22). Under steady-state conditions, fluxes for all reactions and time-course data were obtained.

Model reduction

To study overall model dynamics, unnecessary reactions with little or no influence on output were eliminated to reduce complexity and improve accuracy19,37. Removing transient intermediates simplified the model, enabling more reliable predictions of biological systems. Species concentration, flux, and sensitivity coefficients from the reconstructed model were used to construct a model reduction matrix. This matrix was then analyzed in SigmaPlot to generate a 3D quasi-potential landscape. In this study, the landscape is visualized by projecting high-dimensional dynamics into a 3D plot, using principal component analysis (PCA) to make the system interpretable38.

Crosstalk points

Schwartz introduced the term “crosstalk” to describe interactions between distinct signaling pathways that influence cell growth21. Once an agonist binds to its receptor, multiple signaling cascades are activated, which can interact through crosstalk39. These nodes are critical in signaling systems and can significantly affect model dynamics. In this study, crosstalk was calculated between EGF/EGFR, FGF/FGFR, TNF/TNFR, and IL10/TLR4 by subtracting the degree of a node in a specific pathway from its total degree across the network.

In silico protein structure prediction and domain analysis

Protein structure prediction enables the determination of a protein’s three-dimensional conformation from its amino acid sequence40. We predicted the 3D structures of human DUSP1 (NP_004408.1) and DUSP4 (AAH14565.1) using ab initio modeling, while the structure of human SPROUTY2 (NP_001305467.1) was generated through homology modeling with MODELLER 10.5. Structural validation was performed using RMSD, z-score, and Ramachandran plot analyses with PyMOL, PROSA-web, and PDBsum, respectively. Domains of the predicted structures were mapped through published literature and BLAST analysis to define their functional importance41–44. Protein–protein interaction analyses for these proteins were conducted using the CLUSPRO web server (https://cluspro.org). The resulting docked protein structures were subsequently examined for interaction details using PDBsum20,22.

Cell culture

The A549 (human lung adenocarcinoma) and WI-38 (Normal human lung fibroblast cells) were procured from the National Centre for Cell Science, Pune, and grown in HF-12 (Ham’s F12K) media and Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium, respectively. H1299 (Lung Large cell carcinoma), H1975 (Lung adenocarcinoma cells) cell lines were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI 1640). All the culture media are supplemented with penicillin-streptomycin (Himedia) and 10% FBS (Gibco). Cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

All the experiments were performed by using commercially available cell lines, which have confirmed authentication for experiments, including Mycoplasma testing and STR profiling. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBSC) guidelines of the National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India (Approval ID: 2024246).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from normal and lung cancer cells using RIPA buffer and quantified with the BCA assay. From each sample, 15 µg of protein was loaded onto a 15% SDS-PAGE gel, electrophoresed at 80 V, and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SPRY2 (MA5-57264), DUSP4 (PA5-99652), and GAPDH (2118S) at a 1:1000 dilution. After 16 h, membranes were incubated with anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were developed using luminol enhancer and peroxidase solution (Cyanogen) and visualized with ImageQuant 500. Quantification of target proteins was performed by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software.

bFGF recombinant protein stimulation

To stimulate the signaling axis, each cell line was cultured in growth medium supplemented with or without bFGF recombinant protein (PEPROTECH H2924) and recombinant human EGF protein (Gibco PHG0314). Cells were treated with bFGF at a final concentration of 50 ng/ml and EGF at 100 ng/ml for 24 h, respectively.

Immunocytochemistry staining

All the cells were grown on a coverslip for 16 h and fixed using 4% formaldehyde for 20 min. The fixed cells were washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.1% triton X for 5 min, and blocked with 3%BSA for 30 min at room temperature. After blocking, cells were incubated with primary antibodies SPRY2, DUSP1, and DUSP4 (1:200) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were incubated with secondary antibody (anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by DAPI staining (1 µg/ml) for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted on slides using Fluoromount-G and sealed. Similarly, protein expression of three selected targets was evaluated in SPRY2 lentiviral shRNA–transfected cells. Imaging was carried out using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000), and all acquired images were subsequently analyzed with ImageJ software.

Real time-PCR

Cell pellets from each NSCLC cell line were resuspended in 500 µl of TRIzol reagent, followed by the addition of 100 µl of chloroform. Samples were gently mixed and incubated at room temperature for 15 min, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was carefully transferred to a fresh Eppendorf tube, mixed with 500 µl of isopropanol, incubated for 10 min at room temperature, and centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the RNA pellet was washed with chilled 75% ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. This ethanol wash step was repeated twice, after which the pellet was air-dried. The dried pellet was dissolved in 30 µl of DEPC-treated water, and RNA quantification was performed using a Nanodrop BioSpectrometer (Eppendorf). Sample quality was assessed by recording the 260/280 and 260/230 absorbance ratios.

Only high-quality RNA samples were aliquoted (2 µg of total RNA per sample) into 100 µl tubes and used for cDNA synthesis. Each reaction mixture contained 0.8 µl of 25X dNTP, 2 µl of 10X RT buffer, 2 µl of 10X RT primer, 0.5 µl of reverse transcriptase, and 2 µg of total RNA, with the final reaction volume adjusted to 20 µl using DEPC-treated water. cDNA amplification was performed using a thermal cycler (Bio-Rad MJ Mini) under the following conditions, i.e., incubation at 25 °C for 10 min, followed by 37 °C for 2 h, and a final step at 85 °C for 5 min.

Expression profiling was performed using TaqMan-FAM-MGB chemistry for the targets viz., DUSP1 (Hs00610256_g1), DUSP4 (Hs01027785_m1), SPRY2 (Hs01921749_s1), IL6 (Hs00174131_m1), IL10 (Hs00961622_m1), and IL12 (Hs01073447_m1). Each reaction mixture contained 1 µg of cDNA from individual samples, 5 µl of 2X TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (4366072, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 0.5 µl of the respective TaqMan-FAM-MGB probe, with the final volume adjusted to 10 µl using nuclease-free water. Quantitative RT-PCR was carried out on the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) under the following cycling conditions for a total of 40 cycles with an initial holding stage at 50 °C for 2 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, followed by cycling at 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Comparative CT (ΔΔCT) values were calculated for each sample, with β-actin (Hs01060665_g1, ACTB) serving as the housekeeping gene.

Establishment of stable SPRY2 knockdown NSCLC cell lines via lentiviral transduction

NSCLC cells (5 × 10⁴) were seeded into 60-mm Petri dishes containing complete growth medium and incubated for 24 h prior to transduction. Cells were then treated with 2.4 µl of polybrene and 13.1 µl of SPRY2 lentiviral GFP-tagged shRNA particles, followed by gentle swirling of the plate to ensure uniform mixing. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh growth medium containing Geneticin (G418) at a final concentration of 1 mg/ml. The selection medium was refreshed every three days until resistant colonies were observed, typically within 10–12 days.

Wound healing assay

To evaluate the impact of targeted protein overexpression or downregulation on cell migration, both shRNA-transduced and non-transduced NSCLC cell lines were used. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ per well in a 24-well plate to establish a confluent monolayer. The exact number of cells required for confluency was adjusted according to cell type and dish size. Once 100% confluency was achieved, a straight scratch was made across the monolayer using a 10 µl pipette tip. Detached cells and debris were removed by gently washing with 1X PBS, ensuring smooth wound edges. Fresh complete medium was then added, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Images of wound closure were captured at 0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h using an EVOS XL Core Inverted Microscope.

Co-culture

THP-1 cells (4 × 10⁵ per well) were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with PMA (150 nM). The cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. Adherent THP-1 cells were assessed to confirm proper polarization into Mo macrophages. Following confirmation, cells were washed with 1X PBS, replenished with fresh RPMI medium, and incubated for an additional 24 h under the same conditions. Separately, NSCLC cells (3 × 10⁵) were plated into 0.4 µm pore-size transwell inserts (6-well format) containing 1.5 ml of complete medium. After 24 h, the transwell inserts with NSCLC cells were carefully transferred into wells containing Mo macrophages using sterile forceps. The co-culture was maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. Finally, cell pellets were collected in TRIzol reagent, and qRT-PCR analysis was performed.

Cell cycle analysis

Both shRNA lentiviral-transduced and non-transduced NSCLC cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well. Cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed with 1X PBS, and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min. Each sample was then treated with 3 µl of ribonuclease, 1.5 µl of Triton X, and 1.5 µl of propidium iodide solution, followed by incubation in the dark at 37 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, samples were analyzed using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer to assess cell cycle distribution and related parameters.
