Effects of cognitive dual tasking and tandem walking on gait parameters in chronic neck pain people: a preliminary cross-sectional study
Authors: Mersad ery, Maryam Saadat, Saeideh Monjezi, Masumeh Hessam, Mohammad Mehravar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of the current study was to compare gait characteristics under cognitive dual-tasking and tandem walking in people with and without chronic neck pain.MethodsTwenty-one adults with a history of non-specific neck pain and twenty-two matched control adults walked on a motorized treadmill under normal and tandem walking with and without a cognitive task. Gait spatiotemporal variables including cadence, stride length, and stride velocity were obtained using the motion analysis system.ResultsIn cognitive dual-task condition, people with neck pain had significantly higher dual-task costs (DTC) of cadence (p <.01, ηp2=.45), stride length (p <.01, ηp2=.67), and stride velocity (p <.01, ηp2=.96) compared to controls. The pattern of change was not different between the two groups. All participants showed a shorter stride length, and lower stride length variability in dual-task walking (p <.01, ηp2>.10). Tandem walking resulted in lower cadence in the chronic neck pain group compared to controls (p=.02, ηp2=.11). ConclusionsThis study showed that people with neck pain rely more than healthy subjects on executive-attentional resources to control gait parameters; however, to better understand the dual-task-related gait changes in people with neck pain, further research is required to examine the dual-task gait in more challenging cognitive and walking tasks. Tandem walking should be considered in gait assessment and training of people with neck pain.Keywords: Neck pain, Gait, Attention, Dual task, Tandem walk

Introduction

Neck pain is a serious health problem worldwide, with a lifetime prevalence of 14.2% to 71% in the general population [1]. This condition imposes a remarkable economic burden on individuals and society due to treatment costs, decreased productivity, and work-related problems [2]. Neck pain results in disturbed cervical proprioception function, deficits in oculomotor control, postural stability, and head movement control [3]. It was shown that people with chronic neck pain walked with shorter step lengths, reduced trunk rotation, and slower gait speed compared to controls [4,5]. These changes get more pronounced during walking with head movements, at maximum speed, and in elderly individuals with neck pain [6,7]. Despite our knowledge of impairments related to neck pain, little is known about the interactions between sensorimotor control and cognition. A recent systematic review suggests testing gait under dual-task conditions in people with chronic neck pain [8]. Walking in daily life requires attentional resources and brain activity to adjust gait patterns according to task and environmental demands [9]. A dual-task methodology in which walking is performed simultaneously with a secondary cognitive task is often used to examine the ability to divide attention between the concurrent tasks. Regarding limited attentional resource capacity, cognitive dual-tasking can lead to impaired performance in cognitive and, or walking tasks [10]. Alterations in cervical somatosensory inputs, their integration within the postural control system [5], decline in executive function and attentional control [11] in individuals experiencing neck pain may limit person’s attentional capacity. Consequently, a higher cognitive system may be required to compensate for these deficits. Therefore, when the cognitive system is subjected to the additional burden of a secondary cognitive task, it is anticipated that the decline in performance one or both tasks might be observed. Previous studies reported decreased in gait performance during dual-task walking in people with chronic pain [12,13]. Therefore, examination of gait performance under dual-task conditions could provide information about the walking adaptability of people with chronic neck pain.

The effects of dual-tasks are multifactorial and depend on individual sensorimotor expertise, type of cognitive task and the difficulty of the motor task [14]. Tandem walking increases the balance challenge in the frontal plane and has greater attentional demands than normal walking. Walking on a narrow base of support such as on streets with narrow sidewalks, can happen in daily life. The complexity of walking tasks may affect the attentional loads of cognitive tasks and gait control. Prior studies have indicated that individuals suffering from chronic neck pain demonstrate increased postural sway and a decreased ability to execute tandem standing tasks [15,16]. Additionally, these individuals exhibit a reduction in gait speed during tandem walking when compared to a control group [17]. The impacts of dual-task and tandem walking have been studied in adults with anterior cruciate ligament injuries, those with concussion injuries, and healthy athletes [18–20]. However, to the best of our knowledge, little is known about assessing gait under cognitive dual-tasking and challenging terrain in people with chronic neck pain. Therefore, the current study aimed to compare gait characteristics under cognitive dual-tasking and tandem walking among people with chronic neck pain and controls. We hypothesized that individuals with neck pain demonstrated significant alterations in spatiotemporal gait variables and dual-task cost (DTC) under two bases of support (BOS) conditions (normal and tandem) with and without cognitive task compared to controls.

Methods

Participants

In this study, twenty-one people with a history of nonspecific neck pain were recruited from physical therapy outpatient clinics of the Jundishapur university in Ahvaz, Iran between October 2021 and April 2022. Inclusion criteria for patients were; diagnosed as nonspecific neck (neck pain without specific underlying pathology) [21], a report of neck pain for at least three months, age range between 18 and 50 years old, and a score of higher than 10 out of 100 on the Neck Disability Index (NDI) at the time of testing [6,7]. Twenty-two healthy individuals were matched with the patients according to sex, age, height, and weight. Exclusion criteria for two groups were: a previous history of spinal surgery, traumatic neck injury, cervical radiculopathy, dizziness, lower extremity injuries, diabetes, neurological disorders, rheumatology or chronic respiratory conditions, cognitive impairments, musculoskeletal problems that could interfere with gait, use of medications that interfere on the gait performance, and uncorrected visual or hearing impairments [15,22].

Procedure

A 10-m walk test over the ground was used to determine self-selected walking speed for treadmill walking conditions [23]. Participants were instructed to walked at a comfortable speed along a 10-meter walkway for three trials. Self-selected walking speed was calculated by dividing the distance walked in each trial (ten meters) by the average time of three trials. After determining the speed during the overground test, it was set on the treadmill again. They walked for 65 s at the speed calculated from the overground test, and then the speed was modified by increasing or decreasing it by 0.1 km/h every 15 s until they reported their preferred walking speed.

For familiarization with each condition, the subjects were given 30 s to adjust to it. During this time, they walked barefoot on the treadmill. Data collection began after this initial period and continued for 3 min. If they needed to stop the treadmill at any time, we did it. They were instructed to walk under two bases of support (BOS) conditions (normal and tandem) with and without cognitive task on a motorized treadmill (Biometrix TM, length = 1.5 m, width = 0.5 m). A harness that hung from the ceiling was attached around the waist of each participant to provide safety during treadmill walking. Four conditions were completed: (1) single task, normal BOS, (2) dual-task, normal BOS, (3) single task, tandem, and (4) dual-task, tandem. These conditions were presented in random order, with a rest period of 5 min between them to avoid fatigue. In normal BOS conditions, participants walked at their self-selected walking speed on the treadmill. In tandem conditions, the narrow path was outlined by tape on the walking surface of the treadmill, and the participants were asked to walk inside the marked path. The path width was determined at 50% of the distance between the participant’s anterior superior iliac spines [24]. In the dual-task conditions, participants concurrently performed a continuous cognitive task. Before each dual-task trial, participants were asked to track a specific single digit in the number sequence and mentally count and sum the occurrence of this digit (e.g., count and sum the number of 3s in the following series 337, 145, 326, etc.) [25], Then they walked while listening to a series of 3 digits through headphones. Numbers were presented at 2-seconds intervals, and participants answered accurately at the end of each trial. We applied different sequences to decrease the memorization, and the requested digit was varied for each condition. In addition, participants were instructed not to use their fingers to count or count aloud. If they lost track during the continuous tasks, the trial was discarded and repeated. They received no comments about prioritization of gait or cognitive tasks. The cognitive task also was performed in a sitting position on a chair to determine baseline cognitive performance. Study procedure has been described in Fig. 1. An eight- cameras motion capture system (Vero 2.2, Vicon, Oxford metrics. Ltd., Oxford, United Kingdom) was used to measure spatiotemporal gait parameters during four assessed conditions. Retro-reflective markers were placed for each subject according to the Plug-in-Gait lower body (Bodybuilder, Plug in Gait model, Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK) (Fig. 2).

Data analysis

The analysis was performed offline using a custom-written MATLAB program (Math Works Inc.). Gait spatiotemporal variables were determined using the kinematics of heel and toe markers: Heel strikes and toe-offs were detected based on the anterior –posterior velocity of the heel markers [26], and confirmed visually for each trial. In each walking trial, gait variables were calculated for the first 70 successive gait cycles. Gait spatiotemporal parameters were: cadence (steps/min), mean of stride length (anterior-posterior traveled distance between ipsilateral heel markers of two consecutive cycles), mean of stride velocity (stride length / stride time), and variability of stride length and stride velocity [27]. Variability of stride length and stride velocity described by the coefficient of variation (standard deviation/mean × 100) [28]. Dual-task cost (DTC) was calculated with fallowing formula (Single-task - Dual-task/Single-task * 100) [29]. One participant in the neck pain group was excluded due to incomplete gait data (missing marker trajectories), resulting in final samples of n = 21 (neck pain) and n = 22 (controls).

Statistical analysis

The normal distribution of data was determined using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Independent sample t-test and Chi-square test were performed to compare the demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups. For each gait parameter, Paired sample t-test showed no difference between two limbs. Two (group: neck pain vs. healthy controls) × 2 (BOS: normal vs. tandem) × 2 (cognitive loading: single task vs. dual-task) mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the effects of BOS and dual-task on spatiotemporal gait parameters in two groups. Cognitive performance was analyzed using a 2 (group: neck pain vs. healthy controls) × 3 (BOS: sitting vs. normal vs. tandem) mixed model of ANOVA. Bonferroni post hoc test was performed for multiple comparisons of groups difference. The effect size was measured by Partial eta squared. Values of 0.009, 0.058, and 0.137 were respectively considered small, medium and large effects [30]. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL.), and P < .05 was considered for significance. The sample size of this study was obtained using G*Power, Version 3.0.10 (Franz Faul et al. University at Kiel, Germany). The sample size of 36 subjects (18 per group) was calculated, assuming the effect size of 0.25, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.90.

Gait performance

DTC significantly was greater for people with neck pain in cadence (p < .01, ηp2=0.45), stride length (p < .01, ηp2=0.67) and stride velocity (p < .01, ηp2=0.96) compared to control group; however, there was no significant interaction between group and cognitive loading, and both groups walked with a shorter stride length, and lower stride length variability under the dual-task than in the single task conditions (p<.01, ηp2 ≥ 0.10) (Tables 2 and 3). There was significant interaction group by BOS for cadence. People with chronic neck pain group had lower cadence in compared to control group in tandem walking condition with a large effect size (p = .03, ηp2 = 0.10) (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

The aim of present study was to compare the gait characteristics under cognitive demand and limiting BOS between healthy individuals and individuals with neck pain. In cognitive dual-task walking, neck pain group had higher DTS compared controls but two groups had similar pattern. All participants showed shorter stride lengths and decreased stride variability under cognitive dual-task walking compared to single-task conditions. Decreased stride variability means they froze motor degrees of freedom to walk with enhanced stability. It could be explained by hypotheses describing dual-tasking effects such as the posture first principle in which people prioritize posture regulation to avoid falling under cognitive dual-task walking [31]. Also, drawing away attentional resources from walking toward performing a simultaneous cognitive task is another reason for the recruitment of a more automatic gait patterns [32].

People with neck pain had greater DTC in cadence, stride length, and stride velocity compared to controls. It demonstrated that they maintained gait balance control to a greater degree and recruit more cortical area than healthy subjects while cognitive dual-task walking. Higher DTC may be described by a lowered state of preparation (less increase in neural activation levels of the involved cortico-striatal loops) due to competition for attentional resources [33]. Our data suggest that during dual-task gait conditions, there are reduced attentional resources for the secondary task which might increase reaction times or potential for falling. However, pattern gait was not different between the two groups, and to better understand the dual-task-related gait changes in people with neck pain, further research is required to examine the dual-task gait in more challenging cognitive tasks.

In the current study, the addition of an attention-demanding secondary task had no interference effect on gait and cognitive performance in the people with neck pain compared to controls. Similar findings were reported by Sremakaew et al. to investigate the effect of cognitive dual-task walking on gait speed using a timed 10-m walk test in individuals with chronic idiopathic neck pain. They found that an attention-demanding task did not influence gait performance in individuals with chronic idiopathic neck pain compared to controls [17]. Several possible reasons might explain no significant difference in dual-task walking between the two groups in the current study; participants in the neck pain group had a low level of disability (24 ± 6.13 on the NDI); thus, the cognitive and motor performance of patients in this study might not be long enough impaired to make a different pattern in the dual-task condition in comparison with healthy controls. They probably had the ability to compensate for neck proprioception deficit by engaging visual input or somatosensory inputs from the lower extremities for maintaining postural control during walking. In contrast to our study, Kirmizi et al. demonstrated poorer balance under the dual-task condition (counting backward in quiet standing) in women with chronic neck pain. This result could be due to the severity of symptoms in their neck pain group that 81.8% of them had a moderate disability, and 13.6% had severe disability on the NDI [34]. Second, in the present study, participants walked on the treadmill where speed is fixed, mechanically controlled, and not needed on brain cortex control. It is almost controlled by automatic processes, so freeing up more attentional resources for cognitive performance. Wrightson et al. showed that healthy adults performed the cognitive task without detrimental changes to gait in dual-task testing on the treadmill [35]. Thus it might over ground walking, be more sensitive to detect dual-task interference.

Additional complexity in walking, such as tandem walking, resulted in a substantially lower cadence in the chronic neck pain group than healthy adults. Decreased cadence can be a conservative strategy adopted by the neck pain group in tandem walking. The tandem walk is a challenge to the postural control system due to decreased in medio-lateral stability. They take fewer steps per minute to increase gait stability and prevent falling. This may be attributed to disturbances of postural control associated with chronic neck pain [36]. Abnormal afferent input from the cervical region convergent with normal sensory inputs from the vestibular and visual systems within the central nervous system may lead to a sensory mismatch that could result in postural instability [37]. In line with our results, previous studies showed that people with chronic neck pain had disturbances of postural control in tandem standing compared to asymptomatic controls [15, 16]. Sremakaew et al. reported that individuals with chronic idiopathic neck pain had slower gait speed during the tandem walk than controls [17]. Also, Kirmizi et al. reported a lower cadence in individuals with chronic idiopathic neck pain compared to healthy adults in preferred walking, walking with head rotation, and walking at maximum speed on 16 m over the ground [38]. In contrast with our study, Falla et al., Uthaikhup et al., and Pool et al., found no difference in cadence between the neck pain group and controls [5–7]. It seems, walking in more challenging conditions such as tandem walking can reveal difference in cadence between people with chronic neck pain and controls [38].

This study had some limitations. The participants in the neck pain group had low levels of disability; thus, it could not show a different pattern of cognitive dual-tasking from the control group. It suggests to future studies should examine the dual-task effects in people with the higher disability. Also, in this study, subjects walked on the treadmill and using a treadmill to assess gait may not fully represent real-world conditions. The treadmill maintained a constant speed, which could artificially alter the natural variability and attention needs of walking [39]. We suggest that future research considers the effect of cognitive dual-task walking on the ground and during more challenging gait conditions under visual or surface perturbations in people with neck pain. In addition, we evaluated the spatiotemporal parameters of gait, it suggests the identifying of the gait pattern using electromyography activity of the lower limbs and trunk muscles, cervical and trunk kinematics, and real-time brain activity during dual-task conditions in subjects with chronic neck pain.

In conclusion, Simultaneous performance of cognitive task while walking results in greater DTC in gait performance of people with neck pain compared to controls. It might be related to the increased cost of involvement of attentional resource in neck pain group whilst walking; however, both groups showed similar pattern in dual-task condition. People with chronic neck pain group had lower cadence compared to control group in tandem walking condition. Clinically, these results suggest that both terrain complexity and cognitive load should be considered in gait assessment and rehabilitation for individuals with neck pain. Conventional gait evaluations on flat may fail to fully capture the challenges encountered in everyday environments. Clinicians are advised to incorporate assessments involving narrow-based walking and cognitive dual-tasking into their evaluation protocols. In rehabilitation, a progressive training approach could be used, starting with simple walking and advancing to more complex conditions that integrate cognitive challenges while walking. This targeted training may enhance adaptability, improve functional mobility, and balance control for individuals with neck pain.
