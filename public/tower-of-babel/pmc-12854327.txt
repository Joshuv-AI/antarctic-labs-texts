Understanding the psychological impact of body weight variations: A study of weight-related body image concerns among young adults
Authors: Wareesha Anwar, Piyush Ranjan, Anita Malhotra, Anshul Kandpal, Ashish Datt Upadhyay, Gauri Shanker Kaloiya, Archana Kumari, Naval Kishore Vikram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Weight-related body image concerns significantly impact mental health and weight management in young adults (YAs) and are shaped by cultural, societal, and digital factors. Despite the prevalence of these issues, few studies have explored them in this demographic. This study utilizes a validated tool to examine key aspects of weight-related body image concerns in YAs.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional analytical survey was conducted using a pre-validated tool, in the outpatient settings of a tertiary healthcare in India. Snowball and purposive sampling techniques were employed, and data were analyzed using STATA/SE version 14.2.RESULTS:A total of 1071 YAs (mean age: 22.3 ± 3.5 years) were selected, mostly from middle-income groups (61.9%) and students (63.6%), with a slight female predominance (57.1%). The prevalence of obesity was 25.2%, underweight was 11.5%, and moderate to severe body concerns were present in half of the obese (49.6%) and underweight participants (47.1%), with severe issues highest in obese YAs. Regression analysis showed increased odds of body image issues in the underweight (OR: 1.89 [1.29–2.76], P < 0.001) and obese categories (OR: 2.89 [2.18–3.83], P < 0.001). Subdomain analysis revealed that self-consciousness (P < 0.001) and lack of confidence (P < 0.001) were highest in obese YAs, while anxiety (P < 0.001), loneliness (P < 0.01), and embarrassment (P < 0.001) were highest in the underweight group.CONCLUSION:The study highlights the high prevalence of weight-related body image concerns in YAs, with underweight, overweight, and obese individuals experiencing significant distress. The findings emphasize the need for culturally tailored interventions to improve mental health in YAs with obesity.

Introduction

Body weight plays a crucial role in shaping the self-perception and mental well-being of young adults (YAs).[1] The transition to adulthood, spanning the late teens to mid-twenties, is a critical developmental period marked by significant physical and psychosocial changes.[2] Among these changes, body image concerns are particularly prevalent, driven by societal norms that frequently dictate ideals of thinness or muscularity.[3] The influence of social media and digitalization further increases these pressures, as curated images and unrealistic beauty standards are continuously propagated.[4] These platforms not only increase exposure but also amplify the desire to attain idealized body types, leading to dissatisfaction with one’s own body and heightened anxiety about weight and appearance.[5]

The physical and psychological impact of these concerns is profound, often resulting in poor weight management practices, disordered eating behaviors, low self-esteem, and other mental health issues.[6] Studies have highlighted the burden and consequences of these concerns on mental health, interpersonal relationships, and academic performance.[789] Additionally, studies have found that an increase in self-esteem has positive effects on maintaining a healthy weight in YAs.[1011] This proposes the important role that self-esteem and body image issues play in orientation toward healthy weight management practices. As such, it warrants the exploration of mitigation of psychological issues that may exist with overweight or obesity.

However, despite the increasing recognition of body image as crucial to overall health, there is a lack of data on weight-related body image concerns in young YAs. These concerns can vary significantly across different geographical regions, each with its unique sociocultural values.[9111213] Additionally, existing studies on this topic often lack comprehensiveness and frequently do not employ validated tools.

This observational study seeks to fill this gap by exploring the psychological effects of body weight changes among YAs in India, particularly focusing on how these changes influence body image concerns. By examining the relationship between weight fluctuations and subsequent body image changes, this study aims to provide valuable insights into the mental health challenges faced by YAs as they navigate body image issues within a culturally and demographically specific context.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The study was conducted from October 2023 to December 2023 at the outpatient department (OPD) of the Department of Medicine in a leading teaching medical institution in India. The study is a part of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) project (Project Code: I-1466). The institution serves a diverse patient population, ensuring a varied and representative sample. A conceptual framework was developed to guide the study, as shown in Figure 1. This framework highlights the psychological impact of body weight variations through mediating factors and their influence on mental health outcomes.

Study participants and sampling

Participants were selected through purposive sampling, guided by a well-defined sampling frame to ensure representation across diverse characteristics, including age, gender, education level, and socioeconomic status. This approach allowed for the intentional inclusion of individuals with varied experiences and perspectives, enhancing the depth and relevance of the study findings. Data were collected at a single time point using a validated, structured questionnaire. Eligible participants were YAs aged 18–30 years who could comprehend Hindi or English. Exclusion criteria included individuals with end-stage diseases, pregnant women, and those with diagnosed psychiatric illnesses. Participants were first approached directly, followed by a briefing session, and then enrolled after obtaining informed consent. Eligible participants visiting the OPD for any medical reason were invited to participate. Snowball sampling was also employed to include additional participants who met the inclusion criteria.

Data collection tools and techniques

Anthropometric measurements such as height, weight, and waist circumference were recorded using a stadiometer, weighing scale, and non-stretchable measuring tape, following standard procedures. Body image concerns were assessed using a previously validated scale with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.867 and good model fit indices in confirmatory factor analysis (RMSEA = 0.046, CFI = 0.965, TLI = 0.942, SRMR = 0.050). The scale consisted of six items evaluating aspects such as consciousness about body weight, perceived judgment, anxiety, isolation, loneliness, and embarrassment. Responses were scored on a 3-point Likert scale (0 = Never, 1 = Sometimes, and 2 = Often), with total scores ranging from 0 to 12. Scores were categorized as follows: 0 (no body image concerns), 1–4 (mild concerns), 5–8 (moderate concerns), and 9–12 (severe concerns). The survey took approximately 5 minutes to complete.[14] The independent variables included body mass index, the primary dependent variable was manifestation of body image concerns. The sample size was calculated using a single proportion formula, with an estimated proportion (p) of 50% and a margin of error (E) of 3%. A 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) was used, resulting in a required sample size of 1068 participants.[15]

Ethical consideration

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee (IEC reference no. IEC-798/07.10.2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study.

Statistical analysis methods

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to present demographic characteristics and categorical variables. Continuous data were expressed as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Chi-square tests assessed associations between categorical variables, while multinomial regression analysis examined the relationship between weight categories and body image concerns. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA/SE version 14.2 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA), with a significance level of P ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1100 YAs meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were surveyed for the study. After data purification, 29 incomplete entries were excluded. The final data analysis was conducted on 1071 participants with a mean age of 22.3 ± 3.5 years.

Demographic characteristics

There was nearly equal representation of both genders, with a slight preponderance of females (57.1%). More than half of the participants (61.9%) belonged to the middle-income group, followed by approximately one-fourth (24.2%) in the low-income group. Nearly two-thirds of the participants were students (63.6%), with almost half (44.7%) having attained a graduation degree. One in four participants were obese (25.2%), and one in ten were underweight (11.5%). The detailed demographic characteristics are given in Table 1.

More than half (55.8%) of participants frequently felt conscious about their body weight, with one-third (33.2%) reporting a lack of confidence. Nearly one-third (37.5%) felt judged by others, while one-fourth (24.5%) often experienced anxiety about their weight. Although less common, feelings of isolation and loneliness affected about one-sixth (17.5%) of participants, and nearly one-fourth (22.6%) reported some level of embarrassment due to their body weight.

Obese YAs were notably more self-conscious compared to those who were underweight, normal weight, or overweight (χ2 = 34.63, P < 0.001). Lack of confidence was also most prevalent among the obese, followed by the underweight group (χ2 = 45.32, P < 0.001). Perceived judgment was highest among the underweight, followed by obese and overweight individuals (χ2 = 74.11, P < 0.001). Similar trends were observed for anxiety, isolation, loneliness, and embarrassment, with these concerns being more severe in underweight individuals.

Further analysis using multinomial regression indicated that self-consciousness was significantly associated with obesity (β = 0.79, P < 0.001), while lack of confidence was most severe in underweight (β = −0.71, P < 0.001) and moderately severe in obese participants (β = −0.87, P < 0.001). Perceived judgment was significantly severe across all weight categories, particularly for the underweight (β = −1.45, P < 0.001) and overweight (β = −0.89, P < 0.001). Anxiety was most significant in underweight individuals (β = −0.75, P < 0.001), and feelings of isolation, loneliness, and embarrassment were notably higher in the underweight (β = −1.08, P < 0.001; β = −1.16, P < 0.001) and overweight groups (β = −0.60, P < 0.05).

Discussion

This study comprehensively examines weight-related body image concerns among YAs, highlighting the complex relationship between body weight and psychological well-being. Our findings highlight the impact of body image disturbances—including self-consciousness, lack of confidence, perceived judgment, anxiety, isolation, loneliness, and embarrassment—across all bodyweight categories.

While body weight is a well-established factor influencing psychological well-being in YAs,[11617] our study offers a more nuanced perspective by identifying specific domains affected by body image concerns. Notably, body image distress is not exclusive to individuals with higher body weight; underweight individuals also report significant concerns. This aligns with Mohamed and Idrees,[18] who found similar patterns among medical students (n = 277). Similarly, Divecha et al.[19] observed body dissatisfaction and distorted perceptions in Omani medical students, even among those with low BMI. These findings challenge the conventional association of body dissatisfaction primarily with overweight and obese individuals. Additionally, our study revealed that many individuals within the normal weight range expressed a desire for weight reduction, reflecting the complexity of body image concerns.[20]

Body image concerns affect psychological well-being differently across weight categories. Self-consciousness was most pronounced in individuals with obesity, indicating heightened awareness of appearance in social settings.[10] In contrast, lack of confidence was most prevalent among underweight individuals, suggesting a link between low body weight and self-esteem issues.[21] Interestingly, confidence issues were also observed, though to a lesser extent, among obese individuals. A large-scale study (Robert et al.[22]) on 29,735 participants further supports this, demonstrating lower self-esteem at both extremes of the weight spectrum.

Perceived judgment was a common concern across all weight categories, with underweight and overweight individuals experiencing it most intensely. While past research has largely associated perceived judgment with obesity,[82324] our findings indicate that underweight individuals also experience significant scrutiny. Anxiety was particularly high in underweight individuals, suggesting greater social insecurity, whereas isolation, loneliness, and embarrassment were prevalent in both underweight and overweight groups. Studies have consistently linked body dissatisfaction and loneliness with younger age cohorts.[252627]

These findings align with theoretical frameworks such as Bronfenbrenner’s Socioecological Theory and Objectification Theory.[2829] The Socioecological Theory suggests that societal norms shape body image, as evidenced by participants’ high levels of perceived judgment and anxiety. The Objectification Theory posits that individuals internalize an observer’s perspective on their bodies, contributing to self-consciousness and a lack of confidence. Such experiences can also lead to physical health concerns, including disordered eating, poor self-regulation, and impaired social relationships.

Despite the strong association between body weight extremes and body image concerns, some studies suggest a more complex relationship. For example, individuals with higher BMI may develop resilience against body dissatisfaction, especially within supportive social environments. Similarly, subcultures embracing diverse body types may buffer against negative body image perceptions.[830] Our study did not explore protective factors such as body positivity movements or familial support, which may mitigate body image distress. Additionally, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases, as responses may be influenced by transient emotions or social desirability. Longitudinal research could provide deeper insights into the evolution of body image concerns over time.

Addressing these issues requires a holistic approach that challenges weight biases and promotes body acceptance. Weight-management interventions should prioritize psychological well-being, encouraging healthy behaviors and positive body image. Programs that build self-esteem, foster body positivity, and address societal stigma can help mitigate body image concerns. Healthcare providers and policymakers must create supportive, stigma-free environments, while educational institutions should integrate body positivity and mental health awareness into curricula. Community-based programs should support individuals in managing their weight while improving their body image perceptions.

Future research should explore the underlying mechanisms driving body image concerns in underweight individuals, examining the roles of social media, peer influence, and cultural factors. Qualitative studies could offer deeper insights into personal experiences and coping strategies. Assessing body image disturbances in adolescent and older populations would provide a longitudinal perspective on how these concerns develop across the lifespan.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size and nearly equal gender representation, enhancing its generalizability to YA populations. It is also one of the first studies on South Asian populations using an indigenously developed scale. Our findings offer novel insights, demonstrating that body image concerns extend beyond overweight and obesity to underweight and normal-weight individuals. This research lays a foundation for future studies on weight-related stigma and the psychological impact of body weight.

However, certain limitations should be noted. The non-randomized sampling method may limit generalizability, and self-reported data introduce the possibility of recall and social desirability biases. Future studies should include diverse socioeconomic and educational backgrounds to enhance applicability.

Conclusion

This study highlights the pressing need for comprehensive health policies addressing body image concerns among YAs across the weight spectrum. Current interventions often focus on obesity prevention and management, but our findings highlight the psychological burden faced by underweight individuals as well. Aligning with health policy benchmarks such as the WHO’s Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030, policies should integrate mental health support into weight management programs to promote positive body image and psychological well-being. The policies should be focused on addressing all socioeconomic and weight categories, reducing stigma, and fostering inclusive health promotion strategies. Educational institutions and healthcare systems should incorporate body image literacy and resilience-building programs. By embedding these, policymakers can create a more inclusive, stigma-free healthcare environment that supports both physical and mental well-being in YAs.
