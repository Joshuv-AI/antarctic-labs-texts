Evolution of perceived social support and its association with psychological distress among middle-aged and young-old Japanese: a longitudinal study
Authors: Takashi Oshio, Kemmyo Sugiyama, Toyo Ashida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerceived social support (SS) is a key determinant of successful aging; however, its evolution with age and dynamic association with psychological distress (PD) are largely unexplored.MethodsWe used longitudinal data from nine-wave social surveys conducted in Japan from 2013 to 2022 to track 181,126 observations from 22,419 individuals born between 1946 and 1955. We divided perceived SS into support from family members and from others, and defined PD as a Kessler 6 score (range: 0–24) ≥ 13. We conducted systematic equation modeling to examine how the evolution of SS is associated with PD after controlling for time-invariant individual attributes.ResultsThe prevalence of PD among those who received SS was 2.6% for men and 3.7% for women, which was considerably lower than the 6.5% and 11.4% among those who did not receive SS, respectively. Individuals tended to lose support from family members as they aged but received more support from others, thereby largely sustaining the overall SS. Of the adverse effects of losing support from family members on PD, 53.1% (standard error [SE]: 9.6%) and 55.8% (SE: 6.4%) were moderated by support from others among men and women, respectively.ConclusionThese results suggest that most middle-aged and young-old Japanese individuals respond smoothly to declining support from family members by increasing their reliance on support from others.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25826-y.Keywords: Japan, Perceived social support, Psychological distress, Systematic equation modeling

Background

Perceived social support (SS), which buffers stress [1, 2], is a key determinant of successful aging, as shown in previous surveys [3–5]. Empirical studies consistently demonstrate a positive association between greater SS and improved mental health outcomes [6–13]. However, the sources of SS vary across life stages [3, 8, 11]. Adults and older adults typically rely first on the spouse, followed by other family members, and then neighbors, friends, or workplace colleagues.

Compared with Western countries, family support has a stronger influence on mental health among community-dwelling older adults in Asia [14, 15]. Whereas studies in Western countries emphasize the role of spouses and friends, older adults in Asian countries tend to be more sensitive to family support. Reflecting the tradition of filial piety, older adults often depend on children and other family members for care, making family support a central determinant of mental health in many Asian countries including Japan, especially its rural area.

Recent research has focused on the dynamics of SS and its impact on mental health in aging populations. With age, individuals face potential losses in family support, particularly because of spousal loss. Children moving out may also lead to changes in support received, even if it is not perceived as lost, since support can continue via phone, text, or email. Perceived social relations influence older women’s mental health after spousal loss [16] and highlight the importance of support from others as an important role. These findings suggest that psychological well-being in later life may depend on how effectively perceived SS shifts from family to non-family sources [18, 19].

The sustainability of perceived SS among middle-aged and younger adults, a focus of this study, is consistent with socioemotional selectivity theory [20, 21]. This theory proposes that as people age, they become less interested in forming new relationships and instead concentrate on those that are emotionally meaningful. Guided by this principle, older adults tend to devote their limited time and energy to social partners and interactions likely to be rewarding, even amid role changes and social losses. Understanding how perceived SS evolves with age can therefore provide important insights into psychological well-being.

In the present study, we analyzed the evolution of perceived SS with age and its dynamic association with mental health, measured as psychological distress (PD), using nationwide, population-based longitudinal data from middle-aged and young-old Japanese adults. This study has two distinguishing features that provide new insights into the determinants of successful aging.

First, we conducted a fixed-effects analysis controlling for time-invariant individual attributes, fully leveraging the longitudinal dataset. Most previous studies relied on cross-sectional data or only a few follow-ups to examine the association between perceived SS and mental health, whereas long-wave longitudinal analyses remain scarce [7, 8, 11]. Without adequate control for individual-level fixed effects, estimates may be biased. Perceived SS has been shown to relate more consistently to mental health than received or instrumental SS [2, 11, 13]; however, this relationship could be influenced by personality traits or other innate characteristics affecting both perceived SS and mental health.

Second, we examined associations between two types of perceived SS—support from family members and support from others—and PD using a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework. Drawing on prior studies [17–19], we explored how support from others may respond to a loss of family support and the extent to which enhanced support from others can moderate its impact. The results of this moderation analysis are expected to clarify how individuals adjust to life events by reshaping their social relationships later in life.

Finally, we compared results by sex. Women generally have larger and more diverse social networks and place greater emphasis on social connections than do men [22, 23]. They are also more likely to provide and receive emotional support from multiple social ties, including family and friends, during times of stress [24]. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the evolution of SS with age and its association with PD may have stronger implications for the psychological well-being of women, consistent with previous findings [25].

Methods

Study sample

This study used data from the nationwide panel survey, “The Longitudinal Survey of Middle-aged and Older Adults,” conducted annually by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) since 2005. Under Japan’s Statistics Law, surveys are reviewed for statistical, legal, and ethical compliance. The MHLW provided official permission for the use of these survey data; thus, additional ethical approval was not required. The survey initially enrolled a cohort aged 50–59 years (born between 1946 and 1955), with 34,240 respondents in the first wave (response rate: 83.8%). The second to seventeenth waves of the survey were conducted in early November of each year from 2006 to 2022, with no new participants added. Because perceived SS was measured in the 2013 survey (wave 9), we used nine waves of longitudinal data from 2013 to 2022, comprising 181,126 observations from 22,419 individuals (10,457 men and 11,962 women) aged 58–75 years.

Measures

Perceived social support (SS)

Participants were asked, “Do you have any person on whom you usually rely” by choosing from among (a) co-residing family members, (b) non-co-residing family members, (c) neighbors, (d) workplace colleagues (including former), (e) friends, (f) others, and (g) none. Multiple answers were allowed. Participants were grouped into three categories: (1) support from family members (selected a or b, including those also receiving support from others); (2) support only from others (selected at least one of c–f but not a or b), and (3) no support (selected g).

Psychological distress (PD)

PD was assessed using the Kessler 6 (K6) scale [26, 27], validated in Japanese populations [28, 29]. The respondents were asked to answer a six-item PD questionnaire, “During the past 30 days, about how often did you feel a) nervous, b) hopeless, c) restless or fidgety, d) severely depressed, nothing could cheer you up, e) that everything was an effort, and f) worthless?”, rated on a 5-point scale (0 = none of the time to 4 = all of the time). Furthermore, the sum of the reported scores (range: 0–24) was calculated and defined as the K6 score. Higher K6 scores reflected higher PD levels. The Cronbach’s alpha for the entire study sample was 0.869. Following previous validation studies, a K6 score >13 indicated severe mental illness [27, 29].

Covariates

Covariates included age and its squared value, marital status, limitations in activities of daily living (ADL), employment status, educational attainment (junior high school, high school, junior college, college or above, others, and unanswered), and household spending. Household spending, used as a proxy for income, was adjusted for household size by dividing by the square root of household members [30], categorized into quartiles, and coded as binary variables for each quartile and the unanswered. Educational attainment was excluded from fixed-effects regressions as it is predetermined before the survey years and is invariant.

Statistical analysis

For descriptive analysis, we first summarized the prevalence of perceived SS and compared the prevalence of PD between respondents with and without perceived SS using pooled cross-sectional data. Subsequently, we graphically observed the evolution of perceived SS with age.

For the regression analysis, we first compared the regression results between the pooled ordinary least squares (OLS) model, which used pooled cross-sectional data, and the fixed-effects model, which used longitudinal data, to examine the association between perceived SS and PD after adjusting for a set of covariates. This analysis examines the extent to which time-invariant individual attributes distort the observed association.

Second, we conducted the SEM, which incorporated relationships across support from family members, support from others only, and PD. In this framework, fixed effects at the individual level were controlled for by expressing all variables as deviations from individual-specific means. After conducting SEM, we estimated the magnitudes, along with their standard errors (SEs), of (i) the direct impact of losing support from family members, (ii) the impact moderated by support from others, and (iii) % proportion of (ii) out of (i), which are expressed by –α1, α2β, and α2β/α1 × 100, respectively.

We conducted these analyses separately for men and women, considering possible sex-based differences in the relationship between SS and PD later in life, as suggested in a previous study. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software package (Release 17; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

The trajectory of perceived SS with age (58–75 years) is shown in Fig. 2. Among women, the proportion receiving any SS remained stable over 17 years. In men, it increased after age 70 years. Discussion

Using nine-wave longitudinal data from social surveys conducted in Japan between 2015 and 2022, we examined how changes in perceived SS with age were associated with changes in PD, controlling for time-invariant individual attributes.

First, the observed association between perceived SS and PD was substantially reduced in the fixed-effects model. This suggests that the favorable impact of perceived SS on psychological well-being tends to be overestimated in cross-sectional or prospective follow-up studies that do not fully control for time-invariant individual attributes. Nevertheless, perceived SS maintained a consistently favorable effect on PD, even after controlling for these attributes. In addition, the effect size of perceived SS was substantial, considering that the risk of PD was reduced by 1.1% and 2.5% by SS, compared with 2.9% and 3.9% for the entire sample for men and women, respectively. These results underscore the stress-buffering effects of perceived SS, consistent with prior empirical studies [6–13].

Second, the loss of support from family members was largely compensated by enhanced support from others. Individuals tend to lose family support with age, particularly because of spousal losses. In response, the probability of receiving support from others increased substantially for both sexes. The mechanism behind this shift is unclear—it may reflect respondents actively seeking support, greater psychological reliance on others, or voluntary enhanced support from others recognizing the loss of family support. Regardless of the cause, this shift maintained overall perceived SS, consistent with the socioemotional selectivity theory [20, 21]. This theory argues that older adults tend to devote their time and energy to social partners and interactions likely to be rewarding, even amid the loss of support from family members, presumably explaining the observed stability of SS in later life.

Third, the smooth transition in SS mitigated the adverse impact of losing family support. For both men and women, more than 50% of the negative effect of losing family support was offset by receiving support from others. Sustained overall SS prevents psychological well-being from deteriorating among middle-aged and young individuals. In this sense, the resilience of perceived SS contributes significantly to the sustainability of successful aging, highlighting the importance of a smooth transition from family-based to other sources of support [16–19].

Fourth, sex-related differences were observed in both the evolution of perceived SS and its association with PD. Perceived SS had a larger impact on women, who were more active in replacing lost family support with external support. However, men and women moderated the negative impact of losing family support to a similar extent (slightly above 50%) through reliance on external support. Additionally, men experienced a recovery of perceived support from family members, largely from non-co-residing relatives—a pattern not observed among women. These findings highlight sex-specific differences in the roles of family and external support, consistent with prior studies [16, 25].

This study has some limitations. First, we focused on within-individual variations and did not address between-individual differences in the association between perceived SS and PD. Second, although we controlled for individual-level fixed effects, reverse causation from PD to SS cannot be fully excluded. Third, potential attrition bias was not adjusted for; baseline PD and SS influenced the likelihood of remaining in the survey, potentially affecting observed associations (Table S2). Fourth, the study did not consider public or official support, such as long-term care (LTC) programs. Given the age range studied (58–75 years), only 0.8% of participants were eligible for LTC benefits during the study period. In addition to address these issues due to data limitations, more analysis is needed to examine how individuals would receive external support, when they face a loss of support from family members. Their responses are likely to be affected by various factors including their socioeconomic status, health conditions, and social relationships.

Conclusions

The majority of middle-aged and young-old Japanese individuals can respond smoothly to declining family support by increasing reliance on support from others. However, a considerable number of individuals either lack any SS or cannot adapt to its changes with age, indicating a continued need for policy intervention. Policies that enhance community social interactions and support middle-aged and older adults in maintaining overall SS are recommended.
