Multi-source biological knowledge-guided hypergraph spatiotemporal subnetwork embedding for protein complex identification
Authors: Shilong Wang, Hai Cui, Yanchen Qu, Yijia Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentifying biologically significant protein complexes from protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks and understanding their roles are essential for elucidating protein functions, life processes, and disease mechanisms. Current methods typically rely on static PPI networks and model PPI data as pairwise relationships, which presents several limitations. Firstly, static PPI networks do not adequately represent the scopes and temporal dynamics of protein interactions. Secondly, a large amount of available biological resources have not been fully integrated. Moreover, PPIs in biological systems are not merely one-to-one relationships but involve higher order non-pairwise interactions. To alleviate these issues, we propose HGST, a multi-source biological knowledge-guided hypergraph spatiotemporal subnetwork (subnet) embedding method for identifying biologically significant protein complexes from PPI networks. HGST initially constructs spatiotemporal PPI subnets using the scopes and temporal dynamics of proteins derived from multi-source biological knowledge, treating them as dynamic networks through fine-grained spatiotemporal partitioning. The spatiotemporal subnets are then transformed into hypergraphs, which model higher order non-pairwise relationships via hypergraph embedding. Simultaneously, fine-grained amino acid sequence features and coarse-grained gene ontology attributes are introduced for multi-dimensional feature fusion. Finally, protein complexes are identified from the reweighted subnets based on fused feature representations using the core-attachment strategy. Evaluations on four real PPI datasets demonstrate that HGST achieves competitive performance. Furthermore, a series of biological analyses confirm the high biological significance of the complexes identified by HGST. The source code is available at https://github.com/qifen37/HGST.

Introduction

Proteins are fundamental substances underlying the life activities of all living organisms, playing a crucial role in various biological processes (BPs). Typically, proteins do not function in isolation within cells; instead, they form protein complexes through interactions with one another, participating in processes such as messenger RNA (mRNA) translation, protein degradation, and signal transduction in the form of these complexes [1]. The rapid advancement of high-throughput technologies in recent years has generated a large amount of available protein–protein interaction (PPI) data, providing rich research resources for understanding various forms of PPIs and their effects in diverse BPs [2]. Moreover, disorders are not usually caused by the malfunction of a single protein but rather by the impairment of specific protein complexes. Researchers have explored the components of protein complexes to discover associations between drugs and diseases, thereby facilitating drug development and disease prevention [3].

Protein complex identification is typically defined as detecting densely connected cliques within protein interaction networks (PINs) [4–7]. Network-based calculation methods have recently emerged as the dominant approach for executing this task [8]. Existing methods for protein complex identification can be broadly categorized into two types: identifying complexes based solely on static PINs [9–12] and combining PINs with additional biological resources [5, 13–15].

Identifying complexes based solely on static PINs. Extensive research on PINs has revealed the presence of densely connected cliques, which often represent groups of functionally similar proteins, i.e. potential protein complexes [16]. MCODE [9] weights the PIN network based on the local density of nodes, selects the highest-weighted protein as the seed node, and expands subgraphs based on node weights to form protein complexes. MCL [10] uses a topological distance metric and a PCA-based consensus clustering technique to build an integrated clustering framework. CMC [11] identifies the largest subgraphs within the PIN and scores each protein node in the subgraph, merging or deleting highly overlapping subgraphs to obtain protein complexes. ClusterOne [12] employs a greedy algorithm to expand protein nodes into highly cohesive cliques from which protein complexes are extracted. However, these methods rely solely on raw PPI data and are significantly affected by noise in the original data while failing to leverage other valuable biological resources.

Combining PINs with additional biological resources. Proteins are synthesized through gene expression, making gene ontology (GO) information [17, 18] a significant factor influencing the identification of protein complexes. Additionally, other biological resources such as gene expression profiles [19] and protein sequences [20] also impact the accuracy of complex identification. COACH [13] identifies core structures based on the average degree of nodes in the PIN and then expands these structures by incorporating nodes with correlated gene expression or functional similarity. GANE [5] reweights the entire PIN based on the GO attributes of proteins and employs clique mining methods to generate candidate cores, based on which the final protein complexes are generated. AdaPPI [14] is an adaptive graph convolutional method that effectively integrates protein GO attributes with network topology, utilizing core cliques and attachment structures to discover protein complexes within the PIN. ST-APIN [15] integrates temporal gene expression data and subcellular location information to construct spatiotemporal PINs. However, ST-APIN fails to fully explore fine-grained cellular location information, as it constructs subnets based only on coarse-grained subcellular locations, neglecting the potential locational data available in the GO.

Despite researchers exploring numerous solutions, several issues remain inadequately addressed. These issues are summarized: (1) PINs are typically modeled as static networks, ignoring dynamic temporal and spatial attributes. In reality, protein expression and complex formation vary over time and across different locations within organisms [21]. Static PINs do not reflect the actual conditions within living cells. (2) Existing methods do not fully integrate the vast amount of biological resources. Thus, relying on only a few types of resources fails to describe PPI data accurately. (3) The associations between proteins in PINs are not simple one-to-one relationships. Current embedding methods neither capture higher order associations between proteins in PINs nor effectively explore the structural information and biological characteristics of PPIs.

To alleviate the issues above, we propose a multi-source biological knowledge-guided hypergraph spatiotemporal subnetwork embedding method (HGST) for identifying valuable protein complexes from PINs. We construct fine-grained spatiotemporal-specific protein interaction subnets by integrating the gene expression profiles and protein localization information. Subsequently, we employ hypergraph embedding to learn non-pairwise higher order interaction relationships, rather than merely modeling pairwise relationships [22]. HGST designs a hypergraph embedding module using a hypergraph convolutional neural network (HGNN) [23] to capture higher order topological relationships within PINs. The amino acid sequence information of proteins is also combined to learn potential representations of protein complexes. An autoencoder is then employed to deeply integrate the GO information of proteins with the latent representations obtained through hypergraph embedding to yield the final protein embedding information. Finally, based on this embedding information, we update the weights of each dynamic spatiotemporal PPI subnet and perform the protein complex identification task. Experimental results on four real PPI datasets demonstrate that HGST effectively identifies protein complexes, achieving competitive performance.

Preliminaries

Biological resources

This study utilizes gene expression profiles of Saccharomyces cerevisiae downloaded from the NCBI GEO database [24], amino acid sequence data obtained from the UniProt database [25], and GO information [17, 18] to aid in protein complex identification. Gene expression profiles encompass gene expression across three consecutive metabolic cycles, and we calculate the average expression values at corresponding time points to derive the final protein activity expression values. Amino acid sequence data are encoded using a widely applied method: the conjoint triad method [26]. GO information encompasses essential biological information resources and is a standardized vocabulary for describing gene functions. It categorizes gene functions into three parts: molecular function (MF), BP, and cellular component (CC). The MF and BP are selected as the protein attribute information. We employ the CC attribute (i.e. the active location of gene products) as a more fine-grained source of protein localization information. Specifically, 24 protein localization categories were determined in this study, as shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Specifically, CC attribute annotations provide information about the primary location of each protein within the cell. This information is experimentally verified or inferred based on known protein functions. Therefore, CC attributes usually enable direct mapping to spatial locations.

Hypergraph learning

As graph theory flourished, hypergraphs began to be applied to graph convolution, and HGNN is the pioneer of hypergraph convolution. Recent studies have found that hypergraphs demonstrate superior performance in learning complicated protein interaction relationships compared with traditional graph learning methods [27]. In this paper, a hypergraph embedding module is designed based on HGNN to model the higher order relationships among PPIs.

Methodology

This paper proposes a multi-source biological knowledge-guided hypergraph spatiotemporal subnetwork embedding method to identify valuable protein complexes from PINs. Amino acid sequence feature encoding

It is well known that amino acid sequences determine protein structure, which determines protein function. Interactions between proteins can be predicted based solely on sequence information [26], which further influences the formation of protein complexes. Therefore, we incorporate amino acid sequences to provide fundamental structural features for protein complex identification.

For amino acid sequences, we apply the conjoint triad method for encoding. This method divides protein sequences into a series of adjacent amino acid triplets. For each protein sequence, the frequencies of various triplets are calculated and used as the feature vector (Fig. 1B). Since there are 20 common amino acids, there are theoretically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $20^{3}=8000$\end{document} possible triplet combinations. To reduce dimensionality and account for synonymous mutations, the conjoint triad method groups the 20 amino acids into seven categories, as shown in the mapping table of Fig. 1B. Thus, the final feature dimension of the amino acid sequence is reduced to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $7^{3}=343$\end{document}.

Table 2 shows that the HGST achieves optimal performance across most evaluation metrics, attaining the highest F1-score on three datasets and the highest Accuracy score on four datasets. This indicates that our method not only better balances Precision and Recall but also identifies a greater number of highly matched protein complexes. As illustrated in Fig. 2, HGST achieves the best combined score on the Biogrid, Krogan14K, and DIP datasets and is slightly lower than AdaPPI on the Collins dataset. Moreover, methods such as MCODE, MCL, CMC, and ClusterOne, which rely solely on static PINs, perform considerably worse than those incorporating additional biological data, such as COACH, GANE, AdaPPI, and our HGST. This suggests that biological resources can provide meaningful support for complex identification methods.

Additionally, we calculated the average performance across the four datasets. Compared with AdaPPI, HGST achieved improvements of 11.37%, 2.67%, and 3.23% in Recall, F1-score, and Accuracy, respectively, but underperformed in Precision. The main reason for the superior Precision of AdaPPI lies in its globally adaptive convolution, which captures node density variations within the graph, enabling more precise identification of complexes from the PPI network. In contrast, our method significantly improved Recall, which can be attributed to the characteristics of the spatiotemporal hypergraph. By representing the PPI network as spatiotemporal subnets and constructing hypergraphs based on these subnets, we enhanced the original PPI data in two key ways. Firstly, certain PPI data exist in multiple subnets, participating in the hypergraph construction process more than once, which expands the original data. Secondly, the hypergraph is constructed based on fully connected subgraphs and incorporates both amino acid sequence information and GO attributes, strengthening the connections between nodes. These factors enable our method to identify a large number of highly matched protein complexes, leading to higher Recall.

We also observe that AdaPPI performs poorly on the Biogrid dataset, whereas our HGST achieves the best overall performance across all metrics. As shown in Table 1, the Biogrid dataset has the largest number of proteins, edges, cliques, and average neighbors, reflecting its highly sophisticated network structure and a higher number of false positives. This complicacy typically has a negative impact on protein complex identification methods. Nevertheless, our approach still attains superior performance, demonstrating its robustness in effectively mitigating the effects of network noise and complicated network structures.

Ablation study

To evaluate the impact of spatiotemporal subnets, hypergraph embeddings, and additional biological resources on HGST’s performance, we designed the following variant methods and conducted ablation studies on the Biogrid, Krogan14K, DIP, and Collins datasets: (1) w/o S: without spatial information, only temporal subnets are constructed. This variant divides the original PPI network into 12 temporal subnets. (2) w/o T: without temporal information, only spatial subnets are constructed based on location information, dividing the original PPI network into 24 spatial subnets. (3) w/o ST: without spatiotemporal information, protein complexes are identified directly in the original PPI network. (4) w/o AAS: without amino acid sequence information, omitting part B in Fig. 1. (5) w/o GO: without MF and BP attributes from GO data, only hypergraph embeddings are used to reweight each subnet. (6) w/o HG: without using the HGNN. In this variant, spatiotemporal subnets are not constructed as hypergraphs, and a graph convolutional network (GCN) [38] is applied directly to learn features. The ablation results are shown in Table 3, with the highest scores highlighted in bold.

When temporal and spatial information is not used to constrain the original PIN, all performance metrics drop significantly, with the F1-score decreasing by 21.53% on the DIP dataset. We attribute this to the finer granularity of the spatial location data, which results in more subnets being created. Consequently, w/o T outperforms both w/o S and w/o ST.

Additionally, the additional biological resources significantly improve the performance of protein complex identification methods. The results of w/o AAS and w/o GO variants in Table 3 indicate that GO attribute information provides slightly better support than amino acid sequence data. Both GO attributes and amino acid sequence information are closely tied to the formation of protein complexes, thus playing a positive role in our approach.

To validate the effectiveness of the hypergraph-based network structure, we applied the GCN directly to the spatiotemporal subnets without constructing them in hypergraph form to learn node embeddings. The results indicate that the performance of the w/o HG variant is significantly lower than that of HGST, demonstrating that the hypergraph structure effectively models the topological properties of PPI networks. Moreover, hypergraph convolution successfully integrates protein sequence information while learning the features of the hypergraph subnets.

Conclusion

We propose a multi-source biological knowledge-guided hypergraph spatiotemporal subnetwork embedding method to identify protein complexes from PINs. First, we use gene expression profiles and the CC attributes from GO to construct spatiotemporal PIN subnets, providing a fine-grained partition of the interaction scopes and active periods of proteins. Furthermore, each subnet is structured as a hypergraph and processed with hypergraph convolution while incorporating protein amino acid sequences to obtain hypergraph embeddings enriched with sequence features. These hypergraph embeddings are then normalized and deeply integrated with MF and BP attributes from GO via an autoencoder, allowing for the effective combination of diverse biological resources. Finally, the original spatiotemporal subnets are reweighted, and the core-attachment strategy is applied to identify protein complexes.

Our method not only refines the interaction scopes and active periods of proteins but also integrates fine-grained amino acid sequence information with coarse-grained GO attributes, offering a comprehensive and multi-perspective approach to discovering valuable protein complexes within the PPI network. It should also be noted that mapping CC attributes to active locations of proteins in this study has certain limitations. For instance, the spatial locations inferred based on known protein functions possibly cause incomplete or inaccurate annotations. Additionally, although GO data have been validated, certain proteins exhibit distinct locations under varying experimental conditions, which possibly are not fully consistent with the locations provided in GO annotations. We aim to further improve and address these limitations in future work. On the other hand, we plan to adopt generative methods and incorporate more biological resources to find more biologically significant and valuable protein complexes from refined spatiotemporal subnets.
