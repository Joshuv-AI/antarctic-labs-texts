Risk factors for chemotherapy-induced dysgeusia in patients receiving dose-dense methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin therapy: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Takehiro Tanaka, Toyohito Oriyama, Takehito Yamamoto, Saori Kimura, Alice Fujii, Riri Tauchi, Kyoko Shirai, Satoru Taguchi, Taketo Kawai, Haruki Kume, Tappei Takada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChemotherapy-induced (CI) dysgeusia is a common adverse event that impairs nutritional status and quality of life; however, its risk factors remain poorly understood. This study aimed to identify risk factors for CI dysgeusia in patients receiving dose-dense methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (dd-MVAC) therapy.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective observational study including patients with urothelial carcinoma who received dd-MVAC therapy between August 2018 and March 2023. CI dysgeusia was defined as grade ≥ 2 dysgeusia according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0. Univariable logistic regression was performed to screen candidate predictors (p < 0.20), followed by multivariable logistic regression with backward stepwise selection. Cutoff values were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Predictive performance was evaluated using positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV).ResultsAmong 103 patients, 32 (31.1%) developed CI dysgeusia. Multivariable analysis identified depression (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 23.3, 95% CI 3.01–529; p = 0.010) and HbA1c ≤ 5.8% (aOR 2.86, 95% CI 1.16–7.30; p = 0.024) as independent risk factors. Depression alone showed good predictive performance (PPV 83.3%; NPV 72.2%) and combining it with HbA1c ≤ 5.8% increased PPV to 100%.ConclusionsDepression and HbA1c ≤ 5.8% were independent risk factors for CI dysgeusia in patients undergoing dd-MVAC therapy. These findings suggest that readily available clinical information may enable early identification of high-risk patients and support individualized supportive care strategies. Keywords: Dysgeusia, Dose dense MVAC, Depression, Risk factor, HbA1c

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced (CI) dysgeusia occurs in 30–85% of patients undergoing chemotherapy [1–3] and is clinically important adverse event (AE) that may lead to malnutrition and impaired quality of life (QOL) [4,5]. Although various approaches, including zinc supplementation, professional oral care, and nutritional support, are currently utilized for the management of CI dysgeusia, no standardized treatment protocol has been established. In addition, several assessment methods for taste dysfunction have been reported, ranging from validated patient-reported outcome measures such as the Chemotherapy-Induced Taste Alteration Scale (CiTAS) [6] to objective psychophysical tests including taste strip tests (e.g., the 5-taste strip test) [7], whole-mouth taste tests [8], and electrogustometry [9]; however, standardized objective diagnostic criteria for CI dysgeusia have not yet been established. Moreover, the timing of onset and severity of symptoms varies widely among patients, which complicates the accurate diagnosis of CI dysgeusia at an early stage. Therefore, identifying the risk factors for CI dysgeusia would facilitate more efficient management and help maintain QOL in patients receiving chemotherapy [10].

Several studies have identified risk factors for CI dysgeusia. Kaizu et al. reported that approximately 60% of patients receiving taxane-based chemotherapy experienced taste alterations and identified prior chemotherapy, dry mouth, and peripheral neuropathy as independent risk factors [11]. Denda et al. reported that age and body surface area (BSA) were associated with increased risk of CI dysgeusia in patients undergoing combination epirubicin and cyclophosphamide therapy for breast cancer [12]. These findings highlight the importance of understanding individual risk factors to improve management of CI dysgeusia.

In addition to treat-related factors for CI dysgeusia, several underlying medical conditions may increase the risk of dysgeusia. A population-based study showed that individuals with depressive symptoms were more likely to experience dysgeusia than those without [13]. In Japan, depression affects approximately 9% of the population, the depressive symptoms may occur in 20–30% of cancer patients [14,15]. Dysgeusia has been reported in approximately 40% of patients with type 2 diabetes and 33% of those with type 1 diabetes, respectively [16]. These findings suggest that depression and diabetes may contribute to the development of CI dysgeusia.

However, few studies have comprehensively evaluated these underlying conditions as potential risk factors for CI dysgeusia.

This study aimed to identify the risk factors for CI dysgeusia in patients with urothelial carcinoma receiving dose-dense methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (dd-MVAC) therapy, which comprises multiple agents known to cause CI dysgeusia. In particular, we focused on the underlying conditions associated with CI dysgeusia.

Materials and methods

Patients and treatments

This single-center, retrospective, observational study included patients who received dd-MVAC therapy for urothelial carcinoma at the University of Tokyo Hospital between August 2018 and March 2023. The dd-MVAC protocol consisted of methotrexate (30 mg/m2 intravenously on day1), doxorubicin or pirarubicin (30 mg/m2 intravenously on day2), vinblastine (3 mg/m2 intravenously on day2) and cisplatin (70 mg/m2 intravenously on day2), and peg filgrastim (3.6 mg subcutaneously on day4). At our institution, initial doses of methotrexate and cisplatin were reduced to 75% of their standard doses in patients with normal renal function, in accordance with our institutional protocol [17]. In the absence of severe AEs during the first cycle, full-dose administration was resumed from the second cycle onward. Dose adjustments were based on creatinine clearance (CCr), estimated using the Cockcroft-Gault formula. Doses of all chemotherapeutic agents were reduced by 20% in the event of severe AEs (grade ≥ 3 non-hematologic toxicities, grade 4 hematologic toxicities, febrile neutropenia, or hemorrhagic thrombocytopenia) [17]. Treatment cycles were repeated every two weeks for a total of six cycles and the timing of the start of subsequent cycles was decided at the discretion of the attending physician based on the severity of AEs and the patient’s general condition [17].

We excluded patients who 1) discontinued treatment during the first course or 2) were rechallenged after completing six cycles. We excluded patients who had been taking zinc preparations before the initiation of dd-MVAC therapy because zinc preparations are sometimes used to treat CI dysgeusia [18] and may mask its symptoms.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Tokyo (approval number: 2529).

Data collection and definition

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records using predefined, standardized criteria to minimize subjective bias. Variables included age, sex, ECOG performance status (PS), presence of metastasis, cycles of dd-MVAC, prior chemotherapy and radiation therapy, gastrointestinal resection, smoking history, presence of professional oral care, diabetes, hypertension, depression, use of bisphosphonate agents, use of steroids, and clinical laboratory data. Depression was defined as having already been diagnosed with depression before inclusion or currently taking antidepressants. Similarly, patients with diabetes and/or hypertension were defined as those who had already been diagnosed with diabetes and/or hypertension before inclusion or were currently taking antidiabetic and/or antihypertensive drugs, respectively.

The calculation of average relative dose intensity (ARDI) was based on the work of Hryniuk and Goodyear [19]. The relative dose intensity (RDI) was defined as the average daily dose over the treatment duration received by each patient relative to the full dose.

The primary outcome was the occurrence of CI dysgeusia, defined as Grade ≥ 2 dysgeusia according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0 after initiation of dd-MVAC therapy. Patients who experienced Grade ≥ 2 dysgeusia were assigned to the CI dysgeusia group, and all others to the non-CI dysgeusia group.

Statistical analyses

The primary objective of this study was to identify the factors associated with CI dysgeusia using a multivariable logistic regression. Simulation studies suggest that 10–15 events per predictor are required for stable estimates [20,21]; therefore, our multivariable analysis included at most 3 predictors to produce stable association estimates.

Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test to compare patient demographics between the two groups; however, Fisher’s exact test was used when the expected cell count was less than 5. Numerical variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify potential predictors of CI dysgeusia. Variables with a p < 0.20 in the univariable analysis were subjected to a stepwise multivariable logistic regression analysis. The JMP Pro software version 18.1.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and RStudio version 2924.12.1 + 563 (RStudio Team, RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA, USA) were used for all statistical analyses. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 103 patients who received dd-MVAC at our institution were included in this study (Fig. 1). None of the patients in our cohort were taking zinc preparations before the initiation of dd-MVAC therapy in our cohort. The patients were classified into two groups based on the presence or absence of CI dysgeusia: the CI dysgeusia group (n = 32) and the non-CI dysgeusia group (n = 71).

Risk factors for dysgeusia

To identify the risk factors for CI dysgeusia, univariable logistic regression was first performed. Variables with p-value < 0.20 were selected as candidates for further analysis: PS, cycle of dd-MVAC, depression, HbA1c, ARDI, presence of metastasis, hypertension, LD, γ-GTP and ALP.

These variables were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model using the backward stepwise selection method. Consequently, cycles of dd-MVAC, depression, and HbA1c remained independent explanatory variables for CI dysgeusia. In the final multivariable model, depression was significantly associated with an increased risk of dysgeusia, with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 23.3 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.01–529, p = 0.010). In addition, HbA1c ≤ 5.8% was significantly associated with an increased risk of CI dysgeusia, with an aOR of 2.86 (95% CI: 1.16–7.30, p = 0.024) (Table 2).

Predictive performance of depression and HbA1c

The positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) of depression in patients with CI dysgeusia were 83.3% and 72.2%, respectively (Table 3). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed for HbA1c, identified an optimal cutoff of 5.8%, with a sensitivity of 68.8% and specificity of 56.3%. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.628, suggesting a modest discriminative ability (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study revealed that depression and HbA1c ≤ 5.8% were independent risk factors for CI dysgeusia in patients with cancer receiving dd-MVAC therapy. Furthermore, the precision model using the presence of depression demonstrated relatively good predictive performance, with a PPV of 83.3% and an NPV of 72.2%, indicating a predictive performance suitable for clinical use. These results are expected to contribute to maintaining QOL and supporting treatment adherence by enabling the early identification of patients at risk for CI dysgeusia, using depression and HbA1c as indicators, and intervening with appropriate supportive therapy.

The incidence of Grade ≥ 2 CI dysgeusia in this study was 31.1% (32/103). Previous studies have reported varying incidence of CI dysgeusia. For example, a retrospective observational study that recruited Japanese outpatients receiving chemotherapy, using similar criteria, reported that the incidence of CI dysgeusia was 12.6%. A prospective study that recruited patients with gastrointestinal cancer receiving platinum-based agents reported an incidence of CI dysgeusia of 23.8% [22]. In contrast, a higher incidence of approximately 60% was reported in a study that focused on patients receiving taxane-based anticancer agents [11]. Given the heterogeneity of patient characteristics and treatment regimens, this variability was expected. Within this context, the observed incidence of CI dysgeusia in this study (31.1%) falls within the range reported in existing literature.

Depression was identified as the independent risk factor for CI dysgeusia (aOR: 23.3, 95% CI: 3.01–529, p = 0.010). Consistent with our results, Hur et al. reported that the incidence of dysgeusia in patients meeting the diagnostic criteria for depression was significantly higher than in those who did not meet the criteria (23.7% [95% CI: 18.7–28.7%] vs. 11.9% [95% CI: 10.7–13.1%]) [23]. However, interpretation of these results requires consideration of the possibility that antidepressants can affect taste function. Although the impact of antidepressants on taste function remains debatable, some studies have suggested that tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are potential risk factors for dysgeusia. In this study, none of the five clinically depressed patients with CI dysgeusia used TCAs. The medications used for these patients were SSRI (one patient), multi-receptor acting antipsychotics (MARTA) (two patients), and benzodiazepines (two patients). Therefore, only one patient used medications with a reported risk of developing dysgeusia, suggesting that our results likely reflect the direct effect of depression itself rather than the effect of antidepressants on taste function. Nonetheless, the effects of antidepressants, other than TCAs and SSRIs, on taste function remain unclear and require further investigation. Although the mechanisms by which depression affects taste function may involve physiological, psychological, and pharmacological factors, one plausible explanation is decreased serum zinc levels in patients with depression. Several previous reports have indicated that serum zinc levels are significantly lower in patients with depression than in healthy individuals, suggesting that zinc deficiency may contribute to dysgeusia [24]. Previous studies have suggested that zinc supplementation may help prevent dysgeusia in cancer patients [25]. However, in this retrospective study, serum zinc levels were not measured before the initiation of dd-MVAC therapy. Therefore, zinc deficiency and its effect on the risk of CI dysgeusia have not yet been directly evaluated. Further prospective studies are required to elucidate the mechanisms underlying CI dysgeusia in patients with depression.

HbA1c ≤ 5.8% was also identified as an independent risk factor for CI dysgeusia. This finding seems inconsistent with previous reports focusing on patients with diabetes. Perros et al. reported that poor nutritional status and inadequate glycemic control are risk factors for dysgeusia [26]. In addition, chronic hyperglycemia and diabetic neuropathy have been reported to be associated with dysgeusia [27]. Furthermore, elevated HbA1c levels have been reported as risk factors for dysgeusia [28]. However, because the subjects of this study were not limited to patients with diabetes, the inconsistent results may be attributed to differences in the metabolic and nutritional backgrounds between this study and previous reports. In this regard, the present results may reflect factors specific to patients with cancer, such as impaired nutritional status, systemic inflammatory responses, and alterations in taste function associated with malnutrition or hypoglycemia, rather than a direct causal relationship between HbA1c levels and taste dysfunction. Nonetheless, it should be noted that the absolute difference in HbA1c between the two groups was small (median 5.8% vs. 6.0%), with both values remaining within the normal range, which suggests that the clinical significance of the observed difference in HbA1c may be limited. Although combination use of low HbA1c levels with other clinical indicators (such as depression) may contribute to more accurate risk stratification, future clinical studies incorporating nutritional assessments are required to clarify the role of HbA1c as an indicator of CI dysgeusia.

In this study, the predictive model for CI dysgeusia based on depression demonstrated relatively good predictive accuracy, with a PPV of 83.3% and NPV of 72.2%, suggesting its usefulness as a screening tool in clinical practice (Table 3). Furthermore, when depression was combined with HbA1c ≤ 5.8%, the PPV and NPV were 100% (2 out of 2 cases) and 69.6% (71 out of 102 cases), respectively; the PPV was higher and the NPV was similar to those predicted by the model based solely on the presence of depression. However, the higher PPV with HbA1c ≤ 5.8% may reflect the small number of patients with both HbA1c ≤ 5.8% and depression (2 out of 2 cases). In addition, the HbA1c cutoff was optimized for this study population; therefore, external validation is needed to evaluate the clinical significance of combining low HbA1c levels with depression.

Limitation

This study had several limitations. First, owing to the retrospective nature of the study, we cannot rule out the effects of other possible confounding factors. Second, the assessment of CI dysgeusia relied on the documentation of subjective symptoms in medical records, which may have limited the consistency and completeness of the evaluations. However, three of the agents in the dd-MVAC regimen are associated with CI dysgeusia, and healthcare providers are generally vigilant in assessing CI dysgeusia. Therefore, it is unlikely that underdiagnosis of CI dysgeusia affected the study findings. Nevertheless, objective taste assessment methods, such as taste strip tests and electrogustometry, may yield different results. Future prospective studies incorporating validated objective taste assessments would provide more rigorous evaluation of CI dysgeusia and help clarify the relationship between subjective and objective measures of taste dysfunction.

Third, depression was defined based on either a prior diagnosis or current use of antidepressants, as detailed information on disease severity or symptom burden was not consistently available due to the retrospective design. Consequently, we were unable to perform separate subgroup analyses to distinguish the potential effects of a prior diagnosis from those of active antidepressant use. Future prospective studies with standardized psychiatric assessments would help refine risk stratification for CI dysgeusia.

Conclusion

This study revealed that depression and HbA1c ≤ 5.8% were independent risk factors for CI dysgeusia in patients receiving dd-MVAC therapy. In addition, the presence of depression showed good predictive performance for the onset of CI dysgeusia, and the addition of HbA1c ≤ 5.8% may improve predictivity. Although further clinical studies are warranted, the assessment of the risk of CI dysgeusia based on these risk factors in daily clinical practice may be considered to mitigate the onset of CI dysgeusia in patients receiving dd-MVAC therapy.
