Efficacy and safety of super-mini percutaneous nephrolithotomy using a combination of suction sheath and pneumatic lithotripsy: the Dhangadhi experience
Authors: Sumeet Karna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSuper-mini percutaneous nephrolithotomy (SMP) is a promising minimally invasive approach for upper tract stone management. However, evidence on its performance using suction sheath and pneumatic lithotripsy in resource-limited setting remains scarce. The main objective of this study is to examine the efficacy and safety of SMP done exclusively with this combination.MethodsProspectively collected data of all eligible SMP cases done in two centers between March 2021 and February 2025 were retrospectively processed. After de-identification of the final data, descriptive and analytical statistics were used as appropriate. Regression models were then constructed to identify risk factors for complications.ResultsTotal of 324 prone SMP procedures were performed on 319 patients. The mean stone volume, operating time and fluoroscopy beam-on time were 1473 mm3, 38 min, and 145 s respectively. The mean hemoglobin drop was 1.2 ± 0.9 gm% with 2.1% of patients requiring blood transfusion. Overall, stone-free rate was 96.9% at 1-month follow-up, minor complications (Clavien-Dindo ≤ II) being 13.4%, and major complications (Clavien-Dindo > II) being 2.2%. Multinomial regression analysis revealed operating time > 40 min, positive urine culture, and access through 10th intercostal space as significant predictors of complications. Limitation includes retrospective design, lack of computerized tomography for confirming stone-free status, and lack of intra-renal pressure monitoring.ConclusionSMP using a combination of suction sheath and pneumatic lithotripsy is a safe and effective treatment option for selected cases with upper tract lithiasis in resource-limited settings, with high stone clearance and acceptable complication rates.Keywords: Nepal, Kidney stone, Percutaneous nephrolithotomy, Sepsis, Suction sheath, Super-mini PCNL

Introduction

Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) has been considered the backbone of minimally invasive renal stone surgery, offering the highest stone-free rate (SFR) [1]. But it comes with an added risk of hemorrhage and organ injury associated with tract creation and dilatation [2]. To combat this, reduction of its access tract size and miniaturization of instruments has led to its many variations such as mini-PCNL, super-mini PCNL, ultra-mini PCNL, micro-PCNL, and needle perc [3]. In our previous study comparing different PCNL sizes, we found that decreasing tract size reduces overall complication rate and morbidity [4]. This retrospective cohort study examines the efficacy and safety of super-mini percutaneous nephrolithotomy (SMP) done with the combination of suction sheath and pneumatic lithotripsy.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

A retrospective cohort study was done in two sub-urban centers of Nepal to assess the efficacy and safety of SMP using a combination of suction sheath and pneumatic lithotripter. The secondary objective was to identify potential risk factors associated with any complications. Only cases with limited stone burden, non-staghorn calculi, and favorable renal anatomy were selected for SMP using this combination. Patients with staghorn stones were always treated with larger PCNL sizes. Data from all cases meeting the criteria were extracted from a prospectively maintained PCNL record book after de-identification. An IT staff member and laboratory in-charge were responsible for retrieving data from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Two trained urology outpatient nurses (who were not involved in surgeries) independently cross-verified the extracted data against physical records and ensured data consistency. All personnel worked without direct involvement of the primary investigator or influence from other team members. Discrepancies or uncertainties were addressed during internal review sessions on a case-by-case basis to maintain transparency and reduce potential bias.

Data included demographic details (age, gender); pre-operative parameters (comorbidity, Guy’s Stone Score, location of stone, renal anatomical complexity, stone density, stone volume, urine culture); intra-operative variables (operating time, fluoroscopy beam-on time, puncture site, calyx accessed, number of attempts required before successful puncture), and post-operative outcomes (hemoglobin drop, creatinine change, hospital stay, stone-free status at follow-up and any complications).

Ethical approval

This study complied with the standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to ethical guidelines. Written permission to conduct the study was taken from both institutes. Individual consent of participation was not needed and was waived by the independent review board (IRB) of Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC). Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from Ethical Review Board of Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC), Kathmandu, Nepal, with registration no. 214_2025.

Procedural method

All cases were done in prone position by the same urologist (S. K.) in two centers using the same standardized technique after inducing spinal anesthesia. Every procedure started by ureteric stenting cystoscopically over a 0.035-inch straight tip Terumo glidewire® under fluoroscopy, followed by bladder drainage using a per-urethral catheter. The patient was then changed to prone position, and adequate padding of pressure points achieved before commencement of the main steps.

The puncture site and calyx were chosen based on the location of the stone and visualized anatomy as per retrograde pyelogram. Monoplanar technique [5] was commonly preferred for puncture over triangulation and bull’s-eye technique [6]. Biplanar techniques were used only after a failed attempt using monoplanar method followed by ultrasound-guided puncture. Papillary puncture was made using an 18G COOK® diamond tip needle, and success was determined by clear urine efflux from the needle cannula. Terumo glidewire® would then be passed antegrade through the needle into the ureter whenever possible. Tract dilatation used 12Fr fascial dilator before placing a single-step Shah sheath® with obturator. The sheath cannula has 13Fr inner diameter with suction outlet for connecting the stone collection bottle, which then leads into a vacuum suction device. A gravity-based continuous-flow irrigation system was employed in all cases by connecting a 3-liter normal-saline bottle to the inflow channel of the nephroscope. Occasionally, manual inflation of a pressure bag around the saline bottle was used during periods of poor visibility. Irrigation pressure was not continuously monitored. The suction tubing of the Shah sheath® was connected to a standard vacuum suction device available in the operating theater, maintaining a moderate suction pressure of approximately 150–300 mmHg as read on the device gauge. A 12Fr Nephroscope by Wolf® was used to visualize the pelvicalyceal system, and stones were fragmented using a 0.8 mm pneumatic probe fitted in NIDHI® pneumatic lithotripter device. Fragments of stone were sucked out through the cannula of the sheath. Rarely, tri-prong forceps had to be used if fragments were found impacted or embedded with clot.

For exit strategy, antegrade placement of a double-J stent or placing a 12Fr nephrostomy tube was based on conditions such as wall edema at pelvic-ureteric junction (PUJ), suspicion of bacteriuria due to infected stone, presence of bleeding, or perforation of the pelvicalyceal system. If total tubeless SMP was done, the ureteric catheter was removed the next day. The tract entry site was closed with a non-absorbable suture to be removed on the morning of discharge day. The double-J stent was removed after 2–4 weeks on an outpatient basis.

Operative definitions

Operating time is the time duration between stab incision made for puncture until the closure of the skin. Fluoroscopy time is the total beam-on radiation time (in seconds) that is recorded by the fluoroscopy machine for the entire procedure, with voltage set around 65 kilovolts. Stone volume was calculated by using the ellipsoid formula (SV = π x length x breadth x height x 0.167) [7]. The dimensional values used in the formula were as reported by the radiologist, measured in 1 mm cut sections on computed tomography (CT). Stone density was taken as the positive mean value in Hounsfield unit (HU) given by the reporting radiologist. The puncture site, calyx accessed, and number of attempts before successful puncture were all recorded by the surgeon at the end of each procedure.

Guy’s Stone Score (GSS) was calculated as per definition [8]. Hemoglobin drop is the difference between pre-operative hemoglobin and hemoglobin value obtained on the first post-operative day. Similarly, creatinine change is the value seen on the first post-operative day minus the pre-operative creatinine level. Hospital stay is the number of days admitted to the hospital following surgery and does not take into account pre-operative stay for fair comparison. Stone-free status was determined using ultrasound and plain radiograph kidney-ureter-bladder (KUB) at 1-month follow-up.

Efficacy of the procedure was defined in terms of operating time, stone-free rate and hospital stay whereas safety was determined in terms of fluoroscopy time, hemoglobin drop, creatinine change and complication rate.

Data processing

Data was cleaned in Microsoft® Excel 2021 and exported to IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences® (SPSS v25) for further processing and analysis. All variables were checked for normality distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test before choosing the most appropriate statistical test for analysis. Few missing values in numerical variables were imputed with the median value to maintain statistical power.

To better understand the relationship between different variables and post-operative complications using regression models, all categorical variables having two groups were transformed into binary variables (0 = normal, 1 = abnormal), and similarly, variables having more than two groups were transformed into ordinal groups based on hierarchy. Anatomical complexity of operated kidney was categorized into four ordinal groups based on severity: 0 = no abnormality; 1 = mild abnormality (bifid pelvis, pelvic-ureteric junction obstruction, isolated hydrocalyx, horse shoe kidney, single kidney); 2 = moderate abnormality (moderate hydronephrosis, partial staghorn calculus, polycystic kidney, severe spinal deformity); 3 = severe abnormality (gross hydronephrosis, gross hydronephrosis with partial staghorn, gross hydronephrosis with renal impairment).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive Statistics were used to express linear variables in terms of mean with standard deviation, whereas frequency distributions were calculated for all relevant categorical variables.

Univariate logistic regression models were used initially to identify each variable that had impact on complications, with P-value < 0.05 taken as significant. To determine if operating time was a risk factor for complication, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC-curve) model was used to determine statistically the best cutoff point, and the number with the area under the curve (AUC) at 0.5 taken to divide the operating time into two groups.

Finally, a multinomial logistic regression model was built to check for any independent predictors of complications. The final model was based on collinearity and included pre-operative and intra-operative variables like age, stone volume, stone density, operating time cutoff, urinary culture status, location of the stone, major comorbidities, anatomical complexity, GSS, site of puncture, calyx accessed, and number of attempts before successful puncture. The performance of model was based on regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals and P-values. The variable group was considered statistically significant when P-value < 0.05.

Results

Total 324 SMP procedures were performed on 319 patients from March 2021 to February 2025. Table 1 illustrates the demographics of the patient and the kidney. Five patients underwent bilateral simultaneous SMP in the same anesthesia setting. The mean stone volume was 1473.0 ± 1738.9 (SD) mm3. Tables 2 illustrates the topography of the stone along with Guy’s Stone Score [8] for classification of the complexity of the case. The intra-operative nuances are also described in the table along with post-operative results. Mean operating time was 38 ± 23 (SD) mins, and fluoroscopy time (beam-on time) was 145 ± 121 (SD) seconds. The average hemoglobin drop was 1.2 ± 0.9 (SD) gm% with 2.1% of patients requiring procedure-related blood transfusion. Overall, stone-free rate (SFR) was 96.9% at 1-month follow-up. Complications were segregated based on the Clavien-Dindo classification system [9], with minor (≤ II) complications being 13.4% and major (>II) complications 2.2% (Table 3). The most common complication was post-operative fever, which was 60% of the total complications.

A ROC-curve was used to determine the cutoff value for dividing operating time into two groups. The cutoff value was 39 min at area-under-curve (AUC) = 0.636 (P-value 0.001, sensitivity 0.48, specificity 0.29), resulting in Group 1 having an operating time of less than 40 min and Group 2 having more than 40 min.

The final multinomial logistic regression analysis is depicted in Table 4 with a forest plot for easier visualization. The most significant predictors of complications were operating time > 40 min (OR 2.07 95% CI 1.24 to 3.49, P-value < 0.01), positive pre-operative urine culture growth (OR 2.75 95% CI 1.30 to 5.80, P-value < 0.01) and 10th intercostal space puncture (OR 7.16 95% CI 1.81 to 28.22 P-value < 0.01). Age had a mild effect, showing decreasing risk of complications with increasing age (OR 0.95 95% CI 0.93 to 0.98, P-value < 0.01). On the other hand, multiple calyceal punctures had increased complications with borderline significance (OR 5.8 95% CI 0.9 to 36.86, P-value 0.06). Stone volume, stone density, location of stone, comorbidity, anatomical complexity, GSS, and number of attempts for successful puncture were not significantly associated with complications.

Discussion

Jackman et al. first introduced “mini-perc” in 1998 using a 13Fr ureteroscopy sheath to mitigate the complications associated with standard PCNL [10]. Lahme et al. invented the 12Fr nephroscope in 2001 to be used in mini-PCNL, which brought a paradigm shift in PCNL. This popularized as well as greatly improved the safety and efficacy of mini-PCNL [11]. Since then, there have been many variations in tract size, scope size, and sheath type, all with the aim of reducing complications further and increasing efficiency. In 2016, Prof. Guohua Zeng developed SMP with irrigation-suction sheath [12] which greatly increased the visibility during the procedure, efficiency of stone fragment extraction while decreasing intrarenal pelvic pressure. Similarly, in 2017, Shah et al. described “Superperc” which used a patented 10/12Fr metallic sheath called the “Shah sheath” having a large side outlet for connecting into the suction device [13].

In this study, all SMP used the 13/15Fr Shah sheath, which functions similar to first generation SMP by Zeng et al. [12] The inflow is through the 12Fr nephroscope and outflow through the metallic sheath connected to vacuum suction, creating a flow of irrigation fluid that facilitates stone fragment retrieval and maintains a low intrarenal pelvic pressure. The combined hydrodynamic effects, namely vacuum cleaner effect, active washout, and purging effect from the ureteral catheter, all contribute in effective stone retrieval [14].

For stone fragmentation, we used pneumatic lithotrite, which is low cost, durable and effective in all stone types with wide safety margin and no risk of thermal injury [15]. The only disadvantage is that it has a relatively high rate of retropulsion which sometimes can lead to a difficult situation. For instance, retropulsion of fragment into inaccessible calyx during fragmentation of a partial staghorn calculus. Also, care must be taken during fragmentation of very hard calculus to prevent pelvic wall perforation and migration of stone through the urothelium.

This study represents the real-world population demographics with heterogeneous stone burden that SMP can handle. Note that mini-PCNL with a larger sheath (18/21Fr size) is preferred over SMP in cases of staghorn calculus (GSS 4).

Table 5, which is adapted from systematic review by Nizzardo et al. [16] compares this study with other relevant studies done with similar sheath sizes, showing overall similar complication profile [4, 12, 13, 17–21]. Of total 15.4% complications in this study, 13.2% were minor including fever related complications (Clavien-Dindo ≤ II) whereas 2.2% were major (Clavien-Dindo >II). This study brings forward the very Achille’s heel of SMP, which is infection related complications that contributed to two-thirds of total complication.

The infective complications in our study may be attributed to the increased intrarenal pelvic pressure. The ratio of inflow through the scope to the outflow channel between the nephroscope and sheath may have been suboptimal, leading to increased intrarenal pelvic pressure despite using vacuum suction. Furthermore, the lack of continuous monitoring of irrigation pressures might have contributed to unrecognized fluctuations in intrarenal pressure, potentially leading to higher infection-related complications seen in our cohort. Other studies utilize a smaller caliber nephroscope for the purpose resulting in better drainage of irrigation fluid. Another factor may be presence of infective stone and persistent urinary tract infection. All patients having positive pre-operative urine culture growth underwent at least 5 days of antibiotics according to antibiogram before the procedure as per the guidelines of International Alliance of Urolithiasis (IAU) guideline on percutaneous nephrolithotomy [22].

Operating time was one of the strongest predictors of complication, with time more than 40 min having twice the risk of complication (OR 2.07 95% CI 1.24 to 3.49 P-value 0.00). The reason may be that the operating time was reflecting the true complexity of the case.

In this study, ten cases underwent tract creation through 10th intercostal space. Subgroup analysis shows it has increased risk of complication in comparison to subcostal puncture (OR 7.16 95% CI 1.81 to 28.22 P-value < 0.01). This may be due to our preference of spinal anesthesia over general anesthesia. General anesthesia offers better respiratory control during puncture, decreasing the risk of pleural injury [22]. Similar to this study, Loganapiwat and associates found increased risk of complications (15.3% vs. 1.4%) in supracostal punctures when compared to subcostal punctures during standard-PCNL [23].

Although serious bleeding complications and formation of pseudo-aneurysms is said to have decreased by the use of smaller sheath size in PCNL, it has not been eradicated as shown by the two cases in this study requiring angio-embolisation.

Limitations of the study

Despite being prospectively collected data and every effort to maintain an accurate record, some cases may have been lost to follow-up, leading to selection bias. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of this study limits its ability to establish causal association or fully control for confounding factors. A prospective design in the future would provide stronger validation of the findings and allow better standardization of irrigation and suction parameters. Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) was not used routinely to confirm residual stone, which might have resulted in less accurate SFR. Intrarenal pelvic pressure and suction pressure were not continuously monitored, which would have given a more logical explanation for the cause of higher infectious complications seen in this study.

Conclusion

SMP using a combination of suction sheath and pneumatic lithotripsy is a safe and effective treatment for upper tract lithiasis in selected patients with acceptable complication rate. In this study, operating time more than 40 min, positive urine culture, and tract through 10th intercostal space were prime factors predictive of complications.
