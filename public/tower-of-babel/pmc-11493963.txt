Countermovement jump and reactive strength index of artistic gymnasts improve more with cluster-based plyometric training than with traditional methods
Authors: DeSen Feng, WenXin Yang, Li Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose This randomized controlled study aims to compare the effects of cluster training versus traditional plyometric lower limb training on vertical and horizontal jump performance, as well as the reactive strength index, in young female artistic gymnasts. Methods: A total of 54 female artistic gymnasts (15.4 ± 1.2 years) participated voluntarily this study. Participants were assigned to one of three groups: (i) cluster training (PLYct); (ii) traditional training (PLYtr); and (iii) a control group (not exposed to plyometric training). The intervention spanned 8 weeks, with evaluations conducted before and after the intervention period for the following variables: (i) squat jump; (ii) countermovement jump; and (iii) reactive strength index in a drop jump test. Results: Significant interactions time × group were found in SJ (p < 0.001; =0.505), CMJ (p<0.001; =0.241) and RSI (p < 0.001; =0.492). The time × group analysis in post-intervention revealed significantly greater performance in SJ of PLYct (3.0 cm; p < 0.001) and PLYtr (2.5 cm; p=0.001) in comparison to control group. Significantly higher CMJ height were observed for the PLYct group comparing to PLYtr (1.3 cm; p=0.008) and control (2.9 cm; <0.001), while PLYtr was significantly better than control (1.6 cm; p=0.001). PLYct had significantly greater RSI than PLYtr (0.07 RSI; p = 0.014) and control (0.10 RSI; p<0.001), while PLYtr was significantly better than control (0.10 RSI; p = 0.024). Conclusion: Cluster sets were significantly more effective than traditional sets in improving the stretch-shortening cycle as measured by the CMJ and enhancing the reactive strength of gymnasts. It is recommended to incorporate cluster sets while applying plyometric training to maximize performance with favorable adaptations.

Introduction

Artistic gymnastics demands a combination of strength, flexibility, agility, and balance1. Athletes perform a series of dynamic movements such as flips, twists, and rotations, often requiring explosive muscular power and control2. Among others physical fitness variables, jumping ability is crucial in gymnastics3 as it facilitates the execution of aerial maneuvers and vaults, enabling athletes to generate the necessary height and momentum to perform complex routines effectively. A higher vertical jump allows gymnasts to achieve greater height during aerial elements, enhancing their ability to execute complex movements4. Additionally, a strong jumping capacity enables gymnasts to absorb and control landings more effectively, reducing the risk of injury and ensuring consistent performance throughout routines5. Thus, improving jumping ability is fundamental for gymnasts to achieve optimal performance and excel in this demanding sport.

One effective method for enhancing jumping performance is through the utilization of lower limb plyometric training6. Plyometric exercises involve rapid stretching (eccentric phase) of muscles followed by a quick contraction (concentric phase), exploiting the stretch-shortening cycle7. This training method increases muscle-tendon stiffness, improving the efficiency of force transmission during the propulsion phase of a jump8. It also enhances neuromuscular coordination, optimizing the recruitment and synchronization of motor units, thereby enhancing explosive power output9. Furthermore, plyometric training stimulates adaptations in muscle fibers, particularly type II fast-twitch fibers10, crucial for generating high force and velocity during explosive movements like jumping. Additionally, plyometrics enhance proprioception and reactive strength11, aiding in better control of body positioning and maximizing the utilization of elastic energy stored in tendons12, ultimately leading to improved jumping performance.

Among the training methodologies to introduce, cluster sets offer a promising approach to plyometric training, particularly due to their ability to optimize acute responses during exercise13 and potentially enhance long-term adaptations when compared to traditional plyometric training methods. By incorporating short rest intervals within a set14, cluster sets allow athletes to maintain high-intensity efforts over multiple repetitions, promoting greater power output and neuromuscular activation14. This approach facilitates the recruitment of fast-twitch muscle fibers15, essential for explosive movements like those in plyometrics, while minimizing the effects of fatigue that could compromise performance14. The strategic use of cluster sets may lead to more significant improvements in jump performance13, as it allows athletes to perform more high-quality repetitions with proper technique, leading to greater gains in strength and power over time16.

While this possibility is hypothesized, research in this area is limited and conflicting. Although there is a lack of research specifically focused on this topic in gymnastics, a study conducted on recreationally active young men compared traditional and cluster set plyometrics17. The findings revealed that the effects on dynamic strength and repetitions to failure in exercises such as the back squat and chest press, as well as performance in the standing long jump, 20-meter sprint, and 9-meter shuttle run, were similar between the two methods17. Conversely, a study involving college students who underwent plyometric training showed that cluster sets were significantly more effective in enhancing jumping and agility performance16. Meanwhile, traditional sets proved significantly better at improving sprint performance16.

While previous studies highlight the inconsistencies and lack of comprehensive results in sports research, it is also clear that research specifically pertaining to gymnastics training is notably absent. Given the importance of maximizing the potential benefits of plyometric training in gymnastics by selecting the most effective training methodology to support performance adaptations, there is opportunity for an experimental study aimed at addressing the gap in research regarding the use of cluster sets over the long term to identify adaptations rather than merely acute responses (which constitute the vast majority of studies on this topic). Understanding the long-term adaptations to this training method can not only clarify its effects on gymnastics training but also address the lack of research on this topic among athletes. Based on the reasons mentioned above, this study aims to compare the effects of cluster versus traditional plyometric lower limb training on vertical and horizontal jumping abilities, as well as the reactive strength index, among young female artistic gymnasts. It is hypothesized that cluster-based training will show significantly better post-intervention results in both jumping tests compared to traditional training and the control group. This is based on the positive adaptations and improved acute responses observed in the existing literature16.

Methods

Experimental approach

In this study, a randomized controlled design was utilized, with a single-blind approach in which only the evaluators were blinded to the study. Two experimental groups were established, referred to as cluster set plyometric training group (PLYct) and traditional plyometric training group (PLYtr), in addition to a control group that maintained their regular training routines. The participants, drawn from artistic gymnastics teams, were chosen through a convenience sampling approach. To guarantee the random allocation of participants to the various groups, a system of opaque, sealed envelopes was implemented before the initial assessments. These assessments took place one week before the intervention began and immediately after it concluded, spanning an eight-week timeframe for the intervention.

Participants

Utilizing the G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6, Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) for preliminary sample size calculations, a repeated measures ANOVA, with a focus on the interaction between factors over time and between groups, required a minimum of 48 participants. This calculation was based on an anticipated effect size of 0.25, a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.85, considering three groups across two points in time.

The recruitment process identified 61 potential athletes through convenience sampling. To qualify for the study, these volunteers had to meet several criteria: (i) availability for assessments at two distinct times (before and after the intervention); (ii) participation in at least 90% of the intervention activities; (iii) absence of injuries in the month leading up to and during the study; (iv) female gender; (v) age above 14 years; and (vi) a minimum of three years of experience in the sport, with participation in competitions at least at the regional level. After matching against the eligibility criteria, 5 participants were excluded due to having musculoskeletal injuries in the month prior to the study, and 2 were unavailable for the first round of assessments (Fig. 1).

Fifty-four female regional-level artistic gymnasts (age: 15.4 ± 1.2 years; body mass: 52.7 ± 2.5 kg; height: 1.58 ± 0.03 m; body mass index: 21.0 ± 0.7 kg/m2) participated in this study. Of these, 43 showed a preference for using their right hand, while 45 indicated a preference for their right leg. After allocation to training groups, the PLYct exhibited the following characteristics (n = 18; age: 15.4 ± 1.1 years; body mass: 52.5 ± 2.7 kg; height: 1.58 ± 0.03 m; body mass index: 20.9 ± 0.8 kg/m2), while PLYtr demonstrated the following (n = 18; age: 15.7 ± 1.1 years; body mass: 53.8 ± 2.1 kg; height: 1.60 ± 0.03 m; body mass index: 21.1 ± 0.5 years). The control group displayed the following characteristics (n = 18; age: 15.2 ± 1.3 years; body mass: 51.9 ± 2.4 kg; height: 1.57 ± 0.03 m; body mass index: 21.1 ± 0.7 kg/m2). The participants were members of artistic gymnastics teams, typically exposed to 4 to 5 training sessions per week, each session lasting between 80 and 120 min. The participants and their legal guardians were informed about the study protocol and the implications of participation. After being informed, they signed an informed consent document which disclosed the possibility to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Chengdu, with the assigned code number 10/2024.

Training intervention

Both experimental groups received supplemental plyometric training, which was added to their regular training sessions, to which the athletes were already frequently exposed. Meanwhile, the control group maintained their routine of engaging only in regular training sessions.

The experimental groups underwent an 8-week training intervention, during which athletes participated in two dedicated plyometric sessions per week, with a 48-hour interval between sessions. All plyometric sessions took place before the regular training sessions and were preceded by a standardized warm-up protocol. This warm-up protocol comprised 5 min of jogging, followed by 10 min of dynamic stretching focusing on the lower limbs, and concluded with 5 min of jumping and landing drills. The intervention followed a progressive approach, beginning with Phase 1 for familiarization and load accommodation, progressing to Phase 2 where the number of repetitions per set increased from 8 to 10, and advancing to Phase 3, which added an additional set per drill per session. The total number of repetitions was consistent for both groups, with 64 jumps per session in Weeks 1–2, 80 jumps per session in Weeks 3–5, and 120 jumps per session in Weeks 6–8. The only difference between the experimental groups was that PLYct included a 30-second rest within each set, occurring after every two repetitions, while PLYtr performed all repetitions within each set without rest. The cluster’s structure was chosen based on a prior study13 indicating that incorporating rests between two repetitions is advantageous for mitigating fatigue and amplifying power output during plyometric exercises.

Evaluation procedures

The participants underwent evaluation twice: once the week prior to beginning the intervention period (baseline) and immediately following the conclusion of the training intervention (post-intervention). Both evaluations were preceded by a 24-hour rest period and took place on the same day of the week and at the same time (4 pm). The evaluations were conducted indoors, in a facility maintained at a temperature of 23ºC and a relative humidity of 55%. Participants were instructed not to consume energy drinks on the evaluation day and to avoid engaging in moderate-to-intense physical activity the day before the evaluations.

The evaluation process began with gathering demographic information, followed by anthropometric measurements. This was followed by a standardized warm-up protocol identical to the one utilized in the earlier reported plyometric training sessions. Following a 3-minute rest period at the conclusion of the warm-up, evaluations were conducted in the following sequence: (i) squat jump test (SJ); (ii) countermovement jump test (CMJ); and (iii) drop jump test from a 10-cm box height. Each test was performed twice, with a 1-minute rest interval between repetitions. Additionally, a 3-minute rest period was provided between each test.

Anthropometrics

Anthropometric evaluations were conducted in accordance with established standards to ascertain the height and body mass of the participants. Height was measured employing a stadiometer (Seca 217, Seca, Hamburg), while body mass was evaluated using a precision electronic scale (SECA 813; Seca GmbH & Co., Hamburg, Germany) accurate to 0.1 kg. Participants were dressed in shorts and t-shirts during the measurements, and measurements were taken without footwear.

Jumping evaluations

The evaluation of SJ, CMJ, and drop jumps involved the utilization of the MyJump 2 mobile application (version 1.0.8). This application employed video analysis to assess flight time and estimate jump height. The app calculates the jump height using the formula h = t2 × 1.22625, as outlined by Bosco et al.18. In this equation, h represents the jump height (in meters), and t is the flight time (in seconds). The reactive strength index (RSI) was determined by dividing the flight time by the ground contact time.

Previous studies have confirmed the accuracy and reliability of this analytical approach in determining jump height and the reactive strength index19. In the concurrent validity study comparing the MyJump 2 app to a force platform, it was found that the devices showed almost perfect agreement. At a jump height of 20 cm, the app and force platform had an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of 0.95 for Reactive Strength Index (RSI) and 0.99 for contact time. At 40 cm, the ICC values were 0.98 for RSI, 0.96 for jump height, and 0.92 for contact time. Additionally, at 20 cm, there was very strong agreement for jump height with an ICC of 0.80.

The SJ test involved executing a maximal vertical jump from a squatting stance, where the height achieved indicated the athletes’ performance. Participants began in a squatting position with feet shoulder-width apart, explosively extended their legs, and aimed to jump as high as possible while keeping their hands on their hips. Proper technique involved maintaining a straight back, ensuring knees aligned with toes, and executing a soft landing with bent knees to absorb impact. The athletes conducted one familiarization trial followed by two recorded attempts, with each attempt spaced one minute apart. The average of the two jumps (height, measured in centimeters) was then collected as the output for further data analysis.

During the CMJ test, participants initiated the movement by lowering themselves into a squat position, then explosively extended their legs to jump vertically as high as possible. The depth of the squat was self-selected based on the athletes’ comfort, with instructions to maintain fluid motion without pausing between the descent and ascent phases. Proper technique included maintaining an upright posture, positioning hands on the hips, ensuring knees tracked over toes, and landing softly with bent knees to absorb impact. The athletes conducted one familiarization trial followed by two recorded attempts, with each attempt spaced one minute apart. The average of the two jumps (height, measured in centimeters) was then collected as the output for further data analysis.

The assessment of the player’s reactive strength index (RSI) was conducted through a drop jump test. The drop jump test involved stepping off a 10-cm box and upon landing, immediately performing a maximal vertical jump. This test assessed the individual’s ability to rapidly generate force and absorb shock during the landing phase while effectively transitioning into an explosive jump. To measure jump height, the MyJump 2 mobile application was utilized, which calculates RSI based on the division of flight time for the ground contact time19. Each participant underwent one familiarization attempt followed by two trials, separated by a 3-minute break. The average recorded RSI for each participant was selected for further data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Prior to conducting inferential statistics, the normality of the sample distribution was assessed and confirmed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p > 0.05), while the assumption of homogeneity was verified using Levene’s test (p > 0.05). Given the study’s design involving two assessments across three groups, a mixed ANOVA was employed to explore interactions between time and groups. Discussion

The findings from this controlled experimental study demonstrated that both plyometric lower limb training regimens significantly outperformed the control group in improving jumping performance variables (SJ, CMJ and drop jump) in young female artistic gymnasts. Furthermore, the cluster set training approach was also found to be significantly more effective than the traditional set approach in enhancing CMJ and RSI. In the case of the SJ, both experimental groups significantly outperformed the control group, although there was no significant difference between the two experimental groups themselves.

Our study confirmed that plyometric training, regardless of the regimen, significantly improves CMJ compared to the control group. These results confirm previous findings regarding the effectiveness of plyometric training in enhancing CMJ among gymnasts6, 20. However, our results also revealed that cluster sets were significantly superior to traditional plyometric training sets in enhancing CMJ. This finding is consistent with a prior study indicating that cluster sets were significantly more effective than traditional sets in improving CMJ among college students undergoing plyometric training16. Moreover, this aligns with a trend suggesting that longer recovery periods lead to greater gains in jumping performance. While our study did not specifically test cluster sets, it analyzed the effects of overall recovery between sets21.

The cluster set method allows for a higher intensity of training to be completed within a given time frame while minimizing neuromuscular fatigue compared to traditional continuous sets13. This enhanced intensity may lead to greater adaptations in the neuromuscular system, including increased motor unit recruitment and synchronization22, ultimately resulting in improved CMJ performance. The intermittent nature of cluster sets allows for partial recovery between repetitions, which has been shown to optimize power output and force production during subsequent efforts14, 16, potentially enhancing the quality of each plyometric repetition. Moreover, the strategic manipulation of rest intervals in cluster sets may facilitate greater retention of plyometric technique proficiency throughout the training session, ensuring optimal movement patterns21.

Our results also revealed that both plyometric training groups were significantly effective in improving RSI, outperforming the control group significantly. These findings align with previous studies that investigated similar hypotheses in gymnasts23, 24. Interestingly, our findings also revealed that cluster set was significantly better than traditional set method for plyometric training in enhancing reactive strength index. While it was not possible to directly compare our findings to previous studies due to the absence of a similar study design incorporating RSI, our results are particularly intriguing. This is because a prior study found that RSI was not significantly enhanced by cluster sets in the acute responses to exercise25.

Cluster set method allows for a more structured manipulation of rest intervals, which may optimize the potentiation effect and enhance the stretch-shortening cycle characteristics crucial for RSI improvement26. This strategic distribution of rest intervals within and between sets may help to sustain maximal actions throughout the training session, promoting greater neuromuscular adaptations conducive to RSI enhancement27. Furthermore, the intermittent nature of cluster sets may attenuate neuromuscular fatigue, allowing for more focused and effective training stimuli during each repetition, thereby optimizing RSI development28. Additionally, the inclusion of reactive and short time contacts drills alongside regular more time-lasting contacts in both the cluster and traditional training groups may have contributed to improvements in RSI by targeting specific aspects of neuromuscular function and enhancing the SSC efficiency24. These drills likely facilitated neuromuscular adaptations such as increased muscle stiffness, improved stretch reflex activation, and enhanced motor unit synchronization, all of which are critical determinants of RSI performance29.

Our findings indicate that both experimental groups were significantly more effective in enhancing SJ compared to the control group. However, it is noteworthy that there were no significant differences observed between the two experimental groups in this regard. Our findings regarding the efficacy of plyometric training in enhancing SJ are consistent with previous studies conducted on gymnasts30.

Possibly, both training strategies induce structural adaptations within muscle fibers, including improved muscle-tendon stiffness, which are vital for generating greater force during the SJ. Additionally, plyometric exercises elicit enhancements in motor unit recruitment and synchronization, leading to improved coordination and motor control, essential for proficient execution of the squat jump12. By increasing neural drive and enhanced reflex potentiation, facilitating rapid force production during explosive movements like the SJ31. Moreover, both the cluster set approach, and traditional plyometric regimens integrate progressive overload principles, gradually increasing training volume over time, which was possibly crucial for improvements in SJ performance32.

Our study is among the few that have examined adaptations by comparing cluster set versus traditional set implementations of plyometric training. Despite its originality and the use of a parallel and controlled study design, our research has limitations. Specifically, we did not analyze jump performance in gymnastics technical elements, so any claims about improvements in technical performance are speculative and not supported by our data. Additionally, the study did not measure certain critical force variables, such as power and rate of force development, which can only be assessed with force platforms. This fact further limits the generalizability of our findings to gymnastics performance.

Another limitation is associated with the lack of daily monitoring of training intensity. Consequently, while participants were instructed to exert maximal intensity in each repetition, the absence of data on the actual output in each repetition hinders our understanding of its impact and ability to explain the observed adaptations. Thus, future research should incorporate these parameters into the model to potentially elucidate the observed adaptations. Furthermore, our study utilized a cluster set approach equal to all the athletes in the group. In the future, exploring different cluster set strategies or potentially adopting an individualized cluster approach based on the performance drop in each repetition could be significantly important in realizing how to optimize training management for individual responses.

Despite the study’s limitations, it is important to highlight that this is the first study to utilize cluster sets in plyometric training specifically applied to gymnasts, as far as we are aware. Moreover, this study stands as one of the few within sports training attempting to understand the adaptations that may occur following cluster sets in plyometrics training. Our research, while contextualized to our specific population, reveals that the cluster set approach could potentially be a recommended method to optimize the effectiveness of plyometric training for enhancing both CMJ and RSI. This approach appears to be superior to the traditional set approach in plyometric training. Therefore, coaches may consider integrating rest periods within the sets to allow athletes to recover and maximize intensity in each repetition, possibly positively impacting long-term adaptation potential.

Conclusions

The current research indicates that cluster sets are particularly beneficial in enhancing the magnitude of improvements in CMJ and reactive strength among young female artistic gymnasts undergoing plyometric training, compared to a traditional regimen. While traditional plyometrics also significantly outperformed the control group, cluster sets, which incorporate recovery intervals within sets to boost performance in each repetition, were found to be especially effective. This suggests that integrating recovery intervals plays an important role in the extent of adaptations in jumping-related performance measures. Despite the absence of clear dose-response relationships that would elucidate the mechanisms underpinning these adaptations, the findings of our study offer practical implications. They suggest that coaches should consider incorporating plyometric training to positively affect the jumping performance in SJ and CMJ tests. Specifically, the use of cluster sets is recommended to enhance the magnitude of these adaptations.
