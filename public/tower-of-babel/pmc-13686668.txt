Assessing the Educational Role of Large Language Models in Dental Training: A Decade‐Long Analysis of Text‐Based and Visual Questions in a National Examination
Authors: Sedef Ayse Tasyapan, Didem Özer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLarge language models (LLMs), including ChatGPT, Gemini and DeepSeek, are increasingly explored as supportive tools in health professions education. However, their educational utility across different knowledge domains and question formats, particularly those involving visual content, remains insufficiently understood.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the educational potential of three advanced LLMs in dental training by analysing their performance on a national specialty examination over a 10‐year period, with particular emphasis on domain‐specific accuracy and differences between text‐based and visual questions.MethodsA total of 1560 multiple‐choice questions from 13 administrations of the Turkish Dental Specialty Examination (DUS) between 2012 and 2021 were included. All questions were translated into English and categorized into nine dental specialties. Each question was individually entered into ChatGPT‐4.0, Gemini Advanced and DeepSeek in isolated sessions. Model responses were compared with official answer keys, and accuracy rates were analysed across years, specialties and question types. Statistical analyses included chi‐square tests, one‐way ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis tests.ResultsChatGPT‐4.0 achieved the highest overall accuracy (86.93%), followed by Gemini (82.86%) and DeepSeek (82.34%). Performance varied across specialties, with higher accuracy observed in Basic Sciences and Oral Surgery, and lower performance in Orthodontics and Endodontics. A substantial decrease in accuracy was observed for visual questions (ChatGPT: 52.38%; Gemini: 45.24%; DeepSeek: 2.38%) compared to text‐based items (all models > 83%). ChatGPT demonstrated more stable performance across years, whereas Gemini and DeepSeek showed greater variability.ConclusionsLLMs demonstrate strong potential as supportive tools in dental education, particularly for text‐based knowledge assessment. However, their limited performance in visual question contexts highlights an important constraint for their integration into image‐dependent domains such as dental diagnostics. These findings underscore the need for cautious and context‐aware implementation of AI tools in dental curricula and assessment practices.

Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into health professions education is rapidly transforming traditional learning and assessment strategies. Large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, Gemini and DeepSeek, have demonstrated advanced capabilities in language comprehension, contextual reasoning and domain‐specific knowledge generation [1, 2, 3]. As a result, these models are increasingly being explored as supportive tools for students in high‐stakes examinations, including use as study aids, knowledge reinforcement systems and simulated test‐taking platforms [4, 5, 6].

In dental education, multiple‐choice examinations play a central role in assessing both foundational knowledge and clinical reasoning. However, preparing for such examinations requires not only memorization but also the ability to interpret clinically relevant scenarios, including image‐based content such as radiographs and clinical photographs. Despite this, most existing studies evaluating LLM performance in medical and dental education have focused on text‐based questions and limited datasets, often restricted to a single exam year or a single model [4, 6, 7].

Furthermore, the ability of LLMs to handle visual‐context questions remains insufficiently explored. This represents a critical gap, particularly in dentistry, where diagnostic reasoning is frequently based on visual interpretation. The exclusion or underrepresentation of image‐based questions in previous studies limits the generalizability of findings and obscures an important dimension of LLM performance [7, 8].

In addition, comparative and longitudinal analyses that evaluate multiple LLMs across different specialties and over extended time periods are scarce. Such analyses are essential to determine whether these models provide consistent and reliable performance across diverse knowledge domains and evolving examination formats.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the educational potential of three contemporary LLMs—ChatGPT‐4.0, Gemini Advanced and DeepSeek—by analysing their performance on a national dental specialty examination over a 10‐year period. Particular emphasis was placed on differences across dental specialties and between text‐based and visual questions to better understand their applicability in dental education and assessment.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective comparative study was designed to evaluate the educational potential of large language models (LLMs) using a national dental specialty examination. A total of 1560 multiple‐choice questions (MCQs) from 13 administrations of the Turkish Dental Specialty Examination (DUS) between 2012 and 2021 were included. All questions were obtained from official releases by the Student Selection and Placement Center (ÖSYM), ensuring data authenticity and consistency [9].

AI Models Evaluated

Three advanced LLMs were evaluated: ChatGPT‐4.0 (OpenAI, accessed April 2024), Gemini Advanced (Google, accessed May 2024) and DeepSeek Chat (DeepSeek AI, accessed May 2024). All models were accessed via their native web interfaces without the use of application programming interfaces, plugins, or external prompt engineering strategies.

Question Preparation and Translation

All MCQs were translated from Turkish into English independently by two researchers with clinical and academic expertise in dentistry. Discrepancies between translations were resolved through consensus to ensure semantic and clinical equivalence. The translation process aimed to preserve the original meaning and diagnostic context of each question.

Question Categorization

Questions were categorized into nine dental subspecialties according to the original DUS structure: Basic Sciences (Q1–40), Restorative Dentistry (Q41–50), Prosthodontics (Q51–60), Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (Q61–70), Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology (Q71–80), Periodontology (Q81–90), Orthodontics (Q91–100), Endodontics (Q101–110) and Paediatric Dentistry (Q111–120).

Data Input Procedure

Each question was entered individually into each model in separate, stateless sessions to eliminate contextual memory effects. A standardized prompt was used for all models to ensure consistency: ‘I will ask you a multiple‐choice question with five options and one correct answer. Please select the most appropriate answer.’ No additional guidance, clarification, or follow‐up prompts were provided.

Handling of Visual Questions

Visual‐content questions, including radiographs, clinical images and diagrams, were retained in the dataset. As current LLMs cannot directly process images within standard interfaces, these questions were presented using their associated textual descriptions (e.g., captions or diagnostic hints). This approach was used to assess the models' ability to infer answers based on contextual information.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly answered questions among all valid (non‐withdrawn) items. Secondary analyses included performance comparisons across dental specialties, examination years and question types (text‐based vs. visual).

Response Coding

Model responses were compared with official answer keys provided by ÖSYM. Responses were coded as follows: correct (1), incorrect (0), unanswered/refusal (2) and withdrawn/invalid questions (3). This classification enabled detailed analysis of model behaviour, including omissions (Figure 1).

Ethics Statement

This study utilized publicly available examination data from the official website of OSYM. No human subjects or identifiable personal data were involved in this research. Therefore, ethical approval was not required for this study.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize performance across models. Chi‐square tests were applied for categorical comparisons, one‐way ANOVA was used to compare mean accuracy between models, and Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed for non‐parametric comparisons across specialties. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall Performance Evaluation

Across all exam years and dental specialties, ChatGPT achieved the highest overall accuracy, correctly answering 86.93% of the valid questions. Gemini followed with an accuracy rate of 82.86%, while DeepSeek attained 82.34%. These results demonstrate that all three models were able to perform at a high level when presented with a wide range of professional dental knowledge domains (Table 1).

Performance by Year

As presented in Table 4, ChatGPT consistently achieved the highest yearly accuracy, ranging from 84.24% in 2021 to 91.53% in 2013–1. Its performance remained notably stable over time, particularly in mid‐decade exams (2013–2016). Gemini demonstrated competitive but slightly lower performance, with its highest accuracy observed in 2013–1 (91.53%) and lowest in 2019 (78.33%). Notably, Gemini's accuracy fluctuated more across years compared to ChatGPT. DeepSeek showed the most variability and the lowest overall scores, with an accuracy range from 75.93% (2019) to 90.83% (2012–2). The model exhibited pronounced performance drops in more recent exams (2018–2021), likely reflecting limitations in clinical reasoning or updated content unfamiliarity (Figure 3).

Performance by Question Type: Visual Versus Text‐Based

A total of 42 visual questions were identified across the 2012–2021 DUS exams, which included radiographic images, clinical photographs and diagrammatic content. While all models performed strongly on text‐based questions, there was a marked decline in accuracy when confronted with visual content (Table 5). ChatGPT achieved the highest accuracy on visual questions (52.38%), correctly answering 22 out of 42. Gemini followed with 45.24% accuracy (19 correct answers), while DeepSeek answered only one visual question correctly (2.38%) and left 41 items unanswered. This suggests that the model either failed to interpret the visual prompt or chose not to respond due to limitations in image‐based reasoning. On text‐based questions, all three models exceeded 83% accuracy, with ChatGPT again leading (87.90%), followed by Gemini (83.91%) and DeepSeek (84.57%).

Discussion

This study provides one of the most comprehensive evaluations to date of LLM performance on a national dental specialty examination. By analysing over 1500 questions from 13 iterations of the Turkish Dental Specialty Examination (DUS), this study offers a longitudinal and multidomain comparison of three advanced models—ChatGPT‐4.0, Gemini Advanced and DeepSeek—across multiple dental subspecialties and question formats. Importantly, the inclusion of visual‐content questions allows for a more comprehensive assessment of LLM capabilities in dentistry, where image‐based reasoning plays a critical role.

Of the evaluated models, ChatGPT‐4.0 demonstrated the highest overall accuracy, achieving 86.93% and consistently outperformed Gemini and DeepSeek. These findings are consistent with previous studies evaluating LLM performance in high‐stakes medical examinations. For instance, Kung et al. reported that ChatGPT achieved performance levels comparable to passing thresholds on the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE), while Jang et al. demonstrated that GPT‐4 exceeded 76% accuracy in the Korean Medical Licensing Examination (KMLE) [1, 10]. Similarly, Kınıkoğlu et al. reported that ChatGPT achieved 77.2% accuracy on a single‐year DUS dataset [11]. The present study extends these findings by demonstrating sustained performance across a decade‐long dataset and multiple dental domains, thereby providing stronger evidence of the consistency and robustness of LLM performance in dental education contexts.

A specialty‐based analysis revealed that ChatGPT performed particularly well in Basic Sciences and Oral Surgery, suggesting strong capabilities in foundational biomedical reasoning and clinical decision‐making. In contrast, lower performance was observed in Orthodontics and Endodontics, which may require more advanced spatial reasoning and procedural knowledge. These findings are consistent with previous literature indicating that LLM performance may vary across disciplines depending on the cognitive demands and representation of domain‐specific knowledge in training datasets [3, 12]. Although differences between models did not reach statistical significance across specialties, trends observed in clinically oriented fields such as Oral Surgery and Radiology suggest potential variation in performance that warrants further investigation.

One of the most important findings of this study is the substantial decline in performance on visual‐content questions compared to text‐based items. While all models demonstrated high accuracy on text‐based questions, performance dropped markedly for visual questions, with DeepSeek showing particularly limited capability. This represents a critical limitation, especially in dentistry, where diagnostic reasoning heavily depends on the interpretation of radiographs and clinical images. Similar challenges have been reported in radiology‐focused studies, where LLMs exhibited hallucinations or inaccuracies when dealing with image‐related tasks [13]. These findings highlight that, despite strong performance in text‐based reasoning, current LLMs remain insufficient for tasks requiring reliable visual interpretation.

From an educational perspective, these findings suggest that LLMs may serve as effective supplementary tools for exam preparation and self‐assessment, particularly in domains that rely on text‐based knowledge. Their ability to provide rapid feedback and simulate examination scenarios may support students in reinforcing theoretical knowledge. However, their use should be carefully guided, as overreliance on AI‐generated responses without critical evaluation may lead to the reinforcement of incorrect or incomplete knowledge.

Another important consideration is the potential for misleading or incorrect responses generated by LLMs. Although overall accuracy rates were high, the presence of incorrect answers raises concerns regarding the risk of misinformation, particularly when these tools are used independently by learners. This underscores the importance of incorporating AI literacy into dental education, enabling students to critically appraise AI‐generated content and recognize its limitations.

This study has several limitations. First, although a rigorous translation process was applied, subtle linguistic differences may have influenced model interpretation of certain questions. Second, visual questions were presented only through textual descriptions, as current LLM interfaces do not support direct image processing. Therefore, the results may underestimate the performance of emerging multimodal models. Third, this study focused primarily on answer accuracy and did not evaluate the explanatory quality or pedagogical value of model responses.

Future research should focus on evaluating multimodal LLMs capable of directly processing visual data, as well as assessing the quality and educational usefulness of model‐generated explanations. In addition, incorporating learner‐centered outcomes, such as user confidence, learning efficiency and knowledge retention, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of LLMs in dental education.

In conclusion, large language models—particularly ChatGPT‐4.0—demonstrate considerable potential as supportive tools in dental education, especially for text‐based knowledge assessment. However, their current limitations in visual reasoning and variability across domains highlight the need for cautious and context‐aware integration into educational practice. Rather than replacing traditional learning methods, LLMs should be positioned as complementary tools within a structured and supervised educational framework.
