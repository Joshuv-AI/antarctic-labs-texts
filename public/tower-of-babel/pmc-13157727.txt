Model of care to promote recovery in older people with long COVID: findings from interviews and a co-design workshop
Authors: Kisani Manuel, Aaron Davis, Kate Little, Fanke Peng, Ian Gwilt, Kate Laver, Zoe Adey-Wakeling, Chantel Seaforth, Maria Crotty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to co-design a tailored model of care for older people with long COVID.DesignUsing a human-centred design approach, semistructured interviews were conducted with patients and health professionals from a long COVID service to explore their experiences. Insights were further developed during a co-design workshop involving patients, health professionals and community members who identified as older people and who had experience with chronic illness. Key themes were identified and used to map an ideal patient journey and inform the final model of care.SettingLong COVID outpatient service in a tertiary hospital in Adelaide, South Australia.ParticipantsFour patients and four health professionals participated in the interviews. The workshop included four patients, five health professionals and seven community members.ResultsThe co-design process identified challenges experienced by people with long COVID, including lack of validation, delayed multidisciplinary care, mental health deterioration and difficulties navigating the healthcare system. These challenges were described as having particular relevance for older adults. In response, a model of care was developed focused on comprehensive assessment, coordinated multidisciplinary care, education for self-management, mental health support and opportunities for research participation.ConclusionsA comprehensive and adaptable model of care is needed to address the complex and multifaceted nature of long COVID. This human-centred design approach ensured the model was grounded in lived experience, clinically informed and aligned with patient priorities. While not unique to older adults, the findings highlight areas that may require particular attention in this population, including care coordination, validation and support for comorbidities and social vulnerabilities. While developed in a single tertiary service, these principles may inform the design of services for similar populations in other healthcare settings.Keywords: Chronic Disease, COVID-19, Patient-Centered Care, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

Long COVID, also known as post-COVID-19 condition, refers to persistent health issues that continue after the acute phase of COVID-19. It is estimated that 5%–10% of individuals who recover from the acute infection experience long COVID, which can significantly impair daily life.1 2 Older adults, particularly those aged 65 and above, are especially vulnerable to the effects of persistent symptoms.3 4 Common symptoms include fatigue, shortness of breath, cognitive dysfunction, olfactory impairment, cough and myalgia, all of which impact daily functioning.5 The symptoms of long COVID can lead to deterioration in both physical and mental health, ultimately reducing overall quality of life.6 Furthermore, long COVID can diminish an individual’s ability to engage in physical activity or social interactions, which may lead to functional decline that could be irreversible.7 Given the heightened risk of adverse outcomes in older individuals, effective and acceptable care models are essential to optimise outcomes in this population.

In response to the emergence of long COVID, specialised care models have been rapidly developed and implemented in clinical practice. However, evidence regarding the most effective models of care for long COVID remains limited.8 A recent systematic review identified 49 models of care that have been implemented or proposed; all of which share five key principles: (1) a core ‘lead’ team with specialised expertise, (2) broad multidisciplinary involvement, (3) comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic services, (4) patient-centred, individualised and equitable care and (5) the capacity to meet demand.9

Optimal models of care for people with long COVID are likely to be dependent on both population characteristics and the specific context. Notably, no care models have yet been developed specifically for older individuals with long COVID. Acknowledging that older people are diverse in terms of their capabilities, this cohort may present unique challenges, including a higher burden of comorbidities, possible physical and cognitive frailty, social complexities and an increased need for social support. There is also limited understanding of physiological response to COVID in older people with the majority of studies involving adults aged <65. A tailored model of care is required to respond to this population’s varied and unique needs and preferences.

In 2022, the ‘Health+Long COVID Program’ by Conforma Health+in collaboration with the US Department of Health and Human Services created the Opportunities Framework.10 The Opportunities Framework, which was developed using a human-centred design approach, offers a structured set of recommendations for the care of individuals with long COVID. Human-centred design is an iterative, collaborative and people-focused approach to designing services and systems.11 Also known as design thinking, human-centred design is widely used in various fields such as healthcare, engineering and business12 and is increasingly recognised as an effective tool for addressing complex healthcare challenges. In addition to taking a human-centred approach, the use of co-design where patients, health professionals and stakeholders are involved in the design process ensures the development for more acceptable and useful services.13 14

Aligned with the Health+Long COVID Program’s service priorities, a human-centred co-design process was applied to develop a care model which met the needs of older adults within the Southern Adelaide Local Health Network (SALHN). The SALHN Long COVID Service, based within the Rehabilitation Medicine Department at Flinders Medical Centre (a tertiary hospital in South Australia), is one of several specialised long COVID clinics in Australia, with 16 such clinics offering dedicated services across the country.15 This multidisciplinary service includes a rehabilitation physician, psychiatrist, registered nurse, physiotherapist, occupational therapist and exercise physiologist. The aim of the service is to provide expert multi-disciplinary assessment and care to support recovery in people suffering from long COVID in the Southern Adelaide region. Since opening in 2022, the service has seen over 360 adults, with approximately 25% of patients being 65 years or older (range 18–89 years old).

Objectives

The aim of this project was to employ a human-centred design approach (that uses co-design strategies to integrate both clinician and patient perspectives) to inform, enhance and optimise the care model for older adults at the existing Long COVID Clinic at Flinders Medical Centre.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

People with lived experience, clinicians and researchers have identified service redesign as a priority in the field of long COVID care.16 People with lived experience of long COVID and clinicians working in the long COVID service were involved in study protocol development and were co-investigators on the funding application that enabled this work. We used the British Design Council’s double diamond design process17 18 to structure our community engagement, and involved health professionals, community members and patients of the long COVID Clinic as partners in all stages of the process described in this paper (from ‘Discover’ to ‘Deliver’). Participants

Three groups of participants were recruited to this study: health professionals, patients and community members. All health professionals who were working in the long COVID Clinic at the time of the study were invited to participate (n=5) in addition to six patients who had previously attended the Long COVID Clinic. Patients were eligible if they were community dwelling, had recently attended the clinic and had a diagnosis of long COVID made by a Rehabilitation Physician in the Long COVID Clinic. Patients were identified as suitable by their treating clinician if they fit eligibility criteria, were aged 50 years or older when they experienced long COVID, and did not have symptoms severe enough to hinder their participation. The treating clinician sought the person’s verbal agreement to be contacted by a member of the research team who then contacted the person to gain informed consent.

Community members were recruited through the Council of the Ageing. Eligibility criteria for community members included being aged 65 years or older, residing in metropolitan Adelaide, and either self-identifying as having long COVID or having chronic health conditions with experience accessing the Australian health system. All participants were provided with a study participant information sheet and gave written informed consent. To acknowledge the time and effort involved in participating, patients and community members were offered a small honorarium of $A35/hour for their time. Health professionals attended the interviews and workshops during working hours. Transport assistance, including parking or taxi vouchers, was arranged for participants to attend the workshop, and lunch was provided.

Interviews

We began by discovering and defining the problem (step 1 and 2 of the double diamond model, ‘problem space’) through interviews. Interviews were conducted with four patients and four health professionals. This sample size was considered sufficient to generate the initial journey map and identify key pain points, providing an adequate breadth of information for this exploratory phase of the study.19 20 The interviews were conducted via Zoom (video call) and recorded with current patients and health professionals of the Long COVID Clinic to understand how people with long COVID currently accessed care for their condition. Interviews with patients provided rich qualitative data about their experiences with the condition, their interactions with the health system and their needs and priorities. Health professionals from the Long COVID Clinic also gave insights into long COVID from a clinician’s perspective, as well as the current management approach to patients with long COVID. Interviews were transcribed using a computer-generated transcription and verified against the audio recording by author KLi (PhD, female). Transcripts were analysed using an inductive thematic analysis process (Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis framework)21 and codes used to develop a draft journey map which outlined a sequence of common occurrences and experiences for a patient with long COVID.

Workshop

A workshop was convened to review and explore the ‘problems’ and then develop, explore and refine solutions. The 4-hour workshop was led by an academic with extensive experience in group facilitation and co-design (AD, PhD, male) and engaged patients and health professionals together in workshop-style settings and activities. He was assisted by two other researchers (KLi, PhD, female and FP, PhD, female). The workshop was held in a large meeting room at Flinders Medical Centre and an additional meeting room was made available for any participant who needed a quiet space to rest during the session. The workshop size was designed to capture a range of perspectives and experiences while remaining sufficiently small enough to support and enable meaningful participation and active engagement of all participants during the workshop process.22

The workshop commenced with the facilitators introducing themselves and outlining the purpose and objectives of the session. This was followed by a presentation that established the context and explained the principles of co-design and human-centred service design. The facilitators then shared insights gathered from the interview process, detailing both the 'actual journey’ and the baseline 'ideal journey.’ Participants were guided through a series of interactive activities to complete the ‘define’ phase of the process, including journey mapping refinement, pain-point identification through a value proposition canvas template, and small group discussions, where they mapped challenges onto the baseline 'ideal journey’ service model. After this, the workshop shifted to the ‘develop’ stage of the process and in small groups, participants developed suggestions for service design improvements using a series of iterative brainstorming and ideation templates (see online supplemental materials 1). Opportunities for feedback were provided, allowing participants to share their ideas with the whole group. To ensure participants felt comfortable providing honest feedback in a safe and supportive environment, a participant-led documentation approach was employed, with each participant asked to document their own contributions and understandings of the discussion rather than relying on a scribe for interpretation. This process also allowed for anonymous and private contributions to the research team without the need for public discussion. In the final phase of the workshop, participants collaborated using the MoSCoW prioritisation framework to determine the key elements to include in the service design. MoSCoW (an acronym for Must-haves, Should-haves, Could-haves and Won’t-haves) is a prioritisation method that facilitates a filtering of the relative importance of service design suggestions.23 Participants were directed to review their ideas and discussions and to align these with the MoSCoW framework, with preference given to a higher level of categorisation. That is, rather than seeking consensus through a majority-based process, ideas were placed into the highest categorisation desired by any group member. This process was designed to enable highly-specific needs or preferences of individuals to be considered in the service design process rather than designing only for a majority population.

Analysis of results

Following the workshop, all data were transcribed. This included annotations on printed journey maps and completed participant-led documentation templates. Additions to the journey map were inductively coded and incorporated into a revised version of the journey map.

A hybrid inductive and deductive thematic analysis process24 was used to group ideas and responses, and align these (or propose new categories) to the existing Health+Long COVID service design framework. Ideas were then categorised according to where the locus of control would sit. Service or spatial design ideas and suggestions that were within the control of staff of the Long COVID Service or the Hospital were categorised as ‘control’. ‘Influence’ includes opportunities that require collaboration with other areas of the health system, as well as family and community members. ‘Advocacy’ includes opportunities that require significant collaboration or decision making at scale that are outside of the scope of the Long COVID Service, including those reliant on social and societal shifts. The MoSCoW prioritisation described above was used to select ideas for inclusion in the final recommendations, with must-haves and should-haves included in the final report.

Results

Interview (‘problem space’)

A total of eight participants were involved in interviews (four health professionals and four female patients of the Long COVID Clinic aged between 64 and 80 years old). One of the health professionals who works in the clinic and was invited was unable to attend an interview due to time constraints and two of the patients who were invited declined due to ill health or unavailability. Interviews were conducted through videoconferencing (via Zoom) with each interview lasting approximately 30 min.

Interview data revealed several common key themes that are particularly relevant to the development of a comprehensive model of care for older individuals with long COVID. These themes highlight recurring challenges faced by older people with long COVID and offer insights that may be particularly relevant for older adults living with long COVID.

Theme 1: lack of belief and compassion

One of the most frequently reported issues was the frustration patients faced when their condition was not believed or supported by friends, family, employers or even some healthcare professionals. This lack of validation significantly impacted their mental and emotional well-being.

I think the biggest challenge is just convincing people that there is long COVID. I mean, I actually had someone say to me, ‘Well, are you sure you got COVID? You haven’t got something else?’ I think the hardest thing really was [not] being understood and believed. (Patient)

Theme 2: need for education and support

Both patients and health practitioners emphasised the importance of education and ongoing support throughout their illness. However, they found that finding and accessing high-quality resources was challenging. Social media became a common source of information, but it wasn’t always helpful. While some patients felt a sense of belonging in online communities, others found it risky due to misinformation and unregulated content.

There’s a lot of dubious information online and it is unregulated. [Although] that shared experience can be a benefit to people that don’t feel heard. (Health Professional)

Theme 3: psychological impact and mental health support

All participants noted the significant psychological impact of long COVID, which exacerbated the physical challenges of the condition. This includes mental health struggles such as depression and anxiety.

I think definitely [counselling] is something that should be offered to people because, to get your head around the whole thing, and how it profoundly affects your lifestyle and your everything, is really important. (Patient)

Theme 4: multidisciplinary approach and early intervention

Both health professionals and patients emphasised the importance of a multidisciplinary care team, with a particular focus on the need for early intervention and specialised support. There was concern that patients were often not referred to multidisciplinary care (such as occupational therapy and fatigue management) until months after the onset of long COVID symptoms.

We know that there is such a huge role for education, especially through the OTs with fatigue management and addressing lifestyle factors. So really, they shouldn’t be experiencing long COVID for 12 months and then being referred to a COVID clinic and then being on another wait list before they get access to this quality information, it needs to get to them earlier. (Health Professional)

Theme 5: access to care and system navigation issues

Participants highlighted various logistical challenges, including long waiting lists, difficulties with booking appointments, and poor access to care (eg, parking and transport issues). These barriers delayed patients’ access to appropriate treatment and added frustration to an already challenging journey.

To actually physically get there [was a challenge] because I thought, if I have to park too far away there’s no way I was going to be able to make it there. (Patient)

Qualitative data were used to create an ideal patient journey map (see online supplemental materials 1). The ideal patient journey was mapped based on the Health Plus Long COVID Human-Centred Design Report,10 which considers both the qualitative interview data and necessary adaptations to the local health system. The ideal journey map outlined a comprehensive and idealised experience for older individuals with long COVID. The journey was divided into five key areas: (1) Initial symptoms and Careseeking, (2) Diagnosis and referral to long COVID Clinic, (3) Experience of long COVID Clinic, (4) Access to treatments and (5) Discharge. Each of these had multiple ‘Pain points’ (experiences that deviated from the ‘ideal journey’) and associated improvement opportunities. In presenting these results, participant quotes were added to add depth and context to each stage of the journey (see online supplemental materials 2).

Workshop (‘solution space’)

The workshop was held at Flinders Medical Centre in May 2024. Participants included four patients from the Long COVID Clinic (the same patient participants from the interviews), five health professionals (a physiotherapist, an occupational therapist, an exercise physiologist, a registered nurse and a rehabilitation medicine physician; three females and two males), and eight community members (six women and two men). One of the community members recruited was unable to attend on the day of the workshop. All the interview participants (four patients and four health professionals) attended the workshop. The session was attended by three service design researchers and workshop facilitators (AD, KLi and FP), as well as two clinician researchers (KM and MC), who were also involved in the study.

The workshop was 4 hours in duration (which included multiple rest breaks). A wide range of opportunity areas for improvement were identified and many of these closely aligned to the Opportunities Framework developed by Health+.10 Two novel categories were also identified, in the areas of mental health support and general service design. While the study focused on older adults, the themes identified were not always unique to this population but reflect broader experiences of people living with long COVID. However, these issues were often described as having particular relevance or added impact in older individuals. Community members contributed to workshop discussions and refinement of themes, and their perspectives were included in the analysis, although illustrative key quotes are presented from patient and health professional participants.

Ten overarching areas were identified and are outlined below (quotes presented were obtained from either the interviews or workshop):

General public awareness and validation

Participants described a lack of public awareness of long COVID, which contributed to feelings of dismissal, isolation and disbelief. Many reported that their symptoms were frequently minimised or compared unfavourably with more visible or severe illness experiences, leading to expectations to ‘push through’ rather than being supported. The condition was widely characterised as poorly understood, variable and difficult to explain, even by those experiencing it, which often resulted in participants avoiding discussions about their health altogether.

The condition is just so baffling that people don’t understand it. I mean, I don’t understand it! So, trying to explain it to people, how it affects you, is very confusing and they struggle with it, so most of the time you just don’t end up talking about it. (Patient)

Long COVID was perceived to elicit limited empathy or social recognition, reinforcing a sense of illegitimacy. Several participants highlighted the emotional burden of repeatedly having to justify the existence of their condition, including being questioned about whether they had truly had COVID-19. This lack of understanding and belief was compounded by ongoing uncertainty about symptom recurrence, with prior experiences of long COVID heightening anxiety about future illness. Overall, participants emphasised the need for greater community-level awareness and validation to improve social understanding and support for people living with long COVID.

Personal support systems

Participants reported substantial variability and frequent inadequacy in personal support systems following the onset of long COVID. Participants, particularly older adults, described profound isolation, with limited understanding or support from family and friends, leading some to withdraw from discussing their condition altogether. While partners, most commonly spouses, were often identified as the primary or sole source of support, this support was sometimes strained by disbelief, emotional burden or difficulties coping with the fluctuating and invisible nature of symptoms.

I suppose really, if I’m honest, I’ve had no support as such. I mean, as I say, my husband. But it’s different. (Patient)

When I am not well, he doesn’t really cope that well. (Patient)

The impact of long COVID extended beyond the individual, disrupting family roles, social participation and shared activities, and at times generating guilt and frustration for both patients and their partners.

I think it was probably a bit of a shock to both of us. But it impacted his life too because we just couldn’t go out to friends, or play something, I had brain fog. (Patient)

In contrast, participants noted that when family members were informed and involved, such as attending clinical appointments, support was perceived as more effective and validating.

I’ve had patients where their partner or family is sitting next to them with the phone call, which is absolutely…in fact, I usually say to them, I think it’s a good thing. (Health professional)

Overall, the findings highlighted the need for healthcare services to actively involve patients’ close support networks when appropriate to strengthen engagement with care.

Workplace accommodations and understanding

Participants described workplace accommodations (for both paid work and volunteer roles) and understanding as highly variable, with challenges that may be amplified in older adults. Many reported pressure to return to work prematurely following acute infection, based on assumptions of full recovery, which often preceded symptom exacerbation, ‘crashes’ and repeated periods of sick leave.

Attempts to resume preillness roles were described as physically and cognitively overwhelming, with consequences extending beyond volunteer or paid employment to family and broader life roles. For some, the inability to sustain roles led to significant emotional distress, including grief associated with relinquishing valued jobs and professional identities.

Participants also described the ongoing challenge of adjusting expectations and accepting reduced capacity, necessitating renegotiation of both paid work and unpaid domestic labour. These adaptations often required visible changes, such as delegating household tasks, which at times facilitated greater understanding from partners.

I said to [my husband], ‘We’re going to have to get a house cleaner. I cannot vacuum. I just can’t do this anymore, [husband’s name].’ I think then he could see. Mentally, I was struggling too. He said, ‘We don’t have to get a cleaner in to vacuum.’ And I said, ‘Well, if you vacuum, I can still do the other bits and pieces. The house isn’t going to be up to my standard, but it’ll be alright.’ So, we sort of got through, that way. (Patient)

Mental health support

Participants described significant psychological impacts associated with living with long COVID, including persistent low mood, emotional exhaustion and uncertainty about the future. Distress was frequently linked to the cumulative effects of prolonged symptoms, disrupted identities and difficulty adjusting to reduced capacity over time.

It just wasn’t me. I am one who gets up and does things. (Patient)

By the time individuals accessed post-COVID services, health professionals observed that many were experiencing frustration and a sense of hopelessness, particularly when symptom improvement had not occurred.

Frustrated, tired of feeling the way they do. I feel like a lot of them as well potentially have a sense of hopelessness. (Health professional)

While participants expressed a clear need for opportunities to talk through the emotional and relational consequences of long COVID, formal mental health support was frequently limited. As a result, health professionals reported routinely assuming informal counselling roles during initial consultations, requiring substantial time to address patients’ mental states, family dynamics and lived experiences before clinical assessment could proceed.

There is often a lot to wade through from a clinician’s perspective. They’ve got a lot to get off their chest that they have had to experience and go through to get to that point. (Health professional)

This highlights the need for structured integration of mental health supports within long COVID services.

System navigation

Patients and health professionals described navigating long COVID services as a prolonged and effortful process, characterised by delayed access to information, fragmented care pathways and reliance on individual advocacy. Diagnosis was commonly described as a process of exclusion, involving extensive investigations and prolonged symptom monitoring to meet formal criteria, which often resulted in frustration when test results returned as normal despite ongoing symptoms. These delays meant that many individuals lived with unmanaged symptoms for months before accessing specialist services, contributing to fatigue, uncertainty and reduced capacity to self-advocate.

We know that there is such a huge role for education, especially through the OTs with fatigue management and addressing lifestyle factors. So really, they shouldn’t be experiencing long COVID for 12 months and then being referred to a COVID clinic and then being on another wait list before they get access to this quality information, it needs to get to them earlier. (Health professional)

Health professionals emphasised the potential benefits of earlier access to education, particularly regarding fatigue management and lifestyle adaptations, which was perceived as arriving too late for many patients. Access to specialist services was further shaped by health literacy and practitioner advocacy, raising concerns that some individuals who could benefit from support were not being captured within existing pathways.

You’re really relying on practitioners to advocate for patients, and a lot of our patients have better health literacy, which makes me think that we’re probably missing out on a proportion [who could benefit from the service]. (Health professional)

Overall, participants emphasised the value of early education and coordinated care to reduce delays in treatment and improve outcomes.

Service design

Participants and clinicians identified multiple service design features that posed challenges for people living with long COVID, with particular implications for older adults in the areas of physical access, cognitive load and care coordination. Attending in-person appointments was described as physically demanding, with some participants requiring assistance to manage transport and mobility demands.

To actually physically get there [was a challenge] because I thought, if I had to park too far away there’s no way I was going to be able to make it there. (Patient)

Initial clinic visits were often lengthy and information-dense, which clinicians and participants alike recognised as potentially overwhelming for individuals experiencing fatigue and cognitive impairment; several participants reported symptom exacerbation following appointments.

Often people say, ‘I had a crash after the appointment.’ (Patient)

Difficulties engaging with written materials and completing questionnaires, particularly when delivered in fixed formats, further contributed to cognitive burden. Health professionals highlighted tensions between the need to conduct comprehensive assessments and the risk of overwhelming patients, compounded by time constraints and administrative demands that reduced time available for therapeutic interaction.

I wish that I had a bit more time with patients in my initial review stage in person. But that’s a difficult one because you don’t want to overwhelm patients. (Health professional)

Fragmentation across services, including unclear points of contact and inconsistent information handover, was perceived to undermine continuity of care.

We are sort of separate in the hospital…There is opportunity there to be more coordinated potentially, physically and administratively. (Health professional)

There was a shared desire held by both patients and health professionals for more flexible, coordinated and accessible service design better aligning with the fluctuating capacities of people with long COVID.

Structural capacity

Participants highlighted structural capacity constraints that limited both access to care and sustainability of long COVID services. From a patient perspective, the financial burden associated with ongoing care, including specialist consultations, medications not government-subsidised and general healthcare access, was described as a significant stressor, particularly for those on fixed or limited incomes.

The expense of the whole thing is big. I mean, my husband and I live on the aged pension and we rent. Budget is tight and with this condition, on several medications, 2 or 3 not on the PBS (Patient)

Health professionals reported capacity limitations within services, including insufficient staffing and funding, which constrained the number of new patients that could be accepted and contributed to growing review caseloads and wait times. The delivery of long COVID clinics was also described as placing additional pressure on existing workforce capacity, with clinicians often diverting time from other clinical roles. Beyond healthcare services, participants described difficulties accessing publicly funded support for domestic tasks, further compounding functional and financial strain.

I looked into getting some help [with domestic tasks] through ‘My Aged Care’ (the Australian Government’s portal for publicly funded aged care and in-home support services) but that was a fruitless endeavour because they give you a long list of people to call and nobody’s got availability. So, I just ended up letting that go. (Patient)

Preparation for healthcare providers

Participants described considerable variability in the knowledge of primary care providers regarding long COVID, which often delayed referral to specialist services. Some participants reported initial dismissal of symptoms or misattribution to lifestyle factors, requiring repeated consultations before a referral was made.

I’ve gone to see my doctor and I did say have I got long COVID because I’m feeling tired, I’ve got no energy. And she said, no, it’s exercise. You need exercise. She ended up saying, ‘Okay, I don’t think you have, but I’ll send a referral in.’ (Patient)

Health professionals highlighted that awareness of Long COVID clinics and referral pathways was uneven across primary care providers, with some general practitioners demonstrating familiarity and openness to the diagnosis while others lacked experience or understanding.

You do get a lot of referrals from the same GP practices. So, obviously [some are] a little bit more open to it as a diagnosis. I think that is a big barrier. It’s often a long road for people to get here, unfortunately. (Health professional)

The complexity and heterogeneity of symptoms further challenged primary care providers’ ability to recognise and support patients effectively. Overall, participants emphasised the need for targeted education and resources to equip healthcare providers with the knowledge and confidence to identify long COVID, facilitate timely referral and support patients’ ongoing care.

Discharge planning and handover

Health professionals highlighted uncertainty and inconsistency in discharge planning and handover processes for people with long COVID. Criteria for determining the appropriate timing for discharge were often unclear, with some patients remaining under follow-up for 1 to 2 years or longer.

At present, it is a little hazy as to what the discharge criteria are and what constitutes successful treatment. (Health professional)

Health professionals reported challenges coordinating care with private practitioners, noting that conflicting advice could undermine progress and that patients sometimes became overly reliant on online booking systems developed to compensate for limited administrative support.

You can get patients that become a bit over reliant on [the online booking system], booking in a little too frequently. (Health professional)

There was an identified need for clear discharge protocols, improved administrative processes and consistent, coordinated transitions.

Long COVID research, information and advocacy

Participants highlighted the dual role of social media and online communities in providing information and advocacy for people living with long COVID. These platforms were valued for peer support and shared experiences, particularly for individuals who felt unheard within formal healthcare settings. However, participants also noted limitations, including uneven access for those uncomfortable with social media, the predominance of more severe cases or louder voices shaping discussions, and the circulation of unverified or non-evidence-based information.

Social media has it’s pros and cons, but there is that community that people can tap into for some support. But then it [can be] a bit of an echo chamber, and the people with more severe symptoms, or people with more challenging experiences, are the ones that are loudest in that space. (Health professional)

Health professionals observed that trends in online discourse sometimes influenced patient inquiries about treatments or therapies, underscoring the need for accessible, reliable and evidence-informed resources alongside peer networks. Patients highlighted the importance of reliable, evidence-based information provided through healthcare services. While peer and online support were acknowledged, participants emphasised that professional guidance was critical in shaping their understanding and engagement with treatment.

Collectively, these findings reflect common challenges experienced by people with long COVID, while highlighting areas where impacts may be particularly pronounced for older adults.

Opportunities framework

Opportunities identified by participants as ‘must have’ or ‘should have’ for each of the ten overarching areas were aligned to a sphere of control (Control, influence or advocate). This categorisation allowed key elements to be prioritised according to feasibility and level of responsibility within the health service, and clarified where direct action, partnership or system-level advocacy would be required. These results are in online supplemental materials 3 .

Discussion

This human-centred design process supported the development of a tailored model of care for older individuals with long COVID with involvement from older people with lived experience of long COVID and health professionals who provide care for this cohort. The co-design group prioritised several key elements in the model, including the need for multidisciplinary care, mental health support, patient validation and coordination of care. These priorities reflect the complex needs of people living with long COVID, which may be particularly pronounced in older adults, a group that has been relatively underrepresented in long COVID research and service design to date. Although these priorities were identified by older people and the health professionals who care for them, they are likely to be applicable to the broader population including people younger than 65 years old.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to co-design a model of care with a specific focus on older individuals with long COVID. The human-centred design approach was chosen due to its effectiveness in evaluating and designing patient-centred care models.17 By actively involving patients, community members and health professionals, we gathered rich qualitative data regarding symptoms, experiences, priorities and the facilitators and barriers to care. This approach is particularly important given that long COVID is a relatively new condition, presenting substantial and often unique challenges. Use of interviews (to understand individual perspectives), an expert facilitator and involvement of more patients and community members than health professionals in the workshops reduced power imbalances and meant that the views and perspectives of people with long COVID and the community could be heard.

The findings of our study align with and build on existing research into experiences of long COVID. Patients with long COVID and health professionals described similar symptoms, frustrating interactions with health practitioners and negative impacts on daily life that have been reported in previous studies.25 26 People with long COVID want to be believed by their health professionals and have investigations and specialist reviews streamlined and coordinated.27,29 Patients reported significant challenges navigating a complex healthcare system, which is also a common issue faced by individuals with other chronic conditions and multimorbidity.28 30 These findings also resonate with other care models for long COVID (for people of all ages) that emphasise the importance of multidisciplinary care. Early multidisciplinary intervention can significantly improve self-management, reduce symptom burden and enhance quality of life for individuals of all ages with long COVID.31 32 Although older people are a heterogeneous group, the perspectives of patients and health professionals in this study were largely concordant, highlighting shared system-level challenges and care needs across people with long COVID rather than clearly distinct age-specific experiences.

One aspect of multidisciplinary care emphasised in this study was the inclusion of a team member who could provide adequate mental health support. This was a priority for participants who reported significant decline in mental health due to long COVID symptoms. Long COVID is known to be associated with depression and anxiety33 and the odds of patients with long COVID suffering a mental health condition have been shown to increase over time.34 People with long COVID have identified low levels of well-being, suicidal thoughts, anguish and anxiety and the need to seek help from mental health professionals.35 Based on these findings, it appears important that the multidisciplinary team include a psychologist, counsellor or trained occupational therapist who could provide cognitive behavioural therapy.

A second important aspect of addressing mental health and well-being for older people with long COVID is reducing the sense of loneliness and isolation people experience by connecting with others. Peer support, online support groups and group therapy sessions are all possible formats for addressing this unmet need. In other chronic conditions (both physical and mental health), peer support for older people has been found to improve engagement, improve feelings of loneliness and promote hope, empowerment and quality of life.36 37 The preference for peer support seemed to be in-person, which may reflect the preferences of the older population included in this study. Peer support that could be accessed through social media was felt by some people not to be helpful. This may reflect a preference among some older adults to socialise in-person, as socialising online can lead to greater levels of anxiety.38 39 Older adults have been reported to show a preference for health appointments to be in-person (or face-to-face) as compared with telehealth.40

Group rehabilitation sessions are another possible format for patients with long COVID to access therapy while also being given the opportunity to form peer connections. Groups facilitated by health professionals have been found to be beneficial for adults with long-term illness and improve pain, psychological symptoms, self-efficacy, self-care and quality of life.41 In the field of long COVID treatment, there have been some positive findings for psychotherapeutic/psychoeducational group therapy42 and a rehabilitation education and psychological support programme to date.43

The model of care developed through this co-design process applies to the South Australian health context; however, there are likely features that are transferrable to other contexts, particularly those with similar models of public healthcare. It should also be noted that ‘older persons’ constitute a heterogeneous group; therefore, the experiences explored in this study may have relevance beyond older populations to other people living with long COVID. Broader principles that spanned over multiple areas of this study included the importance of validation and support, multidisciplinary care, service flexibility, care coordination and clear expectation setting. While these elements may be relevant across age groups, their configuration and delivery may be particularly salient for older adults, who often experience greater care complexity and benefit from continuity, coordination and validation within healthcare encounters.44 While the ideal model of care for older people with long COVID is likely to be highly context dependent, these are principles that could be applied to service design in many different health services. Health system factors, resource availability, population and cultural considerations will all impact on what features of a model of care are more or less important in any given setting.

Older people with long COVID may experience additional vulnerabilities that shape their care needs. Participants described feelings of loneliness and lack of validation, particularly in the context of a new and poorly understood condition. For older adults, these experiences may be compounded by retirement, reduced social networks, multimorbidity, functional decline and reliance on complex health services.44 45 These factors informed elements of the model of care that may be particularly relevant for older adults, including emphasis on continuity, coordinated multidisciplinary input, in-person peer support and explicit validation of symptoms and experiences within clinical encounters.

There are some limitations in this co-design project which should be acknowledged. Recruitment of people with long COVID from the service was by convenience sampling, which may have resulted in a skewed population. We also included predominantly female participants and were without representation from culturally and linguistically diverse groups. The interviews and workshop only included a small number of participants and there may be other experiences and opinions held by older people with long COVID in Southern Adelaide that are not reflected here. The workshop included patients of the clinic and members of the treating therapy team and despite our best intentions, this may have caused some power imbalance impacting genuine or more critical participant responses. The findings are necessarily broad, which may enhance their relevance to other populations with long COVID; however, this breadth limits the identification of strategies tailored specifically to older people. While this study focused on older adults, the themes identified were broad and may not be unique to this population. This limits the extent to which age-specific conclusions can be drawn but supports the relevance of these findings to people with long COVID more broadly.

Conclusions

A comprehensive and adaptable model of care has the potential to address the complex needs of older people with long COVID. This study highlights actionable priorities for treatment, including comprehensive patient assessment, coordinated and multidisciplinary care, validation of patient experiences, mental health awareness and support, self-management education and opportunities to participate in research. While the model was developed within a single tertiary long COVID service, the principles identified may be transferable to other healthcare settings with similar structures, although specific operational details may not generalise to all regional systems. Further studies are needed to evaluate feasibility, adaptability and implementation across diverse contexts.
