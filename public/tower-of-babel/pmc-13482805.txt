Robust integrated multi-modal network design for green petroleum distribution under uncertainty
Authors: Mohammad Aghaei, Mahdi Alinaghian, Alireza Goli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Globally, a significant volume of petroleum products is transported daily through logistic networks to meet diverse regional demands, where unreliable or delayed delivery can lead to serious economic, social, and political consequences. The primary contribution of this research is the development of a comprehensive, robust multi-period mathematical model for the integrated planning of a green multi-modal petroleum product logistic network. This model advances current frameworks by simultaneously integrating pipeline, rail, and road transportation; synchronizing strategic facility development with operational flow allocation under uncertainty; and selecting the optimal network topology from both economic and environmental perspectives. It supports decisions on the location of distribution centers and the construction of pipelines and railways within budget constraints, aligning infrastructure investment with operational efficiency. A real-world case study in central Iran, solved via the Augmented Epsilon Constraint method, validates the approach. Targeted rail and pipeline investments reduce total transportation costs and cut CO₂ emissions by ~ 27.5% compared to the cost-only optimum. Out-of-sample tests across different uncertainty scenarios confirm the robust model’s superiority. It achieves 100% feasibility, vs. 50% for the nominal model, lowers average cost by 4.7%, reduces average emissions by 30.4%, and improves uncertainty regret indices by up to ~ 92%. These findings highlight the model’s resilience and ability to deliver sustainable, cost-effective petroleum logistics under real-world uncertainty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55078-8.

Introduction

The transportation sector holds such a special position in economic systems, both in production and services, that it commands a significant portion of any country’s national income. Due to the high volume of daily transfers of petroleum products worldwide, the issues related to the distribution of these products have gained significant importance in recent decades in the field of transportation1. As shown in Fig. 1, the daily consumption of oil and petroleum products has increased by approximately 35% from 2000 to 2023, reaching about 100 million barrels per day2.

Fig. 1Global consumption of oil and petroleum products from 2000 to 2023.

Global consumption of oil and petroleum products from 2000 to 2023.

In an era of escalating global uncertainties, from supply chain disruptions and geopolitical tensions to climate volatility, the efficient, resilient distribution of petroleum products is paramount for energy security, economic stability, and environmental sustainability. However, traditional models often overlook integrated multi-modal planning under uncertainty, leading to inflated costs, heightened emissions, and vulnerability to crises3. The research is essential to bridge these gaps, delivering a robust framework that minimizes economic and ecological impacts while ensuring reliable supply, ultimately safeguarding societies against the cascading consequences of unreliable petroleum networks.

Like other economic activities, selecting the most suitable and cost-effective method for transporting petroleum products to various storage, distribution, and consumption destinations has always been a focus of improvement efforts. In recent decades, environmental compliance, existing time and location constraints, the volume and quantity required for transportation, and more have greatly influenced the choice of transportation mode for petroleum products. The emergence of modern technologies and the uncertainty in the continued consumption of petroleum products in various sectors have affected the methods of storage and distribution of these products. Moreover, the lifespan of a chosen transportation mode and the necessary investment for its implementation are crucial considerations in selecting the transportation mode4.

On the other hand, the manner in which petroleum products are distributed is one of the most crucial factors affecting costs and energy consumption. The accessibility of customers to petroleum products is also of significant importance from political, economic, and even safety perspectives. For example, the disruption of necessary products to locations such as power plants can lead to reduced electricity production and plant shutdowns, imposing substantial costs on commercial and residential consumers. This not only causes social dissatisfaction but can also lead to potential risks, highlighting the importance of the timely supply of petroleum products1.

Today, transportation in the oil industry occurs through four modes: maritime, pipeline, rail, and road. For a more precise classification, it can be said that in the upstream oil sector, or exploration and production, most transfers are carried out via maritime routes or pipelines; however, in the downstream sector, particularly in the distribution and delivery of products, road and rail modes are predominantly used1.

Given that approximately 30% of greenhouse gas emissions are attributed to the transportation sector, in recent decades, with the high pollution levels in industrial cities, the pollution emissions of each of the mentioned modes have become crucial in selecting the transportation modes5. According to Fig. 2, which shows the carbon dioxide emissions produced by various transportation modes worldwide in 2022, the highest level of pollution is associated with road transportation6.

Fig. 2Global Carbon Dioxide Emissions from Different Transportation Modes in 2022.

Global Carbon Dioxide Emissions from Different Transportation Modes in 2022.

The pipeline mode, despite its operational limitations, has the lowest cost and environmental pollution compared to other modes. Additionally, road transportation, while offering fast delivery of goods and high accessibility and flexibility in choosing transportation routes, is suitable for transporting over short to medium distances. On the other hand, rail transportation, known as the best option for moving bulk goods over long distances, cannot handle door-to-door deliveries due to infrastructure limitations. This clearly indicates the necessity of using multiple transportation modes for the distribution of petroleum products7.

In this paper, we develop a comprehensive multi-period mathematical model to design an integrated and green multi-modal transportation network for petroleum products, incorporating pipeline, railway, and road modes. The main contributions and novel aspects of this work are fourfold: (1) it simultaneously integrates three transportation modes (pipeline, rail, and road) within a unified optimization framework; (2) it concurrently addresses the strategic planning of facility location and infrastructure development (for distribution centers, pipelines, and railways) with the operational planning of product flow allocation; (3) it explicitly incorporates uncertainty in key parameters to enhance the model’s robustness and practicality; and (4) it is implemented in Iran which selects the optimal network topology by evaluating both economic and environmental objectives. Overall, this research provides a holistic decision-support tool for the sustainable and resilient design of petroleum product supply chains. Literature review

The evolution of research on multi-modal transportation systems reflects a clear chronological progression in both conceptual focus and methodological sophistication. Initial scholarly attention emerged between 1990 and 2000, a foundational period during which researchers established core definitions and systematically analyzed the characteristics, advantages, and disadvantages of various transport modes9. The subsequent decade (2000–2010) saw a strategic shift toward enhancing process efficiency and economic effectiveness, with studies emphasizing service quality improvements, the integration of information and communication systems, and optimized logistics planning7. In recent years, the field has been dominated by advanced modeling and optimization approaches aimed at addressing the complex, real-world dimensions of combined transportation systems.

Within this broader trajectory, the specific application of multi-modal frameworks to hydrocarbon supply chains has generated a substantial and growing body of literature. The foundational work on terminal location within intermodal networks was conducted by Arnold et al.10,11, who first modeled the optimal placement of terminals in a combined rail-road network for goods transport, comparing direct road shipments with intermodal options. This model was later refined by representing terminals as network arcs to improve computational efficiency.

Focusing on crude oil, Shen et al.12 pioneered a mixed-integer linear model for routing crude from fields to refineries via maritime, pipeline, and land modes, employing metaheuristic solution techniques. Later, Kazemi et al.1 extended the focus to petroleum products, developing a mixed-integer model that incorporated pipeline, rail, maritime, and land transport while also making decisions on the location and capacity of distribution centers, demonstrated with U.S. industry data. The planning perspective was further advanced by Oke et al.13, who applied a game-theoretic equilibrium model to crude oil transport in North America, concluding that a combined rail-pipeline strategy reduced both costs and associated risks.

Model complexity increased with the incorporation of multiple objectives and uncertainty. Najmi et al.14 proposed a multi-period model for a biofuel supply chain using a pipeline-rail-road system, optimizing facility location and network flow. For hazardous materials like petroleum products, Ziaei and Jabarzadeh15 introduced a multi-objective robust model minimizing cost, risk, and pollution in a rail-road network, testing it on an Iranian case study. Addressing uncertainty, Pudasaini et al.16 developed a multi-objective, scenario-based model for Nepal’s oil supply chain, aiming to minimize costs and product losses.

Recent studies continue to expand the scope and realism of these models. Wang et al.17 presented a three-objective, four-level model for an oil supply chain employing pipeline, rail, road, and maritime transport, using fuzzy parameters for demand and costs, and validated it with data from Vietnam. Lima et al.18 formulated a mixed-integer model for a downstream network (refineries, warehouses, customers) incorporating rail, maritime, pipeline, and land transport, evaluated with Brazilian data. Freen et al.19 developed a fuzzy model for rail-pipeline-road distribution of petroleum products, determining optimal warehouse locations and capacities, and applied it to a case study in Pakistan.

Wei et al.20 developed a multi-modal transportation optimization model for refined oil, integrating pipeline, rail, and road transport with daily scheduling. Their case study in China demonstrated that the model effectively coordinates flows across different modes and reduces total transportation costs by 14.5%, highlighting its practical value for improving operational efficiency in petroleum logistics. Popa et al.21 conducted a risk-based optimization study of multi-modal oil product operations, employing simulation and workflow modeling across maritime, rail, road, and inland waterway transport. The findings indicate that this approach enhances operational efficiency, reduces delays, and strengthens the resilience of petroleum logistics networks.

Awan et al.22 evaluated disruption scenarios in the downstream oil supply chain using Monte Carlo simulation, explicitly modeling uncertainty in demand, supply, and multi-modal transportation. Their results show that incorporating uncertainty analysis improves resilience, lowers operational costs, and helps identify vulnerable points within the network, underscoring the importance of proactive risk management and integrated multi-modal planning. Finally, Vardan and Hosseini23 proposed a bi-objective optimization model for designing a green closed-loop petroleum supply chain, explicitly considering transportation constraints and integrating road, rail, and pipeline transport for both forward and reverse flows. The model simultaneously minimizes total costs and environmental impacts, such as emissions, and results from a case study demonstrate that it effectively balances economic efficiency with environmental sustainability, providing a practical framework for designing resilient and environmentally responsible petroleum supply networks.

Collectively, this literature demonstrates a clear advancement from foundational definitions to sophisticated, multi-objective optimization models that address cost, risk, environmental impact, and uncertainty within hydrocarbon logistics, consistently grounded in real-world case applications.

Research gaps and contributions

Based on a review of the literature, a comprehensive integration of the design of a multi-modal transportation network for petroleum products, as well as the development of network facilities and infrastructure, has not been adequately addressed. Moreover, the environmental objectives have been overlooked in selecting the optimal topology of the designed network, focusing only on economic goals. Solving the problem in an integrated approach, rather than using a hierarchical one that addresses network facility development and flow allocation separately, is expected to yield a better solutions.

This research addresses key gaps by developing a comprehensive model for green petroleum product transportation under uncertainty. It simultaneously considers multi-modal transportation (pipeline, rail, and road), integrates network facility development and operation, incorporates uncertainty, and selects the optimal transportation topology from an environmental and economic perspective. The model synchronizes the location and development of network infrastructure with flow allocation between existing facilities.

Accounting for both upfront construction costs of distribution centers, pipelines, and rail lines and subsequent operational transportation costs, the model concurrently optimizes facility location and infrastructure development decisions along candidate routes within available budget limits in each period, yielding cost-efficient network topologies. This is complemented by the application of a robust optimization framework to manage uncertainty in demand and CO₂ emission factors, as well as empirical validation via a detailed case study in central Iran, marking key advancements in resilient and practically relevant petroleum distribution planning.

Problem definition

The major activities in the petroleum product supply chain include the extraction of crude oil from oil fields, its transportation to crude oil storage tanks and refineries, refining and converting crude oil into petroleum products, distributing the products through the storage network, and sending them to consumer markets. The petroleum product supply chain is categorized into upstream, midstream, and downstream sections.

The upstream section involves the location of oil fields, the drilling of discovered oil fields, the extent of their exploitation, the planning of crude oil platforms, and the transportation of crude oil from the oil fields to storage facilities. The midstream section includes the transportation of crude oil from storage tanks to refineries, refining of crude oil, and its sale in the global market. Finally, the downstream activities involve the distribution of petroleum products such as various types of gasoline, diesel, kerosene, fuel oil, and jet fuel via pipeline, road, rail, and sea transport from refineries to distribution warehouses and from warehouses to demand points. The components of the downstream section of the petroleum product supply chain include refineries, distribution warehouses, and demand points24.

The issue addressed in this research is the design of a combined pipeline-rail-road transport network that considers both economic and environmental objectives in the downstream of the petroleum supply chain. The proposed combined transport network in this study pertains to the distribution of petroleum products to meet demand. The transport network under study is a three-tier network where cargo flow begins at refineries. Depending on the chosen transport topology, it reaches end consumers either directly or through distribution centers via a combination of pipeline, rail, and road transport. Considering that the establishment and expansion of new distribution centers and rail and pipeline lines, which are key facilities of the multi-modal transportation network under study, is a time-consuming process not feasible in the short term, a multi-period planning approach is used in the proposed mathematical model for developing the multi-modal transportation network. The study horizon in this research is five years, divided into ten semi-annual periods.

Additionally, due to the impact of numerous parameters on the consumption of petroleum products and the uncertainty in the demand values at consumption points over the long term, considering the uncertainty in demand at consumption points for each period is highly relevant. It brings the proposed model closer to reality. Furthermore, to address environmental considerations, in addition to transportation costs, the level of pollution emissions of each mode is also taken into account when selecting the transportation topology.

There are two primary methods for calculating CO₂ emissions in transportation networks: the fuel-based method and the distance-based method1. The fuel-based method estimates emissions by multiplying the amount of fuel consumed (e.g., in liters or gallons) by a mode-specific CO₂ emission factor (typically in kg CO₂ per unit of fuel), which accounts for combustion efficiency and fuel type1,5. However, this approach requires detailed data on fuel consumption rates, which can vary significantly based on vehicle load, speed, and terrain, making it data-intensive and less feasible for strategic-level planning with aggregate flows.

In contrast, the distance-based method, adopted in this research due to its alignment with available data on travel distances and emission coefficients, calculates emissions by multiplying the distance traveled (in km) by the quantity of product transported (in tons) and a mode-specific emission factor (in grams or kg CO₂ per ton-km). This method is particularly suitable for petroleum product distribution, where distances are well-documented in network topologies, and it simplifies integration into optimization models15. Specifically, for each transportation arc, emissions are computed as: Emissions = Quantity Transported× Distance × Emission Coefficient.

Constraints

The constraints of the proposed model are categorized into five groups: Demand satisfaction constraints, Flow balance constraints, Facility capacity constraints, Budget constraints, and Essential and logical constraints on decision variables. These constraints are detailed below:

Demand satisfaction constraints

Flow balance constraints

Constraint (4) enforces inventory balance at each distribution center for every product and period, stating that the incoming flows (from pipelines and prior inventory) must equal the outgoing flows (to consumers via road/rail) plus ending inventory. Dealing with Uncertainty

Several approaches have been developed to handle uncertainty in parameters within mathematical models, including stochastic optimization, fuzzy optimization, robust optimization, and hybrid approaches25. In this research, a risk-averse and conservative robust optimization approach is used to manage uncertainty and derive a deterministic equivalent of the proposed model. The source of uncertainty in this problem includes uncertainties in input data, such as demand at various destinations for different products and the pollution emissions from each mode of transportation.

In robust optimization, it is not necessary to know the distribution or membership function of the data; it is only required to specify the range of variation. By using this method, the objective function value for a robust solution will be feasible and close to its optimal value in all possible scenarios of uncertain parameters, with minimal deviation from the optimal state25. Therefore, considering the availability of historical data on uncertain parameters, this study employs a robust optimization approach based on interval uncertainty, aiming to find a solution that is insensitive to almost all parameters of uncertainty. To achieve this, future demand for each product is forecasted using several methods based on past multi-year data, and the upper and lower bounds of these forecasts are considered as the interval of uncertainty for the demand parameter.

Solution method

Various methods for solving multi-objective problems have been proposed in the literature. In this research, the augmented epsilon-constraint (AEC) method is used to address the multiple objective functions of the developed model. AEC method generates a Pareto front to obtain a set of efficient solutions for the multiple objective functions of the problem27. AEC method, which is an evolved version of the epsilon-constraint method, prevents the production of weakly efficient solutions and accelerates the solution process, thus avoiding the generation of redundant solutions28. Figures 5 and 6 illustrate the solution space in a problem with two objectives.

Figure 5 shows the solutions generated in the solution space using the epsilon-constraint method, while Fig. 6 shows the Pareto front obtained by removing weakly efficient solutions, demonstrating the efficient solutions using the improved epsilon-constraint method. To execute the improved epsilon-constraint method, the objective functions of the model are first solved separately for each objective function to obtain the boundary values. Then, the Pareto optimal solutions, which lie between these two boundary points, will be produced.

Case study

A real-world case in Iran has been selected as the multi-modal transportation network for this study to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed model. Iran is one of the largest producers of oil and petroleum products in the world, producing and distributing approximately 270 million liters of petroleum products daily across the country. Additionally, air pollution is a serious issue in Iran. On certain days, air pollution in cities like Tehran, Isfahan, Ahvaz, and Mashhad reaches such extreme levels that the government closes schools and restricts vehicle traffic. Therefore, it seems essential to develop a model for transporting petroleum products in Iran that reduces CO2 emissions.

Data sources and model parameters

The case study in this research involves the distribution of petroleum products, including gasoline, fuel oil, diesel, and kerosene, across the central region of Iran to meet the area’s internal needs. The data used in this study are derived from real operational records provided by the National Iranian Petroleum Refining and Distribution Company (NIPRDC), which is the primary authority responsible for petroleum product distribution across Iran.

According to Fig. 7, this case study includes 3 refineries (Tehran, Isfahan, and Arak), 8 distribution centers (6 existing and 2 proposed for construction), and 17 demand points. There is the possibility of direct rail and road transportation from the refineries to several demand points. Additionally, there is the potential to construct new rail routes between some distribution warehouses and demand points, as well as new rail lines between the refineries and several distribution warehouses.

This section first analyzes the parameters for model robustness and determines the appropriate values for the related parameters. Validation of the robust optimization approach

To validate the proposed robust optimization approach for handling uncertainty in demand and CO₂ emission coefficients, an out-of-sample evaluation method is applied by inspiring Rahbari et al.29. This approach assesses the robust model’s performance against the deterministic (nominal) model under realized uncertainty scenarios, focusing on feasibility robustness (ability to satisfy constraints) and optimality robustness (minimal degradation in objective values). The validation demonstrates that the robust model provides reliable solutions with a small conservatism premium, making it superior for real-world volatile conditions in petroleum distribution. The validation process is as follows:

Step 1: Solve the nominal model (Γ = 0 for all uncertain parameters) and the robust model (selected Γ values: 0.4 for demand, 0.5 for emissions) to obtain first-stage decisions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{ij}^{t}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{j}^{qt}$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{Z}_{im}^{t}$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{W}_{jm}^{t}$$\end{document}).

Step 2: Generate 10 out-of-sample scenarios by randomly sampling uncertain parameters: demand \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{m}^{lt}$$\end{document} from a uniform distribution within ± 15% of nominal values (a realistic deviation based on petroleum demand volatility); road emissions pole from the range [88, 94] g/ton-km; rail emissions pols from the range [29, 35] g/ton-km.

Step 3

For each scenario, fix the first-stage decisions and re-solve the model as a linear program to compute realized objectives Z1 (cost) and Z2 (emissions). If infeasible, apply a penalty to make it feasible (large cost addition for Z1 and emission equivalent for Z2), yielding adjusted values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O{F}^{N}$$\end{document}.

Comparison of solutions

According to the results presented in Table 15, the level of pollution emissions and the share of different transport modes in single-objective and multi-objective solutions differ. As expected, when the objective is solely to reduce pollution emissions (Z2), the CO2 emissions are lower compared to other cases. Additionally, comparing the use of transport modes shows that when the objective is to reduce transportation costs solely, the share of road transport is higher than that of other modes. The reason for the higher share of road transport, despite the lower costs of rail and pipeline transport, is that in the network under study, unlike road transport, direct rail transfer from refineries to many consumption points is not feasible. This aligns with findings in the literature, where economic-driven models often prioritize road transport for its flexibility and immediate availability, even though it leads to higher emissions1,15,17. On the other hand, when the goal is to reduce pollution emissions, the share of pipelines is higher than other transport modes due to their negligible pollution emissions, consistent with studies emphasizing modal shifts to pipelines and rail for environmental benefits in petroleum logistics17,31.

On the other hand, there is a Pareto solution where both CO2 emissions and total costs are simultaneously considered. The results comparing the best Pareto solution with the extreme solutions show that the share of pipelines in this Pareto solution is greater than in the economic solution and less than in the environmentally friendly solution. Similarly, in the Pareto solution, the share of road transport is between the shares in the economic and environmentally friendly solutions. These intermediate modal shares reflect the inherent trade-offs in multi-objective optimization, as demonstrated in similar multi-objective optimization frameworks for oil supply chains, where balanced solutions achieve significant emission reductions (e.g., 27–51%) with minimal cost increases through integrated modal planning31,32.

Ultimately, it can be concluded that extreme solutions, due to their strong focus on either economic or environmental aspects, may not be feasible in the real world, as they often overlook practical constraints like infrastructure availability or budget limits7,17. In contrast, the proposed solution from the Pareto set, which simultaneously considers both economic and environmental aspects, can be recommended as a suitable option for decision-makers in this field, echoing reviews of sustainable supply chain optimization that advocate Pareto fronts for resilient, real-world applicability19,32.

Discussion

The findings presented in the preceding sections offer significant insights into the practical trade-offs and strategic implications of designing a sustainable multi-modal petroleum distribution network. This section interprets these results, discussing their alignment with real-world logistics challenges and deriving actionable guidance for decision-makers.

The analysis confirms a fundamental and expected conflict between the two primary objectives: minimizing total network cost and minimizing CO2 emissions. The extreme solutions, where each objective is optimized individually, reveal the limitations of a single-focus strategy. The purely economic solution heavily relies on road transport due to its immediate availability and direct routing capabilities within the existing Iranian network topology. Conversely, the environmentally friendly solution necessitates a substantial shift toward pipeline and rail transport, which requires significant upfront capital investment in infrastructure. This dichotomy underscores a central reality in sustainable supply chain design: environmental gains are often contingent upon strategic, long-term capital expenditure.

The real-world value of the model is clearly demonstrated by its Pareto-optimal solutions, which deliver a flexible range of compromise options that decision-makers can select from based on their specific priorities for cost and carbon emissions. The selected optimal solution, identified using the crowding distance metric, achieves a notable reduction in emissions, approximately 27.5% lower than the economic solution, with a negligible increase in total cost of only about 0.006%. This result is particularly compelling for policymakers and logistics managers, as it demonstrates that considerable environmental benefits can be secured without disproportionate economic sacrifice. The underlying driver of this efficiency is the model’s integrated approach to simultaneous infrastructure development and flow allocation, allowing it to identify configurations where new rail and pipeline segments unlock more sustainable and cost-effective routing patterns over the multi-period horizon.

Furthermore, the successful application of robust optimization enhances the model’s practical utility. By accommodating demand and emission coefficient uncertainties within a defined budget of uncertainty (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:$$\end{document}), the model generates plans that are resilient to parameter fluctuations. This is a crucial feature for the petroleum sector, where demand and emission factors can be volatile. The minimal premium paid for this robustness, only a slight increase in total cost, makes a compelling case for its adoption over deterministic planning, which risks being obsolete under real-world variability.

From a managerial perspective, several important insights emerge. First, the results strongly advocate for proactive investment in rail and pipeline infrastructure as a lever for both cost control and emission reduction. The sensitivity analysis clearly shows that increased budgetary allocation enables the construction of more rail and pipeline links, which in turn systematically reduces both the objective function values over time. This challenges the short-term perspective often prevalent in infrastructure planning, highlighting the long-term payoffs of green investments. Second, the model underscores the importance of modal integration. The optimal solutions consistently utilize a mix of all three modes, leveraging the cost advantage of pipelines, the medium-distance efficiency of rail, and the flexibility of road transport for last-mile delivery. This balanced approach mitigates the inherent weaknesses of any single mode.

Finally, the research situates itself within the broader literature by addressing identified gaps. It moves beyond models that either focus solely on economic factors or treat infrastructure as static. By unifying strategic facility location, multi-modal network design, environmental optimization, and uncertainty management in a multi-period framework, this work provides a more holistic decision-support tool. It confirms earlier findings on the benefits of intermodality while extending them into the realm of robust, green logistics planning for petroleum products, offering a validated approach for regions aiming to decarbonize their transportation logistics without compromising supply reliability or economic viability.

Conclusion

This study presents a robust multi-period mixed-integer linear programming model for the integrated design of a green multi-modal petroleum distribution network incorporating rail, road, and pipeline transportation. From a theoretical perspective, the model makes several key contributions: it simultaneously optimizes economic and environmental objectives within a unified framework; integrates strategic decisions on facility location and infrastructure expansion with operational flow allocation under uncertainty; and employs a robust optimization approach to handle fluctuations in demand and emission parameters, thereby enhancing the model’s applicability to real-world volatile conditions.

On a practical level, the model was validated through a real-world case study involving the distribution of gasoline, fuel oil, diesel, and kerosene across central Iran. The results confirm the model’s effectiveness in generating a Pareto-optimal set of feasible solutions, enabling decision-makers to balance cost and emission reductions. Notably, the analysis demonstrated that strategic investment in rail and pipeline infrastructure can significantly lower CO₂ emissions by approximately 27.5% in the selected Pareto solution, with only a marginal increase in total network cost (about 0.006%). These findings underscore the model’s value as a decision-support tool for policymakers and logistics managers seeking to design resilient, green supply chains without compromising economic feasibility.

Limitations and future research directions

While this research provides a comprehensive framework for strategic network design, certain limitations offer pathways for future inquiry. The model operates at a strategic and tactical planning level, focusing on facility location, infrastructure development, and aggregate flow allocation. Consequently, it does not address the subsequent operational problem of vehicle routing for hazardous materials (HAZMAT) transportation, which involves determining specific vehicle schedules, routes, and loading plans while considering real-time road conditions, traffic, and strict safety regulations. This level of detail is crucial for practical implementation, as routing decisions directly impact accident risk, community exposure, and operational compliance.

Future research could extend this work in several valuable directions. First, integrating a detailed HAZMAT vehicle routing and scheduling model into the proposed multi-modal framework would bridge the gap between strategic network design and operational execution33. Second, incorporating resilience metrics and explicit disruption scenarios would enhance the model’s applicability in risk-prone environments. Third, expanding the modal options to include inland waterways or coastal maritime transport could make the framework relevant for different geographical contexts. Fourth, the social dimension of sustainability, including quantified risks to population centers from transporting hazardous materials, could be integrated as an additional objective. Finally, the development of advanced decomposition algorithms or novel metaheuristics would be beneficial to solve large-scale instances of this complex integrated problem within a reasonable computational time.
