Multi-modal integration of histopathology and transcriptomics reveals STAB1+ macrophage-associated efferocytosis as a suppressive immune mechanism in colon adenocarcinoma
Authors: Zhanhao Chang, Chongli Zhong, Shuo Xu, Yuyang Zhang, Xingqi Guo, Jielin Yu, Zitong Xu, Shukun Han, Bing Han, Chao Lv, Yu Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColon adenocarcinoma (COAD) has a limited response to immunotherapy due to its immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME). Efferocytosis is an important process that regulates the TME, but its mechanism and clinical significance in COAD are unclear.MethodsWe integrated histopathological images, transcriptomic profiles, and clinical data from 387 COAD patients. Image features were extracted using ResNet50 and CellProfiler, followed by construction of a multimodal machine learning model to evaluate prognostic risk. We further combined bulk RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomics to comprehensively characterize efferocytosis-associated immune cell subsets and signaling pathways.ResultsThe efferocytosis-based risk model demonstrated strong prognostic performance across multiple time points and remained independent of conventional clinical indicators. Mechanistically, we identified a subset of STAB1+ tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) enriched in COAD tumors, exhibiting enhanced efferocytosis activity, M2-like polarization, and mTORC1 signaling activation. In vitro, STAB1 expression was essential for IL-4-induced M2 polarization, and its inhibition attenuated the formation of immunosuppressive TAMs. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses revealed that this macrophage population was transcriptionally distinct and increased in abundance following neoadjuvant therapy.ConclusionThis study establishes a multimodal prognostic system that integrates histopathological imaging with molecular profiling, and for the first time reveals the pivotal role of STAB1+ TAMs in orchestrating the immunosuppressive TME via efferocytosis and mTORC1 activation. Our findings provide both a clinically applicable risk assessment tool and a potential therapeutic target. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), encompassing both colon and rectal cancers, is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), the predominant anatomical subtype of CRC, has exhibited a steady rise in incidence, especially among younger patients. Owing to its insidious onset, COAD is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, limiting the potential for curative surgical intervention [2–4]. Although treatment modalities—including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [5, 6]—have significantly evolved, their clinical efficacy remains suboptimal in patients with microsatellite stable (MSS) tumors [7, 8]. Furthermore, many regimens are accompanied by substantial side effects and acquired resistance, posing major challenges to COAD management [9, 10].

Multiple studies indicate that the tumor microenvironment (TME) in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) is often immunologically ‘cold.’ It is characterized by inefficient tumor antigen presentation, limited infiltration of effector T cells, and an accumulation of immunosuppressive myeloid populations—most prominently tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [11–15]. Collectively, these characteristics compromise the effectiveness of immunotherapeutic interventions. Among these immunosuppressive mechanisms, efferocytosis—a macrophage-driven process that clears apoptotic cells—has garnered increasing attention [16, 17]. By suppressing tumor-associated antigen (TAA) presentation, enhancing M2-like polarization, and activating immunosuppressive signaling, efferocytosis markedly diminishes tumor immunogenicity, thereby facilitating tumor progression and metastasis [18–20].

Although efferocytosis is a promising therapeutic target in COAD, prospectively identifying patients who are likely to benefit remains challenging in routine practice. The main barriers are the cost, limited availability, and workflow burden of large-scale, high-quality transcriptomic profiling.

Recent advances in statistical methodology and machine learning are accelerating the move toward personalized care [21–23]. In parallel, the rapid progress of digital pathology has opened new avenues to address this need [24–27]. Integrating deep learning on H&E-stained slides with traditional image analysis enables the extraction of biologically relevant features from routine histopathological images, thereby facilitating disease classification and risk stratification [28–30]. In this study, we integrated histopathological images, transcriptomic profiles, and clinical data from 387 COAD patients. We extracted multi-scale image features using ResNet50 and CellProfiler, and developed a multimodal risk scoring model associated with efferocytosis by evaluating 100 machine learning algorithm combinations. This model integrates the molecular-level precision of transcriptomic data with the clinical applicability of histopathological imaging, demonstrating strong prognostic accuracy and utility in patient stratification.

In addition, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics to systematically characterize the transcriptional profiles and spatial distribution of efferocytosis-associated immune cell subpopulations. Notably, we identified a population of STAB1+ macrophages enriched in tumor regions that play a central role in mediating immunosuppression and promoting M2-like polarization. We systematically investigated of different therapeutic regimens on the efferocytosis-associated immune landscape using single-cell transcriptomic data generated from neoadjuvant-treated COAD samples. These findings offer valuable insights into treatment-induced immunological remodeling and enhance our understanding of immune response mechanisms.

Material and methods

Slide acquisition and quality control

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) whole-slide images (WSIs) from the TCGA-COAD cohort were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) using the GDC Data Transfer Tool. After removing duplicate uploads and non-tumor control slides, a total of 833 diagnostic WSIs were retained for downstream analysis. All slides were scanned at either 40× or 20× magnification and saved in SVS format.

Tissue detection and tile generation

WSIs were accessed using OpenSlide (v3.4.1), and tissue regions were identified using Histolab (v0.7.0) via adaptive Otsu thresholding in the HSV color space in Python 3.8. Each tissue mask was dilated with a 3 × 3 kernel, and small holes ( < 1 × 103 µm2) were filled to ensure tissue continuity. From the resulting tissue regions, non-overlapping tiles of 512 × 512 pixels were extracted. Tiles containing < 60% tissue content or > 20% artefacts (e.g., folds, pen marks) were discarded.

Feature extraction of pathological tiles

For each WSI, image features were extracted from the remaining tiles using a ResNet-50 model pretrained on ImageNet. In parallel, the selected tiles were analyzed with CellProfiler 4.2.4 using two custom pipelines. Briefly, hematoxylin and eosin stains were separated using a color unmixing module; nuclei were segmented using adaptive thresholding followed by watershed refinement; cytoplasmic and stromal compartments were approximated using propagation methods. Extracted features included intensity (e.g., mean/median optical density, Haralick textures), morphology (e.g., nuclear area, eccentricity, solidity), and spatial statistics (e.g., nearest neighbor distances, Moran’s I). All features were exported as CSV files and Z-score normalized across the cohort prior to integration with bulk RNA-seq data. Detailed pipelines and parameter settings are provided in Additional File 1: Supplementary Methods.

Feature selection and correlation with gene programs

Transcriptomic data (TPM format) were retrieved from the TCGA GDC portal. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) scores for curated hallmark gene sets were computed using the GSVA R package (v1.46.0). Spearman correlations were calculated between each pathological feature and each gene set score. Features with significant correlations (p < 0.05) were retained for model development. To further refine the feature set, elastic net regularization (glmnet, α = 0.2) was applied to select robust and non-redundant features.

Survival modelling

Samples with available overall survival (OS) data and status were randomly split into training and validation sets at an 8:2 ratio. Univariate Cox regression (p < 0.05) was used to screen prognostic features, which were then input into the MIME1 framework for risk score construction. A wide range of machine learning (ML) models were tested, and the final signature was derived from the combination of StepCox[both] and random survival forest (RSF) models. Risk scores were further validated in the independent set, and their predictive performance was evaluated by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) and concordance index (C-index) at 1, 3, and 5 years.

Gene module correlation and hub identification

Spearman correlations were computed between final model features and gene expression levels from a curated panel of immune genes. Significant correlations were visualized as network graphs using the ggraph package. Hub genes were identified based on degree centrality and selected for downstream biological interpretation.

Acquisition of single-cell and bulk RNA-seq data

Publicly available single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; accession number GSE161277), and bulk RNA-seq data were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas–Colon Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-COAD) cohort. Spatial transcriptomic data for colon cancer were accessed through the BioProject database (accession number: OEP001756).

In addition, we generated novel scRNA-seq data from five surgically resected COAD tumor specimens collected after different neoadjuvant treatment regimens, including (1) chemotherapy alone, (2) chemotherapy plus PD-1 blockade, (3) chemoradiotherapy, (4) chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab, and (5) no pre-treatment. Sample processing and sequencing were performed following standard protocols provided by BGI Genomics.

Preprocessing and quality control

Gene expression matrices were analyzed using Seurat (v4.2.0) in R (v4.4.0). Standard quality control procedures were applied: cells with low gene counts, high mitochondrial transcript percentages ( > 30%), or potential doublets (identified by DoubletFinder) were excluded. Ambient RNA contamination was corrected using SoupX. Data were normalized, log-transformed, and scaled. Highly variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony to generate an integrated expression matrix for downstream analyses.

Differential expression and enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test in Seurat, with significance defined as adjusted p < 0.05 and a minimum log-fold change. Comparisons included cell clusters and tumor versus adjacent tissue. Enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler, including Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG pathways, and GSEA. Pathway activities were quantified using GSVA on a per-cluster or per-sample basis. Terms with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Cell trajectory analysis

Monocle 2 was used to reconstruct pseudotemporal trajectories for selected cell subsets. Dimensionality reduction (DDRTree) was applied to order cells along developmental paths. Branch points indicated lineage bifurcations. Pseudotime-dependent genes were identified to reveal dynamic transcriptional regulators of state transitions.

Copy number variation and stemness analysis

CNVs were inferred in epithelial cells using CopyKAT and SCEVAN. Stemness scores were calculated using CytoTRACE. Malignant and non-malignant epithelial subpopulations were distinguished based on CNV profiles and stemness differences.

Transcription factor network inference

Transcription factor activity in macrophage subsets was inferred using the SCENIC workflow. Co-expression modules were constructed with GENIE3 and validated using the cisTarget motif database.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Cell–cell communication analysis was performed using CommPath to infer ligand–receptor interactions among defined cell clusters. Key signaling pathways were visualized using circos plots and dot plots.

Animals and BMDM culture

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital, China Medical University, and conducted in accordance with the Ministry of Health of China guidelines (Document No. 55, 2001) and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85–23, revised 1996). C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed under standard conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, ad libitum access to food and water).

For bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs), femurs and tibias were harvested following CO₂ euthanasia. Bone marrow cells were flushed and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 10 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, PeproTech) for 7 days. Adherent macrophages were collected for downstream assays.

M1 polarization was induced by treating BMDMs (0.5–0.7 × 106 cells/well in 12-well plates) with 10 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, InvivoGen). For M2 polarization, cells were treated with 20 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4, PeproTech) for 24 h. STAB1 expression was silenced using a mouse-targeting siRNA (Stabilin-1 siRNA, Santa Cruz).

Generation of tumor-educated macrophages (TEMs):

Apoptotic cells were generated by exposing colorectal cancer cell lines to UV-C (254 nm) for 15 min, followed by incubation under standard culture conditions (DMEM, 37 °C, 5% CO₂) for 2–3 h. This procedure typically yielded > 80% Annexin V–positive cells. The culture medium obtained after the 2–3 h incubation was used as tumor-conditioned medium (TCM). BMDMs were then added and co-incubated for 45 min, after which non-phagocytosed ACs were removed by three vigorous washes with 1× PBS. For controls, BMDMs were maintained under standard conditions without ACs and underwent the same washing procedure.

RNA extraction and RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Only samples with OD260/280 ≥ 1.9 were used for downstream analysis.

cDNA synthesis was performed using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, RR036A) on a SimpliAmp thermal cycler (Thermo Fisher), with incubation at 37 °C for 15 min and inactivation at 85 °C for 5 s. Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted on a QuantStudio system (Applied Biosystems) using iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, #1725120). The cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 59 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Gene expression was quantified using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Primary statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism v9, with additional analyses in R and Python. Data were assessed for normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homoscedasticity (Levene’s test). Two-group comparisons used two-sided unpaired Student’s t-tests (Welch’s correction when variances were unequal) or Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normal data; paired designs used paired t-tests or Wilcoxon matched-pairs tests. For multi-group designs, one- or two-way ANOVA was followed by Tukey’s HSD, and figure panels report adjusted p values from the post-hoc procedure. Pairwise comparisons among subpopulations were evaluated with two-sided Wilcoxon tests; multiplicity was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR; primary threshold q < 0.05), and q-values are reported.

Results

Pathology slide curation and feature extraction from coad whole-slide images

Whole slide images (WSIs) from 387 patients in the TCGA-COAD cohort, with available bulk RNA-seq and clinical data, were included in this study. During initial quality control, we excluded duplicated uploads, non-tumor control tissues, and slides with poor quality (e.g., contamination, discoloration, or tissue overlap). To extract high-quality image regions for model training, WSIs were loaded using OpenSlide (v3.4.1), and tissue regions were identified using Histolab (v0.7.0) to generate tissue masks, allowing removal of non-tissue components and artifacts (e.g., ink marks). From each WSI, ten non-overlapping image tiles were extracted. Tiles were filtered based on two criteria: tissue coverage < 60% or artifact coverage > 20%, while ensuring that at least five high-quality tiles per WSI were retained for downstream analysis. Following joint review by two clinicians and two pathologists, 292 low-quality tiles (due to issues such as bubbles, blurring, handwriting, folding, or blank background) were excluded. In total, 2,678 high-quality tiles were retained to construct a standardized digital pathology dataset, providing a solid foundation for feature extraction and model development.

Pathological images encode information at multiple scales, including tissue architecture and fine-grained cellular morphology/texture. To capture both, we implemented a dual feature-extraction pipeline that combines deep learning with classical image processing. The two branches run in parallel and produce complementary descriptors, yielding a comprehensive multimodal histopathology feature framework.

First, deep features were extracted from each image tile using a pre-trained ResNet50 network [31], resulting in a 2048-dimensional feature vector per tile. The features were then aggregated across all tiles within each whole-slide image by computing their average values. To interpret [10] the model’s focus, Grad-CAM was applied to visualize regions of interest, highlighting the network’s capacity to identify tumor-relevant areas (Fig. 1B). In parallel, conventional quantitative features were extracted using CellProfiler (v4.2.4) [32]. Preprocessing comprised color deconvolution (UnmixColors) and grayscale conversion (ColorToGray). We then computed image-level metrics (image quality, co-localization, texture, granularity) and object-level features (nucleus identification, intensity, morphology, texture). Each feature was summarized using the mean, median, and standard deviation across tiles, yielding a total of 773 image features (Fig. 1C).

In summary, we extracted and integrated ResNet50-derived deep features and CellProfiler-based features from a total of 2,678 tiles, which together constituted the high-dimensional pathology feature set used for subsequent prognostic modeling.

Construction of a prognostic risk model based on efferocytosis-associated pathological features

To assess whether efferocytosis-associated pathological image features have prognostic value in COAD, we first derived a patient-level efferocytosis activity score using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). The score was computed from a literature-curated set of 137 efferocytosis-related genes (Supplementary File 1). Concurrently, tile-level image features extracted via ResNet50 and CellProfiler were integrated to construct a multimodal pathological feature matrix.

Subsequently, all samples were randomly divided into a training set and a validation set in an 8:2 ratio. Within the training set, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the association between each image feature and efferocytosis activity scores. Features that showed stable correlations (p < 0.05) were further refined using Elastic Net regression (α = 0.2), resulting in the identification of 78 efferocytosis-associated image features (Fig. 2A). We constructed a prognostic model using an ensemble of 100 algorithm combinations. The ensemble spanned five families of survival learners: tree-based (RSF, GBM), penalized Cox (Elastic Net, Lasso, Ridge), stepwise Cox (StepCox), boosting (CoxBoost), and dimension-reduction/SVM methods (Cox-PLS, SuperPC, survival-SVM). (Fig. 2C). Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was computed for each model in both training and validation cohorts. The StepCox + RSF combination achieved the highest average C-index and was selected as the optimal model. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the high-risk group had significantly shorter overall survival compared to those in the low-risk group (p < 0.05, Fig. 2D). Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further confirmed the robust performance of the model in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, with area under the curve (AUC) values all exceeding 0.868 (Fig. 2E).

To evaluate whether the risk score adds prognostic information beyond clinical factors, we included it with TNM stage, age, and sex in univariate and multivariate Cox models. In the univariate analysis, the risk score, T stage, and N stage were each significantly associated with overall survival (OS; p < 0.05). In the multivariate analysis, the risk score also remained an independent prognostic factor (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.497, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.452–2.544, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2F), further supporting its potential for clinical application.

Notably, the prognostic performance of our pathology feature-based model approaches that of the widely used TNM staging system. However, unlike TNM staging—which relies heavily on clinical expertise and manual interpretation—our model offers the advantages of full automation, high processing efficiency, and reproducibility. These features position it as a promising auxiliary tool for rapid, objective, and standardized prognostic evaluation and personalized treatment planning in colon cancer.

Multi-omics integration reveals the pro-tumorigenic role of STAB1 and TREM2 in COAD

From a pathological perspective, structural features within histological slides that correlate with patient prognosis often reflect underlying molecular phenotypes [33, 34]. To investigate the molecular mechanisms implied by the key pathological features we identified—and to uncover potential therapeutic targets associated with efferocytosis—we integrated these image-derived features with transcriptomic data from COAD patients. The results showed that several key pathological features were significantly positively correlated with the immune-regulatory molecules STAB1 and TREM2, suggesting that these molecules may play critical roles in driving tissue structural changes associated with efferocytosis. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further confirmed that high expression levels of STAB1 and TREM2 were significantly associated with poor prognosis (p < 0.05), with patients in the high-expression group exhibiting markedly shorter median overall survival (Fig. 3B).

Bulk RNA-seq analysis revealed that both STAB1 and TREM2 were significantly upregulated in colon cancer tissues and exhibited a strong positive correlation (Fig. 3C). Moreover, immunohistochemical staining data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database further validated their elevated expression patterns in COAD (Fig. 3D).

To further elucidate the cellular origin and expression profiles of STAB1 and TREM2, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on primary tumors, paired paracancerous tissues, adenomas, peripheral blood, and normal colon tissues from 13 COAD patients. After stringent quality control and initial clustering, we retained 40,949 cells. We then annotated six major cell types: T/NK cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and B cells (Fig. 3E, Fig. S1A).

The cellular composition exhibited marked heterogeneity across different tissue types. Epithelial cells were most abundant in tumor tissues, whereas T/NK cells were the least represented. In contrast, fibroblasts were enriched in normal and paracancerous tissues but were significantly depleted in tumor samples. Myeloid cells were present across all tissue types; however, their proportion was substantially higher in tumor tissues compared to paracancerous tissues and adenomas, suggesting potential local enrichment or migration (Fig. 3F).

Single-cell gene-expression analysis showed enrichment of STAB1 and TREM2 in tumor tissues. Both genes were predominantly expressed in myeloid subpopulations, with higher levels than in T cells, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells (Fig. 3G).

Efferocytosis-induced STAB1 upregulation drives M2 polarization of TAMs

To delineate the cellular source of STAB1 expression within the myeloid compartment, we performed further dimensionality reduction and subcluster analysis of myeloid cells from the single-cell transcriptomic data. Based on the expression profiles of canonical marker genes, myeloid cells were classified into five subtypes: monocytes, macrophages, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), dendritic cells (DCs), and neutrophils (Fig. 4A, Fig. S1B). Myeloid cells in peripheral blood were predominantly composed of monocytes, DCs, and neutrophils. In contrast, normal and adjacent non-tumor tissues contained a higher proportion of differentiated conventional macrophages, exhibiting similar cellular compositions. Notably, cancer tissues showed a marked increase in TAMs and neutrophils, suggesting their distinct roles within the tumor microenvironment. Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed that the developmental path of myeloid cells originated from monocytes and bifurcated into two distinct branches terminating in conventional macrophages and TAMs, respectively, supporting the biological validity of the clustering results (Fig. 4B, Fig. S1C). Given that TREM2 is recognized as a canonical marker specifically enriched in TAMs [35], we further examined the expression pattern of STAB1. We found that STAB1 was also highly expressed in the TAM population, with statistically significant differences compared to other myeloid subtypes (Fig. 4C). Along the inferred differentiation trajectory, both STAB1 and TREM2 were predominantly expressed in the late stages of lineage progression. Notably, STAB1 expression preceded that of TREM2 (Fig. 4E), suggesting that STAB1 may play a role in the early phase of TAM differentiation.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) frequently adopt an immunosuppressive, M2-like phenotype [36]. We therefore stratified TAMs by STAB1 expression into STAB1+ and STAB1− subsets. We then calculated M2 polarization scores across all monocyte–macrophage lineage cells. The analysis revealed that STAB1+ TAMs exhibited significantly higher M2 scores compared to their STAB1− counterparts (Fig. 4F), suggesting that STAB1 may serve as a key regulator driving the immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs.

To further investigate this phenomenon, we conducted a series of in vitro experiments to assess the role of STAB1 in macrophage polarization. Bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) were stimulated with IL-4 or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 24 hours to examine the association between macrophage polarization and STAB1 expression. We observed that both STAB1 mRNA and protein levels were significantly upregulated in IL-4–induced macrophages (M2), whereas LPS stimulation of M1 macrophages did not alter STAB1 expression (Fig. 4G, H). Notably, silencing of STAB1 significantly attenuated IL-4–induced expression of the canonical M2 markers Arg1 and CD206 (Fig. 4I–K, Fig. S2I). These findings suggest that STAB1 is essential for the IL-4–induced induction of the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, but dispensable for the pro-inflammatory phenotype induced by LPS.

Prior studies suggest that, within the tumor microenvironment, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) clear apoptotic tumor cells via efferocytosis. This process can limit the release and presentation of tumor-associated or tumor-specific antigens (TAAs/TSAs) and, in parallel, bias macrophages toward a tumor-supportive M2 phenotype. To validate this mechanism, we deconvoluted the previously annotated immune cell subpopulations from scRNA-seq data into COAD spatial transcriptomic datasets. Notably, malignant epithelial cells and TAMs were found to be densely co-localized within lesion areas, indicating potential spatial interactions (Fig. 4L, Fig. S1D–G). To model tumor-educated macrophages (TEMs) in vitro, we co-cultured M-CSF–differentiated bone-marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) with apoptotic colorectal cancer cells following established protocols [37, 38]. To exclude artifacts from residual apoptotic cells, non-internalized ACs were removed by serial PBS washes, and sham controls (BMDMs without AC exposure) were processed in parallel with identical handling (see Methods). The co-culture led to a significant upregulation of STAB1, Arg1, and CD206 protein expression in TEMs (Fig. 4M, Fig. S3A), whereas this effect was absent under standard culture conditions.

Subsequently, we conducted an in-depth exploration of the functional characteristics of STAB1+ TAMs. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that this subpopulation exhibited markedly elevated expression of several “eat-me” signal receptors, including MERTK, AXL, and TIMD4 [39] (Fig. 4N). Western blot analysis further confirmed the enhancement of “eat-me” signaling in tumor-educated macrophages (TEMs) (Fig. 4O). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that STAB1+ TAMs were significantly enriched in pathways related to efferocytosis, phagocytic activity, cell adhesion and migration, metabolic reprogramming, and immune suppression (Fig. 4P). Consistent with findings from ovarian cancer studies, STAB1 has been shown to mediate the phagocytosis and antigen degradation of apoptotic tumor cells by macrophages, thereby attenuating subsequent immune activation [40].

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that, within the colon cancer tumor microenvironment, macrophages engage in efferocytosis targeting apoptotic tumor cells, which in turn promotes STAB1 expression and polarization toward a protumoral M2-like TAM phenotype. These results highlight a central role for STAB1 in TAM functional specialization and immune evasion mechanisms.

To validate the impact of STAB1 on mTORC1 activity, we assessed the phosphorylation levels of its downstream effectors, S6 and 4E-BP1. The results demonstrated that p-S6 and p-4EBP1 levels were markedly elevated in TEMs induced by conditioned medium, whereas pharmacological inhibition of STAB1 significantly reduced their phosphorylation. These findings indicate that STAB1 promotes TAM polarization toward an M2-like immunosuppressive phenotype by activating the mTORC1 signaling pathway (Fig. 5B).

To evaluate the dynamic changes and clinical relevance of STAB1 expression under neoadjuvant therapy, we analyzed COAD tumor samples from patients receiving various treatment regimens, including chemotherapy alone, chemotherapy plus anti–PD-1, chemoradiotherapy, chemotherapy plus bevacizumab, and untreated controls. STAB1 expression was significantly upregulated following neoadjuvant therapy, accompanied by increased macrophage infiltration (Fig. 5C, D, Fig. S2A-D, H). We hypothesize that neoadjuvant therapy induces widespread tumor-cell apoptosis and inflammatory responses. These changes enhance efferocytosis, which in turn upregulates STAB1 and skews macrophages toward an immunosuppressive (M2-like) phenotype. To investigate whether STAB1 is associated with therapeutic resistance, we analyzed two chemotherapy response–annotated datasets (GEO: GSE30034 and GSE16648). We compared STAB1 expression between chemotherapy-sensitive and chemotherapy-resistant groups. The resistant group exhibited significantly higher STAB1 expression (Fig. S3B), suggesting that elevated STAB1 may be linked to resistance mechanisms.

To further elucidate the mechanism underlying STAB1+ TAM formation and its functional network within the TME, we conducted transcription factor (TF) regulatory inference and intercellular communication analyses. Transcriptional profiling revealed that STAB1+ TAMs exhibited marked upregulation of the transcription factors MAF and E2F4, along with downregulation of TFAP2C and THAP11 (Fig. 5G), suggesting that STAB1+ TAM polarization may be driven by specific transcriptional regulatory programs involved in phenotypic reprogramming. Cell-cell communication analysis revealed that STAB1+ TAMs serve as central hubs for intercellular signaling interactions within the TME, with particularly strong crosstalk observed with neutrophils (Fig. 5E). This finding aligns with our earlier observation of neutrophil enrichment in tumor tissues (Fig. 4D). Further ligand–receptor interaction analyses delineated a complex signaling network among STAB1+ TAMs, neutrophils, immune cells, and tumor cells (Fig. 5F, Fig. S2E–G). STAB1+ TAMs and neutrophils mutually attract each other via chemotactic factors such as CCL2, CCR1, and HBEGF, collaboratively enhancing the recruitment and maintenance of immunosuppressive phenotypes. Moreover, STAB1+ TAM-derived cytokines, including PD-L1 and IL-10, further drive immune cells toward immunosuppressive states, establishing a positive feedback loop that fosters a tumor-permissive immune microenvironment. In addition, progressively upregulated ligands such as SPP1, CD44, NRG1, and CCR5 were involved in interactions with multiple immune cell subsets, promoting tumor cell proliferation and distant metastasis.

In summary, STAB1+ tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) synergistically promote the establishment and maintenance of a tumor-immunosuppressive microenvironment through mTORC1-mediated signaling and intricate intercellular communication networks. These findings underscore the pivotal regulatory role of STAB1+ TAMs in driving colon cancer progression.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a multimodal prognostic model for COAD by integrating histopathological features, transcriptomic profiles, and clinical data from COAD patients. By identifying key prognostic histopathological features associated with efferocytosis in hematoxylin and eosin (HE)-stained sections, we stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups based on overall survival (OS). Building upon this framework, we further incorporated Bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell RNA sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics to elucidate the underlying immunosuppressive mechanisms. This integrative analysis pinpointed STAB1+ TAMs as central players in shaping the TME, highlighting the power of combining multi-omics data and computational modeling for precise clinical prognostication in COAD.

From a mechanistic standpoint, we identified a subset of TAMs within the colon cancer tumor microenvironment characterized by high STAB1 expression. These STAB1+ TAMs exhibited upregulation of efferocytosis-associated molecules and enhanced activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway, both of which were closely linked to their polarization toward an M2-like, pro-tumoral phenotype. A recent study reported that macrophages with high STAB1 expression exhibit augmented efferocytosis—enhanced uptake and lysosomal degradation of apoptotic cells—while displaying attenuated antigen processing and presentation, thereby limiting antitumor priming [41, 42]. Concordantly, in murine colorectal cancer models, primary tumor growth was largely governed by macrophage STAB1; immunotherapeutic STAB1 blockade reprogrammed TAMs toward a pro-inflammatory, antigen-presenting state and restored CD8+ T-cell activation with antitumor effects [43]. These observations align with our data showing heightened efferocytosis programs in STAB1+ TAMs. Based on this evidence, we propose a potential mechanistic model: persistent apoptotic cell death in the tumor milieu leads to the release of “eat-me” signals, which recruit and activate macrophages to perform efferocytosis. This process, in turn, induces STAB1 upregulation, further augmenting the phagocytic clearance of apoptotic cells and driving the polarization of macrophages into immunosuppressive M2-like TAMs. Collectively, these events establish an efferocytosis-driven, STAB1-mediated positive feedback loop that promotes immune suppression and supports tumor progression. Notably, we observed further enrichment of STAB1+ TAMs in tumor regions following neoadjuvant therapy, likely due to therapy-induced apoptosis that promotes efferocytosis and STAB1 upregulation, thereby exacerbating the immunosuppressive milieu. In ovarian cancer, carboplatin–paclitaxel treatment was associated with upregulation of STAB1 in macrophages. Notably, combining chemotherapy with an anti-STAB1 antibody significantly prolonged survival in two HGSOC syngeneic mouse models [40]. These observations underscore a potentially underappreciated phenomenon: therapy-induced efferocytosis may inadvertently reinforce immune suppression via a STAB1-dependent feedback loop. This highlights the necessity of considering efferocytosis-driven immunosuppression when designing combinatorial treatment strategies in future clinical settings [44].

While TREM2+ macrophages are frequently enriched in immunosuppressive TMEs, accumulating data suggest that TREM2 may serve chiefly as a state marker of macrophage metabolic reprogramming in cancer, rather than a direct driver of immunosuppression in most contexts [35, 45, 46]. In breast cancer, a STAB1+/TREM2high TAM subset—rather than TREM2 positivity alone—was identified as the compartment that suppresses T-cell proliferation and effector function and is enriched in ICB-resistant patients [47]. As a scavenger receptor [34, 48], STAB1 mediates efferocytosis and orchestrates downstream transcriptional reprogramming, conferring potential for therapeutic targeting. Taken together, these observations suggest that STAB1+ TAMs constitute a functionally distinct, phagocytosis-reprogrammed subset. Prior studies indicate that targeting STAB1 can elicit antitumor activity comparable to anti–PD-1 monotherapy and may further enhance efficacy in combination settings [49, 50]. In gastric cancer, STAB1+ TAMs display a dynamic immunosuppressive phenotype with robust suppression of T-cell proliferation and effector function; high STAB1+ TAM infiltration has been associated with poor or adaptive resistance to PD-1 therapy [51, 52]. Experimentally, STAB1 blockade augments CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity and proliferation, supporting the concept of a dual-pronged strategy whereby myeloid reprogramming (STAB1) complements T-cell reinvigoration (PD-1) to promote immune-mediated tumor control and reduce relapse risk. Beyond GC, STAB1 abundance has been associated with inferior neoadjuvant chemotherapy response and overall survival in urothelial bladder cancer, and with adverse prognosis in CN-AML, underscoring its broader clinical relevance. Recent clinical data from the MATINS trial (NCT03733990) support the translational relevance of targeting STAB1/CLEVER-1. The humanized anti-STAB1 antibody bexmarilimab (FP-1305) was well tolerated at higher dose levels. Pharmacodynamic readouts indicated macrophage reprogramming in ~30–40% of patients with refractory or immunologically “cold” tumors [53–55]. These findings collectively underscore STAB1 as a viable therapeutic target in COAD, particularly for patients who are resistant to conventional immune checkpoint inhibitors.

It is biologically plausible that dual blockade of STAB1 and PD-1 could benefit patients who are nonresponsive to PD-1 monotherapy [56–58]. PD-1 targeting can reverse T-cell exhaustion and restore effector function. In parallel, STAB1 blockade may curb TAM-mediated efferocytosis that sequesters antigens and danger signals in the tumor, thereby improving antigen presentation and unmasking tumor immunogenicity. Together, these complementary mechanisms are expected to increase intratumoral trafficking and cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells [49]. Our model identifies efferocytosis-associated histopathological features in colorectal cancer, enabling selection of patients who may respond to efferocytosis/STAB1-directed regimens [50, 59]. On this basis, it could guide STAB1-targeted therapy and potentially improve outcomes in patients refractory to immune-checkpoint inhibitors.

From a translational perspective, our pathology–efferocytosis fusion risk scoring model demonstrates prognostic predictive power comparable to the TNM staging system, and enables the automated transformation of HE-stained histopathology into quantifiable prognostic indicators. This approach provides a clinically feasible tool for individualized risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making in COAD. While aligning with the multi-omics integration frameworks proposed by recent studies, our work uniquely incorporates efferocytosis-associated histopathological features, highlighting their previously underappreciated value in prognostic modeling and immunotherapeutic guidance. Beyond prognostication, we identified STAB1 as a promising immunomodulatory target.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, because our multimodal risk model was developed in the TCGA cohort, prospective, multicenter external validation is required to establish generalizability and mitigate potential selection or spectrum bias. In parallel, targeted accrual of COAD patients with suboptimal response to adjuvant (or neoadjuvant) therapy will enable iterative refinement and extension of the model. As larger datasets become available, future studies should also evaluate more advanced image encoders to further investigate and validate these findings. Second, we performed in-vitro functional assays using BMDMs to evaluate STAB1-related mechanisms. Our experiments suggest that macrophages adopt an immune-suppressive state after efferocytosis. This state matches the features of M2 polarization. These findings support combining STAB1-directed therapy with immune checkpoint blockade. Translational implementation should nonetheless anticipate potential challenges, including on-target effects and compensatory myeloid pathways that may attenuate efficacy. Third, the therapeutic efficacy of targeting the STAB1–mTORC1 axis has not yet been evaluated in vivo or in the context of immune checkpoint blockade, warranting additional preclinical studies. To address these limitations, future work should include validation in expanded patient cohorts, implementation of humanized mouse or organoid models, preclinical testing of ICI-based combination regimens, and development of small-molecule or antibody-based STAB1-targeted interventions.

Conclusion

Advanced imaging analytics and deep learning technologies are reshaping cancer research and clinical decision-making. By integrating multi-omics datasets with histopathological image features, we established a prognostic model that not only enables accurate outcome prediction but also facilitates therapeutic stratification, underscoring its translational potential.

Importantly, our findings bridge the gap between molecular programs and tissue morphology, revealing a pivotal role for STAB1+ TAMs in efferocytosis-driven activation of mTORC1 signaling and immunosuppressive niche formation in COAD. This mechanistic insight offers a compelling rationale for the development of individualized immunotherapeutic strategies targeting STAB1+ TAMs, with the potential to improve outcomes for patients with COAD.
