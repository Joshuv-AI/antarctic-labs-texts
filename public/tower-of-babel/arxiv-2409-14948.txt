On the periodic decompositions of multidimensional configurations
Pyry Herva, Jarkko Kari

Abstract
We consider d d -dimensional configurations, that is, colorings of the d d -dimensional integer grid ℤ d \Z^{d} with finitely many colors. Moreover, we interpret the colors as integers so that configurations are functions ℤ d → ℤ \Z^{d}\rightarrow\Z of finite range. We say that such function is k k -periodic if it is invariant under translations in k k linearly independent directions. 1-periodic functions are called periodic. It is known that if a configuration has a non-trivial annihilator, that is, if some non-trivial linear combination of its translations is the zero function, then it is a sum of finitely many periodic functions. This result is known as the periodic decomposition theorem. We prove two different improvements of it and discuss some applications of these improvements. The first improvement gives a characterization on annihilators of a configuration to guarantee the k k -periodicity of the functions in its periodic decomposition — for any k k . The periodic decomposition theorem is then a special case of this result with k = 1 k=1 . We discuss an application of this result concerning translational tilings. The second improvement concerns so called sparse configurations for which the number of non-zero values in patterns grows at most linearly with respect to the diameter of the pattern. We prove that a sparse configuration with a non-trivial annihilator is a sum of finitely many periodic fibers where a fiber means a function whose support (that is, the set of points where the function gets non-zero values) is contained in a unique line. As an application of this result, we show that ℝ \R -configurations with uniformly discrete supports that have non-trivial annihilators are necessarily periodic.

Introduction

We work mostly with
d
-dimensional configurations
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
, that is, colorings of the
d
-dimensional integer grid
\Z^{d}
with a non-empty finite set of colors
\mathcal{A}
— where
d
is any positive integer.
In this paper we assume that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Z
.
We consider configurations
c
that have non-trivial annihilators meaning that a non-trivial
\Z
-linear combination of some finitely many translated copies of
c
is the zero function.
It is known that such configurations can be decomposed to sums of finitely many periodic functions
[
18
]
— we will refer to this result as the periodic decomposition theorem (Theorem
8
).
In this paper we show that under some additional assumptions the periodic decomposition theorem can be improved.
We prove two different improvements of the periodic decomposition theorem.

In our considerations we apply an algebraic approach to multidimensional symbolic dynamics which was developed in
[
17
,
18
]
to make progress in the famous still open Nivat’s conjecture
[
26
]
which links periodicity and sufficiently small rectangular pattern complexity.
It is a two-dimensional generalization of the Morse-Hedlund theorem
[
24
]
.
In the algebraic approach
d
-dimensional configurations are represented as formal power series with integer coefficients in
d
variables.
An annihilator of a configuration is then a Laurent polynomial such that its formal product with the power series presenting the configuration is the zero power series.
A periodizer of a configuration is a Laurent polynomial such that its formal product with
the power series presenting the configuration is strongly periodic, that is, it has
d
linearly independent period vectors.

In our first improvement of the periodic decomposition theorem we consider arbitrary configurations that have periodizers of particular type.
More precisely, we assume that for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and for every
(k-1)
-dimensional linear subspace
V
of
\R^{d}
the configuration has a periodizer whose support (that is, the set that gives the non-zero terms of the periodizer) contains exactly one point of
V
.
We show that the configuration is then a sum of finitely many functions which all have
k
linearly independent periods (Theorem
13
).
If
k=1
, then the statement says just that any configuration with a non-trivial periodizer is a sum of finitely many periodic functions which is exactly the original periodic decomposition theorem.
Also, for
k=d
the statement is known to be true
[
19
]
.

The result described above is inspired by similar considerations in
[
23
]
by Meyerovitch et al. related to translational tilings and the periodic tiling conjecture.
A translational tiling by a non-empty finite set
D\subseteq\Z^{d}
is a set
C\subseteq\Z^{d}
such that
D\oplus C=\Z^{d}
. The non-empty finite set
D
is called a tile, and the tiling
C
may also be called a co-tiler of
D
.
Considering the indicator function of the co-tiler, we find that it is periodized by the polynomial presenting the tile
D
.
The periodic tiling conjecture states that if a tile has a co-tiler, then it also has a periodic co-tiler
[
13
,
21
,
27
]
.
Newman showed already in 1977 using a simple pigeonholing argument that any translational tiling in dimension
d=1
is necessarily periodic
[
25
]
and hence the periodic tiling problem is true for
d=1
.
For
d\geq 2
, the conjecture is much trickier.
However, Bhattacharya proved
that the periodic tiling problem holds also for
d=2

[
3
]
.
Greenfeld and Tao proved a quantative version of Bhattacharyas result
[
9
]
.
Moreover, they obtained some structural results on translational tilings in any dimension.
In
[
11
]
they showed that the periodic tiling problem fails for sufficiently large
d
.
For recent research around the periodic tiling problem, see
e.g.

[
7
,
12
,
10
,
22
,
23
,
8
,
5
]
.

In our second improvement of the periodic decomposition theorem we consider configurations whose supports (that is, the sets of points where the configurations get non-zero values) are located very “sparsely” in the grid
\Z^{d}
.
Conveniently, we call these configurations sparse.
We show that if a sparse configuration has a non-zero annihilator, then it is a sum of finitely many periodic configurations whose supports are aligned on unique lines (Theorem
20
and Corollary
26
).

The motivation to study sparse configurations with annihilators arises from the authors’ study
[
14
]
concerning Delone sets where the concept of annihilators is generalized to
\R^{d}
-configurations, that is, functions
\R^{d}\rightarrow\Z
of finite range.
Delone sets are uniformly discrete and relatively dense subsets of
\R^{d}
that form mathematical models for crystals and quasicrystals
[
1
]
.
By the support of an
\R^{d}
-configuration we mean the set of points where the configuration gets non-zero values.
Thus, in
[
14
]
we study
\R^{d}
-configurations whose supports are Delone sets.
In
[
14
]
, many results concerning
\Z^{d}
-configurations are generalized to
\R^{d}
-configurations.
Our research question in
[
14
]
related to this article originally asked whether one-dimensional
\R
-configurations with Delone set supports that have non-trivial annihilators are necessarily periodic.
Using our periodic decomposition theorem for sparse configurations we prove that this holds even for
\R
-configurations with uniformly discrete supports that have non-trivial annihilators (Theorem
28
).

This article is an extended version of the conference paper
[
15
]
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

As usual, we denote by
\Z
,
\Q
,
\R
, and
\C
the sets of integers, rational numbers, real numbers, and complex numbers, respectively.
By
\Z_{+}
we mean the set of positive integers and by
\N=\Z_{+}\cup\{0\}
the set of non-negative integers.
For any two sets
A
and
B
, we denote by
A^{B}
the set of all functions from
B
to
A
.
For any vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\R^{d}
, we denote
by
L(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k})=\{a_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+\ldots+a_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}\mid a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\R\}

the linear subspace of
\R^{d}
spanned by them.
Similarly for any (possibly infinite) set
S\subseteq\R^{d}
, we denote by
L(S)
the subspace generated by
S
.
On the other hand, by
\langle\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\rangle
we mean the subgroup of
\R^{d}
generated by the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}
.
We also use the notation
\Z[S]
to denote the abelian group generated by the set
S
.
By
{\mathbb{G}}_{k}
we denote the set of all
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\R^{d}
for a fixed
d\in\Z_{+}
.
Clearly, if
k>d
, then
{\mathbb{G}}_{k}=\emptyset
.

2.2
Configurations and periodicity

In this paper we consider mostly elements of the set
\Z^{\Z^{d}}
, that is, functions

c\colon\Z^{d}\rightarrow\Z

where
d
is a positive integer — the dimension.
However, in some occasions, we also work with the set
\C^{\Z^{d}}
.
and hence most of the definitions and some of the statements are given more generally for the elements of this set.

A (
d
-dimensional)
configuration
or a
\Z^{d}
-configuration
is a function

c\in\Z^{\Z^{d}}
that has only finitely many different values.
In particular, configurations are functions of the set
\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
for some finite
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Z
.
The set
\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
is called the
d
-dimensional configuration space over
\mathcal{A}
.
Notice the distinction between the concepts of configurations and general functions of the set
\Z^{\Z^{d}}
. Indeed, the range of a general such function is allowed to be infinite while the range of a configuration is always finite.

Any function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
is
\mathbf{v}
-periodic
if
c(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})=c(\mathbf{u})
for all
\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}
. In this case
\mathbf{v}
is called a
period
of
c
.
If
c
has a non-zero period, then it is
periodic
.
Let us denote by
\tau^{\mathbf{v}}(c)
the
translation
of
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
by a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\Z^{d}
defined such that
\tau^{\mathbf{v}}(c)(\mathbf{u})=c(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v})
for all
\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}
.
Hence,
c
is
\mathbf{v}
-periodic if and only if
\tau^{\mathbf{v}}(c)=c
.

A function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
is
k
-periodic
if
it has
k
linearly independent periods.
In particular,
d
-periodic functions are called
strongly periodic
.
(Note that if
c\in\Z^{\Z^{d}}
is strongly periodic, then
c
is necessarily a configuration.)
We say that

c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
is
periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}\in\Q^{d}
if it is
k\mathbf{v}
-periodic for some
k\in\Z
.
It is
V
-periodic
for a vector space
V\subseteq\R^{d}
if it is periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}
for all
\mathbf{v}\in V\cap\Q^{d}
.
Clearly, any
k
-periodic function is
V
-periodic for some
V\in{\mathbb{G}}_{k}
.
Finally,
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
is
\mathbf{v}
-periodic in a subset

S\subseteq\Z^{d}
if for all
\mathbf{u}\in S
such that
\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\in S
it follows that
c(\mathbf{u})=c(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})
.

A
(finite) pattern
is a function
p\in\C^{D}
for some finite
shape

D\subseteq\Z^{d}
.
In this case
p
is also called a
D
-pattern
.
We say that a function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}

contains
a
D
-pattern
p\in\C^{D}
if
\tau^{\mathbf{t}}(c)\restriction_{D}=p
for some
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{D}(c)=\{\tau^{\mathbf{t}}(c)\restriction_{D}\mid\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}\}
the set of of all
D
-patterns of
c
and by

\mathcal{L}(c)=\bigcup_{\text{finite }D\subseteq\Z^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{D}(c)

the set of all finite patterns of
c
.

The
support
of
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}

is the set

\text{\rm supp}(c)=\{\mathbf{u}\mid c(\mathbf{u})\neq 0\}.

If
\text{\rm supp}(c)
is finite, then
c
is called
finitely supported
.

Let us denote by
C_{m}=\{-m,\ldots,m\}^{d}
for
m\in\N
the discrete
d
-dimensional hypercube of size
(2m+1)^{d}
centered at the origin.
Any function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
is called
sparse
if there exists a positive integer
a
such that

|\text{\rm supp}(c)\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})|\leq am

for all
m\in\Z_{+}
and for all
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
.
Such
a
is called a sparseness constant of
c
.

The configuration space
\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
can be made a compact topological space by endowing
\mathcal{A}
with the discrete topology and considering the product topology it induces on
\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
– the
prodiscrete topology
.
This topology is
induced by a metric where two configurations are close if they agree on a large area around the origin.
By compactness in this topology every sequence of configurations has a converging subsequence.

A
subshift
is a closed and translation-invariant subset of the configuration space
\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
.
The
orbit
of a configuration
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
is the set
\mathcal{O}(c)=\{\tau^{\mathbf{t}}(c)\mid\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}\}
of its every translate.
The

orbit closure

\overline{\mathcal{O}(c)}
is the topological closure of its orbit under the prodiscrete topology.
It is the smallest subshift that contains
c
.
It consists of all configurations
c^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{L}(c^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{L}(c)
.

2.3
Algebraic concepts

As in
[
17
]
we present a function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
as a formal power series

c(X)=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}}c_{\mathbf{u}}X^{\mathbf{u}}

in
d
variables
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

where we have used the convenient abbreviations
X^{\mathbf{u}}=x_{1}^{u_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{u_{d}}
and
c_{\mathbf{u}}=c(\mathbf{u})
for a vector
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})\in\Z^{d}
.

We consider Laurent polynomials with integer coefficients which we call from now on simply polynomials.
By a non-trivial polynomial we mean a non-zero polynomial.
A multiplication of a formal power series
c(X)=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}}c_{\mathbf{u}}X^{\mathbf{u}}
with complex coefficients by a polynomial
f=f(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}X^{\mathbf{u}_{i}}
is well defined as the formal power series

fc=f(X)c(X)=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}c_{\mathbf{u}}\right)X^{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}_{i}}=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}c_{\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{i}}\right)X^{\mathbf{u}}.

Thus, the value of
fc
at
\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}
is determined by the values of
c
at
\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{n}
.

Remark 1
.

More generally, we could and sometimes need to consider also Laurent polynomials with complex coefficients but in this paper polynomials are Laurent polynomials with integer coefficients as defined above.

By the product of a function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
and a polynomial
f
we mean the above product and denote it conveniently by
fc
as usual.
Since any polynomial
f
can be thought as a finitely supported function in
\Z^{\Z^{d}}
, the above product can also be viewed as the discrete convolution of
f
and
c
(which is always defined because
f
is finitely supported).
By the support of a polynomial
f=\sum f_{\mathbf{u}}X^{\mathbf{u}}
we mean the (finite) support of this function, that is, the set

\text{\rm supp}(f)=\{\mathbf{u}\mid f_{\mathbf{u}}\neq 0\}.

We say that a polynomial

f

annihilates
(or is an
annihilator
of) a function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
if

fc=0.

Since multiplying
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
by the monomial
X^{\mathbf{v}}
corresponds to translating it by the vector
\mathbf{v}
, that is,
X^{\mathbf{v}}c=\tau^{\mathbf{v}}(c)
, it follows that
c
is
\mathbf{v}
-periodic if and only if it is annihilated by the difference polynomial
X^{\mathbf{v}}-1
.
The set of every annihilator of
c
is quite easily seen to be an ideal of the polynomial ring
and hence the question whether
c
is periodic reduces to the question whether its “annihilator ideal” contains a non-trivial difference polynomial.

We say that a polynomial
f

periodizes
(or is a
periodizer
of)
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
if
fc
is strongly periodic.
Clearly, any annihilator of
c
is also a periodizer of
c
.
On the other hand, if
c
has a periodizer
f
, then
fc
is periodic and hence annihilated by some non-trivial difference polynomial
X^{\mathbf{v}}-1
.
Thus,
c
has a non-trivial annihilator if and only it has a non-trivial periodizer.

A
line polynomial

f
is a polynomial whose support contains at least two points and the points of the support are aligned on a line, that is, there exist vectors
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\Z^{d}
such that
\text{\rm supp}(f)\subseteq\mathbf{u}+\Q\mathbf{v}
.
Such
\mathbf{v}
(which is non-zero) is called a
direction
of
f
and we may say that
f
is a
line polynomial in direction
\mathbf{v}
.
As usual, we say that two vectors
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
are
parallel
if
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in\Q\mathbf{v}
(and vice versa),
i.e.
, if they are linearly dependent over
\Q
.
Otherwise, they are
non-parallel
in which case they are linearly independent over
\Q
.
Clearly, any line polynomial in direction
\mathbf{v}
is also a line polynomial in any parallel non-zero direction
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
.

A function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
is a
\mathbf{v}
-fiber
for a non-zero vector
\mathbf{v}\in\Q^{d}
if its support is contained in a line in direction
\mathbf{v}
, that is, if
\text{\rm supp}(c)\subseteq\mathbf{u}+\Q\mathbf{v}
for some
\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}
.
Any
\mathbf{v}
-fiber is called simply a
fiber
.
Note that by interpreting polynomials as functions as discussed earlier, line polynomials are finitely supported fibers.
By a
\mathbf{v}
-fiber of
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
we mean a function that agrees with
c
on
\mathbf{u}+\Q\mathbf{v}
for some
\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}
and gets value 0 elsewhere.
Clearly, any
\mathbf{v}
-fiber of
c
is a
\mathbf{v}
-fiber.

Note that if a configuration
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
is annihilated by a line polynomial, then it is necessarily periodic.
Indeed, annihilation by a line polynomial in direction
\mathbf{v}
defines a recurrence relation on each
\mathbf{v}
-fiber of
c
which implies periodicity of each
\mathbf{v}
-fiber since
\mathcal{A}
is finite.
Moreover, the smallest periods of the
\mathbf{v}
-fibers are bounded and hence also
c
is
\mathbf{v}
-periodic.
In particular, any one-dimensional configuration with a non-trivial annihilator is periodic since all polynomials in only one variable are line polynomials.
Conversely, periodicity implies annihilation by a non-trivial difference polynomial which is a line polynomial.
Thus, a configuration
c
is periodic if and only if it is annihilated by a line polynomial.

Special annihilators

It is known that if a configuration
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
has a non-trivial periodizer, then it has an annihilator which is a product of difference polynomials.
More precisely, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
)
.

Let
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
be a configuration and assume that it has a non-trivial annihilator
f
.
For all
\mathbf{u}\in\text{\rm supp}(f)
, there exist pairwise non-parallel vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\in\Z^{d}
such that each
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is an integer multiple of
\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}
for some
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\text{\rm supp}(f)\setminus\{\mathbf{u}\}
, and
c
is annihilated by the polynomial

(X^{\mathbf{v}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{v}_{m}}-1).

For some considerations of this paper, we need
a more involved formulation of the above theorem.
To do this, we need to review some parts of the proof of the theorem.
A
dilation
of a polynomial

is a polynomial of the form
f(X^{k})
for some integer

.

Lemma 3

(The dilation lemma
[
18
]
)
.

Let
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
be a configuration and
f(X)
a non-trivial annihilator of
c
.
There exists a positive integer
r
such that for every positive integer

with
\gcd(k,r)=1
also

annihilates

.

Let us call a number

that has the property of the above lemma a

of

with respect to

.
The following lemma was proved in the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 4

(
[
18
]
)
.

Let
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
be a configuration and

a non-trivial annihilator of

.
For all
\mathbf{u}\in\text{\rm supp}(f)
, the configuration

is annihilated by the polynomial

\prod_{\mathbf{v}\in\text{\rm supp}(f)\setminus\{\mathbf{u}\}}(X^{r(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u})}-1)

From the proof of the dilation lemma we adapt the following result.

Lemma 5

(Adapted from
[
18

)

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an arbitrary index set, and let

If

Let

.

Let

In the proof of the dilation lemma in

is a dilation constant of any

.
The claim follows.
∎

(

]

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an arbitrary index set, and let
\left(c^{(i)}\right)_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a collection of configurations over the same finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Z
.
If
f
is a non-trivial annihilator of

for every

, then the configurations in the collection have a common annihilator which is of the form

(X^{\mathbf{v}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{v}_{m}}-1)

\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}

Proof.

By Lemma
5
, the configurations
c^{(i)}
have a common dilation constant

g=\prod_{\mathbf{v}\in\text{\rm supp}(f)\setminus\{\mathbf{u}\}}(X^{r(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u})}-1)=(X^{\mathbf{w}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{w}_{k}}-1).

Assume that for some

, the vectors
\mathbf{w}_{i}
and
\mathbf{w}_{j}
are parallel, that is,

q\mathbf{w}_{i}=p\mathbf{w}_{j}
for some

We may replace

X^{p\mathbf{w}_{j}}-1=X^{q\mathbf{w}_{i}}-1

Since

X^{p\mathbf{w}_{j}}-1=(X^{\mathbf{w}_{j}}-1)(X^{(p-1)\mathbf{w}_{j}}+\ldots+X^{\mathbf{v}_{j}}+1),

we may replace
(X^{p\mathbf{w}_{j}}-1)(X^{\mathbf{w}_{j}}-1)
by
(X^{p\mathbf{w}_{j}}-1)^{2}
.
Iterating this argument we conclude that the configurations
c^{(i)}
have a common annihilator

(X^{\mathbf{v}_{1}}-1)^{e_{1}}\cdots(X^{\mathbf{v}_{m}}-1)^{e_{m}}

where the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}
are pairwise linearly independent over
\Q
and
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
are positive integers.
Consequently, by Lemma
6
, we conclude that the configurations
c^{(i)}
have a common annihilator

(X^{\mathbf{v}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{v}_{m}}-1).

∎

Periodic decomposition theorem

The multiplication of
c
by a difference polynomial can be thought of as a “discrete derivation” of
c
.
Theorem
2
says that if a configuration
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
has a non-trivial periodizer, then there is a sequence of derivations which annihilates
c
.
So, by
“integrating” step by step, we have the following periodic decomposition theorem.

Theorem 8

(Periodic decomposition theorem
[
18
]
)
.

Let
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
be a configuration and assume that it has a non-trivial periodizer.
There exist pairwise non-parallel vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}
and functions
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\in\Z^{\Z^{d}}
such that

c=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}

and each
c_{i}
is
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-periodic.

Remark 9
.

The periodic functions
c_{i}
in the periodic decomposition of a configuration

may not be configurations, that is, their ranges may be infinite

]

Lemma 10
.

Let
V\subseteq\R^{d}
be a linear subspace of
\R^{d}
,
and let
\varphi
and
\psi

\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\Z^{d}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}\in\Z^{d}

\langle\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}\rangle\cap V=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

V
-periodic function annihilated by
\psi

•

\varphi c=c^{\prime}
,

•

\psi c=0
, and

•

is
V
-periodic.

Moreover, if

and
\varphi
is a difference polynomial, then the coefficients of

can be chosen to be integers, that is,

Without loss of generality we may assume that

The space

for each such coset
\Lambda
.
Now, we have

\alpha_{n}c_{\Lambda[a_{1}-n,a_{2},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}]}+\ldots+\alpha_{0}c_{\Lambda[a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}]}=c^{\prime}_{\Lambda[a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}]}

(1)

for all

.

.
The rest of

is then determined by the linear recurrence of Equation (
1
) such that
\varphi c=c^{\prime}
.

Let us show that if

Since

\varphi(fc)=f(\varphi c)=fc^{\prime}=0.

(2)

We defined

Now, it follows that

Finally, assume that

Theorem 13
.

Let

be a
d
-dimensional configuration and let

.
Assume that for every
(k-1)
-dimensional linear subspace
V\subseteq\R^{d}
the configuration

such that

\text{\rm supp}(f)\cap V=\{\mathbf{0}\}

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
k
.
If
k=1
, then the assumption implies that the configuration
c
has a non-trivial periodizer and hence by Theorem
8
it is a sum of periodic functions.

Let
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
and assume
that the claim holds for
k
.
Let us prove that the claim holds then also for
k+1
.
So, assume that for all
V\in{\mathbb{G}}_{k}
the configuration
c
has a periodizer
f
such that
\text{\rm supp}(f)\cap V=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Since every
(k-1)
-dimensional subspace is contained in a
k
-dimensional subspace, there exists such periodizer, in particular, also for all
V\in{\mathbb{G}}_{k-1}
.
Thus, by the induction hypothesis there exist
k
-periodic functions
e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}\in\Z^{\Z^{d}}
such that

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
, let

be such that

is
V_{i}
-periodic.
We may assume that for all

since the sum of two

-periodic functions is also
V
-periodic.
Indeed, if for some
i\neq j
we have
V_{i}=V_{j}
, then we replace the functions
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
by the function

V_{i}
-periodic.

Let us fix an arbitrary

. By the assumption the configuration
c
has a periodizer
f

\text{\rm supp}(f)\cap V_{i}=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

By Lemma

we may assume that

is an annihilator of

and hence
by Theorem
2

we conclude that

has an annihilator

(X^{\mathbf{u}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{u}_{r}}-1)

such that
\mathbf{u}_{j}\not\in V_{i}
for all

and the vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{r}
are pairwise non-parallel.

For each

, let
\mathbf{w}_{j}\in V_{j}
be such that
\mathbf{w}_{j}\not\in V_{i}
and

is annihilated by
X^{\mathbf{w}_{j}}-1
.
Since

is annihilated by the product

(X^{\mathbf{u}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{u}_{r}}-1)

and each

is annihilated by

, it follows that

is annihilated by the polynomial

(X^{\mathbf{u}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{u}_{r}}-1)\prod_{j\in\{1,\ldots,l\}\setminus\{i\}}(X^{\mathbf{w}_{j}}-1).

Thus, we have seen that

is annihilated by a polynomial of the form

(X^{\mathbf{v}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{v}_{s}}-1)

\mathbf{v}_{j}\not\in V_{i}
for each

.
Let us assume that

is minimal in the sense that if a product

(X^{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{t}}-1)

We claim that the vectors

(Note that in the above annihilator we may replace any

.

If the vectors

Let us then show that

.
Then there exist integers

\mathbf{v}\in V_{i}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

is periodic in direction

and hence it is

.
If this is not the case, then

\mathbf{v}
by
k\mathbf{v}
and the integers

and

are replaced by

, respectively.
Now,

is
\mathbf{v}
-periodic.
Since

.
Now, we replace the term

in the annihilator

of

\mathbf{v}
-periodic, also the function

\mathbf{v}

\mathbf{v}

Remark 15
.

A linear subspace

is finite
[

]
.

of

]
.

.
For

Application to translational tilings

A
translational tiling
is a binary configuration
c\in\{0,1\}^{\Z^{d}}
such that for some non-empty finite set
D\subseteq\Z^{d}
and a polynomial
f_{D}=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in-D}X^{\mathbf{u}}
we have

f_{D}c=\mathbbm{1}=1^{\Z^{d}}.

In this case
D
is called a
tile
and
c
is a
co-tiler of
D
or a (translational)
tiling by the tile
D
.

Define that
k
tiles
D_{1},\ldots,D_{k}\subseteq\Z^{d}

are
independent
if

\mathbf{0}\in D_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}

and the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}
are linearly independent over
\Q
for every tuple
(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k})\in D_{1}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\times\ldots\times D_{k}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

[
23
]
.
It is known that if
k
independent tiles have a common co-tiler
c
, then
c
is a sum of finitely many
k
-periodic functions from the set
[a,b]^{\Z^{d}}
for some reals
a<b

[
23
]
.
We will prove a similar result as a corollary of Theorem
13
.
First, consider a simple example.

Example 16
.

Consider the two tiles
D_{1}=\{(0,0),(1,0)\},D_{2}=\{(0,0),(0,1)\}\subseteq\Z^{2}
.
They are clearly independent.
Moreover, they have a common co-tiler
c\in\{0,1\}^{\Z^{2}}
defined such that

c(i,j)=1
if and only if
i+j
is even.

We say that
k
polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
satisfying
\mathbf{0}\in\text{\rm supp}(f_{i})
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
are independent if their supports are independent.

Corollary 17
.

Let
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
be a configuration and let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
be
k
periodizers of
c
satisfying
\mathbf{0}\in\text{\rm supp}(f_{i})
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
with
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
If
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
are independent, then

c=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}

where each
c_{i}\in\Z^{\Z^{d}}
is
k
-periodic.

Proof.

Let us prove that for every
V\in{\mathbb{G}}_{k-1}
the configuration
c
has a periodizer
f
such that
\text{\rm supp}(f)\cap V=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Then the claim follows from Theorem
13
.

Assume on the contrary that there exists a
V\in{\mathbb{G}}_{k-1}
such that
\text{\rm supp}(f)\cap V\not=\{\mathbf{0}\}
for any periodizer
f
of
c
.
This implies that for any periodizer
f
of
c
we have either
\text{\rm supp}(f)\cap V=\emptyset
or
|\text{\rm supp}(f)\cap V|\geq 2
.
Since
\mathbf{0}\in\text{\rm supp}(f_{i})
, we have
|\text{\rm supp}(f_{i})\cap V|\geq 2
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Thus, there exist non-zero vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}
such that
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\text{\rm supp}(f_{i})\cap V
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}

and hence the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}
span a linear subspace of dimension at most
k-1
.
This means that the sets
\text{\rm supp}(f_{1}),\ldots,\text{\rm supp}(f_{k})
are not independent and hence the polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
are not independent.
A contradiction.
∎

We have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 18
.

If a
d
-dimensional configuration
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
has
d
independent periodizers, then it is strongly periodic.

Proof.

By the above corollary
c=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}
where each
c_{i}
is strongly periodic. Thus,
c
is strongly periodic as a sum of finitely many strongly periodic configurations.
∎

For any tile
D\subseteq\Z^{d}
, the polynomial
f_{D}=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in-D}X^{\mathbf{u}}
is a periodizer of any co-tiler
c
of
D
.
Thus, by Corollary
17
, any common co-tiler of
k
independent tiles is a sum of finitely many
k
-periodic functions of the set
\Z^{\Z^{d}}
.
So, we have a similar result as the authors of
[
23
]
except that the
k
-periodic functions in the periodic decomposition of the co-tiler are now functions of the set
\Z^{\Z^{d}}
instead of the set
[a,b]^{\Z^{d}}
.

Corollary 19
.

Let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{k}\subseteq\Z^{d}
be
k
independent tiles and let
c\in\{0,1\}^{\Z^{d}}
be their common co-tiler.
Then there exist
k
-periodic functions
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\in\Z^{\Z^{d}}
such that

c=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}.

Proof.

Follows directly from Corollary
17
.
∎

4
Periodic decomposition of sparse configurations

Let us denote by
C_{m}=\{-m,\ldots,m\}^{d}
for
m\in\N
the discrete
d
-dimensional hypercube of size
(2m+1)^{d}
centered at the origin.
Any function
c\in\C^{\Z^{d}}
is called
sparse
if there exists a positive integer
a
such that

|\text{\rm supp}(c)\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})|\leq am

for all
m\in\Z_{+}
and for all
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
.
In this case,
a
is called a sparseness constant of
c
.

In this section we consider sparse configurations with annihilators and prove that they are sums of finitely many periodic fibers.
We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 20
.

Let
c
be a sparse configuration and assume that it is annihilated by a product
\varphi_{1}\cdots\varphi_{n}
of line polynomials
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
in pairwise non-parallel directions
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}
, respectively.
Then

c=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{n}

where each
c_{i}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-fibers and
\varphi_{i}c_{i}=0
.

Before presenting the proof of the theorem
we state some simple observations concerning sparse configurations.

Lemma 21
.

Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
be sparse configurations and let
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\in\Z
.
Then also
k_{1}c_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}c_{n}
is sparse.
In particular, for a sparse configuration
c
and a polynomial
g
also
gc
is sparse.

Proof.

There exist
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
such that

|\text{\rm supp}(c_{i})\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})|\leq a_{i}m

for all
m\in\Z_{+}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
.
Since

\text{\rm supp}(k_{1}c_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}c_{n})\subseteq\cup_{i=1}^{n}\text{\rm supp}(c_{i}),

we have

|\text{\rm supp}(k_{1}c_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}c_{n})\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})|\leq(a_{1}+\ldots+a_{n})m

for all
m\in\Z_{+}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
.
Thus,

k_{1}c_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}c_{n}
is sparse.

For a polynomial
g=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}X^{\mathbf{u}_{i}}
we have

gc=k_{1}c_{1}+\ldots+k_{n}c_{n}
where each
c_{i}=X^{\mathbf{u}_{i}}c
is sparse as a translation,
i.e.
, a monomial multiplication of
c
.
Thus,
gc
is sparse by the first part.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Every element of the orbit closure
\overline{\mathcal{O}(c)}
of a sparse configuration
c
is also sparse.

Proof.

Let
e\in\overline{\mathcal{O}(c)}
.
For all
m\in\Z_{+}
any
C_{m}
-pattern of
e
is also a
C_{m}
-pattern of
c
and hence for any
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
there exists
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\in\Z^{d}
such that

\text{\rm supp}(e)\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})=\text{\rm supp}(c)\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}).

Thus, if
c
is sparse, then also
e
is sparse.
∎

Remark 23
.

To make the above lemma work it is important to define sparse configurations in the way we did.
For example, the weaker class of configurations
c
having a constant
a
such that

|\text{\rm supp}(c)\cap C_{m}|\leq am

for all
m\in\Z_{+}
does not satisfy the above lemma.
Indeed, consider a configuration
c\in\{0,1\}^{\Z^{2}}

such that
c(\mathbf{u})=1
if
\mathbf{u}\in C_{m}+(2^{m},0)
for some
m\in\Z_{+}
and otherwise
c(\mathbf{u})=0
.
Now,
|\text{\rm supp}(c)\cap C_{m}|=O(m)
and hence there exists
a
such that

|\text{\rm supp}(c)\cap C_{m}|\leq am

for all
m\in\Z_{+}
.
However, the constant configuration
1^{\Z^{2}}
is in the orbit closure
\overline{\mathcal{O}(c)}
and does not satisfy the condition.

The following lemma is Theorem
20
in the case
n=1
.

Lemma 24
.

Let
c
be a sparse configuration and assume that it is periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}
for some non-zero
\mathbf{v}
.
Then
c
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
c
is
\mathbf{v}
-periodic by replacing
\mathbf{v}
by
m\mathbf{v}
for suitable
m\in\Z
.

Assume on the contrary that
c
is not a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers, that is, it contains infinitely many distinct non-zero periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers.
Let
m\in\Z_{+}
be such that
\mathbf{v}\in C_{m}
,
and let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{t}
be
t
distinct non-zero fibers of
c
where
t=am+1
and
a
is a sparseness constant of
c
.
Let
n\in\Z_{+}
be such that
\text{\rm supp}(e_{j})\cap C_{n}\neq\emptyset
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
.

Since each
e_{j}
is
\mathbf{v}
-periodic, we have
|\text{\rm supp}(e_{j})\cap C_{n+km}|>k
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
and for all
k\in\Z_{+}
.
Let
k=an+1
.
Then

\displaystyle|\text{\rm supp}(c)\cap C_{n+km}|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{t}|\text{\rm supp}(e_{j})\cap C_{n+km}|

\displaystyle>kt=(an+1)(am+1)=anam+an+am+1

\displaystyle>an+aanm+am=a(n+km).

This is a contradiction with
a
being a sparseness constant of
c
.
∎

In fact, also the converse of the above lemma holds, that is, if a configuration is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers, then it is sparse and periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}
.
Indeed, clearly any
\mathbf{v}
-fiber
e
is sparse since

\text{\rm supp}(e)\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})\subseteq(\mathbf{u}+\Q\mathbf{v})\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})

for some
\mathbf{u}

and
|(\mathbf{u}+\Q\mathbf{v})\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})|\leq 2m+1
.
By Lemma
21
a sum of finitely many sparse configurations is sparse and hence a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers is sparse and periodic.
Thus, a configuration is sparse and periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}
if and only if it is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers.

The following lemma is Theorem
20
in the case
n=2
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
c
be a sparse configuration and assume that it is annihilated by the product
\varphi\psi
of two line polynomials
\varphi
and
\psi
in non-parallel directions
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{u}
, respectively.
Then

c=c_{1}+c_{2}

where
c_{1}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers and
\varphi c_{1}=0
, and

c_{2}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{u}
-fibers and
\psi c_{2}=0
.

Proof.

Consider the configuration
e_{1}=\psi c
.
It is sparse by Lemma
21
.
Moreover,
\varphi e_{1}=\varphi\psi c=0
and hence
e_{1}
is periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}
.
Thus, by Lemma
24
the configuration
e_{1}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers.
Similarly, we conclude that the configuration
e_{2}=\varphi c
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{u}
-fibers.

Let
p\in\Z\setminus\{0\}
be such that
e_{1}
is
p\mathbf{v}
-periodic.
Let
c_{1}
be a limit of a converging subsequence of the sequence

c,X^{p\mathbf{v}}c,X^{2p\mathbf{v}}c,X^{3p\mathbf{v}}c,\ldots

of translated copies of
c
by multiples of
p\mathbf{v}
.
By compactness of the configuration space such subsequence exists.
Since
c_{1}\in\overline{\mathcal{O}(c)}
and
c
is sparse, by Lemma
22
also
c_{1}
is sparse.
Let
k_{1}<k_{2}<k_{3}<\ldots
be such that

c_{1}=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{k_{i}p\mathbf{v}}c
.
We have

\varphi c_{1}=\varphi\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{k_{i}p\mathbf{v}}c=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{k_{i}p\mathbf{v}}\varphi c=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{k_{i}p\mathbf{v}}e_{2}=0.

Above we used the fact that the function
e\mapsto ge
is a continuous function in the topology for a configuration
e
and polynomial
g
.
The final equality holds because
e_{2}
is a sum of finitely many
\mathbf{u}
-fibers that are moved by
k_{i}p\mathbf{v}
-translations arbitrarily far away from the origin and hence disappear when we take the limit.
Moreover, we have

\psi c_{1}=\psi\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{k_{i}p\mathbf{v}}c=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{k_{i}p\mathbf{v}}\psi c=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{k_{i}p\mathbf{v}}e_{1}=e_{1}.

Again, we used the continuity of the function
e\mapsto ge
.
The final equality holds because
e_{1}
is
p\mathbf{v}
-periodic.

Similarly, we take
q\in\Z\setminus\{0\}
such that
e_{2}
is
q\mathbf{u}
-periodic.
Then we define

c_{2}=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{t_{i}q\mathbf{u}}c

for a suitable sequence
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<\ldots
.
Again, since
c_{2}\in\overline{\mathcal{O}(c)}
and
c
is sparse, by Lemma
22
also
c_{2}
is sparse.
By similar arguments as above, we have

\psi c_{2}=\psi\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{t_{i}q\mathbf{u}}c=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{t_{i}q\mathbf{u}}\psi c=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{t_{i}q\mathbf{u}}e_{1}=0

and

\varphi c_{2}=\varphi\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{t_{i}q\mathbf{u}}c=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{t_{i}q\mathbf{u}}\varphi c=\lim_{i\to\infty}X^{t_{i}q\mathbf{u}}e_{2}=e_{2}.

Let us show that
c=c_{1}+c_{2}
.
We have

\varphi(c-c_{1}-c_{2})=\varphi c-\varphi c_{1}-\varphi c_{2}=e_{2}-0-e_{2}=0.

Hence,
c-c_{1}-c_{2}
is periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}
.
By Lemma
21
it is sparse since
c
,
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are all sparse.
It follows by Lemma
24
that
c-c_{1}-c_{2}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers.
Similarly,

\psi(c-c_{1}-c_{2})=\psi c-\psi c_{1}-\psi c_{2}=e_{1}-e_{1}-0=0

and hence
c-c_{1}-c_{2}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{u}
-fibers.
So,
c-c_{1}-c_{2}
is both a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers and a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{u}
-fibers.
Since
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{u}
are non-parallel, it follows that

c-c_{1}-c_{2}=0
.

We have seen that

c=c_{1}+c_{2}

where
\varphi c_{1}=0
and
\psi c_{2}=0
.
Moreover, both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are sparse.
Thus,
c_{1}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}
-fibers and
c_{2}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{u}
-fibers by Lemma
24
.
The claim follows.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
20
.

Proof of Theorem
20
.

The proof is by induction on
n
.
First, let us consider the case
n=1
.
So,
c
is periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}_{1}
.
Then by Lemma
24
it is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}_{1}
-fibers.

Assume then that
n>1
and that the claim holds for
n-1
.
Consider the configuration

c^{\prime}=\varphi_{n}c.

It is sparse and annihilated by
\varphi_{1}\cdots\varphi_{n-1}
.
By the induction hypothesis

c^{\prime}=c_{1}^{\prime}+\ldots+c_{n-1}^{\prime}

where each
c_{i}^{\prime}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-fibers and annihilated by
\varphi_{i}
.

So, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
the configuration
c^{\prime}_{i}
is periodic in direction
\mathbf{v}_{i}
, that is,
k_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
-periodic for some
k_{i}\in\Z\setminus\{0\}
.
Consider the sequence

c^{\prime},X^{k_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}}c^{\prime},X^{2k_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}}c^{\prime},X^{3k_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}}c^{\prime},\ldots.

It converges to
c_{i}^{\prime}
and hence
c_{i}^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{O}(c^{\prime})}
.
Let
e
be a limit of a converging subsequence of the sequence

c,X^{k_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}}c,X^{2k_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}}c,X^{3k_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}}c,\ldots.

By compactness of the configuration space such subsequence exists.

We have
\varphi_{n}e=c_{i}^{\prime}
.
Since
c
is sparse and
e\in\overline{\mathcal{O}(c)}
, by Lemma
22
also
e
is sparse.
Moreover, we have
\varphi_{i}\varphi_{n}e=0
since
\varphi_{i}c_{i}^{\prime}=0
.
By Lemma
25
we have

e=e_{i}+e_{n}

where
e_{i}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-fibers and
\varphi_{i}e_{i}=0
, and
e_{n}
is a sum of finitely many
\mathbf{v}_{n}
-fibers and
\varphi_{n}e_{n}=0
.
It follows that
\varphi_{n}e_{i}=\varphi_{n}e=c_{i}^{\prime}
.

Now, we choose
c_{i}=e_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
and

c_{n}=c-c_{1}-\ldots-c_{n-1}
.
Clearly,
c=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{n}
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}c_{n}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{n}c-\varphi_{n}c_{1}-\ldots-\varphi_{n}c_{n-1}

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}-c_{1}^{\prime}-\ldots-c_{n-1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0.

Since for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
the configuration
c_{i}
is sparse as a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-fibers and
c
is sparse, also
c_{n}=c-c_{1}-\ldots-c_{n-1}
is sparse by Lemma
21
.
Thus, by Lemma
24
the configuration
c_{n}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}_{n}
-fibers.
∎

Theorem
20
together with Theorem
2
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 26
.

Let
c
be a sparse configuration and assume that it has a non-trivial annihilator.
Then it is a sum of finitely many periodic fibers.

Proof.

By Theorem
2
the configuration
c
is annihilated by a polynomial

(X^{\mathbf{v}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{v}_{m}}-1)

where the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}
are pairwise non-parallel and hence
by Theorem
20
there exist configurations
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
such that each
c_{i}
is a sum of finitely many periodic
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-fibers and

c=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}
.
Thus,
c
is a sum of finitely many periodic fibers.
∎

Application to
\R
-configurations with uniformly discrete supports that have annihilators

In the following we consider one-dimensional
\R
-configurations, that is, functions
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\R}
where
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Z
is a finite subset of integers.
Most of the concepts concerning
\Z^{d}
-configurations are naturally expanded for
\R
-configurations.
For example, the translation
\tau^{v}(c)
of
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\R}
by
v\in\R
is defined such that
\tau^{v}(c)(u)=c(u-v)
for all
u\in\R
.
Consequently, we say that
c
is periodic if
\tau^{v}(c)=c
for some non-zero
v
.
Naturally, the support
\text{\rm supp}(c)\subseteq\R
of an
\R
-configuration
c
is defined as the set
where
c
gets non-zero values.

The concept of annihilators is also generalized to
\R
-configurations.
In other words, we say that an
\R
-configuration
c
has a non-trivial annihilator if there exists a non-empty finite set
D=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subseteq\R
and integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\Z
such that
a_{1}\tau^{v_{1}}(c)+\ldots+a_{n}\tau^{v_{n}}(c)=0
.
We then say that
c
is annihilated by the
\R
-polynomial

f=f(x)=a_{1}x^{v_{1}}+\ldots+a_{n}x^{v_{n}}
and may denote
fc=0
.

We say that a subset
S\subseteq\R
is
uniformly discrete
if
there exists a positive real number
r
such that for any two distinct
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
we have

|s_{1}-s_{2}|\geq 2r
.
Largest such
r
is called the
packing radius
of
S
.

Next, we prove that any
\R
-configuration with a uniformly discrete support that has a non-trivial annihilator is necessarily periodic.
In the proof, we use the following lemma, which is based on the fact that every point has a dense orbit with respect to an irrational rotation of the circle
[
20
]
.
We denote by
\lfloor x\rfloor
and
\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
the integer and fractional parts, respectively, of a real number
x\in\R
.

Lemma 27
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,r_{1},r_{2}\in\R
.
Moreover, assume that
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are rationally independent, that is,
m_{1}r_{1}=m_{2}r_{2}
if and only if
m_{1}=m_{2}=0
where
m_{1},m_{2}\in\Z
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
m_{1},m_{2}\in\Z
such that
\alpha+m_{1}r_{1}
and
\beta+m_{2}r_{2}
are distinct and within distance
\varepsilon
from each other.

Proof.

By the assumption
\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}
is an irrational number.
Consider the map
\rho\colon\R\to[0,1),\ x\mapsto\{x+\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}\}
.
It is well known that every point of
[0,1)
has a dense orbit with respect to
\rho
since
\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}
is irrational
[
20
]
.
This means that for every

\varepsilon>0
there exists
m
such that

0<\rho^{m}\left(\frac{\alpha-\beta}{r_{2}}\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{|r_{2}|}

and hence

0<\frac{\alpha-\beta}{r_{2}}+m_{1}\cdot\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}-\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha-\beta}{r_{2}}+m_{1}\cdot\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}\right\rfloor<\frac{\varepsilon}{|r_{2}|}.

Now, by multiplying the inequality by
r_{2}
and denoting
m_{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha-\beta}{r_{2}}+m_{1}\cdot\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}\right\rfloor
we have

0<|\alpha+m_{1}r_{1}-(\beta+m_{2}r_{2})|<\varepsilon.

Thus,
\alpha+m_{1}r_{1}
and
\beta+m_{2}r_{2}
are distinct and within distance
\varepsilon
.
∎

Theorem 28
.

Let
c\in\mathcal{A}^{\R}
be an
\R
-configuration with a uniformly discrete support.
If
c
has a non-trivial annihilator,
then
c
is periodic.

Proof.

Let
f=f(x)=a_{1}x^{t_{1}}+\ldots+a_{s}x^{t_{s}}
be a non-trivial annihilator of
c
and let
d
be the rank of the additive abelian group
\Z[\text{\rm supp}(f)]
.
Let
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{d}\}
be a (minimal) generator set of this group.
Let us define a projection map

\pi\colon\R^{d}\to\R,\ (i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\mapsto i_{1}b_{1}+\ldots+i_{d}b_{d}.

By the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups
[
6
]
the group
\Z[\text{\rm supp}(f)]
is isomorphic to
\Z^{d}
since it has no torsion elements as a subgroup of
\R
.
This means that every element of the group
\Z[\text{\rm supp}(f)]
has a unique presentation of the form
i_{1}b_{1}+\ldots+i_{d}b_{d}
where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\in\Z
, that is,
the restriction
\pi\restriction_{\Z^{d}}
is injective.

Define for all
\alpha\in\R
, a
\Z^{d}
-configuration
c^{(\alpha)}\in\mathcal{A}^{\Z^{d}}
such that

c^{(\alpha)}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})=c(\alpha+i_{1}b_{1}+\ldots+i_{d}b_{d}).

Let us show that
c^{(\alpha)}
is sparse for all
\alpha
.
So, consider an arbitrary
c^{(\alpha)}
.
Since
\text{\rm supp}(c)
is uniformly discrete, there exists

\delta>0
such that each half-open interval

I_{k}=[\alpha+k\delta,\alpha+(k+1)\delta)

contains at most one point of
\text{\rm supp}(c)
for each
k\in\Z
.
Thus, the sets

S_{k}=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in\R^{d}\mid k\delta\leq i_{1}b_{1}+\ldots+i_{d}b_{d}<(k+1)\delta\}

contain at most one element of
\text{\rm supp}(c^{(\alpha)})\subseteq\Z^{d}
by the mentioned injectivity of the map
\pi\restriction_{\Z^{d}}
.
For
m\in\Z_{+}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
, let
N(m,\mathbf{t})
denote the number of sets
S_{k}
that intersect the set
C_{m}+\mathbf{t}
.
Note that the set
C_{m}+\mathbf{t}
intersects
S_{k}
if and only if the set
\pi(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})+\alpha
intersects
I_{k}
.
The set
\pi(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})+\alpha
is an interval of length
(2m+1)l
where
l
is the length of the interval
\pi([0,1]^{d})
— the image of the unit cube under the projection map
\pi
.
So, we conclude that the set
\pi(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})+\alpha
intersects with at most
(2m+1)l/\delta+2
sets
I_{k}
.
In particular, there
exists
a\in\Z_{+}
such that

N(m,\mathbf{t})\leq am
for all
m\in\Z_{+}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
.
Thus,

|\text{\rm supp}(c^{(\alpha)})\cap(C_{m}+\mathbf{t})|\leq N(m,\mathbf{t})\leq am

for all
m\in\Z_{+}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\Z^{d}
and hence
c^{(\alpha)}
is sparse.

Let us show that
the
\Z^{d}
-configurations
c^{(\alpha)}
have a common non-trivial annihilator.
Now, let
\mathbf{t}_{i}\in\Z^{d}
be such that
\pi(\mathbf{t}_{i})=t_{i}
for each
i
.
Denote
f_{\pi}(X)=a_{1}X^{\mathbf{t}_{1}}+\ldots+a_{s}X^{\mathbf{t}_{s}}
.
For any
\alpha\in\R
and
\mathbf{u}\in\Z^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle f_{\pi}c^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=a_{1}c^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{t}_{1})+\ldots+a_{s}c^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{t}_{s})

\displaystyle=a_{1}c(\alpha+\pi(\mathbf{u})-t_{1})+\ldots+a_{s}c(\alpha+\pi(\mathbf{u})-t_{s})

\displaystyle=(fc)(\alpha+\pi(\mathbf{u}))

\displaystyle=0

since
f
is an annihilator of
c
.
Thus,
f_{\pi}
is a common annihilator of the
\Z^{d}
-configurations
c^{(\alpha)}
.

Now, by Theorem
7
there exist pairwise linearly independent vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}\in\Z^{d}
such that every
c^{(\alpha)}
is annihilated by the same polynomial

(X^{\mathbf{v}_{1}}-1)\cdots(X^{\mathbf{v}_{n}}-1).

Thus, by Theorem
20
we have

c^{(\alpha)}=c^{(\alpha)}_{1}+\ldots+c^{(\alpha)}_{n}

where each
c^{(\alpha)}_{i}
is a sum of finitely many
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-periodic
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-fibers.
Denote
\mathbf{v}_{i}=(v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,d})
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Let us show that for all
\alpha
, we have
c^{(\alpha)}=c_{i}^{(\alpha)}
for the same
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

First, we will show that
for all
\alpha
, we have

c^{(\alpha)}=c_{i}^{(\alpha)}
for some
\alpha
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Assume on the contrary that

c_{i}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0
and
c_{j}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0
for some
i\neq j
.
Then
there exist
\mathbf{u}_{i}=(u_{i,1},\ldots,u_{i,d})
and
\mathbf{u}_{j}=(u_{j,1},\ldots,u_{j,d})
such that

c^{(\alpha)}_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\neq 0
and
c^{(\alpha)}_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\neq 0
.
Since
c_{i}^{(\alpha)}
is
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-periodic and
c_{j}^{(\alpha)}
is
\mathbf{v}_{j}
-periodic, it follows that

c^{(\alpha)}_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i}+t\mathbf{v}_{i})\neq 0
and
c^{(\alpha)}_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{j}+t\mathbf{v}_{j})\neq 0
for all
t\in\Z
.
Thus,
c(\alpha_{i}+t(v_{i,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{i,d}b_{d}))\neq 0
and
c(\alpha_{j}+t(v_{j,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{j,d}b_{d}))\neq 0
for all
t\in\Z
where

\alpha_{i}=\alpha+u_{i,1}+\ldots+u_{i,d}

and

\alpha_{j}=\alpha+u_{j,1}+\ldots+u_{j,d}
.
If

m(v_{i,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{i,d}b_{d})=m^{\prime}(v_{j,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{j,d}b_{d})

for some
m,m^{\prime}\in\Z
,
then
mv_{i,1}=m^{\prime}v_{j,1},\ldots,mv_{i,d}=m^{\prime}v_{j,d}

by the injectivity of the map
\pi\restriction_{\Z^{d}}
.
Thus,
m\mathbf{v}_{i}=m^{\prime}\mathbf{v}_{j}
and hence

m=m^{\prime}=0
since
\mathbf{v}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{j}
are linearly independent.
This means that the numbers
v_{i,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{i,d}b_{d}
and
v_{j,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{j,d}b_{d}
are rationally independent.
Consequently,
for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
m,m^{\prime}\in\Z
such that
\alpha_{i}+m(v_{i,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{i,d}b_{d})
and
\alpha_{j}+m^{\prime}(v_{j,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{j,d}b_{d})
are distinct and within distance
\varepsilon
from each other by Lemma
27
.
This is a contradiction with the uniform discreteness of
\text{\rm supp}(c)
.

Finally, let us show that for all
\alpha
we have
c^{(\alpha)}=c_{i}^{(\alpha)}
for the same
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Assume on the contary that we have for
some
\alpha
and
\beta

that
c^{(\alpha)}=c_{i}^{(\alpha)}
and
c^{(\beta)}=c_{j}^{(\beta)}
for
i\neq j
.
Let

\mathbf{u}_{i}=(u_{i,1},\ldots,u_{i,d})
and
\mathbf{u}_{j}=(u_{j,1},\ldots,u_{j,d})
be such that

c^{(\alpha)}_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\neq 0
and
c^{(\beta)}_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\neq 0
and
denote
\alpha_{i}=\alpha+u_{i,1}+\ldots+u_{i,d}
and
\alpha_{j}=\beta+u_{j,1}+\ldots+u_{j,d}
.
Again, we have

c(\alpha_{i}+t(v_{i,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{i,d}b_{d}))\neq 0
and
c(\alpha_{j}+t(v_{j,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{j,d}b_{d}))\neq 0
for all
t\in\Z
.
We noticed already above
that
the numbers
v_{i,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{i,d}b_{d}
and
v_{j,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{j,d}b_{d}
are rationally independent.
Thus, again by Lemma
27
there exist
m,m^{\prime}\in\Z
such that

\alpha_{i}+m(v_{i,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{i,d}b_{d})
and
\alpha_{j}+m^{\prime}(v_{j,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{j,d}b_{d})

are different but arbitrarily close.
A contradiction with the uniform discreteness of
\text{\rm supp}(c)
.

So, we conclude that for all
\alpha\in\R
, we have
c^{(\alpha)}=c_{i}^{(\alpha)}
for the same
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
It follows that every
c^{(\alpha)}
is
\mathbf{v}_{i}
-periodic and hence

c
is
(v_{i,1}b_{1}+\ldots+v_{i,d}b_{d})
-periodic.
∎

Finally, we give an example that shows that the above theorem does not hold for general
\R
-configurations that have annihilators.

Example 29
.

Consider the
\R
-configurations

c_{1},c_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{\R}
defined such that

c_{1}(i)=\begin{cases}1&\text{, if }i\in\Z\\
0&\text{, otherwise}\end{cases}

and

c_{2}(i)=\begin{cases}1&\text{, if }i\in\alpha\Z\\
0&\text{, otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\alpha
is an irrational number.
Both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are periodic, that is, annihilated by non-trivial difference
\R
-polynomials.
More precisely,
c_{1}
is annihilated by
x-1
and
c_{2}
is annihilated by
x^{\alpha}-1
.
Consequently, their sum
c=c_{1}+c_{2}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\R}
has a non-trivial annihilator

(x-1)(x^{\alpha}-1)
. However,
c
is non-periodic since
\alpha
is irrational.
