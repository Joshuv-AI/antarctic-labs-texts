Fusion_f5C-Pred: a dual-branch feature fusion framework for 5-formylcytosine modification sites prediction
Authors: Cong Hui, Junhao Yu, Jianhua Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground5-formylcytidine (f5C) is a unique post-transcriptional RNA modification present at the wobble position of mRNAs and tRNAs, that plays a critical role in mitochondrial protein synthesis and is potentially involved in translation regulation. Recent studies have revealed that f5C modifications may promote cancer metastasis by driving mitochondrial mRNA translation. However, existing computational methods for predicting f5C modification sites are scarce and have significant limitations, which has hindered in-depth investigations into their molecular functions and regulatory mechanisms.ResultsTo address these limitations, we developed Fusion_f5C-Pred, an innovative dual-branch deep learning framework that integrates both sequence and structural features through a gated fusion network. The sequence branch employs a densely connected convolutional network integrated with the convolutional block attention module to capture local sequence patterns, whereas the structural branch utilizes the transformer-encoder to learn RNA secondary structure features. Comprehensive evaluations of independent datasets demonstrate that Fusion_f5C-Pred achieves superior prediction performance with an ACC of 0.7952 and an AUROC score of 0.8684, significantly outperforming existing methods. The t-SNE visualization analysis confirms that the fused features exhibit enhanced inter-class separation in the representation space.ConclusionsNotably, our model's learned sequence patterns strongly agree with known RNA regulatory motifs identified via MEME, indicating biological interpretability. The proposed framework not only offers a robust computational tool for f5C research but also establishes a transferable architecture for studying other RNA modifications. The source code and datasets are publicly available at: https://github.com/HuiCong123/Fusion_f5C-Pred.Keywords: 5-Formylcytidine, Gated fusion network, Deep learning, Interpretability

Introduction

As a core research direction in epigenetics, RNA modifications have been shown to be widely involved in key biological processes such as gene expression regulation, RNA stability, and translation efficiency [1]. Currently, more than 170 types of RNA modifications have been identified [2]. Among the numerous RNA modifications, 5-formylcytosine (f5C) is an important member of the oxidation modification family. As shown in Fig. 1, it is catalyzed by TET enzymes or ALKBH family proteins, which progressively oxidize 5-methylcytosine (m5C) or 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (hm5C) to form f5C [3, 4]. The f5C modification is distributed in tRNA, mRNA, and mitochondrial RNA and may influence RNA secondary structure, protein-binding capacity, and translation regulatory functions [5, 6]. However, owing to the low abundance and dynamic nature of the f5C modification, its precise detection and site prediction across the entire transcriptome remain significant challenges.

The process of m5C being gradually oxidized to f5C by TET/ALKBH proteins

f5C was first discovered at the wobble position of mitochondrial tRNA, where its unique formyl group confers a distinct spatial conformation and hydrogen-bonding capability to the modified molecule [7]. By influencing the interaction between tRNA and the ribosome, f5C plays a crucial role in ensuring the fidelity of both cytoplasmic and mitochondrial translation [8]. Studies have shown that f5C modification in the tRNA anticodon facilitates the recognition of AUA and AUG codons as methionine codons [9]. This modification is not exclusive to tRNA; its presence has also been detected in mRNAs, underscoring its significant biological functions. Notably, the absence of f5C34 modification has been closely linked to the development and progression of various diseases [10, 11]. In highly invasive and metastatic cancers, m5C and f5C modifications promote tumor metastasis by activating the translation of mitochondrial mRNAs [12]. These findings suggest that targeting these RNA-specific modifications to inhibit mitochondrial mRNA translation may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for treating cancer metastasis.

Currently, the identification of f5C modifications primarily relies on experimental methods such as chemical labeling coupled with mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) or high-throughput sequencing [13]. Recently, Wang et al. proposed the f5C-seq method, which utilizes pic-borane to reduce f5C to dihydrouracil (DHU), generating C-to-T mutation signals during reverse transcription to quantitatively detect f5C sites [14]. Li et al. applied Mal-Seq to characterize f5C at the wobble position of mt-tRNA (Met) across different organisms and tissue types, revealing high levels of f5C modification in mammals but lower levels in less complex eukaryotes [15].

However, these techniques are limited by high costs, low throughput, and complex data analysis, making them impractical for large-scale sample screening. Consequently, the development of computational biology approaches—such as traditional machine learning or deep learning models—to predict f5C modification sites has emerged as a critical strategy to overcome the limitations of experimental methods [16, 17]. Among these, support vector machines (SVM) [18–21], XGBoost (XGB) [22, 23], random forest (RF) [24, 25], and logistic regression (LR) [26] are some of the most widely used machine learning algorithms for predicting RNA modification sites. SVM, a versatile algorithm for small-sample datasets [18], is considered one of the most popular machine learning methods. For instance, iRNA-m6A [27], RAMPred [28], RNAm5CPred [29], and m7GHub [30] employ SVM to predict m6A, m1A, m5C, and m7G sites, respectively. Additionally, deep learning-based tools have been increasingly applied to RNA modification site prediction [31–34], achieving remarkable progress in the prediction of common modifications such as m6A and m5C. For example, Tahir et al. proposed iPseU-CNN [35], which uses convolutional neural networks (CNN) to identify pseudouridine. ELMo4m6A [36] employs long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to identify m6A sites. Xiang et al. developed M6ATMR [37], which leverages Transformer to guide the reconstruction of sequence similarity matrices, thereby enhancing feature representation. Jia et al. introduced i5mC-DCGA [38], which innovatively integrates a convolutional block attention module (CBAM) with a densely connected convolutional network (DenseNet) to extract high-level local features for m5C site prediction. In addition to sequence-based prediction methods, some models incorporate additional features, such as secondary structure, to predict RNA modifications. Recently, Bai et al. proposed MultiV_Nm [39], which integrates sequence features, chemical properties, and secondary structure features to comprehensively mine and fuse multi-view features for Nm modification site prediction.

However, accurate prediction models for f5C modifications remain scarce. The most recent advancement in this field is the method proposed by Huang et al. [40], which, in addition to sequence information, incorporates 32 novel domain-derived genomic features. Their best single model, Resf5C-Pred, achieved an ACC of 0.7080 and an AUROC of 0.8076. The final ensemble model, XGB + Resf5C-Pred, achieved an ACC of 0.7463 and an AUROC of 0.8391. However, this method relies solely on the WHISTLE server [41], a web-based tool for obtaining domain-derived genomic features, which is currently unavailable. Moreover, the method simply concatenates sequence and genomic features before feature extraction, without employing network models tailored to different feature types. These limitations highlight the need for further improvements.

In this study, we constructed a deep learning-based f5C modification site prediction model, Fusionf5C-Pred, by integrating more accessible secondary structure information with sequence data. Ultimately, Fusionf5C-Pred achieved an ACC of 0.7952 and an AUROC of 0.8684 on an independent test set, outperforming state-of-the-art methods trained and tested on the same dataset. These results highlight the ability of feature fusion to achieve robust and effective prediction of f5C modification sites.

Methods

Benchmark dataset

The establishment of a high-quality benchmark dataset is paramount for developing reliable predictive models. In this study, we employed a dataset previously described in [40]. The positive dataset, comprising experimentally validated f5C sites, was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession number GSE133138, from the f5C-seq methodology [14]. It provides high-quality, experimentally validated f5C sites, making it a reliable benchmark for model development. Specifically, our benchmark dataset consists of: (1) positive samples: 1,892 base-resolution experimentally verified f5C modification sites in yeast mRNA; and (2) negative samples: an equal number of unmodified cytosine (C) sites randomly selected from the same transcripts containing the positive sites, maintaining a 1:1 ratio. To ensure robust model training and evaluation, the final dataset was randomly partitioned into training and test sets at a 4:1 ratio.

Overall architecture

For multi-feature fusion, existing methods either directly concatenate features [40] or extract and then concatenate them [42, 43]. In contrast, Fusionf5C-Pred adopts a dual-branch architecture: one branch employs sequence-based feature extraction, while the other uses secondary structure features obtained via the RNAfold tool from ViennaRNA [44]. For each branch, we compared existing mainstream machine learning and deep learning algorithms, selecting the network model that demonstrated superior feature extraction performance for the respective branch. We then innovatively introduced a gated fusion network to integrate the features extracted from both branches. Fig. 2 shows the development process of our method, starting from feature generation (Fig. 2 A, B), development of a branched model architecture (Fig. 2 C-E) and feature fusion and classification (Fig. 2 F, G). Moreover, to validate our deep learning model's ability to learn biologically meaningful RNA sequence features, we compared its first-layer convolutional filter patterns with known regulatory motifs identified by MEME. This analysis confirmed the model's capacity to detect authentic sequence features while maintaining predictive performance.

Data preprocessing and feature encoding

The 41-nucleotide window length has been widely adopted in previous studies [45–47] to capture the relevant sequence context information. Since we used the same dataset as Resf5C-Pred [40], we selected the same window size to facilitate model comparison. To characterize the molecular features associated with f5C modifications comprehensively, we developed an integrative feature encoding framework that simultaneously captures nucleotide sequence information and RNA secondary structure characteristics.

Sequence feature encoding

The sequence-based features were encoded via a one-hot representation system (Fig. 2A), where each of the four nucleotides was transformed into a distinct 4-dimensional binary vector (A: [1,0,0,0]; C: [0,1,0,0]; G: [0,0,1,0]; U: [0,0,0,1]). To further enrich the sequence context representation, we incorporated a fifth feature channel representing nucleotide density (ND) [48], which quantifies the cumulative frequency distribution of each nucleotide along the sequence by calculating the ratio of a given nucleotide's occurrence count to its position index (). For the sequence “AUAGUCAUAA”, the densities of A at positions 1, 3, 7, 9, and 10 are 1, 0.67, 0.43, 0.44, and 0.50, respectively. This encoding effectively reflects the distribution characteristics of nucleotides during sequence extension. Each encoded RNA sequence has a feature dimension of 41 × 5 for sequence characteristics.

Structural feature encoding

For structural characterization, we employed ViennaRNA's RNAfold package [44] (Fig. 2B) to extract three key RNA secondary structure parameters: minimum free energy (MFE) as a measure of thermodynamic stability, dot-bracket notation representing base-pairing patterns, and GC content reflecting sequence composition. The dot-bracket notation provides an intuitive textual representation of base pairing relationships within RNA secondary structures, corresponding one-to-one with the structure predicted by MFE. It precisely describes the base pairing status of each nucleotide in the RNA sequence through a string composed of symbols such as parentheses and dots: matched parentheses pairs (“(” and “)”) indicate base pairing at corresponding positions, forming double-stranded stem structures; dots (“.”) represent unpaired single-stranded regions, such as loops.

This multi-dimensional feature integration strategy enables our model to simultaneously consider both the primary sequence context through one-hot encoding and ND features, as well as higher-order structural information through MFE, secondary structure motifs, and compositional characteristics, thereby providing a comprehensive representation of the molecular environment surrounding potential f5C modification sites. Finally, all features are processed based on 41-nt sequences. Numerical features like MFE and GC content undergo Z-score normalization to ensure they share a comparable numerical scale with features like one-hot encoding, thereby enhancing compatibility with downstream machine learning algorithms and improving model performance and training speed. Each encoded RNA sequence has a feature dimension of 20 × 1 for structural characteristics.

Feature extraction

To enhance feature extraction performance, we address the limitations of single-feature approaches by implementing a dual-feature fusion strategy. Accordingly, we design a dual-branch network architecture: the first branch employs our sequence-based feature extraction method, while the second branch utilizes a transformer architecture for structural feature learning. This dual-branch design effectively leverages the complementary advantages of different feature types, thereby improving model performance through enriched feature representations.

Sequence branch

Inspired by the i5mC-DCGA [38], this study proposes a hybrid neural network architecture named Dense_CBAM (Fig. 2C, D) based on one-dimensional RNA sequence information. Structural branch

The transformer architecture represents a groundbreaking advancement in deep learning, demonstrating exceptional performance in sequence modeling through its unique self-attention mechanism, which effectively addresses the vanishing gradient problem inherent in traditional recurrent neural networks when processing long sequences [49]. In structural biology applications such as RNA secondary structure prediction and protein folding analysis, transformer models have exhibited remarkable advantages [50], primarily attributable to their capability for synergistic modeling of both local and global sequence features.

Inspired by these developments, we implemented a transformer-encoder-based feature extraction architecture, as illustrated in Fig. 2E. The architecture begins with an embedding layer that maps scalar inputs to a high-dimensional space through linear transformation, followed by Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) activation and layer normalization. Notably, we incorporated learnable positional embedding parameters—a design choice that effectively preserves sequential order information while overcoming the positional information loss typically associated with the permutation invariance of standard transformer models.

The encoder component consists of three stacked encoder layer modules, with the multi-head self-attention mechanism [49] serving as its core. This mechanism not only enhances model interpretability but also helps uncover underlying predictive patterns. Performance evaluation metrics

In this study, we employed the following evaluation procedures and metrics to comprehensively validate model performance: First, we calculated multiple metrics including sensitivity (Sn), specificity (Sp), overall accuracy (ACC), F1-score, and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). We further visualized the model's classification capability by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with sensitivity on the y-axis and 1-specificity on the x-axis, and calculated the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) as an evaluation metric.

Among them, TP represents the number of true positives, while TN represents true negatives; FP stands for the number of false positives, and FN stands for the number of false negatives.

To assess model performance more comprehensively, our experimental design adopted a stratified fivefold cross-validation approach, where 80% of the total data was used as the training set for model training and validation (4 folds for training and onefold for validation in each iteration), while the remaining 20% of the data served as an independent test set for objective final performance evaluation. This evaluation strategy ensures both rigorous model validation and effective prevention of overfitting.

Implementation details

The implementation of this study was conducted via Python 3.10.14, with deep learning models implemented in PyTorch 2.3.1 and traditional machine learning models developed via Scikit-learn 1.5.1 for comparative analysis. Model optimization was performed using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-3 and a weight decay coefficient of 1e-4. All the models were trained for a maximum of 50 epochs, with early stopping implemented on the basis of validation set performance monitoring to balance convergence efficiency and computational resource utilization. To accelerate training, all the deep learning models were executed on GPU-accelerated hardware.

Results

Sequence-only method

To evaluate the performance of sequence-based feature extraction methods systematically, we conducted a comparative analysis of our proposed Dense_CBAM framework against three conventional machine learning classifiers (SVM, LR, and XGB) and two deep learning approaches (CNN and LSTM) via six standard evaluation metrics (Sn, Sp, ACC, F1, MCC, and AUROC). The assessment was performed through rigorous five-fold cross-validation on the training set followed by independent testing on a held-out test set. As shown in Fig. 3, our results demonstrate that Dense_CBAM achieved superior predictive performance compared to all baseline methods, with an ACC of 0.7107 and an AUROC of 0.7812. This is further supported by the comprehensive metric comparison and ROC curve analysis in Fig. 4. However, the observed performance ceiling suggests inherent limitations of sequence-only approaches, likely due to their inability to capture critical higher-order structural information that may play significant roles in f5C modification mechanisms.

Structural-only method

For the structure-only approach, we implemented a systematic evaluation of structure-only approaches for f5C site prediction, employing RNAfold-derived secondary structure features (including minimum free energy, dot-bracket notation, and GC content) as model inputs. Our transformer-based architecture (8 attention heads, 128-dimensional embeddings) demonstrated superior performance compared with conventional methods in both cross-validation and independent testing, with detailed ROC analysis presented in Fig. 5, Fig. 6. It achieves superior prediction performance with an ACC of 0.7662 and an AUROC score of 0.8231 in independent testing. The attention mechanism effectively captured long-range structural dependencies, suggesting its particular suitability for modeling RNA folding patterns. However, the observed performance plateau indicates that while secondary structure features provide valuable discriminative information, they likely require integration with sequence context for comprehensive prediction. These findings provide compelling justification for our subsequent development of an integrated sequence-structure feature extraction framework to achieve more comprehensive and accurate prediction of f5C modification sites.

Feature-fusion method

Here, we present an integrated approach to overcome the limitations of single-feature methods by combining both sequence and structural information. Our hybrid architecture retains the sequence-based Dense_CBAM as one branch while incorporating a parallel transformer branch processing RNAfold-derived secondary structure features. The key innovation lies in our novel gated fusion network (GFN), which dynamically integrates features from both modalities, as opposed to conventional concatenation or weighted fusion methods. As shown in Fig. 7, comparative evaluation demonstrates superior performance of the GFN over alternative fusion strategies in both cross-validation and independent testing, with the ROC analysis detailed in Fig. 8. It achieves superior prediction performance with an ACC of 0.7952 and an AUROC score of 0.8684 in independent testing. Through systematic validation, we established GFN-based fusion as the optimal approach, yielding our final model Fusion_f5C-Pred. This architecture effectively captures complementary information from the sequence context and structural determinants, significantly outperforming unimodal approaches while maintaining the biological interpretability of both feature spaces.

Feature visualization

To provide quantitative evidence for the enhanced discriminative power of our feature fusion approach, we conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [52] visualization of the latent feature spaces learned by three distinct models: a sequence-only baseline model, a structural-only reference model, and our integrated Fusion_f5C framework combining sequence and structural features. As shown in Fig. 9, the t-SNE projections reveal striking differences in class separation quality. The Fusion_f5C model demonstrates superior cluster separation between f5C-modified (positive) and unmodified (negative) sites. This clear demarcation indicates that our fusion mechanism successfully captures biologically relevant discriminative features that are inaccessible to either unimodal approach alone. In contrast, the sequence-only model exhibits substantial overlap between positive and negative clusters, suggesting limited capacity to distinguish modification sites based solely on primary sequence information. The superior separation achieved by Fusion_f5C directly correlates with its enhanced predictive performance in our benchmark tests, validating our hypothesis that simultaneous consideration of both sequence context and structural determinants is essential for accurate f5C site identification.

Generalization capability validation

To further assess the generalization ability of Fusion_f5C-Pred, we extended our framework to another RNA modification type, m5C. For this purpose, we selected the benchmark m5C dataset from Arabidopsis thaliana (as reported in [54]), as its scale is comparable to that of our yeast dataset. This ensures a fair evaluation of model transferability without the confounding effects of dataset imbalance. Importantly, all model parameters and hyperparameters were kept identical to those used for f5C prediction, ensuring that the evaluation truly reflects the intrinsic generalization capacity of our method rather than dataset-specific optimization.

As shown in Table 2, on the independent m5C test set, Fusion_f5C-Pred achieved an ACC of 0.7500 and an AUROC of 0.8232, which are highly comparable to the performance reported by Feadm5C [54], the current state-of-the-art m5C predictor. Despite being originally trained for f5C site prediction, our model was able to maintain robust performance on m5C, demonstrating that the proposed framework captures generalizable sequence and structural signals shared across different types of cytosine modifications.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed Fusion_f5C-Pred, a novel framework that integrates both sequence and structural features through a gated fusion network (GFN) to predict f5C modification sites. Our experimental results demonstrate that this feature-fusion approach outperforms sequence-only and structure-only models, as well as other existing methods such as Resf5C-Pred and F5C-finder, in terms of both accuracy and AUROC. These findings highlight the importance of integrating complementary types of features to improve predictive performance.

A closer examination of the results provides several important insights. First, while the sequence-only model captures nucleotide-level patterns and the structure-only model focuses on structural contexts, both single-modality approaches show limited predictive power. This indicates that f5C modification sites cannot be fully explained by either local sequence signals or secondary structures alone. In contrast, the fusion model effectively leverages the complementary information, leading to a substantial improvement in classification performance. The t-SNE visualization further supports this conclusion, as the feature-fusion model achieves a clearer separation between positive and negative samples compared to individual modalities. These observations demonstrate that a comprehensive representation of RNA context is critical for accurate modification site prediction.

When comparing with existing tools, Fusion_f5C-Pred exhibits several advantages beyond higher predictive performance. For example, unlike methods that depend on specialized external servers, our approach relies only on sequence-derived and readily accessible structural features, ensuring wider applicability and reproducibility. Furthermore, the use of GFN for adaptive weighting of features allows our method to dynamically balance sequence and structure information, which is more effective than simple concatenation or static weighting strategies. These methodological improvements contribute to the robustness and generalizability of the proposed model.

To further assess the generalization ability of Fusion_f5C-Pred, we conducted a generalization experiment on an independent Arabidopsis thaliana m5C dataset of comparable scale. Without any parameter tuning, Fusion_f5C-Pred achieved accuracy and AUROC values that were highly comparable to the current state-of-the-art predictor. This result demonstrates that our framework is not limited to f5C prediction in yeast but can be effectively extended to other types of cytosine modifications and across species, highlighting its robustness and adaptability.

Importantly, our analysis also provides biological insights. The identification and learning of characteristic sequence patterns and motifs are crucial for interpreting the predictive mechanisms of deep learning models. To validate the biological relevance of our deep learning model's RNA sequence feature extraction, we systematically analyzed the sequence motifs enriched in the training dataset via the conventional motif discovery tool MEME [55] (Multiple EM for Motif Elicitation). Comparative analysis revealed a striking concordance between the sequence patterns detected by the first-layer convolutional filters and the most statistically significant RNA regulatory motifs (ranked by E-value) identified by MEME. As illustrated in Fig. 10, the upper panel displays the significant motifs identified by MEME along with their corresponding E-values, whereas the lower panel visualizes the sequence activation patterns captured by the model's corresponding convolutional kernels. This validation demonstrates that our deep learning model not only achieves strong predictive performance but also learns biologically meaningful sequence features at its lower layers that align with those identified by classical bioinformatics approaches, thereby providing important evidence for the model's interpretability.

Despite these promising results, our study has several limitations. First, we note that in higher eukaryotes, f5C modification is widely present across various types of RNA, whereas in Saccharomyces cerevisiae they are relatively limited. However, comprehensive f5C datasets for higher organisms remain limited at present, insufficient to support robust computational modeling. As such datasets become more comprehensive in the future, we plan to extend our research framework to higher eukaryotes to explore the mechanisms underlying f5C modification in complex biological processes, including cancer progression and metastasis. Second, our model currently incorporates only sequence and structural features, while other potentially informative signals, such as evolutionary conservation or chemical reactivity profiles, were not considered. Addressing these limitations will be an important step for future work. In addition, we plan to extend our framework to other RNA modifications and explore integration with RNA foundation models or large-scale pre-trained language models, which may further enhance prediction accuracy and cross-species generalization. Finally, the development of a user-friendly web server would facilitate broader adoption of our tool by the biological research community.

In summary, Fusion_f5C-Pred represents a robust and interpretable framework for f5C site prediction, combining sequence and structural features in a complementary manner. Our results not only demonstrate state-of-the-art predictive performance but also provide meaningful biological insights, underscoring the value of feature integration for studying RNA modifications.

Conclusion

Here, we present Fusion_f5C-Pred, an innovative dual-branch deep learning framework for high-accuracy prediction of f5C RNA modification sites at single-nucleotide resolution. Distinct from existing methods, our framework innovatively integrates both sequence and structural features through a gated fusion network (GFN), where the sequence branch employs Dense_CBAM for sequence feature extraction while the structural branch utilizes transformer to learn RNA secondary structure features that are more readily obtainable than those used in conventional approaches. Evaluation on independent datasets demonstrates that, compared to state-of-the-art algorithms, Fusion_f5C-Pred achieves significantly superior predictive performance (ACC = 0.7952, AUROC = 0.8684).

The t-SNE visualization analysis reveals enhanced inter-class separation of the fused features in the representation space, confirming the effectiveness of multimodal feature integration. Notably, comparative analysis reveals high consistency between the sequence patterns detected by our model and the most significant RNA regulatory motifs (ranked by E-value) identified by MEME, demonstrating that our deep learning model not only exhibits excellent predictive performance but also learns biologically meaningful sequence features at its lower layers that align with classical bioinformatics methods—providing crucial evidence for model interpretability.

However, the application of this method to other types of modification site datasets has not yet been undertaken in order to systematically validate the universality of the proposed dual-branch architecture and gated fusion strategy. Future improvements will be concentrated on three primary areas: firstly, the augmentation of the validation scope to encompass major RNA modifications (e.g. m6A, and Ψ) in diverse biological contexts; secondly, the development of an adaptive feature weighting mechanism to automatically adjust the contribution ratios of sequence features and structural features across different modification types; and thirdly, the introduction of evolutionary conservation patterns as an additional feature branch to enhance prediction robustness.

This study not only provides a reliable computational tool for f5C modification research but also establishes a methodological framework through its dual-branch architecture and feature fusion strategy that can be adapted for other RNA modification site prediction studies. With advances in deep learning technology, integrating this model with large language models holds significant potential to further enhance both prediction accuracy and interpretability in epitranscriptomics research. We also intend to develop a user-friendly web server to facilitate broader application of our method in biological research.
