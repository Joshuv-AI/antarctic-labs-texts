Telenursing to Improve Quality of Life in Women With Breast Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Serena Ricchiuto, Diego Lopane, Stefano Mancin, Daniela Cattani, Alessandra Dacomi, Simone Cosmai, Mattia Bozzetti, Beatrice Mazzoleni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBreast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy in women and a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality. Beyond the physical burden, the disease significantly impacts emotional well‐being and quality of life (QoL). Telenursing, as a nurse‐led telemedicine intervention, has emerged as a promising strategy to support patients remotely.ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of nurse‐led telenursing interventions on QoL in women with breast cancer and to compare the impact of different technological delivery modes.MethodsA systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted following PRISMA 2020 and the Cochrane Handbook. Studies were identified through five databases. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi‐experimental studies comparing nurse‐led telenursing to usual care were included. The primary outcome was QoL. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2.0 and ROBINS‐I; GRADE was used to rate the certainty of evidence.ResultsSeventeen studies (n = 4199) were included in the systematic review. Seven studies provided sufficient quantitative data for meta‐analysis. Overall, telenursing interventions showed a small to moderate pooled effect on quality of life (SMD = 0.40, 95% CI [0.09, 0.70]; p = 0.019); however, after trim‐and‐fill correction for potential publication bias, the adjusted estimate was attenuated and did not reach statistical significance (adjusted SMD = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.17, 0.45]; p = 0.376). Subgroup analyses—to be interpreted as exploratory—suggested a larger effect for mobile application–based interventions (SMD = 0.63, 95% CI [0.46, 0.80], I 2 = 0%, k = 3) compared to telephone follow‐up (SMD = 0.21, 95% CI [−0.34, 0.76], I 2 = 85.2%, k = 4), though the small number of contributing studies limits the reliability of this comparison.ConclusionsThese findings provide preliminary evidence that nurse‐led telenursing may be associated with improvements in quality of life in women with breast cancer, though the certainty of the evidence is low (GRADE: Low). Results should be interpreted with caution given the attenuation of the pooled effect to non‐significance after trim‐and‐fill adjustment, substantial methodological heterogeneity, and the exploratory nature of subgroup comparisons. High‐quality randomized trials are needed to confirm these findings.Keywords: breast cancer, digital health, meta‐analysis, quality of life, systematic review, telenursing

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide, with approximately 2.3 million new cases in 2022 (11.6% of all cancer diagnoses) and an estimated 666,000 deaths, making it the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality in women across 112 countries [1]. Projections indicate that incidence and mortality will increase by 38% and 68%, respectively, by 2050 [2]. Beyond its physical burden, breast cancer exerts a profound psychological toll—spanning diagnosis, treatment, and survivorship—with stress‐related symptoms consistently associated with increased disability and reduced quality of life (QoL) [3, 4].

Traditionally, the follow‐up care of breast cancer patients has relied heavily on in‐person hospital visits, yet concerns about the cost‐effectiveness of these frequent encounters have led to the exploration of alternative, less resource‐intensive models [5]. In recent years, telemedicine has emerged as a promising approach, offering potential benefits not only for patients but also for healthcare providers and systems [6]. The World Health Organization has long acknowledged the essential role of telemedicine in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals by 2030 [7].

At the same time, the nursing profession has undergone significant evolution, with a growing number of highly trained and specialized nurses assuming a key role in addressing the informational and psychosocial needs of breast cancer patients [8]. Nurse‐led remote follow‐up interventions have been associated with positive quality of life outcomes and may serve as a viable alternative to in‐person care, without compromising clinical effectiveness [9, 10]. Technologies such as landline and mobile phones, internet‐based platforms, and smartphone applications have been widely employed in oncology settings, and evidence suggests that substituting conventional in‐person care with remote care does not negatively impact patients' psychological well‐being [10, 11].

To provide a theoretical foundation for evaluating telenursing effectiveness, we drew upon Ferrell's Quality of Life Model for Cancer Survivors [12], which conceptualizes QoL across four domains: physical well‐being, psychological well‐being, social concerns, and spiritual well‐being. This model is particularly relevant for breast cancer populations, as it recognizes the multidimensional impact of cancer diagnosis, treatment, and survivorship on women's lives. The model posits that effective interventions must address QoL holistically across these interconnected domains rather than focusing narrowly on physical symptoms alone. Additionally, we considered the Nursing Care Continuity Framework [13], which emphasizes that continuous nursing support—whether delivered in person or through technology‐mediated channels—enhances patient outcomes through consistent assessment, education, symptom management, and emotional support. Telenursing interventions operationalize this continuity principle by extending nursing presence beyond traditional healthcare settings into patients' homes and daily lives, enabling more frequent monitoring and timely intervention.

Telenursing—defined as the delivery of nursing care and patient support through telecommunications technologies by a registered nurse acting as the primary provider—is conceptually and operationally distinct from broader telehealth or multidisciplinary digital health interventions led by physicians, pharmacists, or interprofessional teams. This distinction is clinically meaningful: in line with the International Council of Nurses (ICN) scope‐of‐practice framework, nurse‐led remote interventions emphasize relational care, continuous symptom monitoring, patient education, and psychosocial support—competencies central to nursing practice but not always present in multidisciplinary telehealth models. Prior systematic reviews in this field [9, 13] have pooled interventions delivered by heterogeneous provider types, thereby limiting conclusions attributable specifically to nursing input. To the authors' knowledge, the present review is the first to isolate nurse‐led telenursing as the unit of analysis. By focusing exclusively on interventions in which a registered nurse serves as the primary provider, this review enables attribution of effects to the nursing component of telemedicine, avoiding the conflation of effects attributable to technology platforms with those attributable to nursing expertise and relational care.

By integrating these theoretical frameworks, we hypothesized that nurse‐led telenursing interventions would improve QoL in women with breast cancer by providing: (1) accessible and timely symptom monitoring and management, (2) continuous emotional support and evidence‐based patient education, (3) early detection of physical or psychological complications requiring clinical intervention, and (4) reinforcement of self‐management skills and patient empowerment. Furthermore, we anticipated that the specific delivery modality (mobile applications versus telephone calls) might differentially influence effectiveness.

This study was guided by a central research question: Can nurse‐led interventions delivered via telemedicine improve the quality of life of women with breast cancer? The primary aim was to evaluate the impact of telenursing on QoL outcomes compared to standard care, with the broader goal of informing the development of innovative, effective, and sustainable care models.

In light of the variability in digital tools used across studies, a secondary objective was to identify which technologies are most strongly associated with improved QoL, in order to guide both clinical practice and future research toward more promising solutions.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.3 [14]. The reporting followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [15]. The research question was formulated using the PICOS framework [16], which is well suited to quantitative studies comparing nurse‐led telenursing interventions against usual care in women diagnosed with breast cancer. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi‐experimental studies that assessed the effectiveness of telenursing on quality of life outcomes. This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [15]. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Table S1. The review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/2P3MK) on March 10, 2025, prior to data extraction (commenced March 14, 2025). No deviations from the registered protocol were introduced.

The inclusion of quasi‐experimental study designs alongside randomized controlled trials was pre‐specified in the registered protocol, motivated by the limited number of RCTs identified during preliminary scoping (n = 5) and the need to maximize comprehensiveness of the evidence base. Consistent with the registered protocol, quasi‐experimental studies were included in the narrative synthesis but were subject to exclusion from the quantitative meta‐analysis if they were rated as high overall risk of bias using ROBINS‐I or if they did not provide sufficient quantitative data for effect size calculation. This threshold was determined a priori and applied independently by two reviewers.

Search Strategy

The research question was structured using the PICOS model (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study design) to ensure clarity and methodological consistency [16]. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined accordingly. The complete, verbatim search strategy for PubMed—including all MeSH terms, free‐text variants, field tags, date and language filters, and the number of records retrieved—is provided in Table S2. Search strategies for the remaining databases (Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science) are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Eligibility Criteria

The target population included adult women diagnosed with breast cancer, either undergoing treatment or in follow‐up for no more than 5 years. Eligible interventions were nurse‐led telenursing interventions delivered via any technology platform (e.g., telephone calls, video conferencing, mobile health applications, messaging tools, websites). Telemedicine interventions led by non‐nursing professionals or not primarily delivered by nurses were excluded. Studies were required to include a comparison group receiving usual care. Those comparing different types of telenursing technologies or other telehealth interventions without a usual care comparator were excluded. The primary outcome of interest was quality of life (QoL), and studies included whether QoL was reported as a primary or secondary endpoint.

Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cluster‐randomized trials, non‐randomized controlled trials, and quasi‐experimental pre‐post studies. These designs allow for direct comparison between the intervention and standard care, thus minimizing the risk of bias.

Study Selection Process

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across five electronic databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Scopus, and Web of Science. Search strategies combined relevant keywords and MeSH terms and were tailored for each database. The final search was conducted on March 14, 2025. Study selection occurred in two phases by two independent reviewers using Rayyan [17]. The first phase involved screening of titles and abstracts, followed by full‐text evaluation of potentially eligible articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized form adapted from the Cochrane Handbook [12]. Extracted variables included study characteristics (year, country, design), participant characteristics (sample size, age, type of cancer treatment), and details of the telenursing intervention (delivery mode—telephone, videoconferencing, mobile app, etc.). QoL measurement tools were also recorded. To ensure clarity and transparency in intervention reporting, we employed a TIDieR‐based table [18] summarizing the following components: description of the intervention, comparator, intervention provider, delivery method (where, how, and how often), and personalization elements. Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized form adapted from the Cochrane Handbook. Extracted variables included study characteristics (year, country, design), participant demographics, intervention description, comparator, outcome measures, and key findings.

Risk of Bias Assessment

To reduce selection and interpretation bias, all stages of the review were independently conducted by two reviewers, with conflicts resolved by a third. The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Risk of Bias 2.0 tool (RoB 2.0) for randomized trials [19] and the ROBINS‐I tool for non‐randomized studies [18]. The overall certainty of the evidence for the primary outcome was evaluated using the GRADE approach (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) [20].

Statistical Analysis and Data Synthesis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.0) with the meta and metafor packages. The primary effect size was the standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), calculated for each study using the escalc function from metafor. Mean values, standard deviations, and sample sizes for intervention and control groups were used to compute individual study estimates.

Regarding data prioritization, when a study reported both post‐intervention scores and change‐from‐baseline scores, post‐intervention means and standard deviations were used preferentially for SMD calculation, in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [14]. This decision was pre‐specified in the OSF protocol. For two studies that reported change scores only, these values were used directly without imputation of post‐intervention standard deviations to avoid the introduction of additional estimation error.

Random‐effects meta‐analyses were conducted using the inverse‐variance method, with between‐study variance (τ 2) estimated via the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I 2 statistic, τ 2, and Cochran's Q test. I 2 thresholds were interpreted as follows: low (25%), moderate (50%), and high (75%).

Subgroup analyses were performed based on the type of digital intervention (mobile application vs. telephone follow‐up). Meta‐regressions were conducted using mixed‐effects models to evaluate study design (randomized vs. non‐randomized) and follow‐up duration (in weeks) as potential moderators. The proportion of heterogeneity explained was expressed as R 2.

Funnel plots were visually inspected to explore potential small‐study effects. Egger's test for funnel plot asymmetry was performed separately for each subgroup, and the trim‐and‐fill method was applied to assess the impact of potential publication bias. Finally, a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of individual studies, and influence diagnostics were used to detect outliers contributing disproportionately to overall heterogeneity. The overall certainty of the evidence for the primary outcome (QoL) was assessed using the GRADE framework [20]. This assessment was limited to studies included in the meta‐analysis, in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 6.3, 2022) [14]. Five core GRADE domains were evaluated: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Initial evidence quality was determined by study design—high for RCTs, low for quasi‐experimental studies—and subsequently downgraded or maintained based on methodological limitations across the domains. The GRADE assessment was independently performed by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. Between‐study variance (τ 2) was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) for all primary analyses; a pre‐specified sensitivity analysis using the DerSimonian‐Laird estimator yielded directionally consistent results (Table S3). Standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for continuous quality of life outcomes. SMD were interpreted according to Cohen's guidelines: 0.2 = small effect, 0.5 = moderate effect, and 0.8 = large effect [21]. When studies reported multiple QoL measures, we prioritized validated cancer‐specific QoL instruments over generic measures. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I 2 statistic and tau‐squared (τ 2). I 2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as low, moderate, and substantial heterogeneity, respectively [14]. When substantial heterogeneity was detected (I 2 > 75%), we explored potential sources through prespecified subgroup analyses by: (1) intervention type (mobile application versus telephone follow‐up), (2) intervention duration, (3) cancer treatment phase, and (4) geographic region. Publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plot examination and statistically using Egger's regression test when ≥ 10 studies were available [22].

Results

The systematic search across five electronic databases yielded a total of 794 records. After removing 258 duplicates and excluding 91 articles through automated filters, 445 records remained for screening by two independent reviewers. Of these, 31 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full‐text evaluation, 14 studies were excluded for reasons detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). Ultimately, 17 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 11 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), 1 cluster‐randomized trial, and 5 quasi‐experimental studies.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) studies reported appropriate randomization procedures, with a low risk of bias in the domain related to the randomization process (D1), thereby ensuring balanced allocation and minimizing selection bias. (Figure 2).

Risk of bias judgments in randomized controlled trial. RoB 2.0 summary [17]. Risk of bias across randomized controlled trials (RCTs) by domain: D1–randomization process, D2–deviations from intended interventions, D3–missing outcome data, D4–measurement of the outcome, D5–selection of the reported result.

Most trials reported complete outcome data or adequately addressed missing data using appropriate statistical methods. However, two studies [22, 33] were rated as having “some concerns” in the domain related to missing data (D3), as both adopted per‐protocol analyses without providing detailed information on how missing data were handled. Although the potential impact on the overall findings appears limited, the lack of transparency implies a moderate risk of bias in this area.

Additionally, the study of Sandgren and McCaul [33] showed a significantly uneven distribution of dropouts between groups (p = 0.036), despite a relatively low overall attrition rate (8%) and mostly documented reasons for withdrawal. As a result, a “some concerns” rating was also applied to domain D2 of the RoB 2.0 tool, in line with a precautionary approach consistent with methodological guidance.

In the outcome measurement domain (D4), most studies were rated as having a moderate risk of bias, mainly due to the nature of the interventions and the outcomes assessed. In particular, the use of patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs), combined with the impossibility of blinding participants and personnel, raised concerns about potential performance and detection bias. This limitation is inherent to behavioral and psychosocial interventions, where blinding is often not feasible. Shi et al. [31] achieved a low risk of bias across all domains, including outcome measurement. In that trial, both participants and intervention providers were blinded to allocation, and the outcome assessment was conducted in a standardized and independent manner. These procedures minimized the risk of detection bias and strengthened the overall methodological rigor of the study.

The risk of bias assessment for quasi‐experimental studies was conducted using the ROBINS‐I tool (Figure 3). All included studies were judged to be at high overall risk of bias, primarily due to the lack of control or inadequate adjustment for potential confounding factors. This limitation significantly affected the internal validity of the findings and contributed to the high‐risk ratings across all studies.

Risk of bias judgments in non randomized control trials. ROBINS‐I assessment [19]. Risk of bias in non‐randomized studies (NRSI) across domains: confounding, selection, classification, deviations, missing data, outcome measurement, and reporting.

In summary, of the 12 randomized studies assessed with RoB 2.0, 1 (8.3%) was rated as low risk of bias across all domains, 9 (75.0%) were rated as having some concerns in at least one domain, and 2 (16.7%) were rated as high risk. All 5 quasi‐experimental studies assessed with ROBINS‐I were rated as high overall risk of bias, primarily due to the absence of adequate control for confounding factors.

Effects of Mobile Application and Telephone Follow‐Up Interventions

Seven studies reporting on quality of life (QoL) outcomes were included in the meta‐analysis. A random‐effects model was used to synthesize the results, yielding a pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.36 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.07 to 0.65; p = 0.014). This suggests a small to moderate beneficial effect of digital interventions compared to control. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I 2 = 91.5%), indicating considerable variation in effect estimates across studies.

Subgroup analyses were performed based on the type of digital intervention. For mobile application‐based interventions (k = 3), the pooled effect was 0.63 (95% CI 0.52 to 0.75; p < 0.0001), with no observed heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%). The 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.38 to 0.89. For telephone follow‐up interventions (k = 4), the pooled effect was smaller and not statistically significant (SMD 0.21, 95% CI –0.14 to 0.55; p = 0.236), with high heterogeneity (I 2 = 85.2%) and a wide 95% prediction interval from −0.95 to 1.37. The formal test for subgroup differences indicated a statistically significant interaction (Q = 5.65, degrees of freedom [df] = 1; p = 0.017), suggesting that the type of intervention may moderate the treatment effect. These exploratory subgroup findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating pending replication in adequately powered trials.

Meta‐Analysis of Intervention Effects on Quality of Life

Seven studies provided sufficient quantitative data for meta‐analysis. Overall pooled analysis demonstrated a small to moderate positive effect of telenursing interventions on QoL (random effects: SMD = 0.40, 95% CI [0.09, 0.70], Z = 3.17, p = 0.019), with substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 93.1%, τ 2 = 0.1016, ChI 2 = 86.64, df = 6, p < 0.0001). Given the high heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analysis by intervention type, which revealed clinically and statistically significant differences between mobile application and telephone follow‐up modalities.

Mobile Application Interventions

Three randomized controlled trials [28, 29, 36] evaluated mobile app‐based telenursing interventions. Meta‐analysis showed a moderate positive effect on QoL (SMD = 0.63, 95% CI [0.46, 0.80], Z = 7.34, p < 0.0001), with negligible heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%, τ 2 = 0, ChI 2 = 0.91, df = 2, p = 0.635). The I 2 of 0% indicates highly consistent effects across studies despite differences in patient populations (ranging from post‐surgery to chemotherapy phases), geographic locations (China and Turkey), and specific app features. Individual study effect sizes were homogeneous; Zhou et al. [28] showed SMD = 0.57 [0.38, 0.76], instead Aydin and Gürsoy [36] SMD = 0.61 [0.36, 0.86], and Wang et al. [29] SMD = 0.70 [0.51, 0.88]. All three studies demonstrated statistically significant benefits (p < 0.001). Notably, the common effect and random effects models produced identical pooled estimates (SMD = 0.63), reflecting the complete absence of between‐study heterogeneity. These results are hypothesis‐generating and should be interpreted as exploratory, given that the subgroup comprises only k = 3 studies. While the consistency of effects across these trials is encouraging, the small number of contributing studies precludes definitive conclusions about the superiority of mobile app–based telenursing over other modalities.

Telephone Follow‐Up Interventions

Four studies [22, 23, 24, 25] examined telephone‐based telenursing follow‐up. The pooled effect using a random‐effects model was smaller and non‐significant (SMD = 0.21, 95% CI [−0.34, 0.76], Z = 0.74, p = 0.46), with substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 85.2%, τ 2 = 0.1013, ChI 2 = 20.33, df = 3, p = 0.0001). Individual study results varied considerably. Two studies showed near‐null or negative effects: Kimman et al. SMD = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.19, 0.04], p = 0.18, and Lai et al. SMD = −0.02, 95% CI [−0.23, 0.19], p = 0.84. In contrast, two others demonstrated moderate positive effects; Kim et al. [24] showed SMD = 0.59, 95% CI [0.29, 0.89], p < 0.001, and Yazicioglu et al. SMD = 0.49, 95% CI [−0.02, 1.01], p = 0.06 (marginally non‐significant). This variability suggests that telephone intervention effectiveness depends substantially on implementation characteristics such as call frequency (which ranged from weekly to monthly across studies), call duration, nurse training, protocol standardization, and patient selection criteria. When analyzed using a common effect model (assuming homogeneity), the pooled telephone effect was near‐null (SMD = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.08, 0.11]), with the marked discrepancy between common effect and random effects estimates confirming substantial true heterogeneity beyond sampling error.

Test of Subgroup Differences

Formal testing indicated a statistically significant subgroup difference (ChI 2 = 5.65, df = 1, p = 0.0175), suggesting that delivery modality may moderate intervention effectiveness. These findings are, however, to be considered exploratory: the mobile app subgroup comprised only k = 3 studies, and the small number of observations limits the reliability of subgroup comparisons. These results should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating and confirmed in future adequately powered head‐to‐head trials.

Meta‐regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. A mixed‐effects model including study design (randomized versus non‐randomized) as a moderator did not identify a significant effect (QM = 0.42; p = 0.518), and no reduction in between‐study variance was observed (τ 2 = 0.112; I 2 = 91.5%). When restricting the analysis to randomized controlled trials only (k = 6), the effect remained statistically significant (SMD = 0.36, 95% CI [0.07, 0.65]; p = 0.013), with persistently high heterogeneity. The model accounted for 31.5% of the between‐study variance (R 2 = 31.52%), although the association between follow‐up duration and effect size did not reach statistical significance (slope = −0.033; 95% CI [–0.072, 0.005]; p = 0.088).

A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the influence of individual studies. The overall findings remained directionally consistent and statistically significant across iterations, with pooled SMDs ranging from 0.17 to 0.49. The largest change in effect size occurred when the study by Kimman was omitted, resulting in the highest effect estimate and the lowest residual heterogeneity (I 2 = 83.6%), suggesting that this study may have attenuated the overall effect.

Assessment of Publication Bias

The certainty of the evidence for the outcome “Quality of Life” was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach. The initial certainty was rated as high for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and low for quasi‐experimental studies. Downgrades were applied for risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision. Specifically, methodological limitations—highlighted by studies rated as having “some concerns” or “high” risk of bias according to RoB 2.0 and ROBINS‐I—led to a downgrade for risk of bias. A downgrade for inconsistency was applied due to the high level of heterogeneity across studies (I 2 = 93%). An additional downgrade for imprecision was deemed necessary because of the wide confidence intervals observed in several analyses. No downgrade was applied for indirectness, as the study populations, interventions, and outcomes were directly relevant to the research question.

Although Egger's test was underpowered given the small number of included studies (k = 7), the trim‐and‐fill analysis identified three potentially missing studies on the left side of the funnel. After imputation of these studies, the pooled effect estimate was substantially attenuated and no longer statistically significant (adjusted SMD = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.17, 0.45]; p = 0.376). This pattern is consistent with the presence of meaningful publication bias. Accordingly, following re‐deliberation between the two GRADE reviewers, a one‐level downgrade was applied in the publication bias domain. The GRADE rating was applied to the outcome “Quality of Life” as a synthesis of the aggregated effect emerging from the meta‐analysis, following the GRADE approach for rating the certainty of evidence at the outcome level. In conclusion, the overall certainty of the evidence for the “Quality of Life” outcome was rated as low. (Table 3).

Discussion

Summary of Findings

This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the impact of nurse‐led telenursing interventions on the quality of life (QoL) of women with breast cancer. To our knowledge, no prior systematic review has exclusively focused on nurse‐led telenursing interventions for women with breast cancer and quantitatively synthesized their impact on quality of life. A total of 17 studies were included, using various digital technologies—primarily telephone calls and mobile applications—to deliver remote nursing care across the treatment continuum. Only one study employed videoconferencing technology [35], highlighting that while such tools may be effective, they often require complex infrastructures—such as computers, dedicated software, and stable internet connections—which may not be universally accessible to all patients.

Comparison With Literature

The findings revealed a small to moderate overall effect of telenursing on QoL compared to usual care, with exploratory subgroup analyses suggesting that mobile app–based interventions may be associated with larger effect sizes. Specifically, the subgroup analysis indicated a standardized mean difference of 0.63 (95% CI [0.52–0.75]) in favor of mobile apps, while telephone interventions did not achieve statistical significance. These results suggest that the interactivity, personalization, and real‐time feedback capabilities of mobile apps may enhance patient engagement and perceived support. Telephone‐based interventions remain the most commonly used telenursing strategy, due to their widespread accessibility and familiarity among patients. These interventions were frequently employed to monitor symptoms, address emerging needs, and provide psychosocial support during or after oncological treatment [23, 24, 25, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. Several studies also designed telephone follow‐up models as alternatives to traditional physician‐led visits, in line with the chronic care model increasingly applied to cancer survivorship [39]. Although some trials reported no significant between‐group differences in QoL [41, 42] the equivalence of outcomes, combined with demonstrated cost‐effectiveness [43, 44], supports the integration of telephone‐based telenursing—particularly in underserved or rural settings [45]. The use of mobile applications reflects a growing trend in oncology care. Apps included in the reviewed studies were used for patient education, symptom tracking, self‐management, and direct communication with nurses. Many were structured to allow either proactive nurse‐led feedback or reactive patient‐initiated contact. These features, along with asynchronous support and continuous data collection, may explain the greater impact of app‐based interventions. Similar effects have been observed in studies involving other cancer populations [46, 47]. Nevertheless, the increasing reliance on digital health tools raises important concerns about equitable access. While mobile health (mHealth) interventions are promising, they may unintentionally exclude individuals with low digital literacy, limited technological access, or older age [48]. Nevertheless, the adoption of telenursing reflects a shift toward person‐centered and proactive cancer care. Remote nursing support can enhance continuity of care, early symptom recognition, and patient empowerment—all critical elements in survivorship care plans. These models also hold potential to reduce healthcare system burden and improve the overall quality and efficiency of care delivery [49, 50].

To contextualize the magnitude of effects observed in the present review, it is instructive to compare them against those reported in meta‐analyses of other non‐pharmacological supportive interventions for QoL in breast cancer populations. Recent high‐quality systematic reviews have reported pooled SMDs of approximately 0.40–0.55 for aerobic exercise‐based interventions and 0.30–0.50 for psychosocial or cognitive‐behavioral supportive care programmes [51] Two recent meta‐analyses in cancer populations reported comparable pooled SMDs in the range of 0.30–0.45 for supportive interventions, providing further contextual benchmarks [52, 53]. The overall pooled effect observed in the present meta‐analysis (SMD = 0.40, 95% CI [0.09, 0.70]) falls within this range, and the mobile app subgroup effect (SMD = 0.63) is at the upper end of the comparator evidence base, suggesting potential clinical competitiveness with more established modalities. However, direct comparisons across reviews are limited by differences in study populations, outcome instruments, and meta‐analytic methods; furthermore, the low certainty of the present evidence precludes equivalence claims [51]. Despite these promising findings, caution is warranted. The high degree of heterogeneity (I 2 > 90%), along with moderate‐to‐high risk of bias in several studies, limits the strength of conclusions. Most trials used self‐reported QoL outcomes, and blinding of participants and personnel was often not feasible due to the nature of the intervention, increasing risk of performance and detection bias. According to the GRADE framework, the certainty of the evidence was rated as low. Therefore, further high‐quality randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these effects, explore optimal delivery modalities, and identify population‐specific determinants of efficacy.

Interpretation of Heterogeneity and Subgroup Findings

The substantial overall heterogeneity (I 2 = 93.1%) and the stark contrast between mobile app (I 2 = 0%) and telephone interventions (I 2 = 85.2%) merit careful interpretation, as they have important implications for clinical practice and future research. The absence of heterogeneity among mobile app studies (I 2 = 0%) is remarkable given that these trials were conducted across different countries, involved varying cancer stages and treatment phases, and utilized different app platforms with distinct features. This consistency suggests that mobile app‐based telenursing may possess inherent characteristics producing robust QoL improvements relatively independent of contextual factors. Possible explanations include: (1) standardization—apps provide consistent content, interfaces, and features to all users, reducing variability in intervention delivery; (2) patient control—apps enable access to support at patients' convenience rather than being constrained by scheduled call times, potentially increasing engagement; (3) multimodal support—apps integrate text, images, videos, and interactive tools, providing richer educational content than voice‐only communication; and (4) continuous availability—24/7 access may provide psychological reassurance and practical support between healthcare contacts. In contrast, the substantial heterogeneity among telephone studies (I 2 = 85.2%) indicates that telephone intervention effectiveness depends heavily on implementation details. Potential sources include: (1) protocol variation—call frequency ranged from daily to monthly and call duration varied from brief check‐ins to extensive counseling sessions; (2) nurse training—studies rarely reported nurse credentials, training in telephone counseling, or supervision mechanisms; (3) intervention timing—some studies initiated calls during active treatment when symptom burden peaks, while others focused on post‐treatment survivorship when patient needs differ; (4) cultural context—telephone interventions may be differentially acceptable across cultures, particularly regarding discussing emotional distress; and (5) concurrent care—studies varied in what constituted “usual care” for control groups, affecting the incremental benefit attributable to telephone intervention. The significant subgroup difference (p = 0.0175) indicates that exploratory subgroup analyses suggest that mobile applications may be associated with larger effect sizes than telephone follow‐up for QoL in breast cancer patients, though this finding should be considered hypothesis‐generating given the small number of contributing studies (k = 3). This exploratory finding may tentatively inform clinical decision‐making, but—given that it rests on k = 3 studies in the mobile app subgroup—should not be interpreted as definitive guidance. Head‐to‐head comparative trials are needed before clinical recommendations favoring one modality over the other can be made with confidence. However, telephone interventions should not be dismissed entirely for patients lacking smartphones or digital literacy.

Strengths and Limitations

Despite the encouraging findings, several methodological limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the results. One common limitation relates to the subjective nature of QoL measurements, which were based on patient‐reported outcome measures. Although these tools are validated, they remain susceptible to response bias. The lack of blinding among participants and outcome assessors—an inherent issue in psychosocial interventions—also poses a risk for performance and detection bias. The risk of bias assessment conducted using the RoB 2.0 and ROBINS‐I tools revealed several areas of concern that negatively influenced the overall quality of the evidence. Moreover, the high degree of heterogeneity observed across studies (I 2 > 90%) further reduced the certainty of conclusions based on GRADE criteria. While funnel plot analysis indicated slight asymmetry suggestive of potential publication bias, the small number of studies limited the robustness of this assessment. Overall, the certainty of the evidence for the “Quality of Life” outcome was rated as low. Future research should prioritize methodological rigor, larger sample sizes, and consistent outcome measures to enhance comparability and reduce bias. The inclusion of quasi‐experimental studies, all rated as high overall risk of bias on ROBINS‐I, represents a further methodological limitation; these studies were excluded from the meta‐analysis and contributed to narrative synthesis only.

Clinical Implications

Telenursing interventions can be effectively integrated into clinical practice to ensure continuity of care for patients undergoing outpatient treatments, support the management of adverse events, and identify emerging needs between medical appointments. They may also serve as structured post‐treatment follow‐up tools, allowing for the management of late effects and the implementation of long‐term, individualized interventions—particularly those addressing psychosocial needs, which often remain unmet within the limited time frame of traditional outpatient visits [54]. Recent evidence highlights the clinical efficacy of telenursing in improving health outcomes across oncological settings. A pilot quasi‐experimental study by Muñoz‐Villaverde et al. [55] demonstrated that an advanced practice nurse (APN)‐led telenursing intervention significantly improved health literacy, patient empowerment, and quality of life (QoL) among patients with solid tumors, while also reducing care needs and enhancing satisfaction with received care. Similarly, Wang et al. [29] showed that digital interventions could effectively mitigate fatigue and improve psychological well‐being in cancer patients undergoing follow‐up care [56]. Beyond clinical outcomes, mobile health (mHealth) technologies—particularly nurse‐led applications—hold promise for enhancing operational efficiency. The following observations extend beyond the evidence base of this review and should be interpreted as contextual interpretation rather than conclusions drawn from the synthesized data. Telenursing may contribute to broader system‐level efficiencies—including reduced unnecessary outpatient visits, enhanced continuity of care, and potential workforce adaptability in remote monitoring roles—but these implications require dedicated health economic and implementation science studies to be empirically validated in the context of breast cancer care [57, 58].

Additionally, a randomized controlled trial conducted by Saraç et al. found that the use of an informative mobile application significantly reduced anxiety and distress levels among women undergoing breast cancer surgery, highlighting the potential of such digital tools in alleviating psychological distress during the early postoperative period [59, 60]. Collectively, these findings support the clinical integration of nurse‐led telenursing—particularly mobile‐based approaches—as a potentially effective strategy to improve patient outcomes, optimize resource utilization, and respond to contemporary healthcare system challenges.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta‐analysis provides preliminary evidence that nurse‐led telenursing interventions, when integrated with standard oncological care, may be associated with improvements in quality of life in women with breast cancer. However, this finding must be interpreted cautiously: the overall pooled effect did not reach statistical significance after trim‐and‐fill correction for potential publication bias (adjusted SMD = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.17, 0.45]; p = 0.376), and the certainty of the evidence was rated as Low according to the GRADE framework. In exploratory subgroup analyses, mobile application‐based interventions (k = 3; SMD = 0.63, 95% CI [0.46, 0.80], I 2 = 0%) were associated with larger effect sizes than telephone follow‐up (k = 4; SMD = 0.21, 95% CI [−0.34, 0.76], I 2 = 85.2%); this finding should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating given the small number of contributing studies. These findings support the integration of telenursing as a complementary component of multidisciplinary breast cancer care, enhancing patient outcomes beyond standard medical treatment alone. In contrast, the substantial heterogeneity among telephone interventions (I 2 = 85.2%) indicates that effectiveness depends heavily on implementation factors such as call frequency, protocol standardization, and nurse training. Mobile application–based telenursing represents a promising area for further investigation, but definitive implementation recommendations await confirmation from adequately powered comparative trials. However, telephone interventions remain valuable for patients lacking smartphone access or digital literacy, provided that protocols are carefully standardized and nurses receive adequate training in telephone counseling techniques. Despite these promising findings, several methodological limitations warrant consideration. The predominance of studies from high‐income countries limits generalizability to resource‐constrained settings. The moderate to high risk of bias in several included studies, particularly regarding blinding of outcome assessment—an inherent challenge in behavioral interventions—may have influenced effect estimates. Additionally, the absence of individual participant data precluded exploration of patient‐level moderators such as age, disease stage, treatment phase, baseline quality of life, and digital literacy, which may influence intervention effectiveness. Future research should focus on high‐quality randomized controlled trials with adequate sample sizes, longer follow‐up periods, and rigorous blinding procedures where feasible. Comparative effectiveness trials directly comparing mobile applications to telephone interventions would provide valuable guidance for resource allocation. Individual participant data meta‐analyses would enable identification of patient subgroups most likely to benefit from specific intervention modalities. Additionally, implementation science studies examining barriers and facilitators to telenursing integration within routine oncology care, including cost‐effectiveness analyses and reimbursement models, are essential for sustainable adoption. Telenursing represents a promising, scalable approach to enhancing supportive care for women with breast cancer. As digital health technologies continue to evolve and healthcare delivery increasingly incorporates remote monitoring and support, nurse‐led telenursing interventions—particularly those delivered via mobile applications—offer an accessible, cost‐effective means to improve quality of life as a complementary component of comprehensive breast cancer care across diverse healthcare settings.
