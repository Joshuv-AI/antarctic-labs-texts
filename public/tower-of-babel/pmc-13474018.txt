Successful salvage of an inflatable penile prosthesis following aseptic ischemic scrotal necrosis using a pedicled SCIP flap: a case report
Authors: Quang Duy Thai, Dinh Ich Do, Bac Hoai Nguyen, Kien Van Tran, Viet Van Nguyen, Hoang Xuan Duc Nguyen, Thai Hong Nguyen, Thang Cao Nguyen, Quan Minh Pham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Scrotal necrosis is a rare complication following inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) implantation. However, the management of aseptic ischemic scrotal necrosis following IPP implantation remains controversial. A 60-year-old male patient with a history of diabetes mellitus underwent implantation of a three-piece IPP. Postoperatively, he developed scrotal skin necrosis. Instead of removing the prosthesis, we performed delayed surgical debridement of necrotic tissue and reconstruction with a pedicled superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap to preserve the pump. After 8 months of follow-up, the flap remained viable, the prosthesis functioned normally, and the patient reported satisfactory functional and cosmetic outcomes.

Introduction

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is a common condition that significantly affects psychosocial well-being and quality of life for both patients and their partners.1

The use of inflatable penile prostheses (IPP) has increased in recent years because of favorable outcomes and updated clinical recommendations supporting earlier surgical intervention.2 Long-term durability of IPP devices has been well established, with reported device survival rates of up to 96% at 5 years.3

While considering safety, the IPP complications have been reported, including pain, infection, mechanical failure, device malposition, and tissue erosion. Necrosis of genital soft tissue is extremely rare, and only a limited number of cases have been reported in the literature, with no standardized treatment guidelines available.4

Since infection is the predominant pathophysiologic driver in most cases, the management strategy favors prosthesis removal to minimize the risk of secondary infection and wound-healing failure.5,6

On the other hand, pure ischemic scrotal necrosis without overt infection appears to be exceptionally rare and may be underrecognized, as clinicians often favor aggressive device removal once necrosis is observed. Consequently, the optimal management strategy in such situations remains unclear. Herein, we report a case of progressive ischemic scrotal necrosis following IPP implantation that was successfully managed with preservation of the three-piece prosthesis after scrotal reconstruction using a pedicled superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating the potential role of a SCIP flap in the salvage management of scrotal necrosis while preserving an inflatable penile prosthesis.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old man with well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus had long-standing erectile dysfunction refractory to maximal medical therapy and elected to undergo implantation of a three-piece inflatable penile prosthesis (Coloplast Titan® Touch, 20 cm).

Strict infection-prevention measures were implemented, including optimizing glycemic control and administering prophylactic systemic antibiotics (levofloxacin) prior to surgery. The surgical field was prepared using a chlorhexidine-based solution. The procedure was performed through a penoscrotal incision under endotracheal general anesthesia. A “no-touch” technique was employed, including glove changes before handling the prosthesis and minimizing contact between the device and the skin.7 After corporal dilation and measurement, a three-piece IPP was symmetrically implanted into both corpora cavernosa. The reservoir (75 mL) was placed in the right retropubic space, and the pump was positioned within the scrotum. During the procedure, the implant space was irrigated with an antibiotic solution containing vancomycin, amikacin, and gentamicin. The system was tested for proper function and was left 60-80% inflated before 3-layer closure. Concerning the postoperative edema and hematoma, a mummy wrap was applied to compress the scrotum.8

Postoperatively, the patient was instructed to loosen the bandage himself every 6 hours and to immediately report to the nurse if feeling any pain or discomfort. However, due to the fear of surgical failure, the patient did not strictly follow the instructions. Since there were no complaints or abnormal signs from the patient and no further risk of progressive bleeding, dressing saturation, or scrotal tension suggestive of significant hematoma, the scrotal dressing was removed at 24 hours postoperative.8 At that point, a scrotal ecchymosis with a small subcutaneous hematoma was noted (Fig. 1). On postoperative day (POD) 3, the scrotal skin showed a darker discoloration, accompanied by signs of localized tissue hypoperfusion. The patient was admitted for close clinical observation, with daily wound care and dressing changes. Intravenous antibiotics (vancomycin and amikacin) were administered to reduce the risk of prosthesis-related infection in the presence of soft-tissue injury and an implanted device. The prosthesis remained deactivated, and the patient was instructed to avoid manipulating the pump. After discussion of potential risks and benefits, the patient elected conservative management to allow complete demarcation of necrotic tissue prior to further surgical intervention. Over the subsequent days, the lesion progressed slowly with increasingly well-defined demarcation between viable and ischemic tissue, while the patient remained clinically stable without local or systemic signs of infection. Inflammatory markers showed only a mild, transient elevation in C-reactive protein (CRP), peaking at 2.68 mg/dL on POD 5, then gradually declining and returning to normal levels (<0.5 mg/dL) by POD 16. The absence of fever, lack of purulent discharge or foul odor, decreasing CRP levels, and negative procalcitonin results supported the diagnosis of aseptic ischemic necrosis rather than infection. In addition, no signs of device exposure or pump erosion through the skin were observed. According to the progression of the lesion, in-depth discussions had taken place to persuade the patient to decide on further salvage surgeries. And finally, at POD 21, the patient was taken to the operating room for radical debridement. Debridement of the necrotic tissue was performed. The scrotal space was thoroughly washed with diluted povidone-iodine and antibiotic solutions. Intraoperatively, the surgical field was clean, with no purulent material, inflammatory fluid, or odor suggestive of infection. Only the IPP pump was exposed. However, the tunica vaginalis remained intact and provided full coverage of the pump. Based on these findings, preservation of the 3-piece IPP was considered feasible. The fluid from the scrotal cavity, later submitted for microbiological culture, returned negative results. This laboratory finding further supported the initial assumption of ischemic necrosis from the beginning.

Since the remaining skin after debridement was not enough to reconstruct the scrotal sac, a pedicled superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap (Fig. 3, Fig. 4) was considered. The flap was designed in the groin region based on a perforator identified preoperatively with a handheld Doppler device. It was elevated in the suprafascial plane while preserving a sufficiently long vascular pedicle to allow rotation into the scrotal defect without tension. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. The flap remained well perfused, with no evidence of hematoma, secondary necrosis, or infection. The prosthesis remained in situ, with no signs of exposure or erosion. The patient was discharged 10 days after the salvage surgery, once the wound was stable, and was followed regularly in the outpatient clinic.

During the first postoperative month, weekly visits were scheduled to monitor wound healing and flap viability. By the second postoperative month, the wound had healed completely, and the prosthesis was confirmed to be functioning normally. The patient was then instructed to begin pump cycling under physician supervision before continuing self-activation at home.

Sexual activity was permitted from the third postoperative month after complete soft-tissue recovery and stable prosthesis function. During follow-up, no complications related to the flap or prosthesis were observed.

Discussion

Inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) implantation is considered a safe procedure with a relatively low complication rate. The most commonly reported complications include postoperative pain, mechanical failure, device malposition, erosion, and infection.9 Of greatest concern is infection, with a rate of 1.61-9.9%, but it has decreased substantially since the introduction of antibiotic-coated devices and innovation in surgical technique.7,9, 10, 11 In severe cases, infection may lead to necrosis of the genital skin, typically with rapid progression, resulting in extensive tissue destruction accompanied by systemic inflammatory manifestations.12 Thus, when ecchymosis is detected, the patient needs to be carefully inspected to prevent progression to a skin infection. If infectious necrosis was suspected, the predominantly used strategy would be device removal followed by extensive irrigation and replacement with a malleable prosthesis according to the Mulcahy salvage protocol.7,12,13 This approach has shown favorable outcomes.3,14 Salvage procedures using malleable prostheses have demonstrated favorable outcomes in selected patients with prosthesis infection.14,15 However, management recommendations for genital skin necrosis without evidence of infection remain poorly defined. In low- and middle-income countries, preservation of a functioning prosthesis may be an important consideration because replacement imposes a substantial financial burden.

The presented case illustrates an extremely rare ischemic scrotal necrosis after IPP in the literature. Mummy wrap has been widely practiced as one of the most important perioperative infection-control measures, reducing swelling and promoting healing.7,8 However, despite following the strict rule of the above practice, the prolonged excessive compression from the dressing might result in local ischemia of the scrotum, leading to progressive necrosis in the presented case. Indeed, excessive local pressure may compromise tissue perfusion, particularly in patients with microvascular disease risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, thus increasing the risk of ischemic necrosis after IPP.5,16 Since this potential complication has not been widely recognized, choosing the best strategy to manage these cases remains challenging.

From both clinical and pathophysiological perspectives, ischemic necrosis can be distinguished from infectious necrosis. The former usually evolves more slowly and tends to develop a clear demarcation between viable and nonviable tissue. This distinction is critical, as it directly influences the therapeutic strategy. While we cannot be assured of being free of infection in this situation, with necrosis continuing to progress slowly, we still applied the multidisciplinary approach used in suspected infectious cases.7 Notably, delayed debridement in cases of ischemic necrosis is widely acceptable, as it allows accurate assessment of tissue viability and avoids unnecessary excision of potentially recoverable tissue.17,18 On the other hand, hasty intervention undertaken before a complete assessment of the lesion progression might result in treatment failure of ischemic necrosis.17,18 Therefore, the decision to delay intervention in this case should not be interpreted as delayed treatment, but rather as a deliberate strategy based on the lesion's underlying pathophysiology.

Without the evidence of infection and the pump externally unexposed through skin, preservation of IPP might be considered in the presented case. Furthermore, intraoperative findings suggested that deeper tissues remained viable, and the tunica vaginalis was intact. This anatomical barrier likely helped isolate the IPP from potential bacterial contamination. The success of our case provides an effective approach to managing ischemic necrosis of the genital skin following IPP implantation.

Another important factor contributing to the successful outcome in this case was the choice of reconstructive flap. Primary closure or local flaps may be appropriate for small defects, but may result in excessive tension when the tissue loss is extensive. Several reconstructive options have been described for large scrotal soft-tissue defects, including autologous skin grafts, local advancement flaps, fascio-cutaneous flaps, and muscle flaps.19 Muscle flaps provide robust vascularized coverage and may be advantageous in contaminated or infected fields; however, their bulk may lead to less favorable cosmetic outcomes in scrotal reconstruction.

In the present case, since debridement resulted in a defect involving approximately half of the scrotal surface, a pedicled superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap was selected for reconstruction. This approach shows several advantages for scrotal reconstruction.20 It provides a reliable independent vascular supply, ensuring stable perfusion even in a compromised recipient bed. In addition, the flap thickness is appropriate for scrotal reconstruction, allowing adequate soft-tissue coverage while maintaining a satisfactory cosmetic appearance. The donor site is well concealed and associated with minimal morbidity.

Conclusion

The presented case introduces a rare case of scrotal ischemic necrosis following IPP implantation, highlighting practical points to consider. Firstly, despite the importance of compressive dressings in this type of surgery, close postoperative monitoring with appropriate checks within 1 day is essential for early detection of complications related to scrotal tissue perfusion, particularly in patients at high risk of microvascular disease. In cases of scrotal necrosis without evidence of infection, delayed debridement and preservation of pre-existing IPP are considered as an economically and clinically efficient approach. For the large scrotal skin defect, a pedicled superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap remains the option of choice, providing effective soft-tissue coverage, adequate cosmetic outcomes, and long-term patient satisfaction.
