Development and internal validation of a 30-day readmission risk model for older adults using Swiss electronic health record data: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Laura Maria Steiner, Sandra M G Zwakhalen, Loris Bonetti, Sabine Hahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnplanned 30-day readmissions in discharged older adults often reflect unmet needs and fragmented care transitions. Existing prediction models frequently lack geriatric specificity, clinical interpretability, and applicability to decentralised European healthcare systems. This study aimed to develop a clinically interpretable risk model for older adults using routine discharge data. Findings may provide a basis for targeted interventions.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using electronic health record (EHR) data from a Swiss multi-site hospital network. Adults aged ≥ 65 years discharged alive in 2024 were eligible, excluding planned readmission, oncology/palliative cases, in-hospital deaths, and records with missing predictors. Of 15,635 discharges, 12,814 met inclusion criteria. 9,429 (60.3%) were retained for complete-case analysis. A multivariable logistic regression model with backward selection predicted unplanned 30-day readmission. Model performance was evaluated by AUC, calibration, decision curve analysis (DCA), and bootstrap validation, with a Fine-Gray model addressing competing risk of mortality.ResultsAmong 9,429 patients (mean age 78.0 years; 45.5% female), 1,845 (19.6%) had an unplanned 30-day readmission. Independent predictors were polypharmacy (OR 1.83), home care (OR 1.61), male sex (OR 1.42), low fall risk (vs. high; OR 1.41), moderate care intensity (vs. high; OR 1.30), comorbidity count (OR 1.12/diagnosis), younger age (OR 0.99/year), and shorter stay (OR 0.98/day). The model showed an AUC of 0.716 (95% CI 0.706–0.726), but calibration was poor, with risk overestimation at low predicted probabilities. At a 10% threshold, sensitivity was 7.5%, specificity 98.1%, positive predictive value 49.5%, and negative predictive value 81.4%. Compared with LACE index (AUC 0.707, 95% CI 0.694–0.720), performance improved modestly but significantly (p < 0.01).ConclusionsNearly one in five older adults was readmitted within 30 days, underscoring the burden of rehospitalisation and its link to quality-of-care. The model identified clinically relevant, EHR-available predictors, including medical complexity and care dependency. Despite acceptable discrimination, calibration was suboptimal and sensitivity low, limiting immediate clinical application. Future refinement and external validation may enhance utility. This model may provide a pragmatic starting point for risk-stratified discharge planning research and guiding transitional care interventions. Introduction

Background

Thirty-day hospital readmissions in older adults often reflect suboptimal quality of care and fragmented care transitions. Multiple factors, including medical complexity, functional limitations, care dependency, and discharge organization, influence readmission risk in this population [1–3]. Inadequate coordination and unmet functional or social needs frequently lead to avoidable acute care use, reflecting missed opportunities for anticipatory discharge planning. These readmissions affect up to 20% of older adult discharges and are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [1, 2].

Numerous models have been developed to predict readmission risk, including the LACE index [4] developed in Canada, and the HOSPITAL score [5], derived in the United States (US). However, their performance in older adults is limited, with reported C-statistics often below 0.65 and as low as 0.58 in some geriatric subgroups [6–8]. Most were derived from general adult cohorts and omit geriatric-specific predictors, such as delirium risk, care dependency, and post-discharge support, despite their known relevance [3, 9–11]. For example, physical function, a key risk factor for post-discharge complications, is rarely included [3]. Machine learning (ML)-based models for heart failure similarly faced challenges with clinical interpretability, limiting their adoption [11]. Moreover, most models were developed and validated in North America, which differs substantially from the Swiss and broader European healthcare context. Switzerland’s acute care is decentralised, with multi-site hospital networks and no penalties for readmissions, unlike the US. Discharge coordination spans fragmented community providers, and post-acute rehabilitation and transitional care pathways are common. These features affect readmission drivers and model transferability, motivating a context-sensitive, locally validated approach.

To address these gaps, we developed and internally validated a prediction model based on multivariable logistic regression. By integrating medical, organisational, geriatric, and care-dependency variables, the model aims to improve early identification of older adults at risk for unplanned 30-day readmission and to inform future transitional care interventions. Importantly, because readmissions and post-discharge mortality are competing outcomes, we also examined mortality risk separately to contextualise model performance. This prognostic study, part of a broader Medical Research Council (MRC) framework project, provides a quantitative foundation to inform co-designed hospital-to-home interventions [12].

Objectives

The primary objective was to develop and internally validate a multivariable logistic regression-based model to predict unplanned hospital readmission within 30 days of discharge among patients aged ≥ 65 years. Secondary objectives were (1) modelling time to first readmission, and (2) predicting 30-day post-discharge mortality.

Methods

Study design and source of data

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using routinely collected EHR data from the Ente Ospedaliero Cantonale (EOC), a public hospital group, structured as a centrum-level hospital network with four acute-care regional hospitals, serving about 350,000 residents in the Canton of Ticino, southern Switzerland [13]. The dataset included all hospitalisations of patients aged ≥ 65 years who were admitted and discharged between 1 January and 31 December 2024. Follow-up for 30-day readmissions and post-discharge mortality extended through 31 January 2025. The study protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF: https://osf.io/vkte2/) .The study was reported in accordance with the TRIPOD + AI guidelines [14], which provides harmonised standards for reporting prediction model studies using regression or machine-learning methods.

Ethical approval for the overall project was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Canton Ticino (Protocol 2025-00025, Rif. CE 475). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective use of de-identified routine data.

Participants

Eligible participants were all patients aged ≥ 65 years who were discharged alive to home, with or without home care. We excluded patients with planned readmissions (e.g. scheduled procedures), those admitted to oncology or palliative care units, and patients who died during the index hospitalisation. Admissions for post-acute rehabilitation and short observation stays were also excluded, as these do not represent acute unplanned readmissions. These criteria were applied to focus the analysis on unplanned readmissions that are more likely to reflect gaps in discharge planning or care quality [7].

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was unplanned hospital readmission within 30 days of discharge, identified from EHR admission records. U nplanned 30-day readmission was defined as any acute, unscheduled inpatient admission to the same hospital network (EOC) within 30 days of discharge. This timeframe is a commonly used quality indicator in Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries [15, 16]. Planned admissions, scheduled procedures, post-acute rehabilitation, and palliative care admissions were excluded. Emergency department (ED) visits not resulting in admission and short observation stays were not counted as readmissions. Readmissions to hospitals outside the EOC network were not captured.

Secondary outcomes were (1) time to first readmission (in days) and (2) 30-day post-discharge mortality. Mortality was analysed separately to account for its potential role as a competing event for readmission.

Candidate predictors

Predictors were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, prior literature, and routine availability in standardised EHR discharge forms completed by nurses, physicians, and administrative staff (Table 1). Candidate predictors included demographic, clinical, geriatric, hospital-related, and post-discharge factors. Geriatric measures comprised the Care Intensity Score (IAS), fall risk, and delirium risk, which were routinely assessed by nursing staff. The IAS is a 5-domain composite measure derived from the ePA-AC© [17] nursing assessment system and was used as a proxy for care dependency during hospitalisation, with scores ranging from 0 (low needs) to 14 (high needs). Higher scores indicate greater care complexity. Reference categories for categorical variables are summarised in Appendix 1.

Sample size

The eligible sample included 15,635 hospitalizations. With a 30-day readmission rate of 19.6%, the sample supports inclusion of approximately 15–20 predictors, consistent with the recommended 10–20 events-per-variable (EPV) rule for prognostic model development [31, 32]. Although a formal power calculation was not performed, this EPV-based justification is widely accepted for sample size determination in prediction model research and indicates that the study had an adequate sample size to meet its objectives [31, 32].

Missing data

Of the 15,635 eligible hospital discharges, 9,429 (60.3%) were included in the final analysis after excluding records with missing values for retained predictors. Nutrition risk had 61% missingness and failed MCAR (Little’s test p < 0.001), likely reflecting workflow variability and was excluded from the primary model. This exclusion reduced the analytic sample and may have introduced selection bias. Most retained predictors had low missingness (< 5%) in the analytic dataset; in, although in the full cohort, some variables (e.g., marital status, home care) exceeded 5%. We therefore applied complete-case analysis, while acknowledging its limitations, for transparency [33].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) (main packages: glmnet, caret, randomForest, xgboost, survival, pROC, car, ResourceSelection, rmda). Continuous predictors were entered on their original scale, and categorical variables were dummy coded with reference categories reported in Appendix 1.

Primary model

We developed a multivariable logistic regression model using backward stepwise guided by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) selection, prioritising parsimony and interpretability [31]. To address potential instability, we ran a robustness analysis with LASSO (nested cross-validation, 200 bootstrap resamples) and addressed multicollinearity via variance inflation factors (VIF > 5 = problematic).

Comparators

As a baseline comparator, we implemented a simplified LACE index (LOS, comorbidity count as a proxy for the Charlson index, and admission acuity). The HOSPITAL score was not calculated due to missing components (see Appendix 3). Non-linear comparators included Random Forest (RF) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), with hyperparameters tuned using repeated k-fold cross-validation. Full tuning grids are reported in Appendix 4. LASSO, ridge regression and k-nearest neighbours (kNN) were run as sensitivity analyses.

Performance assessment

Model performance was evaluated by AUC (95% CI, bootstrap), calibration (slope, intercept, Brier score, Hosmer–Lemeshow test), and DCA, sensitivity, specificity, positive (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPV), confusion matrices, and precision–recall (PR) curves. Differences in AUC between models were tested using DeLong’s test with Holm correction. Internal validation was performed using 200 bootstrap resamples [32].

Secondary analyses and competing risks

Time to first readmission was analysed using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. Because 30-day post-discharge mortality was rare, competing-risk bias was expected to be limited. Nevertheless, we performed a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model treating death as a competing event.

Results

Participants characteristics

Of the 15,635 patients aged ≥ 65, 12,814 (82.0%) met the inclusion criteria. After applying all exclusion criteria, including planned readmissions (n = 511, 3.3%), oncology or palliative care admissions (n = 1,210, 7.7%), in-hospital deaths (n = 1,100, 7.0%), and restricting the dataset to records with complete predictor information 9,429 patients (60.3%) remained for complete-case analysis. Mean age was 78.0 years (SD 7.6), 45.5% were female. Overall, 1,845 patients (19.6%) were readmitted within 30days. The cohort was clinically complex (median 6 comorbidities), and more than four out of five patients exposed to polypharmacy. Table 2 shows characteristics by readmission, with diagnostic details in Appendix 2 and mortality in Appendix 5. Readmitted patients were more likely to be male and to have greater comorbidity, polypharmacy, delirium risk, care intensity, and home care needs. Age and LOS did not differ between groups. The baseline differences were consistent with predictors retained in the regression model reinforcing their clinical plausibility.

Exploratory findings

Comorbidity correlated with LOS (r = 0.57). Readmission was weakly correlated with comorbidity (r = 0.23) and ED visits (r = 0.30). Time to readmission was inversely related to readmission status (r = − 0.50). Age was weakly correlated with comorbidity (r = 0.21). Details are reported in Appendix 7.

Model development

Backward AIC selection retained 17 predictors (full coefficients in Appendix 8). Eight predictors had 95% CI that excluded the null: comorbidity count, male sex, polypharmacy, home care at discharge, lower fall risk, medium care intensity, younger age, and shorter LOS. Several other variables (e.g. ICU admission, marital status, hospital site) were retained by the selection procedure , despite wider CIs. These variables improved overall model fit but were not interpreted as clinically meaningful individual predictors (Appendix 8). The events-per-variable (EPV) ratio was > 100, reducing concerns about overfitting. Variance inflation factors for all predictors were < 5, indicating no relevant multicollinearity. A robustness analysis using LASSO regression identified a largely similar set of predictors (Appendix 8), supporting the stability of the model (Table 3).

Model performance

The logistic regression model achieved an AUC of 0.716 (95% CI 0.706–0.726). Internal validation using 200 bootstrap replications indicated minimal optimism (bias = 0.0015; standard error (SE) = 0.0062). Calibration of the original model showed systematic miscalibration (intercept − 2.60, slope 5.45, Brier 0.172, Hosmer-Lemeshow X²= 27.2, p < 0.001), indicating overestimation of risk at lower predicted probabilities. This pattern reflects substantial overestimation of absolute risk across most of the distribution, despite acceptable discrimination. Figure 1 shows calibration before recalibration. In addition, the Precision-Recall (PR) curve (Fig. 2) highlights limited sensitivity for identifying readmitted patients, with a PR-AUC of 0.364, consistent with the severe class imbalance.

Competing risks

Fine-Gray competing-risks regression, treating post-discharge mortality as a competing event, confirmed the main findings. Higher comorbidity was associated with an increased risk of 30-day readmission (sHR 1.11, 95% CI 1.10–1.12, p < 0.001), LOS was protective (sHR 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99, p < 0.001). Age and ICU were not significant (Appendix 9).

Comparators

The logistic regression model achieved a modest but statistically significant improvement in discrimination compared with the LACE index (AUC 0.716 vs. 0.707, 95% CI 0.694–0.720; DeLong p < 0.01, Holm-adjusted p < 0.01). Among machine learning methods, RF (AUC 0.710, 95% CI 0.682–0.739) and XGBoost (AUC 0.707, 95% CI 0.697–0.752) achieved discrimination similar to logistic regression. Ridge regression performed comparably (AUC 0.708, 95% CI 0.681–0.733), whereas LASSO (AUC 0.685, 95% CI 0.661–0.708) and kNN (AUC 0.696, 95% CI 0.670–0.721) were significantly outperformed by logistic regression (Holm-adjusted p = 0.01 and 0.04, respectively). Detailed pairwise comparisons are provided in Appendix 10.

Classification performance

At a 10% threshold, logistic regression model achieved sensitivity 7.5%, specificity 98.1%, PPV 49.5%, NPV 81.4%. The F1-Score was 0.11, highlighting poor balance between precision and recall. PR analysis also showed only modest precision at higher levels of sensitivity, reflecting outcome imbalance. Confusion matrix and detailed metrics are provided in Appendix 11.

Decision curve analysis

DCA showed net benefit between thresholds of 5% and 20%, outperforming “treat all” and “treat none”. At thresholds above 30% net benefit declined, indicating reduced utility for restrictive intervention targeting (Figure in Appendix 12).

Risk estimates

The model generates continuous risk estimates. No fixed risk categories were predefined, in line with recommendations to allow local adaptation [14].

Secondary outcomes

Among 3,441 patients, older age was associated with a longer time to readmission (β = 0.71, p = 0.0006). In contrast, male sex, a greater number of comorbidities, discharge with home care services, and low fall risk were associated with a shorter time to readmission (Appendix 12). Other predictors were not statistically significant (Appendix 13).

Among 9,429 discharges, 80 patients (0.85%) died within 30 days. Patients who died were older and had higher comorbidity burden, longer hospital stays, and more frequent polypharmacy than survivors (Appendix 5). Mortality was also more common among patients discharged with home care. The mortality prediction model showed good discrimination (AUC 0.790, 95% CI 0.743–0.838), but poor calibration (slope 10.88, intercept − 4.88) reflecting the very low number of events and severe class imbalance (Appendix 14a–b).

Discussion

We developed and internally validated a prediction model based on LR for 30-day unplanned readmission in older adults using routinely collected discharge data from nurses, physicians, and administrative staff. The model showed acceptable discrimination and retained clinically interpretable predictors spanning medical complexity, geriatric vulnerability, and transitional care needs. Calibration was poor with overestimating risk at lower predicted probabilities, limiting the use of absolute risk estimates without local recalibration, though relative risk ranking remained reliable. Fine–Gray competing-risks analysis supported the consistency of the main findings: comorbidity increased risk, LOS was protective, and age and ICU admission were not significant. These results suggest that mortality as a competing event does not substantially alter the main determinants of readmission in this cohort. Core predictors, such as comorbidity burden, polypharmacy, and home care status, are consistent with prior evidence [4, 5, 7]. Nurse-reported measures (care intensity, fall risk) provided geriatric-specific insight that is often absent from generic models [4, 6, 7]. Because these indicators are routinely collected, the model may be feasible for integration into future discharge workflows, pending local recalibration and external validation, to inform risk-stratified transitional care. Nutrition risk, a well-known geriatric predictor, could not be modelleddue to non-random missingness. Improving systematic assessment is a priority for future work.

Unexpectedly, patients classified as having low fall risk or moderate care intensity had higher readmission odds than those classified as high risk. This may reflect an “inverse care” effect, whereby high-risk patients receive targeted interventions (e.g., rehabilitation, follow-up), whereas apparently lower-risk patients receive less structured support. This finding highlights the need to look beyond categorical labels and to consider unmeasured vulnerabilities, such as health literacy and caregiver availability [34, 35].

Compared with LACE and HOSPITAL, our model incorporates geriatric assessments and reflects the decentralised Swiss context. However, calibration remained imperfect, and sensitivity was uniformly low across models. This suggests that performance may be limited more by the available data than by modelling strategy [36, 37]. Algorithmic complexity did not compensate for the absence of data on functional recovery, social support, or outpatient use.

DCA showed net clinical benefit across threshold probabilities between 5% and 20%, suggesting potential care programmes. For example, patients at very high predicted risk could be prioritised for bundled interventions (home nursing visits, medication review, community linkage), while those at intermediate risk might receive structured follow-up calls [38, 39]. This probability-based approach is consistent with best practices in transitional care [37, 38].

Secondary analyses added limited clinical insight: both time to readmission and 30-day mortality were only weakly explained by discharge-level predictors. For 30-day mortality, the model showed acceptable discrimination but substantial miscalibration because of the very low number of events and severe class imbalance. These results should therefore be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating only. Overall, the model provides transparent, context-sensitive predictions of readmission in older adults. Although absolute risks require recalibration and sensitivity is low, relative risk ranking may still support stratified transitional care and future integration into discharge summaries, in line with learning health system principles.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the use of a large, real-world dataset from a multi-site hospital network, clinically interpretable predictors, and the incorporation of geriatric variables such as the IAS and delirium risk. The inclusion of nurse-reported measures supports feasibility of use in routine practice. Reporting adhered to TRIPOD + AI, and the study was guided by the MRC framework and a preregistered protocol.

A major limitation is the high level of missingness for nutrition risk (61%) and the consequent exclusion of 40% of patients, which may have introduced selection bias. Excluded patients may have differed systematically in frailty and readmission risk, potentially biasing both calibration and generalisability. Other limitations include the single-hospital network setting, which limits generalisability; the locally defined IAS score; and the absence of social determinants (income, education, access to primary care), which may left residual confounding [40, 41]. Readmissions to hospitals outside our network could not be captured, leading to potential under-ascertainment of events. The model also showed poor calibration, requiring local recalibration before clinical use. Machine learning comparators did not outperform logistic regression, underscoring the value of interpretable models, although some jointly predictive features may have been omitted through backward elimination [42]. We chose stepwise AIC for parsimony and interpretability and confirmed robustness using LASSO, which yielded a similar predictor set. Finally, routine EHR data may be subject to misclassification, although documentation was standardised.

Implications

Clinically, this model may help inform risk-stratified transitional care for older adults by providing continuous, interpretable risk estimates. Even with low sensitivity, DCA suggested potential value for relative risk stratification, enabling flexible, threshold-based planning [36, 38]. With further recalibration and evaluation, integration into discharge workflows and coordination platforms could be explored [43]. In future, such models might also be adapted dynamically by updating risk estimates with post-discharge information (e.g., symptom changes, missed appointments), in line with learning health system approaches [44, 45].

For research, the model contributes to the development of complex intervention under the MRC framework [12]. It may help identify patient subgroups who could benefit from enhanced transitional care and support the co-design of multi-component interventions. Future work should prioritise external validation, improved capture of geriatric and social determinants, and a prospective impact evaluation. Comparisons with established scores such as HOSPITAL, alongside strategies to improve calibration, will be essential to determine the model's added value. In larger cohorts, broader application of competing-risk methods may further refine understanding of readmission dynamics.

Conclusions

We developed and internally validated a pragmatic, interpretable model for predicting 30-day readmission in older adults. The model identified geriatric and care-related predictors, although calibration and sensitivity were suboptimal. With further refinement, recalibration, and external validation, the model may help inform resource planning and design of interventions to reduce avoidable readmissions.
