DUSP4 promotes anti-tumor CD8+ T cell function and boosts CAR-T cell efficacy in mouse colorectal cancer
Authors: Heng Li, Clara K. T. Koh, Tianshuai Zhang, Yi Lu, Chin Wen Png, Dong Van Hoang, Min Hui Oon, Choon Seng Chong, Kai Zhuang, Guanyu Yu, Jie Wan, Ker-Kan Tan, Huanhuan Luo, Jun Siong Low, Wei Zhang, Nicholas R. J. Gascoigne, Yongliang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The activation and function of CD8+ T cells, which are central for anti-tumor immunity, are tightly regulated by extracellular and intracellular signaling pathways. Dual-specificity phosphatases (DUSP), including DUSP4, dephosphorylate serine/threonine and tyrosine residues of proteins to modulate cellular signaling. Here, we investigate the role of DUSP4 in cancer immunity. Patients with colorectal cancers (CRC) with lower DUSP4 expression across the CRC tissue exhibit shorter survival compared to those with higher DUSP4 expression. By contrast, in AOM/DSS-induced male mouse CRC models, global DUSP4 deficiency enhances tumorigenesis and compromises CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. Mechanistically, DUSP4 knockout increases ERK2-mediated expression of the T cell activation regulator Klf2, thereby suppressing KLF2-mediated cytotoxic programs. In a CD19+ CRC xenograft mouse model, CRISPRa-mediated DUSP4 activation promotes anti-CD19 CAR-T cell proliferation and anti-tumor cytotoxicity. Thus, our data show that DUSP4 supports CD8+ T cell cytotoxic functions and suggest that DUSP4 may be a promising therapeutic target for enhancing anti-tumor immunity.

Introduction

CD8+ T cells are central mediators of anti-tumor immunity across diverse cancer types, directly recognizing and eliminating malignant cells through cytotoxic mechanisms that uphold immune surveillance1,2. The abundance and functional activity of CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment have been strongly correlated with patient outcomes in multiple cancers. For instance, high infiltration of CD8+ T cells is associated with prolonged survival in melanoma, ovarian cancer, and colorectal cancer (CRC)1,3,4. In CRC, which remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 10 percent of all cases and deaths5,6, intra-tumoral CD8+ T cells serve as key prognostic markers and predictors of therapeutic responses7,8. The pathogenesis of CRC is driven by a complex interplay of genetic mutations, epigenetic alterations, and chronic inflammation, which not only initiates and accelerates tumorigenesis but also profoundly shapes the immunological landscape of the colon9,10. Persistent inflammatory signaling in the intestinal environment disrupts epithelial integrity and promotes immune dysregulation9,11. Together with tissue remodeling, these pathological changes collectively contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment that impairs CD8+ T cell function and facilitates malignant progression12–14. Within such hostile tumor conditions, sustained antigenic stimulation, metabolic stress, and immunosuppressive cytokines drive CD8+ T cells into a state of exhaustion characterized by reduced proliferative capacity, diminished effector function, and upregulation of inhibitory receptors such as LAG3 and Tim315,16. The exhaustion of CD8+ T cells not only impairs anti-tumor immunity but is also linked to poor responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade therapy17,18. To address these challenges, adoptive cell therapies such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy have been actively developed for CRC and other types of cancer19. CAR-T cells are genetically engineered T cells to recognize tumor-associated antigens independently of MHC, offering the potential to overcome tumor immune evasion20. However, despite encouraging preclinical results, the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T cells in CRC and other solid tumors remains limited by multiple factors, including antigen heterogeneity, limited CAR-T cell persistence, and persistence of T cell exhaustion, even in engineered cells19. Continued elucidation of the molecular pathways driving T cell exhaustion, including in CAR-T cells, along with the development of rational immunotherapeutic combinations, holds promise for restoring robust cytotoxic T cell responses and improving clinical outcomes in cancer patients21.

Intracellular signaling pathways act as fundamental regulators of processes such as proliferation, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis, and dysregulation of these pathways is well-recognized as a hallmark of cancer development and progression22. Among these, the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascade plays a central role in oncogenesis by orchestrating cell fate decisions and mediating responses to environmental cues23,24. Persistent activation of MAPK signaling promotes uncontrolled cell proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and metastasis, positioning components of the MAPK pathway as compelling targets for cancer therapy23,24. MAPK activity is tightly regulated by a complex network of proteins, including dual-specificity phosphatases (DUSP), which dephosphorylate both threonine and tyrosine residues essential for MAPK activation, thereby attenuating MAPK-mediated cellular processes25. Emerging evidence highlights the multifaceted roles of DUSP in cancer biology. For instance, DUSP16 has been reported to promote chemoresistance and support tumor cell survival under cytotoxic stress by regulating mitochondria-mediated cell death, while DUSP6 facilitates CRC progression through the regulation of NOTCH1 signaling26,27. Additionally, other DUSP, such as DUSP1, DUSP4, and DUSP9, have been implicated in diverse aspects of tumorigenesis, including proliferation, migration, and response to therapy28. Collectively, these findings position DUSP as attractive targets for therapeutic strategies in the treatment of diverse malignancies.

DUSP4, also known as MAPK kinase phosphatase 2 (MKP2), has been identified as an important regulator of immune responses to microbial infection29. For instance, in response to retinoic acid inducible gene-I (RIG-I) or stimulator of interferon genes (STING) stimulation, DUSP4 negatively regulates ERK1/2 and TBK activation, thereby suppressing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF, as well as type I interferons. As a result, mice lacking DUSP4 exhibited increased resistance to infections with both H1N1 influenza and HSV-1 virus, but also showed enhanced susceptibility to Plasmodium burgher ANKA infection compared to wild-type (WT) mice29. In cancer, loss or silencing of DUSP4 in breast cancer or pancreatic adenocarcinoma has been shown to enhance oncogenic MAPK signaling, resulting in increased tumor aggressiveness and resistance to therapy30,31. However, the function of this molecule in CD8+ T cell responses to tumorigenesis remains unclear.

Here, we set out to investigate whether DUSP4 regulates CD8+ T cell responses in the context of CRC using an AOM/DSS-induced CRC mouse model. The results demonstrate that while DUSP4 does not play a major role in tumor cell-intrinsic growth, it regulates anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response through modulating ERK-KLF2 axis. In addition, the role of DUSP4 in regulating CD8+ T cell responses can be leveraged to enhanced CAR-T cell performance. These findings suggest that fine-tuning DUSP4 activity may represent a broadly applicable strategy for overcoming T cell dysfunction and enhancing the efficacy of adoptive cell therapies against cancer.

Results

Deficiency of DUSP4 increases colorectal tumorigenesis in mice

To examine the regulatory function of DUSP4 in CRC, WT and DUSP4 KO mice were treated with AOM/DSS to induce CRC development. Significantly increased tumor burden, with increases in tumor number, size, and volume in DUSP4 KO mice compared to WT counterparts, was observed (Fig. 1a, b). Histological examination revealed increased incidence of tubular and tubular-villous adenomas in KO mice compared to WT (Fig. 1c, d), along with higher scores for crypt erosion, immune infiltration, and ulceration (Fig. 1e), confirming a more severe neoplastic phenotype in DUSP4 KO mice compared to WT mice. In addition, reduced serum and mucosal inflammatory cytokine, including TNF, IFN-γ, and IL-2, were observed in DUSP4 KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 1f), suggesting impaired mucosal inflammatory responses in DUSP4 KO mice. Tumors from DUSP4 KO mice exhibited elevated pERK1/2 levels, as evidenced by both pERK1/2 staining area and intensity compared to those from WT mice (Fig. 1g). However, the adjacent normal tissue showed no significant differences between WT and DUSP4 KO mice (Fig. 1h). High expression of DUSP4 was associated with better overall survival in CRC patients

To further explore the clinical relevance of DUSP4 in CRC, we examined DUSP4 protein expression in human CRC tissues and corresponding adjacent normal tissues using immunohistochemistry. Quantitative analysis revealed that DUSP4 expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to matched normal adjacent tissues (Fig. 1i, j). Moreover, DUSP4 expression showed a decreasing trend with advancing stages of CRC (Fig. 1k). Based on expression levels of DUSP4 in tumors, CRC patients in the cohorts were stratified into DUSP4 high or low groups, respectively. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with low DUSP4 expression exhibited reduced overall survival compared to patients with high DUSP4 expression (Fig. 1l). In addition, we further analyzed a CRC dataset using the GEPIA (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) web server. Survival analysis using the GEPIA dataset demonstrated that patients with low DUSP4 expression had poorer survival compared to those with high DUSP4 expression (Fig. 1m). These findings highlight the protective role of DUSP4 in CRC and underscore its potential as a favorable therapeutic target.

DUSP4 expression in hematopoietic cells mediates tumor suppression in CRC

To delineate the contribution of hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic compartments to DUSP4-mediated CRC development, first, lethally irradiated WT mice were transferred with WT or DUSP4 KO bone marrow (BM) cells to generate BM chimeric mice (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Successful hematopoietic reconstitution was confirmed by genotype PCR detecting donor-derived DUSP4 alleles in blood, mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN), and colonic tissues (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Upon AOM/DSS CRC induction, mice receiving DUSP4-deficient BM cells developed significantly more and larger colonic tumors compared to mice receiving WT BM cells (Fig. 2a, b). Histological analysis revealed greater epithelial damage, inflammation, and dysplasia in mice reconstituted with DUSP4-deficient BM cells compared to mice reconstituted with WT BM cells (Fig. 2c). Flow cytometric profiling revealed a pronounced reduction of frequencies of CD8+ T cells in the lamina propria (LP), MLN, and peripheral blood from chimera mice reconstituted with DUSP4 KO BM cells compared to chimera reconstituted with WT BM cells, whereas the frequencies of CD11b+ myeloid and CD4+ T cells were comparable between WT and DUSP4 KO chimera mice (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). These results demonstrate that the increased tumorigenesis in chimera mice receiving DUSP4 KO BM cells was associated with a selective impairment in CD8+ T cells.

To evaluate the contribution of non-hematopoietic compartment of DUSP4 KO mice to CRC development, we generated reciprocal chimeras by reconstituting lethally irradiated WT (CD45.2+) and DUSP4 KO (CD45.2+) recipients with BM cells from CD45.1+ WT donors (Supplementary Fig. 2a). After confirming the successful engraftment (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c), mice were treated with AOM/DSS to induce colon tumors. Interestingly, no significant differences in tumor burden or size were observed between the WT and DUSP4 KO chimeras. Gross morphological assessment and quantification demonstrated comparable tumor load and average tumor dimensions (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). Histopathological evaluation of colonic tissues revealed similar degrees of epithelial damage and inflammatory cell infiltration between reconstituted WT and DUSP4 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 2f). Immune profiling of MLNs and LP showed that the frequencies of CD11b+ myeloid cells, as well as CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets, were comparable between WT and DUSP4 KO chimeras (Supplementary Fig. 2g). In addition, immunohistochemical staining of Ki67 of the colonic tissues revealed no significant differences in epithelial cell proliferation between WT and KO chimeras upon AOM/DSS treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2h). Moreover, to understand the regulatory role of DUSP4 in CRC cells, DUSP4 KO HCT116 cells were generated (Supplementary Fig. 2i). Interestingly, DUSP4 KO HCT116 showed no difference in proliferation compared to vector control cells (Supplementary Fig. 2j). Next, HCT116 xenograft models were employed to evaluate the function of DUSP4 in CRC cell growth in vivo. Loss of DUSP4 in HCT116 cells did not affect tumor formation (Supplementary Fig. 2k), suggesting that DUSP4 has a minimal role in CRC cell growth. Together, these findings indicate that DUSP4 expression in hematopoietic cells, rather than in epithelial tissues, plays an essential role in restraining CRC development, potentially by sustaining effective CD8+ T-cell responses.

DUSP4 expression is positively correlated with CD8+ T cell activation and effector function

To understand the regulatory roles of DUSP4 in the colon, we analyzed the transcript levels of DUSP4 in various immune and non-immune cell populations using a scRNA-seq dataset (GSE178341) from Single Cell Portal. DUSP4 was found to be highly expressed in the T/NK/ILC clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3a), and was enriched in T cells, particularly in CD8+ T cells, compared to other immune subsets such as B cells, granulocytes, or macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 3b). This suggests a potential functional role of DUSP4 in CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. DUSP4 expression was also increased in CD4+ and γδT cells, relative to non-T cells, but to a less extent. Consistent with this observation, the immunofluorescence staining further showed increased expression of DUSP4 in tumor tissues, accompanied by a higher number of CD8+DUSP4+ cells compared with adjacent normal tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3c), indicating that DUSP4 is enriched within the intra-tumoral CD8+ T-cell compartment. To address the function of DUSP4 in CD8+ T cells, WT and DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells were isolated from mice, followed by TCR stimulation. The KO CD8+ T cells were impaired in upregulation of activation markers, including CD25, CD69, and IL-2, compared to WT cells (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Similarly, DUSP4 overexpression enhanced the expression of TNF, IFN-γ, and GZMB in human CD8+ T cells upon TCR stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 4c). To determine whether DUSP4 plays a similar function in CD4+ T cells, WT and DUSP4 KO CD4+ T cells were activated by TCR stimulation. Interestingly, the WT and KO CD4+ T cells showed comparable proliferation and induction of CD25, CD69, or IL-2, indicating that DUSP4 is not required for CD4+ T cell activation (Supplementary Fig. 4d–f). In addition, to evaluate the relationship between DUSP4 and T cells in human CRC, we conducted a correlative analysis using bulk transcriptomic datasets. A significant positive correlation between the expression of DUSP4 and CD8, but not DUSP4 and CD4, or DUSP4 and CD11b, were observed (Supplementary Fig. 4g). These findings support a specific association between DUSP4 and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in CRC.

Next, WT and KO naïve CD8+ T cells were differentiated for 4 days under stimulation with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies. Significantly reduced proliferation of KO cells compared to WT cells was observed (Fig. 3a). In addition, the KO cells had reduced secretion of effector cytokines, including TNF, IFN-γ, and IL-2 (Fig. 3b–d), as well as diminished expression of the degranulation marker CD107a and the cytolytic effector Granzyme B in both non-divided and divided CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3e, f), compared to WT cells. On the other hand, there was no difference between WT and KO CD4+ T cells in their differentiation into various effector cell subsets, including Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4h, i). To further elucidate the mechanistic role of DUSP4 in regulating CD8+ cytotoxic T cell function, naïve CD8+ T cells from WT and DUSP4 KO mice were stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies, followed by examination of MAPK activation. The results demonstrated that DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells had increased phosphorylation of ERK1/2, but not JNK or p38, compared to WT cells (Fig. 3g), indicating that it specifically regulates ERK activation in CD8+ T cells.

DUSP4 deficiency compromises CD8⁺ T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses in CRC in vivo

To validate the functional importance of DUSP4 in anti-tumor CD8+ T cell immunity in vivo, we examined CD8+ T cells in multiple murine CRC models in both WT and DUSP4 KO mice. Though the frequencies of CD8+ T cells were comparable between WT and KO mice across lymphoid and peripheral tissues under steady status (Supplementary Fig. 5), in AOM/DSS-induced CRC model, DUSP4-deficient mice exhibited markedly reduced frequencies of CD8+ T cells in both the LP and MLNs compared with WT mice (Fig. 3h). In addition, DUSP4-deficient CD8+ T cells expressed significantly lower CD44, GZMB, and CD107a (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b), but higher levels of exhaustion markers PD-1, LAG3, and Tim3, compared to WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). In addition, compared to WT CD8+ T cells, DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells displayed reduced expression of cytokine, including TNF, IFN-γ, and IL-2 (gated on CD8+CD44+ cells), across mucosal, lymphoid, and circulating compartments (Supplementary Fig. 6d–f). To determine whether this defect was T cell-intrinsic, we next examined CD8+ T cell response in WT BM chimeric mice reconstituted with WT or DUSP4 KO BM cells following CRC induction by AOM/DSS treatment (Fig. 2a). The results showed that chimeric mice reconstituted with DUSP4 KO BM cells exhibited significantly reduced frequencies of CD44+CD62L‒, GZMB+, and CD107a+ CD8+ T cells, accompanied by an increased proportion of LAG3+PD-1+ and LAG3+Tim3+ exhausted cells in the LP and MLNs, compared with chimeras reconstituted with WT BM cells (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d). Similar defects in cytokine expression were observed in CD8+CD44+ T cells from chimeric mice reconstituted with DUSP4 KO BM cells compared with those reconstituted with WT BM cells (Supplementary Fig. 7e–g). Collectively, these results demonstrate that DUSP4-deficient CD8+ T are intrinsically defective in their anti-tumor function in CRC.

To further corroborate the role of DUSP4 in CD8+ T-cell-mediated tumor surveillance, MC38 murine CRC cells were subcutaneously inoculated into WT and DUSP4 KO mice. DUSP4 KO mice exhibited significantly accelerated tumor growth and increased tumor weights compared to WT controls (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 8a). Notably, the frequency of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells was reduced in DUSP4 KO mice compared to WT (Fig. 4c). However, the frequencies of CD4+ T cells in MC38 tumors were comparable WT and DUSP4 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 8b). In addition, CD8+ T cells in MC38 tumors from DUSP4 KO mice displayed a pronounced loss of effector activity, evidenced by reduced CD107a degranulation (Fig. 4d), diminished granzyme B expression (Fig. 4e), and impaired production of TNF and IFN-γ (Fig. 4f, g), compared to those from WT mice. DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells are functionally impaired in controlling tumor growth

To assess whether DUSP4 regulates antigen-specific CD8+ cytotoxicity, CD8+ T cells from OVA-immunized WT or DUSP4 KO mice were isolated and re-stimulated with OVA, followed by co-culture with MC38-OVA tumor cells to test their cytotoxic function (Supplementary Fig. 9a). DUSP4-deficient CD8+ T cells exhibited significantly reduced cytolytic activity across multiple effector-to-target ratios compared to WT CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 9b), indicating impaired antigen-specific killing capacity upon DUSP4 KO. We next examined antigen-specific anti-tumor immunity in vivo using the MC38-OVA tumor challenge model post OVA immunization (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Compared to WT mice, DUSP4 KO mice had smaller draining LNs and larger tumors (Fig. 4h, i), accompanied by reduced frequencies of CD8+ T cells in both LNs and tumors (Fig. 4j). Moreover, tumor-infiltrating DUSP4-deficient CD8+ T cells displayed impaired effector function, evidenced by diminished CD107a degranulation (Fig. 4k), reduced granzyme B expression (Fig. 4l), and decreased TNF and IFN-γ production (Fig. 4m, n), compared to WT cells. Together, these data demonstrate that DUSP4 is essential for mounting robust antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell cytotoxic responses and effective anti-tumor activity both in vitro and in vivo.

Next, murine MC38 CRC cells were inoculated subcutaneously into immunodeficient NSGS mice. Four days after tumor implantation, either WT or DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells were intravenously infused into the tumor-bearing mice to investigate their capacity to control tumor growth (Supplementary Fig. 9d). By day 14, compared to mice without T cell infusion, those receiving WT or KO CD8+ T cell infusion showed significantly reduced tumor size and weight (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). Notably, tumors in mice receiving DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells were larger than those in mice receiving WT CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 9e–g). These findings indicate that DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells are less effective in controlling MC38 tumor growth compared to WT CD8+ T cells. Next, MC38 tumors were harvested to analyze tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. The results showed that the frequency of CD8+ T cell infiltration was comparable between WT and KO groups (Supplementary Fig. 9h). However, DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells exhibited higher expression of exhaustion markers LAG3 and Tim3 compared to their WT counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 9i). Functionally, DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells showed impaired degranulation capacity, as indicated by reduced CD107a expression (Supplementary Fig. 9j), along with significantly lower expression of GZMB compared to WT CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 9k). These findings suggest that, although the KO CD8+ T cells infiltrate tumors at levels comparable to WT cells, they fail to mount effective cytotoxic responses due to increased exhaustion and reduced effector function.

DUSP4 expression may be inversely associated with CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion

To further verify the in vivo findings that deficiency of DUSP4 increased the exhaustion of CD8+ T cells, we explored the publicly available RNA-seq datasets, including tumor-associated exhausted CD8+ T cells, in addition to datasets comparing acute versus chronic TCR stimulation. These results consistently showed that Dusp4 expression is increased in exhausted or chronically stimulated CD8+ T cells compared to naïve or acutely activated counterparts (Fig. 5a), supporting the conclusion that DUSP4 is associated with CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Next, WT and DUSP4 KO naïve CD8+ T cells were activated with anti-CD3 plus anti-CD28 for 2 days, followed by repeated TCR stimulation for 8 days (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The results revealed an increased proportion of LAG3+PD-1+ and LAG3+Tim3+ cells (Fig. 5b) and a decreased proportion of TNF+IFN-γ+ and CD8+GZMB+ cells in CD8+ T cells in the KO group compared to WT controls (Fig. 5c), suggesting that deficiency of DUSP4 might result in the increased exhaustion of CD8+ T cells. To further explore the relationship between DUSP4 and CD8+ T cell exhaustion, we analyzed the expression of DUSP in a public bulk RNA-seq dataset (GSE150120) on OVA-induced OT-I CD8+ T cell exhaustion. The results showed that among all identified DUSP family members, Dusp4 expression exhibited the highest fold change (FC) in exhausted cells compared to non-exhausted cells (Supplementary Fig. 10b)32. To verify this, we stimulated OT-I cells either once or repeatedly with OVA. Consistently, cells with repeated OVA stimulation had a significantly increased population of exhausted cells and an increased expression of Dusp4, compared to those with no OVA stimulation or with single OVA stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). As expected, the exhausted OT-I cells showed lower expression of several cytokines, including Tnf, Ifng, and Il2, compared to non-exhausted cells (normal CD8+ T cells) (Supplementary Fig. 10d). DUSP4 negatively regulates ERK2-mediated KLF2 expression to sustain cytotoxic T cell effector capacity

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which DUSP4 regulates CD8+ T cells, we performed bulk RNA-seq analysis on WT and DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells following anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation. DUSP4 deficiency resulted in a broad transcriptional remodeling following TCR stimulation for 1 or 3 days, compared to WT cells (Fig. 6a). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between WT and DUSP4 KO CD8⁺ T cells were preferentially enriched in pathways related to T cell activation and MAPK/ERK signaling (Fig. 6b). Among the top differentially regulated genes, Klf2, a transcription factor and established regulator of T cell activation and effector functions, was markedly upregulated in DUSP4-deficient CD8+ T cells at both early and late activation time points (Fig. 6c). Consistent with the transcriptional changes identified in transcriptome analysis, DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells displayed sustained ERK1/2 phosphorylation, and earlier and sustained KLF2 protein expression in KO cells compared to WT upon TCR stimulation (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Fig. 11a). Upon inhibition of ERK1/2, KLF2 expression in both WT and DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells was suppressed (Fig. 6f, g), indicating that DUSP4 regulates KLF2 expression through ERK1/2. Further analysis using dual-luciferase reporter assay showed that DUSP4 suppressed Klf2 promoter activity (Fig. 6h). Interestingly, expression of ERK2, but not ERK1, enhanced Klf2 promoter activity, whereas a phospho-deficient ERK2 mutant failed to do so (Fig. 6i), suggesting an essential role for ERK2 activation in the induction of Klf2 expression. Importantly, DUSP4 expression abolished ERK2-driven Klf2 activation, establishing DUSP4 as a crucial negative regulator of the ERK2-KLF2 signaling axis. Conversely, KLF2 expression inhibited Gzmb promoter activity (Fig. 6j), and this inhibitory effect was further enhanced by ERK2 (Fig. 6k), but not ERK1, while DUSP4 boosted Gzmb promoter activity also in the presence of KLF2 (Fig. 6j, k). Consistently, phospho-mutant ERK2 was unable to enhance KLF2-mediated inhibition of Gzmb promoter activity. Strikingly, DUSP4 reversed the effect of wild-type ERK2 on KLF2-mediated suppression on Gzmb promoter activity (Fig. 6k). In addition, KLF2 suppressed the promoter activities of Ifng and Il2 and increased the promoter activity of Sell, an effect that was reversed by DUSP4 expression (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Interestingly, KLF2 preferentially associated with ERK2 rather than ERK1 in co-immunoprecipitation assays (Fig. 6l), in agreement with the selective role of ERK2 in promoting Klf2 transcription. Moreover, DUSP4 and KLF2 co-localized in nucleus and could be pulled down each other (Supplementary Fig. 11c, d). Mutational analysis suggested that the MAPK-binding domain and phosphatase activity of DUSP4 are important for this regulatory interaction (Supplementary Fig. 11d). To verify whether increased KLF2 expression functionally contributed to the impaired cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells caused by DUSP4 deficiency, we examined GZMB expression following KLF2 overexpression or knockdown. Under vector control conditions, DUSP4 KO CD8⁺ T cells exhibited a lower proportion of GZMB⁺ cells than WT cells (Fig. 6m, n and Supplementary Fig. 11e). However, KLF2 overexpression reduced the GZMB⁺ population in both WT and DUSP4 KO CD8⁺ T cells, and either KLF2 overexpression or knockdown abolished the difference between the two groups. (Fig. 6m, n and Supplementary Fig. 11e). CRISPRa-mediated DUSP4 activation improves CAR-T cells performance

To investigate whether upregulation of DUSP4 could potentiate CD8+ T cell function, we employed a CRISPRa approach to activate DUSP4 expression in primary human T cells engineered with a second-generation CD137CAR (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Quantitative RT-PCR and western blot analysis confirmed robust induction of DUSP4 mRNA and protein in CD137CAR-DUSP4 T cells compared to control CAR-T cells at day 25 of culture (Fig. 7a). Flow cytometric analysis of T cell memory subsets from the in vitro co-culture of CAR-T and NALM6 cells revealed a significant increase in the proportion of TEM cells (CD45RA‒CD62L‒) from DUSP4-activated CAR-T cells, compared to control (Fig. 7b). Proliferative capacity, as measured by CFSE dilution, was markedly increased in total T cells and CD8+ T subsets under both monoculture and antigen-specific co-culture conditions with NALM6-GFP target cells (Supplementary Fig. 12b, c). Functionally, DUSP4-activated CAR-T cells exhibited superior cytotoxic activity in real-time killing assays, achieving greater clearance of NALM6 tumor cells relative to control CAR-T cells (Supplementary Fig. 12d). In parallel, DUSP4-activated CAR-T cells displayed significantly greater cytotoxicity against DLD1-CD19 cells at 6, 12, and 24 h compared with control CAR-T cells (Fig. 7d). This enhanced anti-tumor activity was further supported by increased induction of apoptosis in tumor cells and a corresponding reduction in the proportion of live tumor cells following co-culture with DUSP4-activated CAR-T cells (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 12e). To further assess the clinical relevance of DUSP4 in CAR-T cell therapeutic performance, we analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from infused CD8+ CAR-T cells, as well as apheresis CD8+ non-naïve and naïve T cells, from lymphoma patients treated with CAR-T therapy. It was found that, compared to naïve and non-naïve CD8+ T cells, CD8+ CAR-T cells exhibited higher DUSP4 expression (Supplementary Fig. 12g). Importantly, CAR-T cells from patients who achieved complete remission (CR) showed higher DUSP4 expression than those with progressive disease (PD) following CAR-T cell therapy (Supplementary Fig. 12h), and the expression of DUSP4 was positively associated with CD8+ T cells cytotoxicity but negatively associated with exhaustion (Supplementary Fig. 12i). In the TCGA CRC dataset from Human Protein Atlas, KLF2 expression was found to be negatively correlated with GZMB expression (Supplementary Fig. 12j), which was in line with its negative regulatory function in cytotoxic T cells. Consistent with the mechanistic findings above, DUSP4 activation in CD137CAR-T cells reduced the protein expression of KLF2 compared to the control CAR-T cells (Fig. 7i). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DUSP4 upregulation in CAR-T cells enhances effector differentiation, proliferation, and cytotoxic activity, thereby contributing to improved patient outcomes. These results identify DUSP4 as a critical regulator of CD8+ CAR-T cell function and a potential predictive target for CAR-T therapy responsiveness.

Discussion

CD8+ T cells play a central role in anti-tumor immunity; however, their function is often compromised by exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment, leading to diminished cytotoxic activity and impaired tumor control16,18. The development of T cell exhaustion is driven by multiple cellular signaling pathways that impair T cell function over time17. Upon sustained antigenic stimulation, engagement of the inhibitory receptors on CD8+ T cells recruits intracellular phosphatases such as SHP-2, resulting in interruption of TCR and co-stimulatory signals and downstream cascades, notably the PI3K/AKT and RAS/ERK pathways33,34. In addition, immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β contribute to an exhausted phenotype by activating STAT3 and SMAD2/3 signaling33. These cytokine-driven pathways induce transcriptional and epigenetic changes that enforce a dysfunctional state of CD8+ T cells. Among the various intracellular networks implicated, the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK pathway is an important downstream effector of TCR and co-stimulatory signaling35. Indeed, ERK signaling plays an important role in CD8+ T cell differentiation and effector function. For instance, using P14 transgenic CD8+ T cells, Damasio et al. demonstrated that inhibiting ERK activation with the MEK inhibitor PD184352 reduced TCR-induced cytokine expression, including IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF, as well as the expression of perforin and granzyme B36. Furthermore, Liu et al. showed that ERK activation in CD8+ T cells phosphorylates SMAD4 at the Ser367 residue, thereby facilitating its nuclear translocation to promote CD8+ T cell activation and cytotoxic function37. Together, these findings highlight the essential role of ERK signaling in promoting CD8+ T cell activation and cytotoxic function. Here, our findings demonstrate that DUSP4 inhibits ERK1/2 phosphorylation, thereby promoting CD8+ T cell activation and cytotoxic function while inhibiting exhaustion. These results suggest that excessive ERK activation may impair CD8+ T cell activation and effector function and contribute to T cell exhaustion. The expression of DUSP4 thus provides a regulatory checkpoint to fine tune the strength of the ERK signaling, ensuring optimal CD8+ T cell activation and effector function. Importantly, this function of DUSP4 can be harnessed to mitigate T cell exhaustion and improve the therapeutic performance of CAR-T cells (Figs. 5 and 7).

Dual-specificity phosphatases (DUSP) are established as pivotal regulators of MAPK activity, involved in regulation of CD8+ T cell activation, differentiation, and effector function38,39. For instance, DUSP10 is required for CD8+ T cell activation and proliferation but negatively regulates their effector function, thereby contributing to excessive inflammatory cytokine production and earlier mortality in DUSP10 KO mice compared to WT controls following secondary lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) infection38. Deficiency in DUSP5 has been reported to promote the survival of short-lived CD8+ effector cells while suppressing memory precursor effector cells, without affecting CD8+ T cell proliferation or effector function during acute LCMV infection40. However, the impact of DUSP5-mediated regulation of CD8+ effector cells on disease outcome remains unclear. Interestingly, DUSP2 (PAC1) has been shown to promote CD8+ T cell exhaustion, which is independent of its regulation of MAPK activation41. Instead, it recruits the NuRD complex, leading to epigenetic remodeling of effector T cells that promotes exhaustion, a process that depends on its N-terminal unstructured basic region. Here, we report a regulatory function of DUSP4 in CD8+ T cells. While it negatively regulates ERK activation and KLF2 expression, DUSP4 paradoxically promotes CD8+ T cell activation, proliferation, and effector function, thereby safeguarding anti-tumor immunity in CRC. DUSP4 deficiency thus led to reduced abundance and impaired function of CD8⁺ T cells, resulting in compromised anti-tumor immune responses (Fig. 3).

Beyond defining DUSP4 as a negative regulator of ERK activity, our study uncovered a DUSP4-ERK2-KLF2 axis governing CD8+ T-cell effector programming. We show that loss of DUSP4 results in sustained ERK2 phosphorylation and inappropriate upregulation of KLF2, which functions as transcriptional repressor of CD8+ T cell effector programs (Fig. 6). Early studies showed that KLF2 is a key regulator for naïve T cell trafficking through secondary lymphoid organs by promoting expression of S1PR1 and CD62L42, and negatively regulates the establishment of tissue resident memory CD8+ T cells (TRM)43, which are critical sentinels for protective immunity. Recently, KLF2 was shown to be required for the generation and maintenance of stem-like CD62L+ TPEX populations44, and for maintaining effector lineage fidelity of CD8+ T cells and suppression of exhaustion-related gene expression during acute LCMV infection45. These studies highlight the context-dependent nature of KLF2 activity. In addition, conclusions were drawn from models employing complete knockout of KLF2. In the present study, we compared DUSP4 knockout cells exhibiting elevated KLF2 expression with wild-type cells, where KLF2 expression was regulated by physiological stimuli. This elevation of KLF2 was found to suppress the effector function of CD8+ T cells and promote the expression of exhaustion-related genes, including Pdcd1, Lag3, and Tim3. Given that KLF2 expression can be regulated by various stimuli, including TCR signaling and various cytokines such as IL-12, IL-15, IL-33, TNF, and TGFβ, either individually or in combination43, our study provides insights that are highly relevant to physiological T-cell regulation. In addition, our findings position DUSP4 as an essential molecular calibrator that restrains ERK2-driven transcriptional drift toward a dysfunctional state, thereby sustaining cytotoxic T-cell fitness within the tumor microenvironment of CRC.

While our findings indicate a predominant role of DUSP4 in regulating CD8+ T cell function, with minimal impact on CD4+ T cells, prior studies suggest that DUSP4 may modulate CD4+ T cell responses in a context-dependent manner. Huang et al. reported that DUSP4 KO enhanced CD25 expression and proliferation of CD4+ T cells in vitro46. However, our results showed that DUSP4 deficiency did not affect CD4+ T cell activation and differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating that DUSP4 is dispensable for CD4+ T cell polarization under these conditions. One possible explanation is that CD4+ T cells possess more flexible or redundant mechanisms for MAPK regulation, particularly in the absence of inflammatory cues47. CD4+ T cell lineage commitment is a highly integrated process influenced by multiple upstream signals beyond just ERK amplitude. Unlike CD8+ T cells, which often rely on the strength and duration of TCR signaling (ERK/MAPK pathway) for effector programming, CD4+ T cells interpret a broader signal landscape, including co-stimulation, metabolic state, and cytokine gradients47–49. Moreover, phosphatases such as DUSP2 or DUSP10, which are more abundantly expressed in CD4+ T cells, may compensate for the absence of DUSP450. Whether DUSP4 contributes to CD4+ T cell function in vivo, particularly within tissue-specific inflammatory niches, remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Our findings position DUSP4 as a pivotal immune modulator in the intestinal microenvironment, with broad implications for translational immunotherapy. CD8+ T cell exhaustion, which involves progressive loss of effector function, sustained expression of inhibitory receptors, and impaired proliferative capacity, is a major barrier to durable anti-tumor responses in CRC16,51. This study demonstrates that DUSP4 deficiency accelerates exhaustion and impairs the cytotoxic and proliferative capabilities of CD8+ T cells, thereby undermining effective immune surveillance in the gut. Conversely, by utilizing a CAR-T cell engineering strategy that employs CRISPRa-mediated upregulation of DUSP4, we observed enhanced memory phenotype, proliferation, and anti-tumor cytotoxicity in CAR-T cells. Of note, when co-cultured with their targets ex vivo, DUSP4-activated CAR-T cells exhibited increased proportion of effector memory cells (TEM) (Fig. 7b). However, increased stem cell-like memory T cells (TSCM) (Fig. 7h), instead of TEM, was found to be increased in vivo. These differences in memory phenotypes may be due to the fact that the in vivo environment provides additional regulatory cues that influence CAR-T cell maintenance. Notably, these DUSP4-augmented CAR-T cells maintained their functional capacity and resisted exhaustion, even under conditions that typically induce T cell dysfunction (Fig. 7). This approach not only underscores the mechanistic role of DUSP4 in maintaining CD8+ T cell fitness but also highlights its therapeutic potential for overcoming functional decline in adoptive cell therapies. Future studies investigating the upstream regulation and downstream targets of DUSP4 in both native and engineered T cell settings will be important for translating these insights into improved immunotherapies for CRC and other solid tumors.

While our study elucidates the downstream consequences of DUSP4 KO in immune cells, the upstream mechanisms regulating its expression in the tumor microenvironment remain incompletely understood. In several immune contexts, DUSP4 transcription is responsive to pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF, suggesting a role in feedback regulation of MAPK signaling29,52. However, whether this transcriptional response occurs uniformly across tissues or is shaped by the local environment remains unclear. Given that DUSP4 is predominantly expressed in immune cells (Supplementary Fig. 3), its presence in inflamed tissue likely reflects increased immune cell infiltration rather than de novo induction within epithelial or stromal compartments. This interpretation is further supported by the lack of major changes in epithelial proliferation in the absence of DUSP4 (Supplementary Fig. 2). These observations highlight the necessity of distinguishing between intrinsic transcriptional regulation and environment-driven shifts, which is critical to further understand the role of DUSP4 in anti-tumor immunity and for evaluating its potential as a therapeutic target to improve cancer patient outcomes. Future studies employing spatial transcriptomics, cell-type-specific reporters, or conditional deletion models will be essential to further elucidate the regulatory dynamics of DUSP4 in the tumor microenvironment.

In summary, our findings identify DUSP4 as a central immune-intrinsic regulator of MAPK signaling, vital for maintaining CD8+ T and CAR-T cell function, important for anti-tumor immunity, and for CAR-T therapeutic efficacy. The beneficial function of DUSP4 in cytotoxic T cells underscores the context- and lineage-specific nature of phosphatase-mediated immune regulation and suggests its potential as a therapeutic target in cancer.

Methods

Animal studies

DUSP4 KO (dusp4tm1a/tm1a, Stock #J:155845) mice were obtained from the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute and backcrossed with C57BL/6 mice for 12 generations. NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rg tm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG, Stock #005557) mice were obtained from InVivos Pte Ltd (Singapore). CD45.1+ WT (Stock #002014), NSGS (Stock #013062), and OT-I TCR mice (Stock #003831) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (West Grove, PA, USA). Wild-type (WT) and DUSP4 KO, CD45.1+ WT, NSGS, NSG, and OT-I TCR transgenic mice were housed and bred separately under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a controlled environment maintaining a temperature of 25 °C and a 12-h light-dark cycle. All animals were provided with a standard diet. All animal studies conformed to ethical compliance were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of National University of Singapore under the research protocols, including R21-1188, R23-0733, and R23-0731. Age- and sex-matched WT and DUSP4 KO mice were used for the experiments.

To induce colorectal cancer (CRC), 8–10-week-old male WT and DUSP4 KO mice were administered a single intraperitoneal injection of azoxymethane (AOM, 10 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in sterile saline. Starting on Day 7 post-AOM injection, mice underwent three cycles of 1% dextran sodium sulfate (DSS, MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA) in drinking water ad libitum for 5 days, each followed by a 2-week recovery period. Mice were sacrificed using CO2 inhalation at Week 16 post-AOM injection. Colonic tumor incidence, multiplicity, and size were evaluated macroscopically under a light microscope. Tumor size was measured at the widest dimension of each lesion.

Bone marrow chimeras

To delineate the contributions of hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic compartments to CRC development involving DUSP4, bone marrow chimera was established by transplanting CD45.1+ WT bone marrow cells into the lethally irradiated CD45.2+ WT and DUSP4 KO recipient mice or reconstituting lethally irradiated WT recipients with bone marrow cells from either WT or DUSP4 KO donors. Briefly, 8-week-old male recipient mice underwent lethal irradiation (600 rads × 2) to ablate the endogenous hematopoiesis, followed by intravenous injection of 10 million donor bone marrow cells within 24 h post-irradiation. The efficacy of bone marrow reconstitution was evaluated 4 weeks post-transplantation by flow cytometry analysis of the population of CD45.1+ and CD45.2+ cells or genotyping PCR. Upon confirmation of successful engraftment, the reconstituted recipient mice were treated with AOM/DSS to induce CRC.

MC38 subcutaneous tumor model

8–10-week-old male WT and DUSP4 KO mice were subcutaneously injected in the right dorsal flank with MC38 cells (50,000 cells per mouse) using a 27-gauge needle. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured with digital calipers, and the tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W2)/2. The maximum tumor size permitted by the institutional ethical board was 1.5 cm in maximal diameter, and all experiments adhered to this size limit. Pre-specified humane endpoints included ulceration, impaired mobility, or maximal diameter over 1.5 cm under the approval of IACUC. Animals were monitored daily for general health and welfare. Animals meeting any of these criteria were promptly euthanized using CO2 inhalation.

In vitro CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity assay

8–10-week-old male WT and DUSP4 KO mice were immunized subcutaneously with 200 µL of the OVA/CFA emulsion (containing 200 µg OVA) administered at 2 dorsal back sites. Ovalbumin (OVA; Grade V, Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared in sterile PBS at 2 mg/mL and emulsified 1:1 (v/v) with CFA (Chondrex) to generate a stable water-in-oil emulsion. Isolated CD8+ T cells were cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol. Cells were re-stimulated with OVA for 24 h to expand OVA-specific effector CD8+ T cells. Effector CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with labeled MC38-OVA target cells at indicated effector-to-target (E:T) ratios in round-bottom 96-well plates for 48 h.

In vivo assessment of anti-tumor function of CD8+ T cells

To evaluate the intrinsic cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells in vivo, 8–10-week-old male immunodeficient NSGS mice were injected subcutaneously with 300,000 MC38 tumor cells and intravenously transferred with 500,000 WT or DUSP4 KO CD8+ T cells on day 4. The control mice received saline injection. Tumor growth was monitored by measuring tumor volume at the indicated time points. Tumors were harvested on day 14 for downstream analyses. To assess antigen-specific anti-tumor immunity, WT and DUSP4 KO mice were immunized with OVA protein emulsified in CFA. 14 days later, mice were challenged subcutaneously with MC38-OVA (50,000) tumor cells. Tumor-draining lymph nodes and tumors were harvested on day 28.

CD137CAR-DUSP4 generation and functional assay

CARs incorporating the CD137 (4-1BB) co-stimulatory domain included a CD8α hinge and transmembrane region. Non-targeting control (NTC) sgRNA libraries for the Synergistic Activation Mediator (SAM) system were generated by synthesizing NTC sgRNAs with flanking sequences compatible with the LentiSAMv2 vector (Addgene, #75112, a gift from Feng Zhang53), followed by HiFi DNA assembly into the vector. For integration of CAR transgenes into the CRISPRa SAM system, the hygromycin resistance cassette in the pLentiMPHv2 vector (Addgene, #89308, a gift from Feng Zhang53) was replaced with CAR sequences using HiFi assembly. For functional validation, human T cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by magnetic positive selection using Dynabeads Human T-Expander CD3/CD28 beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11141D). Time-course cytotoxicity assays were performed using the IncuCyte S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius) in 96-well flat-bottom plates with NALM6-GFP and CAR-T cells. Time-lapse live-cell images were acquired every 2–3 h over a 48-h period (2–4 images per well per time point) and analyzed with IncuCyte Analysis Software, with residual tumor cells quantified as percentage GFP confluence and normalized to baseline (0 h).

DLD1-CD19 xenografts

The human colorectal cancer cell line DLD1 was engineered to stably express CD19 (DLD1-CD19) by lentiviral transduction of human CD19 cDNA, cloned from an in-house cDNA library into a custom-designed lentiviral vector under the control of an EF1α promoter. Transduced cells were selected, and stable CD19 expression was confirmed by flow cytometry. Cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under standard culture conditions. To evaluate the anti-tumor activity of CD137CAR-T cells, 8–10-week-old male NSG mice were randomly grouped and subcutaneously inoculated with 5 million DLD1-CD19 cells in Matrigel, followed by the treatment with 5 million CAR-T cells, with or without CRISPRa-mediated activation of DUSP4. The tumor size was monitored every 2 days before the tumor size reached humane endpoints.

Clinical specimens and survival analysis

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections from 105 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients were obtained from National University Hospital (NUH) Tissue Repository under approved protocol DSRB 2017-01096. Informed consent was obtained for the application in research. Clinical characteristics of the patients were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Based on the IHC staining of DUSP4, these patients were stratified into DUSP4 low or high groups. Overall survival was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test. Additionally, gene expression levels of DUSP4 in normal and CRC tumor tissues were analyzed using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA2) platform, including comparisons across different CRC stages and correlation with T effector memory (TEM) cell gene signatures. Survival analysis based on DUSP4 gene expression was performed using TCGA CRC patient data. RNA-seq dataset (GSE223655) from the GEO database were used to assess DUSP4 expression in infused CD8+ CAR-T cells, apheresis CD8+ naïve and non-naïve T cells from lymphoma patients receiving CAR-T therapy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.0 software (La Jolla, CA, USA). Data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were conducted by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests. For comparison of 3 or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used. p-value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Additional information is available in Supplementary Methods.
