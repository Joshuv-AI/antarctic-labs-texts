Bessel functions on G ​ L ​ ( n ) GL(n) , II - the case n = 4 n=4 Thanks: During the time of this research, the author was supported by NSF grant DMS-2348653.
Jack Buttcane

Abstract
. The purpose of this article is to verify the conjectures of the previous paper in the particular case of G ​ L ​ ( 4 ) GL(4) . We accomplish this in general, but observe two failures of the conjectures: First, that the Strong Interchange of Integrals conjecture is perhaps false for a single Weyl element w 2 , 2 w_{2,2} , though we prove the Weak Interchange of Integrals still holds. Second, again for a single Weyl element w 2 , 1 , 1 w_{2,1,1} and its conjugate w 1 , 1 , 2 w_{1,1,2} , it appears that the space of solutions to the Bessel differential equations may not be spanned by the Frobenius series solutions. We discuss what refinements, namely to the Asymptotics Theorem, would be necessary to uniquely identify the Bessel functions for such Weyl elements, and prove them in the exceptional cases for G ​ L ​ ( 4 ) GL(4) .

Such a method would skip past the interchange of integrals (see below), differential equations, and power series representations, which are certainly desirable in their own right, and proceed directly to the integral representations, which are the most important for applications.

1.1.
The Kuznetsov formula

The Kuznetsov formula for a discrete subgroup
\Gamma\subset GL(4,\mathbb{R})
is an equality between a sum of the Fourier-Whittaker coefficients of automorphic forms in some spectral family and sums of generalized Kloosterman sums.
For integral diagonal matrices
m
and
n
, the Kuznetsov formula has the rough form

\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi}\rho_{\varphi}(n)\overline{\rho_{\varphi}(m)}f(\mu_{\varphi})+\text{ Eisenstein series terms}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in W}\sum_{c}\frac{S_{w}(m,n,c)}{\mathopen{}\left|c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}\right|\mathclose{}}H_{w}(f,mcwn^{-1}w^{-1}),

where
\varphi
runs through the family of cusp forms in
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash GL(4,\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}^{+})
sharing a common minimal weight
\Lambda
and discrete spectral parameters
\delta
,
W
is the Weyl group of
GL(4)
, and
c
runs through the diagonal part of the Bruhat decomposition of
\Gamma
.
Here,
\mu_{\varphi}
are the continuous spectral parameters of
\varphi
and
f(\mu)
is some nice test function,
\rho_{\varphi}(m)
is the Fourier-Whittaker coefficient of
\varphi
at
m
,
S_{w}(m,n,c)
is the Kloosterman sum, and
H_{w}(f,y)
is an integral transform of
f
.

A theorem of Friedberg
[
15
]
implies that only the “relevant” Weyl elements
w\in W^{\text{rel}}\subset W
, which are reverse-block diagonal matrices formed from identity matrices in the form

(1)

\displaystyle w_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell}}=\begin{pmatrix}&&I_{r_{1}}\\
&\mathinner{\mkern 1.0mu\raise 1.0pt\vbox{\kern 7.0pt\hbox{.}}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 4.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 7.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 1.0mu}\\
I_{r_{\ell}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad r_{1}+\ldots+r_{\ell}=4,

contribute to the Kuznetsov formula, and similarly, we define the Kloosterman sums to be zero unless a certain “compatibility” condition is met, which corresponds to the argument of
H_{w}(f,\cdot)
lying in a subspace
Y_{w}
of the diagonal matrices.

The most technically difficult theorem of this paper is that each
H_{w}(f,y)
can be written as a kernel integral transform of
f
, and we call the kernel functions
K_{w}(y,\mu,\delta)
the
GL(4)
Bessel functions.
As this theorem is also somewhat technical to state, we wait until
section

1.4
to do so.

We tend to suppress commas in the subscript and double subscripts, so, e.g.,
w_{121}:=w_{1,2,1}
and
K_{121}(y,\mu,\delta):=K_{w_{1,2,1}}(y,\mu,\delta)
.
In this notation, the relevant Weyl elements for
GL(4)
are

w_{4}=I,w_{13},w_{31},w_{22},w_{112},w_{121},w_{211},w_{1111}=:w_{l}.

From the symmetry
[
13
, Prop. 2]
of the Bessel function
K_{w}(y,\mu,\delta)
under the involution
w\mapsto w^{\iota}
with
\iota:G\to G
defined by
g^{\iota}=w_{l}{\left(g^{-1}\right)}^{T}w_{l}
, it suffices to consider the elements

w_{31},w_{22},w_{121},w_{211},w_{1111}.

Note that
K_{I}(y,\mu,\delta)=1
, so there is no further need to study this function.

1.3.
Some notation

Let
G:=GL(n,\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}^{+}

K:=O(n)
.
As usual, we treat elements of

for the diagonal matrices,
Y^{+}\subset Y
for the positive diagonal matrices,

for the upper-triangular unipotent matrices with standard generic character

for the Weyl group,
\overline{U}_{w}=U\cap(w^{-1}{U}^{T}w)

As always, the left and right actions of the Weyl group on

We extend the power function via the Iwasawa decomposition to

\displaystyle I^{I}_{\mu,\delta,\sigma}(xyk)=

\displaystyle I_{\mu,\delta}(y)\Sigma_{\delta,\sigma}\sigma(k),\qquad\Sigma_{\delta,\sigma}:=\frac{1}{\mathopen{}\left|V\right|\mathclose{}}\sum_{v\in V}I_{-\rho,\delta}(v)\sigma(v),\qquad\sigma\in\widehat{K},\qquad V:=Y\cap K,

G_{w_{l}}:=U(\mathbb{R})Y{U(\mathbb{R})}^{T}

\displaystyle I^{B}_{\mu,\delta}(xy{u}^{T})=

\displaystyle I_{\mu,\delta}(y).

Lastly, we define a Whittaker-like power function on

\displaystyle I^{W}_{\mu,\delta}(xy{u}^{T})=

\displaystyle\psi_{I}(x)I_{\mu,\delta}(y).

For an irreducible, unitary representation

We will refer to (principal series) representations of

Families of such representations

The weight of a representation of

1.4.
The conjectures

We summarize the ideas of
[
13
, Sect. 4 and 5.5.1]
as follows

•

The Interchange of Integrals Conjecture.

If we were able to interchange the
u
and
\mu

H_{w}^{*}
we could conclude

(29)

\displaystyle I_{0,0}(y)H_{w}^{*}(f,y,g)=\int_{i\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{*}(\Lambda)}f(\mu)K_{w}(y,\Lambda,\mu,\delta)W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)d_{\text{spec}}\mu,

for some function
K_{w}(y,\Lambda,\mu,\delta)
defined by

(30)

\displaystyle K_{w}(y,\Lambda,\mu,\delta)W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)=\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}W_{\sigma}(ywug,\mu,\delta)\overline{\psi_{I}(u)}du.

Here we have applied
[
13
, Prop. 1]
which uses Shalika’s multiplicity one theorem to separate the functions
K_{w}
and
W_{\sigma}
, and shows that
K_{w}
will not depend on
\sigma
by considering the action of the Lie algebra.

The latter integral does not converge absolutely, so we cannot apply the theorems of Fubini and Tonelli. Instead we interpret the integral conditionally by considering a particular choice of coordinates on
u
, introducing a smooth partition of unity, and treating the smooth, compactly supported integral as an oscillatory integral.
The conjecture is then that the series in the dyadic partition converges absolutely (the weak conjecture), which allows the interchange, and hopefully converges rapidly (the strong conjecture).

As the (iterated) integrals (

This conjecture is then that there are operators which avoid the missing coordinates, and below the statement of the conjecture in

As usual, this integral does not converge absolutely, but it does converge in a nice enough manner for a nice choice of coordinates.

•

The Mellin-Barnes Integrals Conjecture.

We extend the usual definition of a Mellin-Barnes integral a bit to include finite sums of multi-dimensional inverse-Mellin transforms of quotients of gamma functions.
(Some of the variables in the inverse-Mellin transforms will actually be one.)
It is a fact that the

J_{w}

1.5.
Caveats

Now, where we need exceptions to the conjectures:

1.

The higher weight case for
w_{22}
.
Though we prove the Weak Interchange of Integrals (existence of an integral kernel for the defining integral transform) for the
w_{22}
Weyl element, it appears the Strong Interchange of Integrals (rapid convergence of the defining integral) is likely just false in this case.
In hindsight, this is perhaps not too surprising and follows from two difficulties:

\overline{U}_{22}
is symmetric about the reverse diagonal and has minimal presence (a single nonzero entry) on the first super-diagonal, leading to a small number of terms in the phase of the oscillatory integral.

The computations given here cover the principal-series (i.e.
\Lambda=0
) case, but since the Weak Interchange of Integrals does not imply the Analytic Continuation Conjecture, this means we need to extend the Asymptotics Theorem to the generalized principal series case and argue that the Differential Equations and Power Series Conjecture holds in that case, as well; this is the approach we use for
w_{22}
in
section

8.1
.
For the former, the author only retreated in face of notational complexities (and the hope that it was unnecessary), and the latter should also pose no trouble as this is a standard fact about Frobenius series solutions.

2.

The complete solution to the differential equations in the
w_{211}
case.
In the other cases, we are able to show the Frobenius series span the solution set by dimensionality arguments – i.e. showing the dimension of the solution space is equal to the number (dimension) of Frobenius series solutions, but for
w_{211}
, though we have a complete set of Frobenius series solutions, the author was unable to compute the dimension of the full space of solutions.

2.2.
Gamma Functions

In addition to the

function defined above, we will need

R_{\eta}(s)=i^{\eta^{\prime}}\cos\frac{\pi(s-\eta^{\prime})}{2}.

Notice that

\displaystyle G_{\eta}(s)=

From the reflection property and known residues of the gamma function as well as the usual properties of trigonometric functions, we have the following useful identities:

(37)

\displaystyle G_{\ell}(s,t,\eta)=

\displaystyle G_{\ell}(s+u,(t_{1}-u,\ldots,t_{n}-u),\eta),

(38)

\displaystyle G_{\eta}(s)G_{\eta}(1-s)=

\displaystyle(-1)^{\eta}

(39)

\displaystyle R_{\eta}(s+n)=

\displaystyle i^{n}R_{\eta+n}(s),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(40)

\displaystyle R_{\eta}(s)=

\displaystyle(-1)^{\eta}R_{\eta}(-s),

(41)

\displaystyle R_{\eta}(s)R_{\eta}(1-s)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{\eta}\sin(\pi s),

(42)

\displaystyle\res_{s=n}\frac{1}{R_{\eta}(s)}=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{2i}{\pi}(-1)^{\eta}i^{n}&\mbox{if }n\equiv\eta+1\pmod{2},\\

(43)

\displaystyle R_{\eta}(s)\Gamma(s+n)=

\displaystyle R_{\eta}(s)\Gamma(s)\left(s\right)_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{s}G_{\eta}(s)\left(s\right)_{j},

where
\left(s\right)_{j}=\Gamma(s+j)/\Gamma(s)
is the (rising) Pochhammer symbol.
Furthermore,
G_{\eta}(s)
is entire except for simple poles at each
s=-n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n\equiv\eta\pmod{2}
with residue

\res_{s=-n}G_{\eta}(s)=2\frac{(2\pi i)^{n}}{n!}.

The poles of

are at
s=n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\equiv\eta+1\pmod{2}
by the symmetry (
38
).

We note that Stirling’s formula implies

\displaystyle\log\mathopen{}\left|\Gamma(\sigma+it)\right|\mathclose{}=

\displaystyle\log\Gamma(\sigma)+\left(\sigma-\frac{1}{2}\right)\log\mathopen{}\left|1+i\frac{t}{\sigma}\right|\mathclose{}-\mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{}\arctan\frac{\mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{}}{\sigma}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\right),

for
\sigma>0
and

, so that

(44)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\left|\Gamma(\sigma+it)\right|\mathclose{}\asymp_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\Gamma(\sigma)\mathopen{}\left|1+i\frac{t}{\sigma}\right|\mathclose{}^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{}\arctan\frac{\mathopen{}\left|t\right|\mathclose{}}{\sigma}\right),

for
\sigma>\epsilon>0
and

We refer to

as the “factorial part”, the factor

2.3.
Hypergeometric Series

The (generalized) hypergeometric series for
p,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
a\in\mathbb{C}^{p},b\in\mathbb{C}^{q},z\in\mathbb{C}
is defined by

{{}_{p}F_{q}}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};\\
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};\end{matrix}\,z\right):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\frac{\left(a_{1}\right)_{n}\cdots\left(a_{p}\right)_{n}}{\left(b_{1}\right)_{n}\cdots\left(b_{q}\right)_{n}},

provided the series converges.
We sometimes use the completed (“regularized”) series

{{}_{p}F_{q}^{*}}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};\\
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};\end{matrix}\,z\right):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\frac{\left(a_{1}\right)_{n}\cdots\left(a_{p}\right)_{n}}{\Gamma(b_{1}+n)\cdots\Gamma(b_{q}+n)}={{}_{p}F_{q}}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};\\
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};\end{matrix}\,z\right)/\prod_{i=1}^{q}\Gamma(b_{i}),

which has no poles (when it converges).
Finally, the fully completed

{{}_{p}F_{q}^{\dagger}}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};\\
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};\end{matrix}\,z\right):=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}\Gamma(s)\right){{}_{p}F_{q}^{*}}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};\\
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};\end{matrix}\,z\right),

will prove convenient for recurrence relations.

Some useful hypergeometric identities are

(45)

\displaystyle{{}_{2}F_{1}^{*}}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},a_{2};\\
b_{1};\end{matrix}\,1\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(b_{1}-a_{1}-a_{2})}{\Gamma(b_{1}-a_{1})\Gamma(b_{1}-a_{2})}\qquad\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Bailey}{}{}, eq. (1)]}},

(46)

\displaystyle{{}_{p}F_{q}}\left(\begin{matrix}0,a_{2},\ldots,a_{p};\\
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};\end{matrix}\,z\right)=

\displaystyle 1,

for
-1\leq n\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a,b,c,d,e,f\notin\mathbb{Z}
,

(47)

\displaystyle{{}_{4}F_{3}^{*}}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b,c,d;\\
-n,e,f;\end{matrix}\,1\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a\right)_{k}\left(b\right)_{k}\left(c\right)_{k}\left(d\right)_{k}}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(k-n)\Gamma(e+k)\Gamma(f+k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(a+k+n+1)\Gamma(b+k+n+1)\Gamma(c+k+n+1)\Gamma(d+k+n+1)}{\Gamma(k+n+2)\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(e+k+n+1)\Gamma(f+k+n+1)\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c)\Gamma(d)}

\displaystyle=\left(a\right)_{n+1}\left(b\right)_{n+1}\left(c\right)_{n+1}\left(d\right)_{n+1}\,{{}_{4}F_{3}^{*}}\left(\begin{matrix}1+n+a,1+n+b,1+n+c,1+n+d;\\
2+n,1+n+e,1+n+f;\end{matrix}\,1\right),

for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
a,b,c,d,e\notin\mathbb{Z}
,

(48)

\displaystyle{{}_{4}F_{3}^{*}}\left(\begin{matrix}-m,a,b,c;\\
-m-n,d,e;\end{matrix}\,1\right)=0.

2.4.
Mellin-Barnes Integrals

If we define the signed Mellin transform

(49)

\displaystyle\hat{f}(s,\ell):=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\chi_{s-1}^{\ell}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(f(x)+(-1)^{\ell}f(-x)\right)x^{s-1}dx,

then Mellin inversion becomes

(50)

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\in\mathopen{}\left\{0,1\right\}\mathclose{}}\int_{{\mathop{Re}}(s)=\frac{1}{2}}\hat{f}(s,\ell)\chi_{-s}^{\ell}(x)\frac{ds}{2\pi i}.

As in
[
11
, eq. (3.17)]
, we have

(51)

\displaystyle e\left(x\right)=

\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to\frac{\pi}{2}^{-}}\int_{{\mathop{Re}}(s)=c}\mathopen{}\left|2\pi x\right|\mathclose{}^{-s}e^{is\theta{\mathop{\,sgn}}(x)}\Gamma\left(s\right)\,\frac{ds}{2\pi i},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to\frac{\pi}{2}^{-}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\in\mathopen{}\left\{0,1\right\}\mathclose{}}\int_{{\mathop{Re}}(s)=c}\frac{\cos(\theta s-\frac{\pi}{2}\ell)}{R_{\ell}(s)}\chi_{-s}^{\ell}(x)G_{\ell}(s)\,\frac{ds}{2\pi i}

for
x\neq 0
and
c>0
, so in a formal sense,

\widehat{e}(s,\ell)=G_{\ell}(s).

For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
and
\mathopen{}\left|{\mathop{Re}}(\mu)\right|\mathclose{}<1
,

(52)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\chi_{s-1}^{\eta}(x)e\left(ax+\frac{b}{x}\right)dx=

\displaystyle\chi_{-\frac{s}{2}}^{\eta}(a)\chi_{\frac{s}{2}}^{0}(b)\mathcal{Z}_{s}^{\eta}(ab).

which follows from
[
18
, 3.871.1-4, 8.403.1]
.
Note that
\mathcal{Z}_{s}^{\eta+2}(a)=\mathcal{Z}_{s}^{\eta}(a)
.

For
a>0
and
\mathopen{}\left|{\mathop{Re}}(s)\right|\mathclose{}<2c<1
, by
[
18
, 17.43.16, 17.43.18]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\eta}(-a)=

\displaystyle 2i^{\delta}\int_{{\mathop{Re}}(s)=c}\cos\frac{\pi}{2}\left(t-\eta\right)\Gamma\left(s+\frac{t}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s-\frac{t}{2}\right)\left(4\pi^{2}a\right)^{-s}\frac{ds}{2\pi i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\eta}(+a)=

\displaystyle 2i^{\delta}\int_{{\mathop{Re}}(s)=c}\cos\frac{\pi}{2}\left(2s-\eta\right)\Gamma\left(s+\frac{t}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s-\frac{t}{2}\right)\left(4\pi^{2}a\right)^{-s}\frac{ds}{2\pi i}.

It follows that for
\eta\in\mathopen{}\left\{0,1\right\}\mathclose{},\ell\in\mathbb{Z}

(53)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}(s,\ell+\eta_{2})=

\displaystyle G_{\ell}\left(s,\left(\frac{t}{2},-\frac{t}{2}\right),(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\right).

-Bessel function, i.e.

{\mathop{Re}}(s)=0

The

From the definition of the Euler beta function

and some trigonometry, we have

(54)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{s_{1}-1}^{\eta_{1}}(x)\chi_{s_{2}-1}^{\eta_{2}}(1-x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{\eta_{1}}(s_{1})G_{\eta_{2}}(s_{2})}{G_{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}(s_{1}+s_{2})}.

7.1.
The BKY Lemma

The reader may find the formulation of the following lemmas somewhat silly, but rather than attach some new notation to the problem, the author has simply listed out all of the relevant cases.

Lemma 9
.

Let
C\geq 1
and suppose

x\in\left\{\begin{matrix}[-4C,-C]\cup[C,4C]&\mbox{if }C>1,\\
[-4,4]&\mbox{if }C=1.\end{matrix}\right.

Then for

given by any of

\displaystyle\frac{Cx}{1+x^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{C^{2}}{1+x^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}{C},

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}},

\displaystyle\frac{C^{2}x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}},

\displaystyle\frac{x}{C},

\displaystyle\frac{C}{x},

we have

\left(C\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{j}f(x)\ll_{j}1,

for all

, provided
C>1
for the case

.

Proof.

The lemma is trivially true for
C=1
, so suppose
C>1
, then all but one of these may be written in the form

{\mathop{\,sgn}}(x)^{\delta}\left(\frac{x}{C}\right)^{\alpha}\left(1+x^{-2}\right)^{\beta}={\mathop{\,sgn}}(x)^{\delta}\left(\frac{x}{C}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\beta}{k}x^{-2k},

which clearly satisfies the conclusion, by the known properties of the binomial series (i.e. the ratio test).
In the exceptional case, we have

C\frac{d}{dx}\frac{C^{2}x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}=\frac{C^{3}}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}},

and the same argument applies.
∎

Directly from
[

W_{\sigma}(y^{*},\mu,\delta)
has super-polynomial decay in the coordinates of
y^{*}
unless
y_{i}^{*}\ll 1
and for the choice of coordinates of
[
13
, Sect. 10]
, the entries of
\sigma(k^{*})
may be expressed as trigonometric polynomials in (the argument of)
\frac{1+ix_{j}}{\sqrt{1+x_{j}^{2}}}
for each coordinate.
Thus the factor
W_{\sigma}(y^{*},\mu,\delta)\sigma(k^{*})
satisfies

\left(\prod_{j}x_{j}^{m_{j}}\frac{\partial^{m_{j}}}{\partial x_{j}^{m_{j}}}\right)W_{\sigma}(y^{*},\mu,\delta)\sigma(k^{*})\ll_{m,\mu}1.

Finally,
[
13
, Sect. 10]
applies a dyadic partition of unity to the integral.
We roll
W_{\sigma}(y^{*},\mu,\delta)\sigma(k^{*})
into some
g(\cdot,C):\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying

\left(\prod_{j}x_{j}^{m_{j}}\frac{\partial^{m_{j}}}{\partial x_{j}^{m_{j}}}\right)g(x,C)\ll_{m}\mathopen{}\left|C\right|\mathclose{}^{\epsilon\mathopen{}\left|m\right|\mathclose{}}

that is smooth and supported on

x_{j}\in\left\{\begin{matrix}[-4C_{j},-C_{j}]\cup[C_{j},4C_{j}]&\mbox{if }C_{j}>1,\\
[-4,4]&\mbox{if }C_{j}=1.\end{matrix}\right.

Then the explicit Strong Interchange of Integrals Conjecture becomes

(102)

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}e\left(\phi(x)\right)g(x,C)dx\ll_{A,\epsilon}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\left|C\right|\mathclose{}^{-A}

with
t\in Y
,
y\in Y_{w}
satisfying
\mathopen{}\left|t_{i}\right|\mathclose{},\mathopen{}\left|y_{i}\right|\mathclose{}\asymp 1
.
We say the oscillatory integral satisfying (
102
) is “negligible” and we assume, for a contradiction, that this is not the case.
The use of contradiction is purely for notational simplicity and the contradiction will generally be reached when we have forced all
C_{j}\ll^{*}1
, which implies
\mathopen{}\left|C\right|\mathclose{}\ll 1
so that (
102
) is trivially true.

Suppose some addend
f(x)
of
\phi(x)
satisfies
\phi_{i}(x)\ll^{*}1
.
It is generally the case that differentiating the terms of
\phi(x)
save powers of the
C_{j}
, i.e. such an
f(x)
will then satisfy

\left(\prod_{j}C_{j}^{m_{j}}\frac{\partial^{m_{j}}}{\partial x_{j}^{m_{j}}}\right)f(x)\ll_{m}\mathopen{}\left|C\right|\mathclose{}^{\epsilon\mathopen{}\left|m\right|\mathclose{}},

and hence the same for
e\left(f(x)\right)
.
We may then include such a factor in the weight function
g(x,C)
without altering our assumptions, and so we will.
Similarly, for
C_{j}>1
, we can write, e.g.

\frac{x_{j}}{\sqrt{1+x_{j}^{2}}}={\mathop{\,sgn}}(x_{j})+f(x_{j}),\qquad f(x_{j})=\frac{-{\mathop{\,sgn}}(x_{j})}{\sqrt{1+x_{j}^{2}}\left(\mathopen{}\left|x_{j}\right|\mathclose{}+\sqrt{1+x_{j}^{2}}\right)}

where now
f(x_{j})
is smooth on the support of
g
(which excludes
x_{j}=0
) with

C_{j}^{m}\frac{d^{m}}{dx_{j}^{m}}f(x_{j})\ll_{m}C_{j}^{-2},

and this will allow us to further simplify the phase function.
The goal at each step is to reduce the number of non-negligible terms in
\phi(x)
.

We start with a simple case, which can be done for all
GL(n)
and we work this out by hand.

7.3.
Interchange of integrals for
w_{n,1}

For the Weyl element
w_{n,1}
, naming the coordinate at position
1,i+1
of the
x
-matrix
x_{i}
and the same for
C_{i}
, the phase is

\displaystyle\phi(x)=

\displaystyle-t_{1}x_{1}+y^{*}_{n}x_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{y_{i}x_{i}x_{i+1}}{\sqrt{1+x_{i}^{2}}},

where

\displaystyle y^{*}_{i}:=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1+x_{i+1}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+x_{i}^{2}}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n-1,

\displaystyle y^{*}_{n}:=

\displaystyle\frac{y_{n}}{\sqrt{1+x_{2}^{2}}\cdots\sqrt{1+x_{n-1}^{2}}(1+x_{n}^{2})}.

The middle term in the phase is
\ll 1
, so we move it to the weight function and replace the phase with

\phi(x)=-t_{1}x_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{y_{i}x_{i}x_{i+1}}{\sqrt{1+x_{i}^{2}}}.

The conditions
y^{*}_{i}\ll^{*}1
imply

(103)

\displaystyle C_{i+1}\ll^{*}C_{i}

for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.

From
11
, if it were the case that
C_{n}>\!\!>\!\!>^{*}1
, we would conclude the bound (
102
) holds unless
C_{n-1}=1
, but this violates the assumption (
103
).
Assuming
C_{n}\ll^{*}1
, we may remove the
i=n-1
term to the weight function and apply the same reasoning inductively to conclude the bound holds unless all
C_{i}\ll^{*}1
, in which case the bound is trivially true.

7.4.
The algorithm

For the remaining cases on
GL(4)
, we apply a computer algebra package.
The algorithm is quite simple and is given in Mathematica code in
appendix

A
; most of the code presented there is actually for converting human-readable formulas into the format required for the algorithm.
We note that the code uses the coordinates
x_{i,j}
for the entry at position
(i,j)
in the
x
-matrix, rather than the coordinates (
33
) that we use in the paper.

To start, we take some subset
\mathcal{S}
of the indices
j
of the coordinates of
\overline{U}_{w}
and assume that
C_{j}=1
for all
j\in\mathcal{S}
while
C_{j}>1
for all
j\notin\mathcal{S}
.
We will repeat the following process for all possible choices of
\mathcal{S}
; the case where
\mathcal{S}
contains all of the indices is trivial, so we exclude it.
The goal is to build a Boolean expression which minimally describes the every non-negligible case resulting from applying
11
to the
x_{j}
derivative for every coordinate of
\overline{U}_{w}
(including those of
\mathcal{S}
).

All of the constraints may be viewed as linear inequalities (sometimes equalities) on the logarithms of the
C_{j}
, up to a summand
O\left(\epsilon\mathopen{}\left|C\right|\mathclose{}\right)
; e.g. something of the form

\frac{\sqrt{1+x_{1}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+x_{3}^{2}}}\ll^{*}1

translates to

\log C_{1}\leq\log C_{3}+O\left(\epsilon\mathopen{}\left|C\right|\mathclose{}\right).

The term
+O\left(\epsilon\mathopen{}\left|C\right|\mathclose{}\right)
is assumed on (and dropped from) all of the inequalities in the algorithm.

A complication for those indices
j\in\mathcal{S}
is that
x_{j}
and
1-x_{j}^{2}
may fail to be
\asymp C_{j}
or
\asymp C_{j}^{2}
, respectively, when considering the support of
g(x,C)
at
C_{j}=1
; we use the phrase “might be small” to describe terms which contain such factors (but only those with
j\in\mathcal{S}
).
For an expression, whether it might be small or not, we compute its “potential size” by assuming
x_{j}\asymp C_{j}
and
1-x_{j}^{2}\asymp C_{j}^{2}
.

For each choice of
\mathcal{S}
, we iteratively build the Boolean expression from the initial constraints
y_{i}^{*}\ll^{*}1
and
C_{j}\geq 1
(a conjunction of inequalities).
We first write the phase
\phi(x)
as a sum of products (we refer to the products as the “terms” of
\phi(x)
and the same for its derivatives) and for each coordinate
x_{j}
we apply
11
as follows:
In the derivative
C_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\phi(x)
, either all (a conjunction) of the terms are small, i.e.
\ll^{*}1
, or (a disjunction) one of the terms must have the largest potential size and this must be
>\!\!>\!\!>^{*}1
(a conjunction).
If the term of the largest potential size might be small, we may draw no further conclusions from
11
; otherwise, there must be a second term of the same size (a disjunction on which term), and we may assume that this term is also not one which might be small (since the new term would then have the largest potential size).
The final Boolean expression is then the conjunction of the initial constraints and these Boolean expressions resulting from the conclusions of
11
for each derivative.

We then rely on the computer algebra package to reduce the Boolean expression.
The code in
appendix

A
optimizes the Boolean reduction process by starting with the simplest derivative and working up to the most complex and outputs only the non-trivial cases.

We remark that a minor tweak to the algorithm – removing the initial conditions
y^{*}_{i}\ll^{*}1
and dropping
\psi_{I}(x^{*})
from the phase – also proves a strong form (rapid convergence) of the Jacquet-Whittaker Direct Continuation Conjecture of
[
13
, Sect. 10]
for
GL(4)
.
Finally, the algorithm can obviously be applied to
GL(5)
and higher, but on the author’s PC, Mathematica largely fails to simplify the resulting Boolean expression and for the few Weyl elements it succeeds, the expression does not reduce to a small number of cases (except for
w_{n,1}
).

8.
Tidying Up

We hope the combination of the following methods would close the gaps in this differential equations-and-power series method for arbitrary Weyl elements on
GL(n)
.
That is, if there fails to be sufficient convergence, then there will be a corresponding
y
-coordinate for which smoothness and power-series-ness is obvious, while for the remaining coordinates, we can power-series expand by the rapid decay.

8.1.
The
w_{22}
hiccup

In this section, we prove the Analytic Continuation Conjecture for
K_{22}
.
To that end, we reintroduce the notation
K_{22}(y,\Lambda,\mu,\delta)
.
The proof of the Weak Interchange of Integrals implies that, for each
\Lambda
,
K_{22}(y,\Lambda,\mu,\delta)
is holomorphic in a tube domain containing
i\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{0}(\Lambda)
.
We also know that
K_{22}(y,\Lambda,\mu,\delta)
satisfies the differential equations of
section

4.2
.
The Asymptotics Theorem tells us that
K_{22}(y,0,\mu,\delta)
is a particular linear combination of the power series solutions, which gives it an expression which is meromorphic on all of
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{4}
.
We need to show then that
K_{22}(y,\Lambda,\mu,\delta)
agrees with this extension of
K_{22}(y,0,\mu,\delta)
to
i\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{0}(\Lambda)
.

Note that we have two cases to consider:
The first case, call it Case I, is
\Lambda=(\frac{k_{1}-1}{2},-\frac{k_{1}-1}{2},0,0)
,
\mu-\Lambda=(r_{1},r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})
with
2\leq k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
,
r\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
,
2r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}=0
and
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\equiv k_{1}\pmod{2}
.
The second case, call it Case II, is
\Lambda=(\frac{k_{1}-1}{2},-\frac{k_{1}-1}{2},\frac{k_{2}-1}{2},-\frac{k_{2}-1}{2})
,
\mu-\Lambda=(r_{1},r_{1},-r_{1},-r_{1})
with
2\leq k_{2}\leq k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
,
r_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\equiv k_{1}\pmod{2}
,
\delta_{3}+\delta_{4}\equiv k_{2}\pmod{2}
.

We have only considered the differential equations for
K_{22}
in the case
\Lambda=0
, but their extension to
\Lambda\neq 0
follows the typical path for the method of Frobenius.
A set of representatives for
W/W_{w}
is given by
\mathopen{}\left\{I,w_{(1\,3)},w_{(2\,3)},w_{(1\,4)},w_{(2\,4)},w_{(1\,3)(2\,4)}\right\}\mathclose{}
.
In Case I, we have
J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(1\,3)}})={\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{1}-1}J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(2\,3)}})
and
J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(1\,4)}})={\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{1}-1}J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(2\,4)}})
, and we can see the remaining two solutions are

\displaystyle Y_{22,1}(y,\mu):=

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\to k_{1}-1}\frac{J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(1\,3)}})-{\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{1}-1}J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(2\,3)}})}{\sin\pi(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})},

\displaystyle Y_{22,2}(y,\mu):=

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\to k_{1}-1}\frac{J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(1\,4)}})-{\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{1}-1}J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(2\,4)}})}{\sin\pi(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})}.

In Case II, we furthermore have
J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(2\,4)}})={\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{2}-1}J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(2\,3)}})
and
Y_{22,2}(y,\mu)={\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{2}-1}Y_{22,1}(y,\mu)
, and the remaining solutions are

\displaystyle Y_{22,3}(y,\mu):=

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu_{3}-\mu_{4}\to k_{2}-1}\frac{J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(2\,4)}})-{\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{2}-1}J_{22}(y,\mu^{w_{(2\,3)}})}{\sin\pi(\mu_{3}-\mu_{4})},

\displaystyle Y_{22,4}(y,\mu):=

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu_{3}-\mu_{4}\to k_{2}-1}\frac{Y_{22,2}(y,\mu)-{\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{2}-1}Y_{22,1}(y,\mu)}{\sin\pi(\mu_{3}-\mu_{4})}.

One could conceive that further degeneracy of the
J_{22}
functions occurs at
d_{1}=d_{2}
or
d_{1}=2d_{2}
, but it does not.

Note: If the reader is unsatisfied with the limit notation above, set, e.g.,
\mu_{z}:=(\frac{z}{2}+r_{1},-\frac{z}{2}+r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})
and we mean

\displaystyle Y_{22,1}(y,(\tfrac{k_{1}-1}{2}+r_{1},-\tfrac{k_{1}-1}{2}+r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}))

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{k_{1}-1}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(J_{22}(y,\mu_{z}^{w_{(1\,3)}})-{\mathop{\,sgn}}(y_{2})^{k_{1}-1}J_{22}(y,\mu_{z}^{w_{(2\,3)}})\right)\right|_{z=k_{1}-1}.

So we have verified the space of solutions to the
w_{22}
differential equation is spanned by the Frobenius series solutions.
The analysis of the Mellin-Barnes integrals for the Whittaker function in
[
13
, Sect. 9.2]
shows that the Whittaker functions are also linear combinations Frobenius series with leading terms
I_{\mu^{w},\delta^{w}}
.
In particular, this holds at
{\mathop{Re}}(\mu)=0
and for the Whittaker function, the higher-weight case
{\mathop{Re}}(\mu)=\Lambda\neq 0
is, in fact, obtained by analytic continuation.
In combination with
[
13
, eq. (52)]
, this implies the result, as in the proof of the Asymptotics Theorem in
[
13
, Sect. 11]
.
The reader should be concerned about convergence of the
\overline{U}_{22}(\mathbb{R})
integral, but actually, we may safely expand both
e\left(y_{2}^{*}x_{4}\right)
and the Whittaker function (considered as a function of

alone) into power/Frobenius series because the higher terms actually make the
\overline{U}_{22}(\mathbb{R})

8.2.
The
w_{211}
hiccup

Though it would be nice to prove something like Hashizume’s results
[
19
]
for Bessel functions of arbitrary Weyl elements on
GL(n)
, the author was unable to force through any ellipticity arguments, and we now give a concrete method through which one may argue that the Bessel function lies in the span of the Frobenius series solutions for the Weyl element
w_{211}
.

First, note that
K_{211}(y,\mu,\delta)
is a scalar-valued function satisfying the differential equations (
67
) and (
68
), and we can only talk about the Bessel functions on representations/at
K
-types for which the Whittaker function is not identically zero.
If (the matrix-valued Whittaker function)
W_{\sigma}(t,\mu,\delta)
is not identically zero take
i,j
so that the entry
W_{\sigma,i,j}(t,\mu,\delta)
is not identically zero.
Then the Mellin-Barnes integral
[
13
, eq. (49)]
implies a rapidly convergent Frobenius series expansion which implies in turn holomorphy in
t
, so we can take
t
such that the
Y

w_{211}tw_{211}^{-1}
are arbitrarily small and still
W_{\sigma,i,j}(t,\mu,\delta)\neq 0
, and we get

\displaystyle K_{211}(y,\mu,\delta)=

\displaystyle\frac{I_{0,\delta}(yw_{211}tw_{211})}{W_{\sigma,i,j}(t,\mu,\delta)}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\int_{{\mathop{Re}}(s)=\epsilon}\widehat{W}_{\sigma,i,k}(s,\mu,\delta)F_{\sigma,k,j}(yw_{211}tw_{211},t,s,\mu,\delta)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{\ell=1}^{3}(yw_{211}tw_{211})_{\ell}^{-s_{\ell}}\frac{ds}{(2\pi i)^{3}},

where

F_{\sigma,k,j}(y,t,s,\mu,\delta)=\int_{\overline{U}_{211}(\mathbb{R})}\overline{\psi_{t}(u)}\psi_{yw_{211}tw_{211}^{-1}}(x^{*})\sigma_{k,j}(k^{*})I_{0,\delta}(y^{*})\prod_{\ell=1}^{3}(y^{*}_{\ell})^{-s_{\ell}}du,\quad x^{*}y^{*}k^{*}=wu,

formally, but also actually if we were to hold the
u
integral outside and include the
\mu
integral between the
u
and
s
integrals.

We will derive a Mellin expansion of
F_{\sigma,k,j}
, justified by the absolute convergence of the original
u-\mu-s
integral and the rapid convergence of (
51
) for
\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}
, that can be used to shift the contours (in the Mellin expansion of
K_{211}
) to
-\infty
.
Without worrying about the particular locations of the poles of
\widehat{W}_{\sigma,i,k}
and
\widehat{F}_{\sigma,k,j}
, it’s enough to know that they occur along finitely many horizontal lines in each coordinate, so the contour shifting produces some linear combination of Frobenius series.

Now
\sigma_{k,j}
is a trigonometric polynomial in the hyper-spherical coordinates (see
[
13
, Sect. 6.1]
), so after applying the Iwasawa decomposition of
section

3.1
and expanding, we see that

is a linear combination of terms

\displaystyle F_{m}^{*}(y,t,u):=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{5}}e\Biggl(-t_{1}x_{1}-\frac{t_{2}x_{1}x_{2}}{\sqrt{1+x_{1}^{2}}}-\frac{t_{2}x_{3}\sqrt{1+x_{2}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+x_{1}^{2}}}-\frac{y_{1}x_{2}x_{4}}{\sqrt{1+x_{2}^{2}}}+\frac{y_{3}x_{2}x_{3}}{\left(1+x_{1}^{2}\right)\sqrt{1+x_{2}^{2}}}-\frac{y_{1}x_{3}x_{5}\sqrt{1+x_{4}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+x_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{1+x_{3}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{\ell=1}^{5}\left(1+x_{\ell}^{2}\right)^{\frac{-1+u_{\ell}}{2}}\left(\frac{1+ix_{\ell}}{\sqrt{1+x_{\ell}^{2}}}\right)^{m_{\ell}}dx,

for some

\displaystyle-s_{2}+2s_{3}-\mu_{3}+\mu_{4},

\displaystyle u_{2}=

\displaystyle s_{1}+s_{3}-\mu_{1}+\mu_{4},

\displaystyle u_{3}=

\displaystyle s_{1}+s_{2}-s_{3}-\mu_{1}+\mu_{3},

\displaystyle u_{4}=

\displaystyle-s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}-\mu_{2}+\mu_{4},

\displaystyle u_{5}=

\displaystyle-s_{1}+2s_{2}-s_{3}-\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}.

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\chi_{a-1}^{\delta}(x)\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{-\frac{b+a}{2}}\left(\frac{1+ix}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\right)^{m}dx

\displaystyle q_{1}=

\displaystyle 1+m_{11}-v_{2},

\displaystyle q_{2}=

\displaystyle m_{12}+v_{1}-v_{2}-2v_{3}-2v_{4},

\displaystyle q_{3}=

\displaystyle 1+m_{21}-v_{3}

\displaystyle q_{4}=

\displaystyle m_{22}-u_{2}-v_{4},

\displaystyle q_{5}=

\displaystyle 1+m_{31}-v_{3}

\displaystyle q_{6}=

\displaystyle m_{32}-u_{3}-v_{1}+v_{3}+v_{4},

\displaystyle q_{7}=

\displaystyle 1+m_{41}-v_{4}

\displaystyle q_{8}=

\displaystyle m_{42}-u_{4}-v_{1},

\displaystyle q_{9}=

\displaystyle 1+m_{51}-v_{1}-v_{4}

\displaystyle q_{10}=

\displaystyle m_{52}-u_{5}-v_{1}+v_{4},

and each

m_{\ell,1}+m_{\ell,2}=\mathopen{}\left|m_{\ell}\right|\mathclose{}
.

Recall the terminology following (
44

Away from its poles,
F_{\eta}^{\sharp}
has exponential convergence due to the exponential decay of the beta functions
B(a-r,b+r)
in the region
\mathopen{}\left|{\mathop{Im}}(r-\frac{a-b}{2})\right|\mathclose{}<\mathopen{}\left|{\mathop{Im}}(\frac{a+b}{2})\right|\mathclose{}

Shifting the

y_{1}
and localizes the integral near
{\mathop{Im}}(r)\approx 0
, so that
F_{\eta}^{\sharp}
is essentially bounded by the polynomial part of the beta functions at
{\mathop{Im}}(r)=0
, plus the residues picked up from the beta functions, which are also polynomial in
q
and for which an analysis similar to the following applies.

For
{\mathop{Re}}(r)
highly negative and
{\mathop{Im}}(r)\approx 0
, the polynomial parts of the beta functions want to localize the

{\mathop{Im}}(q_{2j})=0
,

, while the odd indexed

Treating
u
as fixed for the moment, the combined effect, including the decay of the factors

for

is to localize near
{\mathop{Im}}(v)\approx 0
.

Lastly, the

\widehat{W}_{\sigma,i,k}
in each
\mathopen{}\left|{\mathop{Im}}(s_{j})\right|\mathclose{}\gg 1
, treating
\mu
as fixed, so our earlier assumption that

is essentially fixed is valid (as there is no exponential growth coming from

So we may shift the
s
,
v
and
r
contours to the left.
The factorial parts of the gamma functions in

and
F_{m,\eta,\eta^{\prime}}^{\dagger}
are either balanced (some of the beta functions) or induce factorial-type decay (the other beta functions and all of the
G_{\delta}
functions), and the factorial part of
\widehat{W}_{\sigma,i,k}
always induces factorial-type decay.
Hence we may shift the
s
,
v
and
r
contours to
-\infty
and conclude that
K_{211}(y,\mu,\delta)
is a sum of Frobenius series.

Appendix A
Mathematica Code for The Interchange of Integrals Algorithm

Here we provide the code to implement the algorithm of
section

7.4
as described in the text; the code has been formatted for direct copy-and-paste, aside from the page breaks.

The functions xforms, formIndex, logFormExponent, exprToIndices, phiToIndices, indicesToPhi are all concerned with converting human-readable input to the format required by the algorithm and vice versa.
The algorithm itself is comprised of initialCase, applyBKYLemma, listAllCases with supporting functions logCoefs, dervLogCoefs, exprDerv, doDerv, intyStarToLogs, yStarToLogs, hasIntersection, intMightBeSmall, intMightBeSmallDerv, mightBeSmall, and smallsSub.
The function doReduceAll optimizes the reduction of the Boolean expression with helpers indexFrequencies, doSimplify, toOrList and doReduce.
Finally, DoTest packages the algorithm into a simple format with assistance from properSubsets, testAllCases, printAllCases and makeVars.

{AllTrue[Join[largeDerv, smallDerv], # <= 0 &]}]
