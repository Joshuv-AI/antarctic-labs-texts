Modulating intermolecular interactions and high-order triplet states of anthracene host materials for efficient TTA-UC with a Naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole scaffold and its N-substituents
Authors: JiangXue Pei, BoHua Zhang, YangYi Li, ChaoBo Hao, Yi Chen, QingYu Jia, Hua Dong, Bo Jiao, ZhaoXin Wu, DongDong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Three naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole (NDB)-functionalized anthracene host materials (Ph-NDB, Np-NDB, and DBF-NDB) were designed and synthesized. The NDB skeleton and variable N-atom substituents synergistically modulate intermolecular interactions and higher-order triplet energy levels. Theoretical and photophysical investigations reveal that the S1 and T1 states of the three compounds are localized on the anthracene core, while the T2 state is dominated by the NDB skeleton. The T3 state exhibits distinct substituent-dependent characteristics: in Np-NDB, it is exclusively governed by naphthyl groups on N-atoms, whereas in Ph-NDB and DBF-NDB, it is co-regulated by the anthracene core and phenyl groups on N-atoms. As a result, Np-NDB exhibits stronger TTA-UC capabilities than the reference sample DPA and other compounds. The three compounds possess bipolar charge transport properties and excellent thermal stability, with melting temperatures above 300 °C. An Np-NDB-based blue OLED achieves a maximum EQE of 8.00%, surpassing the commercial anthracene-based host BH (7.55%). This work clarifies the unique modulation mechanism of NDB skeletons and N-substituents toward high-order triplet states and intermolecular interactions, providing a feasible molecular design strategy for developing high-efficiency TTA-UC host materials for blue OLEDs.

Introduction

Blue organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) constitute a core component of high-resolution full-color displays. However, blue OLEDs still face long-standing bottlenecks associated with low internal quantum efficiency (IQE) and stability. Phosphorescent blue OLEDs, represented by iridium(iii)1–4 and Pt(ii) complexes,5–7 theoretically achieve 100% IQE by harvesting both singlet and triplet excitons, but they suffer from short device lifetimes due to high-energy excited states inducing bond dissociation and long-lived triplet excitons causing severe triplet–triplet annihilation (TTA) and triplet-polaron quenching. Thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) materials,8–10 especially multi-resonance TADF emitters,11–15 have emerged as metal-free alternatives to promote the rapid development of narrowband blue OLEDs. However, the severe efficiency roll-off at practical luminance levels means that they are still unable to meet the lifespan requirements for industry. Alternatively, triplet–triplet annihilation upconversion (TTA-UC) blue devices employing anthracene-based hosts combined with fluorescent emitters have been widely adopted in industrial panels, as they can theoretically boost the IQE to 62.5% by converting two non-radiative triplet excitons into one radiative singlet exciton, offering balanced efficiency and stability.16–19 Even so, industrial anthracene-based TTA-UC devices exhibit limited external quantum efficiencies (EQEs) and lifespans, with EQEs typically remaining ≤ 9% in most reported systems with narrowband emission. This performance bottleneck is primarily attributed to low TTA-UC efficiency and triplet–triplet annihilation in the TTA-UC components, thereby limiting the overall exciton utilization. Therefore, the rational molecular design of anthracene-based TTA-UC hosts is continuously required to improve the overall performance of blue OLEDs.

For TTA-UC blue host design, optimization of the energy level alignment of excited states and upconversion dynamics is critically important for enhancing TTA-UC efficiency. If the energy levels of an anthracene-based derivative are in the order of S1(host) < 2T1(host) <T2(host), Q1(host), the exciton utilization efficiency (EUE) can theoretically be as high as 62.5%; however, for most anthracene-based derivatives, the energy levels are typically in the order of S1(host), T2(host) < 2T1(host) <Q1(host) and, thus, EUE is theoretically limited to 40%.20,21 Yet the energy gap between T2/Tn and T1 can also be sufficiently large to suppress fast non-radiative internal conversion between T2/Tn and T1, enabling additional “hot-exciton” reverse intersystem crossing (RISC) paths to the emissive S1 state, thereby improving the EUE. For example, in the reported compound 2M-pCzAnBzt (4-(10-(4-(9H-carbazol-9-yl)-2,5-dimethylphenyl)anthracen-9-yl)benzonitrile), TTA synergizing with hot-exciton RISC enables an OLED to achieve an IQE of 72% and EQEmax of 10.44% (CIE: 0.151, 0.057).22 Furthermore, inhibiting excimer formation and maintaining the necessary intermolecular TTA activity are equally important for designing anthracene-based blue hosts.19 Theoretically, the intermolecular stacking distance of anthracene chromophores must be controlled within the range of 5–10 Å because a distance less than 5 Å will cause excimer formation of triplet pairs before annihilation, resulting in reduced upconversion efficiency, while a distance exceeding 10 Å will lead to the failure of the TTA-UC process.23 Despite these advances, simultaneously achieving a balanced stacking distance and optimized energy levels remains a key design challenge.

Recently, 1,3,2-diazaborole and its derivatives have attracted extensive attention in the field of organic optoelectronic materials due to their unique electronic structures, in which the vacant pz orbital of the boron atom and the electron-donating effect of the nitrogen atom endow these compounds with adjustable frontier molecular orbital energy levels, high triplet energy, excellent luminescence performance and good charge transport capacity.24–27 In addition, the B–N bond length (∼1.42 Å) is between single and double bonds, imparting high rigidity and thermal stability. The properties of 1,3,2-diazaborole derivatives can be finely adjusted by introducing different substituents (such as aryl, electron-withdrawing, or electron-donating groups) on the nitrogen atoms or the boron atom. For example, a series of bis-1,3,2-diazaborole derivatives connected with phenyl28 and thiophene/dithiophene has been synthesized,29 and these emit bright blue light under UV irradiation. 1,3,2-benzodiazaboroles (BDBs) formed by fusing 1,3,2-diazaborole to benzene substituted with perfluoroaryl30 or carbazole building blocks31 also show intense blue luminescence. These results have shown the great application potential of 1,3,2-diazaborole derivatives in the field of organic optoelectronic materials.

Encouraged by these results, 1,3,2-diazaborole is fused with naphthalene to afford the building unit of 2,3-dihydro-1H-naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole (NDB), which possesses a stable 14π-electron conjugated system, and the extended conjugated structure is expected to enhance the charge transport capacity and luminescence stability in NDB compared with BDBs in some scenarios. Then, we designed a series of host materials with TTA-UC features by combining an anthracene core with the NDB unit. Here, the anthracene moiety is selected as the key functional unit for TTA-UC due to its high fluorescence quantum yield, suitable triplet energy level (ET∼ 1.7 eV) and excellent TTA-UC abilities, which are essential for the formation and annihilation of triplet excitons. The NDB core is introduced as the molecular-stacking- and excited-state-regulating unit, which is conducive to optimizing the frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs), energy level structure, and stacking distance of the anthracene core. The boron atom of the NDB core with a vacant pz orbital can tune the electron density of the anthracene moiety through reasonable B–C linking modes, and appropriate substituent modification on N atoms can carefully optimize the twist angle between the anthracene core and NDB plane to ensure an efficient TTA-UC process, as well as adjusting the HOMO and LUMO of the target compound to match the energy levels of adjacent device layers. Based on the considerations above, three compounds named 1,3-diphenyl-2-(10-phenylanthracen-9-yl)-2,3-dihydro-1H-naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole (Ph-NDB), 1,3-di(naphthalen-1-yl)-2-(10-phenylanthracen-9-yl)-2,3-dihydro-1H-naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole (Np-NDB), and 2-(10-(dibenzo[b,d]furan-4-yl)anthracen-9-yl)-1,3-diphenyl-2,3-dihydro-1H-naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole (DBF-NDB) were designed and synthesized, as shown in Fig. 1. Among the three compounds, Np-NDB shows the highest TTA-UC abilities and more balanced hole and electron transport. As a result, a device based on this compound as a host combined with a commercial narrowband blue dopant shows the best EQE of 8.00%, with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 23 nm, better than that of a commercial AND-based host (7.55%). This work revealed that NDB can serve as a potential building unit for developing blue hosts with tunable energy levels, high fluorescence quantum yields, and good charge transport properties.

Results and discussion

Synthesis, thermal properties, and electrochemical properties

Scheme 1 illustrates the synthetic routes to the three target compounds. Specifically, NDB derivatives were constructed from naphthalene-2,3-diamine precursors through nitrogen-directed electrophilic borylation. Commercially available (10-phenylanthracen-9-yl)boronic acid and (10-bromoanthracen-9-yl)boronic acid underwent intermolecular dehydration with naphthalene-2,3-diamine, efficiently affording the key precursors 2,3-dihydro-1H-naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole (1a) and 2-(10-bromoanthracen-9-yl)-2,3-dihydro-1H-naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole (1b) in high yields. Subsequent N-arylation of 1a with phenyl and naphthyl groups via the Ullmann reaction delivered Ph-NDB and Np-NDB, respectively. As for DBF-NDB, intermediate 2b was first synthesized by the Suzuki coupling of 1b with 2-(dibenzo[b,d]furan-4-yl)-4,4,5,5-tetramethyl-1,3,2-dioxaborolane. The target molecule DBF-NDB was finally obtained by subjecting intermediate 2b to an identical Ullmann arylation procedure. All synthesized compounds were fully characterized by 1H NMR, 13C NMR and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) to verify their chemical structures. The detailed synthesis procedures are provided in the SI.

The electrochemical properties of these compounds were investigated via cyclic voltammetry (CV) (Fig. S1 and Table 1). As observed in the CV profiles, all compounds exhibit irreversible redox waves during the anodic scan. From the half-wave onset potentials derived from the CV curves, the HOMO energy levels of compounds 1–3 were determined to be −5.61, −5.63, and −5.61 eV, respectively. Combined with the optical band gaps obtained from UV-vis absorption spectra in toluene, the corresponding LUMO energy levels of compounds 1–3 were calculated to be −2.54, −2.57, and −2.56 eV, respectively.

The thermal properties of compounds 1–3 were characterized using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), with the corresponding curves shown in Fig. S2. All compounds display high 5% weight-loss temperatures (Td), with values of 394, 434, and 407 °C for compounds 1–3. Furthermore, glass transition temperatures (Tg) were not observed for compounds 1–3. However, the three compounds show significantly high melting temperatures (Tm), with Tm values of 335, 361, and 307 °C, indicating strong intermolecular interactions. Clearly, these compounds possess excellent thermal and morphological stability.

Calculation analysis

Theoretical calculations were performed using density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level. The optimized molecular geometries and relevant geometric parameters are provided in the SI. The optimized ground structures, HOMOs (highest occupied molecular orbitals) and LUMOs (lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals) of Ph-NDB, Np-NDB, and DBF-NDB and their energy levels are shown in Fig. S3. When the substituents of N atoms are phenyl groups, the twist angle between the anthracene core and the BDB plane is 75°, and as the substituent switches to naphthyl from phenyl, the angle increases to 80° for Np-NDB, yielding larger steric hindrance. For all three compounds, their LUMO orbitals are exclusively localized at the anthracene core. In contrast, the HOMO distributions are significantly modulated by the N-substituted groups of the NDB unit, resulting in differentiated electronic distribution features among the different molecules. For Np-NDB with naphthyl modification on the NDB nitrogen atom, the HOMO is fully confined to the anthracene core, endowing the molecule with typical localized excitation (LE)-state characteristics. Differently, Ph-NDB and DBF-NDB with phenyl-based substituents at the N atoms present hybrid orbital features, where their HOMOs are jointly contributed to by the NDB functional unit and anthracene core, forming a partially delocalized orbital distribution. Due to their consistent HOMO/LUMO electron distributions, Ph-NDB and DBF-NDB possess nearly identical HOMO and LUMO energy values. Np-NDB with 1-naphthyl substitution exhibits an overall shift of the HOMO and LUMO energy levels toward the vacuum level. Despite this, the three compounds show similar HOMO–LUMO energy gaps, ranging from 3.45 to 3.47 eV. This is because their HOMOs and LUMOs are contributed to by similar units.

To further explore the excited-state energy level arrangements, excited-state energy levels were also calculated by TD-DFT calculations, where all the computed S1 and T1–T4 energies correspond to vertical excitation energies obtained from the optimized S0 ground-state geometries, and the corresponding results are summarized in Fig. 2a. The excited-state energy level arrangements of the three compounds all follow the rule of S1, T2 < 2T1, which is consistent with the reported energy level characteristics of anthracene derivatives.32 Np-NDB exhibits unique high-order triplet-state energy level advantages. Its energy difference between T4 and T3 reaches 0.39 eV, a sufficiently large gap that suppresses rapid non-radiative internal conversion loss from T4 → T3. This characteristic presents the possibility for the T4 → S1 high-order reverse intersystem crossing (RISC) process. Natural transition orbital (NTO) analysis was further adopted to clarify the electronic transition nature and excited-state attributes of singlet and triplet excited states, as illustrated in Fig. 2b. For the three compounds, the hole and electron orbitals of the lowest singlet excited state (S1) and lowest triplet excited state (T1) are completely localized on the anthracene ring, verifying that both the S1 and T1 states are anthracene-dominated LE states. Meanwhile, the T2-state orbitals of these molecules are entirely distributed on the NDB unit, demonstrating that the T2 state is an NDB-centered triplet excited state. These results indicate that structural modification of the NDB heterocyclic block can precisely regulate the T2 energy level and directionally modulate the second-order triplet excited state. The T3 excited-state orbitals show strong structural dependence and substituent-modulated effects across different molecules. For Np-NDB, the T3-state hole and electron orbitals are mainly contributed to by the naphthyl ring substituted on the N atom, confirming that the N-aryl substitution structure is the key factor controlling the T3 energy level. For Ph-NDB and DBF-NDB with phenyl-substituted N atoms, the T3 state exhibits obvious hole–electron separation characteristics: the hole orbital is weakly contributed to by the NDB and anthracene units, while the electron orbital is fully localized on the anthracene core, presenting prominent charge transfer (CT) excitation properties. This proves that the T3 energy levels of phenyl-modified molecules are synergistically regulated by the anthracene luminescent core and NDB unit. Clearly, precise regulation of the N-atom substituents and 1,3,2-diazaborole fused ring skeleton can effectively modulate the high-order triplet energy level distribution, adjust the relaxation path of high-order triplet excitons, and controllably regulate the high-order RISC process.

Photophysical properties

Fig. 3 shows ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption and photoluminescence (PL) spectra of the anthracene-naphtho[2,3-d][1,3,2]diazaborole derivatives in toluene solution (1.0 × 10−5 mol L−1). The absorption spectra of all the compounds show the same vibrational structure, which is attributed to vertical transitions of the anthracene moiety. This reveals that the perpendicular NDB unit exerts negligible impact on the ground-state structure. The experimental HOMO–LUMO gaps (Eg) were nearly the same for the three compounds, about 3.05–3.07 eV. Similarly, their PL spectra exhibit a peak wavelength range of 415–435 nm with the same spectral profiles in toluene, indicating their emission from LE states of the AND core uniquely. Furthermore, upon increasing the solvent polarity by changing from toluene to tetrahydrofuran, the spectral shapes are unchanged, further supporting the LE feature of singlet states, as shown in Fig. 3b–d. This can be supported by the calculated NTOs for these compounds, in which both the electron and hole distributions of the S1 states were uniquely localized on the AND core with typical LE characteristics. Switching to pure solid film (see SI Fig. S4), the three compounds show similar blue emission centered at ∼430 nm, which is exclusively derived from the intrinsic emission of the anthracene core. All these experimentally and theoretically calculated results indicate that the large steric hindrance of substituents on N atoms disrupts the π-conjugation between the NDB core and the AND unit, and, as a result, the S1 states are determined by the AND unit.

UV spectra (a), PL spectra (b–d), transient PL spectra (e), and TTA-UC spectra (f) of Ph-NDB, Np-NDB, and DBF-NDB.

All of these compounds are strongly emissive in solution. The PL quantum yields (PLQYs) of Ph-NDB, Np-NDB, and DBF-NDB in toluene solution are 0.76, 0.72, and 0.63, respectively. These values are comparable to commercial AND-based host materials.33

One of our goals is to focus on investigating how molecular design affects TTA-UC (UCQY). Therefore, UCQY was measured via the method of relative actinometry according to eqn (1):191where ϕ is UCQY, A represents the absorbance at the excitation wavelength, F represents the integrated emission intensity, and η represents the refractive index. The subscripts STD and UC denote the reference standard and upconversion sample, respectively. Herein, DPA (9,10-diphenylanthracene) is selected as a reference, as it is extensively utilized in TTA-UC research with a reported value of 0.17. (ref. 34) The concentrations of the annihilator and triplet sensitizer PtOEP (platinum octaethylporphyrin) are fixed at 0.10 mM and 0.10 mM, respectively, for every upconversion sample, and samples were illuminated with a 532 nm xenon lamp from an FLS980 spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments). The measured PL spectra are shown in Fig. 3f. Given the identical PtOEP concentration and solvent, the A and η values for all the samples are assumed to be identical. Thus, the UCQYs for the three samples are calculated relative to DPA (0.17). Among all the samples, the compound Np-NDB exhibits the most optimal TTA-UC performance, and it presents an impressive value of 0.28, superior to all the measured samples, including DPA (0.17) and the other two compounds. In addition, the compound Ph-NDB shows a slightly better UCQY (0.26) than that of the reference material DPA. In contrast, DBF-NDB shows the poorest UCQY among the three compounds, with an UCQY of only 0.15. Given that both Ph-NDB and Np-NDB have identical benzene substituents at the 9-position of the anthracene ring but different N-substituents in the NDB unit, the highest UCQY of Np-NDB is presumably related to the larger steric hindrance of naphthyl. This steric hindrance provides an appropriate π–π distance for the anthracene chromophore, resulting in the most efficient TTA-UC. These results clearly illustrate that incorporating the NDB unit with AND and precisely regulating the substituents on the N-atoms and at the 9-position of AND are crucially important for enhancing upconversion efficiency.

Single-carrier devices

For host applications, the charge carrier transport properties of films are crucial for device performance. The charge-transporting capacities of the three materials were evaluated using single-carrier devices. The structures for electron-only and hole-only devices are ITO/TmPyPB (10 nm)/host (80 nm)/TmPyPB (5 nm)/LiF (1 nm)/Al (80 nm) and ITO/HAT-CN (5 nm)/TAPC (5 nm)/host (80 nm)/TAPC (10 nm)/Al (80 nm), respectively. The J–V curves of hole-only and electron-only devices are shown in Fig. 4. All three compounds exhibit efficient electron and hole transport capabilities, demonstrating typical bipolar charge transport features. Moreover, Ph-NDB possesses superior electron transport abilities to Np-NDB, while Np-NDB shows better hole transport performance than Ph-NDB. To quantitatively assess the carrier transport rates, the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) model was adopted to fit the test curves, and the fitting results are displayed in Fig. S5. The fitted electron mobilities of Ph-NDB, Np-NDB, and DBF-NDB are 1.71 × 10−5, 2.62 × 10−5, and 2.05 × 10−5 cm V−1 s−1, respectively, while their corresponding hole mobilities are 8.36 × 10−7, 2.32 × 10−7, and 4.67 × 10−7 cm V−1 s−1. The mobility data further verify the bipolar transport nature of the three compounds, which can balance electron and hole transport rates within the emitting layer. Notably, Np-NDB exhibits the most balanced bipolar transport behavior, with the closest electron and hole mobility values among the three materials. It is expected that this endows it with promising electroluminescence (EL) performance as a host material. Here, by introducing NDB and appropriate substituents on N-atoms, compounds with balanced electron and hole transport were realized.

The electroluminescence performance

To evaluate the host–guest matching and electroluminescence performance of the three materials, OLED devices were fabricated using Ph-NDB, Np-NDB, and DBF-NDB as host materials and the commercial blue fluorescent dye BD (460 nm) with a narrow full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 22 nm as the dopant. The devices adopted the structure of ITO/HAT-CN (5 nm)/TAPC (40 nm)/TCTA (5 nm)/EML (20 nm)/TPBi (40 nm)/LiF (1 nm)/Al (80 nm), and an energy level diagram and the molecular structures of the functional layers are illustrated in Fig. 5. In this architecture, HAT-CN (dipyrazino[2,3-f:2′,3′-h]quinoxaline-2,3,6,7,10,11-hexacarbonitrile) and LiF are hole/electron injection layer (HIL) materials, TAPC (4,4′–(cyclohexane-1,1-diyl)bis(N,N-di-p-tolylaniline)) and TmPyPB (3,3′–(5′–(3-(pyridin-3-yl)phenyl)–[1,1′:3′,1″-terphenyl]-3,3″-diyl)dipyridine) function as hole/electron transport layer (HTL) materials, and TCTA (tris(4-(9H-carbazol-9-yl)phenyl)amine) is a hole-transporting and exciton-blocking material. Devices with BD doping concentrations of 3, 5, 7 and 9 wt% were prepared, and the 3 wt% doping system delivered the optimal device performance, with detailed parameters summarized in Fig. 5 and Table 2. Additionally, an OLED based on a commercial AND-based host (denoted as BH) was fabricated as a reference for comparison, and its EL performance is provided in Fig. S6.

All devices emitted blue light originating from BD, with emission peaks in the range of 460 nm and a FWHM of 23 nm (Fig. 5c). No host- or exciplex-related emission peaks were detected in the voltage range of 3–13 V. This indicates complete energy transfer from the host material to the BD guest during device operation. The four devices present CIE coordinates of (0.138, 0.099), (0.134, 0.102), (0.142, 0.096) and (0.144, 0.078), respectively, with turn-on voltages of 3.5 V, 3.5 V, 4.5 V and 3.5 V for Ph-NDB, Np-NDB, DBF-NDB and BH-based devices, respectively. Detailed device performance data are summarized in Table 2.

Among the three hosts, the device using Np-NDB as the host achieved the most favourable performance, exhibiting a maximum external quantum efficiency (EQEmax) of 8.00%, along with a maximum current efficiency (CEmax) of 5.30 cd A−1 and a maximum power efficiency (PEmax) of 5.90 lm W−1. These values are slightly higher than those of the commercial host BH (EQE: 7.55%, CEmax: 5.50 cd A−1, PEmax: 2.62 lm W−1) and significantly superior to those of the Ph-NDB host (EQE: 6.21%, CEmax: 4.50 cd A−1, PEmax: 2.62 lm W−1). Evidently, the substituents on the N-atoms play a crucial role in regulating device performance. As discussed earlier, the naphthyl group in Np-NDB possesses greater steric hindrance than the phenyl group in Ph-NDB, and this large steric hindrance completely disrupts the conjugation between the AND and BDB cores and ensures a sufficient π–π distance to facilitate efficient TTA-UC. This conclusion is supported by the measured higher UCQY of Np-NDB compared to Ph-NDB and DBF-NDB. Moreover, the good electron and hole transport balance further contributed to better device performance. In contrast, DBF-NDB exhibited the poorest device performance among the three compounds, with EQE of 5.49% and CEmax of 4.14 cd A−1. This inferior performance is presumably associated with its lower UCQY. Therefore, the substituent at the 9-position of the AND core is also critical in determining device performance. We propose that the dibenzo[b,d]furan group possesses larger steric hindrance than the phenyl group, expanding the π–π distance of the AND core, thereby reducing UCQY. In conclusion, these results demonstrate that both the N-substituent of NDB and the 9-position substituent of AND play key roles in regulating TTA-UC efficiency and overall device performance.

Conclusions

In this work, a series of anthracene-NDB hybrid blue host materials was successfully developed via rational molecular structural modulation, and their structure–optoelectronic performance relationships were systematically explored. The introduced NDB unit endows molecules with excellent thermal stability. Different substituents allow the controllable regulation of molecular photophysical properties. Naphthyl-substituted Np-NDB exhibits pure LE emission and an appropriate intermolecular stacking distance, realizing the most efficient TTA-UC behavior. By contrast, phenyl substitution on N atoms results in slightly inferior upconversion efficiency. Notably, the high-order triplet energy levels of these compounds can be well regulated by the NDB core and the substituents on N atoms. Electroluminescence tests verify that an Np-NDB-based OLED exhibits superior blue emission performance and higher EQE than commercial anthracene-based hosts, and this is attributed to its balanced bipolar charge transport and optimized triplet–triplet annihilation abilities. In conclusion, this work demonstrates that substituent modification and NDB heterocyclic fusion are efficient strategies to tune molecular stacking, excited-state properties, and charge transport. This provides a reliable reference for the design of high-efficiency, stable TTA-UC blue OLED host materials for display applications.
