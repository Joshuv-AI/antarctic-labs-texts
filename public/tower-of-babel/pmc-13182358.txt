Association between physical fitness and anthropometric indicators according to area of residence in Chilean adolescents: a cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundResearch has yielded contradictory results regarding differences in physical fitness and cardiometabolic risk between children and adolescents living in rural and urban areas.ObjectivesThe present study aimed to analyse the moderating role of area of residence on the association of physical fitness and anthropometric parameters in Chilean adolescents.Design and settingCross-sectional analysis of a nationally representative school-based sample from Chile.Participants and measuresA total of 7,833 adolescents with an average age of 15.8±0.7 years participated in both rural (n=759) and urban (n=7,074) settings. Physical fitness tests were evaluated using the Assessing Levels of Physical Activity and Fitness (ALPHA-Fitness) battery and anthropometric variables such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR). Generalised linear models with Gaussian distributions were constructed to estimate moderation models, with anthropometric-related variables as dependent variables, physical fitness variables as independent variables and area of residence as a moderator. Moderation analyses were conducted to examine whether the area of residence influences the association between physical fitness and anthropometric indicators (WC, WHtR and BMI).ResultsIn all models, place of residence did not moderate the potential associations between physical fitness and anthropometric indicators; for example, cardiorespiratory fitness with WC (B=0.13, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.31; 0=0.160), WHtR (B=0.08, 95% CI −0.03 to 0.20; p=0.143) or BMI (B=0.08, 95% CI −0.03 to 0.20; p=0.207).ConclusionsThese findings suggest the associations between physical fitness and anthropometric outcomes do not differ significantly between rural and urban adolescents.Keywords: Exercise, Cardiovascular Disease, Physical Fitness

Introduction

Childhood and adolescent obesity represent a major global public health concern with long-term adverse consequences.1 2 If current trends continue, the global number of overweight or obese children is expected to reach 70 million by 2025.3 Among the primary contributors to obesity are sedentary lifestyles and physical inactivity.4

Physical activity and fitness play critical roles in the prevention and development of obesity and cardiometabolic risk.5,7 Specifically, greater muscular fitness in adolescence has been prospectively associated with lower levels of adiposity and improved cardiometabolic profiles in adulthood.8 Likewise, higher levels of cardiorespiratory fitness during adolescence are linked to better health parameters across the lifespan.9 In this context, cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness have emerged as protective factors against chronic diseases, supporting the prioritisation of health-related fitness assessments in youth.10 11

Geographical disparities in health outcomes have been consistently documented in the literature, particularly in relation to obesity and cardiovascular disease risk among children and adolescents. Multiple studies have identified differences between rural and urban populations, with a general trend indicating a higher prevalence of obesity, sedentary behaviour and poor dietary habits in rural areas.12,14 This trend is often attributed to barriers such as limited access to recreational facilities, fewer organised sports programmes and lower health literacy in rural settings. However, the empirical evidence remains inconclusive. Some research, such as that by Xu et al,15 suggests that rural youth are more vulnerable to obesity and related health risks due to environmental and socioeconomic disadvantages. Conversely, studies from South America and parts of Asia have observed protective patterns in rural environments, where children may engage more in physical labour, have lower screen time and live in less obesogenic environments.16,18 For example, Nuotio et al19 reported that urban residents had more favourable cardiometabolic profiles, yet rural individuals exhibited healthier markers in certain subgroups, emphasising the complexity and context-dependence of these findings.

These inconsistencies likely stem from multifactorial and context-sensitive influences. Differences in the built environment such as the availability of parks, walkable neighbourhoods and safe spaces for recreation play a critical role in shaping health behaviours. Socioeconomic status (SES) further compounds this dynamic, as lower-income families in both urban and rural settings often face barriers to healthy nutrition and structured physical activity.20 21 In urban areas, adolescents may have greater access to fitness centres, trained physical education staff and extracurricular sports, but may also be exposed to high-density environments with limited green spaces and increased exposure to screen-based entertainment. On the other hand, rural adolescents may lack formalised infrastructure but benefit from open natural environments and lifestyles that encourage spontaneous physical movement.22 Studies such as those by Wattelez et al23 and Regis et al24 highlight how socioenvironmental factors including perceptions of safety, transportation access and cultural norms can shape activity patterns. Moreover, these patterns may be further influenced by food systems, availability of fresh produce and even air pollution levels, suggesting the need for a nuanced, multilevel approach to interpreting rural urban differences in adolescent health.

Despite an expanding body of literature linking physical fitness to improved cardiometabolic profiles, few studies especially in developing countries have explored whether the area of residence moderates this relationship. In Chile, a country with notable urban–rural inequalities, childhood obesity prevalence reached 23.9% in 201725 and cardiometabolic risk factors remain highly prevalent among children.16

Given these disparities and the potential role of the environment, it is necessary to examine whether the benefits of physical fitness on anthropometric parameters are dependent on the place of residence. Therefore, the present study aimed to analyse the moderating role of residential area (urban or rural) in the relationship between physical fitness and anthropometric indicators among Chilean adolescents. We hypothesise that the positive effects of physical fitness on cardiometabolic health are consistent across both urban and rural contexts, underscoring the need for inclusive health promotion strategies.

Methods

Participants

A total of 7,833 adolescents, with an average age of 15.8±0.7 years, from both rural (n=759) and urban (n=7,074) areas participated. The sample is part of a nationally representative study conducted by the Chilean Ministry of Education’s education quality agency to measure the quality of physical education (i.e., Sistema de Medición de la Calidad de la Educación Física [SIMCE-EFI]).25 For the development of the study, a simple stratified sampling by sex and region was carried out. The unequal distribution between urban and rural participants reflects the demographic structure of the Chilean adolescent population’s representativeness rather than equal subgroup sizes. Participants with missing data on key variables, including physical fitness measures, anthropometric indicators, area of residence, sex, age or SES, were excluded from the analytical sample. Patients and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plan of this research.

Evaluations

The evaluations were carried out in the educational establishments by teams of previously trained professionals. First, anthropometric evaluations were carried out, followed by physical condition evaluations.

Socio-demographic variables

Age, sex, area of residence, rural communes were defined as those in which more than 50% of the population lives in census districts with a population density of less than 150 inhabitants/km2. Urban communes were defined as those in which less than 25% of the population lives in census districts with a population density of less than 150 inhabitants/km2.26 SES is distributed into five categories: low, medium-low, medium, medium-high and high.27

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adolescents of both sexes aged 13–16 years were included if they had complete data for at least one physical fitness variable (maximal oxygen consumption or muscular strength) and at least one anthropometric variable (body mass index (BMI), WC or waist-to-height ratio [WHtR]). Only participants with valid records from the SIMCE-EFI assessments conducted by the Chilean Ministry of Education were considered. Participants with incomplete records, extreme outliers, inconsistencies in key variables or self-reported medical conditions that could limit physical activity were excluded from the analysis.

Missing data management

To ensure the quality of the analyses, an initial data-cleaning process was performed. All cases with missing data on key variables (physical fitness level, anthropometric indicators, area of residence, age, sex and SES) were excluded. No imputation techniques were applied, as the final sample size retained sufficient statistical power after case deletion. This approach was chosen to preserve the internal validity of the analyses.

Sample calculation

A complex two-stage sampling design was used, beginning with stratification of educational establishments by region and administrative jurisdiction. Subsequently, cluster sampling was applied, with each selected school serving as a unit and all students at that level evaluated. Anthropometric variables

In this study, BMI was calculated via the formula BMI=weight (kg)/height2 (m2).28 Z-scores were not calculated since the analyses were adjusted for age and sex to avoid collinearity issues. The WHtR was determined by dividing WC by height, which serves as an effective index for assessing cardiovascular risk in adolescents.29 WC, which is an indicator of obesity and cardiometabolic risk, was also evaluated.30

Physical fitness

The physical condition was evaluated with a battery proposed by the Ministry of Education of Chile. The tests evaluated were the abdominal strength endurance (Sit-ups test) in 1 min. Then, the horizontal jump (standing long jump (SLJ)) was evaluated, the push-ups were measured for 30 s and the flexibility was evaluated with the sit-and-reach test. Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) was assessed using the 20 m shuttle run test (Léger test) following the original protocol described by Léger et al31 CRF (mL/kg/min) was estimated from the final running speed achieved and expressed in mL/kg/min.26 Muscular fitness was analysed by individual test components rather than as a single composite score. This approach was chosen to preserve the physiological specificity of each test and to allow the identification of potentially distinct association patterns between different neuromuscular attributes and anthropometric or cardiometabolic indicators. Although composite fitness scores can provide a useful summary measure, analysing individual components reduces the risk of obscuring the association within the global index.

Statistical analysis

For descriptive data, categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Conversely, continuous variables are reported as the median and first quartile-third quartile. Generalised linear models with robust methods for Gaussian distributions were constructed to estimate moderation models using anthropometric-related variables as dependent variables (Y), physical fitness variables as independent variables (X) and area of residence as moderator variables (W). The theoretical model is represented as follows: Y=iY + B1X + B2W + eY, where B1 and B2 are estimated regression coefficients, eY is an estimation error and iY is a regression intercept. This model estimates Y (outcome) from two priors X (independent variable) and W (moderator variable). These models account heteroscedasticity and outliers.32 Preliminary analyses revealed no interaction when sex was included as a moderating variable (p>0.05 in all cases). Therefore, we performed the analyses without being divided by boys and girls. All analyses were adjusted for age, sex and SES. All the statistical analyses were executed via R statistical software (V.4.4.0) developed by the R Core Team in Vienna, Austria, and RStudio (V.2024.04.1 +748) from Posit in Boston, Massachusetts, USA. A significance level of p<0.05 was employed.

Results

Table 1 presents the descriptive characteristics of the study participants, segmented into the total sample (N=7,833), urban population (n=7,074) and rural population (n=759). The median age in overall categories was 15.8 years. In terms of sex, 55% of the participants were boys and 45% were girls, with a similar distribution between urban and rural areas. SES is distributed into five categories: low, medium-low, medium, medium-high and high. Most of the participants were at medium-low and medium levels, with a notable difference between urban and rural areas, where in the latter, the low level predominated. Overall, median CRF was 27.5 mL/kg/min in the total sample and did not differ according to area of residence, with identical median values observed in urban and rural participants. In terms of performance in physical fitness tests, the distance achieved in the SLJ shows similar values between the groups, with a median of approximately 145 cm in the urban population and 143.5 cm in the rural population. Participants performed a median of 15.3 push-ups and 25.4 sit-ups and reached approximately 30 cm in the sit-and-reach test. In addition, BMI, WC and WHtR values were homogeneous between urban and rural populations.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to analyse whether the area of residence (urban or rural) moderates the relationship between physical fitness and anthropometric indicators in Chilean adolescents. Contrary to reports in some international studies suggesting environmental disparities, our results showed no significant moderation effect of residential area. That is, the beneficial association between physical fitness and anthropometric outcomes was consistent across urban and rural settings. This suggests that the positive effects of physical fitness on obesity-related indicators are robust across different geographical contexts. These findings align with a growing body of literature suggesting that physical fitness is a protective factor for cardiometabolic health irrespective of socio-demographic or environmental conditions.9 33 34 In our study, adolescents who demonstrated higher levels of muscular and cardiorespiratory fitness exhibited more favourable anthropometric profiles, a relationship observed equally in both urban and rural populations. This reinforces the idea that promoting physical fitness may yield widespread benefits, independent of the residential setting.

Nonetheless, when comparing our findings with previous studies, the literature shows mixed results on rural–urban differences in obesity and physical fitness. For instance, Singleton et al16 reported that children in rural areas across South America had a slightly lower prevalence of obesity and cardiometabolic risk than their urban counterparts, possibly due to more active lifestyles or dietary habits. Similarly, Regis et al24 found that Brazilian adolescents in rural areas engaged in more physical activity and had lower sedentary behaviour. However, other studies, such as those by Nuotio et al19 and Ogden et al,14 suggest urban adolescents tend to have better cardiometabolic profiles, attributed to higher access to sports facilities and structured exercise programmes.

These divergent findings underscore the importance of understanding the specific sociocultural and economic contexts that shape physical activity patterns. For example, urban adolescents may benefit from greater institutional support for exercise but may also be more exposed to sedentary behaviours due to screen time, limited outdoor space and higher environmental risks.21 22 In contrast, rural youth may lack structured physical education but engage in more spontaneous physical activity through unstructured play or household responsibilities.23 Our findings suggest that despite these contextual differences, the physiological benefits of physical fitness on anthropometric outcomes are broadly generalisable.

Despite the absence of a moderating effect from the area of residence in our study, previous literature emphasises the complexity of interpreting rural–urban differences in adolescent health. Boraita et al35 reported that Spanish adolescents living in urban areas had better lifestyle indicators, including well-being, autonomy and a better school environment. However, such findings are not necessarily transferable to other settings, particularly in countries with differing levels of economic development and infrastructure disparities. In a Kosovan adolescent sample, fitness performance was associated with overweight/obesity risk regardless of residence area, despite the absence of consistent rural–urban differences in anthropometric measures.36 Similarly, work in the primary school context suggests that urban–rural context location may be differentially related to specific fitness measures, but without a unified pattern across outcomes.37 In the Latin American context, research in Ecuador38 and Mexico39 showed that urban adolescents had better physical fitness than rural peers, a difference attributed to greater access to organised sports and professional instruction in urban schools. These findings underscore the need for studies, particularly in the Latin American setting, that explicitly test for moderation rather than simply comparing means.

Nevertheless, our study reflects the unique socio-demographic reality of Chile, where disparities in public service access, including sports and health education, are not always aligned with geographical divisions. As reported by Yáñez-Sepúlveda et al,40 SES plays a more critical role than geography per se in determining physical fitness and health outcomes. Adolescents attending schools with higher SES demonstrate significantly better fitness and lower cardiometabolic risk. These findings imply that SES may be a stronger moderator than urban or rural residence, especially in countries where urban poverty and rural wealth coexist.

Although SES is strongly associated with both physical fitness and anthropometric indicators, it was treated as a covariate rather than as a moderator in the present analyses. This decision was driven by the primary objective of the study, which focused on evaluating the contextual role of the area of residence, as well as by the need to control for socioeconomic disparities that are structurally linked to rural–urban residence, moderating fitness-anthropometry association independently of SES, rather than to model multiple interaction moderation pathways within a single cross-sectional framework.

This perspective is supported by international evidence. For example, Stojanović et al,41 in a systematic review, found that increases in daily step counts were consistently associated with better cardiometabolic outcomes in youth, regardless of place of residence. Moreover, studies conducted in green or less urbanised areas show that exposure to nature and open space may reduce sedentary behaviour and promote spontaneous movement, contributing to better overall health profiles.42

Together, these findings reinforce the interpretation that the benefits of physical fitness are broadly generalisable and not exclusively contingent on urbanisation level. They also suggest that public health efforts should shift away from geographical targeting alone and instead prioritise equity in access to physical activity opportunities and health education, especially among disadvantaged communities, whether urban or rural.

From a public health perspective, this study’s findings suggest that interventions to improve adolescent health should emphasise physical fitness promotion across all geographical areas. Since the benefits of physical fitness for anthropometric indicators are consistent across urban and rural settings, national and regional strategies should not assume uniform vulnerability based solely on place of residence. Instead, educational policies should ensure the universal availability of high-quality physical education, regardless of school location and integrate physical activity programmes during and after school hours.

Importantly, our results support calls from international organisations for more equitable access to health-promoting environments.22 As highlighted by Hillsdon et al43 and Tandon et al,44 disparities in recreational infrastructure, access to sports facilities and socioeconomic support systems contribute more significantly to adolescent health than rural–urban geography alone. Equitable investment in green spaces, active transportation routes, school-based programmes and community sports, especially in lower SES neighbourhoods, can help bridge these gaps and reduce cardiometabolic risk at the population level.

From a methodological perspective, an alternative analytical approach would involve using a global or composite physical fitness score that integrates multiple fitness components. Such scores may facilitate communication and compatibility across studies. However, they may also mask a specific test association. Future studies could compare components-based and composite approaches, particularly in longitudinal designs or prediction-oriented analyses to determine their relative utility for clinical and public health applications.

However, this study is not without limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between physical fitness and anthropometric outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how sustained levels of physical activity and fitness influence cardiometabolic health over time. Second, although SES was controlled for in the models, more granular data on family income, parental education and environmental characteristics (eg, walkability, food access, pollution) were not available. Future research should integrate multilevel and geospatial analyses that consider social determinants, neighbourhood characteristics and built environment metrics. Additionally, although the sample size was large and representative, the rural subgroup was relatively small, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Lastly, future investigations should explore the differential effects of physical activity type (structured vs unstructured; individual vs team-based), duration and intensity on cardiometabolic markers. Given the mental health benefits associated with physical activity, such as reduced depression risk, a more holistic assessment of adolescent well-being is warranted. Finally, because the analyses were restricted to participants with complete data, selection bias cannot be ruled out if the probability of missingness was related to physical fitness or anthropometric outcomes. Although we report the participant selection process transparently, this limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Finally, it is important to note that new trends in research should not only address physical fitness from a general health perspective but also delve into sport-specific demands. In this regard, future studies should consider research in combat sports such as Taekwondo,45 where the nature of the effort is intermittent and technical. Following this line of thought, new studies need to incorporate assessments that analyse changes in direction, with the aim of integrating complex movement patterns into the classic variables of physical fitness.46 This integration would allow for a deeper understanding of how motor competence and agility interact with cardiorespiratory capacity and adolescent development.

Practical applications

Our results suggest that physical fitness is consistently associated with more favourable cardiovascular-related profiles among adolescents, irrespective of urban or rural residence. Although physical activity and nutrition were not directly measured in this study, these findings supported the relevance of public policies that promote environments conducive to physical fitness, including school-based strategies that provide adequate opportunities for physical activity and nutrition education across both urban and rural settings. Such approaches may help address sedentary behaviours and unhealthy dietary patterns during school hours and at home, particularly in urban contexts where these challenges are more prevalent.

Future studies should consider conducting longitudinal research to identify changes in obesity levels across the course of life. It is also relevant to consider environmental and socioeconomic factors in the analysis to better understand the moderating roles; access to services, parks, infrastructure and pollution levels, among other factors, should be taken into account. The development of multicentre studies that integrate developed and developing countries should consider places with diverse geographical and socioeconomic conditions. Finally, it is recommended to analyse the interaction and effects of different types of physical activity, considering physical education classes, unstructured exercise and daily physical activity and identifying the effects of these interactions on anthropometric characteristics and cardiometabolic risk.

Conclusion

Based on the results, we conclude that the area of residence does not moderate the associations between physical fitness, anthropometric characteristics and cardiometabolic risk among Chilean adolescents. Higher levels of physical fitness are consistently associated with more favourable body composition and cardiometabolic profiles, independent of residential areas. This study provides relevant information for developing public policies and nutritional and physical intervention programmes that account for the characteristics of urban and rural environments to combat obesity and inactivity.
