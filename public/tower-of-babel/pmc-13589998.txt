A Case Report of Refractory Allergic Bronchopulmonary Aspergillosis: The Art of Selection, Discontinuation and Switching Biologics
Authors: Yaqian Li, Shuo Yang, Saisai Ren, Pei Cao, Zhiqing Zhang, Yakun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiologic therapies have transformed the management of allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), improving symptom control and reducing exacerbations. However, a subset of patients exhibits suboptimal or absent response to these therapies. We present a case of refractory ABPA with secondary nonresponse to omalizumab treatment, and subsequently symptom improvement after switching to mepolizumab.Case ReportA 71-year-old male patient was diagnosed with ABPA in August 2020. He was initially managed with inhaled dual therapy, itraconazole, and oral corticosteroids (OCS) for exacerbations. Since 2022, the patient has experienced recurrent acute exacerbations and disease progression, with serum total immunoglobulin E (TIgE) rising to 2150 IU/mL. Subcutaneous omalizumab 600mg every 4 weeks was initiated, which gradually reduced exacerbations and corticosteroids exposure. The biological therapy was then discontinued after 11 months treatment. In 2024, asthma symptoms were uncontrolled despite reinitiation of omalizumab therapy, with 3 exacerbations throughout the year and absolute eosinophils rose to 760 cells/μL. After full evaluation and obtaining informed consent, the patient was switched to mepolizumab in January 2025, which resulted in improved clinical and radiological outcomes. Within 6 months of combination therapy, absolute eosinophils dropped to 30 cells/μL, FEV1 improved from 0.89 to 1.10 L, OCS was gradually tapered and discontinued. The patient continued treatment with mepolizumab, and over a period exceeding 12 months, no recurrence of clinical symptoms or abnormal laboratory findings was observed.ConclusionMepolizumab was effective in our patient with persistent eosinophilia and secondary nonresponse to omalizumab. Although its long-term efficacy and adverse effects require further follow-up and monitoring, our case adds clinically meaningful evidence and offers valuable insights into personalized biologic decision-making for patients with refractory ABPA.

Introduction

Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) is an allergic lung disease caused by sensitization to Aspergillus.1 Aspergillus spores deposition in the small airways, causing inflammation, bronchiectasis, fibrosis, and eventual loss of lung function.1,2 The International Society for Human and Animal Mycology (ISHAM) working group recommends two components for the diagnosis of ABPA. The first is to document sensitisation against the implicated fungus by using fungus-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE), while the other is to demonstrate immunological activity (raised serum total IgE).3 Besides the essential components, the presence of two of the following features, including fungal-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG), peripheral blood eosinophilia and consistent imaging, confirms the diagnosis of ABPA.3 ABPA treatment goals are to relieve symptoms, prevent exacerbations, control asthma, halt bronchiectasis progression, and minimise treatment-related adverse effects.4 The principles of treating ABPA involve using corticosteroids and antifungal agents.3,4 The use of corticosteroids suppresses excessive immune reactions while alleviating Aspergillus-induced inflammatory damage.1,5 Most patients achieve remission with systemic corticosteroids, but up to 45% relapse or develop chronic dependence, which can be associated with predictable adverse effects.6 Triazole antifungal drugs inhibit the cytochrome P-450-dependent 14-α-demethylase, thereby blocking ergosterol synthesis and causing damage to the fungal cell membrane.7 Antifungal agents such as voriconazole and itraconazole can reduce airway fungal colonisation and lower systemic corticosteroids exposure in patients of ABPA.2 However, they are now reserved for use only in patients with severe or persistent symptoms, owing to concerns regarding antifungal resistance, drug–drug interactions, interindividual variability in absorption and significant adverse events.2,8

Therefore, improved salvage treatments are warranted. The pathogenesis of ABPA is driven by innate or adaptive immune responses and mediated by inflammatory mediators including IgE, interleukin 4 (IL-4), interleukin 5 (IL-5), and interleukin 13 (IL-13), as well as cells such as eosinophils, mast cells, and lymphocytes.3 Biologics targeting those inflammatory pathways have been used in patients who have failed to achieve long-term remission with standard therapy. Omalizumab, an anti-IgE antibody, is the first biologic for the treatment of refractory ABPA.3 Other biologics, such as anti-interleukin 5 (anti-IL5) agent (mepolizumab), anti-interleukin 5 receptor (anti-IL-5R) agent (benralizumab), and anti-interleukin 4 receptor alpha (anti-IL-4Rα) agent (dupilumab), still require larger clinical trials to provide robust evidence.3 Response to biologic therapy can be estimated 6 months after starting treatment. In primary non-response, the drug is ineffective, with no clinical response within the initial treatment period, while in secondary non-response, the effectiveness of the drug is lost after a period of initial response.9 Possible causes of secondary non-response to biologics may include the development of anti-drug antibodies, change in the initial inflammatory endotype and other factors.9 The inflammatory endotypes of patients exhibit dynamic variability, occurring in up to 50% of patients and necessitating periodic re-evaluation of phenotypes and biomarkers.10,11 While international frameworks like the ISHAM-ABPA guidelines recommend using biologics for treatment-dependent cases, they still do not provide clear management strategies for patients who experience secondary non-response after initial biologic therapy. The therapeutic impact of switching between biologic agents remains poorly documented, with only limited case reports.12–14

Here, we present a case of ABPA with secondary nonresponse to omalizumab treatment, and subsequently symptom improvement after switching to mepolizumab. We detail the patient’s clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, therapeutic course, and over 12 months of follow-up. Furthermore, based on the existing evidence and pathogenesis insights, we attempt to propose a strategy for individualized biologic agent selection in patients with refractory ABPA.

Case Presentation

The patient was a 71-year-old male with body mass index (BMI) of 19.2 kg/m2. He suffered from allergic rhinitis, severe asthma (SA) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) started at the age of 63 and was maintained on Fluticasone/Salmeterol accuhaler 500/50 micrograms twice daily, montelukast, and expectorant (eucalyptol, limonene and pinene enteric soft capsules). He experienced 2 severe exacerbations in March and August 2020. Spirometry (2020/08): Forced expiratory volume in 1 sec (FEV1) 1.40 L (52% predicted), Post-Bronchodilator FEV1/(forced vital capacity, FVC) 0.50. Serum total immunoglobulin E (TIgE) 1351 IU/mL, Aspergillus fumigatus specific immunoglobulin E (Af-sIgE) 6.85 KUA/L (2020/08). Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed patchy consolidation, most pronounced in the right lower lobe, emphysema, pulmonary bullae, and chronic inflammatory nodules in both lungs. A diagnosis of ABPA was considered according to ISHAM guidelines. Oral prednisone (20 mg/day) and itraconazole (200 mg/12 hour) were started from August 2020, resulting in significant symptom improvement in the first two weeks. In October 2020, itraconazole was discontinued due to hypokalemia (2.8 mmol/L) after more than 2 months of treatment, maintenance therapy with prednisone (5–20 mg/day) was systematically tapered.

In February 2022, the patient experienced worsening chest tightness, dyspnea, cough, and sputum production. TIgE was measured at a level of 2150 IU/mL. Chest CT revealed a new lesion in the right lower lobe of the lung, with mild localized bronchiectasis in both lower lobes. Prednisone was increased to 15mg a day to control the symptoms. Given the development of ABPA during prednisone therapy, the omalizumab was initiated at a dose of 600 mg, administered every 4 weeks from February to December 2022. Symptoms of the patient gradually improved during this period, and prednisone was tapered to 5 mg/d.

In January and October 2024, the patient experienced two severe exacerbations. Chest CT indicated progressive lesions in both lungs. In October 2024, omalizumab and itraconazole were reinitiated for treatment. However, merely 2 months later, the patient experienced an acute exacerbation of ABPA again. Spirometry revealed severe mixed ventilatory dysfunction, with FEV1 at 0.89 L (35.09% predicted) and Post-Bronchodilator FEV1/FVC at 0.46. Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO): 16ppb. Peripheral blood eosinophil count (EOS) rose to 760 cells/μL, TIgE rose to 2451 IU/mL and Af-sIgE 7.18 KUA/L (2024/12). Given the development of ABPA during omalizumab therapy, omalizumab was discontinued and mepolizumab was initiated at a dose of 100 mg, administered every 4 weeks from January 2025.

Following the modification of the treatment regimen, the patient’s symptoms significantly improved. In February 2025, the EOS decreased to 30 cells/μL, TIgE concentration declined to 573 IU/mL, and itraconazole therapy was discontinued. In April 2025, the TIgE had declined further to 378 KU/L, Af-sIgE was 1.83 KUA/L, FEV1 rose to 1.07 L (42.62% predicted), Post-Bronchodilator FEV1/FVC rose to 0.51. Chest CT exhibited the absorption of lesions in both lungs, right sided but stable mucus impaction with no progression of bronchiectasis compared to those in October 2024. In September 2025, the patient’s condition stabilized, FEV1 rose to 1.10 L (43.64% predicted) and oral corticosteroids (OCS) were tapered to discontinuation. Follow-up continued until December 2025, during which the patient remained asymptomatic with no exacerbations and adverse effects. Discussion

Studies have shown that almost 10%–25% of ABPA patients become treatment-dependent due to the chronic relapsing nature of the illness and the propensity for developing severe complications.15 In our case, the patient had been receiving systemic hormone therapy for a long time but continued to experience recurrent acute exacerbations, suggesting a treatment-dependent ABPA. According to ISHAM-AWG guidelines, long-term itraconazole, nebulised amphotericin B or biological agents are recommended options for managing treatment-dependent ABPA.3 However, many patients’ treatment failed, owing to antifungal resistance, drug–drug interactions, interindividual variability in absorption and significant adverse events.2,8 Similarly, our patient discontinued the itraconazole treatment after 2 months due to hypokalemia. A randomised, multicentre trial (139 patients) have evaluated the role of nebulised amphotericin B as maintenance therapy in ABPA, while the time-to-first exacerbation was significantly longer with nebulised amphotericin B compared to the control group, the primary outcome was not significant.16 Furthermore, nebulised amphotericin B not only falls under off-label use but also presents operational challenges for the elderly.

Therefore, combination therapy with biologics may be considered, and which biological agent can provide best risk/benefit ratio for a given patient requires evidence-informed deliberation. The management of ABPA through biologic therapy necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the underlying molecular and immunological mechanisms. Persistent fungal colonization in the airways elicits both innate and adaptive immune responses, resulting in a predominantly T helper type 2 (Th2)-mediated hypersensitivity reaction of ABPA.1 Activation of Th2 subsets promotes the release of cytokines such as IL‑4, IL‑9, IL‑5, IL‑13, and others, resulting in elevated IgE levels, mast cell activation, eosinophilia, and neutrophil infiltration, leading to the unique immunological and pathological features of ABPA.1,17–19 Studies have confirmed that the use of anti-IgE (omalizumab) can reduce exacerbations and corticosteroids exposure in patients with treatment-dependent ABPA.6,20

Omalizumab is a humanized anti-IgE monoclonal IgG1 antibody. It specifically binds to the Cε3 region of IgE to form immune complexes, which prevents the binding of IgE to high-affinity IgE receptor (FCεRI) on mast cells, dendritic cells and basophils, thereby reducing allergic cascade.21 Systematic review and meta-analysis20,22 have shown that omalizumab not only provided a significant reduction in the use of OCS, serum levels of IgE, and exacerbation rates but also improved asthma control and lung function in patients with ABPA. Given the development of ABPA and high serum TIgE levels in our patient, omalizumab was an appropriate initial biologic treatment. The dosing recommendations for omalizumab are based on serum IgE levels and body weight,23 but the required dose for our patient exceeds the upper limit of dosing (TIgE > 2000 IU/mL). The ISHAM-ABPA guidelines recommend that the dose of omalizumab should not exceed 375mg every two weeks.3 Li (2017)22 reported several cases with TIgE higher than 7000 IU/mL used 375 mg every two weeks and control of ABPA symptoms clearly improved over the follow-up period. Given that our patient’s TIgE level was 2150 at the time, we decided to use a slightly lower dose than 375 mg every two weeks. Considering the cost-effectiveness of the biologic agent and patient treatment adherence, we ultimately adopted a dose of 600 mg every four weeks.

However, as shown in Figure 1, serum TIgE levels rose gradually from 2150 IU/mL (2022/02) at baseline to 3211 IU/mL (2022/11) over the first ten months after subcutaneous injection of omalizumab. This pattern initially prompted clinical concern regarding potential disease progression or suboptimal treatment response. As TIgE levels generally serve as one of the key indicators for immunological activity in ABPA, and a 20% decline beyond 8 weeks is traditionally taken as criteria for response.3 Does a 50% increase in baseline TIgE levels indicate inadequate response to omalizumab in our case? A retrospective analysis have shown that not all patients achieve a significant reduction in TIgE beyond 6 weeks even with institution of high dose glucocorticoid therapy despite clinical and radiological improvement, and the decline was smaller in patients with baseline TIgE levels less than 2500 IU/mL.24 Moreover, multivariate analysis showed that the decline in serum TIgE levels did not predict clinical outcomes.24 The TIgE measured in clinical practice is the sum of free IgE and omalizumab‑IgE complexes. A population pharmacokinetics study25 have established a mechanism-based population PK/PD model for omalizumab–IgE complex. The results showed that omalizumab–IgE complex exhibited a 5.86 mL/h higher clearance and a more restricted volume of distribution than free omalizumab. The half-lives of omalizumab‑IgE complex and free IgE were 23 and 2.4 days, which all contribute to the observed increase in TIgE. Based on the following studies, the increase of TIgE during omalizumab treatment could occur by two mechanisms: (i) the IgG region of omalizumab–IgE complexes specifically binds to the neonatal Fc receptor, thereby protecting the complexes from lysosomal degradation;26 (ii) the complexes have a higher molecular mass (340–1000 kDa) than free IgE (190 kDa) or IgG (150 kDa),27 resulting in reduced vascular endothelial permeability and a more restricted volume of distribution.28

Therefore, TIgE may rise initially after omalizumab initiation, but the magnitude of TIgE levels cannot be used as an efficacy indicator. After reaching peak levels, omalizumab suppresses the allergic inflammatory cascade, decreasing IgE production and TIgE levels in patients. As remission in ABPA is diagnosed when the patient has no asthma or ABPA exacerbations, is not dependent on oral glucocorticoid therapy and has the best possible lung function.3,4 Over the first ten months, our patient treated with omalizumab demonstrated symptomatic improvement and radiological stabilization, suggesting an effective initial treatment with omalizumab despite a 50% increase in baseline TIgE levels.

Another unresolved clinical question is whether to continue, taper, or discontinue omalizumab treatment once the condition has stabilized. A retrospective cohort study (n = 124) have compared the long-term effectiveness of omalizumab in patients with severe allergic asthma and confirmed the benefits of persistent omalizumab treatment (≥10 months), while a 4-month boost of omalizumab would worsen their long-term outcomes.29 Current consensus guidelines recommend that, for patients demonstrating a good response to biologic therapy, a trial of treatment withdrawal should not be initiated before completing at least 12 months of therapy.3 A favorable treatment response was observed after 11 months of omalizumab treatment in our patient, while TIgE levels continued to rise (up to 3350 IU/mL). Omalizumab was discontinued depended on the judgment and suggestion of the clinicians and pharmacists after reviewing the medical records. The patient was continuously followed up for 12 months after discontinuing biologics, during which symptoms remained stable with no acute exacerbations, confirming that our treatment strategy was appropriate in this stage.

After approximately one year of down-regulation therapy, the patient experienced 3 acute exacerbations, and symptoms remained poorly controlled even after resuming omalizumab treatment. How should treatment be continued when the patient responds poorly to omalizumab? The inflammatory endotypes of patients exhibit dynamic variability, modulated by external factors including systemic corticosteroids exposure, acute infections, and other therapeutic interventions. This variability was observed in up to 50% of patients and necessitates a re-evaluation of phenotypes and biomarkers (EOS, IgE, FeNO etc) periodically.10,11 Peripheral blood EOS of the patient rose to 760 cells/μL, this result may indicate the need for a biologic targeting a different inflammatory pathway.

Excess of eosinophils is a process driven by cytokines such as IL-5, IL-4, and IL-13.30 Over the last two decades, several trials of monoclonal antibodies against IL-4Rα (dupilumab), IL-5 (mepolizumab), and IL-5R (benralizumab) have demonstrated clinical benefit in severe eosinophilic asthma, and they also be recommended in the management of treatment-dependent ABPA according to 2024 ISHAM-ABPA guidelines (evidence level III). Dupilumab is a monoclonal antibody that selectively targets the IL-4 receptor, inhibiting the inflammatory cascade induced by IL-4 and its reaction to IL-13, limiting mastocytes and eosinophils activity.31 The most common adverse effects of dupilumab include head/neck dermatitis (up to 19.5%), hypereosinophilia (up to 10%; patients with blood eosinophils >1500/µL are at higher risk of hypereosinophilia with dupilumab).31,32 Given the patient’s elevated EOS level (up to 760 cells/μL and higher EOS levels may have been masked by OCS maintenance therapy) in this case, we do not consider using dupilumab for treatment.

Mepolizumab is a monoclonal antibody that selectively targets IL-5 with high affinity, inhibiting the connection to its receptor and the cascade of eosinophilic airway inflammation.33 Benralizumab is a monoclonal antibody that selectively targets the IL-5 receptor, inhibiting the connection of IL-5 to its receptor on both eosinophils and basophils, limiting the recruitment, differentiation, maturation, and activation of those cells.33 In recent years, an increasing body of real-world evidence supports clinical decision-making in this area. Several case reports have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of mepolizumab/benralizumab in ABPA treatment.34–36 Tomomatsu K. et al,37 retrospectively, evaluated the efficacy and safety of mepolizumab and benralizumab in 29 patients with asthma-complicated ABPA. The two biologics were equally effective in reducing the exacerbation rates and doses of OCS and improving lung function. And benralizumab may be more effective than mepolizumab in removing mucus plugs from the airways (100% vs 76%). Unfortunately, there are currently no head-to-head comparisons among the approved biologics.

A meta-analysis38 of the efficacy of biological therapies for ABPA indicated that omalizumab (n = 265), mepolizumab (n = 49), and dupilumab (n = 20) significantly lowered the acute exacerbations, OCS dosage, and TIgE levels. Notably, mepolizumab decreased eosinophil levels and improved FEV1% (P < 0.05), with no significant adverse reactions. Additionally, in some case reports, switching from omalizumab to anti-IL-5/5R resulted in improved clinical and radiological outcomes, as well as a reduction in OCS dosage.12–14 Furthermore, Menzies-Gow et al39 analyzed biologic use and switching in 3531 patients with SA across 11 countries. Of these,10.8% (384/3531) switched to a different biologic, and the most common switch involved moving from omalizumab to an anti-IL-5/5R treatment, which occurred in 49.6% (187/377) of all switches. In general, IL-5/5R mAb can inhibit the biological effects of IL-5, thereby suppressing eosinophilic inflammation, making them suitable for patients with eosinophilia-type ABPA, but prolonged follow-up studies are required to clarify risks.34

After a comprehensive evaluation of disease progression, drug accessibility, and financial status, the patient in this study received combined itraconazole and mepolizumab therapy from January 2025. According to ISHAM-ABPA guidelines, a good response is indicated by a major improvement in symptoms and chest radiographs, along with at least a 20% reduction in TIgE. In February 2025, TIgE concentration declined from 1642 to 573 IU/mL (65% reduction), the patient’s symptoms significantly improved and itraconazole therapy was discontinued. Following approximately 6 months of treatment courses, significant improvement was observed in cough and sputum production without subsequent acute exacerbations. Subsequent follow-ups were conducted every 3 months, both pulmonary function and chest CT showed improvement, indicating disease stability. No treatment restriction due to adverse occurred, suggesting that mepolizumab offers a favorable safety and efficacy profile in patients with ABPA who suffer from diminished efficacy of omalizumab. The patient stated that although the treatment process was lengthy, the timely and appropriate treatment measures as well as the supportive communication provided by the medical team greatly alleviated his anxiety. He particularly mentioned that the doctors and clinical pharmacists explained the treatment plan in detail and respected his right to make choices, making him feel valued and involved. The long-term follow-up for this patient is still ongoing at present.

Ultimately, the clinical implications of our manuscript are substantial, advocating for a paradigm shift toward the dynamic, personalized management of refractory ABPA. Nevertheless, our report also has some limitations. The main limitations of the case are that it represents a single-patient report, which limits generalizability. In addition, the presence of multiple concurrent therapies may confound interpretation of the observed outcomes, and the long-term effectiveness of mepolizumab in this context remains uncertain.

Conclusion

We present a case of refractory ABPA that secondary nonresponse to omalizumab treatment, with subsequent symptom improvement following a switch to mepolizumab. The switch of biologics requires a comprehensive, patient-centered strategy informed by clinical phenotypes, biomarker profiles, individual characteristics, cost-effectiveness and long-term treatment adaptability. Although the long-term efficacy and adverse effects require further follow-up and monitoring, our report provides a reference regarding the feasibility of using different biological targeted therapies in the management of ABPA. Additionally, large-scale prospective studies are needed to better determine the safety and efficacy of biological therapies and address cost-effectiveness considerations.
