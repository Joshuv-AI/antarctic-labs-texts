Cogent argument extensions are weakly admissible but not vice versa
Gustavo Bodanza

Abstract




1
Introduction

In this research note, we show the relationship between two non-admissible argumentation framework semantics: cogent
[
3
,
4
]
and weakly admissible semantics
[
2
]
. We prove that, while cogent extensions are weakly admissible, the converse is not true.

2
Argumentation frameworks

Dung’s argumentation frameworks
[
5
]
are intended to represent debates or argument situations in which arguments interact through attacks.

Definition 1.

[
5
]
An
argumentation framework
is a structure
F

=

(A,R)
, where
A
is a set whose elements are called
arguments
and
R\subseteq A\times A
is a relation of
attack
among arguments.

We say that
x

attacks

y
iff
(x,y)\in R
. Moreover, given susbets
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq A
we will also say that
S
attacks
x
(resp.,
x
attacks
S
) iff
y
attacks
x
(resp.,
x
attacks
y
) for some
y\in S
, and
S
attacks
S^{\prime}
iff there exists
x
in
S
such that
x
attacks
S^{\prime}
.

The arguments emerging as victorious are collected in subsets called
extensions
, which satisfy certain specified “semantics” or requirements for defense. All of Dung’s semantics satisfy the following “admissibility” criterion.

Definition 2.
A subset
S\subseteq A
is
admissible
iff:

1.

for all
x,y\in S
,
(x,y)\notin R
(
S
is conflict-free);

2.

for all
x\in A
, if
x
attacks
S
then
S
attacks
x
. (
S
is defensible)

3
Non-admissible semantics: cogency and weak admissibility

Several authors have introduced argumentation framework semantics that depart from the notion of admissibility
[
6
,
1
,
3
,
4
,
2
]
. The aim is to capture other, weaker yet sensible defense criteria. Here we deal with cogency and weak admissibility criteria. Both are intended to avoid the undesired effects of self-attacking arguments. The idea is that any attacks resulting from those arguments should be ignored because they are irrational. For example, given
F

=

(\{a,b\},\{(a,a),(a,b)\})
, we have that
\emptyset
is the only admissible subset of arguments, but
b
should be accepted because it is only challenged by the self-attacking argument,
a
.

In
[
3
,
4
]
, the notion of
cogency
is proposed to capture the intended semantics.

Definition 3.

[
4
]
Given an argumentation framework
F

=

(A,R)
, and two subsets
E
,
E^{\prime}

\subseteq

A
, we say that
E
is
at least as cogent as

E^{\prime}
, in symbols,
E\geq_{cog}E^{\prime}
, iff
E
is admissible in the restricted argumentation framework
F\mid_{E\cup E^{\prime}}=(A,R\mid_{E\cup E^{\prime}})
. We say that
E
is
strictly more cogent than

E^{\prime}
, in symbols,
E>_{cog}E^{\prime}
, iff
E\geq_{cog}E^{\prime}
and not
E^{\prime}\geq_{cog}E
.

Definition 4.

[
4
]
Given an argumentation framework
F=(A,R)
, we say that a subset

E\subseteq A
is (simply)
cogent
iff
E
is maximal w.r.t.
>_{cog}
, i.e.,
\forall E^{\prime}\subseteq A

E^{\prime}\not>_{cog}E
. We also define
Cog(AF)=\{E\subseteq A:E
is
cogent
\}
.

Later, in
[
2
]
the notion of
weak admissibility
is introduced, yielding a similar semantics but with a different formal apparatus.

Definition 5.

[
2
]
Given an argumentation framework
F=(A,R)
and
E\subseteq A
, define:

•

E^{+}=\{a\in A:E\ \text{attacks}\ a\}

•

E^{\oplus}=E\cup E^{+}

Definition 6.

[
2
]
Given an argumentation framework
F=(A,R)
, let
E\subseteq A
. The
E
-reduct of
F
is the argumentation framework
F^{E}=\left(E^{*},R\cap\left(E^{*}\times E^{*}\right)\right)
where
E^{*}=A\backslash E^{\oplus}
.

Definition 7.

[
2
]
Given
F=(A,R)
,
E\subseteq A
is called
weakly admissible
in
F\left(E\in ad^{w}(F)\right)
iff

1.

E
is conflict-free, and

2.

for any attacker
y
of
E
we have
y\notin\bigcup ad^{w}\left(F^{E}\right)
.

4
Relationship between cogency and weak admissibility

The following results demonstrate the relationships between cogency and weak admissibility.

Theorem 1

Cog(F)

\subseteq

ad^{w}(F)
.

P
roof. Let
E\in Cog(F)
and assume for the sake of contradiction that
E\not\in ad^{w}(F)
. From this last, it follows that either
E
is not conflict-free or that there exists
E^{\prime}

\in

ad^{w}(F^{E})
such that
E^{\prime}
attacks
E
. But, because
E
is cogent,
E
is conflict-free. Hence, there exists
E^{\prime}

\in

ad^{w}(F^{E})
such that
E^{\prime}
attacks
E
. Now,
E^{\prime}
is conflict-free —since it is weakly admissible in
F^{E}
— and, by definition of
F^{E}
,
E
does not attack
E^{\prime}
. Then, we have that
E^{\prime}
is admissible in
F\mid_{E\cup E^{\prime}}
. But
E
is not, since it does not counterattack
E^{\prime}
. Therefore,
E^{\prime}>_{cog}E
, which contradicts the hypothesis that
E\in Cog(F)
.
\Box

The converse of the previous theorem is not true in general, as can be seen from the following example. Consider
F=(\{a,b,c\},

\{(a,b),(b,c)\})
. Then
\{c\}
is weakly admissible, since
b
—the attacker of
c
— does not belong to any weakly admissible set in
F^{\{c\}}
. However, it is not cogent, since
\{b\}
is admissible in
F\mid_{\{a,b\}}
but
\{a\}
is not. Instead, if we look at maximal (w.r.t.
\subseteq
) weakly admissible and cogent subsets, we get an agreement in
\{a,c\}
. However, this correspondence cannot be generalized to any argumentation framework either. There could exist maximal weakly admissible subsets which are not cogent, as in the argumentation framework
(\{a,b,c,d\},\{(a,b),(b,c),(c,a),(b,d)\})
, where
\{d\}
is such a subset.

In consequence, we can say that cogent semantics is more skeptic than weakly admissible semantics or, conversely, weakly admissible semantics is more credulous than cogent semantics.
