Gender disparities, causes and predictors of immediate and short-term cardiovascular readmissions following COVID-19-related hospitalisations in the USA
Authors: Ufuk Vardar, Hafeez Shaka, Dennis Kumi, Rohan Gajjar, Olva Bess, Philip Kanemo, Abdultawab Shaka, Naveen Baskaran
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIn this study, we aimed to identify the causes, predictors and gender disparities of 30-day and 90-day cardiovascular readmissions after COVID-19-related hospitalisations using National Readmission Database (NRD) 2020.SettingWe used the NRD from 2020 to identify hospitalised adults with a principal diagnosis of COVID-19 infection.ParticipantsWe included subjects who were readmitted within 30 days and 90 days after index admission. We excluded subjects with elective and traumatic admissions. We used a multivariate Cox regression model to identify independent predictors of readmission.Primary and secondary outcomes measuresOur outcomes were inpatient mortality, 30-day and 90-day cardiovascular readmission rates following COVID-19 infection.ResultsDuring the study period, there were 1 024 492 index hospitalisations with a primary diagnosis of COVID-19 infection in the 2020 NRD database, 644 903 (62.9%) were included for 30-day readmission analysis, and 418 122 (40.8%) were included for 90-day readmission analysis. Of patients involved in the 30-day analysis, 7140 (1.1%) patients had a readmission within 30 days; of patients involved in the 90-day analysis, 8379 (2.0%) had a readmission within 90 days due to primarily cardiovascular causes. Cox regression analysis revealed that the female sex (aHR 0.89; 95% CI 0.82 to 0.95; p=0.001) was associated with a lower hazard of 30-day cardiovascular readmissions; however, congestive heart failure (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 2.2 to 2.72; p<0.001), arrhythmias (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 2.2 to 2.72; p<0.001) and valvular disease (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 2.2 to 2.72; p<0.001) had a higher hazard. The most common causes of cardiovascular readmissions were heart failure (34.3%), deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism (22.5%) and atrial fibrillation (9.5%).ConclusionOur study demonstrates that male gender, heart failure, arrhythmias and valvular disease carry higher hazards of 30-day and 90-day cardiovascular readmissions. Identifying risk factors and common causes of readmission may assist with lowering the burden of cardiovascular disease in patients with COVID-19 infection.

Introduction

The SARS-CoV-2 or COVID-19 is a viral disease that initially surfaced in December 2019 causing the WHO to declare a pandemic epidemic in March 2020.1 COVID-19 primarily affects the upper and lower respiratory tracts.2 However, current evidence suggests that COVID-19 can cause a wide range of clinical manifestations, affecting multiple systems in the human body.3 Post-COVID-19 is a syndrome defined as persistent symptoms and/or delayed or long-term complications of SARS-CoV-2 infection beyond 4 weeks from the onset of symptoms, which is further divided into subacute or ongoing symptomatic COVID-19 (4–12 weeks after onset) and chronic or post-COVID-19 syndrome (>12 weeks after onset).4 5

Cardiovascular manifestations of COVID-19 have pathophysiological, consequential and time-dependent distributions, including myocardial injury, thrombotic events, heart failure and arrhythmias; and ranging from acute COVID-19 cardiovascular syndrome to post-COVID-19 short-term and long-term manifestations.6–10 The cause of this injury is uncertain but is suspected to be related to myocarditis, microvascular injury, systemic cytokine-mediated injury or stress-related cardiomyopathy.10

In this study, we aimed to investigate the causes, predictors and rates of 30-day and 90-day cardiovascular readmissions after COVID-19 infection and the impact of gender on these rates using the National Readmission Database (NRD) from 2020.

Methods

Design and data source

This was an observational retrospective study involving COVID-19 infection-related adult hospitalisations, obtained from the NRD for 2020. The NRD is one of the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient healthcare readmission databases in the USA, drawn from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) Healthcare Cost and Utilisation Project (HCUP) State Inpatient Databases (SID) (dataset)11 It provides longitudinal information about patients’ initial hospitalisation and subsequent readmissions in a calendar year. It contains a weighted sample of hospitalisations in the USA, and this can be used to derive national estimates of various hospitalisations directly.

The NRD 2020 contains discharge data from approximately 17 million unweighted discharges from 31 geographically dispersed states accounting for 62.2% of the total USA resident population and 60.8% of all USA hospitalisations. It comprises both patient-level and hospital-level information. Up to 40 discharge diagnoses and 25 procedures are collected for each patient using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), Clinical Modification (ICD-10/PCS). Diagnoses are classified as a principal diagnosis, which is the reason for hospitalisation, and secondary diagnosis which is any other discharge diagnosis. Hospitals are stratified according to ownership control, the number of beds, teaching status, urban/rural location and geographical region. The NRD allows for weighted analysis to obtain 100% of the USA hospitalisations within a given year.

Study population and variables

The study involved hospitalisations with COVID-19 infection as the principal diagnosis using ICD-10 diagnostic codes (U.071, U.00, U.49, U.50, U.85, J.1282 and B.342). We deemed this as the index admission. Hospitalisations involving patients less than 18 years old and elective hospitalisations were excluded. We excluded December hospitalisations from the 30-day readmission analysis and October–December hospitalisations from the 90-day readmission analysis, due to lack of an adjoining period to assess readmission. Using the index admission and unique hospitalisation identifiers available in the NRD, we identified one subsequent hospitalisation within 30 and 90 days. This was tagged as readmission. Elective and traumatic admissions were excluded from readmissions.

The NRD includes variables on patient demographics, including age, sex, median household income (income quartiles referred to patients as 1-low income, 2-middle income, 3-upper middle income and 4-high income) and primary payer. It also contains hospital-specific variables including bed size, teaching status and location. We assessed the comorbidity burden using the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI).12 13

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the rate and reasons for cardiovascular causes of readmission, defined by ICD-10 codes, ranging from I00 to I99, indicating ‘diseases of the circulatory system’. Secondary outcomes included assessment of readmission mortality, mean length of hospital stay, total hospital charges, cost of hospitalisation (COH) and independent predictors of 30-day and 90-day all-cause readmissions. The COH was obtained from the cost-to-charge ratio data provided by HCUP.

Statistical analysis

We analysed the data using Stata V.17 software (StataCorp). We conducted all the analysis using the weighted samples for national estimates in adjunct with HCUP regulations for using the NRD. Age was grouped as 18–44 years representing young adults, 45–64 years representing middle-aged adults and 65 years above representing the elderly. We calculated comorbidities as proportions of the cohorts and used the χ2 test to compare characteristics between males and females. We employed a univariable prescreening model to identify variables associated with readmissions related to cardiovascular causes following COVID-19 hospitalisations to obtain independent predictors of 30-day and 90-day all-cause readmission. We screened age categories, sex, hospital location, hospital teaching status, hospital bed size, mean household income and the 31 ECI comorbidities. We included those variables having a p<0.1 in the final multivariable regression analysis. Subsequently, we ran a multivariable Cox regression analysis to identify independent predictors of readmissions with p values<0.05 set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Patient and public involvement

This study uses the NRD, which is a publicly available all-payer inpatient healthcare readmission database in the USA, drawn from the AHRQ HCUP SID.

Data availability statement

The NRD is a large publicly available all-payer inpatient care database in the USA, containing data on more than 18 million hospital stays. Its large sample size provides sufficient data for analysis across hospital types and the study of readmissions for relatively uncommon disorders and procedures.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Among 1 024 492 index hospitalisations with a primary diagnosis of COVID-19 infection in the 2020 NRD database, 644 903 (62.9%) were included in the 30-day readmission analysis, and 418 122 (40.8%) were included in the 90-day readmission analysis. Of patients involved in the 30-day analysis, 7140 (1.1%) patients had a readmission within 30 days; and of patients involved in the 90-day analysis, 8379 (2.0%) had a readmission within 90 days due to primarily cardiovascular causes. Among patients who were readmitted in 30 days, 3199 (44.8%) were female and 3941 (55.2%) were male. Females had a higher mean age (70.4 vs 66.8 years, p<0.001), Medicaid use (74.9% vs 63.8%, p<0.001), pulmonary circulation disorders (32.7 vs 25.4, p<0.001); however, a lower ratio of peripheral vascular disorders (10.8 vs 13.8, p=0.016), alcohol abuse (0.5% vs 4.0%, p<0.001) and drug abuse (1.7% vs 5.1%, p<0.001). Readmissions

The overall cardiovascular-cause 30-day readmission rate for the entire cohort was 1.1%. Index hospitalisations had a higher rate of in-patient mortality (11.6% vs 4.3%, p<0.001) and mean length of stay (8.1 vs 6.3 days, p<0.001). Among patients that were readmitted in 30 days, similar mortality rates and mean length of stay were observed among females and males (3.9% vs 4.6%, p=0.28 and 6.3 days vs 6.2 days, p=0.17, respectively). When compared with males, females had lower mean total hospitalisation costs (US$15 670 vs US$17,471, p=0.012). Thirty-day readmissions with primarily cardiovascular causes correspond to a total length of stay of 44 767 days and a total hospitalisation cost of US$1 190 000 000.

Cox regression analysis adjusting for confounders revealed that female sex (aHR 0.89; 95% CI 0.82 to 0.95; p=0.001) and private insurance (aHR 0.89; 95% CI 0.82 to 0.95; p=0.001) was associated with lower hazards of 30-day readmission from cardiovascular causes; however, congestive heart failure (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 2.2 to 2.72; p<0.001), cardiac arrhythmias (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 2.2 to 2.72; p<0.001) and valvular disease (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 2.2 to 2.72; p<0.001) had higher hazards, demonstrated in forest plot in figure 1. Discussion

Our study demonstrated that cardiovascular readmissions are common in patients who were hospitalised with COVID-19 infection, which carries an important burden for the healthcare system in the USA. Cox regression analysis revealed that females have a lower risk of cardiovascular causes for 30-day readmission (aHR 0.89; 95% CI 0.82 to 0.95; p=0.001), and the most common causes for cardiovascular readmissions after COVID-19 are heart failure, venous thromboembolism, stroke and AF. These findings are comparable to previous studies, which signify the short-term and long-term effects of COVID-19 on the cardiovascular system.14–16 A study conducted by Abdeldayem et al looked at cardiac MRI in 86 patients with cardiac symptoms and COVID-19 positivity, 52 patients had a radiological diagnosis of myocarditis-like acute cardiac syndrome, 23 had Takotsubo cardiomyopathy and 11 had acute coronary syndrome due to coronary vasculitis or oxygen demand mismatch.17 Another study by Tudoran et al evaluated 383 patients recovering from COVID-19 who had persistent symptoms and discovered abnormalities in transthoracic echocardiogram in 102 (26.6%) patients; the most frequent pattern was diastolic dysfunction with normal left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).18 Ruge et al presented 60-day readmission data following COVID-19 infection, involving 1406 patients who survived the index hospitalisation. In this trial, LVEF<50% or >65% was not associated with readmissions, however, discharge disposition (acute rehab, long-term acute care or skilled nursing facility) was predictive of 60-day readmission, compared with those who were discharged home.19

The most common cause for 30-day readmission in our study was heart failure (31.3% in females and 30.5% in males), which could be related to myocarditis, myocardial involvement, myocardial injury, sequelae of COVID-19 infection, with or without the presence of established heart failure.14 20 The presence of heart failure was a significant risk factor for 30-day readmissions in our study (aHR 2.45; 95% CI 2.20 to 2.72; p<0.001). One study investigated cardiac MRI findings of 100 patients recently recovered from COVID-19, revealed cardiac involvement of the disease in 78% and ongoing myocardial inflammation in 60% of the patients.21 Another study by Tuvali et al suggested that male sex was associated with a higher risk of developing myocarditis or pericarditis in the setting of post-COVID-19 infection in a large unvaccinated population.22 One meta-analysis conducted by Zuin et al revealed that recovered COVID-19 patients showed an increased risk of incident HF (HR: 1.90, 95% CI 1.54 to 3.24, p<0.001) compared with patients without COVID-19 infection in the same period.23 Zahid et al assessed 30-day readmissions in NRD, both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular causes with the most common causes of cardiovascular readmission demonstrated as acute heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, venous thromboembolism and stroke, which correlates with our findings.24 Our study evaluated cardiovascular causes of readmissions and gender disparities and causes of readmissions in males and females separately.

Venous thromboembolism events (VTE) were a significant cause of readmission in our study population (22.2% in females and 19.3%), which is comparable to prior studies with COVID-19 patients. A retrospective study evaluating CT pulmonary angiograms between 1 March 2020 and 16 April 2020 in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, reported an overall 24% (95% CI 17% to 32%) cumulative incidence of PE in these patients.25 This finding compares to our study, with PE being one of the most common causes of cardiovascular readmission. One meta-analysis conducted by Suh et al revealed pooled incidence rates of PE and DVT of 16.5% (95% CI 11.6% to 22.9; I2=0.93) and 14.8% (95% CI 8.5% to 24.5%; I2=0.94), respectively.26 A review article by Tudoran et al suggested an increased risk of VTEs during and post-COVID-19 infection, however, bleeding risk may also be elevated, evident with the so-called ‘COVID-19-associated coagulopathy’.27

Cardiac arrhythmia rates were strikingly high in our patient population, both as a risk factor for 30-day readmission (aHR 1.52; 95% CI 1.41 to 1.64; p<0.001) and as a cause of readmission. AF was the most common cause of arrhythmia as a cause of readmission in patients with COVID-19 infection (7.5% in females, 9.1% in males). A review of possible mechanisms of AF in COVID-19 patients suggested an increased propensity of AF due to increased myocardial inflammation, fibrosis, increased tissue oedema and interstitial hydrostatic pressure.28 Handy et al demonstrated that anticoagulation may play an important prognostic role in patients with AF with COVID-19.29

Our study has several strengths. The data were sourced from a large national database, providing a large sample size that enabled us to compare mortality outcomes. The nature of the database allows us to provide insights into the comparison of baseline demographics and hospital outcomes between the genders to statistically significant levels.

There are some limitations to our study. The database we used has limitations related to coding, missing data and dependency on inpatient admissions (does not involve outpatient COVID-19 infections), among others. Also, COVID-19 treatment evolved significantly over the past years. This database includes patients from 2020 hence treatments involved in our cohort could be outdated compared with current management and guidelines of COVID-19 infections. Our methods cannot lead to conclusions in terms of causality, due to the retrospective and non-randomised nature of the study, as there may be confounding factors that are not properly controlled.

Conclusion

Our study identified that in patients who are hospitalised for COVID-19 infection, significant factors such as male gender, and underlying renal, pulmonary, and liver disease are associated with higher cardiovascular readmission hazard. These data can enable the healthcare system to help improvise strategies to minimise readmission.
