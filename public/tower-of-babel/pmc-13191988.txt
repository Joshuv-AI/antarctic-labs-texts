A mixed methods evaluation of mechanisms for facilitation in pediatric primary care
Authors: Jennifer M Boggs, Nathaniel Williams, Claire R Waller, Mallika Pandey, Christina Johnson, Brian K Ahmedani, Celeste Pappas, Katy Bedjeti, Melissa Maye, Courtney Benjamin Wolk, Alison M Buttenheim, Kristin A Linn, Leslie Wright, Matthew F Daley, Dylan S Small, Rinad S Beidas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding how implementation strategies work is vitally important for the deployment of evidence-based practices (EPBs) in healthcare settings. Specifically, mechanistic inquiry provides information on specific targets (e.g., buy-in, saliency) that must be engaged for a strategy to be successful. We used mixed-methods to investigate mechanisms of facilitation as part of a trial to implement S.A.F.E. Firearm, a brief evidence-based practice which includes firearm storage counseling and offering free cable locks in pediatric primary care during well-child visits.MethodsWe used a mixed-methods approach to ascertain mechanisms. Quantitative analyses determined whether clinic-level adaptive reserve (e.g., mechanism) mediated the impact of facilitation (e.g., strategy) on reach (e.g., primary outcome). Adaptive reserve, evaluated via clinician survey, refers to a clinic’s ability to broadly make and sustain change and includes multiple components (i.e., relationship infrastructure, facilitative leadership, sensemaking, teamwork, work environment, and culture of learning). Importantly, adaptive reserve is not the specific capacity to implement a given EBP. Second, qualitative interviews guided by the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, and analyzed using deductive and inductive approaches, were conducted with pediatric clinicians, clinic change agents, and health system leaders to ascertain other mechanisms. Mixed methods integration occurred at completion of both activities.ResultsQuantitative analyses indicated that adaptive reserve did not mediate the effect of facilitation on reach. Qualitative findings illustrated other potential mechanisms of facilitation including: increasing buy-in and saliency to help overcome initial inertia and to support application of training principles in practice; strong collaborative relationships between facilitators and clinics to improve accountability; in-person visits or electronic communications that serve as reminders; reinforcing the why and how of the intervention (sensemaking); and fostering team collaboration to troubleshoot logistical barriers.DiscussionWhile prior studies have found that adaptive reserve was related to facilitation, our mediation analysis did not support this hypothesis. Contextual factors may offer potential explanations, particularly a lower-intensity facilitation in this context commensurate with a smaller practice change (e.g., a brief program intended to take < 1 min), but further study is needed. Our qualitative results offer a potentially new mechanistic model of facilitation to be investigated in future studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00941-y.

Introduction

In healthcare settings, implementation strategies can enhance the adoption, implementation, and sustainability of evidence-based practices [1]. Understanding how implementation strategies work represents the next frontier of implementation science [2]. For example, an implementation trial that demonstrates the effectiveness of an audit and feedback strategy for enacting clinician behavior change is more informative when we understand how it works, so that it can be replicated, adapted, and scaled for new applications [3]. When an implementation strategy fails to produce the intended change in practice, understanding mechanisms is important so ineffective aspects are avoided in the future. Mechanisms provide vital information on specific targets (e.g., workflow design elements, clinician training) that need to be engaged for a strategy to be successful [4]. Given the high variability in implementation strategies, contexts, and evidence-based practices studied within the filed, some have argued that understanding implementation strategy mechanisms is essential for generating knowledge that generalizes to new settings and across different types of practices [3]. In this secondary analysis of a hybrid type III effectiveness-implementation trial of implementation strategies for a brief firearm injury prevention program in pediatric primary care [5], we use mixed methods to explore how an implementation strategy (i.e., facilitation) worked. Specifically, using a sequential mixed-methods design, we tested a prospectively specified quantitative mechanism (i.e., practice adaptive reserve) and leveraged qualitative methods to further probe additional mechanisms.

Testing adaptive reserve as a mechanism for facilitation

Facilitation (also known as practice facilitation) is “a cyclical, dynamic learning process in which facilitators apply diverse strategies through interactive problem-solving in a supportive relationship among healthcare employees that may lead to performance improvement” ([6], pg. 3). It involves specially trained facilitators, working collaboratively with care delivery teams, to implement quality improvement changes for patient outcomes [6]. Facilitators use participatory processes with teams to assist with overcoming barriers, optimizing resources (e.g., electronic health records [EHRs], clinician time), setting goals, providing audit and feedback reports, and fostering a culture of learning and teamwork [7].

Most studies examining facilitation mechanisms have used observational and qualitative methods with very limited use of more robust methods including randomization and control groups. Potential mechanisms identified previously include proficient organizational culture [8]; clinician readiness and motivation [6, 9]; problem solving and tailoring to context [6, 10]; knowledge and attitudes [9]; capacity building [6, 11]; trusting relationship with facilitator [6, 12]; enhancing coherence and sensemaking for the innovation [6]; role clarity [12]; and communication, coordination and routinization [6]. Importantly, there are likely multiple mechanisms that must be engaged for practice facilitation to be successful, dependent on contextual factors. Innovative new approaches such as mechanism mapping can help understand connections between the aforementioned mechanisms and implementation outcomes like reach [6].

Prior to study deployment, we identified adaptive reserve as the hypothesized mechanism of facilitation for this study based on several prior trials in the primary care context [13–15]. Practice adaptive reserve is a composite measure representing some of the aforementioned mechanisms [13–15]. It is broadly defined as an organization’s (or unit within an organization such as a clinic, department, etc.) ability to make and sustain change. It is measured as a composite of relationship infrastructure, facilitative leadership, sensemaking, teamwork, work environment, and culture of learning [13]. Practice adaptive reserve was targeted as a key facilitation mechanism in the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s EvidenceNOW program, which implemented a heart health program in 1500 small to medium sized U.S. primary care practices [15, 16]. They found practices with higher adaptive reserve when dichotomized as the top 25% and bottom 75%, predicted higher uptake of the intervention, maintained quality improvements, and engagement with facilitators despite disruptions, and had lower clinician burnout [17, 18]. Of note, adaptive reserve was not formally tested in this large study, or any other ones to date as a moderator or mediator of facilitation.

Facilitation is theorized to improve adaptive reserve by encouraging teams to clarify roles, supporting teamwork (e.g., protecting time for reflection within a team), normalizing experimentation within the clinic, providing data to clinics to support care quality, and helping teams understand the why & how for practices (sensemaking) [13]. Adaptive reserve is thought to impact implementation outcomes via improved practice capacity and greater ability to engage in and sustain changes associated with implementation efforts [14]. It has traditionally been measured through surveys completed by clinic staff, clinicians, and leaders. Importantly, adaptive reserve refers to a practice setting’s general capacity to enact change; it does not refer to a practice’s specific capacity to implement a particular new intervention [13]. For example, relationship infrastructure, which involves respectful interactions with co-workers, level of trust in leadership and co-workers and work environment (e.g., whether it feels “easy to be at work”) are likely general qualities of a work environment that facilitate making changes in practice; they are not specific to implementing a particular new evidence-based practice. Depending on the intensity of the facilitation, it may be difficult to change these enduring and general qualities of a clinic environment whereas making more specific, targeted changes associated with a specific evidence-based practice may be feasible. This highlights that mechanisms may be specific to a particular intervention, or they may represent a broader capacity to implement innovations [19], where adaptive reserve is defined as the latter. Importantly, no trials to date (including EvidenceNOW) have tested practice adaptive reserve as a mediator of facilitation in a randomized trial to determine whether facilitation actively engages adaptive reserve to better enact newly introduced evidence-based practices.

Our primary research question was whether clinic-level adaptive reserve mediated the effect of facilitation on reach of a pediatric primary care firearm injury prevention program. We hypothesized that facilitation would increase practice adaptive reserve and that improvement in practice adaptive reserve would explain (i.e., mediate) the effect of facilitation on reach. Given the complexity of facilitation as an implementation strategy, we sought to identify other potential mechanisms beyond adaptive reserve via qualitative inquiry, using both a deductive and inductive approach. Interviews were conducted with clinicians, clinic change agents, and leaders (referred to collectively as constituents below). In interviews, we sought to learn additional context that might support or refute our quantitative findings and to identify other potential mechanisms beyond adaptive reserve.

Methods

The present investigation/study

Firearm injuries are the leading cause of death for youth 1 to 17 years of age in the United States [20]. The Adolescent and child Suicide Prevention In Routine clinical Encounters (ASPIRE) trial was an unblinded parallel cluster randomized hybrid type III effectiveness-implementation study testing the comparative effectiveness of two implementation strategies to promote delivery of a brief evidence-based secure firearm storage intervention, S.A.F.E. Firearm [21–23] (See Supplemental Figure) in pediatric primary care. S.A.F.E. Firearm is a universal brief (~1 min) program that includes a conversation between a pediatric clinician and parent about the importance of secure firearm storage and the offer of a cable lock. Importantly, this program does not include screening for firearm ownership due to privacy concerns among firearm owners and is universally delivered. Professional organizations including the American Academy of Pediatrics have issued recommendations underscoring the importance of deployment of such programs [24], however, there is a substantial know-do gap (when there is evidence supporting a practice, but it is not actually happening) for implementation in pediatric primary care. ASPIRE aimed to reduce this gap by studying the comparative effectiveness of two implementation approaches in thirty clinics.

Fifteen clinics were randomized to the “Nudge” condition, which included an EHR reminder in the well-visit documentation template for 5–17-year-olds to deliver S.A.F.E. Firearm. Clinics also received other supportive materials including patient handouts on the importance of secure firearm storage, clinician guides, and a brief 30-min training webinar. Fifteen clinics were randomized to “Nudge +,” which included everything in the Nudge condition plus facilitation for 12 months. Facilitators were trained through the Veteran Affairs Quality Enhancement Research Initiative’s facilitation program [25]. For the overall study, the primary outcome was implementation reach, which was significantly higher in the Nudge + clinics with 49% (95% CI, 37–61) of well-visits receiving S.A.F.E Firearm compared to 22% of visits in Nudge clinics (95% CI, 13–31) [26].

Knowing that facilitation effectively increased S.A.F.E. Firearm delivery may be helpful for future replications or expansions of this work, but understanding how facilitation worked is critical to inform our understanding of when and where to use facilitation, and what aspects of facilitation should be emphasized in future applications. In our present mechanistic analysis, therefore, we centered the question of whether clinic-level adaptive reserve mediated the relationship between receipt of facilitation and reach of S.A.F.E. Firearm. We used a clinician survey, administered pre- and post-S.A.F.E. Firearm implementation, to evaluate change in adaptive reserve among clinics who did and did not receive facilitation and we formally tested whether change in adaptive reserve mediated the effect of facilitation on reach.

Setting

The ASPIRE trial was implemented in two large integrated health systems that serve approximately 1.5 million patients—Kaiser Permanente Colorado and Henry Ford Health in Michigan. Both systems are part of the Health Care Systems Research Network [27], which means they employ embedded researchers who conduct health services research and that the care delivery teams are committed to a learning culture of continuous quality improvement. These systems provide adult and pediatric primary care as well as medical and mental health specialty services to demographically and culturally diverse patients in urban, suburban, and rural communities in Michigan and Colorado. The ASPIRE study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania single Institutional Review Board (sIRB), with Northwestern, Kaiser Permanente, and Henry Ford Health Institutional Review Boards relying.

Study design

A mixed methods approach was used with a predominant focus on the quantitative data, (QUAN > qual) [28]. Quantitatively, we used a mediation model to test whether the impact of facilitation on reach of S.A.F.E. Firearm was mediated by clinician-reported adaptive reserve (i.e., evaluated at a clinic-level). Reach was measured by EHR documentation of program delivery in visits. We used a complementarity function, where qualitative information helped explain the “how” of quantitative findings [29]. To integrate quantitative and qualitative results, a merging technique was implemented where qualitative information explains, supports, or refutes quantitative findings.

Participants, study procedures, and measures

All pediatric primary care clinicians (MDs, DOs, NPs, PAs) in both systems were invited to participate in the survey (n = 105). At one of the two systems, family medicine clinicians also delivered S.A.F.E. Firearm; those who had at least 10% of their panel as pediatric patients were also invited to complete the survey (n = 99). A handful of system-level clinician leaders were invited as part of their clinician role, but we did not analyzed these leader surveys separately. The Dillman method was used to recruit clinicians for surveys. Specifically, study investigators met with pediatric leaders in each health system to develop the best recruitment methods within each system to achieve a high response rate. Both systems used an emailed survey that came from the study investigators with supportive messages from primary care leadership in each healthcare system. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of Pennsylvania (T1 survey) and Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine (T2 survey) [30, 31]. REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies,. The questionnaire included approximately 45 questions (exact amount varied between the two surveys and based on skip logic) and took approximately 15 min to complete. Part of the questionnaire administered the Practice Adaptive Reserve Scale [14, 32] to assess clinic-level adaptive reserve. There are six subscales: relationship infrastructure (mindfulness, respectful interactions, heedful interactions, reflection on practice, trust, communication), facilitative leadership (enjoyable place to work, leadership support for practice change, time/space to discuss care improvements), sensemaking (tools and knowledge to accomplish job; focused problem solving), teamwork, work environment (easy to be at work), and culture of learning (mistakes lead to positive change; ease of practice change). There is more extensive information on this scale in the supplemental appendix.

For qualitative interviews, pediatric and family medicine clinicians who delivered well-child care were purposively sampled; we sought to interview a varied sample regarding the clinic in which the clinician practiced, study arm, gender, race, and ethnicity. Additionally, clinicians who reported firearm ownership on the survey were oversampled for interviews to ensure we had adequate representation. Clinic change agents (individuals who were points of contact for a clinic, typically a nurse or administrator) were sampled purposively based on their clinic’s illustrated high and low reach (e.g., EHR documented delivery of S.A.F.E. Firearm). Leaders for pediatric primary care delivery within each health system were also interviewed.

Facilitation

We deployed a low intensity facilitation strategy consistent with the smaller practice change we were implementing (i.e., adding a brief prevention program to a well-child visit) compared to prior interventions that have involved more complex facilitation strategies (i.e., integrated behavioral health/primary care programs [13]). Facilitation staff completed logs of all activities delivered including a description of the activity and the amount of time to complete [33] (i.e., to support a forthcoming cost analysis) at three pre-specified timepoints throughout the study during active implementation (pre-implementation, early, and late implementation). Note, in the sustainment period of the study, facilitation was discontinued, while the electronic health record nudge continued. We do not report on the sustainment period in this manuscript. Practice facilitators met with pediatric teams at the time of trial launch and co-created implementation planning guides that determined clinic-specific workflows, facilitator meeting cadence, and identified any additional supports needed prior to implementing S.A.F.E. Firearm. Facilitation was primarily delivered virtually, but facilitators occasionally completed in-person visits to gain face time with the clinicians, deliver locks, and troubleshoot site-specific issues that arose. Audit and feedback reports were provided to facilitation clinics with monthly descriptive statistics detailing clinician-reported delivery of S.A.F.E. Firearm and offering of gun locks. Facilitators used these reports to target and motivate lower-performing clinics to problem-solve and address barriers to uptake of the program; and to highlight and encourage high performing clinics. There was also a competitive feature because each report showed every clinic’s performance. Facilitators sent out short emails monthly to all facilitation clinic change agents that included clarifications, reminders, and education about how to deliver S.A.F.E. Firearm effectively. The content of these messages was driven by feedback from clinic change agents (e.g., clarifying misunderstanding about who should receive S.A.F.E. Firearm or best practices for documentation). Regarding intensity, facilitators spent about 8.7 h interacting with clinics staff (e.g., emailing, meetings) over 12 months. This time did not include non-interactive activities, like preparing audit and feedback reports or training activities. Table 1 shows the facilitation activities (reproduced from Beidas et al. [26]) and crosswalks each activity to the targeted component of adaptive reserve. This illustrates the connection between the implementation strategy (facilitation) and the hypothesized mechanism (adaptive reserve).

Data analysis

Quantitative

Our primary mediation hypothesis was that the effect of facilitation on patient-level S.A.F.E. Firearm reach would be mediated by improvement in clinic-level adaptive reserve (i.e., change in survey-reported unit scores pre (T1) to post (T2)). To test our primary mediation hypothesis, we used the product of coefficients approach as applied to multilevel designs [34, 35]. Under this approach, the total effect of the implementation strategy (facilitation) on the outcome (reach) is parsed into an indirect effect, which occurs through the mediator (i.e., via improvement in adaptive reserve), and a direct effect, which is not explained by the mediator. A point estimate of the indirect effect is generated by combining coefficients from two simultaneous regression models, shown conceptually in Fig. 1. The first model estimates the “a” path, which represents the clinic-level effect of the implementation strategy (Nudge, Nudge +) on improvement in the clinic-level mediator (i.e., clinic adaptive reserve) from pre- to 1-year post-implementation. The second model estimates the “b” path, which represents the relationship between the clinic-level mediator (i.e., adaptive reserve) and the binary patient-level outcome of reach (controlling for facilitation). All models controlled for covariates of baseline adaptive reserve (to capture change), health system, presence of adaptation champion (handful of leaders who helped adapt SAFE Firearm for each health system), clinic size, and patient ethnicity. Further model details are described in the appendix.

Mediation model

As a post-hoc analysis, we used the same procedure to test one subscale from the Adaptive Reserve measure as a potential mediator, based on findings from the qualitative interviews illustrating a strong signal that sensemaking may operate as a mechanism for facilitation.

Qualitative

We have more extensive details on the qualitative interviews in the supplemental appendix, briefly, our CFIR-informed [36, 37] qualitative interview guide used open-ended questions to prompt constituents to share their perspectives on acceptability of S.A.F.E. Firearm, implementation determinants, and how our implementation strategies worked (e.g., by asking constituents what aspects of facilitation were most helpful to the uptake of the program) [38]. Interviews were professionally transcribed and were coded by authors CRW and MP in NVivo Version 14. Coders initially double coded 5 transcripts, meeting frequently to bring coding to consensus. After reaching 99.5% agreement on the initial transcripts, coders moved to independent coding of the remaining transcripts. Thereafter, 21% of remaining transcripts were double coded, with excellent inter-rater reliability (Kappa = 0.87). An integrated approach [37] was applied with both deductive and inductive methods. Deductive codes were generated by the study team, practice facilitators, health system leaders, and qualitative interviewers, guided by the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research. Inductive codes were generated and adjusted iteratively by authors CRW and MP throughout coding. Transcripts and findings were not reviewed by constituents. Congruent with a complementarity function for mixed methodologies (e.g., qualitative information explains the “how” of quantitative findings), for the purposes of the present analyses, a refined list of inductive and deductive codes addressing the question “How did Nudge or Nudge + influence delivery of S.A.F.E. Firearm?” are described here. Integration involved 6 meetings between qualitative coders (CW, MP), study investigators (RSB, JMB), and the quantitative team (NW, KB) to discuss overlap between the components of adaptive reserve and qualitative findings. Summaries of findings were then discussed with the larger team of investigators and facilitation staff.

Clinic-level adaptive reserve as a mediator of facilitation

We found no evidence that improvement in practice adaptive reserve mediated the effect of facilitation on S.A.F.E. Firearm reach (see supplemental Table). In the first model (a path), there was no evidence that facilitation improved practice adaptive reserve from pre to post relative to control (B = 0.037, SE = 0.217, p = 0.865). In the second model (b path), there was no evidence that improvement in practice adaptive reserve was related to higher S.A.F.E. Firearm reach after controlling for condition (AOR = 1.263, SE = 0.357, p = 0.418). Thus, neither the ‘a’ nor the ‘b’ paths of the indirect effect were supported. Mean [SD] clinic-level Adaptive Reserve increased slightly from baseline to 12-month follow-up for both the Nudge (mean change = 1.34, SD = 6.58) and Nudge + conditions (mean change = 2.44, SD = 9.96), though these estimates exhibited considerable variability. Post-hoc analyses tested the sensemaking sub-scale of the practice adaptive reserve measure as a potential mediator, based on integration findings from the qualitative work, but this also failed to produce any evidence of mediation. In the first model (a path), there was no evidence that facilitation improved the sensemaking sub-scale from pre to post, relative to control (B = 0.136, SE = 0.300, p = 0.654) and in the second model (b path) there was no evidence of association between improvement in the sensemaking sub-scale and reach (AOR = 1.18, SE = 0.294, p = 0.511).

Qualitative findings

We completed 38 interviews. The participation rate among invited clinicians was 23% (20/87), 89% (8/9) for leaders, and 36% for clinic change agents (10/28). Of those invited who did not participate, 7 declined while the remaining 117 did not respond to contact. No interviewed constituents dropped out of the study. Of the 10 clinic change agents, n = 4 were from clinics with high reach and n = 6 from clinics with low reach.

Facilitation codes, mechanisms, and quotes

Mechanisms for low-intensity facilitation

Facilitators fostered relationships with and between constituents that increased delivery, often by checking in with constituents via electronic means (e.g., email, Teams messages) and organizing meetings to discuss S.A.F.E. Firearm. Constituents described how these positive, frequent interactions with facilitators and colleagues within their clinic increased buy-in for the program and improved commitment to deliver it. Further, constituents shared that these interactions kept the program salient for them, serving as a reminder to deliver the program. Constituents similarly shared that these increased opportunities to speak with facilitators and colleagues about S.A.F.E. Firearm supported them in sensemaking—in coming to understand the why and how of delivering the program.

Further, facilitators promoted delivery by bringing the S.A.F.E. Firearm training (a 30-min webinar) to life, making it more conversational and grounded in constituents’ day-to-day practices, often by offering personalized synchronous support. While constituents in both Nudge and Nudge + clinics viewed the webinar training favorably, those who received facilitation emphasized that training was highly effective in increasing their likelihood of program delivery. In other words, constituent input underscored how facilitation helped clinicians integrate the initial training into their daily routines. Facilitators further supported their process for coming to understand the “why” and “how” of delivery by giving them opportunities to engage more deeply with delivery preparation.

Finally, facilitators increased delivery by providing logistical support tailored to clinic needs. This tailored logistical support streamlined the S.A.F.E. Firearm program with existing site processes, especially administrative workflows, and made program processes a habitual part of constituents’ routines. Relatedly, constituents highlighted how tailored logistical support further promoted their S.A.F.E. Firearm delivery by mitigating disorganization, thereby lowering their cognitive load.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The null results from the clinician survey evaluation of adaptive reserve (QUAN) meant that integration of qualitative findings to explain the “how” of the quantitative findings necessarily highlighted new potential mechanistic pathways beyond adaptive reserve (see Fig. 2). We had overlap in one component, sensemaking, which was a theme from the qualitative interviews and also a subscale on the Practice Adaptive Reserve Scale. However, in post-hoc mediation analyses that only considered this subscale, we did not observe significant mediation by this component. The other mechanisms were not related directly to any of the practice adaptive reserve subscales.

Discussion

Few studies rigorously evaluate how implementation strategies work. To date, most studies on implementation mechanisms, broadly and specific to facilitation, have been hypothesis generating (e.g., scoping qualitative interviews) or correlational [6, 39, 40]. No studies to date have used mediation modeling in a randomized design as we have done here for practice facilitation and few studies have used mixed methods to engage in prospective hypothesis testing of a specific mechanism (in this study, adaptive reserve) on a specific strategy (in this study, facilitation), as done here.

Our previous findings indicated that facilitation was effective in the ASPIRE trial; in fact, facilitation produced substantial benefit by more than doubling the reach of S.A.F.E. Firearm above an EHR implementation strategy alone [26]. However, our hypothesized mechanism, adaptive reserve, was not changed by facilitation. In other words, facilitation had no discernable effect on adaptive reserve (a path) and improvement in adaptive reserve was not related to higher reach (b path). Adaptive reserve involves a learning culture that is reflective on quality, promotes teamwork and shared responsibility, takes a growth mindset towards change, and is resilient in the face of organizational stress and adversity. These are all general and enduring qualities that may or may not be present at baseline and likely require a substantial force to change. Importantly, adaptive reserve is not a measure of a specific clinic capacity to adopt an evidence-based practice, but a general measure of qualities thought to be important for quality improvement and general adoption of evidence-based practices [19]. Two prior studies in primary care found adaptive reserve was a baseline predictor of better adoption of an evidence-based practice, but the findings from the b path in our analysis (adaptive reserve’s impact on reach) did not support this finding. One other study aimed at improving cardiovascular care in the primary care setting, where facilitators spent 18.2 h per clinic over 12 months (compared to 9 h in our study over 12 months), found that adaptive reserve was not an implementation determinant [41], which is consistent with our findings. The reasons for these mixed findings likely lies in different contextual factors that distinguish these studies.

A key difference for our study compared to work that found adaptive reserve to be predictive of success was that the practice changes were far more substantive in other studies in terms of the amount of practice change required (e.g., implementing a Patient Centered Medical Home) [13, 15]. In the setting of one of those prior studies [13], facilitators spent up to 5 days in person at clinics at the start of the study, then monthly conference calls with each clinic, plus additional time observing clinic practices for data collection. Intensity is particularly important contextual considerations because it drives the cost of facilitation and is heavily tied to recent mechanistic work on facilitation that focuses on “organizational slack,” or the surplus of organizational resources beyond those minimally required to meet demand for services [42]. We used a lower intensity facilitation approach that was appropriate for the smaller change in practice required when implementing S.A.F.E. Firearm and consistent with a lower amount of organizational slack in the busy pediatric primary care environment. The differences in intensity and reduced organizational slack may explain these mixed findings on adaptive reserve – which may assume a larger amount of organizational slack to be engaged, and our null findings. Another key contextual component of this study was that it was conducted in two relatively large integrated learning healthcare systems that have more administrative supports, but also potentially more burdens for physicians than small/medium sized practices where prior studies (e.g., EvidenceNOW) were conducted [41]. Other potential explanations for our null results compared to prior studies might be that our facilitation approach had less in-person contact (i.e., could reduce trust in facilitator) and facilitators working mostly through clinic change agents, instead of directly with implementation staff (i.e., diluted effect of facilitation impact on clinic culture when only 1–2 people targeted per clinic). There may also need to be a different mechanistic pathway for facilitation to be considered with smaller practice changes that our qualitative work helped elucidate (see Fig. 2).

Taken together, these findings suggest that adaptive reserve may be a mediator of facilitation when certain elements of facilitation are present, such as intensity. A limitation of this study was that we didn’t pre-specify a specific mechanistic pathway for facilitation like that described in Kilbourne 2025 (i.e., this study was conceptualized in 2019 and started in 2020). We did our best to stay aligned with our analysis plan while also acknowledging the fast-moving and evolving pace of the field. This supports the call for more explanatory implementation study designs that map out all the components of implementation strategies such as determinants, mediators, moderators, and proximal and distal outcomes [43]. New thinking emphasizes that mechanisms and their interactions with contextual factors are the most important aspect of implementation strategy research because they enable generalizability across EBPs and settings [3]. Mechanistic path analyses could evaluate how low vs. high intensity facilitation works through different mechanisms and how specific intervention or practice-level determinants play a role [3, 44]. We posit that facilitation for more focused behavior change may operate in a microcosm that is less susceptible to environmental stressors, or general capacity for implementation changes within the primary care context; whereas things like general capacity for quality improvement, etc. may impact clinicians’ overall ratings on adaptive reserve. The fact that our b-path was not significant, illustrating that changes in adaptive reserve did not impact reach of SAFE Firearm, supports the call for different mechanistic pathways for lower intensity facilitation. The only existing measure of adaptive reserve (which we used here) evaluates clinic-level qualities [32], not qualities specific in an intervention’s implementation process.

While our quantitative results did not elucidate quantitative mediators driving the impact of facilitation on S.A.F.E. Firearm delivery, our qualitative findings illustrated potential mechanisms that should be considered for low-intensity facilitation efforts. The first potential mechanism is increased buy-in and improved saliency: facilitation helped encourage clinician delivery of S.A.F.E. Firearm by keeping the program on people’s radars. Constituents reported that facilitators helped overcome the initial “hump” of getting started. This occurred by reinforcing training concepts such as those that directly addressed clinician uncertainty about how to sensitively discuss firearms with families. Important context for this theme is that clinicians reported that once they delivered S.A.F.E Firearm a few times, they found that many parents welcomed the conversation and locks, which further reinforced the clinicians’ buy-in for the program [45]. While training addressed how to appropriately broach the subject of firearms for all sites, facilitation made this facet of training more effective, in part through the collaborative learning it fostered. Next, interviewees discussed the power of a positive rapport between the facilitator and the clinician that increased their feelings of accountability for implementing S.A.F.E. Firearm. Consistent with these findings, a recent systematic review of qualitative studies on facilitation illustrated relationship building with facilitators and providing non-technical support (e.g., reinforcing training concepts) as key components, further supporting the need to test these as mediators [46].

An important contextual characteristic of secure firearm storage programs like S.A.F.E. Firearm is that clinician attitudes are generally favorable, but clinicians lack sufficient understanding, time, or administrative capacity to implement these programs with fidelity [47–50]. Therefore, facilitation was most helpful when it supported clinicians’ practical skills, improved sensemaking by helping cement the “why” and “how” of the practice, and relieved time and administrative burdens (i.e., considered organizational slack). An important potential determinant for facilitation activities and intensity may be general attitudes towards a practice change, where positive attitudes may lend to activities that are more efficient and pragmatic (e.g., troubleshoot logistical barriers to implementation like who monitors cable locks, where they are stored, how to keep cable locks stocked in exam rooms) versus taking more extensive in-person time to gain buy-in for a practice change to start.

Limitations of our study include a modest response rate (51%) to the quantitative survey, meaning that some viewpoints may have been missed, particularly those of pediatric clinicians who were less engaged with the program, although we did try to represent these viewpoints in interviews by targeting clinicians and change agents with low reach. The survey targeted pediatric clinicians and MD leaders who were the primary targets for this intervention (e.g., we measured reach via their documentation), but nursing or other administrative staff may have supported implementation (i.e., by restocking cable locks), and we may have missed some viewpoints. The study was large and served a demographically diverse group of families but was situated within two large health systems in two states (Michigan and Colorado). These findings may not generalize to the unique challenges in resource-limited settings, such as federally qualified health centers, or to systems that serve primarily rural [51] or tribal communities [52] with different values about firearm access. Importantly, while the two participating health systems have a strong learning culture [53], the clinics within these systems that participated in this study displayed variable levels of adaptive reserve at baseline (see Table 2), illustrating considerable variation in clinic culture. This also further illustrates that our findings were not due to a ceiling effect (e.g., all clinics did not have high adaptive reserve at baseline). We found limited guidance in the literature for incorporating mixed methods to elucidate mechanisms, with most approaches being entirely quantitative and others being entirely qualitative in nature. Finally, we only looked at reach within the first year of implementation and did not consider reach during subsequent years, additional work on sustainment phases is forthcoming.

Our findings are instrumental in illustrating that engaging adaptive reserve is not a required mechanism for facilitation success. Our qualitative findings elucidate several other mechanisms that may be important but will require more robust future study to test quantitatively. One potential reason that adaptive reserve was not found to mediate facilitation’s impact on reach is that it was lower intensity in this study, compared to prior literature, commensurate with more focused practice change. It is important to further study more efficient facilitation, considering it is less costly and produced robust improvements in reach in this study. Without understanding mechanisms, designing effective facilitation programs in new settings and with different evidence-based practices will remain elusive.
