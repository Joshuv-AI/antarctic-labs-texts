A new nuclear marker, the “VKORC1 gene exon 3 region”, for the identification of Anatolian Rattus spp.
Authors: Derya Çetintürk, Mustafa Türker Duman, Nuri Yiğit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, exons 1, 2, and 3 of the vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1 (VKORC1) gene, which has been used to indicate anticoagulant rodenticide resistance, were analyzed to investigate whether it is an effective marker for the differentiation of black rats (Rattus rattus Linnaeus, 1758) and brown rats (Rattus norvegicus Berkenhout, 1769) distributed in Anatolia. Although cranial and karyological features distinguish these rat species, morphological discrimination is difficult due to the color variations. In this context, on the basis of the data obtained from genetic diversity values, genetic distance values, Bayesian MCMC dendrograms and Median-joining TCS networks, the Exon 3 region of the VKORC1 gene was determined to function in the discrimination of black and brown rat samples, whereas the Exons 1 and 2 regions did not separate the rat samples via phylogenetic approaches. The results proved that the sequences of the VKORC1 gene in the Exon 3 region are useful as a genetic marker alongside mitochondrial and other nuclear markers for identifying both rat species.Keywords: Rattus rattus, Rattus norvegicus, VKORC1, Species identification

Introduction

The genus Rattus (Fischer de Waldheim 1803) originated in southeastern Asia and has spread to nearly all parts of the World through human-mediated activities such as transportation. Two Rattus species (Rattus rattus (Linnaeus 1758) and Rattus norvegicus (Berkenhout 1769)) are distributed in Türkiye.

The difficulty of identifying rodent bone remaining in archaeological sites in the Pacific due to the overlap in size between specimens has been stressed [1, 2]. Robins et al. [2–4] also reported that speciation is very high in Pacific rats, making it difficult to identify species. To date, genetic markers such as Cytb, COI, and rRNAs in mitochondrial DNA, along with nuclear genes, have commonly been used for the identification of rat species and to establish phylogenetic relationships of taxa, and numerous studies have aimed at the genetic characterization, identification and phylogenetic analysis of rat species, mostly using mtDNA markers and microsatellite loci [1–11] in addition to nuclear markers such as IRBP, GHR, BRCA1, vWF and AP5 [6, 9, 12, 13]. The morphological identification of Turkish rats is also difficult and can be performed on the basis of external, cranial, and karyologic peculiarities and allozyme data, as described by Yiğit et al. [14]. Díaz et al. [15] analyzed polymorphisms in vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1 (VKORC1) sequences in some rat species in Uganda and reported that the sequences were distinctive. The VKORC1 gene has been investigated via single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to determine the degree of rodenticide resistance to missense mutations causing amino acid substitutions, including in Turkish samples [16–22]. In this study, exons 1, 2, and 3 of the VKORC1 gene were evaluated in terms of their taxonomic importance as a genetic marker, due to their ability to distinguish between these rat species.

Materials and methods

A total of 93 samples (34 from R. norvegicus, 59 from R. rattus) were obtained from the Ankara University Mammalian Research Collection (AUMAC-http://mammalia.ankara.edu.tr/) (Permission Document Number: 2018-14-81 approved by the Ankara University Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments) and identified according to their external and skull morphologies and karyologies following Yiğit et al. [14]. DNA isolation was performed with the GeneAll® ExgeneTM Tissue SV mini kit (Atlas Biotechnology, Türkiye) using liver, kidney, and heart tissues. PCR was performed via VKRC1ex1 primers (Forward: 5’-ATTCCTAGCTGTCACGCCTAA-3’; Reverse: 5’-CCTCCGCCAATCTTCCAATC-3’); VKRC1ex2 primers (Forward: 5’-TGGAGCTTCTTGCTAATCACTT-3’; Reverse: 5’-AGCCACGGTTACACAGAGA-3’); VKRC1ex3 primers (Forward: 5’-CCTCCTGCCTTTGCTTCTTG- 3’; Reverse: 5’ -GGACCC ACACACGATACACT-3’) designed in the Primer3 input (version 0.4.0) program (http://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3-0.4.0/). Electrophoresis was performed in a 0.8% agarose gel for 1 h at 70 V in 1×TAE. The PCR bands were visualized via the SYNGENE Bio Imaging System. Sanger sequencing process was performed by BMLabosis (Ankara, Turkiye) and funded by Ankara University Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit (Project no: 18B0430005) within the scope of anticoagulant resistance study published by Yiğit et al. [19].

The sequences were displayed and controlled in Chromas Lite 2.1.1 (http://www.technelysium.com.au) and aligned in MEGAX [23] with twenty R. rattus sequences (accession numbers: LC270887.1-LC270890.1, LC164808.1, LC164809.1-LC164812.1, HM181980.1, HM181981.1, HM181981.3-HM181985), one Rattus tanezumi (JN129981.1), one Rattus tiomanicus (JN129977.1) and one outgroup (Mus musculus, accession number: NM_178600.2) sequence. Amplified intron regions were removed from the ENSEMBL reference VKORC1 sequence (ENSRNOG00000050828, R. norvegicus), and final alignment data (Exon 1: 182 base pairs (bp), Exon 2: 110 bp, Exon 3: 400 bp) were generated. K80 (Exon 1) [24], GTR (Exon 2) [25] and HKY (Exon 3) [26] were selected as appropriate evolutionary models on the basis of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) with the help of jModelTest 2.1.7 [27, 28]. Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (Bayesian MCMC) dendrograms were also generated in Beast 1.75 [29]. Five independent runs of 10,000,000 iterations each were performed, and a total of 5000 burn-in data points were eliminated. The accuracy of the Bayesian MCMC analyses was evaluated via Tracer 1.5 (http://beast.bio.ed.ac.uk/Tracer), and analysis results were considered when the ESS (effective sample size) values were 200 or greater. FigTree 1.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree) was used to visualize the Bayesian MCMC dendrograms. Median-joining TCS networks were established via TCS v.1.21 software [30] by selecting default options and discounting the outgroup. The mean genetic distance values were calculated on the basis of the K80 [24] parameter with 1000 replications in MEGAX [23]. Genetic diversity values such as haplotype and nucleotide diversity, polymorphic sites and mutations were defined via DNASP v6 software [31].

Results

Exon 1 and exon 2 results

According to the 182-base paired Exon 1 analyses, only 6 haplotypes were defined (black rat: 2, brown rat: 1, R. tanezumi: 1, common haplotype (black and brown rats): 1, common haplotype (black rat and R. tiomanicus): 1), and these haplotypes were clustered in the Bayesian MCMC dendrogram and Median-joining TCS Network.

A total of 110 base-paired Exon 2 region analyses revealed that while 9 of the 23 haplotypes belong to black rats and 11 belong to brown rats, 1 haplotype is shared by R. tanezumi and R. tiomanicus, and 2 haplotypes are shared by black and brown rats. Similar to the Exon 1 results, the haplotypes were clustered in the Bayesian MCMC dendrogram and Median-joining TCS Network. Because these regions did not split the rat species, the data from Exons 1 and 2 were not considered in this study.

Exon 3 results

With respect to genetic diversity values (Table 1), Turkish black rats presented a total of 12 haplotypes, two of which were shared with samples from India and Uganda. The Hd value was 0.711, and the Pi value was 0.003. The number of polymorphic sites was 10, the number of mutations was 10, the number of parsimony-informative sites was 5, and the number of singleton sites was 5. In addition, Turkish brown rats presented 6 haplotypes, and the Hd and Pi values were 0.276 and 0.001, respectively. The number of polymorphic sites and number of mutations were 12, while the number of parsimony-informative sites was 1, and the number of singleton sites was 11. From a phylogenetic perspective, the mean genetic distance values (d) based on the K80 parameter [24] were found to be low in both species, as expected for the VKORC1 gene (Table 2). Rattus species were completely separated according to the Bayesian MCMC dendrogram and Median-joining TCS network (Figs. 1 and 2). R. norvegicus formed a basal group separate from R. rattus, R. tanezumi and R. tiomanicus, whereas R. rattus, R. tanezumi and R. tiomanicus clustered as phylogenetically closely related species. However, no intraspecific differences in terms of location or geographical region were detected in either R. rattus or R. norvegicus. Mutations that separate these species phylogenetically existed in 22 species-specific sites in the sequences. The results proved that the VKORC1 gene exon 3 region is a useful nuclear marker alongside mitochondrial markers for identifying both species.

Discussion

It is clear that Murinae evolved in southern Asia approximately 20 Mya and then spread to other continents [3, 6, 13, 32]. Within this time interval, the genus Rattus diverged approximately 2–3 Mya [33], and approximately 65 species have been described in this taxonomically complex genus [34]. Black and brown rats were probably disseminated in the Middle East and continental Europe by cargo shipping during Neolithic times (12,000 AC). These rats are also considered invasive alien species and were identified as accidentally introduced, and their origins and zoonotic importance were explained in detail [33, 35–38]. Many researchers have noted the difficulties in identifying rat species and have used genetic markers for this purpose [1, 2, 4, 8, 39].

The actual recorded locations of the rat species in Turkiye were given by Yiğit et al. [40]. While R. rattus is distributed throughout Türkiye, R. norvegicus has been mostly recorded in the northern part of the country. In the recorded locations of R. norvegicus in the Mediterranean provinces of Türkiye, which is an international port area [41], we captured some samples from the same locations that were morphologically reminiscent of R. norvegicus. However, we identified these samples as R. rattus according to the karyological and cranial peculiarities described in Yiğit et al. [14]. This is a simple example of the difficulties that arise in the identification of two species considering the complexity of the genus Rattus. For this reason, attempts to utilize genetic markers are becoming increasingly important for species identification.

At this point, it is important to use genetic markers to identify species, and researchers have focused on different markers to develop DNA-based methods for species identification. Matisoo-Smith and Allen [1] noted that morphological characteristics are useless for distinguishing Pacific rat remains and instead distinguished black rat (R. rattus) and Pacific rat (Rattus exulans) remains by RFLP (restriction fragment length polymorphism). Jalal et al. [8] distinguished black, brown and Himalayan rats (Rattus pyctoris) with RFLP band profiles via the usage of mitochondrial markers (16S rRNA and Cytb). Robins et al. [2] reported that COI can be used as a barcoding gene for the identification of rat species and that the species were grouped correctly in a phylogenetic tree constructed using COI, D-loop, and Cytb sequences. Additionally, phylogenetic relationships of rat species consistent with morphological designations were generated via the usage of mitochondrial markers such as D-loop and Cytb [4]. Shirako et al. [11], in an analysis of Muridae genera (Maxomys, Niviventer and Rattus) with COI, Cytb and D-loop markers, stated that while the COI and Cytb sequences produce a Maximum Likelihood tree with phylogenetically accurate grouping, the D-loop sequence does not establish a similar tree topology. In the study conducted with COI by Li et al. [10] reported that Murinae species, including black and brown rats, are separated and that the COI can be used for barcoding. However, the DNA-based studies described above have focused mainly on mitochondrial markers. There are very few studies with nuclear genes for the identification of rat species, and these studies are generally focused on calculating divergence times and establishing phylogenies by using nuclear genes such as IRBP, GHR, BRCA1, vWF and AP5 [6, 9, 12, 13]. In accordance with our results (0.7–1.8% distance values between R. rattus, R. norvegicus, R. tanezumi, and R. tiomanicus), Pagés et al. [9] calculated the K80 genetic distance values between Rattus argentiventer, Rattus exulans, Rattus tanezumi and Rattus sakeratensis as 0–1.8% for the IRBP gene.

Iacucci et al. [42] analyzed mutations of the VKORC1 exon 3 sequences in brown rats from a large geographical area, including samples from Europe, Asia and Africa, and reported Hd values of 0.166 and Pi values of 0.00084 with 5 polymorphic sites and 3 singleton sites. These genetic parameters appeared markedly high in Turkish R. norvegicus. Díaz et al. [15] did not observe divergence sites between R. rattus and R. norvegicus, which diverged from each other 2.8 Mya, and suggested that these rats cannot be distinguished on the basis of VKORC1 exons; rather, they distinguished rats on the basis of the mitochondrial marker COI. However, considering the species-specific sites in the Exon 3 sequences of both rat species and after confirming the cranial features, the Turkish species could be clearly distinguished. On the other hand, the reason Exons 1 and 2 did not separate species may be that they yielded short base-paired sequences. As a result, our findings showed that the VKORC1 gene Exon 3 region can be used as a genetic tool alongside mitochondrial and other nuclear markers and that the positions p.107, p.137, and p.143 in Exon 3 differ greatly (>95%) between the two species. On the basis of sequence differences, these species can be distinguished from each other via DNASP v6 software [31]. On the other hand, the accumulation of VKORC1 mutations observed in rodents should be evaluated as an adaptive response to environmental pressure, i.e., intensive anticoagulant rodenticide use, rather than a phylogenetic signal [16, 20, 21, 43–45].
