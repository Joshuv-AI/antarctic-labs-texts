Altered Scaling Dynamics in Liquid Crystal Droplet Coalescence: The Role of Surfactants and Marangoni Stresses
Authors: Wuttipan Satienpaisan, Jutarat Kaewthong, Pimphisa Toikaew, Nattaporn Chattham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the clean-interface limit, the early-stage coalescence of liquid droplets typically follows the standard neck-growth scaling r∝(γR/ρ)1/4 t 1/2 where inertia dominates. When a surfactant is present, the dynamics can depart substantially from the baseline due to additional interfacial stresses, including Marangoni stresses, interfacial crowding, and liquid-crystal elastic distortion. Here, we quantify the temporal evolution of the contact neck during surfactant-laden coalescence and observe a pronounced retardation of neck expansion. The neck radius is described by a power law, but with a reduced prefactor and apparent exponent relative to the clean limit. We attribute these reductions to the coupled effects of surfactant redistribution, transient Marangoni stresses, interfacial crowding or jamming, and liquid-crystal elasticity, which together resist capillary-driven flow and promote arrested or partially merged coalescence.

Introduction

The coalescence of liquid droplets is a fundamental hydrodynamic process underpinning a wide range of natural and technological phenomena, including cloud coalescence, raindrop formation, and inkjet printing. When two drops are brought into contact, a microscopic liquid neck forms at the contact point. This neck expands rapidly as surface tension drives capillary flow, and the merged interface relaxes toward a lower-energy shape, typically approaching a spherical surface. For Newtonian liquids, the temporal evolution of this neck is governed by the balances between capillary driving forces and either viscous or inertial resistance. The dynamics are always viscously dominated at sufficiently early times, as shown by Eggers et al. The system then crosses over into an inertial regime, where viscosity becomes negligible, and neck expansion is set by a capillary-inertial balance. Neck growth in the inertial regime is commonly reported to follow the capillary–inertial scaling , r(t)∝t 1/2. Numerous experiments and simulations demonstrate that, once inertia becomes dominant, Newtonian coalescence dynamics collapse onto the universal inertial-regime scaling law. Akella et al. used dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) to examine droplet coalescence across a wide range of material–contrast parameters (e.g., viscosity ratio and related dissipative control parameters). A key outcome is that, when the coalescence dynamics fall in the inertial regime, the temporal evolution of the neck follows the universal inertial scaling. However, when the two drops are extremely different in size, the coalescence becomes strongly asymmetric and the neck-growth can deviate from the standard inertial scaling, indicating a limit to the universality of the inertial regime. Ristenpart et al. studied the coalescence of two sessile droplets spreading on a highly wettable solid substrate and reported that the neck-growth is consistent with the inertial scaling. Varying the droplet height and width changes the growth rate, but the scaling exponent remains essentially unchanged. Wu et al. provided one of the clearest experimental validations of inertial regime neck-growth. Using high-speed imaging, they directly resolved the neck contours at the earliest accessible times and showed that the neck radius follows a robust inertial scaling. Importantly, their results demonstrated strong data collapse across different drop radii, and they confirmed the same behavior for multiple Newtonian liquids including water, methanol, and water–glycerol mixtures, indicating that the inertial scaling is largely independent of fluid properties once inertia dominates.

In contrast to the Newtonian case, non-Newtonian droplets can exhibit neck-growth dynamics that deviate from universal behavior. When the neck initially forms, the flow surrounding the neck is highly confined, and the resistance can arise not only from viscous and inertial forces but also from the fluid’s internal structure and rate-dependent rheology. These additional effects can slow neck expansion, resulting in a power-law exponent smaller than the Newtonian inertial value of 0.5, or even leading to arrested coalescencei.e., a regime in which the neck growth ceases, and the droplets fail to fully merge into a single spherical droplet. In polymeric (viscoelastic) fluids, neck growth is accompanied by polymer-chain stretching and subsequent relaxation. The associated elastic stresses counteract capillary forces and can therefore reduce the neck-growth rate compared with Newtonian liquids. Varma et al. , demonstrated power-law neck growth with a concentration-dependent, nonuniversal exponent, with deviations from Newtonian dynamics becoming more pronounced as viscoelasticity increases. Dekker et al. investigated the coalescence of aqueous poly­(ethylene oxide) (PEO) solutions over a broad concentration range in both freely suspended and sessile droplet geometries. They found that polymers can strongly reshape the local neck region producing a distinct spatial structure compared with Newtonian liquids. Reported neck-growth dynamics show some variation. In particular, the growth exponent is close to 1/2 for freely suspended drops and remains around 2/3 for sessile drops. In colloidal/particle-laden suspensions, particles can be swept into the narrow neck, where they accumulate and may crowd or jam. This adds extra resistance to the capillary-driven flow and can reduce the neck-growth exponent. Sudheer et al. studied aqueous cornstarch suspensions in a sessile-pendant coalescence configuration and found that the neck radius follows a power law with an exponent below 0.5 as the particle concentration increases toward a jamming fraction. In contrast, at lower particle loading or more dilute concentrations, the coalescence remains Newtonian-like, with an exponent close to the classical value consistent with the observation reported by Rajput et al.

Liquid-crystal droplets introduce further non-Newtonian effects because of their orientational order and mesoscopic internal structure. In nematic liquid crystals, elastic distortion, surface anchoring, and topological defects can couple to interfacial deformation and influence the local flow field near the growing neck. Although capillary pressure is expected to remain the primary driving force for neck formation, liquid-crystal-specific mechanisms can provide additional resistance, particularly in regions of large curvatures and strong director reorientation. A related example is provided by the works of Klopp et al., , in which the coalescence of isotropic droplets in quasi-two-dimensional freely suspended smectic liquid-crystal films was systematically investigated. Their studies indicate that as the droplet surface area decreases during coalescence, the surrounding smectic layers must reorganize. This layer rearrangement can generate defects or dislocations, introducing additional dissipation that slows the coalescence process. Similarly, Nguyen et al. investigated the coalescence of islands in freely suspended smectic films and showed that smectic-layer ordering can govern dynamics through line tension-driven coalescence.

In surfactant-laden systems, the redistribution of surfactant molecules along a deforming interface can generate spatial gradients in interfacial tension, giving rise to Marangoni stresses that oppose the capillary-driven interfacial motion. During neck formation, the interfacial area and curvature vary rapidly near the liquid neck. If surfactant adsorption, desorption kinetics, or interfacial transport is insufficient to maintain a uniform surface concentration, nonuniform surfactant coverage can develop, producing interfacial-tension gradients that retard neck expansion. Such surfactant-induced resistance has been reported in numerical studies of coalescing droplets, where surfactant accumulation and nonuniform interfacial distribution near the neck generate Marangoni stresses that modify the local flow field and alter the coalescence dynamics. Experimental evidence was provided by Nowak et al., who visualized and quantified Marangoni-driven interfacial flows during the coalescence of surfactant-laden and surfactant-free droplets, showing that the surfactant reduces the neck-growth prefactor and induces a strong convective motion. In addition, numerical simulations by Constante-Amores et al. demonstrated that surfactant-induced Marangoni stresses can produce an interfacial rigidifying effect and inhibit stagnation point motion during coalescence. These studies support the interpretation that surfactant concentration gradients can provide additional hydrodynamic resistance to capillary-driven neck growth.

At sufficiently high surfactant concentrations, coalescence can become arrested, where the neck growth stops before complete relaxation to a spherical droplet. Similar arrested states have been reported in Pickering and structured droplets. Although Marangoni stresses are expected to be transient, the localized surfactant adsorption layer near the neck region can reduce interfacial mobility and act as an additional resistance to the capillary flow that normally drives neck expansion. In addition, liquid crystal elasticity, including distortion and anchoring at the interface, may further resist droplet relaxation.

Despite these advances in complex fluids, the coupling between surfactant adsorption and the intrinsic structural resistance of nematic interfaces remains poorly understood. This leaves a fundamental question unresolved: how do surfactant-induced Marangoni stresses and localized interfacial jamming compete with capillary forces to alter the early-stage coalescence scaling of structurally anisotropic droplets? While the effects of surfactants on simple Newtonian interfaces are well-documented, the localized crowding of surfactants like cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) on a nematic liquid crystal (5CB) interface introduces nontrivial, highly confined flow resistance that classical models cannot predict.

To address this gap and define the limits of classical scaling laws, we investigate the early-stage coalescence of 5CB droplets in the presence of the cationic surfactant cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB). CTAB is widely recognized for its potential to tune the alignment of 5CB nematic liquid-crystal droplets. However, in this investigation, we focus on the use of CTAB to investigate the dynamics of droplet coalescence through a system of 5CB nematic liquid-crystal sessile droplet coalescence on a freely suspended liquid-crystal film and to determine how surfactant adsorption modifies the classical Newtonian coalescence picture. Specifically, we measure the temporal neck evolution during the droplet coalescence as a function of time and quantify the resulting power law r(t)∼t b app, focusing on the observed slowdown and reduced exponent compared with the universal inertial scaling. By systematically varying the CTAB concentration and identifying the conditions under which coalescence becomes delayed or arrested, we quantify how the neck-growth dynamics deviate from the Newtonian regime. These deviations are interpreted as being consistent with surfactant-driven Marangoni stresses, which oppose interfacial flow by creating surface-tension gradients and longer-lived interfacial resistance arising from CTAB crowding, together with liquid-crystal effects such as elastic distortion, surface anchoring, and molecule rearrangement near the growing neck. The experimentally measured neck evolution is then compared with computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to obtain a physics-based approximation of the system behavior. Simulations are carried out in ANSYS Fluent using a multiphase formulation for droplet coalescence, where the Volume of Fluid (VOF) method captures the evolving droplet interface and allows for the direct extraction of the time-dependent neck radius for comparison with experiments.

Methodology

4-Cyano-4′-pentylbiphenyl (5CB) was selected as the specimen for generating microdroplets due to its well-established properties as a nematic liquid crystal with a high degree of sensitivity to external influences, such as surfactants. In its nematic phase, 5CB maintains a well-defined molecular alignment over a broad temperature window of approximately 22.5–35 °C, thereby providing a stable phase under the typical room temperature conditions. For the substance preparation, 5CB was mixed with Cetyltrimethyl Ammonium Bromide (CTAB) with bulk concentrations of 0.1–1.0 mM, covering conditions from below the critical micelle concentration (CMC) to near the CMC, which is reported to be 0.9–1.0 mM for CTAB in pure water at room temperature. , This concentration range allows us to examine coalescence at different surfactant aggregations and to quantify the temporal evolution of the liquid neck during coalescence. MX12805, a smectic C* liquid crystal, was used as a wetting substrate in the sessile droplet coalescence setup, allowing for the easy formation of microdroplets. In this system, MX12805 serves as the surface on which the sessile droplets are suspended, and it is used to account for the contact angle of the sessile droplet system.

In the system of sessile droplet coalescence, the contact angle is a significant parameter that characterizes the wetting properties of the droplet and the interfacial interaction between the liquid and the substrate material. To accurately determine this angle, contact angle measurement instruments or drop shape analyzers are commonly employed. These instruments typically consist of a syringe for precise droplet deposition and a high-resolution camera to capture the shape of the droplet in real time. The mixture of 5CB and CTAB dissolved in DI water was prepared and filled into the syringe, the syringe was then used to inject a droplet of this mixture onto the MX12805 substrate. Upon deposition, the 5CB droplet formed, with CTAB molecules aggregating at the liquid–gas interface, modifying the surface properties of the droplet. The CTAB aggregation at the surface influences the surface tension, which affects the shape of the droplet. The video capture of the droplet, including the curvature at the contact point with the substrate, is then analyzed to calculate the contact angle. The measurement varies significantly with changing CTAB concentration, as the concentration influences the extent of surfactant adsorption at the liquid–gas interface, leading to a modest alteration in the surface tension and wetting behavior. The capture of the droplet shape can be further analyzed to determine the surface tension using a nonlinear regression method. The method begins with applying the Laplace equation, relating the surface tension (γ) with the radii of curvature (R i ) at any point of the droplet surface, resulting in the following expression 1 bRi+bsin⁡θx=2+βzb where β = gρb 2/γ, b is the radius of curvature at the droplet apex, θ is the angle of the radii of curvature at any point on the droplet shape, g is the gravitational acceleration, and ρ is the density of the droplet. The x and z are the Cartesian coordinates at any point of the droplet surface, described by d̵x = R i cos θd̵θ and d̵z = R i sinθd̵θ, respectively. To calculate the surface tension between the droplet and its surroundings, β and b need to be optimized. This is done by minimizing the measured droplet shape coordinates among the iteration measurements to obtain the best fit parameters for β and b. The surface tension can be calculated using the relationship between these parameters and the physical properties of the droplet.

The experimental setup for observing the coalescence of sessile 5CB droplets mixed with CTAB on a freely suspended MX12805 film is shown in Figure . The procedure begins with the preparation of the film substrate, which is carried out in an INSTEC chamber designed to ensure stable and reproducible film fabrication. The chamber operates as a closed, controlled environment with temperature regulation to maintain a constant temperature throughout the experiment, which is essential to stabilize the liquid-crystal phases and to maintain consistent surfactant behavior. Accordingly, the temperature was maintained at 29 °C to preserve the target phases of both 5CB and MX12805. This operating temperature is also above the Krafft temperature of CTAB, approximately 26 °C. The Krafft temperature is the minimum temperature at which the solubility of an ionic surfactant increases to the critical micelle concentration, enabling micelle formation in the aqueous phase. By maintaining the temperature above this threshold, the CTAB surfactant self-assembles into micelles, which is essential for modulating the surface tension. Initially, the experiment began by preparing a thin freely suspended MX12805 film by spreading a small amount of MX12805 across a circular hole using a film spreader. After the film fabrication process, 5CB-CTAB droplets were generated using an ultrasonic humidifier and deposited onto the freely suspended MX12805 film. During deposition, the water carrier evaporated, leaving only the 5CB–CTAB droplets on the film. This method generates microdroplets uniformly distributed across the film with a controlled size distribution, typically around 4–6 μm in diameter. Upon settling on the surface, surface tension and molecular director fluctuations drive the droplets to spread and conform to the underlying film. Over time, neighboring droplets migrate toward one another and interact as the system seeks to minimize surface energy, eventually resulting in droplet coalescence. The entire process was observed under a polarized light microscope using a 10x objective lens, and the coalescence dynamics were recorded with a CMOS high-speed camera to resolve evolutions in droplet shape, size, and interactions. To examine CTAB redistribution during coalescence, fluorescence analysis was conducted by incorporating a small amount of Rhodamine-B, approximately 10–20 μM, into the 5CB/CTAB mixture. Fluorescence excitation was achieved using a 532 nm laser beam with a maximum power of 200 mW, allowing spatial changes in the fluorescence intensity to be monitored during the coalescence process. An in-depth analysis of the experiment, including the contact angle measurement, surface tension calculations, and temporal liquid neck evolution during the coalescence was performed using Python. A range of data processing libraries, such as OpenCV and NumPy, were employed to extract and process the information from the recorded video. To investigate neck-growth dynamics, the temporal evolution was fitted using a power-law model r(t) = at b over a selected time window. This fitting window was chosen after the initial linear-growth region and before the late-stage plateau or arrested state, where the data exhibited an approximately power-law behavior. The resulting prefactor, which reflects the overall neck-growth rate and the exponent factor, which captures how strongly the neck expansion accelerates, were used as compact descriptors of the coalescence dynamics at different CTAB concentrations.

Finite-volume simulations were performed to compare with the experiment by resolving the early-stage neck formation and subsequent neck growth during droplet coalescence. The fluid flow was modeled by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, while the evolving liquid–air interface was captured using the Volume of Fluid (VOF) formulation, which governs the transport of the phase fraction field to reconstruct the interface at each time step. Capillary-driven flow was included in the multiphase model by introducing the surface tension as a force acting at the interface. The computational domain was initialized with two identical droplets placed on a wetting substrate by using the experimentally measured droplet size and contact angle. Boundary conditions consisted of a no-slip condition at the substrate and stress-free conditions at the outer boundaries to represent the surrounding air. To accurately capture the rapid change during neck initiation, the mesh was locally refined in the anticipated coalescence region, providing sufficient resolution of the thin-neck region. Small time steps were used at the beginning of the simulations to ensure the numerical stability. This allowed the neck radius to be extracted and compared to experimental measurements of its evolution during droplet coalescence.

Model Formulation

We consider two identical sessile 5CB microdroplets in the presence of CTAB-laden interfaces with an initial radius R resting on a wetting substrate, which is a freely suspended film in the experiment, with an equilibrium contact angle θ, as schematically illustrated in Figure . The surrounding environment is therefore multiphase, consisting of 5CB liquid-crystal droplets, the surrounding air phase, CTAB-laden interfaces, and the freely suspended MX12805 liquid-crystal film acting as the wetting substrate. Coalescence begins when two droplets come into close proximity, and the thin liquid film between their approaching interfaces drains and ruptures, forming a microscopic liquid neck at the first point of contact. The system is modeled as an incompressible fluid, in which the velocity field u and pressure p satisfy the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations for conservation of mass and momentum, as follows 2a ∇·u=0 2b ρ(∂u∂t+u·∇u)=−∇p+μ∇2u+f where ρ and μ are the density and viscosity of the droplet phase, respectively. The term f represents an effective additional force that accounts for interfacial and liquid-crystal resistance mechanisms. It is written phenomenologically as 3 f=fM+fcrowd+fLC where f M denotes the Marangoni contribution arising from surface-tension gradients associated with nonuniform CTAB distribution, f crowd represents the effective resistance due to CTAB-induced interfacial crowding or rigidification, and f LC accounts for liquid-crystal effects, including elastic distortion, surface anchoring, and possible molecule rearrangement near the growing neck.

In the clean interface limit, focusing on the early-time inertia-dominated regime, the neck expansion is primarily driven by the capillary (Laplace) pressure associated with the highly curved neck, characterized by the surface tension γ, while viscous effects and external forces are negligible. Immediately after rupture, the neck radius r(t) is much smaller than the droplet size (r≪R), so the neck region is highly curved. The capillary pressure driving flow into the neck can be estimated from the characteristic curvature of the meniscus neck, yielding ΔP ∼ γR/r 2. By balancing capillary pressure with fluid inertia, , this gives 4 ρ(drdt)2∼γRr2

In the surfactant-laden interface limit, the interface can no longer be treated as having a constant surface tension. Instead, the dynamics must be coupled to the surface surfactant concentration Γ, which evolves due to advection, diffusion, and interfacial deformation. This is described by the surfactant transport equation, , as follows 5 ∂Γ∂t+∇s·(Γus)+Γ(∇s·n)(u·n)=DΓ∇s2Γ+jn

Here, Γ is the surface surfactant concentration, u s is the tangential interfacial velocity, n is the unit normal to the interface, D Γ is the surface diffusivity, and j n represents adsorption/desorption exchange with the surrounding phase. The terms in the transport equation account for local temporal variation, surface advection, interfacial dilation, or compression associated with the normal motion of a curved interface, surface diffusion, and exchange with the bulk phase. The presence of surfactants modifies the surface tension through an interfacial equation of state. Following a Langmuir finite-packing model, , the surface tension γ­(Γ) is related to the surface pressure Π­(Γ) by 6 γ(Γ)=γ0−Π(Γ) where γ0 is the clean-interface surface tension and Γ is the surface surfactant concentration. The additional force term (3) in momentum eq can be decomposed phenomenologically into contributions from Marangoni stresses, interfacial crowding or rigidification, and liquid-crystal elastic distortion. The Marangoni contribution is localized at the interface and arises from surface-tension gradients generated by nonuniform surface coverage 8 fM=δs∇sγ=δsdγdΓ∇sΓ where δ s is a surface delta function and ∇ s γ is the local gradient of surface tension. This expression shows that Marangoni stresses are present only when ∇ s γ≠0. If the surfactant redistributes and ∇ s Γ→0, then fM→0, indicating that the Marangoni contribution is expected to be transient during neck growth.

With the CTAB accumulation, interfacial crowding or rigidification provides an additional resistance to neck expansion. This contribution can be expressed through the divergence of a surface stress tensor 9 fcrowd=δs∇s·σs where σ s is the surface viscous or elastic stress. The general Boussinesq–Scriven-type form is 10 σs=γPs+(λs−ηs)(∇s·us)Ps+ηsDs where η s is the surface shear viscosity, D s is the surface rate-of-strain tensor, u s is the tangential interfacial velocity, and P s is the surface projection tensor.

The liquid-crystal contribution is introduced phenomenologically to represent additional elastic resistance associated with director distortion, surface anchoring, and possible defect formation near the growing neck. In continuum liquid-crystal hydrodynamics, , these effects can enter the momentum balance through elastic stresses associated with spatial variations of the director field. Therefore, the liquid-crystal contribution may be written in an effective form as 11 fLC=∇·σLCel where σLC el is the elastic stress associated with director distortion. In the one-constant Frank approximation, the elastic free energy of the nematic droplet can be expressed as 12 FLC=K2∫|∇d|2dV+W2∫[1−(d·d0)2]dA where K is the Frank elastic constant, d is the nematic director, W is the anchoring strength, and d 0 is the preferred anchoring direction at the interface. This term is included to indicate that liquid-crystal elasticity and anchoring may provide additional resistance to capillary-driven neck growth.

Under clean-interface conditions, corresponding to the classical inertial regime where the surface tension is effectively uniform and the external forces are neglected, the early-stage neck growth follows the capillary–inertial scaling as 13 r(t)R=Ci(γρR3)1/4t1/2=Ci(tτi)1/2 where τi=ρR3/γ is the inertial–capillary time scale and C i is a dimensionless prefactor characteristic of the inertial regime for a clean interface.

In surfactant-laden liquid-crystal droplet coalescence, neck formation may be affected by several coupled mechanisms beyond classical capillary-inertial balance. During the early stage, rapid interfacial deformation can redistribute surfactants toward the newly formed contact region, producing a nonuniform surfactant coverage and transient Marangoni stresses. Molecular simulations have suggested that surfactant accumulation near the first contact point can influence the onset of neck formation. In addition, surfactant adsorption can reduce the effective interfacial tension, increase interfacial resistance through crowding or rigidification, and modify surface viscosity. Because the droplets are nematic liquid crystals, liquid crystal effects may also contribute to the resistance against neck expansion.

For this reason, the measured neck evolution is fitted using an empirical apparent power law 14 r(t)R=Aeff(tτi)bapp where A eff and b app are concentration-dependent fitting parameters representing the apparent neck-growth rate and apparent growth exponent, respectively. The exponent b app is not interpreted as a universal scaling exponent or as a direct measure of Marangoni stress. Instead, a decrease in b app with increasing CTAB concentration is interpreted as evidence of increased overall resistance to capillary-driven neck expansion, arising from the combined influence of surfactant-mediated interfacial effects, liquid-crystal elasticity, and rigidification.

Results and Discussion

To clarify the role of CTAB in modifying the coalescence behavior of 5CB microdroplets on a freely suspended MX12805 film, we first examined the CTAB transport between coalescing droplets using a fluorescence-based control experiment. In this experiment, rhodamine B was used as a fluorescent tracer to visualize CTAB redistribution. Two 5CB droplets containing different CTAB concentrations were used to represent low- and high-surfactant conditions. As shown in Figure , the fluorescence signal was initially stronger in the droplet containing a higher CTAB concentration. After the two droplets came into contact and coalesced, the fluorescence signal gradually redistributed toward the droplet with a lower CTAB concentration. This result demonstrates that migration can be observed between droplets during the coalescence. The observed transfer from the high-CTAB droplet to the low-CTAB droplet supports the interpretation that CTAB is mobile at the interface and can be redistributed during the merging process.

As illustrated in Figure , the relative fluorescence intensity at the neck region increases with time, indicating the redistribution of the CTAB-associated material during coalescence. This time-dependent increase suggests a local enrichment of CTAB at the interfacial connection between the two droplets.

An additional control experiment was conducted using 5CB droplets without CTAB to examine coalescence behavior in the absence of surfactant effects. This experiment was intended to determine whether the slowed or arrested coalescence observed in CTAB-containing droplets originates from the presence of CTAB rather than from the 5CB droplet or the freely suspended MX12805 film alone. In the absence of CTAB, the droplets exhibited extremely rapid coalescence, occurring on a time scale shorter than the temporal resolution of the high-speed imaging system as shown in Figure . As a result, the neck growth process could not be fully resolved. This observation indicates that, without CTAB, the droplet interface remains highly mobile, allowing coalescence to proceed rapidly without noticeable suppression of neck expansion.

To measure how the neck radius, r(t), changes over time during the merging of micrometer-sized nematic 5CB droplets containing CTAB on a freely suspended MX12805 film, we analyzed the coalescence morphology using a high-speed imaging technique. As illustrated in Figure , coalescence is initiated immediately upon droplet contact, where a microscopic liquid neck forms near the apparent contact region and expands rapidly with time. The neck originates at the first point of interfacial connection and then grows outward as liquid is drawn into the neck by capillary forces, progressively reducing the curvature at the junction, and merging the two droplets into a single body. As coalescence proceeds, CTAB-containing 5CB droplets deviate from a complete relaxation. Neck growth slows and approaches a finite radius even at relatively low CTAB concentrations, while higher CTAB concentrations strengthen this arrested or partially merged state, suggesting increased interfacial resistance associated with greater CTAB surface coverage. This behavior indicates that late-stage relaxation is not governed solely by capillary-driven forces but may be limited by coupled resistance mechanisms, including CTAB-induced rigidification, transient Marangoni stresses, liquid-crystal elastic distortion, and surface anchoring. The high temporal resolution of imaging allows for resolving both the onset of neck formation and the subsequent rapid growth, enabling a direct measurement of r(t) throughout the early-stage evolution. Extracting data from multiple coalescence events for each CTAB concentration minimizes droplet size variability and facilitates a reliable comparison across surfactant conditions. Although droplet-size asymmetry was present in some experimental observations, it is not expected to be the dominant cause of the observed decrease in b app. Droplet-size mismatch may introduce minor quantitative variations in neck growth through asymmetric capillary pressure and flow fields; however, this asymmetry did not vary systematically with the CTAB concentration. In parallel, we carried out VOF-based CFD simulations in the Newtonian, constant surface-tension limit to establish a clean-interface baseline for direct comparison with the experiments. In this model, the interface is assumed to be clean with a constant surface tension that is spatially uniform and time-invariant. As a result, effects such as surfactant redistribution, liquid-crystal elasticity, and possible interfacial rigidification due to molecular crowding are neglected. The simulations are parametrized using experimentally measured inputs that define the sessile geometry and bulk hydrodynamics, namely, the initial droplet sizes, the fluid properties, the measured surface tensions, and the apparent contact regions. As shown in Figure , the simulated interface sequences capture the overall progression observed experimentally, including the emergence of a liquid neck at the apparent contact region and its subsequent expansion as the droplets merged. Importantly, the goal of this Newtonian VOF framework is not to reproduce the CTAB-dependent coalescence dynamics but rather to provide a reference response for a clean interface. The simulations serve as a hydrodynamic control, quantifying the neck-growth dynamics expected when capillary forces drive the flow in the absence of additional interfacial resistance. Establishing this baseline allows for the evaluation of deviations observed in the experiment including alterations in temporal neck growth, scaling prefactors, and exponent factors, or arrested coalescence in the presence of CTAB.

Focusing on the inertia-dominated interval, the experimental data exhibits a pronounced and systematic dependence on CTAB concentration. As the CTAB concentration increases, neck expansion is progressively retarded, evidenced by a monotonic decrease in the fitted power-law exponent from b app ∼ 0.429 to b app ∼ 0.338 as illustrated in Figure . Accordingly, at a given time, the measured neck radius is consistently smaller at higher CTAB concentrations, indicating that external resistance associated with interfacial rigidification and liquid-crystal distortion increasingly suppresses capillary-driven flow into the growing liquid neck. This concentration-dependent behavior shows a significant difference from the Newtonian VOF baseline simulation, which prescribes a spatially uniform, time-invariant surface tension, therefore interfacial surfactant transport, adsorption, and Marangoni stresses can be neglected. Under these assumptions, the interface remains effectively stress-free, and the neck dynamics are governed solely by inertial–capillary hydrodynamics. The simulated neck growth is therefore represented by inertial scaling, and the corresponding power-law fits to the simulation data are shown as solid lines in Figure . Consistent with the clean-interface limit, the simulated evolution follows a robust power law with an exponent close to 0.5, exhibiting no sensitivity to CTAB concentration because surfactant-mediated mechanisms are not incorporated in the model.

The viscous-dominated regime is also examined to understand whether CTAB affects the early time dynamics. Both the experimental and simulated data exhibit an approximately linear time dependence consistent with viscous-controlled scaling. This suggests that changing the CTAB concentration does not significantly affect how the system evolves in the viscous regime. Instead, the surfactant only causes a small change in the prefactor at early times, indicating that CTAB does not significantly alter the viscous scaling behavior. A physically consistent mechanism for the inertial-regime deviation is the development of dynamic Marangoni stresses arising from surfactant redistribution during rapid interfacial deformation, liquid-crystal elasticity, and interfacial crowding. As the interface stretches and new surface area is created on short time scales, surfactant equilibration via surface diffusion and adsorption proceeds more slowly; consequently, surface convection toward the neck generates spatial gradients in the surface coverage along the newly formed neck region. In the present liquid-crystal droplet system, these surfactant-induced gradients are expected to act together with liquid-crystal elastic resistance, including director distortion and anchoring effects near the deforming neck. At a higher CTAB coverage, interfacial crowding may also reduce surface mobility and promote a more rigidified or jammed interfacial layer. Through the equation of state (6), γ = γ­(Γ), these gradients produce surface-tension gradients ∇ s γ, which generate tangential Marangoni stresses opposing the capillary-driven flow. The resulting interfacial resistance reduces interfacial mobility and diminishes the effective capillary driving force in the neck region, thereby reducing the neck-growth rate. Because this resistance evolves with the time-dependent surfactant distribution, it can modify both the prefactor A eff and the apparent exponent b app.

The crossover from the viscous-dominated regime to the inertia-dominated regime, illustrated in Figure , is accompanied by a clear retardation of neck growth. As the system enters the early stage of coalescence, liquid must be supplied through an increasingly confined neck region, however, in the presence of CTAB, this flow is no longer governed solely by capillary driving. Instead, surfactant accumulation and interfacial crowding near the neck can generate a resistance that behaves elastically over the coalescence time scale. This resistance can suppress the capillary-driven flow, thereby slowing the expected crossover time as reported by Paulsen et al.

As time progresses beyond the early stage growth regimes, the coalescence dynamics increasingly deviate from the conventional behavior and transition to arrested coalescence. At this stage, neck expansion decelerates sharply, approaching a stationary neck radius instead of progressing toward complete relaxation into a single droplet. Such arrested coalescence is consistent with the development of a mechanically resistive interfacial layer in the surfactant-laden system. During the rapid interfacial deformation accompanying neck formation, CTAB is advected and redistributed along the interface, leading to local accumulation near the neck region where the interfacial area is created and stretched most strongly. The resulting high local surface coverage can induce interfacial crowding and a strong surface tension gradient. Consequently, Marangoni stresses emerge that oppose capillary-driven drainage in the neck region. , Additionally, once the interface becomes densely populated, interfacial jamming can occur, effectively locking the neck at a finite radius for extended times. Consistent with this limit, the normalized neck-radius evolution depicted in Figure indicates incomplete coalescence, evidencing interfacial crowding or rigidification. Moreover, increasing the CTAB concentration systematically reduces the final normalized neck radius, suggesting stronger crowding and a more jammed interfacial layer concentrated near the neck region. Notably, this behavior differs from prior studies that report continued coalescence. , One key difference here is the transport dynamics and the high surface-to-volume ratio inherent to the microdroplet system. Rather than being constrained by the 1D length of the molecule, the arrested coalescence is driven by rapid changes in the local surface coverage and the finite cross-sectional area of the CTAB headgroup at the interface (typically reported as (≈40–60 Å2) per molecule). During the initial stages of microdroplet merging, strong convective flows actively transport surfactant molecules toward the forming neck region. Because microdroplets possess a naturally high surface-to-volume ratio, even modest bulk concentrations of CTAB can establish a substantial baseline surface coverage prior to contact. As the interfacial area at the neck rapidly contracts during the initial bridging, the local surface concentration of CTAB spikes, swiftly approaching the maximum physical packing density. This extreme localized accumulation and subsequent jamming of the surfactant molecules generates strong opposing Marangoni stresses and a rigid interfacial layer at the neck, which acts as a physical barrier that ultimately arrests further coalescence. ,,,

Although convective flow and Marangoni-driven transport dominate the earliest stages of neck formation, classical Marangoni stresses are inherently transient and rely on interfacial tension gradients that should eventually be relaxed. Therefore, they cannot account for the permanent macroscopic arrest of the droplet neck. Instead, the permanent suppression of coalescence can be attributed to the onset of severe interfacial rigidity. Because the local surface area at the neck contracts drastically during merging, the localized concentration of CTAB rapidly eclipses the maximum packing density. This triggers an interfacial jamming transition, effectively transforming the local boundary from a fluid interface to a highly rigid, viscoelastic 2D layer. As the bulk CTAB concentration increases, this jamming threshold is reached at earlier stages of neck evolution, resulting in a narrower arrested neck. This jammed surfactant layer possesses high dilatational elasticity, which acts synergistically with the Frank elastic resistance of the internally anchored 5CB nematic phase to create a permanent mechanical barrier against further capillary-driven expansion.

While the localized jamming and subsequent Marangoni stresses described herein dictate the primary mechanism for arrested coalescence, it is important to distinguish the behavior of the nematic 5CB system from that of an ordinary isotropic oil droplet. In an isotropic system, interfacial flow is opposed purely by viscous dissipation and Marangoni forces. In contrast, the nematic nature of 5CB introduces an additional structural resistance. CTAB adsorption enforces strong homeotropic anchoring at the droplet interface. During the rapid topological evolution of the microscopic neck, maintaining this anchoring condition imposes severe splay and bend distortions upon the internal nematic director field. The resulting Frank elastic stresses serve as an energy penalty that intrinsically dampens capillary-driven flow. This structure-dependent resistance is a defining feature of liquid crystalline interfaces; recent studies emphasize how nematic elasticity inherently stabilizes droplet interfaces and fundamentally alters coalescence trajectories compared to purely isotropic fluids. Furthermore, literature confirms that internal nematic ordering poses a dominant barrier to coalescence, as the complete removal of this structural resistancesuch as heating surfactant-stabilized nematic emulsions above their nematic–isotropic transition temperaturehas been shown to rapidly induce spontaneous coalescence. Consequently, we conclude that the observed arrested coalescence is not solely a conventional surfactant phenomenon, but rather the result of a synergistic coupling between Marangoni-induced surface rigidity and the internal elastic resistance of the highly confined nematic phase.

A further mechanistic distinction can be made between the arrested coalescence observed in the present system and the extraordinary stability reported for surfactant-free bubbles suspended in an ultrasonic field. In the latter case, bubble stabilization is governed by an externally imposed acoustic radiation pressure acting on both sides of the liquid film. This pressure counteracts the hydrostatic driving force responsible for film drainage, thereby substantially extending the bubble lifetime without the need for surfactants. By contrast, the arrested coalescence of the 5CB droplets investigated here occurs in the absence of external acoustic forcing and is attributed to the intrinsic interfacial and elastic resistance. This resistance is proposed to arise from the coupled effects of CTAB redistribution, interfacial rigidification, and liquid crystal elasticity associated with director distortion. Thus, although both systems exhibit prolonged stability, their underlying stabilization mechanisms are fundamentally different.

Conclusion

We investigated the sessile droplet coalescence of nematic 5CB microdroplets containing CTAB at varying concentrations on a freely suspended MX12805 film, focusing on the temporal evolution of the neck radius from first contact to the late-stage arrested state. In the early stage, the neck exhibits the rapid expansion characteristic of conventional coalescence, with an initial viscous-dominated regime that transitions toward inertia-dominated dynamics as time progresses. However, within the inertia-dominated regime, the power-law growth shows a reduced apparent exponent b app, indicating a weakened inertial scaling relative to the classical expectation. The systematic decrease in the apparent exponent b app with an increasing CTAB concentration suggests that surfactant-mediated interfacial resistance progressively weakens the capillary-driven growth of the liquid neck.

This deviation suggests that additional interfacial resistance mechanisms become dynamically relevant in the surfactant-laden system. During rapid neck formation, deformation of the interface and creation of new interfacial area can redistribute CTAB nonuniformly along the droplet interface. The resulting gradients in surface concentration, Γ, generate surface-tension gradients and transient Marangoni stresses that oppose the capillary-driven flow. In addition, increasing the CTAB concentration raises the surface coverage relative to the finite adsorption capacity of the interface. As Γ approaches the limiting coverage, excluded-area effects and dense interfacial packing can reduce interfacial mobility and enhance surface viscoelastic resistance. These effects are expected to be the strongest near the neck, where surface material is convected, compressed, and continuously reorganized during coalescence.

Consequently, as the surfactant accumulates in the neck region, the crossover time can shift away from that expected for the clean interface limit, in which the viscous–inertial transition is set primarily by the balance between capillary driving and dominant forces, yielding a crossover that depends on the liquid properties. In the surfactant-laden limit, however, the effective capillary driving near the neck region is reduced by coupled interfacial effects, including transient Marangoni stresses, surfactant crowding, and liquid-crystal elastic resistance associated with director distortion and anchoring near the deforming interface. With continued coalescence, the interface becomes increasingly populated by surfactants, leading to the progressive buildup of interfacial resistance that suppresses further capillary-driven flow and culminates in arrested coalescence. At this critical packing limit, the interface undergoes a transition from a fluid state into a highly rigid 2D layer characterized by immense dilatational elasticity. Furthermore, in contrast to isotropic oils, this jammed CTAB layer enforces strict homeotropic anchoring upon the nematic 5CB phase. The synergistic combination of extreme interfacial rigidity and the internal Frank elastic energy penalty required to distort the nematic director field at the neck causes a permanent arrest.

The ability to fundamentally arrest the coalescence of nematic microdroplets through strict interfacial jamming has significant practical implications for the design of advanced soft materials. First, it offers a purely physicochemical pathway to formulate hyperstable liquid crystal emulsions that resist bulk phase separation without relying on complex polymeric stabilizers. Second, because 5CB microdroplets are widely utilized as highly sensitive optical biosensors, preventing droplet coalescence is critical for maintaining the high surface-to-volume ratio required for optimal analyte detection. By leveraging the synergistic coupling of surfactant rigidification and nematic elasticity, sensor shelf life and optical stability can be markedly improved. Finally, the capacity to permanently arrest microscopic liquid bridges opens new avenues for fabricating interconnected, anisotropic droplet networks that can serve as structural templates for tunable microlens arrays or responsive soft metamaterials.
