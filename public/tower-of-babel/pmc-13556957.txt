Isotopic tracing in Mycobacterium tuberculosis-infected alveolar epithelial cells indicates metabolic specificity to their virulence and drug-resistance status
Authors: Subia Akram, Shyam Kumar Masakapalli, Ranjan Kumar Nanda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTuberculosis (TB) remains a global health threat aggravated by the emergence of drug-resistant strains. Mycobacterium tuberculosis promotes metabolic remodeling within host cells to support its survival. Type II alveolar epithelial cells have been established as a primary niche for bacterial infection, adherence, and invasion. However, compared to macrophages, metabolic interactions within these cells, particularly the strain-specific exploitation of host metabolism, remain inadequately explored. To address this, we integrated intracellular bacterial growth and host cell viability assays with C-based proteinogenic amino acid kinetics to gain insights into metabolic changes in A549 alveolar epithelial cells infected with laboratory strains (virulent H37Rv, avirulent H37Ra) and clinical isolates (drug-resistant S6 and S11; drug-sensitive S4 and S5). Virulent strain H37Rv showed higher intracellular growth (6-fold increase) but significantly reduced host cell viability relative to avirulent H37Ra. Interestingly, the multidrug-resistant isolate S6 showed the highest intracellular growth (18-fold increase) while maintaining host cell viability. Mass isotopomer distribution analysis revealed that virulent strains showed increased reliance on central carbon metabolism, including glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. H37Rv showed greater 13C enrichment in host amino acids, such as alanine (1.6-fold), compared to H37Ra. Drug-resistant isolates displayed distinct 13C enrichment in TCA-derived threonine (0.21–0.25%), methionine (0.18–0.28%), and lysine (0.12–0.27%). We observed 13C enrichment in essential amino acids (lysine, methionine, threonine, valine) across all infected conditions, confirming the pathogen’s dependence on host-derived metabolic intermediates. These findings highlight the need to explore host-to-pathogen amino acid transport as a precise host-directed therapeutic target.KEYWORDS: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, alveolar epithelial cells, virulence, clinical isolates, drug-resistance, 13C isotopic tracing, metabolomics, central carbon metabolism, proteinogenic amino acids, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent. According to the WHO Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, an estimated 10.7 million people fell ill with TB in 2024, resulting in 1.23 million deaths. The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and, most recently, totally drug-resistant (TDR) strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) has further aggravated the problem [1].

Following inhalation, Mtb interacts with different cell types. While alveolar macrophages are the primary and most well-studied host cells, type II alveolar epithelial cells (AECs) also serve as a crucial niche for mycobacterial adherence, invasion, and replication [2–5]. A higher Mtb replication rate is observed in AECs, and such infection leads to host cell death and impairs the epithelial barrier, potentially contributing to the spread from the lungs [6]. AECs also regulate innate immunity by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and chemokines that interact with immunocytes to activate adaptive immunity [7,8] emphasizing that Mtb exploits AECs as a “safe haven” to expand and further disseminate during primary infection [9].

To support its intracellular survival across diverse niches, Mtb rewires host cellular metabolism. Mtb changes its metabolism from active replication to a dormant, non-replicating state to adapt to the host cellular niches. Upon infection, it accumulates mycolic and fatty acids in its cell wall to survive the hostile phagosome environment [10]. The metabolic adaptations of host cells to intracellular bacteria depend on the specific bacterial metabolic capacities, virulence factors, and the host’s antimicrobial defense mechanisms [11]. To meet its nutritional demands, mycobacteria alter the host’s lipid and cholesterol metabolism [12]. A vast majority of these metabolic interaction studies have focused exclusively on macrophages. Although studies on molecular and transcriptional profiling have provided insights into the response of AECs to Mtb infection [9,13], the differential reprogramming of host cell central carbon metabolism by various Mtb strains remains to be fully elucidated. Drug-resistant strains also exhibit specialized adaptations that facilitate their persistence and virulence within the host [14,15]. A deeper understanding of the survival, proliferation, and metabolic perturbations induced by these diverse strains of Mtb infection in AECs could enhance our understanding of the pathogenesis and immunological mechanisms underlying TB.

Steady-state metabolomics provides a snapshot of metabolite pool sizes but cannot determine the direction or rate of metabolic fluxes. To overcome this, stable isotope tracing with 13C-labeled nutrients (glucose, glutamine, etc.) is increasingly used to map host-pathogen metabolic interactions [16]. By feeding infected cells a 13C-carbon source and measuring the mass isotopomer distributions (MIDs), carbon flow through central metabolic pathways can be precisely traced. This approach is useful for identifying the host metabolic rewiring that intracellular pathogens, such as Mtb, induce to support intracellular survival.

In this study, we aimed to provide comparative, strain-specific metabolic insights relevant to Mtb pathogenesis and drug resistance. Using an in vitro human A549 alveolar epithelial cell model, we performed 13C-glucose kinetic tracing coupled with gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) to analyze cells infected with laboratory strains (virulent H37Rv and avirulent H37Ra) alongside well-characterized drug-sensitive and drug-resistant clinical isolates. We integrated these metabolic profiles with intracellular bacterial growth and host cell viability assays. Our findings reveal that virulent and drug-resistant Mtb strains differentially exploit host central carbon metabolism. Notably, multidrug-resistant isolates exhibited the highest intracellular growth while preserving host cell viability, demonstrating a distinct reliance on host-derived essential amino acids for their survival. These strain-specific metabolic insights highlight host amino acid transport and assimilation as viable targets for host-directed therapies.

A preprint version of this manuscript has been previously made available [17].

Results

Laboratory strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis established infection in the human alveolar epithelial cell line, A549

A549 cells cultured in 2.0 mL Advanced DMEM/F12 flex media supplemented with glucose (U-12C6 or U-13C6) were infected with virulent (H37Rv) and avirulent (H37Ra) laboratory Mtb strains at an MOI of 1:10 (cell: bacteria). Uninfected and heat-killed H37Rv-infected A549 cells were used as controls. Mtb-infected and control cells were harvested at specified time points (i.e. 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 h post-infection, h.p.i.) to monitor different parameters Figure 1(A). Mtb infection impacts host cell viability, affecting disease progression and outcome. The Trypan blue assay showed similar viability between uninfected and heat-killed Mtb-infected A549 cells. Virulent Mtb H37Rv infection led to lower A549 cell viability than the H37Ra strain Figure 1(B). Axenic H37Rv and H37Ra cultures showed similar growth patterns in 7H9 broth media Figure 1. As intracellular Mtb replication leads to cell lysis and release into the culture media, which are lost during washing steps, the CFU data were normalized to the number of viable cells Figure 1. Both H37Ra and H37Rv showed intracellular survival over 48 h.p.i. in A549 cells, and the CFU was significantly higher upon H37Rv infection than with H37Ra at 36 and 48 h.p.i. Z-stack images were acquired by confocal microscopy to assess the intracellular localization of bacteria. Imaging results showed that the bacteria were distributed mainly around the nucleus, close to the mitochondria, in clusters or string-like formations Figure 1(E) and Figure S1. H37Rv Mtb strains exhibited higher infection efficiency on A549 cells than avirulent H37Ra. The number of A549 cells infected with H37Ra decreased from 37.5% to 20.5%, whereas it increased from 58.4% to 78.6% during 12 to 48 h.p.i. upon H37Rv-infection, corroborating the CFU results. As only two biological replicates were used, statistical significance was not calculated Figure (1-F).

Overview of identified amino acids among all study samples

The metabolic interaction between host and pathogen significantly influences the outcome of the infection. Several studies have revealed alterations in host cell metabolism in response to Mtb infection [18–20]. However, during the infection of AECs with different Mtb strains, the variations in the metabolic response, if any, are not studied in detail. Proteinogenic amino acids are stable and present in abundance inside the host cells; therefore, we aimed to monitor 13C enrichment to retro-biosynthetically map the flow of the central metabolic precursors. To achieve this, proteins harvested at designated time points were acid hydrolyzed and analyzed by GC-MS to determine the average 13C-label in the proteinogenic amino acids derived from [U-13C6] glucose Figure 2(A). In mammalian cells, most proteinogenic amino acids, except the essential amino acids, are synthesized from the central carbon metabolism, i.e. glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle Figure 2(B). A total of 16 proteinogenic amino acids (alanine, glycine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, proline, methionine, aspartate/asparagine, glutamate/glutamine, serine, cysteine, histidine, tyrosine, lysine, threonine, and phenylalanine) were identified. Due to chemical decomposition during acid hydrolysis, tryptophan and arginine were undetected. Amino acid-derived mass isotopomer fragments of the 13C‑labeled cells were compared with those derived from the parallel unlabeled (12C control) samples. None of the fragments for leucine and isoleucine were found to be valid, so these amino acids were excluded from the comparative study (Dataset S1). As amino acid pairs like glutamate and glutamine, aspartate and asparagine, could not be distinguished, they were combined as glutamate/n and aspartate/n, respectively.

Monitoring average 13C enrichment kinetics in the proteinogenic amino acids derived from Mtb-infected and uninfected A549 cells provided insights into the central carbon metabolism

Most proteinogenic amino acids showed isotopic labeling post-24 h.p.i. Therefore, we focused on monitoring 13C enrichment (%) until 60 h.p.i. to capture the maximum differences (Figure 3, Dataset S1, and Figure 2). Proteinogenic amino acids (alanine, serine, and glycine) synthesized from the precursor of glycolysis intermediates showed varied levels of labeling between Mtb-infected and uninfected A549 cells. The average 13C incorporation of alanine (m/z 260) derived from H37Rv-infected cells was significantly less than that of the uninfected controls, as well as that of the heat-killed H37Rv-infected cells (Figure 3(A) and Figure S2-D). The average 13C incorporation in serine (m/z 390) was lower in H37Rv- and H37Ra-infected A549 cells compared to HK-H37Rv-infected and uninfected controls (Figure 3(B) and Figure S2-B). This lower enrichment over the 60-h period suggests that live Mtb infection might be suppressing the de novo host serine biosynthetic pathway. Glycine is synthesized from serine as one of the precursors [21], and the proteinogenic glycine (m/z 288) depicted a similar labeling pattern to serine. Uninfected A549 cells showed maximum 13C enrichment, followed by HK-H37Rv-infected cells. H37Rv-infected cells exhibited lower 13C levels in glycine than H37Ra-infected cells (Figure 3(C) and Figure S2-C). The average 13C levels in the proteinogenic amino acids valine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine were lower than the expected natural abundance of ~1.13% in most cases (Figure 3 and Figure S2). However, the contribution of glucose to de novo proteinogenic valine (m/z 288) was significantly more in both H37Rv- (1.35 ± 0.16%) and H37Ra-infected cells (0.59 ± 0.19%) in comparison with the HK-H37Rv-infected cells and uninfected controls at 60 h.p.i (Figure 3(D) and Figure S2-A). Phenylalanine (Figure 3(E) and Figure S2-E) and tyrosine (Figure 3(F) and Figure S2-F) are synthesized from the precursors generated from glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). The Mtb-infected cells acquired 13C enrichment in tyrosine (H37Rv: 1.24 ± 0.18%; H37Ra: 0.66 ± 0.25%), which was lower than that in HK-H37Rv-infected cells and uninfected controls at 60 h.p.i.

TCA cycle intermediates contribute to glutamate/n (m/z 432), proline (m/z 258), aspartate/n (m/z 418), methionine (m/z 320), threonine (m/z 404), and lysine (m/z 431). The 13C‑label incorporation of these proteinogenic amino acids provided insights into the contribution of the TCA cycle toward de novo protein synthesis (Figure 3). 13C-incorporated glutamate/n, and proline were observed in all study groups, and they followed a similar pattern. 13C incorporation into glutamate/n was observed in all four groups, i.e. Mtb-infected and uninfected control (Figure 3(G) and Figure S2-G). Live Mtb-infected A549 cells showed slightly lower 13C labels than the controls (HK-infected and uninfected A549 cells); however, no significant differences among the groups were observed (uninfected: 17.03 ± 1.82%; HK-H37Rv: 17.16 ± 2.83%; H37Rv: 15.14 ± 1.75%; and H37Ra-infected cells: 14.84 ± 1.60%). Proteinogenic proline (Figure 3(H) and Figure S2-H) from Mtb-infected A549 cells had significantly lower average 13C enrichment (H37Rv-infected cells: 9.38 ± 0.47%; and H37Ra-infected cells: 8.43 ± 0.50%) compared to HK-H37Rv-infected (10.68 ± 0.21%) and uninfected (10.56 ± 0.46%) control A549 cells. Proteinogenic aspartate/n (Figure 3(I) and Figure S2-I) had higher fractions of labeled carbon in uninfected (19.15 ± 0.25%) and HK-H37Rv-infected (19.53 ± 0.72%) A549 cells compared to Mtb-infected A549 cells (H37Rv: 17.95 ± 0.37%; H37Ra: 17.71 ± 0.54%). Interestingly, HK-H37Rv-infected and uninfected control A549 cells demonstrated no detectable 13C labeling in any of the three aspartate-derived amino acids, i.e. methionine (Figure 3(J) and Figure S2-J), threonine (Figure 3(K) and Figure S2-K) and lysine (Figure 3(L) and Figure S2-L). These proteinogenic amino acids in Mtb-infected A549 cells showed marginally higher average 13C incorporations in comparison to uninfected control cells. Virulent laboratory Mtb strain‑infected A549 cells showed the average 13C levels in threonine (H37Rv: 0.62 ± 0.10%; and H37Ra: 0.21 ± 0.07%) and lysine (H37Rv: 0.67 ± 0.11%; and H37Ra: 0.18 ± 0.11%). Proteinogenic methionine of H37Rv-infected A549 cells showed similar labeling (H37Rv: 0.38 ± 0.04%; H37Ra: 0.33 ± 0.30%).

The average 13C‑labeling data suggested that glucose contributed to de novo protein synthesis in A549 cells through continued metabolic activity post-infection. These data also suggested that after 48 h.p.i. (and at 60 h.p.i.), variations in 13C levels of several de novo proteinogenic amino acids derived mainly from the pathway precursors emanating from glycolysis (alanine, serine, and glycine) and the TCA cycle (glutamate/n, aspartate, and proline) were captured. Analysis of the mass isotopomer distributions (MIDs) of all the amino acids is further warranted and is presented in the sections below.

Comparative mass isotopomer distribution in proteinogenic amino acids of A549 cells upon infection with laboratory Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains

Next, we evaluated and compared the MIDs of proteinogenic amino acids derived from Mtb-infected A549 cells and controls (60 h.p.i.) fed [U-13C6] glucose (Figure 4 and Dataset S2). The MIDs of the amino acid fragment ions were corrected for the contributions of natural 13C‑isotope abundances. Mtb-infected A549 cells indicated lesser 13C labeling in the serine Figure 4(A) and glycine Figure 4(B), as reflected by the higher fractional M + 0 (unlabeled pool) compared to the uninfected control cells. The M + 3 abundance in alanine was significantly lower in H37Rv-infected A549 cells than in other groups Figure 4. This is also reflected by a higher abundance of unlabeled M + 0 in alanine of H37Rv-infected cells. In infected cells, these observations of higher unlabeled M + 0 (and lower abundances of other isotopomers) in glycine and alanine can be attributed to either a slower rate of de novo protein biosynthesis or to a greater contribution from pre-existing or exogenous metabolic intermediates that enter these soluble amino acid pools via the glycolytic pathways. Contrary to the patterns in alanine, 13C incorporation in valine was higher in both H37Rv and H37Ra Mtb-infected cells than in uninfected or heat-killed controls Figure 4(E). This is reflected by the lower levels of unlabeled M + 0 and higher levels of M + 4 and M + 5. No significant differences in the levels of glutamate/n mass isotopomers were observed between Mtb-infected and uninfected controls Figure 4. However, H37Rv Mtb infection exhibited higher unlabeled M + 0 and lower 13C levels of other MIDs in aspartate (M + 2, Figure 4(H) and proline (M + 2 and M + 3, Figure 4 compared to the controls (uninfected, HK-infected cells). These variations in the 13C labeling of aspartate and proline indicate possible differences in the TCA cycle activity in the Mtb-infected cells. Mtb infection of A549 cells induced significant changes in the MIDs of tyrosine (M + 4, M + 5 and M + 6, Figure 4(F)) but not of phenylalanine (M + 5, Figure 4), which also points to possible variation in the pentose phosphate pathway activities in Mtb-infected cells. Interestingly, in the Mtb-infected cells, the lower levels of M + 0 of threonine Figure 4(G), methionine Figure 4(K), and lysine Figure 4(J) fragments point to the 13C label incorporation, as can be confirmed from the higher levels of other mass isotopomers due to 13C incorporation (threonine M + 3 and M + 4; methionine M + 3 and lysine M + 3, M + 4, and M + 5). These observations suggest the active metabolism of intracellular Mtb to biosynthesise its amino acids, harnessing the metabolic intermediates produced by infected cells and using substrates available in exogenous growth media.

Clinical Mycobacterium tuberculosis (mtb) isolates successfully infect the human alveolar epithelial cells

The rising emergence of multi-, poly- and extensively drug-resistant Mtb strains (MDR, PDR and XDR) presents significant challenges to TB management. Understanding the metabolic pathways specific to drug-resistant strains in infected host cells might direct effective novel treatment strategies [22]. Therefore, we focused next on comparing the interaction of A549 cells with well-characterized drug-sensitive (S4 and S5) and drug-resistant (S6 and S11) clinical Mtb isolates infected with an MOI of 1:10. A549 cells were cultured in Advanced DMEM/F12 flex media supplemented with glucose (U-12C6/U-13C6), and the proteinogenic amino acids harvested at specified time points (i.e. 0, 24 and 48 h.p.i.) were analyzed Figure 5(A). Mtb H37Rv, clinical S4 and S5 isolates were sensitive to Isoniazid (INH), Rifampicin (RIF), Streptomycin (STM), Moxifloxacin (MOX), and Kanamycin (KAN). The clinical Mtb isolate S6 was resistant to INH, RIF, and STM (categorized as MDR strain), and S11 was resistant to STM, INH, and MOX (categorized as PDR strain) Figure 5(B), Figure S3 and Table S1. These clinical Mtb isolates showed similar growth kinetics up to 4 weeks in 7H9 broth media Figure 5. However, strain-specific differences in intracellular survival and replication, such as CFU per viable cell, were observed. The S4, S5, and S11 clinical Mtb isolates exhibited a low mycobacterial burden at early time points, whereas at 48 h.p.i., intracellular bacilli increased, as observed from the CFU data. Bacterial CFU for the S4 isolate at 48 h.p.i. was below the limit of detection (LOD). H37Rv and S6 Mtb strains showed a six- and eighteen-fold increase in intracellular bacillary load at 48 h.p.i. compared to the early time point (0 h.p.i.). Compared to uninfected A549 cells, Mtb infection decreased cell viability over time, and H37Rv-infected cells showed the lowest viability compared to cells infected with clinical isolates Figure 5. Interestingly, A549 cells infected with clinical MDR or PDR Mtb isolates exhibited higher viability and a higher number of Mtb accumulation than those infected with drug-sensitive clinical isolates Figure 5(E,F) and Figure S4. At the selected MOI of 1:10, most (80–100%) of the A549 cells were infected Figure 5(G). Hence, the observed perturbed phenotypes may largely be attributed to the drug resistance patterns.

13C enrichment in the proteinogenic amino acids derived from alveolar epithelial cells (A549) infected with laboratory Mtb H37Rv strain and clinical isolates provides insights into the central metabolism and the dependence of exogenous amino acids

Proteinogenic amino acids extracted from the [U-13C6] glucose-supplemented A549 cells infected with laboratory Mtb H37Rv strain and clinical isolates at 0, 24, and 48 h.p.i. were monitored to ascertain the average 13C-label.

Among all the proteinogenic amino acids, average 13C labeling of alanine (m/z 260) (Figure 7(A) and Figure S5-A) at 48 h.p.i. was observed to be the highest and exhibited enrichment in drug-sensitive Mtb-infected cells (including H37Rv) among the uninfected and infected cells (uninfected: 43.88 ± 0.57%; H37Rv: 47.16 ± 0.81%; S4: 44.49 ± 0.36%; S5: 46.04 ± 0.73%; S6: 43.80 ± 0.40%; S11: 42.70 ± 0.76%). Average 13C labeling of serine (Figure 7(B) and Figure S5-B) was observed in all groups (uninfected: 8.83 ± 0.89%; H37Rv: 10.36 ± 0.29%; S4: 9.70 ± 0.38%; S5: 9.67 ± 0.20%; S6: 9.20 ± 0.28%; and S11: 8.45 ± 0.68%). Proteinogenic glycine (m/z 288) (Figure 7(C) and Figure S5-C displayed a similar labeling pattern to serine (uninfected: 3.81 + 0.10%; H37Rv: 4.34 ± 0.14%; S4: 3.58 ± 0.40%; S5: 3.76 ± 0.16%; S6: 3.38 ± 0.21%; and S11: 3.21 ± 0.10%). A549 cells infected with Mtb clinical isolates (S4, S5, S6 and S11) exhibited less 13C enrichment than those infected with the laboratory strain H37Rv. The cells infected with drug-resistant clinical isolates (MDR: S6 and PDR: S11) showed the least enrichment among all the infected groups. The 13C enrichments and the variations observed in the proteinogenic alanine, serine, and glycine can be attributed to changes in the activities of glycolytic pathways and other metabolic reactions in a strain-specific manner (Figure 7 and Figure S5).

In uninfected cells, the proteinogenic valine (m/z 288) (Figure 7(D) and Figure S5-D) and tyrosine (m/z 466) (Figure 7(E) and Figure S5-E) showed no detectable 13C enrichment (i.e. less than the natural abundance of ~1.13%), probably indicating the contribution of these amino acids from exogenous media. However, when compared with the uninfected controls, the Mtb-infected cells appear to exhibit strain-specific variations, albeit at lower levels, which require further critical examination of MIDs to infer metabolic activities (Figure 7).

The incorporation of 13C into proteinogenic amino acids from the TCA cycle, i.e. aspartate/n, glutamate/n, methionine, threonine, lysine, and proline at 48 h.p.i., was monitored. It was observed that 13C from glucose was incorporated into glutamate/n (Figure 7(F) and Figure S5-F) and aspartate/n (Figure 7(H) and Figure S5-H) in all groups, including uninfected controls. In glutamine/n (m/z 432), the 13C enrichment (%) varied slightly among the cells (uninfected: 15.31 ± 1.42%; H37Rv: 15.13 ± 0.30%; S4: 15.76 ± 0.48 %; S5: 14.99 ± 0.38 %; S6: 14.07 ± 0.45%; S11: 14.06 ± 0.92%). For aspartate/n (m/z 418), minor differences in 13C enrichment (%) were observed (uninfected: 17.45 ± 1.12%; H37Rv: 18.80 ± 0.21%; S4: 19.08 ± 0.27%; S5: 18.61 ± 0.28%; S6: 17.88 ± 0.38%; and S11: 17.33 ± 0.42%). The 13C enrichment of proline (m/z 258) (Figure 7(G) and Figure S5-G) derived from the clinical isolates infected A549 cells was significantly lower (S4: 7.74 ± 0.13 %; S5: 8.09 ± 0.57%; S6: 7.25 ± 0.39%; S11: 6.76 ± 0.25%) than other uninfected (8.52 ± 0.43%) and H37Rv infected cells (8.50 ± 0.33%). The Mtb-infected A549 cells displayed no considerable 13C enrichment in the proteinogenic amino acids derived from aspartate, i.e. methionine (m/z 320), threonine (m/z 404), and lysine (m/z 431), and were less than the natural abundance of ~1.13%. On careful observation of the average 13C enrichment, although lower than the natural abundance, it appears there are significant variations in a strain-specific manner. In the case of methionine (Figure 7(I) and Figure S5-I), the 13C enrichment (%) was different between groups (H37Rv: 0.32 ± 0.07%; S4: 0.26 ± 0.03%; S5: 0.28 ± 0.06%; S6: 0.28 ± 0.04%; S11: 0.18 ± 0.02%). For threonine (Figure 7(J) and Figure S5-J), the 13C enrichment (%) showed Mtb strain‑specific differences (H37Rv: 0.34 ± 0.11%; S4: 0.24 ± 0.07%; S5: 0.37 ± 0.09%; S6: 0.25 ± 0.08%; and S11: 0.21 ± 0.05%). Varying incorporation of 13C label in lysine (Figure 7(K) and Figure S5-K) across groups (H37Rv: 0.21 ± 0.09%; S4: 0.14 ± 0.08%; S5: 0.11 ± 0.08%; S6: 0.27 ± 0.05%; and S11: 0.12 ± 0.07%) was observed. Careful examination of the MIDs is further warranted to make any reasonable conclusion.

A549 cells infected with drug-resistant Mtb clinical isolates showed a minor decrease in the 13C label compared to other infected groups. However, no significant differences were observed in the labeling patterns of A549 proteinogenic amino acids infected with the laboratory H37Rv strain and the drug-sensitive isolates S4 and S5 (Figure 7, Figure S5, and Dataset S3). These findings point toward drug resistance–pattern-specific selective metabolic adaptations observed in the Mtb-infected type 2 alveolar epithelial cells. Low 13C enrichment in a few proteinogenic amino acids likely reflects the contribution of exogenous carbon sources from the host or culture media or preexisting unlabeled metabolite pools of cells before incubation with labeled glucose. Also, variations in the rates of de novo protein biosynthesis and central metabolic fluxes in the uninfected and infected cells can contribute to these differences. Analysis of MIDs can further shed light on the phenotypes.

Discussion

In the present study, we aimed to gain insights into the host type 2 alveolar epithelial cell (AEC2) response to Mtb infection using laboratory-established and clinical strains with varied drug resistance. We performed a time-dependent comparative evaluation for bacterial growth rate in axenic culture, host cell viability, intracellular bacterial viability, infected cell numbers, and proteinogenic amino acid analyses of Mtb-infected A549 cells cultured in 13C‑glucose-containing media. Heat-inactivated H37Rv served as a control to match the particle size of live Mtb. 13C stable-isotope tracing was performed to quantify 13C enrichment in proteinogenic amino acids obtained from uninfected and Mtb-infected A549 alveolar epithelial cell adenocarcinoma cell line as the in vitro infected host model to study the robustness of host–pathogen interactions.

Our observations indicate that live Mtb, particularly the laboratory-adapted virulent H37Rv strain, significantly reduced A549 cell viability, consistent with similar reports on Mtb-infected macrophages and dendritic cells [23,24]. Conversely, A549 cells infected with drug-resistant clinical isolates showed greater viability than those infected with drug-sensitive isolates. These findings indicate differential effects of Mtb strains on host cell viability, reiterating that distinct bacterial genotypes and the host’s immune response may contribute to these differences. Further studies using a precise technique such as flow cytometry would be useful to gain deeper insights into the strain-specific loss of viability observed in Mtb-infected cells [25].

Mtb H37Ra and H37Rv strains persist and replicate within A549 cells [4,26,27]. Differential growth of H37Rv within cells can be attributed to the presence of virulence factors like serine protease Rv2569c, which facilitates the translocation of Mtb across the epithelial barrier by disrupting E-cadherin [28], leading to varying degrees of apoptosis [25], and the distinct metabolic responses elicited in host cells [29]. The MDR clinical isolate S6 exhibited maximum intracellular growth (eighteen-fold), followed by the H37Rv (six-fold). The S6 clinical Mtb isolate belongs to the Beijing lineage [30] and different Mtb lineages, particularly the Beijing genotype, manipulate intracellular signaling pathways in lung epithelial cells to enhance survival and replication [31,32]. This may be one of the reasons for its significantly higher bacterial burden relative to the other strains evaluated [33,34]. Clinical isolates of Mtb are reported to exhibit faster proliferation in human macrophages than H37Rv [35]. These findings suggest that the CFU counts of clinical Mtb isolates in host cells vary significantly depending on the strain’s ability to adapt and survive within the host cellular environment.

The 13C incorporation, determined by GC-MS analysis, predominantly occurred in six proteinogenic amino acids in the order alanine > aspartate/n > glutamate/n > proline >serine >glycine. 13C enrichment confirmed that heat-killed H37Rv do not engage actively in metabolic perturbation. The observed enrichment primarily represents host metabolites, whereas 13C enrichment, if any, in essential amino acids (valine, phenylalanine, threonine, methionine, and lysine) indicates their origin from the bacterial fraction, highlighting active metabolic activity and protein synthesis by intracellular Mtb. Our 13C enrichment study revealed the robustness of the central carbon metabolism of A549 cells during infection with laboratory strains and clinical Mtb isolates, with appreciable perturbations observed at later time points. Infection with different Mtb virulent strains showed a consistent pattern in the labeling of the proteinogenic amino acids. Despite low 13C labeling, cells infected with the H37Rv strain showed increased 13C enrichment in the essential proteinogenic amino acids compared to the controls. In contrast, the enrichment was slightly lower in cells infected with drug-resistant Mtb. This reduced isotopic enrichment in the drug-resistant Mtb-infected A549 cells reflects slower metabolic activity. Drug-resistant Mtb tends to enter a dormant state with decreased metabolic processes, allowing it to adapt and survive longer within host cells [36,37]. Furthermore, this metabolic deceleration reflects the fitness costs associated with multidrug resistance required to establish distinct metabolic niches within the host [38]. Proline enrichment significantly increased in A549 cells infected with the drug-sensitive Mtb compared to those infected with MDR and PDR isolates [39].

The Mtb-infected A549 cells displayed minimal 13C labeling in methionine, threonine, and lysine in a strain-specific manner. These essential amino acids, produced through the aspartate pathway, synthesize diaminopimelate (DAP) and S-adenosyl methionine (SAM), which are vital for the survival of mycobacteria [40]. These observations underlined the dependence of Mtb on the de novo synthesized host metabolic precursors for its sustenance.

The metabolic variations among A549 cells infected with different Mtb strains seem to become more distinct over time. Alterations in the 13C isotopomer distribution profiles of alanine, glycine, serine, aspartate/n, glutamate/n, and proline in Mtb-infected A549 cells suggested minor variations in the host cell carbon flux through glycolysis and the TCA cycle as a result of Mtb infection. This highlights the robustness of metabolic activities during intracellular growth of Mtb, possibly a survival strategy to meet its cellular demands. Recent studies on alveolar epithelial cells corroborate our findings, confirming that strain-specific alteration in host metabolism and cellular function is a primary survival strategy for Mtb during early infection [41,42]. Also, contrary to findings from a comparative macrophage study, where 13C isotopomer distribution in serine showed M + 3 predominance in uninfected and M + 1 in infected macrophages [43], we observed no difference in serine isotopomer distribution between uninfected and infected A549 cells. However, the abundance of M + 3 isotopomer increased in Mtb-infected cells compared to uninfected control cells. These distinct distributions suggest cell-specific metabolic responses to Mtb infection or stimuli. An earlier report on the Mtb-infected macrophages indicated that 13C3-labeled host pyruvate is not a significant carbon source for intracellular Mtb [44]. Our work on A549 cells suggested that the M + 5 valine isotopomer in Mtb originates from two 13C3-labeled pyruvate precursors. The 13C‑tyrosine distribution profile showed notable differences between uninfected and infected groups in a strain-specific pattern.

Serine M + 3 isotopomer distribution indicates that it is synthesized from the isotopomer precursors of 3-phosphoglycerate. Another possible source of 13C-serine is 13C-glycine through the reversible serine hydroxymethyl transferase (SHMT) reaction, though this does not explain the higher fraction of M + 1 serine compared to its M + 2 [45]. Further analysis is needed to understand the contributions of these metabolic pathways.

Our 13C isotopic tracing reveals that Mtb, including drug-resistant clinical isolates, actively relies on host-derived metabolic intermediates to acquire essential amino acids such as lysine, methionine, threonine, and valine [46]. This nutrient scavenging feature and the dependence of Mtb on host metabolic precursors present a specific clinical vulnerability [47–49]. Targeting strain-specific host-to-pathogen amino acid transport mechanisms and restricting overall nutrient availability could be a highly promising area for host-directed therapies (HDTs) [50,51].

Conclusion

We present the data here as a resource to offer a comprehensive overview of the metabolic insights in type 2 alveolar epithelial cells infected with different strains of Mtb. Our findings reveal how diverse Mtb strains predominantly adopt robust metabolic strategies with differential activities in the central carbon metabolism of host cells. However, further studies using parallel 13C feeding (with positionally labeled substrates) and isogenic strains are needed to shed light on disease mechanisms underlying Mtb load and to determine whether these changes stem from direct pathogen manipulation or host metabolic responses. Our study shows that different Mtb strains, based on their pathogenicity and drug-resistance status, interact with host cell metabolism in distinct ways (Figure 9). Exploring these differences may provide invaluable insights to guide future research toward understanding drug-resistant infections.

Metabolic insights in type 2 alveolar epithelial cells infected with different strains of Mtb. Based on their pathogenicity and drug resistance status, different Mtb strains interact with host cell metabolism in distinct ways.

Limitations of the study

While we aim to advance our understanding of strain-specific metabolic dynamics between alveolar epithelial cells and Mtb during infection, our study has certain limitations. First, these studies included A549 cells, and many such cell lines have undergone significant metabolic changes, potentially resembling those induced by bacterial pathogens, which could obscure pathogen-specific host responses. Primary human AEC2 cells are known to lose their phenotype and surfactant synthesis capacity during standard in vitro culture [52]. However, validation in primary alveolar epithelial cells will be useful. Second, we cultured the cells in high‑glucose media (17.5 mM). This was done to ensure the carbon source was not exhausted during the extended 48–60-h infection timeline required for our kinetic tracing. Since all experimental groups were cultured under identical conditions, the relative metabolic differences we observed remain valid. Third, a larger number of clinical Mtb isolates with varying resistance profiles are needed to confirm these observations. Lastly, further comprehensive and follow-up research with isogenic strains differing in their drug-susceptibility profiles is required for a more robust and convincing conclusion. Nevertheless, the current work, despite its limitations, suggests metabolic specificity associated with drug-resistant Mtb clinical isolates.

Overview of the study design

Biosafety declaration

All the experimental procedures used in this study were approved by the Institutional Bio-Safety Committee (DBT Memorandum No. BT/BS/17/006/96-PID, dated 17/02/2016) and carried out at the Tuberculosis Aerosol Challenge Facility (TACF), a DBT-sponsored national facility of the International Centre for Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology, New Delhi, following the Biosafety Level-III guidelines.

Cell culture

Type 2 alveolar epithelial adenocarcinoma cell line A549 was procured from the National Center for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune (India), and authenticated by the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Before the experiments, the cells were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free using a MycoAlert detection kit (Lonza, LT07-218). A549 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 (2.0 mL, Gibco, 12,400,024) and 10% v/v heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (US origin, Gibco, 16,000,044) supplemented with sodium bicarbonate (1.2 g/L, Sigma-Aldrich, S5761-500 G) in a humidified incubator (Steri-Cycle CO2 incubator, Thermo Scientific) at 37°C with 5% CO2. For 13C‑isotopic glucose labeling experiments (post-infection), cells were maintained in SILAC Advanced DMEM/F-12 Flex Media (2.0 mL, Gibco, A2494301) and 5% v/v heat-inactivated dialyzed fetal bovine serum (US origin, Gibco, 26,400,044) supplemented with glucose (17.50 mM, U-12C6: Sigma-Aldrich, G7021-1 KG; U-13C6: Sigma-Aldrich, 389,374-1 G), L-Glutamine (2.50 mM, Sigma-Aldrich, G8540-1 KG), L-Arginine (0.69 mM, Sigma-Aldrich, A8094-100 G), L-Lysine monohydrochloride (0.49 mM, Sigma-Aldrich, L8662-100 G), HEPES (15.01 mM, Sigma-Aldrich, PHG0001-100 G) and Phenol Red (0.02 mM, Sigma-Aldrich, P3532-25 G) in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every 2 to 3 d with fresh DMEM/F12 (Gibco, 12,400,024). Cells were passaged till 75–90% confluent. Cells between passages 5–8 were used for all experiments. Cell morphology was monitored in all cultures using a light microscope (Nikon).

Mycobacteria source and culture

Laboratory Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains (H37Rv and H37Ra) were obtained from TACF, ICGEB and maintained under appropriate biosafety conditions. Clinical isolates (drug-sensitive: S4, S5; drug-resistant: S6, S11) were sourced from the laboratory repository as part of an ongoing project. Briefly, sputum samples were collected from subjects presenting with TB-like symptoms after written informed consent and processed by Thyrocare (Mumbai, India), a NABL-accredited laboratory, using Mycobacterium Growth Indicator Tubes (MGIT). Only culture-positive samples were characterized through whole-genome sequencing. Primary culture analyses for clinical isolates were conducted by Thyrocare, and selected isolates were further subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) in our research laboratory using the Alamar Blue assay, as described in the subsequent method section.

All laboratory Mtb strains (H37Rv and H37Ra) and clinical isolates (drug‑sensitive isolates S4 and S5, drug‑resistant isolates S6 and S11) were grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth (10.0 mL, BD Biosciences, 271,310) supplemented with glycerol (0.2% v/v, Sigma-Aldrich, G5516-1 L), Tween-80 (0.05% v/v, Sigma-Aldrich, P1754-500 ML) and Middlebrook OADC supplement (10% v/v, BD Biosciences, 211,886). Mtb cultures (10 mL) were incubated in 50 mL conical tubes at 37°C under constant rotation at 180 rpm. Growth was monitored by measuring the optical density (OD) at 600 nm. Cultures were harvested in the mid-logarithmic phase (OD600 nm = 0.6). Stocks were prepared in 50% glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich, G5516-1 L) and stored in 1 mL aliquots at −80°C. Before the experiment, aliquots were thawed and expanded in Middlebrook 7H9 broth. Aliquots of bacterial cultures were plated on tryptic soy agar (HiMedia, M1968-500 G) to detect any potential contamination. All Mtb strains were tested for phthiocerol dimycocerosates) positivity status using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) of lipid extracts from cultures before performing the infection experiments.

PDIM positivity test

Mtb cultures were grown to the mid-logarithmic phase (O.D.600 nm = 0.6). The cultures were pelleted, and a mixture of 10:10:3 phenol:chloroform:water (10 mL, phenol: Amresco, 0945-100 ML; chloroform: Merck, 1,024,321,000; water: Merck, 1,037,021,000) was added to each sample. The samples were then incubated overnight (~16 h) in a shaker incubator at 37°C and centrifuged at 3,500 g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was filtered using a 0.2 µm nylon membrane syringe filter. The filtered supernatant was vacuum-dried in 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Lipid samples were resuspended in methanol (100 µL, Merck, 322,415-1 L) and aliquots were spotted onto thin-layer chromatography (TLC) plates (250 µm silica gel 60 F254 plates, Merck, 105,554). Plates were developed in petroleum ether/ethyl acetate (98:2 v/v, petroleum ether: HiMedia, 32,267–2.5 L; ethyl acetate: Merck, 1.94512) and visualized in an iodine chamber (Figure S6).

Heat-killed Mtb preparation

A single-cell suspension of the H37Rv strain was heated at 95°C in a water bath for 15 min, cooled to room temperature, and resuspended in DMEM/F-12 Flex medium for infection experiments [53]. Heat-killed H37Rv Mtb strain was characterized using electron microscopy (Figure S7).

Antibiotic susceptibility test of clinical mycobacterial isolates

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) of selected clinical Mtb isolates was reperformed in the laboratory using the Alamar Blue assay. Harvested bacterial cells in the log growth phase and cell counts were determined by measuring optical density (OD) at 600 nm. An aliquot of the Mtb culture (~6 × 106 bacteria) was transferred to a microcentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh 7H9 medium supplemented with OADC (10%, 500 µL). A single-cell suspension of the Mtb culture was prepared, and the cell count was adjusted to 1 × 105 bacteria per well. Bacterial cells were treated with various concentrations of anti-tuberculosis drugs (Isoniazid: Sigma-Aldrich, I3377-5 G; Rifampicin: Duchefa Biochemie R0146.0005; Streptomycin: Sigma-Aldrich, S6501-25 G; Kanamycin: Sigma-Aldrich, K4000-1 G; and Moxifloxacin: Sigma-Aldrich, SML1581-250 MG) in 96-well plates. All outer perimeter wells were filled with sterile water to prevent dehydration of the medium in the experimental wells. In the control wells, only bacteria or media were added. The bacterial cells were incubated with the drugs for 5 d. Ten percent AlamarBlue (20 µL, Bio-Rad, BUF012A) was added aseptically to the wells. The plates were incubated with alamarBlue dye for 24 h and the color change from blue to pink was monitored at 12 and 24 h.

Cell infection

Mtb cultures in the mid-logarithmic phase (O.D.600 nm = 0.6) were centrifuged at 4,000 g for 5 min at RT, and the pellets were resuspended in serum-free DMEM/F12 culture media. Mycobacteria were passed through a 23-gauge needle, followed by a 26-gauge needle 10 times each, to prepare a single-cell suspension. The bacterial culture at 0.6 O.D. corresponds to 100 million bacteria per mL at a wavelength of 600 nm [54]. The required number of bacteria was added to the seeded cells in a complete medium at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:10 (cell: bacteria). At 8 h post-infection, the media was removed, the cells were washed three times with 800 µL sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove extracellular bacilli, and 2.0 mL of fresh complete media was added. Cells were placed in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 for the specified time. This infection protocol was followed for all subsequent experiments.

Cell proliferation and viability assay

Cells were then infected with Mtb at an MOI of 1:10 and harvested at designated time points for manual counting using the trypan blue reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, T8154-100 ML). The cells were diluted at 1:10 or 1:20 with trypan blue, and 10 μL of cell suspension was added to a Neubauer hemocytometer and counted under a light microscope (Nikon). Intracellular bacteria viability assay (colony-forming unit or CFU)

A colony-forming unit (CFU) assay using the drop-plate method was performed to determine intracellular bacterial counts. The extracellular medium was removed and the infected cells were washed three times with sterile PBS (1×). Infected cells were then lysed with SDS (0.06% prepared in PBS, Sigma-Aldrich, 436,143-100 G). Diluted cell lysates (1,000 times dilution in 7H9 media) were plated onto Middlebrook 7H11 Agar (BD Biosciences, 212,203) plates in duplicate and incubated at 37°C. CFU counts were enumerated after 21 and 42 d of incubation for Mtb laboratory strains and clinical isolates, respectively, and expressed as CFU per viable cell. The remaining cells were incubated for further time points before lysis, dilution, and plating. Samples yielding zero colonies were recorded as <LOD. The limit of detection (LOD) is the lowest concentration that yields a single colony on the plate.

Confocal microscopy

Mtb H37Rv bacteria were stained with PKH67 (Sigma-Aldrich, MINI67-1KT), a green fluorescent lipophilic dye, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, Mtb cultures (O.D.600 nm = 0.6) were centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 5 min, and the bacterial pellet was washed with PBS. Each pellet was resuspended in diluent (250 µL). Dye was added to stain the bacteria at a concentration of 2 µL per 1,000 million bacteria and incubated for 7 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark. FBS (500 µL, twice the volume of the diluent) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 2 min at RT in the dark. The bacteria were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in serum-free DMEM/F12 to prepare single-cell suspensions.

A549 cells were seeded at a density of 0.06 million/mL onto 18 mm coverslips in 12-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24–36 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. The cells were infected with fluorescent dye-labeled Mtb. Cells were washed with PBS, and mitochondria were stained with MitoTracker® red CMXRoS (200 nM, Invitrogen, M7512) for 30 min in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma-Aldrich, P6148-500 G) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The fixed cells were washed three times with PBS and permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, T8787-250 ML) for 5 min. It was followed by washing the cells thrice with PBS and blocking with 1% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, A7906-100 G) for 20 min. For actin staining, Alexa FluorTM 680 phalloidin (~1 unit/sample, Invitrogen, A22286) was added to the cells and incubated for 20 min at RT. The cells were washed thrice with PBS, and the cell nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1 µg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, D9542-10 MG) for 15 min at RT in the dark. The cells were washed three times with PBS and mounted onto glass slides using ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Invitrogen, P36934). All solutions were prepared in PBS. Images were acquired using NIS-Elements AR analysis software (version 4.0, Life Technologies, USA) on a Nikon A1R Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope equipped with a Nikon Plan Apo VC × 60, NA 0.75, and Plan Apo VC × 100 oil immersion objective lens with a 1.40 numerical aperture (NA). They were visualized using DAPI (Ex/Em-358/461 nm), PE/AlexaFluor680 (Ex/Em-679/702 nm), TRITC (Ex/Em-579/599 nm), FITC (Ex/Em-490/502 nm), and TD (bright field) filters and analyzed using NIS-Elements AR analysis software (version 4.0, Life Technologies, USA).

Metabolite extraction

A549 cells were seeded at the required density in 6-well plates, infected with Mtb, and incubated at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. At 8 h post-infection, the media was removed, the cells were washed three times with sterile PBS, and 2.0 mL of fresh complete media (supplemented with U-12C6/U-13C6 glucose, 17.50 mM) was added. This step was considered to be time-point 0 (time zero). The cells were then placed in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cell culture media (ccm) were collected at designated time points, filtered using 0.22 µm PES membrane syringe filters (MDI, SYPL0601MNXX204), and stored at −80°C until further analysis. The cells were washed with 0.9% ice-cold saline (Sigma-Aldrich, 31,434-500 G-R) and quenched with 800 μL pre-chilled methanol (Merck, 1,008,371,000), followed by the addition of 800 μL ice-cold water (Merck, 1,037,021,000). The cells were scraped and collected into cold 15 mL conical centrifuge tubes. A 1.6 mL pre-chilled chloroform (Merck, 1,024,321,000) was added to the tubes and vortexed at 4°C for 30 min. The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The upper aqueous phase (methanolic) and lower organic phase (chloroform) were separated and stored at −80°C until further analysis. The junction layers of the aqueous and organic phases comprising proteins were collected in screw-capped tubes and selected for amino acid analysis.

Protein hydrolysis

The collected protein samples (n = 450) were acid-hydrolyzed by resuspending in 100 μL of 6 M HCl (Honeywell Fluka, 84,436-1 L). The tubes were partially sealed and placed inside a fume hood cabinet in a heating block (Neolab, HB-221) for 12–14 h at 100°C. The hydrolysates were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min to separate water-soluble amino acids from the insoluble pellets. The supernatant from each sample was collected in a fresh tube and vacuum-dried at 22°C for approximately 6–8 h in a speed vacuum evaporator (Labconco). The dried hydrolysates were subjected to derivatization for Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS).

Chemical derivatization of proteinogenic amino acids

Equal volumes (10 µL each from 1 µg/mL working stock concentration) of 22 commercially available amino acid standards (Merck, LAA21-1KT) were pooled to prepare quality control (QC) samples. Dried protein hydrolyzates were subjected to tert-butyldimethylsilyl (TBDM) derivatization. Pyridine (25 μL, Sigma-Aldrich, 270,407-100 ML) was added to each dried sample, and the tubes were incubated at 37°C for 30 min on a thermomixer (Eppendorf, 5,382,000.015) set to 900 rpm. Then, 35 μL MTBSTFA +1% t-BDMCS (N-tert-Butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide + % tert-Butyldimethylchlorosilane, N-Methyl-N-tert-butyldimethylsilyltrifluoroacetamide, Sigma-Aldrich, M-108-5×1 ML) was added, followed by incubation at 60°C for 30 min in a thermomixer at 900 rpm. All processed samples were centrifuged (10 min; 16,000 g at RT). The supernatant was transferred to 200 µL glass vial inserts in a GC vial (2 mL capacity) and analyzed by GC-MS.

GC-MS data acquisition

GC-MS data acquisition of all samples was performed using a 7890 gas chromatograph and a 5975C quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). All samples (n = 450) with separate sets of QCs were run in a single batch. Samples were randomized using an online tool (www.randomizer.org) and processed in batches of 20 samples per day, with 2 QC samples to run at the beginning and end of the sequence. Derivatized samples (1 μL) were injected into the GC column (Restek, RTX-5, 30 m × 250 µm × 0.25 µm) in a purged splitless mode.

For the TBDMS-derivatized samples, helium gas was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.3 mL/min with a solvent delay of 6 min. The oven program was set at 120°C for 5 min, ramped to 270°C at 4°C/min, held for 3 min, ramped to 320°C at 20°C/min, and held for 1 min. The EI ion source temperature was set to 230°C, the quadrupole temperature to 150°C, and data were acquired in full-scan mode from 50 to 600 m/z for a total run time of 49 min. Ions were generated using a 70 eV electron beam in electron ionization mode. An Agilent ChemStation software (version G1701EA E.2 February 1431) was used to control data-acquisition parameters during GC separation and mass-spectrometry acquisition for all sample runs. Data acquisition of samples was completed within 24–48 h of derivatization.

GC-MS data analysis

The GC-MS spectra were baseline-corrected using MetAlign software [55] to estimate the accurate mass isotopomer distributions (MIDs) for all amino acids. The peaks of the baseline-corrected spectra were identified using ChemStation software (version G1701EA E.2 February 1431) based on the m/z of different fragments, their retention times, and hits against the NIST17 (National Institute of Standards and Technology, Maryland) library. Their identity was confirmed by running commercial 4 (Figure S8). The incorporated natural abundance of the 13C isotopes contributes to the measured MIDs. For accurate estimation, MIDs were corrected for natural isotope correction using Isocor software [56]. The derivatization and metabolite formula required for Isocor were calculated manually for all the metabolite fragments. Before statistical analysis, manual data curation was carried out to remove outliers. MetaboAnalyst 5.0 online tool was used for principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least square-discriminate analysis (PLS-DA). Missing values were replaced by 1/5 of the minimum positive values of their corresponding variables. Log transformation of data with auto-scaling was selected to obtain a near-normal distribution. Analytes with a Variable Importance projection (VIP) score > 1.0 from the PLS-DA model were selected as important features for classifying the groups.

Statistical analysis

Data compilation was performed using Microsoft Excel (2019). Graphs were prepared and analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1) with the following statistical tests: multiple t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA with multiple comparison analyses, as indicated in the respective figure legends. p-values are reported in the figures. In metabolomics data figures, differences are marked with asterisks: [*] ≤0.05, [**] <0.005, [***] <0.0005, [****] <0.0001, at a 95% confidence interval. Error bars represent the standard deviation between replicates. The illustrations were created using BioRender (biorender.com).
