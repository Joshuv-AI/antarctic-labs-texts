Prevalence and Associated Factors of Chronic Diseases Among Adults in Somaliland: A Multilevel Analysis From the 2020 SLHDS
Authors: Abdirahman Omer Ali, Yusuf Abdi Hared, Hodo Abdi Abdillahi, Muhyadin Yusuf Dahir, Abdisalam Mahdi Hassan, Md Moyazzem Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic diseases represent a growing public health challenge globally, particularly in developing regions like Sub‐Saharan Africa. Somaliland faces a dual burden of communicable and non‐communicable diseases amidst post‐conflict recovery, yet data on non‐communicable disease (NCD) prevalence and determinants remain scarce. This study aimed to ascertain the prevalence and identify socio‐demographic factors associated with self‐reported chronic diseases among adults in Somaliland.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study utilized data from the Somaliland Health and Demographic Survey (SLHDS) 2020. The final sample of 11,153 adults had a highly skewed age distribution (94.6% aged 35–44), a primary limitation of this analysis. The outcome was a self‐reported physician diagnosis of any chronic disease. A multilevel mixed‐effect logistic regression model was employed to identify significant determinants while adjusting for confounders.ResultsThe analysis identified several factors associated with reporting a chronic disease. Higher odds were observed among female‐headed households (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.06–1.48) and divorced individuals (AOR: 1.92; 95% CI: 1.55–2.37). Conversely, lower odds were associated with higher education (AOR: 0.53; 95% CI: 0.34–0.82) and nomadic residence (AOR: 0.34; 95% CI: 0.22–0.51). Unexpectedly, lack of electricity and no savings were also associated with lower odds, likely reflecting diagnostic access bias and reverse causation.ConclusionFindings suggest that self‐reported chronic diseases are associated with specific socio‐demographic vulnerabilities in Somaliland. The results should be interpreted with extreme caution and viewed as hypothesis‐generating at best. Future research using objective measures and representative sampling is urgently needed to validate these associations and accurately quantify the NCD burden.Keywords: chronic diseases, health surveys, multilevel analysis, non‐communicable diseases, socio‐demographic determinants, Somaliland, Sub‐Saharan Africa

Introduction

Chronic diseases, often referred to as non‐communicable diseases (NCDs), are long‐duration health conditions that represent the predominant cause of global mortality, responsible for approximately 71% of all deaths worldwide [1]. This global health crisis disproportionately affects low‐ and middle‐income countries, which bear nearly 80% of the NCD mortality burden, often in the form of premature deaths that devastate families and economies [2, 3]. The primary drivers of this burden—cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, and diabetes—arise from a complex interplay of genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioral factors [4]. Modifiable risk factors such as tobacco use, unhealthy diets, and physical inactivity are central to their proliferation, making prevention a global health priority and a key component of sustainable development goal (SDG) 3.4 [5, 6].

The African continent exemplifies the complexity of this challenge, as it grapples with a “dual burden” of disease, where high rates of communicable diseases persist alongside a rapidly escalating NCD epidemic [7]. This situation places an immense strain on already fragile health systems, which are often underfunded, understaffed, and oriented towards managing acute infectious diseases rather than chronic care [8]. Projections suggest that Africa will experience the world's most significant rise in NCD mortality in the coming decade, a trend driven by rapid urbanization, globalization, and associated lifestyle changes [8, 9]. In Eastern Africa, NCDs already account for over a third of all mortality, signaling an urgent need for evidence‐based public health strategies [10]. This challenge is acutely amplified in post‐conflict and humanitarian settings like Somalia and Somaliland. Decades of civil conflict have decimated health infrastructure, dismantled public health programs, and created a critical vacuum of reliable health data [11, 12]. While humanitarian efforts traditionally focus on immediate crises like malnutrition and infectious disease outbreaks, the “silent epidemic” of NCDs often goes unaddressed, despite evidence that it is a major cause of morbidity and mortality [13]. In Somalia, NCDs were estimated to cause 42% of deaths in 2019—a figure alarmingly high for a country with some of the world's lowest health indicators [13].

Within this context, Somaliland represents a particularly critical, yet under‐researched, area. While some preliminary data from the 2020 Somaliland Health and Demographic Survey (SLHDS) indicate that hypertension (41.1%) and diabetes (19.1%) are common among those with a chronic condition, these top‐level statistics provide little insight into the distribution and drivers of the overall NCD burden across the population [14]. To date, no comprehensive, population‐based study has investigated the socio‐demographic determinants of chronic diseases across Somaliland's diverse urban, rural, and nomadic populations. Understanding who is most at risk is the first and most critical step for designing equitable and effective interventions in a resource‐constrained environment. This knowledge gap prevents policymakers and public health practitioners from targeting interventions, allocating limited resources, and tracking progress towards health equity. Therefore, this study aims to fill this critical research gap by leveraging the SLHDS 2020 data set to answer the following research questions: What is the prevalence of self‐reported chronic diseases among adults in Somaliland? What are the individual and community‐level socio‐demographic factors associated with self‐reported chronic diseases in this population? By answering these questions, this study will provide foundational evidence to inform the development of targeted health policies aimed at mitigating the growing burden of chronic diseases in Somaliland.

Methods and Materials

Study Design and Setting

This study employed a cross‐sectional design, utilizing secondary data from the SLHDS 2020. The survey was conducted across all six officially recognized regions of Somaliland: Awdal, Marodijeh, Sahil, Togdheer, Sool, and Sanaag. It was designed to be nationally representative, covering urban, rural, and nomadic populations to provide a comprehensive snapshot of health and demographic indicators in the region.

Data Source and Sampling

The data source was the SLHDS 2020, a comprehensive nationwide survey implemented by the Somaliland National Bureau of Statistics with support from the United Nations Population Fund. The survey used a stratified, multi‐stage probability cluster sampling design. For urban and rural areas, a three‐stage design was used, while a two‐stage design was applied to nomadic areas. Primary sampling units were selected with probability proportional to size, ensuring that the final sample was representative of the entire Somaliland population.

Study Participants and Age Distribution Anomaly

The SLHDS 2020 collected data from households across Somaliland. For this analysis, we included all adult respondents aged 18 years and older who had complete data for the outcome variable (self‐reported chronic disease). This resulted in a final analytical sample of 11,153 individuals. It is critical to note that the sample has a highly skewed age distribution, with 94.6% of respondents falling within the 35–44 age group. A thorough review of the original SLHDS 2020 survey report reveals that this anomaly is a result of the survey's primary objective to estimate the maternal mortality ratio, which led to an oversampling of households containing women of reproductive age 15–49 and their partners. This severe deviation from the expected population structure is a major limitation of our analysis and profoundly impacts the study's validity and generalizability, particularly preventing any meaningful analysis of age as a risk factor.

Outcome Variable

The outcome variable was the presence of a self‐reported chronic disease. It was derived from a survey question asking participants if a doctor or other health professional had ever told them they had any of a list of conditions (including hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, asthma, etc.). A binary variable was created where “Yes” (coded as 1) indicated a report of at least one chronic condition, and “No” (coded as 0) indicated a report of no chronic conditions.

Explanatory Variables

Explanatory variables were selected based on their established association with chronic diseases in the literature. These included:

Individual‐level variables: Age, sex of the household head, educational attainment, marital status, smoking status, and a food security indicator (lack of food).

Community‐level variables: Region of residence (Awdal, Waqooyi‐Galbeed, Togdheer, Sool, and Sanaag) and type of residence (urban, rural, and nomadic).

Household‐level variables: Wealth index (derived by the SLHDS using principal component analysis and categorized into quintiles), access to electricity, and television ownership.

Statistical Analysis

The data analysis was conducted using STATA version 17 and structured to address the study's two research questions. All analyses incorporated survey weights to account for the complex sampling design and ensure that the results are nationally representative. First, the analysis was conducted on a complete‐case basis, excluding participants with missing data on the outcome or key explanatory variables. Second, multicollinearity among the explanatory variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). No significant collinearity was detected (mean VIF < 2.5), confirming the suitability of the variables for the regression model. Third, a multilevel mixed‐effect logistic regression model was employed. The use of this model was statistically justified by the data structure. The null model, containing no explanatory variables, revealed an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.15, indicating that 15% of the total variance in reporting a chronic disease can be attributed to differences between communities. This significant clustering effect confirms the necessity of a multilevel approach over standard logistic regression. The results from the final model were presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with their corresponding 95% CIs. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Methodological Limitations of the Data

Before presenting the results, it is imperative to acknowledge the three severe limitations inherent in the data set, which constrain the interpretation of the findings.

Abnormal Age Distribution

As detailed above, the sample is not representative of the adult age structure in Somaliland. This prevents analysis of age—the single most important predictor of chronic disease—and limits the generalizability of all findings.

Self‐Reported Diagnosis

The outcome relies entirely on the self‐report of a physician's diagnosis. This is subject to recall bias and, more critically, diagnostic access bias. Individuals without access to healthcare cannot be diagnosed and will be misclassified as healthy, leading to a likely substantial underestimation of the true prevalence.

Broad Disease Definition

The outcome variable combines all chronic conditions into a single binary category. This prevents any disease‐specific analysis and masks potentially different risk profiles for major NCDs like hypertension versus diabetes or asthma.

Ethical Considerations

This study is based on publicly available, anonymized secondary data from the SLHDS 2020. The original survey protocol was approved by the relevant institutional review boards. As we did not have access to any personal identifiers, further ethical approval was not required for this analysis. Permission to use the data set was obtained from the official data repository.

Results

The frequency distribution of the 11,153 study participants by their socio‐demographic characteristics is presented in Table 1. It is observed, the sample has a highly skewed age distribution, with 94.6% of respondents in the 35–44 age group. Out of the total sample, 1373 individuals reported having been diagnosed by a physician with at least one chronic disease. This yields an overall prevalence of 12.3% (95% CI: 11.7%–12.9%) among the adult population in Somaliland. About 28.14% of the sampled population was from rural areas. More than half of the individuals (63.98%) were from urban areas, and the remaining 7.87% were from nomadic areas. Nearly 15.04% of the respondents were poor, while 18.76% belonged to rich households, and 23.63% of households were middle. A significant portion of the sample does not smoke cigarettes/tobacco (93.84%) or chew Khat/Miraa (93.63%). The majority of the individuals have a normal weight (87.97%), with a smaller percentage being overweight (10.74%) or obese (2.12%). The majority of the sample (94.64%) falls within the 35–44 age group, with a small percentage (4.60%) in the 18–34 age group and an even smaller percentage (0.76%) in the 45‐or‐more age group (Table 1).

The multilevel logistic regression model identified several individual and community‐level factors significantly associated with reporting a chronic disease, and the findings are presented in Table 3. At the individual level, education and marital status were strong predictors. As detailed in Table 3, compared to individuals with no education, those with primary education (AOR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.49–0.74) or higher education (AOR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.34–0.82) had significantly lower odds of reporting a chronic disease. In terms of marital status, divorced individuals had nearly double the odds of having a chronic disease compared to those who were married (AOR: 1.92, 95% CI: 1.55–2.37). Household characteristics and economic factors also played a significant role. Individuals from female‐headed households were more likely to report a chronic condition than those from male‐headed households (AOR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.06–1.48). Furthermore, not experiencing a lack of food was associated with lower odds of chronic disease (AOR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.67–0.95), as was not smoking (AOR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.57–0.96). At the community level, significant geographic disparities were observed. The results in Table 3 show that residents of the Sool region had more than twice the odds of reporting a chronic disease compared to residents of the Awdal region (AOR: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.26–3.34). Additionally, individuals living a nomadic lifestyle had substantially lower odds of disease compared to their urban counterparts (AOR: 0.34, 95% CI: 0.22–0.51) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study explored the key factors significantly associated with higher odds of reporting a chronic disease in the multilevel analysis, and findings depict that being in a female‐headed household, being divorced, residing in the Sool region, and smoking are associated with higher odds of reporting a chronic disease. Conversely, factors associated with lower odds included having primary or higher education, nomadic residence, lack of electricity access, not having savings, not smoking, and not experiencing a lack of food. The findings indicate a significant burden of chronic conditions, intricately linked with various individual and community‐level factors, highlighting the complex health landscape of this post‐conflict setting, which faces a dual burden of communicable and non‐communicable diseases [7, 11].

The finding that female‐headed households have higher odds of chronic disease aligns with previous studies [12, 15, 16], and may experience increased stress or socioeconomic vulnerability when heading a household [7]. The significantly higher odds among divorced individuals compared to married individuals could reflect the psychosocial stress associated with marital dissolution or potential loss of socioeconomic support, factors known to negatively impact health [17, 18, 19]. The effect of primary and higher education against chronic diseases, and this finding is supported by previous research [4, 5]. This finding underscores the importance of education as a social determinant of health in Somaliland. Moreover, the lower odds of chronic disease are observed among nomadic populations compared to urban dwellers, and this aligns with other settings where traditional lifestyles may involve higher physical activity and different dietary patterns [9]. However, this finding must be interpreted cautiously, as lower access to diagnostic services in nomadic areas could lead to under‐reporting of conditions [7, 20]. The significantly higher odds of chronic disease reported in the Sool region compared to Awdal highlight potential regional disparities that warrant further investigation, possibly related to localized conflict impacts, environmental factors, or variations in healthcare infrastructure [20]. The finding that not smoking is protective strongly aligns with established evidence on tobacco as a major risk factor for numerous chronic diseases [5, 21, 22]. Similarly, not experiencing a lack of food being protective suggests a link between food security and better health outcomes, potentially reflecting better nutrition or lower stress [23, 24, 25, 26].

Several findings present apparent contradictions or diverge from typical expectations. For example, the association of lacking electricity with lower odds of chronic disease is counterintuitive, as electricity access usually correlates with higher socioeconomic status, often linked to increased NCD risk through sedentary lifestyles and dietary changes [9]. This finding might reflect unmeasured confounding factors, specific lifestyle patterns in electrified versus non‐electrified households in Somaliland, or potentially differential access to healthcare leading to diagnosis (i.e., those with electricity live in areas where diagnosis is more likely). Similarly, the finding that not having savings is associated with lower odds of chronic disease is unexpected, as savings typically indicate greater financial security. This could potentially be explained if individuals with chronic diseases deplete their savings for healthcare costs, thus reporting no savings, or it might reflect complex interactions between income, expenditure, and health status not fully captured by the wealth index (which was nonsignificant in the final model).

By employing multilevel modeling, the study accounts for potential clustering effects within communities, offering more robust estimates than standard regression. It identifies specific vulnerable subgroups (female‐headed households, divorced individuals, less educated, residents of Sool region) and protective factors (education, nomadic lifestyle) within the Somaliland context. This evidence is crucial for a region where health data have been historically scarce due to prolonged conflict and instability [11, 20]. The findings have direct relevance to achieving SDG 3 (good health and well‐being), particularly target 3.4, which aims to reduce premature mortality from NCDs by one‐third by 2030 [6]. The identified associations highlight that addressing chronic diseases in Somaliland requires multi‐sectoral action beyond the health sector. The protective role of education links directly to SDG 4 (Quality Education). The vulnerability of female‐headed households underscores the importance of SDG 5 (gender equality) in achieving health equity.

The findings suggest a need for targeted public health interventions in Somaliland. Health promotion programs should prioritize less educated groups, women (particularly household heads), divorced individuals, and residents in high‐risk regions like Sool. Strengthening primary healthcare services is crucial for improving early detection, diagnosis, and management of chronic diseases across all populations, including nomadic groups, where under‐reporting is a concern. Integrating NCD screening and care into existing health services, potentially leveraging community health workers, could be a viable strategy in this resource‐constrained setting [7]. Addressing underlying social determinants, such as education and food security, will also be vital. Future research should aim to focus on specific chronic diseases (e.g., hypertension, diabetes) using both self‐report and objective measurements (e.g., blood pressure, blood glucose), which would provide more precise estimates and allow for disease‐specific determinant analysis. Incorporating detailed assessments of behavioral risk factors (diet, physical activity, alcohol use) and mental health status is essential. Qualitative studies could provide deeper insights into the contextual factors driving the observed associations, such as the experiences of female‐headed households, the reasons for regional disparities, and the counterintuitive findings related to electricity and savings. Finally, future surveys should strive for a more representative age distribution to better understand the impact of aging on chronic disease burden in Somaliland.

The study's main strength is its use of a large, national data set, providing a first‐of‐its‐kind overview for a data‐poor region. However, most critically, the abnormal age distribution makes the sample entirely unrepresentative of the adult population, preventing any analysis of age as a risk factor and severely limiting the generalizability of all findings. Second, the reliance on self‐reported diagnoses leads to a likely underestimation of the true prevalence and introduces profound bias, as discussed above. Third, the composite outcome variable masks important clinical differences between specific diseases, preventing any nuanced, disease‐specific analysis.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant presence of self‐reported chronic diseases among adults in Somaliland and identifies key socio‐demographic determinants, including education, marital status, household headship, residence type, region, smoking, and economic factors like electricity access and savings. Therefore, this study's primary contribution is not to provide definitive risk factors but to highlight the urgent and critical need for more robust epidemiological research on NCDs in Somaliland. Future studies must employ representative sampling strategies, particularly for age, and incorporate objective clinical measurements (e.g., blood pressure, blood glucose) to overcome the significant limitations identified here. While limited by the cross‐sectional design, self‐reported data, a broad outcome measure, and a skewed age sample, the findings provide valuable baseline evidence for a data‐poor region. Addressing the growing burden of chronic diseases is critical for Somaliland's health system development and achieving SDG 3.4. Targeted, context‐specific interventions focusing on vulnerable populations and strengthening primary healthcare are urgently needed.
