Crossed pseudopotential − - functional calculations made simple: An extended Kohn-Sham framework
Kuiyu Ye, Jiale Shen, Haitao Liu, Yuanchang Li

Abstract
Modern density-functional-theory (DFT) calculations rely heavily on pseudopotentials, yet their impact on accuracy is barely addressed. In this work, we derive from the Kohn-Sham equation that the use of pseudopotentials invariably introduces a “dropping” error, which leads to a deviation from the Hohenberg-Kohn theorem. Crossed pseudopotential-functional calculations provide a pragmatic way to balance accuracy and efficiency, enabling the right results for the right reasons. This paradigm goes beyond the (generalized) Kohn-Sham framework, which we name the extended Kohn-Sham framework. We support our assertion with a bandgap study on 54 monovalent-Cu semiconductors. The crossed calculations, compared to consistent ones, not only remove all 11 erroneous metal predictions, but also drastically reduce the mean relative error from 80% to 20%. The accuracy even exceeds that of the hybrid functionals and GW due to the role of pseudopotentials in modelling the external potentials of Cu-valence electrons that cannot be compensated by exchange-correlation.

In this work, we derive exact conditions for the pseudopotentials directly from the KS equations, which show that pseudopotentials constructed from different functional approximations introduce “dropping” errors of unequal magnitude. Although pseudopotentials and functionals are communicable with each other, they affect the accuracy in their own unique ways. Thus, the accuracy of pseudopotential-DFT is essentially a joint result of the two variables, and crossed pseudopotential-functional calculations that abandon the consistency constraint can better balance accuracy and efficiency. The crossed paradigm falls out of the (generalized) KS framework, which we refer to as the extended KS framework. We support the assertion with a bandgap study of 54 monovalent-Cu semiconductors that are notorious examples of KS-DFT failures. With the same PBE functional, consistent PBE pseudopotentials incorrectly predict about a quarter as metals, and more than three-quarters having bandgap underestimation
>
1 eV. Replacing the PBE with hybrid pseudopotentials not only opens the bandgap for all but also about three-quarters of the error is within 0.4 eV. The mean relative error is significantly reduced from 80% to 20%. This accuracy statistically outperforms even the HSE hybrid functional and GW, but with a significant advantage in computational efficiency.
Finally, we show that this improvement belongs to the right results for the right reasons, originating from the hybrid pseudopotential correctly describing the external potential of the Cu-valence electrons.

The KS equation
KS
reads

\displaystyle\{-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+v_{nu}({\bf r})+v_{H}([n];{\bf r})+v_{xc}([n];{\bf r})\}\phi_{i}({\bf r})=\varepsilon_{i}\phi_{i}({\bf r}),\ \ [n]=\sum_{i}^{N}|\phi_{i}({\bf r})^{2}|.

(1)

Here
v_{nu}({\bf r})
,
v_{H}([n];{\bf r})
and
v_{xc}([n];{\bf r})
are the nuclear, Hartree and exchange-correlation potentials, respectively. Inserting a potential
v_{ps}({\bf r})
one can rewrite Eq. (1) as

\displaystyle\{-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+v_{ps}({\bf r})+v_{H}([n];{\bf r})+v_{xc}([n];{\bf r})\}\phi_{i}({\bf r})+\{v_{nu}({\bf r})-v_{ps}({\bf r})\}\phi_{i}({\bf r})=\varepsilon_{i}\phi_{i}({\bf r}).

(2)

If

\{v_{nu}({\bf r})-v_{ps}({\bf r})\}\phi_{i}({\bf r})=0\ \ \ (r>r_{0}),

(3)

a set of wavefunctions
\psi_{i}({\bf r})
can be found such that

\displaystyle\{-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+v_{ps}({\bf r})+v_{H}([n];{\bf r})+v_{xc}([n];{\bf r})\}\psi_{i}({\bf r})=\varepsilon_{i}\psi_{i}({\bf r}),\ \ \psi_{i}({\bf r})=\phi_{i}({\bf r})\ \ (r>r_{0}).

(4)

This is exactly the equation that the pseudopotential-DFT self-consistently solves
Cohen1983
;
Hellmann
. Therefore, any
v_{ps}({\bf r})
satisfying Eq. (3) is a pseudopotential. Physically, Eq. (3) states that at
r>r_{0}
, the attraction of the nucleus to the outer valence electrons is completely cancelled out by
v_{ps}({\bf r})
, thus making them nearly-free-electrons and suitable for the plane-wave basis.

The
v_{ps}({\bf r})
derived from Eq. (3) is exact and does not introduce additional errors into the solution of the KS equations. However, solving Eq. (3) requires
\phi_{i}({\bf r})
, which comes from solving Eq. (1). The exact exchange-correlation is unknown, so is the corresponding
\phi_{i}({\bf r})
. Consequently, the actual
v^{ac}_{ps}({\bf r})
derived from an approximate functional leads to

\{v_{nu}({\bf r})-v^{ac}_{ps}({\bf r})\}\phi_{i}({\bf r})\neq 0\ \ \ (r>r_{0}).

(5)

This means an incomplete cancellation of
v^{ac}_{ps}({\bf r})
to the nuclear attraction. As a result, a non-zero term is dropped in the process of transferring from the exact KS Eq. (2) to the actually solved pseudopotential Eq. (4), resulting in a “dropping” error.

Although essentially derived from the functional approximation, the “dropping” error is physically different from the error induced by the functional approximation in Eq. (4). The former is embedded in the pseudopotential construction and depends only on the accuracy of the functional used to compute atoms. More precisely, it depends on the accuracy of the functional used to characterize the fact that the attraction of the nucleus to the outer valence electrons is completely screened by the core electrons. In contrast, the functional error in Eq. (4) manifests itself in characterizing the interactions between the outer valence electrons. It is straightforward that only a functional that characterizes both atom-like core electrons and free-electron-like valence electrons well can have simultaneously a small pseudopotential and a small functional error. Unfortunately, however, there are almost no such functionals available. For example, the PBE functional, which is routinely used by the condensed matter community, performs poorly when calculating atoms
Zhang
. As such, when the same functional is used in constructing the pseudopotential and calculating the solid, it will inevitably produce serious errors in one or the other. In other words, if the pseudopotential-functional consistency is abandoned and they are treated as two independent variables, the total DFT error can be reduced by rationally pairing the pseudopotential and the functional in Eq. (4). This is the physics behind why crossed calculations sometimes substantially improve accuracy. Such bivariate philosophy coincides with the underlying principles of the pseudopotential, which distinguishes between atom-like core electrons and free-electron-like valence electrons to describe them differently. Rather, pseudopotential-functional consistency amounts to smoothing out this distinction.

The pseudopotential affect the accuracy can also be seen by a formal transformation of Eq. (4). Changing
v_{ps}
to
v_{ps^{\prime}}
under a frozen functional
v_{xc}([n];{\bf r})
, one can rewrite Eq. (4) as

\displaystyle\{-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}+v_{ps}({\bf r})+v_{H}([n];{\bf r})+[v_{xc}([n];{\bf r})+v_{ps^{\prime}}({\bf r})-v_{ps}({\bf r})]\}\psi^{\prime}_{i}({\bf r})=\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}\psi^{\prime}_{i}({\bf r}).

(6)

Notably, it is equivalent to changing the functional
v_{xc}([n];{\bf r})
to
v_{xc}([n];{\bf r})+v_{ps^{\prime}}({\bf r})-v_{ps}({\bf r})
under a frozen
v_{ps}({\bf r})
, which of course changes the electronic structure. Equation (6) also shows that the pseudopotential and the functional are communicating with each other and are not completely independent. This is quite understandable. Changing the pseudopotential implies a change in the interactions between the core and valence electrons, corresponding to a change in the functional that embodies the intricate interactions between the electrons.

Uniqueness of pseudopotentials affecting DFT accuracy is seen by comparing Eqs. (4) and (1). Both have the same structure and thus
v_{ps}({\bf r})
plays the same role as
v_{nu}({\bf r})
. In other words,
v_{ps}({\bf r})
can be regarded as the effective external potential of the outer valence electrons. It is immediately known from the Hohenberg-Kohn theorem that
v_{ps}({\bf r})
determines the ground-state electron density. An incorrect
v_{ps}({\bf r})
in Eq. (4) corresponds to specifying the wrong atomic species in Eq. (1). In this sense, the pseudopotential in solving the electronic structure through Eq. (4) plays a decisive role, which cannot be compensated by the functional. Unfortunately, the pseudopotential has long been overlooked by the DFT community, which has focused its efforts on developing advanced functionals.

With this in mind, we propose the use of a crossed rather than a consistent pseudopotential-functional paradigm when performing pseudopotential-DFT of Eq. (4). This paradigm emphasises that it is the two variables, namely, the pseudopotential and the functional, that collectively determine the DFT accuracy.

In the following, we elaborate on this with the notorious DFT bandgap problem. It is well known that KS gaps (even with exact functionals) underestimate fundamental gaps
DDP
;
DDS

;
Perdew2017

1/{\bf r}
. However, the situation is different in the crossed paradigm, which allows the external potential (i.e., the pseudopotential) to vary. By definition, the fundamental gap of an
N
-electron extended system is the discontinuity of its energy derivative at
N

Fuchs1999

E^{tot}=\sum_{occ}<\psi_{i}({\bf r})|T+v_{ps}({\bf r})|\psi_{i}({\bf r})>+E_{H}+E_{xc},

(7)

the pseudopotential is capable of contributing a derivative discontinuity. Thus, it is possible to reproduce the fundamental gap even for (semi-)local functionals, provided that they are combined with appropriate pseudopotentials. In this respect, the crossed pseudopotential-functional paradigm not only provides a practical route to improve the DFT accuracy, but also holds theoretical value because it goes beyond the (generalized) KS framework. Here, we name it the extended KS framework.

Then, a practical question arises as to which is the appropriate pseudopotential. Since pseudopotentials model pseudo-atoms, those generated by functionals that describe atoms more accurately will naturally be more accurate. In conclusion, we demonstrate both theoretically and numerically that the role of the pseudopotential in electronic structure is as important as that of the exchange-correlation, only in a different way. The pseudopotential simulates the external potential experienced by the outer valence electrons and is completely determined by the atomic properties. Thus, in principle, quantum chemistry methods that perform better on atoms would produce better pseudopotentials. Our bandgap study of 54 monovalent-Cu semiconductors using the hybrid pseudopotential generated by the PBE0 functional supports this assertion. Our findings refute the conventional view that pseudopotential-functional consistency contributes to accuracy. Instead, the crossed paradigm provides a better balance between accuracy and efficiency, which steps outside the (generalized) KS framework and is named the extended KS framework.

The DFT bandgap problem is theoretically understood from an all-electronic perspective, but most practical calculations employ pseudopotential-DFT. Our work fills the gap between the two. If the pseudopotential-DFT is positioned to reproduce all-electron results, one has to constrain the pseudopotential-functional consistency. However, pseudopotentials constructed from approximate functionals suffer from inherent “dropping” errors that can lead to serious deviations from the Hohenberg-Kohn theorem. In this context, no matter how the functional is improved, it is impossible to produce correct results in both the atomic and bulk limits. This may be one of the reasons why DFT studies sometimes yield the correct results for the wrong reasons
Schiffer
. We hope that our work will evoke the DFT community to revisit the importance of pseudopotentials. Although being correct in both the atomic and bulk limits is only a necessary but not a sufficient condition for obtaining physically correct results, once this is achieved it will be possible to accurately describe the entire evolutionary process of the solid formation from the atom.
