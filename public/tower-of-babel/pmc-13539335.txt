Hepatic steatosis risk in nonobese Chinese adults with type 1 diabetes
Authors: Zehua Jin, Jianhua Ma, Yuanyang Zhao, Yi Luo, Christopher K Rayner, Tongzhi Wu, Yong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) may be an important comorbidity in nonobese Chinese adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D). We assessed the likelihood of hepatic steatosis using the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) in 47 nonobese Chinese adults with T1D (mean age 46.9 ± 14.1 years; BMI 22.6 ± 2.5 kg/m2) and examined its association with insulin requirements. Overall, 27.7% of participants had an HSI >36, indicating a high likelihood of MASLD. Compared with those with HSI <30 (low likelihood of MASLD), individuals with HSI >36 required higher bolus and total daily insulin doses, while achieving comparable glycemic profiles. These findings demonstrate that an HSI >36 is relatively common in nonobese Chinese adults with T1D and is associated with higher insulin requirements. MASLD may therefore represent an under‐recognized but clinically relevant comorbidity in nonobese Chinese adults with T1D, warranting greater attention in screening and management strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously termed nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is the most common chronic liver disease. 1 It is strongly associated with insulin resistance and confers increased risks of cardiovascular disease, liver failure, and hepatocellular cancer. 2 , 3 Given its high prevalence in obesity (~75%) 4 and type 2 diabetes (T2D; ~70%), 5 the metabolic consequences of MASLD have been well characterized in these settings. By contrast, the prevalence and clinical relevance of MASLD in type 1 diabetes (T1D), which is traditionally associated with lower body weight, have received limited attention. Emerging evidence from Western populations suggests that MASLD occurs commonly in T1D, 6 contributes to insulin resistance, 7 and predisposes to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease 8 and chronic kidney disease. 9 However, scarce data are available from Asian populations. In China, most adults with T1D are not obese, such that MASLD and its potential adverse metabolic consequences may be under‐recognized. We evaluated the likelihood of MASLD in a cohort of nonobese Chinese adults with T1D using the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) and examined its relationship with insulin requirements.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Forty‐seven Chinese adults with T1D were recruited from the diabetes outpatient clinic of Nanjing First Hospital, China. The study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of Nanjing First Hospital (approval number: KY20230915‐11) prospectively registered (NCT06173934) and conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Thirty participants were admitted for approximately 1 week for optimization of insulin therapy (based on the 7‐point blood glucose profile), while 17 were managed in outpatient clinics. Once acceptable glycemic control was achieved, basal and bolus insulin doses were recorded. Laboratory assessments included urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR), serum creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol, triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C). Medical records were reviewed to document the presence of autonomic neuropathy and diabetic retinopathy.

The likelihood of hepatic steatosis was estimated using the HSI, calculated as 8 × (ALT/AST ratio) + BMI, with an additional 2 points applied for female sex and 2 points for diabetes mellitus. As established previously, 10 , 11 an HSI <30 was considered to indicate a low likelihood of MASLD and an HSI >36 a high likelihood of MASD. Because HSI values of 30–36 are indeterminate for screening purposes, inferential comparisons were restricted to the HSI <30 and HSI >36 groups; the intermediate group was retained in the descriptive analyses. Given the modest sample size, these comparisons were considered exploratory. Groups were compared using unpaired Student's t‐tests or chi‐squared tests, as appropriate (GraphPad Prism, La Jolla, CA, USA). For insulin‐dose outcomes, between‐group mean differences and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported in addition to P values. Data are reported as means ± SD; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Among the 47 participants, none had a BMI greater than 28 kg/m2 (mean age 46.9 ± 14.1 years; 35 male; BMI 22.6 ± 2.5 kg/m2; duration of known T1D 11.7 ± 8.3 years; HbA1c 8.9 ± 1.8%; n = 17 managed by insulin pump therapy and n = 30 by multiple daily insulin injections). Fourteen participants (29.8%) had an HSI <30 and 13 (27.7%) had an HSI >36. Compared with those with HSI <30, participants with HSI >36 had similar clinical characteristics, except for higher body weight, BMI, and ALT and AST levels (Table 1). Following adjustment of insulin therapy, 7‐point glucose profiles were comparable between the two groups (Figure S1). Notably, participants with HSI >36 required higher bolus insulin doses (0.12 IU/kg/day; 95% CI 0.03–0.21 IU/kg/day, P = 0.014) and greater total daily insulin requirements (0.15 IU/kg/day; 95% CI 0.01–0.29 IU/kg/day, P = 0.039) than those with HSI <30 (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In this cohort of nonobese Chinese adults with T1D, nearly one third of participants had an HSI >36, indicating a high likelihood of MASLD. Individuals in this group required higher bolus and total daily insulin doses than those with HSI <30 despite comparable 7‐point glucose profiles. Although higher weight‐adjusted insulin requirements may be compatible with lower insulin sensitivity, insulin sensitivity was not measured directly in this study. The observed differences should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of a mechanistic relationship between hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance.

MASLD is known to be associated with overweight/obesity. 12 However, a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis has revealed that MASLD is also common in individuals without obesity, with prevalence ranging from 10 to 20% in some countries (e.g., Malaysia and Pakistan) to >50% in others (e.g., Austria, Mexico, Sweden). 13 Consistent with this, a cross‐sectional study of Chinese adults with BMI <24 kg/m2 reported an 18% prevalence of MASLD. 14 These data, together with our findings, underscore that MASLD is likely to be common even in lean/nonobese populations.

The management of T1D in China remains focused predominantly on insulin deficiency, with limited attention to insulin resistance and its associated metabolic comorbidities. These issues are increasingly recognized in western T1D populations, in which obesity is rising in prevalence 15 but have been largely overlooked in Asians with T1D probably due to their relatively normal BMI. In our study of nonobese Chinese adults with T1D, participants with HSI >36, consistent with a high likelihood of MASLD, required higher bolus and total daily insulin doses than those with HSI <30. However, hepatic fat and insulin sensitivity were not measured directly; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as an association with the HSI category rather than evidence that hepatic steatosis caused insulin resistance. This cross‐sectional association is hypothesis‐generating and may also be influenced by determinants of insulin dose unrelated to hepatic fat. Nevertheless, future studies are warranted to investigate whether adjunctive therapies that target insulin resistance or hepatic lipid metabolism, such as metformin or glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, could provide complementary benefits to insulin therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest and participants were recruited from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the risk of hepatic steatosis was estimated indirectly using the HSI rather than imaging‐based methods. The HSI incorporates BMI, the ALT/AST ratio, sex, and diabetes status and has not been specifically validated in nonobese Chinese adults with T1D. Consequently, the association between HSI category and insulin requirement may partly reflect the variables used to construct the index or other unmeasured determinants of insulin dose, including body composition, diet, physical activity, and injection technique, rather than hepatic fat itself. Adjustment for the HSI components would also be difficult to interpret because these variables mathematically define the exposure, and the modest sample provides limited scope for multivariable modeling. Third, direct measures of insulin sensitivity were not available, precluding more precise characterization of the relationship between HSI and insulin resistance. Finally, the cross‐sectional study design does not allow causal inferences. Confirmation using direct quantification of hepatic fat and insulin sensitivity in a larger cohort is required.

Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that a high HSI is not uncommon in nonobese Chinese adults with T1D and may identify a subgroup warranting further evaluation for hepatic steatosis. Larger longitudinal studies employing direct assessments of hepatic fat are needed to determine the prevalence and metabolic consequences of MASLD in T1D in this population and to inform targeted screening and management strategies.
