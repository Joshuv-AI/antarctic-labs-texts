Avoidance of vincular patterns by flattened derangements
Toufik Mansour

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the problem of avoiding a single vincular pattern of length three by derangements in the flattened sense and find explicit formulas for the generating functions enumerating members of each corresponding avoidance class according to the number of cycles. We make frequent use of the kernel method in solving the functional equations that arise which are satisfied by these (ordinary) generating functions. In the case of avoiding 23-1, which is equivalent to 32-1 in the flattened sense, it is more convenient to consider the exponential generating function instead due to the form of the recurrence. This leads to an explicit expression for the distribution of the number of cycles in terms of Stirling numbers of the second kind and the determinant of a certain tridiagonal matrix. Finally, the cases of 3-12 and 3-21 are perhaps the most difficult of all, and here we make use of a pair of auxiliary statistics in order to find a system of recurrences that enumerate each avoidance class.



Keywords:
pattern avoidance, flattened permutation, vincular pattern, derangement.

Introduction

Let
\rho=\rho_{1}\cdots\rho_{n}
and
\tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{m}
be permutations of
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
[m]
, where
1\leq m\leq n
. Then
\rho
is said to
contain
an occurrence of
\tau
if there exists a subsequence of
\rho
that is order-isomorphic to
\tau
and is said to
avoid

\tau
otherwise. In this context,
\tau
is often referred to as a
pattern
. Suppose dashes are inserted between some of the letters of
\tau
such that
\tau=\tau^{(1)}\text{-}\tau^{(2)}\text{-}\cdots\text{-}\tau^{(k)}
for some
k\geq 1
, where
\tau^{(i)}
for each
i\in[k]
represents a sequence of consecutive letters of
\tau
none of which are separated by dashes. Then
\rho
is said to contain
\tau
, as a
vincular
, or
dashed
, pattern if
\rho
contains a subsequence
s
that is isomorphic to
\tau
such that any pair of letters of
s
corresponding to adjacent entries within some
\tau^{(i)}
must be adjacent in
\rho
. If no such subsequence
s
exists that is isomorphic to
\tau
and meeting the adjacency requirements, then
\rho
avoids
\tau
as a vincular pattern. If
a_{i}=|\tau^{(i)}|
for
1\leq i\leq k
, then the vincular pattern
\tau=\tau^{(1)}\text{-}\cdots\text{-}\tau^{(k)}
is said to be of
type

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
. For example, the permutation
\rho=4315762\in\mathcal{S}_{7}
contains an occurrence of the vincular pattern 23-1, as witnessed by the subsequence 572. However,
\rho
avoids the pattern 3-12, though it contains a couple of subsequences that are isomorphic to 312. A vincular pattern for which
a_{i}=1
for all

is referred to as
classical
, meaning that there is no adjacency requirement for letters within an occurrence of the pattern. Hence, in the preceding example,
\rho
contains two occurrences of the classical pattern 3-1-2. We refer the reader to the text by Kitaev
[
7

for a full discussion of vincular and other types of patterns.

Suppose
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is expressed in standard cycle form, meaning that its cycles are written from left to right in ascending order of their first elements, with the first element the smallest in each cycle. The

of
\pi

\text{flat}(\pi)

\pi
and considering the resulting permutation in one-line notation. For example, if
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{8}
has disjoint cycles
(784)
,
(62)
and
(135)
, then its standard cycle form is
(135)(26)(478)
and
\text{flat}(\pi)=13526478
. Flattened permutations were apparently first considered by Carlitz
[
4
]
in his definition of a certain kind of inversion statistic on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, which was further studied in
[
14
]
. Later, Callan
[
3
]
introduced the concept of a flattened partition of
[n]
, where the idea is basically the same except now brackets enclosing the various blocks are erased so as to obtain the flattened form. He considered the avoidance problem on flattened partitions and found the cardinality of each avoidance class corresponding to a single classical pattern of length three. Further variants of the notion of flattening have been put forth on other discrete structures, such as Catalan words
[
1
]
, Stirling permutations
[
2
]
and parking functions
[
5
]
.

In analogy with the definition given in
[
3
]
for set partitions, we will say that
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
contains or avoids a pattern
\tau

in the flattened sense
if
\pi^{\prime}=\text{flat}(\pi)
contains or avoids
\tau
in the usual sense. In this setting,
\tau
may be any kind of pattern, i.e., classical, dashed or consecutive. For example, let
\pi=419738265\in\mathcal{S}_{9}
, which has standard cycle form
(1472)(395)(68)
. Then
\pi
contains the vincular pattern 2-31 in the flattened sense as
\pi^{\prime}=147239568
contains an occurrence of the pattern (witnessed by the subsequence 795). Further, it is seen that
\pi
avoids 12-3 and contains 3-21 in the usual sense, though it avoids 3-21 and contains 12-3 in the flattened sense, with both
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
containing 1-23. In general, the avoidance or containment of a pattern by a permutation in one sense is not related to its avoidance or containment in the other.

A

is a permutation containing no fixed points, i.e., each of its cycles is of length at least two. We will denote the set of all derangements of

by
\mathcal{D}(n)

in the flattened sense. This extends prior work concerning pattern avoidance in the flattened sense as well as related statistics on such structures as permutations
[
9
,
11
]
, set partitions
[
3
]
, Catalan words
[
1
]
, Stirling permutations
[

]
and parking functions
[

[

,
13

[

Given a pattern

The organization of this paper is as follows. In the next section, we consider the avoidance of a pattern of type (2,1) by derangements in the flattened sense. The case 13-2 may be dealt with quickly. For three other patterns, we refine the avoidance class in question by considering the second or third letter in the flattened form, which enables one to write recurrences enumerating members of each class according to the number of cycles. These recurrences lead to functional equations satisfied by the corresponding ordinary generating functions, for which we make use of the

[
6

In the third section, we provide a comparable treatment for patterns of type (1,2). Avoiding a vincular pattern of type (2,1)

Throughout this section, we will denote

We first consider the case of avoiding 13-2, which can be dealt with quickly. Let
f_{n}(y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n-k}{k}y^{k}

denote the

Proposition 2.1
.

If

, then
d_{13\text{-}2}(n;y)=yf_{n-2}(y)
.

Proof.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{D}_{13\text{-}2}(n)
, where
n\geq 3
. Note that the second letter of
\pi^{\prime}
must be 2 in order to avoid an occurrence of 13-2. This in turn implies 3 must be the third letter, and so on, whence
\pi^{\prime}=12\cdots n
. Suppose
\pi
contains

and let
S
denote the set of cycle starters of
\pi
. Note that

always contains 1, never contains

and contains no two consecutive elements. Thus, there are

choices for

for some

as desired.
∎

2.1
The pattern 31-2

Let
a_{n}=a_{n}(y)
denote the distribution
d_{31\text{-}2}(n;y)
for

. Given

, let
a_{n,i}=a_{n,i}(y)

Lemma 2.2

a_{n,i}=\delta_{i,3}\cdot ya_{n-2}+\sum_{j=i-1}^{n-1}a_{n-1,j},\qquad 3\leq i\leq n-1,

Let

enumerated by

Define the generating function
A(x,v)=A(x,y,v)
by

A(x,v)=\sum_{n\geq 2}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}a_{n,i}v^{i-2}\right)x^{n}.

Then
A(x,v)
satisfies the following functional equation.

Lemma 2.3
.

We have

(1-v+xv^{2})A(x,v)=x^{2}y(1-v)+x(1+xy-xyv^{2})A(x,1).

(2)

Proof.

First note

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 4}x^{n}\sum_{i=3}^{n-1}a_{n-1,i-1}v^{i-2}=\sum_{n\geq 3}x^{n+1}\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}a_{n,i}v^{i-1}=xv\left(A(x,v)-\sum_{n\geq 2}a_{n,n}x^{n}v^{n-2}\right)

\displaystyle=xv\left(A(x,v)-\frac{x^{2}y}{1-xv}\right)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 4}\sum_{i=3}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}a_{n-1,j}x^{n}v^{i-2}=\sum_{n\geq 4}\sum_{j=3}^{n-1}a_{n-1,j}x^{n}\sum_{i=3}^{j}v^{i-2}=\sum_{n\geq 3}\sum_{j=3}^{n}a_{n,j}x^{n+1}\left(\frac{v-v^{j-1}}{1-v}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{xv}{1-v}\sum_{n\geq 2}\sum_{j=2}^{n}a_{n,j}x^{n}(1-v^{j-2})=\frac{xv}{1-v}\left(A(x,1)-A(x,v)\right).

Multiplying both sides of (
1
) by
x^{n}v^{i-2}
, summing over all
n\geq 4
and
3\leq i\leq n-1
, and considering the contributions from
a_{n,2}
and
a_{n,n}
then yields

\displaystyle A(x,v)-x^{2}y-x^{3}y(1+v)

\displaystyle=xv\left(A(x,v)-\frac{x^{2}y}{1-xv}\right)+\frac{xv}{1-v}\left(A(x,1)-A(x,v)\right)+yv\sum_{n\geq 4}a_{n-2}x^{n}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n\geq 4}(a_{n-1}+ya_{n-2})x^{n}+y\sum_{n\geq 4}x^{n}v^{n-2}

\displaystyle=xv\left(A(x,v)-\frac{x^{2}y}{1-xv}\right)+\frac{xv}{1-v}\left(A(x,1)-A(x,v)\right)+x^{2}yvA(x,1)

\displaystyle\quad+x(1+xy)A(x,1)-x^{3}y+\frac{x^{4}yv^{2}}{1-xv}.

The last equality may be rewritten as

\left(1+\frac{xv^{2}}{1-v}\right)A(x,v)=x^{2}y+\left(\frac{x}{1-v}+x^{2}y(1+v)\right)A(x,1),

which implies (
2
).
∎

Let
C=C(x)
denote the Catalan number generating function
\sum_{n\geq 0}C_{n}x^{n}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x}
. One can find an explicit formula for
A(x,v)
and also for the coefficients of

as follows.

Theorem 2.4
.

The generating function enumerating members of
\mathcal{D}_{31\text{-}2}(n)
for
n\geq 2
jointly according to the number of cycles and the second letter in the flattened form (marked by
y
and
v
, respectively) is given by

A(x,v)=\frac{x^{2}y(1-v)}{1-v+xv^{2}}+\frac{x^{2}y(1+xy-xyv^{2})(C-1)}{(1-v+xv^{2})(1-xy(C^{2}-1))}.

(3)

Moreover, the number of members of
\mathcal{D}_{31\text{-}2}(n)
with
m
cycles, where
1\leq m\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, is given by

\frac{2(m-1)}{n-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-2}{j}\binom{2n-2j-3}{n-m-2}+\frac{1}{n-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{2n-2j-2}{n-m-1}.

Proof.

To solve (
2
), we apply the kernel method and let
v=C(x)
. By the fact
xC^{2}=C-1
, this cancels out the left-hand side of (
2
) and solving for
A(x,1)
gives

A(x,1)=\frac{xy(C-1)}{1-xy(C^{2}-1)}.

(4)

Substituting (
4
) back into (
2
) yields (
3
).

Note that the number of members of
\mathcal{D}_{31\text{-}2}(n)
with
m
cycles is
[x^{n}y^{m}]A(x,1)
for
1\leq m\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. By (
4
), we have

\displaystyle[x^{n}y^{m}]A(x,1)

\displaystyle=[x^{n}y^{m}]\left(\sum_{r\geq 1}(C-1)(C^{2}-1)^{r-1}(xy)^{r}\right)=[x^{n-m}]\left((C-1)(C^{2}-1)^{m-1}\right)

\displaystyle=[x^{n-m}]\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-1}{j}C^{2m-2j-1}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-1}{j}C^{2m-2j-2}\right).

Applying now the formula
[x^{a}]C^{b}=\frac{b}{a+b}\binom{2a+b-1}{a}

a\geq 0
and
b\geq 1

[

, we have

\displaystyle[x^{n}y^{m}]A(x,1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\frac{2m-2j-1}{n+m-2j-1}\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{2n-2j-2}{n-m}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{j}\frac{2m-2j-2}{n+m-2j-2}\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{2n-2j-3}{n-m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\frac{2m-2j-1}{n-m}\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{2n-2j-2}{n-m-1}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{j}\frac{2m-2j-2}{n-m}\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{2n-2j-3}{n-m-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{n-m}\binom{2n-2m}{n-m-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{n-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-1}{j}\left((2m-2j-1)\binom{2n-2j-2}{n-m-1}-(2m-2j-2)\binom{2n-2j-3}{n-m-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{n-m}\binom{2n-2m}{n-m-1}+\frac{1}{n-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-1}{j}\left((2m-2j-2)\binom{2n-2j-3}{n-m-2}+\binom{2n-2j-2}{n-m-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{n-m}\binom{2n-2m}{n-m-1}+\frac{2}{n-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{j}(m-j-1)\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{2n-2j-3}{n-m-2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{n-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{2n-2j-2}{n-m-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(m-1)}{n-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-2}{j}\binom{2n-2j-3}{n-m-2}+\frac{1}{n-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{2n-2j-2}{n-m-1},

as desired.

(22)

where
\det(M_{0})=1
and the matrix
M_{j}
for
j\geq 1
is as defined above.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{n}=-\frac{n}{n+2}
and
\beta_{n}=\frac{y+(y+1)n}{(n+1)(n+2)}
. Then recurrence (
21
) may be written as
a_{n+2}=\alpha_{n}a_{n+1}+\beta_{n}a_{n}
for
n\geq 2
, with
a_{2}=\beta_{0}=y/2
and
\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{1}\beta_{0}
. This implies
a_{n+2}
for
n\geq 1
is given as a determinant by

a_{n+2}=\frac{y}{2}\left|\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\alpha_{1}&-1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\beta_{2}&\alpha_{2}&-1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\beta_{3}&\alpha_{3}&-1&\cdots&0\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
0&\cdots&0&\beta_{n-1}&\alpha_{n-1}&-1\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\beta_{n}&\alpha_{n}\\
\end{array}\right|,

and hence

a_{n+2}=\frac{y}{(n+2)!}\left|\begin{array}[]{lllllll}-1&-3&0&0&\cdots&0\\
y+\frac{2}{3}&-2&-4&0&\cdots&0\\
0&y+\frac{3}{4}&-3&-5&\cdots&0\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
0&\cdots&0&y+\frac{n-1}{n}&-(n-1)&-(n+1)\\
0&\cdots&0&0&y+\frac{n}{n+1}&-n\\
\end{array}\right|.

By (
19
) and the definitions, we then have

e_{n}(y)=\sum_{k=2}^{n}S(n,k)k!a_{k}=y\sum_{k=2}^{n}S(n,k)\det(M_{k-2}),

as desired.
∎

3
Patterns of type (1,2)

In this section, we consider the problem of avoiding a vincular pattern of type (1,2) in the flattened sense. We first remark that the case 1-23 is trivial in that a derangement
\pi
which avoids 1-23 must have flattened form
1n(n-1)\cdots 2
, and hence only
(1n(n-1)\cdots 2)
is possible, by the ordering of cycles. Also, if
\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)
avoids 1-32, then
\pi^{\prime}=1\cdots n
, and hence
d_{1\text{-}32}(n;y)=yf_{n-2}(y)
, upon reasoning as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
. In particular, we have
d_{1\text{-}32}(n)=f_{n-2}
for all
n\geq 2
.

We now argue that 2-31 is logically equivalent to the classical patterns 2-3-1, with the avoiders of the latter having already been enumerated in
[
10
, Theorem 2.4]
. Clearly, we have
\mathcal{D}_{2\text{-}3\text{-}1}(n)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{2\text{-}31}(n)
. To show the reverse inclusion, we argue that if
\pi\in\mathcal{D}(n)
is such that its flattened form
\pi^{\prime}=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}
contains an occurrence of 2-3-1, then it must also contain 2-31. Suppose
\pi_{i}\pi_{j}\pi_{k}
is such that
\pi_{k}<\pi_{i}<\pi_{j}
, where
1\leq i<j<k\leq n
. Let
\ell_{0}
denote the smallest index
\ell\in[j+1,k]
such that
\pi_{\ell}<\pi_{i}
; note that
\ell_{0}
exists since
\pi_{k}<\pi_{i}
. Then each of
\pi_{j},\pi_{j-1},\ldots,\pi_{\ell_{0}-1}
must be greater than
\pi_{i}
, by the minimality of
\ell_{0}
. In particular, we have that
\pi_{i}\pi_{\ell_{0}-1}\pi_{\ell_{0}}
is an occurrence of 2-31 in
\pi^{\prime}
. Similar reasoning shows that 2-13 and 2-1-3 are logically equivalent as well, with avoiders of the latter having been enumerated in
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
.

The remaining two patterns of type (1,2), namely 3-12 and 3-21, are treated in the following two subsections.

3.1
The case 3-12

To determine a recurrence enumerating the members of
\mathcal{D}_{\tau}(n)
in this and the subsequent case, we will consider a refinement of the distribution
d_{\tau}(n;y)
as follows. Let
z_{n}=d_{3\text{-}12}(n;y)
for
n\geq 2
, with
z_{1}=0
, and let
z_{n,i,j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
denote the restriction of
z_{n}
to the members of
\mathcal{D}_{3\text{-}12}(n)
whose last cycle (in standard cycle form) starts with
i
and ends in
j
.
Note
z_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}z_{n,i,j}
, by the definitions. For example, when
n=4
, we have
z_{4,1,2}=z_{4,1,4}=2y
,
z_{4,1,3}=y
,
z_{4,2,3}=0
,
z_{4,2,4}=z_{4,3,4}=y^{2}
, and hence
z_{4}=2y^{2}+5y
.

The array
z_{n,i,j}
is given recursively as follows.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
n\geq 3
, then

z_{n,i,j}=\sum_{\ell=j}^{n-1}z_{n-1,i,\ell},\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq n-1,

(23)

and

z_{n,i,n}=\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{n-1}z_{n-1,i,\ell}+y\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}z_{n-1}{\ell,i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n-2,

(24)

with
z_{n,n-1,n}=yz_{n-2}
and the initial condition
z_{2,1,2}=y
.

Proof.

We may assume
n\geq 3
, the initial condition for
n=2
being clear. Let
\mathcal{Z}_{n,i,j}
denote the subset of
\mathcal{D}_{3\text{-}12}(n)
whose members are enumerated by
z_{n,i,j}
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Let
\pi\in\mathcal{Z}_{n,i,j}
, with
\pi^{\prime}=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{n}
, and first suppose
j<n
. Note that we then must have
\pi_{n-1}=\ell+1
for some
\ell\in[j,n-1]
, for otherwise there would be an occurrence of 3-12 as witnessed by the subsequence
\pi_{s}\pi_{n-1}\pi_{n}
of
\pi^{\prime}
, where
s<n-1
is such that
\pi_{s}=n
. Deleting
j
, and then reducing each letter of
\pi
in
[j+1,n]
by one, is seen to yield an arbitrary member of
\mathcal{Z}_{n-1,i,\ell}
, as the first element of the last cycle is unchanged, with this cycle initially being of length at least three. Considering all
\ell\in[j,n-1]
then implies (
23
).

Now suppose
\pi\in\mathcal{Z}_{n,i,n}
, with
i\in[n-2]
. If the last cycle of
\pi
has length three or more, with
\pi_{n-1}=\ell
, then
\ell\in[i+1,n-1]
, by the ordering of cycles, and considering all possible
\ell
yields a contribution towards the weight of
\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{n-1}z_{n-1,i,\ell}
. On the other hand, if the final cycle of
\pi
has length two, then we may delete the terminal cycle
(in)
and append
i
to the penultimate cycle to obtain a derangement of length
n-1
, where
n\geq 3
implies
i\geq 2
in this case. Indeed, all of the members of
\cup_{\ell=1}^{i-1}\mathcal{Z}_{n-1,\ell,i}
arise uniquely in this fashion since
i<n-1
implies each derangement in this set has terminal cycle length at least three. Taking into account the deleted cycle
(in)
, we get a contribution of
y\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}z_{n-1,\ell,i}
, and combining with the prior case yields (
24
). Finally, members of the sets
\mathcal{Z}_{n,n-1,n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{3\text{-}12}(n-2)
where
n\geq 4
are seen to be synonymous, upon deleting the terminal cycle containing
n-1
and
n
from members of the former. This implies
z_{n,n-1,n}=yz_{n-2}
and completes the proof.
∎

Let
Z_{n}(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}z_{n,i,j}u^{n-1-i}v^{n-j}
for

and

. Then

satisfies the following functional equation.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have

\left(1-\frac{xuv}{1-v}\right)Z(x,u,v)=\frac{x^{2}y}{1+xy}-\frac{xuv^{2}}{1-v}Z(x,uv,1)+xuZ(x,u,1)+xyZ(x,1,u)-\frac{x^{2}y^{2}}{1+xy}Z(x,1,1).

(25)

Proof.

Using (
23
) and (
24
), one can show

\displaystyle Z_{n}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{uv}{1-v}\left(Z_{n-1}(u,v)-vZ_{n-1}(uv,1)\right)+uZ_{n-1}(u,1)

\displaystyle\quad+y\left(Z_{n-1}(1,u)+Z_{n-2}(1,1)-Z_{n-1}(1,0)\right),\qquad n\geq 3,

(26)

with
Z_{1}(u,v)=0
and
Z_{2}(u,v)=y
. By (

), we then have

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{xuv}{1-v}\right)Z(x,u,v)

\displaystyle=x^{2}y-\frac{xuv^{2}}{1-v}Z(x,uv,1)+xuZ(x,u,1)

\displaystyle\quad+xy(Z(x,1,u)+xZ(x,1,1)-Z(x,1,0)).

(27)

We may eliminate the

term from (

\displaystyle Z_{n}(1,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{n,i,n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{n-1}z_{n-1,i,\ell}+y\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}z_{n-1,\ell,i}+yz_{n-2}

\displaystyle=Z_{n-1}(1,1)+yZ_{n-2}(1,1)+y(Z_{n-1}(1,1)-Z_{n-1}(1,0))

\displaystyle=(1+y)Z_{n-1}(1,1)+yZ_{n-2}(1,1)-yZ_{n-1}(1,0),\qquad n\geq 3.

Multiplying both sides of (

(29)

Formula (

29

∎

Though (
25
) does not appear to have an explicit solution for

Z_{m}(x,u,v)=[y^{m}]Z(x,u,v)

Z^{\prime}_{m}(x,u)=(-x)^{m+1}+xZ_{m-1}(x,1,u)-\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}(-x)^{j}Z_{m-j}(x,1,1),\qquad m\geq 2,

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
m\geq 1
be fixed. The generating function

enumerating the members of
\mathcal{D}_{3\text{-}12}(n)
for

and

and

\displaystyle Z_{m}(x,u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-v}{1-v-xuv}Z^{\prime}_{m}(x,u)-\frac{xuv^{2}}{1-v-xuv}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(xuv)^{j}}{\prod_{i=1}^{j+1}(1-ixuv)}Z^{\prime}_{m}\left(x,\frac{uv}{1-(j+1)xuv}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{xu(1-v)}{1-v-xuv}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{(xu)^{j}}{\prod_{i=1}^{j+1}(1-ixu)}Z^{\prime}_{m}\left(x,\frac{u}{1-(j+1)xu}\right),\qquad m\geq 1,

where

and

denote the first and last letters respectively of the final cycle in a member of
\mathcal{D}_{3\text{-}12}(n)

Proof.

Extracting the coefficient of

on both sides of (

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{xuv}{1-v}\right)Z_{m}(x,u,v)

\displaystyle=(-x)^{m+1}-\frac{xuv^{2}}{1-v}Z_{m}(x,uv,1)+xuZ_{m}(x,u,1)+xZ_{m-1}(x,1,u)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}(-x)^{j}Z_{m-j}(x,1,1),

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{xuv}{u-v}\right)Z_{m}(x,u,v/u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{xv^{2}}{u-v}Z_{m}(x,v,1)+xuZ_{m}(x,u,1)+Z^{\prime}_{m}(x,u),\qquad m\geq 1,

(31)

where
Z^{\prime}_{m}(x,u)
is as stated, upon replacing

with

. Applying the kernel method, and taking

Z_{m}(x,v,1)=\frac{xv}{(1-xv)^{2}}Z_{m}\left(x,\frac{v}{1-xv},1\right)+\frac{1}{1-xv}Z^{\prime}_{m}\left(x,\frac{v}{1-xv}\right).

Z_{m}(x,v,1)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{x^{j}v^{j}}{\prod_{i=1}^{j+1}(1-ixv)}Z^{\prime}_{m}\left(x,\frac{v}{1-(j+1)xv}\right),

from which (
30

).
∎

Formula (

for

, as

depends only upon the generating functions
Z_{j}(x,v,u)
for

. Note that the number of cycles parameter, in addition to the statistics marked by

and

3.2
The case 3-21

Let
r_{n,i,j}
be defined in analogy with
z_{n,i,j}
above, but in conjunction with the pattern 3-21. To aid in finding a recursion for
r_{n,i,j}
, let
s_{n,i,j}
denote the restriction of
r_{n,i,j}
to those members in which the final cycle has length at least three. For example, when
n=4
, we have
r_{4,1,2}=y
,
r_{4,1,3}=r_{4,1,4}=2y
,

, with

if

.

The arrays
r_{n,i,j}
and

satisfy the following system of intertwined recurrences, where
[S]
equals
one or zero depending upon the truth or falsehood of the statement
S
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
n\geq 3
, then

r_{n,i,j}=[j<n]r_{n-1,i,n-1}+\sum_{k=i+1}^{j-1}r_{n-1,i,k}+y\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}s_{n-1,\ell,i},\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq n,

(33)

and

(34)

with
r_{2,1,2}=y
and
s_{2,1,2}=0
.

Proof.

The initial conditions when
n=2
are clear, so assume
n\geq 3
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{n,i,j}
and
\mathcal{S}_{n,i,j}
denote the subsets of
\mathcal{D}_{3\text{-}21}(n)
whose members are enumerated by
r_{n,i,j}
and
s_{n,i,j}
, respectively. We first show the
j=n
case of (
33
), that is,

r_{n,i,n}=\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}r_{n-1,i,k}+y\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}s_{n-1,\ell,i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n-1.

(35)

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{R}_{n,i,n}
, where
n\geq 3
and
1\leq i\leq n-1
. If the final cycle of
\pi
has length at least three, then deletion of
n
implies that one gets a contribution of
\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}r_{n-1,i,k}
towards

(in)

\pi

\pi\in\mathcal{R}_{n,i,j}

Let
