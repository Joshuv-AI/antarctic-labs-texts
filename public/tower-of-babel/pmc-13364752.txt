Muscle Mass as a Causal Factor in MASLD: Insights from a Genome-Wide Association Study and Bidirectional Mendelian Randomization Using Data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study
Authors: Kiook Baek, Jung Gil Park, Yu Rim Lee, Soo Young Park, Young Oh Kweon, Won Young Tak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is an emerging global health concern due to its increasing prevalence and associated risk of severe complications. Although body composition, such as fat and muscle mass, has been linked to MASLD, evidence on the causality of this relationship remains limited. This study investigated the potential causal relationships between body composition and MASLD using epidemiological methods.MethodsData from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study were analyzed. MASLD patients were defined according to the hepatic steatosis index (≥36) and the fibrosis 4 index (≥1.30); individuals with both of these values in normal ranges were considered nonpatients. Body composition was assessed according to fat mass, muscle mass, and the waist-to-hip ratio using bioelectrical impedance analysis. Genome-wide association studies were conducted to identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to body composition and MASLD. These SNPs were then used as instrumental variables in bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to determine causality.ResultsA total of 258 MASLD patients and 3,498 nonpatients were included. The MR analysis showed that individuals in the lowest tertile of muscle mass had a higher prevalence of MASLD than those in the 2nd and 3rd tertiles (odds ratio, 1.57; 95% confidence interval, 1.09 to 2.27; p=0.02), whereas the reverse causal effect of MASLD on muscle mass was not significant. No significant causal relationships were identified between fat mass or the waist-to-hip ratio and MASLD, although these analyses were limited by several statistical constraints, such as relatively low F-statistics and potential heterogeneity.ConclusionsThe findings demonstrate that muscle mass exerts a causal effect on the prevalence of MASLD, suggesting that modulating muscle mass could serve as an effective strategy for managing the disease.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously called nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is recognized as the most common chronic liver disease and the second leading cause of liver transplantation in the United States, posing a significant global public health concern.1 While MASLD and NAFLD are not identical concepts, they share substantial overlap, with MASLD emerging as an updated nomenclature to emphasize the metabolic underpinnings of the disease.2 MASLD is a major risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma, progressing from steatosis to fibrosis and cirrhosis, and potentially leading to hepatocellular carcinoma.3 Furthermore, epidemiological studies have shown that MASLD significantly increases liver-related mortality, all-cause mortality,4 and even cardiovascular disease mortality.5 Consequently, MASLD, once regarded as a benign or incidental finding, is now increasingly recognized as a condition requiring prevention and management, making it a higher priority in public health.6

MASLD has been reported to be associated with body composition, such as muscle mass, and shares certain mechanisms with conditions such as sarcopenia.7,8 Additionally, excessive body fat, particularly abdominal fat, has been found to have a significant correlation with MASLD.9 However, most studies are cross-sectional, and studies capable of verifying causality are limited in design, making it unclear whether there is a directional relationship between body composition and MASLD, or NAFLD.10,11 Against this backdrop, this study aims to clarify the causal relationship between MASLD and body composition.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Participants and data source

This study utilized data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study, specifically the cohort data collected from Ansan and Ansung. This cohort initially collected baseline data from 2000 to 2001. The Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study baseline involved collecting epidemiological data, genetic information, blood, urine, and other biological samples, and body composition measurements from a general population aged 40 to 69 years. Participants with missing data for variables of interest and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were excluded from the analysis, and cancer patients were excluded.

2. SNP testing

SNP testing was performed using the Affymetrix Genome-Wide Human SNP Array 5.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA), using 352,228 markers. The data underwent quality control, excluding data with a Bayesian Robust Linear Model with Mahalanobis distance call rate <96%, heterozygosity <39%, identity by state <0.8, and sex mismatch. The final raw data used in this study was subject to further quality control before analysis, with criteria including missingness per individual <10%, missingness per marker <10%, allele frequency >5%, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium p>0.001.

3. MASLD classification

For the classification of MASLD, two indices— hepatic steatosis index (HSI)12,13 and fibrosis 4 (FIB-4)14-16—were used, which were originally developed for NAFLD, and also have been validated for MASLD. The MASLD group was defined as those meeting both criteria of HSI ≥36 and FIB-4 ≥1.30, while the non-case group met neither criterion. Participants with exceeding the cutoff in both indices were classified as having MASLD, while those with neither elevated index were classified as non-cases (controls). In order to ensure a stricter case–non-case contrast, participants who satisfied only one of the two diagnostic criteria were excluded from the analytic sample.

Considering that the indices used were developed for the traditional definition of “non-alcoholic,” this study applied a strict alcohol consumption category to include only “pure MASLD” by excluding metabolic alcoholic liver disease cases. Metabolic alcoholic liver disease is typically defined as alcohol consumption of 140 g/week for women and 210 g/week for men;17 however, this study applied a stricter definition by excluding participants with alcohol consumption exceeding 140 g/week for both sexes.

4. Body composition measurement

Participants underwent tests at local medical facilities. Body mass index was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height (m2). Body composition was measured using multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (Inbody 3.0, Biospace, Seoul, Korea).

In this study, muscle mass, fat mass, and waist-hip ratio were used as variables. The ratio of soft lean mass to body weight was used to calculate muscle mass.18 The fat mass was calculated by dividing total fat by body weight. The waist-hip ratio, used as an index of abdominal obesity,19 was calculated by dividing waist circumference by hip circumference, automatically by multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis.20 For each index, both continuous values and values categorized by tertiles of each sex were analyzed (1 tertile vs 2 and 3 tertiles). When categorizing tertiles of each body composition index, all available data were used, regardless of HSI and FIB-4 scores.

5. Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yeungnam University Hospital (IRB number: 2023-07-041). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the IRB.

6. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented for the general demographic characteristics, clinical chemistry tests used for the clinical classification of MASLD, and body composition data collected from the study participants. Bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) was used to identify a bidirectional relationship.21 Genome-wide association study (GWAS) results were used to identify SNPs strongly associated with the exposure variable, which were then selected as instrumental variables (IVs) for the MR analysis. GWAS for MASLD prevalence, muscle mass, fat mass, and waist-hip ratio was performed. Linear regression was performed for continuous variables, and logistic regression was performed for binary variables, based on an additive model. This study used a cutoff of p<1×10-5. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed to account for LD among genetic variants (SNPs), selecting the SNP with the lowest p-value while applying an LD threshold of R2 >0.1 and a distance <250 kb.

Statistical tests were performed on each confounder, outcome, and SNP, excluding SNPs with p-values <0.05 to avoid violating the assumption of MR. The robust inverse variance-weighted method (IVW) was used for MR,22 and when more than two SNPs were available as IVs, results from the Egger method were presented to check for the possibility of pleiotropy.23 When the MR results involve a mixture of linear regressions (for continuous variables) and logistic regressions (for binary variables), it is appropriate to use these results primarily to confirm the null hypothesis rather than calculating causal estimates or effect sizes.24

To conduct bidirectional MR, SNPs affecting MASLD were used as IVs in the first analysis, treating MASLD as the exposure and each body composition index as the outcome. In the second analysis, SNPs affecting each body composition index were used as IVs, treating body composition as the exposure and MASLD as the outcome.

In this study, when both the exposure and the outcome were binary, odds ratios together with p-values were reported. In analyses combining binary exposures with continuous outcomes, we presented estimates with p-values but placed greater emphasis on the p-values for interpretation. When more than two IVs were used, the heterogeneity test statistic, Cochran’s Q and I2, were provided. Confounders, including sex, age, current smoking status, alcohol consumption, and diabetes status (fasting blood glucose level ≥126 mg/dL, a 2-hour post-load glucose level ≥200 mg/dL, or a self-reported history of diabetes medication use) were adjusted for in the analysis (GWAS and MR). GWAS were conducted using PLINK 2.0.25 Other statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.3.2 (https://r-project.org), and the “MendelianRandomization” package was used for MR.26

RESULTS

1. General characteristics

Among the total of 8,840 individuals, GWAS analysis for body composition was conducted on 6,709 individuals who met inclusion criteria. A total of 258 patients, categorized as MASLD, and 3,498 non-MASLD were included in the analysis. A total of 2,953 individuals who met only one of the two criteria (HSI ≥36 or FIB-4 ≥1.30) were excluded from the analysis. Given the importance of case definition, the distribution of HSI and FIB-4 values is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1 using both a dot plot and a heatmap.

The general characteristics of the two groups and the results for variables related to the FIB-4 and HSI indices used for MASLD classification are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the MASLD group was 58.8±7.6 years, which was significantly higher than the mean age of the non-MASLD group (49.9±8.2 years) (p<0.001). Regarding sex distribution, 29.9% of the MASLD group were male, and 70.2% were female, compared with 37.7% of male and 62.3% of female in the non-MASLD group, indicating a significant difference (p=0.01). The aspartate aminotransferase levels were significantly higher in the MASLD group (42.2±50.6 IU/L) than in the non-MASLD group (21.1±5.8 IU/L, p<0.001), as were alanine aminotransferase levels (51.0±102.1 IU/L vs 18.0±7.9 IU/L, p<0.001). Significant differences in alcohol consumption and smoking rates were observed between the two groups (p<0.001 and p<0.01, respectively). The prevalence of diabetes was significantly higher in the MASLD group than in the non-MASLD group (41.5% vs 5.5%, p<0.001).

For men, the tertile cutoff values for muscle mass are 0.720 and 0.763, while for women, they are 0.622 and 0.661. For fat mass, the tertile cutoff values for men are 0.192 and 0.237, and for women, they are 0.299 and 0.339. The tertile cutoff values for waist-hip ratio are 0.88 and 0.91 for men, and 0.88 and 0.92 for women. The MASLD group had lower muscle mass than the non-MASLD group (0.62±0.06 vs 0.67±0.07, p<0.001). Fat mass was higher in the MASLD group (0.34±0.07) than in the non-MASLD group (0.29±0.08, p<0.001). Significant differences were also observed between the two groups in terms of tertile-based categorized values of muscle mass (p<0.001), fat mass (p<0.001), and waist-hip ratio (p<0.001).

2. Genome-wide association study

The GWAS results are summarized in Table 2. For MASLD, six SNPs were identified as associated variants, and after LD clumping, a single lead SNP was selected (chromosome 22, position 42663903). For muscle mass (categorized), eight associated SNPs were identified, three of which were designated as lead SNPs. For fat mass (categorized), three associated SNPs were identified, with one confirmed as the lead SNP after LD clumping. A total of seven SNPs were associated with waist-hip ratio (continuous), four of which remained after LD clumping. Notably, one SNP (chromosome 1, position 105411552) was directly associated with MASLD at p<0.05, rendering it unsuitable as an IV. For waist-hip ratio (categorical), six associated SNPs were identified, and three lead SNPs were confirmed after LD clumping. No associated SNPs were identified for fat mass (continuous) or muscle mass (continuous). The Manhattan plots of the GWAS results for each variable are presented in Fig. 1.

3. Mendelian randomization

After GWAS and LD clumping, one SNP was selected as an IV for MASLD, with an F-statistic value of 30.9. No significant associations were identified between MASLD and muscle mass, fat mass, or waist-hip ratio. The p-values for continuous and tertile muscle mass were 0.19 and 0.66, respectively. Meanwhile, the p-values for the continuous fat mass and tertile-based categorized fat mass were 0.18 and 0.98, respectively. For the continuous waist-hip ratio, the p-value was 0.33, and for the tertile-based categorized waist-hip ratio, it was 0.66 (Table 3).

MR analysis results with body composition as the exposure are presented in Table 4. For the muscle mass treated as a continuous variable, no suitable SNPs were identified as IVs in the GWAS analysis. However, for the muscle mass categorized into tertiles, three SNPs were identified as suitable IVs after LD clumping, with an F-statistic value of 11.4. Individuals in the lowest tertile of muscle mass had a significantly higher risk of MASLD, with an OR of 1.57 (95% confidence interval, 1.09 to 2.27; p=0.02). The heterogeneity test statistic for these three SNPs (Cochran’s Q) was 0.80, with two degrees of freedom (p=0.67) and I2=0.0%, indicating low heterogeneity and consistent effects among the IVs.

For fat mass treated as a continuous variable, no suitable SNPs were identified as IVs in the GWAS analysis. For the fat mass categorized into tertiles, one SNP was considered an IV. The IVW analysis did not show a significant association with NAFLD (p=0.74), and the F-statistic value was 2.8, indicating the potential for weak instrument bias.

When the waist-hip ratio was considered a continuous variable, four SNPs were identified as lead SNPs, and three were identified as suitable IVs. The IVW analysis did not reveal a significant association with MASLD (p=0.061). The F-statistic value was 14.3. The heterogeneity test statistic (Cochran’s Q) was 4.24 with 2 degrees of freedom (p=0.12) and I2=52.9%. Although heterogeneity was not significant in the Cochran’s Q test, the I2 value indicated moderate heterogeneity. For the categorized variable of waist-hip ratio, three SNPs were lead SNPs and suitable IVs. The IVW analysis did not reveal a significant association with MASLD (p=0.54). The F-statistic value was 11.7, and the heterogeneity test statistic (Cochran’s Q) was 4.9, with two degrees of freedom (p=0.09) and I2=59.3%. Although heterogeneity was not significant in the Cochran’s Q test, the I2 value indicated moderate heterogeneity.

DISCUSSION

This study explored the directional causal relationship between body composition and MASLD showing that muscle mass has a causal effect on the prevalence of MASLD. However, MASLD prevalence did not significantly affect muscle mass. Fat mass and waist-hip ratio did not show significant causal relationships with MASLD.

Although the primary focus of this study was to assess causal effects through MR, the accompanying GWAS analyses also provided insight into genetic variants associated with the traits of interest. In particular, the SNP located at position 42663903 on chromosome 22 (rs2281135) was identified as associated with MASLD with fibrosis, as defined in our study, and was employed as an IV. This locus is situated near PNPLA3 and has previously been reported to be linked to histological NAFLD and elevated serum alanine aminotransferase levels, thereby lending biological plausibility to its role in our MASLD definition.27 Importantly, this variant has been shown to be relatively specific to NAFLD, with limited associations with other metabolic traits, which further strengthens the interpretation that our laboratory-based case definition reliably captured individuals with true MASLD rather than reflecting nonspecific metabolic abnormalities. Beyond MASLD, several variants were identified for body composition traits. Interestingly, for some traits, significant associations were observed only when exposures were modelled as tertile-based categories rather than as continuous variables. This phenomenon may suggest underlying nonlinear genetic influences, whereby loci exert stronger effects at the extremes of the phenotype distribution. Such nonlinear dependence has recently been highlighted in GWAS of complex traits,28 supporting the plausibility of the patterns observed in our analyses.

Previous studies have consistently suggested three possible biological pathways linking muscle mass and MASLD (or NAFLD): shared mechanisms such as insulin resistance and chronic inflammation, adverse effects of MASLD on muscle wasting, and the direct influence of reduced muscle mass on hepatic steatosis.29-31 Among these, our study demonstrated a significant causal relationship in the direction from muscle mass to MASLD. This pathway is biologically plausible, as loss of muscle mass impairs glucose uptake and exacerbates insulin resistance, thereby promoting hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipid deposition.32 In addition, reduced secretion of protective myokines such as irisin33 and interleukin-6,34 together with increased myostatin activity, may further aggravate hepatic steatosis and inflammation.31 Crosstalk between hepatokines and myokines, including fibroblast growth factor-21, provides additional support for this mechanism,35 whereby muscle preservation may play a protective role against MASLD.

Numerous epidemiological studies have reported an association between sarcopenia and NAFLD, with a systematic review and meta-analysis showing that sarcopenia significantly increases the risk of NAFLD and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis; however, the review noted that many of the studies conducted to date were cross-sectional, limiting their ability to identify causality.8 Previous studies investigating the relationship between NAFLD and body composition using methods similar to this study, such as MR based on genetic variation, have mostly been conducted using two-sample MR with summary statistics rather than raw data analysis. Chen et al.11 conducted a bidirectional MR and found that appendicular muscle mass was only significant in one cohort (FinnGen) among three GWAS cohorts (FinnGen, Namjou B, and Anstee Q), with the meta-analysis showing no significant association. However, this study had limitations due to its reliance on GWAS results rather than individual-level data, lacking nonlinear causal effect analyses. Our study sought to overcome these limitations by analyzing body composition as both continuous and standardized variables in various forms. Zhao et al.10 also conducted a two-sample MR study on whole-body lean mass, appendicular lean mass, and NAFLD based on existing GWAS data. However, this study also did not secure individual-level data and instead synthesized summary data. Despite using SNPs as IVs, it could not conclusively determine the causal relationship. Given the lack of clear causal relationships identified in previous MR analyses, some hypothesized that the relationship between NAFLD and body composition might share common conditions like insulin resistance, and that addressing these common conditions could be the clinical intervention to solve both issues. However, unlike previous MR studies, this study considered confounders at the individual level and performed GWAS and MR analyses on body composition indices, checking both raw and categorized data for each index to account for linear and limited nonlinear associations. Therefore, this study’s finding that muscle mass causally influences MASLD occurrence is a significant result.

Furthermore, as suggested by Chen et al.,11 most studies on the association between sarcopenia and NAFLD, as well as interventions, have been conducted on Asian populations, reporting their effects. However, previous MR studies used GWAS data from European ancestry, which is a limitation. This study identified the causal relationship between muscle mass and MASLD using individual-level data from Asian, particularly Korean, populations, consistent with previous epidemiological studies. These results could be useful for the management of MASLD in Asian populations, particularly in Koreans. The finding that muscle mass has a causal relationship with MASLD suggests that exercises targeting this aspect could be beneficial, providing an epidemiological basis for such interventions.

The increase in body fat, especially abdominal visceral fat, has also been reported to have pathophysiological links with MASLD. Adipocytes, as endocrine organs, secrete various bioactive peptides that affect various organs at both local and systemic levels.36 A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that obesity is significantly associated with MASLD.9,37 However, this study did not find statistically significant bidirectional causal relationships between fat mass or waist-hip ratio and MASLD.

In our study, MASLD was defined using laboratory-based criteria (HSI and FIB-4), without confirmation from imaging or biopsy, which would have provided more direct diagnostic evidence. Although the definition demonstrated concordance with a well-established MASLD-specific SNP identified in previous studies,27 the absence of direct imaging or histologic confirmation remains a limitation. Also, the laboratory result-based case definition of MASLD in this population-based cohort inevitably involved a trade-off between specificity and sample size. Applying stricter laboratory thresholds to maximize diagnostic specificity would have resulted in an insufficient number of cases, precluding meaningful analyses, particularly given that this cohort was derived from a general population sample. As such, we adopted a dual-criterion approach, requiring both HSI ≥36 and FIB-4 ≥1.30, while excluding individuals who fulfilled only one criterion. This strategy was intended to enhance the comparability between cases and non-cases and to reduce potential misclassification. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that some degree of misclassification may persist, which could bias effect estimates towards the null. This implies that the significant association observed between genetically predicted low muscle mass and MASLD is likely robust, whereas non-significant findings—such as the fat mass–MASLD association—may partly reflect insufficient power to detect very small effect sizes.

With respect to the GWAS analyses, the sample size was relatively modest, which limited the ability to apply the conventional genome-wide significance threshold of p<5×10-8. While this could be viewed as a shortcoming, the threshold of <1×10-5 used here has been reported to carry a low risk of weak instrument bias in MR38 and is widely applied in GWAS as a “suggestive association” level.39,40 Importantly, the central focus of our study was the MR analyses rather than locus discovery, and the instruments selected demonstrated adequate strength for the key analysis of muscle mass and MASLD, with no evidence of heterogeneity. In addition, the lead SNP associated with MASLD itself surpassed the conventional genome-wide threshold (p<5×10-8) and showed strong instrument strength, further supporting the robustness of the findings. By contrast, the null results for fat mass and waist-to-hip ratio should be interpreted with caution, as these analyses relied on weaker instruments and involved fewer variants. In the case of fat mass, the instruments had particularly low F-statistics, raising concerns about insufficient strength and the possibility of weak instrument bias attenuating the results. For waist-hip ratio, only two SNPs were available, which substantially reduced statistical power. Although the I2 statistic suggested moderate heterogeneity, Cochran’s Q test was non-significant, most likely due to the small number of variants, and MR-Egger regression provided no evidence of directional pleiotropy. While these factors could have introduced bias and attenuated potential associations toward non-significance, we sought to mitigate such risks by leveraging the strengths of the one-sample MR design, particularly by adjusting for key confounders using individual-level data. This approach helped to minimize potential distortions, although further large-scale studies are warranted to validate these secondary findings.

Although the use of a single-ethnic Korean cohort provides valuable evidence in an Asian context, this restriction may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. The use of soft lean mass as a proxy for skeletal muscle may be subject to debate. Nevertheless, previous validation studies have demonstrated that lean mass reliably reflects skeletal muscle,41 with strong correlations between dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and bioelectrical impedance analysis-derived measurements,42 supporting the appropriateness of this substitution in epidemiological research.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that muscle mass has a causal effect on the prevalence of MASLD. This finding suggests that increasing muscle mass and preventing sarcopenia could be important strategies in the management of MASLD. The study attempted to clarify the direction of causality using large-scale genetic epidemiological data, employing GWAS and bidirectional MR, and provided results considering regional characteristics by analyzing data from an Asian, particularly Korean, population. The findings from this study are expected to contribute to more clearly guiding interventions for managing MASLD.
