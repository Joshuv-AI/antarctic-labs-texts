A 5-µm-thick electrolyte with a high-entropy interphase for practical solid-state lithium metal batteries
Authors: Xuexia Lan, Yi Zeng, Ye Chen, Zhengjie Chen, Zhen Li, Xinyuan Hu, Ziyong Li, Qiutan Liu, Renzong Hu, Jing Peng, Hui-Ming Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSolid-state lithium metal batteries with ultrathin and lightweight electrolytes are promising for high-energy-density storage but are prone to dendrite penetration under practical current densities. This limitation arises primarily from the uncontrolled and chemically heterogeneous solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI), inducing obvious interfacial instability and leading to uneven and sluggish Li+ flux at the interface. Here, we design a homogeneous interphase with high configurational entropy, with its optimized composition screened by machine learning, providing dual functions by stabilizing the SEI structure and promoting efficient Li+ transport. The high-entropy nature of this interphase is experimentally supported and promotes uniform and fast interfacial Li+ flux, enabling a 5-µm-thick electrolyte to sustain a critical current density of 5.2 mA cm−2 and maintain stable long-term lithium plating/stripping at 3 mA cm−2 for 850 h. Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 pouch cells with this ultrathin and lightweight electrolyte deliver an areal capacity of 5.4 mAh cm−2 and an energy density of 573 Wh kg−1, showing great potential for practical solid-state lithium metal batteries.Keywords: ultrathin electrolyte, solid–electrolyte interphase, high entropy, current density, energy density

INTRODUCTION

Solid-state lithium metal batteries (SSLMBs), using solid Li+ conductors instead of flammable liquid electrolytes, are considered as a promising route for addressing the energy density and safety limitations of conventional liquid systems [1–4]. Reducing the thickness of solid electrolytes is an effective strategy to decrease the fraction of inactive components and thereby increase cell-level energy density, particularly for low-density polymer electrolytes [5,6]. For example, by thinning the polymer electrolytes to 20 µm, an energy density exceeding 400 Wh kg−1 is achievable [7,8]. As ionic conductance scales inversely with electrolyte thickness [9], ultrathin electrolytes mitigate the limitations of insufficient ionic conductivity in some electrolyte systems [8,10], by shortening the Li+ transport distance and reducing internal resistance. However, SSLMBs with ultrathin electrolytes remain unable to operate reliably under practical conditions, such as a current density of 3 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 3 mAh cm−2 [11,12]. The primary limitation arises from internal short circuiting caused by lithium dendrite penetration through ultrathin electrolytes.

A dominant mechanistic origin of dendrite formation is a spatially uneven Li+ flux at the Liǀelectrolyte interface, which drives localized lithium deposition and subsequent dendritic growth. This uneven flux originates from the formation of an unstable solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer with chemically heterogeneous components, such as LiF, Li2S, Li2O, Li3N and Li3P [8,13]. These components have large disparities in ionic conductivity and structural stability, causing pronounced interface-potential fluctuations and inducing localized Li+ flux concentration [14,15]. Such irregular Li+ distribution promotes dendrite growth and penetration through ultrathin electrolytes, even at a low current

density (≤1 mA cm−2) [16–18]. To address these challenges, extensive efforts have focused on electrolyte engineering [4], interfacial regulation [12], artificial SEI construction [19] and ion-transport modulation [20] for stabilizing lithium metal anodes. Representative strategies include increasing the content of inorganic fillers [9], modifying the interfacial layer with high-entropy alloys and Li-compensation components [21,22], promoting interfacial fluorination [23,24], constructing dual protective layers [23,25,26] and introducing electrochemically robust coating layers to improve SEI stability [27–29]. However, these approaches often increase mechanical brittleness, thereby raising the risk of membrane fracture, or introduce high interfacial Li+ nucleation barriers that partially shield Li+ flux [17,27]. To enable ultrathin electrolytes to maintain mechanical integrity without compromising interfacial electrochemical performance, an effective interface design strategy is needed to integrate SEI uniformity with rapid Li+ transport.

Here, we designed an interfacial region for ultrathin solid electrolytes to enable uniform and fast Li+ nucleation and deposition, through an SEI enriched with a homogeneous high-entropy interphase (HEI). This interphase provides dual functionality by stabilizing the SEI structure and facilitating Li+ transport. Its composition, screened by machine learning, combines entropy-driven stability with a low Li+ diffusion barrier. Experimentally validated by using poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) with pronounced interfacial instability as a model system, a 5-µm-thick PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte sustains a critical current density of 5.2 mA cm−2 and stable lithium plating/stripping at 3 mA cm−2 for 850 h. Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cells achieve stable cycling over 1000 cycles at 3 mA cm−2, with an average Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 99.95%. Pouch cells with this ultrathin and lightweight electrolyte deliver an areal capacity of 5.4 mAh cm−2 and an energy density of 573 Wh kg−1. This HEI strategy provides a new avenue for developing high-performance ultrathin electrolytes and practical SSLMBs.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Optimized composition screened by machine learning

In a pure PVDF-HFP electrolyte, strong interactions between lithium salts and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent form a [Li(DMF)x]+ solvation structure (Fig. 1a). It is prone to electron transfer with reactive lithium metal, resulting in unstable SEI with multicomponents [14,15]. This interface degradation becomes exacerbated at high current densities, producing uncontrolled and thick SEI [2]. The resulting heterogeneous SEI impedes efficient Li+ transport, causes uneven Li+ flux and accelerates lithium dendrites growth [30,31]. Constructing an SEI enriched with a homogeneous interphase that combines high stability with fast ionic transport is expected to significantly improve interfacial electrochemical performance at high current densities. Advances in inorganic ionic conductors show that high-entropy strategies can integrate entropy-driven stability with improved ionic migration efficiency [32,33]. For example, high-entropy superionic conductors, including Li9.54[Si0.6Ge0.4]1.74P1.44S11.1Br0.3O0.6 [32] and Li5.5PS4.5Cl0.8Br0.7 [34], achieve ionic conductivities of 32 and 22.2 mS cm−1 at 25°C, revealing the role of anion-sublattice disorder in promoting Li+ transport [35,36].

To construct an HEI composed of multi-anions, it is necessary to identify the anion types. Using a typical SEI component LiF as a parent model, due to its chemical stability and mechanical robustness [28], density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to compare the formation energies of LiF structures doped with various anions (Fig. S1). The examined anions included B, C, N, O, P, S, Cl and Br. The results show that doping LiF with N, O, P or S has significantly lower formation energies than others, indicating that they are more likely to form a single, highly miscible phase structure. This behavior likely arises from their comparable ionic radii and similar electronegativities [37], which facilitate synergistic interactions that promote the formation of an HEI. As illustrated in Fig. 1b, the Liǀelectrolyte interface region enriched with these anions (F, N, O, P and S) facilitates the generation of an SEI containing an HEI.

Even after identifying the anion species, determining the optimal compositional ratios within the HEI remains challenging due to its complex tunability. Machine learning provides a powerful alternative, allowing the prediction of formation energies and accelerating composition screening. It has accelerated the discovery of electrode and electrolyte materials [38–43], offering valuable guidance for screening HEIs. In this work, a dataset was constructed from DFT calculations of 200 high-entropy electrolytes with varying anion ratios. The normalized fractions of F, N, O, P and S were used as input features, and the formation energy was used as the prediction target for thermodynamic stability. Considering the high computational cost of first-principles calculations for high-entropy systems, the dataset is relatively small compared with large-scale materials databases. Therefore, the models were developed as data-efficient screening tools based on a curated and internally consistent DFT dataset, rather than as universal predictors for all interphase chemistries. This strategy is consistent with recent studies showing that reliable materials screening under data-scarce conditions can be achieved using high-quality datasets, physically meaningful descriptors and rigorous validation protocols [40–42,44].

As shown in Fig. 1c, six predictive models, including a mean predictor, linear regression, ridge regression, support vector regression, random forest and XGBoost, were benchmarked using the coefficient of determination (R2) and mean absolute error (MAE) as the main evaluation metrics (Fig. S2), supported by a comparison of root mean squared error (RMSE) values and five-fold cross-validation MAE values (Table S1). Among these models, XGBoost showed the best overall performance on both the validation and independent test datasets. In particular, the independent test set showed an R2 value of 0.911 and an MAE of 0.806 eV per formula unit, corresponding to 0.014 eV per atom. This test-set error is below the typical uncertainty of DFT calculations (∼0.05 eV per atom) [40,42], indicating that the model predictions are within the intrinsic accuracy limit of first-principles calculations and suggesting robust generalization with minimal overfitting. Additional deep-learning baselines, including a simple fully connected multilayer perceptron (Simple MLP), an ElemNet-like model and an ElemNet-based transfer-learning model pretrained on the Matbench Materials Project formation-energy dataset, were further evaluated as supplementary benchmarks (Fig. S3 and Table S2). Comparison of the R2, MAE and RMSE values further supported the selection of XGBoost for compositional screening. The superior performance of XGBoost can be attributed to its suitability for the present small-scale, low-dimensional, composition-only screening task. Its tree-based ensemble structure can efficiently capture non-linear and coupled anion effects in tabular data while reducing the risk of overfitting, while deep-learning methods remain valuable alternatives for larger and more chemically diverse datasets [44,45].

The XGBoost model predicted Li40F4N2O4P6S2 as the optimal interphase composition (Fig. 1d), with a formation energy of −8.1 eV, closely matching the DFT-calculated value of −7.8 eV. The configurational entropy of the anion sublattice was calculated using ΔSconfig = −R , where xi represents the molar fraction of each anion. The calculated ΔSconf​ of Li40F4N2O4P6S2 is 1.523 R, exceeding the commonly used high-entropy threshold of 1.5 R. The XGBoost model was further optimized through hyperparameter tuning with early stopping to ensure robust convergence and avoid overfitting (Fig. S4). The narrow, zero-centered residual distributions across all datasets (Fig. S5) confirm the reliability of the XGBoost model and the absence of systematic bias. The optimized XGBoost model was then applied to an expanded virtual composition library of 500 candidate Li-F-N-O-P-S interphase compositions (Fig. 1e). These candidates were ranked by predicted formation energies and compared with the original DFT dataset. None showed a lower predicted formation energy than Li40F4N2O4P6S2, supporting its selection as the most stable composition identified in this screening workflow. This virtual screening step enables efficient exploration of a larger compositional space that would otherwise require extensive first-principles calculations, highlighting the value of data-efficient materials design under limited-data conditions. To improve model interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed for the XGBoost model (Fig. S6). The SHAP summary plot shows the contribution direction and magnitude of each element to the predicted formation energy, while SHAP interaction analysis identifies pairwise compositional interactions among different anions. These results indicate that the XGBoost predictions are influenced by both individual anion fractions and pairwise compositional interactions. Feature importance analysis (Fig. 1f) reflects the contributions of F, N, O, P and S to interphase stability, highlighting their distinct atomic effects [27]. Overall, this compositional screening workflow demonstrates that a limited but carefully curated DFT dataset can support reliable prediction and guide the discovery of stable HEIs, provided that the model scope is clearly defined and the predictions are rigorously validated [40–42,44].

Characteristics of the high-entropy interphase

To evaluate the effect of the high-entropy Li40F4N2O4P6S2 phase on the Li+ transport kinetics within the SEI, we performed calculations to compare the energy barriers of Li+ diffusion across the Li40F4N2O4P6S2 and several interphases containing single anions, including LiF, Li3N, Li2O, Li3P and Li2S (Fig. 2a). Calculations were conducted on the most stable interface of each crystal, where the adsorption of Li adatoms on the surface was analyzed to define the initial and final states of the diffusion path (Fig. S7). The Li+ migration barrier in the high-entropy Li40F4N2O4P6S2 is 0.11 eV, comparable to the 0.12 eV of Li3N and 0.16 eV of Li3P, which are fast ionic conductors with ionic conductivities of 10−4–10−3 S cm−1 at 25°C [46,47]. This value is markedly lower than that of the LiF phase with 0.43 eV, which is almost inert to Li+ transport [27,28]. To assess interfacial stability, DFT calculations were performed for lithium metal in contact with Li40F4N2O4P6S2 and the other interphases, and the interface energies are shown in Fig. 2b. The high-entropy Li40F4N2O4P6S2 interphase shows the lowest interface energy (−0.79 J m−2) with lithium metal, comparable to that of LiF (−0.75 J m−2) with high lithiophobicity [48], and significantly lower than those of Li3N (−0.49 J m−2) and Li3P (−0.52 J m−2) with lithiophilicity [46,49]. Further comparative calculations on interphases with progressively increasing configurational entropy (Fig. S8), including LiF, Li40F34N2, Li40F26N2O4, Li40F8N2O4P6 and Li40F4N2O4P6S2, show that higher configurational entropy is associated with a lower Li+ migration barrier and a more favorable interface energy between the interphase and lithium metal. These calculations indicate that the high-entropy Li40F4N2O4P6S2 phase acts as a fast-conducting component in the SEI, facilitating rapid Li+ transport, while its highly stable interface with lithium metal promotes uniform and reversible lithium plating/stripping.

The anion ratios of the machine-learning-screened Li40F4N2O4P6S2 composition were used to quantitatively introduce F−, N3−, O2−, P3− and S2− multi-anion sources during electrolyte fabrication. PVDF-HFP/HEI, prepared via a multistep casting process (Fig. S9), forms a uniform, semi-transparent membrane with a thickness of 5 μm (Fig. 2c). Despite this ultrathin structure, it has superior mechanical properties, achieving a tensile strength of 8.9 MPa (Fig. S10), surpassing PVDF-HFP (3.1 MPa) and other reported thicker polymer electrolytes (Fig. S11). Magnified cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and elemental maps (Fig. 2d and Fig. S12) show that the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte consists of a PVDF-HFP substrate sandwiched between upper and lower multi-anion layers enriched with F, N, O, P and S elements. These multi-anion layers are designed to undergo interfacial reconstruction upon contact with lithium metal, thereby forming a chemically stable HEI in the SEI and enabling fast and uniform Li+ nucleation.

Surface SEM images show that the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte has a dense and uniform morphology (Fig. S13), while the PVDF-HFP electrolyte is porous, indicating that multi-anion interactions enhance polymer chain adhesion and produce a compact structure that improves solvent uniformity and interfacial stability. Time of flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) images (Fig. S14) further reveal homogeneous [Li(DMF)x]+ distribution in the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte, while [Li(DMF)x]⁺ is aggregated in PVDF-HFP electrolyte. Thermogravimetric analysis shows [Li(DMF)x]⁺ contents of 5.7 wt% and 17.2 wt% in PVDF-HFP/HEI and PVDF-HFP, with the reduced [Li(DMF)x]+ residue helping to mitigate interfacial side reactions and enhance interface stability. The lower residual [Li(DMF)x]+ content in PVDF-HFP/HEI is mainly attributed to the improved membrane uniformity and denser morphology induced by the multi-anion sources, which suppress local accumulation of [Li(DMF)x]+ solvates. In addition, abundant anionic coordination sites weaken [Li(DMF)x]+ coordination and facilitate the removal of [Li(DMF)x]+-related species during vacuum drying.

Even with a lower residual content, the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte has improved ionic conductivity across a wide temperature range from −40 to 60°C compared with the PVDF-HFP electrolyte (Fig. 2e and Fig. S15). Specifically, its ionic conductivity at 25°C reaches 5.4 × 10−4 S cm−1, markedly higher than the 0.98 × 10−4 S cm−1 of the pure PVDF-HFP electrolyte and those of PVDF-HFP-based electrolytes containing a single component, including LiF, Li2O, Li2S, Li3P and Li3N (Fig. S16). It also outperforms electrolytes with anion ratios of low-entropy and medium-entropy interphases, including Li40F34N2, Li40F26N2O4 and Li40F8N2O4P6. This improvement originates from the incorporation of HEI, which helps facilitate Li+ transport. The activation energy for Li+ migration decreases from 0.34 eV in PVDF-HFP electrolyte to 0.20 eV in PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte (Fig. S17), while the Li+ transference number (tLi⁺) increases from 0.31 to 0.72 (Fig. 2f and Fig. S18), indicating improved Li+ transport efficiency. To evaluate the interfacial Li+ charge-transfer kinetics between the electrolyte and lithium metal, Tafel analysis was performed (Fig. S19). The PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte shows a higher exchange current density of 0.18 mA cm−2 than the pure PVDF-HFP electrolyte (0.11 mA cm−2), indicating faster interfacial Li+ transport. Moreover, the electrochemical stability window extends to 4.8 V (Fig. 2g), compared with 4.1 V for PVDF-HFP electrolyte, implying improved compatibility with high-voltage positive electrodes. Voltage-dependent electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements show that the PVDF-HFP electrolyte has a pronounced increase in interfacial impedance after high-voltage polarization (Fig. S20), especially at 4.8 V, whereas the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte shows a much smaller impedance increase. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the high-voltage-side surface after polarization to 4.8 V further reveals weaker oxidation/decomposition-related signals for the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte than for the PVDF-HFP electrolyte (Fig. S21). These results indicate that the HEI suppresses high-voltage interfacial degradation, thereby contributing to the expanded electrochemical stability window from 4.1 V for PVDF-HFP to 4.8 V for PVDF-HFP/HEI.

Characterization of the high-entropy interphase in the SEI

The multi-anion layers modulate electrochemical reactions at the Liǀelectrolyte interface, promoting in situ reconstruction into an HEI and thereby altering the SEI characteristics. To investigate the morphology and chemical composition of the SEI formed at the Li|PVDF-HFP/HEI and Li|PVDF-HFP interfaces, cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) experiments were conducted. As shown in Fig. 3a, in Li|electrolyte|Cu cells, after lithium plating at 0.5 mA cm−2 for 10 h, lithium metal with SEI was deposited on a TEM Cu grid. During this process, the cell with PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte maintains a low resistance with minimal variation, indicating the formation of a stable SEI with good ionic conduction. In contrast, the cell with PVDF-HFP electrolyte shows a significant increase in resistance, indicating excessive interphase formation with poor ionic conduction. The cryo-TEM image of the SEI in a Li|PVDF-HFP|Cu cell (Fig. 3b) reveals unevenly distributed nanoparticles embedded in an organic matrix formed in situ from electrolyte decomposition, including polymer fragments and lithium salt/solvent. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and fast Fourier transform (FFT) images (Fig. 3c) identify that these nanoparticles are LiF, Li3N, Li2O, Li3P and Li2S, with non-uniform sizes of 3–15 nm. This SEI with heterogeneous multicomponents, due to the differences in stability and ionic conductivity among its phases (Fig. 3d), tends to induce uneven and sluggish Li+ flux at the Li|PVDF-HFP interface, promoting dendrite growth and hindering fast Li+ transport under high current densities.

When using the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte, a dense SEI enriched with inorganic nanoparticles is formed, with the nanoparticles embedded in the organic matrix (Fig. 3e). These nanoparticles have remarkable uniformity, with an average size of 4–6 nm (Fig. S22). The HRTEM and FFT images (Fig. 3f) show that these nanoparticles possess a consistent lattice spacing of 0.28 nm, suggesting a homogeneous inorganic phase. The elemental maps (Fig. 3g) and electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) analysis (Fig. 3h) of an individual inorganic nanoparticle further reveal its multi-anion composition. The elemental maps reveal the coexistence of P, S, N, O and F elements. Corresponding EELS spectra display distinct peaks from the Li K-edge, P L-edge, S L-edge, N K-edge, O K-edge and F K-edge, supporting the incorporation of lithium ions and multiple anions within the same nanoparticle (Fig. 3i). This uniform composition is enabled by the homogeneous multi-anion layers of the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte, with an anion ratio designed according to the targeted Li-F-N-O-P-S composition of Li40F4N2O4P6S2. Upon contact with lithium metal and during lithium deposition, these surface-enriched anions participate in interfacial reconstruction, while entropy-stabilized mixing of F, N, O, P and S may help suppress phase separation and promote the formation of HEI-enriched nanoparticles within the SEI. Thermodynamically, multi-anion incorporation increases the configurational entropy of the interphase, thereby lowering the Gibbs free energy of mixing and stabilizing the high-entropy state. Consistently, the optimized Li40F4N2O4P6S2 composition exhibits a configurational entropy of 1.523 R, a low formation energy and a low interfacial energy with lithium metal, supporting the favorable thermodynamic and interfacial stability of the designed Li-F-N-O-P-S interphase. Moreover, after 100 cycles of lithium plating/stripping, the HEI nanoparticles remain uniformly distributed with similar sizes of 5–7 nm (Fig. S23), retain the characteristic lattice spacing of 0.28 nm, and still contain F, N, O, P and S within individual nanoparticles, indicating the structural and compositional stability of the HEI-enriched SEI during interfacial volume changes. These cryo-TEM results show that the synergistic incorporation of diverse anions induces the formation of a dense and uniform SEI enriched with a homogeneous multi-anion interphase with high-entropy characteristics, which imparts both fast interfacial ionic conduction and strong interfacial structural stability.

Interface stability and electrochemical performance of SSLMBs

Using the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte, symmetric LiǀǀLi cells sustain a high critical current density of 5.2 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4a), while the PVDF-HFP electrolyte fails as the current density is increased to 1.0 mA cm−2. As shown in Fig. 4b, a comparison of critical current densities with other thin electrolytes (see Table S3 for details) reveals that, despite being only 5 µm thick, the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte delivers a higher critical current density than thicker counterparts. This is primarily attributed to the HEI, which stabilizes the Liǀelectrolyte interface and enables fast and uniform Li+ transport, while the uniformity and densification of the electrolyte further suppress lithium dendrite formation. In this process, structural stability and fast Li+ transport are coupled. The structurally stable HEI suppresses phase separation, thereby maintaining continuous Li+ transport pathways, while fast and homogeneous Li+ transport reduces concentration polarization and local current concentration, helping to preserve interfacial structural integrity during repeated lithium plating/stripping. These features support stable cycling performance at high current densities. With the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte, symmetric LiǀǀLi cells achieve highly stable lithium stripping/plating over 6000 h at 0.5 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4c), maintaining a low and steady polarization below 100 mV. In contrast, the PVDF-HFP electrolyte, which has a lower ionic conductivity and limited interfacial stability, has continuously rising polarization and fails after 480 h. Moreover, PVDF-HFP electrolytes containing a single component, such as LiF, Li2O, Li2S, Li3P or Li3N, show unstable cycling behavior or large overpotentials in LiǀǀLi cells (Fig. S24), indicating that none of these components alone can simultaneously provide sufficient interfacial stability and fast Li+ transport. Similar instability or high polarization is also observed for PVDF-HFP electrolytes with anion ratio of low-entropy or medium-entropy interphases (Fig. S25). These comparisons highlight the importance of the HEI in stabilizing Li stripping/plating. To distinguish the high-entropy effect from simple multi-anion doping, we compared low-, medium- and high-entropy anion ratios containing the same F, N, O, P and S species. The high-entropy Li40F4N2O4P6S2 composition shows a lower Li+ migration barrier, more favorable lithium metal interface energy, more homogeneous SEI structure and more stable Li||Li cycling than the low- and medium-entropy counterparts (Fig. S26). These results indicate that the enhanced interfacial stability and Li+ transport originate from high configurational entropy rather than simple multi-anion doping. Accordingly, when the current density is increased to 1.0 mA cm−2 (Fig. S27) and 3.0 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4d), the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte enables the LiǀǀLi cells to achieve stable cycling for 2000 and 850 h.

To monitor and visualize in real time the interfacial evolution and stability of the Liǀelectrolyte interface under a practical current density, the lithium stripping process (Fig. S28) on the lithium metal side was observed by using in situ optical microscopy during the first cycle at 3 mA cm−2. From the recorded video (Video S1) and corresponding in situ images (Fig. 4e), the initially deposited lithium layer at the Li|PVDF-HFP/HEI interface has a smooth and uniform morphology. As the stripping process proceeds, this layer gradually becomes thinner, and no lithium dendrites are observed throughout the process. In contrast, the Li|PVDF-HFP interface initially fails to form a uniformly deposited lithium layer (Fig. 4f and Video S2), leading to pronounced lithium dendrites. The stripping process at 3 mA cm−2 over 1 h is also highly uneven, resulting in a rough and porous Li|PVDF-HFP interface after stripping. These in situ observations show that the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte enables uniform and rapid Li+ flux even at high current densities, effectively suppressing the formation and growth of lithium dendrites. During the in situ experiments, the recorded Nyquist plots show that the LiǀǀLi cells with the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte maintain a low and stable interfacial resistance (Fig. S29), whereas cells with PVDF-HFP electrolyte have higher resistance that increases markedly over time.

The PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte, with high interfacial stability and rapid Li+ transport capability, enables Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (Fig. 5) and Li||LiFePO4 (Figs S30–S33) solid-state batteries to achieve superior rate performance and long-term cycling stability at high current densities. Within the current density range of 0.05–5 mA cm−2, which corresponds to 0.1–10 C (Fig. 5a and b), Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cells with the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte have higher capacities and minor voltage polarization than those using the PVDF-HFP electrolyte (Fig. S34). Specifically, the Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cells with the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte show capacities of 202, 197, 172, 158, 144, 124, 108 and 84 mAh g−1 at 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5 mA cm−2, respectively. When the current density is returned to 0.05 mA cm−2, the cells retain a high capacity of 200 mAh g−1.

(e) Initial charge/discharge profile of a pouch cell with the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte. (f) Comparison of the energy densities of solid-state batteries with the PVDF-HFP/HEI and other reported thin electrolytes [8,17,53,55–59]. (g) Cycling performance of a cell with a high areal capacity, using the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte. The pouch cells in panels (e) and (g) were tested without external stacking pressure or clamping device during electrochemical performance evaluation.

In terms of cycling performance, Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cells with the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte have much higher capacity retention and durability than those with the PVDF-HFP electrolyte (Fig. 5c and Fig. S35). At 1 mA cm−2, the PVDF-HFP/HEI-based cell shows an ultralong lifespan, retaining 80% of its initial capacity after 2000 cycles. The highly stable CEs of 99.95% indicate superior interfacial stability and minimal side reactions. In contrast, cells with the PVDF-HFP electrolyte suffer rapid capacity degradation and pronounced CE fluctuations, reflecting severe interfacial side reactions that are detrimental to cycling stability and Li+ transport efficiency. Morphological characterization of the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte confirms its structural robustness with preserved integrity after 2000 cycles (Fig. S36), while the PVDF-HFP electrolyte experiences severe electrolyte structure damage after 500 cycles.

The cycling performance of the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte was evaluated at higher current densities, as shown in Fig. 5d. At 2 mA cm−2, the Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cells have an 80% capacity retention after 1000 cycles. As the current density is increased to 3 mA cm−2, the initial capacity has a 60% capacity retention after 1000 cycles. In both cases, the CEs remain stable at 99.95%, further confirming the role of the uniform HEI in stabilizing the SEI and supporting efficient Li+ transport under high-current-density conditions. This stability is further supported by the preserved [Li(DMF)x]+ solvation environment in the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte. After 1000 cycles at 3 mA cm−2, PVDF-HFP/HEI maintains a homogeneous [Li(DMF)x]+ distribution and nearly unchanged content of 5.7 wt% (Figs S14 and S37), whereas PVDF-HFP electrolyte shows aggravated aggregation and a decrease in [Li(DMF)x]+ content from 17.2 wt% to 11.3 wt%. These results indicate that the HEI suppresses [Li(DMF)x]+ decomposition and redistribution, thereby reducing interfacial side reactions during long-term cycling.

The ultrathin PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte minimizes the mass contribution of the electrolyte in the solid-state battery systems, thereby enhancing the overall energy density. At a thickness of 5 µm, its areal weight is only 0.58 mg cm−2, which is much lower than that of conventional polymer electrolytes (>30 µm, >3 mg cm−2) [60,61], which are typically required to suppress lithium dendrite growth under practical current densities. To meet the demand for high energy density in solid-state batteries, we assembled a pouch cell comprising the lightweight PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte, lithium foil with a thickness of 20 µm and thick LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 electrode with a high mass loading of 25 mg cm−2 (Fig. 5e). This configuration delivers an initial charge and discharge areal capacities of 5.4 and 4.9 mAh cm−2. Based on the total weight of the bare cell, including positive electrode, negative electrode, electrolyte and Al current collector (Table S4), an energy density of 573 Wh kg−1 is achieved, representing the highest values reported for solid-state batteries with thin electrolytes (Fig. 5f; see Table S5 for details). To assess practical safety, a fully charged Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 pouch cell with the PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte was subjected to folding and cutting tests (Fig. S38). The pouch cell continuously powered a light-emitting diode during deformation and partial cutting, with no smoke, fire or explosion observed, showing good mechanical abuse tolerance and safety. Moreover, the Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cell with a high areal capacity exhibits stable cycling at 1 mA cm−2, retaining 73% of its initial capacity after 150 cycles with an average CE of 99.92% (Fig. 5g). To clarify the capacity decay mechanism under high mass loading, EIS measurements were conducted before and after cycling and compared with those of the cell with low mass loading. The cell with high mass loading shows a larger impedance after cycling, with a more pronounced increase in interphase/contact and charge-transfer resistances after cycling (Fig. S39), indicating that its capacity fading mainly arises from increased interfacial impedance, sluggish charge-transfer kinetics and Li+ transport limitations in the thick electrode. These results show that, although the HEI effectively stabilizes the Li|electrolyte interface and promotes uniform Li+ transport, practical high-areal-capacity cells still require further optimization of transport within thick electrodes.

CONCLUSION

In summary, we develop a strategy to suppress dendrite penetration in ultrathin solid-state electrolytes under practical current densities by constructing an SEI with an HEI. Guided by machine learning screening, an optimized composition (Li-F-N-O-P-S) was incorporated into a 5-µm-thick PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte, forming an SEI enriched with a homogeneous interphase with high-entropy characteristics. This engineered HEI supports uniform and fast interfacial Li+ flux, enabling the ultrathin electrolyte to resist dendrite penetration at 5.2 mA cm−2 and maintain long-term cycling stability at a practical current density of 3 mA cm−2. Li||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cells show high cycling CEs of 99.95%, indicating superior interfacial stability and minimal side reactions. Pouch cells using this ultrathin electrolyte and a high-mass-loading LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 electrode deliver an energy density of 573 Wh kg−1. This work highlights that HEI engineering provides a dual-function benefit, reinforcing interfacial stability and promoting fast Li+ transport, offering an effective strategy for ultrathin electrolytes to meet the demanding requirements of practical current density.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

General information

Additional details on the materials, the characterization techniques employed and the electrochemical measurements are presented in the Supporting Information.

Machine-learning-driven compositional ratios of the high-entropy interphase

We used an XGBoost-based machine learning framework with SHAP interpretation to screen the optimal composition of the HEI. The workflow included dataset generation, model benchmarking, hyperparameter tuning, final training, virtual screening and feature-importance analysis (see Materials and Methods in the online Supplementary file for details).

DFT calculations

All DFT computations were carried out using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional within the generalized gradient approximation was employed to describe the exchange-correlation effects [62], and electron–ion interactions were treated using the projector-augmented wave method [63]. A plane-wave cutoff of 400 eV was applied throughout the calculations. The self-consistent field cycles were converged to 1 × 10−5 eV in total energy, with atomic forces optimized to below 0.03 eV Å−1. Dispersion interactions were incorporated using the Grimme D3 correction to improve the accuracy of structural optimizations [63].

Formation energies were calculated to guide the selection of anion types for the HEI and to compare the structural stability of HEIs with varying anion ratios. Li+ migration energy barriers across the HEI and other single-anion interphases were evaluated using the nudged elastic band method with eight intermediate images uniformly distributed along the diffusion pathway. Preparation of PVDF-HFP and PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolytes

PVDF‑HFP and PVDF‑HFP/HEI electrolytes were prepared by solution casting and drying procedures. The PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolyte consisted of a PVDF‑HFP layer sandwiched between two multi‑anion layers with defined LiF, Li3N, Li2O, Li3P and Li2S ratios. Control electrolytes with varied anion compositions were fabricated identically (see Materials and Methods in the online Supplementary file for details).

Materials characterization

The morphology and composition of the PVDF-HFP and PVDF-HFP/HEI electrolytes were examined using field‑emission SEM and TOF-SIMS. Mechanical properties were evaluated with a universal testing machine at room temperature, and thermal stability was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis. Surface chemical states were analyzed by XPS, with binding energy referenced to the C 1s peak. The SEI morphology and composition were characterized by cryo‑TEM equipped with EELS, using a liquid‑nitrogen holder and inert‑atmosphere transfer to prevent air exposure. Interfacial stability between lithium and electrolyte was monitored by in situ optical microscopy at 3 mA cm−2 (see Materials and Methods in the online Supplementary file for details).
