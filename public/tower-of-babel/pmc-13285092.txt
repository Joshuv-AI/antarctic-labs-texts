Optimized bio-fertilization drives the synergistic recovery of vegetation productivity and soil multifunctionality by reshaping fungal interaction patterns in degraded alpine mines
Authors: Zongcheng Cai, Shouquan Fu, Fayi Li, Yunfei Xing, Jun Ma, Enqi Yang, Ran Qin, Quanfang Lei, Jianjun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRestoring degraded alpine mining ecosystems is critically constrained by soil infertility and the functional decoupling of plant–soil–microbe interactions. While optimized bio-fertilization represents a promising restoration strategy, the mechanisms linking vegetation recovery, shifts in fungal community structure, and soil multifunctionality (SMF) remain poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a three-year field restoration experiment at the Muli coal mine on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau to evaluate the integrated responses of plant communities and soil functional networks to various fertilization regimes.ResultsThe optimized regime (W3J1, comprising 375 kg·hm⁻2 of forage-specific fertilizer and 350 kg·hm⁻2 of microbial inoculant) elicited the most robust ecological recovery. By the end of the 2024 growing season, it increased absolute vegetation coverage to 77.31% and enhanced aboveground biomass to a peak of 352.67 g·m⁻2. Concurrently, the SMF index in W3J1 reached a peak of 0.95, effectively reversing the functional impairment observed in the degraded control (CK). Furthermore, co-occurrence network analysis revealed enhanced fungal community complexity, with the W3J1 treatment expanding network connectivity to 900 edges (compared to 728 edges in the CK), while the W1J3 treatment achieved the highest OTU richness (693). Notably, excessive nutrient inputs in the W3J3 treatment failed to yield additional benefits, as SMF and fungal diversity indices stabilized or declined due to resource imbalances. Random Forest modeling identified vegetation density as the paramount predictor of fungal diversity. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM, R2 = 0.67) further elucidated a cascading pathway where fertilization directly promoted fungal diversity (standardized path coefficient β = 0.76) and initiated vegetation establishment, which subsequently facilitated shifts in the fungal community structure and interaction networks.ConclusionsThe optimized co-application of microbial agents and fertilizers facilitates ecosystem reconstruction by orchestrating the coupling between vegetation density and fungal stability. This provides a theoretical basis for the sustainable restoration of severely degraded alpine mines.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05054-z.

Introduction

Alpine mining areas are characterized by extreme environmental stressors, including low temperatures, hypoxia, intense ultraviolet radiation, and frequent freeze–thaw cycles. Prolonged anthropogenic mining activities in these fragile zones have led to severe surface exposure, vegetation loss, soil structural degradation, and substantial nutrient depletion. This results in ecosystems with extremely limited self-restoration capacity [24, 26]. Under such harsh conditions, the artificial establishment of cold-tolerant forage species is a primary strategy for ecological restoration. However, the success of revegetation is not merely a matter of planting. It critically depends on the functional re-establishment of the belowground ecosystem, particularly the regulation of soil microbial communities [29]. Recently, international research has increasingly emphasized the central role of soil fungi in maintaining ecosystem functions [12]. As primary drivers of organic matter decomposition and nutrient cycling, fungi are essential for the biogeochemical turnover of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus. While symbiotic associations are important [48], shifts in saprotrophic fungal classes sensitively reflect the progression of soil recovery. This is due to their critical involvement in organic matter decomposition [7]. Therefore, targeted manipulation of soil fungal community structure has emerged as a promising approach to improve the sustainability of artificial grasslands in alpine mining ecosystems.

To accelerate belowground recovery, the application of combined microbial inoculants consisting of functionally diverse strains has emerged as a prominent research focus [34]. These strains include nitrogen-fixing bacteria, phosphate-solubilizing bacteria, and plant growth-promoting fungi. These inoculants synergistically optimize the rhizosphere microenvironment and facilitate nutrient transformation [38]. Traditional ecological monitoring relies on isolated soil or vegetation indicators. However, these isolated metrics fail to capture the holistic evolution of ecosystem functions [33]. Consequently, soil multifunctionality (SMF) has become a central paradigm for evaluating restoration success in degraded ecosystems. SMF provides a comprehensive measure of the capacity to simultaneously maintain multiple services, including nutrient cycling, primary production, and environmental regulation [9, 46]. Recent studies on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau further underscore that SMF is a critical integrative metric for quantifying ecosystem performance during mine reclamation [41, 45]. Within this framework, exogenous inoculation serves as a biological catalyst. It directly replenishes functional taxa and indirectly reshapes rhizosphere interaction networks to drive the succession of native beneficial microbes [15]. Furthermore, microbial inoculants restore soil functionality by enhancing physicochemical properties and upregulating functional genes associated with biogeochemical cycling [18].

However, microbial recovery requires adequate nutritional substrates. Forage-specific fertilizers can provide essential nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium support [4]. Conversely, their sole application often leads to soil acidification, salt accumulation, and microbial imbalance [17]. Emerging evidence suggests that the integrated application of microbial inoculants and fertilizers can mitigate these risks and generate significant synergistic effects [31]. In semi-arid regions, Zhang et al. [46] reported that co-application significantly enriched key bacterial and fungal taxa, promoting ecosystem stability. Long-term field experiments further support this, showing that combined treatments increased fungal diversity while enhancing plant productivity [13]. Despite these findings, a critical knowledge gap remains. Current research primarily focuses on conventional fertility indicators. It often neglects how gradient co-application regimes quantitatively reshape the topological structure of soil fungal networks. Specifically, there is a lack of evidence regarding a potential dose-dependent threshold. It remains unclear whether excessive nutrient-microbe inputs disrupt the coupling between microbial community stability and ecosystem functions beyond this threshold [21].

To address this, the present study proposes that the combined application of composite microbial inoculants and forage-specific fertilizers follows a dose-dependent optimization model. To test these hypotheses, we integrated ITS high-throughput sequencing, ecological network analysis, and structural equation modeling (SEM). Through this integrative approach, we comprehensively evaluated the shifts in fungal diversity and community structure, identified key functional taxa and their environmental drivers, and deciphered the cascading mechanisms among management inputs, vegetation recovery, and soil multifunctionality. Ultimately, this study seeks to provide a robust theoretical foundation and a scalable technical framework for the sustainable ecological restoration of severely degraded alpine grasslands and mining areas on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau.

Materials and methods

Study site description

The field experiment was conducted at the former coal storage area of the Muli Coal Mine (38°9′34″N, 99°9′40″E), located in Tianjun County on the northeastern margin of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau (Fig. 1a). The regional climate is harsh, characterized by low temperatures, intense solar radiation (2164.9 h annually), and a marked water-heat imbalance. The mean annual temperature is − 5.3 °C. Annual precipitation averages 626 mm (70% occurring from June to August), which is significantly offset by 1049.9 mm of annual evaporation. Winters are prolonged (7–8 months) with severe diurnal temperature fluctuations (> 15 °C) and snow cover exceeding 200 days, restricting the effective plant growing season to approximately 120 days (Fig. 1b) [25].

The study site represents a severely degraded ecosystem dominated by weathered coal gangue debris. Baseline soil sampling conducted prior to the experiment (May 2022) confirmed an extremely nutrient-poor and physically degraded matrix: soil organic matter (60.34 g·kg⁻1), total nitrogen (1.05 g·kg⁻1), and total phosphorus (0.84 g·kg⁻1) were low, while electrical conductivity (19,090 μS·cm⁻1) and alkalinity (pH 8.50) were high, coupled with a poor water-holding capacity (15.0% moisture content). The historical indigenous vegetation is a typical alpine meadow community dominated by Kobresia species (e.g., Carex parvula O. Yano and Carex alatauensis S. R. Zhang). The landscape characteristics and subsequent vegetation recovery process are illustrated in Fig. 1c–e.

Experimental materials

The composite microbial inoculant (Hubei Qiming Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) used in this study is a water-soluble powder with a viable bacterial count of ≥ 1 × 10⁸ CFU·g⁻1. It consists of a consortium of three specific strains at a mass ratio of 2:1:1: Trichoderma harzianum (THZ-1), Bacillus subtilis (BS-15), and Streptomyces albus (SA-01). The carrier system comprises maltodextrin and modified cellulose, supplemented with trace yeast extract and KH2PO4, and is entirely free of synthetic preservatives.

According to manufacturer specifications, all three strains are classified as Biosafety Level 1 (BSL-1) organisms. The formulation strictly complies with the ecological and hygienic safety limits of the Chinese National Standard for Agricultural Microbial Inoculants (GB 20287–2006). This includes undetectable fecal coliforms (< 100 MPN·g⁻1) and safe trace levels of heavy metals, ensuring its environmental safety for application in fragile alpine ecosystems.

A forage-specific blended fertilizer (Gansu Sudi Fertilizer Co., Ltd., China) was utilized for nutrient supply. It adheres to the National Standard for Blended Fertilizers (GB/T 21633–2020), with a total nutrient content of ≥ 35.00%. The specific stoichiometric ratio is 18.00% N, 12.00% P2O5, and 5.00% K2O, which is optimally balanced for the growth requirements of alpine forage grasses.

Four native graminoid species highly adapted to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau were selected for vegetation establishment: Festuca sinensis Keng, Poa pratensis L., Poa crymophila Keng, and Elymus breviaristatus Keng f. All seeds were sourced from the Institute of Grassland Science, Qinghai Academy of Animal and Veterinary Sciences, with standardized purity (≥ 92%) and germination rates (≥ 85%).

Experimental design

Study design

A randomized complete block design with 30 plots was established to evaluate the ecological efficacy of nutrient–microbe synergistic systems. The experiment comprised an unamended control (CK) and a 3 × 3 full factorial matrix of combined treatments.

Notably, single-factor amendments (microbial inoculation or chemical fertilization alone) were strategically excluded due to the extreme degradation of the Muli mining area. Without supporting nutritional substrates, exogenous microbes cannot successfully colonize or maintain metabolic activity in the weathered coal gangue. Conversely, high-dose chemical fertilizers applied in isolation risk secondary ecological stressors, such as soil acidification and salt accumulation. Therefore, we prioritized the "Functional Inoculant-Nutrient Matching" principle to evaluate the synergistic effects of combined bio-fertilizer regimes, which is critical for triggering self-sustaining recovery in severe alpine substrates [2].

The full factorial design cross-applied three levels of forage-specific fertilizer (W1: 225.00, W2: 300.00, W3: 375.00 kg·hm⁻2) and three dosages of composite microbial inoculants (J1: 350.00, J2: 500.00, J3: 650.00 kg·hm⁻2). The median fertilizer rate (W2: 300.00 kg·hm⁻2) was selected based on established optimal thresholds for restoring alpine meadows on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau [37, 45]. Concurrently, the median inoculant dosage (J2: 500.00 kg·hm⁻2) reflects manufacturer guidelines and empirical evidence for overcoming biological inertia in abandoned mines [18]. These proportional gradient extensions (W1/W3 and J1/J3) were designed to evaluate non-linear ecological responses while preventing the metabolic burden associated with excessive inputs.

Field applications were conducted annually in early June (2023 and 2024). Amendments were manually broadcast and immediately incorporated into the topsoil via light raking (depth ≤ 5 cm). To establish a rigorous baseline, the CK plots underwent identical site preparation and seeding protocols (including tilling and sowing of cold-tolerant species) but received no exogenous inputs. All plots were subjected to uniform field management to minimize confounding variables.

Seeding and establishment management

Seeding was performed on May 5, 2022, using a mechanical row-seeding system. The seed mixture comprised four native forage species adapted to the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau: Festuca sinensis Keng, Poa pratensis L., Poa crymophila Keng, and Elymus breviaristatus Keng f. Seeds were mixed in equal proportions (by weight) and sown at a total rate of 18 g·m⁻2 to a depth of 2 ~ 3 cm. Immediately after sowing, the soil surface was covered with polypropylene non-woven fabric (weight: 30 g·m⁻2; light transmittance: 72% ± 3%; air permeability: 230 ± 15 L·m⁻2·s⁻1) to promote establishment. The fabric was anchored using U-shaped pins spaced at 2 m intervals in a grid pattern. To facilitate seedling acclimation (hardening off) and prevent environmental stress, the cover was gradually removed in three phases at 5-day intervals once the seedlings reached a mean height of 5 cm.

Field layout

Each experimental plot measured 20 m2 (4 m × 5 m). To minimize cross-treatment interference, a 2 m wide buffer zone separated adjacent plots. Additionally, protective guard rows were established around the perimeter of the entire experimental site to mitigate edge effects. Sampling and observations were restricted to the central area of each plot, excluding a 0.5 m marginal zone to further eliminate edge bias.

Plant community survey and sampling

Vegetation surveys were conducted in September 2023 and September 2024, coinciding with the end of the growing season, to evaluate restoration efficacy. Key parameters included canopy height, vegetation coverage, plant density, and aboveground biomass.

Within each plot, five 50 cm times 50 cm quadrats were established using a systematic random sampling approach. Quadrat placement adhered to three criteria to ensure representativeness: (1) a minimum distance of 50 cm from the plot boundary; (2) a minimum spacing of 1 m between quadrat centers; and (3) avoidance of areas with significant topographic irregularities or anomalous vegetation [29].

Plant height was measured for the four target species: Festuca sinensis Keng, Poa pratensis L., Poa crymophila Keng, and Elymus breviaristatus Keng f. In each quadrat, 10 healthy, mature individuals per species were randomly selected. Height was recorded as the vertical distance from the soil surface to the tip of the highest leaf or inflorescence under natural growth conditions (precision: 1 mm). The mean value of these 10 individuals was recorded as the representative height for the species in that quadrat.

Vegetation density was calculated by recording the number of live individuals (N) within each quadrat and normalizing to the quadrat area (0.25 m2). Aboveground Biomass Measurement: Aboveground biomass was determined using a destructive harvest method. Plant samples underwent a two-step drying process: initial enzyme deactivation at 105 °C for 30 min, followed by drying at 65 °C for 48 h to a constant weight (mass change < 0.01 g between weighings). Dry matter was weighed using an analytical balance to calculate the aboveground biomass index.

Measurement indicators and methods

Soil sampling and processing

Soil samples were collected concurrently with vegetation surveys from the topsoil layer (0–10 cm depth) using a five-point sampling method. This specific sampling depth was determined by both biological and physical constraints of the study site: (1) in the early stages of alpine mine restoration, active root systems and intensive nutrient cycling are primarily concentrated in the uppermost 10 cm of the soil profile; and (2) the soil layer at the Muli coal mine is characteristically thin, with the profile below 10 cm consisting predominantly of impenetrable weathered coal gangue and large stones, which restricted deeper core extraction. A stainless steel auger (inner diameter: 5 cm) was used to extract soil cores vertically, ensuring consistent depth and minimal surface disturbance [44]. Raw samples were passed through a 2 mm sieve to remove roots, gravel, and organic debris. The five subsamples from each plot were homogenized into a single composite sample to minimize intra-plot spatial heterogeneity.

Each composite sample was divided into three sub-samples for specific subsequent analyses. The first portion was air-dried in a dark, well-ventilated room at 25 ± 2 °C to determine soil organic matter (SOM), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), pH, and electrical conductivity (EC). The second portion was immediately sealed and stored at –20 °C to preserve moisture for analysing the soil gravimetric water content (SWC). The final portion was snap-frozen and stored at –80 °C to prevent DNA degradation, being strictly reserved for the high-throughput sequencing of microbial communities.

Soil physicochemical properties

Soil physicochemical properties were analyzed in triplicate following standard protocols described by Bao [3]. Physical and Electrochemical Properties: Soil gravimetric water content (SWC) was determined by oven-drying at 105 °C to a constant weight. Soil electrical conductivity (EC) and pH were measured in soil–water suspensions with ratios of 1:5 (w/v) and 1:2.5 (w/v), respectively. Measurements were taken using a conductivity meter (DDS-307A, Leici, Shanghai, China) and a precision pH meter (FE28, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Nutrient Analysis: Soil organic matter (SOM) was quantified using the potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) oxidation method (external heating at 170–180 °C), followed by titration with ferrous sulfate. Total nitrogen (TN) was determined via the Kjeldahl method using an auto-analyzer (Kjeltec 8400, FOSS, Sweden) after digestion with H2SO4 and a mixed catalyst. Total phosphorus (TP) was analyzed using the molybdenum blue colorimetric method. Samples were digested with an H2SO4-HClO4 mixture, and absorbance was measured at 700 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan).

Calculation of soil multifunctionality

Soil multifunctionality (SMF) was assessed by integrating six key soil physicochemical indicators: soil organic matter (SOM), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), soil gravimetric water content (SWC), pH, and electrical conductivity (EC). These variables were selected as they represent critical ecosystem functions related to carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycling, water regulation, and soil environmental quality. The SMF index was calculated using the Z-score averaging method [11, 30]. Soil fungal DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted in triplicate from 0.5 g fresh soil using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA). High-quality extracts (A260/A280 ratio 1.8–2.0; intra-sample concentration CV < 5%) were pooled for PCR amplification. The fungal ITS1 sub-region was amplified using the specific primers ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). Briefly, the 25 μL reaction mixtures contained 10 ng of template DNA and TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech, China). Thermocycling consisted of 95 °C for 3 min; 27 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Sterile water was used as a negative control to preclude contamination. Amplicons were purified using a PCR Clean-Up Kit (Yuhua, China) and quantified via a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEXTFLEX® Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (PerkinElmer, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp paired-end). Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.19.6; removing reads < 50 bp, ambiguous bases, or Phred < 20) and merged via FLASH (v1.2.11). Sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity using UPARSE (v7.1), explicitly excluding chimeric and non-fungal sequences. To standardize sequencing depth, data were rarefied to 20,000 sequences per sample, ensuring Good’s coverage > 99%. Taxonomy was assigned against the UNITE database (v8.3) using the RDP Classifier (v2.11) with a 70% confidence threshold. Fungal functional guilds were predicted using the FUNGuild database (v1.0).

Statistical and bioinformatics analysis

Basic statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Inc., USA). After confirming data normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Duncan’s multiple range test (α = 0.05) was used to evaluate differences in plant and soil properties among treatments. To quantitatively rank the overall ecological restoration efficacy, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed. where PC1 and PC2 are the scores of the first two principal components, and W1 and W2 represent their respective variance contribution rates. Simple visualizations were generated using Origin 2022.

Advanced multivariate analyses were executed in R (v4.3.1). Random Forest (RF) modeling (randomForest and rfPermute packages) was used to identify key environmental drivers of fungal diversity, with predictor importance quantified via the percentage increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE) after 1,000 permutations. Furthermore, a piecewise structural equation model (piecewiseSEM package) was constructed to disentangle the direct and indirect causal relationships among fertilization, vegetation, soil properties, and fungal diversity. Based on an a priori conceptual model, categorical fertilization gradients were explicitly converted into continuous numerical variables representing actual application rates (kg·hm⁻2), and data were Z-score standardized. Model fit was validated using the directional separation test (Fisher’s C statistic, P > 0.05), alongside AIC and BIC metrics.

Bioinformatic pipelines were executed via the Majorbio Cloud Platform. Alpha-diversity indices were calculated using mothur (v1.47.0) and compared via Kruskal–Wallis tests. Beta-diversity was assessed using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities, validated by PERMANOVA (999 permutations). Taxonomic biomarkers were identified using LEfSe (LDA score > 3.0, P < 0.05). Fungal functional guilds were predicted using the FUNGuild database (v1.0); crucially, to ensure prediction accuracy, only functional assignments with "Highly Probable" and "Probable" confidence rankings were retained for downstream analysis.

To overcome statistical limitations associated with small sample sizes per treatment, a "global meta-network construction and treatment-specific sub-network extraction" strategy was employed [43]. Briefly, a robust global meta-network was first generated using the relative abundance matrix of all samples based on Spearman's rank correlations (|r|> 0.7, P < 0.05 with Bonferroni correction). Subsequently, treatment-specific induced sub-networks were extracted by mapping the taxa present in each fertilization regime back to the global network. This strategy yielded reliable, comparable topological parameters (e.g., nodes, edges, positive linkages) for each treatment regime while effectively precluding spurious correlations.

Results

Changes in restored vegetation communities

Compared to the unamended control (CK), the co-application of microbial inoculants and forage-specific fertilizers significantly improved vegetation characteristics across the restoration period (Table S1). In 2024, the W3J1 treatment achieved the maximum plant height (35.87 cm) and aboveground biomass, which surpassed the CK by 80.23% (P < 0.05). Additionally, the W3J1 treatment recorded the highest vegetation coverage, exhibiting a 30.72% increase relative to the CK (P < 0.05). Conversely, the maximum plant density was observed in the W1J3 regime (440.33 plants·m⁻2), representing a significant 28.50% increase over the control (P < 0.05).

Responses of soil multifunctionality to fertilization regimes

Soil multifunctionality (SMF) was assessed to evaluate the effects of different treatments on belowground ecosystem functions (Fig. 2). The combined application of microbial inoculants and fertilizers increased SMF to varying degrees. The W3J1 regime yielded the highest SMF index among all combinatorial treatments, maintaining a peak value of 0.95 in both growing seasons (P < 0.05). At the phylum level, the soil fungal community was dominated by Ascomycota across all treatments, with relative abundances ranging from 70.54% to 90.29%, followed by unclassified Fungi, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Olpidiomycota (Figure S1a). Compared to the unamended control (CK), the combined application of microbial inoculants and fertilizers generally promoted the enrichment of Basidiomycota and Chytridiomycota. Specifically, the W1J3 treatment exhibited the highest relative abundance of Basidiomycota, whereas the W3J1 regime maximized the prevalence of Chytridiomycota.

At the genus level, the community was characterized by several core taxa, including Thelebolus (21.98%–34.21%), Lophotrichus (2.23%–23.43%), and unclassified Sordariales (4.38%–9.19%) (Figure S1b). Although these genera were ubiquitous across all samples, their abundance patterns shifted significantly in response to different treatment regimes. Notably, compared to the CK, the W3J1 treatment significantly increased the relative abundances of Thelebolus and Schizothecium, while simultaneously suppressing the prevalence of Lophotrichus.

Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was employed to identify biomarker taxa with significant differential abundance among the treatment groups (Figure S2). To ensure the robustness of the identified biomarkers and minimize noise, an LDA score threshold of > 3.0 was applied. This rigorous filtering detected a total of 57 discriminant taxa, revealing strong structural differentiation across the treatment matrix. The regimes that yielded superior vegetation and functional outcomes, specifically W1J3 and W3J1, harbored the highest number of indicator taxa (10 each). In contrast, the unamended control (CK) was primarily characterized by higher-level taxa such as the phylum Ascomycota and the class Sordariomycetes.

When examining specific lower taxonomic ranks (e.g., from class to genus levels), distinct lineages were found to be significantly enriched depending on the nutrient-microbe combinations. For instance, the W1J3 treatment was uniquely characterized by the class Dothideomycetes, whereas the W3J1 treatment was marked by the significant enrichment of the phylum Chytridiomycota. At the genus level, distinct management-specific associations were observed, such as the significant enrichment of Thelebolus in the W1J2 treatment and Iodophanus in W1J1. These biomarker taxa clearly indicate that variations in fertilizer and inoculant inputs selectively enrich specific fungal lineages, rather than merely altering overall community abundance.

Discussion

Mechanisms of synergistic restoration through coupled vegetation establishment and soil multifunctionality

This study systematically elucidates the synergistic mechanisms by which combined microbial inoculants and forage-specific fertilizers drive ecosystem reconstruction in alpine mining areas. Our findings confirm that restoration extends beyond mere biomass accumulation; it fundamentally relies on coupling vegetation establishment with soil multifunctionality (SMF).

Regarding vegetation recovery, the optimized co-application (treatment W3J1) maximized ecological responses, validating the efficacy of a "function–nutrient" co-delivery system. Inorganic fertilizers provide essential substrates (N, P, K). Concurrently, exogenous microbial inoculants introduce functional taxa that act as biological catalysts, transforming these nutrients into bioavailable forms through phosphorus solubilization and nitrogen fixation [10]. This synergy alleviates the severe stoichiometric constraints of alpine mining soils. Furthermore, the concurrent improvements in vegetation metrics align with the "microbial-assisted phytostabilization" framework. In this model, functional microbes ameliorate rhizosphere conditions, facilitating host plant establishment and initiating community reassembly in degraded habitats [16].

A critical breakthrough of this study is establishing the mechanistic link between microbial restructuring and SMF restoration. The synergistic treatments reversed severe functional degradation, peaking under optimal inputs. This supports the "biodiversity–multifunctionality" hypothesis, suggesting that complex microbial communities simultaneously drive multiple ecosystem functions [11]. The optimized inputs enriched specific saprotrophic and functional guilds, which accelerated organic matter decomposition and nutrient turnover. Because soil biodiversity is a primary determinant of ecosystem multifunctionality [42], the stabilization of fungal networks observed here indicates that restoration success is contingent upon constructing a resilient microbiome.

Crucially, this synergy exhibits a strict dosage threshold. Excessive inputs (e.g., treatment W3J3) yielded no additional benefits and even suppressed SMF. Excessive fertilization disrupts the balance of rhizosphere microbial communities and triggers competitive exclusion [36]. Moreover, imbalanced inoculant-fertilizer combinations in alpine meadows cause soil C:N imbalances and elevate microbial metabolic costs, thereby reducing resource use efficiency [45]. The decline in ecological benefits at maximum input levels represents a biological trade-off: the "metabolic burden" of processing excess nutrients outweighs the benefits of resource addition. Ultimately, optimal ecological outcomes depend on precisely matching microbial functional potential with nutrient supply intensity.

Shifts in fungal community structure and co-occurrence networks

The combined application of inoculants and fertilizers exerted a profound deterministic selection pressure on soil fungi. It drove a compositional shift from stress-tolerant taxa toward resource-responsive functional guilds. Although Ascomycota maintained dominance due to its inherent resilience in oligotrophic environments [41], the optimized fertilization regime significantly enriched specific saprotrophic genera, notably Thelebolus and Schizothecium. These coprophilous and cellulolytic taxa are key drivers of nutrient mineralization [32]. Their enrichment suggests that synergistic inputs accelerate the decomposition of recalcitrant organic matter, unlocking previously inaccessible carbon and nutrient pools [19]. This dynamic aligns with the "resource-based selection" mechanism, where nutrient amendments reshape communities by favoring taxa capable of exploiting newly available resources [5].

Furthermore, the response of fungal α-diversity validates the "intermediate disturbance hypothesis." Diversity indices significantly increased under moderate treatments (W1J3 and W3J1). Alleviating nutrient limitations relaxes environmental filtering, allowing rare taxa to colonize available niches [39, 40]. Conversely, diversity declined under excessive application. Nutrient oversaturation likely triggers competitive exclusion, enabling fast-growing dominant taxa to outcompete slower-growing specialists [6]. Therefore, successful restoration requires balanced nutrient inputs to maximize niche differentiation without inducing competitive displacement.

Co-occurrence network analysis further revealed that optimal co-application transformed the fungal network from a fragmented state into a highly connected structure. The increase in network edges and positive correlations suggests enhanced cooperative interactions, such as metabolic syntrophy [22]. The emergence of Glomeromycota (arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi) as a hub taxon indicates the restoration of critical plant–fungal symbioses that facilitate phosphorus uptake [27]. Functionally, fertilization suppressed multi-pathogenic guilds while promoting beneficial saprotrophic-symbiotic groups. The minimized pathogen abundance observed under optimized treatments implies that synergistic inputs enhance community-level ecological safety through competitive exclusion or pathogen suppression [1]. Consequently, the W3J1 regime not only recovers phylogenetic diversity but also re-establishes the interaction networks essential for self-sustaining ecosystem stability.

Interactions among rhizosphere microbiomes, environmental factors, and vegetation characteristics

By integrating Mantel tests and Random Forest (RF) modeling, this study reveals that fungal community succession in alpine mining areas is co-driven by vegetation characteristics and soil properties. Vegetation density emerged as the paramount regulatory factor, significantly outperforming other variables in predicting fungal diversity. To ensure robustness, we verified that this relationship was not confounded by spatial autocorrelation. The influence of vegetation density remained significant after accounting for geographic proximity. This indicates that the observed patterns are driven by niche-based processes, such as increased root exudation and microhabitat heterogeneity, rather than mere spatial distance. This finding echoes observations in Qinghai-Tibetan alpine grasslands, where environmental and vegetation-driven niche selection strongly govern microbial assembly [23].

The RF model also identified soil organic matter (SOM) as a critical driver. Carbon availability remains a primary constraint for heterotrophic fungal proliferation in oligotrophic mining substrates. Mantel tests corroborated this, highlighting strong correlations among fungal diversity, SOM, pH, and vegetation density. Synergistic improvements in soil fertility, particularly organic matter accrual, are inextricably linked to microbial functional recovery and plant growth in semi-arid restorations [14]. Accumulated SOM fuels microbial metabolism and improves soil structure, creating a positive feedback loop for vegetation establishment. Interestingly, while vegetation height and biomass are traditional restoration indicators, they possessed less independent explanatory power than density. At this early restoration stage, the spatial frequency of plants (density) is more critical for re-establishing belowground networks than sheer biomass. Higher density maximizes the root-soil interface, fundamentally enhancing rhizosphere interactions. Regulating soil pH and electrical conductivity also mitigated chemical stress, a crucial mechanism for remediating gangue mountains [47].

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) indicated that soil physicochemical properties exerted no immediate direct influence on fungal diversity. This lack of correlation may reflect a "lag effect," wherein chemical alterations in the soil matrix manifest more slowly than biological responses. Alternatively, fungal communities may respond more rapidly to biotic interactions and microenvironmental shifts than to bulk soil nutrient pools. This aligns with recent studies demonstrating that bio-organic fertilizers can enhance plant growth and restructure microbiomes without immediately altering bulk nutrients [28]. Thus, our findings support a cascading restoration pathway: fertilization drives vegetation growth, which subsequently bridges comprehensive soil improvement.

Finally, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) confirmed a highly coordinated ecological response across all metrics. The W3J1 treatment demonstrated a superior capacity to synergistically drive vegetation recovery, soil amelioration, and fungal optimization. High-rate forage fertilizers alleviate nutrient limitations, promoting vigorous plant growth and rhizodeposition. Concurrently, moderate microbial dosages ensure efficient colonization without triggering intense interspecific competition [20]. This supports the "functional inoculant dependence on nutrient matching" hypothesis, emphasizing that microbial colonization is strictly regulated by soil resource availability [35]. Unlike the degraded control, the W3J1 regime effectively balanced resource supply with biological activity, driving ecosystem succession toward higher functionality [47].

Conclusions

This study establishes a mechanistic framework for restoring degraded alpine mining areas through the strategic coupling of microbial inoculants and nutritional substrates. The optimized regime (W3J1: 375 kg·hm⁻2 of forage-specific fertilizer and 350 kg·hm⁻2 of microbial inoculant) emerged as the most effective strategy, successfully overcoming nutrient-function bottlenecks to maximize vegetation productivity, soil multifunctionality, and fungal community stability. Conversely, excessive inputs triggered non-linear threshold effects, compromising restoration efficacy by inducing resource imbalances and competitive exclusion. Crucially, integrated modeling revealed a cascading restoration pathway: fertilization primarily drives vegetation establishment, and the resulting high plant density serves as the paramount driver for re-establishing soil fungal networks by enhancing rhizosphere heterogeneity and carbon inputs. Future research should integrate multi-omics approaches to dissect the precise metabolic synergies between functional microbes and host plants, and further evaluate the long-term ecological safety and generalizability of this synergistic restoration model across diverse alpine degradation gradients.
