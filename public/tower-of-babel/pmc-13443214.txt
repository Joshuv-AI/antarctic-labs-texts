Trial-by-trial auditory brainstem response detection
Authors: George S Liu, Noor-E-Seher Ali, Dáibhid Ó Maoiléidigh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe neural response of the brainstem to brief sounds, known as the auditory brainstem response (ABR), is widely employed in the laboratory and the clinic to diagnose hearing loss. Current ABR approaches are limited to analyzing the average ABR to hundreds of stimulus trials. Historically, trial-by-trial ABR analysis has not been possible owing to each trial’s small signal-to-noise ratio. Here we overcome this limitation and show how to score individual ABR trials. We use the distribution of individual-trial ABR scores to define thresholds, which we call auditory brainstem detection (ABD) thresholds, and to assess suprathreshold hearing. Scores based on individual-trial amplitude and similarity produce lower ABD thresholds than scores based on individual-trial amplitude alone. Our method enables us to rank the suprathreshold hearing of individual subjects. Despite having normal ABR thresholds, some subjects appear to have suprathreshold hearing deficits.Keywords: Hearing, Auditory system, Evoked potentials, Signal detection, Threshold, SuprathresholdSubject terms: Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

The most basic function of the auditory system is to determine whether a sound is present each time a subject is exposed to a sound stimulus. Many behavioral tests address the ability of the auditory system to perform this task1,2. For each stimulus interval, the auditory system classifies a stimulus as being present or absent. The absolute threshold of hearing is defined for a subject as the sound pressure level at which a certain fraction of stimulus presentations are correctly classified by the subject.

Behavioral thresholds are fundamentally different from thresholds based on the electrical response of the brainstem to sounds, known as the auditory brainstem response (ABR). Averaging hundreds of ABR trials to repeated presentations of the same sound stimulus reveals a stereotypical temporal pattern3–6. The ABR threshold is defined as the sound pressure level at which the average ABR evidences the presence of a stimulus7,8. An extensive array of methods have been developed to objectively determine ABR threshold (9–11 and references therein). Although the ABR threshold is a useful diagnostic, it does not access how well the auditory system responds to each sound presentation.

Suprathreshold hearing can also be assessed using the average ABR. The amplitudes and latencies of characteristic peaks in the average ABR, known as Jewett waves, respectively rise and fall with increasing stimulus levels12–16. Waves are, however, difficult to identify in all subjects and at low levels17,18. Although the disappearance of a wave may indicate hearing impairment, how to associate changes in wave amplitudes or latencies with suprathreshold hearing loss remains an open question15,16,19,20.

Here we show that trial-by-trial scoring of ABRs is possible and propose measures of threshold and suprathreshold hearing based on classifying ABR trials using their scores. We show that trial-by-trial thresholds, which we call auditory brainstem detection (ABD) thresholds, are higher than those based on the average ABR. We describe two scores that do not require Jewett wave identification. Scores based on trial similarity and amplitude yield lower ABD thresholds than scores based on ABR amplitude alone. Our goal is not to find a method that lowers ABR thresholds or that reproduces behavioral thresholds. Instead, we propose and demonstrate a conceptually new way to use ABR information, which complements methods based solely on the average ABR.

The voltage-difference score

A simple score that captures the magnitude of an ABR is the maximum peak-to-trough difference, for neighboring peaks and troughs, over the response time T. We use this voltage-difference score instead of alternative scores like the root-mean-squared amplitude or the peak-to-peak amplitude of a specific Jewett wave, because the voltage-difference score is larger than these alternative amplitude scores.

ABR threshold based on the average score

For a given score, an ABR threshold can be defined as the stimulus level at which the average score owing to stimulation can be distinguished from the average score in the absence of stimulation using a hypothesis test, in which the null hypothesis is that the average scores are the same. We choose threshold to be the stimulus level at which a test’s p-value equals 0.05 and for which the p-values at all higher stimulus levels are less than 0.05. This approach does not require a multiple-comparisons correction because we are requiring that all (instead of any) higher-level p-values are less than 0.05.

To determine the ABR threshold, we compare the average in the absence of a stimulus to averages arising from the presence of stimuli using Welch’s t-test, in which the ABR in the absence of a stimulus is approximated by the ABR at 0 dB SPL (sound pressure level). The corresponding ABR threshold is 25 dB SPL for the representative rat (Fig. 3(b)).

We classify an individual stimulus as being detected if its individual ABR score exceeds a cutoff (). The fraction of individual stimuli that are correctly classified as detected when there is a stimulus is the true positive rate (TPR) and the fraction that are incorrectly classified as detected when there is no stimulus is the false positive rate (FPR). To account for both the TPR and FPR using a single number, we use the area under each ROC curve (AUC) given by

which equals the probability that a stimulus of size s evokes a score larger than scores arising from no stimulus26,30. The AUC is independent of the score cutoff and becomes independent of the number of stimulus presentations (N) as N increases. Larger AUCs correspond to better discrimination between the stimulus and no-stimulus conditions.

We define the ABD threshold to be the sound pressure level at which the AUC achieves a particular value. The stimulus level at which the AUC = 0.75 is 56 ± 2 dB SPL for the voltage difference (Fig. 4(c)). We choose an AUC criterion of 0.75 because it is half way between the AUC corresponding to no discrimination between the stimulus and no stimulus conditions (AUC = 0.5) and the AUC corresponding to perfect discrimination (AUC = 1). An AUC criterion of 0.75 means that the probability that the stimulus yields a voltage difference larger than a noise fluctuation when there is no stimulus is 0.75. As long as the AUC criterion is not too close to 0.5 or 1.0, larger or smaller values for the AUC criterion yield qualitatively similar results. A criterion too close to 0.5 or to 1.0 causes large threshold errors because the AUC values at different levels are very close to each other when the AUC curves are close to 0.5 or 1.0 (Fig. 4(c)).

Scoring individual trials enables us to quantify the classification performance of the auditory brainstem in terms of the AUC. Larger AUCs correspond to fewer false positive and false negative classifications of individual trials.

The inner-product score

Although the voltage difference quantifies an ABR’s amplitude, it does not account for the similarity between individual ABR trials and the voltage difference only uses two time points per trial. To account for an ABR’s amplitude and the similarity between ABR trials, and to use the entire time series, we employ the inner-product score given by

in which are the times at which the ABR is sampled and is the average ABR without trial n (Fig. 5(a))31. The inner product quantifies the similarity between each individual ABR and a template, given by the average ABR, and is proportional to the covariance between two time series that have zero means (like the band-pass filtered ABRs analyzed here). Essentially, we propose that the average ABR is a matched filter that changes with stimulus level and is subject specific32. As the stimulus intensity grows, the mean inner product increases and the inner-product distribution widens and shifts to higher values of the inner product (Fig. 5(b,c)). At all stimulus levels, the inner-product distribution is non-Gaussian (Q-Q plots indicate heavier tails than normal distributions, Anderson-Darling tests , kurtosis equals 7–17) (Fig. 5(c)). We choose the inner product to quantify similarity instead of the correlation because the inner product retains information about the amplitudes of the ABRs.

Like before, we use Welch’s t-test to find the ABR threshold at which the average inner-product score at stimulus level s can be distinguished from the average inner-product score in the absence of a stimulus (Fig. 5(b)). Welch’s ABR threshold using the inner product is 25 dB SPL.

Like the voltage-difference distributions, we use the inner-product distributions to calculate ROC curves and AUCs for each curve (Fig. 4(b, c)). The ABD threshold for the inner product (the stimulus level at which the AUC = 0.75) is 42 ± 1 dB SPL, 14 dB SPL lower than the ABD threshold for the voltage difference.

The AUC quantifies suprathreshold hearing

We show that it is possible to assess suprathreshold hearing using the mean score of individual trials (Figs. 3(b) and 5(b)). Like other measures of suprathreshold hearing, including wave amplitudes and latencies, mean scores may differ between subjects with and without hearing loss, but the raw values of amplitudes, latencies, voltage differences, or inner products do not directly quantify hearing performance because they also depend on other factors like head size, age, and species22,23. In contrast, the AUC has a clear interpretation. An AUC value of 0.5 corresponds to no sound-signal detection and an AUC value of 1.0 corresponds to perfect sound-signal detection.

For both the voltage-difference and inner-product scores, the AUC increases with stimulus level, because suprathreshold hearing performance improves with increasing stimulus levels (Fig. 4(c)). ROC curves for the inner product illustrate that perfect detection is almost possible at 95 dB SPL (Fig. 4(b)). At and above 29 dB SPL, the inner product’s AUC exceeds that of the voltage difference (Welch’s t-test: p łe 0.05) (Fig. 4(c)). However, the inner-product and voltage-difference AUCs must become indistinguishable as the level rises (beyond the levels employed here), because AUCs converge to unity for high-intensity stimuli. The inner-product and voltage-difference grow differently with stimulus level because the inner product accounts for response size and similarity using all the time points in the responses, whereas the voltage difference only accounts for response size using two time points.

Like Welch’s t-test, the Mann-Whitney U test defines an ABR threshold as the lowest level for which the score distribution arising from a stimulus of size s differs statistically (p łe 0.05) from the score distribution in the absence of a stimulus and for which the distributions at all higher levels also differ from the no-stimulus distribution. The Mann-Whitney U inner-product threshold of 24 dB SPL is slightly lower than the Mann-Whitney U voltage-difference threshold of 25 dB SPL (Fig. 4(c)). Because the p-value associated with the U statistic decreases with the number of stimulus presentations, the Mann-Whitney U ABR threshold decreases as N increases. Unlike Welch’s t-test, the Mann-Whitney U test does not assume that the distribution of the mean scores is normal. However, ABR thresholds found using Welch’s t-test are similar to those found using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Discussion

There have been many efforts to relate ABR thresholds to behavioral thresholds33–42. Although these empirical relationships are useful, ABR thresholds are inherently different from behavioral thresholds. Behavioral thresholds quantify hearing for each stimulus presentation, whereas ABR thresholds quantify hearing using the average response to a group of stimuli. In addition, ABRs are limited to the brainstem, ABRs possess measurement noise, the ABR is a poor representation of the neural signal, and behavioral thresholds usually use stimuli with long durations (> 500 ms), whereas ABRs use brief stimuli (< 10 ms)1,9,11,37–40,43–50.

We highlight an additional difference from behavioral thresholds. In principle, ABR thresholds based solely on finding statistically-significant differences between distributions, a temporal pattern in the average ABR, or signal-to-noise criteria for the average ABR decline without bound as the number of stimulus trials rises (assuming that the subject has some brainstem response to sound)18,51–53. Indeed, ABR threshold has been observed to decrease with increasing trial number18,53. In other words, an ABR threshold can be decreased as much as one wants by increasing the number of trials and can even be decreased below the behavioral threshold18.

Like behavioral thresholds, ABD thresholds quantify hearing for each stimulus presentation and do not depend, in principle, on the number of stimulus presentations. ABR thresholds determine whether a subject has any response to sound, whereas ABD thresholds quantify the stimulus size needed for good hearing performance. However, ABD thresholds have the other limitations listed for ABR thresholds. Owing to these limitations, ABD thresholds are higher than behavioral thresholds.

Unlike ABR thresholds, ABD thresholds quantify individual-trial hearing. The fact that ABD thresholds differ from ABR thresholds demonstrates that ABD thresholds quantify hearing differently from ABR thresholds. Because the ABR threshold and the ABD threshold answer different questions about hearing, it is possible that hearing deficits cause a change in one and not the other. Whether ABD thresholds enable the diagnoses of different types of hearing loss to those diagnosed by ABR thresholds remains to be demonstrated.

Using the AUC, we find that suprathreshold hearing improves with rising stimulus levels in a subject-dependent manner. At each stimulus level, small AUC values for some subjects relative to their peers may indicate that these rats possess suprathreshold hearing deficits, possibly arising from defective or lost high-threshold auditory nerve fibers, despite having normal ABR thresholds15,54,55. A direct link between decreased AUCs and hearing pathology remains to be shown.

To demonstrate how to use individual-trial ABRs, we limit our study to a small number of subjects (5 rats) at a single stimulus frequency (16 kHz). Although different stimulus frequencies produce average ABRs with different amplitudes and latencies, we still expect the voltage-difference and inner-product scores to be applicable, because these scores not make any assumptions about the ABR waveform shape56,57. Like ABR thresholds, wave amplitudes, and wave latencies, we expect ABD thresholds and AUCs to depend on stimulus frequency56,57

Additionally, we have not demonstrated our approach in subjects with hearing loss. We expect ABDs and AUCs to decrease in subjects with hearing loss, but how they decrease may depend on the type of hearing loss. The analysis of larger datasets, including subjects with hearing loss, may validate the utility of ABD thresholds and AUCs53.

Most prior studies only use individual ABR trials to calculate the average ABR (9–11 and references therein). One prior study uses individual ABR trials to quantify the error in the average ABR and the error in the correlation between average ABRs53. In contrast, we quantify each individual trial and use the inner product (covariance) between each individual trial and the average ABR. We use individual trials to calculate more than the average ABR and errors. Essentially, we show that scoring individual trials provides information about hearing performance that is not captured by the average ABR alone.

We find that inner-product ABD thresholds are lower than voltage-difference thresholds because the inner product uses all of the time series and accounts for ABR amplitude and similarity. Quantifying threshold and suprathreshold hearing using the inner-product AUC has several advantages over using Jewett wave amplitudes or latencies: the inner product uses all of the ABR time series (increasing sensitivity to level changes and robustness to randomness); the inner product does not require identification of Jewett waves (which may be difficult to identify); and the AUC does not depend on the voltage scale (which depends on head-size, age, and species)22,23.

The inner product is similar to crosscorrelation methods used to analyze ABRs. Crosscorrelations between two average ABRs53,58–67, the average ABR and a template based on the average ABR at certain stimulus levels10,32,68–70, and the average ABR and a template based on a set of normal ABRs across subjects65,69,71, as well as the average correlation between pairs of individual ABRs31 have been employed. The average inner product has been introduced previously, but its use is not widespread31.

There are two types of noise associated with ABRs. There is unavoidable noise intrinsic to the auditory system. The auditory system must distinguish sound signals from this intrinsic noise. ABRs also possess measurement noise, which obscures measurements of the auditory system’s response to sound. Increasing intrinsic noise or measurement noise increases ABR and ABD thresholds and decreases AUCs. Our trial-by-trial approach takes both types of noise into account by reframing the analysis of ABRs as a signal detection in noise problem.

Consistent interference, like electrical interference, that changes the average ABR limits all ABR analysis methods (9–11,53 and references therein). Like prior methods, we average an equal number of rarefaction and condensation stimuli to create the average ABR, which decreases the effects of electrical interference. However, there may be electrical-interference effects in the individual ABRs. As a result, the ABD thresholds may be overestimated and the AUCs may be underestimated. In the future, averaging pairs of individual condensation and rarefaction trials to create pseudo-individual trials may decrease the effects of consistent interference.

Because signal-detection is inherently stochastic, trial-by-trial classification of signals is frequently used to evaluate artificial and natural systems72–76. The classification methodology we employ here does not require training data and provides objective and interpretable measures of signal-detection performance that can be compared across systems. We show this approach is feasible in rodents, yields thresholds different to traditional ABR analysis techniques, and provides information about a subject’s suprathreshold hearing.

Individual ABR trials have not been quantified previously, in part because most ABR equipment did not save individual ABR trials and instead only saved the average ABR. Motivated by our results, we encourage researchers to save individual ABR trials for analysis. Trial-by-trial ABR classification holds promise as a new method to detect hearing impairment in animal models and in humans.

Methods

The Administrative Panel on Laboratory Animal Care at Stanford University (APLAC #14345) approved all animal procedures. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Sprague Dawley rats of both sexes were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA). We measured ABRs in five rats at ages P21-P25 using Tucker Davis Technologies (TDT) System 3 RZ6. Rats were anesthetized by administering 3% isoflurane for induction and 1.5–2% isoflurane for maintenance and their body temperatures were held constant at 37C (FHC, DC-temperature controller and heating pad) until they fully recovered. Needle electrodes were placed in the vertex (non-inverting), below the left ear (inverting), and in the ipsilateral hindleg (ground). Subjects were placed in a sound attenuating enclosure within a Faraday cage. Sound stimuli for eliciting ABRs were delivered using a high-frequency speaker (TDT, MF1) that was calibrated using a free-field prepolarized microphone (Model 377C01, PCB Piezotronics) prior to experiments. The rat was positioned 10 cm away from the speaker. The stimulus was a 5 ms sine wave tone pip with cosine-squared envelope rise and fall times of 0.2 ms. The repetition rate was 21 pips per second, the stimulus frequency was 16 kHz, and the intensity was increased in 5 dB SPL steps from 0 dB SPL to 90–100 dB SPL. The sampling rate was 200 kHz, the response was bandpass filtered between 500 Hz and 3 kHz, and artifact rejection was not employed. We recorded responses to 514 stimulus presentations (an equal number of alternating rarefaction and condensation stimuli) at each sound pressure level for offline analysis. Each response trial began at the onset of the stimulus, without correcting for the acoustic delay, and had a duration of 7.755 ms. We did not correct for the small acoustic delay of approximately 0.3 ms because our analysis methods do not require the correction.

Data analysis was performed in MATLAB (version R2018b, MathWorks). Error bars represent standard errors and, apart from the average scores, were estimated from studentized confidence intervals based on bootstrapping for each stimulus level and rat using 500 resamples for each observed set of scores. Doubling the number of bootstrapped samples changed the error estimates by less than 10%. P-values, thresholds, cutoff values, false-positive rates, true-positive rates, and standard errors at points that were not directly sampled were estimated by linear interpolation between sampled points. We linearly interpolate between measurement points instead of fitting curves to the data, because fitting curves would introduce additional parameters and would make the quantities in question depend on stimulus levels very far from threshold53. Defining thresholds or threshold differences using p-values does not necessitate multiple-comparisons corrections because we are requiring that many (instead of any) p-values are less than 0.05.
