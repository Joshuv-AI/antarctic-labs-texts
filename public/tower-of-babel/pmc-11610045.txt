Neutrophil extracellular traps are associated with airways inflammation and increased severity of lung disease in cystic fibrosis
Authors: Sheonagh M. Law, Gareth Hardisty, Jonathan L. Gillan, Nicola J. Robinson, Donald J. Davidson, Moira K.B. Whyte, Ian Dransfield, Robert D. Gray
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCystic fibrosis (CF) is characterised by inflammatory lung disease and large numbers of airways neutrophils. In health, neutrophils undergo apoptosis and removal from the airway. Since CF neutrophils are known to engage in apoptosis less efficiently, we wanted to assess whether alternative forms of neutrophil clearance such as NETosis were prominent in the CF airway.MethodsSputum and blood were collected from 45 CF and 15 healthy control (HC) participants. Neutrophil morphology and biochemical properties were assessed in CF and HC sputum. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) were measured by a novel histone-calprotectin ELISA. NET levels were compared with established measurements of airway inflammation. CF participants were followed up for 1 year and the number of exacerbations recorded. Neutrophil and macrophage co-culture experiments were undertaken with cells from CF and HC.ResultsNeutrophil numbers were significantly higher in CF and associated with abnormal morphology. Several inflammatory mediators were elevated in CF sputum, as was cell-free DNA. This was highly correlated with sputum calprotectin, a known NET-associated protein. Using a histone-calprotectin NET ELISA, we demonstrated higher levels of NETs in the CF airway. CF participants treated with DNase had fewer sputum NETs, and in neutrophil/macrophage co-culture experiments, DNase effectively attenuated the pro-inflammatory potential of NETs, suggesting a previously unrecognised anti-inflammatory role for this treatment.ConclusionsNETs in the CF airway are associated with increased levels of inflammatory mediators and more severe lung disease. NET effects on macrophages can be blocked by DNase, suggesting an anti-inflammatory role for this treatment in CF.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a life-limiting autosomal recessive disease characterised by chronic lung infection, bronchiectasis and lung function decline [1]. The advent of new CF specific therapies offers hope of long-term disease management strategies for CF, but lung inflammation remains a feature of CF lung disease even when CFTR protein function is corrected [2]. Lung inflammation in established CF is characterised by increased levels of neutrophils and neutrophil products such as calprotectin and neutrophil elastase. Furthermore, we have demonstrated that CF neutrophils behave abnormally in culture by having decreased levels of constitutive apoptosis and increased susceptibility to neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation [3]. Previous studies have suggested the presence of NETs in sputum samples from people with CF, and furthermore have postulated that NETs may be a key driver of inflammation and tissue damage [4] with the interaction of NETs and other immune cells [3] and lung epithelial cells [5] being possible key factors. NETs are a key feature of non-CF bronchiectasis [6] and additionally have been implicated in COPD [7]. Most recently NETs have been connected to COVID-19 where the histone components of NETs have been suggested as a major pathological entity [8].

There is no standardised method for the measurement of NETs in biological samples but to enable the most accurate quantification of NETs, techniques that co-localise DNA, histones and protein constituents are recommended [9]. Several approaches have been proposed using immunoassays that measure DNA–histone complexes in tandem with myeloperoxidase [10] and elastase [7]. Another major protein constituent of NETs is the S100 protein calprotectin [11], which has previously been demonstrated as a biomarker of CF in both sputum [12] and serum [13–15]. We therefore designed an assay that measured the co-localisation of calprotectin and DNA–histone complexes in sputum as a relevant NETs assay for CF, and consequently other respiratory diseases.

In this study, we investigated the association of NETs in the CF airway with established measurements of airway inflammation and clinical features in a group of people with CF prior to the widespread adoption of CFTR modulators, and as such our study will serve as an important baseline for the measurement of NETs and inflammation in future studies of CF. Noting that NET levels in sputum were associated with increased levels of other inflammatory markers we also investigated the pro-inflammatory potential of NETs as a stimulus to macrophages and whether CF NETs were potentially more pro-inflammatory than non-CF NETs.

Methods

Study population

This was an observational study of 45 people with CF and 15 healthy controls (HCs) and all participants provided written informed consent (REC reference number 16/WS/0239 and AMREC 15/HV/013). CF participants were recruited from the Scottish National CF service outpatient clinic at the Western General Hospital, Edinburgh. As this was an exploratory study, statistical powering was not possible, so we selected patient numbers in line with previous studies of CF sputum [12]. All people with CF were over 16 years of age, free of pulmonary exacerbation for the preceding 2 weeks, nonlung transplant recipients and nonsmokers. HCs were recruited from the University of Edinburgh Centre for Inflammation Research, were over 16 years, had no past medical history of respiratory disease or recent respiratory infection and were nonsmokers. Clinical data from people with CF were collected on the day of the sample visit and recorded from patient case records. Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity were measured according to European Respiratory Society (ERS)/American Thoracic Society guidelines.

Sputum collection and processing

Spontaneous sputum was collected from people with CF. Induced sputum samples were obtained from HCs using hypertonic saline. Sputum was processed within 2 h of collection, as described previously [12]. In brief, sputum plugs were collected and homogenised using 0.1% dithiothreitol (DTT) (Sputolysin; Calbiochem Corp., San Diego, CA, USA) added in equal volume to the weight of sputum (1 mL DTT to 1 g sputum) and rotated at room temperature for 15 min before the addition of the same volume of Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) to stop digestion. Samples were filtered through a 48-µm mesh then centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cells. Cell-free supernatants were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Cell pellets were resuspended in DPBS, cytocentrifuge preparations made and stained with Shandon™ Kwik-Diff™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) for differential cell counting using a cell counter function of ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/download.html). All samples contained <40% squamous epithelial cells ensuring samples were from the lower airways.

Blood collection and processing

Whole blood was obtained by venepuncture and collected into tubes containing sodium citrate (Coagulation 9 NC/3-mL tubes, Sarstedt Monovette, Sarstedt AG and Co., Germany) to allow the use of cellular and serum components in further experiments. To obtain serum, samples were centrifuged at 350×g for 20 min at 20°C. Serum was obtained by recalcification of platelet-rich plasma and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Immune cells were processed as below.

Sputum and serum ELISAs and activity assays

ELISAs for interleukin (IL)-6 (BioLegend®, UK), IL-8 (DuoSet, R&D Systems, UK), tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α (DuoSet, R&D Systems, UK) and calprotectin (Hycult Biotech, Germany) were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity was assessed by combining sputum samples with 0.3 mM H2O2 (Sigma) and 1× TMB solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Plates were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 5 min. The reaction was stopped by adding Stop Solution and absorption was measured at 450 nm. Neutrophil elastase (NE) (NE Assay kit (Cayman Chemical, MI, USA)) and PicoGreen® assays were performed as per the manufacturer's instructions.

Human NETs ELISA

An in-house, double-sandwich ELISA was used to measure NET abundance by the assay of histone-bound calprotectin. A mouse anti-histone antibody (clone H11-4) available as part of from the Cell Death Detection ELISA kit (Roche, UK) was used to capture histone-bound proteins. Calprotectin detection antibody (biotinylated tracer antibody from HK379 Human Calprotectin ELISA kit, Hycult, Germany) was used in line with the manufacturer's instructions, including TMB reagent and Stop Solution. Optical density (OD) (excitation 450 nm and emission 630 nm) was measured and expressed as fold change in OD from mean HC values calculated to generate NET arbitrary units.

Polymorphonuclear and peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation

Polymorphonuclear (PMN) cells and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated as previously described [3]. Monocytes were isolated from PBMCs using a negative selection pan-monocyte isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotech Ltd., UK) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Monocytes were resuspended in Iscove's modified Dulbecco's medium (IMDM) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine and 25 mM HEPES, 10% autologous serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Pen/Strep) (10 000 U·mL−1 penicillin and 10 000 µg·mL−1 streptomycin) and plated at 2.5×105 per well in 48-well plates (Corning™, Costar™, Life Sciences, UK). Adherent monocytes were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2 for seven days to generate monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs), with medium changes on days one and five as previously described [3]

“NETing” neutrophil protocol

Isolated PMNs were resuspended in Hanks’ buffered salt solution (without cations) and NETosis was induced (NETing neutrophils) as described previously by stimulating with 100 nm PMA for 10 min in a rolling culture to avoid aggregation followed by multiple washing steps to remove PMA before being used in co-culture experiments [3]. Using this protocol around 20% of neutrophils have formed NETs by 4 h once plated in culture. In this manner, neutrophils are added to co-culture before they formed NETs at a constant ratio before NETs are formed.

Macrophage and NETing neutrophil co-culture

Co-culture experiments were performed between 7-day MDM and NETing neutrophils to assess the effects of both non-CF and CF NETs on non-CF and CF macrophages, respectively. Neutrophils (control or NETing) were added at 5×105 per well onto MDM (2:1 neutrophil to MDM ratio) in 500 µL IMDM supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine and 25 mM HEPES, 5% foetal calf serum (Gibco, UK) and 1% Pen/Strep. Plates were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 24 h, in line with previous data [3]. Then, 100 U·mL−1 DNase (Roche, UK) was added to some cultures. Supernatants were collected at 24 h, centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min at 20°C to remove cell debris then frozen at −80°C until analysis. Cytokines were quantified by ELISA as above.

Scanning electron microscopy of NETs and co-culture experiments

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to provide detailed microscopic evaluation of the morphological features of co-cultured cells. Following 24 h of MDM co-culture with NETs/control neutrophils, cell samples were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde (Sigma) in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (CAC) buffer and processed for electron microscopy. Samples were viewed and imaged using a Hitachi S-4700 scanning electron microscope.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Shapiro–Wilk normality tests were performed and data were assumed to follow a nonparametric distribution where sample sizes were insufficient to test for normality. Descriptive statistics were used to characterise the demographic and baseline characteristics and included the mean and sd and median and 95% confidence interval (CI), where specified. Significance testing was performed using an unpaired, two-tailed t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test when comparing two groups, or one-way/two-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test when comparing three or more experimental groups with appropriate post hoc tests. Results were considered significantly different when p-values were <0.05. Correlations were determined using Pearson rank or Spearman correlation coefficient. Multivariate linear regression modelling to predict FEV1 was performed using R software.

Sputum from people with CF contains greater numbers of neutrophils with an abnormal phenotype

We have previously demonstrated a decrease in constitutive apoptosis in CF peripheral blood neutrophils in culture [3] and so we sought to assess the abundance and cytological morphology of neutrophils in CF and non-CF sputum. CF neutrophils had an abnormal hyper-vacuolated morphology on haematoxylin and eosin staining (figure 1a,b) suggesting a degree of hyperactivation or an autophagic process, both of which are associated with NET formation [4]. Sputum for people with CF has more neutrophils per gram of sputum (figure 1c) and when quantified, a significantly increased proportion of neutrophils with vacuoles (figure 1d).

NETs immunoassay demonstrates increased levels of NETs in CF sputum

Sputum NETs were increased in CF compared with HC (figure 2a, p<0.001) but were not negatively correlated with FEV1 in CF (figure 2b). Surprisingly the highest levels of sputum NETs were observed in people without Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) (figure 2d, p<0.05 and figure 2g). Additionally, having fungal infection in sputum was not associated with having higher levels of NETs (figure 2e), and levels of NETs did not differ between frequent and infrequent exacerbators (figure 2f).

NETs correlated with other sputum biomarkers and were reduced in those taking DNase therapy but not by other potential inflammation-modifying treatments

Next, we measured several neutrophil biomarkers and cytokines (table 2). Neutrophil elastase (p<0.001), myeloperoxidase (p<0.001), TNF (p<0.001) and IL-8 (p<0.001) were elevated in CF versus HC. IL-6, previously shown to be a serum biomarker of CF was suppressed in sputum (p=0.0102). We then compared these biomarker levels with sputum NET levels. Correlation matrix (figure 3a) demonstrated that sputum NETs had a positive correlation with MPO activity, neutrophil elastase, IL-8 and TNF but was only statistically significant for MPO (Spearman r=0.428, p=0.003), IL-8 (Spearman r=0.419, p=0.004) and neutrophil elastase (Spearman r=0.581, p=0.023). MPO and neutrophil elastase are well-described constituents of NETs, but IL-8 is not. We next considered whether IL-8 might be present in sputum and correlated with NETs because of NETs interacting with other cells such as macrophages. The level of NETs in sputum was no different in people either taking or not taking the long-term antibiotic azithromycin (figure 3b) but were reduced in those taking DNase (figure 3c), correspondingly the level of IL-8 was also reduced in the sputum of these individuals, but this did not reach statistical significance (supplementary figure 1a). 13 subjects were also taking hypertonic saline (HTS) as a mucolytic treatment, and this had no significant impact on sputum NETs level (supplementary table 1 and supplementary figure 1b). Only four patients in the study were taking CFTR modulator therapy (three ivacaftor and one lumacaftor/ivacaftor), and they had no difference in sputum NETs level (supplementary table 1 and supplementary figure 1c).

Level of sputum NETs predicts FEV1 using multivariate linear regression modelling

There was no apparent correlation of sputum NET levels with FEV1 in people with CF using simple linear regression (figure 2b). However, multivariate linear regression analysis, which provides more accurate assessments of association between variables, demonstrated that the level of NETs in sputum was an independent, significant predictor of FEV1 (table 3). The number of respiratory exacerbations experienced by the participant in 12 months prior to sputum sampling, sputum neutrophils per gram, age and sex were also independent significant predictors of FEV1, whereas DNase treatment was not. Our analysis suggested that MPO, NE and dsDNA were less-effective predictors of FEV1 (adjusted r2=0.6 versus 0.64 when modelled with sputum NETs) and none of those parameters was shown to be significant independent predictors of FEV1, demonstrating the potential power of our NET assay if deployed in future studies and suggesting that NETosis is a feature of CF even in those with milder disease, potentially offering additional insights into CF pathophysiology.

Co-culture with NETing neutrophils causes IL-8 release from monocyte-derived macrophages that is blocked by DNase

EM images of MDM cultured with NETing neutrophils show that the interaction of NETs and macrophages leads to changes in macrophage morphology (figure 4a–d). Consistent with previous data, co-culture of NETing neutrophils with MDM led to IL-8 release from both HC and CF macrophages (figure 4e). Interestingly CF NETs trended to inducing higher levels of IL-8 release than HC NETs from control macrophages, but this did not reach statistical significance (figure 4f). The effects of both CF NETs and HC NETs were reduced by the addition of DNase to cultures suggesting that dissolution of the NET structure is associated with a decrease in their pro-inflammatory potential (figure 4e,f).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that NETs are present at higher levels in CF and associated with other biomarkers of airway inflammation. Interestingly, NETs are present in the sputum of people with CF regardless of disease severity as measured by FEV1. Furthermore, NETs are lower in people taking DNase therapy as might be predicted. Other treatments such as azithromycin have no impact on NETs in CF, contrary to the effect reported in non-CF bronchiectasis [6]. Our study pre-dates the widespread adoption of CFTR modulators and the influence of these compounds on NETosis is presently unknown. However, our data provide a benchmark for future studies of NETosis in CF, demonstrating measurement of NETs with a disease relevant assay and showing the relationship of NETs with inflammation and its propagation in CF. Furthermore, our data suggest that DNase has a sound rationale for use as not only a mucolytic but also an anti-inflammatory treatment in those with CF who cannot take a CFTR modulator, and as an adjunct anti-inflammatory in those that can. Indeed, the CF storm study underway in the United Kingdom at present will address the whether the stopping of DNase therapy is detrimental to patients taking modulators, albeit by looking at clinical outcomes rather than impact on inflammation [2].

Neutrophil-predominant inflammation is a well-described feature of CF, and previous studies have focussed on the measurement of neutrophil transcriptional profile [16], neutrophil proteins [12] and cellular markers of neutrophil activation [17, 18]. The disposal of neutrophils on arrival to the airway is less well understood, but this could be crucial to the propagation and continuation of inflammation. We have previously demonstrated that neutrophil apoptosis is delayed in CF, and this may make CF neutrophils more likely to undergo NETosis [3]. Our present data suggest that the CF airway contains high numbers of abnormal neutrophils and large amounts of DNA and NET-associated proteins such as calprotectin. We therefore used this as a basis for our NETs assay and demonstrate high levels of NET complexes in the CF airway that correlate with several clinical and inflammatory parameters, confirming that NETs are a major presence in the CF airway.

NETs have previously been described in the CF airway, but our study represents a prospectively collected dataset, allowing the interpretation of contemporaneous clinical and biological data. Interestingly we have found that NETs are present in CF patients in levels that do not map to a particular bacterial organism. Furthermore, the abundance of NETs in sputum was a significant, independent predictor of FEV1 using multivariate analysis, suggesting that they are associated with severity of lung disease. These data are consistent with a previous study in children demonstrating that sputum elastase levels (neutrophil elastase is a major constituent of NETs [11, 19]) are associated with lung function decline in CF [20].

Our observation that patient treatment with DNase was associated with lower levels of sputum NETs merits further discussion. DNase treatment was also associated with lower levels of airway inflammation measured by IL-8, and crucially IL-8 is not released from neutrophils by NETosis [19]. DNase has previously been shown to reduce IL-8 levels in the CF airway in children [21], and we have demonstrated that NETs can induce IL-8 production from macrophages [3]. Furthermore, several studies in COVID-19 have now associated increased levels of NETs with increased IL-8 levels. It therefore seems likely that NETs could be perpetuating inflammation by causing the release of IL-8 (a major neutrophil chemoattractant) and driving further neutrophil recruitment. This would be consistent with the classic vicious cycle of inflammation described in CF and suggests an additional role for DNase therapy as an anti-inflammatory agent discrete from its role as a mucoactive agent.

To interrogate the interaction of CF and non-CF neutrophils with CF and non-CF macrophages, we performed primary cell co-culture experiments. Electron microscopy intimates a direct interaction of NETs with macrophages that is associated with a morphological change in macrophages. The interaction of NETs with monocytes has been demonstrated in vascular disease [22], suggesting that the NET–monocyte interface may be a potential therapeutic target with widespread potential given the presence of NETs in a wide range of illnesses from inflammatory arthritis to lung cancer. The effects of DNase on abrogating the pro-inflammatory effects of NETs on macrophages underlines its role as a potential disease modifying therapy in CF and other diseases with a high airway load of NETs.

The effect of DNase in non-CF bronchiectasis (NCFB) has been studied previously but was associated with an increased risk of exacerbation and lung function loss [23]. Importantly NCFB represents a heterogeneous group of patients and the potential for specific endotypes of NCFB to respond to the anti-inflammatory effects of DNase should be considered. Furthermore, the presence of NETs in COPD and their association with disease severity and polymicrobial infection [7] may highlight a further group of patients that may benefit from an “anti-NET” therapy such as DNase. Data recently published by Chalmers et al. [24] have shown that inhibition of dipeptidyl peptidase 1 (DPP-1) with an orally active drug improved clinical outcomes in NCF. DPP-1 is a key activator of serine proteases, including neutrophil elastase, which is not only a component of NETs, but also implicated in NET formation [25]. This suggests that targeting NET formation and the consequences of such in NCFB may be possible, further underlining the need to reassess a potential role of DNase therapy in stratified groups of people with NCFB.

A potential weakness of this study is that it does not address the impact of CFTR modulator therapy on NET levels. The study collection period pre-dated the approval and widespread access to highly effective CFTR modulator therapy. Three of our study group were taking ivacaftor therapy for approved mutation classes and one was taking lumacaftor/ivacaftor on compassionate access. These individuals did not have significantly lower levels of NETs than those (the majority) not taking a modulator. Highly effective CFTR modulators have now been shown to have a significant impact on inflammation [26–28], although the effect on NETs is not yet known. The absence of a reduction in NETs in our four subjects taking modulators does not allow us to draw any definitive conclusions, and as such, further studies of NETs in the CF population will be required. The inclusion of other patient groups such as those unresponsive to DNase therapy would allow the relationship between NET levels and CF therapies to be investigated but this was not possible in this study. This study also highlights the usefulness of less-expensive CF therapies such as DNase, particularly when we consider that CFTR modulator drugs are only available to the minority of people with CF living in Europe, North America, Israel, Australia and New Zealand [29].

In summary, we demonstrate that sputum NETs are a potentially useful biomarker in CF that are associated with increased disease severity and other markers of airway inflammation such as IL-8. NETs are effective in promoting IL-8 release from macrophages and this can be blocked by the addition of DNase, an established CF therapeutic, underlined by the finding that patients taking DNase consequently have lower levels of sputum NETs. Taken together these data suggest that targeting NET–macrophage interactions may be a viable anti-inflammatory strategy in CF and other diseases characterised by excessive NET accumulation.
