Analyzing qPCR data: Better practices to facilitate rigor and reproducibility
Authors: Thomas H Hampton, Lily Taub, Kiyoshi Ferreria-Fukutani, Bruce A Stanton, Todd A MacKenzie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractQuantitative PCR (qPCR) remains a widely used, cost-effective method for RNA quantitation, yet many published studies inadequately comply with MIQE (Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments) and FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reproducible) data principles. Widespread reliance on the 2−ΔΔCT method often overlooks critical factors such as amplification efficiency variability and reference gene stability. Furthermore, the absence of raw data and analysis code limits the community's ability to evaluate potential biases and reproduce findings. Here, we primarily aim to encourage researchers to share raw qPCR fluorescence data along with detailed analysis scripts that start from raw input and produce final figures and statistical tests. Using our recently published dataset, we model the complete qPCR analytical workflow—from raw fluorescence curves through to differential expression—highlighting key decision points that can influence results. We provide fully documented R scripts illustrating how ANCOVA (Analysis of Covariance), a flexible multivariable linear modeling approach, generally offers greater statistical power and robustness compared to 2−ΔΔCT. Additionally, simulations support ANCOVA's applicability across diverse experimental conditions. We also demonstrate how general-purpose data repositories (e.g., figshare) and code repositories (e.g., GitHub) facilitate adherence to FAIR principles and promote transparency in qPCR research. Finally, we offer graphical examples that transparently depict both target and reference gene behavior within the same figure, enhancing interpretability. This work establishes practical resources and conceptual foundations to improve rigor, reproducibility, and openness in qPCR data analysis.Image 1Keywords: qPCR, Delta-delta CT, Rigor, Reproducibility, FAIR, MIQE, RDML

Introduction

Science has been experiencing what has been termed a reproducibility crisis for many years [1], and the MIQE standard [2] directly responds to the problem of reproducibility in the context of qPCR by enumerating over 75 distinct pieces of metadata that should accompany a published qPCR experiment. Related works propose a sophisticated, special-purpose XML data representation for raw qPCR data [3], tools to create compliant data [4] and a special purpose qPCR data repository in which qPCR data could be exchanged http://www.rdmldb.org. Neither MIQE itself [5] nor specialized tools for data sharing have gained wide acceptance, and a recent recommendation by Untergasser et al. [6] suggests simpler systems for sharing qPCR data.

The main goal of this manuscript is to identify simple practices that facilitate rigor and reproducibility in qPCR data analysis. We apply established data management concepts from biomedical research [7] including sharing raw data [8], sharing analytical code [9] and showcase a statistical approach (ANCOVA) that is common in epidemiology but has also been previously used for qPCR [10]. Our simulations demonstrate that ANCOVA, a kind of multivariable linear model, performs better than 2−ΔΔCT 11, the most commonly used qPCR data analysis approach according to a recent survey by Bustin et al. [5] We reproduced Bustin's result below to familiarize ourselves with the most frequently cited qPCR data analysis approaches and the extent to which raw data and code are being shared. Although we are not the first to suggest using ANCOVA models for qPCR analysis [[10], [11], [12]] we believe we are the first to demonstrate that incorporating a reference gene and a gene of interest CT values as separate covariates in a single model, improves power, particularly when responses of the gene of interest and reference gene are uncorrelated. In addition, we believe we are the first to point out that qPCR ANCOVA models with separate terms for the reference gene and gene of interest produce accurate P-values in tests of differential gene expression, even when amplification efficiencies differ between the gene of interest and the reference gene, addressing one of the most frequently cited reproducibility problems associated with 2−ΔΔCT.

Many laboratories currently use software such as GraphPad Prism or Excel for qPCR data analysis. While these tools are valuable for many applications, script-based approaches like R offer advantages for reproducibility because they create a shareable record of all analysis steps and explicitly link analyses to source data. Adopting script-based workflows represents a transition for laboratories accustomed to graphical interfaces [13]. Whether this transition is worthwhile depends on each laboratory's specific needs, resources, and priorities. To facilitate adoption for those who choose to pursue these approaches, we have designed our worked example to clearly communicate key concepts, including how fluorescence curves establish cycle of quantification, amplification efficiency estimation, normalization of gene of interest response to reference genes, and statistical assessment of treatment effects. We provide complete, annotated R code as a working template for performing all these steps and generating publication-quality graphs. The materials are designed to be accessible to those new to R and qPCR, while readers already familiar with these concepts can focus on sections most relevant to their needs.

We used a recently published data set from cystic fibrosis research [14] to demonstrate an ANCOVA-based analytical approach in R, visualize within-group variability, assess normality and reference gene stability, detect amplification efficiency differences, and demonstrate how to share raw data and code. Not surprisingly, analyzing the demonstration data using ANCOVA yielded similar results to analyzing the same data using 2−ΔΔCT. People use 2−ΔΔCT because it generally works. Nonetheless, simulations using data unrelated to the cystic fibrosis set revealed ANCOVA's advantages over 2−ΔΔCT in the general case. These simulations included right skewed data (typical of many biological phenomena), left skewed data (which could result if bell shaped data is log-transformed) and normally distributed data (which we expect cycle threshold data to approximate [15]). Simulations showed that when data is left or right skewed, ANCOVA is less biased than 2−ΔΔCT and that ANCOVA is at least as powerful as 2−ΔΔCT in every case. This makes ANCOVA a logical choice for qPCR data analysis, especially given that ANCOVA delivers accurate P-values even when genes of interest and reference genes amplify at different efficiencies, a known deficiency of 2−ΔΔCT 17. Best practices include measuring amplification efficiency, following the example of Pfaffl [16], but amplification efficiency is rarely reported in qPCR papers [5].

We acknowledge several limitations in our analysis. As the title suggests, we are not attempting to cast our recommendations as best practices, but better practices that foster greater reproducibility. For example, ANCOVA models rely on primer specificity. Primer cross-reactivity is a measurement validity issue that cannot be corrected by normalization approaches. Achieving primer specificity is particularly problematic for miRNA families [17] because miRNAs are short, but specificity is more difficult to achieve for members of gene families in general [18]. The ANCOVA models presented here do not detect differences in efficiency nor do they use amplification efficiency information, therefore, if exact fold changes are required, additional steps beyond those presented here would be required. In addition, although ANCOVA is generally robust to distributions that are not perfectly normal, ANCOVA itself does not detect distributional problems such as lack of normality or heteroskedasticity, and we therefore advise visual exploration of CT values such as those we provide in our worked example to identify problems in data distribution that could violate ANCOVA's distributional assumptions. In the same vein, ANCOVA itself does not detect very low expression genes, but graphing individual CT values, as we recommend, enables readers to assess whether a product is very low expression and may be more problematic to quantify [19]. Finally, although mathematics and simulations clearly demonstrate ANCOVA's theoretical advantages compared to 2−ΔΔCT under controlled conditions where we specify the true underlying model, practical improvements achieved by ANCOVA will depend on experimental designs, effect sizes, and technical details.

qPCR plays a crucial role in molecular biology research and clinical practice. It is fundamental for quantifying nucleic acids, enabling researchers to analyze gene expression, detect pathogens, and assess genetic variations [5]. Although digital PCR offers greater sensitivity than qPCR [20,21], and next-generation sequencing measures far more targets simultaneously, qPCR is fast, relatively inexpensive, and accurate when analyzed correctly [22].

Mathematical principles and simulations demonstrate that ANCOVA is more powerful than 2−ΔΔCT in most cases. Amplification efficiencies can be estimated from raw qPCR data. Tools exist to establish reference gene stability [23] and general-purpose repositories can make qPCR data and analyses publicly available. Collectively, taking advantage of these resources could improve qPCR reproducibility consistent with emerging standards in the qPCR community [5,6] and science as a whole [24].

Overview of how raw qPCR data is generated and normalized

qPCR uses short, sequence-specific primers and repeated heating/cooling cycles to roughly double the target DNA each round. Because of this exponential amplification, even one DNA molecule (or cDNA made from mRNA) can be boosted until it is visible with fluorescent dyes like SYBR Green or TaqMan probes. Beyond simply detecting a sequence's presence, qPCR can also estimate how much DNA was in the original sample either as an absolute quantity or relative to a reference.

The fundamental principle underlying qPCR is that when more DNA is present in a sample, it takes fewer cycles of amplification for the fluorescent signal generated by the DNA to reach a chosen threshold value. When amplification efficiency is 100 %, each round of amplification doubles the amount of DNA present, so a difference of one cycle between two samples might suggest a two-fold difference between them. However, it is not unusual for qPCR reactions to be less than 100 % efficient, so best practices include measuring amplification efficiency [25] and adjusting relative quantification for possible differences in efficiency, e.g., using the calculations recommended by Pfaffl [16]. Bustin's 2024 literature survey [5] showed that the 2−ΔΔCT method for qPCR data analysis, which assumes 100 % efficiency, remains popular and showed that very few studies follow MIQE guidelines, creating possible reproducibility problems.

Tracking fluorescence across amplification cycles

Our demonstration uses qPCR data from a recent study in which primary human airway epithelial cells from five cystic fibrosis (CF) donors were exposed to ETI (Elexacaftor, Tezacaftor, and Ivacaftor, a drug combination that vastly improves lung function in people with cystic fibrosis [26,27]) or vehicle (DMSO) for 48 h [14]. Cycle thresholds (Ct) were calculated for several target genes (DEFB1, MMP10, MMP12, IL1B, TNF) and several reference genes (HSP90AB, GAPDH, HPRT1, GUSB, and UBC). ANCOVA, a kind of multivariable linear model (MLM), was used to analyze treatment response instead of 2−ΔΔCT 11.

Because each round of amplification (cycle) copies a single strand of DNA to make double stranded DNA, approximately doubling the amount of DNA present, one might expect the fluorescent signal from such a system to rise exponentially: plotting the log of fluorescence as a function of cycle should form a straight line. While this is true for a small section of typical amplification curves, fluorescence curves are sigmoidal, as shown in Fig. 1A, which shows raw data from our recent study [14]. Fluorescence curves are sigmoidal rather than log linear because the first few cycles of amplification produce fluorescent signals that are indistinguishable from background, and the tail end of the curve is flattened because materials required to make new DNA have been partly consumed. Throughout the rest of the manuscript, the term background refers to the baseline fluorescence signal measured in the assay that originates from non-specific sources, such as instrument noise, autofluorescence of reagents, or inherent fluorescence of the sample matrix. Background-corrected fluorescence (Delta Rn) is calculated by subtracting background fluorescence from the total fluorescence signal measured during the assay. This correction helps isolate the true signal generated by the specific detection event (e.g., target amplification in qPCR), improving accuracy and comparability between samples. It is important to note that very low expression genes are more difficult to quantify accurately [19].

Technical replicates

A typical qPCR setup involves measuring the same sample in three adjacent wells on a 96 well plate. Each well contains all the ingredients necessary to amplify a specific sequence and detect a fluorescent signal for a specific gene. Fig. 1A shows six curves tracking the amplification of GAPDH, a reference gene, in control samples from two human donors measured in triplicate. Reassuringly, fluorescence curves from replicate measurements of donor KK32G (wells C1, C2, C3) lie almost on top of each other, a bit to the left of the three replicates of a sample from donor KK29H (wells D1, D2, D3). In this case, one would predict that shifts in curve positions are due to DNA concentration differences between donors, because samples from both donors were collected under identical conditions. However, pipetting errors are one of many sources of potential differences between samples. Other problems include variability in the quality of the DNA one is attempting to amplify, sample mixing, the presence of contaminants, differences in sample handling and storage, reagent variability, and instrumental fluctuations.

Reference genes can account for variability unrelated to experimental design

Most approaches to qPCR include measuring one or more genes of interest and at least one gene that is predicted to be stable across treatment conditions, a reference. Subtracting reference gene values from the values for the gene of interest should leave only variability that is related to experimental conditions (i.e., control versus experimental treatment). GAPDH is a popular reference gene in qPCR experiments because its expression is often unresponsive to typical treatments [28]. However, the stability of GAPDH or any other candidate reference gene should be validated in every new experimental design before it is used as a reference [23] because GAPDH and other commonly used reference genes sometimes respond to experimental conditions [29]. For example, exposure to serum may induce β-actin and GAPDH by up to nine- and three-fold, respectively [30], hypoxia can induce GAPDH mRNA expression by 21.2–75.1 % [31] and GAPDH can be increased by roughly two-fold in response to epidermal growth factor and thyroid stimulating hormone [32]. Although the fact that others have published using a specific gene in a similar experimental context is encouraging, only studies that provide direct evidence of stable reference gene response strongly endorse using a reference gene in a specific context. High-throughput gene expression data are more frequently shared publicly and can help identify candidate reference genes [33].

Fig. 1B shows averages of technical replicate measurements of GAPDH in cells from 5 different donors exposed to either control conditions (DMSO, red) or a drug (ETI, blue). GAPDH curves in Fig. 1B lie close to one another and do not segregate by treatment, suggesting that GAPDH is stably expressed and could be used as a reference gene in this experiment.

Using qPCR to answer scientific questions

qPCR's popularity stems from its ability to address specific scientific questions. For example, we used qPCR to validate an RNA-seq result showing that ETI exposure decreases the expression of metalloprotease genes including MMP10 [14]. Fig. 1C provides qualitative validation of our RNA-seq result because ETI curves in any given donor (blue), lie to the right of control DMSO curves (red).

There are many ways to make quantitative statements about fluorescence curve data [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38]] but a simple example illustrates the general principle as follows. MMP10 fluorescence curves in Fig. 1C are well-separated when they achieve about 2 units of fluorescence (horizontal dashed line, Fig. 1C). Therefore, the number of cycles required for a sample to achieve 2 units is a reasonable objective measure of the abundance of MMP10. Pairing measurements from the same donor, as shown in Fig. 1D, ensures compatibility with the statistical independence assumption of a t-test and shows that ETI treatment significantly increases the number of cycles required to reach 2 fluorescence units (P = 0.0046, 1-sample t-test).

Computational approaches to compare qPCR fluorescence curves remain an area of active research. Using cycle thresholds (CT, Ct, or Cycle of Quantitation, Cq) is one approach [34] and CT values of the same sample calculated by the software provided by different qPCR equipment manufacturers often yield substantially different results [39]. Note that Cq is preferred by members of the qPCR community who are striving to standardize terminology [2,5]. Below we will use CT because our PCR thermocycler uses that terminology, and because the abbreviation CT remains in broad circulation.

Fig. 1D demonstrates that simply counting the number of cycles required to reach a manually selected threshold yields adequate results without using software to identify CT values or normalizing the data to a reference gene. In practice, reference genes are almost always used to account for pipetting errors and other random errors in qPCR. The 2−ΔΔCT method published by Livak and Schmittgen in 2001 [40] is widely accepted and works well [41]. However, reference genes are most effective when A) target and reference measurements are equally affected by variability in sample quality and B) only the gene of interest is affected by treatment conditions. For the data shown in Fig. 1, these constraints seem to hold. The gene of interest MMP10 is much more affected by ETI (Fig. 1C) than the reference GAPDH is (Fig. 1B).

2−ΔΔCT assumes that each round of amplification doubles the amount of material in both the reference gene and gene of interest from the last round. When this is not the case, 2−ΔΔCT will exaggerate or underestimate fold changes in gene expression [25] and efficiency differences as little as 3 % can alter calculated expression ratios by 200 % after 30 cycles [42]. Consequently, the 2009 guidelines on the minimum information required to publish real-time PCR experiments (MIQE) [2] lists amplification efficiency calibration curves as an essential piece of information. The seminal Pfaffl report [16] published in 2001 describes one way to incorporate amplification efficiency estimates into qPCR data analysis and recommends estimating amplification efficiency from the slope of the linear portion of the fluorescence curve. For our demonstrations data set, plotting log base 2 fluorescence as a function of cycle (Fig. 2A) revealed that the linear portion varies somewhat between wells, that the slopes of these lines (Fig. 2B) varied by donor (x-axis) and that efficiency estimates were generally less than 2 (i.e., they had a slope of less than 1 on a log base 2 scale). Moreover, the efficiency of the reference gene and the gene of interest was often different (e.g., donors KK18G and KK22F).

The slope of the linear portion of the fluorescence curve [16] is one of many ways to estimate efficiency directly from fluorescence curves [43] including nonlinear regression models [35]. Nonetheless, current reviews generally recommend running sample dilution curves to establish efficiency estimates [2,5,[44], [45], [46]], even though sample dilution curves also have technical limitations [25,39]. Sample dilution curves perhaps represent a cost-effective compromise between using expensive digital droplet PCR to estimate amplification efficiency [5] and the technically challenging task of inferring amplification efficiency from fluorescence curves [37,47].

qPCR remains popular but following data analysis best practices is not

Papers mentioning qPCR in their methods section have grown steadily throughout the 21st century (Fig. 3A) as have citations of the original 2−ΔΔCT paper [40] but citations of the highly recommended Pfaffl method that incorporates amplification efficiency into its calculations [16] have not (Fig. 3B). This suggests that contemporary laboratories continue to use 2−ΔΔCT as opposed to methods that require measuring amplification efficiency. Our survey of 20 qPCR publications appearing in late 2024 is consistent with the belief that relatively few laboratories assess amplification efficiency directly or follow other best-practices advice. Our sample (Table 1) replicated findings reported by Bustin et al. [5] and revealed that about 75 % of published qPCR results use the 2−ΔΔCT method and fewer than 5 % explicitly take amplification efficiency into account. None of the authors in our sample mentioned amplification efficiency or how they measured it. None of the publications surveyed provided raw data or metadata consistent with recent standards [6]. Although most authors in our sample identified the reference gene they used, typically GAPDH or ACTB, none explained how they selected their reference or provided data or figures that would enable readers to assess reference gene stability. Leaving out these details falls short of the current level of rigor and reproducibility recommended by the NIH.

Estimating amplification efficiency directly from fluorescence curves

Although performing a dilution series to estimate amplification efficiency is widely recommended [44,45], tools like LinRegPCR [37] can infer efficiency and differential expression directly from raw fluorescence curves. LinRegPCR requires RDML-formatted input [4]. To be compatible with the rest of our analysis, we used the R packages readxl [50] and qpcR [51] to analyze fluorescence curves exported in spreadsheet format by QuantStudio Design and Analysis Software v1.5.2. Specifically, qpcR [51] functions pcrfit () and efficiency () were used to extract amplification efficiency from each curve using the spline method.

In this case, efficiencies inferred from our example data (Fig. 4) identified a 2.6 % relative efficiency difference between the reference and target genes consistent with the assumptions of the 2−ΔΔCT method [52], but also suggested low amplification efficiency. GAPDH mean efficiency was 1.53 (Fig. 4A) and MMP10 mean efficiency was 1.57 (Fig. 4B). On one hand, efficiencies for GAPDH (SD = 0.01, Fig. 4A and C) and MMP10 (SD = 0.02, Fig. 4B and D) were highly consistent, and Fig. 4D shows that MMP10 values from the same donor (color) have similar efficiency values. On the other hand, MMP10 efficiency values associated with ETI treatment (triangles) are slightly shifted to the right (greater efficiency) compared to the same donor treated with DMSO (circles) in Fig. 4D. Increased amplification efficiency of MMP10 in the presence of ETI would bias results toward the conclusion that ETI increases the abundance of MMP10. This bias was not sufficiently large to reverse the finding that ETI reduces the abundance of MMP10, as reported in our paper [14], and validated by the larger CT values for MMP10 in response to ETI (Fig. 5A) and the ANCOVA analysis results shown in Table 3.

Assuring adequate reference gene stability

Reference genes commonly respond to treatment conditions and should therefore be validated in each experimental condition [23,29,55,56] and the behavior of reference genes should be shared. Researchers may be reluctant to share reference gene behavior when reference gene responses suggest technical problems that could detract from a result they would like to report. This is especially true when the researcher hopes to demonstrate a specific response to treatment by a gene of interest and the reference gene happens to respond in the opposite direction, amplifying a desired effect. Graphical approaches that enable readers to establish that the magnitude of treatment responses in the gene of interest are much larger than responses of the reference gene directly address this problem, as shown in Fig. 5A. Mean GAPDH CT values (black circles) are about the same in control (DMSO) and treated (ETI), forming a line parallel to the x-axis. On the other hand, mean values of MMP10 (black triangles) rise by almost one CT in ETI compared to DMSO showing that ETI treatment reduces MMP10 abundance. Presenting a figure like Fig. 5A quickly communicates that the gene of interest responds to treatment but not the reference gene.

If Fig. 5A had instead revealed treatment responses in the reference genes, that would have signaled the need to methodically identify a more stable reference gene. There are several tools available for this purpose that work in similar ways [57]. BestKeeper [55], geNorm [58], and NormFinder [59] select the most stable reference genes from a set of candidates and can create an average that may be more accurate than a single reference gene [58]. NormFinder [59] can use gene expression data from microarrays or RNA-seq to identify candidate reference genes as input. All three tools use correlation between different reference genes to establish stability and give candidate references with divergent responses low stability scores [55,58,59]. In fact, both reference genes and genes of interest should be similarly affected by systematic sample-to-sample differences other than treatment effects, and their CT values should therefore be correlated. As shown in Fig. 5B, MMP10 and GAPDH CT values were highly correlated (Pearson r = 0.93, r squared = 0.87, p = 9.37e-05). Assuming GAPDH is a stable reference (i.e., GAPDH expression is not affected by ETI), the r squared value of 0.87 in this case means that about 87 % of the variability in CT can be attributed to factors other than exposure to ETI, for example, differences between donors.

In summary, graphical representations that show responses of both reference gene and gene of interest can reveal cases where a chosen reference gene itself responds to treatment. In such cases, it is recommended to run a reference gene panel [29] and use software tools to identify better references [57]. Averaging multiple stable reference genes may improve results [58].

ANCOVA models of qPCR compared to 2−ΔΔCT

In this section, we compare the commonly used 2−ΔΔCT approach to a multivariable linear model like ANCOVA for qPCR data analysis. For our demonstration data set, both approaches yielded comparable conclusions but the mathematical properties of ANCOVA may make it superior to 2−ΔΔCT in general. The mathematical basis for using ANCOVA in this context of qPCR can be found in the Appendix.

2−ΔΔCT uses two levels of control, a control for the treatment, e.g. treatment vs control, and a control for the quality of the sample, namely the reference gene. The practice of raising 2 to the difference of cycle thresholds (CT) reflects that during each round of amplification in qPCR, the amount of material should roughly double. Raw CT values are therefore naturally on a log base 2 scale and suitably distributed for tests that assume normal distribution. 2−ΔΔCT calculates the average difference between the target gene CT and the reference gene CT in the experimental groups and control groups. Next, the difference in these averages is typically calculated and compared using a t-test or paired t-test. Alternatively, the distribution of the gene of interest and reference gene differences can be compared between the exposed and control samples using a rank-based approach. Users may choose to “back-transform” the average difference of the differences by raising 2 to the difference in CT. In that case, because CT is inversely related to the amount of starting material, an intuitively meaningful back-transformation incorporates a minus sign in the exponent: 2−ΔΔCT. However, remaining on the log scale throughout can avoid possible statistical problems involved with back transformation [15].

The reasoning underlying 2−ΔΔCT is difference-in-differences, a ubiquitous study design in economics, social sciences and epidemiology [60]. For example, in economics it is common to evaluate the effect of a policy, such as the introduction of a new therapy into Medicare or Medicaid, by comparing results between states that had the policy change and states that did not have the policy change, before and after the introduction of the policy. An example of difference-in-differences in clinical research is the randomization of patients into two groups, a treatment group and control group, with measurements of the dependent variable at baseline and post-baseline.

In most difference-in-difference designs the dependent variable is the same, for instance, one might use mortality rate as the dependent variable in different states, before and after a policy change. 2−ΔΔCT does not use the same dependent variable: CT for the target gene and reference gene are independent. Consequently, these two measures may have little or no correlation, and may have vastly different variation. Mathematically, 2−ΔΔCT assumes that sample quality and other factors affect the value of the target gene and the reference gene by the same amount. However, it is possible, and even likely, that sample quality and other factors such as primer design, amplification efficiency and cycling conditions affect reference and target genes in different amounts. For instance, it may be that if sample quality and/or primer design and cycling conditions are impacting the reference gene by x, they impact the target gene by the amount k∗x, where k is some number. 2−ΔΔCT assumes that k = 1. A value of zero would indicate the target gene has no ability to inform on the quality of the sample whereas a statistically significant negative value would be hard to interpret biologically and would suggest an error. One advantage of multivariable linear models (e.g., ANCOVA) is that they control for variation due to sample quality and cycling conditions to the extent that the reference gene reflects that variability. If the reference gene is not capturing sample quality and cycling conditions, a multivariable linear model will essentially ignore it. The ability of the reference gene to capture variation in sample quality and cycling conditions can also be assessed using the related method of Pearson or Spearman (rank based) correlations applied to the reference and target genes as presented in Fig. 5B and D. If there is no correlation between the target and reference genes, the 2−ΔΔCT method is dubious, and subtracting the reference gene CT from the target gene CT actually reduces the power of the study. Coefficients different from k = 1 can also account for amplification efficiency differences between target and reference genes, making ANCOVA significantly more robust than 2−ΔΔCT when amplification efficiency differences are an issue.

In summary, ANCOVA models of qPCR use a reference to control for differences in sample quality. However, instead of simply subtracting reference values from the gene of interest values, ANCOVA uses regression to establish the level of correction to apply. Applying ANCOVA to qPCR data requires fewer steps than using 2−ΔΔCT to normalize results and perform a statistical test: ANCOVA uses a reference to account for sample quality variability and assesses the significance in one step.

ANCOVA handles reaction efficiency differences between target and reference gene

The ANCOVA multivariable linear model offers another advantage over the 2−ΔΔCT approach. Unlike 2−ΔΔCT it is invariant to any difference in reaction efficiency between the target and reference genes. Let eT (a number between 0 and 1, i.e. 0 %–100 %) be the reaction efficiency of the target gene. In general, we should be using the logarithm with base 1+eT. Using the change of base property of logarithms, logbc=logac∗logba, the cycle threshold calculation is off by a factor log21+eT. Similarly, if eR is the reaction efficiency of the reference gene, its cycle threshold will be off by the factor log21+eR. Fortunately, it is a property of multivariable linear models that the P-values are invariant to scale changes. That is, any difference in reaction efficiency (i.e., cycling conditions) between the target and reference gene will not change the P-values from the multivariable linear model we propose.

Rules of thumb for the sample size necessary to conduct a linear regression, e.g., ANCOVA, range from at least two [61] to up to eight observations [62] for every parameter estimated in the experiment. If this ratio of sample size to parameters estimated is too small then the P-values reported are less reliable. This is because the P-values from linear regression, like the P-values from two sample t-tests, assume that the underlying distribution is Gaussian (i.e., bell shaped). The P-values reported may be biased toward accepting or rejecting the null hypothesis when the underlying distribution is not Gaussian. Ideally, one should reject the null hypothesis about 5 % of the time when the null hypothesis is true, e.g., when there is no true difference between experimental groups. Rejecting the null hypothesis less than 5 % of the time when there is no true difference suggests that a test may be too conservative. Tests that are too conservative reduce power and inflate Type II errors. The flip side, rejecting the null hypothesis more than 5 % of the time when there is no true difference suggests that a test is too liberal and inflates Type I errors. In our simulation, we estimated treatment, reference gene response, and the intercept, and a variance parameter, amounting to a total of four degrees of freedom (parameters to estimate). Our simulation models a typical situation in bench science where there are barely enough samples (n = 10) to meet the minimum recommended ratio for estimating parameters because ten is greater than two times four.

We assessed: (i) any bias in the rejection rate of the MLM in comparison to 2−ΔΔCT, as well as ΔCT (which does not use a reference gene), and (ii) the power of MLM in comparison to 2−ΔΔCT and ΔCT. We evaluated the performance of the MLM when the underlying distribution is right skewed and left skewed. We considered the scenario when there are a total of 10 observations (e.g. 10 donors) in which 5 are treated and 5 are controls. To compare how MLM and 2−ΔΔCT perform in terms of the ability of the reference gene to capture sample quality, we evaluated performance with respect to the correlation of the target and reference gene; specifically, we considered a very strong Pearson correlation of 0.9, a moderate correlation of 0.5 and zero correlation. The code used in the simulation is included in GitHub (https://github.com/DartCF/qPCR-data-analysis-review).

As shown in Table 5, the empirical type I error rate of the MLM (ANCOVA) approach is close to the nominal value (5 %) when the distribution is not Gaussian (right skewed, left skewed) for each of the three correlation parameters (0, 0.5 and 0.9). This indicates that the multivariable approach is a statistically valid approach (e.g. little or no bias in Type I error). MLM showed slight bias in the conservative direction, that is, empirical Type I error rate was a bit less than 5 %. 2−ΔΔCT was more conservative in every scenario, in other words, less powerful than MLM.

Table 5 illustrates the ability of the MLM to control for the reference gene to the extent that it is relevant. When there is very little shared variation in sample quality or cycling conditions between the target and reference genes, their correlation is close to zero, reducing the power of 2−ΔΔCT in comparison to the best approach in that case, ΔCT (not using a reference gene at all). However, MLM has almost the power of ΔCT, whether the distribution is Gaussian, right skewed or left skewed. When there is a high correlation (e.g., 0.9), 2−ΔΔCT outperforms ΔCT (100 % vs 78.8 % for Gaussian) but MLM is just as good (100 %). When there is moderate correlation of the target and reference gene, MLM outperforms both 2−ΔΔCT and ΔCT.

Table 6 shows the results of simulations when the treatment has an effect on the reference gene equal to one standard deviation. This is a big problem. In this setting, only ΔCT (ignoring the reference gene) is a valid approach. 2−ΔΔCT is invalid when the exposure affects the reference gene, because it will identify an effect on the target gene in the opposite direction. For instance, if the correlation of the target and reference gene is high, the 2−ΔΔCT falsely rejects the null hypothesis with a frequency of 93 %. MLM is also invalid in this setting unless there is zero correlation between target and reference gene.

We created an R tutorial that demonstrates the rigor and reproducibility efforts we suggest be incorporated into qPCR data analysis. The tutorial explores reference gene stability, amplification efficiency and ANCOVA modeling. Specifically, we demonstrate how to measure candidate reference gene stability, visualize treatment effect on reference gene CTs, estimate and visualize amplification efficiency, how to weight CT by amplification efficiency, and how to format qPCR data for the ANCOVA modeling approach to treatment effect analysis.

Better FAIR practices for qPCR data analysis

At the highest conceptual level, qPCR data is just one of many kinds of data whose life cycle should be detailed in a data management plan [63] consistent with FAIR practices [24,49]. Ideally, all data and metadata should be collected according to standards developed specifically for the type of data in question and stored permanently to support research findings and for reuse by others [7]. High-throughput data is expensive to create and of great potential value, so many journals and many grants require researchers to make it available. For example, the National Library of Medicine supports the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [64] which currently contains downloadable data from over 7 million samples. Many of these data sets are annotated using the MIAME standard for microarray data [65] or compatible with the emerging MINSEQE standard for high throughput sequencing data [66]. Although GEO does contain about 1000 samples of type “Expression profiling by RT-PCR,” these data sets are limited to previously defined PCR arrays that simultaneously measure the expression of many genes. GEO does not contain typical qPCR data that measures the expression of a single gene.

The qPCR community is promoting FAIR practices relevant to low throughput qPCR data in many ways [2,[4], [5], [6]]. For example, the MIQE [2] standard is the qPCR community's version of the MIAME microarray standard [65]. MIQE defines roughly 75 attributes of qPCR experiments, ranging from experimental design, sample collection, nucleic acid isolation, reverse transcription, qPCR target information, validation, to the details of qPCR data analysis relevant to this review. However, not every attribute is relevant to every experiment, and the RDML format was developed to flexibly capture qPCR data [3]. The RDMLdb repository [4] enables qPCR researchers to store RDML files in a central, publicly accessible location, but fewer than 10 researchers have deposited RDML files in the RDMLdb repository in the last 10 years.

Popular low throughput data repositories might be a better solution for qPCR data [7], for example, figshare (https://figshare.com/) [67], Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/) and OSF (https://osf.io/). Because these systems minimally enforce metadata standards, it's very easy for researchers to upload their data. Perhaps sharing qPCR data in a general-purpose repository is enough. We have been reusing publicly available high throughput data for many years [29,68,69] and have built systems to facilitate its reuse [[70], [71], [72], [73]], but we think the main motivation for publicly accessible qPCR data is probably reproducibility rather than reuse. We therefore recommend storing raw data such as CT values or fluorescence in a general-purpose repository like figshare and storing code used to analyze data in a code repository like GitHub. For example, the original CT data used in the example can be found here:

https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Cycle_threshold_values_of_PCR_measurements_for_target_and_reference_genes_/25997890?file=46925539.

Major limitations

Both ANCOVA and 2−ΔΔCT assume reference gene stability. The ANCOVA model presented here offers greater statistical power and handles variability in amplification efficiency better than 2−ΔΔCT. However, neither the ANCOVA model presented here nor 2−ΔΔCT considers amplification efficiency directly. Consequently, our ANCOVA model estimates are in CT units that will not correspond to log base 2 fold change unless amplification efficiency is 100 %.

Discussion

qPCR remains a foundational technique in molecular biology, with about 5000 new citations every month (Fig. 3A). Most practitioners control for possible variability using a single, well-known reference gene such as GAPDH without explaining how this gene was chosen or providing evidence of its stability [5]. This practice could obscure true treatment effects [23], degrading reproducibility. Most researchers assess qPCR differential gene expression using the 2−ΔΔCT method, and therefore tacitly assume perfect amplification efficiency, potentially distorting findings [16], but it is impossible to know because researchers rarely share raw data [6]. Solutions to many reproducibility problems have been proposed and reviewed [45] but largely ignored [5]. Although surveying authors of recent papers using qPCR to ask why they continue to use the 2−ΔΔCT method is beyond the scope of this report, we are confident that this is the case because reviewers and journals accept 2−ΔΔCT results and because 2−ΔΔCT is simple. In the same way, following MIQE guidelines is more work than ignoring MIQE guidelines.

Concepts identified in this manuscript can be applied to digital PCR and more complex experimental designs such as mixed effects models. Although digital PCR can detect smaller amounts of target DNA [74,75] and is less susceptible to errors resulting from differences in amplification efficiency between target and reference genes [76,77], digital PCR often normalizes target gene expression to reference gene expression [78,79]. When reference genes are used, ANCOVA models should improve digital PCR power compared to using 2−ΔΔCT to analyze the data. Digital PCR counts should be log transformed (e.g., log base 2) because count data are typically right-skewed, prior to an ANCOVA analysis. Our recommendations about careful reference gene selection, sharing of raw data and code, and use of candid graphics that show reference gene responses apply equally to digital PCR and qPCR.

Power improvements achieved by ANCOVA compared to 2−ΔΔCT are greatest when reference gene responses are uncorrelated with responses in genes of interest. Low correlation indicates that the reference gene contains little useful information about the gene of interest and may occur when experimental variability due to pipetting errors, sample mixing, the presence of contaminants, differences in sample handling and storage, reagent variability, and instrumental fluctuations is minimal. ANCOVA recognizes low correlation by estimating a coefficient near zero, effectively ignoring the uninformative reference, whereas 2−ΔΔCT forces subtraction and adds noise that erodes power. Power matters most when one is hoping to detect small, but biologically meaningful changes in expression. ANCOVA is also particularly attractive when there are small differences in amplification efficiency between the reference gene and the gene of interest, and the primary scientific question is whether treatments are statistically different. ANCOVA provides accurate P-values regardless of efficiency differences, while 2−ΔΔCT may yield misleading statistical conclusions. Finally, ANCOVA naturally accommodates complex experimental designs (e.g., factorial designs, multiple treatment groups, continuous covariates) that are cumbersome to analyze using the 2−ΔΔCT approach, which was originally designed for simple two-group comparisons. For the growing number of researchers for whom R is a familiar statistical environment [80], making the switch from 2−ΔΔCT to ANCOVA would simplify analysis because one line of R can replace the multiple calculations required to calculate two differences in 2−ΔΔCT and perform a statistical test on the result. Conversely, for those with no background in R, overcoming the challenge of learning R and implementing ANCOVA may not be worth it in the short term, especially when reference and target genes are highly correlated and when effect sizes are large relative to measurement error.

Better qPCR graphics, statistics and data management could improve reproducibility. First, candid graphics could reveal problems with reference gene and amplification efficiency. For example, Fig. 5A communicates that GAPDH responded to ETI much less than MMP10, suggesting that reference gene instability is probably not a major issue in this case. Had GAPDH responded to ETI, tools like geNorm [58], NormFinder [59], and BestKeeper [55] could identify better reference genes. Fig. 4 communicates that amplification efficiency was mostly stable, but always less than 2: a CT difference of 1 may equate to somewhat less than a fold change of 2. If the precise magnitude of fold change were important to the research question, running a sample dilution curve could establish efficiency, and those estimates could be used to create efficiency-weighted CT values suitable for downstream statistical analysis [12]. If what matters most is whether a treatment significantly affects gene expression in the statistical sense (P < 0.05), our simulations show that multivariable linear models like ANCOVA are superior to 2−ΔΔCT. Assuming familiarity with R, modifying the accompanying tutorials to apply ANCOVA to a new qPCR data set requires very little coding. ANCOVA provides P-values that are invariant to amplification efficiency differences between the reference and gene of interest. Moreover, because ANCOVA can be more sensitive than 2−ΔΔCT, ANCOVA can detect experimental differences with fewer samples, saving time and resources. Multivariable linear models like ANCOVA naturally accommodate the more complex experimental designs that are increasingly common in science. Based on simulations, ANCOVA offers a lower false negative rate than 2−ΔΔCT, especially when the reference gene does not provide useful information about the gene of interest. Finally, we have found that storing raw CT values in figshare and depositing analysis code in GitHub is not difficult and makes qPCR data analysis more compatible with FAIR data principles [24].
