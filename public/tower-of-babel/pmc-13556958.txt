Predictors of influenza vaccination among Iranian pregnant women based on a combined Health Belief Model and Theory of Planned behavior
Authors: Masoud Yousefi, Mohammad Ansarizadeh, Farzaneh Ghaleh Golab, Andishe Hamedi, Hossein Lashkardoost, Ali Khani Jeihooni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza poses a significant public health risk, particularly for pregnant women due to physiological and immunological changes. Despite strong recommendations and proven safety of influenza vaccination, uptake remains suboptimal. This study aimed to identify predictors of influenza vaccination among Iranian pregnant women using an integrated Health Belief Model (HBM) and Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among 630 pregnant women attending urban and rural health centers and private clinics in Fasa and Shiraz, Iran. Participants were selected using multistage stratified sampling. Data were collected by a validated, researcher-developed questionnaire based on HBM and TPB constructs. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, Spearman correlation, logistic regression, and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). The mean age of participants was 29.36y, and 73% received the influenza vaccine during the current pregnancy. The most common barriers were concerns about side effects (49.4%), limited access (28.2%), lack of provider recommendation (27.1%), and insufficient information (34.1%). SEM results indicated that perceived susceptibility (beta = 0.18), perceived barriers (beta = -0.15), and attitude (beta = 0.13) were significant predictors of behavioral intention. Behavioral intention had a small but significant effect on actual vaccination (beta = 0.08). Subjective norms, self-efficacy, and perceived behavioral control were not significant predictors. Perceived susceptibility, common barriers, and attitude play key roles in shaping vaccination intention, while structural factors limit the translation of intention into action. Interventions focusing on education, improving provider–patient communication, and reducing access barriers may enhance vaccination uptake among pregnant women.

Introduction

Influenza remains a significant global public health concern, causing substantial morbidity and mortality each year, particularly among high‑risk populations.1 Pregnant women are among the most vulnerable groups due to physiological and immunological changes that occur during pregnancy. Increased cardiac output, elevated oxygen demand, and modulation of the maternal immune response heighten susceptibility to respiratory infections and increase the likelihood of severe influenza compared with non‑pregnant women.2

Influenza infection during pregnancy can result in serious complications for both mother and fetus. Maternal outcomes may include severe respiratory distress, hospitalization, and intensive care unit admission. Fetal consequences include preterm birth, premature rupture of membranes, intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, and even fetal or neonatal death. Moreover, maternal fever-one of the hallmark symptoms of influenza – has been associated with congenital anomalies, miscarriage, and developmental impairments.3,4 These risks highlight the necessity of effective preventive strategies during pregnancy.

Influenza vaccination is recognized as the most effective method of prevention. The vaccine is updated annually and has demonstrated a strong safety profile for pregnant women. Evidence indicates that maternal vaccination reduces influenza-related complications and provides passive immunity to infants through transplacental antibody transfer, protecting them during the first months of life when vaccination is not yet possible. Accordingly, major health authorities, including the World Health Organization (WHO), the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), recommend influenza vaccination for all pregnant women during any trimester.5,6

Despite these recommendations and well‑established safety evidence, vaccination coverage among pregnant women remains suboptimal worldwide, including in Iran. Many pregnant women hesitate or refuse vaccination even when it is recommended by healthcare providers. This gap between scientific evidence and actual behavior reflects the influence of psychological, social, cultural, and environmental determinants beyond simple knowledge deficits.7

Common barriers include fear of side effects, concerns about fetal harm, distrust in healthcare systems, and exposure to conflicting or misleading information – often amplified by social media. Cultural misconceptions, such as the belief that vaccines should be avoided during pregnancy, further contribute to hesitancy. Conversely, strong recommendations from healthcare providers, previous positive experiences, family or spousal support, and convenient access to vaccination services facilitate vaccine uptake.8 Understanding these multidimensional influences requires the application of robust theoretical frameworks.

The Health Belief Model (HBM) is one of the most widely used frameworks for explaining preventive health behaviors. According to HBM, individuals are more likely to engage in a health behavior if they perceive themselves as susceptible to a serious condition, believe in the benefits of the action, perceive fewer barriers, and feel confident in their ability to perform the behavior (self‑efficacy).9,10 In the context of influenza vaccination, a pregnant woman who perceives high susceptibility and severity, recognizes the benefits of vaccination, and feels capable of overcoming obstacles is more likely to be vaccinated. However, HBM does not fully address the impact of social pressures and interpersonal influences.

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) complements this limitation by emphasizing behavioral intention as the primary determinant of action.11,12

Because vaccination behavior is influenced by both individual health beliefs and social determinants, neither HBM nor TPB alone provides a comprehensive explanation. Consequently, integrated models combining constructs from both frameworks have gained attention. Such integration captures cognitive factors (perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, self‑efficacy) alongside psychosocial determinants (attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control), thereby improving prediction of both intention and actual vaccine uptake.

In Iran, sociocultural norms, family influence – especially from spouses – and uneven access to healthcare services play an important role in maternal vaccination behaviors. Yet, most existing studies have examined only basic awareness or attitudes, without applying comprehensive behavioral frameworks. Using an integrated HBM–TPB model can therefore provide a more complete understanding of the factors shaping influenza vaccination among pregnant women. Such insights can guide targeted educational strategies, strengthen supportive social norms, reduce barriers, and ultimately improve vaccination coverage. Accordingly, this study aims to identify determinants of influenza vaccination in Iranian pregnant women using a combined Health Belief Model and Theory of Planned Behavior approach.

Methods

Study design

This research was a cross-sectional (descriptive–analytical) study conducted to investigate the factors influencing influenza vaccination uptake among pregnant women based on an integrated framework of the Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Data collection was conducted between October 2022 and February 2023, coinciding with the national influenza vaccination campaign and the peak influenza season in Iran. During this period, the Iranian Ministry of Health actively distributed free influenza vaccines to high-risk groups, including pregnant women, through public comprehensive health centers.

Study population and sampling method

The study population consisted of all pregnant women over 18 y of age who were receiving prenatal care at urban and rural comprehensive health centers, health houses, health posts, and private obstetric clinics in Fasa and Shiraz, Iran. Sampling was performed using a multistage stratified sampling method. In the first stage, the counties were stratified into urban and rural areas. In the second stage, a random selection of prenatal care centers (comprehensive health centers, health houses, health posts, and private clinics) was conducted within each stratum. In the final stage, eligible pregnant women who attended the selected centers during the data collection period were randomly selected and enrolled in the study.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on previous similar studies, considering a statistical power of 80% and a confidence level of 95%. The minimum required sample size was estimated at 570, and considering possible attrition, the final sample size was set at 630 participants.13

Inclusion criteria

Pregnant women aged 18 y or older, the ability to read and write in Persian, and willingness to participate and provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Having severe underlying medical conditions that could prevent vaccination and unwillingness to complete the questionnaire or having more than 20% missing responses.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a researcher-made questionnaire developed based on the integrated framework of the Health Belief Model and the Theory of Planned Behavior. The questionnaire consisted of four sections. The items were extracted and adapted from previously published studies,14–16 and the full list of source‑based questions is provided in the Supplementary file.

1. Demographic and Pregnancy Characteristics

Eight items assessed age, place of residence, educational level, occupation, gravidity, gestational age, history of influenza vaccination, and underlying medical conditions.

2. Constructs of the Health Belief Model (HBM)

This section included 18 items covering perceived susceptibility (3 items), perceived severity (3 items), perceived benefits (3 items), perceived barriers (4 items), cues to action (3 items), and self-efficacy (2 items). All items were scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

3. Constructs of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)

This section consisted of 11 items assessing attitude toward vaccination (3 items), subjective norms (3 items), perceived behavioral control (3 items), and behavioral intention (2 items), also scored on a five-point Likert scale.

To reduce conceptual overlap between self-efficacy and perceived behavioral control, self-efficacy in the HBM was defined as the women’s confidence in their ability to decide to receive the influenza vaccine despite personal concerns or practical difficulties. In contrast, perceived behavioral control in the TPB focused on perceived control over actually performing the vaccination behavior, including access, time, and service availability.

4. Actual Vaccination Behavior

This section included two yes/no questions regarding receiving the influenza vaccine during the current pregnancy and, if not, the reason for non-vaccination.

Validity and reliability

Content validity was assessed by a panel of 10 experts in health education, epidemiology, and obstetrics. The Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were calculated for all items. Items with a CVR below 0.62 were revised or removed. The overall CVI of the instrument was 0.89, indicating acceptable content validity. Construct validity was assessed using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) in AMOS software. Model fit indices including χ2/df less than 3, RMSEA below 0.08, and CFI and TLI above 0.90 indicated an acceptable model fit. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which was 0.87 for the total questionnaire and ranged from 0.72 to 0.89 across subscales, confirming internal consistency. Test–retest reliability was evaluated on 30 participants over a two-week interval, resulting in an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of 0.84, indicating good temporal stability.

Data collection procedure

After obtaining approval from the Ethics Committee of the university, the researcher visited the selected centers. The study objectives were explained, written informed consent was obtained, and participants completed the questionnaires in person. The researcher was present to answer any questions. Completion of the questionnaire took approximately 15–20 minutes.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used for demographic variables and HBM and TPB constructs. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Spearman correlation tests were applied to assess relationships between variables. Independent t-tests and ANOVA were used for group comparisons. Multiple logistic regression was conducted to identify predictors of vaccination behavior. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) in AMOS software was used to examine structural relationships and test the integrated model. A significance level of 0.05 was considered for all analyses.

Result

A total of 630 pregnant women participated in the study. The mean age of the participants was 29.36 y (SD = 5.10), ranging from 18 to 44 y. The mean gestational age was 24.19 weeks (SD = 8.09), and the average number of pregnancies (gravidity) was 3.06 (SD = 1.36).

Regarding the categorical variables, most participants lived in urban areas, with only a smaller proportion residing in rural settings. Educational attainment was highest among women with a diploma, while postgraduate education represented the smallest group. The majority of participants were housewives, with fewer women being employed or students. Most women reported no underlying medical conditions, and approximately two‑thirds had no history of influenza vaccination in prior pregnancies. Perceived susceptibility showed significant positive correlations with self‑efficacy (r = 0.196, p < .001) and behavioral intention (r = 0.175, p < .001). Perceived severity demonstrated a small positive association with self‑efficacy (r = 0.136, p = .001), but it was not related to behavioral intention.

Perceived benefits were positively correlated with cues to action (r = 0.129, p = .001), although benefits showed no meaningful relationship with intention. Perceived barriers showed a small but significant negative association with behavioral intention (r = −0.148, p < .001).

Within HBM-to-TPB cross‑links, cues to action were moderately correlated with attitude (r = 0.258, p < .001) and weakly correlated with behavioral intention (r = 0.085, p = .033).

Among the TPB constructs, attitude demonstrated a positive association with behavioral intention (r = 0.131, p = .001). Subjective norms were significantly associated with behavioral control (r = 0.111, p = .005), although neither of these variables showed a significant relationship with behavioral intention. Behavioral control was not correlated with intention (p > .05).

Analysis of the correlations showed that the three demographic variables – age, gestational week, and number of pregnancies (gravidity) – had generally weak associations with the psychological constructs measured in the study. Among these variables, age was negatively associated with perceived severity (r = −0.115, p = .004), indicating that older pregnant women reported slightly lower perceptions of disease severity. Additionally, age showed a small positive significant correlation with subjective norms (r = 0.093, p = .019), suggesting that as age increased, women experienced a slightly greater sense of social pressure or influence regarding the behavior. In contrast, gestational week did not show any significant correlation with any of the study variables (all p > .05). Regarding the number of pregnancies, a small negative correlation was observed with subjective norms (r = −0.083, p = .038), indicating that women with more pregnancies reported slightly lower perceived social influence.

One-way ANOVA tests were conducted to determine whether HBM and TPB constructs differed across demographic and background characteristics, including place of residence, education level, employment status, previous influenza vaccination, and comorbidity status. Across all analyses, no statistically significant differences were found between groups (all p-values > 0.05). Specifically, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, self-efficacy, attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention did not differ across categories of residence, education, job status, prior vaccination history, or comorbidity. In addition to the demographic analyses, differences in HBM and TPB constructs were examined with respect to influenza vaccination during the current pregnancy. ANOVA results indicated no significant group differences in perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, self-efficacy, attitude, subjective norms, or perceived behavioral control (all p-values > 0.05). The only significant effect was observed for behavioral intention (F (1, 628) = 4.857, p = .028), with vaccinated. The mean difference in behavioral intention between vaccinated and unvaccinated women was 0.28 points, with vaccinated women reporting higher intention (M = 8.15) than unvaccinated women (M = 7.87). Although the mean scores of behavioral intentions were slightly lower among women who reported concerns such as side effects, lack of recommendation, cost, access, or insufficient information, none of these differences reached statistical significance (all p > .05). Therefore, these factors cannot be considered significant predictors of behavioral intention in this sample.

Direct, indirect, and total effects of study variables.

Predictors of behavioral intention

Among the HBM constructs, perceived susceptibility showed a significant positive association with behavioral intention (β = 0.181, p < .001), indicating that women who perceived themselves at higher risk of influenza were more likely to intend to receive the vaccine. Conversely, perceived barriers had a significant negative effect (β = −0.152, p < .001), suggesting that greater perceived barriers reduced intention.

From the TPB constructs, attitude was a significant positive predictor of intention (β = 0.139, p < .001). However, self-efficacy (β = −0.030, p = .433), subjective norms (β = 0.065, p = .092), and perceived behavioral control (β = −0.041, p = .283) were not significant predictors.

Several indirect paths were also observed. Cues to action influenced intention indirectly through attitude (β = 0.036), while perceived benefits and severity showed very small indirect effects via self-efficacy or cues to action, none of which were substantively meaningful.

Predictors of vaccination behavior

Behavioral intention exerted a significant direct effect on actual vaccination (β = 0.088, p = .027), although the magnitude of the effect was small. In addition, perceived susceptibility (β = 0.016), attitude (β = 0.012), and cues to action (β = 0.003) indirectly influenced vaccination behavior through behavioral intention. Perceived barriers also showed an indirect negative effect (β = −0.013). Given the large sample size of 630 participants, these indirect effects, while statistically significant, are practically negligible. Therefore, the direct pathways remain the primary and most meaningful findings of the structural model.

Model fit

The overall model demonstrated excellent absolute fit. The RMSEA value was 0.029, indicating a very good fit with a narrow confidence range. In addition, the GFI (0.982) and AGFI (0.972) were both above commonly accepted thresholds, supporting strong model adequacy. The chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio also indicated a high-quality fit (χ2/df = 1.547)

Discussion

The present study examined the psychosocial determinants of influenza vaccination among pregnant women using an integrated framework of the Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). The findings showed that perceived susceptibility, perceived barriers, and attitude were the strongest predictors of behavioral intention, while behavioral intention itself had a small but significant effect on actual vaccination uptake. These results offer valuable insights into the cognitive mechanisms underlying vaccination behavior during pregnancy and align closely with findings from both national and international research.

Consistent with prior Iranian studies, such as those by Maryam Dehshiri. (2024)17 and Zohreh Khalajinaia,18 a substantial proportion of pregnant women reported concerns about vaccine side effects, inadequate information, and limited provider recommendations as major barriers to vaccination. In our sample, nearly half of the unvaccinated women cited concerns about side effects, which mirrors the pattern observed in these studies. This consistency suggests that anxiety regarding fetal safety and vaccine-related complications remains a widespread concern among pregnant women in Iran, regardless of region or study design. International studies conducted in high-income countries, including research by Xiao Qiu et al., 202119 and E. Castillo et al., 2021,20 similarly report safety concerns as a dominant barrier. However, the intensity of fear appears to be higher in Iran, possibly due to lower trust in vaccine safety communication systems, reduced access to evidence-based counseling, and cultural emphasis on maternal–fetal vulnerability.

This pattern is consistent with a recent systematic review of cross-sectional studies by Ługowski et al. (2025),19 which found that low maternal influenza vaccine uptake was mainly driven by concerns about vaccine safety, fear of fetal harm, misunderstandings about vaccine effectiveness, and the absence of a clear recommendation from healthcare providers. The review also highlighted that education and antenatal counseling substantially improve willingness to vaccinate.

The SEM findings demonstrated that perceived susceptibility was a significant positive predictor of behavioral intention. This is highly consistent with previous studies in Iran and abroad. For instance, N. Weinstein et al21 found that perceived susceptibility strongly predicted willingness to receive the influenza vaccine among Iranian mothers. Studies from Turkey22 and the United States23 Also, show that women who consider themselves at higher risk for influenza are more likely to intend to vaccinate. The similarity across countries suggests that risk perception plays a universal motivational role in health behavior. However, the magnitude of the effect in our study was slightly smaller than that observed in some Western studies, which may be due to differing cultural perceptions of vulnerability during pregnancy or variations in prior exposure to health education.

In contrast, perceived severity did not significantly predict behavioral intention in this study. Similar nonsignificant effects have been reported in research from Iran and several Asian countries, such as studies by B. Boedeker et al. (2015),24 which found that Pregnant women perceive the risk of influenza vaccination as higher than the risk of the disease, leading to low vaccination uptake. Also, pregnant women often underestimate the threat of influenza despite being at high risk, as shown in a systematic review of 45 studies.25 Conversely, studies conducted in America frequently report a significant effect of perceived severity.26 This discrepancy may originate from differences in public health messaging: in many Western countries, campaigns explicitly highlight the severe maternal and neonatal consequences of influenza, whereas in Iran and some Asian settings, risk messaging may be less prominent or less consistently communicated by healthcare providers.

The negative effect of perceived barriers on behavioral intention observed in our findings aligns with a large body of literature. Such studies consistently show that logistical and psychological barriers – such as vaccine availability, cost, safety concerns, and provider recommendation – strongly influence vaccine hesitancy.27,28 Internationally, similar trends are observed, but the relative contribution of each barrier differs. For example, such studies report that misinformation and distrust of vaccine safety are dominant barriers, whereas in lower-income contexts, structural barriers such as cost and access play a more prominent role. In our sample, both psychological and structural barriers were present, explaining the moderate negative effect observed.29,30 These findings align with the systematic review by Ługowski et al. (2025), which showed that misconceptions about vaccine safety and limited provider recommendations are among the most common reasons for maternal influenza vaccine hesitancy, underscoring the importance of provider communication and health education.19

Among the TPB constructs, attitude emerged as a significant predictor of intention, consistent with TPB-based studies in Iran and globally. Positive attitudes toward vaccination – grounded in perceived benefits, trust in healthcare providers, and confidence in vaccine effectiveness – have repeatedly been shown to increase intention. Our findings match those of Damnjanović et al. (2023)31 and Haoran et al. (2021),32 suggesting that attitude remains one of the most consistent predictors of maternal vaccination across cultural contexts.

Unexpectedly, subjective norms, self-efficacy, and perceived behavioral control were not significant predictors of intention in our study. This contrasts with some studies, which typically report strong effects for subjective norms – particularly the influence of healthcare providers and partners.33 However, such studies found these constructs to be weak or nonsignificant predictors.34,35 One explanation is that decisions regarding maternal health in Iran may be highly individualized and less influenced by social pressure. Additionally, although subjective norms showed a weak positive correlation with age, this did not translate into predictive power within the SEM. The lack of predictive value for self-efficacy and perceived behavioral control may also be linked to the fact that vaccination is a behavior with relatively low behavioral autonomy for many women; access, clinic rules, and healthcare provider recommendations may limit actual control, reducing the relevance of these constructs.

The effect of behavioral intention on actual vaccination behavior was statistically significant but small (beta = 0.08), highlighting the well-documented intention-behavior gap in maternal immunization. Furthermore, while several indirect pathways reached statistical significance in our model, their effect sizes were exceptionally small, ranging from 0.01 to 0.03. These statistically significant indirect effects are primarily a reflection of the study’s large sample size and high statistical power, rather than representing practically meaningful relationships. Therefore, these indirect paths should not be overstated. From a public health and practical intervention standpoint, the direct effects of perceived susceptibility (beta = 0.18), perceived barriers (beta = −0.15), and attitude (beta = 0.13) serve as the only robust and actionable targets for designing educational and behavioral campaigns. Research by Lehmann et al. (2014),36 shows statistically significant results on the effect of behavioral intention on actual vaccination behavior. The study conducted by Liuqing Yang shows vaccination intention significantly mediates the relationship between attitudes and subjective norms with vaccination behavior.37

In the Iranian healthcare context, this intention–behavior gap may also reflect localized structural barriers that were not directly measured in the present study. These may include inconsistent vaccine availability at health centers, delays or interruptions in vaccine supply, limited coordination between prenatal care services and vaccination programs, and difficulties in obtaining timely appointments. Even when pregnant women intend to receive the vaccine, the absence of the vaccine at the point of care or the need to return for a separate visit may prevent intention from translating into actual vaccination. In addition, variation in provider recommendations and the limited integration of influenza vaccination into routine antenatal services may further weaken the transition from intention to action. Future research should directly assess vaccine stock-outs, waiting time, appointment availability, referral pathways, and the frequency and quality of provider recommendations.

Finally, the high overall vaccination rate in our sample (73%) is noteworthy, as it exceeds the 10–40% range typically reported in previous Iranian studies. Several contextual factors explain this discrepancy. First, data collection took place during the peak influenza season (October to February) and coincided with active national health campaigns that prioritize pregnant women for free vaccine distribution at public health centers. Second, because our sample was recruited from active attendees of comprehensive health centers, it is subject to a ‘health-seeking bias.’ These women were already engaged in routine prenatal care and had direct, continuous exposure to healthcare providers’ recommendations and integrated vaccination services. Third, the study was conducted in the post-COVID-19 era, during which public awareness, health literacy, and acceptance of respiratory vaccines had generally heightened. These factors combined to facilitate a significantly higher vaccine uptake in this specific cohort compared to older studies or the general population without regular prenatal care access.

Overall, the similarities between this study and previous research reinforce the centrality of susceptibility, attitude, and perceived barriers as key determinants of maternal vaccination. Differences, on the other hand, appear to stem from variation in cultural norms, trust in medical systems, communication quality, and accessibility of services. Understanding these cultural and contextual nuances is essential for designing targeted interventions aimed at reducing vaccine hesitancy and improving maternal vaccination coverage.

This study possesses several notable strengths that enhance both its scientific value and practical relevance. The use of an integrated theoretical framework combining the Health Belief Model and the Theory of Planned Behavior enabled a comprehensive examination of cognitive, psychological, and social determinants of influenza vaccination during pregnancy – an approach rarely applied in Iranian research. The relatively large sample size and the use of stratified multistage sampling ensured adequate statistical power and improved the representativeness of women from both urban and rural settings. Furthermore, the study instrument underwent rigorous psychometric evaluation, including assessments of content validity, construct validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability, all of which confirmed the robustness of the measurement tool. The application of Structural Equation Modeling allowed for an in-depth analysis of both direct and indirect pathways, providing a nuanced understanding of the mechanisms underlying vaccination behavior. Additionally, in-person data collection under researcher supervision reduced missing data and enhanced the accuracy of responses. Focusing on pregnant women, a high-risk and clinically important population, also adds significant public health value to the study.

Despite these strengths, the study is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal inferences and prevents a clear understanding of how beliefs evolve over time or how they influence subsequent vaccination behavior. Self-reported measures of intention and vaccination status may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly within the context of maternal healthcare. Although sampling was conducted across multiple urban and rural health centers, the research was confined to Fasa and Shiraz, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to other regions of Iran with differing cultural or structural characteristics. In addition, several influential factors – such as the strength of provider recommendations, trust in healthcare systems, exposure to misinformation, or the role of family decision-making – were not directly assessed, although previous international evidence indicates that these variables can strongly shape vaccine uptake. While absolute fit indices of the SEM model were excellent, some comparative fit indices were moderate, suggesting that other important determinants might not have been fully captured within the current theoretical framework. The relatively small effect of behavioral intention on actual vaccination behavior also indicates the presence of structural or contextual barriers that contribute to the well-recognized intention–behavior gap.

Given these limitations, several directions for future research are recommended. Longitudinal or prospective study designs would allow for a clearer understanding of causal pathways and changes in beliefs across pregnancy. Incorporating provider-level variables – such as the frequency and quality of healthcare provider recommendations, communication competence, and patient trust – may substantially improve the predictive power of future models. Examining the influence of misinformation, social media exposure, and digital health literacy is increasingly important, given their growing impact on vaccine-related decision-making. Qualitative approaches, including interviews or focus groups, can reveal deeper cultural, emotional, and motivational factors that quantitative instruments might overlook. Incorporating structural variables such as distance to health centers, vaccine availability, scheduling barriers, and affordability could clarify the determinants of the intention–behavior gap. Comparative modeling using alternative theories, such as Protection Motivation Theory or the COM-B model, may also enhance explanatory power. Finally, designing and evaluating targeted interventions based on the key predictors identified – such as increasing perceived susceptibility, improving attitudes, and reducing perceived barriers – would offer practical implications for enhancing vaccine uptake.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that perceived susceptibility, perceived barriers, and attitude play substantial roles in shaping behavioral intention to receive the influenza vaccine during pregnancy, while behavioral intention itself – despite its modest strength – remains a significant predictor of actual vaccination behavior. The findings emphasize that although cognitive and psychological factors are important, structural and contextual barriers also impede the translation of intention into action. The integrated HBM–TPB model provided meaningful insights into the multifaceted nature of vaccination behavior and highlighted key pathways that influence decision-making among pregnant women. Given the preventable risks associated with influenza during pregnancy and the proven safety and effectiveness of vaccination, these results underscore the importance of developing targeted educational strategies, enhancing provider–patient communication, and reducing structural obstacles to improve vaccine uptake. Ultimately, the study offers valuable evidence to inform public health practices and policies aimed at increasing influenza vaccination coverage among pregnant women in Iran.
