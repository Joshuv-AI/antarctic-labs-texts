Restricted Dispersal and Phenotypic Response to Water Depth in a Foundation Seagrass
Authors: Erik E. Sotka, A. Randall Hughes, Torrance C. Hanley, Cynthia G. Hays
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSpecies conservation and management benefit from precise understanding of natural patterns of dispersal and genetic variation. Using recent advances in indirect genetic methods applied to both adult plants and dispersed seeds, we find that the mean seed dispersal in a threatened marine foundation plant (the eelgrass Zostera marina) is approximately 100–200 m. This distance is surprisingly more similar to that of wind‐dispersed terrestrial seeds (~10s to 100s of meters) than the passive dispersal of marine propagules via currents (~10s to 100s of kilometres). Because nearshore marine plants like Zostera are commonly distributed across strong selective gradients driven by bathymetry (depth) even within these restricted spatial scales, seeds are capable of dispersing to novel water depths and experiencing profound shifts in light availability, temperature and wave exposure. We documented strong phenotypic variation and genome‐wide differentiation among plants separated by approximately the spatial scale of mean realised dispersal. This result suggests genetic isolation by environment in response to depth‐related environmental gradients as one plausible explanation for this pattern. The ratio of effective to census size (or Ne/Nc) approximated 0.1%, indicating that a fraction of existing plants provides the genetic variation to allow adaptation to environmental change. Our results suggest that successful conservation of seagrass meadows that can adapt to microspatial and temporal variation in environmental conditions will be low without direct and persistent intervention using large numbers of individuals or a targeted selection of genotypes.

Introduction

The distances that seeds disperse drive the dynamics of plant ecology, evolution and conservation (Harper 1977; Clobert et al. 2012), but quantifying the spatial extent of dispersal remains a vexing problem (Cain, Milligan, and Strand 2000; Wu et al. 2022). Typically, dispersal distances are described by a skewed probability distribution (Levin et al. 2003; Nathan et al. 2012), where most seeds disperse near the natal plant and fewer seeds disperse more widely. Estimating the width of this dispersal kernel has remained challenging, largely because of the difficulty of quantifying the extent, frequency and outcome of long‐distance dispersal events.

For terrestrial wind‐dispersed seeds, direct observations and biophysical models indicate maximal dispersal distances of 10s to 100s of metres (Katul et al. 2005; Ellstrand 2014), though not universally (Wu et al. 2022). In contrast, using analogous lines of direct observations and biophysical models of oceanographic currents, the general consensus is that dispersal for water‐dispersed seeds from marine plants such as seagrasses is typically 10s to 100s of kilometres (McMahon et al. 2014; Kendrick et al. 2017; Pazzaglia et al. 2021). Seagrasses are flowering plants that entered the marine environment approximately 50–64 million years ago (Chen et al. 2022) and serve as foundation species in many coastal ecosystems (Unsworth et al. 2022). While the seeds themselves are negatively buoyant and show limited dispersal when released directly from the plant (Ruckelshaus 1996; Van Tussenbroek et al. 2016), seeds often remain attached to fruits and shoots that readily float with along‐shore surface currents (Harwell and Orth 2002; Kaellstroem et al. 2008; Erftemeijer et al. 2008; Sinclair et al. 2018). Thus, maximal dispersal distances for many seagrasses are considered more similar to those of pelagically dispersed fishes and invertebrates (Bradbury et al. 2008; Shanks 2009) than to terrestrial plants.

Direct observations of seed dispersal do not provide information on effective dispersal—that is, when the seed both disperses and successfully establishes (Nathan et al. 2012). Potential and effective dispersal may be strongly decoupled in seagrasses, given that seagrasses typically have low (< 1%) recruitment success (Orth, Harwell, and Inglis 2006; McMahon et al. 2014; Kendrick et al. 2017). Indirect genetic methods, which quantify patterns of spatial genetic variation in order to infer gene flow, provide a proxy for effective dispersal that accrues across multiple generations. One commonly used indirect method is the well‐established relationship between mean dispersal, effective population size (Ne) and the slope of genetic isolation by geographic distance, or IBD (Slatkin 1993; Rousset 1997). While simplistic inference of effective dispersal from spatial genetic patterns is fraught with known theoretical and practical issues (Hellberg 2009), several recent studies demonstrated that dispersal estimates inferred from IBD are congruent with those from direct (ecological) approaches (e.g., Pinsky et al. 2016; Naaykens and D'Aloia 2022) when certain conditions are met. First, the estimate of Ne uses recent analytical advances that have shown themselves to be robust (Waples, Larson, and Waples 2016). Second, the geographic scale across which IBD is measured is restricted to geographic scales where drift and gene flow equilibrate within a few generations (Slatkin 1993; Hutchison and Templeton 1999). Finally, IBD is measured across no obvious secondary contact zones and where there is little spatial variation in mating system, patch size and quality (Ellstrand 2014; Cruzan and Hendrickson 2020) in order to minimise the effect of genetic isolation by environment, or IBE; (see Sexton, Hangartner, and Hoffmann 2014 for review).

Nearly all species are distributed across environmentally heterogeneous landscapes, but species differ in the spatial scale of this environmental shift relative to the spatial scale of their dispersal (sensu Levins 1968). When dispersal is smaller than the spatial scale of environmental change, which can happen even across narrow environmental gradients, local adaptation may arise in which both adults and newly settled individuals contain locally favourable genotypes (Kawecki and Ebert 2004; Richardson et al. 2014). For widespread marine species such as seagrasses, corals and other invertebrates and fishes, bathymetry (i.e., ocean depth) commonly generates a profound environmental gradient in light availability, temperature and wave exposure (Hays et al. 2021b). For example, continuous meadows of the seagrass Zostera marina in coastal Massachusetts (USA) span deep (4–5.5 m below mean low water, or MLLW) and shallow (1–2 m below MLLW) environments separated by only 130–200 m (Figure S1). In response, plants growing in the shallow ends of the meadow tend to be more dense and taller than those at deeper depths (von Staats et al. 2021). Many seagrass morphological traits have a genetic basis (Hughes, Stachowicz, and Williams 2009; Abbott and Stachowicz 2016), and thus phenotypic differences across depth could reflect the outcome of local adaptation at microgeographic scales (Hays et al. 2021b).

Here, we addressed multiple questions through genotyping single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from two life history stages of Z. marina (adults and recently dispersed seeds) collected from two depths at four meadows in a spatially explicit sampling design: (1) Does population genetic structure occur across hierarchical spatial scales and replicate environmental gradients? (2) What are the effective population sizes within and between meadows? and (3) What is the scale of dispersal within and between meadows? Quantifying the appropriate scale of effective dispersal is critical for reaching conservation and restoration goals (Waples and Gaggiotti 2006) for this globally declining species (Waycott et al. 2009), as well as predicting when and where populations may adapt to spatially variable environments (Richardson et al. 2014).

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

We examined four Z. marina meadows in Massachusetts separated by approximately 5–15 km. At each site, we collected plants at deep (approximately 4–5.5 m below MLLW) and shallow (approximately 1–2 m below MLLW) depths separated by between 130 and 200 m (Figure S1; von Staats et al. 2021); an average of 170, 160, 193 and 133 m separate the quadrats between habitats for Curlew, Lynch, West and Niles, respectively. Within each habitat (deep or shallow depths), we created three 8 × 4 m spatially explicit grids separated by approximately 10 m and collected 15 plants at set locations (nodes) within each grid such that ramets ranged from 2.0 to 8.9 m apart (Figure S1). This spatial arrangement allowed us to test for population differentiation and among‐plant relatedness at multiple spatial scales.

During the June peak of seasonal flowering (von Staats et al. 2021) in 2019, divers on SCUBA collected a single vegetative shoot at each node within each sampling grid for genetic analysis (n = 15 ramets per grid, 45 per depth, 90 per site). At approximately alternating nodes (i.e., 5–7 of the 15 nodes in each grid, depending on the location of bare patches), divers also harvested all shoots from within a 25 × 25 cm quadrat (n = 15–21 per depth) to estimate shoot density and canopy characteristics. Samples were returned to the lab on ice for processing. The second and third leaves of each genetic sample were preserved in silica. In the lab, we counted the number of vegetative and flowering shoots in each quadrat sample, measured the maximum shoot length of up to five vegetative shoots per quadrat and then dried all tissue (including the associated genetic shoot, sans two leaves) at 60°C to estimate the aboveground biomass.

After the flowering season and once seeds had dropped (September 2019; von Staats et al. 2021), we returned to each sampling grid to collect dispersed seeds. Sediment cores (10 cm in diameter and 10 cm in depth) were taken at three locations within each grid at Curlew Beach and at four locations within each grid at the other three field sites (n = 9 or 12 cores per depth). Sediment cores were bagged and kept cold (4°C) until they could be processed; each core was hand‐sieved for intact seeds within 3 days of collection. We counted all intact seeds encountered and assessed viability by firmness (the squeeze test; Marion and Orth 2008). Seeds that were deemed viable were individually weighed and stored in microfuge tubes and frozen at −80°C until DNA extraction.

Reduced Representation Genomic Sequencing

Leaf samples (2–4 mg of dried tissue from the middle third of the leaf) were ground with a Retsch mixer mill MM400; seeds were ground by hand with a micropestle after removing the seed coat. DNA extraction was done with the Omega Bio‐Tek E‐Z 96 Tissue DNA kit, and samples were stored at −20°C. We prepared one genomic library of 464 individuals (359 adults, 105 seeds) by following the ddRADseq protocol of Parchman et al. (2012) and sequenced the library using two lanes of Illumina Novaseq. We digested gDNA with two restriction enzymes, EcoRI and MseI, and ligated adaptors containing unique 8–10 bp barcodes to the digested DNA of each individual. The products were then PCR‐amplified in two independent reactions with standard Illumina primers. All amplicons were pooled and shipped to the University of Texas Genomic Sequencing and Analysis Facility, which used Blue Pippen Prep to isolate the 300–450 bp fraction. This fraction was then single‐read‐sequenced (approximately 100 bp) with both lanes of an Illumina Novaseq machine. We used custom scripts to demultiplex into sample‐specific FASTQ‐formatted files and aligned these to the Z. marina genome 3.0 (Ma et al. 2021) with bwa mem 0.7.17 (Li and Durbin 2010) and samtools/bcftools 1.9 (Danecek et al. 2021) with default settings. We then removed samples with relatively few reads (i.e., < 100 K) manually, leaving 367 individuals (268 adults and 99 seeds), with an average of 1.3 million reads per individual.

We used bcftools to generate genotype calls and filter out loci with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1% and then used vcftools (Danecek et al. 2011) to filter loci with missingness ratios of 50%, 80%, 90% and 95%. We used angsd (Korneliussen, Albrechtsen, and Nielsen 2014) to generate genotype likelihoods (using the SAMtools model) and output these in a beagle‐formatted text file. The genotype calls were pulled from vcf files using R::vcfR library (Knaus and Grünwald 2017). Genotype likelihoods provide the benefit of building statistical certainty of a given genotype into downstream analyses, while genotype calls do not. Genotype likelihoods also enable greater genomic sampling (i.e., more SNPs) because they can be generated with fewer reads. Genotype likelihoods from 10,327 SNPs (80% of individuals with 1+ reads) were used for clonal structure and admixture analyses. Genotype calls from 3372 SNPs (95% of individuals had 1+ reads) were used for clonal structure, while genotype calls from 7770 SNPs (90% of individuals with 1+ reads) were used for all other analyses.

Analysis—Adult and Seed Phenotypes

Vegetative shoot density and aboveground biomass data met the assumptions of normality and were analysed using two‐way ANOVAs, with site and depth as fixed factors and Tukey HSD test for post hoc comparisons of depths within sites. Flowering shoot density was zero‐inflated and analysed with a generalised linear model using a Poisson distribution. The interaction between site and depth was not significant, and thus we report only site and depth as independent fixed factors. Vegetative shoot height was taken from up to five haphazardly selected plants within a single quadrat; as such, we used a linear mixed model to assess the effects of site, depth and their interaction, with quadrat as a random variable to account for non‐independence of shoots from the same quadrat. We used a general linear model with binomial expectations to assess the presence/absence of seeds within our cores, with site, depth and their interaction as independent variables. We used an analysis of variance to measure the effect of site on per‐seed biomass.

Analysis—Clonal Structure

Because ramets from a single Z. marina genet can be spread across several metres, we identified and removed duplicate clonal ramets before all genetic analyses using both Hedrick's r and dissimilarity indices. We generated estimates of relatedness (r) using ngsRelate on genotype likelihoods and dissimilarity using poppr:bitwise.dist (Kamvar, Brooks, and Grünwald 2015) on genotype calls (3372 SNPs in which 95% of individuals had 1+ reads) for all seeds and adults simultaneously. Because genotyping errors and somatic mutations generate differences between ramets (Arnaud‐Haond, Stoeckel, and Bailleul 2020), we used visual inspection of the distribution of relatedness values to define a threshold (r = 0.8 and dissimilarity < 5%) for clonal replicates (see Figures S2 and S3 for bimodal curves). For each of the 27 genets with between 2 and 6 ramets, we randomly chose a single ramet to pursue subsequent analyses (40 clonal replicates removed, n = 214 unique adult genotypes remaining). We quantified clonal spread with R::geosphere (Hijmans 2022) and used one‐way ANOVA to assess the effect of depth (shallow vs. deep) on genotypic diversity (number of unique genotypes/total genotypes) and spatial extent of clonal spread. Analyses repeated across 80%, 90% and 95% genotype likelihood datasets yielded identical sets of clones. All seeds produced unique SNP genotypes; no genotypes were shared by multiple seeds nor between a seed and an adult sampled in the same meadow.

Analysis—Population Genetic Structure

We used R::hierfstat (Goudet and Jombart 2022) to generate estimates of Nei's observed heterozygosity (H o) and gene diversity (H s) for each site‐by‐depth combination of genotype calls from bcftools. Using one‐way ANOVA, we analysed whether these parameters differed between adults by depth and whether all adults (deep and shallow combined) versus seeds (shallow only) differed.

We implemented maximum likelihood admixture methods (NGSadmix; Meisner and Albrechtsen 2018) to infer the ancestry of individuals using genotype likelihoods of 214 unique adults and 99 seeds generated by angsd. We used an a priori range of genetic clusters (k = 1–10) and replicated 10 times. An analysis of Δk (as suggested by Evanno, Regnaut, and Goudet 2005) indicates that the best fit k to the dataset with adults and seeds was k = 2, while k = 5 and 9 had an elevated Δk that revealed finer‐scale spatial contrasts (Figure S5). We ran NGSadmix on adults within each of the four sites independently, using k = 1–5 and replicating 10 times. We present the results for the best‐fit k (k = 3 for West and k = 2 for Lynch, Niles and Curlew; Δk analyses not shown).

We performed an hierarchical analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) to assess how genetic variation is partitioned among sites and between depths within sites in adults, and among sites in seeds. We generated Euclidean distances using genotype calls of unique genets and then used R::pegas::amova (Paradis and Schliep 2019) to calculate the proportion of total variance among sites and among habitats within sites (i.e., ΦST). The analysis was permuted 1000 times to generate statistical significance. We also calculated pairwise ΦST values and assessed whether ΦST increased with geographic distance using a Mantel test, with Pearson correlations and 10,000 replicates in ecodist::mantel (Goslee and Urban 2007).

We used R::ade4 to generate PCAs from genotype calls from bcftools. We assigned seeds to adults using discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC as implemented in R::adegenet), a fast and accurate approach to maximise differentiation among groups of individuals defined a priori (Jombart, Devillard, and Balloux 2010). We created genetic clusters with eight a priori groups (four sites X two depths) among adults and then assigned seeds to these clusters with R::predict. In both the PC and DAPC analyses, we replaced SNP × individual combinations that were missing data with the most common genotype for that SNP (6.8% of the approximately 1.4 million combinations).

For non‐clonal adults, we assessed patterns of spatial genetic structure (SGS) using spatial autocorrelation analysis implemented in SPAGeDI 1.5 (Hardy and Vekemans 2002) on genotype calls of non‐clonal adults. For each pair of plants within a distance class, SPAGeDI calculates kinship coefficients (F ij ). Results were consistent when using bins of 8, 9 or 10 distance categories, each with approximately equal numbers of samples, and we chose 10 for finer spatial resolution. Significantly elevated kinship values indicate more closely related individuals than random expectations, while significantly lower kinship values indicate less related individuals than random expectations. Confidence intervals and significance tests were generated using 1000 permutation replicates of spatial locations.

Effective Population Size

We used two approaches to assess the effective population size. The first approach was to use the spatial autocorrelation generated among genotype calls of non‐clonal adults within each site by depth combination. That is, the number of breeders (Nb) is negatively correlated with the steepness of the decline in kinship (F ij ) versus the logarithm geographic distance (Hardy and Vekemans 1999): (1) Nb=−1−Fnblog where Nb is the number of effective breeders, F n is the inbreeding coefficient, and blog is the regression slope of pairwise kinship values against the logarithm of spatial distances. We followed Waples, Antao, and Luikart (2014) in converting Nb to effective population size (Ne) from a single cohort: (2) Nbadj=rawNb1.03−0.245×logALα (3) Ne=Nbadj0.485+0.758×logALα where AL is the expected reproductive lifespan (approximately 3 years), and α is the minimum time to reproduction (approximately 2 years; Yu et al. 2023).

The second approach was to use NeEstimator (Do et al. 2014) to estimate the effective number of breeders (Nb) among genotype calls of seeds (i.e., a single dispersed generation). We repeated the analysis for seeds from West Beach only (n = 47) and from Curlew Beach only (n = 38); we did not have enough seeds at Lynch (n = 3) nor Niles (n = 11) to robustly assess the effective Nb. Because some of our SNPs are possibly linked by physical distance, we estimated Ne with a randomly chosen set of ~20% of the polymorphic SNPs (i.e., ~1300 SNPs) and replicated these across 10 separate SNP sets to calculate mean ± SE. Because these estimates depend in part on the presence of rare alleles, NeEstimator generates estimates for all loci and when filtering out loci with minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 1%, 2% and 5%. We similarly converted these estimates of Nb to Ne, as described in Equations 2 and 3.

Our estimates of Ne appear robust across distinct SNP filtering criteria. For example, we filtered a set of SNPs across individuals with > 10 K reads per individual, yielding 245 non‐clonal adults and 8684 SNPs. Despite the distinct SNPs and individuals, the range of Ne estimated with spatial autocorrelation was of similar magnitude (Ne = 29–222) to that reported here (Ne = 52–282; see Section 3). The range from NeEstimator of a distinct set of filtered SNPs and individuals (Ne = 97–186) was similar to that reported here (Ne = 106–191).

Estimation of Dispersal Kernal

We used the approach of Rousset 1997 to estimate the dispersal kernel parameter (σ), otherwise known as the standard deviation of the dispersal kernel, with guidance from Pinsky et al. (2016): (4) σ=14DeM where De is the effective population density and M is the isolation‐by‐distance (IBD) slope. Effective density (De) is the effective population size (Ne) divided by the geographic distance of the neighbourhood, estimated here as 40 m of linear coastline (i.e., the approximate extent of a single site‐by‐depth location). We calculated the slope and its standard error (0.0025465 ± 0.0003331) from a linear regression of standardised genetic distance (ΦST/(1−ΦST)) against the geographic distance (in km).

Following Pinsky et al. (2016), we accounted for the sources of error in both the estimation of Ne and IBD slopes by sampling from a normal distribution of each mean and standard error of 100,000 replicate estimates and using this to create a mean sigma and its 95% confidence intervals. We then converted sigma into mean dispersal distance (D) by assuming a Gaussian kernel (Siegel et al. 2003): (5) D=σ×2π and the kernel was generated as: (6) Kx=1σ2π×expx22σ2

A Gaussian kernel is typically applied to marine species (Siegel et al. 2003), and it is unlikely that application of other kernel types would yield differences in mean dispersal estimates (Pinsky et al. 2016).

Meta‐Analysis of IBD in Zostera marina

On 4 June 2023, we performed a search on all collections within Web‐of‐Science with the search terms genet* and ‘Zostera marina’ and identified 38 studies that estimated genetic differentiation between multiple populations. Of these, we focused on 21 studies (22 when including this dataset) that (1) examined four or more populations that were native (i.e., not restored), (2) reported pairwise geographic distances and (3) reported F st, standardised F st (e.g., F st/(1−F st)) or their analogues (e.g., G st or ΦST). When only a figure was available, we digitised figures with R::metaDigitize (Pick, Nakagawa, and Noble 2019). For each of the 34 regressions, we calculated the linear slope and intercept and their standard error using linear regressions (F st ~ kilometre) for those populations within a single estuary or along a single continental margin. We report the Mantel p‐value reported by the study or using R::mantel.rtest in ade4 (Thioulouse et al. 2018) with 10,000 replicates.

To address whether the frequency of a significant Mantel test varied with geographic range, we used a Chi‐square test on significant versus non‐significant Mantel tests (α = 0.05) with a threshold of 100 km (spatial scale above and below 100 km). We used log‐linear regressions (i.e., IBD~log10(km)) to assess whether the geographic range across which IBD was measured correlates with the IBD slope (following Bradbury and Bentzen 2007). We repeated these analyses using (1) all data, (2) only significant IBD slopes and (3) only significant IBD slopes estimated with microsatellites. This was done because non‐significant slopes may reflect the lack of an IBD relationship (i.e., IBD slope = 0) or reflect a sampling design with low statistical power, and thus the IBD slope estimate would be poor.

We used the number of populations to assess whether these relationships were biased by the sampling intensity. The number of populations did not correlate with the geographic range (Pearson's correlation test; df = 32; r = 0.668; p = 0.509) nor IBD slope (df = 32; r = −0.109; p = 0.540). A one‐way ANOVA indicates that the Mantel test outcome (significant vs. non‐significant) did not predict the number of populations (F 1,32 = 0.436; p = 0.514).

Outlier Analysis

An outlier analysis was implemented using Latent Factor Mixed Models (or LFMMs) implemented in the LEA package (Frichot and François 2015) using default parameters. This regression model uses a genotype matrix of non‐clonal adults as a response variable and depth (deep vs. shallow) as the explanatory variable, and it was repeated for each location. As a conservative approach to detecting outlier loci, we applied a false‐discovery rate of 20% following Benjamini and Hochberg (1995). We also assessed whether genotypes differ between depths at all sites using a generalised linear model (GLM) framework (genotype~site + habitat + error). We included site as a blocking effect of site‐specific variation in genotype frequency. We did not include site × environment interactions because of limited power. Alpha for these GLMs was adjusted via the Bonferroni correction.

Results

Adult and Seed Traits

Phenotypic variation of adult plants was mostly partitioned between deep and shallow depths (Figure 1). Shoot density and aboveground biomass varied as an interaction between site and depth (Table S2A,B), but the interaction explained a relatively low proportion of the variance in these traits (< 2%). Shallow habitats had approximately three‐ to fivefold greater shoot density than did deep habitats at all sites (Figure 1A), and depth described 72% of the total variance. Shallow habitats had approximately five‐ to sevenfold greater aboveground biomass than did deep habitats (Figure 1B), and depth described 70% of the total variance in biomass. Flowering shoot density was greater in shallow habitats than in deep habitats (Figure 1C, Table S2C). Plants were approximately 40%–72% taller at shallow habitats than at deeper habitats at three of four sites sampled (Figure 1D), with the exception of Niles Beach where the pattern was reversed, yielding a significant interaction between the site and habitat (Table S2D).

Clonal Structure

We first examined spatial variation in clonal and genetic diversities, which play important roles in population, community and ecosystem processes of marine plants (Arnaud‐Haond, Stoeckel, and Bailleul 2020). Of the 268 adults that were well sequenced, we detected 60 clonal ramets from 27 genets and between 2 and 6 ramets per genet (Table S1). The spatial spread of these clonal replicates averaged 2.97 m (SE ± 0.18 m) and did not differ between deep and shallow habitats (see Figure S4 for a map; F 1,58 = 1.009; p = 0.319). Consistent with prior surveys in this region (Hays et al. 2021a), there was no significant difference between deep and shallow habitats in adult clonal diversity (F 1,6 = 0.850, p = 0.392), suggesting that the contribution of clonal expansion to population growth was similar in both habitats.

Mean observed heterozygosity (H o) and mean gene diversity (H s) per site × depth combination were calculated for seeds and adults (see Table S1). Mean H o did not significantly differ between deep versus shallow adult collections (F 1,6 = 2.04; p = 0.203) nor between adults and seeds overall (Table S1; F 1,9 = 0.25; p = 0.627). Shallow non‐clonal adults had slightly greater gene diversity than did deeper adults (mean H s ± SE = 0.204 ± 0.001 vs. 0.200 ± 0.002; F 1,6 = 6.26; p = 0.046). There was no significant difference in mean H s between adults and seeds (F 1,9 = 0.88; p = 0.371).

Spatial Genetic Structure: Adults

We detected spatial genetic structure at all hierarchical spatial scales. At scales of 8 m or less within each meadow, pairwise kinship values (F ij ) were significantly greater than random expectations at all site‐by‐depth combinations for at least one distance category (Figure 3). At larger spatial scales (20–40 m), kinship values were significantly lower than random expectations at four of eight site‐by‐depth combinations in at least one distance category. Moreover, kinship significantly declined with the increasing geographic distance at five of eight site‐by‐depth combinations (Figure 3; Table S7); the exceptions were at Niles' deep and shallow and Curlew shallow, where the relationship was not statistically significant.

Spatial Genetic Structure: Dispersed Seeds

Genetic analyses of dispersed seeds collected in sediment cores consistently pointed to mostly local dispersal (i.e., within sites). Strong genetic differentiation observed in seeds was similar to that of adults in an analysis of molecular variance (global ΦST = 0.127; p < 0.001; Table S4B). Pairwise ΦST values ranging from 0.042 to 0.180 were all statistically significant (Table S5B). The admixture analyses (Figure 4A and Figure S5) revealed that seeds had similar genetic clusters as those found among local adults (i.e., Niles seeds were like Niles adults). This was also reflected in a PCA, where the seed population structure mirrored that of adults (Figure 4B).

A DAPC‐based assignment test assigned seeds from a site to adult genetic clusters from the same site in nearly all cases (Table S6; Figure S6), indicating that most seeds disperse within a single meadow. There were exceptions: Some seeds collected from shallow‐water cores at Curlew Beach were assigned to deep‐water adults at Curlew Beach and shallow‐water adults at West Beach (~20 km north). One seed collected in deep‐water West was assigned to shallow‐water West adults, and some seeds collected from shallow‐water West were assigned to shallow‐water and deep‐water adults at Lynch Beach (~5 km south). We note these assignment probabilities may not reflect real dispersal events, however, as statistical power to delineate among alternative group assignments can be low, and DAPC will force assignments onto a priori groups even when the true population source was not sampled.

Meta‐Analysis of IBD

Thirty‐four IBD estimates were generated from microsatellite genotypes (31), SNPs (2) and RAPDs (1) across a geographic range of 5–10,000 km (Table S9; Figure 5B). At smaller spatial scales (i.e., < 100 km), 25% (3 of 12) of the IBD slopes were statistically significant (this paper; Tanaka et al. 2011; Schiebelhut et al. 2023). At larger spatial scales, 81% of IBD slopes (18 of 22) were statistically significant. This increase in the frequency of significant IBD slopes at larger spatial scales was statistically significant (χ 2 = 8.345, df = 1, p = 0.004) and remained significant when only microsatellite‐based estimates were analysed (χ2 = 10.644, df = 1, p = 0.001).

There is a non‐linear decline in the IBD slope of Z. marina with an increasing geographic range. This was detectable when all data were included (Figure 5B; n = 34; adj r 2 = 0.162; p < 0.010), among significant IBD slopes only (n = 21; adj r 2 = 0.596; p < 0.001) and among significant IBD slopes estimated by microsatellites only (n = 19; adj r 2 = 0.314; p = 0.007). Thus, IBD slopes calculated across larger spatial scales are shallower but more frequently significant, relative to IBD slopes calculated across shorter spatial scales.

Effective Population Size

Effective population size (Ne) is a proxy for the magnitude of genetic drift in a population and is particularly crucial for a genetic perspective on population sustainability, restoration and management (Hauser and Carvalho 2008; Hare et al. 2011). Two independent methods yielded similar Ne estimates for these Zostera beds. First, the slope of kinship to spatial distance among non‐clonal adults (Hardy and Vekemans 1999; Figure 3) was used to estimate Ne for all eight site‐by‐depth combinations (Table S7). Ne ranged from 52 to 281 (average Ne = 160). Second, an estimator based on linkage disequilibrium (Waples, Larson, and Waples 2016) and applied to the dispersed seeds (i.e., a single generation) yielded Ne values ranging from 106.3 ± 2.3 to 180.9 ± 4.6 (mean Ne ± SE) at West Beach and 148.3 ± 5.1 to 191.0 ± 6.0 at Curlew Beach depending on the minor allele frequency (MAF) threshold used (Table S8).

We computed a rough estimate of the ratio of effective to census population size (Ne/Nc) in each site‐by‐depth combination. Census size (Nc) was the product of mean shoot density (# per 0.0625 m2 quadrat), the proportion of ramets that are unique (i.e., genotypic diversity; Table S1) and the number of quadrats in a 40 × 40 m area (i.e., the approximate area in which we estimated the effective population size). Census size was between approximately 143,000 and 991,000 in a 1600 m2 area before adjusting for clonal diversity; after adjusting for clonal diversity, Nc ranged between 103,000 and 935,000 individuals. Shallow sites had significantly more density than did deeper sites (Figure 1). This yielded an Ne/Nc ratio that ranged from 0.00011 to 0.00188. There was no significant difference in Ne between deep and shallow depths (Wilcox test; n = 4; p > 0.2), as Ne was greater at shallow sites at Curlew and West and greater a deeper sites at Lynch and Niles.

Dispersal Estimate

The dispersal kernel parameter (σ; Rousset 1997), otherwise known as the standard deviation of the dispersal kernel and an approximation of mean dispersal, can be estimated using the IBD slope and Ne (see Section 2). Using a Monte Carlo‐style propagation of error across our calculation, we estimate that mean dispersal (σ) is approximately 100–200 m (Figure 7, inset). The estimate varied slightly with the minor allele frequency (MAF) threshold used. Mean dispersal averaged 153 m [95% CI = 136–178 m] with loci of MAF > 5%, and mean dispersal averaged 111 m [104–136 m] with all loci. Proposed Gaussian dispersal kernels are given in Figure 4.

Outlier Analysis

GLMs detected four SNPs (Chr01_26978455, Chr01_32236989, Chr01_42138975 and Chr02_34775824) that differed significantly between depths (see Figure S8 for genotype frequencies). However, an LFMM analysis failed to confirm this result and indicated no SNPs that were significantly elevated between deep and shallow samples across sites (Figure S7).

Discussion

Mean dispersal distance is a key ecological parameter that influences both the population demography and contemporary evolution of species on land and in the sea. Our estimate of mean dispersal (σ) in the foundation seagrass Z. marina is approximately 100–200 m, which provides important guidance for understanding the population biology and restoration of this coastal foundation species. Despite the currents and wind that may push floating seeds and reproductive shoots long distances (Harwell and Orth 2002), the restricted dispersal distances of most seagrass seeds are likely more terrestrial plant‐like than marine animal‐like (Bradbury et al. 2008; Shanks 2009), that is, their dispersal kernel is more similar to wind‐dispersed terrestrial plants (distances up to 10s to 100s of metres; Katul et al. 2005; Ellstrand 2014) than current‐dispersed marine larvae. The presence of microspatial genetic structure, where kinship between non‐clonal plants declines with increasing geographic distance, is further indication of low dispersal distances. Elevated kinship at short scales (< 10 m) likely reflects the low dispersal of offspring, while significantly elevated kinship at longer scales (20–40 m) likely reflects dispersal from nearby, unsampled plants (Hardy and Vekemans 1999).

While direct observations indicate that a large proportion of seagrass pollen and seeds disperses less than 10 m (Orth, Luckenbach, and Moore 1994; Ruckelshaus 1996; Furman et al. 2015; Van Tussenbroek et al. 2016; Follett, Hays, and Nepf 2019), direct observations and oceanographic modelling have also indicated that reproductive shoots raft kilometres in a single generation (e.g., Harwell and Orth 2002; Tulipani and Lipcius 2014; Hosokawa et al. 2015; Van Tussenbroek et al. 2016) or more rarely are dispersed by herbivores (Sumoski and Orth 2012). Direct observations at smaller spatial scales likely underestimate true dispersal (Levin 1981; Cain, Milligan, and Strand 2000) because stochastic long‐distance dispersal events are difficult to witness. Conversely, long‐distance dispersal estimates likely overestimate effective dispersal because the survival of recruits is typically low (Kaellstroem et al. 2008; Kendrick et al. 2012, 2017; McMahon et al. 2014).

Indirect evolutionary approaches such as isolation‐by‐distance (IBD) capture effective mean dispersal by integrating the outcome of dispersal and establishment over multiple generations. The significant but weak IBD slopes over enormous spatial scales (100 s of kilometres) documented in most Z. marina studies (Figure 5B) have been widely interpreted as reflecting rampant, widespread dispersal (e.g., Kendrick et al. 2012; McMahon et al. 2014). Yet, genetic differentiation at such enormous spatial scales is more likely to reflect disequilibrium between drift and migration, the presence of historical and/or ongoing barriers to gene flow or a combination of these factors (Slatkin 1993; Hutchison and Templeton 1999; Bradbury and Bentzen 2007) and therefore will likely yield a gross overestimation of dispersal distance.

It has been recommended that inference of dispersal should occur at spatial scales on the order of 10–50× mean dispersal (σ), where drift and migration equilibrate within a few generations (Slatkin 1993; Hardy and Vekemans 1999; Pinsky et al. 2016). The most distant populations across this study are 30 km apart, which is approximately 100–200× mean dispersal, meaning that our estimate is generated only slightly above the recommended scale. Based on theoretical expectations (Crow and Aoki 1982), we argue that drift and migration are likely close to or at equilibrium among these populations, given the lack of strong phylogeographic barriers along this stretch of shoreline as well as Z. marina's relatively low Ne (< 200), expected reproductive lifespan (approximately 3 years) and minimum time to reproduction (approximately 2 years; Yu et al. 2022).

Zostera marina meadows that span a depth gradient experience rapid declines in light, temperature and wave exposure with increasing depth (von Staats et al. 2021). In Massachusetts, shallow meadows have greater shoot density, taller plants and greater aboveground biomass than do deeper meadows (Figure 1), and they also tend to flower earlier (von Staats et al. 2021). It is possible that these depth‐related phenotypic differences may reflect the outcome of local adaptation at microgeographic scales, a type of isolation by environment (or IBE; Sexton, Hangartner, and Hoffmann 2014). Specifically, if our estimate of dispersal is not grossly overestimated, then the spatial scale of differentiation is comparable to that of the spatial scale of dispersal. Richardson et al. (2014) propose ‘Wright’, a spatial analogue to the ‘Haldane’ or ‘Darwin’ (sensu Hendry and Kinnison 1999) to quantify to what extent phenotypic divergence between habitats should be considered as microgeographic and thus more likely to reflect non‐neutral processes. Here, habitat‐related phenotypic differences appear to occur within a single genetic neighbourhood, defined by Wright (1969) as approximately 2 times the mean dispersal distance. In this case, deep and shallow ends of the meadow were sampled between 130 and 200 m apart and a single genetic neighbourhood is 200–400 m.

This inference, however, has several caveats. We assume that dispersal rates are equal in both parallel and perpendicular directions relative to the shore and that meadows produce equal numbers of recruits. This latter assumption appears not to be the case, as shallow beds produce more seeds on a per‐area basis (Figure 1; von Staats et al. 2021) and have greater recruitment rates (Figure 2) than deeper habitats. Moreover, while there is strong evidence that many traits we document have a genetic basis (Hughes, Stachowicz, and Williams 2009; Abbott and Stachowicz 2016), it is possible that plasticity plays a principal role. The relative importance of genetic and phenotypic effects or their interaction remain unmeasured in this system. We also do not know the mechanisms of selection, nor which life history stage they may operate on. Finally, it is possible that genetic barriers to gene flow may arise from the differences in phenology between depths (von Staats et al. 2021), which can reinforce patterns of differential selection. Thus, while microgeographic adaptation does have some support from our comparison of the spatial scales in environmental gradients versus seed dispersal, further testing is needed. Teasing apart the relative importance of geographic distance versus habitat requires a sampling design that genotypes adults at equal geographic scales within each habitat (i.e., 100–200 m apart within deep and shallow, which themselves are separated by 100–200 m), and assessing the source and fitness consequences of microgeographic differentiation is best tested with common garden or reciprocal transplant experiments that measure performance across these ecotones.

Indirect evidence for local adaptation can come from genome scans for outlier loci that might be responsible for the alternative phenotypes across habitats, though the power and interpretation of outlier analyses remain under close scrutiny (Nei, Suzuki, and Nozawa 2010; Lasky, Josephs, and Morris 2022). In Pacific coast Z. marina, Schiebelhut et al. (2023) used low‐coverage whole‐genome sequencing (lcWGS) to generate 446 K SNPs and identified regions that were repeatedly differentiated across thermal and tidal environments in two bays, a pattern that is unlikely to be due to non‐neutral processes (e.g., genetic drift nor mutational biases). Here, we found little evidence for consistent differences between depths; four SNPs were significantly different when analysed in a GLM framework, but this pattern was not confirmed by another outlier analysis. Thus, we tentatively conclude that differential selection across these depths did not yield a strong and consistent selective signal in the genome. It is possible that our genomic sampling (i.e., several thousand SNPs) may have precluded a robust test for the presence of genomic differentiation with depth, or that our sites differ in the molecular mechanism of adaptation.

Our results have important implications for the restoration and conservation of seagrasses, including Z. marina. First, only a fraction of the shoots in an seagrass meadow (between approximately 1 in 500 and 1 in 9000 genetically unique plants) contribute to the gene pool in each generation. Both sets of contemporary Ne estimates (< 200) are generally smaller than previous estimates in Z. marina, likely because previous studies did not account for overlapping generations, used less accurate estimators of Ne, were based on lower‐resolution markers or reflect some mix of these explanations (e.g., Reusch, Stam, and Olsen 2000; Kim et al. 2017; Martínez‐García, Hansson, and Hollander 2021). The ratio of effective to census size in Z. marina is thus more similar to marine animal (Hauser and Carvalho 2008; ranging from 10−2 to 10−8) and algal species (approximately 10−3; Coyer et al. 2008) with high fecundities, mortalities and variance in reproductive success (Hedgecock and Pudovkin 2011) than to terrestrial plants and animals (approximately 10−1; Frankham 1995). As a result, Z. marina beds may have less genetic variation to adapt to environmental change than is predicted from locally high abundance.

Second, the restricted spatial scale of dispersal suggests that local maintenance and expansion of these meadows is due to both clonal growth and localised seed dispersal. Expansion to other locations that are 1s to 10s of kilometres away is clearly possible (Harwell and Orth 2002), but our estimates suggest that these are likely the consequence of rare, stochastic events. The rate at which new seagrass beds are colonised will likely be low without direct intervention and active restoration efforts, especially for those meadows in which seed dispersal is more rare (e.g., Ries et al. 2024). Finally, large numbers of individuals (as proposed for seed‐based restoration efforts) or a targeted selection of particular genotypes (Katwijk et al. 2016; Vanderklift et al. 2020; Pazzaglia et al. 2021) will need to be incorporated into these efforts to provide enough genetic diversity for adaptive responses to future environmental changes.
