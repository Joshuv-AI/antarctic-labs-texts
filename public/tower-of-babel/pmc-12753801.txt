Alterations in neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative biomarkers among long-term residents of a critically polluted area: a cross-sectional comparative study
Authors: Rakesh Balachandar, Ankit Viramgami, Dhirendra Pratap Singh, Nikhil Kulkarni, Beena Chudasama, P. Sivaperumal, Kuldip Upadhyay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ambient air pollution is increasingly recognized as an emerging risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases. However, evidence from community-based biomarker studies in highly polluted Indian regions remains sparse. To investigate the neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative effects of chronic exposure to ambient air pollutants in long-term residents of a critically polluted area compared to a control region. This cross-sectional study included 203 adults (aged 40–60) residentially exposed to critical levels of air pollutants for ≥ 10 years and 202 geo-demographically matched controls residing at locations with very low / minimal air pollution. Air pollutant levels across all seasons were measured according standard protocols. Blood samples were analyzed for neurological biomarkers (viz. Aβ1−42, Total Tau, α-Synuclein, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) using ELISA. Additionally, demographic, clinical (blood pressure, random blood sugar, lipid profile) and occupational data were collected. Appropriate, descriptive, comparative and regression statistics were applied after checking for the normality. Annual PM2.5 and ozone concentrations were significantly higher at the exposed site (PM2.5: 69.76 ± 15.99 µg/m³; ozone: 33.76 ± 11.58 µg/m³) compared to controls (p < 0.001). Exposed participants showed significantly elevated GFAP (p < 0.001) and Aβ1−42 (p = 0.044), and significantly reduced levels of total tau, α-synuclein, and BDNF (p < 0.001), suggesting glial activation and impaired neuroprotection. Regression analyses confirmed exposure as a key predictor of biomarker variance, independent of age, BMI, blood pressure, and lipid levels. Chronic exposure to critical levels of ambient air pollutant is associated with subclinical alterations in neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative plasma biomarkers. These findings underscore the potential for air pollution to contribute to neurological dysfunction and support the need for public health interventions and longitudinal studies. Further, plasma-based biomarkers replicated results previously reported using cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and post-mortem tissue samples, thereby enabled minimally invasive detection of neurobiological alterations at the community level, supporting their potential utility in population-level environmental health research.

Introduction

Ambient air pollution is emergingly recognized as a significant environmental determinant of neurological health. Traditionally associated with respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, recent evidence suggests that long-term exposure to pollutants such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ozone may also heighten the risk of neurodegeneration1,2. Additionally, these pollutants, on crossing the blood–brain barrier, could trigger neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and pathological protein accumulation, culminating as neuronal death3,4.

Recent epidemiological studies report the association between chronic PM2.5 exposure and increased risk of cognitive decline, and alterations in brain structure and function5–7. Pre-clinical studies suggested exposure to air pollutants aggravated amyloid beta (Aβ) accumulation, τ (tau) hyperphosphorylation, glial activation, and synaptic dysfunction8,9.

Clinical studies investigating the association between exposure to air pollutants and neuroinflammation and –degeneration have often reported Aβ1−42, total τ, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), α-synuclein, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or post-mortem samples10,11. Recent progress in diagnostic methodologies—including automated assays, mass spectrometry, and highly sensitive immunoassays—has made it possible to quantify these proteins using blood specimens12. These technological improvements extend the ability to assess neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory disease burden beyond clinical settings to population-based evaluations13. Utilizing these innovations, a recent investigation linked exposure to air pollutants with neurodegenerative processes by measuring plasma amyloid concentrations in a community-dwelling elderly cohort14.

Despite this growing body of international evidence linking exposure to air pollution and neurogeneration, and neuroinflammation is unavailable. The present study aims to characterize the serum profile of neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative biomarkers (Aβ1–42, total τ, α-synuclein, BDNF, and GFAP) among individuals residing in critically polluted areas, as designated by regulatory authorities, and to compare these profiles with those of individuals from regions with relatively lower ambient air pollution. By integrating environmental monitoring, demographic and clinical profiling, and advanced biomarker quantification, the study provides a more robust understanding of how sustained exposure to critical pollutant levels may influence neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative pathways.

Methodology

Selection of study site and control site

The selection of participants and their demographic details have been previously described in detail13. Briefly, the study adopted a site-based exposure classification to compare individuals residing in areas with contrasting levels of ambient air pollution.

Participants categorized under the exposed group were residents of, an industrial area located within the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation limits. Critically Polluted Area (CPA) are identified by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) based on its high Comprehensive Environmental Pollution Index (CEPI) score (> 70), indicating sustained and significant environmental pollution from industrial sources15. As per data from CPCB’s Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring System16, the annual average concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10 were 42.43 ± 18.92 µg/m³ and 106.66 ± 36.43 µg/m³, respectively, indicating a considerable burden of particulate matter. Gaseous pollutant levels included NO (5.28 ± 3.33 µg/m³), NO₂ (26.42 ± 11.56 µg/m³), NOₓ (16.63 ± 7.51 ppb), SO₂ (32.73 ± 19.00 µg/m³), CO (0.81 ± 0.49 mg/m³), and ozone (37.64 ± 16.74 µg/m³), with ozone levels approaching regulatory thresholds. Meteorological parameters indicated moderate relative humidity (RH: 52.79 ± 22.48%), average wind speed (WS: 3.83 ± 2.31 m/s), predominant wind direction (WD: 179.41 ± 78.39°), solar radiation (SR: 134.93 ± 53.44 W/m²), average ambient temperature (AT: 29.48 ± 4.35 °C), mean barometric pressure (1001.03 ± 0.13 mmHg), and low vertical wind speed (VWS: 0.81 ± 0.44 m/s). These annual average conditions reflect limited atmospheric dispersion potential, contributing to pollutant build up and chronic community exposure—factors that validate the area’s designation as a CPA.

In contrast, the control group consisted of participants residing in non-industrial, predominantly residential zones with minimal vehicular traffic and industrial activities. Peri-urban areas of Gandhinagar city, located approximately 35–40 km from the industrial cluster identified for critical polluted area, were selected as the control site. These areas are known for better urban planning, greener infrastructure, and lower average concentrations of air pollutants as per CPCB monitoring data. Importantly, both sites fall under similar meteorological zones, minimizing confounding due to climatic variability.

The selection of study and control sites was based on long-term air quality surveillance reports, land-use classification, and CPCB data to ensure distinct environmental exposure profiles while maintaining socio-demographic comparability.

Recruitment of participants

As part of an exploratory approach, the study initially aimed to recruit approximately 200 adult participants aged 40–60 years from each of the two selected sites—one representing a high-exposure area (Critically Polluted Area) and the other a low-exposure (control) environment. This age group was chosen to capture early biological alterations potentially associated with prolonged environmental exposure while minimizing confounding from advanced age-related neurodegenerative changes.

A stepwise depiction of the recruitment process is presented in the flow diagram as Fig. 1. Following inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 405 adults were enrolled. Of these, 203 participants (103 males and 100 females) residing in Critically Polluted Area (CPA) were included in the exposure group. All exposed participants had lived in the area for a minimum of 10 years prior to recruitment. In parallel, 202 participants (94 males and 108 females) from selected areas of Gandhinagar, were included as the control group.

The recruitment strategy ensured balanced sex distribution and matching on age and socio-demographic variables across both groups to minimize confounding and strengthen the validity of intergroup comparisons. Participants were enrolled using a stratified, site-specific outreach model that included community health workers, resident welfare associations, and informal neighborhood networks.

Inclusion criteria comprised adults aged 40–60 years who had continuously resided at their respective site for at least 10 years. Exclusion criteria included individuals with a past diagnosis or on current treatment for neurological, psychiatric conditions, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, renal, hepatic disorders and those with a documented family history of hereditary neurodegenerative diseases.

Study details were communicated verbally and in writing through trusted local stakeholders. Interested individuals were invited to attend designated data collection venues, including primary health centres, community halls, and temporary mobile field stations established within the localities. At these sites, eligibility screening, informed consent, and data collection were conducted.

Consenting eligible participants were interviewed using a pre-validated, semi-structured questionnaire to collect information on demographic and socio-economic background, occupational history, medical status, lifestyle behaviours, and dietary habits. Trained field investigators also conducted general and system-based clinical examinations. Venous blood samples were obtained using standardized protocols for biomarker analysis. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Human Ethics Committee, and all procedures adhered to the national ethical guidelines for biomedical research involving human participants and ‘Helsinki declarations.

Biological sample collection

Trained laboratory personnel collected 5 mL of venous blood from each participant using EDTA-coated vacutainers (BECTON DICKINSON, Cat#367864). Samples were transported under cold chain conditions to the laboratory. Plasma was separated through standard centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Assessment of neurodegenerative markers

Levels of specific plasma biomarkers related to neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration—namely Aβ1–42, total τ, α-Synuclein, BDNF, and GFAP—were quantified using high-sensitivity sandwich ELISA kits (Elab Science Inc., USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Detection ranges for the respective biomarkers were: Aβ1–42 (15.63–1000 pg/mL), Total τ (7.81–500 pg/mL), α-Synuclein (15.63–1000 pg/mL), BDNF (31.25–2000 pg/mL), and GFAP (15.63–1000 pg/mL). Appropriate quality control measures were applied to rule out any technical anomalies, with missing values attributed only to measurements falling below the detectable limit.

Additional biochemical and clinical assessments

Serum levels of total cholesterol, triglycerides, and creatinine were measured using the Meril Autoquant 100 analyzer. Anthropometric data (height and weight) and blood pressure readings were obtained using standardized and calibrated equipment, as described in previous studies17. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the formula: weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²).

Ambient air quality monitoring

Airborne concentrations of PM2.5 and ozone were assessed following CPCB guidelines under the National Ambient Air Quality Standards15. A total of 96 samples were collected during March 2023 to March 2025 from each study and control site across three seasons as defined by the Indian Meteorological Department: winter (December to early April), summer (April to June), and post-monsoon (October to December). Sampling was conducted continuously for 24 h on Wednesdays and Thursdays to ensure consistency and to represent typical weekday environmental conditions, minimizing variability due to industrial shutdowns or weekend traffic fluctuations.

Statistical analysis

Data were initially evaluated for distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The frequency of readings exceeding the regulatory limits specified by NAAQS for PM₂.₅ and O₃ was compared between the CPA and control sites using the Chi-square test.

Chi-square (χ²) test and t-test were respectively used for categorical and continuous variables. In view of non-normal distribution, plasma biomarkers (viz. Aβ1−42, Total τ, α-Synuclein, BDNF, and GFAP) were reported as median & IQR, and the group differences were assessed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test. The association between plasma biomarkers and chronic exposure to critical pollutants were explored using linear regression models, regressing covariates (e.g., age, BMI, blood pressure, cholesterol and triglycerides). Regression models were constructed separately for each biomarker, and results are presented as unstandardized β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals. Model fit was evaluated using regression coefficients (R) and significance values from ANOVA. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

Current exploratory study aimed to characterize the plasma levels of key neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory biomarkers among individuals chronically exposed to high levels of ambient air pollution, specifically PM2.5, in comparison to a demographically similar population residing in a region with relatively lower pollutant exposure. This study offers novel community-level evidence linking chronic exposure to elevated ambient PM2.5 and ozone levels with alterations in plasma neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative biomarkers, including Aβ1–42, total τ, α-synuclein, BDNF, and GFAP. Our findings align with and extend existing literature by providing plasma based biomarker- data from an Indian Critically Polluted Area (CPA), a context previously underrepresented in neurotoxicological research. Importantly, unlike previous studies that largely relied on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or post-mortem tissue to assess such neurobiological changes, the present investigation provides a non-invasive approach by profiling these markers in peripheral plasma samples—making it potentially more scalable and applicable for population-level surveillance.

The environmental data indicate a clear disparity in pollutant exposure between the two sites, supported by objective seasonal air quality monitoring data. Notably, the critically polluted site recorded significantly higher frequencies of PM2.5 levels exceeding the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) threshold of 60 µg/m³, with exceedances in over 72% of annual measurements, compared to only 22.9% at the control site (p < 0.001). Seasonal differences were particularly marked during summer, with 93.8% exceedance at the exposed site versus 18.8% at the control site.

Recruitment was demographically balanced with matched age and sex distributions, although a higher prevalence of obesity was observed in the exposed group suggesting possible metabolic stress associated with residents of exposed group. Interestingly, prevalence of hypertension, hyperglycemia and dyslipidaemia did not differ significantly between the groups.

Among the five biomarkers studied, exposed participants exhibited significantly elevated plasma concentrations of Aβ₁₋₄₂ and GFAP, while showing reduced levels of total τ, α-synuclein, and BDNF. The observed elevation in Aβ1−42 among exposed individuals, supports prior findings that air pollution may accelerate amyloidogenic processing, a hallmark of Alzheimer’s pathology11,18. Specifically, exposure to PM2.5 and ozone has been shown to increase Aβ accumulation in both human and animal studies, possibly through oxidative stress and microglial activation pathways10,19. Supporting this evidence of association, Calderon et al. (2004) reported similar increases in Aβ in children exposed to high pollution, suggesting lifelong vulnerability and reinforcing the present study observations20.

The present study observed a marked reduction in total τ and α-synuclein levels among individuals with chronic exposure to high ambient air pollution, which diverges from conventional expectations, as both proteins are typically elevated in neurodegenerative conditions. However, prior studies have reported mixed findings regarding their association with air pollutant exposure. Notably, Li et al.21 reported significantly elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) total τ levels in cognitively healthy older adults exposed to air pollution, whereas Casey et al.10 found no significant association between air pollution and τ levels. Interestingly, Wang et al.22 observed an inverse association between CSF α-synuclein and pollutant exposure. These discrepancies may reflect the complex pathophysiological responses elicited by chronic low-dose pollutant exposure. It has been hypothesized that reductions in peripheral or circulating τ and α-synuclein may result from intracellular sequestration, altered transport across the blood-brain barrier, or impaired clearance mechanisms—processes potentially triggered by sustained pollutant exposure9,12. Such findings highlight the need for nuanced interpretation of these biomarkers in environmental exposure contexts and call for further investigation into their mechanistic underpinnings.

Taking into account the role of BDNF in synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and neuronal resilience, a significant decrease in the exposed group increases their risk of neurotropic deficits23. Previous investigations have also reported reduced BDNF levels in pollution-exposed humans, indicating impaired neuroprotective signalling as a potential mechanistic pathway toward cognitive decline24–26.

In contrast, GFAP elevation among exposed individuals indicates astrocytic activation, a hallmark of neuroinflammation. This is in agreement with studies by Block et al. (2012) and Calderón-Garcidueñas et al. (2008)1,3, which identified GFAP upregulation in response to long-term air pollutant exposure. Elevated GFAP may reflect glial response to ongoing oxidative and inflammatory insults in the central nervous system27.

Importantly, the association between some of these markers and chronic exposure to air pollutants were reinforced by regression analysis, factoring in covariates viz. age, BMI, blood pressure, and lipid profile. These findings complement previous cohort studies that adjusted for cardiovascular/metabolic factors and still found strong pollution-neurodegeneration associations5,6,14.

To summarize, plasma biomarker analysis revealed that participants in the exposed group exhibited significantly altered profiles of neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative markers similar to those previously reported using CSF and post-mortem tissue samples. Compared to controls, exposed group had lower plasma concentrations of Total τ, α-synuclein, and BDNF, and higher levels of Aβ1−42 and GFAP—all statistically significant differences. These results extend prior evidence by demonstrating that chronic exposure to PM2.5 may be associated with both neurodegenerative changes and astroglial activation, now observed through peripheral blood-based biomarkers.

The reduction in BDNF—a key neurotrophin involved in synaptic plasticity and neuronal survival, and the elevated GFAP levels are suggestive of reactive astrogliosis, a hallmark of neuroinflammation and glial response to sustained central nervous system stress. Importantly, linear regression models—adjusted for age, sex, BMI, lipid profile, and blood pressure—confirmed the statistical association between residence in a critically polluted area and key biomarkers. These findings underscore the plausibility that ambient air pollution, particularly in regions exceeding regulatory PM2.5 thresholds, may contribute to early neuropathological alterations, even in cognitively healthy adults.

Present study is perhaps the earliest Indian study to report an association between chronic exposure to ambient air pollution and plasma biomarkers indicative of neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation among community-dwelling middle-aged and elderly adults. This study’s strengths include the use of validated biomarker assays, rigorous control selection, seasonal pollutant monitoring, and adjustment for metabolic confounders. This study, while pioneering in its application of plasma biomarkers to assess the neurological impact of ambient air pollution in a community-based setting, has certain limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer causality or establish temporal relationships between pollutant exposure and neurobiological alterations. Although the analysis adjusted for key covariates such as age, BMI, blood pressure, and lipid levels, the potential for residual confounding remains, particularly from unmeasured The site-based classification of exposure—residence in a critically polluted area versus a control region—may not fully capture individual-level exposure variability, especially in the absence of personal exposure monitoring or indoor air quality assessments. Participant recruitment through community outreach rather than random sampling may have introduced selection bias, potentially affecting the representativeness of the findings. Other notable limitations include lack of neuroimaging or cognitive assessments, validation of plasma markers with brain tissue / CSF markers, and inability to account for genetic susceptibility (e.g., APOE status), which are known modifiers of pollutant sensitivity9.

These findings highlight a pressing public health concern: subclinical neurodegeneration and / neuroinflammation may be triggered among residents of Critically Polluted Areas (CPAs), where sustained exposure to PM2.5 and ozone persists above national safety limits15,28. Future research should prioritize longitudinal cohort designs that track biomarker trajectories and cognitive outcomes, incorporate neuropsychological assessments to link molecular changes with functional impairments, pathophysiological mechanisms, reversibility of damage on cessation of further exposure and explore gene–environment interactions to identify vulnerable subpopulations. Broader replication across diverse geographic and demographic settings will be essential to enhance generalizability and strengthen global efforts to address the neurotoxic burden of ambient air pollution2,4. Until further evidence are available, current observations reiterate the adverse effect of exposure to chronic air pollution and warrant early detection strategies, guide region-specific interventions, and support evidence-based regulatory policies to mitigate pollution-linked health risks.

Conclusion

This study suggests that long-term residence in areas with elevated ambient air pollution, particularly higher PM2.5 levels, may be associated with measurable alterations in plasma biomarkers linked to neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. Compared to individuals from lower-pollution regions, participants from critically polluted areas showed higher levels of GFAP and Aβ₁₋₄₂, and lower levels of totalτ, α-synuclein, and BDNF— patterns that could be indicative of glial activation, reduced neurotrophic support, and possible neurobiological stress. Some of these differences persisted after adjusting for potential confounding factors, including metabolic and cardiovascular variables. While causality cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, the minimally invasive use of blood-based biomarkers offers a promising approach for investigating environmental impacts on neurological health at the community level. These findings warrant further investigation through longitudinal studies to clarify the temporal dynamics and potential clinical relevance of such biomarker alterations in relation to air pollution exposure. Public health strategies aimed at reducing ambient pollutant levels may have ancillary benefits in protecting neurological health, particularly in high-exposure populations.
