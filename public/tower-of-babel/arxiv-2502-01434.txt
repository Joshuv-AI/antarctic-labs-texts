Regularity and positivity of solutions of the Consensus-Based Optimization equation: unconditional global convergence
Massimo Fornasier, Lukang Sun

Abstract
Introduced in R. Pinnau, et al. (Math. Models Methods Appl. Sci., 2017) , Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) has rapidly emerged as a significant breakthrough in global optimization. This straightforward yet powerful multi-particle, zero-order optimization method draws inspiration from Simulated Annealing and Particle Swarm Optimization. Using a quantitative mean-field approximation, CBO dynamics can be described by a nonlinear Fokker-Planck equation with degenerate diffusion, which does not follow a gradient flow structure. In this paper, we demonstrate that solutions to the CBO equation remain positive and maintain full support. Building on this foundation, we establish the unconditional global convergence of CBO methods to global minimizers. Our results are derived through an analysis of solution regularity and the proof of existence for smooth, classical solutions to a broader class of drift-diffusion equations, despite the challenges posed by degenerate diffusion.



Keywords:
global optimization,
consensus-based optimization,
nonlinear Fokker–Planck equations, degenerate diffusion, regularity and positivity of solutions

Introduction

Traditional mathematical optimization techniques, such as gradient descent and quasi-Newton methods, rely on local higher-order information to guide the search for global minimizers. However, due to their reliance on local information, these approaches often fail when applied to nonconvex and nonsmooth problems, which pose the greatest challenge in mathematical optimization.
Metaheuristic algorithms, including Simulated Annealing, Genetic Algorithms, Particle Swarm Optimization, Ant Colony Optimization, and various evolutionary strategies

CBO not only demonstrates strong empirical success in solving a wide range of global optimization problems but, even more surprisingly, also comes with rigorous theoretical guarantees and convergence rates for a broad class of nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization problems; see our discussion below for details.

The equations defining the iterates
V^{i}_{k}
of the CBO algorithm read (
i=1,\dots,N
labelling the particles, and
k=0,\dots,K
denoting the iterates)

\displaystyle V^{i}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=V^{i}_{k}-\Delta t\lambda(V^{i}_{k}-v_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho}_{k}^{N}))+\sqrt{\Delta t}\sigma\|V^{i}_{k}-v_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho}_{k}^{N})\|_{2}B_{k}^{i},

(1)

where

\displaystyle v_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho}_{k}^{N})=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(V_{k}^{i})}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_{k}^{i}\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(V_{k}^{i}),

(2)

and
f
is the objective function to be minimized,
v_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho}_{k}^{N})
is the so-called consensus point,
\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(v)=e^{-\alpha{f}(v)}
is the Gibbs weight, and
B^{i}_{k}
is an independent standard Gaussian random vector. The initial particles
V_{0}^{i}
are drawn i.i.d. at random according to a given probability distribution
\rho_{0}
.

The algorithm (
1
) has a straightforward formulation (it can be implemented in just a few lines of code
[
2
]
) with a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N)
and is highly parallelizable; see, for instance,
[
5
,
18
]
. Additionally, it relies exclusively on pointwise evaluations of the objective function
f
, meaning that no higher-order derivatives are required.

The core mechanism of CBO involves a combination of a relaxed drift toward the weighted mean of the particles and their stochastic perturbation, embodying an exploitation-exploration strategy. Notably, the original formulation of CBO, given in equation (
1
), was developed under the restrictive assumption that the objective function
f
possesses a
unique
global minimizer
v^{*}
. More recent adaptations extend the approach to accommodate multiple global minimizers
[
12
,
27
]
.

The proofs of convergence to global minimizers as devised in
[
14
,
24
]
rely on the following three-level approximation argument. The first approximation goes for
\Delta t\to 0
: standard results, see
[
44
]
, allow to interpret (
1
) as the Euler–Maruyama numerical approximation for
\Delta t\to 0
to solutions

,
i=1,\dots,N
of the empirical system

d{V}^{i}_{t}=-\lambda\left(V^{i}_{t}-v_{\alpha}(\hat{\rho}_{t}^{N})\right)dt+\sigma\|V^{i}_{t}-v_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho}^{N}_{t})\|_{2}dB_{t}^{i},\quad v_{\alpha}(\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{N})=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(V_{t}^{i})}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_{t}^{i}\omega_{\alpha}^{f}(V_{t}^{i}).

(3)

The second approximation level comes as a quantitative mean-field limit for
N\to\infty
, see
[
24
, Proposition 3.11]
and also
[
29
, Theorem 2.6]
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{i=1,\dots,N}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\overline{V}^{i}_{t}-V^{i}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq CN^{-1},

(4)

where
\overline{V}^{i}_{t}
are
N
particles satisfying the self-fulfilling mono-particle dynamics

d\overline{V}_{t}=-\lambda\left(\overline{V}_{t}-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\right)dt+\sigma\|\overline{V}_{t}-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\|_{2}dB_{t},

(5)

with
\overline{V}^{i}_{0}=V_{0}^{i},i=1,\ldots,N
,
and
v_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{\int v\omega^{f}_{\alpha}(v)d\rho(v)}{\int\omega^{f}_{\alpha}(v)d\rho(v)}
and
\rho_{t}=\operatorname{Law}(\overline{V}_{t})
, which fulfills the nonlinear Fokker–Planck equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\lambda\operatorname{div}\big(\!\left(v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\right)\rho_{t}\big)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\big(\|v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\rho_{t}\big),

(6)

here

is the variable of the equation. Notably, the existence of probability measure solutions of (

) was established in
[
14
]
through the analysis of (
5
).
The third and last approximation level is provided by the large time asymptotics of the solution
\rho_{t}
. Namely, the global convergence is resulting from the quantification of the approximation

at all times, for
\alpha>0

and the crucial technical assumption that

has mass around

, i.e.,

\rho_{0}(B_{r}(v^{*}))>0,\quad r>0.

(7)

See
[

, Proposition 4.6]

Consequently, under mild assumptions on the objective function

–local Lipschitz continuity and a local growth around the global minimizer–,
[

, Theorem 3.7]
shows that the Wasserstein distance

is a natural Lyapunov functional for (
6
) at finite time, with exponential decay

W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\delta_{v^{*}})\leq W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\delta_{v^{*}})e^{-(1-\theta)(2\lambda-d\sigma^{2})t},

(8)

for a suitable
\theta\in(0,1)
for

and

W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{T^{*}},\delta_{v^{*}})\leq\epsilon.

(9)

The combination of these three level approximation as in

yields the quantitative convergence in probability of the algorithm (

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_{K}^{i}-v^{*}\|\leq\varepsilon\Bigg)\geq 1-\left[\varepsilon^{-1}(C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t+C_{\mathrm{MFA}}N^{-1}+\epsilon)-\delta\right].

(10)

At this point, we turn our attention to the technical assumption (
7
), which was introduced in the earlier work
[
14
,
24
]
. This condition constrains the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
used for sampling particles, requiring it to include the global minimizer

in its support (for instance
\rho_{0}
may be a Gaussian distribution). However, from the results of practical numerical experiments, this assumption did not seem necessary for the method to achieve global convergence. This suggests that CBO actually converges

.
The difficulty in providing an obvious statement about condition (
7

6
).

One of the results of this paper is to prove that, as long as
\rho_{0}
is a smooth density–not necessarily requiring

– and the dimensionality of the problem

, the density
\rho_{t}(v)>0
for all

and for all

4.5

7
) completely superfluous and, combined with

, Theorem 3.7, Theorem 3.8]

The positivity result we obtain leverages regularity results of solutions of (
6
), in particular we show existence and uniqueness of classical solutions. Although, we believe that one could obtain positivity even without such regularity results, the second goal of this paper is in fact to have a finer description of the solution

. Indeed, while existence of probability measure solutions was established in
[
14
]
, neither well-posedness nor regularity results were known so far.
We will do that by proving intermediate results for more general (linear) drift-diffusion equations with degenerate diffusion of the type (

)-(

The regularity will be consequence of a nontrivial interplay between the degenerate diffusion and drift terms. As long as the diffusion term “dominates” the drift in a controlled manner (see Assumption
2.5
), then there is no solution degeneracy occurring.
Let us conclude this introduction by mentioning that only after the preparation of the present work and in the phase of its revision, we became aware of the recent study
[
51
]
, which also
investigates the well-posedness of (
6

[
51

establish the existence of regular solutions via a compactness argument applied to a regularized version of the PDE (

Remark 3.3

\displaystyle\int_{B_{i}}\partial_{t}\rho_{i}^{K}(v,t)\psi(v,t)dv

\displaystyle+\int_{B_{i}}\rho_{i}^{K}(v,t)\psi(v,t)dv+\int_{B_{i}}g_{i}(v,t)\psi(v,t)dv.

In rest of this subsection, for simplicity, we will omit

in
\rho_{i}^{K}

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{i}}\Big\langle G_{i}\nabla\rho,\nabla\rho\Big\rangle dv+\int_{B_{i}}\Big\langle\boldsymbol{J}_{i},\nabla\rho\Big\rangle\rho dv+\int_{B_{i}}\rho^{2}dv+\int_{B_{i}}g_{i}\rho dv

Next, for

For

\displaystyle+C(T,d,m,\epsilon,\norm{g}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))},\norm{\varrho}_{H^{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}).

The right hand sides of (

For

Next, for

\{a_{2j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

\{a_{(n+1)j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
of

.

Finally, we define the diagonal subsequence

Hence, by construction, we have

Since the sequence

K_{j}
, the Banach–Alaoglu theorem (see the predual of
W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i}))
in Appendix
7
) implies that, up to extracting a further subsequence, still denoted by
\{K_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
, there exists

\rho_{i}^{\prime}\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i}))

such that

\rho_{i}^{K_{j}}\rightharpoonup^{\ast}\rho_{i}^{\prime}\quad\text{in }W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i})).

Moreover,

\|\rho_{i}^{\prime}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i}))}\leq C.

On the other hand, along the same subsequence, the convergence of the
coefficients used to define
\rho_{i}
implies that, for every fixed

t\in[0,T]
,

\rho_{i}^{K_{j}}(\cdot,t)\rightharpoonup\rho_{i}(\cdot,t)\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(B_{i}).

Indeed, for each basis function
\psi_{i,k}
, we have

\int_{B_{i}}\rho_{i}^{K_{j}}(v,t)\psi_{i,k}(v)\,dv=C_{i,k}^{K_{j}}(t)\longrightarrow C_{i,k}(t)=\int_{B_{i}}\rho_{i}(v,t)\psi_{i,k}(v)\,dv.

Since the linear span of
\{\psi_{i,k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is dense in

L^{2}(B_{i})
, and the sequence
\rho_{i}^{K_{j}}(\cdot,t)
is uniformly
bounded in
L^{2}(B_{i})
, this coefficient convergence extends to all test
functions in
L^{2}(B_{i})
, giving the claimed weak convergence.
Therefore, the weak-
*
limit
\rho_{i}^{\prime}
coincides with
\rho_{i}
. Consequently,

\rho_{i}\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i})),\qquad\|\rho_{i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i}))}\leq C.

This verifies the estimates (
57
) and (
60
).

Next, we invoke the following compactness lemma, which may be viewed as a variant of the Aubin–Lions lemma. For completeness, its proof is postponed to Appendix

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded smooth domain, and let
m\geq 0
be an integer.
Assume that
M>m+\frac{d}{2}
. Suppose that

\rho_{j}\rightharpoonup^{\ast}\rho\quad\text{in }W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{M}(\Omega)).

Then

\rho_{j}\to\rho\quad\text{strongly in }\mathcal{C}(0,T;C^{m}(\Omega)).

We apply the above lemma to
\rho_{i}^{K_{j}},\rho_{i}
, then Fatou’s lemma yields

\displaystyle\sum_{0<s\leq m+1}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{i}}G_{i}\|\rho_{i}^{(s)}\|^{2}\,dv\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\sum_{0<s\leq m+1}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{i}}G_{i}\|(\rho_{i}^{K_{j}})^{(s)}\|^{2}\,dv\,dt

(66)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(T,d,m+1,\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))},\|\varrho\|_{H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big)<\infty.

Thus the estimate (
58
) is also verified.

By the weak-

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T))

z^{\epsilon}\to z\qquad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)).

Passing to the limit in (
122
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\eta_{R,\delta}(v,t)(\rho(v,t)-\tilde{\rho}(v,t))\big|^{2}\,dvdt=0.

(123)

Therefore,

\eta_{R,\delta}(v,t)(\rho(v,t)-\tilde{\rho}(v,t))=0\qquad\text{for a.e. }(v,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T).

By construction,
\eta_{R,\delta}=1
on
B_{R}\times[\delta,T-\delta]
. Hence

\rho(v,t)=\tilde{\rho}(v,t)\qquad\text{for a.e. }(v,t)\in B_{R}\times[\delta,T-\delta].

Since
R>0
is arbitrary and
\delta>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small, we let
R\to\infty
and
\delta\to 0
to conclude that

\rho=\tilde{\rho}\qquad\text{a.e. in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T).

This proves uniqueness of weak solutions.

The condition
\epsilon\ll\delta
is used to ensure that the time mollification does not interact with the endpoints
t=0
and
t=T
. More precisely, for fixed
\delta>0
, we choose
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that the support of the mollified function remains strictly inside
(0,T)
on the region where the time cut-off
\chi^{\delta}_{[\delta,T-\delta]}
is active. Thus
h=\eta_{R,\delta}(z^{\epsilon})^{\epsilon}
is a smooth compactly supported function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)
, and it is admissible in the duality argument above.

Remark 3.12
.

The Galerkin approximation procedure described above also applies when the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is substituted with a compact manifold
\mathcal{M}
without boundary. Therefore, the main theorem remains valid for equations (
11
) and (
12
) on smooth compact manifolds. In fact, when
\mathcal{M}
is a smooth compact manifold without boundary, we can simply set
B_{i}=\mathcal{M}
and utilize the first
K
eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathcal{M}
in place of
\mathcal{H}_{i,K}
. Consequently, all the analyses from the previous sections continue to hold.

4
Applying the main theorem to CBO

Here we make the following assumption: the cost function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bounded from below with
\underline{f}:=\inf f
. Moreover, there exist constants
L_{f},M,c_{l}
and
c_{u}>0
such that

\begin{cases}|f(v)-f(u)|\leq L_{f}(\norm{v}+\norm{u})\norm{v-u}&\text{ for all }v,u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
f(v)-\underline{f}\leq c_{u}\left(1+\norm{v}^{2}\right)&\text{ for all }v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
f(v)-\underline{f}\geq c_{l}\norm{v}^{2}&\text{ for all }\norm{v}\geq M.\end{cases}

(124)

Then
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
ensures that the CBO dynamics

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\mathrm{div}\Big((v-v_{\alpha}(\rho,t))\rho\Big)+\Delta\Big(\norm{v-v_\alpha(\rho,t)}^{2}\rho\Big)

(125)

\displaystyle\rho(v,0)=\varrho(v),

here
v_{\alpha}(\rho,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ve^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho(v,t)/\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho(v,t)
,
has a solution in
\mathcal{C}(0,T,\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
(see Remark
4.2
below), which is also a weak solution defined in this work by Itô’s lemma. We denote this solution as
\bar{\rho}
and
v_{\alpha}(\bar{\rho},t)
is
1/2
-Hölder continuous in time, see also
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
.
Let
v_{\alpha}(t):=v_{\alpha}(\bar{\rho},t)
. Then, by Theorem
2.4
, the following linear equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\mathrm{div}\Big((v-v_{\alpha}(t))\rho\Big)+\Delta\Big(\norm{v-v_\alpha(t)}^{2}\rho\Big)

(126)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\Big(\norm{v-v_\alpha(t)}^{2}\nabla\rho\Big)+3\Big\langle v-v_{\alpha}(t),\nabla\rho\Big\rangle+3d\rho,

\displaystyle\rho(v,0)=\varrho(v),

where
G(v,t):=\norm{v-v_\alpha(t)}^{2},\boldsymbol{J}(v,t):=v-v_{\alpha}(t),g(v,t)=0
, satisfies Assumptions
2.5
,
2.6
and
3.7
. By Remark
3.8
equation (
126
) has a unique weak solution
\rho
and
\rho\in\Gamma_{G}
, thus
\bar{\rho}=\rho\in\Gamma_{G}
.

4.1
Uniqueness of the CBO dynamics

Now, we show that the measure solution to the non-linear equation (
125
) is unique.

Definition 4.1
.

We say
\rho
is a measure solution to the CBO dynamics, if
\rho\in\mathcal{C}(0,T;\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and for any
\psi(v,t)\in\mathcal{C}^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T])
with
\norm{\nabla\psi},\norm{\partial_i\partial_j\psi}
bounded, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\partial_{t}\psi(v,t)+\Delta\psi(v,t)\norm{v-v_\alpha(\rho,t)}^{2}d\rho(v,t)-\Big\langle\nabla\psi(v,t),v-v_{\alpha}(\rho,t)\Big\rangle\Big)d\rho(v,t)dt

(127)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(v,s)d\rho(v,s)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(v,0)d\varrho(v),

for any
s\in[0,T]
.

Remark 4.2
.

What we mean with
\rho\in\mathcal{C}(0,T;\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is more explicitly explained as follows: for each
t\in[0,T]
,
\rho(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the space of probability measure, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(v)d\rho(v,t)
is continuous for any
h\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the space of bounded continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

By Sobolev embedding, it is direct to derive that if
\psi\in\Gamma_{G}
, then
\psi
satisfies the condition in the above definition. Therefore, if
\rho
is a measure solution, then it is also a weak solution defined by Definition
2.3
.

Let
\rho
be a measure solution and choose the test functions
e^{-\alpha f(v)}
and
v_{i}e^{-\alpha f(v)},i=1,\ldots,d
which are all bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
so that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho(v,t)
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ve^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho(v,t)
are continuous on
[0,T]
, since
\rho\in\mathcal{C}(0,T;\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
; combine this with the fact that for each
t\in[0,T]
,
\|v_{\alpha}(\rho,t)\|<\infty
, then we have
v_{\alpha}(\rho,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ve^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho(v,t)/\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho(v,t)
is continuous on
[0,T]
. Thus by Theorem
2.4
, for this fixed
v_{\alpha}(\rho,t)
, the following equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\dot{\rho}=\Delta\Big(\norm{v-v_\alpha(\rho,t)}\dot{\rho}\Big)+\mathrm{div}\Big((v-v_{\alpha}(\rho,t))\dot{\rho}\Big),

(128)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(v,0)=\varrho,

has a unique weak solution, which we denote as
\dot{\rho}
. Such solution belongs to
\Gamma_{G}
, because
\rho
is a measure solution to equation (
128
), so it is also a weak solution, hence
\rho=\dot{\rho}\in\Gamma_{G}
. Since the law of the process
V_{t}
, which satisfies the following SDE

dV_{t}=-(V_{t}-v_{\alpha}(\rho,t))dt+\norm{V_t-v_\alpha(\rho,t)}dB_{t},

(129)

is also a measure solution (by Itô’s lemma, thus a weak solution) of (
128
), so
\rho
must be the law of the process
V_{t}
. So in summary, we have shown that any measure solution to the non-linear PDE (
125
) is in function class
\Gamma_{G}
and is the law of the distribution-dependent SDE

dV_{t}=-(V_{t}-v_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Law}(V_{t}),t))dt+\norm{V_t-v_\alpha(\operatorname{Law}(V_t),t)}dB_{t},

(130)

and vice versa by
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
. By
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
, the nonlinear SDE has a unique strong solution, so the nonlinear PDE (
125
) also has a unique measure solution, and this measure solution is in
\Gamma_{G}
.

4.2
Positivity of CBO solutions for
d>1

Since the measure solution of equation (
125
) is unique and in the class
\Gamma_{G}
, in what follows, for simplicity, we will write
v_{\alpha}(\rho,t)
as
v_{\alpha}(t)
, thus equation (
125
) becomes

\partial_{t}\rho(v,t)=\mathrm{div}((v-v_{\alpha}(t))\rho(v,t))+\Delta(\norm{v-v_{\alpha}(t)}^{2}\rho(v,t)).

(131)

Let
v=v+v_{\alpha}(t)
, then we have

\partial_{t}\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t)=\mathrm{div}(v\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t))+\Delta(\norm{v}^{2}\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t)).

(132)

Now, let
\tilde{\rho}(v,t):=\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t)
, then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}(v,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t)

(133)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t)+\Big\langle\nabla\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t),\frac{d}{dt}v_{\alpha}(t)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}(v\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t))+\Delta(\norm{v}^{2}\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t))

\displaystyle+\Big\langle\nabla\rho(v+v_{\alpha}(t),t),\frac{d}{dt}v_{\alpha}(t)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}(v\tilde{\rho}(v,t))+\Delta(\norm{v}^{2}\tilde{\rho}(v,t))+\Big\langle\nabla\tilde{\rho}(v,t),P(t)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\norm{v}^{2}\Delta\tilde{\rho}(v,t)+\Big\langle 5v+P(t),\nabla\tilde{\rho}(v,t)\Big\rangle+3d\tilde{\rho}(v,t),

here
P(t):=\frac{d}{dt}v_{\alpha}(t)
(we can prove this is bounded by assuming the growth rate of
\nabla f,\Delta f
at most polynomial, see Lemma
4.4
below).
At this point, we have to assume
d>1
.
Then on
B_{R}(0)\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)\times[0,T]
for any
0<\epsilon<R<\infty
, we have
\tilde{\rho}
is the classical solution to

\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}(v,t)=\norm{v}^{2}\Delta\tilde{\rho}(v,t)+\Big\langle 5v+P(t),\nabla\tilde{\rho}(v,t)\Big\rangle+3d\tilde{\rho}(v,t),

(134)

and on
B_{R}(0)\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)\times[0,T]
, the equation is uniform parabolic and then by the parabolic Harnack inequality see
[
22
, Section 7.1, Theorem 10]
, we have

\sup_{B_{R}(0)\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,t_{1})\leq C\inf_{B_{R}(0)\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,t_{2}),

(135)

for any
0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
. If
\rho(v,0)=\varrho
is not identically
0
, then we can find some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\varrho(B_{r}(v))>0
. Then by
[
24
, Proposition 23]
, we have
\rho(B_{r}(v),t_{1})>0
for any
t_{1}>0
and thus we can find
R>0
large enough and
\epsilon<0
small enough, such that

\sup_{B_{R}(0)\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,t_{1})>0,

(136)

and thus by the parabolic Harnack inequality, we have

\inf_{B_{R}(0)\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,t_{2})>0,

(137)

for any
t_{2}>t_{1}>0
. Since
R,\epsilon,t_{1},t_{2}
are arbitrary, so we can conclude that
\rho(v,t)>0
for any
v\not=v_{\alpha}(t)
, for any
t>0
.

Remark 4.3
.

To apply the parabolic Harnack inequality, we need
B_{R}(0)\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)
to be connected, this is only possible when
d>1
. Actually, when
d=1
, for the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(v,t)=\mathrm{div}((v-v^{*})\rho(v,t))+\Delta(\norm{v-v^*}^{2}\rho(v,t))

(138)

where
v^{*}
is any fixed value in
\mathbb{R}
, if the initial distribution
\rho(0,\cdot)=\varrho
has support included in
(-\infty,v^{*})
, then
\rho(t,v)=0
, for any
t\geq 0,v>v^{*}
. To prove it, just choose the test function
\phi(v)=\chi_{[v^{*},\infty)}(v)
, here
\chi_{[v^{*},\infty)}
is the indicator function of the set
[v^{*},\infty)
. Then by using integration by parts (which is applicable, since on the boundary
v-v^{*}=0
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int\phi(v)d\rho(t,v)=0,

(139)

thus
\int\phi(v)d\rho(t,v)=0
, for any
t\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume

\displaystyle\norm{\Delta f}\leq C(1+\norm{v}^{p}),

(140)

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla f}\leq C(1+\norm{v}^{q}),\text{ for some polynomial order of $p,q>0$}.

Then it holds
\norm{\frac{d}{dt}v_{\alpha}(t)}<C<\infty
.

Proof.

Use Itô formula to functions
e^{-\alpha f(v)},ve^{-\alpha f(v)}
, and obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ve^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}}=\frac{\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ve^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}}-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\frac{\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}},

(141)

where

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}ve^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\langle\nabla(ve^{-\alpha f(v)}),v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\Big\rangle d\rho_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta(ve^{-\alpha f(v)})\norm{v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_t)}^{2}d\rho_{t}

(142)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}+\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\langle\nabla f(v),v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\Big\rangle ve^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v\norm{v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_t)}^{2}\norm{\nabla f(v)}^{2}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}

\displaystyle-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v\norm{v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_t)}^{2}\Delta f(v)e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}-2\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_t)}^{2}\nabla f(v)e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t},

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\langle\nabla(e^{-\alpha f(v)}),v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\Big\rangle d\rho_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta(e^{-\alpha f(v)})\norm{v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_t)}^{2}d\rho_{t}

(143)

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\langle\nabla f(v),v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\Big\rangle e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}+\alpha^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_t)}^{2}\norm{\nabla f(v)}^{2}e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}

\displaystyle-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{v-v_{\alpha}(\rho_t)}^{2}\Delta f(v)e^{-\alpha f(v)}d\rho_{t}.

Therefore by a direct generalization of
[
14
, Lemma 3.3]
, see
[
29
, Theorem 2.3, Proposition A.3]
, and an application of the growth assumption on
\nabla f,\Delta f
, we have
\norm{\partial_tv_{\alpha}(t)}<C<\infty
.
∎

4.3
Conclusion

In summary in Section
4
, we proved the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let the objective
f
satisfies condition (
124
), then the non-linear PDE (
125
) has a unique measure solution
\rho
, and this solution belongs to function class
\Gamma_{G}
. If we further assume condition (
140
), then
\rho(v,t)>0
, for any
t>0,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{v_{\alpha}(\rho,t)\}
, for
d>1
.

Remark 4.6
.

For a connected smooth compact manifold
\mathcal{M}
without boundary and embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can consider the following CBO equation

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\lambda\mathrm{div}_{\mathcal{M}}\Big(P(v)\left(v-v_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}\right)\right)\rho_{t}\Big)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\|v-v_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}\right)\|^{2}\rho_{t}\right),\quad t>0,v\in\mathcal{M},

(144)

where
\lambda,\sigma>0
are constants and
P(v)
is the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space at
v\in\mathcal{M}
. The model (
144
) was introduced, for instance, in
[
23
]
for the case of smooth hypersurfaces (e.g., torus and hypersphere) . In this case of
\mathcal{M}
being an hypersurface, we have

P(v)=\mathrm{I}_{d}-(\nabla\operatorname{dist}(v,\mathcal{M}))(\nabla\operatorname{dist}(v,\mathcal{M}))^{\top}
, where the signed distance function is defined by
\operatorname{dist}(v,\mathcal{M}):=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{M}}\norm{v-u}
if

is on the exterior of
\mathcal{M}
, else
\operatorname{dist}(v,\mathcal{M}):=-\inf_{u\in\mathcal{M}}\norm{v-u}
, and it can be verified that all the assumptions are satisfied. Thus, by Remark
3.12
, Theorem
4.5
also holds to the CBO equation (
144
) on
\mathcal{M}
.

The following multivariable Faa Di Bruno formula is from

21

\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
is: if
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\left(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d}\right)

\boldsymbol{\nu}=

2.5

(151)

then using (

.

∎

7
A Predual of
W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i}))

Let

X:=H^{m}(B_{i}).

Then
X
is a Hilbert space, and its dual is

X^{\prime}=H^{-m}(B_{i}).

We want to identify a natural predual of

W^{1,\infty}(0,T;X).

Define the map

\Phi:W^{1,\infty}(0,T;X)\to X\times L^{\infty}(0,T;X)

by

\Phi u=\bigl(u(0),\partial_{t}u\bigr).

This map is linear and injective. Moreover, for every

(x,g)\in X\times L^{\infty}(0,T;X),

the formula

u(t)=x+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,ds

defines an element
u\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;X)
such that

u(0)=x,\qquad\partial_{t}u=g.

Hence
\Phi
is a linear isomorphism:

W^{1,\infty}(0,T;X)\simeq X\times L^{\infty}(0,T;X).

Since
X=H^{m}(B_{i})
is reflexive and separable, we have

X=(X^{\prime})^{\prime},\qquad L^{\infty}(0,T;X)=\bigl(L^{1}(0,T;X^{\prime})\bigr)^{\prime}.

Therefore

X\times L^{\infty}(0,T;X)=\left(X^{\prime}\times L^{1}(0,T;X^{\prime})\right)^{\prime}.

Substituting
X^{\prime}=H^{-m}(B_{i})
, we obtain the canonical predual

{\mathcal{P}_{i}:=H^{-m}(B_{i})\times L^{1}(0,T;H^{-m}(B_{i})),}

which is a normed space.

The corresponding duality pairing is

\left\langle(\eta,f),u\right\rangle=\left\langle\eta,u(0)\right\rangle_{H^{-m},H^{m}}+\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle f(t),\partial_{t}u(t)\right\rangle_{H^{-m},H^{m}}\,dt,

where

(\eta,f)\in H^{-m}(B_{i})\times L^{1}(0,T;H^{-m}(B_{i})),

and

u\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i})).

Thus, under the identification

u\longleftrightarrow\bigl(u(0),\partial_{t}u\bigr),

we have

{W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i}))\simeq\left[H^{-m}(B_{i})\times L^{1}(0,T;H^{-m}(B_{i}))\right]^{\prime}.}

Remark 7.1
.

This identification is isometric if one equips

W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i}))
with the norm

\|u\|_{*}=\|u(0)\|_{H^{m}(B_{i})}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(B_{i}))}.

This norm is equivalent to the usual norm

\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{m})}=\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m})}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m})}.

Therefore the predual above is canonical up to equivalent norms.
