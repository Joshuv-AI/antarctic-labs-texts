Evaluation of oral and dental health of institutionalized orphan children residing in children’s homes in Türkiye
Authors: Arzu Kockanat, Sukran Acipinar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral health is an integral part of general health. Studies evaluating the oral and dental health of orphaned children who have lost family support at an early age are relatively limited. The current study was aimed to evaluate the oral and dental health of institutionalized orphan children residing in the children’s homes institution model in Türkiye for the first time.MethodsIn this study, 90 institutionalized orphan children aged between 6 and 18 years residing in children’s homes in Sivas province were included. Before starting the study, legal permissions were obtained from official institutions and informed consent forms were approved by the children’s caregivers. The examination procedures were performed by two calibrated dentists, as recommended by the WHO. DMFT and deft, GI and PI indices were employed to assess dental caries experience, gingival inflammation, and the amount of plaque on the tooth surfaces, respectively. Additionally, periodontal status and treatment needs were evaluated using the CPITN index.ResultsThe mean value of DMFT index was 2.87 ± 2.41 in females and 1.55 ± 1.78 in males. The DMFT index was found to be significantly higher in females (p = 0.023). The mean value of deft index was 2.60 ± 1.67 in females and 3.47 ± 2.31 in males. The prevalence of decayed teeth was 54.54% in females and 63.04% in males. The prevalence of filled teeth was 38.88%. The prevalence of filled teeth was significantly higher in females (p = 0.017). The mean value of PI was 0.93 ± 0.45 in females and 1.15 ± 0.52 in males. The mean value of GI was 1.03 ± 0.51 in females and 1.37 ± 0.47 in males. The mean PI and GI values were significantly lower in females (p = 0.032, p = 0.002). CPI 1 score was 42.22% and CPI 2 score was 51.11%.ConclusionThis study revealed that orphaned children in children’s homes may receive more attention to their oral health, yet still face high rates of dental caries, calculus, and gingival bleeding. These findings underscore the need for targeted preventive oral health policies, including age-appropriate education, caregiver training, and regular access to dental care. Background

The family is the basic structure that plays the most important role in a child’s physical, emotional and mental development. Parental love, guidance, and education are crucial for a child’s healthy growth [1]. Family education shapes children’s tooth brushing habits, attitudes towards oral health and caries experiences, especially at an early age. The family is responsible for the prevention of dental caries, the early treatment of caries and the protection of oral and dental health [2, 3].

UNICEF defines an orphan as a child who has lost his/her family or family support before the age of eighteen. Socially, these children are considered as a vulnerable group in society [4, 5]. Children who grow up deprived of their family environment are brought up with service models developed within the social protection system. In commonly used traditional orphanage models, lack of individual care, lack of oral hygiene education, lack of regular dental check-ups, malnutrition, lack of access to hygiene materials, lack of caregivers and overcrowding are the main factors that endanger oral and dental health [6, 7].

In order to eliminate the aforementioned disadvantages of children residing in orphanages, the concept of children’s home care model has been adopted in Türkiye. The children’s home is an institution model in which 5–8 children between the ages of 0–18 live together considering their age and gender status, and are usually supported by professional caregivers [8, 9]. Orphans under institutional care may experience varying levels of risk regarding their oral and dental health, influenced by the structural and functional characteristics of the institutions in which they reside. The children’s homes, designed as family-type models, typically accommodate fewer children and provide individualized care, facilitating closer monitoring of daily oral hygiene practices. In contrast, traditional orphanages tend to be more crowded and organized in a more institutional manner, making it challenging to oversee individual hygiene. This environment may contribute to a higher prevalence of oral and dental health issues. Consequently, the structure of the institutions residing these children is a critical factor to consider when evaluating their oral and dental health status [10].

Oral health is of great importance as an integral part of general health. Dental caries and periodontal diseases are among the most common oral diseases and dental caries is one of the most common bacterial diseases in children and adolescents [11]. Microbial dental plaque is recognised as one of the most important predisposing factors for periodontal diseases. The accumulation of plaque on the tooth surface and the inability to remove it effectively leads to deterioration in oral and dental health [12]. Regular tooth brushing, flossing, routine dental visits, and oral and dental health education are essential components in preventing and managing these health issues.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends conducting epidemiological studies to assess the oral and dental health status of communities [13]. Although numerous studies have evaluated the oral and dental health of children in different populations, studies focusing specially on orphaned children are relatively limited. The literature indicates that this vulnerable population experiences poorer oral and dental health outcomes [14–17].

To the best of our knowledge, there is no study evaluating the oral and dental health of orphaned children residing in children’s homes institution model in Türkiye. The studies in the literature on institutionalized orphan children have mostly focused on dental caries, and there are very few studies evaluating the prevalence of dental caries and periodontal status together. The aim of this study is to evaluate both dental caries prevalence and periodontal status of institutionalized orphan children residing in children’s homes, where a small number of children reside together in the family-like environment under the supervision of professional caregivers, despite being deprived of family care and education, and to identify the treatment needs to protect their oral health.

Subjects and methods

Study design

This descriptive cross-sectional study was carried out in 16 state-protected children’s homes in Sivas under the Directorate of Coordination of Children’s Homes Centres of the Ministry of Family and Social Policies of Türkiye. In the first quarter of 2024, the caries prevalence and periodontal health status of all children were assessed by two specialist dentists. Our study was conducted in accordance with STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) protocol [18].

Ethical approval

Ethical Approval of this study was obtained with the permission number 2023-12/27 from Cumhuriyet University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee. The ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed.

Sample size estimation

With α = 0.05, 1-β = 0.95 and an effect size of 0.40, the power of the test for 84 samples is 0.9518794. G*Power statistical software version 3.1.9.7 was used to calculate the sample size. Although 84 participants were sufficient for this study, 90 institutionalized orphan children participated in the study.

Authorities’ approval and ethical regulations

Since all participants of this study are under state protection, Sivas Children's Homes Center Coordination Directorate was contacted. The aim of the study and the procedures were explained in detail and official permission was obtained. The children's caregivers signed informed consent forms approved by the Ethics Committee. As a result of the examination procedures, all orphans in need of treatment were directed to Sivas Cumhuriyet University Faculty of Dentistry and all necessary dental treatments were performed free of charge.

Participants

All institutionalized orphan children residing in the 16 state-protected children’s homes in Sivas were invited and included in the study, provided they met the inclusion criteria. Thus, no sampling technique such as randomization or convenience sampling was employed; instead, the entire target population was comprehensively included. During the visits to each children’s home, oral examinations were conducted in random order, depending on the availability and cooperation of the children. A total of institutionalized 90 orphan children between the ages of 6 and 18, who did not have any chronic diseases, were not taking medication, and were compliant and healthy, were included in the study.

Data collection and examination

Demographic information and the medical history of each participant were collected through face-to-face structured questionnaires. In the questionnaire, simple questions were asked to assess the participants’ tooth brushing and flossing habits, frequency of dental visits, whether they had received training on oral and dental health care, and if so, where they had received the training. Subsequently, the examination of institutionalized orphan children was performed under normal daylight, on an ordinary chair, using a dental mirror, probe, dental tweezers, CPI probe, cotton roll and personal protective equipment, according to the criteria and methods suggested by WHO [19]. The prevalence of dental caries, caries experience and periodontal status of the participants were evaluated by experienced pediatric dentist and periodontist who had completed their calibration. Within the calibration process, both researchers independently evaluated the indices applied to 10 children within a similar age group, who were not included in the study. Intra- and inter-observer agreement was analysed using Cohen’s Kappa statistic by comparing the obtained scores, which revealed a high level of agreement (kappa > 0.80). Where necessary, discrepancies between the scores were discussed, and common evaluation criteria were clarified.

DMFT (D: decayed, M: missed, F: filled, T: teeth) and deft (d: decayed, e: extracted due to caries, f: filled, t: teeth) indices were used for caries experience [20]. Gingival index (GI) was used to evaluate the severity and amount of inflammation in the gingiva. The inflammatory state of the gingiva was evaluated by giving a score between 0 and 3 (0: Healthy gingiva, 1: Mild inflammation; discolouration and oedema present, no bleeding on probing, 2: Moderate inflammation; redness, oedema present, bleeding on probing, 3: Severe inflammation; marked redness, oedema and ulceration present, spontaneous bleeding present). The GI of a total of 4 regions from the buccal and lingual surfaces of each tooth, from the mesial and distal parts of the tooth was evaluated and scored, and the degree of inflammatory condition in the gums was recorded [21]. Plaque index (PI) was used to evaluate the amount of plaque on the tooth. It was evaluated with 0–3 scores (0: There is no plaque on the gingival margin, 1: There is film-like plaque on the gingival margin that is not visible to the eye, 2: There is moderate plaque accumulation on the gingival margin and adjacent tooth surface that is clearly visible to the eye, 3: There is intense plaque accumulation on the gingival margin and adjacent tooth surface). The PI was evaluated and scored from the buccal and lingual regions of each tooth, from the mesial and distal parts of the tooth from a total of 4 regions and the individuals’ oral hygiene status was recorded [22]. Periodontal status and treatment need were evaluated using the Community Periodontal Index of Treatment Needs (CPITN) [23]. CPI was evaluated with 0–3 scores (0: Healthy periodontium, 1: Presence of haemorrhage after contact with the probe, by direct visual inspection or with a mouth mirror, 2: On probe examination, there is a sensation of calculus or the presence of overhanging restoration margins, the entire black band of the probe is visible, 3: The presence of a 4–5 mm periodontal pocket is detected). TN was evaluated with 0–2 (TN 0: No treatment requirement, TN I: There is a need for oral hygiene education, TN II: TNI + professional tartar cleaning). The dentition was divided into 6 sextant. CPI probe was inserted into the gingival pocket and circulated around the pocket in such a way that it can recognise the calculus in the pocket and the depth and morphology of the pocket. After the highest values on each sextant were determined, the highest value between these values was determined as the score of each individual and the treatment requirement was determined accordingly. Probing Pocket Depth (PPD) was measured from the buccal and lingual regions, mesial-mid-distal regions of each tooth from 6 regions in total. PPD was determined by measuring the distance between the gingival margin and the base of the gingival groove. Since false pocket depth may occur in erupting teeth, PPD measurement was performed only for children in the permanent dentition period [24].

Statistical analysis

All collected data were entered into IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 package programme and analysed. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables (arithmetic mean and standard deviation) were expressed as percentage and number for categorical variables. Normality assumption was checked according to the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Both parametric and nonparametric tests were used for the data obtained from statistical analyses. For binary comparisons, t-test was applied for parametric variables, Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric variables. Also, Chi-square test and Fisher exact test were applied in the evaluation of categorical variables. In this study, the significant value is p < 0.05.

Discussion

Oral and dental health diseases are among the most common childhood diseases. Orphaned children represent a vulnerable group of society and often do not receive the same level of care as children living with their parents [6]. It has been reported in studies that orphaned children have poorer oral hygiene and a higher risk of caries development compared to children living with their parents [6, 16, 25]. Studies evaluating oral and dental health of orphaned children are relatively limited in the literature. To the best of our knowledge, there is no study evaluating the oral and dental health of orphaned children residing in children’s homes institution model in Türkiye. This study was conducted to evaluate both dental caries prevalence and periodontal health status of orphan children residing in children’s homes in Sivas province. In the present study, the orphaned children are aged between six and eighteen years. This interval includes both mixed dentition and permanent dentition and is crucial in that it allows the evaluation of caries experience and periodontal health status at both dentition and genders.

In this study, the mean values of the DMFT index were found to be significantly higher in females and the values were 2.87 ± 2.41 in females and 1.55 ± 1.78 in males, respectively. Early eruption of teeth and prolonged exposure to a harmful oral environment may be the reason why female are more likely to caries experience in their permanent teeth. The mean values of the deft index were 2.60 ± 1.67 in females and 3.47 ± 2.31 in males. Kavayashree and Babu, in their study conducted on orphan children aged 6–14 years in India, reported that the mean values of DMFT index were significantly higher in females and the values were 0.97 ± 1.44 in females and 0.41 ± 0.86 in males, respectively. The mean values of deft index were 0.68 ± 0.23 in females and 0.69 ± 0.15 in males [26]. The mean DMFT and deft index values found in their studies are lower than the values found in this study. The reason for this may be that the prevalence of dental caries in different populations varies depending on multiple factors including socioeconomic factors, demographic characteristics, physical environment and clinical care [27–29]. In a study conducted on orphan children in Türkiye, DMFS score was analysed and a high value of 13.18 ± 5.68 was found in females [17]. In Iran, Abedassar et al. reported the mean value of DMFT index to be 2.93 ± 3.25 in females and 2.66 ± 2.66 in males, while the mean value of dmft index was found to be 3.89 ± 3.98 in females and 3.78 ± 3.51 in males, similar to present study [30]. Contradictory to our findings, some authors have also observed higher caries experience among males [31, 32]. The authors attributed this to the fact that females are more conscious about aesthetic appearance, cleanliness and hygiene.

The mean value of DMFT index was 1.81 ± 1.99 in the 6–12 age group and 2.39 ± 2.31 in the 13–18 age group. The mean value deft index was 3.43 ± 2.12 in the 6–12 age group. In our study, since the 13–18 age group completely transitioned into the permanent dentition period, the deft index was not reported. The high prevalence of dental caries among children in the mixed dentition period can be attributed to their limited oral hygiene skills, a lack of self-motivation, and the necessity for more intensive external supervision during this developmental stage. Marasouli et al. reported the mean values of DMFT index to be 1.38 in the 6–12 age group and 2.96 in the 13–18 age group in their study conducted on orphan children in Iran, similar to current study results [33]. Abedassar et al. reported mean values of DMFT index 4.98 ± 3.60 in the 13–18 age group and 4.13 ± 3.80 in the 6–12 age group [30]. The values they found are higher compared to current study. The lower values observed in this study may be attributed to the special dietary plans implemented in children’s homes, which emphasize healthy, low-sugar foods, along with improved individual dental hygiene care.

In the present study, the prevalence of dental caries was 54.54% in females and 63.04% in males. It was found to be 64.67% in the 6–12 age group and 53.57% in the 13–18 age group. While there was no significant difference between genders in terms of caries prevalence, caries prevalence was significantly higher in the 6–12 age group. In their study, Shah et al. reported that the prevalence of dental caries was 70.45% in females, 65.2% in males, 69.1% in the 7–11 age group and 66.2% in those over 12 years old [25]. In the current study, it was reported that 81.8% of females and 54.4% of males brushed their teeth regularly. The lower caries prevalence in females may be due to the fact that they brush their teeth more regularly than males. The higher prevalence of caries in the 6–12 age group may be due to the lack of complete development of oral hygiene habits and the risk of transmission of decayed deciduous teeth to permanent teeth during the mixed dentition period. The prevalence of filled teeth (47.72%) was significantly higher in females. This may be due to the fact that females attach more importance to aesthetics and are more conscious about cleaning and oral hygiene. The prevalence of filled teeth in present study (38.88%) is higher than that reported in other studies in the literature [25, 30, 34]. This indicates that these children have access to professional dental care when necessary.

The mean values of PI were 0.93 ± 0.45 in females and 1.15 ± 0.52 in males, 1.08 ± 0.44 in the 6–12 age group and 1.02 ± 0.53 in the 13–18 age group. The mean values of GI were 1.03 ± 0.51 in females and 1.37 ± 0.47 in males, 1.28 ± 0.49 in the 6–12 age group and 1.16 ± 0.53 in the 13–18 age group. Koyuncuoglu et al. reported that the mean PI value was 1.33 ± 0.45 and the mean GI value was 1.35 ± 0.37 in a study conducted on orphaned females aged 12–18 years in Türkiye [17]. The mean values of PI and GI reported in their study were higher than the current study. The authors attributed this to poor oral hygiene status of orphaned children, lack of knowledge and motivation of their caregivers about oral hygiene, and low rate of dental visits. The lower mean values of PI and GI in the current study may be due to differences in institutional models between the two studies. Additionally, fewer orphaned children resided together in the children’s homes institution model and allowing for more individual attention. The mean values of PI and GI were significantly lower in females. According to current survey results, 65.9% of females brush their teeth 2 or more times a day, while only 19.6% of males brush their teeth two or more times a day. Furthermore, 84.1% of females reported changing their toothbrushes every six months or less, whereas 41.3% of males indicated that they never change their toothbrushes. This may explain why females have lower mean values of PI and GI.

The mean value of pocket depth in the 13–18 age group was 2.10 ± 0.16 in females and 2.30 ± 0.17 in males. Pocket depth was found to be significantly lower in females. In current study, the mean values of DMFT were reported significantly higher in females. This may be attributed to differences in the dominant microorganisms associated with dental caries and periodontal diseases. Huang et al. identified a negative correlation between DMFT and pocket depth in their study [35].

CPI 1 score was 42.22% and CPI 2 score was 51.11%. CPI 2 score was significantly higher in the 13–18 age group. According to current survey results, 76.5% of children aged 6–12 years brush regularly, while 62.5% of children aged 13–18 years brush regularly. The higher CPI 2 score in the 13–18 age group compared to the 6–12 age group may be explained by the lower regular tooth brushing habits of this age group. Kong et al. conducted the prevalence of gingival bleeding and calculus in Chinese orphaned children aged 12–15 years to be 85.71% and 76.09%, respectively. The authors attributed the high prevalence of periodontal problems to poor oral hygiene, neglect of personal oral health and lack of regular dental care [34]. Additionally, the prevalence of gingival bleeding in Indian orphaned children was reported to be 79.49% by Thetakala et al. [36]. The lower prevalence of gingival bleeding and dental calculus observed in the present study, compared to previous research, may be attributed to the individualized care provided within the children’s home model. This model facilitates the development of regular oral hygiene habits, ensures more consistent supervision of daily oral care practices, such as tooth brushing by caregivers, and places a greater emphasis on oral health education. However, while only 6.66% of orphaned children did not need any treatment, 51.11% needed TN2 periodontal treatment and oral hygiene improvement, and 42.22% needed TN1 oral hygiene improvement.

Limitations

This cross sectional study has several limitations. The children’s homes model is one of the institutional models adopted for orphaned children in Türkiye. In present study, the oral and dental health of orphaned children residing in children’s homes in Sivas province of Türkiye was evaluated. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to other different institutional models and geographical regions. The oral and dental health of orphaned children is influenced not only by individual factors but also by multidimensional elements, including the institutional care model, socioeconomic conditions, access to healthcare services, and national health policies. It is often challenging to make direct comparisons between the findings of this study and similar research in the literature due to variations in care models, dietary habits, education levels, and access to health services across different countries. Additionally, the quality of oral hygiene education provided in institutional settings and the knowledge and awareness levels among caregivers emerge as significant determinants. Therefore, there is need to expand epidemiological data focusing on orphaned children, who represent one of the most vulnerable groups regarding oral health. One of the other limitations of present study, there is no control group consisting of orphaned children from different institutional models or parented children. Future research should include comparative analyses with children living with parents and provide detailed descriptions of the physical and social characteristics of the institutions where orphaned children reside, in order to enhance the interpretability and generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the use of the CPITN index in the present study has certain limitations, including the evaluation of only specific teeth and the exclusion of clinical attachment loss. Nevertheless, the CPITN index offers several advantages, such as ease of application, rapid implementation, international comparability, and the ability to identify treatment needs at the population level. Additionally, the inclusion of parameters such as the PI, GI, and pocket depth in our study allowed for a more comprehensive assessment of periodontal status. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable information on the oral and dental health status of orphaned children residing in children’s homes and offers important insights for oral health policies and interventions targeting this vulnerable group.

Conclusions

This study indicates that children residing in smaller, institutional models such as children’s homes may receive relatively more attention regarding their oral and dental health compared to those in overcrowded orphanages. However, despite these efforts, the findings reveal a persistently high prevalence of dental caries, calculus, and gingival bleeding among institutionalized orphaned children. These outcomes highlight the urgent need for comprehensive, preventive oral health policies tailored specifically to vulnerable child populations. Preventive strategies should include structured oral hygiene education programs that are age-appropriate and engaging, as well as formal training for caregivers to reinforce daily hygiene routines. Moreover, institutional frameworks must prioritize regular access to professional dental services through routine screenings and timely interventions. Policymakers should consider integrating oral health into the broader context of child welfare and public health planning, ensuring equity in service delivery. Ultimately, improving the oral health outcomes of institutionalized children requires a coordinated, long-term commitment to early prevention, caregiver empowerment, and system-wide accessibility to care
