Design of vanadium complexes with a thiophene-based hydrazone ligand for selective benzyl and salicyl alcohol oxidation
Authors: Emanuel Novak, Josipa Sarjanović, Adriana Kenđel, Ivica Đilović, Nenad Judaš, Jana Pisk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two vanadium complexes incorporating a thiophene-2-carboxylic acid hydrazone ligand (H2L) were synthesized and structurally characterized. The ligand was obtained by mechanochemical condensation of 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde with thiophene-2-carboxylic acid hydrazide, while the vanadium complexes were prepared via a solution-based synthetic approach. Crystallization from methanol afforded compound [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), whereas crystallization from dichloromethane or acetone yielded compound [V2O3(L)2] (2). The ligand and complexes were characterized by attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR), elemental analysis (EA), and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Additionally, the crystal structures of the vanadium complexes were determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). Structural stability of the material later employed as a catalyst, the methanol-derived complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), was investigated through temperature-dependent FTIR spectroscopy (TD-FTIR) and temperature-dependent powder X-ray diffraction (TD-PXRD). Catalytic investigations of [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) and [V2O3(L)2] (2) demonstrated that both vanadium complexes efficiently catalyzed the oxidation of benzyl alcohol and salicyl alcohol under mild reaction conditions. The combined structural, thermal, and catalytic studies provide insight into the relationships among the coordination environment, structural stability, and catalytic performance of vanadium complexes containing thiophene-2-carbohydrazide-derived ligands.

Introduction

The selective catalytic oxidation of primary alcohols to aldehydes is a cornerstone transformation in organic synthesis,1,2 providing essential building blocks for the production of pharmaceuticals,3 fragrances,4 flavours,5 and fine chemicals.6 Benzyl alcohol and salicyl alcohol (2-hydroxybenzyl alcohol) are particularly attractive model substrates for such studies. Benzyl alcohol (BnOH) serves as a simple, representative benzylic alcohol that allows straightforward evaluation of catalyst activity and selectivity towards benzaldehyde, usually without complications from over-oxidation or functional group interference.7,8 Salicyl alcohol (SalOH), bearing an ortho-hydroxy group, introduces additional coordination possibilities and electronic effects, making it an excellent probe for assessing catalyst performance with more complex, multifunctional substrates relevant to natural product synthesis and medicinal chemistry.9–11 Benzaldehyde and salicylaldehyde are particularly valuable target products, with salicylaldehyde being of special interest due to its widespread use as a precursor in ligand synthesis.6,12,13 Consequently, their production enhances the overall sustainability of the process, as reflected in the life cycle assessment (LCA), which shows a favourable environmental impact associated with the production of these high-value chemicals.

These oxidations are ideally performed using green oxidants, such as aqueous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), under mild conditions, to minimize environmental impact and avoid the use of toxic stoichiometric reagents (e.g., chromium- or manganese-based oxidants).14–16 Vanadium complexes with hydrazone ligands have emerged as highly promising catalysts for these transformations due to their structural versatility, accessible V(iv)/V(v) redox chemistry, and ability to mimic the reactivity of vanadium-dependent haloperoxidases.17–19 Hydrazone ligands, typically formed by condensation of aroylhydrazides with salicylaldehyde or related carbonyl compounds, function as tridentate O, N, O-donors. They effectively stabilize high-valent vanadium centers and enable electronic and steric tuning via ligand substituents.20,21

Two important structural motifs in this class are the mononuclear vanadium(v) species of the type [VO(Lx)(OMe)(MeOH)] (or related alkoxo-methanol solvates) and the dinuclear [V2O3(Ly)2] complexes featuring a µ-oxo (V–O–V) bridge, where Lx and Ly refer to the ligands adopted from the corresponding literature reports.22,23 The mononuclear complexes possess a [VO]-core with labile methoxo and methanol ligands that facilitate substrate access and formation of reactive peroxo intermediates.24 The dinuclear species benefit from potential cooperative effects between the two metal centres bridged by the oxo ligand, enhancing catalytic robustness. Both types are readily prepared from common precursors such as [VO(acac)2], (acac = C5H7O2−) or VOSO4 in methanolic solution and can interconvert in solution.20,21,23

Vanadium-hydrazone complexes effectively catalyse the peroxidative oxidation of benzyl and salicyl alcohols under mild conditions (typically 25–80 °C), delivering high selectivity for the corresponding aldehydes with minimal over-oxidation.20,25 The mononuclear species readily generate peroxo-vanadium intermediates upon addition of H2O2 or tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP), while the dinuclear complexes leverage the µ-oxo unit for efficient redox cycling.25,26 These systems exhibit good turnover numbers, tolerance to aqueous media, and performance that can be optimised through ligand design.

Thiophene-containing hydrazone ligands were selected because the thiophene ring is an electron-rich heteroaromatic donor that can modulate the electronic properties of the metal centre while providing a rigid and stable coordination framework. Such ligands have demonstrated excellent coordination ability toward high-valent transition metals and have shown promising performance in oxidation catalysis, making them attractive platforms for the development of efficient Mo(vi) oxidation catalysts. The suitability of this ligand framework for oxidation catalysis has recently been demonstrated for Mo complexes with analogous thiophene-derived hydrazone ligands. Pavić et al. reported the mechanochemical synthesis of Mo(vi) complexes with a thiophene-2-carboxylic acid hydrazone ligand.7 The mononuclear complex [MoO2(L)(MeOH)] efficiently catalysed the oxidation of benzyl alcohol under mild conditions with H2O2 as the oxidant, achieving good conversions and selectivity towards benzaldehyde (up to 82%). This study demonstrated the influence of catalyst nuclearity and loading on activity and highlighted the advantages of green synthetic and catalytic protocols.

In this context, the present work explores vanadium compounds with thiophene-2-carboxylic acid hydrazone ligand of [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] and [V2O3(L)2], used as catalysts for the selective oxidation of benzyl alcohol and salicyl alcohol, contributing to the development of efficient, sustainable, and environmentally benign catalytic systems Scheme 1.

Results and discussion

Synthesis, thermal stability assessment and spectroscopic characterization

The ligand H2L was prepared by a previously reported synthetic approach.7 Its purity was confirmed by DSC analysis, showing intensive endothermic maxima, implying the melting process, see SI, Fig. S1. ATR-FTIR spectroscopy (see SI, Fig. S2), analysed the characteristic IR bands, specified in the Experimental section. The ligand H2L was dissolved under gentle heating in methanol, acetone, or dichloromethane and subsequently mixed with [VO(acac)2] in a 1 : 1 molar ratio. In all cases, the reaction mixtures were heated under reflux for 2 h, then cooled slowly to room temperature, yielding precipitates. The obtained products were isolated by filtration, affording brown solid complexes in each case.

The thermal behavior of isolated products was further investigated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The TGA curve of the product isolated from acetone or DCM was the same, and so-called product 2 indicates an overall single-step decomposition process. In contrast, compound 1, isolated from MeOH, shows a more complex thermal pattern with an initial endothermic peak followed by an exothermic decomposition step SI Fig. S3. The behaviour of compound 1 implies a preliminary solvent-loss process at 110 °C (see SI, Fig. S3) that could not be clearly separated as an independent mass-loss step, followed by the degradation of the coordination framework. For that reason, the suggested formula of the complex was [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), and the final residue corresponding to V2O5 of 24.4%, is in good agreement with the calculated value of 24.3%. The existence of V2O5 as a residue was confirmed by ATR-FTIR and PXRD and compared with the commercially available V2O5. Further, the products obtained from acetone and dichloromethane showed the same thermal decomposition, indicating the presence of the same species. TGA indicated a single-step decomposition, in the range 275–520 °C, yielding a residue corresponding to V2O5 of 27.6%, which is in agreement with the theoretical value of 29.9%, with the supposed formula of the complex being [V2O3(L)2] (2), SI Fig. S4. The close agreement between experimental and calculated values supports the proposed stoichiometry and suggests a simpler decomposition pathway compared to the methanol-derived system.

The ATR-FTIR spectrum of the proposed complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) displays the characteristic vibrational features of a vanadium(v) complex containing coordinated methanol and methoxide ligands SI, Fig. S6 and Scheme S1. In contrast, the ATR-FTIR spectrum of the oxo-bridged dinuclear complex [V2O3(L)2] (2) exhibits spectral features consistent with formation of a µ-oxo vanadium(v) species; see SI, Fig. S7 and Scheme S1. Furthermore, the appearance of a new band at 690 cm−1, assigned to the ν(V–O–V) stretching vibration, provides characteristic evidence for the presence of a bridging oxo ligand and supports the formation of the dinuclear oxo-bridged vanadium(v) structure.28 Overall, the ATR-FTIR data align with the TGA analysis of the synthesis complexes.

Upon thermal treatment in a Büchi glass oven at 150 °C, the complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) undergoes conversion into the corresponding µ-oxo vanadium(v) species [V2O3(L)2] (2) through removal of the methanol moieties and reorganization of the vanadium–oxygen coordination environment. This transformation is supported by TG analysis of the sample heated at 150 °C for 1 h, which confirms the loss of methanol moieties and the formation of a thermally more stable species consistent with the composition of [V2O3(L)2] (2), see SI, Fig. S5.

To further support this behavior, temperature-dependent FTIR measurements were performed, allowing direct monitoring of spectral changes during the thermal transformation (Fig. 1, see SI S8 and S9). The disappearance of the absorption band at 1050 cm−1 is attributed to the ν(C–O) stretching vibration of the coordinated methanol molecule.20,23 The loss of this band upon heating indicates the elimination of coordinated methanol from the vanadium coordination sphere. The concomitant disappearance of the band at 549 cm−1, assigned to vibrational modes involving the V–O–Me and V–O coordination environment, further supports the removal of the methoxide-containing fragment and subsequent rearrangement of the vanadium center.

FT-IR temperature dependent spectra for mononuclear complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) from 1500 to 400 cm−1 for chosen temperatures.

The band observed at 965 cm−1, assigned to the terminal ν(VO) stretching vibration of the monoxo V(v) species, disappears after thermal treatment and is replaced by a new absorption band at 995 cm−1. This shift is consistent with the transformation of the monoxo vanadium(v) center into a µ-oxo vanadium(v) [V2O3]4+ unit, where changes in the V–O bonding environment and increased contribution of the terminal oxo character modify the VO stretching frequency. The weak band observed at ca. 780 cm−1 disappears upon heating could be attributed to a ligand–metal vibrational mode that is sensitive to the presence of coordinated methanol and the corresponding coordination environment around the vanadium centre. Furthermore, the appearance of a new band at 690 cm−1, typically associated with V–O–V stretching vibrations, indicates that the thermal transformation definitely does result in the formation of oxo-bridged vanadium species, as observed for complex [V2O3(L)2] (2). Therefore, the combined TD-FTIR and TGA results confirm the thermal conversion of the complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) into the stable [V2O3(L)2] (2) complex.

Investigation of the crystal and molecular structure of V(v) complexes

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) analyses were performed for the ligand H2L and two V(v) complexes, [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) and [V2O3(L)2] (2) (SI, Tables S1–S3). Suitable single crystals of all compounds were obtained directly from their respective reaction solutions. The crystal structure of the ligand H2L has been reported previously and corresponds to CCDC deposition entry EROJOO.7

The molecular structures of [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) and [V2O3(L)2] (2) reveal geometrical features characteristic of vanadium(v) hydrazone complexes (Fig. 2, see SI, S15 and S16). In both compounds, the Schiff base ligand is coordinated in its doubly deprotonated form (L2−), with deprotonation occurring at the phenolic hydroxyl and hydrazone amide functionalities. The ligand adopts an essentially planar conformation upon coordination, as evidenced by the torsion angles O1–C5–C6–C7, C6–C7–N1–N2, and N1–N2–C8–O2, which are all close to 0° or 180°. These values indicate that the conjugated ONO donor framework remains nearly coplanar and is favourably oriented for the V(v) coordination. Furthermore, the C8–C9 bond lengths of approximately 1.45 Å in both complexes are characteristic of a single C–C bond, whereas the shorter C8–N2 bond lengths of approximately 1.30 Å indicate pronounced CN double-bond character. These structural parameters are consistent with electron delocalization within the hydrazone backbone and support coordination of the ligand in its doubly deprotonated form.

ORTEP representation for the molecular structures of: (a) [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) and (b) [V2O3(L)2] (2). The displacement ellipsoids are drawn at a 30% probability level. Hydrogen atoms are presented as spheres of arbitrary small radii.

Complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) crystallizes in the monoclinic P21/c space group. The vanadium(v) centre adopts a distorted octahedral coordination geometry, with the tridentate ONO donor set of the ligand occupying three positions of the equatorial plane. The remaining equatorial site is occupied by a methoxide group, while a thermal oxygen atom and a coordinated methanol molecule fill the axial positions. The crystal packing is dominated by O–H⋯N hydrogen bonds between the methanol hydroxyl group and the imine nitrogen, which generate supramolecular dimers [graph-set notation R22(10)] (see Fig. S15(b)). The dimers are further connected via C–H⋯O hydrogen bonds, forming zigzag chains propagating in the [100] direction. Adjacent chains are subsequently linked by weaker C–H⋯S contacts, extending the supramolecular assembly along the crystallographic a axis (see Fig. S15(c)). The PXRD pattern calculated from SCXRD structure shows excellent agreement with the experimental PXRD pattern of the bulk material obtained from complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), confirming the formation of a single crystalline phase and the high phase purity of the sample (see SI Fig. S10).

Compound [V2O3(L)2] (2) crystallizes in the tetragonal Ia space group. The molecular structure consists of a dinuclear oxido-bridged species in which two vanadium(v) centres are connected through a µ-oxo bridge. Each centre exhibits a square-pyramidal coordination geometry (τ5 = 0.02),27 with the ONO donor set of the ligand defining the basal plane together with the bridging oxygen atom, while a terminal oxygen atom occupies the apical position. Examination of the crystal packing reveals a layered arrangement generated through the stacking of the dinuclear units. The crystal packing is supported by weak C–H⋯O intermolecular contacts, which contribute to the formation of an extended chain network along the crystallographic a axis (see SI, Fig. S16(b and c)). The PXRD patterns of the products isolated from acetone and DCM are consistent with the calculated PXRD pattern, providing further evidence that the same crystalline vanadium(v) species is formed under both solvent conditions. These results demonstrate the structural uniformity of the obtained material (see SI Fig. S11).

The coordination environments and overall structural features of both complexes are consistent with those reported previously for related vanadium(v) hydrazone complexes.20,21,23,26,28

Powder X-ray diffraction investigation

To further investigate the structural behaviour of the complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), temperature-dependent PXRD studies were performed to characterise the solid-state structural changes (Fig. 3 and SI S12).

PXRD temperature-dependent powder pattern for mononuclear complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) for chosen temperatures. The brown powder pattern at 30 °C is the mononuclear complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), and the pink powder pattern at 30 °C is obtained after heating the sample to 150 °C and cooling it down.

Before the TD-PXRD measurements, the temperature range of 30–150 °C was selected based on the TGA results, as this interval corresponds to the removal of the coordinated methanol and methoxy groups from the complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1). The PXRD patterns recorded in this temperature range reveal a gradual evolution of the new phase with temperature increase, indicating a thermally induced solid-state transformation.

Up to 70 °C, the diffraction pattern remains essentially unchanged, with only a gradual decrease in peak intensities and no appearance of new reflections or disappearance of existing ones, indicating that the crystal structure remains stable over this temperature range (see Fig. S12). Nevertheless, subtle structural modifications start at 70 °C. The reflections at 15.8°, 30.2°, and 32.2° (2θ) disappear, while those located at 9.2°, 10.8°, and 17.4° (2θ) exhibit a noticeable decrease in intensity. In addition, a weak shoulder begins to develop at approximately 23.0° (2θ).

Between 90 and 100 °C, the diffraction patterns undergo more pronounced changes. Most reflections originating from the initial crystalline phase progressively decrease in intensity, while the reflections at 24.2°, 34.7°, and 37.2° (2θ) disappear completely.

Further heating to 120 °C results in the formation of a new diffraction pattern characterized by the appearance of weak reflections at 10.2°, 20.1°, 20.4°, 22.9°, and 36.9° (2θ). Simultaneously, several remaining reflections broaden and partially merge, indicating development of a new phase.

The diffraction patterns recorded at 130 and 150 °C exhibit the largest deviations from the initial structure. Most reflections above 29° (2θ) become barely distinguishable, while the reflections at 9.8°, 10.8°, 17.2°, 18.4°, and 24.4° (2θ) disappear completely compared with the pattern recorded at 30 °C. In contrast, the newly formed reflections at approximately 10.2°, 16.3°, 20.0°, 22.9°, and 28.3° (2θ) become more pronounced, confirming the formation of a new crystalline phase. Above 140 °C, no further changes are observed in the diffraction patterns, indicating that the transformation is complete and that the resulting phase is thermally stable within the investigated temperature range.

Importantly, after cooling the sample back to 30 °C, the PXRD pattern remained identical to that recorded at the highest temperature, confirming that the thermally induced transformation is irreversible (see SI Fig. S13). The absence of any reappearance of the original diffraction peaks demonstrates that the initial crystal structure is not restored upon cooling and that the newly formed phase is stable under ambient conditions.

Comparison of the final PXRD pattern of the transformed complex with that of the product synthesized directly in acetone shows that the two diffraction patterns are identical, see SI Fig. S14. This demonstrates that the thermally induced transformation of [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) does lead to the formation of the previously reported dinuclear complex [V2O3(L)2] (2).

The gradual disappearance of reflections characteristic of the initial phase, accompanied by the emergence of new diffraction peaks, demonstrates a solid-state phase transformation rather than thermal decomposition. This transformation is driven by the loss of the coordinated methanol and methoxy groups, resulting in a rearrangement of the vanadium coordination sphere and the formation of [V2O3(L)2] (2) as confirmed by TG analysis and TD-PXRD (see SI, Fig. S5 and S14). The observed transition is consistent with the changes in the TD-FTIR, providing complementary evidence for the thermally induced structural reorganization.

Catalytic performance

Kinetic profiles of alcohol oxidation over V-based catalysts

The oxidation kinetics of benzyl alcohol and salicyl alcohol were investigated using three vanadium-based catalysts, [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), [V2O3(L)2] (2) and [VO(acac)2] (as a referent system), under identical reaction conditions (Fig. 4, see SI, Scheme S2). Although [VO(acac)2] contains vanadium in the +IV oxidation state, it was selected as the reference catalyst because it served as the synthetic precursor for complexes 1 and 2, thereby providing a direct benchmark for assessing the effect of ligand coordination on catalytic performance. Furthermore, under the applied oxidative conditions, [VO(acac)2] is readily oxidized by tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP) to catalytically active vanadium(v) species, including oxo- and tert-butylperoxo intermediates, which are generally accepted as the active oxidants in vanadium-catalyzed alcohol oxidation. Consequently, the comparison primarily reflects the influence of the ligand environment and nuclearity on catalytic activity and selectivity rather than differences in the formal oxidation state of the precatalyst. Catalytic data for V2O5 as a reference catalyst, under identical benzyl alcohol oxidation conditions have been reported previously by our research group.29 V2O5 afforded benzyl alcohol conversions of 78% without any solvent and 43% with the addition of acetonitrile, with corresponding benzaldehyde selectivities of 27% and 65%, respectively. In addition, both [VO(acac)2] and V2O5 have frequently been employed as benchmark catalysts in vanadium-catalyzed alcohol oxidation studies, although typically under different reaction conditions.30 Reactions were monitored by measuring substrate conversion as a function of time, allowing the influence of the reaction medium and substrate structure on catalytic performance. All catalytic experiments were performed at 80 °C. TD-FTIR and TD-PXRD confirmed that the structure of [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) remains unchanged at the reaction temperature, demonstrating that the catalyst is thermally stable under the applied oxidation conditions. Nevertheless, all investigated vanadium complexes were found to dissolve in the reaction medium during catalysis. Therefore, the observed catalytic activity cannot be unequivocally attributed to a heterogeneous process, and the reactions are likely mediated by soluble vanadium species present in the reaction medium. However, no evidence of catalyst deactivation was observed during the reaction.

Kinetic profiles for alcohol oxidation with V-catalysts: pink – [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)], blue – [V2O3(L)2], green – [VO(acac)2]. (a) Conversion of benzyl alcohol without addition of co-solvent, (b) conversion of benzyl alcohol with addition of 3 mL of MeCN, (c) conversion of salicyl alcohol with addition of 3 mL of MeCN. The reaction was carried out with 3.30 mmol of benzyl alcohol or salicyl alcohol and 0.0165 mmol of the catalyst.

Benzyl alcohol oxidation without co-solvent

Fig. 4a presents the oxidation of benzyl alcohol in the absence of acetonitrile. All three catalysts exhibit a gradual increase in conversion throughout the reaction, indicating stable catalytic activity with no significant catalyst deactivation. However, only moderate differences are observed between the catalysts. The catalysts [VO(acac)2] and [V2O3(L)2] (2) provide the highest conversion, reaching approximately 78–84% after 120 min, followed closely by [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) with a final conversion of about 75%. The nearly linear increase in conversion suggests that the oxidation proceeds at a relatively constant rate under solvent-free conditions, where mass-transfer limitations may restrict overall catalytic performance.

Effect of acetonitrile on benzyl alcohol oxidation

The addition of 3 mL of acetonitrile substantially modifies the catalytic behavior (Fig. 4b). A strong and clear enhancement is observed for [VO(acac)2], while the activity of complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) remains almost the same. The increase in activity for [VO(acac)2] can be explained by the very good solubility of [VO(acac)2] in MeCN, meaning improved accessibility of the vanadium center. The activity of [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) is expected to be unchanged since MeOH is already an easily exchangeable ligand, making the vanadium center already accessible, and substrate coordination is not strongly improved by replacing MeOH with MeCN. In contrast, [V2O3(L)2] (2) seems to be inhibited by MeCN, and conversion reaches only around 20%, implying specific interaction between MeCN and the dimeric species.

These results indicate that acetonitrile strongly promotes the activity of the [VO]3+ species, facilitating oxygen transfer and enhancing accessibility of the catalytically active vanadium centers.

Salicyl alcohol oxidation in acetonitrile

The oxidation of salicyl alcohol in the presence of acetonitrile (Fig. 4c) reveals an even greater differentiation among the catalysts. The reference catalyst [VO(acac)2] exhibits outstanding catalytic performance, achieving almost complete conversion (≈100%) within 60 min, after which the conversion remains constant, indicating rapid substrate depletion. Complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) also shows excellent activity, although the conversion increases more gradually, reaching approximately 80% after 120 min. In contrast, [V2O3(L)2] (2) remains significantly less active, with conversion not exceeding 24% during the reaction. The superior activity observed for the vanadium acetylacetonate complexes may arise from their homogeneous nature and greater accessibility of the active vanadium centers. Furthermore, the ortho-hydroxyl group of salicyl alcohol can coordinate to vanadium, facilitating substrate activation and accelerating oxidation. This interaction appears to be particularly favorable for [VO(acac)2], potentially explaining its exceptional catalytic efficiency.

Overall conversion comparison

Overall, the kinetic profiles demonstrate that both the catalyst structure and the reaction medium play decisive roles in determining oxidation efficiency. Under solvent-free conditions, all catalysts show relatively similar behavior, although [V2O3(L)2] (2) is slightly more active. Upon the addition of MeCN, however, the vanadium acetylacetonate complexes become substantially more efficient than the other catalysts. Among the investigated materials, complex [VO(acac)2] exhibits the highest catalytic activity, especially for salicyl alcohol oxidation. These findings suggest that molecular vanadium complexes [VO]-type are more effective oxidation catalysts in acetonitrile-containing media, while substrate coordination further enhances the oxidation of salicyl alcohol. This behavior highlights the importance of solvent selection and catalyst coordination environment in optimising vanadium-catalysed aerobic alcohol oxidation.

Selectivity towards aldehyde products

The selectivity data presented in Fig. 5 highlight clear differences in performance between the standard catalyst ([VO(acac)2]) and the two vanadium complexes, [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) and [V2O3(L)2] (2), under different reaction media.

Conversion and selectivity bars for alcohol oxidation with V-catalysts: complex 1 – [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)], complex 2 – [V2O3(L)2], standard – [VO(acac)2]. Pink – conversion of benzyl alcohol and selectivity toward benzaldehyde without addition of co-solvent, blue – conversion of benzyl alcohol and selectivity toward benzaldehyde with addition of 3 mL of MeCN, green – conversion of salicyl alcohol and selectivity towards salicylaldehyde with addition of 3 mL of MeCN.

Under solvent-free conditions, the selectivity towards benzaldehyde (PhCHO) remains generally low for all catalysts, indicating significant over-oxidation of the primary aldehyde product. The standard catalyst [VO(acac)2] shows a PhCHO selectivity of 27%, while [V2O3(L)2] (2) exhibits the lowest selectivity at 19%. [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) performs slightly better with 31% selectivity, representing the highest PhCHO selectivity under neat conditions among the three catalysts. These results confirm that, in the absence of solvent, rapid consecutive oxidation of benzaldehyde dominates, limiting overall selectivity despite appreciable alcohol conversion.

A marked improvement in PhCHO selectivity is observed upon the introduction of acetonitrile, demonstrating a strong solvent effect in suppressing over-oxidation pathways. The enhancement is particularly pronounced for [V2O3(L)2] (2), where selectivity increases dramatically from 19% to 78%, indicating that acetonitrile effectively stabilizes benzaldehyde and inhibits further oxidation to carboxylic acid. A moderate increase is also observed for the [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) (31% to 38%), whereas [VO(acac)2] shows only a slight improvement (27% to 28%). This suggests that the catalytic environment of [V2O3(L)2] (2) is highly sensitive to solvent coordination effects, which strongly modulate its selectivity-determining steps.

In the case of salicyl alcohol oxidation, the selectivity towards salicylaldehyde is significantly lower across all catalysts, ranging from 11% to 36%. The [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) again shows the highest selectivity (36%), while standard catalyst [VO(acac)2] and [V2O3(L)2] (2) yield 23% and 11%, respectively. Despite higher conversion in some cases, particularly with [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), the low salicylaldehyde selectivity reflects its rapid secondary oxidation. This behavior is further amplified by the ortho-hydroxyl substituent, which increases salicylaldehyde's susceptibility to over-oxidation through intramolecular activation effects.

Overall, the results demonstrate that while [VO(acac)2] provides more balanced selectivity towards both aldehydes, [V2O3(L)2] (2) benefits most significantly from acetonitrile in terms of PhCHO selectivity enhancement. In contrast, salicylaldehyde formation remains intrinsically less selective across all systems due to its higher reactivity, highlighting the persistent challenge of controlling over-oxidation in ortho-substituted alcohols. Following the reaction, the reaction mixture separated into two phases, an aqueous phase and an organic phase. Since GC analysis was performed only on the organic phase, a significant proportion of the reaction products, particularly the more polar oxidation products, remained in the aqueous phase and could not be quantified. Furthermore, the chromatograms of the organic phase exhibited several additional peaks that can be attributed to carboxylic acid products; however, these compounds were not quantified because the applied GC method was not suitable for their reliable determination. Consequently, the reported selectivity toward the desired aldehyde reflects only the quantified products present in the organic phase (see SI, Fig. S17).

TON and TOF analysis

Catalytic activity is expressed through TON and TOF20 min. For benzyl alcohol oxidation in the absence of acetonitrile (pink bars), all three catalysts exhibit relatively high TON values (150–168). The complex [V2O3(L)2] (2) shows the highest TON (168), followed by the reference catalyst [VO(acac)2] (156) and [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) (150). Catalyst [V2O3(L)2] (2) has the highest TOF20 min values, reaching 110 h−1, implying the fastest transformation of the catalysts to the active species. The addition of 3 mL of acetonitrile (blue bars) affects the catalysts differently. For [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1), TON slightly increases from 150 to 152, but [V2O3(L)2] (2) experiences a dramatic decrease in TON (to 40), indicating substantial inhibition of catalytic activity. The reference catalyst benefits most from acetonitrile, achieving a TON of 166, while the TOF20 min increases to 121 h−1.

For salicyl alcohol oxidation (green bars), the catalysts show markedly different behavior. As aldehyde yield is calculated from the product of substrate conversion and aldehyde selectivity, it does not represent an independent catalytic parameter. Therefore, the catalytic performance is discussed primarily in terms of conversion and selectivity, while the corresponding aldehyde yields are included in the SI (Fig. S18) for completeness.

Comparison with similar Mo systems

The catalytic performance comparison between molybdenum and vanadium complexes in the oxidation of benzyl alcohol reveals a pronounced trade-off between activity and selectivity. Vanadium-based catalysts exhibit significantly higher conversions (approximately 75–85% after 120 min), consistent with their strong oxidative capability; however, this high activity is accompanied by low benzaldehyde selectivity (19–31%), indicating extensive consecutive oxidation of the primary aldehyde to over-oxidized by-products. In contrast, molybdenum complexes show markedly lower conversions (8–13%) but substantially enhanced selectivity toward benzaldehyde (51–82%), reflecting a more controlled oxidation pathway that limits further transformation of the aldehyde intermediate. These results highlight that vanadium systems favor kinetically driven deep oxidation processes, whereas molybdenum catalysts promote milder and more selective oxidation, underscoring their distinct mechanistic tendencies in alcohol oxidation reactions.

Materials and methods

Materials

All the chemicals were commercially available and used without purification. Methods

ATR-FTIR spectroscopic analysis was used to characterize all synthesized compounds. The method was carried out using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two FT-IR spectrometer equipped with a diamond ATR attachment. The samples were placed on the diamond reflection element, and the spectrum of each sample was recorded in the wavenumber range from 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1.

TD-FTIR

Temperature-dependent FTIR spectra were recorded on a Bruker VECTOR 22 spectrometer equipped with a Bruker A599 heatable sample stage with a temperature control unit (using the KBr pellet technique, 4000–400 cm−1 range, 2 cm−1 spectral resolution, 128 scans) from 30 to 160 °C. A thin KBr pellet was placed in a temperature-adjustable holder. After each spectrum acquisition, the temperature was raised by 10 °C and kept constant for 10 minutes to ensure equal heat distribution (in the pellet). After the experiment, the pellet partially lost its transparency and color (it became brownish, which is consistent with other observations).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the samples was performed using a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC 3+ instrument. Measurements were conducted in aluminum oxide (Al2O3) crucibles, under an oxygen flow, over the temperature range of 25 to 600 °C, with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1.

PXRD

Temperature-dependent powder X-ray diffraction (TD-PXRD) patterns were collected on a Malvern PANalytical Empyrean diffractometer (Malvern PANalytical, Malvern, UK) in reflection mode in 2θ range from 3 to 40° (step size: 0.0066°; exposure: 9.5 s) using a PIXcel3D-MEdipix3 detector and Cu Kα source. The sample was lightly homogenised and mounted on a zero-background holder inside the Anton Paar TTK 600 chamber. The data were collected in air at temperatures from 30 to 150 °C. After single-temperature data collection, the temperature was increased by 10 °C increments and kept constant for 10 min before subsequent measurement. After collecting the highest-temperature diffraction data, the sample was cooled to 30 °C, and data were recollected. HighScore Plus and Data Viewer were used for data analysis and visualization.

PXRD data were collected using a Malvern Panalytical Aeris powder diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical B.V., Almelo, The Netherlands) in the Bragg–Brentano geometry with a PIXcel1D detector, using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) and a zero-background sample holder. Powder patterns were collected at room temperature over a 5–50° (2θ) range.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) was used to determine the molecular and crystal structures of [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1) and [V2O3(L)2] (2). Data collection was performed on a Rigaku XtaLAB Synergy-S diffractometer equipped with a Dualflex source using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54184 Å) and a HyPix detector. Diffraction data were acquired using ω-scan techniques at 170 K for complex [V2O3(L)2] (2) and at 300 K for complex [VO(L)(OMe)(MeOH)] (1). Hydrogen atoms bonded to carbon were positioned geometrically and refined using a riding model, with Uiso values set to 1.2Ueq for CH and CH2 groups and 1.5Ueq for methyl groups. Hydrogen atoms bound to heteroatoms were located from difference Fourier maps during the final refinement stages. Catalysis

The catalytic oxidation of benzyl alcohol and salicyl alcohol was carried out using vanadium-based coordination complexes as catalysts in a magnetically stirred 50 mL three-neck round-bottom flask equipped with a reflux condenser. The reaction mixture consisted of benzyl alcohol or salicyl alcohol (3.30 mmol), aqueous tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP, 6.60 mmol) as the oxidant, biphenyl (0.324 mmol) as the internal standard, and the catalyst (0.0165 mmol), in the presence or absence of acetonitrile (3 mL). The reactions were performed at 80 °C under continuous stirring for 2 h. Aliquots were withdrawn at defined time intervals, immediately diluted with acetonitrile, and analyzed by gas chromatography (GC). GC analyses were carried out using an Agilent 8860 gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and an HP-5 capillary column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 µm), with helium as the carrier gas. The injection port and detector temperatures were maintained at 250 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Quantification was achieved using calibration curves generated from authentic standards of all relevant species. Benzyl alcohol, salicyl alcohol conversion, and aldehyde formation were calculated relative to the biphenyl internal standard, with calibration curves exhibiting excellent linearity (r2 = 0.999).

Conclusions

In this work, two new vanadium complexes based on a thiophene-2-carbohydrazone ligand were successfully synthesized and characterized by spectroscopic, thermal, and crystallographic techniques. The structural stability of the methanol-derived complex was further investigated by temperature-dependent FTIR spectroscopy and PXRD, providing insight into its behavior under conditions relevant to catalysis.

The catalytic studies demonstrated that both the catalyst structure and the reaction medium strongly influence the aerobic oxidation of benzyl alcohol and salicyl alcohol. The slightly higher activity of the dinuclear complex [V2O3(L)2] (2) suggests that its multinuclear structure and coordination environment may favour the catalytic oxidation process, although further mechanistic studies are required to elucidate the nature of the active species. Comparison with vanadium acetylacetonate catalysts confirmed the importance of the coordination environment in determining catalytic performance. The lower selectivity observed for salicylaldehyde also highlights the persistent challenge of suppressing over-oxidation of ortho-substituted alcohols, likely due to the additional hydroxyl group, which can promote stronger interactions with the vanadium center and facilitate secondary oxidation reactions.

Overall, this study expands the relatively unexplored family of thiophene-carbohydrazone vanadium complexes and demonstrates their potential as oxidation catalysts. The results establish a clear relationship between molecular structure, stability, and catalytic behaviour, providing valuable guidelines for the rational design of improved vanadium-based oxidation catalysts. Future work will focus on modifying the ligand framework to enhance catalytic activity and selectivity, as well as on mechanistic studies to identify the active catalytic species.
