Lipid droplets-mediated delivery of curcumin for targeted mitochondrial photodynamic therapy
Authors: Wen Lin, Shuni Zeng, Qiwei Chen, Zhitao Wang, Defang Ouyang, Yan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipid droplets (LDs), intracellular organelles characterized by a phospholipid monolayer shell enclosing a neutral lipid core, serve as ideal reservoirs for lipophilic drugs. By exploiting the β-oxidation pathway, lipids stored in LDs can be transported to mitochondria, enabling targeted drug delivery. Notably, by altering the culture conditions, the particle size of LDs within cells can be regulated, demonstrating their suitability for development as drug carriers. Curcumin (CUR), a natural lipophilic photosensitizer, exhibits considerable potential for applications in photodynamic therapy (PDT). We confirmed that exogenous LDs can be taken up by cells and effectively localized around mitochondria. Then we exploited LDs as drug carriers to develop curcumin-loaded LDs (CUR-LDs) that capitalize on the intrinsic interplay between LDs and mitochondria to enable targeted mitochondrial delivery for photodynamic therapy. In vitro, CUR-LDs irradiated with 450 nm light (100 mW/cm2, 5 min) significantly increased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels (5.2-fold), together with inactivation of mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1 red/green ratio: from 26.50 ± 4.81 to 1.76 ± 0.04), and ultimately promoted apoptotic cell death. These results highlight LDs as promising mitochondria-targeted drug carriers and demonstrate an effective curcumin formulation with potential applications in cancer therapy.

Introduction

The pursuit of researchers in the pharmaceutical field is to develop efficient and non-toxic drug delivery systems for enhancing anticancer therapy efficacy (Chen et al., 2019). In recent decades, there has been a growing interest in using biomaterials such as cells (Xie et al., 2017) and cell extracellular vesicles (Jiang et al., 2024) as drug delivery vehicles. Their biocompatibility and safety are generally considered superior to those of many synthetic polymeric materials (Sharma et al., 2025), and natural biomacromolecules also possess unique features, such as natural tropisms and specific cell entry mechanisms (Yoo et al., 2011). Organelles, subcellular biological effectors, participate in vital life processes such as molecular and ionic transport, cellular metabolism, and protein synthesis (Satori et al., 2013). They interact and coordinate to maintain organismal homeostasis. Owing to their suitable size and specialized functions, organelles constitute a unique class of biomaterials with considerable potential for precision medicine (Hu et al., 2023).

Lipid droplets (LDs), a class of cellular organelles, are vital for lipid metabolism and the maintenance of energy homeostasis, and they serve as the primary intracellular storage sites for lipids. They feature a simple structure, consisting of a hydrophobic neutral lipid core encapsulated by a single-layer phospholipid membrane, with various functional and structural proteins embedded on its surface (Olzmann and Carvalho, 2019). Their lipid core can serve as an ideal reservoir for lipophilic drugs (Liang et al., 2021). LDs are widely present in organisms and exhibit excellent biocompatibility (Zhao et al., 2025). It is well established that LDs dynamically interact with multiple intracellular organelles, including the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, and lysosomes (Valm et al., 2017). Numerous studies have explored the interactions between LDs and mitochondria. Such interactions are most prominent in cells with high energy demand and active lipid metabolism(Liao et al., 2022). Tumor cells depend strongly on lipid droplet-mitochondria tethering and fatty acid β-oxidation to resist energy stress, and key proteins related to fatty acid oxidation are significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Meng et al., 2024). Nutrient starvation activates the energy sensor (AMP-activated protein kinase, AMPK), enhances LD-mitochondria interactions, and facilitates fatty acid transfer from LDs to mitochondria for β-oxidation (Herms et al., 2015). This process relies on neutral lipase-mediated lipolysis rather than lysosomal lipophagy. Autophagy instead promotes lipid accumulation within LDs, replenishing LDs with new fatty acids that subsequently flux into mitochondria (Rambold et al., 2015). Furthermore, the well-established techniques for isolation and purification enable efficient separation of LDs from other cellular components, thereby offering a solid foundation for scalable production processes (Ding et al., 2013). This suggests that LDs have potential for development into mitochondrial-targeted drug delivery systems.

Curcumin (CUR), a bioactive component derived from Curcuma longa, demonstrates a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, encompassing anti-inflammatory (Soetikno et al., 2019), antioxidant (Jakubczyk et al., 2020), and anticancer effects (Liu et al., 2023). Additionally, it functions as a photosensitizer in photodynamic therapy (PDT) (Kah et al., 2023). However, its clinical translation has been hindered by its inherently low aqueous solubility and poor bioavailability (Li et al., 2023). PDT is a tumor treatment modality that relies on three core elements: light, oxygen, and photosensitizer (Lou et al., 2014). Compared with conventional cancer therapy methods, PDT possesses several advantages, such as minimal invasion and fewer side effects (Li et al., 2018). Of note, curcumin exhibits antioxidant properties in normal cells where the uptake is low, unlike in tumor cells where uptake is high, and curcumin increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and cell death (Yu et al., 2019). Curcumin-mediated PDT exerts stronger anti-proliferative effects on oral squamous cell carcinoma cells than on normal HaCaT cells (Beyer et al., 2017) (Woźniak et al., 2021). These results indicate that curcumin-based PDT demonstrates favorable tumor-selective cytotoxicity. Notably, some studies have found that mitochondrion-targeting PDT agents exhibit enhanced selective antitumor efficacy (Guo et al., 2021). Therefore, developing a mitochondria-targeted curcumin formulation represents a rational strategy to advance CUR-based therapeutics and expand its application in PDT.

Currently, mitochondrial-targeting strategies primarily involve lipophilic cations, mitochondrial-targeting peptides, and chemically synthesized nanocarriers (Fialova et al., 2021). However, the clinical application of these sophisticated synthetic materials remains limited. This limitation is due to uncertain biocompatibility or complex manufacturing processes (Cho et al., 2020). As a result, there is still a lack of an efficient and safe drug carrier for mitochondrial targeting.

In light of the inherent characteristics of lipid droplets, this study sought to explore their potential as drug carriers for the lipophilic photosensitizer curcumin. By leveraging the natural interaction between LDs and mitochondria, we aimed to achieve targeted mitochondrial delivery and enhance the antitumor efficacy of curcumin-loaded LDs. We systematically evaluated the cytotoxicity of this approach and its effects on mitochondrial function, ROS levels, and mitochondrial membrane potential. Our findings demonstrate the feasibility and developmental potential of LDs as drug carriers, providing novel strategies for mitochondria-targeted drug delivery systems and expanding the pharmaceutical formulations of curcumin in PDT.

Materials and methods

Materials

Curcumin and coumarin-6 were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sodium oleate was acquired from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The Oil Red O staining kit was obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Lipid droplet isolation kit was purchased from Abcam Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Nile red dye was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich Company (USA). Triglyceride quantification kit, BCA protein assay kit, and ATP content assay kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Acadesine (AICAR) was obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Atorvastatin was acquired from Shanghai Yien Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos, Bodipy 493/503, Mito-Tracker Green, ROS reactive oxygen species kit, JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit, and propidium iodide dye were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Janus Green B was acquired from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Bodipy 558/568 Red-C12 was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA).

The HepG2 human hepatoma cell line was procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and subsequently maintained in our laboratory.

Isolation and characterization of LDs

Induction and isolation of LDs

To induce LD biogenesis, HepG2 cells were cultured in medium supplemented with 10% FBS (fetal bovine serum) and 0.4 mM oleic acid (OA) for 24 h. The hyperlipidic culture environment promotes massive fatty acid uptake by HepG2 cells, accelerates intracellular triglyceride synthesis, and LD accumulation. To obtain smaller LDs, cells were further starved in serum-free medium for 12 h after induction. Intracellular lipid accumulation was assessed via Oil Red O staining (see Supplementary Materials Section S1 for detailed procedures). Cellular morphology and lipid droplet distribution were subsequently analyzed using either an EVOS XL Core inverted microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or a BX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan).

The isolation of LDs was conducted using a commercial lipid droplet isolation kit. Briefly, about 3 × 107 cells were resuspended in 200 μL Reagent A and incubated on ice for 10 min. After adding 800 μL 1× Reagent B and thorough mixing, the mixture was incubated on ice for another 10 min. Cells were homogenized by repeated passage five times through a 27-gauge syringe needle, followed by careful overlaying of 600 μL 1× Reagent B onto the homogenate. Samples were centrifuged at 20,000g for 3 h at 4 °C. The uppermost white LD fraction was collected into a new microcentrifuge tube. Isolated LDs were stored at −80 °C or lyophilized for long-term preservation (see Supplementary Materials Section S2).

Characterization of LDs

The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of LDs were quantitatively assessed through dynamic light scattering (DLS) utilizing a Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS instrument (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). Morphological characteristics and structural integrity of the LDs were examined using a JEM-1200EX transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Japan). The detailed procedures are supplied in the Supplementary Materials Section S3.

Nile red, a lipophilic dye that specifically stains neutral lipids (Greenspan et al., 1985), was used to visualize the purified LDs. The LDs were incubated with 1 μg/mL Nile red solution for 30 min in the dark, after which fluorescence imaging was performed using a BX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan). The stability of the LDs was assessed by storing the LDs at 4 °C and measuring their size and zeta potential at predetermined time intervals for a period of 21 days.

Component analysis of LDs

Lipid analysis was performed according to previous literature (Wang et al., 2016). In brief, total lipids of LDs were extracted using a chloroform/acetone (7:3, v/v) mixture. The organic phase was carefully collected, subsequently dried under nitrogen, and redissolved in chloroform. The resulting solutions were then spotted onto silica gel thin-layer chromatography (TLC) plates and subjected to chromatographic separation using a solvent system composed of chloroform, methanol, acetic acid, and water in a volumetric ratio of 75:13:9:3 (v/v/v/v). When the mobile phase had reached 30% of the plate height, the TLC plate was taken out and dried in a fume hood. And then the plate was developed with an n-hexane/ethyl ether/acetic acid (80:20:2, v/v/v) solvent mixture. After development, lipid spots were visualized through exposure to iodine vapor and subsequently quantified using ImageJ software for analytical assessment.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was utilized for the separation and analysis of lipid droplet-associated proteins (see Supplementary Materials Section S4). Additionally, the triglyceride content within the LDs was quantitatively determined using a triglyceride quantification kit (see Supplementary Materials Section S5 for detailed procedures).

Interaction between lipid droplets and mitochondria

Fluorescence co-localization experiment

HepG2 cells were seeded into a 48-well plate that had been pre-coated with coverslips and cultured for 24 h. Following this, the cells underwent treatments for 12 h using four different medium formulations, each uniformly supplemented with LDs labeled with coumarin-6 (Coumarin-6-labeled LDs, C6 LDs; see Supplementary Materials Section S6 for preparation details). The experimental groups comprised: (1) serum-fed group (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS), (2) serum-starved group (serum-free DMEM), (3) acadesine-treated group (DMEM with 10% FBS and 0.625 mM acadesine), and (4) atorvastatin-treated group (serum-free DMEM with 50 μM atorvastatin). Following that, mitochondrial labeling was carried out with Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos. Ultimately, the coverslips were affixed to glass slides and examined through fluorescence microscopy. The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) was generated by ImageJ to quantify the extent of co-localization among the fluorophores.

Fatty acid transport assay

HepG2 cells were divided into two groups: endogenous LDs and exogenous LDs. Cells in the endogenous LDs group were precultured with 10 μM fluorophore-labeled fatty acid analog Bodipy558/568-C12 (Red—C12) and 0.4 mM sodium oleate for 16 h. While the exogenous LDs group was pre-treated with DMEM containing 10 μM Red-C12 and LDs containing a triglyceride concentration of 70 μM. After these interventions, both experimental groups were incubated in either serum-supplemented or serum-deprived fresh culture medium for 24 h. Cellular components were subsequently labeled with Bodipy 493/503 and Mito-Tracker Green fluorescent probes for specific visualization of LDs and mitochondria, respectively, followed by fluorescence microscopic imaging.

Measurement of ATP content

HepG2 cells were cultured overnight at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well in a 6-well plate. The cells were allocated into four experimental groups: serum-fed group (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS), serum-starved group (serum-free DMEM), Serum-fed + LDs group (DMEM with 10% FBS and 38 μM triglyceride-equivalent LDs), and serum-starved + LDs group (serum-free DMEM and 38 μM triglyceride-equivalent LDs). After a 12-h incubation, cells were harvested and subjected to sonication in an ice bath for 1 min (2 s on, 1 s off) at 200 W. The ATP content of the resulting lysates was then determined using the ATP assay kit according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

. HepG2 cells were incubated with exogenous LDs overnight in serum-free DMEM, followed by cell harvesting and preparation of ultrathin sections using an ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Germany). The intracellular distribution of LDs and mitochondria was then examined via TEM. Detailed procedures are provided in Supplementary Materials Section S7.

Preparation and characterization of curcumin-loaded LDs

To fabricate curcumin-loaded LDs, 40 μL of curcumin ethanol solution (2 mg/mL) and 1 mL of LDs solution (purified LDs containing 4 mM triglycerides) were added to 1 mL of PBS. The mixture was stirred at 50 °C for 15 min, followed by centrifugation at 22,000 ×g for 15 min (at 4 °C). The uppermost yellow layer was collected, and the morphology of curcumin-loaded LDs was assessed using TEM.

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of curcumin within the LDs were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent Technologies Inc., USA). LDs were mixed with methanol and subjected to ultrasonic disruption in an ice-water bath (300 W; 5 s on, 10 s off; total duration 2 min). The supernatant was then collected by centrifugation for subsequent analysis of curcumin content. Separation was achieved using an Agilent Plus C18 column (4.6 × 100 mm, 3.5 μm) under isocratic elution conditions with a mobile phase composed of 4% acetic acid aqueous solution and acetonitrile (50:50, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min at 30 °C, while detection was performed at a wavelength of 416 nm.

Antitumor effects of curcumin-loaded LDs

Cytotoxicity of curcumin-loaded LDs

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight under standard culture conditions. Two experimental groups were established: irradiated and non-irradiated. The culture medium was replaced with fresh serum-free DMEM supplemented with curcumin-loaded LDs at distinct curcumin concentrations, specifically 0, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 12.5 μM. After 2 h of incubation, the medium was refreshed, and the irradiated group was exposed to 450 nm LED light (intensity: 100 mW/cm2) for 5 min using an LED device (Shenzhen Height-LED Opto-electronic Co., Ltd., China), while the non-irradiated group was kept in the dark. Both groups were then cultured for an additional 24 h. Next, 20 μL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h. The medium was removed, and 150 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to solubilize the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using an ELx800 microplate reader. Cytotoxicity was expressed as the percentage of viable cells in treated wells relative to control wells.

Janus green B staining assay

HepG2 cells were divided into three groups: irradiated (CUR-LDs-PDT), non-irradiated (CUR-LDs-Dark), and control (cells without irradiation or LDs treatment). Both irradiated and non-irradiated groups were treated with 7.5 μM curcumin-loaded LDs (curcumin equivalent) and incubated in serum-free medium for 2 h. After medium replacement, the irradiated group was exposed to 450 nm LED light for 5 min, while the non-irradiated group was maintained in darkness. Following an additional 24-h incubation, cells were stained with 1% Janus Green B solution (diluted 1:5000 in PBS) for 30 min and imaged via light microscopy.

Propidium iodide staining assay

The experimental design and treatment protocols for cell groups were identical to those described in Section 2.5.2. Post-treatment, cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI) and analyzed using fluorescence microscopy.

ROS production assay

Intracellular ROS generation was quantified through dual analytical approaches: fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry, utilizing 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) as the fluorescent probe. For microscopic analysis, treated cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 20 min after a 24-h post-treatment culture period, followed by fluorescence microscopic observation. For flow cytometric analysis, cells were harvested post-treatment, incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, USA) with excitation at 488 nm and emission detection at 525 nm.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assessment

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was evaluated using a commercial Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Detection Kit. Following respective treatments, cells from all three groups (irradiated, non-irradiated, and untreated control) were trypsinized, collected, and incubated with 500 μL JC-1 dye working solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 600 ×g for 4 min at 4 °C, cells were washed twice with staining buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry. ΔΨm was determined by calculating the ratio of red fluorescence (JC-1 aggregates) to green fluorescence (JC-1 monomers).

Statistical analysis

Experimental results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Characterizations of LDs

In our study, HepG2 cells were cultured in a high-fat medium supplemented with 0.4 mM sodium oleate to induce significant lipid accumulation. Lipid droplet formation was evaluated via Oil Red O staining, which visualized LDs as red-orange intracellular structures (Fig. 1A and B). Following sodium oleate-induced lipid loading, LDs were isolated from high-fat cells by high-speed centrifugation. The upper white lipid layer was collected and stained with Nile Red. We next investigated the modulation of lipid droplet size. Following treatment of HepG2 cells with 0.4 mM sodium oleate for 24 h, the extracted LDs exhibited an average particle size of 499.60 ± 24.03 nm (PDI: 0.64 ± 0.03), and a zeta potential of −16.13 ± 0.45 mV (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, LDs were formulated into a lyophilized product. The lyophilized LDs formed a uniform white porous powder (Fig. S1). Upon resuspension, LDs showed excellent dispersibility with no visible precipitation, and particle size (486.53 ± 48.75 nm, PDI: 0.73 ± 0.09) remained consistent pre- and post-lyophilization, facilitating long-term storage and commercial applications.

Although this simple and efficient induction method generates abundant LDs, the resulting particle size may not be optimal for drug delivery. To address this issue, we optimized the preparation procedure by adjusting OA concentration, incubation time, nutrient conditions, cell sources (including 3 T3-L1 cells), and filtration strategies. Serum-free starvation was found to effectively reduce LD size, as large perinuclear LDs undergo fragmentation and disperse into smaller LDs in response to lipolytic stimuli (Nguyen et al., 2017). However, prolonged starvation induced significant cell death, as evidenced by cell detachment, reduced cell density, and the presence of cellular debris, which consequently decreased LD yield (Supplementary Materials Section S8, Fig. S2). Considering experimental efficiency and LD production, we finally introduced an additional serum-free starvation treatment for 12 h after 24 h of LD induction with 0.4 mM sodium oleate. This strategy successfully reduced the average LD size to 246.20 ± 30.13 nm (PDI: 0.41 ± 0.05), making them more conducive to cellular uptake and drug delivery. TEM confirmed that these LDs were spherical structures with a monolayer membrane (Fig. 1E). When dispersed in PBS, the LDs exhibited stable size retention for 21 days without significant alteration (Table S1).

Principal component analysis

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis revealed that the LDs contained 1,2-Di (9Z-octadecenoyl)-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC), triacylglycerol (TAG), and some other neutral lipids. TAG was the major lipid component, while phospholipids represented a smaller fraction (Fig. 2A). Liu et al. (Ding et al., 2013) developed an equation to determine the purity of isolated LDs. They modeled LDs as concentric spheres, with R as the total LD radius and r as the neutral lipid core radius (excluding the phospholipid layer). Thus, the volume of the phospholipid monolayer (Vp) is derived as the difference between the volume of the outer LD and that of the inner neutral lipid core. A phospholipid ratio higher than the calculated value may indicate membrane contamination. Given that the phospholipid monolayer is approximately 3.0 nm thick, the theoretical volume of the neutral lipid core can be calculated from the measured lipid droplet size. The lipid droplets used in this study exhibited a DLS-determined size distribution of 590–800 nm, corresponding to a theoretical DOPC-to-total lipid (DOPC + TAG) ratio of 2.23–2.98%. ImageJ-based analysis of TLC plate grayscale values revealed an experimental DOPC-to-total lipid ratio of 2.24%, which falls within the theoretical range. This finding confirms both the high purity and the monolayer structure of the LDs.Fig. 2Component analysis of lipid droplets: (A) LDs lipid analysis. (B) Protein electrophoresis.Fig. 2

The surface of mammalian lipid droplets hosts dozens to hundreds of proteins involved in cellular homeostasis (Farese Jr. and Walther, 2009). Protein information was obtained from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org, accessed April 2025), including PLIN5 (50.8 kDa), Rab18 (23.0 kDa), DGAT2 (43.8 kDa), CCT (41.7 kDa), and TIP47 (47.1 kDa), which underlie LD functional integrity. SDS-PAGE of isolated LDs displayed protein bands spanning 35–100 kDa, covering the typical molecular weight range of known LD proteins (20–70 kDa) (Fig. 2B, Fig. S3). This confirmed that the isolated spherical organelles contained proteins, consistent with the canonical biological characteristics of LDs. Additional minor bands outside the LD protein range likely corresponded to co-isolated membrane and mitochondrial contaminants, a common phenomenon in subcellular fractionation (Cui and Liu, 2020).

Interaction between lipid droplets and mitochondria in cells

Before preparing drug-loaded LDs, we investigated the interaction between purified LDs and cellular mitochondria. In view of the TLC results, we selected triacylglycerol (TAG, also termed triglyceride) as a characteristic marker to quantify the concentration of purified LDs. An aliquot of freshly isolated LDs was diluted 10-fold with PBS, and its triglyceride content was determined using a triglyceride quantification kit. The original LD stock suspension was then diluted with fresh culture medium to the required working concentration (16–70 μM) for subsequent cell treatment experiments. Cytotoxicity test (MTT assay) of blank LDs results showed that incubating cells with LDs containing ≤70 μM triglycerides for 24 h did not significantly affect cell viability (Supplementary Materials Section S9, Fig. S4). Based on these results, a concentration of 70 μM was used in subsequent experiments. Co-incubation of HepG2 cells with LDs revealed that maximal cellular uptake was achieved within 2 h, with no significant difference observed between 2 h and 6 h of incubation. (Supplementary Materials Section S10, Fig. S5).

Subsequently, C6 LDs (Fig. S6) were incubated with HepG2 cells under four different medium formulations. As shown in Fig. 3A, with the corresponding Pearson correlation coefficients provided in Table S2, under nutrient-sufficient conditions, C6 LDs exhibited negligible colocalization with mitochondria (PCC = 0.19 ± 0.06). In contrast, AMPK activation via acadesin treatment enhanced the proximity between LDs and mitochondria (PCC = 0.58 ± 0.15), in line with previous mechanistic reports (Herms et al., 2015). The activation of the energy sensor AMPK promotes lipid droplet dispersion along detyrosinated microtubules to increase mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation. Under nutrient starvation, LD redistribution from aggregation to peripheral localization supports robust LD-mitochondrial crosstalk, which also requires AMPK activation. Starvation-induced AMPK activation synchronously enhances LD motility, reorganizes detyrosinated microtubule networks, and activates mitochondria (Herms et al., 2015). In addition, the interaction between LDs and mitochondria is tightly regulated by LD-associated proteins (Miner et al., 2023). PLIN5 overexpression can promote the mutual recruitment of mitochondria and LDs (Benador et al., 2018). Comparison of the serum-starved and atorvastatin-treated groups revealed marked co-localization between mitochondria and LDs under starvation conditions, whereas atorvastatin-mediated PLIN5 suppression attenuated this interaction, with a statistically significant difference in PCC between the two groups (PCC = 0.80 ± 0.05 vs. 0.23 ± 0.03, p < 0.01). As reported (Selen et al., 2021), exogenous fatty acids are predominantly converted to triglycerides and stored in LDs under nutrient-rich conditions. During energy demand, triglycerides are hydrolyzed to release free fatty acids, which are transported to mitochondria for β-oxidation. BODIPY lipid droplet dyes can specifically stain neutral lipids (Yu et al., 2012). We utilized the Bodipy 558/568 Red-C12 (Red—C12) to visually track the transport of fatty acids between LDs and mitochondria. In the endogenous LDs group, Red-C12 was mixed with a high-fat medium and incubated with cells. Red-C12 was taken up into the cytoplasm along with fatty acids and subsequently incorporated into endogenous LDs. As shown in Fig. 3B-C, under serum-containing conditions, Red-C12 exhibited strong colocalization with LDs (PCC = 0.97 ± 0.01) but weak colocalization with mitochondria (PCC = 0.47 ± 0.05). In contrast, under serum-free conditions, Red-C12 colocalization with LDs was reduced, accompanied by partial cytoplasmic dispersion of Red—C12, whereas its colocalization with mitochondria increased (PCC = 0.75 ± 0.048), indicating efficient fatty acid translocation to mitochondria after 24 h of starvation.

In the exogenous LDs group, cells were incubated with Red-C12 and LDs, then cultured in serum-containing or serum-free medium. Fluorescence microscopy revealed granular LDs aggregated perinuclearly under serum-containing conditions, with Red-C12 highly colocalized with these LDs (PCC = 0.91 ± 0.04) but only slight overlap with mitochondria (PCC = 0.41 ± 0.06). Serum starvation dispersed Red-C12 fluorescence in the cytoplasm and increased Red-C12-mitochondria overlap, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.68 ± 0.03 (Fig. 3B and D). These observations are consistent with those from the endogenous LDs group, confirming that fatty acids derived from exogenous LDs share the same intracellular transport fate as those from endogenous LDs.

ATP quantification revealed that supplementation of LDs during starvation significantly elevated ATP levels (Fig. 3E). TEM imaging of ultrathin cellular sections confirmed that upon addition to starved cell cultures, LDs were internalized and localized adjacent to mitochondria (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these results validate the interaction between exogenous LDs and cellular mitochondria.

Antitumor activity of curcumin-loaded LDs and their effects on mitochondrial function

Given their biocompatibility, stability, and controllable size, LDs hold great promise for drug delivery. Curcumin was model drug loaded into LDs via physical methods of heating and magnetic stirring (Fig. 4A), yielding green-fluorescent curcumin-loaded LDs (Fig. 4B). The drug loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency of curcumin-loaded LDs were quantitatively assessed using HPLC, yielding values of 1.03 ± 0.28% and 44.36 ± 12.01%, respectively. TEM images showed that curcumin-loaded LDs exhibited a spherical morphology with a phospholipid monolayer and a size distribution predominantly below 200 nm (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, we evaluated the antitumor efficacy of curcumin-loaded LDs in photodynamic therapy. Cytotoxicity assays showed no significant viability loss in HepG2 cells treated with curcumin-LDs at ≤7.5 μM under light-proteclited culture conditions. Next, serum-free starvation was applied to facilitate the targeting of drug-loaded LDs to mitochondria. Cells were incubated with curcumin-loaded LDs in serum-free medium for 2 h. Subsequently, the experimental group was irradiated with 450 nm light (100 mW/cm2, 5 min), followed by comparison with the untreated control group. Several detection reagents, including Janus Green B, propidium iodide, DCFH-DA probe, and JC-1 probe, were further used to investigate the effects of curcumin-loaded LDs on cells and intracellular mitochondria after light irradiation. Janus green B dye was utilized to assess the impact of curcumin-loaded LDs on mitochondrial function. Active cytochrome oxidase in functional mitochondria maintains the dye in an oxidized blue-green state, whereas inactive mitochondria fail to oxidize the dye, resulting in a colorless phenotype. Following 5 min of irradiation with 450 nm light (100 mW/cm2), cells in the CUR-LDs-PDT group showed light blue staining, indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction and cytochrome oxidase inactivation. In contrast, intense dark blue staining in control and CUR-LDs-Dark groups confirmed relatively intact mitochondrial function (Fig. 5B).

Propidium iodide (PI), a membrane-impermeable nucleic acid dye, stains nuclei red in late apoptotic/necrotic cells with compromised membrane integrity (Dengler et al., 1995). HepG2 cells treated with curcumin-loaded LDs under 450 nm light (100 mW/cm2, 5 min) exposure showed a marked increase in PI-positive cells compared to control and CUR-LDs-Dark groups, as validated by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 5C).

Photodynamic therapy employs photosensitizers and specific visible light irradiation to generate ROS that are locally cytotoxic to target sites (Idris et al., 2012). ROS generation was detected using the DCFH-DA probe. Under dark conditions, both control and CUR-LDs-Dark groups exhibited basal ROS levels. In contrast, A substantial ROS burst was observed in the CUR-LDs-PDT group upon light irradiation. As shown in Fig. 5D and E, the CUR-LDs-PDT group exhibited a 66.1% increase in FITC-A fluorescence intensity compared to the untreated control group. The mean IOD value (a.u.) was 5.2-fold higher in the CUR-LDs-PDT group (2354.67 ± 265.50) than in the control group (453.33 ± 24.11, p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed between the CUR-LDs-Dark group (876.67 ± 98.50) and the control group (p > 0.05), whereas a significant increase was detected in the CUR-LDs-PDT group compared to the CUR-LDs-Dark group (p < 0.001). In parallel, bright-field microscopy showed that CUR-LDs-PDT treatment induced morphological alterations (cell swelling, rounding, and apoptotic body formation), as well as elevated apoptosis/necrosis (Fig. 5F). These results indicated that CUR-LDs can act as photosensitizers in PDT. Upon light irradiation, they produce cytotoxic ROS to exert anti-tumor effects.

To further explore the mechanism of mitochondrial dysfunction, JC-1 probe-based flow cytometry was employed to assess mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). Cells were subjected to either light irradiation (450 nm, 100 mW/cm2, 5 min) or dark conditions. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 5G, Table 1) showed the CUR-LDs-PDT group exhibited a significantly lower red/green fluorescence ratio (1.76 ± 0.04) compared to the control group (26.50 ± 4.81) and the Dark group (11.30 ± 1.21) (p < 0.05), indicating MMP collapse. MMP depolarization is an early hallmark of mitochondria-mediated apoptosis (Fernando et al., 2022). Taken together, we propose that the combined treatment of CUR-LDs and photodynamic therapy exerts damaging effects on HepG2 cells and intracellular mitochondria by significantly increasing intracellular ROS levels and triggering cellular oxidative stress. Excessive ROS attacks mitochondria, inducing mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization and inactivating cytochrome oxidase, thereby impairing redox activity. Subsequently, cell membrane integrity is disrupted, osmotic homeostasis is altered, cells become swollen and rounded, and apoptotic bodies form, ultimately driving cells into apoptosis or necrosis.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that isolated lipid droplets retain their intrinsic mitochondrial tropism while exhibiting tunable physicochemical properties, making them promising candidates for organelle-specific drug delivery. By developing curcumin-loaded LDs (CUR-LDs), we engineered a mitochondria-targeted photodynamic therapy (PDT) system that leverages the natural β-oxidation pathway for drug transport. We reduced LD size via post-isolation starvation to obtain appropriately sized lipid droplets as carriers for curcumin loading. Short-term starvation further promoted the translocation of lipid droplets toward mitochondria to improve lipid transfer efficiency. Functional validation revealed that CUR-LDs combined with PDT markedly impaired mitochondrial function and exerted cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells, as evidenced by increased ROS production, inactivation of mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase, disrupted redox activity, mitochondrial membrane potential collapse, altered cell morphology, apoptotic body formation, and massive cell apoptosis and necrosis. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of this drug delivery strategy, inspire the design of novel carriers, expand the nanoformulation landscape of curcumin, and facilitate the translation of lipophilic phytochemicals into precision oncology therapies.

Although our results validate this concept at the cellular level, the comprehensive exploitation of lipid droplets as drug carriers needs detailed in vivo validation and optimization of curcumin dosing protocols. Notably, the biological functions of lipid droplets are intrinsically linked to their surface-associated proteins. Previous reports have documented that proteins can be recruited to lipid droplets via incubation methods (Zhao et al., 2022). Future engineering efforts to develop protein-decorated artificial LDs may further enhance targeting specificity, thereby advancing precision cancer therapy. Overall, LDs hold substantial potential for the development of effective drug delivery strategies, meriting further exploration in pharmaceutical research.
