Bases of Lebesgue spaces formed by neural networks
Vladimir Kulbatov, Jan Lang, Cornelia Schneider, Jan Vybíral

Abstract
The seminal work of Daubechies, DeVore, Foucart, Hanin, and Petrova introduced in 2022 a sequence of univariate piece-wise linear functions, which resemble the classical Fourier basis and which, at the same time, can be easily reproduced by artificial neural networks with ReLU \ReLU activation function. We give an alternative way how to calculate the inner products of functions from this system and discuss the spectral properties of the Gram matrix generated by this system. The univariate system was later generalized to the multivariate setting by two of the authors of this work. Instead of the usual tensor product construction, this generalization relied on the inner products inside of the argument of the univariate sequence. It turned out that such a system forms a Riesz basis of L 2 ​ ( 0 , 1 ) n L_{2}(0,1)^{n} for every n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 with Riesz constants independent of n n . In this work, we investigate the properties of these new sequences of functions in L q ​ ( 0 , 1 ) n L_{q}(0,1)^{n} for q ≠ 2 . q\not=2. First, we show that the univariate system is a Schauder basis in L q ​ ( 0 , 1 ) L_{q}(0,1) for every 1 < q < ∞ 1<q<\infty . By a general argument, it follows that the tensor products of this system also form a Schauder basis in L q ​ ( 0 , 1 ) n L_{q}(0,1)^{n} for every n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 and 1 < q < ∞ . 1<q<\infty. The same fact can also be shown by measuring the distance of the tensor product system to the classical multivariate Fourier basis, but - surprisingly - this argument only works for n ≤ 3 n\leq 3 . If, on the other hand, we replace the outer tensor products by inner products directly in the argument of the univariate system, the same approach is applicable for an arbitrary dimension n ∈ ℕ . n\in\mathbb{N}. Key Words: Riesz basis, Schauder basis, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Tensor product MSC2020 Math Subject Classifications: Primary 42C15; Secondary 46B15, 68T07

Introduction

In their groundbreaking paper
[
5
]
, Daubechies, DeVore, Foucart, Hanin, and Petrova introduced a sequence of univariate piece-wise linear functions,
which, on one hand, can be easily reproduced by (deep) ReLU neural networks and, on the other hand, resemble the standard Fourier basis.
The topic was further developed in
[
27
]
, where a multivariate analogue of this basis was investigated.

To recall the known results, we first define two auxiliary functions

\mathcal{C}(x)=4\left|x-\frac{1}{2}\right|-1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{S}(x)=\left|2-4\left|x-\frac{1}{4}\right|\right|-1,\quad x\in[0,1].

These functions are extended to
x\in\mathbb{R}
periodically, i.e.,
{\mathcal{C}}(x)=\mathcal{C}(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)
and
\mathcal{S}(x)=\mathcal{S}(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)
.
Finally, we consider integer dilates of these functions formally defined as
\mathcal{C}_{k}(x):=\mathcal{C}(kx)
and
\mathcal{S}_{k}(x):=\mathcal{S}(kx)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}.

Figure 1
:
The plot of
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
.

It was shown in
[
5
]
(with an alternative proof given in
[
27
]
) that

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}:=\{1\}\cup\{\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{C}_{k},\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{S}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}

(1.1)

forms a so-called Riesz basis of
L_{2}(0,1)
. Recall, that a sequence
(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset L_{2}(0,1)

is called a Riesz sequence, if there are two positive and finite constants
0<A\leq B<\infty
such that

A\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}^{2}\leq\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}\right\|^{2}\leq B\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}^{2}

(1.2)

holds for every square-summable sequence
(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
.
In such a case, the constants
A
and
B
are usually called Riesz constants of
(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
.
Furthermore, if the closed linear span of
(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
is the whole
L_{2}(0,1)
, then we call it a Riesz basis.
With this notation, it was shown in
[
5
]
that the Riesz constants of (
1.1
) can be chosen as
A=\frac{1}{2}
and
B=\frac{3}{2}.

The main aim of this work is to complement our knowledge regarding the properties of (
1.1
).

First, our proof that (
1.1
) forms a Riesz basis of
L_{2}(0,1)
was based on the properties of the Gram matrices
(\langle\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{l}\rangle)_{k,l\in\mathbb{N}}

and
(\langle\mathcal{S}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{l}\rangle)_{k,l\in\mathbb{N}}
and the values of the inner products
\langle\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{l}\rangle

and
\langle\mathcal{S}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{l}\rangle
were calculated by expanding
\mathcal{C}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{l}
(or
\mathcal{S}_{k}
and
\mathcal{S}_{l}
) into their Fourier series.
Given the simplicity of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
and
\mathcal{S}_{l}
, one might wonder if there is not a direct way to compute these inner products.
In Section
2.1
we provide such a calculation, which avoids any use of Fourier analysis (but is based on the Chinese remainder theorem instead).

Our next contribution to
[
27
]
is the proof that the Gram matrices
(\langle\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{l}\rangle)_{k,l\in\mathbb{N}}

and
(\langle\mathcal{S}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{l}\rangle)_{k,l\in\mathbb{N}}
have the same spectra, although their entries differ sometimes in sign.
We observed this effect already numerically when working on
[
27
]
and Section
2.2

presents a short proof of this fact.

The next aim of our present work is to investigate the properties of (
1.1
) outside of the Hilbert space setting.
To be more specific, we consider Lebesgue spaces
L_{q}(0,1)
for
1<q<\infty.

In that case, there is a number of different ways how to define a basis and the subject was studied extensively in functional analysis
[
1
,
11
,
20
,
29
]
.
Our focus lies on the notion of a Schauder basis, which we recall for the reader’s convenience.

Definition 1.1
.

If
X
is a Banach space, then the sequence
(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset X
is a Schauder basis of
X
if for every element
x\in X

there is a unique sequence of scalars
(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that

x=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}.

(1.3)

Note, that the series in (
1.3
) does not need to converge unconditionally and that the ordering of the basis elements
(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}

may be crucial. This effect was studied in detail in harmonic analysis in connection with the convergence of multivariate Fourier series, cf.
[
12
,
13
]
.
We return to this issue later on in Section
3
, where we study Schauder bases of spaces of multivariate functions.

It is a classical result from harmonic analysis (cf.
[
32
, Example II.B.11]
) that the complex exponential functions

(e^{2\pi ikx})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
form a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)
for every
1<q<\infty.

To deal with function spaces of real-valued functions and to simplify the notation, we use a variant of this result, namely that the system
(\sqrt{2}\sin(j\pi t))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

is a Schauder basis of the real
L_{q}(0,1)
space.
The piece-wise linear approximation of this system is defined by setting
S(t):=\mathcal{S}(t/2)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
\mathcal{S}(x)=\mathcal{S}_{1}(x)
is one of the functions from the Riesz basis (
1.1
) above, and
S_{j}(t)=S(jt)
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Clearly, the family
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is again easily reproducible by artificial neural networks, and Theorem
2.6
shows that it also constitutes
a Schauder basis in
L_{q}(0,1)
. The argument used to prove Theorem
2.6
is rather standard and commonly used in the context of Schauder bases.
Namely, we show that there is a bounded linear operator
T
, mapping
L_{q}(0,1)
onto itself, which has a bounded inverse and which maps the sequence

(\sqrt{2}\sin(j\pi t))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
onto
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. This allows to transfer the property of being a Schauder basis from one of these systems to the other.

Section
3
is devoted to spaces of multivariate functions, defined on
(0,1)^{n}.
Let us first comment on the Hilbert space setting
q=2
and the known results.
The usual and most natural way how to create an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
– once we have an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}(0,1)
– is to consider the tensor products
of this univariate basis. This idea can easily be translated to Riesz bases but, unfortunately, this approach comes with certain drawbacks. First,
the Riesz constants of the multivariate basis are the products of the Riesz constants of the univariate basis. Given the results of
[
5
]
, this would lead to an
exponential dependence of the Riesz constants on the underlying dimension
n
. Another obstacle is that
\ReLU
artificial neural networks
cannot reproduce the multiplication function
f:(x,y)\to x\cdot y
exactly, cf.
[
10
,
30
]
, and, therefore, the tensor products of the elements of (
1.1
) are not easily
reproducible by
\ReLU
artificial neural networks.

A more viable direction was pursued in
[
27
]
, where the multivariate analogue of (
1.1
) was defined using inner products as follows

{\mathcal{R}}_{n}:=\{1\}\cup\{\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}},\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{k}}:\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbf{k}+\!\!\!>0\}.

(1.4)

Here,
\mathbf{k}+\!\!\!>0
for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
means that the first non-zero entry of
\mathbf{k}
is non-negative
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}}(x):=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{k}}(x):=\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)
.
Using this definition, it was shown in
[
27
]
that
\mathcal{R}_{n}
is a Riesz basis of
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
with Riesz constants
A=1/2
and
B=3/2

independent of
n
. In this sense, taking the inner products of
\mathbf{k}
and
x
inside of the argument of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{S}
is clearly superior
to taking simply the tensor products of the elements of (
1.1
). The potential of (
1.4
) in approximation
of functions from Sobolev and Barron spaces was then investigated in
[
28
]
.

Section
3
investigates the behavior of the different sequences of multivariate functions outside of the Hilbert space setting,
i.e., as subsets of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
for
1<q<\infty.
First, we use the fact that the tensor products of functions from
(\sqrt{2}\sin(j\pi t))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

form a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
and apply the standard proof method to show in Theorem
3.4
that the same holds also for tensor products
of the elements of
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Interestingly, the distance between the corresponding multivariate bases grows with the dimension and the proof method of Theorem
3.4

only works for
n\leq 3.
In Section
3.2
, we apply the abstract theory of tensor products of Banach spaces
to show, that this restriction on
n
is mainly the artifact of the proof method used, and that the same result holds also for
n>3.

In the next step, we compare this result to the construction involving inner products. To be more precise, in Theorem
3.7

we show that

\{1\}\cup\{\sqrt{2}\,\cos(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x):\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbf{k}+\!\!\!>0\}\cup\{\sqrt{2}\,\sin(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x):\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbf{k}+\!\!\!>0\}

is an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
. Next we show in Proposition
3.8
that it also constitutes a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1<q<\infty.
Finally, the proof method of Theorem
2.6
then allows us (cf. Theorem
3.9
) to conclude that
\mathcal{R}_{n}

is a Schauder basis in
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}.
Note that in this case this approach is independent of the dimension
n
.

Our final remark concerns again the setting of Hilbert spaces. Without much additional effort, the proof method, which we use
to deal with Schauder bases, can also be applied to Riesz bases. When doing so, we not only re-prove the results
of
[
5
]
and
[
27
]
for
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
, but we also improve the lower Riesz constant

A
to
A=0.5787\dots>1/2
in both, the univariate and the multivariate setting, cf. Theorem
2.7
and Theorem
3.10
, respectively.

Univariate bases

The aim of this section is twofold. First, we revisit some properties of the system (
1.1
), which were already discussed in
[
27
]
,
but we provide alternative proofs, which shed new light on this system and, possibly, might also allow to extend these properties to other systems in the future.
The second aim of this section is to investigate the properties of (
1.1
) outside of the Hilbert space setting, i.e., to study the properties
of (
1.1
) as a subset of
L_{q}(0,1)
for
q\not=2.

2.1
Inner products of functions from
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}

The inner products of functions from
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}
were calculated in
[
27
, Lemma 2.1]
and they played a crucial role
in the estimates of the Riesz constants of
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}
. The original proof method, inspired by
[
5
]
, was based on the Fourier decomposition
of the elements of (
1.1
). As the functions from (
1.1
) are piece-wise linear, it seems quite intuitive that there should also be a simpler
and more direct way to compute these inner products. In this section, we indeed present such a way, based on direct calculations and the Chinese remainder theorem
[
18
, Theorem 121]
.

For the reader’s convenience, we restate the result, which we are going to reprove.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
27
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Let
j,k\in\mathbb{N}.
Then

1.

\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle=0
;

2.

\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle=0
if the prime factorizations of
j
and
k
contain a different power of 2;

3.

If the prime factorizations of
j
and
k
contain the same power of two, then

3\,\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle=3\,|\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle|=\frac{\gcd(j,k)^{4}}{j^{2}\cdot k^{2}}.

The sign of
\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle
is negative if, and only if,
(j+k)/(2\gcd(j,k))
is even.

Proof.

First, we show that it is enough to prove the lemma for
j
and
k
co-prime, i.e., if
\gcd(j,k)=1.
Indeed, assume that the lemma
was already proven for co-prime indices and assume that
j
and
k
are arbitrary positive integers. Then we write

j=\ell\cdot J
and
k=\ell\cdot K
, where
\ell=\gcd(j,k)
and
J
and
K
are co-prime. Using periodicity, we get

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}(jx)\mathcal{C}(kx)dx=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}(\ell Jx)\mathcal{C}(\ell Kx)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\ell}{\mathcal{C}}(Jy)\mathcal{C}(Ky)\frac{dy}{\ell}=\int_{0}^{1}{\mathcal{C}}(Jy)\mathcal{C}(Ky)dy=\langle\mathcal{C}_{J},\mathcal{C}_{K}\rangle.

If
j
and
k
contain a different power of two in their prime factorization, then so do
J
and
K
and the last expression is equal to zero.
On the other hand, if
j
and
k
contain the same power of two, then
J
and
K
are odd and assuming the lemma holds for
J
and
K
co-prime, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{C}_{J},\mathcal{C}_{K}\rangle=\frac{1}{J^{2}\cdot K^{2}}=\frac{\ell^{4}}{(\ell\cdot J)^{2}\cdot(\ell\cdot K)^{2}}=\frac{\gcd(j,k)^{4}}{j^{2}\cdot k^{2}}.

The same argument applies also for the calculation of
\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle
.

Hence, for the rest of the proof we consider positive co-prime integers
j
and
k
and calculate

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}(jx)\mathcal{C}(kx)dx=\sum_{m=0}^{jk-1}\int_{\frac{m}{jk}}^{\frac{m+1}{jk}}\mathcal{C}(jx)\mathcal{C}(kx)dx=\frac{1}{jk}\,\sum_{m=0}^{jk-1}\int_{m}^{m+1}\mathcal{C}(y/k)\,\mathcal{C}(y/j)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{jk}\,\sum_{m=0}^{jk-1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{m+z}{k}\right)\,\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{m+z}{j}\right)dz.

For every integer
m
with
0\leq m<jk
, there is a unique pair of integers
(a,b)
with

\displaystyle 0\leq a<j,\quad m\bmod j=a,

\displaystyle 0\leq b<k,\quad m\bmod k=b.

The Chinese remainder theorem states that, if
j
and
k
are co-prime, then every such pair
(a,b)
appears exactly once
when
m
is running from 0 to
jk-1
. This and the periodicity of
\mathcal{C}
allow us to write

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{jk}\,\sum_{a=0}^{j-1}\sum_{b=0}^{k-1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{b+z}{k}\right)\,\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{jk}\,\int_{0}^{1}\left[\sum_{a=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)\right]\,\cdot\,\left[\sum_{b=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{b+z}{k}\right)\right]dz.

Due to symmetry, it is enough to evaluate the first sum. If
j
is even, this sum is equal to zero. Indeed, then

\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{j/2-1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)+\sum_{a=j/2}^{j-1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{j/2-1}\left[4\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a+z}{j}\right)-1\right]+\sum_{a=j/2}^{j-1}\left[4\left(\frac{a+z}{j}-\frac{1}{2}\right)-1\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{j/2-1}\left[1-4\,\frac{a+z}{j}\right]+\sum_{a=0}^{j/2-1}\left[4\,\frac{a+j/2+z}{j}-3\right]=0.

If
j
is odd, we consider first
0\leq z<1/2
and obtain in a similar way

\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{\frac{j-1}{2}}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)+\sum_{a=\frac{j+1}{2}}^{j-1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)

(2.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{\frac{j-1}{2}}\left[4\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a+z}{j}\right)-1\right]+\sum_{a=\frac{j+1}{2}}^{j-1}\left[4\left(\frac{a+z}{j}-\frac{1}{2}\right)-1\right]

\displaystyle=1-4\,\frac{z}{j}+\sum_{a=1}^{\frac{j-1}{2}}\left[1-4\,\frac{a+z}{j}+4\,\frac{a+\frac{j-1}{2}+z}{j}-3\right]

\displaystyle=1-4\,\frac{z}{j}+\frac{j-1}{2}\left(2\cdot\frac{j-1}{j}-2\right)=\frac{1-4z}{j}.

If
1/2\leq z<1
, we proceed similarly and split the sum in (
2.1
) into
0\leq a\leq\frac{j-3}{2}
and
\frac{j-1}{2}\leq a\leq j-1
, which leads to

\sum_{a=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{C}\left(\frac{a+z}{j}\right)=\frac{4z-3}{j}.

Finally, we obtain

\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{jk}\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{1-4z}{j}\cdot\frac{1-4z}{k}dz+\frac{1}{jk}\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{4z-3}{j}\cdot\frac{4z-3}{k}dz=\frac{1}{3j^{2}k^{2}}.

The mixed inner products
\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle
vanish due to the symmetry around the middle point
1/2.

Indeed,
\mathcal{C}(x)=\mathcal{C}(1-x)
and
\mathcal{S}(x)=-\mathcal{S}(1-x)
for
x\in(0,1)
and this relation extends easily also to
\mathcal{C}_{j}
and
\mathcal{S}_{k}.

Consequently,
\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle=0
for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}.

Finally, the inner products
\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle
could be calculated similarly to the method used above for
\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle
,
but they can actually be derived easily from what we have shown already. For example, if
j
and
k
are both odd, we get
\mathcal{S}(jx)=\mathcal{C}(jx-1/4)=\mathcal{C}(jx-j/4)

if
4
divides
j-1
and
\mathcal{S}(jx)=-\mathcal{C}(jx-3/4)=\mathcal{C}(jx-j/4)
if
4
divides
j-3.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{S}(jx)\mathcal{S}(kx)dx=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}(j(x-1/4))\mathcal{C}(k(x-1/4))dx=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{C}(jx)\mathcal{C}(kx)dx=\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle

if
4
divides both
j-1
and
k-1
. Similarly, one can argue if 4 divides
j-3
and
k-3
. In both these cases
\frac{j+k}{2\gcd(j,k)}
is odd and the result follows.
The other cases are treated similarly.
∎

2.2
Spectral properties of
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}

The Gram matrix of (
1.1
) played a crucial role in
[
27
]
as its spectrum determines the Riesz constants of (
1.1
).
Also, it was observed that it is somehow simpler to study first the spectrum of the Gram matrix of the truncated system

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}^{N}=\{1\}\cup\{\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{C}_{k},\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{S}_{k}:1\leq k\leq N\}

and then pass to the limit
N\to\infty.
It is quite natural to re-order the Gram matrix of
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}^{N}
as

G_{N}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&0&0\\
0&G_{N}^{\mathcal{C}}&0\\
0&0&G_{N}^{\mathcal{S}}\end{matrix}\right],

where
G_{N}^{\mathcal{C}}=(\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle)_{j,k=1}^{N}
is the Gram matrix of
\{\mathcal{C}_{k}:1\leq k\leq N\}
and

G_{N}^{\mathcal{S}}=(\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle)_{j,k=1}^{N}
is the Gram matrix of
\{\mathcal{S}_{k}:1\leq k\leq N\}
.
By Lemma
2.1
, the entries of
G_{N}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
G_{N}^{\mathcal{S}}
are equal in the absolute value, but they might have a different sign.
Interestingly, when working on
[
27
]
, numerical evidence suggested that the spectra of
G_{N}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
G_{N}^{\mathcal{S}}

are equal. The aim of this section is to explain this phenomenon.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
N\geq 1
. Then the spectra of
G_{N}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
G_{N}^{\mathcal{S}}
coincide.

Proof.

We use the fact (cf. Lemma
2.1
) that
\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle=0

if the prime factorizations of
j
and
k
contain a different power of two. This allows us to re-group the indices
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}

in such a way that
G_{N}^{\mathcal{C}}
becomes again a block-diagonal matrix. We put for
0\leq r\leq\lfloor\log_{2}N\rfloor

I_{N}^{r}=\{1\leq j\leq N:2^{r}\mid j\ \text{and}\ 2^{r+1}\nmid j\}

and denote
G_{N,r}^{\mathcal{C}}=(\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle)_{j,k\in I_{N}^{r}}
(and similarly for
G_{N,r}^{\mathcal{S}}
).
Then we can write

G_{N}^{\mathcal{C}}=\left[\begin{matrix}G_{N,0}^{\mathcal{C}}&0&0&\dots\\
0&G_{N,1}^{\mathcal{C}}&0&\dots\\
0&0&G_{N,2}^{\mathcal{C}}&\dots\\
0&0&0&\ddots\end{matrix}\right]

and, again, similarly for
G_{N}^{\mathcal{S}}
. We show that
G_{N,r}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
G_{N,r}^{\mathcal{S}}
have the same spectrum for all
r
’s.

We start with
r=0
. Note, that
I_{N}^{0}
collects all odd integers between 1 and
N
. We show that
G_{N,0}^{\mathcal{S}}=D_{N,0}G_{N,0}^{\mathcal{C}}D_{N,0}
,
where
D_{N,0}
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{3},\dots\in\{-1,+1\}
on the diagonal, where we put

\varepsilon_{j}:=\begin{cases}+1,&\quad\text{if}\ 4\mid(j-1),\\
-1,&\quad\text{if}\ 4\mid(j-3).\end{cases}

Indeed, with this choice we have

\varepsilon_{j}\cdot\varepsilon_{k}=\begin{cases}+1,&\quad\text{if}\ 4\mid(j+k-2),\\
-1,&\quad\text{if}\ 4\mid(j+k).\end{cases}

As
\gcd(j,k)
is odd,
\varepsilon_{j}\cdot\varepsilon_{k}=-1
if, and only if,
\frac{j+k}{2\gcd(j,k)}
is even.
Consequently, by Lemma
2.1
,

\langle\mathcal{S}_{j},\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle=\varepsilon_{j}\cdot\varepsilon_{k}\cdot\langle\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle.

Hence,
G_{N,0}^{\mathcal{S}}=D_{N,0}G_{N,0}^{\mathcal{C}}D_{N,0}
and the result follows.

If
r\geq 1
, a similar argument goes through by setting
\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{j/2^{r}}
for
j\in I_{N}^{r}.
We leave the details to the reader.
∎

2.3
Schauder bases of Lebesgue spaces

The aim of this section is to extend our study of bases of piece-wise linear functions to Lebesgue spaces
L_{q}(0,1)
with
1<q<\infty.

To simplify the notation, we use the following standard trick. If
f
is an (integrable) function defined on
(0,1)
, we consider its odd extension (denoted again by
f
) to
(-1,1)

and consider its Fourier series expansion into the basis formed by
\{1\}\cup\{\cos(j\pi x),\sin(j\pi x):j\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Due to the symmetry of this extension, the
\cos
-part of this expansion vanishes, which allows us to represent
f
on
(0,1)
as its
Fourier sine series
,
i.e., in terms of
\sin(j\pi x)
with
j\in\mathbb{N}.

It is a well known result (essentially due to Riesz
[
25
]
who investigated the convergence of partial Fourier sums in Lebesgue spaces, see also
[
9
,
17
,
34
]
)
that
(\sin(j\pi\cdot))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is not only an orthogonal basis of
L_{2}(0,1)
but also a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)
for any
q\in(1,\infty)
. In the sequel we abbreviate

e_{j}(t):=\sqrt{2}\sin(j\pi t),\quad j\in\mathbb{N},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.2)

We now introduce the piece-wise linear analogue of (
2.2
).
First, we observe that the hat function

S(t)=\begin{cases}2t&\text{for }0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
2(1-t)&\text{for }\frac{1}{2}<t\leq 1,\\
\end{cases}

is a piece-wise linear approximation of
\sin(\pi t)
and that
S(t)
and
\sin(\pi t)
coincide for
t\in\{0,1/2,1\}
.
Then,
S
is extended by the anti-symmetric reflection rule to
[0,2]
and, finally, periodically to
\mathbb{R}
with period 2, i.e.,

S(t)=(-1)^{\lfloor t\rfloor}\,S(\{t\}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
t=\lfloor t\rfloor+\{t\}
is the decomposition of
t
into its integer and fractional part.

Figure 2
:
Hat function
S(t)
and scaled version
S_{2}(t)

With this extension we define

S_{j}(t)=S(jt),\quad j\in\mathbb{N},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.3)

Intuitively, the system
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a piece-wise linear analogue of
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Indeed,
S_{j}(t)
interpolates
e_{j}(t)
at points
t\in\{\frac{0}{2^{j}},\frac{1}{2^{j}},\dots,\frac{2^{j}}{2^{j}}\}.

The main aim of this section is to prove Theorem
2.6
, i.e., to show that the system
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Schauder basis in
L_{q}(0,1)
for every
1<q<\infty
.
The proof is based on the following observation (and we refer to
[
19
, p. 75]
for details).

Let us assume that
(\varphi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are two sequences in a Banach space
X
. Further, we assume that there is a bounded and invertible linear operator

T
mapping
X
onto
X
such that
T(\varphi_{j})=\psi_{j}
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}.

Then it holds that if
(\varphi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Schauder basis of
X
then the same is true also for
(\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.

The main idea behind this observation is rather straightforward: for every
f\in X
, the expansion of
T^{-1}f

T^{-1}f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}

is transformed by
T
into

f=T\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\,T\varphi_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\,\psi_{j}.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that compared to the Riesz basis
\mathcal{R}_{1}
from (
1.1
) we now study one scale of functions
\{S_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
only. In particular, the scales partly coincide, i.e., we have
S_{2}=\mathcal{S}
which implies
S_{2m}(\cdot)=S_{2}(m\cdot)=\mathcal{S}(m\cdot)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(\cdot)
.

The link between the two sequences is given by the expansion of
S(t)=S_{1}(t)
which is

S(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{S}(k)e_{k}(t)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{S}(k)\sin(k\pi t),

(2.4)

where

\tau_{k}:=\widehat{S}(k)=\int_{0}^{1}S(t)e_{k}(t)dt=\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{1}S(t)\sin(k\pi t)dt.

(2.5)

The behavior of the Fourier coefficients
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is crucial for our later studies. Due to the simple form of the function
S(t)
, they can be easily calculated.

Proposition 2.4
.

For the sequence
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
it holds

\tau_{2k-1}=(-1)^{k-1}\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{2k}=0\quad\text{for}\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Using integration by parts we compute

\displaystyle\tau_{2k-1}=\int_{0}^{1}S(t)e_{2k-1}(t)\,dt=2\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{1/2}2t\sin((2k-1)\pi t)\,dt=(-1)^{k-1}\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{(2k-1)^{2}\pi^{2}},

which gives the desired result. Moreover, we obtain
\tau_{2k}=0
by the symmetry of
S
around the point
t=1/2
.
∎

Let us now consider a general function
f
on
[0,1]
. First, we extend it to
(-1,1]
by the anti-symmetric reflection rule, i.e., we put

f(t):=-f(-t),\quad-1<t<0.

Finally, we extend it to
\mathbb{R}
(still denoting it by
f
) periodically with period 2.
Alternatively, if
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
t=\lfloor t\rfloor+\{t\}
is the decomposition of
t
into its integer and fractional part, the extension of
f
is given by

f(t):=(-1)^{\lfloor t\rfloor}f(1-|1-2\{t/2\}|),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We now construct a linear homeomorphism
T
of
L_{q}(0,1)
onto itself that maps each
e_{j}
to
S_{j}
.
The definition is based on (
2.4
) and (
2.5
). Essentially, we need that
T

maps
e_{1}
to
S_{1}
and that
T
commutes with integer dilations.
Once this is done it will follow from the general considerations sketched above that
the family
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a basis of
L_{q}(0,1).

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
q\in(1,\infty)
and define the map
T
by

Tg(t)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\tau_{m}g(mt),\qquad t\in(0,1).

(2.6)

Then
T
has the following properties:

(i)

The map
T:L_{q}(0,1)\rightarrow

L_{q}(0,1)
is bounded and linear with
\left\|T\right\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

(ii)

Te_{j}=S_{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)

T
has a bounded inverse.

Proof.

(i)

Linearity is clear.
For every
g\in L_{q}(0,1)
it holds

\displaystyle\|g(m\cdot)\|_{q}^{q}=\int_{0}^{1}\left|g(mt)\right|^{q}dt

\displaystyle=m^{-1}\int_{0}^{m}\left|{g}(s)\right|^{q}ds=m^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\int_{k-1}^{k}\left|{g}(s)\right|^{q}ds=\int_{0}^{1}\left|g(s)\right|^{q}ds=\|g\|_{q}^{q},

(2.7)

and by the triangle inequality and Proposition
2.4
, we obtain

\left\|T\right\|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\tau_{2k-1}|=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{(2k-1)^{2}\pi^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

(ii)

Next we show that
Te_{j}=S_{j}
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
. For that purpose, we calculate

\displaystyle S_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{S_{j}}(k)e_{k}=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e_{k}\int_{0}^{1}S_{j}(t)\sin(k\pi t)dt=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e_{k}\int_{0}^{1}S(jt)\sin(k\pi t)dt

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\widehat{S}(m)\sin(jm\pi t)\sin(k\pi t)dt=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e_{k}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\widehat{S}(m)\int_{0}^{1}\sin(jm\pi t)\sin(k\pi t)dt

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\widehat{S}(m)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\sin(jm\pi t)\sin(k\pi t)dt=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\widehat{S}(m)e_{jm}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\tau_{m}e_{jm}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\tau_{m}e_{j}({m\,\cdot})=Te_{j}.

(2.8)

(iii)

By the definition of
T
and (
2.7
) we observe that

\left\|T-\tau_{1}\text{Id}\right\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\tau_{2j+1}\right|,

and so the invertibility of
T
will follow from Theorem II.1.2 of
[
33
]
(Neumann Series)
if we can show that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\tau_{2j+1}\right|<\left|\tau_{1}\right|.

(2.9)

From Proposition
2.4
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\tau_{2j+1}\right|\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}-1\right)<\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}=\tau_{1},

(2.10)

from which (
2.9
) follows.

∎

We conclude with the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
q\in(1,\infty).
Then the family
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)
.

Proof.

Since the sequence
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a basis of
L_{q}(0,1)
and
T
is a linear
homeomorphism of
L_{q}(0,1)
onto itself with
Te_{j}=S_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows from
[
19
, p. 75]
, or
[
29
, Theorem 3.1, p. 20]
,
that the sequence
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
forms also a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1).

∎

With only a minor modification of the argument, we can apply the same procedure also to the Hilbert space setting and
Riesz bases. Recall, that it was shown already in
[
5
]
(with an alternative proof provided in
[
27
]
) that
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}

is a Riesz basis with Riesz constants
A=1/2
and
B=3/2
.
It is quite interesting that a straightforward modification of the proof of Theorem
2.6
(see
[
16
, Section VI.2.]
for details)
provides the same conclusion with an improved lower Riesz constant
A
.

Theorem 2.7
.

The sequence
(\sqrt{3}\,S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Riesz basis of
L_{2}(0,1)

with Riesz constants

A:=\left\{\frac{4\sqrt{6}}{\pi^{2}}\left(2-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\right)\right\}^{2}=0.5787\dots\quad\text{and}\quad B:=\frac{3}{2}.

Proof.

The proof is based on the following observation. Let
(\varphi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of a (separable) Hilbert space
H
and let
(\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of elements of
H
.
Furthermore, let us assume that there is a bounded and invertible linear mapping
R
of
H
onto itself, such that
R\varphi_{j}=\psi_{j}
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}.

Then

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\psi_{j}\right\|^{2}=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}R(\varphi_{j})\right\|^{2}=\left\|R\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}\right)\right\|^{2}\leq\|R\|^{2}\cdot\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}\right\|^{2}=\|R\|^{2}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}^{2}

and, similarly,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\psi_{j}\right\|^{2}=\left\|R\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}\right)\right\|^{2}\geq 1/\|R^{-1}\|^{2}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}^{2}.

Hence,
(\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Riesz basis and its Riesz constants can be chosen as
A=1/\|R^{-1}\|^{2}

and
B=\|R\|^{2}.

To apply this approach to our setting, we define
R:=\sqrt{3}\,T
where
T
was defined in (
2.6
). With this notation,
R(e_{j})=\sqrt{3}\,S_{j}

and
(\sqrt{3}\,S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is correctly normalized. Then we obtain, that
(\sqrt{3}\,S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Riesz basis of
L_{2}(0,1)

with Riesz constants
B:=\|R\|^{2}=3\,\|T\|^{2}\leq 3/2
and
A:=1/\|R^{-1}\|^{2}.
To bound
A
, we rewrite
R
as
R=\sqrt{3}\tau_{1}\mathrm{Id}-M
,
where

Mg(t)=-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{3}\tau_{2m+1}g(m\cdot t).

Therefore,

\|M\|\leq\sqrt{3}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|\tau_{2m+1}|=\frac{4\sqrt{6}}{\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}-1\right).

Finally, we denote
a:=\sqrt{3}\tau_{1}
and the Neumann series technique (applicable since
\|M\|/a\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}-1<1
) gives

\displaystyle a\|R^{-1}\|=\left\|(R/a)^{-1}\right\|=\left\|(\mathrm{Id}-M/a)^{-1}\right\|\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\|M\|^{m}}{a^{m}}=\frac{1}{1-\|M\|/a}.

This gives

A=1/\|R^{-1}\|^{2}\geq\big(a-\|M\|\big)^{2}\geq\left\{\frac{4\sqrt{6}}{\pi^{2}}\left(2-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\right)\right\}^{2}=0.5787\dots.

∎

3
Multivariate bases

The main aim of this section is to investigate the multivariate counterparts of the results presented in Section
2.3
.
We start with the tensor product analogue of the basis
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.

3.1
Tensor product basis in
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
generated by
S

Throughout this section we shall again assume that
1<q<\infty
.
Our aim is to prove (cf. Theorem
3.4
) that the tensor products of functions from the family
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
form a basis in the Lebesgue space

L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
, provided
n\leq 3
.

First, we introduce the notation connected to multivariate functions and indices, cf.
[
2
]
. Let

\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}),\quad\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}

be multi-indices, and let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We write

\mathbf{m}x=(m_{1}x_{1},\ldots,m_{n}x_{n}),\quad\mathbf{m}\mathbf{k}=(m_{1}k_{1},\ldots,m_{n}k_{n})

and denote

\mathbf{m}\leq\mathbf{k}\quad\text{if}\quad m_{i}\leq k_{i}\quad\text{for each}\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

Moreover, we set

\mathbf{1}:=(1,\ldots,1).

For a given function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we put

\varphi_{\mathbf{m}}(x):=\varphi(m_{1}x_{1})\cdots\varphi(m_{n}x_{n}).

(3.1)

Below we choose
\varphi(t)=S(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and study the properties of the system
(S_{\mathbf{m}})_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
. Note that later on we replace the tensor products by inner products and consider also systems

\varphi(\mathbf{m}\cdot x)=\varphi(m_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+m_{n}x_{n}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3.2)

i.e.,
\mathbf{m}\cdot x
is the scalar product, since these functions can be reproduced by NNs in an efficient way where we do not suffer the ’curse of dimension’.

Remark 3.1
.

Recall that the system
\{\varphi_{\mathbf{m}}\}_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
is a Schauder basis in
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
,
q\in(1,\infty)
, if,
and only if, for any
f\in L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
, there exists a unique sequence
\{\alpha_{\mathbf{m}}\}_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
of scalars such that

f=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{m}}\varphi_{\mathbf{m}}\quad\text{in}\quad L_{q}(0,1)^{n}.

(3.3)

The convergence in (
3.3
) is interpreted as

\lim_{\min\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\}\to\infty}\left\|f-\sum_{\mathbf{k}\leq\mathbf{m}}\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\right\|_{L_{q}(0,1)^{n}}=0,

which is sometimes referred to as the convergence in the
Pringsheim sense
.

Let us recall, cf. (
2.2
), that for
e(t)=\sqrt{2}\sin(\pi t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we denoted

e_{j}(t)=e(jt)=\sqrt{2}\sin(j\pi t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

We use that the system
(e_{j}(t))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis in
L_{2}(0,1)
and consider the corresponding tensor product orthonormal basis

(e_{\mathbf{m}})_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
in
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
, which is defined as

e_{\mathbf{m}}(x)=e_{m_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots e_{m_{n}}(x_{n})=2^{n/2}\sin(\pi m_{1}x_{1})\cdots\sin(\pi m_{n}x_{n}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

(3.4)

It is very well known that the sequence
(e_{\mathbf{m}})_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}

is orthonormal in
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
. Indeed, the orthonormality is straightforward and
the completeness of this system in
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
follows from
[
24
, Sect. II.4, Prop. 2 and p. 50]
. In what follows, using classical results from the analysis of multivariate Fourier series, we show that

(e_{\mathbf{m}})_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
is a Schauder basis in the Lebesgue space
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f\in L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
, where
n\geq 1
and
q\in(1,\infty)
, and set

\hat{f}(\mathbf{k}):=\int_{(0,1)^{n}}f(x)e_{\mathbf{k}}(x)\,dx,\quad\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

(3.5)

Then

f=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\hat{f}(\mathbf{m})e_{\mathbf{m}}

(3.6)

in the sense of (
3.3
).

Proof.

Extend
f\in L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
in each of its variables to
(-1,1)
by
oddness
(i.e., reflect
f
across each coordinate axis with a sign flip). Thus, define
f:(-1,1)^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits(x_{1})\dots\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits(x_{n})f(|x_{1}|,\ldots,|x_{n}|),\qquad x\in(-1,1)^{n}.

(3.7)

Using the result of Weisz
[
31
, Thm. 4.1]
and the oddness of
f
in all its variables we obtain

f=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}c_{f}(\mathbf{m})e_{\mathbf{m}}\quad\text{in}\quad L_{q}(-1,1)^{n},

where

c_{f}(\mathbf{k})=\int_{(-1,1)^{n}}f(x)e_{\mathbf{k}}(x)\,dx,\quad\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

Consequently, we obtain (
3.6
) with

\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})=2^{-n}c_{f}(\mathbf{k}),\quad\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.\qed

Now put
\varphi(t):=S(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and according to (
3.1
) consider for
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
the functions

S_{\mathbf{m}}(x)=S(m_{1}x_{1})\cdots S(m_{n}x_{n}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Following the ideas from
[
2
]
we proceed similarly to the one-dimensional case: Again we construct a linear
homeomorphism
T
of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
onto itself, decomposing into a linear combination of certain isometries, that maps
e_{\mathbf{m}}
to

S_{\mathbf{m}}
for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. From this we deduce that

(S_{\mathbf{m}})_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
forms a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}.

To define the homeomorphism
T
, we need the following notation.
Given any function
f
on
[0,1)^{n}
, we extend it to
(-1,1)^{n}
as in (
3.7
).
In the next step,
f
is extended to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
periodically, with period 2 in each variable, see Fig.
3
. With a slight abuse of notation,
this extension is denoted by
f
again.

Figure 3
:
Illustration of the signs of
f
in case
n=2

Furthermore, the definition of
T
features the Fourier coefficients of
S_{\mathbf{1}}(x)=S(x_{1})\cdots S(x_{n})
as described in (
3.6
)
and which are denoted and calculated as follows

\tau_{\mathbf{m}}:=\widehat{S_{\mathbf{1}}}(\mathbf{m})=\int_{(0,1)^{n}}S_{1}(x)e_{\mathbf{m}}(x)dx=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}S(x_{i})e_{m_{i}}(x_{i})dx_{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{m_{i}}.

(3.8)

Here,
\tau_{m_{i}}
was defined in (
2.5
) as the
m_{i}
-th Fourier coefficient of
S
.
Note that
\widehat{S_{\mathbf{1}}}(\mathbf{m})=0
for every
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})

with some even
m_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
q\in(1,\infty)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and define the map
T
by

Tg(x):=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\tau_{\mathbf{m}}g(\mathbf{m}x),\qquad x\in(0,1)^{n}

(3.9)

(convergence is considered in the sense of (
3.3
)).
Then
T
has the following properties:

(i)

The map
T:L_{q}(0,1)^{n}\rightarrow

L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
is bounded and linear with
\left\|T\right\|\leq(\sqrt{2})^{-n}.

(ii)

Te_{\mathbf{m}}=S_{\mathbf{m}}
for
all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.

(iii)

T
has a bounded inverse if the coefficients
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}
satisfy

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\,\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{1}}\left|\tau_{2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1}}\right|<|\tau_{\mathbf{1}}|.

(3.10)

Proof.

(i)

Linearity is clear. Moreover,
for
g\in L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
we see that

\displaystyle\|g(\mathbf{m}\cdot)\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,1)^{n}}\left|g(\mathbf{m}x)\right|^{q}dx=\int_{(0,1)^{n}}\left|g(m_{1}x_{1},\ldots,m_{n}x_{n})\right|^{q}dx

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}^{-1}\right)\int_{[0,m_{1})\times\ldots[0,m_{n})}\left|{g}(s)\right|^{q}ds=\int_{(0,1)^{n}}\left|g(s)\right|^{q}ds=\|g\|_{q}^{q}.

(3.11)

Thus, from (
3.8
) and Proposition
2.4
we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|T\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}|\tau_{2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1}}|=\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\frac{1}{(2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)^{n}\left(\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{k_{n}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k_{1}-1)^{2}\cdots(2k_{n}-1)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{2}}\right)^{n}=\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\right)^{n}=(\sqrt{2})^{-n}.

(ii)

To show that
Te_{\mathbf{m}}=S_{\mathbf{m}}
for every
{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we argue similarly as in (
2.8
).
We employ Proposition
3.2
and calculate

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{S_{\mathbf{m}}}(\mathbf{k})e_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}e_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{(0,1)^{n}}S_{\mathbf{m}}(x)e_{\mathbf{k}}(x)dx=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}e_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{(0,1)^{n}}S_{\mathbf{1}}(\mathbf{m}x)e_{\mathbf{k}}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}e_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{(0,1)^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{S_{\mathbf{1}}}(\mathbf{l})e_{\mathbf{l}}(\mathbf{m}x)e_{\mathbf{k}}(x)dx=\sum_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{S_{\mathbf{1}}}(\mathbf{l})\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}e_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{(0,1)^{n}}e_{\mathbf{l}\mathbf{m}}(x)e_{\mathbf{k}}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{S_{\mathbf{1}}}(\mathbf{l})e_{\mathbf{l}\mathbf{m}}=\sum_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\tau_{\mathbf{l}}e_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{l}\cdot)=Te_{\mathbf{m}}.

(iii)

By (
3.8
),
\tau_{\mathbf{1}}>0
.
To show the invertibility of
T
, we apply again the theory of Neumann series, cf.
[
33
, Theorem II.1.2]
.
For that sake, we rewrite
T
as
T=\tau_{\mathbf{1}}(\text{Id}-M)
, where

Mg(x)=-\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathbf{1}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\,\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{1}}\tau_{2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1}}g((2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1})x),\qquad x\in(0,1)^{n}.

If condition (
3.10
) holds, we obtain

\|M\|\leq\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathbf{1}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\,\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{1}}\left|\tau_{2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1}}\right|<1

and
T
has a bounded inverse. ∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
n\in\{1,2,3\}
. Then the sequence
\left\{S_{\mathbf{k}}(\cdot)\right\}_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
is a Schauder basis in
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
for
any
q\in(1,\infty)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
(iii), the operator
T
is a
homeomorphism and the assertion follows if we can show that

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\,\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{1}}\left|\tau_{2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1}}\right|<|\tau_{\mathbf{1}}|.

From (
3.8
), Proposition
2.4
, and the observation

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\,\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{1}}\frac{1}{(2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1})^{2}}=-1+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{2}}\right)^{n}=\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\right)^{n}-1,

we obtain that

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\,\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{1}}|\tau_{2\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{1}}|=\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)^{n}\left(\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\right)^{n}-1\right).

This quantity is smaller than

\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)^{n}=\tau_{\mathbf{1}}

for
n=1,2,3
.
∎

3.2
Tensor products of Schauder bases

It is a very natural question if the restriction to
n\in\{1,2,3\}
in Theorem
3.4

is necessary or if it is only an artifact of the proof method. Furthermore,
as the tensor product of two orthonormal bases of two Hilbert spaces is an orthonormal basis
of the tensor product of these Hilbert spaces (cf.
[
24
, Sect. II.4]
), it is natural to hope that a similar statement
should be true for Schauder bases and tensor products of Banach spaces.

The aim of this section is to shed light on these questions. We refer to
[
4
,
6
]
or
[
23
]

for an extensive treatment of the theory of tensor products of Banach spaces.
If
X
and
Y
are two Banach spaces, then
X\otimes Y
is their algebraic tensor product, i.e., the set of all finite sums
of the form
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}\otimes y_{j}
, where
x_{j}\in X
and
y_{j}\in Y
for all
j=1,\dots,m.

The space
X\otimes Y
may be equipped with different norms, which (after possible completion) can give rise
to different tensor product Banach spaces.

If the sequence
(\varphi_{j})_{j}
is a Schauder basis of
X
and
(\eta_{l})_{l}
is a Schauder basis of
Y
,
we would like to know if
(\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l})_{j,l}
is a Schauder basis for the tensor product space of
X
and
Y
.
According to
[
14
,
15
]
or
[
23
, Prop. 4.25]
for general
X
and
Y
it turns out that
(\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l})_{j,l}
(with the square ordering) is a Schauder basis
for
X{\hat{\otimes}}_{\pi}Y
and
X{\hat{\otimes}}_{\varepsilon}Y
, where the tensor product spaces are equipped with the projective and injective norms, respectively.
In view of the Lebesgue spaces
L_{q}(0,1)
this is not satisfactory, since the projective and injective tensor products of
L_{q}(0,1)
with itself
do not coincide with the standard product space
L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
for
q\in(1,\infty)
,
q\neq 2
.

On the other hand, it is known (cf.
[
4
, Cor. 1.52]
) that

L_{q}(0,1)\ {\otimes}_{\alpha_{q}}L_{q}(0,1)=L_{q}(0,1)^{2},

(3.12)

where the tensor product space is equipped with the
q
-nuclear norm
\alpha_{q}
, see
[
4
, Def. 1.45]
or
[
23
, Section 6.2]
, where
this norm is denoted
g_{p}
and called Chevet-Saphar tensor norm.

By
[
23
, Prop. 6.6]
, the
q
-nuclear norm is a
uniform crossnorm
(or reasonable crossnorm in notation of
[
4
]
, see
[
4
, Lem. 1.46]
), which means that for
every bounded linear operator
S:X\to X
, every bounded linear operator
T:Y\to Y

and every pair of
n
-tuples
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}\subset X
and
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}\subset Y
the following inequality holds

\alpha_{q}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Sf_{i})\otimes(Tg_{i})\right)\leq\|S\|\cdot\|T\|\cdot\alpha_{q}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}\otimes g_{i}\right).

This allows to define the tensor product of
S
and
T
on
X\otimes Y
as

(S\otimes T)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}\otimes g_{i}\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Sf_{i})\otimes(Tg_{i})

and to extend it by continuity to all
X\otimes_{\alpha_{q}}Y.
Furthermore,
\|S\otimes T\|\leq\|S\|\cdot\|T\|
.

As the next piece of the puzzle, it is known (cf.
[
14
,
15
]
or
[
29
, Theorem 18.1]
), that if the tensor product space is generated by a uniform crossnorm,
then the tensor product of two Schauder bases is a Schauder basis of the tensor product space (if we keep the square-ordering).

Therefore, the following proposition is available in the literature on tensor product spaces if one combines several notions and sources.
For the sake of completeness, we summarize these findings and show directly that the tensor product
of two Schauder bases of
L_{q}(0,1)
is a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
and let
(\varphi_{j})_{j\geq 1}
and
(\eta_{l})_{l\geq 1}
be Schauder bases of
L_{q}(0,1)
.
Enumerate the elementary tensors
\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l}
by
the square ordering and write
(u_{k})_{k\geq 1}
for the resulting sequence. Then
(u_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
X=L_{q}(0,1)
. By the Schauder property, every
x\in X
can be decomposed (in a unique way) as

x=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\varphi_{j},

which allows us to define the
partial-sum projection

P_{N}

as

P_{N}x:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\,\varphi_{j}.

Similarly, we define
Q_{N}
associated to the Schauder basis
(\eta_{l})_{l\geq 1}
.
The partial-sum projections are known to be uniformly bounded, i.e., they satisfy
\sup_{N}\|P_{N}\|=:C_{1}<\infty
and
\sup_{N}\|Q_{N}\|=:C_{2}<\infty
.
This can be seen as follows: For each
x\in X
the sequence
P_{N}x
converges to
x
in norm,
hence
\sup_{N}\|P_{N}x\|_{q}\leq\sup_{N}\|P_{N}x-x\|_{q}+\|x\|_{q}<\infty
for every
x\in X
. By the Uniform Boundedness Principle, this implies

\sup_{N}\|P_{N}\|<\infty
. A similar argument holds for
Q_{N}
.

Identify the algebraic tensor product
X\otimes X
with the linear span of product functions in
L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
via
g\otimes h\mapsto((s,t)\mapsto g(s)h(t))
.
Using the fact, see
[
4
, Lem. 10.15]
, that simple (finite) linear combinations of product functions are dense in

L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
, we obtain that

\overline{L_{q}(0,1)\otimes L_{q}(0,1)}^{\|\cdot\|_{L_{q}(0,1)^{2}}}\;=\;L_{q}(0,1)^{2},\qquad q\in[1,\infty),

(3.13)

where the closure is taken with respect to the norm in
L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
.

For each
N\geq 1
define the finite-rank operator
S_{N}=P_{N}\otimes Q_{N}
on
X\otimes X
by

(P_{N}\otimes Q_{N})\bigg(\sum_{k}g_{k}\otimes h_{k}\bigg):=\sum_{k}(P_{N}g_{k})\otimes(Q_{N}h_{k}).

These operators extend by continuity to bounded operators on
L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
and satisfy

\|S_{N}\|=\|P_{N}\otimes Q_{N}\|\leq\|P_{N}\|\|Q_{N}\|\leq C_{1}C_{2}=:C.

Observe, that
S_{N}(f)
lies in
{\rm span}\{\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l}:1\leq j,l\leq N\}
for every
f\in X\otimes X
and,
by the continuity of the extension of
S_{N}
, also for every
f\in L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
.
Therefore,
S_{N+1}(f)
“extends”
S_{N}(f)
and its coefficients for
\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l}
with
1\leq j,l\leq N
are the same.
If
f=g\otimes h
we can write

\displaystyle S_{N}(f)-f

\displaystyle=(P_{N}\otimes Q_{N})(g\otimes h)-g\otimes h=(P_{N}g)\otimes(Q_{N}h)-g\otimes h

\displaystyle=-(g-P_{N}g)\otimes(h-Q_{N}h)-(P_{N}g)\otimes(h-Q_{N}h)-(g-P_{N}g)\otimes Q_{N}h

and all three terms converge to zero (using that
P_{N}g\to g
,
Q_{N}h\to h
and by the uniform boundedness of
\|P_{N}\|
and
\|Q_{N}\|
).
Therefore,
\lim_{N\to\infty}S_{N}(f)=f
. By linearity, the same is true for all
f\in X\otimes X

and by density,
\lim_{N\to\infty}S_{N}(F)=F
follows for all
F\in L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
.
Indeed, for
F\in L_{q}(0,1)^{2}
, find
f\in X\otimes X
with
\|F-f\|_{q}<\varepsilon.
Then

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\|S_{N}(F)-F\|_{q}\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\|S_{N}(F)-S_{N}(f)\|_{q}+\limsup_{N\to\infty}\|S_{N}(f)-f\|_{q}+\limsup_{N\to\infty}\|f-F\|_{q}\leq(C+1)\varepsilon.

Moreover, uniqueness follows in the following way: Assume that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j,l\leq N}a_{j,l}\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l}=0.

(3.14)

Then we get for every
M,N\geq 1

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j,l\leq N}a_{j,l}\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l}\right\|_{q}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{j,l\leq N}a_{j,l}(P_{N}\varphi_{j})\otimes(Q_{N}\eta_{l})\right\|_{q}=\left\|S_{N}\left(\sum_{j,l\leq M+N}a_{j,l}\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l}\right)\right\|_{q}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\sum_{j,l\leq N+M}a_{j,l}\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l}\right\|_{q}.

Taking the limit
M\to\infty
, we obtain that

\left\|\sum_{j,l\leq N}a_{j,l}\varphi_{j}\otimes\eta_{l}\right\|_{q}=0,

i.e., that
a_{j,l}=0
for all
1\leq j,l\leq N
. As this holds for every
N\geq 1
, we observe that (
3.14
)
implies that
a_{j,l}=0
for all
j,l\geq 1.

∎

Corollary 3.6
.

The sequence
\left\{S_{\mathbf{k}}(\cdot)\right\}_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
(with the square-ordering) is a Schauder basis in
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
for
any
q\in(1,\infty)
and any
n\geq 1
.

3.3
Bases with the inner product structure

The concept of tensor products is surely important and in some sense standard if we want to transfer results from univariate functions to functions of many variables.
It was, however, one of the main achievements of
[
27
]
to show that replacing tensor products by inner products can bring essential advantages.
Recall, that it was shown there, that the system
{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
, defined in (
1.4
) as

{\mathcal{R}}_{n}:=\{1\}\cup\{\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}},\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{k}}:\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbf{k}+\!\!\!>0\}

(3.15)

is a Riesz basis of
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
with constants
A=1/2
and
B=3/2
. The aim of this section is to study the behavior of (
3.15
)
outside of the scale of Hilbert spaces.

We use the same approach as we used in the proof of Theorem
2.6
.
First, we consider the trigonometric analogue of (
3.15
) and show in Theorem
3.7
, that it constitutes an orthonormal basis in
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}.

Then we derive, that it constitutes also a Schauder basis of every
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
,
1<q<\infty.

Afterwards we construct a bounded and invertible linear mapping
T
of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
onto itself, which maps this trigonometric basis onto (
3.15
),
similarly to Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
3.3
.

Theorem 3.7
.

The system

\{1\}\cup\{\sqrt{2}\,\cos(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x):\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbf{k}+\!\!\!>0\}\cup\{\sqrt{2}\,\sin(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x):\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbf{k}+\!\!\!>0\}

(3.16)

is an orthonormal basis of the real Hilbert space
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
.

Proof.

We use that
\{e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}:k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis of the complex Hilbert space
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
.
First, let us consider any non-zero
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Taking the real and imaginary part of
\langle e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x},1\rangle=0
,
we obtain that the constant function is orthogonal to the remaining elements of (
3.16
).
Applying the same idea to the equation
\langle e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x},e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}\rangle=1
,
we get that
\cos(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x)
and
\sin(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x)
are orthogonal for every
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

and, by symmetry, that
\sqrt{2}
is the correct normalization factor in (
3.16
).
Similarly, for non-zero

\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
with
\mathbf{k}\not=\mathbf{l}
,
we separate the real and imaginary part of
\langle e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x},e^{2\pi i\mathbf{l}\cdot x}\rangle=\langle e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x},e^{-2\pi i\mathbf{l}\cdot x}\rangle=0
.
We obtain a system of four (real) equations, which shows that all the terms in (
3.16
) are orthogonal to each other.

To show that (
3.16
) is also a basis, we take a real function
f\in L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
and consider its unique decomposition

f=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}.

(3.17)

Then the conjugate of
f
has the Fourier series

\bar{f}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}e^{-2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}.

(3.18)

As
f=\bar{f}
, we get
\alpha_{-\mathbf{k}}=\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}
by comparing (
3.17
) and (
3.18
). That allows us to write

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}+\!\!>0}\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}+\!\!>0}\alpha_{\mathbf{-k}}e^{-2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}+\!\!>0}\cos(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x)\cdot[\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}+\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}]+\sin(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x)[i\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}+\overline{i\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}}],

which is a decomposition of
f
into (
3.16
) with real coefficients.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
1<q<\infty
. Then the system (
3.16
) is a Schauder basis of the Lebesgue space
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the properties of multivariate trigonometric series
[
31
, Theorem 4.1]
.
Indeed, this result shows that if
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and

(S^{\mathbf{k}}f)(x)=\sum_{|\ell_{1}|\leq k_{1}}\dots\sum_{|\ell_{n}|\leq k_{n}}\hat{f}(\bm{\ell})e^{i\bm{\ell}\cdot x}

(3.19)

denotes the rectangular partial sum of the Fourier series of
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
,
then

\lim_{\mathbf{k}\to\infty}S^{\mathbf{k}}f=f

in the
L_{q}
-norm for every
1<q<\infty
. The convergence
\mathbf{k}\to\infty
means that
\min(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\to\infty
,
i.e., the convergence in the Pringsheim sense. By splitting the complex exponential, we may rewrite (
3.19
) as

\displaystyle(S^{\mathbf{k}}f)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\ell_{1}|\leq k_{1}}\dots\sum_{|\ell_{n}|\leq k_{n}}\hat{f}(\bm{\ell})e^{i\bm{\ell}\cdot x}

\displaystyle=\hat{f}(0)+\mkern-30.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\ell}+\!\!>0\\
|\ell_{1}|\leq k_{1},\dots,|\ell_{n}|\leq k_{n}\end{subarray}}\mkern-30.0mu[\alpha_{\bm{\ell}}\cos(\bm{\ell}\cdot x)+\beta_{\bm{\ell}}\sin(\bm{\ell}\cdot x)],

where
\alpha_{\bm{\ell}}=\hat{f}(\bm{\ell})+\hat{f}(-\bm{\ell})
and
\beta_{\bm{\ell}}=i[\hat{f}({\bm{\ell}})-\hat{f}(-\bm{\ell})].

∎

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
q\in(1,\infty).
Then the system
{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
is a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
.

Proof.

We apply again the argument of
[
19
, p. 75]
, sketched and applied already in Section
2.3
.
Let us denote
c_{j}(t):=\cos(2\pi jt)
and
s_{j}(t):=\sin(2\pi jt)
for
j\geq 1.

Furthermore, for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we put
c_{\mathbf{k}}(x):=\cos(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x)
and
s_{\mathbf{k}}(x):=\sin(2\pi\mathbf{k}\cdot x)
.
By Proposition
3.8
, the system
\{1\}\cup\{c_{\mathbf{k}},s_{\mathbf{k}}:\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbf{k}+\!\!\!>0\}

is the Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
.

We use the Fourier decomposition of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{C}=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2m+1)^{2}}c_{2m+1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{S}=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(2m+1)^{2}}s_{2m+1}.

(3.20)

For
f\in L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
defined on
[0,1)^{n}
, we denote by
f
also its 1-periodic extension onto
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We now define a bounded linear and invertible operator
T:L_{q}(0,1)^{n}\to L_{q}(0,1)^{n}
in such a way that it maps (
3.16
) onto (
3.15
).
For that sake, we put

T(f)(x)=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{f((4j+1)x)}{(4j+1)^{2}}+\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{f((4j+3)(\mathbf{1}-x))}{(4j+3)^{2}}.

(3.21)

With this definition, we obtain

T(1)=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}=1

and, using (
3.20
), also

\displaystyle T(c_{\mathbf{k}})(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{\mathbf{k}}((4j+1)x)}{(4j+1)^{2}}+\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{\mathbf{k}}((4j+3)(\mathbf{1}-x))}{(4j+3)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{4j+1}(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)}{(4j+1)^{2}}+\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{4j+3}(\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{1}-x))}{(4j+3)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{4j+1}(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)}{(4j+1)^{2}}+\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{4j+3}(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)}{(4j+3)^{2}}=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}}(x).

The same calculation with
s_{\mathbf{k}}
instead of
c_{\mathbf{k}}
and with
s_{4j+3}(\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{1}-x))=-s_{4j+3}(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)
gives

T(s_{\mathbf{k}})(x)=\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)=\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{k}}(x).

The boundedness and invertibility of
T
follows similarly as in Lemma
2.5
, using the technique of Neumann series.
Indeed, we observe that the first summand in (
3.21
) is equal to
\displaystyle\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}f(x)=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\,\text{Id}(f)(x)
.
We therefore rewrite (
3.21
) as
\displaystyle\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}T=\text{Id}-M
, where

\|M\|=\left\|\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}T-\text{Id}\right\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}-1<1.

(3.22)

We conclude, that
\text{Id}-M
is invertible and, therefore, also
T
is invertible.
Finally, the result follows using the fact that (
3.16
) is a Schauder basis of
L_{q}(0,1)^{n}.

∎

Again, applying the same method to
q=2
leads to an improvement of the lower Riesz constant of
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Then
{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
is a Riesz basis of
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}
with Riesz constants
A=0.5787...
and
B=3/2.

Proof.

The proof is based on the same idea as the proof of Theorem
2.7
. As the constant function is in both (
3.16
) and
{\mathcal{R}}_{n}

and is orthogonal to the rest of these two systems, we may leave out the constant function and only work on its complement in
L_{2}(0,1)^{n}.

On this complement, we define
R:=\sqrt{3}/\sqrt{2}\,\cdot T.
Then
B:=\|R\|^{2}\,\leq\,3/2
since

\|T\|\leq\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}=1.

To estimate the lower Riesz constant
A
, we rewrite again
R
as
R=a\,\mathrm{Id}-M
, where

a=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad-Mf(x)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{f((4j+1)x)}{(4j+1)^{2}}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{f((4j+3)(\mathbf{1}-x))}{(4j+3)^{2}}\Biggr).

Then

\displaystyle\|M\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\Bigl(\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}-1\Bigr)<\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}=a

and we obtain (exactly as in the proof of Theorem
2.7
)

A\geq(a-\|M\|)^{2}\geq\left\{\frac{4\sqrt{6}}{\pi^{2}}\left(2-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\right)\right\}^{2}=0.5787\dots.

∎
