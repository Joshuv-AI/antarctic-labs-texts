Confronting complexity: toward equitable healthcare technology design and evaluation for socioeconomically marginalized patients
Authors: Tiffany C Veinot, Alicia K Stone, Sage Davis, Rupa S Valdez, Lorraine R Buis, Vaishnav Kameswaran, Marcy G Antonio, Graciela Lagraba, Tawanna R Dillahunt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesInvestigate how patients at a Federally-Qualified Health Center (FQHC) perform with, and perceive, complex telehealth tasks. Determine which complexity dimensions most affect patients’ performance and perceptions, and compare this to researcher-evaluators’ complexity walkthrough results.Materials and MethodsA novel complexity walkthrough inspection method was implemented by researcher-evaluators (n = 8) to identify theory-informed complexity dimensions in 6 FQHC-required telehealth tasks. Remote user testing (n = 24) where FQHC patients were observed performing tasks, then completed newly-developed complexity-focused interviews and surveys. Descriptive statistics regarding complexity dimension presence, task performance, cognitive load, and perceived difficulty were integrated with qualitative data analyzed using inductive and deductive coding.ResultsPatients completed 33.9% of the required subtasks without issues. Cognitive load and perceived difficulty were high for 2 tasks. Complexity dimensions of ambiguity (unclear inputs/processes; new concepts/words) and relationship (context switching; deep navigational hierarchies) most affected patient-perceived difficulty. Patients spent twice as long as walkthrough evaluators on tasks, and encountered broader complexity dimensions: new concepts/words, and errors. Many patients ended tasks early, asserting that they would abandon them outside of a study or had previously done so.DiscussionTechnology-mediated task complexity may explain some telehealth uptake inequities. The complexity dimensions that challenge patients extend known usability heuristics by enhancing their equity sensitivity. Complexity walkthroughs surface design patterns that challenge patients, but complexity-focused user testing with patients reveals additional difficulties. Findings support complexity reduction of tasks via structuring, familiar concepts/words, feature integration, and shallow/broad navigation.ConclusionThis paper’s novel, theoretically-grounded complexity-focused methods and findings may inform future equitable design and evaluation of technology-mediated tasks for socioeconomically marginalized patients.Keywords: telehealth, healthcare equity, access to primary care, human factors, usability

Background and significance

Expanded access to technology-mediated healthcare is a policy goal in the United States (US). Federal Meaningful Use incentives1 prompted widespread healthcare provider adoption of patient portals (henceforth, “portals”). Furthermore, 25 US states passed legislation supporting long-term video visit use.2 Hence, US healthcare organizations have increasingly transitioned to telehealth using patient-facing technologies like portals and video visit platforms (henceforth, “telehealth technologies”). These transitions can result in differential access for socioeconomically marginalized patients—those with low incomes or educational attainment,3,4 lower-status occupations3,4 or unemployment,5 and/or public or no health insurance.6 Such patients often receive healthcare at safety net providers like Federally-Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs).

Differential uptake of telehealth technologies among socioeconomically marginalized people is consistently observed across the US,6–13 and in groups with high rates of socioeconomic marginalization: Black or Hispanic/Latino,14–16 non-English-speaking,6,8 disabled,6 and rural people.15 There is a similar inequity in the usage of telehealth technologies.17 Yet, little research explains socioeconomic differences. Explanation is critical because portals can improve preventive health care and treatment adherence11 and video visits are often more clinically effective than phone visits.18,19

Additionally, prior research has not sufficiently considered the design of telehealth technologies as a potential explanation of differential uptake and usage.11 Usability challenges with commercial portals and telehealth technologies have been documented11,20–22 for first-time users23 and patients with low literacy.22,24 However, this work has sparsely analyzed design patterns that contribute to usability challenges. Moreover, the general standards (“heuristics”) operationalized in commonly used usability inspection methods may fail to meet the needs of marginalized groups.25 Consequently, researchers lack a systematic understanding of what matters for designing patient-facing healthcare tasks using telehealth technologies (henceforth, “technology-mediated tasks”) to foster equitable adoption, usage, and healthcare access for socioeconomically marginalized patients. New, equity-focused heuristics and methods for evaluating patient-facing technology-mediated tasks are needed to assess current technology-mediated tasks and to inform future design. This also addresses field-wide calls for new methods of assessing patients’ healthcare technology use.26

Therefore, this study investigated the complexity of technology-mediated tasks as a potential design-related equity issue for telehealth access (Appendix A for definitions). Complexity is the focus for 3 theoretical reasons derived from learning theory, economics, and sociology. First, complex tasks produce higher cognitive load. Cognitive load is reduced when a person can draw from prior knowledge of completing similar tasks. Because technology experience may be constrained by differential educational experiences, occupations, and device/Internet access, socioeconomically marginalized people may experience higher cognitive load when encountering complex technology-mediated tasks. This can impede learning27 and task completion. Second, more complex tasks typically take more time. Because socioeconomically advantaged people spend more time on health-related tasks, economic literature shows that they typically benefit more when tasks are more complex.28 This may be linked to time scarcity and control studied in sociology: lower-income people typically have less control over their time than higher-income people.29 Yet, technologies that simplify healthcare tasks have equitable benefits across socioeconomic positions.28 Third, socioeconomically marginalized people are often under greater psychological stress than people who are more socioeconomically advantaged (see Appendix A for a definition). Due to stress, poverty in particular can tax cognitive and emotional resources30–32 and negatively affect executive cognitive functions such as working memory and attention.33,34 Notably, these functions are employed when interacting with digital systems (eg,32,35), and even more so when complex tasks increase the cognitive load required to use them.27 Nevertheless, there is a lack of empirical evidence regarding how socioeconomically marginalized patients may perform with, and experience, complex, technology-mediated tasks for telehealth.

While complexity is sometimes acknowledged, research has not simultaneously considered the multiple dimensions of complexity interrogated in multiple fields. For instance, in human-computer interaction (HCI), complexity is investigated as an obstacle to usability.36–38 HCI and health informatics research studies single complexity-related problems, recommending removal of unnecessary features, automating tasks, creating templates,39 and interface design consistency.40 Organizational studies research assesses the complexity of work tasks41–43 and its relationship to performance.41,42,44,45 Engineering research centers process complexity (ie, a series of tasks and subtasks) encountered when configuring software.46,47

To enrich the treatment of complexity in health informatics and investigate its posited equity implications, the simultaneous assessment of multiple complexity dimensions from these fields is needed. Therefore, we synthesized complexity dimensions and related measurements from these literatures43 by comparing definitions and mapping similar concepts and measures to create a “Task Complexity Dimensions Theoretical Framework” (Box 1). This theoretical framework incorporates both subjective and objective complexity dimensions and intentionally brings different disciplines into conversation. For instance, the likelihood of completing a task without error (mentioned in engineering46) is linked in the framework to task reliability from organizational behavior42 and measured by the occurrence of errors during task completion. The result is the set of integrated complexity-related concepts and measures shown in Box 1. Here, each complexity dimension is classified into a high-level category drawn from extant theoretical literature43: action complexity, task ambiguity, task relationship, task reliability, task variety, task size, and temporal demands. This framework permits identification and probing of complexity dimensions through our novel, sequential task-focused inspection and user testing methods: a “complexity walkthrough” and “complexity-focused user testing.”

Research aims and objectives

Investigate how patients at a FQHC perform with, and perceive, complex telehealth tasks. Determine which complexity dimensions most affect patients’ performance and perceptions, and compare this to researcher-evaluators’ (henceforth, “walkthrough evaluators”) complexity walkthrough results.

Methods

Research setting

The FQHC had 5 clinical sites in a Midwestern state, providing care for over 20 000 patients primarily insured through public insurance (Medicaid/Medicare) (57%). The FQHC had implemented a new electronic health record (EHR) and portal 5 months before the COVID-19 pandemic, with only 4.5% of patients having used the portal by March 2020. Video visits were unavailable via the portal vendor; hence, the FQHC adopted a standalone platform that did not require software installation in March 2020.

Data collection

This study involved 2 stages informed by the “Task Complexity Dimensions Theoretical Framework”: (1) complexity walkthrough evaluations completed by the research team (n = 8) followed by (2) complexity-focused user testing conducted remotely with randomly selected FQHC patients (n = 24) (Figure 1).

Study design.

Stage-1 involved 2 steps. First, with FQHC staff advice and following a task analysis approach,54 we identified 6 tasks and related subtasks required for a telehealth visit (Figure 2). Then, we extended existing technology evaluation inspection methods55 to create a novel “complexity walkthrough” approach guided by the theoretical framework (henceforth, “walkthrough”). In the walkthrough, research team members with no prior portal or telehealth platform experience served as walkthrough evaluators. They performed the 6 tasks while completing Qualtrics forms to capture complexity dimensions using the measures in Box 1.

Tasks for a telehealth visit.

Stage-2 involved complexity-focused user testing (henceforth, “tests”) conducted remotely in which adult, English-speaking, active FQHC patients (defined as patients who had a visit in the previous year) participated. Randomly-selected patients were contacted by telephone and then emailed invitations to participate. Of 101 patients reached, 24 participated (23.7% acceptance rate). Tests were conducted using FQHC-provided accounts and data (eg, upcoming appointments). Participants received mailed “technology kits” containing: (1) a printed participant guide and visual survey response scales; (2) an Internet hotspot; (3) a cell phone with Zoom and a mount; and (4) an Android tablet pre-loaded with screen-recording software and the portal and telehealth platform. Researchers provided remote help with setting up the provided technology, and gave participants time to familiarize themselves with the technology. The tests were conducted and recorded over Zoom, transcribed, and verified.

Participants were instructed to complete the 6 tasks (Figure 2) and were assisted or redirected by the researcher when done. Participants shared their actions on the screen and verbalized their thoughts.56 Researchers completed a structured form while observing participant performance (Appendix B). Following each task, researchers asked participants to answer 5 questions from the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX)57 plus one perceived difficulty38 using a visual paper scale given to participants. When participants’ responses were greater than 10 (midpoint), the researcher asked follow-up questions about which complexity dimensions, if any, made the task more difficult, and why. These task-based questions were selected based on the Stage-1 walkthrough results, establishing whether a given complexity dimension was present or not in the task. Finally, researchers asked semi-structured questions to gather perspectives on areas of difficulty (Appendix B).

Participants completed a demographic and technology experience survey. They were also asked to report their self-appraised levels of stress based on situations in their lives through the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10).58 The instrument asks how often participants had experienced feeling overloaded, or that events were unpredictable or uncontrollable in the past month (0 = Never, 1 = Almost Never, 2 = Sometimes, 3 = Fairly Often, and 4 = Very Often). The PSS-10 has high internal consistency and test-retest reliability,59 and is associated with depression and anxiety.59 It is also positively associated with measures of socioeconomic marginalization including lower income,30,60 educational attainment,30,32,60 unemployment,30 and neighborhood disadvantage.32

Data analysis

For Stage-1, counts, means, and proportions for evaluators’ assessment of complexity dimensions for each task were calculated. Given a lack of objective, validated complexity standards, they were then classified by distributions as no, low, moderate, and high complexity for each task/dimension pairing.

Stage-2 quantitative analysis involved compiling descriptive statistics of the structured observations, perceived difficulty, and NASA-TLX questions for each task. Two proportions were calculated. One in which the “number of all subtasks completed without issues” was divided by “total subtasks” and one in which “number of all subtasks completed without issues” was divided by “total subtasks attempted.” An “abandonment rate” was calculated using the “number of subtasks not attempted,” divided by the “total subtasks.” We compiled the total time for each task using screen recording timestamps and prepared graphs to compare the time taken by walkthrough evaluators and participants. Participants completed the five NASA-TLX items and one additional perceived-difficulty item. Each item was rated on a 21-point scale ranging from 1 to 21. Aligned with widespread practice,61–64 we averaged the NASA-TLX and related perceived difficulty scale items with equal weights. We then transformed scores to a 0–100 scale for comparability. Following prior work,65–68 we treated NASA-TLX scores as cognitive load measures.

Adhering to published guidance,58 we also created a measure of perceived stress that sums the total of responses to the PSS-10. Although there is no official cutoff for the PSS-10,60 this study adopted the ordinal categorization of perceived stress often found in studies using this measure: low (0–13), moderate (14–26), and high (27–40).69–71 Stage-2 qualitative analysis began with deductive structural coding of user test transcripts by task and complexity dimension (Figure 1). In a second inductive coding cycle using structural codes, themes were produced (eg, it should be “right there,” wasting time).72

Data from all stages were integrated to illustrate the relationship between complexity types and difficulty. First, the frequency with which complexity dimensions contributed to patients’ perceived task difficulty was categorized. Next, Stage-2 themes about patients’ experiences with each complexity dimension were compared to this issue-frequency categorization (Figure 1).

Ethical considerations

The study was exempted from oversight by the University of Michigan’s Institutional Review Board. To protect participant confidentiality, participants were assigned a pseudonymous code, and this pseudonymization process was used for all participant data. Data were also de-identified and are stored on a password-protected server and will be retained for a 7-year period as set out in the informed consent materials. Test accounts were also used to avoid video recording patients’ portal data through screen sharing. Participants were instructed to use the label “USER” rather than entering their own name when prompted to join the video call for the test. Patient study participants gave informed consent and received $75 for test participation.

To ensure accessibility, the research team members ran multiple pilot tests to refine the study materials shipped to patients. For literacy-related accommodations, patient-facing materials underwent plain language review using the Flesch-Kincaid readability tests to ensure that materials were at no more than an eighth-grade reading level. The materials also deliberately included photos to assist with set-up and participation. When starting the user test, researchers verbally walked individuals through the setup while on the phone with them. Researchers repeatedly emphasized that the research was testing the system and not the participant. To reduce participant burden, the researchers encouraged them to take breaks, and they were given the opportunity to split the user test across 2 sessions if desired (though none did so). Additionally, if participants were becoming frustrated with a task, the researcher offered them the ability to stop that task and move on.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Table 1 shows that most participants had multiple indicators of socioeconomic marginalization. In addition to receiving safety net health care at an FQHC, 16 participants (66.7%) had less than a bachelor’s degree. Seven participants (29.2%) had individual incomes below $12 000 per year. The poverty level in 2020 was $12 760 annually for a single-person household and $26 200 annually for a 4-person household.73 An additional 3 (12.5%) were at or below the 133% poverty level based on Medicaid eligibility. Three participants (12.5%) disclosed limited reading ability. User test participants felt moderately stressed on average across the PSS-10 questions (mean: 20.9; SD = 6.4; minimum = 9.0-maximum = 37.0; see Table 1 for distribution). Most used the Internet daily, but 66.7% (16/24) had low technology experience, primarily due to never having used the patient portal or video visit platform (Table 1).

Task complexity dimensions and patients’ performance and perceptions of technology-mediated telehealth tasks from user tests

Action complexity

Participants believed that a higher number of navigational interactions made the task more difficult frequently (Table 4). High numbers of navigational interactions often co-occurred with subtasks not attempted. Qualitatively, such results may be explained by participants’ difficulties with seeing everything on the screen. For instance, P30 said that the most difficult part of Task 2, completing the telehealth consent, was “I had to make sure I scrolled all the way down and looked at all the forms.” (Subtask 2.6, Figure 2).

Task ambiguity

Input/Process Clarity and Unstructured Guidance

Both walkthrough evaluators and participants experienced high rates of uncertainty about what to do across all tasks. For participants, this complexity dimension contributed to difficulty very frequently (Table 4). P8 said, “Not knowing what you’re doing is a problem…it makes it difficult.”

Clarity challenges were linked to the portal’s unclear labeling systems. Some participants could not align their task-related goals with the topical labels provided. For example, in Task 2, the consent form was located under the second-level label “Medical Forms” (Subtask 2.2, Figure 2). But as P21 complained, “I feel confused… it shouldn’t be in medical forms.” For 6 participants, another input/process clarity issue arose from the requirement to enter free text into the “subject” line and “message” boxes, neither of which included structured guidance. P14 said, “…I might have had a hard time explaining [why I want to see the doctor]…”

Lack of clarity concerning information to be input by participants was not only difficult but could also lead to errors. For example, lacking the telehealth visit option in Task 1 (Request telehealth visit), 6 participants chose a site name that included the word “Mobile” from a dropdown list. P17 explained, “I figured that would be the mobile, since it’s going to be a mobile phone call.” However, this option designated a health team serving unhoused patients, making it a system-facilitated error.

New concepts/words.

Participants encountered new concepts/words in Tasks 1, 2, 3, and 6, while walkthrough evaluators only found new concepts/words in Task 1. Specifically, almost two-thirds of participants identified new concepts/words that walkthrough evaluators knew. At the participant level, new concepts/words made tasks more difficult frequently (Table 4).

To illustrate, most patients had phoned the health center to schedule an appointment. However, the portal’s process conceptually divided “requesting” from “scheduling” when it had previously all occurred together on a call. Thus, P24 erroneously thought that, “Instead of when you on the phone, they be like, ‘Hold on. Let me see what we’ve got available.’ They tell you what [appointment times] they got available.” Alternatively, a unitary “visit” metaphor resonated with participants, and multiple participants wanted all subtasks associated with a visit combined into one task process.

Furthermore, because the portal organization was based on healthcare concepts and workflows that were unfamiliar, participants found it difficult to recognize appropriate actions. For example, during Task 6, P10 looked in the forms section, which was the right place to locate a laboratory requisition for a COVID-19 test, but was confused because nothing looked like a request form. Visiting a lab to obtain the COVID-19 test also confused some participants: P11 found herself browsing information about COVID-19 vaccine locations after following a link out of the portal. When participants could not recognize the right content, they resorted to time-consuming searches. P21 said, “I just clicking on a lot of different things.”

Task relationship

Context switches made tasks more difficult very frequently (Table 4). One explanation for this was the violation of expectations. For instance, for Task 5 (Complete telehealth visit), many participants expected to start the video visit from the portal, despite having received a visit reminder via text with an embedded link (Task 4). P17 explained, “Because you’re going through the patient portal, you’re thinking that it’s just going to be on there, you’re not really thinking that the text is going to be your check-in.”

Three to four hierarchy levels alongside insufficient navigation guidance contributed to difficulty frequently (Table 4). When asked what made a task difficult or confusing, participants often discussed hard-to-find features, nested within deep hierarchies. Qualitatively, participants preferred features to be immediately evident, and a recurrent phrase was having something “right there.” This meant the presence of prominent cues that made it possible to find features with little searching. For example, P18 said, “Right on that home screen it tells you you have a visit scheduled. There should…be a button that said, ‘Fill out a consent form.’” Similarly, this was difficult for P18 to find as it was on the fourth level of a hierarchy. P14 also suggested the following to make it easier to find the lab requisition that was also located at a fourth-level hierarchy, “…another tab saying follow-up…recent events…”

Task reliability

More than half of the participants encountered errors, a key form of task unreliability. Aside from Task 6 (Follow-up after visit), errors did not often emerge when the walkthrough evaluators performed tasks. Table 4 shows that errors contributed to difficulty frequently.

Participant-experienced errors occurred both during and outside of attempting a specific task. Participants made typos in researcher-provided passwords. Some participants also accidentally logged out of the platform and had trouble returning. For example, P17 logged out during Task 6 (Follow-up after visit): “I’m trying to go back. I backed out of the whole thing.”

Task size

Participants rated the number of subtasks as occasionally contributing to task difficulty. Participants indicated that it was a comparatively less common contributor to difficulty (Table 4). For some, this view was linked to the belief that any task would involve subtasks. P31 explained: “I don’t think the [number of] steps would make it difficult you have to go through a procedure to do anything.”

Task variety

Participants felt that the diversity of inputs contributed to task difficulty occasionally (Table 4). Generally, participants focused on the ease of performing each action rather than the range in their assessments. P7 said, “…check boxes. So, like a date… that was good.” P25 said this was not difficult because “there was literally only one thing to do on each step.”

Temporal demands

Figure 4 compares the time taken for each task by walkthrough evaluators and patient participants. As shown, participants took longer than walkthrough evaluators for every task except Task 4, and longer for all six tasks together. In this context, participants saw the amount of time required for tasks as contributing to difficulty frequently (Table 4).

Several participants had a sense of how long a given task “should” take, and their assessment of task time length referred to it. Where duration was less than that level, time was not a problem. However, when the time taken exceeded that limit, the judgment of task duration was the opposite. P8 said of Task 1 (Request a telehealth visit), “It’s just too time-consuming… I can make a phone call, two minutes, it’s over with. I got an appointment.”

Participants’ views about the amount of time to complete a task could not be separated from their strong views that their time was precious. As P6 asserted, “That’s some of the things that I have, my time…So, I try to make the best of my time.” P16 similarly said, “That’s just my thing, I don’t waste time.”

With a lack of task-related progress and time spent accumulating, many participants expressed frustration while abandoning the task early or asking for help. P17 said, “instead of wasting my time because I’m not that good with electronics anyways…if I ran into a problem and there’s a telephone number…I’m… going to call that and stop my aggravation.” Such patients also asserted that they would abandon the tasks outside of a study, or had previously done so. P30 said, “…you give up.”

Discussion

Study findings provide a foundation for equitable healthcare technology design for socioeconomically marginalized patients. This paper contributes a “Task Complexity Dimensions Theoretical Framework” motivated by learning,27 economic28 and sociological29 theory and research, and which synthesizes task complexity dimensions from multiple fields. This grounds novel complexity-focused methods theoretically, increasing their analytic generalization potential.74 Empirical results also validated these methods with a socioeconomically marginalized sample by showing that multiple complexity dimensions present in 6 technology-mediated telehealth tasks co-occurred with modest task performance success, high cognitive load, and perceived difficulty. Relatedly, 2 of 6 tasks had high cognitive load scores; these scores aligned with those found for demanding work tasks like control and operation of airplanes.75

Participants reacted negatively to high cognitive and temporal demands alongside modest task performance success. Relatedly, frustration when using computer systems is correlated with lost time.76 Subsequently, some participants abandoned tasks, sought help, or stated that they would not continue telehealth tasks in everyday life. Similarly, research elsewhere has documented patient abandonment of technologies that they do not find easy to use.77,78 At the FQHC, patients may have been unable or unwilling to complete telehealth-related tasks. This may be linked to the perceived stress levels of participants, which, though moderate at 20.9 (SD = 6.4), exceeded low perceived stress levels found in national probability samples of adults in the US60 and Germany.79 However, in line with the timing of this study, these PSS-10 scores are closer to the moderate North American average found in a survey conducted in the early COVID-19 pandemic (M = 16.8, SD = 5.2; March-May 2020).123 Such patient stress levels alongside limited ability or willingness could have contributed to the number of video visits at the FQHC decreasing substantially when pandemic isolation waned, as reported by FQHC staff. This also aligns with national research showing inequitable adoption and ongoing usage.6–13,17 Further research is needed to determine whether the low adoption and usage of telehealth among socioeconomically marginalized patients at the study FQHC (and perhaps elsewhere) can be explained by inappropriate design complexity. Such future research should aim to establish whether task complexity is quantitatively associated with patient performance and uptake/usage differentials. Future research should also be conducted in other settings to facilitate wider generalization.

Study results further support the theoretically-derived27–29 claim that complexity is an equitable technology design issue via comparisons between the task complexity-related experiences of university-affiliated walkthrough evaluators and the patient sample. For instance, participants experienced difficulties with complexity dimensions that walkthrough evaluators encountered less often: new concepts/words and errors. Additionally, telehealth tasks took patients more than twice as long as walkthrough evaluators across all tasks; this was problematic given participants’ aversions to wasting time. These time-related findings should be contextualized among socioeconomically marginalized people,29,80–82 for whom greater precarity in labor markets83 may mean less time control due to irregular work schedules and working multiple jobs.84 Socioeconomically marginalized people also frequently face “time penalties”85 from filling out forms for public benefits86 and from unreliable public transportation.87,88 Thus, equitable technology design might require designing technologies to take as little time as possible while retaining effectiveness. Relatedly, technologies that simplify health-related activities and thus take less time tend to narrow health outcome inequities.28

The novel methods for assessing technology-mediated tasks presented here may facilitate future equity-focused technology evaluation. This is critical since findings showed that the complexity dimensions that mattered depart from traditional usability heuristics.89 This could be linked to the fact that prevailing technology inspection methods of heuristic evaluation and cognitive walkthroughs were initially developed and/or validated with undescribed samples,90,91 “average adults”92 or technologically savvy subjects who were likely to be socioeconomically advantaged, like industrial engineers and usability specialists.93–96 Critically, recent work argues that the use of existing, general heuristics may produce “equity failures” because they cannot accommodate the needs of users with varied socioeconomic positions.25 In contrast, the complexity walkthrough method introduced in this paper identified key difficulties for socioeconomically marginalized users. However, findings also suggest that complexity-focused user testing surfaces additional issues not found in the walkthrough. Consequently, socioeconomically marginalized patients themselves may need to identify new concepts/words, and perform tasks themselves to accurately assess error occurrence. Because many errors occurred outside required tasks, such testing should include all parts of task completion: the tasks, and all required actions before, between, and after tasks.

Furthermore, in the service of equitable healthcare technology design, this study tells us what complexity dimensions mattered. First, input/process clarity and unstructured guidance (task ambiguity) contributed very frequently to participants’ perceived difficulty. Notably, the importance of input/process clarity and unstructured guidance echoes prior HCI theory on “the Gulf of Execution” and the “Gulf of Evaluation.”97 In this work, there are gaps between what patients want to do and the actions the systems make available (Execution) and between the system’s actual state and patients’ ability to perceive what the system is actually doing (Evaluation). We also noted usability heuristics aligning with Nielsen’s Visibility of System Status (Heuristic #1), which emphasizes timely, interpretable feedback about system state. However, study data go further by elaborating theoretically-grounded task characteristics that can contribute to those gaps specifically for socioeconomically marginalized patients. Second, context switching (task relationship) mattered very frequently. Such context switching was marked by a violation of expectations (ie, mismatches in patients’ mental models) that can widen the gulfs of execution and evaluation. Specifically, patients’ “mental models”98 were that the entire telehealth process would occur in the portal. The present work, therefore, specifies some of the task flow expectations that were present among study participants. Third, new concepts/words (task ambiguity) mattered frequently.97 This may relate to socioeconomically marginalized people having fewer healthcare experiences than more advantaged people (for example, due to insurance gaps)99,100 and less access to primary care.101 This highlights the limits of the well-known technology design principle that “recognition is easier than recall”102 for patient-facing, technology-mediated healthcare tasks. Indeed, this heuristic assumes prior familiarity with relevant concepts, which may not be evenly distributed across socioeconomic groups. Fourth, deep hierarchies (alongside insufficient navigation guidance) (task relationship) contributed to difficulty frequently. A common approach to navigation guidance is to provide menus, which is typically rationalized with the same “recognition is easier than recall”102 design principle. This is more applicable to this case since the inability to find the appropriate menu item meant that participants needed to remember where it was located.

Other complexity dimensions that mattered frequently were: amount of time (temporal demands); errors (task reliability); and number of navigational interactions (action complexity). Conversely, the following factors only occasionally had an influence on participants’ perceptions of difficulty: task size and variety. However, most telehealth tasks in this study were not particularly large (an average of 4.8 subtasks) or diverse (an average of 2.3 input types), and there were other major areas of difficulty that took precedence for participants. It is possible that size or variety could matter more if tasks have a larger size (following known working memory limits103) or greater diversity, or in situations with fewer other complexities at play. Nevertheless, identifying (un)important factors is needed because consistently associating patient demographics with technology adoption and usage without assessing or improving the technologies risks inappropriately individualizing systemic technology design issues.24,104 Equity-focused design allows researchers and practitioners to focus upon (re)designing technology-mediated tasks, which is easier than changing the socioeconomic positions of individual patients. We now turn to concrete design recommendations based on study findings.

Technology design recommendations for complexity reduction

Several overall design recommendations emerge from study findings to reduce the complexity of technology-mediated tasks for socioeconomically marginalized patients (Box 2). While some scholarship articulates low complexity as a goal,105 this goal is rarely unpacked to determine when difficulty emerges from complexity. Therefore, this section discusses design alternatives for the complexity dimensions that participants believed mattered most for task difficulty: task ambiguity and task relationship.

Recommendation 1: Provide an action-oriented task structure

Task ambiguity can be reduced if more task structure is provided37,106,107 by using task-focused conceptual models to guide user interface design (Recommendation 1a, Box 2). When task structure is provided, task performance requires less task knowledge,107 and lowers cognitive load.108 Similarly, since plain language guidelines advocate action-oriented text to make documents more widely accessible,109 navigation labels (eg, menus and buttons) should use action-oriented text (Recommendation 1a). Task structure is also provided by making explicit the starting place, goal, and process (Recommendation 1b).107 To facilitate this, overall tasks should be chunked into subtasks and placed into a sequence (Recommendation 1c).50,110 This could be helpfully accompanied by supplemental task navigation systems, particularly those that involve clicking forward and backward. Likewise, although few participants disclosed literacy challenges, it is notable that people with low literacy perform better in assigned tasks using linear (versus hierarchical) navigation.111–113

Recommendation 2: Use familiar concepts and words

New concepts and words make tasks more complex102 due to introduced ambiguity, an issue rarely faced by walkthrough evaluators but common among participants. Designers should avoid arranging patient-facing workflows using concepts and words that are primarily meaningful within healthcare organizations (Recommendation 2a, Box 2), and leverage what patients do already know and use those concepts. To understand what words and concepts are familiar, designers should prospectively collect data through design activities, such as concept mapping (eg,114–116) and/or card sorting (eg,117,118) (Recommendation 2b). They should then present and organize tasks according to known concepts and words as discovered through data collection. For instance, this study showed that “visits” are promising as a central concept for future telehealth task organization.

Recommendation 3: Minimize and support context switching

Task relationship-associated complexity may be addressed by avoiding shifting between applications and devices whenever possible (Recommendation 3a, Box 2). For telehealth, we posit that integration of all tasks within one application will go a long way toward supporting socioeconomically marginalized patients. Any unavoidable context switches should be facilitated through links that automatically open another application (Recommendation 3b), with task progress being tracked when moving away from an application. If neither is possible, designers can associate and collect resources across contexts and make it easier to pause and resume tasks (Recommendation 3c).119

Recommendation 4: Flatten hierarchies and provide shallow, task-driven navigation

Providing navigation guidance can help to elucidate task relationships (Recommendation 4a, Box 2). Many participants said they wanted the features “right there.” This aligns with the design principle that commonly-used features should be more visible and that it is vital to provide the initial steps and subsequent paths to accomplish tasks.102 Participants’ difficulties with navigating the portal’s narrow and deep hierarchical navigation system suggest a need for a broad and shallow system (Recommendation 4b).112 The mockup for a redesigned Task 2, with only 2 hierarchy levels, is shown in Appendix D. Given participants’ difficulties locating specific documents, special effort is needed to make documents visible (Recommendation 4c). Embedding them within a given healthcare organization’s customized task processes and/or facilitating known-item searching or browsing might also improve access.50 Still, to reduce action complexity, it is important to minimize navigational interactions like scrolling within subtasks (Recommendation 4d).113 Thus, all necessary content should be visible on one screen.

Limitations

This study had limitations. It focused on one telehealth process using 2 platforms, at one US clinic, and during one historical period, after the lifting of COVID-19 isolation measures. This was a time in which US stress levels were higher than usual,123 and participants in our sample exhibited moderate stress on average. As expected given their socioeconomic status,30,32 participants’ moderate stress levels were still higher than the general US population during this time period. Since there is no clear threshold for stress causing difficulties with performing novel technology-mediated tasks, the generalizability of empirical findings across settings and time periods is unclear. However, a rich description of telehealth tasks and patients’ perspectives may facilitate transferability to similar settings.74 Future research should continue to evaluate participants’ perceived stress levels (ie, using measures like PSS-10), to determine whether, and how, they affect findings. This was also an observational study; hence, it is impossible to assign causality to any observed relationships. Some aspects of the user tests may have made tasks more difficult than “in the wild” (ie, when performed on patients’ own devices during a real visit). Participants were not able to seek help from the researcher before trying tasks themselves, but healthcare staff often provide helpful instructions when patients seek care in daily life.124 Participants were also given pre-configured devices and Internet hotspots. While this ensured access, they had less familiarity with the test device than with their own devices. Future research should investigate task complexity when telehealth tasks are “in the wild.” The NASA-TLX scale was originally developed for workplace tasks, and it is used less often with socioeconomically disadvantaged people in non-work contexts. To address this, researchers provided participants with a paper-based visual scale and explained the meaning of different numbers on the scale, as well as using clarifying words or questions to ensure understanding and accurate completion.

Conclusion

This study, through novel complexity-focused methods, revealed socioeconomically marginalized patients’ performance difficulties and negative perceptions when performing complex technology-mediated telehealth tasks. Task ambiguity and task relationship contributed the most to perceived task difficulty, suggesting novel design heuristics for evaluation. Structuring tasks, using familiar concepts, smoothing context switches, and making navigation task-oriented, broad, and shallow could address these difficulties. Taken together, this paper’s methods and findings may guide future equitable design and evaluation of technology-mediated tasks for FQHC patients.
