Breast Modular Resection Surgery Teaching Model Improves the Effect of Teaching Under Regional Anesthesia for Breast Conserving Surgery and Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy in Day Surgery
Authors: Ru Yao, Aocheng Wu, Jie Lian, Linjuan Tan, Lu Gao, Bo Pan, Qiang Sun, Yidong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBreast‐conserving surgery (BCS) combined with sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) is a standard treatment for early‐stage breast cancer. However, surgical variability and patient discomfort under regional anesthesia present challenges for training programs and consistent clinical outcomes. To address this, we implemented a breast modular resection (BMR) surgical teaching course designed to deconstruct BCS + SLNB into standardized modules and evaluated its impact on training effectiveness and patient outcomes in the day surgery setting under regional anesthesia.MethodsA total of 273 patients underwent BCS with SLNB between 2019 and 2022, performed by six attending breast surgeons. Patients were divided into a classical procedure resection group (n = 124) and a BMR group (n = 149). All surgeries were performed under regional anesthesia. Clinical and subjective outcome indicators, such as re‐excision rate, pain scores, cosmetic satisfaction, and hospital stay, were analyzed.ResultsThe BMR group showed significantly more favorable perioperative outcomes, including lower re‐excision rates (3.85% vs. 11.88%, p = 0.038), reduced intraoperative pain (p < 0.05), decreased seroma incidence (9.62% vs. 20.79%, p = 0.032), and enhanced breast symmetry satisfaction (p = 0.008). A substantially higher proportion of BMR patients were discharged on postoperative Day 1 (79.81% vs. 31.68%, p < 0.001), confirming the feasibility of safe, same‐day BCS + SLNB. There were no differences in long‐term complications or recurrence.ConclusionsThe BMR‐based teaching model improved surgical performance, standardized the technique, and enhanced the patient experience for BCS + SLNB under regional anesthesia. This structured modular approach provides a replaceable framework for surgical education and may facilitate broader adoption of day‐case breast cancer surgery without compromising oncologic safety or patient comfort.Keywords: breast modular resection, breast‐conserving surgery, day surgery, regional anesthesia, sentinel lymph node biopsy, surgical training

Background

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy among women as of 2025 [1]. Breast‐conserving surgery (BCS) has long been the standard approach for treating most primary breast cancers, with axillary staging through sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) effectively mitigating the complications typically associated with axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) [2]. The combination of BCS and SLNB is widely recognized in the management of early‐stage breast cancer. However, this surgical approach is complex and often varies according to individual surgeon practices, which can interfere with the training of surgical learners and negatively impact surgical outcomes. These include inter‐surgeon variability in operative sequence and incision planning, the need to balance oncologic adequacy with cosmetic preservation, the difficulty of pain management in awake patients, the additional complexity introduced by axillary anatomical variation and complications such as wound infection, subcutaneous edema, fluid accumulation/seroma, upper limb edema, and axillary web syndrome. Therefore, we propose the integration of the breast modular resection (BMR) concept into the surgical curriculum to improve standardization, reproducibility, and training consistency without altering the fundamental operative principles, in order to enhance the training efficiency and proficiency of surgeons in performing these procedures.

The concept of modular resection was first introduced in pancreaticosplenectomy, aiming to deconstruct a complex surgical procedure into anatomically supported steps, which are then completed in the form of a task list. The concept has since been extended to the training of other surgical specialties [3, 4, 5]. Notably, approximately 25%–60% of patients experience persistent breast pain for over 3 months after BCS [6]. Postoperative pain is a significant factor contributing to patients' fear of surgery, potentially prolonging recovery time, increasing hospital stay, and escalating medical costs. Regional anesthesia has been reported to significantly reduce pain and lower postoperative inflammatory marker levels in breast cancer surgery [7]. At our center, several surgeons have performed BCS under regional anesthesia with satisfactory results. Additionally, some patients request surgery under regional anesthesia to shorten hospitalization, reduce costs, and accelerate postoperative recovery. Consequently, our curriculum incorporates regional anesthesia. It should be noted that, because the primary objective of this study was to evaluate surgical teaching outcomes of the BMR course, cosmetic goals in all cases were achieved through basic glandular reshaping alone, without deliberately employing oncoplastic surgical techniques [8, 9].

To our knowledge, this is the first study aimed at evaluating the surgical outcomes of a breast‐conserving therapy and SLNB training course for fellow surgeons incorporating the BMR concept.

Methods

Clinical Patients and Study Design

This was a retrospective, single‐center before‐after observational evaluation of a training intervention. A total of 273 female breast cancer patients who agreed to undergo the surgery between November 2019 and February 2022 were enrolled in this study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. A total of six rotating attending surgeons in the field of breast surgery participated in the training program. These six attending surgeons performed BCS and SLNB based on their prior clinical experience and understanding of the surgical procedure, and these patients were enrolled in the classical procedure resection (CPR) group. Subsequently, under the guidance of two senior expert surgeons, the attending surgeons were trained in the concept of modular resection, and patients treated after this training were assigned to the BMR group.

The inclusion criteria for this course were: (1) age 18–69 years; (2) no severe underlying diseases and able to tolerate surgery under local/regional anesthesia; (3) strong desire for breast preservation and considered suitable for BCS based on preoperative exam and imaging, with indications for SLNB. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patient refusal of local/regional anesthesia (preference for general anesthesia or intravenous sedation); (2) not a candidate for BCS or contraindications to SLNB based on preoperative evaluation; (3) stage IV breast cancer. Clinical and pathological data of the patients were collected using electronic medical records at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, and postoperative outcomes were followed up via telephone, outpatient visits, and initial questionnaires (Figure 1).

BMR‐Based Surgical Training Course Design

Step 1: Preoperative Assessment of the Breast and Axilla

Both the CPR and BMR groups adhered to the “no tumor touch” principle. All patients underwent preoperative breast ultrasound and mammography to confirm they were appropriate candidates for BCS and SLNB. For SLNB mapping, either a single‐dye method (methylene blue alone) or a dual‐dye method (methylene blue plus indocyanine green, or methylene blue plus ultrasound contrast agent) was used. Using a 1 mL syringe, 0.2–0.3 mL of air was first drawn up, followed by 0.3–0.5 mL of contrast agent. The air and contrast mixture was then injected subcutaneously around the areola—a technique we refer to as the “blue dye injection with air” method (Figure 2).

The blue dye intradermal injection with air technique. (a) Using a 1 mL syringe, 0.2–0.3 mL of air was first drawn up; (b) intra‐dermal injection of air firstly; (c) followed by 0.3–0.5 mL of contrast agent; (d) usually 2 spots were injected around the areola.

Step 2: Local Infiltration Anesthesia

We utilized a combined local anesthetic solution composed of 20 mL of 2% lidocaine (400 mg) plus 10 mL of 0.75% ropivacaine (75 mg), diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 300 mL (ratio 1∶10). To uphold the no‐tumor‐touch principle, the injection sites for local anesthesia were planned to avoid the tumor area; importantly, axillary anesthetic infiltration was performed first (especially in cases where a diagnostic biopsy had already been done on the breast tumor). The local anesthesia procedure took approximately 20–30 min in total, with incremental injections in multiple planes (intradermal, subcutaneous, and beneath the glandular tissue) to block the intercostal nerves, and around the axillary lateral thoracic nerve at the junction of the pectoralis major and minor muscles (as well as along the anterior border of the latissimus dorsi) (Figure 3). No more than 10 mL of diluted anesthetic solution was injected at any single site, not exceeding the maximal weight‐adjusted doses for lidocaine (5 mg/kg) and ropivacaine (3 mg/kg). Each injection was followed by gentle massage to encourage anesthetic spread. During surgery, the patient's responsiveness and comfort level were monitored frequently. If the patient's pain, as rated on the 0–10 numerical rating scale (NRS), exceeded 7 or if the patient showed any involuntary movement, the procedure was paused and an additional 5 mL of local anesthetic was administered, with a 3‐min wait after massage before resuming surgery. For patients' safety, the institutional preparedness plan for local anesthetic systemic toxicity, including immediate availability of airway equipment, resuscitation drugs, and 20% lipid emulsion, is always ready for emergency. Meanwhile, if the patient suffers from persistent uncontrolled pain, unsafe movement, hemodynamic instability, or other predefined triggers, we would consider escalation or conversion to general anesthesia.

Local infiltration anesthesia of the axilla. (a) The local anesthesia of the axillary lateral chest to block the pectoral nerve, and (b) the anterior border of the latissimus dorsi.

Step 3: Breast Incision Site Selection

Common incision sites for local anesthesia biopsy include: around breast tumor incision, lateral‐upper incision, periareolar incision, lateral transverse straight incision, and inframammary fold incision (Figure 4). Under the premise of ensuring complete tumor excision, the incision location should be selected to be as inconspicuous as possible based on the tumor's position, such as the circum‐areola incision and inframammary fold incision.

Choice of location of the incision. (a) Tumor local incision; (b) upper outer quadrant single incision; (c) peri‐areolar incision; (d) lateral transverse radial incision; (e) infra‐mammary fold incision.

Step 4: Key Points in Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy

One key anatomical consideration during SLNB is the possible presence of Langer's axillary arch, the most common anatomical variation in the axilla (Figure 5). A typical Langer's axillary arch originates from the anterior border of the latissimus dorsi, extends to the posterior border of the pectoralis major, and connects these two muscles. It is about 6.5 cm in length and 1.0 cm in width, coursing across the axilla above the neurovascular bundle to insert into the humerus, resembling an arch (hence also called the axillary arch) [10]. The sentinel lymph node can reside either superficial or deep to this arch, so it is essential to palpate/probe the deeper area; however, deep probing can increase patient discomfort, so additional local anesthetic should be administered if a Langer's arch is encountered.

BMR Course Effectiveness Evaluation

The evaluation of course effectiveness focused on patient‐centered and workflow‐related proxies, which are divided into objective and subjective indicators. Objective indicators include the placement of drainage tubes postoperatively, BCS success rate, length of hospital stay (days), choice of incision type, choice of incision location, secondary wide excision rate, time for sentinel dye disappearance, incidence of fluid accumulation in surgical areas, incidence of upper limb edema, incidence of axillary web syndrome, and long‐term recurrence/metastasis rates. Subjective indicators focus on the patient's self‐reported experience, with pain assessed using the NRS scale [11]. Pain scores are selected from a scale of 0–10, where 0 represents no pain, 1–3 represents mild pain, 4–6 represents moderate pain, and 7–10 represents severe pain. Indicators of perioperative pain include the pain score for sentinel lymph node injection, breast surgery pain score, axillary surgery pain score, postoperative pain score at 3 weeks, and postoperative pain score at 3 months. Postoperative patient satisfaction with aesthetics or overall surgical outcome is assessed on a scale from 0 to 10. The definitions are as follows: poor: very unattractive/very dissatisfied (0); compromised: somewhat unattractive/somewhat dissatisfied (1–3); moderate: average aesthetics/moderate satisfaction (4–6); fair: relatively attractive/relatively satisfied (7–9); perfect: very attractive/very satisfied (10). The primary indicators include the aesthetic effect of breast volume, breast symmetry score, incision aesthetic score, and overall surgical satisfaction score. Other subjective indicators include symptoms of adrenaline exhaustion, defined as postoperative symptoms such as fainting, excessive sweating, and dizziness that require the patient to lie down.

Statistical Analysis

Data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test or Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while skewed variables are given as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables are summarized as frequency and percentage. For between‐group comparisons, Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test was used for nominal data, and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used for ordinal data. All data analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) or SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, United States), with a two‐sided p‐value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

The baseline characteristics of the two groups were generally similar, except for significant differences in the postoperative pathological N‐stage (p = 0.031) and molecular subtype distribution (p = 0.045). A significantly higher proportion of CPR group patients had a family history of breast cancer compared to the BMR group (13.71% vs. 4.03%, p = 0.005).

Postoperative Outcomes and Training Effectiveness

Postoperative outcomes were collected for a total of 205 patients through outpatient visits, telephone follow‐ups, and questionnaires, with 101 patients in the CPR group and 104 patients in the BMR group (Table 2). In terms of surgical outcomes, there were no significant differences between the CPR and BMR groups in postoperative drain placement rates (p = 0.308), success of breast conservation (p = 0.718), whether a single or double incision was used (p = 0.624), or the aesthetic effect on breast volume (p = 0.205). However, the two groups differed in incision location preferences (p = 0.040). Surgeons in the BMR group were significantly less likely to choose an “around‐tumor” incision and more likely to use a periareolar incision compared to those in the CPR group (around‐tumor incision: 53.85% in BMR vs. 70.30% in CPR; periareolar incision: 25.00% in BMR vs. 11.88% in CPR; p < 0.05 for both; Table 3). The BMR group also had a significantly lower secondary re‐excision rate than the CPR group (3.85% vs. 11.88%, p = 0.038). There were no differences between BMR and CPR in the rates of postoperative upper limb edema (4.81% vs. 9.90%, p = 0.188) or axillary web syndrome (21.15% vs. 23.76%, p = 0.738). Importantly, the incidence of seroma (fluid accumulation in the surgical area) was significantly lower in the BMR group (9.62%) compared to the CPR group (20.79%, p = 0.032). Long‐term oncologic outcomes were comparable: the recurrence/metastasis rates were low and similar in both groups (p = 0.745).

In terms of patient comfort and pain management, the BMR training model yielded significantly better results. Patients in the BMR group reported less pain during the SLNB tracer injection (lower sentinel lymph node injection pain scores, p < 0.001) and during the axillary dissection (p = 0.002) compared to the CPR group. The intraoperative breast surgery pain score was also lower in the BMR group (p = 0.012). Patient satisfaction with cosmetic outcomes was high in both groups, but BMR patients were significantly more satisfied with breast symmetry than CPR patients (p = 0.008). Other satisfaction measures, including incision appearance and overall surgical satisfaction, did not significantly differ between BMR and CPR (both p > 0.1). Finally, the BMR training program facilitated significantly faster recovery: a much higher proportion of BMR patients were able to be discharged on postoperative Day 1 (79.81%) compared to CPR patients (31.68%, p < 0.001), underscoring the feasibility of safe day‐case surgery for BCS + SLNB when using the BMR approach.

Discussion

BCS has been widely recognized for providing excellent cosmetic outcomes without increasing the risk of local recurrence, and it is now extensively applied in clinical practice [12]. In fact, over 43% of eligible patients opt for BCS, making it the preferred choice over total mastectomy or mastectomy with reconstruction [13]. A large study of 3000 patients confirmed that those who underwent BCS reported better psychological health, sexual well‐being, and breast satisfaction scores compared to patients who had mastectomy with reconstruction [14]. Axillary staging is a critical component of breast cancer surgery. Randomized trials have shown that in clinically node‐negative patients, SLNB provides equivalent regional control, disease‐free survival, and overall survival compared to ALND [15]. Moreover, SLNB significantly improves quality of life by reducing the impact on upper limb function relative to ALND [15]. Consequently, there is a trend towards performing SLNB under local anesthesia at many hospitals. Local anesthesia avoids the risks associated with general anesthesia, improves operating room efficiency, reduces hospital stay, and allows immediate intraoperative evaluation of nodes via frozen section pathology.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the SLN identification rate under local anesthesia is comparable to that under general anesthesia, with no significant differences in postoperative complications such as axillary web syndrome, seroma, sensory changes, hematoma, or wound infection [16]. However, performing breast and axillary surgery with the patient awake poses unique challenges: due to the dense network of nerves in the breast and axilla, surgical maneuvers (especially electrocautery) can cause considerable pain and discomfort under local anesthesia. This may influence patients' anesthesia preferences and reduce postoperative satisfaction. It has been noted that SLNB under local anesthesia is not technically difficult for surgeons who are already experienced with the procedure under general anesthesia [17]. Nonetheless, in the era of enhanced recovery after surgery, hospitals are increasingly shifting toward SLNB with local anesthesia, meaning that junior surgeons may have fewer opportunities to first master the procedure under general anesthesia. Currently, there is no specialized training program dedicated to teaching SLNB under local/regional anesthesia conditions.

The concept of modular resection was first applied to pancreatic surgery in 1999. By evaluating preoperative imaging and designing surgical anatomical layers, the surgical steps are broken down and modularized according to the sequence of operations, which facilitates R0 resection and thorough lymph node dissection [4]. Subsequent studies confirmed that employing the radical antegrade modular pancreatosplenectomy procedure significantly improved negative margin rates and extended patient survival in pancreatic cancer [18]. Likewise, a rectal modular resection technique developed by Chen et al. significantly shortened the duration of total mesorectal excision for low rectal cancer without increasing complications [19]. Unlike operations under general anesthesia, surgeries performed with the patient under local anesthesia demand that the surgeon constantly manage patient pain and anxiety. Inexperienced surgeons may inadvertently cause more pain or become hesitant due to the patient's reactions, which adds psychological pressure and complicates the teaching process. The modular approach, by breaking the operation into discrete steps with a checklist‐like framework, clearly outlines each phase of the procedure and highlights key considerations at each step. This structured, anatomy‐focused approach makes the procedure more teachable and is particularly well‐suited for surgical education. We previously applied the BMR concept to teaching nipple‐sparing mastectomy, and found that it significantly reduced complications and operative time for novice surgeons [20]. However, prior to the current study, the BMR concept had not been applied to BCS under local anesthesia or to SLNB.

In our evaluation of the BMR course, we used both objective metrics and patient‐reported outcomes. The value of the BMR course lies in converting BCS + SLNB under local/regional anesthesia into a reproducible stepwise workflow, including standardized preoperative planning, more structured incision selection, anatomy‐based dissection, targeted axillary infiltration, and predefined pain‐triggered supplementation of local anesthetic. All these are proposed explanatory mechanisms consistent with our findings. We found that the BMR training significantly shortened hospital stays and dramatically increased the proportion of patients eligible for discharge on the first postoperative day, providing strong evidence for the safety and feasibility of day‐case surgery. In recent years, advances in anesthesia and minimally invasive techniques have expanded the range of patients who can be treated as day cases, and the safety and benefits of day surgery have become widely recognized [21, 22]. BCS and SLNB have been included on the British Association of Day Surgery's list of day‐case procedures since 2007, and in the United States even some mastectomies with axillary clearance are being performed as day surgeries [23]. Studies and systematic reviews have shown that breast cancer patients undergoing day‐case surgery experience faster physical and psychological recovery, lower economic burden, and reduced rates of hospital‐acquired infections and thromboembolism. The main reasons patients fail to meet day‐surgery discharge criteria are usually persistent postoperative nausea/vomiting and pain [21, 24]. Because our protocol uses exclusively local/regional anesthesia, postoperative nausea is minimal, making pain control the key factor for successful same‐day discharge. Thus, surgeons performing day‐case BCS must be adept not only in surgical technique but also in perioperative pain management to ensure patient safety and comfort after discharge. To our knowledge, there is still a lack of formal teaching methods for day‐case BCS + SLNB. The effectiveness of our BMR course suggests it could help surgeons improve the success rate of day‐surgery breast procedures.

Although the overall rate of using a single incision (as opposed to two separate incisions) did not differ significantly between the CPR and BMR groups (35.74% vs. 22.12%, p = 0.624), the BMR training led to a notable shift in the preferred incision location. The BMR group had a significantly lower tendency to use an “around tumor” incision and a higher tendency to use a periareolar incision compared to the CPR group. Proper incision choice is important: while BCS generally yields better cosmetic outcomes than mastectomy, a poorly placed incision can result in hypertrophic scarring or glandular deformity (especially after radiotherapy), which can adversely affect the overall cosmetic outcome of BCS [25]. In our course, we recommend selecting a periareolar incision whenever oncologically feasible, echoing the “moving window” concept described in the literature. The “moving window” approach involves using a small periareolar or other inconspicuous incision for tumor excision or SLNB, combined with the use of wound retractors to adequately expose the surgical field. This method has been shown to achieve good oncologic safety and aesthetic results [26, 27]. Consistent with that, our study found that patients in the BMR group were significantly more satisfied with postoperative breast symmetry. Furthermore, despite the shift toward more cosmetically favorable incisions, the BMR group did not compromise surgical efficacy—in fact, they had a lower secondary excision (re‐excision) rate than the CPR group (3.85% vs. 11.88%, p = 0.038). The BMR group also exhibited a lower incidence of postoperative fluid collections/seroma compared to CPR (9.62% vs. 20.79%, p = 0.032). Importantly, long‐term follow‐up showed no increase in cancer recurrence or metastasis with the BMR approach (recurrence rates −4% in both groups, p = 0.745). These findings confirm that our BMR course improves cosmetic outcomes while maintaining oncologic safety.

As mentioned previously, the primary causes of day surgery discharge failure are persistent vomiting and pain [24]. Since our BMR protocol relies solely on local anesthesia, postoperative nausea is rarely an issue, which means pain management is paramount. We emphasized patient‐reported outcomes for pain in our evaluation, as integrating patient‐reported outcome measures into routine care has been shown to facilitate timely interventions and even improve patient survival and prognosis in oncology [28]. The results demonstrated significantly better pain control in the BMR group, with lower pain scores for both sentinel lymph node injection and axillary dissection compared to CPR. In developing our anesthesia technique, we combined lidocaine and ropivacaine to take advantage of lidocaine's rapid onset and ropivacaine's longer duration; this combination provided effective local anesthesia for the procedure, consistent with known pharmacokinetic profiles [29, 30]. Moreover, our protocol involves comprehensive local infiltration: we avoid injecting anesthetic into the tumor‐bearing area, but thoroughly infiltrate the surrounding breast tissues (intradermal, subcutaneous, and beneath the gland to block intercostal nerves) and the axillary region. Gentle massage after each injection was performed to speed the onset of anesthesia. Given the innervation of the axilla, we ensure anesthetic is delivered around the axillary and lateral thoracic nerves (near the intersection of pectoralis major and minor) to block both the lateral and medial pectoral nerves [31, 32]. Additionally, special attention is paid to identifying Langer's axillary arch, a common variant encountered during SLNB [33]. Langer's axillary arch is typically a muscular or fibromuscular structure, often spindle‐shaped [34]. Previous studies have suggested that it is part of the extension of the pectoralis major muscle into the axilla, also known as the axillopectoral muscle [35]. The presence of a Langer's arch can alter the expected location of the sentinel lymph nodes, potentially making them harder for an inexperienced surgeon to find if nodes are tucked deep under the arch. Therefore, when a Langer's arch is recognized, we deliberately probe its deep aspect (where a sentinel node might hide), and we administer additional local anesthetic to mitigate patient discomfort during this maneuver.

However, there are some limitations. Firstly, the generalizability of this study is limited by its single‐center design and relatively specialized teaching environment. The BMR course was implemented in a tertiary referral center with experienced breast surgeons, an established day‐surgery pathway, and institutional experience in BCS and SLNB under local/regional anesthesia. Therefore, successful implementation in other centers may require experienced trainers and trainees with institutional support for perioperative monitoring and postoperative follow‐up. The core BMR components, including preoperative assessment, incision planning, no‐tumor‐touch resection principles, structured SLNB steps, and recognition of axillary anatomical variations, may be broadly adaptable. In contrast, local/regional anesthesia protocols, intraoperative pain‐control strategies, discharge timing, and follow‐up procedures should be modified according to local resources and patient‐selection criteria. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate the external applicability of this teaching model. Secondly, the postoperative outcome analysis was based on patients with available follow‐up data, which may lead to potential attrition bias. Future prospective studies should use standardized follow‐up schedules and electronic patient‐reported outcome systems to improve the completeness of outcome capture.

Conclusions

The breast modular resection teaching model was significantly associated with more favorable surgical training outcomes and patient outcomes for BCS with SLNB performed under regional anesthesia. This structured modular approach enabled a higher rate of successful day‐case surgeries by shortening hospital stays, without increasing complications or compromising oncologic safety. Surgeons trained with the BMR model achieved better cosmetic results (through optimal incision selection and fewer re‐excisions) and more effective pain control, highlighting the value of this training program for enhancing surgical education and patient care in breast oncology.
