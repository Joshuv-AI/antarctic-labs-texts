Mini‐Open Versus Open Transforaminal Lumbar Interbody Fusion in Patients Aged ≥ 75 Years: Perioperative Surgical Burden and Clinical Outcomes
Authors: Zhendong Huang, Kelv Shen, Fengxian Jiang, Fengyu Ma, Xiang Wang, Hui Wang, Zhengfeng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposePatients aged ≥ 75 years are at increased risk of perioperative stress and delayed early recovery after lumbar fusion, making the choice of surgical approach clinically relevant. However, direct comparative data on mini‐open transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MO‐TLIF) and conventional open TLIF in this population remain limited. This study compared perioperative surgical burden, postoperative pain and functional recovery trajectories, and 12‐month radiographic fusion and satisfaction between MO‐TLIF and conventional open TLIF in patients aged ≥ 75 years.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 172 patients aged ≥ 75 years with degenerative lumbar disease who underwent MO‐TLIF (n = 84) or open TLIF (n = 88). Eligible patients considered suitable for both procedures were alternately assigned to the MO‐TLIF or open TLIF group according to the order of enrollment. Perioperative variables included operative time, blood loss, time to first ambulation, length of stay, and complications. Laboratory markers on postoperative day (POD) 3 were assessed to reflect muscle injury and inflammatory response. Patient‐reported outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at POD 5, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months using VAS (leg and back pain), Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score, and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Radiographic fusion at 12 months was graded using the Bridwell classification, and satisfaction at 12 months was evaluated with the modified MacNab criteria.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups. Compared with open TLIF, MO‐TLIF was associated with shorter operative time (106.49 ± 21.69 vs. 129.40 ± 26.13 min; p < 0.001), less blood loss (104.00 ± 21.97 vs. 149.97 ± 37.12 mL; p < 0.001), earlier ambulation (2.18 ± 0.39 vs. 2.82 ± 0.56 days; p < 0.001), and shorter length of stay (9.27 ± 1.88 vs. 11.40 ± 2.31 days; p < 0.001). On POD 3, CK was lower after MO‐TLIF (274.83 ± 119.09 vs. 363.50 ± 104.72 U/L; p < 0.001), while CRP and white blood cell count were similar (p > 0.05). PROMs improved over time in both groups; MO‐TLIF showed faster early recovery, with significantly better pain and functional scores at POD 5 or 1 month (all p < 0.05), whereas between‐group differences were not significant at 3 and 6 months (p > 0.05). At 12 months, Bridwell fusion grades and modified MacNab satisfaction were comparable between groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionsIn patients aged ≥ 75 years, both MO‐TLIF and open TLIF provided sustained pain relief and functional improvement. MO‐TLIF reduced perioperative burden and was associated with faster early recovery while achieving similar 12‐month radiographic fusion and patient satisfaction compared with open TLIF. Trail registration: Chinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2500103261.

Introduction

With the acceleration of global population aging, the burden of degenerative spine disorders continues to rise, and degenerative lumbar spine disease has become a major contributor to pain, functional disability, and healthcare utilization [1, 2]. In this context, the use of lumbar fusion in elderly and very elderly populations has increased steadily, making perioperative risk and the quality of postoperative recovery in patients aged ≥ 75 years key considerations in clinical decision‐making [3].

TLIF is a commonly used procedure for degenerative lumbar spine disease requiring neural decompression and reconstruction of segmental stability [4]. Conventional open TLIF can provide reliable symptom improvement; however, it typically requires wider posterior exposure and paraspinal muscle retraction, which may result in greater blood loss, increased postoperative pain, and longer hospital stay, all of which are clinically important in patients aged ≥ 75 years with limited physiological reserve [5, 6, 7]. Available evidence suggests that open TLIF can still achieve acceptable radiographic outcomes in this age group, yet older patients remain more susceptible to perioperative stress and delayed early functional recovery [8].

To reduce approach‐related soft‐tissue injury, minimally invasive TLIF (MI‐TLIF) and endoscopic fusion techniques have been increasingly adopted. Multiple systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials indicate that MI‐TLIF, compared with open TLIF, can reduce perioperative blood loss and shorten hospital stay [9]. However, minimally invasive and endoscopic workflows may also entail increased fluoroscopy and radiation exposure, higher technical demands, and learning‐curve–related variability in operative time; these trade‐offs between efficiency and accessibility warrant particular caution in patients aged ≥ 75 years [9, 10]. Accordingly, for very elderly patients, the goal of procedure selection is not minimal invasiveness per se, but rather an appropriate balance among soft‐tissue preservation, operative efficiency, technical complexity, and perioperative safety.

Against this clinical background, we developed MO‐TLIF aimed at preserving soft tissue while maintaining operative efficiency. Built upon a conventional open surgical platform, MO‐TLIF reduces incision length and the extent of dissection and optimizes the working corridor to accomplish decompression, instrumentation, and interbody fusion without reliance on dedicated endoscopy or complex tubular systems, thereby offering broad applicability and scalability. In prior prospective work, we evaluated clinical improvement, paraspinal muscle morphological changes, and 1‐year radiographic follow‐up, suggesting that MO‐TLIF may lessen soft‐tissue injury while achieving decompression and fusion goals [11]. Nevertheless, in patients aged ≥ 75 years, direct comparative evidence between MO‐TLIF and traditional open TLIF remains limited, and studies providing a comprehensive assessment of perioperative surgical burden, soft‐tissue injury, longitudinal pain and functional recovery, and 1‐year fusion‐related outcomes and overall patient satisfaction are lacking.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective comparative study of MO‐TLIF and conventional open TLIF in patients aged ≥ 75 years with degenerative lumbar disease. The specific aims were: (i) to compare perioperative surgical burden, including operative time, blood loss, ambulation time, length of hospital stay, drain use, and early postoperative markers of muscle injury and inflammation; (ii) to evaluate postoperative pain and functional recovery trajectories during the first 6 months using VAS for back and leg pain, JOA score, and ODI; and (iii) to assess 12‐month radiographic fusion and overall patient satisfaction using the Bridwell fusion classification and modified MacNab criteria.

Methods

Study Design

This was a single‐center prospective comparative cohort study comparing MO‐TLIF with conventional open TLIF in patients aged 75 years or older with degenerative lumbar spine disease. Eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria and were considered suitable for both procedures were alternately assigned to the MO‐TLIF or open TLIF group according to the order of enrollment. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No. JD‐LK2023045‐I01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Consecutive patients aged ≥ 75 years who underwent TLIF for degenerative lumbar spine disease at our institution between May 2023 and January 2025 were enrolled. Degenerative lumbar spine disease was confirmed using radiographs, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and surgery was indicated when decompression with segmental stabilization and interbody fusion was required.

Patients were included if they (1) were aged ≥ 75 years, (2) had imaging‐confirmed degenerative lumbar spine disease, and (3) underwent one‐ or two‐level TLIF. Exclusion criteria were: previous lumbar surgery, significant spinal deformity, non‐degenerative pathology (trauma, tumor, or infection), incomplete key clinical or imaging data, and inability to complete scheduled follow‐up. All procedures were performed by the same spine surgery team under a standardized perioperative management pathway.

Surgical Procedures

The MO‐TLIF technique and operative steps have been described in detail in our previous reports [11, 12]. Key intraoperative steps of MO‐TLIF are illustrated in Figure 1. In the present study, the procedure was performed as follows. Under C‐arm fluoroscopy, the index level and the surface projection of the pedicles were identified on standard anteroposterior views and marked on the skin. After routine disinfection and sterile draping, a longitudinal skin incision of approximately 3 cm was made along the preoperative posterior lumbar marking. The subcutaneous tissue and thoracolumbar fascia were incised sequentially. The paraspinal muscles were detached along the symptomatic side to expose the pars interarticularis of the cranial vertebra, the lamina, and the facet joint at the target level. A laminar retractor was placed at the lateral margin of the superior articular process of the caudal vertebra to expose the operative field. The working corridor could usually be established within approximately 5 min. The inferior articular process of the cranial vertebra and part of the superior articular process of the caudal vertebra were removed using an ultrasonic osteotome or a conventional osteotome. Part of the ligamentum flavum was then resected to expose the dural sac and nerve root. The dorsal portion of the ligamentum flavum and epidural fat were preserved as much as possible to minimize disturbance of the normal intraspinal structures. The nerve root and dural sac were gently retracted medially. The annulus fibrosus was incised with a sharp blade, and the intervertebral space was gradually distracted. The nucleus pulposus was removed, the cartilaginous endplate was curetted, and the bony endplate was exposed. After irrigation and hemostasis of the intervertebral space, impacted bone grafting was performed. An appropriately sized interbody cage filled with morselized autologous bone was inserted into the central portion of the intervertebral space. The cage position was confirmed by fluoroscopy. The dural sac and nerve root were then explored to confirm adequate mobility, with no residual compression or spinal canal stenosis. After thorough irrigation and hemostasis, no drain was routinely placed. Approximately 1 cm skin incisions were then made over the lateral margin of the bilateral pedicle projections of the vertebrae adjacent to the index disc space. Under C‐arm fluoroscopic guidance, pedicle puncture was performed, followed by percutaneous pedicle screw insertion and rod fixation. The wounds were irrigated with normal saline, meticulous hemostasis was achieved, and all incisions were closed in layers and covered with sterile dressings. After recovery from anesthesia, lower‐limb motor function was routinely examined.

Open TLIF was performed according to previously described unilateral transforaminal principles, including unilateral facetectomy to establish the working corridor, discectomy with endplate preparation, interbody grafting with cage insertion, and subsequent bilateral pedicle screw fixation with compression and cage locking [13].

Perioperative Variables and Laboratory Markers

Perioperative variables included operative time, estimated blood loss, time to first ambulation, and postoperative length of hospital stay. To reflect perioperative inflammatory response and muscle injury, C‐reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell count, and creatine phosphokinase (CK) were measured preoperatively and on postoperative Day 3 (POD3).

Patient‐Reported Outcomes and Follow‐Up

Patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) were collected preoperatively and at POD5, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively. PROMs included the visual analogue scale (VAS) for back and leg pain, the Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score, and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Overall patient satisfaction was assessed at 12 months using the modified MacNab criteria.

Radiographic Assessment

Radiographic follow‐up consisted of lumbar anteroposterior and lateral radiographs and flexion–extension views obtained preoperatively and at approximately 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Fusion status at 12 months was graded using the Bridwell fusion classification (Grades I–IV). Radiographs were independently reviewed by two spine surgeons blinded to clinical outcomes; disagreements were resolved by consensus, with adjudication by a third surgeon if required.

Statistical Analysis

An a priori sample size calculation was performed before patient enrollment using G*Power software, version 3.1.9.7. The calculation was based on the main between‐group comparison of perioperative surgical burden, using preliminary data and previous experience with MO‐TLIF and conventional open TLIF. With a two‐sided α level of 0.05, 80% power, a 1:1 allocation ratio, and an assumed moderate effect size (Cohen's d = 0.50), at least 64 patients were required per group. Allowing for approximately 15% incomplete data or loss to follow‐up, the planned minimum sample size was approximately 150 patients. The final sample size of 172 patients exceeded this requirement. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed before analysis. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed and were compared using the independent‐samples t‐test; non‐normally distributed variables are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Longitudinal patient‐reported outcomes (VAS, JOA, and ODI) across follow‐up time points were analyzed using a repeated‐measures general linear model, with group as a between‐subject factor and time as a within‐subject factor. When the sphericity assumption was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. All tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 172 patients aged ≥ 75 years with degenerative lumbar disease were included, with 84 patients in the MO‐TLIF group and 88 patients in the open TLIF group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups, with no significant differences in age, BMI, ASA class, disease duration, diagnostic distribution, or follow‐up duration (all p > 0.05). In addition, there were no significant between‐group differences in major preoperative comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic kidney disease (all p > 0.05), indicating that the two groups were generally comparable in terms of preoperative comorbidity burden.

Perioperative Outcomes

The distribution of fused levels was comparable between groups (single level: 43/84 vs. 49/88; multilevel: 41/84 vs. 39/88; p = 0.662). Overall, MO‐TLIF was associated with a shorter operative time (106.49 ± 21.69 vs. 129.40 ± 26.13 min; p < 0.001) and less intraoperative blood loss (104.00 ± 21.97 vs. 149.97 ± 37.12 mL; p < 0.001) than open TLIF (Table 1). After stratification by the number of fused levels, MO‐TLIF remained associated with shorter operative time and less intraoperative blood loss for both single‐level (operative time: 88.14 ± 7.69 vs. 111.00 ± 12.41 min; blood loss: 87.21 ± 10.34 vs. 123.24 ± 16.45 mL; both p < 0.001) and multilevel procedures (operative time: 125.73 ± 13.15 vs. 152.51 ± 19.59 min; blood loss: 121.61 ± 16.39 vs. 183.55 ± 27.09 mL; both p < 0.001) (Table 1). In addition, MO‐TLIF was associated with earlier ambulation (2.18 ± 0.39 vs. 2.82 ± 0.56 days; p < 0.001) and a shorter postoperative length of stay (9.27 ± 1.88 vs. 11.40 ± 2.31 days; p < 0.001). Drains were not routinely used after MO‐TLIF, whereas they were routinely placed after open TLIF (Table 2).

Pain and Functional Outcomes (PROMs) and Recovery Trajectories

PROMs improved over time in both groups. For VAS leg pain, MO‐TLIF showed better scores at POD 5 (2.65 ± 0.65 vs. 3.22 ± 0.75; p < 0.001) and 1 month (1.83 ± 0.64 vs. 2.28 ± 0.73; p < 0.001), whereas differences were not significant at 3 and 6 months (p = 0.372 and 0.686). For VAS back pain, MO‐TLIF was lower at POD 5 (3.14 ± 0.70 vs. 3.47 ± 0.83; p = 0.006), with no significant differences at 1, 3, or 6 months (all p > 0.05). JOA scores were higher after MO‐TLIF at POD 5 (21.21 ± 2.98 vs. 20.27 ± 2.60; p = 0.028) and 1 month (23.18 ± 1.98 vs. 22.27 ± 1.81; p = 0.006), but not at 3 or 6 months (p = 0.128 and 0.688). ODI was lower after MO‐TLIF at 1 month (27.27 ± 5.15 vs. 29.08 ± 5.82; p = 0.032), while between‐group differences were not significant at POD 5 or at 3 and 6 months (all p > 0.05). Overall, the recovery pattern suggested an early advantage with subsequent convergence (Table 4; Figure 2).

Discussion

Main Findings

In this study, we compared perioperative outcomes and postoperative trajectories of pain and functional recovery between MO‐TLIF and conventional open TLIF in patients aged ≥ 75 years with degenerative lumbar disease. Two main findings emerged. First, MO‐TLIF was associated with a lower perioperative burden, reflected by shorter operative time, less intraoperative blood loss, a shorter length of stay, and lower CK on postoperative Day 3. Second, both groups achieved sustained pain relief and functional improvement; however, recovery was faster in the early postoperative period after MO‐TLIF, whereas between‐group differences in patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) diminished by 3–6 months. At 12 months, radiographic fusion and overall satisfaction were comparable. Collectively, these findings suggest that, in very elderly patients, the incremental value of MO‐TLIF lies primarily in reducing perioperative stress and facilitating early recovery, while mid‐term clinical gains and 1‐year radiographic outcomes are similar to those of open TLIF.

Perioperative Surgical Burden and Early Recovery

Older patients have reduced physiological reserve and stress resilience, rendering them more vulnerable to perioperative surgical stress. Prolonged anesthesia exposure, greater blood loss, and a higher soft‐tissue trauma burden may amplify postoperative inflammatory and metabolic stress, thereby delaying early mobilization and initiation of rehabilitation [14, 15]. In our cohort, MO‐TLIF was associated with shorter operative time, less blood loss, and a shorter length of stay than open TLIF, indicating a lower overall perioperative burden. Importantly, time to first ambulation was significantly earlier after MO‐TLIF, suggesting that reductions in perioperative burden can translate into an earlier start of functional recovery.

The length of stay observed in both groups should be interpreted in the context of local discharge practice. At our institution, discharge after lumbar fusion in patients aged ≥ 75 years is generally considered only after wound condition is stable, pain is adequately controlled, independent or assisted ambulation is achieved, and major perioperative concerns have been excluded. Accordingly, the absolute length of stay in this cohort may be longer than that reported by institutions using enhanced recovery or POD 2–3 discharge pathways after minimally invasive TLIF. Nevertheless, because both groups were managed under the same standardized perioperative pathway, the between‐group difference in length of stay remains clinically interpretable and likely reflects the lower perioperative burden and faster early recovery associated with MO‐TLIF.

Soft‐Tissue Injury and Postoperative Laboratory Markers

From a tissue‐injury perspective, lower CK on postoperative Day 3 in the MO‐TLIF group suggests less paraspinal muscle damage. CK is a commonly used biochemical marker of skeletal muscle injury, and its postoperative rise has been associated with the extent of muscle dissection, retraction intensity, and ischemic duration [2, 16], which is consistent with the technical profile of MO‐TLIF in minimizing dissection and sustained retraction. In addition, drains were not routinely used after MO‐TLIF but were commonly placed after open TLIF; this practice difference may reflect less soft‐tissue oozing with the mini‐open approach, potentially reducing early nursing demands and facilitating earlier mobilization. Overall, by lowering surgical stress and mitigating muscle injury, MO‐TLIF may enable very elderly patients to ambulate earlier and enter rehabilitation more promptly.

Postoperative Pain and Functional Recovery Trajectories

This temporal recovery pattern has a clear clinical rationale. Prior work suggests that early postoperative PROMs are particularly sensitive to short‐term factors such as incision‐related pain, acute paraspinal muscle injury/edema, and restricted mobility, all of which are closely linked to the degree of approach‐related soft‐tissue disruption [17, 18]. Compared with open surgery, minimally invasive TLIF can reduce muscle dissection and prolonged retraction, thereby lowering postoperative pain, analgesic requirements, and time to mobilization. As tissue healing progresses and rehabilitation advances, mid‐term outcomes increasingly reflect the adequacy of decompression, segmental stability, and neurological recovery, and the marginal impact attributable to the surgical approach gradually diminishes. Therefore, the benefit of MO‐TLIF appeared to be most evident during the early postoperative recovery period.

Positioning of MO‐TLIF Among Less Invasive Lumbar Fusion Techniques

Meanwhile, the clinical relevance of these early differences should be interpreted in the context of the overall recovery profile. Although the absolute between‐group differences in some PROMs, such as VAS, JOA, and ODI, were modest at certain time points, they were consistent with other perioperative findings, including shorter operative time, less intraoperative blood loss, lower postoperative CK levels, earlier ambulation, and shorter hospital stay in the MO‐TLIF group. These concordant findings suggest that the value of MO‐TLIF lies not in a marked improvement in a single patient‐reported outcome, but in reducing perioperative surgical burden and facilitating early recovery. For patients aged 75 years or older, earlier mobilization and shorter hospitalization may be clinically meaningful because they can reduce nursing burden, support postoperative rehabilitation, and potentially decrease risks associated with prolonged bed rest [19, 20]. The convergence of PROMs at 3–6 months, together with comparable 12‐month fusion outcomes and overall satisfaction, further indicates that MO‐TLIF provides an early recovery benefit while maintaining similar mid‐term clinical and radiographic outcomes. This pattern is consistent with the concept of enhanced recovery after surgery.

Our findings are directionally consistent with two previous studies from our group. A prospective case series suggested that MO‐TLIF can achieve effective decompression and fusion within a conventional open framework, with less pronounced muscle injury–related changes and stable perioperative parameters. A previous randomized controlled study from our group further suggested that MO‐TLIF was associated with shorter operative time and less intraoperative blood loss than Wiltse‐approach MIS‐TLIF in a selected patient population [21]. However, that study was not specifically conducted in patients aged ≥ 75 years, and MIS‐TLIF was not included as a comparator in the present cohort. Therefore, the current findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence that MO‐TLIF is superior to MI‐TLIF in elderly patients. Based on the findings of the present cohort of patients aged 75 years or older, MO‐TLIF may be better positioned as a modified technique based on conventional open TLIF. Its purpose is to reduce approach‐related soft tissue injury while preserving the advantages of direct visualization and procedural controllability associated with open surgery. For elderly patients, operative time, blood loss control, and procedural reproducibility are closely related to perioperative safety. Therefore, MO‐TLIF may provide a modified open fusion strategy that balances trauma control with procedural feasibility in this patient population.

Notably, our findings should not be interpreted as negating the value of endoscopic or other minimally invasive fusion techniques. Many studies suggest that endoscopic fusion may reduce soft‐tissue injury and accelerate short‐term recovery [22]. However, in very elderly patients, a practical concern is that these techniques rely heavily on surgeon experience and specific instrumentation; during the learning‐curve phase, operative time may be prolonged and more variable. Excessively long operative time entails longer anesthesia exposure and greater perioperative stress, which may offset some of the benefits of minimal invasiveness [23]. By contrast, MO‐TLIF more closely follows a conventional open workflow, with a relatively flatter learning curve and more predictable operative time, potentially making it a more scalable and reproducible muscle‐sparing option for very elderly patients. Future direct comparative studies, including open TLIF, MO‐TLIF, and MIS or endoscopic fusion, are warranted to better define the indications, advantages, and clinical value of each technique in elderly patients.

Strengths and Limitations of This Study

Although data were collected prospectively, the non‐randomized design may be subject to selection bias. In the present study, eligible patients considered suitable for both procedures were alternately assigned to the MO‐TLIF or open TLIF group according to the order of enrollment; however, this allocation method cannot fully replace randomized allocation. In addition, all procedures were performed by a single surgical team; therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to centers with different levels of experience. Furthermore, although radiographic follow‐up was scheduled to 12 months, longer‐term follow‐up remains important to evaluate fusion durability and late complications. Future studies should incorporate objective metrics that better capture early rehabilitation, such as postoperative analgesic and opioid consumption, as well as cost‐effectiveness analyses and longer‐term imaging and clinical endpoints.

Conclusion

MO‐TLIF represents an effective alternative approach for lumbar interbody fusion in elderly patients. In patients aged ≥ 75 years with degenerative lumbar disease, both MO‐TLIF and open TLIF achieved clinically meaningful pain relief and functional improvement. Compared with open TLIF, MO‐TLIF was associated with a lower perioperative burden and faster early recovery in terms of pain relief and functional restoration, whereas patient‐reported outcomes largely converged by 3–6 months postoperatively. At 12 months, radiographic fusion outcomes assessed by Bridwell grading and overall patient satisfaction were comparable between groups, indicating that MO‐TLIF can improve perioperative soft‐tissue preservation and facilitate early rehabilitation while maintaining similar 1‐year radiographic outcomes and overall patient benefit compared with open TLIF.
