1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite simple graph on
n
vertices and set
R=\Bbbk[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
Associated to
G
are the edge ideal
I(G)
and the cover ideal
J(G)
.
This paper studies the interaction between two invariants of
R/J(G)
that have independently played
central roles in combinatorial commutative algebra: the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity

\reg(R/J(G))
and the degree of the
h
-polynomial
\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)
.
Our emphasis throughout is on understanding
\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)
and how it constrains (and is constrained by) regularity.

Regularity measures the complexity of a minimal free resolution, while the
h
-polynomial controls the
Hilbert series and encodes subtle information about growth and asymptotics of the Hilbert function.
Despite their common origin in the graded structure of
R/J(G)
, these invariants are often studied
through different lenses, and a systematic understanding of their
joint behavior
for fixed
n

remains limited. For edge ideals, this joint behavior has been investigated in

[
2
;
11
;
12
]
, and more generally for monomial ideals
in
[
13
]
, for binomial edge ideals in
[
14
]
, and for toric ideals of graphs in
[
8
]
. In contrast, the corresponding picture for cover ideals appears to be
largely unexplored. Motivated by extensive computations, we ask:

For connected graphs on a fixed number of vertices, which pairs

\bigl(\reg(R/J(G)),\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\bigr)

can occur, and what structural features of
G

force the pair to lie on (or away from) the diagonal and its neighboring lines?

This question becomes more tractable once one brings in a
purely combinatorial object: the independence polynomial

P_{G}(x)=\sum_{i\geq 0}g_{i}x^{i},

where
g_{i}
counts independent sets of size
i
in
G
.

Independence polynomials have a long history in graph theory and statistical physics, and their roots
have been studied from many perspectives (
[
1
;
3
;
4
;
5
;
6
;
20
]
). Here we focus on a single distinguished root,
x=-1
,
and introduce the invariant

M(G)\ :=\ \text{the multiplicity of $x=-1$ as a root of $P_{G}(x)$}.

To our knowledge, this invariant has not previously been studied as an invariant in its own right,
and it does not appear explicitly in the existing graph theory or commutative algebra literature.
Our results suggest that
M(G)
is a remarkably
effective “bridge” invariant: it packages a delicate cancellation phenomenon in
P_{G}(x)
at
-1

into an integer that directly controls degrees of
h
-polynomials for both
R/I(G)
and
R/J(G)
.
In particular,
M(G)
is sensitive to graph operations that preserve the combinatorics of independent
sets in a controlled way (such as whiskering), and it admits clean
recursions in families where regularity is already well understood (such as chordal graphs).
This makes
M(G)
a natural new tool for translating between enumerative graph data and homological
invariants of edge and cover ideals.

Our starting point is an explicit description of the
h
-polynomial for cover ideals (
3.5
), developed in
Section
3
. This result is an analogue of
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
, which treats edge ideals. In addition, one can generalize both expressions for the edge and cover ideals to any squarefree monomial ideal and its Alexander dual. Lastly, in this section, we relate the degrees of the

h
-polynomials for edge ideals and cover ideals since their
a
-invariants coincide (
3.8
).

The key link to independence polynomials and the invariant
M(G)
is developed in Section
4
,
where we show that
\deg h
is determined by the
invariant
M(G)
(
4.4
), yielding formulas of the form

\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\alpha(G)-M(G)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=n-2-M(G).

Thus, the problem of computing
\deg h
is reduced to understanding
M(G)
.

The remainder of the paper develops methods to compute
M(G)
and applies them to families that
populate (or obstruct) regions of the
(\reg,\deg h)
-plot for cover ideals. In Section
5
we obtain explicit
formulas for
\reg(R/J(G))
and
\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)
for trees of radius at most two (
5.9
).
To handle forests more generally, Section
6
establishes a
recursion for
M(G)
(
6.6
), giving an effective reduction to induced subforests as an analogue of Jacques and Katzman’s work on projective dimension from
[
16
]
.
Section
7
studies whiskering operations and derives closed formulas for uniform
whiskering (
7.1
), together with a vertex-whiskering formula expressed in terms of
G
and
G\setminus v
(
Section

7
).

We then turn to structured chordal classes with particularly rigid behavior.
In Section
8
we focus on split graphs. Using a split decomposition

V(G)=C\sqcup I
, where
C
is a clique and
I
is an independent set, we obtain an explicit formula for
P_{G}(x)
and deduce closed formulas for
\alpha(G)
and
M(G)
.
In addition, we show that for connected split
graphs the pair
\bigl(\reg(R/J(G)),\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\bigr)
lies on the diagonal (
Section

8
), with a precise
description of which diagonal values occur for fixed
n
(
8.10
).
Section
9
constructs chordal families satisfying
\reg(R/J(G))=n-2
while

\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)
varies, producing vertical strings in the
(\reg,\deg h)
-plot.
Section
10
provides a further chordal construction on the line
\deg h=\reg-1
.
In contrast, Section
11
proves a restriction for block graphs: connected block
graphs lie on or above the line
\deg h=\reg-1
, so this class cannot appear strictly below the diagonal (
11.5
).

Finally, in Section
12
, we compile computational data for connected graphs on
n

vertices up to a fixed range, discuss the feasible region for
(\reg,\deg h)
-pairs, and
propose the bound

\bigl|\reg(R/J(G))-\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\bigr|\leq\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-2

as
Section

12
.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout, let
G
be a finite simple graph on vertex set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
,
and we work in the standard graded polynomial ring
R=\Bbbk[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

Definition 2.1
.

The
independence complex
of
G
, denoted
\Delta(G)
, is the simplicial complex on

[n]
whose faces are the independent sets of
G
.

For a simplicial complex
\Delta
on
[n]
, let
I_{\Delta}
denote its Stanley–Reisner ideal.
The Alexander dual of
\Delta
is

\Delta^{\vee}:=\{[n]\setminus F:F\notin\Delta\}.

The Alexander dual of a squarefree monomial ideal
I
is denoted
I^{\vee}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
edge ideal
of
G
is

I(G):=\bigl(x_{i}x_{j}:\{i,j\}\in E(G)\bigr)\subseteq R.

The
cover ideal
of
G
is

J(G):=\Bigg(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i}:\mathcal{C}\text{ a minimal vertex cover of }G\Bigg)\subseteq R.

Remark 2.3
.

The edge and cover ideals of
G
are related through Alexander duality:

J(G)=I(G)^{\vee}.

Equivalently,
J(G)=I_{\Delta(G)^{\vee}}
and

J(G)=\bigcap_{\{i,j\}\in E(G)}(x_{i},x_{j}).

Note that, if
G
has at least one edge, then
\height(J(G))=2
and hence

\dim(R/J(G))=n-2.

Definition 2.4
.

A
graded minimal free resolution
of
R/J(G)
is an exact sequence of the form

0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}R(-j)^{\beta_{p,j}}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}R(-j)^{\beta_{p-1,j}}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}R(-j)^{\beta_{0,j}}\longrightarrow R/J(G)\longrightarrow 0.

The exponents
\beta_{i,j}=\beta_{i,j}(R/J(G))
are invariants of
R/J(G)
called the
graded Betti numbers
of
R/J(G)
. The
Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity
, denoted by
\reg(R/J(G))
, is defined in terms of the non-zero graded Betti numbers of
R/J(G)
:

\reg(R/J(G))=\max\{j-i:\beta_{i,j}(R/J(G))\neq 0\}.

One can study the regularity of
R/J(G)
through the projective dimension of
R/I(G)
due to the following celebrated result of Terai from
[
21
]
. We use this result repeatedly throughout the paper.

Remark 2.5
.

Regularity is dual to projective dimension.

\reg(R/J(G))=\pdim I(G)=\pdim(R/I(G))-1.

Projective dimension of edge ideals was studied in
[
7
]
by Dao and Schweig. In this paper, we often focus on chordal graphs and the following result from
[
7
, Corollary 5.6]
is useful for our regularity computations.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
G
be a chordal graph. Then

\pdim(R/I(G))-1=n-i(G)

where
i(G)
is the independent domination number of
G
.

The independent domination number
i(G)
is the minimum size of an independent dominating set. It is also equal to the minimum cardinality among all maximal independent sets of
G
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
A=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}A_{d}
be a standard graded
k
-algebra. Its Hilbert series is

H_{A}(t):=\sum_{d\geq 0}HF_{A}(d)t^{d},

where
HF_{A}(d)=\dim_{k}A_{d}
is the Hilbert function of
A
in degree
d
. It is well-known that

H_{A}(t)=\frac{h_{A}(t)}{(1-t)^{\dim A}}

for a unique polynomial
h_{A}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
, called the
h
-polynomial
of
A
.
The
\mathfrak{a}
-invariant
of
A
is

\mathfrak{a}(A):=\deg h_{A}(t)-\dim A.

Next, we encode independent sets via a generating function.
Let
\alpha=\alpha(G)
denote the independence number of
G
, and for each
i\geq 0
let
g_{i}

denote the number of independent sets of
G
of size
i
(so
g_{0}=1
,
g_{1}=n
, and
g_{i}=0
for
i>\alpha
).

Definition 2.8
.

The
independence polynomial
of
G
is

P_{G}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha}g_{i}x^{i}.

Definition 2.9
.

Following
[
2
]
, define

g(G):=\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}(-1)^{i-1}g_{i}.

Remark 2.10
.

Let
\Delta(G)
be the independence complex of
G
. An independent set of size
i

corresponds to a face of
\Delta(G)
of dimension
i-1
, so
f_{i-1}=g_{i}
.
Hence the (unreduced) Euler characteristic of
\Delta(G)
is

\chi(\Delta(G))=\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}f_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}(-1)^{i-1}g_{i}.

In particular,
g(G)=\chi(\Delta(G))
, and the reduced Euler characteristic is

\widetilde{\chi}(\Delta(G))=g(G)-1
.
Moreover,

P_{G}(-1)=\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha}g_{i}(-1)^{i}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}g_{i}(-1)^{i-1}=1-g(G).

Thus
g(G)=1\iff\widetilde{\chi}(\Delta(G))=0\iff P_{G}(-1)=0
.

Remark 2.11
.

Since
R/I(G)
is the Stanley–Reisner ring of
\Delta(G)
and
\dim\Delta(G)=\alpha-1
,
the relationship between the
f
- and
h
-vectors gives

h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha}g_{i}t^{i}(1-t)^{\alpha-i}.

Equivalently, in terms of the independence polynomial,

(1)

h_{R/I(G)}(t)=(1-t)^{\alpha}P_{G}\!\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right).

The degree of the
h
-polynomial for edge ideals is studied in
[
2
]
.
In particular,
[
2
]
gives an explicit formula for
\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)

and characterizes when this degree attains its maximal possible value. More broadly,
the problem of determining when the degree of an
h
-polynomial of a squarefree monomial ideal is maximal
is well understood in the literature; see, for example,
[
15
]
. We recall the relevant results
from
[
2
]
below.

Theorem 2.12

(
[
2
, Theorem 3.2 and Corollary 3.4]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph.
Then

(2)

h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\Bigl(1-\sum_{s=0}^{\alpha-1}D_{s}\Bigr)(1-t)^{\alpha}+\sum_{s=0}^{\alpha-1}D_{s}(1-t)^{\alpha-s-1}

where

D_{s}=\sum_{j=s+1}^{\alpha}(-1)^{j-1-s}\binom{j}{s+1}g_{j}.

Moreover,

•

\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\alpha(G)
if and only if
g(G)\neq 1
;

•

if
g(G)=1
, then
\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\alpha-d-1
, where

d=\min\{s:D_{s}\neq 0\}.

Remark 2.13
.

The proof of
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
extends verbatim to an arbitrary squarefree monomial
ideal after interpreting it as the edge ideal of a hypergraph. In this setting, the relevant graph
invariants are replaced by their hypergraph analogues, which we record in the following notation.

Notation 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V(\mathcal{H}),E(\mathcal{H}))
be a hypergraph on
n
vertices with at least one edge. For each
i\in[n]
, set

g_{i}:=\bigl|\{W\subseteq V(\mathcal{H}):|W|=i\text{ and }W\text{ contains no edge of }\mathcal{H}\}\bigr|,

and define

\alpha:=\max\{i\in[n]:g_{i}\neq 0\},\qquad\delta:=\min\{|E|:E\in E(\mathcal{H})\}.

Let
I(\mathcal{H})
denote the edge ideal of a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
such that

I(\mathcal{H}):=\Bigl(\prod_{x\in E}x:E\in E(\mathcal{H})\Bigr).

By replacing
I(G)
with
I(\mathcal{H})
in
Equation

2
and using
Section

2
, one obtains the expression for the
h
-polynomial of any squarefree monomial ideal.

3.
Degrees of
h
-polynomials for cover ideals

In this section, we give explicit formulas for the
h
-polynomial of cover ideals. In the spirit of

Section

2
, we then extend these formulas to the Alexander dual of an arbitrary squarefree
monomial ideal. Finally, using that the
a
-invariant is preserved under Alexander duality for
squarefree monomial ideals, we relate the degrees of the
h
-polynomials of edge and cover ideals.

We begin by recalling the standard-monomial description of Stanley–Reisner
rings (see
[
10
, Proposition 1.5.1]
).

Proposition 3.1
.

The set of monomials
x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
such that

\supp(a):=\{i:a_{i}\neq 0\}\in\Delta(G)^{\vee}

forms a
k
-basis of
R/I_{\Delta(G)^{\vee}}=R/J(G)
.

Observation 3.2
.

Let
F\subseteq[n]
. Then
F\in\Delta(G)^{\vee}
if and only if
[n]\setminus F\notin\Delta(G)
.
Equivalently,
F\in\Delta(G)^{\vee}
if and only if
[n]\setminus F
is
not
an independent set of
G
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph on
n
vertices. For all
d\geq 1
,

HF_{R/J(G)}(d)=\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}-n\binom{d-1}{n-2}-\binom{d-1}{n-1}-\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}g_{j}\binom{d-1}{n-j-1}.

Proof.

By
Section

3
, a degree-
d
standard monomial is determined by its support

F\in\Delta(G)^{\vee}
together with a choice of positive exponents on the variables
indexed by
F
. If
|F|=i
, then the number of degree-
d
monomials supported
exactly on
F
is
\binom{d-1}{i-1}
(the number of compositions of
d
into
i

positive parts). Therefore,

(3)

HF_{R/J(G)}(d)=\sum_{F\in\Delta(G)^{\vee}}\binom{d-1}{|F|-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i-1}(\Delta(G)^{\vee})\binom{d-1}{i-1},

where
f_{i-1}(\Delta(G)^{\vee})
denotes the number of faces of
\Delta(G)^{\vee}

of cardinality
i
.

To evaluate
f_{i-1}(\Delta(G)^{\vee})
, use
Section

3
: a subset

F\subseteq[n]
with
|F|=i
lies in
\Delta(G)^{\vee}
precisely when its
complement
[n]\setminus F
(of size
n-i
) is not independent. Hence

f_{i-1}(\Delta(G)^{\vee})=\binom{n}{i}-g_{n-i},

and (
3
) becomes

(4)

HF_{R/J(G)}(d)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\binom{d-1}{i-1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{n-i}\binom{d-1}{i-1}.

We now use the binomial identity

(5)

\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\binom{d-1}{i-1},

which counts the number of weak compositions of
d
into
n
parts by grouping
according to the number of nonzero parts.

Finally, note that
g_{n-i}=0
unless
n-i\leq\alpha
, and that the terms
corresponding to
i=0
or
i=n
in (
4
) contribute

g_{n}\binom{d-1}{-1}=0
and
g_{0}\binom{d-1}{n-1}=\binom{d-1}{n-1}
, respectively.
Also the term
i=n-1
contributes
g_{1}\binom{d-1}{n-2}=n\binom{d-1}{n-2}
.
Reindexing the remaining sum via
j=n-i
yields

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{n-i}\binom{d-1}{i-1}=n\binom{d-1}{n-2}+\binom{d-1}{n-1}+\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}g_{j}\binom{d-1}{n-j-1}.

Substituting this and (
5
) into (
4
) gives the stated formula.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

For later use, write
HF_{R/J(G)}(d)=A_{d}-B_{d}
for
d\geq 1
, where

A_{d}:=\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}-n\binom{d-1}{n-2}-\binom{d-1}{n-1},\qquad B_{d}:=\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}g_{j}\binom{d-1}{n-j-1}.

Then the Hilbert series can be written as

H_{R/J(G)}(t)=1+\sum_{d\geq 1}HF_{R/J(G)}(d)t^{d}=\Bigl(1+\sum_{d\geq 1}A_{d}t^{d}\Bigr)-\sum_{d\geq 1}B_{d}t^{d}.

We now provide an expression for the
h
-polynomial of cover ideals.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph on
n
vertices with
\alpha=\alpha(G)
.
Then the
h
-polynomial of
R/J(G)
is

h_{R/J(G)}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-\alpha-1}(k+1)t^{k}+\sum_{s=-1}^{\alpha-3}E_{s+3}t^{n-s-3},

where, for
-1\leq s\leq\alpha-3
,

E_{s+3}=(n-s-2)-\sum_{j=s+3}^{\alpha}\binom{j-2}{s+1}(-1)^{j-s-1}g_{j}.

Proof.

We simplify the two pieces appearing in
Section

3
.

Using the standard generating functions

\sum_{d\geq 0}\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}t^{d}=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n}},\qquad\sum_{d\geq 0}\binom{d}{r}t^{d}=\frac{t^{r}}{(1-t)^{r+1}},

one obtains

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{d\geq 1}A_{d}t^{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 0}\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}t^{d}-n\sum_{d\geq 1}\binom{d-1}{n-2}t^{d}-\sum_{d\geq 1}\binom{d-1}{n-1}t^{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n}}-n\cdot\frac{t^{n-1}}{(1-t)^{n-1}}-\frac{t^{n}}{(1-t)^{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-t^{n}}{(1-t)^{n}}-\frac{nt^{n-1}}{(1-t)^{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+t+\cdots+t^{n-1}-nt^{n-1}}{(1-t)^{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(t^{i}-t^{n-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+2t+3t^{2}+\cdots+(n-1)t^{n-2}}{(1-t)^{n-2}}.

Since
g_{j}=0
for
j>\alpha
, we may sum only up to
\alpha
:

\displaystyle\sum_{d\geq 1}B_{d}t^{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 1}\Bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{\alpha}g_{j}\binom{d-1}{n-j-1}\Bigg)t^{d}=\sum_{j=2}^{\alpha}g_{j}\sum_{d\geq 1}\binom{d-1}{n-j-1}t^{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{\alpha}g_{j}\cdot\frac{t^{n-j}}{(1-t)^{n-j}}.

Factoring out
(1-t)^{-(n-2)}
, expanding
(1-t)^{j-2}
, and switching the order of summation gives

\displaystyle\sum_{d\geq 1}B_{d}t^{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-2}}\sum_{j=2}^{\alpha}g_{j}t^{n-j}(1-t)^{j-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-2}}\sum_{j=2}^{\alpha}g_{j}t^{n-j}\sum_{m=0}^{j-2}\binom{j-2}{m}(-1)^{m}t^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-2}}\sum_{k=n-\alpha}^{n-2}\Bigg(\sum_{j=n-k}^{\alpha}\binom{j-2}{k-n+j}(-1)^{k-n+j}g_{j}\Bigg)t^{k}.

Combining contributions from
A_{d}
and
B_{d}
in
Section

3
yields

\displaystyle H_{R/J(G)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-2}}\Biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(k+1)t^{k}-\sum_{k=n-\alpha}^{n-2}\Bigg(\sum_{j=n-k}^{\alpha}\binom{j-2}{k-n+j}(-1)^{k-n+j}g_{j}\Bigg)t^{k}\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-2}}\Biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{n-\alpha-1}(k+1)t^{k}+\sum_{k=n-\alpha}^{n-2}\Bigg((k+1)-\sum_{j=n-k}^{\alpha}\binom{j-2}{k-n+j}(-1)^{k-n+j}g_{j}\Bigg)t^{k}\Biggr).

Now set
k=n-s-3
. Then
k
ranges from
n-\alpha
to
n-2
exactly when

s
ranges from
\alpha-3
down to
-1
, and the coefficient of
t^{n-s-3}
becomes

(n-s-2)-\sum_{j=s+3}^{\alpha}\binom{j-2}{s+1}(-1)^{j-s-1}g_{j}=:E_{s+3}.

This gives

H_{R/J(G)}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-2}}\Biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{n-\alpha-1}(k+1)t^{k}+\sum_{s=-1}^{\alpha-3}E_{s+3}t^{n-s-3}\Biggr).

Finally, the first sum has degree at most
n-\alpha-1
, while the second sum
has all terms of degree at least
n-\alpha
, so no cancellation occurs in the
numerator. Hence the displayed numerator is the
h
-polynomial of
R/J(G)
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

As in
Section

2
, one can generalize the expression of the
h
-polynomial of cover ideals from
3.5
to Alexander dual of any squarefree monomial ideal (using Notation
2
) as follows:

\displaystyle h_{R/I(\mathcal{H})^{\vee}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-\alpha-1}\binom{k+\delta-1}{\delta-1}t^{k}+\sum_{k=n-\alpha}^{n-\delta}\left(\binom{k+\delta-1}{\delta-1}-\sum_{j=n-k}^{\alpha}\binom{j-\delta}{k-n+j}(-1)^{k-n+j}g_{j}\right)t^{k}

where
\dim R/I(\mathcal{H})^{\vee}=n-\delta
.

As in the case of edge ideals, the invariant
g(G)
governs when
\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)
achieves its maximal possible value
n-2
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph on
n
vertices with independence number
\alpha
.

(a)

n-\alpha-1\leq\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\leq n-2
.

(b)

\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=n-2
if and only if
g(G)\neq 1
.

(c)

If
g(G)=1
, then
E_{2}=0
and:

(i)

if
E_{3}=\cdots=E_{\alpha}=0
, then
\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=n-\alpha-1
;

(ii)

otherwise,

\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=n-d-3,\qquad d=\min\{s:E_{s+3}\neq 0\},

where
0\leq s\leq\alpha-3
.

Proof.

(a) This is immediate from
3.5
.

(b) By
3.5
, we have
\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=n-2
if and only if the
coefficient of
t^{n-2}
is nonzero, i.e.,
E_{2}\neq 0
.
Plugging
s=-1
into the formula for
E_{s+3}
gives

E_{2}=n-1-\sum_{j=2}^{\alpha}(-1)^{j}g_{j}.

Since
g_{1}=n
, this can be rewritten as

E_{2}=-1+\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}(-1)^{j-1}g_{j}=-1+g(G).

Hence
E_{2}\neq 0
if and only if
g(G)\neq 1
.

(c) If
g(G)=1
, then
E_{2}=0
by (b), so
\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)<n-2
. The remaining
statements follow by inspecting the explicit form of
h_{R/J(G)}(t)
in

3.5
.
∎

Part (c)(ii) is analogous to the corresponding statement for edge ideals in

[
2
, Corollary 3.4]
.

We conclude this section by relating the degrees of the
h
-polynomials of edge and cover ideals.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph.
Then there exists an integer
d
with
0\leq d\leq\alpha-1
such that

\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=n-2-d\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\alpha-d.

Proof.

It follows from
[
18
, Theorem 4.36]
that

\mathfrak{a}(R/I(G))=\mathfrak{a}(R/J(G)).

Since
\dim(R/I(G))=\alpha
and
\dim(R/J(G))=n-2
, we have

\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)-\alpha=\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)-(n-2).

Thus, the statement holds.
∎

4.
Degree of the
h
-polynomial via the independence polynomial and
M(G)

A guiding theme of this section and the next is that the degrees of the
h
-polynomials of
R/I(G)
and

R/J(G)
can be studied through the independence polynomial
P_{G}(x)
. We first express the

h
-polynomial of
R/I(G)
in terms of
P_{G}(x)
, and then derive degree formulas for both edge and
cover ideals in terms of the invariant
M(G)
, the multiplicity of
x=-1
as a root of
P_{G}(x)
.

We also clarify the relationship between the present approach and
[
2
]
. In

[
2
]
, the degree
\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)
is governed by the invariant
g(G)
and a
collection of alternating sums
D_{s}
(see
2.12
). We show that these same quantities
are controlled by the behavior of
P_{G}(x)
at
x=-1
: the invariant
g(G)
detects whether
x=-1
is a
root of
P_{G}(x)
, while the first nonzero
D_{s}
determines the multiplicity of that root. Moreover, we
interpret each
D_{s}
as the value of an appropriate derivative of
P_{G}(x)
at
x=-1
.

To make this precise, we adopt the following notation for root multiplicities.

Notation 4.1
.

For a polynomial
f(x)
and a scalar
a
, we write
\ord_{x=a}f(x)
for the multiplicity of
x=a

as a root of
f(x)
.

With this notation in place, we record the multiplicity of the distinguished root
x=-1
as a graph invariant.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with independence polynomial
P_{G}(x)
. Define

M(G):=\ord_{x=-1}P_{G}(x),

the multiplicity of
-1
as a root of
P_{G}(x)
.

Since
M(G)
is the multiplicity of a root of
P_{G}(x)
, we have

M(G)\leq\deg P_{G}(x)=\alpha
. If
G
has at least one edge, this bound can be improved.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
G
has at least one edge, then

M(G)\ \leq\ \alpha-1.

Proof.

Assume
G
has at least one edge. If
M(G)\geq\alpha
, then
(1+x)^{\alpha}
divides
P_{G}(x)
.
Since
\deg P_{G}(x)=\alpha
, we get
P_{G}(x)=c(1+x)^{\alpha}
for some constant
c\in\Bbbk
.
Because
P_{G}(0)=1
, we must have
c=1
, so
P_{G}(x)=(1+x)^{\alpha}
.
Comparing the coefficients of
x
gives
n=\alpha
, which forces
G
to have no edges, a contradiction.
∎

We now state the main result of this section, which relates
M(G)
to the degrees of the

h
-polynomials of
R/I(G)
and
R/J(G)
.

Theorem 4.4
.

We have

\displaystyle\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha-M(G),

\displaystyle\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)

\displaystyle=n-2-M(G).

In particular,
\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\alpha
if and only if
P_{G}(-1)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Setting
u=\frac{1}{1-t}
in (
1
) yields

h_{R/I(G)}(t)=(1-t)^{\alpha}P_{G}(u-1)=u^{-\alpha}P_{G}(u-1).

Let
M=M(G)
. So we may write

P_{G}(x)=(x+1)^{M}Q(x)

with
Q(-1)\neq 0
. Substituting
x=u-1
gives

h_{R/I(G)}(t)=u^{M-\alpha}Q(u-1).

Note that
Q(u-1)
is a polynomial in
u
of degree
\deg Q=\alpha-M
which has nonzero constant term

Q(-1)
. Furthermore, the term
Q(-1)u^{M-\alpha}=Q(-1)(1-t)^{\alpha-M}
is the largest degree term of
h_{R/I(G)}(t)
as a polynomial in
(1-t)
, and therefore also as a polynomial in
t
. Hence
\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\alpha-M
.

Lastly, the expression for the degree of the
h
-polynomial of
R/J(G)
follows from
3.8
.
∎

Using
4.4
, we can translate the degree formulas for the
h
-polynomials into a relationship between
M(G)
and the
\mathfrak{a}
-invariants of
R/I(G)
and
R/J(G)
.

Remark 4.5
.

Since
\mathfrak{a}
-invariants of
R/I(G)
and
R/J(G)
coincide, it follows from
4.4
that

\mathfrak{a}(R/J(G))=\mathfrak{a}(R/I(G))=-M(G).

Recall from
2.12
that
\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)
can be read off from the first nonzero
D_{s}
, for
s\geq 0
, where

(6)

D_{s}=\sum_{j=s+1}^{\alpha}(-1)^{j-1-s}\binom{j}{s+1}g_{j}.

On the other hand,
4.4
shows that the same
degree is determined by the invariant
M(G)
. The next lemma makes the link between these two
descriptions explicit by expressing each
D_{s}
in terms of the derivatives of the independence polynomial
evaluated at
x=-1
, thereby connecting the
M(G)
approach directly to
[
2
]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For
s\geq 0
, we have

D_{s}=\frac{1}{(s+1)!}P_{G}^{(s+1)}(-1).

Proof.

Differentiate the independence polynomial
(s+1)
times:

P_{G}^{(s+1)}(x)=\sum_{j=s+1}^{\alpha}g_{j}j(j-1)\cdots(j-s)x^{j-(s+1)}=(s+1)!\sum_{j=s+1}^{\alpha}g_{j}\binom{j}{s+1}x^{j-(s+1)}.

Evaluating at
x=-1
yields

\frac{1}{(s+1)!}P_{G}^{(s+1)}(-1)=\sum_{j=s+1}^{\alpha}(-1)^{j-1-s}\binom{j}{s+1}g_{j}=D_{s}.

∎

Thus the sequence
\{D_{s}\}_{s\geq 0}
encodes the order of vanishing of
P_{G}(x)
at
x=-1
, and hence the invariant
M(G)
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
M=M(G)
. Then

P_{G}(-1)=P_{G}^{\prime}(-1)=\cdots=P_{G}^{(M-1)}(-1)=0,\quad P_{G}^{(M)}(-1)\neq 0,

equivalently,

D_{0}=D_{1}=\cdots=D_{M-2}=0,\quad D_{M-1}\neq 0.

In particular, the first nonzero
D_{s}
records the order of vanishing of
P_{G}
at
-1
.

The following result recovers the expression of the
h
-polynomial of edge ideals from
[
2
]
.

Proposition 4.8
.

We have

h_{R/I(G)}(t)=P_{G}(-1)(1-t)^{\alpha}+\sum_{s=0}^{\alpha-1}D_{s}(1-t)^{\alpha-s-1}.

Proof.

As in the proof of
4.4
, let
u=\frac{1}{1-t}
so that

h_{R/I(G)}(t)=u^{-\alpha}P_{G}(u-1)
. Expand
P_{G}
at
-1
:

P_{G}(u-1)=\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha}\frac{P_{G}^{(k)}(-1)}{k!}u^{k}.

Hence

h_{R/I(G)}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha}\frac{P_{G}^{(k)}(-1)}{k!}u^{k-\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha}\frac{P_{G}^{(k)}(-1)}{k!}(1-t)^{\alpha-k}.

The stated formula follows from
Section

4
(with
k=s+1
).
∎

Remark 4.9
.

4.4
is also immediate from
Section

4
:
the first nonzero derivative at
-1
occurs at order
M(G)
. So, the largest power of
t

comes from the term
(1-t)^{\alpha-M(G)}
.

Since
M(G)
controls
\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t)
by
4.4
, it is useful to understand how

M(G)
changes under basic graph operations.

Question 1
.

Under which graph operations does the multiplicity
M(G)
behave in a predictable way?

As a first test case, we examine induced subgraphs; the next example shows that
M(G)
need not vary monotonically.

Example 4.10
.

Let
P_{n}
denote the path on
n
vertices. For
n=2,3,4,5
the independence polynomials are

\displaystyle P_{P_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=1+2x,

\displaystyle P_{P_{3}}(x)

\displaystyle=1+3x+x^{2},

\displaystyle P_{P_{4}}(x)

\displaystyle=1+4x+3x^{2},

\displaystyle P_{P_{5}}(x)

\displaystyle=1+5x+6x^{2}+x^{3}.

Evaluating at
x=-1
gives

P_{P_{2}}(-1)=-1,\qquad P_{P_{3}}(-1)=-1,\qquad P_{P_{4}}(-1)=0,\qquad P_{P_{5}}(-1)=1.

Hence
M(P_{4})=1
, while
M(P_{2})=M(P_{3})=M(P_{5})=0
.
In particular, although
P_{4}
is an induced subgraph of
P_{5}
, we have
M(P_{4})>M(P_{5})
; and although
P_{3}
is an induced subgraph of
P_{4}
, we have
M(P_{3})<M(P_{4})
.

Thus
M(G)
can increase, decrease, or remain unchanged when passing to induced subgraphs. This motivates a search for graph operations under which
M(G)
behaves more systematically,
and we hope it prompts further study of
M(G)
in its own right.

5.
Trees of radius at most two:
(\reg,\deg h)
for cover ideals

In this section we study trees of radius at most
2
. Throughout, let
T
be a tree of radius at most 2 and
R_{T}=\Bbbk[x_{v}:v\in V(T)]
. We first compute
\pdim(R_{T}/I(T))
, and hence
\reg(R_{T}/J(T))
by Alexander duality. Then, we determine the degree of the
h
-polynomial of
R_{T}/J(T)
via the multiplicity

M(T)
from
Section

4
.

We begin by setting our notation.

Notation 5.1
.

Let
T
be a tree of radius at most
2
. In other words, there is a vertex
c
such that

\dist_{T}(c,v)\leq 2
for all
v\in V(T)
. Fix such a vertex
c
and set
B=\deg_{T}(c)
.
Write the neighbor set of
c
as a disjoint union

N_{T}(c)=\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{L_{1}}\}\sqcup\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\},

where
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{L_{1}}
are leaves adjacent to
c
, and each
v_{i}
has
at least one leaf away from
c
(equivalently,
\deg_{T}(v_{i})\geq 2
). Thus
m=B-L_{1}
.
For each
i
, set

t_{i}=\bigl|N_{T}(v_{i})\setminus\{c\}\bigr|\ \geq 1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad L_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}.

Then
L_{2}
is the number of vertices at distance
2
from
c
(all of which are leaves).
Note that
T
has radius
2
if and only if
L_{2}>0
(equivalently
m>0
), and radius
1

if and only if
L_{2}=0
(so
T\cong K_{1,B}
).

Using this notation, we have
|V(T)|=n=1+B+L_{2}
. Set
p(T)\ :=\ \pdim(R_{T}/I(T))
.

with parameters as in

.

5.1.
Projective dimension and regularity

The following recursive result of Jacques and Katzman is our main tool.

Theorem 5.2
.

[
16
, Theorem 4.8]

Let
F
be a forest containing a vertex of degree at least two. Then there exists a vertex

v
with neighbors

N_{F}(v)=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\},\qquad n\geq 2,

such that
v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}
are leaves. Define

F^{\prime}=F\setminus\{v_{1}\},\qquad F^{\prime\prime}=F\setminus\{v,v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}.

Then

\pdim(R_{F}/I(F))=\max\bigl\{\pdim(R_{F^{\prime}}/I(F^{\prime})),\ \pdim(R_{F^{\prime\prime}}/I(F^{\prime\prime}))+n\bigr\},

where
R_{H}=\Bbbk[x_{u}:u\in V(H)]
for any forest
H
.

Remark 5.3
.

The forest
F^{\prime\prime}
in
5.2
may contain isolated vertices. These do not affect
projective dimension; they contribute extra variables but no generators to the edge ideal.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
T
be as in
Section

5
. Then

p(T)=\max\{B,\ L_{2}+1\}.

In particular,
\reg(R_{T}/J(T))=\max\{B-1,\ L_{2}\}
.

Proof.

We induct on
L_{2}\geq 0
.

If
L_{2}=0
, then
T\cong K_{1,B}
(a star) and
p(T)=B
. So, the formula holds for the base case.

Assume
L_{2}\geq 1
and the formula holds for all trees of radius at most
2
with strictly fewer
distance–
2
leaves. Choose an index
i
. Then
v_{i}
has neighbors

N_{T}(v_{i})=\{c,w_{i1},\dots,w_{it_{i}}\},

where
w_{i1},\dots,w_{it_{i}}
are leaves and
\deg_{T}(v_{i})=t_{i}+1
.
Apply
5.2
with
v=v_{i}
,
v_{1}=w_{i1}
, and the remaining neighbors

v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
taken to be
w_{i2},\dots,w_{it_{i}},c
(so
n=t_{i}+1
). Then

T^{\prime}=T\setminus\{w_{i1}\},\qquad T^{\prime\prime}=T\setminus\{v_{i},c,w_{i1},\dots,w_{it_{i}}\}.

In
T^{\prime}
, the vertex
c
is still a center of degree
B
and
L_{2}
drops by
1
. Hence, by induction,

p(T^{\prime})=\max\{B,(L_{2}-1)+1\}=\max\{B,L_{2}\}.

In
T^{\prime\prime}
, removing
c
makes each
\ell_{j}
isolated, and for each
j\neq i
the vertex
v_{j}
becomes the
center of a star
K_{1,t_{j}}
. Thus
T^{\prime\prime}
is a disjoint union of stars together with isolated vertices, and
by the additivity of projective dimension under disjoint union (and
Section

5.1
),

p(T^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{j\neq i}p(K_{1,t_{j}})=\sum_{j\neq i}t_{j}=L_{2}-t_{i}.

Therefore
5.2
yields

\displaystyle p(T)

\displaystyle=\max\{p(T^{\prime}),\ p(T^{\prime\prime})+\deg_{T}(v_{i})\}

\displaystyle=\max\bigl\{\max\{B,L_{2}\},\ (L_{2}-t_{i})+(t_{i}+1)\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\max\{B,L_{2}+1\},

as claimed.

The regularity statement follows from
5.4
.
∎

5.2.
Degree of the
h
-polynomial

We next compute
P_{T}(x)
and
M(T)
, and then apply

4.4
to obtain the degree.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
T
be as in
Section

5
. Suppose
L_{2}>0
. Then

P_{T}(x)=(1+x)^{L_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Bigl(x+(1+x)^{t_{i}}\Bigr)+x(1+x)^{L_{2}}.

Proof.

We count independent sets in two disjoint cases.

Suppose
c
is not chosen.
We may choose any subset of the
L_{1}
leaves
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{L_{1}}
, contributing
(1+x)^{L_{1}}
.
Independently, for each branch
v_{i}
we either choose
v_{i}
(contributing
x
) or do not choose
v_{i}

and then choose any subset of its
t_{i}
leaves (contributing
(1+x)^{t_{i}}
). Thus branch
i
contributes

x+(1+x)^{t_{i}}
. Multiplying gives

(1+x)^{L_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(x+(1+x)^{t_{i}}\bigr).

Suppose
c
is chosen.
This contributes
x
, forces all neighbors of
c
to be absent, and allows any subset of the
L_{2}

distance–
2
leaves, contributing
(1+x)^{L_{2}}
. Thus this case contributes
x(1+x)^{L_{2}}
.

Adding the two cases yields the stated formula.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Let
T
be as in
Section

5
. Then

\alpha(T)=\begin{cases}B,&\text{if }L_{2}=0,\\[2.0pt]
L_{2}+1,&\text{if }L_{2}>0\text{ and }L_{1}=0,\\[2.0pt]
L_{1}+L_{2},&\text{if }L_{2}>0\text{ and }L_{1}>0.\end{cases}

One can express
M(T)
using the parameters of
T
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
T
be as in
Section

5
. Then the multiplicity
M(T)
is

M(T)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }L_{2}=0,\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{if }L_{2}>0\text{ and }L_{1}=0,\\[2.0pt]
\min\{L_{1},L_{2}\},&\text{if }L_{1}>0\text{ and }L_{1}\neq L_{2},\\[2.0pt]
L_{1},&\text{if }L_{1}=L_{2}\text{ and }m\text{ is odd},\\[2.0pt]
L_{1}+1,&\text{if }L_{1}=L_{2}\text{ and }m\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
L_{2}=0
, then
T\cong K_{1,B}
and
P_{T}(x)=(1+x)^{B}+x
. Hence
P_{T}(-1)=-1\neq 0
and
M(T)=0
.

Assume
L_{2}>0
. If
L_{1}=0
, then
Section

5.2
gives

P_{T}(-1)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(-1+(1-1)^{t_{i}}\bigr)=(-1)^{m}\neq 0,

so again
M(T)=0
.

Now assume
L_{1}>0
and set
L=\min\{L_{1},L_{2}\}
. By
Section

5.2
,

P_{T}(x)=(1+x)^{L}Q(x),

where

Q(x)=(1+x)^{L_{1}-L}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(x+(1+x)^{t_{i}}\bigr)+x(1+x)^{L_{2}-L}.

If
L_{1}\neq L_{2}
, then
Q(-1)\neq 0
:

•

If
L_{1}<L_{2}
, then
Q(-1)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(-1)+(-1)\cdot 0=(-1)^{m}\neq 0
.

•

If
L_{2}<L_{1}
, then
Q(-1)=0\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}(-1)+(-1)=-1\neq 0
.

Thus
M(T)=L=\min\{L_{1},L_{2}\}
.

Finally, assume
L_{1}=L_{2}=U
. Then

P_{T}(x)=(1+x)^{U}Q(x)\qquad\text{with}\qquad Q(x)=\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(x)\Big)+x,\quad f_{i}(x)=x+(1+x)^{t_{i}}.

Since
f_{i}(-1)=-1
for all
i
, we have

Q(-1)=(-1)^{m}-1=\begin{cases}-2,&\text{if }m\text{ odd},\\
0,&\text{if }m\text{ even}.\end{cases}

If
m
is odd then
Q(-1)\neq 0
and
M(T)=U
.

If
m
is even, then
Q(-1)=0
. Differentiating,

Q^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\prime}(x)\prod_{j\neq i}f_{j}(x)+1,

so using
f_{j}(-1)=-1
gives

Q^{\prime}(-1)=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\prime}(-1)+1.

Moreover
f_{i}^{\prime}(x)=1+t_{i}(1+x)^{t_{i}-1}
. Hence
f_{i}^{\prime}(-1)\in\{1,2\}
and in particular

\sum_{i}f_{i}^{\prime}(-1)\geq m
. Since
m
is even,
(-1)^{m-1}=-1
, and therefore

Q^{\prime}(-1)=1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\prime}(-1)\leq 1-m\neq 0.

Thus
-1
is a simple root of
Q
. So
M(T)=U+1=L_{1}+1
.
∎

Now, we are ready to express the degree of the
h
-polynomial of cover ideals.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let

6.
A Jacques–Katzman type recursion for
M(G)
on forests

In this section, we develop an analogue of Jacques–Katzman’s recursion

[
16
, Theorem 4.8]
to compute the degree of the
h
-polynomial
associated to the edge (and cover) ideal of a forest. It follows from
4.4
that

\deg h_{R_{F}/I(F)}(t)=\alpha(F)-M(F).

Thus it suffices to compute
\alpha(F)
and
M(F)
recursively.

Notation 6.1
.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph with independence polynomial
P_{G}(x)
.
Set
u=x+1
and consider
P_{G}(u-1)\in\Bbbk[u]
. Then
M(G)=\ord_{u=0}P_{G}(u-1)
.

We also record the first nonzero Taylor coefficient at
u=0
by

c(G):=[u^{M(G)}]P_{G}(u-1)\in\Bbbk^{\times},

so that

P_{G}(u-1)=c(G)u^{M(G)}+\text{(terms of degree $>M(G)$)}.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
A(u),B(u)\in\Bbbk[u]
be nonzero and set
\nu(A)=\ord_{u=0}A(u)
.
Then

\nu(A+B)\geq\min\{\nu(A),\nu(B)\},

with equality unless
\nu(A)=\nu(B)
and the leading coefficients cancel.
More precisely, if
\nu(A)=a
and
\nu(B)=b
, then:

(1)

if
a<b
, then
\nu(A+B)=a
and
[u^{a}](A+B)=[u^{a}]A
;

(2)

if
b<a
, then
\nu(A+B)=b
and
[u^{b}](A+B)=[u^{b}]B
;

(3)

if
a=b
, then
\nu(A+B)\geq a
and
[u^{a}](A+B)=[u^{a}]A+[u^{a}]B
.

Proof.

Write
A(u)=a_{0}u^{a}+\text{(terms of degree $>a$)}
and

B(u)=b_{0}u^{b}+\text{(terms of degree $>b$)}
with
a_{0},b_{0}\neq 0
.
If
a<b
(resp.
b<a
), then the term
a_{0}u^{a}
(resp.
b_{0}u^{b}
) cannot be cancelled by any term
of the other polynomial, so
\nu(A+B)=\min\{a,b\}
and the leading coefficient is inherited from the
smaller-order summand. If
a=b
, then the coefficient of
u^{a}
in
A+B
is
a_{0}+b_{0}
, which is nonzero
exactly when no cancellation occurs.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

If
H=G\sqcup K_{1}
is obtained from
G
by adding an isolated vertex, then

P_{H}(x)=(1+x)P_{G}(x)
. Hence
M(H)=M(G)+1
and
\alpha(H)=\alpha(G)+1
, so

\alpha(H)-M(H)=\alpha(G)-M(G),

and consequently
\deg h_{S_{H}/I(H)}(t)=\deg h_{S_{G}/I(G)}(t)
.
In particular, isolated vertices may be ignored when computing
\deg h
for forests.

We follow the general setup of Jacques and Katzman

, modifying it slightly to suit our purposes.

Notation 6.4

7.
Whiskering and degree formulas

In this section we record formulas for the degree of the
h
-polynomial of edge and cover ideals under whiskering operations. We treat two versions: uniform
q
-whiskering, where
q
pendant vertices are attached to every vertex of
G
, and whiskering at a single vertex. The uniform construction yields a closed expression in terms of
\alpha(G)
, whereas the one-vertex version is described in terms of the corresponding invariants of
G
and
G-v
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices, and fix an integer
q\geq 1
.
Let
G_{q}
be the graph obtained from
G
by attaching
q
whiskers to each
vertex of
G
and
R_{q}=\Bbbk[v:v\in G_{q}]
. Then

\deg h_{R_{q}/I(G_{q})}(t)=q\alpha(G)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\deg h_{R_{q}/J(G_{q})}(t)=n-2+q\alpha(G).

Proof.

Recall from
3.8
and
4.4
that, for any graph
H
, one has

\deg h_{R/I(H)}(t)=\alpha(H)-M(H)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\deg h_{R/J(H)}(t)=|V(H)|-2-M(H).

So, it suffices find
M(G_{q})
. For this purpose, we focus on the independence polynomial of
G_{q}
.

Observe that
G_{q}
is the corona product of
G
and
qK_{1}
, i.e.,
G_{q}=G\circ qK_{1}
. Set
H=qK_{1}
, so
P_{H}(x)=(1+x)^{q}
. Then by the following corona identity from
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]

P_{G\circ H}(x)=\bigl(P_{H}(x)\bigr)^{n}P_{G}\!\left(\frac{x}{P_{H}(x)}\right),

we have

P_{G_{q}}(x)=(1+x)^{qn}P_{G}\!\left(\frac{x}{(1+x)^{q}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha(G)}g_{k}x^{k}(1+x)^{q(n-k)}.

Let
\alpha=\alpha(G)
and factor the independence polynomial as follows:

P_{G_{q}}(x)=(1+x)^{q(n-\alpha)}\Bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha}g_{k}x^{k}(1+x)^{q(\alpha-k)}\Bigg).

Evaluating the remaining factor at
x=-1
, notice that every term with
k<\alpha

vanishes, leaving only
g_{\alpha}(-1)^{\alpha}\neq 0
.
Thus
M(G_{q})=q(n-\alpha(G))
.

Since
\alpha(G_{q})=qn
, we have

\displaystyle\deg h_{R_{q}/I(G_{q})}(t)

\displaystyle=qn-q(n-\alpha(G))=q\alpha(G),

\displaystyle\deg h_{R_{q}/J(G_{q})}(t)

\displaystyle=(q+1)n-2-q(n-\alpha(G))=n-2+q\alpha(G),

as claimed.
∎

Whiskering one vertex still yields a useful expression for
\deg h
, but it depends on
G
and
G\setminus v
with
v
the whiskered vertex.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and fix a vertex
v\in V(G)
.
Let
G^{v}
be the graph obtained from
G
by whiskering
v
. Set
a=M(G)
and
b=M(G-v)
.
Then

M(G^{v})=\min\{a,b\}\quad\text{if }a\neq b,

and if
a=b
then
M(G^{v})\geq a
, with equality unless a cancellation occurs.

Moreover,
\alpha(G^{v})=\max\{\alpha(G),1+\alpha(G-v)\}
.

Proof.

Let
v^{\prime}
be the new vertex where
\{v,v^{\prime}\}\in E(G^{v})
.

If we consider a maximum independent set of
G^{v}
, we can take
v^{\prime}
plus any independent set of
G-v
,
giving maximum size
1+\alpha(G-v)
. If we do not include
v^{\prime}
, we can take a maximum
independent set of
G
, giving size
\alpha(G)
. Hence, we have
\alpha(G^{v})=\max\{\alpha(G),1+\alpha(G-v)\}
.

Any independent set in
G^{v}
either does not use the new leaf
v^{\prime}

(contributing
P_{G}(x)
), or it uses
v^{\prime}
, in which case
the remaining vertices form an independent set in

, contributing

xP_{G-v}(x)
. Summing the two results yields

P_{G^{v}}(x)=P_{G}(x)+xP_{G-v}(x).

Since
a=M(G)
and
b=M(G-v)
, one has

P_{G}(x)=(1+x)^{a}A(x),\qquad P_{G-v}(x)=(1+x)^{b}B(x),

where
A(-1)\neq 0
and
B(-1)\neq 0
.
Then

P_{G^{v}}(x)=(1+x)^{a}A(x)+x(1+x)^{b}B(x).

If
a<b
, factoring out
(1+x)^{a}

P_{G^{v}}(x)=(1+x)^{a}\bigl(A(x)+x(1+x)^{b-a}B(x)\bigr),

and evaluating the second factor at

kills the second term, leaving
A(-1)\neq 0
. Thus
M(G^{v})=a
.
The case
b<a
is symmetric and gives
M(G^{v})=b
. So, if
a\neq b
,

M(G^{v})=\min\{a,b\}
.

If
a=b
, then

P_{G^{v}}(x)=(1+x)^{a}\bigl(A(x)+xB(x)\bigr).

So, we have
M(G^{v})\geq a
. Notice that
M(G^{v})=a
unless
A(-1)=B(-1)
, in which case the multiplicity is larger.
∎

Corollary 7.3

Let
G
be a graph on
n

Let

8.
Split graphs and diagonal
(\reg,\deg h)
behavior

Split graphs form a well-behaved chordal class for which the independence polynomial admits an explicit description. In this section we exploit a partition of split graphs to compute
P_{G}(x)
. Hence, we obtain closed formulas for the invariants
\alpha(G)
and
M(G)
that control the degrees of the
h
-polynomials of
R/I(G)
and
R/J(G)
. As a consequence, cover ideals exhibit particularly rigid behavior in this class: for connected split graphs the pair
\bigl(\reg(R/J(G)),\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\bigr)
always lies on the diagonal, and we determine exactly which diagonal values occur for fixed
n=|V(G)|
.

Definition 8.1
.

A graph
G
is a
split graph
if its vertex set admits a partition

V(G)=C\sqcup I

such that
C
induces a clique and
I
induces an independent set.
Fix such a partition for the statements below.
Throughout, we assume
G
is connected and has at least one edge.

Remark 8.2
.

It was shown in
[
9
]
that split graphs are exactly
(2K_{2},C_{4},C_{5})
-free. Notice that
2K_{2}
-free is equivalent to gap-free.

We adopt the following notation throughout this section.

Notation 8.3
.

Let
G
be a split graph. Fix a split partition
V(G)=C\sqcup I
and set
m:=|I|
.
For each
c\in C
, define

\displaystyle a(c)

\displaystyle:=\bigl|I\setminus N_{G}(c)\bigr|,

\displaystyle\deg_{I}(c)

\displaystyle:=\bigl|N_{G}(c)\cap I\bigr|.

Thus
m=a(c)+\deg_{I}(c)
for each
c\in C
.
Also set

\displaystyle\Delta_{I}

\displaystyle:=\max\{\deg_{I}(c):c\in C\},

\displaystyle\delta_{I}

\displaystyle:=\min\{\deg_{I}(c):c\in C\},

\displaystyle a_{\min}

\displaystyle:=\min\{a(c):c\in C\},

\displaystyle a_{\max}

\displaystyle:=\max\{a(c):c\in C\}.

Notice that

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
G
be a split graph as in Notation

.
Then its independence polynomial is

P_{G}(x)=(1+x)^{m}+x\sum_{c\in C}(1+x)^{a(c)}.

Proof.

Every independent set is of exactly one of the following types:

•

a subset of
I
, contributing
(1+x)^{m}
, or

•

it contains a vertex

. Since

is a clique, it contains exactly one

.
This contributes

for each

.

Summing these disjoint contributions gives the formula for the independence polynomial.
∎

Corollary 8.5
.

With notation as above,

\alpha(G)=\max\Bigl(m,\ 1+a_{\max}\Bigr)=\max\Bigl(m,\ 1+m-\delta_{I}\Bigr).

Proof.

An independent set either lies in
I
(giving size at most
m
), or it contains a vertex

, in which case it contains exactly one vertex of

and then can include at most

, giving size at most

. Taking the maximum over
c\in C

yields the desired formula.
∎

Proposition 8.6

Let

be a split graph as in Notation

Proof.

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{p}\underbrace{\Bigl(2x+x(1+x)^{p+k-3}+(1+x)^{p+k-2}\Bigr)}_{Q(x)}.

Observe that
Q(-1)=-2
if
p+k-3\geq 1
(i.e.
p\geq 4
or
k\geq 1
),
and
Q(-1)=-3
in the remaining case
(p,k)=(3,0)
. In all cases
Q(-1)\neq 0
.
Hence
M(H_{n,p})=p
.
∎

Remark 10.5
.

Corollary 10.6
.

Fix an integer

\Bigl\{(r,r-1):\ \left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\leq r\leq n-3\Bigr\}.

By Lemmas

11.
Block graphs lie on or above the line
\deg h=\reg-1

In this section we show that block graphs exhibit a strong restriction in the
(\reg,\deg h)
-plane for cover ideals: if
G
is a connected block graph and we set

r:=\reg(R/J(G))\qquad\text{and}\qquad d:=\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t),

then necessarily
d\geq r-1
. Equivalently, block graphs cannot contribute points strictly below the line
d=r-1
.
Since block graphs are chordal, one has
\reg(R/J(G))=n-i(G)-1
, while

\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=n-2-M(G).

So the inequality
d\geq r-1
is precisely the statement
M(G)\leq i(G)
.
We prove this by exploiting the clique-tree structure of block graphs: removing a leaf maximal clique yields a recursion for the independence polynomial, and comparing the resulting orders at
x=-1
with a parallel recursion for the independent domination number gives
M(G)\leq i(G)
by induction on the number of maximal cliques.

Definition 11.1
.

A graph
G
is a
block graph
if every biconnected component (block) of
G

is a clique. Equivalently,
G
is chordal and any two distinct maximal cliques intersect
in at most one vertex.

Definition 11.2
.

Let
G
be a chordal graph. A
clique tree
of
G
is a tree
T
whose vertices are the maximal
cliques of
G
and such that for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, the set of maximal cliques containing
v

forms a connected subtree of
T
.

If
G
is a connected block graph, then
G
admits a clique tree
T
.
Moreover, if two maximal cliques
K
and
K^{\prime}
are adjacent in
T
, then

K\cap K^{\prime}
consists of a single vertex
s
, and this vertex is a cut vertex of
G
, i.e.
G\setminus\{s\}
is disconnected.
A maximal clique corresponding to a leaf of
T
is called a
leaf maximal clique
.

Notation 11.3
.

Let
G
be a connected block graph that is not a clique, and fix a clique tree of
G
.
Let
K
be a leaf maximal clique, and let
K^{\prime}
be its unique neighbor in the clique tree. Set

s:=K\cap K^{\prime},\qquad C:=K\setminus\{s\},\qquad r:=|C|\geq 1,

and define the induced subgraphs

H:=G\setminus K,\qquad L:=G\setminus N_{G}[s].

The following leaf-clique recursion is used in the proof of
11.5
.

Lemma 11.4
.

Let
G
be a connected block graph and use Notation
11
.
Then

P_{G}(x)\ =\ (1+rx)P_{H}(x)\ +\ xP_{L}(x).

Proof.

We partition the independent sets of
G
into three disjoint classes according to how they meet
K
.

(1)

Independent sets avoiding
K
.

These are exactly the independent sets of the induced subgraph
H=G\setminus K
, contributing
P_{H}(x)
.

(2)

Independent sets meeting
C
.

Since
K
is a clique, an independent set can contain
at most one
vertex of
K
. Hence it contains
at most one vertex of
C
and cannot contain
s
.
Moreover, because
K
is a leaf in the clique tree, every vertex
v\in C
belongs to no maximal clique
other than
K
. In particular,
v
has no neighbors outside
K
. Therefore, after choosing
v\in C
,
the rest of the independent set can be
any
independent set of
H
.
Thus this class contributes

\sum_{v\in C}xP_{H}(x)\ =\ rxP_{H}(x).

(3)

Independent sets containing
s
.

If an independent set contains
s
, then it avoids
N_{G}[s]
, and its remaining vertices form an
independent set of
L=G\setminus N_{G}[s]
. This contributes
xP_{L}(x)
.

Adding the contributions from (1)–(3) gives

P_{G}(x)\ =\ P_{H}(x)\ +\ rxP_{H}(x)\ +\ xP_{L}(x)\ =\ (1+rx)P_{H}(x)+xP_{L}(x),

as claimed.
∎

Now we are for the main result of this section.

Theorem 11.5
.

Let
G
be a connected block graph. Then

M(G)\ \leq\ i(G).

Consequently,

\reg(R/J(G))-\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=M(G)-i(G)+1\leq 1.

Proof.

We induct on the number of maximal cliques of
G
.

If
G
is a clique, then
P_{G}(x)=1+|V(G)|x
. So
M(G)=0
while
i(G)=1
. Hence
M(G)\leq i(G)
.

Assume now that
G
is not a clique. Fix a leaf maximal clique
K
in the clique tree as in Notation
11
where
K^{\prime}
be
its unique neighbor, and

s:=K\cap K^{\prime},\qquad C:=K\setminus\{s\},\qquad r:=|C|\geq 1,

together with the induced subgraphs

H:=G\setminus K,\qquad L:=G\setminus N_{G}[s].

Since
K
is a clique, any independent dominating set
D
meets
K
in at most one vertex.
Moreover
D
must dominate every vertex of
C
, and the only vertices that can dominate
C
lie in
K
.
Thus
D
contains either (a) a vertex
v\in C
, or (b) the cut vertex
s
.

If
v\in C
is chosen, then
v
has no neighbors outside
K
. So
D\setminus\{v\}
is an independent
dominating set of
H
. Hence
|D|\geq 1+i(H)
.
If
s
is chosen, then no vertex of
N_{G}[s]
may lie in
D
, and
D\setminus\{s\}
is an independent
dominating set of
L
. Hence
|D|\geq 1+i(L)
.
Conversely, choosing any
v\in C
together with an independent dominating set of
H
gives an
independent dominating set of
G
. Similarly, choosing
s
together with an independent dominating set of
L

also gives one. Therefore

(10)

i(G)=1+\min\{i(H),i(L)\}.

Set
u:=x+1
and
Q_{F}(u):=P_{F}(u-1)
, so that
M(F)=\ord_{u=0}Q_{F}(u)
.
The leaf-clique recursion from Lemma
11
gives

P_{G}(x)=(1+rx)P_{H}(x)+xP_{L}(x),

and substituting
x=u-1
yields

(11)

Q_{G}(u)=\bigl(ru-(r-1)\bigr)Q_{H}(u)+(u-1)Q_{L}(u).

Let
m:=\min\{M(H),M(L)\}
. Write
Q_{H}(u)=u^{m}h(u)
and
Q_{L}(u)=u^{m}\ell(u)
with
h,\ell\in\Bbbk[u]
.
Then (
11
) becomes

Q_{G}(u)=u^{m}\Bigl(A(u)+uB(u)\Bigr),

where
A(u)=-(r-1)h(u)-\ell(u)
and
B(u)=rh(u)+\ell(u)
.
Hence

M(G)=m+\ord_{u=0}\bigl(A(u)+uB(u)\bigr).

If
A(0)\neq 0
, then
\ord_{u=0}(A+uB)=0
, so
M(G)=m
. If
A(0)=0
, then
\ell(0)=-(r-1)h(0)
, and consequently

B(0)=rh(0)+\ell(0)=rh(0)-(r-1)h(0)=h(0)\neq 0.

Indeed, if
h(0)=0
, then
\ell(0)=0
as well. So both
Q_{H}(u)=u^{m}h(u)
and

Q_{L}(u)=u^{m}\ell(u)
are divisible by
u^{m+1}
, which implies

M(H),M(L)\geq m+1
, contradicting
m=\min\{M(H),M(L)\}
.
Hence
\ord_{u=0}(A(u)+uB(u))=1
in this case. Therefore,

(12)

M(G)\ \leq\ m+1\ =\ 1+\min\{M(H),M(L)\}.

By the induction hypothesis (applied componentwise if
H
or
L
is disconnected),
we have
M(H)\leq i(H)
and
M(L)\leq i(L)
. Combining (
10
) and (
12
),

M(G)\ \leq\ 1+\min\{M(H),M(L)\}\ \leq\ 1+\min\{i(H),i(L)\}\ =\ i(G),

which proves the first claim.

Hence

\reg(R/J(G))-\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=M(G)-i(G)+1\leq 1.\qed

Remark 12.1

Let

n

\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\leq n-2

Moreover,
\reg(R/J(G))=\pdim(R/I(G))-1

\pdim(R/I(G))\leq n-1

\reg(R/J(G))\leq n-2

Notice that both bounds are sharp.

To relate
\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)
to combinatorial data, recall from

(13)

M(G)=(n-2)-\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=\alpha(G)-\deg h_{R/I(G)}(t).

Corollary 12.2
.

If
\alpha(G)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor+1

(a)

\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\ \geq\ \left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-2
,

(b)

\reg(R/J(G))\ \geq\ \left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-2

Proof.

(a) By Lemma

M(G)\ \leq\ \alpha(G)-1\ \leq\ \left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor.

Using

\deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)=n-2-M(G)\ \geq\ n-2-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-2.

(b) For the regularity bound, note that
\reg(R/J(G))=\pdim(R/I(G))-1

\pdim(R/I(G))\ \geq\ \mathrm{bight}I(G),

where
\mathrm{bight}I(G)
is the maximum size of a minimal vertex cover. In particular,

\mathrm{bight}I(G)\geq\tau(G)
, where
\tau(G)
is the minimum size of a vertex cover.
Since
\tau(G)=n-\alpha(G)
, we obtain

\pdim(R/I(G))\ \geq\ n-\alpha(G)\ \geq\ n-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)=\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-1,

and therefore
\reg(R/J(G))=\pdim(R/I(G))-1\geq\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-2

Lemma 12.5

There is no connected graph

13.
Conclusion and Future Directions

A recurring goal throughout this paper has been to understand, for fixed
n
, which pairs

\bigl(\reg(R/J(G)),\ \deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\bigr)

arise from connected graphs on
n
vertices, and to identify graph families that populate large
portions of the feasible region. The results of
Sections

5
,
8
,
10
,
9
and
11

together with the computational data in
Section

12
suggest that a surprisingly small
collection of structured classes already accounts for most observed pairs.

In particular, trees of radius at most two appear to realize most pairs on the diagonal
\deg h=\reg

and above it, while our chordal constructions provide systematic families below the diagonal.
This motivates the following question.

Question 2
.

Fix
n
. Do there exist explicit graph classes (for instance, radius-
2
graphs and/or chordal graphs)
whose members realize every pair

\bigl(\reg(R/J(G)),\ \deg h_{R/J(G)}(t)\bigr)

that occurs among connected graphs on
n
vertices?

We hope that the invariant
M(G)
, together with its recursions and behavior under graph operations,
provides a useful framework for further study of these realizability problems.
