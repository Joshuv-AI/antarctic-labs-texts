ENERGY CRITICAL FOURTH-ORDER SCHRÖDINGER EQUATION SYSTEM WITH POWER-TYPE NONLINEARITIES IN THE RADIAL CASE
Maicon Hespanha, Renzo Scarpelli

Abstract
. In this paper, we study a system of focusing fourth-order Schrödinger equations in the energy-critical setting with radial initial data and general power-type nonlinearities. The main idea is to generalize the analysis of such systems: we first establish several hypotheses on the nonlinearities and prove their implications. These implications are then used to establish a local well-posedness result in H 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) and and to prove the existence of ground state solutions. Using a virial argument, we demonstrate a blow-up result for initial data with negative energy or with kinetic energy exceeding that of the ground state. Finally, employing the concentration-compactness/rigidity method, we prove a scattering result for solutions whose energy and kinetic energy are below those of the ground state.

Introduction

The fourth-order Schrödinger equation

(1.1)

i\partial_{t}u+\Delta^{2}u+\epsilon\Delta u+\lambda|u|^{p-1}u=0

was introduced by
[

]

In
[
4

[
14

When

[

[

In this paper we will consider the energy-critical case for the following Cauchy problem to the fourth-order coupled nonlinear Schrödinger system in the homogeneous
(\epsilon=0)

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\alpha_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}+\gamma_{k}\Delta^{2}u_{k}-f_{k}(\mathbf{u})=0,\\
(u_{1}(0,x),...,u_{l}(0,x))=(u_{10},...,u_{l0})\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad k=1,...,l,\end{array}\right.

where
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},...,u_{l})
is a complex-valued function in the space-time
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

5\leq d\leq 16
,
\Delta^{2}
stands for the biharmonic operator and
\alpha_{k},\gamma_{k}>0

satisfy a power-type growth and are some suitable assumptions that will be displayed later. One may notice that we have a constraint in the dimension, the lower bound is a consequence of the energy-critical regime and the power of the nonlinearities. The upper bound, is a consequence of a necessary condition to apply Strichartz-type estimates (see Proposition
2.5

3.7

Motivated by the work in NLS systems made in
[
34
]
-
[
38
]
, the idea of this paper is provide sufficient conditions on the nonlinearities

to study the dynamics of a system of fourth-order nonlinear Shcrödinger equations with general power-type nonlinearities. Our intention here is study the energy critical regime, that is, the exponent of the power-type growth will be
\frac{d+4}{d-4}
. As mentioned before, to reach our goal will be necessary assume suitable conditions on the nonlinear terms
f_{k}
. Therefore, in what follows we will assume

(H1)
.

We have

\displaystyle f_{k}(\mathbf{0})=0,\qquad k=1,\ldots,l.

(H2)
.

For any
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{l}
we have

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{m}}[f_{k}(\mathbf{z})-f_{k}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})]\right|+\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}_{m}}[f_{k}(\mathbf{z})-f_{k}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})]\right|&\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{l}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|^{\frac{8}{d-4}},\qquad k,m=1,\ldots,l,\end{split}

(H3)
.

There exists a function
F:\mathbb{C}^{l}\to\mathbb{C}
, such that

f_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\overline{z}_{k}}(\mathbf{z})+\overline{\frac{\partial F}{\partial z_{k}}}(\mathbf{z}),\qquad k=1,\ldots,l.

(H4)
.

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{l}
,

\mathrm{Re}F(e^{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\gamma_{1}}}z_{1},...,e^{\frac{\alpha_{l}}{\gamma_{1}}}z_{l})=\mathrm{Re}F(\mathbf{z})

(H5)
.

Function
F
is homogeneous of degree
\frac{2d}{d-4}
, that is, for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{l}
and
\lambda>0
,

F(\lambda\mathbf{z})=\lambda^{\frac{2d}{d-4}}F(\mathbf{z}).

(H6)
.

There holds

\left|\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{u})\;dx\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\!\!\big\bracevert\!\!\mathbf{u}\!\!\big\bracevert\!\!)\;dx.

(H7)
.

Function
F
is real valued on
\mathbb{R}^{l}
, that is, if
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{l})\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
then

F(y_{1},\ldots,y_{l})\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, functions
f_{k}
are non-negative on the positive cone in
\mathbb{R}^{l}
, that is, for
y_{i}\geq 0
,
i=1,\ldots,l
,

f_{k}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{l})\geq 0.

Summarizing, we use hypothesis
(H1)
and
(H2)
to prove local well-posedness for (
1.2
). The Mass and Energy of (
1.2
) are, respectively, given by

(1.3)

M(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}

and

(1.4)

E(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\|\Delta u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\mathrm{Re}\int F(\mathbf{u})dx:=\frac{1}{2}K(\mathbf{u})-P(\mathbf{u}),

where
K
and

are respectively called by kinetic and potential energy. To prove conservation of the mass and the energy its enough to assume
(H3)
-
(H5)
(see
[
13
, Lemma 2.1]
for details).

We start by defining the concept of solution to (
1.2
).

Definition 1.1
.

By a solution of (
1.2
), we will understand
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{H}^{2}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is a non-empty time interval containing
0
and satisfies the Duhamel formula

(1.5)

u_{k}(t)=U_{k}(t)u_{k0}-i\int_{0}^{t}U_{k}(t-s)f_{k}(\mathbf{u}(s))ds,\quad k=1,\ldots,l,\,t\in I

where
U_{k}(t)=e^{it\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\Delta^{2}}
,
k=1,\ldots,l
, is the biharmonic Schrödinger propagator. The time interval
I
is said to be the lifespan of
\mathbf{u}
. We say that
\mathbf{u}
is a maximal-lifespan solution if the solution cannot be extended to an interval
J\supset I
strictly larger than
I
. We say that the solution is global if
I=\mathbb{R}
.

It is easy to see that for
\nu>0
, the equation (
1.2
) is preserved by the scaling

(1.6)

f_{\nu}(t,x)=\nu^{\frac{d-4}{2}}f(\nu^{4}t,\nu x),\quad\nu>0.

This scaling preserves the homogeneous
\dot{H}^{2}
-norm of the original solution
\mathbf{u}(t)
. Moreover, the equation (
1.2
) also satisfy time reversal and time translation symmetries.

Another important concepts that will be discussed throughout the paper is the following notions of Scattering size and blow-up.

Definition 1.2

(Scattering size)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be a solution of (
1.2
). The scattering size of
\mathbf{u}
on a time interval
I
is defined as

S_{I}(\mathbf{u}):=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int_{I}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{k}(t,x)|^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d-4}}dxdt

Definition 1.3

(Blow-up)
.

We say that a solution
\mathbf{u}
of (
1.2
) blows-up forward in time if there exists
t_{1}\in I
such that

S_{[t_{1},\sup I)}(\mathbf{u})=\infty,

and
\mathbf{u}
blows-up backward in time, if there exists
t_{2}\in I
such that

S_{(\inf I,t_{2}]}(\mathbf{u})=\infty.

We say that
\mathbf{u}
blows-up in time if it blows-up both forward and backward in time.

Our main interest is to give conditions to deal with the dichotomy global existence versus blow-up in finite time when the initial data is radial. To this, we need to set the threshold for such dichotomy. Is well know, from NLS equation that ground state solutions plays a important role in this kind of studies. So, the next step is to guarantee the existence of such type of solutions. To give a precise description of this solution we start with standing waves solutions, that are a special kind with the form

(1.7)

u_{k}(t,x)=e^{i\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2}\omega t}\psi_{k}(x),\quad k=1,\ldots,l,

where
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi_{k}
are real-valued functions decaying to zero at infinity. By Lemma
3.4
, one may see that the
\psi_{k}
satisfy the following semilinear elliptic system

(1.8)

-\gamma_{k}\Delta^{2}\psi_{k}=f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),\quad k=1,\ldots,l.

We will see in Section 4 that solutions of (
1.8
) can be seen as critical points of the energy functional
E
. More precisely

Definition 1.4
.

We say that
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a weak solution of (
1.8
) if is critical point of
E
, that is, if for any
\mathbf{g}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(1.9)

\gamma_{k}\int\Delta\psi_{k}\Delta g_{k}=\int f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}dx,\quad k=1,\ldots,l.

Among all the solutions of (
1.8
), the ground state solutions is the one that minimizes
E
. Precisely, we say that
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
ground state
solution if

E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\inf\{E(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$});\,\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}\in\mathcal{C}\},

where
\mathcal{C}
is the set of non trivial solutions of (
1.8
). We refer the set of ground state solutions by
\mathcal{G}
.

Our main result in this direction is the following:

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists at least one ground state solution
\psi
for system (
1.8
), that is,
\mathcal{G}
is non-empty.

As we will see, the ground state solution is direct related with a critical Sobolev-type inequality. From this inequality we will derive a minimization problem, which will be proved by using the Lions concentration-compactness method, introduced in
[
32
]
. Once this problem is solved, we can prove the existence of ground state solutions. Also, we also obtain a optimal constant for the critical Sobolev inequality.

Once the existence of ground state is guaranteed, we can set as the threshold for our dichotomy. More specifically, we will see that if either the kinetic energy of the initial data is above of the kinetic energy of the ground state or the energy is negative, the solution must blow-up in finite time. Our result in this topic reads as follows

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
5\leq d\leq 16
. Suppose that
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is radial. We assume either
E(\mathbf{u}_{0})<0
or

(1.10)

E(\mathbf{u})<E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\quad\hbox{and}\quad K(\mathbf{u}_{0})>K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),

where
\psi
is any ground state solution of (
1.8
), then the solution
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{H}_{x}^{2}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
to (
1.2
) blows-up in finite time, i.e.,
T<\infty
and
S_{[0,T)}(\mathbf{u})=\infty.

As in NLS case, to prove this kind of result we will apply the convexity method, which consists in deriving a contradiction by working with some virial type equality. For our purpose, we will follow the ideas presented in
[
5
]
and working with the function

\mathcal{V}_{R}(t)=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int\nabla\phi_{R}\cdot\nabla u_{k}\bar{u}_{k}dx.

and its derivative
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(t)
in order to reach a contradiction with the length of the existence time interval
I
.

In the last part of the paper we will be concerned with the global existence and scattering of radial solutions in the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Here we will prove that if we invert the second inequality in (
1.10
), the solution to (
1.2
) is global and scatters. The results are the following.

Theorem 1.7
.

(Spacetime bounds) Let
5\leq d\leq 16
and
\mathbf{u}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a radial solution of (
1.2
). If

(1.11)

E(\mathbf{u}_{0})<E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\quad\hbox{and}\quad K(\mathbf{u}_{0})<K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})

Then
S_{I}(\mathbf{u})<\infty.

As a consequence,

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
5\leq d\leq 16
and let
\mathbf{u}
be a maximal lifespan solution to (
1.2
) on the time interval
I
. Assume that (
1.11
) holds. Then,
I=\mathbb{R}
and
S_{R}(\mathbf{u})<\infty
. In particular, there exist asymptotic states
\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\|\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{U}(t)\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\|_{\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\rightarrow 0,\quad\hbox{as }r\rightarrow\pm\infty,

where
\mathbf{U}(t)=(U_{1}(t),....,U_{l}(t))
.

To this end, we will follow the ideas presented in
[
33
]
, which is a variant of the concentration-compactness and rigidity method in
[
28
]
, with the fourth order Schrödinger equation settings. See also
[
25
]
and
[
27
]
. The main difference is that we are working on the setting of coupled nonlinear system with general power-type nonlinearities.

The whole idea is to use some properties of the nonlinearities
f_{k}
to guarantee that the method works for our general system. We start assuming by contradiction that Theorem
1.7
fails. This allows us to reduce the solution of (
1.2
) to the case of almost periodic solutions. Therefore, we will see that this kind of solutions has three particular properties, which are called “the enemies” in the literature. The next step is to prove that none solutions satisfies this properties. To this end, we prove some adapted versions of the coercivity lemmas in
[
25
]
involving the kinetic and potential energies and establish the energy trapping result for our setting, which are important for precluding the three-enemies scenario. Next, we prove a virial-type inequality, which is necessary to ensure that almost periodic solutions cannot exist, thereby deriving a contradiction.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 is devoted to introduce some notations and preliminary results. In Section 3, we establish consequences of our assumptions
(H1)
-
(H7)
. In Section 4, we work on local theory for (
1.2
). The existence of ground state solutions is proved in Section 5. In Section 6, we prove virial identities results and the blow-up in finite time. Finally, section 7 is devoted to prove global well-posedness and scattering.

The first result we present here about the biharmonic Schrödinger equation is the linear estimates, that are directly consequences of the dispersion estimates presented in
[
4
]
. The result reads as follows

Lemma 2.4

(Linear estimates)
.

Let
2\leq p\leq\infty
and
g\in L^{p^{\prime}}
. For all time
t\neq 0
, we have

\|e^{it\Delta^{2}}g\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim|t|^{-\frac{d}{4}\left(1-\frac{2}{p}\right)}\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}.

The above result is a key ingredient to derive Strichartz type estimates associated to the biharmonic Schrödinger propagator presented in
[
41
]
. Before states the result we need to set some concepts. We say that the pair
(q,r)
is biharmonic Schrödinger admissible (B-admissible for short) if satisfies

(2.3)

\frac{4}{q}=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d}{r},\quad 2\leq q,r\leq\infty,\,(q,r,d)\neq(2,\infty,4).

and a pair
(q,r)
is said to be Schrödinger admissible (S-admissible for short) if satisfies

(2.4)

\frac{2}{q}=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d}{r},\quad 2\leq q,r\leq\infty,\,(q,r,d)\neq(2,\infty,2).

The result states the following.

Proposition 2.5

(Strichartz-type estimates)
.

Let
I=[0,T]
be an interval,
h\in(L^{1}_{\rm loc})_{t}H_{x}^{-4}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
u\in C_{t}H_{x}^{-4}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a solution of

u(t)=e^{it\Delta^{2}}u_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta^{2}}h(s)ds,

where
u_{0}\in L^{2}
.

Definition 2.7
.

(Almost periodicity modulo symmetries).
A solution
\mathbf{u}
to (
1.2

The functions

Remark 2.8
.

Recall that a set

is compact if and only if

is norm-bounded in

and, for all

.

4.
Local well-posedness

In this section we will develop the local theory for (
1.2
) in the energy critical case. To begin with, we will set some norms that will be useful along the work. For
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, we set the following norms

(4.1)

\begin{split}\|f\|_{M(I)}&:=\|\Delta f\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d-4}}L_{x}^{\frac{2d(d+4)}{d^{2}+16}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
\|f\|_{W(I)}&:=\|\nabla f\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d-4}}L_{x}^{\frac{2d(d+4)}{d^{2}-2d+8}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
\|f\|_{Z(I)}&:=\|f\|_{L_{t,x}^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d-4}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
\|f\|_{N(I)}&:=\|\nabla f\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
\|f\|_{B(I)}&:=\|f\|_{L_{t,x}^{\frac{2(d+4)}{d}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
\end{split}

Remark 4.1
.

Let us establish here some estimates that will be used several times during the section. We start noticing that for
\mathbf{u}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by Sobolev’s inequality we have

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{Z}(I)}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{W}(I)}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I)}.

Moreover, by using Lemma
3.1
, one can easily see that

\|f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\|_{N(I)}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{Z}(I)}^{\frac{8}{d-4}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{W}(I)}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{Z}(I)}^{\frac{8}{d-4}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I)}.

Another useful consequence of Lemma
3.1
is that if
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{W}(I)
then
f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\in N(I)
. Also, observe that the pairs in the norm
M(I)
and
B(I)
are B-admissible and the pair in the norm
N(I)
is a exponent conjugate of an S-admissible pair, namely
(2,2d/(d-2))
. Then, by Strichartz estimate (
2.7
), we have

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I)}\lesssim\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}+\|f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\|_{N(I)},

which means that we can control the second derivative of
\mathbf{u}
using only one derivative of
f_{k}
. One can also notice that, if
I_{t}=[0,t]\subset I
by using the above estimates we arrive at
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I_{t})}\leq C\left(\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I_{t})}^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}\right)
. Then, provided that all involved norms are finite, one can use a continuity argument to get

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I)}\lesssim\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}.

The local existence result is a straight forward adaptation of the standard contraction map argument presented in
[
9
, Chapter 4]
, so we will omit the proof. The result reads as follows.

Theorem 4.2

(Local existence)
.

Let
5\leq d\leq 16
. There exist
\delta>0
such that for any initial data
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{2}
, and a time interval
I=[0,T]
. If

(4.2)

\|U_{k}(t)u_{k0}\|_{W(I)}\leq\delta,\quad k=1,\ldots,l,

then there exists a unique solution
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{H}^{2}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{M}(I)\cap\mathbf{B}(I)
. Moreover, the following estimates hold

(4.3)

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{W}(I)}\leq 2\delta,\hbox{ and}\quad\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I)}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{L}_{t}^{\infty}\mathbf{H}_{x}^{2}}\leq C\left(\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}}+\delta^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}\right).

Corollary 4.3

(Small data global existence)
.

Let
5\leq d\leq 16
. There exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for any
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
obeying
\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}\leq\delta_{0}
, the solution
\mathbf{u}
for (
1.2
) obtained in Theorem
4.2
extends globally and satisfies

(4.4)

\|\Delta\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}},

Proof.

Note that by using Sobolev’s inequality and Strichartz (
2.7
) we have for
k=1,\ldots,l
and any compact interval

\|U_{k}(t)u_{k}(t_{0})\|_{W(I)}\lesssim\|U_{k}(t)u_{k}(t_{0})\|_{M(I)}\lesssim\|u_{k}(t_{0})\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\leq\delta_{0},

Therefore, by Proposition
4.2
, the solution exists in any compact

and, hence, is global. To prove (

) notice that for compact interval

,

\begin{split}\|\Delta\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I)}&\leq\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}+C\|f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\|_{\mathbf{N}(I)}\\
&\leq\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}+C\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I)}^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}\\
\end{split}

where we have used Strichartz (

) and Lemma

. Since these norms are finite, a continuity argument gives that

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{M}(I)}\lesssim\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}

for all compact interval

, which proves (

).
∎

Proposition 4.6

I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval containing
0
. Let
\mathbf{v}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
be an approximate solution of (
1.2
) in the sense that

i\alpha_{k}\partial_{t}v_{k}+\gamma_{k}\Delta^{2}v_{k}+\varepsilon\Delta v_{k}-f_{k}(\mathbf{v})=e_{k},

for some
\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},...,e_{l})\in\mathbf{N}(I)
. Assume also that
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{Z}(I)}=Z<\infty
and
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{L}_{t}^{\infty}\dot{\mathbf{H}}_{x}^{2}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}=L<\infty
. For any
\eta>0
there exists
\delta_{0}>0
,
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\eta,Z,L)
, such that if
\|\mathbf{e}\|_{\mathbf{N}(I)}\leq\delta
, and
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{2}
satisfies

(4.6)

\|\mathbf{v}(0)-\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\eta\hbox{ and }\left\|U_{k}(t)\left(v_{k}(0)-u_{k0}\right)\right\|_{W(I)}\leq\delta\quad k=1,\ldots,l

for some
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
, then there exists
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{H}_{x}^{2}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
a solution of (
1.2
) such that
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}
. Moreover,
\mathbf{u}
satisfies

(4.7)

\begin{split}&\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{W}(I)}\leq C\left(\delta+\delta^{\frac{15}{(d-4)^{2}}}\right),\\
&\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{L}_{t}^{q}\dot{\mathbf{H}}_{x}^{2,r}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\left(\eta+\delta+\delta^{\frac{15}{(d-4)^{2}}}\right),\hbox{ and}\\
&\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{L}_{t}^{q}\dot{\mathbf{H}}_{x}^{2,r}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C,\end{split}

for all B-admissible pairs
(q,r)
, where
C=C(\eta,Z,L)
.

Notice that if we let
e_{k}=0
for
k=1,\ldots,l
, then Proposition
4.6
wields the uniform continuity for the solution map
\mathbf{u}_{0}\mapsto\mathbf{u}
. Indeed, in this case we have that
\mathbf{v}
is a solution to (
1.2
) with initial data
\mathbf{v}_{0}
. Moreover from Strichartz and Remark
4.1
, one can see that

\left\|U_{k}(t)\left(v_{k}(0)-u_{k}(0)\right)\right\|_{W(I)}\leq C\|v_{k}(0)-u_{k}(0)\|_{\dot{H}^{2}},\quad k=1,\ldots,l.

Thus, if we choose
\delta=C\eta
, we can make the respectively solutions close enough by controlling
\delta
when
\eta
is small.

5.
Ground state solutions

This section is devoted to prove existence of ground state solution. We start by proving that the functionals
K
and
P
are
C^{1}
functions. This allow us to see solutions to (
1.8
) as critical points of the energy functional, which give sense to the Definition
1.4
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The functionals
K
and
P
are in
C^{1}(\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\mathbb{R})
. In addition, if
\mathbf{g}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}
, then

(5.1)

K^{\prime}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})(\mathbf{g})=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int\Delta\psi_{k}g_{k}dx,

and

(5.2)

P^{\prime}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})(\mathbf{g})=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}dx.

Proof.

The proof of (
5.1
) is similar to those presented in
[
2
, Exemple 1.3(d)]
. One may also check
[
3
, Examples 1.3.13]
.

To prove (
5.2
), we start showing that
P\in C^{1}(\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2},\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, let
(\mathbf{u}_{m})\subset\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}
be a sequence such that
\mathbf{u}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbf{u}
in
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}
as
m\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, Lemma
3.3
, Hölder’s inequality and Sobolev embedding yields

(5.3)

\begin{split}|P(\mathbf{u}_{m})-P(\mathbf{u})|&\leq\int|F(\mathbf{u}_{m})-F(\mathbf{u})|dz\\
&\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\int\left(|u_{jm}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}+|u_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}\right)|u_{km}-u_{k}|dx\\
&\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left(\|u_{jm}\|_{L^{\frac{2d}{d-4}}}^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}+\|u_{j}\|_{L^{\frac{2d}{d-4}}}^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}\right)\|u_{km}-u_{k}\|_{L^{\frac{2d}{d-4}}}\\
&\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left(\|u_{jm}\|_{H^{2}}^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}+\|u_{j}\|_{H^{2}}^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}\right)\|u_{km}-u_{k}\|_{H^{2}}.\\
\end{split}

Since the right-hand side goes to zero as
m\rightarrow\infty
, the claim follows. Left us to prove that
P
is Gatêaux differentiable and its derivate is given by (
5.2
). Indeed, let
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}
and
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{l}
. If
0<t\leq 1
, by Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{t}\left[F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}+t\mathbf{g})\right.&-\left.F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})-t\sum_{k=1}^{l}f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}\right]\leq\frac{1}{t}\left[|F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}+t\mathbf{g})-F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})|+t\sum_{k=1}^{l}|f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})||g_{k}|\right]\\
&\leq\frac{C}{t}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left(|\psi_{j}+tg_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}+|\psi_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}\right)t|g_{m}|+t\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}|\psi_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}|g_{k}|\right]\\
&\leq C\left[\sum_{m=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\left(|\psi_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}+t^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}|g_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}\right)|g_{m}|+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}|\psi_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}|g_{k}|\right]\\
&\leq C\left[\sum_{m=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}|g_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}|g_{m}|+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}|\psi_{j}|^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}|g_{k}|\right]\end{split}

where we used that
t\leq 1
in the last inequality. Also, a similar argument used in (
5.3
) and the Sobolev embedding
H^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\frac{2d}{d-4}}
shows that the right-hand side is a
L^{1}
function. On the other hand, the chain rule and (
3.8
) gives us

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\left[F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}+t\mathbf{g})-F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})-t\sum_{k=1}^{l}f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}\right]=0,

which, together with Dominated Convergence, implies that

\begin{split}P^{\prime}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})(\mathbf{g})-\int\sum_{k=1}^{l}f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}dx&=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\left[P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}+t\mathbf{g})-P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})-t\int\sum_{k=1}^{l}f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}dx\right]\\
&=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\int\left[F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}+t\mathbf{g})-F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})-t\sum_{k=1}^{l}f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}\right]\\
&=\int\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\left[F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}+t\mathbf{g})-F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})-t\sum_{k=1}^{l}f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}\right]\\
&=0.\end{split}

Therefore,
P
is Gatêaux differentiable. To finish the proof, notice that Lemma
3.6
implies that
P^{\prime}
is continuous and, consequently, its Gatêaux and Fréchet derivatives coincide.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Note the one may take
\mathbf{g}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}
in the previous proof by using a density argument.

Once the definition of ground state is well established, we can now turn our attention to prove the existence. We start by observing that given
\psi
a solution of (
1.8
), if we take
g_{k}=\psi_{k}
in (
1.4
), summing over
k
and using Lemma
3.3
, we have

(5.4)

K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\psi_{k}dx=\left(\frac{2d}{d-4}\right)P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),

which gives us the following relations between the energy functional
E
, the kinetic energy
K
and the potential energy
P
,

(5.5)

P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\left(\frac{d-4}{4}\right)E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\quad\hbox{and}\quad K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Moreover, one can also notice that (
5.4
) implies that if
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\mathcal{C}
then
P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})>0
, that is, the set of all non trivial solutions lies in
\mathcal{P}:=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\mathcal{C};\,P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})>0\}
. Moreover, by using Lemma
3.3
and Sobolev’s inequality we have

(5.6)

P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\leq CK(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})^{\frac{d}{d-4}}.

Thus, if we set the functional
J(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
by

J(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}):=\frac{K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})^{\frac{d}{d-4}}}{P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})},

for all
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\mathcal{P}
, by (
5.4
) and (
5.5
),

(5.7)

J(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\left(\frac{d-4}{2d}\right)\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{\frac{4}{d-4}}E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})^{\frac{4}{d-4}}:=C_{d}E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})^{\frac{4}{d-4}}.

Hence, a non-trivial solution of (
1.8
) is a ground state if, and only if, minimizes
J
. Therefore, in order to prove existence of ground state solution, we have to find the best constant in (
5.6
), that is, the best
C>0
such that

\frac{1}{C}\leq J(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\mathcal{P}.

The optimal constant to place is given by

(5.8)

C^{-1}_{\rm opt}:=\inf\{J(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$});\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\mathcal{P}\}.

In particular, by (
5.7
) and the definition of energy (
1.4
), a non-trivial solution of (
1.8
) ia a ground state if and only if has least energy among all non-trivial solution of (
1.8
).

Remark 5.3
.

By using the homogeneity of the functionals
K
and
P
one may notice the following:

(i)

A straightforward computation shows that
K(\!\!\big\bracevert\!\!\mathbf{u}\!\!\big\bracevert\!\!)\leq K(\mathbf{u})
and together with
(H6)
yields that
J(\!\!\big\bracevert\!\!\mathbf{u}\!\!\big\bracevert\!\!)\leq J(\mathbf{u})
. Thus, there is no loss of generality in assuming that minimizing sequences are always non-negative.

(ii)

C_{\rm opt}^{-1}=I^{\frac{d}{d-4}}.
Therfore, to prove that the infimum (
5.8
) is attained we can consider the normalized problem given by

(5.9)

I:=\inf\{K(\mathbf{u});\,\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{P},\,P(\mathbf{u})=1\}

and solve (
5.9
) instead of (
5.8
).

(iii)

The functionals
K
and
P
are invariant under the transformation

(5.10)

\mathbf{u}\mapsto\mathbf{u}^{R,y}=R^{-\frac{d-4}{2}}\mathbf{u}\left(R^{-1}(x-y)\right),\quad R>0,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Before solve the minimization problem, we set a minimizing sequence for (
5.8
) to be a sequence
\mathbf{u}_{m}
in
\mathcal{P}
such that
J(\mathbf{u}_{m})\rightarrow C_{\rm opt}^{-1}
. In the same way, a minimizing sequence for (
5.9
) is a sequence
\mathbf{u}_{m}
in
\mathcal{P}
such that
P(\mathbf{u}_{m})=1
for all
m
.

5.1.
Concentration-compactness

In order to solve (
5.9
), we will apply the concentration compactness method introduced in
[
32
]
. This is a well known method, so we will present here just a outline of the proof. For a detailed version in the NLS regime, one may check
[
40
, Theorem 3.11]
. See also
[
21
, Theorem 3.12]
and
[
13
, Theorem 3.8]
. The result reads as follows

Theorem 5.4
.

Suppose that
(\mathbf{u}_{m})
is a minimizing sequence for (
5.9
). Then, up to translation and dilation
(\mathbf{u}_{m})
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{P}
, that is, there exist a subsequence
(\mathbf{u}_{m_{j}})
and sequences
(R_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}
,
(y_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathbf{v}_{j}
given by

\mathbf{v}_{j}:=R_{j}^{-\frac{d-4}{2}}\mathbf{u}_{m_{j}}(R_{j}^{-1}(x-y_{j})),

strongly converges in
\mathcal{N}
to some
\mathbf{v}
, which minimizes (
5.9
).

Outline of the proof.

The proof proceeds in several steps. First, we will need a result that is an adapted version of Lemma 1.7.4 in
[
9
]
concerning the concentration function given by

(5.11)

Q_{m}(R):=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B(y,R)}F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx,\quad R>0,

so we will omit the details. The results reads as follows.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
(\mathbf{u}_{m})\subset{\mathbf{L}}^{\frac{2d}{d-4}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\mathbf{u}_{m}\geq\mathbf{0}
and
\int F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx=1
, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
Q_{m}(R)
be the concentration function of
F(\mathbf{u}_{m})
defined by (
5.11
). Then, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there is
y=y(m,R)
such that

Q_{m}(R)=\int_{B(y,R)}F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx.

The next step is to built a suitable sequence of probability measures. We do this by considering
(\mathbf{u}_{m})
to be any minimizing sequence of (
5.9
), that is,

(5.12)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}K(\mathbf{u}_{m})=I\quad\hbox{and}\quad P(\mathbf{u}_{m})=\int F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx=1.

Thus, by using the invariance (
5.10
) and Lemma (
5.5
) we get sequences
(R_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}
and
(y_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathbf{v}_{j}:=R_{j}^{-\frac{d-4}{2}}\mathbf{u}_{m_{j}}(R_{j}^{-1}(x-y_{j})),

satisfies

(5.13)

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B(y,1)}\varphi(\mathbf{v}_{m})dx=\int_{B(0,1)}\varphi(\mathbf{v}_{m})dx=\frac{1}{2}.

Once again we use the invariance (
5.10
) and conclude that
(\mathbf{v}_{m})
is also a minimizing sequence for (
5.9
). In particular,
(\mathbf{v}_{m})
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{P}
. Hence, up to a subsequence, there exist
\mathbf{v}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(5.14)

(\mathbf{v}_{m})\rightharpoonup\mathbf{v}\quad\mbox{in }\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Next, we set the sequences of measures
(\mu_{m})
and
(\nu_{m})
by

\mu_{m}:=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\Delta v_{km}|^{2}dx,\quad\hbox{and}\quad\nu_{m}:=F(\mathbf{v}_{m})dx.

Since
(\mathbf{v}_{m})
is a minimizing sequence of (
5.9
), then
(\nu_{m})
is a probability sequence of measures. This allow us to use the following auxiliary result, which is inspired in
[
31
, Lemma I.1]
.

Lemma 5.6

(Concentration-compactness lemma I)
.

Suppose that
(\nu_{m})
is a sequence in
\mathcal{M}_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, there is a subsequence, still denoted by
(\nu_{m})
, such that one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

(Vanishing) For all
R>0
it holds

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu_{m}(B(x,R))\right)=0.

(ii)

(Dichotomy) There is a number
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
R>0
and a sequence
(x_{m})
with the following property: given
R^{\prime}>R

\nu_{m}(B(x_{m},R))\geq\lambda-\epsilon,

\nu_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(x_{m},R^{\prime}))\geq 1-\lambda-\epsilon,

for
m
sufficiently large.

(iii)

(Compactness) There exists a sequence
(x_{m})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for each
\epsilon>0
there is a radius
R>0
with the property

\nu_{m}(B(x_{m},R))\geq 1-\epsilon,

for all
m
.

Proof.

See
[
15
, Lemma 23]
.
∎

As a consequence,
\nu_{m}
must satisfy one of the three above conditions. The next step is show that
\nu_{m}
cannot satisfy vanishing and dichotomy and, thus, implying the compactness of sequence. The vanishing is ruled out immediately by (
5.13
). To eliminate the possibility of dichotomy, we need to construct a localized Sobolev-type inequality. We do this by adapting Lemma 8 in
[
15
]
to our settings. The result is the following.

Lemma 5.7
.

For every
\delta>0
there is a constant
C(\delta)>0
satisfying the following: if
0<r<R
with
r/R\leq C(\delta)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then there is a cutt-off function
\chi_{R}^{r}\in H^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\chi_{R}^{r}=1
on
B(x,r)
,
\chi_{R}^{r}=0
outside
B(x,R)
,

(5.15)

K(\chi_{R}^{r}\mathbf{u})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int_{B(x,R)}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy+\delta K(\mathbf{u}),

and

(5.16)

K((1-\chi_{R}^{r})\mathbf{u})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(x,r)}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy+\delta K(\mathbf{u}),

for any
\mathbf{u}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

The proof follows the ideas in
[
15
, Lemma 8]
.There is no loss of generality in assuming that
x=0
, so we will assume that. Let
\chi_{R}^{r}
be given by

(5.17)

\chi_{R}^{r}(y)=\begin{cases}1,&|y|\leq r,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\log(|y|/R)}{\log(r/R)},&r\leq|y|\leq R,\\
0,&|y|\geq R.\\
\end{cases}

A straightforward computation yields that
\chi_{R}^{r}\in H^{2,\infty}
and

(5.18)

\int_{B(0,R)}|\nabla\chi_{R}^{r}|^{d}dy=\frac{\omega_{d}}{(\log(R/r))^{d-1}},\quad\int_{B(0,R)}|\Delta\chi_{R}^{r}|^{\frac{d}{2}}dy=\frac{(d-2)^{\frac{d}{2}}\omega_{d}}{\log(R/r)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}},

where
\omega_{d}
is the measure of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Now, by using (
5.18
), Sobolev, Young and Hölder’s inequalities, for any
\epsilon>0
and
k=1,\ldots,l
,

\begin{split}\int_{B(0,R)}|\Delta[\chi_{R}^{r}u_{k}]|^{2}dy&\leq(1+\epsilon)\int_{B(0,R)}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy+\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\frac{C(d-2)^{2}\omega_{d}^{\frac{4}{d}}}{\log(R/r)^{\frac{2(d-2)}{d}}}\int|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy\\
&\quad+\left(1+\epsilon+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\frac{C\omega_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}}{(\log(R/r))^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d}}}\int|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy.\end{split}

Multiplying by
\gamma_{k}
and summing over
k
, we arrive at

\begin{split}K(\chi_{R}^{r}\mathbf{u})&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int_{B(0,R)}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy\\
&\quad+\left[\epsilon+\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\frac{\zeta_{1}^{2}}{\log(R/r)^{\frac{2(d-2)}{d}}}+\left(1+\epsilon+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\frac{\zeta_{2}^{2}}{(\log(R/r))^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d}}}\right]K(\mathbf{u}),\end{split}

where
\zeta_{1}=\sqrt{C}(d-2)\omega_{d}^{\frac{2}{d}}
and
\zeta_{2}=\sqrt{C}\omega_{d}^{\frac{1}{d}}
. So, for any
\delta>0
, one may find a
C(\delta)
such that if
r/R\leq C(\delta)
than

\left[\epsilon+\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\frac{\zeta_{1}^{2}}{\log(R/r)^{\frac{2(d-2)}{d}}}+\left(1+\epsilon+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\frac{\zeta_{2}^{2}}{(\log(R/r))^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d}}}\right]\leq\delta

and (
5.15
) follows. Also, (
5.16
) follows as in (
5.15
), since
|\Delta(1-\chi_{R}^{r})|^{2}=|\Delta\chi_{R}^{r}|^{2}
,
|\nabla(1-\chi_{R}^{r})|^{2}=|\nabla\chi_{R}^{r}|^{2}
and
\chi_{R}^{r}=0
outside
B(0,R)
and

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(0,r)}|\Delta[(1-\chi_{R}^{r})u_{k}]|^{2}dy&\leq(1+\epsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(0,r)}|1-\chi_{R}^{r}|^{2}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy\\
&\quad+\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(0,r)}|u_{k}|^{2}|\Delta(1-\chi_{R}^{r})|^{2}dy\\
&\quad+\left(1+\epsilon+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(0,r)}|\nabla(1-\chi_{R}^{r})|^{2}|\nabla u_{k}|^{2}dy.\\
\end{split}

∎

With this in hand, we state the localized Sobolev-type inequality as follows. The proof is the same as in
[
15
, Corollary 9]
, so the details will be omitted.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathbf{u}\geq\mathbf{0}
. Fix
\delta>0
and
r/R\leq C(\delta)
as in Lemma (
5.7
). Then,

(5.19)

\int_{B(x,r)}F(\mathbf{u})dy\leq I^{-\frac{d}{d-4}}\left[\int_{B(x,R)}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy+\delta K(\mathbf{u})\right]^{\frac{d}{d-4}},

(5.20)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(x,R)}F(\mathbf{u})dy\leq I^{\frac{-d}{d-4}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(x,r)}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dy+(2\delta+\delta^{2})K(\mathbf{u})\right]^{\frac{d}{d-4}}.

Now, we proceed by contradiction and invoke Corollary
5.8
to preclude the dichotomy case. Therefore, Lemma (
5.6
) yield the existence of a sequence
(x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for all
\epsilon>0
, there is a radius
R>0
with

(5.21)

\nu_{m}(B(x_{m}))\geq 1-\epsilon,\quad\forall m,

which together with (
5.13
) and the fact that
(\nu_{m})
is a sequence of probability measures, we get that
(\nu_{m})
is a uniformly tight sequence. Therefore, up to a subsequence,
(\nu_{m})
converges weakly to some
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see for instance
[
8
, Theorems 30.6 and 31.2]
), that is,

(5.22)

\int fd\nu_{m}\rightarrow\int fd\nu,\quad\forall f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Now, notice that
(\mu_{m})
is vaguely bounded because
(K(\mathbf{v}_{m}))
is uniformly bounded. Then, up to a subsequence, there is
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(5.23)

\mu_{m}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\mu\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

In particular,
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})\leq\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The next step is to use the “second concentration compactness lemma”. Such result is the last key ingredient to prove Theorem
5.4
, and is an adapted version of the limit case lemma presented in
[
32
, Lemma I.1]
. The proof follows the same approach as in the NLS regime presented in
[
36
, Lemma 6]
, regarding the differences for the BNLS regime, so we will omit the details. The result reads as follows.

Lemma 5.9

(Concentration-compactness lemma II)
.

Let
(\mathbf{u}_{m})\subset\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a sequence such that
\mathbf{u}_{m}\geq\mathbf{0}
and

(5.24)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}(\mathbf{u}_{m})\rightharpoonup\mathbf{u},&\hbox{in}&\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\mu_{m}:=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\Delta u_{km}|^{2}dx\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\mu&\hbox{in}&\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\nu_{m}:=F(\mathbf{u})dx\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\nu,&\hbox{in}&\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).\\
\end{array}\right.

Then,

(i)

There exists an at most countable set
J
, a family of distinct points
\{x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d};\,j\in J\}
, and a family of non-negative numbers
\{\nu_{j};\,j\in J\}
such that

(5.25)

\nu=F(\mathbf{u})dx+\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}.

(ii)

Moreover, we have

(5.26)

\mu\geq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dx+\sum_{j\in J}\mu_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}

fore some family
\{\mu_{j};\,j\in J\},b_{j}>0,
such that

(5.27)

\nu_{j}\leq I^{-\frac{d}{d-4}}\mu_{j}^{\frac{d}{d-4}},\quad\forall j\in J.

In particular,
\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}^{\frac{d-4}{d}}<\infty
.

Next, (
5.14
), (
5.22
) and (
5.23
) allow us to use Lemma
5.9
to obtain

(5.28)

\mu\geq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\Delta v_{k}|^{2}dx+\sum_{j\in J}\mu_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\nu=F(\mathbf{v})dx+\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}\delta_{x_{j}},

for some family
\{x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d};\,j\in J\}
, with
J
countable and
\mu_{j},\nu_{j}
non-negatives numbers satisfying

(5.29)

\nu_{j}\leq I^{-\frac{d}{d-4}}\mu_{j}^{\frac{d}{d-4}},\quad\forall j\in J,

with
\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}^{\frac{d-4}{d}}
convergent. Therefore, since
\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=1
, (
5.6
) and (
5.29
) yields

(5.30)

\begin{split}I=\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})&\geq\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})\geq K(\mathbf{v})+\sum_{j\in J}\mu_{j}\geq I\left[P(\mathbf{v})^{\frac{d-4}{d}}+\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}^{\frac{d-4}{d}}\right]\\
&\geq I\left[P(\mathbf{v})+\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}\right]^{\frac{d-4}{d}}=I[\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})]=I,\end{split}

where we also used that
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{\frac{d-4}{d}}
is a strictly concave function. Since
P(\mathbf{v})\neq 0
, we observe that
\nu_{j}=0
must hold for all
j\in J
, otherwise, if
\nu_{j_{0}}\neq 0
, for some
j_{0}
, then (
5.28
) would yield that
\nu=\nu_{j_{0}}\delta_{x_{j0}}
and hence

(5.31)

1=\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\nu_{j_{0}}.

However, the normalization (
5.13
) leads to

\frac{1}{2}\geq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{m}(B(x_{j_{0}},1))=\nu(B(x_{j_{0}},1))=\int_{B(x_{j_{0}},1)}d\nu=\nu_{j_{0}},

which contradicts (
5.31
). As a result, we deduce that
\nu=F(\mathbf{v})dx
. Moreover, since
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have that
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{P}
.

Finally, notice that by the definition of

, we know that
I\leq K(\mathbf{v})
. On the other hand, the lower semi-continuity of the weak convergence (

) yields
K(\mathbf{v})\leq\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}K(\mathbf{v}_{m})=I
. Hence,
K(\mathbf{v})=I=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}K(\mathbf{v}_{m})
and, moreover,
\mathbf{v}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbf{v}
strongly in
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, as desired.
∎

Note that actually we have proved the following:

Corollary 5.10
.

There exists
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
P(\mathbf{v})=1
and
K(\mathbf{v})=C_{\rm opt}^{\frac{d-4}{d}}
, where

is the best constant in the general critical Sobolev-type inequality (

We are now in a position to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem

.

Let
\mathbf{v}
be the minimizer of (

), that is,
K(\mathbf{v})=I
and

. So, by Remark (

), we have

Therefore,
\mathbf{v}
is also a minimizer of

. By (

),

is a minimum of

and hence is a critical point of
E
, that is, for any

. By Lemma
5.1
, follows that

is a solution to (

∎

Remark 5.11

One may notice that we do not have any information about the uniqueness of the ground state solutions for (

(ii) implies that

6.
Blow-up

As mentioned before, this section is devoted to prove that solutions of (
1.2
) with positive initial energy below that of the ground states but
with kinetic energy above that of the ground states, or negative energy must blow up in finite time. Our strategy rely on the argument introduced in
[
5
]
. The first step is to prove a localized virial identity. Our first result is the following

Lemma 6.1
.

(Virial identity) Let
\phi
to be a smooth function and consider
\phi_{R}=R^{2}\phi(x/R)
for
R>0
. Set

\mathcal{V}_{R}(t)=-2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int\nabla\phi_{R}\cdot\nabla u_{k}\bar{u}_{k}dx.

Then

(6.1)

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{R}^{\prime}(t)&=8\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\sum_{j,n,m}\int\partial^{2}_{n,m}\phi_{R}\partial^{2}_{m,j}u_{k}\partial^{2}_{j,n}\bar{u}_{k}dx\\
&\quad-4\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\sum_{j,n}\int\partial_{j}\bar{u}_{k}\partial^{2}_{j,n}\Delta\phi_{R}\partial_{n}u_{k}dx\\
&\quad-2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int\Delta^{2}\phi_{R}|\nabla u_{k}|^{2}dx+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int\Delta^{3}\phi_{R}|u_{k}|^{2}dx\\
&\quad-\frac{16}{d-4}\mathrm{Re}\int F(\mathbf{u})\Delta\phi_{R}.\\
\end{split}

Proof.

We start by using that
2i\mathrm{Im}(z)=z-\bar{z}
to get

(6.2)

i\mathcal{V}_{R}(t)=-2i\mathrm{Im}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}\bar{u}_{k}\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}dx=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}u_{k}\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}dx-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}\bar{u}_{k}\nabla u_{k}\cdot\phi_{R}dx.

Using integration by parts we have

(6.3)

\int\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}\Delta\phi_{R}=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}u_{k}\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}dx-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}\bar{u}_{k}\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}dx.

Combining (
6.2
) and (
6.3
) we get

(6.4)

\begin{split}i\mathcal{V}_{R}(t)&=-2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}\bar{u}_{k}\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}dx-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}\bar{u}_{k}\Delta\phi_{R}u_{k}dx\\
&=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}\bar{u}_{k}[\nabla\phi_{R}\cdot\nabla+\Delta\phi_{R}]u_{k}dx-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}\bar{u}_{k}\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}dx.\end{split}

By the definition of the Morawetz potential we conclude that

\mathcal{V}_{R}(t)=-2\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\alpha_{k}\bar{u}_{k}\Gamma_{\phi_{R}}u_{k}dx,

where
\Gamma_{\phi_{R}}:=-i\left(\nabla\phi_{R}\cdot\nabla+\Delta\phi_{R}\right)
. By introducing the notation

\mathcal{V}_{R}(t):=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\langle\alpha_{k}u_{k},\Gamma_{\phi_{R}}u_{k}\rangle=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\langle i\alpha_{k}u_{k},i\Gamma_{\phi_{R}}u_{k}\rangle,

taking the time derivative and using (
1.2
), we obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{R}(t)&=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\langle u_{k},\left[\Delta^{2},i\Gamma_{\phi_{R}}\right]u_{k}\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\langle u_{k},\left[f_{k}(\mathbf{u}),i\Gamma_{\phi_{R}}\right]u_{k}\rangle\\
&:=A-B,\end{split}

where
[X,Y]=XY-YX
. Note that the term
A
is essentially the same as
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[1]}
in
[
5
, Lemma 3.1]
, taking into account the suitable modifications for the vectorial case. Then we get

(6.5)

\begin{split}A&=8\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\sum_{j,n,m}\int\partial^{2}_{n,m}\phi_{R}\partial^{2}_{m,j}u_{k}\partial^{2}_{j,n}\bar{u}_{k}dx\\
&\quad-4\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\sum_{j,n}\int\partial_{j}\bar{u}_{k}\partial^{2}_{j,n}\Delta\phi_{R}\partial_{n}u_{k}dx\\
&\quad-2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int\Delta^{2}\phi_{R}|\nabla u_{k}|^{2}dx+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int\Delta^{3}\phi_{R}|u_{k}|^{2}dx.\\
\end{split}

For
B
, we use integration by parts and Lemma
3.3
to get

(6.6)

\begin{split}B&=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Re}\left[\int f_{k}(\mathbf{u})i\Gamma_{\phi_{R}}\bar{u}_{k}dx-\int\bar{u}_{k}i\Gamma_{\phi_{R}}f_{k}(\mathbf{u})dx\right]\\
&=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Re}\left[\int f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\nabla\phi_{R}\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}dx+\int f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\bar{u}_{k}\Delta\phi_{R}dx\right]\\
&\quad-2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Re}\left[\int\bar{u}_{k}\nabla\phi_{R}\cdot\nabla f_{k}(\mathbf{u})dx+\int f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\Delta\phi_{R}\bar{u}_{k}dx\right]\\
&=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Re}\left[2\int f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\nabla u_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}dx+\int f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\bar{u}_{k}\Delta\phi_{R}dx\right]\\
&=4\mathrm{Re}\int\nabla F(\mathbf{u})\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}dx+\frac{4d}{d-4}\mathrm{Re}\int F(\mathbf{u})\Delta\phi_{R}dx\\
&=\frac{16}{d-4}\mathrm{Re}\int F(\mathbf{u})\Delta\phi_{R}dx.\end{split}

The proof is complete by summing (
6.5
) and (
6.6
).
∎

Now, consider
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to be a radial function with regularity property
\nabla^{j}\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
1\leq j\leq 6
and such that

\phi(r)=\begin{cases}r^{2}/2,&r\leq 1\\
\hbox{constant},&r\geq 10\\
\end{cases}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\phi^{\prime\prime}(r)\leq 1,\hbox{ for }r\geq 0,

and we define, for
R>0
, the rescaled function
\phi_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\phi_{R}(r):=R^{2}\phi\left(\frac{r}{R}\right).

A straightforward calculation gives us that for all
r\geq 0
,

(6.7)

\phi_{R}^{\prime\prime}(r)\geq 1,\quad 1-\frac{\phi_{R}^{\prime}(r)}{r}\geq 0,\quad\Delta\phi_{R}(r)\leq d.

Then, we have the following result.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
5\leq d\leq 16
. Suppose that
\mathbf{u}
is radial solution of (
1.2
) defined on the interval
[0,T)
.

Then for any
t\in I
, we have

(6.8)

\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{R}(t)\leq\frac{16d}{d-4}E(\mathbf{u}_{0})-\frac{32}{d-4}K(\mathbf{u})+O\left(R^{-4}+R^{-\frac{4(d-1)}{d-4}}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{d-4}}+R^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be a radial solution of (
1.2
). Then, using integration by parts we have the bound (see
[
5
, Section 3.1]
for details)

(6.9)

8\langle u_{k},\partial_{j,n}^{2}(\partial^{2}_{n,m}\phi_{R})\partial_{m,j}u_{k}\rangle\leq 8\int|\Delta u_{k}|^{2},\quad k=1,\ldots,l.

Also, by definition of
\phi_{R}
, one can easily check that, for
k=1,\ldots,l
,

(6.10)

\begin{split}|\langle u_{k},\partial_{j}(\partial^{2}_{j,n}\Delta\phi_{R})\partial_{n}u_{k}\rangle|&\lesssim R^{-2}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
|\langle u_{k},\partial_{j}(\Delta^{2}\phi_{R})\partial_{l}\rangle|&\lesssim R^{-2}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
|\langle u_{k},\Delta^{3}\phi_{R}u_{k}\rangle|&\lesssim R^{-4}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\end{split}

Therefore, combining (
6.9
), (
6.10
) with Lemma
6.2
, we get

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{R}(t)&\lesssim 8K(\mathbf{u})+R^{-4}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+R^{-4}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{16}{d-4}\mathrm{Re}\int\left(\Delta\phi_{R}-d\right)F(\mathbf{u})dx,\end{split}

where we have used the fact that for
r\leq R
,
\phi_{R}(r)=r^{2}/2
and hence
\Delta\phi_{R}-d\equiv 0
. Now, Lemma
3.3
and Strauss inequality combined yields, for
k=1,\ldots,l
,

\int_{|x|\geq R}|u_{k}|^{\frac{2d}{d-4}}\leq R^{-\frac{4(d-1)}{d-4}}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{2d-4}{d-4}}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{d-4}},

which implies

\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{R}(t)\leq 8K(\mathbf{u})-\frac{16d}{d-4}P(\mathbf{u})+O\left(R^{-4}+R^{-\frac{4(d-1)}{d-4}}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{d-4}}+R^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

By de definition of energy (
1.4
) and energy conservation we arrive at (
6.8
).
∎

Now we are in position to prove Theorem
1.6

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Case 1:

E(\mathbf{u}_{0})<0
. By Lemma

we have that

\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{R}(t)\leq\frac{16d}{d-4}E(\mathbf{u}_{0})-\frac{32}{d-4}K(\mathbf{u})+O\left(R^{-4}+R^{-\frac{4(d-1)}{d-4}}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{d-4}}+R^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

A simply interpolation argument, combined with mass conservation gives us

. This allow us to choose

) implies that

for all

7.
Scattering

This section is devoted to prove the scattering result and, consequently, the global well-posedness in
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This will be done by the concentration-compactness and rigidity method. The argument here is very similar to the one in
[
33
]
, so we will focus on the proofs that the nonlinearities play important roles, which is the main difference in our work. Before start the procedure, we need the local well-posedness in
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This can be done as in the NLS case. Using the same argument as in
[
29
, Chapter 3]
, one can take a initial data in the inhomogeneous Sobolev space
\mathbf{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and get a local solution by using Corollary
4.2
. Once we have the solution, Lemma
4.6
plays a fundamental role in the argument. In addition to ensuring continuous dependence on the initial data, it also allows us to work with the initial data in the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by using an approximating procedure, since
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
functions is well approximated by
\mathbf{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
functions. The procedure is standard and for this reason we will omit the details. We summarize the result in the following Theorem.

Theorem 7.1
.

(Local well-posedness). Let
5\leq d\leq 16
. Given
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists a unique maximal-lifespan solution
\mathbf{u}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
to (
1.2
) with initial data
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}
. In addition, the solutions satisfies the following properties:

(i)

(Local existence)
I
is a open neighborhood of
0
.

(ii)

(Continuous dependence) If
\mathbf{u}_{0}^{(n)}
is a sequence converging to
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathbf{u}^{(n)}:I_{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
are the associated solutions to (
1.2
), then
\mathbf{u}^{(n)}
converge locally uniformly to the corresponding solution
\mathbf{u}
with initial data
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}
.

(iii)

(Blow-up criterion) If
\sup(I)
is finite, the
\mathbf{u}
blows-up forward in time. If
\inf(I)
is finite, then
\mathbf{u}
blows-up backward in time.

(iv)

(Small data global existence) If
K(\mathbf{u}_{0})
is sufficiently small (depending on
d
), then
\mathbf{u}
is a global solution which does not blow-up either forward or backward in time. In this case,
S_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{u})\lesssim K(\mathbf{u})^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}
.

We start by proving the existence of a critical solutions that is almost periodic modulo symmetries.

7.1.
Reduction to almost periodic solutions

We start this Section noticing that for any
0\leq K_{0}\leq K(\boldsymbol{\psi})
, the function

L(K_{0}):=\sup\left\{S_{I}(\mathbf{u});\,\mathbf{u}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}\hbox{ is a solution to \eqref{SISTB} such that }\sup_{t\in I}K(\mathbf{u}(t))\leq K_{0}\right\}.

Therefore,
L:[0,K(\boldsymbol{\psi})]\longrightarrow[0,\infty]
is a nondecreasing function and since the ground states are time independent we get
L(K(\boldsymbol{\psi}))=\infty
, for any
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{G}
. Also, from Corollary
4.3
, if
K_{0}
is sufficiently small then we deduce that
L(K_{0})\lesssim K_{0}^{\frac{d+4}{d-4}}
. Moreover, Lemma
4.6
yields that for any solution of (
1.2
), it is possible to find another solution with kinetic energy and scattering size close to the initial one; this implies the continuity of
L
. Hence, there must exist a
critical energy
, denoted by
K_{c}
, such that

L(K_{0})\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}<\infty,&\mbox{if}\quad K_{0}<K_{c},\\
=\infty,&\mbox{if}\quad K_{0}\geq K_{c}.\end{array}\right.

In particular, if
\mathbf{u}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
is a maximal solution such that
\sup_{t\in I}K(\mathbf{u}(t))<K_{c}
, then, from Proposition
4.5
,
\mathbf{u}
is global and

S_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{u})\leq L\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}K(\mathbf{u}(t))\right)<\infty.

The next result is essential to reach the goal of this subsection.

Proposition 7.2

(Palais-Smale condition)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{n}:I_{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
be a sequence of solutions of (
1.2
) such that

(7.1)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sup_{t\in I_{n}}\|\Delta\mathbf{u}_{n}(t)\|_{\mathbf{L}_{x}^{2}}^{2}\right)=K_{c}.

Assume there is a sequence of times
(t_{n})
with
t_{n}\in I_{n}
obeying

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S_{\geq t_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S_{\leq t_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{n})=\infty.

Then, the sequence
(\mathbf{u}_{n}(t_{n}))
has a subsequence that converges in
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}_{x}
modulo symmetries.

Proof.

We will omit the details since the proof of this result is essentially the same as the one in
[
28
, Proposition 3.1]
(see also,
[
29
, Proposition 5.6]
and
[
48
, Proposition 7.2, page 263]
). The only differences is that now we need to work with vector functions and in
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
instead of
{\dot{\mathbf{H}}}^{1}
. We also need Lemma
3.2
to work with the nonlinearities part in the proof. One can also check a fourth order Schrödinger equation version in
[
33
, Section 6]
.
∎

The main result of this subsection is the following.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
5\leq d\leq 16
be such that Theorem
1.7
fails. Then there exists a maximal-lifespan solution
\mathbf{u}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
to (
1.2
) such that

(7.2)

\sup_{t\in I}K(\mathbf{u}(t))<K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),

\mathbf{u}
is almost periodic modulo symmetries and blows-up in time. In addition,
\mathbf{u}
has minimal kinetic energy among all blow-up solutions, that is,

\sup_{t\in I}K(\mathbf{u}(t))\leq\sup_{t\in J}K(\mathbf{v}),

for all maximal-lifespan solutions
\mathbf{v}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
that blows-up at least in one direction.

Proof.

Since the nonlinearities plays no role in this proof we will omit the details. The reader can check the original proof, for the NLS case, in
[
28
, Section 3.2]
or the BNLS version in
[
33
, Theorem 6.1]
.
∎

7.2.
Coercivities estimates

In this subsection we establish some adapted versions of the coercivity lemmas appearing in
[
25
]
.

Lemma 7.4

(Coercivity I)
.

Assume that
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}_{x}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
\mathbf{u}
be a solution of (
1.2
) with maximal existence interval
I
. Let
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{G}
be a ground state. Suppose that

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{0})<(1-\tilde{\delta})\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\psi})
and
K(\mathbf{u}_{0})<K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

then there exists
\tilde{\delta}_{1}=\tilde{\delta}_{1}(\tilde{\delta})
such that

K(\mathbf{u}(t))<(1-\tilde{\delta}_{1})K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

for all
t\in I
.

Proof.

From the conservation of the energy and (
5.6
), we deduce

(7.3)

K(\mathbf{u}(t))\leq E(\mathbf{u}_{0})+C_{\rm opt}K(\mathbf{u}(t))^{\frac{d}{d-4}},\quad\forall t\in I.

Let
G(t)=K(\mathbf{u}(t))
,
a=E(\mathbf{u}_{0})
,
b=2C_{\rm opt}
and
q={\frac{d}{d-4}}
in Lemma
2.9
. Using (
5.32
), we get

\gamma=(bq)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}=\left(\frac{d-4}{2d}\frac{1}{C_{\rm opt}}\right)^{\frac{d-4}{4}}=K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Lemma
2.9
implies the result.
∎

Lemma 7.5

(Coercivity II)
.

Under hypothesis of Lemma
7.4
we have
If
K(\mathbf{u}_{0})<K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),
then there exists
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(\tilde{\delta})>0
such that

(7.4)

K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2d}{d-4}P(\mathbf{u}(t))\geq\delta^{\prime}K(\mathbf{u}(t)),

for all
t\in I
. Moreover,
E(\mathbf{u})\geq 0
.

Proof.

Using (
5.32
) Lemma
2.9
and Lemma
7.4
, we deduce

1-\frac{2d}{d-4}\frac{P(\mathbf{u}(t))}{K(\mathbf{u}(t))}\geq 1-\frac{2d}{d-4}C_{\rm opt}K(\mathbf{u}(t))^{\frac{4}{d-4}}=1-\left[\frac{K(\mathbf{u}(t))}{K(\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right]^{\frac{4}{d-4}}\geq 1-(1-\tilde{\delta}_{1})^{\frac{2}{d-4}}=:\delta^{\prime}.

Multiplying both sides by
K(\mathbf{u}(t))
we get the result. To prove that the energy in non negative, we observe that if
E(\mathbf{u})\geq E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
the result is trivial since
E(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})>0
. Otherwise, since

E(\mathbf{u})=K(\mathbf{u}(t))-2P(\mathbf{u}(t))=\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t))+\frac{d-4}{d}\left[K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2d}{d-4}P(\mathbf{u}(t))\right],

inequality (
7.4
) implies that
E(\mathbf{u})\geq 0
.
∎

Lemma 7.6

(Energy trapping)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be a solution of (
1.2
) with maximal existence interval
I
and initial data
\mathbf{u}_{0}
. If
E(\mathbf{u}_{0})\leq(1-\delta)E(\boldsymbol{\psi})
and
K(\mathbf{u}_{0})\leq(1-\delta^{\prime})K(\boldsymbol{\psi})
, then

K(\mathbf{u}(t))\sim E(\mathbf{u}(t)),\quad\forall t\in I.

Proof.

By (
5.32
) and
E(\mathbf{u}_{0})\leq(1-\delta)E(\boldsymbol{\psi})
we obtain

\begin{split}E(\mathbf{u}(t))&\leq K(\mathbf{u}(t))+2|P(\mathbf{u}(t))|\\
&\leq K(\mathbf{u}(t))+2C_{\rm opt}|K(\mathbf{u}(t))|^{\frac{d}{d-4}}\\
&\leq\left(1+2C_{\rm opt}[(1-\tilde{\delta}_{1})K(\boldsymbol{\psi})^{\frac{4}{d-4}}]\right)K(\mathbf{u}(t)).\end{split}

On the other hand,

\begin{split}E(\mathbf{u}(t))&\geq\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t))+\frac{d-2}{d}[K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2d}{d-2}P(\mathbf{u}(t))]\\
&\geq\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t))+\frac{d-2}{d}\delta^{\prime}K(\mathbf{u}(t))\\
&=\frac{2}{d}\left(1+\frac{d-2}{d}\delta^{\prime}\right)K(\mathbf{u}(t)).\end{split}

Combining both inequalities, we get the result.
∎

7.3.
The enemies

The next step to achieve our goal is to prove that the solution
\mathbf{u}_{c}
obtained in Theorem
7.3
cannot exist. In order to do this, we will see that this solution satisfy some extra properties with respect to the scale functions
N(t)
. The following result was first obtained in
[
27
]
and
[
28
]
for the Schrödinger equation and in
[
33
]
proved a similar result for the fourth order Shcrödinger equation. The proof does not rely on the specific form of the nonlinearity, so will be omitted. The reader can check the details on
[
28
, Theorem 1.17]
ou
[
33
, Theorem 7.1]
. The result is the following

Proposition 7.7

(The enemies)
.

Suppose that Theorem
1.7
fails. Then there exists a maximal solution
\mathbf{u}_{c}:I_{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
, which is almost periodic modulo symmetries and satisfies

(7.5)

S_{I_{c}}(\mathbf{u}_{c})=\infty\quad\hbox{and}\quad\sup_{t\in I_{c}}K(\mathbf{u}_{c}(t))<K(\boldsymbol{\psi}).

Moreover, the time interval
I_{c}
and the scale function
N(t)
satisfy one of the three following scenarios:

(i)

We have
|\inf I_{c}|<\infty
or
|\sup I_{c}|<\infty
;

(ii)

We have
I_{c}=\mathbb{R}
and

N(t)=1,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R};

(iii)

We have
I_{c}=\mathbb{R}
and

\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}N(t)\geq 1,\quad\hbox{and}\quad\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}N(t)=\infty.

In the literature,(see, for instance,
[
28
]
) the three scenarios are known, respectively, as
finite-time blow-up
,
soliton-like solution
and
low-to-high frequency cascade
. The whole idea is to prove that none of this kind of solutions can exist and then, arrive at a contradiction with the existence of
\mathbf{u}_{c}
. The first result in this direction is to preclude the existence of finite-time blow-up solution, but before we state the result, we need the following Lemma.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
\mathbf{u}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
be a maximal-lifespan solution to (
1.2
) that is almost periodic modulo symmetries with frequency scale function
N:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. If
\sup I<\infty
, then

(7.6)

\liminf_{t\nearrow\sup I}N(t)=\infty.

A similar results holds if
|\inf I|<\infty
.

Proof.

Since is related to almost periodic modulo symmetries condition and does not rely on the nonlinearities, we will omit it. The details can be find as part of the proof of Theorem 5.1 in
[
28
]
or in
[
27
, Corollary 3.7]
.

∎

Now we are able to preclude the finite-time blow-up solution. The idea of the proof is analogous as the one in
[
28
, Theorem 5.1]
and since the nonlinearities does not play a important role, we will just sketch it for completion. The result read as follows.

Theorem 7.9
.

Let
5\leq d\leq 16
. There are no maximal radial almost periodic modulo symmetries solutions obeying which blow-up in finite time, in the sense of Proposition
7.7
.

Sketch of the proof.

Suppose, contrary to our claim, that there exist a maximal radial solution
\mathbf{u}_{c}:I_{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{l}
that is almost periodic modulo symmetries and blows-up in finite time. We assume that
\sup I<\infty
, the other case is proved in the same way. As a consequence of (
7.6
) and Hölder’s inequality we get, for
\mathbf{u}_{c}=(u_{c1},\ldots,u_{cl})
, that

(7.7)

\limsup_{t\nearrow\sup I_{c}}\int_{|x|\leq R}|u_{ck}|^{2}dx=0,\quad\forall R>0,\,k=1,\ldots,l.

Now, for
t\in I
, we define

V_{R}(t):=\int\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}|u_{ck}|^{2}\right)dx,

where
\phi
is a smooth radial function obeying
\phi(r)=1
for
r\leq 1
and
\phi(r)=0
for
r\geq 2
. Thus, (
7.7
) yields

(7.8)

\limsup_{t\nearrow\sup I_{c}}V_{R}(t)=0,\quad\forall R>0.

On the other hand,

V_{R}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\left(-2\mathrm{Im}\int\alpha_{k}\Delta\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{ck}\Delta u_{ck}dx-2\mathrm{Im}\int\nabla\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\nabla\bar{u}_{ck}\Delta u_{ck}dx\right).

Hence, by Hardy’s inequality (see
[
46
, Lemma A.2]
) and (
7.5
), we get

\begin{split}|V^{\prime}_{R}(t)|\lesssim K(\mathbf{u}_{c})\lesssim K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).\end{split}

where the implicit constant is independent of
R>0
. Applying the fundamental theorem of calculus, using (
7.8
) and letting
t_{2}\nearrow\sup I_{c}

leads us to

V_{R}(t_{1})\lesssim|\sup I_{c}-t_{1}|K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

If we take
R\rightarrow\infty
, by the conservation laws we obtain
\mathbf{u}_{c,0}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Finally, taking
t_{1}\nearrow\sup I_{c}
, we conclude that
\mathbf{u}_{c}=0
, contradicting (
7.5
).
∎

In order to exclude the solitons and cascade we will need some tools. We first show a virial-type inequality as follows.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
\phi
be a smooth function with
\phi(r)=1
if
r\leq 1
and
\phi(r)=0
if
r\geq 2
. Set

z_{R}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Im}\int\alpha_{k}x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}u_{k}dx.

Then,

(7.9)

z_{R}^{\prime}(t)\geq 4\left(K(\mathbf{u})-\frac{2d}{d-4}P(\mathbf{u})\right)-O\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int_{R\leq|x|\leq 2R}\left(\frac{|u_{k}||\Delta u_{k}|}{R^{2}}+\frac{|\nabla u_{k}||\Delta u_{k}|}{R}+|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}\right)dx\right).

Proof.

We start noticing that by using integration by parts and
\mathrm{Im}(\bar{z})=-\mathrm{Im}(z)
, we have

(7.10)

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Im}\int\alpha_{k}&x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla(\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{k})u_{k}dx=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\partial_{x_{j}}(\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{k})u_{k}dx\\
&=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{j}}\left(x_{j}\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)u_{k}\right)\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{k}dx\\
&=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\left[\int\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{j}}\left(x_{j}\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right){u}_{k}\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{k}dx+\int\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\partial_{x_{j}}{u}_{k}\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{k}dx\right]\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int\nabla\cdot\left(x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right)\bar{u}_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}dx+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}dx.\end{split}

Therefore, differentiating
z_{R}
with respect to t and using (
7.10
) we get

\begin{split}z_{R}^{\prime}(t)&=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Im}\int\alpha_{k}x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\partial_{t}\left(\nabla\bar{u}_{k}u_{k}\right)dx\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Im}\int\alpha_{k}x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla(\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{k})u_{k}dx+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Im}\int\alpha_{k}x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}dx\\
&=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}dx+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int\nabla\cdot\left(x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right)\bar{u}_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}dx\\
&:=A+B\\
\end{split}

Let us compute
A
. Using (
1.2
), integration by parts and Lemma
3.3
(iii)

(7.11)

\begin{split}A&=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\Delta^{2}u_{k}dx-2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Re}\int x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}f_{k}(\mathbf{u})dx\\
&=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\Delta\left(x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\right)\Delta u_{k}-2\mathrm{Re}\int x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla F(\mathbf{u})dx\\
&:=A_{1}-A_{2}\\
\end{split}

For
A_{1}
, first notice that since
\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)
is radial, a straightforward calculation gives us

(7.12)

\nabla\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)=\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{x}{R|x|}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\Delta\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)=\frac{1}{R^{2}}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)+\frac{(d+1)}{R|x|}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right).

Also, using integration by parts, for
k=1,\ldots,l
,

(7.13)

2\mathrm{Re}\int x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla(\Delta\bar{u}_{k})\Delta u_{k}dx=-\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{|x||\Delta u_{k}|^{2}}{R}dx-d\mathrm{Re}\int\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dx.

Therefore, (
7.12
) and (
7.13
) yields

(7.14)

\begin{split}A_{1}&=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\left[2\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\Delta\bar{u}_{k}+2\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)+x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\cdot\nabla(\Delta\bar{u}_{k})\right.\\
&\quad+\left.2x\cdot\nabla\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\Delta\bar{u}_{k}+x\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\Delta\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right]\Delta u_{k}dx\\
&=(4-d)\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dx+2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{(d+2)x\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}}{R|x|}dx\\
&\quad+3\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{|x||\Delta u_{k}|^{2}}{R}dx+2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{x\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}}{R^{2}}dx.\end{split}

For
A_{2}
, using once again integration by parts,

(7.15)

\begin{split}A_{2}&=-2\mathrm{Re}\int\nabla\left(x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right)\nabla F(\mathbf{u})dx=-2d\mathrm{Re}\int\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)F(\mathbf{u})dx-2\mathrm{Re}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{|x|F(\mathbf{u})}{R}dx.\\
\end{split}

Before we calculate B, we notice that

\nabla\cdot\left(x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{j}}\left(x_{j}\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right)=d\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)+\frac{|x|}{R}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right).

Therefore, using (
1.2
)

\begin{split}B&=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int\nabla\cdot\left(x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right)\bar{u}_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}dx\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\nabla\cdot\left(x\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\right)\bar{u}_{k}\left(\gamma_{k}\Delta^{2}u_{k}-f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\right)\\
&=d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{k}\Delta^{2}u_{k}dx+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\frac{|x|}{R}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{k}\Delta^{2}u_{k}dx\\
&\quad-d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{k}f_{k}(\mathbf{u})dx-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Re}\int\frac{|x|}{R}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{k}f_{k}(\mathbf{u})dx\\
&:=B_{1}+B_{2}-B_{3}-B_{4}.\end{split}

Using integration by parts twice we have

\begin{split}B_{1}&=d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{k}\Delta^{2}u_{k}dx\\
&=d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}\left(\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{k}\right)\Delta u_{k}dx\\
&=d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\frac{(d-1)}{R|x|}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}dx+d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}}{R^{2}}dx\\
&\quad+2d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{x\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}}{R|x|}dx+d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\int\phi|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}dx.\\
\end{split}

In the same way,

\begin{split}B_{2}&=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{|x|\bar{u}_{k}}{R}\right)\Delta u_{k}dx\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{(d-1)\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}}{R|x|}dx+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{(d+1)\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}}{R^{2}}dx\\

\end{split}

Gathering all together, we have

\begin{split}B&=d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\phi\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\left(\gamma_{k}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}-\bar{u}_{k}f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\right)dx+2d\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{x\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}}{R|x|}dx\\

&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{|x|(\gamma_{k}|\Delta u_{k}|^{2}-\bar{u}_{k}f_{k}(\mathbf{u}))}{R}dx\\
&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{(d-1)}{R|x|}+\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{(d+1)}{R^{2}}+\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{|x|}{R^{3}}\right]\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}dx\\
&\quad 2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Re}\int\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{1}{R|x|}+\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)\frac{1}{R^{2}}\right]x\cdot\nabla\bar{u}_{k}\Delta u_{k}dx.\end{split}

) follows by using Lemma
