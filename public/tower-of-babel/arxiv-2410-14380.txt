Dual-Label Learning With Irregularly Present Labels
Mingqian Li, Qiao Han, Yiteng Zhai, Ruifeng Li, Yao Yang, Hongyang Chen

Abstract
In multi-task learning, we often encounter the case when the presence of labels across samples exhibits irregular patterns: samples can be fully labeled, partially labeled or unlabeled. Taking drug analysis as an example, multiple toxicity properties of a drug molecule may not be concurrently available due to experimental limitations. It triggers a demand for a new training and inference mechanism that could accommodate irregularly present labels and maximize the utility of any available label information. In this work, we focus on the two-label learning task, and propose a novel training and inference framework, Dual-Label Learning (DLL). The DLL framework formulates the problem into a dual-function system, in which the two functions should simultaneously satisfy standard supervision, structural duality and probabilistic duality. DLL features a dual-tower model architecture that explicitly captures the information exchange between labels, aimed at maximizing the utility of partially available labels in understanding label correlation. During training, label imputation for missing labels is conducted as part of the forward propagation process, while during inference, labels are regarded as unknowns of a bivariate system of equations and are solved jointly. Theoretical analysis guarantees the feasibility of DLL, and extensive experiments are conducted to verify that by explicitly modeling label correlation and maximizing the utility of available labels, our method makes consistently better predictions than baseline approaches by up to a 10% gain in F1-score or MAPE. Remarkably, our method provided with data at a label missing rate as high as 60% can achieve similar or even better results than baseline approaches at a label missing rate of only 10%.

In multi-task learning

, labels across samples often exhibit irregular patterns. For instance, in drug analysis, multiple toxicity properties of a drug molecule might not be concurrently available due to experimental limitations
(

)
, material synthesis
(
Luo et al. 2022
)
, and even beyond scientific research, such as in image annotation
(
Huynh and Elhamifar 2020
;
Cour, Sapp, and Taskar 2011
)
and music emotion classification
(
Trohidis et al. 2008
)
.

Irregular label presence presents a significant challenge for machine learning tasks. Traditional multi-task learning methods
(
Zhang and Yang 2021
)
, particularly multi-label learning
(
Tarekegn, Ullah, and Cheikh 2024
)
, typically assume fully labeled samples during training, rendering them ineffective when handling missing labels. Although approaches like matrix completion or graph neural networks have been proposed to address missing labels, they are largely confined to multi-label classification tasks
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
.
Neither the recent advancements like large language models
(
Romera-Paredes et al. 2024
;
Xie et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2022
)
nor foundation models
(
Moor et al. 2023
;
Bodnar et al. 2024
)
are applicable for tackling irregularly present labels in practice when large-scaled data supports are unavailable. Irregular label presence would deteriorate the model performance in prediction tasks.

The practical challenge necessitates a new learning mechanism capable of accommodating irregular labels and maximizing the use of any available label information from data. To meet this need, we envision a novel training and inference framework, which is tailored to handle any potential existence of missing labels and thoroughly learn from all available labels during the training process, and to fully utilize the known partial labels to infer the unknown labels during the inference process. This can be achieved by explicitly modeling correlations among labels. Labels are often correlated, yet a closed form expression of the correlation may be undefined. Existing multi-task learning methods learn label correlations rather indirectly via shared feature inputs and share-bottom architecture among tasks (Figure

(
Zhang and Yang 2021
;
Tarekegn, Ullah, and Cheikh 2024
)
.

To address these limitations, we draw inspiration from dual learning to enhance multi-task learning performance. Originally designed for learning from unlabeled data, Dual Learning
(
Qin 2020
)
leverages structural duality between tasks, where the output of one task serves as the input to another, creating a feedback loop (Figure

(b)). Similarly, we design a feedback loop for the two-label correlation

The DLL framework formulates the problem as a dual-function system, as shown in Figure

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We address the significant challenge of irregularly present labels in multi-task learning by introducing a novel Dual-Label Learning (DLL) framework. This framework uniquely formulates the problem into a dual-function system, wherein both functions are required to simultaneously satisfy standard supervision, structural duality and probabilistic duality (Section
2.1
). Through rigorous theoretical analysis, we establish a generalization bound for DLL, grounded in Rademacher complexity, providing a robust theoretical foundation for its efficacy (Section
2.2
).

•

DLL introduces an innovative dual-tower model architecture, specifically designed to harness the interdependencies between labels. Such architecture allows for explicit information exchange between labels, effectively maximizing the utility of partially available labels and enhancing the understanding of label correlations (Section
3
). Further, we propose a novel training scheme that incorporates label imputation directly into the forward propagation (Section
3.2
), and an inference scheme that jointly solves for missing labels as unknowns within a bivariate system of equations (Section
3.3
).

•

Extensive experiments demonstrate the superiority of DLL in various domains, showcasing its ability to outperform baselines consistently. Our results indicate that DLL achieves up to a 10% improvement in F1-score or MAPE, demonstrating its effectiveness in scenarios with high rates of missing labels. Notably, DLL maintains robust performance even when up to 60% of the labels are missing, achieving results comparable to or better than baseline methods that operate under far less challenging conditions (Section
4
). This highlights the framework’s resilience and potential for broad application across different fields where data irregularities are prevalent.

2.1
Problem Formulation

Consider a two-label learning problem where
i)
labels are missing irregularly and independently and
ii)
labels are correlated without a defined closed form expression. We propose a Dual-Label Learning (DLL) framework, which formulates the problem into a dual-function system.

DLL operates on a feature space
\mathcal{X}
and two label spaces
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Z}
. Assume we have a dataset
\mathcal{S}={\{(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})\}}_{i=1}^{n}
drawn
i.i.d.
from the space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}
according to an unknown underlying distribution
\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}}
. We aim to predict the two labels
y
and
z
simultaneously from features
x
by finding the following two functions: (i) a
primal
function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}
to predict
z
as close as possible to its ground truth; and (ii) a
dual
function
g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
to predict
y
as close as possible to its ground truth (Figure
2
(d)). The two models
f
and
g
are parameterized by
\theta_{yz}
and
\theta_{zy}
, respectively:

\begin{split}f(x,y;\theta_{yz}):=arg\mathop{max}\limits_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}P(z^{\prime}|x,y;\theta_{yz}),\\
g(x,z;\theta_{zy}):=arg\mathop{max}\limits_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}P(y^{\prime}|x,z;\theta_{zy}),\end{split}

(1)

and are supervised by losses
l_{1}(f(x,y),z)
and
l_{2}(g(x,z),y)
, respectively. The values of labels
y
and
z
for a particular sample can be inferred by solving a bivariate system of equations in a simple, symmetric format as follows:

\begin{cases}z=f(x,y;\theta_{yz}),\\
y=g(x,z;\theta_{zy}).\end{cases}

(2)

We consider irregualrly present labels, where the labels
y_{i}
’s and/or
z_{i}
’s are missing completely at random. Therefore, the samples in dataset
\mathcal{S}
can be divided into 4 sub-datasets:
\mathcal{S}_{l}
,
\mathcal{S}_{y}
,
\mathcal{S}_{z}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{u}
as illustrated in Figure
2
(e). Let
I_{l},I_{y},I_{z},I_{u}
denote the indices of samples in
\mathcal{S}_{l},\mathcal{S}_{y},\mathcal{S}_{z},\mathcal{S}_{u}
respectively.

We first state two objectives that are naturally derived from the two loss functions via supervised learning on
\mathcal{S}_{l}
:

\mathop{min}\limits_{\theta_{yz}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{l}|}\sum_{i\in I_{l}}l_{1}(f(x_{i},y_{i};\theta_{yz}),z_{i}),

(3)

\mathop{min}\limits_{\theta_{zy}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{l}|}\sum_{i\in I_{l}}l_{2}(g(x_{i},z_{i};\theta_{zy}),y_{i}).

(4)

We also leverage the partially available labels in
\mathcal{S}_{y}
and
\mathcal{S}_{z}
. Inspired by the dual learning framework in machine translation
(
He et al. 2016
)
, we utilize the structural duality of
f
and
g
, and state two additional objectives to minimize the reconstruction errors in a two-agent communication game between
f
and
g
:

\mathop{min}\limits_{\theta_{yz},\theta_{zy}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{l}|+|\mathcal{S}_{y}|}\sum_{i\in I_{l}\cup I_{y}}l_{2}(g(x_{i},f(x_{i},y_{i};\theta_{yz});\theta_{zy}),y_{i}),

(5)

\mathop{min}\limits_{\theta_{yz},\theta_{zy}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{l}|+|\mathcal{S}_{z}|}\sum_{i\in I_{l}\cup I_{z}}l_{1}(f(x_{i},g(x_{i},z_{i};\theta_{zy});\theta_{yz}),z_{i}).

(6)

To further illustrate the idea of the structural duality, taking a sample
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{S}_{y}
, a well-trained
f
would map
(x_{i},y_{i})
to
\hat{z}_{i}
close to the underlying ground truth
z_{i}
, and from
(x_{i},\hat{z}_{i})
, a well-trained
g
would reconstruct
\hat{y}_{i}
close to the ground truth
y_{i}
. This explains the formulation of the reconstruction loss in objective (
5
). A similar logic applies to objective (
6
).

We also utilize the probabilistic duality of
f
and
g
, similar to
(
Xia et al. 2017b
)
. According to Bayes’ Theorem,
f
and
g
should satisfy the following equality constraint:

\begin{split}P(y|x)P(z|x,y;\theta_{yz})=P(z|x)P(y|x,z;\theta_{zy}),\ \forall x,y,z,\end{split}

(7)

where
P(y|x)
can be estimated via supervised learning on
\mathcal{S}_{l}\cup\mathcal{S}_{y}
, and
P(z|x)
can be estimated via supervised learning on
\mathcal{S}_{l}\cup\mathcal{S}_{z}
.

To summarize, DLL aims to solve the constrained multi-objective optimization problem:

\begin{split}\mathop{min}\limits_{\theta_{yz},\theta_{zy}}\ &\text{(\ref{obj1}), (\ref{obj2}), (\ref{obj3}), (\ref{obj4})}\\
s.t.\ &\text{(\ref{probabilistic_duality})}.\end{split}

(8)

2.2
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we prove a theoretical guarantee for DLL in terms of the generalization error bound based on Rademacher complexity.

For ease of notation, we first introduce an auxiliary vector
u\in\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
to indicate which labels are known/missing in a sample
(x,y,z)
:

u:=(u^{(y)},u^{(z)}):=(\mathbb{I}[y\text{ is known}],\mathbb{I}[z\text{ is known}]).

(9)

For example, a sample
i\in I_{l}
is associated with an indicator
u_{i}=(1,1)
. By the randomness assumption, we have
u\perp(x,y,z)
.

Consider the space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{U}
. Then the dataset
\mathcal{S}
can been seen as drawn together with
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from a fixed distribution
\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{U}}
. Let
\mathcal{F}=\{f(x,y;\theta_{yz});\theta_{yz}\in\Theta_{yz}\}
be the primal function space, and
\mathcal{G}=\{g(x,z;\theta_{zy});\theta_{zy}\in\Theta_{zy}\}
be the dual function space, where
\Theta_{yz}
and
\Theta_{yz}
are parameter spaces.

Theoretically, DLL is to minimize the expected risk defined as

R(f,g)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[l_{f,g}(x,y,z,u)],\forall(f,g)\in\mathcal{H},

(10)

where the loss function
l_{f,g}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is

\begin{split}l_{f,g}(x,y,z,u)=&\ \alpha_{1}\mathbb{I}[u=(1,1)]\cdot l_{1}(f(x,y),z)\\
+&\ \alpha_{2}\mathbb{I}[u=(1,1)]\cdot l_{2}(g(x,z),y)\\
+&\ \alpha_{3}\mathbb{I}[u^{(y)}=1]\cdot l_{2}(g(x,f(x,y)),y)\\
+&\ \alpha_{4}\mathbb{I}[u^{(z)}=1]\cdot l_{1}(f(x,g(x,z)),z),\end{split}

(11)

which is a linear combination of objectives (
3
-
6
) given an arbitrary set of coefficients
\alpha\in[0,1]^{4}
subject to
\sum_{j=1}^{4}\alpha_{j}=1
, and
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{G}
denotes the subspace of
(f,g)
that satisfies the equality constraint (
7
). For simplicity, we only consider loss functions
l_{1}:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow[0,1]
and
l_{2}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,1]
, and obviously, it follows that
l_{f,g}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[0,1]
.

The corresponding empirical risk is defined on the
n
samples as follows:

R_{n}(f,g)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in n}l_{f,g}(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i},u_{i}),\forall(f,g)\in\mathcal{H}.

(12)

Now we are ready to introduce Rademacher complexity
(
Mohri, Rostamizadeh, and Talwalkar 2018
)
.

Definition 1

(Rademacher complexity)
.

The Rademacher complexity of DLL is defined as

R_{n}^{DLL}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\mathop{sup}\limits_{(f,g)\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\cdot l_{f,g}(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i},u_{i})]],

(13)

where
\sigma_{i}
’s are i.i.d. variables uniformly drawn from
\{-1,1\}
, known as Rademacher variables, and
\mathcal{D}
is short for
\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{U}}
.

Then according to
(
Mohri, Rostamizadeh, and Talwalkar 2018
)
, we can derive a guarantee of the generalization error bound for DLL based on Rademacher complexity.

Theorem 1
.

For any
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following inequality holds for all
(f,g)\in\mathcal{H}
:

R(f,g)\leq R_{n}(f,g)+2R_{n}^{DLL}+\sqrt{\frac{log\frac{1}{\delta}}{2n}}.

(14)

3.1
Model architecture

We devise a novel dual-tower architecture to model the dual functions
f
and
g
in a single framework, as shown in Figure
3
(a-e). Here,
f
is modeled by Tower 2 along with its parent nodes, while
g
is modeled by Tower 1 along with its parent nodes. Note that
f
and
g
partially share parameters to model their common input
X
. To facilitate notation, for the rest of the paper, we denote the shared parameters separately by
\theta_{0}
(in blue blocks), leaving the rest of the parameters as
\theta_{yz}
(in green blocks) and
\theta_{zy}
(in orange blocks). Duality manifests itself on the top of the architecture, where the output
z
of
f
serves as an input in
g
, while the output
y
of
g
serves as an input in
f
. It ties with the symmetric equation system in (
2
).

The configurations of the two towers and their parent nodes are flexible, subject to the specific task to fulfil (
e.g.
binary classification, multi-class classification, or regression), the input data format (
e.g.
images, text, or graphs), and the choice of building blocks (
e.g.
attention, fully connected layer, or mixture of experts).

4
Experiments

We conduct extensive experiments on both real-world and synthetic datasets from various domains to evaluate the effectiveness of the DLL framework, including the dual-tower model and its training and inference algorithms.

4.1
Datasets

Experiments are conducted on three dataset: Tox21, HIGGS and MOF. The Tox21 dataset
(
Huang et al. 2016
)
is a real-world dataset that entails the toxicity properties of 7,831 chemical compounds for drug analysis. The HIGGS dataset
(
Baldi, Sadowski, and Whiteson 2014
)
is a synthetic dataset that entails the kinetic properties of 10,000 particles in high-energy physic. The MOF dataset
(
Luo et al. 2022
)
is a real-world dataset that entails the structure information and synthesis conditions of 729 metal-organic framework materials.
Full details are available in the Appendix.

4.2
Experimental settings

Tasks and evaluation metrics:

Subject to label presence, prediction tasks fall into two types: single-label prediction for semi-labeled samples, of which only one label is to be predicted; and double-label prediction for unlabeled samples, of which both labels are to be predicted. As for evaluation metrics, the F1-score is primarily adopted to evaluate binary classification on the Tox21 dataset, for which a higher F1-score indicates better performance; and the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) is adopted to evalute regression on HIGGS and MOF datasets, for which a lower MAPE indicates better performance.

Baseline approaches:

DLL is compared with the most comparable learning schemes, including
Independent Decomposition (ID) which ignores label correlation, Co-Occurrence Learning (COL) and Semi-Supervised Learning (SSL) which model label correlation indirectly, Label Stacking (LS) and Dual Set Multi-Label Learning (DSML)
(
Liu et al. 2018
)
which directly model label correlation, but in limited label communication stages. Details of baseline approaches are available in the Appendix.

Detailed settings of the hardware, data pre-processing, baseline selection, the model architecture, training and inference are available in the Appendix.

5
Related Work

5.1
Dual Learning

The dual learning paradigm was first proposed in machine translation
(
He et al. 2016
)
, which introduces the structural duality between two tasks to leverage unlabeled data. Dual Supervised Learning
(
Xia et al. 2017b
)
introduced the probabilistic duality as a regularization term. Dual Inference
(
Xia et al. 2017a
)
leverages structural duality during the inference stage, combining the loss functions of dual tasks to improve inference. Dual learning was connected to various domains including transfer learning
(
Wang et al. 2018
)
, computer vision
(
Yi et al. 2017
;
Guo et al. 2020
)
, and speech processing
(
Xu et al. 2020
)
.
(
Qin 2020
)
provides a systematic review of recent research on dual learning. DLL also leverages structural duality and probabilistic duality during training and inference. The difference is discussed in the introduction.

5.2
Multi-task Learning and Multi-label Learning

Multi-task learning
(
Zhang and Yang 2021
)
is a learning scheme that leverages useful information from multiple related tasks. DLL is proposed for multi-task learning with irregularly present labels. Popular multi-task learning approaches include Multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts (MMoE)
(
Ma et al. 2018
)
, which features multiple expert networks which are selectively activated during training and inference by a gating mechanism to serve difference tasks.
In DLL, the dual-tower model is adapted from MMoE. We replace the gating mechanism with output labels from the other tower
2
2

2

This is equivalent to an implicitly hard-coded 0-1 gate
to facilitate the special treatment for missing labels.

Multi-label learning
(
Tarekegn, Ullah, and Cheikh 2024
)
is a special case of multi-task learning. The term is typically reserved for the context in computer vision where multiple binary labels are assigned to the same image, and tasks often share the same input space and feature representation. In comparison, DLL caters to both classification and regression, and it features a feedback mechanism among tasks.
In multi-label learning, Dual Set Multi-Label Learning (DSML)
(
Liu et al. 2018
)
is the closest to DLL in terms of problem definition, but DSML is limited to binary classification tasks and fails to accommodate missing labels.

5.3
Partial Label Learning

“Partial label learning” is ambiguously termed in two contexts. It may refer multi-label classification that deals with partially missing labels in training data
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Bucak, Jin, and Jain 2011
)
, where labels often share the same space. In comparison, DLL applies to both classification and regression tasks with irregularly present labels, where dual labels can come from difference label spaces. The term may also refer to ambiguously labeled learning
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, where each training sample is associated with a candidate label set, only one or a few out of which are ground-truth labels
(
Tian, Yu, and Fu 2023
)
.

5.4
Others

We compare DLL with a few more works that share similar inspirations. First, DLL relies on a fundamental assumption that the two predicted labels are highly correlated and the correlation can be learnt from data. Similarly, PEMAL
(
Li et al. 2024
)
leverages an explicit formula describing the label correlation and directly integrates it into the model architecture. Second, the dual-tower model is designed for direct information exchange between the two towers to complement missing labels. This is similar to X-learner
(
Künzel et al. 2019
)
in causal inference, which cross-trains models by interchanging predicted responses between two treatment groups to complement counterfactuals. Finally, DLL aims at finding solutions to certain equations during alternate inference. Similarly, PINN
(
Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis 2019
)
learns a data-driven solution to partial differential equations by enforcing physical laws as regularization terms during training.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduce Dual-Label Learning (DLL), a novel framework for training and inference in multi-task learning scenarios where labels are irregularly present. DLL defines a dual-function system that simultaneously fulfills standard supervision, structural duality, and probabilistic duality. At its core, DLL employs a dual-tower model architecture and innovative training and inference algorithms designed to directly model label correlations, maximizing the utility of available labels. Extensive experiments show that DLL consistently outperforms baseline methods.

Looking ahead, there are opportunities to extend DLL beyond its current limitations. Currently, DLL is designed for two-label scenarios, and while it can be adapted to multi-label cases by enumerating label pairs and running DLL multiple times, this approach may limit its ability to fully capture complex label correlations. We aim to develop more sophisticated algorithms to address the intricate patterns that arise with an increasing number of labels per sample. Additionally, the alternative inference scheme used in DLL raises questions about convergence, particularly as the number of unknown labels grows. Future research should focus on establishing theoretical guarantees for convergence in both two-label and multi-label contexts. We also plan to enhance DLL’s capabilities to address a broader range of tasks and applications.
