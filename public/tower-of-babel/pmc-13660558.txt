Pixel-Wise Statistical Mapping with Voltage-Sensitive Dye Imaging Reveals Differential Hippocampal Circuit Effects of Bisphenol A and Related Phenolic Analogs
Authors: Yoko Tominaga, Makiko Taketoshi, Kentaro Tanemura, Takashi Tominaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exposure to environmental chemicals such as bisphenol A (BPA) may affect neural circuit function in subtle and spatially distributed ways that are difficult to attribute to a single circuit element. Here, we developed a voltage-sensitive dye (VSD) imaging analysis pipeline for acute male mouse hippocampal slices. This approach measures stimulus-evoked fractional changes in VSD fluorescence (ΔF/F0) to generate pixel-wise normalized maps, averages response magnitude within anatomically defined regions of interest (ROIs), and visualizes control-versus-compound differences using pixel-wise statistical maps before and after false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment. We applied this pipeline to compare the acute effects of BPA with those of two structurally related phenolic analogs. Evoked responses were quantified by their peak amplitude and time integral across hippocampal trisynaptic pathways at two stimulation levels determined from input–output relationships, with or without the GABAA receptor antagonist gabazine (SR-95531; 10 µM). The phenolic analogs reduced circuit responses across multiple ROI-defined conditions, whereas BPA produced comparatively limited effects. These alterations varied with pathway, subfield, age, response metric, and inhibitory state. These findings demonstrate the utility of integrating ROI averages with pixel-wise statistical maps for detecting condition-dependent circuit-level differences between structurally related compounds and indicate that BPA-related phenolic analogs can differ from BPA itself in their acute effects on hippocampal circuitry.

Significance Statement

Exposure to environmental chemicals may alter brain circuit activity in subtle, spatially distributed ways. We developed a voltage-sensitive dye imaging pipeline for acute mouse hippocampal slices that combines regional summaries with pixel-wise statistical maps, quantifying regional response magnitude while localizing where statistically supported differences are expressed within evoked response fields. Using this framework, we compared the acute hippocampal circuit effects of bisphenol A (BPA) with those of two structurally related phenolic analogs. Under acute slice conditions, statistically supported response reductions were detected more consistently for the phenolic analogs than for BPA, depending on age, inhibitory state, hippocampal subfield, and response metric. These results show how integrated regional and pixel-wise analysis improves circuit-level phenotyping of structurally related compounds.

Introduction

Environmental chemicals are widely encountered and difficult to avoid, and exposure may influence neural development and function; the central nervous system is a plausible target for such perturbations (Mendola et al., 2002; Grandjean and Landrigan, 2006, 2014; Bellinger, 2018; Aggarwal et al., 2022). However, linking chemical exposure to circuit-level physiological mechanisms remains challenging because functional effects may be subtle, spatially distributed, and dependent on developmental stage and network state.

Bisphenol A [BPA; 2,2-bis(4-hydroxyphenyl)propane] is widely used in polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins and is commonly used as an experimental reference compound in studies of endocrine-disrupting chemicals (Rochester, 2013; Bao et al., 2020; La Merrill et al., 2020). BPA has been reported to affect endocrine signaling and multiple physiological systems, including neural and behavioral endpoints (Fisher et al., 1999; Wetherill et al., 2007; Palanza et al., 2008; Romano et al., 2016; Donato et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2019; Sasaki et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). At the same time, the increasing use of BPA-related phenolic compounds has raised concern about possible regrettable substitution and about whether structurally related compounds have similar biological activities (Trasande, 2017; Pelch et al., 2019). Whether such compounds produce similar or distinct acute effects on neural circuit function remains poorly understood.

Two such compounds, 4,4′-butylidenebis(6-tert-butyl-m-cresol) (BB; CAS 85-60-9) and 2,2′-methylenebis(6-tert-butyl-p-cresol) (MB; CAS 119-47-1), are structurally related phenolic antioxidants whose acute effects on hippocampal circuit function have not been directly compared with those of BPA under standardized physiological conditions.

The hippocampus provides a well-characterized and tractable circuit for quantifying mesoscale network dynamics because its trisynaptic pathways connecting the dentate gyrus (DG), CA3, and CA1 support robust stimulus-evoked propagation and are described by extensive theoretical and experimental frameworks (Eichenbaum, 2004; Squire, 2009; Bartsch and Wulff, 2015; Schapiro et al., 2017). Acute hippocampal slices offer a controlled preparation for comparative circuit-level phenotyping of perturbations that alter synaptic and network functions.

A key obstacle in circuit phenotyping is that region-of-interest (ROI) averaging provides a robust summary of regional response magnitude but does not resolve where within an evoked response field condition-dependent effects are spatially expressed. Single-site electrophysiology provides high temporal resolution but samples only restricted locations. Voltage-sensitive dye (VSD) imaging enables simultaneous measurement of fast membrane potential dynamics over large fields of view (Salzberg et al., 1973; Cohen et al., 1978; Grinvald and Hildesheim, 2004; Knöpfel and Song, 2019) and has been applied to quantify spatially extended activity patterns in hippocampal and cortical preparations (Tominaga et al., 2018; Kajiwara et al., 2019; Gusain et al., 2023; Milicevic et al., 2023; Utsumi et al., 2023; Ojima et al., 2024; Tsukuda et al., 2025). However, pixel-wise statistical evaluation of VSD imaging data remains less standardized than ROI-based analysis, particularly when spatial maps are interpreted together with slice-level replication and correction for multiple pixel-wise comparisons.

A framework that integrates ROI averages, uncorrected pixel-wise p value maps, and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted q value maps (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), while maintaining the hippocampal slice as the unit of replication (n), can help separate regional response magnitude, nominal spatial organization, and spatial effects that remain detectable after correction across pixels. In this framework, p value maps provide technically informative summaries of nominal spatial organization in VSD responses, whereas FDR-adjusted q value maps localize pixel-wise differences that remain detectable after correction for multiple comparisons.

Here, we present such an analysis framework and demonstrate its utility in a comparative chemical perturbation assay. The framework combines ROI averages, uncorrected pixel-wise p value maps (p-maps), and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (BH-FDR)-adjusted q value maps (q-maps; Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), with the hippocampal slice retained as the unit of replication throughout. Using this framework, we compared the acute hippocampal circuit effects of BPA with those of BB and MB across pathway-specific stimulation of the trisynaptic circuit, stimulation level, developmental stage, response metric, and inhibitory state (Juliandi et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2017; Bi et al., 2021; Sasaki et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). This study provides a practical analytical pipeline for spatially resolved comparative circuit phenotyping and a demonstration dataset illustrating how ROI averages, p-maps, and q-maps capture complementary levels of regional and spatial information.

Materials and Methods

Unless otherwise noted, slice preparation, VSD staining, and optical recording procedures followed those described previously (Tominaga et al., 2000, 2019).

Animals

C57BL/6N male mice aged 2 and 8 weeks (juvenile and young-adult stages, respectively) were used in accordance with protocols approved by the institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval numbers: KP20-83-2 and subsequent annual renewals through KP25-83-2). All applicable international, national, and institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals were followed. All experiments were performed between 2020 and 2025.

Slice preparation

Transverse hippocampal slices (350 µm) were prepared in ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing (in mM) 124 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 2 MgSO4, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, and 10 glucose, equilibrated with 95% O2/5% CO2. Slices were placed on a fine-mesh membrane filter (Omnipore, JHWP01300, 0.45 µm pores) within a plexiglass ring and maintained in a moist, oxygenated chamber, as described previously (Tominaga et al., 2000; 2019). Slices were incubated at 28°C for 25 min before equilibrating to room temperature.

For each experimental day, slices were prepared from a single animal. Each slice was assigned to a single chemical condition (control, BPA, BB, or MB; see below, Chemical agents, for preparation details), such that chemical conditions were distributed across slices within animal/day when feasible. Slices were excluded for a given condition when recordings did not meet predefined quality requirements (e.g., unstable baseline or loss of viability) or when the stimulation/recording sequence for that condition could not be completed. Analyses were performed on an available-case basis, and n is reported for each condition and comparison.

VSD staining

VSD staining was initiated after a 40 min stabilization period. Slices were stained for 20 min with 110 µl of VSD solution containing 0.2 mM Di-4-ANEPPS (dissolved in a mixture of ethanol, Cremophor EL, distilled water, fetal bovine serum, and ACSF) as described previously (Tominaga et al., 2000, 2019). For recording, slices were transferred to an immersion-style chamber and continuously perfused with oxygenated ACSF (1 ml/min) heated to 31°C (Tominaga et al., 2000).

VSD imaging and optical recording

Imaging was performed using a tandem-lens epifluorescence macroscope configuration (Ratzlaff and Grinvald, 1991; Tominaga et al., 2000). The system utilized an f=20mm water-immersion objective lens (NA 0.35; Brainvision) and a PLAN APO 1.0× lens (No. 10446230; Leica Microsystems) as the projection lens. Illumination was provided by a stabilized green LED (LEX-2G; Brainvision) with appropriate filter sets (dichroic mirror, 575 nm; excitation, 530 nm; emission, 590 nm).

Because wide-field epifluorescence does not provide optical sectioning, the recorded VSD signal represents a depth-weighted population response rather than activity from a defined axial plane.

Fluorescence was recorded with a CMOS camera (MiCAM-02; Brainvision) at a sampling rate of 1.0 ms/frame. Each acquired image comprised 92×80 pixels. The calibrated sampling dimensions at the specimen plane were 15.625 µm/pixel in the x direction and 16.13 µm/pixel in the y direction, corresponding to a field of view of ∼1.44 × 1.29 mm. These values describe the spatial sampling of the recorded image; the optical point-spread function was not measured independently. Image processing, ΔF/F0 conversion, temporal and spatial filtering, and metric definitions are described in Preprocessing and metric definition.

Experimental procedure

Stimulation was controlled via an Arduino Uno and ESTM-8 stimulator (Brainvision) using custom Igor Pro macros. To ensure slice stability, field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) were monitored in the distal stratum radiatum of CA1. Stimulation was repeated every 30 s.

Stimulation intensities were set based on an input–output (stimulation–response) curve: low (L) was ∼20% of the maximal response (L: 10 V, 0.5 ms), and high (H) was near the plateau (near-maximal) response level (H: 40 V, 0.5 ms). For each condition, four recordings were averaged to produce one ΔF/F0 image sequence (time series) per slice per condition. Glass stimulating electrodes filled with ACSF (<1 MΩ) were placed at three locations targeting distinct segments of the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit (Fig. 1A): the Schaffer collateral pathway at the CA1/CA2 border region (stratum radiatum, ∼200 µm from the pyramidal cell layer) to evoke CA1 responses, the dentate hilar region near the opening of the DG to activate the mossy fiber pathway and evoke CA3 responses, and the molecular layer near the apex of the DG to stimulate the perforant path and evoke DG responses. These stimulation sites correspond to the CA1, CA3, and DG response fields, respectively, as referenced throughout the Results. Following baseline imaging, slices were incubated with gabazine (SR-95531; 10 µM) for at least 20 min before repeating the stimulation paradigm under identical conditions.

Chemical agents

BPA (CAS 80-05-7; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), BB (CAS 85-60-9; Tokyo Chemical Industry), and MB (CAS 119-47-1; Tokyo Chemical Industry) were prepared as 50 mg/ml ethanol stocks. Final bath concentrations were 5 mg/L, equivalent to ∼21.9 µM (BPA), 13.1 µM (BB), and 14.7 µM (MB). Vehicle controls contained a matching ethanol concentration. This concentration was selected to allow direct mass-based comparison across compounds of differing molecular weights, consistent with previous in vivo studies of phenolic compounds (Sasaki et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

Definition of biological replicates (n)

In all analyses, the unit of observation (n) was the number of hippocampal slices. For each slice and condition, four trials were averaged to produce a single representative ΔF/F0 image sequence and derived maps. Pixels within an individual slice were not considered independent replicates to avoid sacrificial pseudoreplication (Hurlbert, 1984). Because one animal was used per experimental day and treatment conditions were distributed across slices from the same animal/day when feasible, animal/day was included as a blocking factor in the ROI analysis.

No a priori power calculation was performed. Sample size was determined by the complete set of available experiments that met the prespecified quality requirements and by the practical feasibility of the slice-recording design.

Preprocessing and metric definition

Fluorescence image sequences were converted to ΔF/F0, where F0 was defined as the mean fluorescence over a fixed prestimulus baseline window from −45 to −2 ms relative to stimulus onset. For each slice and condition, four trials were averaged to obtain one ΔF/F0 image sequence. The image sequence was spatially and temporally filtered using a 5×5×3 Gaussian kernel, applied identically across all conditions.

From the slice-averaged ΔF/F0 image sequence, pixel-wise response metrics were computed over a fixed poststimulus window (0–400 ms; Fig. 1D). Peak-response amplitude (PEAK) was defined as the maximum ΔF/F0 value within this window (the arrowheads mark the respective PEAK values of the normal ACSF and gabazine traces in Fig. 1D), and response integral (SUM) was defined as the time integral (area under the response curve) of ΔF/F0 over the same window (shaded region in Fig. 1D; units of ΔF/F0⋅ms).

Pixel-wise statistics and FDR control

Pixel-wise analyses were performed over the recorded field of view. To focus inference on stimulus-responsive tissue and avoid diluting statistical power by including nonresponsive background pixels, hypothesis testing was restricted to an a priori response mask defined from control data for each condition. Specifically, for each stimulation condition (high or low stimulation, with or without gabazine), we computed the control mean map of the relevant response metric (PEAK for Fig. 2; SUM for Fig. 3) and defined the mask as pixels exceeding 50% of the maximum control value within the full field of view for that condition. Within this mask, group-level comparisons across four conditions (control, BPA, BB, and MB) were performed independently at each pixel using one value per slice (slice as the unit of replication; n equals the number of slices) with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD. For each control-versus-compound comparison (control vs BPA, control vs BB, and control vs MB), the resulting p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons across pixels using the BH-FDR procedure applied across pixels within the mask to yield BH-FDR-adjusted q value maps (q-maps). Pixel-wise statistical maps are shown as q-maps thresholded at q<0.05. Corresponding uncorrected pixel-wise p-maps are provided in Extended Data Figure 2-1.

Slice-averaged maps and ROI averages

For visualization, PEAK and SUM metric maps were averaged across slices within each condition (n=4−6) to obtain slice-averaged maps. For ROI averages, PEAK and SUM values were averaged within anatomically defined ROIs (CA1, CA3, and DG) for each slice (one value per ROI per slice per condition). The primary dataset comprised 22 slices from six animal/days at each age (2 weeks, control/BPA/BB/MB = 6/5/5/6 slices; 8 weeks, 6/6/4/6 slices). For each prespecified age × ROI × stimulation intensity × inhibitory state × response metric stratum, ROI averages were analyzed with an animal/day-blocked linear model containing animal/day and treatment as categorical factors. The omnibus treatment effect was evaluated by type II ANOVA. Model-based pairwise contrasts among control, BPA, BB, and MB were adjusted by Tukey's method across all six treatment pairs within that stratum; only treatment-versus-control contrasts are marked in Figures 2–4. No additional correction was applied across the 48 prespecified strata. Residual normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variance across treatment groups with the median-centered Levene test. Complete omnibus statistics, degrees of freedom, effect estimates, simultaneous 95% confidence intervals, Tukey-adjusted p values, and assumption checks are provided in Extended Data Figure 2-2.

Handling of incomplete datasets

Not all slices yielded complete datasets for all conditions. For each analysis, slices were included if the required condition(s) for that specific comparison were available and met quality requirements. Consequently, sample size (n) may differ across conditions and comparisons; n is reported for each condition and comparison.

Results

Effect of BPA and its analogs (BB and MB) on hippocampal activity (PEAK)

We investigated the acute effects of BPA, BB, and MB across the three primary pathways of the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit: the Schaffer collateral projection to CA1, the mossy fiber projection to CA3, and the perforant pathway to DG. PEAK maps represent the spatial distribution of peak-response amplitude, defined as the maximum ΔF/F0 within 0–400 ms after stimulation. For visualization, PEAK maps were averaged across n=4−6 slices for each condition. Pixel-wise statistical results are shown as BH-FDR-adjusted q-maps thresholded at q<0.05 (Fig. 2A,B), whereas ROI-averaged PEAK values provide one average per ROI and slice (Fig. 2C,D). These two analyses were used as complementary readouts: q-maps localized FDR-controlled response differences within the evoked response field, whereas ROI averages quantified region-averaged response magnitude. After ROI-level comparisons were evaluated using the animal/day-blocked model and Tukey-adjusted contrasts, the significant ROI-level differences broadly paralleled the major patterns observed in the BH-FDR-corrected q-maps, while the q-maps further indicated where within the response fields these differences were spatially expressed. All preprocessing steps and analysis windows were identical across conditions.

Pathway-specific reductions in young-adult mice (8 weeks)

In young-adult slices under normal ACSF, BB, and MB reduced PEAK responses most prominently in CA1. ROI-averaged PEAK values showed significant reductions in CA1 after BB and MB exposure under both high (H) and low (L) stimulation, together with an additional BB-associated reduction in CA3 under high stimulation (Fig. 2C). The corresponding BH-FDR-corrected q-maps showed FDR-controlled response differences within the CA1 response field for BB and MB (Fig. 2A). Thus, the ROI averages and q-maps converged on CA1 as the principal site of BB- and MB-associated PEAK reduction under normal ACSF, while the q-maps further showed that these differences were expressed within restricted portions of the CA1 response field rather than uniformly across the entire anatomical ROI. In contrast, BPA produced no comparable ROI-level or q-map-detectable PEAK reduction under normal ACSF.

Gabazine (10 µM) markedly changed the pattern of compound-associated PEAK reductions. The CA1 reductions observed under normal ACSF were no longer evident in the ROI averages after gabazine application (Fig. 2C). Instead, the statistically supported ROI-level effect was restricted mainly to the DG response under high stimulation, where MB reduced PEAK amplitude relative to control. Consistent with this ROI-level result, the corresponding q-map showed a spatially restricted FDR-controlled signal in the DG response field for MB under high stimulation (Fig. 2A). These results indicate that pharmacological blockade of GABAA receptor-mediated inhibition attenuated the CA1-dominant BB/MB effect observed under normal ACSF and shifted the detectable young-adult PEAK phenotype toward a more limited DG-associated effect. Overall, in young-adult slices, ROI averages and q-maps provided consistent support for the major PEAK effects, while q-maps added spatial information about where within the evoked response fields the statistically supported differences were expressed.

Smaller and state-dependent effects in juvenile mice (2 weeks)

In juvenile slices under normal ACSF, compound-associated PEAK changes were limited. ROI-averaged PEAK values did not show clear reductions across CA1, CA3, or DG for any compound (Fig. 2D). The corresponding BH-FDR-adjusted q-maps showed only small, spatially restricted signals in the CA3 response field for BB and MB under high stimulation (Fig. 2B).

After gabazine application, compound-associated PEAK changes became more evident in CA3. ROI averages showed a CA3-centered PEAK reduction for BB, whereas the q-maps revealed broader spatial involvement: the BB-associated signal under high stimulation extended across the CA3 response field and into adjacent CA1, while the MB-associated signal remained restricted to a small stimulation-proximal region within CA3 (Fig. 2B,D). Overall, juvenile PEAK effects were modest under normal ACSF but became more detectable under gabazine, particularly for BB in the CA3-centered response field.

Impact on response duration and integrated activity (SUM)

To assess compound effects on integrated circuit responses, we analyzed the response integral (SUM), defined as the time integral of the ΔF/F0 signal over the fixed poststimulus window (0–400 ms). SUM captures both response amplitude and duration within this window. Pixel-wise statistical results are shown as BH-FDR-adjusted q-maps thresholded at q<0.05 (Fig. 3A,B), and ROI-averaged SUM values provide one average per ROI and slice (Fig. 3C,D). As for PEAK, ROI averages quantify region-averaged activity, whereas q-maps localize SUM differences that survived pixel-wise FDR correction.

Young-adult mice (8 weeks)

In young-adult slices under normal ACSF, ROI-averaged SUM values indicated reduced integrated activity in CA1 after BB and MB exposure, together with a BPA-associated reduction under high stimulation (Fig. 3C). The corresponding BH-FDR-adjusted q-maps showed FDR-controlled SUM differences mainly within the CA1 response field, with signals detected for BB under both stimulation levels but little or no detectable signal for BPA or MB (Fig. 3A). This dissociation indicates that ROI-level SUM reductions were not always accompanied by spatially localized FDR-controlled effects.

After gabazine application, the CA1-centered SUM reductions observed under normal ACSF were no longer detected in the ROI averages. Juvenile mice (2 weeks)

In juvenile slices under normal ACSF, ROI-averaged SUM values were reduced predominantly after BB exposure, with effects in CA1 under both stimulation levels and in CA3 under high stimulation (Fig. 3D). An additional MB-associated reduction was detected in CA1 under high stimulation, whereas no ROI-level SUM reduction was detected for BPA. The corresponding BH-FDR-adjusted q-maps showed spatially restricted BB-associated signals: under high stimulation, the signal was limited to part of the CA1 response field, whereas under low stimulation, it extended across CA1 and into CA3 (Fig. 3B).

After gabazine application, ROI-level SUM reductions remained BB-selective but were concentrated in CA3 under both stimulation levels (Fig. 3D). The corresponding q-maps showed a broad BB-associated FDR-controlled signal under high stimulation, extending across the CA3 response field and into adjacent CA1 (Fig. 3B). No clear ROI-level or q-map signal was detected for BPA or MB under gabazine.

Overall, juvenile SUM effects were predominantly BB-associated, with an isolated MB effect in CA1 under normal ACSF, and shifted toward a more CA3-centered BB pattern after gabazine application. ROI averages identified the affected subfields, whereas q-maps showed the spatial extent of BB-associated FDR-controlled effects when detectable.

To summarize ROI-based response patterns across subregions, stimulation levels, inhibitory conditions, and response metrics, we generated heatmaps of normalized PEAK and SUM values (Fig. 4). For each compound condition, ROI-averaged values were normalized to the corresponding control condition (control, 1.0). These heatmaps provide a compact overview of region-averaged response magnitude and should be interpreted together with the q-maps in Figures 2 and 3, which localize FDR-controlled pixel-wise effects.

Overall patterns in young-adult mice (8 weeks)

In young-adult slices, the heatmaps showed more consistent ROI-level reductions after BB and MB exposure than after BPA exposure (Fig. 4A). Under normal ACSF, statistically supported reductions were concentrated mainly in CA1 for BB and MB and were observed in both PEAK and SUM; BB also reduced CA3 PEAK under high stimulation, and an isolated BPA-associated CA1 SUM reduction was detected under high stimulation. After gabazine application, the CA1-centered pattern was no longer dominant; statistically supported reductions were restricted to DG under high stimulation after MB exposure for both PEAK and SUM. Overall, the 8 week heatmaps indicate that BB and MB produced broader ROI-level effects than BPA and that inhibitory state shifted the dominant affected region from CA1 under normal ACSF toward DG under gabazine.

Overall patterns in juvenile mice (2 weeks)

In juvenile slices, statistically supported ROI-level reductions were more selective than in young adults (Fig. 4B). Under normal ACSF, reductions were detected mainly after BB exposure, particularly in SUM, with one additional MB-associated CA1 SUM reduction under high stimulation; BPA showed no statistically supported reduction. After gabazine application, the clearest ROI-level pattern remained BB-associated and was centered mainly on CA3, again most evident in SUM. Thus, unlike the broader BB/MB-associated pattern observed in young-adult slices, juvenile effects were more restricted to specific compound, metric, and inhibitory state combinations.

Overall, the heatmaps emphasize that phenolic analog effects were broader than BPA-associated effects in young-adult slices but more selective and mainly BB-associated in juvenile slices.

Discussion

The primary goal of this study was to develop and validate an integrated VSD imaging analysis pipeline for the spatially resolved comparison of acute circuit responses. The hippocampal trisynaptic circuit provides a well-characterized, tractable preparation for this purpose, as VSD imaging effectively captures fast, spatially extended population membrane potential dynamics across the CA1, CA3, and DG subfields (Cohen et al., 1978; Grinvald and Hildesheim, 2004; Knöpfel and Song, 2019). While previous studies have demonstrated the utility of VSD imaging for assessing hippocampal and cortical dynamics (Tominaga et al., 2018; Utsumi et al., 2023), robust quantitative comparison across experimental conditions remains a challenge due to the high spatiotemporal complexity and interslice variability of VSD signals. Addressing this limitation requires analytical procedures that can simultaneously summarize regional response magnitudes, visualize global spatial organization, and appropriately treat the biological receptacle (the brain slice)—rather than individual pixels—as the independent unit of replication, thereby avoiding pseudoreplication and inflated statistical support.

We applied this pipeline to a demonstration dataset evaluating the acute effects of BPA and two structurally related phenolic analogs, BB and MB. These alternatives were selected due to rising concerns over the “regrettable substitution” of BPA (Trasande, 2017; Pelch et al., 2019) and growing evidence that such analogs alter neural or behavioral endpoints (Palanza et al., 2008; Sasaki et al., 2023). The pipeline successfully decoupled regional response magnitude from spatial expression, revealing subtle, spatially nonuniform, and condition-dependent circuit effects. Within this dataset, the phenolic analogs induced more detectable suppressions of circuit responses than BPA, with these outcomes highly modulated by pathway, age, response metric, and baseline inhibitory state. These findings illustrate the central utility of our framework: it uncovers complex circuit phenotypes that would remain obscured by single-site electrophysiology or conventional ROI averaging alone.

Multiscale spatial and statistical interpretation of VSD signals

A core methodological contribution of this framework is the parallel integration of ROI averages, uncorrected pixel-wise p-maps, and BH-FDR-controlled q-maps. Rather than acting as competing statistical tests, these readouts operate across complementary spatial and statistical scales. ROI averages quantify the mean response magnitude within anatomically defined subfields, maximizing statistical power for regional comparisons. Uncorrected p-maps preserve and visualize the nominal spatial topology of the evoked activity, acting as a technically informative spatial summary. Finally, BH-FDR-controlled q-maps rigorously isolate the specific pixel clusters where compound-associated effects survive correction for multiple comparisons.

This multilevel approach is vital for functional optical imaging, where neural population activity propagates across broad fields of view. Compound-associated perturbations may be highly localized or, conversely, spatially coherent but too weak to survive conservative pixel-wise FDR thresholds. For example, a spatially restricted effect may emerge clearly on a q-map yet contribute minimally to an ROI average if it spans only a small fraction of that subfield. Conversely, widespread subtle shifts may register in ROI averages but fail to pass pixel-wise thresholding. Furthermore, because imaging data exhibit high spatial autocorrelation and are sensitive to preprocessing steps such as spatial smoothing, q-maps are optimally interpreted as spatial localization summaries rather than rigid anatomical boundaries of affected microdomains.

Distinct acute circuit phenotypes of BPA-related phenolic analogs

The demonstration dataset establishes that BPA and its structurally related phenolic analogs, BB and MB, produce distinct acute hippocampal circuit phenotypes. Under the tested ex vivo conditions, BB and MB drove more robust, detectable reductions in evoked population responses than the parent compound, BPA. This divergence does not imply that BPA is biologically inert in this system; rather, it demonstrates that within the parameters of this acute assay, BPA-associated suppressions were limited, whereas its structural analogs exerted clear circuit-level target engagement. These results underscore the necessity of empirically profiling replacement compounds rather than assuming toxicological equivalence based on structural similarity (Sasaki et al., 2023).

The observed response reductions match reports that specific bisphenol alternatives attenuate excitatory synaptic transmission (Bi et al., 2021, 2025). However, population-level VSD imaging evaluates integrated membrane potential dynamics and cannot inherently resolve the precise cellular or subcellular locus of action (Knöpfel and Song, 2019). The observed circuit-level suppression could stem from diminished presynaptic glutamate release, altered postsynaptic receptor currents, enhanced local interneuron recruitment, or depressed intrinsic membrane excitability. The utility of the present pipeline is therefore not to isolate a single molecular mechanism but to successfully differentiate compound-specific circuit phenotypes as a function of experimental condition.

Age-dependent sensitivity and network-state constraints

The juvenile dataset highlights a critical distinction between immediate circuit sensitivity to acute chemical exposure and cumulative developmental vulnerability. Although developing neural networks are highly sensitive to environmental insults, juvenile slices in this acute assay did not exhibit uniform hypersensitivity. Instead, xenobiotic effects in the juvenile hippocampus were highly selective, predominantly associated with BB with an isolated MB-associated CA1 SUM effect, and strongly dependent on the chosen response metric and baseline inhibitory state.

This age-specific selectivity does not indicate developmental resistance to phenolic analogs. Early-life exposure designs typically evaluate cumulative toxicity across critical windows of differentiation, synaptogenesis, and network maturation, which manifest as delayed behavioral deficits in adulthood (Sasaki et al., 2023). In contrast, our assay captures immediate population responses to acute exposure in isolated ex vivo networks. Evoked responses in the juvenile network may be buffered or masked by the unique biophysical properties of the immature circuit. During the second and third postnatal weeks, the rodent hippocampus undergoes the final phases of the GABAergic switch—where GABAergic signaling transitions from depolarizing to hyperpolarizing—alongside the continuous maturation of recurrent inhibition and network gating (Garaschuk et al., 1998; Ganguly et al., 2001; Ben-Ari, 2002; Ben-Ari et al., 2007). This highly dynamic network state provides a plausible neurodevelopmental context for the distinct acute phenotypes observed between 2-week-old and 8-week-old preparations, confirming that acute pharmacological sensitivity and cumulative developmental vulnerability represent distinct experimental endpoints.

The network-state dependence observed during GABAA receptor blockade provides further mechanistic constraints. The circuit profiles of BB and MB were not fixed, intrinsic properties of the chemicals alone but were fundamentally shaped by baseline inhibitory tone. In young-adult slices, the CA1-centered suppression observed under normal ACSF was attenuated by gabazine treatment, whereas juvenile CA3-centered effects became unmasked and more pronounced under gabazine, particularly for BB. This pattern suggests that the spatial manifestation of compound toxicity is tightly coupled to the local excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance, likely involving differential actions on recurrent collaterals or cell-type-specific interneuron networks (Menzikov et al., 2024).

This multicomponent action is supported by the divergent patterns observed between the PEAK and SUM metrics. While PEAK captures maximal population response amplitude, SUM integrates activity over a wide temporal window (0–400 ms), reflecting both initial amplitude and response duration. The finding that juvenile perturbations were more evident in the SUM metric suggests that acute analog exposure may preferentially alter the persistence or integrated output of population activity rather than simply dampening initial peak amplitudes.

Methodological limitations and future directions

Several scope conditions apply to the interpretation of these findings. First, the lower number of statistically supported effects for BPA must not be construed as evidence of safety; this outcome is strictly bounded by the acute timing, specific concentrations, and statistical thresholds of the current protocol. Second, while ex vivo slices are ideal for high-throughput, highly controlled circuit perturbations, they do not recapitulate in vivo pharmacokinetics, blood–brain barrier penetration, hepatic metabolism, or chronic, low-dose cumulative exposure.

Third, because the axial sensitivity of the wide-field epifluorescence measurement was not calibrated, the recorded population responses cannot be assigned to a specific axial depth or cell layer. Fourth, resolving the precise synaptic and cellular mechanisms underlying these population-level shifts will require complementary single-cell or pathway-specific approaches, such as patch-clamp recordings, targeted field potentials, or high-speed, high-resolution optical quantifications of single cells. Fifth, pixel-wise inference remains sensitive to spatial autocorrelation and preprocessing configurations. Although the analysis window and smoothing parameters were standardized here to eliminate bias, future iterations could incorporate spatially informed cluster-level statistics, effect-size mapping, or spatial hierarchical models to enhance the detection of weak, spatially coherent signals.

Sixth, the slice was retained as the treatment-level unit of observation and animal/day was included as a blocking factor to account for the shared animal origin of slices. Scaling the design to larger animal cohorts would nevertheless permit more flexible mixed-effects modeling and more precise partitioning of animal-level and slice-level variance. Finally, this validation was conducted exclusively in male mice. Given that bisphenol-related compounds are established endocrine disruptors that interact with estrogen receptor signaling pathways, these acute circuit phenotypes cannot be assumed to generalize across sexes. Future studies must incorporate female cohorts and be powered to evaluate explicit sex-by-compound, sex-by-age, and sex-by-inhibitory state interactions.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated an integrated VSD imaging analysis framework for quantitative, spatially resolved comparisons of acute hippocampal circuit responses while preserving the brain slice as the independent unit of replication. By combining ROI averages, uncorrected pixel-wise p-maps, and BH-FDR-controlled q-maps, this multiscale approach distinguished region-averaged response magnitudes from the nominal and FDR-controlled spatial patterns of compound-associated effects. In an acute slice assay using BPA and its phenolic analogs, BB and MB, analog-associated reductions in response magnitude were strongly modulated by compound identity, developmental age, inhibitory network state, response metric, and hippocampal subfield. Specifically, BB and MB produced more consistent, statistically supported suppression than the parent compound BPA in young-adult slices, whereas juvenile responses were highly selective and showed prominent BB-associated alterations only under specific PEAK and SUM conditions. ROI averages and q-maps provided complementary insights: ROI analysis quantified global, region-averaged response reductions, whereas q-maps localized the spatial expression of FDR-controlled pixel-wise differences when such effects were detectable. Taken together, these results establish a practical and reproducible analytical pipeline for comparative circuit-level phenotyping of chemical or environmental perturbations under standardized ex vivo conditions.
