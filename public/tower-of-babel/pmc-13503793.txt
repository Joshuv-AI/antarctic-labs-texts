Latent profiles of apathy in the general population and their associations with psychopathology and functioning
Authors: Alexandra Straková, Jakub Januška, Sára Majsniarová, Valentína Cviková, Vladimír Ivančík, Natália Čavojská, Hana Hollá Kutlíková, Michal Hajdúk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Apathy is a multidimensional construct associated with functional impairments across neuropsychiatric disorders and the general population. While distinct apathy subtypes have been reported, its heterogeneity remains insufficiently understood, particularly in relation to psychosis-spectrum traits and functioning. Latent profile analysis was conducted in a representative community sample (N = 1200) using three apathy dimensions assessed by the Apathy Motivation Index (behavioral, social, emotional). Identified profiles were compared on social and occupational functioning, psychotic-like experiences (PLEs), depression, and anhedonia, with additional analyses adjusting for affective symptoms. Demographic predictors of profile membership were examined using exploratory logistic regression. A two-profile solution provided the best fit, reflecting differences in overall apathy severity rather than qualitatively distinct subtypes. The elevated apathy profile (17.2% of the sample) showed significantly higher levels of PLEs (p < 0.001) and poorer functioning in activities, work-related skills, and social appropriateness (all ps < 0.001). In contrast, participants in this profile reported higher interpersonal relationship functioning (p < 0.001). Most profile differences remained significant after controlling for depressive symptoms and anhedonia. Younger age was robustly associated with elevated apathy profile membership (OR = 0.97, 95% CI [0.95, 0.98]). Overall, apathy in the general population appears best understood as a severity-based motivational phenomenon with domain-specific functional consequences and relevance for psychosis-spectrum traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53164-5.

Introduction

Apathy is considered a transdiagnostic motivational syndrome characterized by a reduction in self-initiated, goal-directed behavior across a range of psychiatric and neurological conditions, including schizophrenia, depression, and neurodegenerative disorders1. The multidimensional nature of apathy reflects impairments across dissociable motivational domains. According to the revised consensus criteria for apathy in brain disorders2, these domains encompass behavior and cognition, emotion, and social interaction. Specifically, behavioral and cognitive domain involves reduced initiation, planning, and persistence of goal-directed actions. The emotional dimension refers to diminished emotional responsiveness, including loss or reduction of spontaneous emotional reactions to salient stimuli. Finally, the social interaction dimension captures diminished drive for social affiliation, social approach, and the maintenance of interpersonal bonds3. Previous findings suggest that these dimensions map onto partially distinct underlying neural mechanisms. This supports the view that apathy represents a constellation of motivational deficits with different psychological and neurobiological substrates rather than a single unitary process4–7.

Although apathy has traditionally been studied in neurological and psychiatric disorders8,9, there is evidence that motivational deficits are continuously distributed in the general population. Using the Apathy Motivation Index (AMI), Ang et al.10 demonstrated that subclinical apathy is both prevalent and heterogeneous, identifying four subtypes of apathy-motivation in an adult community sample: generally apathetic (2.1%), emotionally apathetic (11.9%), behaviorally/socially apathetic (25.3%), and generally motivated (60.8%). These profiles showed distinct patterns of association with psychopathology. Behavioral and social apathy showed stronger links to co-occurring symptoms such as depression, anhedonia, and fatigue, whereas emotional apathy alone showed weaker or less stable associations. Psychometric studies further supported the multidimensional structure of the AMI in non-clinical samples11–13, underscoring the conceptual and empirical relevance of differentiating between apathy dimensions in the general population.

Evidence from community sample suggest that higher levels of apathy are associated with depressive symptoms and anhedonia10, reflecting shared transdiagnostic disturbances in reward sensitivity and goal-directed behavior1. Despite the overlap, apathy is considered primarily a disorder of motivation, whereas depression is more closely related to alterations in emotional valence and mood14. In a sample of university students, depressive symptoms have been shown to predict executive apathy longitudinally (i.e., difficulties in goal-directed behavior related to executive functioning). However, apathy remained relatively stable even when depression increased, supporting its partial independence from depression15. Both apathy and anhedonia also occur in individuals from the general population, particularly with an increasing age16–18. Although impairments in reward anticipation or consummatory pleasure, central to anhedonia, may contribute to apathy; they do not fully account for the broader motivational deficits observed in apathy1.

Disturbances in motivation and reward processing associated with apathy may be relevant not only to affective symptomatology but also to psychosis-spectrum vulnerability. Examining apathy in relation to positive psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) is therefore meaningful, as alterations in motivation and reward processing may co-occur with disturbances in salience attribution, a mechanism implicated in the formation and maintenance of positive symptoms of psychosis19–21. In the general population, higher levels of PLEs has been associated with functional difficulties22,23 and may encompass not only positive symptoms but also negative-symptom experiences such as avolition24. Network-based studies further indicate that positive and negative psychotic experiences tend to co-occur within interconnected symptom systems. Although direct evidence linking positive PLEs to motivational deficits in community samples is limited, existing findings suggest a complex relationship. Some studies suggest that higher positive PLEs may be associated with altered effort allocation under uncertainty27, whereas others suggest that higher levels of motivation may co-occur with elevated PLEs28.

Apathy in clinical populations has been consistently associated with broad functional and psychosocial consequences and reduced quality of life, as well as heightened distress and burden reported by caregivers or family members of people experiencing apathy34–36. In non-clinical populations, the evidence linking apathy to functional outcomes is limited. However, some studies linked motivational deficits to altered effort allocation and reduced willingness to exert effort for reward1,37. In everyday functioning, such mechanisms are important for engagement and productivity in work settings. Moreover, apathy has previously been associated with reduced empathy38 and with altered processes of encoding and weighting prior beliefs about action outcomes39. Even at subclinical levels, apathy has been associated with poorer quality of life40, and potential vulnerability to later cognitive decline41. Among university students, different apathy dimensions have been associated with difficulties in academic performance and daily life15,42. These findings highlight the real-world consequences of motivational impairments in the general population.

Overall, current evidence supports conceptualizing apathy as a multidimensional construct with functional consequences that vary in the general population. However, variable-centered approaches that estimate average associations may obscure informative heterogeneity in motivational functioning. While such approaches allow modeling of individual variability, they typically assume that constructs are continuously distributed within a single population, whereas latent profile analysis enables the identification of subgroups with distinct configurations of apathy dimensions. In the present study, we aimed to identify latent apathy profiles in the general population sample based on behavioral, emotional, and social apathy domains. The second aim was to examine whether apathy profiles differed in social and occupational functioning as well as in psychopathology, including depressive symptoms, anhedonia, and positive PLEs. Additionally, we assessed the robustness and external validity of apathy profiles by examining whether profile differences persisted after accounting for overlapping affective symptoms and by exploring demographic predictors of profile membership.

Sample characteristics.

Most participants were employed (68.4%), with smaller proportions reporting student status, maternity/parental leave, unemployment, disability pension, or old-age pension. Educational attainment varied across the sample, with the majority having completed secondary education and a smaller proportion holding university degrees. Regarding relationship status, most participants were married or in a partnership. Overall, a self-reported history of mental health diagnosis was present in 23.3% of participants. Among those reporting a mental health diagnosis, the most frequently reported conditions were depression and anxiety disorders, whereas psychotic disorders were rare in both profiles (≤ ~ 4%). The majority of individuals in both profiles did not report any mental health diagnosis. The distribution of specific diagnoses was generally similar across profiles, with no indication that the elevated apathy profile was primarily driven by individuals with psychotic disorders (see Supplementary Table S1 for details).

A chi-square test of independence indicated a statistically significant association between apathy profile membership and self-reported history of diagnosed mental disorder, χ2 (1, N = 1200) = 4.05, p = 0.044; however, the effect size was small (Cramér’s V = 0.058), suggesting only a weak relationship.

Latent profiles analysis of apathy dimensions

Model comparison across one to six profiles indicated that a two-profile solution provided the best fit to the data, with the lowest BIC, good classification quality (entropy = 0.84), high average posterior probabilities for both profiles (AvePP = 0.97 and 0.83) and low mean classification uncertainty (0.05). Profile memberships were robust to indicator scaling, with perfect agreement between solutions estimated using standardized and raw indicators (Adjusted Rand Index = 1.00).

The first profile (Class 1, n = 994, 82.8%) was characterized by lower apathy levels across all domains. In contrast, the second profile (Class 2, n = 206, 17.2%) exhibited consistently elevated apathy across domains. Descriptive statistics for each apathy domain are presented in Table 3. Figure 1 illustrates the standardized AMI subscale scores across two identified apathy profiles—low and elevated. The magnitude of differences between profiles varied across domains, with large effects observed for behavioral activation (d = 1.09) and emotional sensitivity (d = 1.15), but only a small effect for social motivation (d = 0.15).

To further evaluate whether solutions with more profiles might capture qualitatively distinct apathy subtypes, we additionally examined the three- and four-profile solutions. Although these solutions were estimable, they showed poorer classification quality relative to the two-profile model (Supplementary Table S3). Neither solution yielded clearly interpretable domain-specific profiles (Supplementary Fig. S1). Instead, the additional classes reflected heterogeneous patterns and splits of the elevated apathy group, without forming distinct configurations across behavioral, social, and emotional domains.

Associations with psychopathology and functioning

Latent apathy profiles differed significantly across most examined psychopathological, motivational, and functional outcomes (Table 4). Compared to low apathy profile, individuals in the elevated apathy profile reported significantly higher levels of positive PLEs, F(1, 1198) = 70.75, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.055, as well as higher levels of social anhedonia, F(1, 1198) = 15.81, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.012. Large and robust differences were observed for both anticipatory anhedonia, F(1, 1198) = 107.12, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.081, and consummatory anhedonia, F(1, 1198) = 162.82, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.119, with higher anhedonia in the elevated apathy profile. In contrast, no significant difference between profiles was observed for depressive symptoms assessed by the DASS-21 depression subscale, F(1, 1198) = 0.05, p = 0.83, ω2 < 0.001.

Covariate-adjusted and sensitivity analyses

After adjusting for depressive symptoms, differences between apathy profiles in positive PLEs remained significant, F(1, 1197) = 97.16, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.054. Similarly, differences remained significant after controlling for anticipatory anhedonia, F(1, 1197) = 53.75, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.042, and consummatory anhedonia, F(1, 1197) = 44.77, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.035. In all models, the corresponding covariates were statistically significant (depressive symptoms and consummatory anhedonia: ps < 0.001, anticipatory anhedonia: p = 0.009).

For interpersonal relationships, significant main effects of apathy profile were observed across all covariate-adjusted models, including adjustment for depressive symptoms (F(1, 1197) = 51.19, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.037), anticipatory anhedonia (F(1, 1197) = 69.56, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.053), and consummatory anhedonia (F(1, 1197) = 56.94, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.044). All corresponding covariates were statistically significant (all ps < 0.001).

Profile differences in social appropriateness also remained significant after adjustment for depressive symptoms (F(1, 1197) = 67.66, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.044), anticipatory anhedonia (F(1, 1197) = 35.45, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.027), and consummatory anhedonia (F(1, 1197) = 25.41, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.019), with all covariates showing significant associations (all ps < 0.001).

Differences between apathy profiles in activities remained significant after controlling for depressive symptoms (F(1, 1197) = 36.45, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.026), anticipatory anhedonia (F(1, 1197) = 14.67, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.011), and consummatory anhedonia (F(1, 1197) = 14.67, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.011). All covariates were statistically significant in the respective models (all ps < 0.001).

For working skills, a significant main effect of apathy profile was observed after adjusting for depressive symptoms, F(1, 1197) = 6.30, p = 0.012, ω2 = 0.004, with depressive symptoms also showing a significant covariate (F = 234.74, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.162). In contrast, apathy profile effects were not statistically significant after adjustment for anticipatory (p = 0.45) or consummatory anhedonia (p = 0.89), although both covariates demonstrated significant effects (ps < 0.001).

Detailed results of all models adjusted for affective symptoms (depressive symptoms and anhedonia) are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

After adjusting for age, sex, educational attainment, employment status, and history of diagnosed mental disorder, the main effect of apathy profile remained significant across multiple outcomes, including positive PLEs (F(1, 1192) = 48.25, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.032), interpersonal relationships (F(1, 1193) = 49.62, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.039), social appropriateness (F(1, 1193) = 40.95, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.030), activities (F(1, 1193) = 23.80, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.018), social anhedonia (F(1, 1193) = 11.30, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.008), anticipatory anhedonia (F(1, 1193) = 104.83, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.078), and consummatory anhedonia (F(1, 1193) = 144.55, p < 0.001, ω2 = 0.104). In contrast, the difference in work skills was no longer significant after adjustment (F(1, 1193) = 2.69, p = 0.10, ω2 = 0.001).

Detailed results of all models adjusted for demographic covariates are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

A binary logistic regression including age, sex, educational attainment, employment status, and history of diagnosed mental disorder showed a significantly better fit compared to an intercept-only model, Δχ2 (5) = 36.19, p < 0.001, and explained approximately 5.0% of variance in elevated apathy profile membership (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.050) (Supplementary Table S6). Younger age was associated with higher odds of belonging to the elevated apathy profile (OR = 0.97, 95% CI [0.95, 0.98], p < 0.001). The history of diagnosed mental disorder showed a trend toward increased odds of elevated apathy profile membership (OR = 1.38, p = 0.070), although this effect was not statistically significant in multivariate mode. Sex, educational attainment, and employment status were not significantly associated with profile membership.

Discussion

The present study examined the latent structure, psychopathological and functional correlates, and demographic predictors of apathy in a representative general population sample. Apathy was best characterized by differences in overall severity rather than by qualitatively distinct domain-specific subtypes. Individuals in the elevated apathy profile showed significant differences in patterns of functioning and higher levels of positive PLEs that were not fully explained by depressive symptoms or anhedonia. Younger age emerged as the only significant demographic predictor of elevated apathy profile membership. Education level was not significantly associated with apathy profile membership, whereas employment status and history of diagnosed mental disorder showed only weak or trend-level associations. Overall, these findings support apathy as a meaningful and partially distinct dimension of vulnerability in the general population and highlight implications for both everyday functioning and psychosis-spectrum traits.

Contrary to expectations based on prior work of Ang et al.10 apathy in the present community sample was organized primarily along a severity continuum rather than into qualitatively distinct domain-specific subtypes. Behavioral activation, emotional sensitivity, and social motivation covaried strongly, yielding a low-apathy and an elevated-apathy profile. Notably, the separation between profiles was substantially smaller for social motivation compared to behavioral activation and emotional sensitivity, with effect sizes indicating only minimal differences between groups. This suggests that social motivation may be less tightly associated with overall apathy severity in the general population. One possible explanation is that social domain is more strongly shaped by contextual and interpersonal factors, which may sustain a baseline level of engagement even in individuals with elevated apathy. In contrast, behavioral and emotional domains may more directly reflect internally driven motivational processes and therefore show stronger differentiation between profiles.

Consistent with this interpretation, inspection of higher-profile solutions (three- and four-profile models) did not reveal clearly interpretable domain-specific subtypes. Instead, these solutions produced heterogeneous and partially unstable profile patterns, primarily reflecting further splitting of the elevated apathy group rather than distinct configurations across domains. This suggests that increasing the number of profiles may artificially fragment a fundamentally severity-based structure rather than identify meaningful subtypes.

These findings may help explain discrepancies with earlier study reporting differentiated apathy subtypes10. In that study, the identified solution included a very small class and was based on raw scores, which may increase sensitivity to spurious or unstable profile differentiation. Differences in sampling characteristics may further contribute to these discrepancies, as the sample studied by Ang et al.10 was substantially younger (M = 28.7 years, SD = 14.9), whereas the present study included a broader adult age range with a higher mean age. Additionally, the inclusion of a more heterogeneous population in the present study may have emphasized shared variance across apathy domains, favoring a severity-based structure over domain-specific differentiation.

While the identified profiles primarily reflected severity differences, the profile-based approach remains informative by capturing heterogeneity and potentially non-linear associations with external outcomes that may be obscured in variable-centered analyses40,41. Thus, the value of the latent profile approach in this context lies not in identifying categorical subtypes per se, but in revealing distinct constellations of risk across motivational, functional, and psychosis-spectrum domains.

Elevated apathy was robustly associated with higher levels of positive PLEs, even after controlling for depressive symptoms and both anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia.

These findings are consistent with transdiagnostic models linking motivational and salience-related impairments to psychosis-spectrum vulnerability20,42. Reduced goal-directed behavior, diminished social drive, and impaired action initiation may increase susceptibility to anomalous experiences by limiting exposure to corrective environmental feedback and social reality testing43,44. In this context, apathy may represent a motivational pathway contributing to psychosis-spectrum vulnerability that is at least partly independent of affective symptom burden. However, reverse or bidirectional relationships are also plausible. Positive PLEs may contribute to processes such as distress, social withdrawal, or depressive symptoms, which in turn may be associated with increased apathy. Given the cross-sectional design, these associations should therefore be interpreted as non-directional.

Importantly, these associations remained significant after adjusting for demographic variables. This is particularly relevant given that younger individuals were more likely to belong to the elevated apathy profile, yet the persistence of the association indicates that the link between apathy and positive PLEs cannot be explained solely by age differences. Although, individuals with a self-reported mental health diagnosis were somewhat more prevalent in the elevated apathy profile, this association was small in magnitude, and the majority of individuals in this group did not report a diagnosis. Moreover, adjusting for diagnostic status alongside other demographic variables did not meaningfully alter the observed differences between profiles, suggesting that the findings are not driven by the inclusion of individuals with mental illness.

Notably, depressive symptoms did not differ between profiles, whereas large and consistent differences were observed for both anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia. This dissociation provides further support for the conceptual distinction between apathy and depression. While depression is primarily characterized by alterations in mood and emotion valence14, apathy appears to be more closely linked to motivational and reward-processing mechanisms. The present findings therefore suggest that elevated apathy cannot be reduced to a subthreshold manifestation of depression in the general population1,9,48,49. At the same time, although anhedonia overlaps conceptually with the emotional and experiential aspects of apathy, the constructs are not equivalent. Whereas anhedonia primarily reflects alterations in reward processing (including anticipatory and consummatory components)1, apathy encompasses a broader disruption of goal-directed behavior, emotional engagement, and social motivation. Examining both constructs concurrently therefore allows for differentiation between shared and distinct mechanisms.

Moreover, the findings of greater social anhedonia and deficits in both reward anticipation and consumption in elevated apathy profile are consistent with computational and neurobiological models linking apathy to impaired reward prediction, reduced incentive salience, and engagement with reward outcomes rather than generalized negative affect1,39,50,51. Notably, these effects remained robust after adjusting for demographic characteristics, with particularly large effect sizes observed for both anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia. Together, these results suggest that apathy profiles in the general population are primarily distinguished by motivational and reward-processing abnormalities across both anticipatory and consummatory components.

Elevated apathy was associated with poorer functioning in activities, work-related skills, and social appropriateness representing domains that require sustained effort, initiative, and regulation of behavior in accordance with social norms. Most of these associations remained significant after adjustment for depression and anhedonia, and importantly, also after accounting for demographic variables. This is consistent with previous work linking apathy with everyday functioning difficulties in both clinical and non-clinical samples40,49,52,53. While several comparisons reached statistical significance, some were associated with relatively small effect sizes (e.g., working skills), indicating limited practical importance. Consistent with this, the difference in work-related functioning was attenuated and no longer significant after adjusting for demographic variables, suggesting that this domain may be more strongly influenced by contextual and demographic factors.

At the same time, the elevated apathy profile reported better interpersonal relationship functioning. Several explanations may account for this finding. Reduced engagement in socially demanding situations may limit exposure to interpersonal stressors, leading to a subjective perception of more stable relationships despite diminished social motivation. Similar patterns have been described previously in schizophrenia-spectrum research51,52. Measurement characteristics should also be considered as the SLOF was originally developed for clinician ratings of observable functioning53. Self-reported interpersonal functioning may therefore reflect perceived adequacy within low-demand social contexts rather than active social competence or engagement. This dissociation underscores the importance of examining discrete functional domains rather than aggregated functioning indices.

Most profile differences in psychosis-spectrum traits and functioning persisted after accounting for depressive symptoms, anhedonia and demographic variables, further supporting the interpretation that apathy represents a meaningful and partially distinct dimension of individual differences in the general population. An exception was observed for work-related functioning, suggesting that vocational outcomes may be more sensitive to reward-processing capacity and contextual factors than to apathy severity in general.

Exploratory demographic analyses showed that younger individuals were more likely to belong to the elevated apathy profile. This finding appears to contrast with previous studies reporting higher levels of apathy with increasing age16–18. One possible explanation is that the relationship between apathy and age may be non-linear rather than strictly linear. For example, apathy may be elevated at both younger and older ages, potentially reflecting different underlying mechanisms across different developmental stages.

The present findings are consistent with developmental perspectives suggesting that motivational disengagement may be pronounced in early and middle adulthood54,55. These life periods are characterized by greater psychosocial instability and role transitions which may disrupt sustained goal-directed motivation. In contrast, older adults may benefit from greater structural stability such as established social networks56, while population-based estimates in later adulthood may also reflect selective retention effects, whereby individuals with higher levels of functioning are more likely to remain represented57. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as indicators of demographic associations rather than causal predictors. Moreover, the relatively low proportion of variance in profile membership explained by sociodemographic variables suggests that apathy profiles are only weakly determined by these factors in the general population and may instead reflect more proximal psychological or motivational processes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional and long-term stability of LPA results limit causal inference regarding the temporal relationships between apathy, functioning, and PLEs. The inclusion of individuals with self-reported mental health diagnoses may have increased variability in apathy and related outcomes; however, supplementary analyses indicated that the main findings were robust to adjustment for diagnostic status. Functional outcomes were assessed via self-report, which may not fully capture objective performance, particularly in interpersonal domains. The absence of behavioral or performance-based measures of motivation limits inferences about underlying mechanisms. Finally, although the representative sample enhances external validity, findings may be culturally specific and warrant replication in other populations.

Future research should examine longitudinal trajectories of apathy profiles to clarify their developmental stability and prognostic significance. Incorporating behavioral and performance-based measures of motivation would help differentiate subjective perceptions of functioning from observable behavior. Additionally, neurobiological and cognitive mechanisms related to apathy severity may also clarify why motivational impairments co-occur globally rather than being fractionated across the general population.

Conclusion

In summary, apathy in the general population appears best understood as a severity-based motivational phenomenon with meaningful, domain-specific functional implications. Elevated apathy is associated with increased psychosis-spectrum traits and selective impairments in role-based and behaviorally demanding domains that are not reducible to depression or anhedonia. These findings underscore the importance of apathy as a transdiagnostic construct relevant for understanding variation in functioning and psychosis-spectrum expression beyond traditional affective symptom dimensions.

Methods

Study design

Data were collected using an online survey administered between November and December 2024. Participants were recruited through a professional survey agency. A sample of 1200 individuals from the Slovak Republic was recruited and quota-matched to the general population with respect to age (18–65 years), sex, education level, and region of residence.

All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and respondents received compensation in accordance with the survey agency’s standard procedures. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics committee of the Faculty of Arts, Comenius University in Bratislava, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria were defined a priori and included age between 18 and 65 years, residence in the Slovak Republic, and sufficient Slovak language proficiency to complete the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria comprised of incomplete or inconsistent responses, failure to meet predefined quota requirements, and withdrawal of informed consent. Data quality screening (including enforcement of complete responses and consistency checks) was conducted by the survey agency prior to data delivery, and only responses meeting these criteria were included in the final dataset.

Measures

Apathy

Apathy was assessed using the Apathy Motivation Index (AMI)10 an 18-item self-report questionnaire designed to capture multidimensional motivational deficits. The AMI comprises three subscales: Behavioral Activation, Social Motivation, and Emotional Sensitivity. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Completely true) to 4 (Completely untrue). All items were negatively scored and reverse-coded, such that higher scores indicate greater apathy. Subscale scores were computed by averaging item responses within each domain. The questionnaire was translated into Slovak using a standard forward–backward translation procedure with permission from the original authors.

Psychopathology

PLEs were assessed using the Community Assessment of Psychic Experiences (CAPE-42)24, a self-report measure developed to assess subclinical psychosis-spectrum experiences in the general population. The CAPE includes 42 items covering positive, negative, and depressive symptom dimensions. Each item is rated on separate frequency and distress scales. For the purposes of the present study, only the frequency scale of positive dimension of psychotic-like experiences (hereafter referred to as positive PLEs) was used, reflecting how often participants experienced them. The Slovak version of the CAPE-42 has been previously validated25,58.

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21)59. The instrument consists of 21 items referring to experiences over the past week, and yields three subscale scores: Depression, Anxiety, and Stress. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Only the Depression subscale was used in the present analyses.

Anhedonia was assessed using the Temporal Experience of Pleasure Scale (TEPS)60, an 18-item self-report measure capturing dissociable anticipatory and consummatory pleasure experiences. Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = Very true for me to 6 = Very false for me), with higher scores indicating higher levels of experienced pleasure. Scores in both subscales were reverse-coded so that higher scores indicated higher levels of anhedonia.

Revised Social Anhedonia Scale–Brief (RSAS-B)61 was used to assess social anhedonia, measuring diminished pleasure derived from social engagement and interpersonal relationships. The scale consists of 15 dichotomous items (true/false), yielding a total score reflecting the severity of social anhedonia. The Slovak version of the RSAS-B has been previously translated, culturally adapted, and applied in non-clinical samples62.

Functioning

The Specific Levels of Functioning Scale (SLOF)53 was used to assess behavioral functioning and everyday living skills. The 31-item, self-report version evaluates real-world functioning across four domains: Interpersonal Relationships, Social Appropriateness, Work Skills, and Activities. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better real-world functioning.

Although some instruments are typically reported using total scores, mean scores were used in the present study. Observed score ranges for all measures are provided in Table 2 to facilitate interpretation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using JASP (version 0.95.4) and R (version 4.5.1). Latent profile analysis (LPA) was performed in R using the mclust package, while descriptive statistics, group comparisons, and regression analyses were conducted in JASP. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and observed ranges) were computed for all variables. Internal consistency of multi-item scales was assessed using McDonald’s ω. Correlations among key variables were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients.

Latent profiles of apathy (Aim 1)

Latent profiles of apathy were identified based on the three subscales of the AMI (behavioral, emotional, and social). Prior to model estimation, AMI subscale scores were standardized to z-scores to ensure comparability across indicators and to minimize scale-related bias in profile formation. Models specifying one to six latent profiles were estimated. Model selection was guided primarily by the Byesian Information Criterion (BIC), supported by entropy, posterior classification diagnostics, and interpretability of the resulting profiles.

Between-profile differences in everyday functioning and psychopathology (Aim 2)

Between-profile differences were examined for continuous outcome variables, including SLOF subscales scores, depressive symptoms (DASS-21 Depression), anticipatory and consummatory anhedonia (TEPS), and positive psychotic-like experiences (CAPE-42 positive symptom frequency). Group differences were tested using one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA). Effect sizes were reported for all analyses, and 95% confidence intervals were provided where appropriate (e.g., for odds ratios in logistic regression models).

Covariate-adjusted and sensitivity analyses (Aim 3)

Analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) were conducted to examine whether profile-based differences in psychopathology and functioning remained after adjusting for potential confounding variables. In addition to affective symptom dimensions, models included sociodemographic covariates (age, sex, educational attainment, employment status, and history of diagnosed mental disorder) to account for potential differences in sample composition across profiles. Depressive symptoms and anhedonia were included as covariates in separate models to avoid multicollinearity and to allow domain-specific interpretation of affective symptom dimensions.

To examine demographic and clinical predictors of profile membership, binary logistic regression analyses were performed, with elevated apathy profile membership coded as the outcome variable. Predictors included age (continuous), sex (female vs. male), educational attainment (higher vs. lower education), employment status (employed vs. not employed), and self-reported diagnosis of a mental disorder (yes vs. no). All predictors were entered simultaneously. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), which were within acceptable limits. Model fit was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests and pseudo-R2 indices (McFadden, Nagelkerke, Tjur, and Cox & Snell). Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.
