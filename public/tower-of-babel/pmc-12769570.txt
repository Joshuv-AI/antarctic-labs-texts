ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study analyzed road transport–related CO₂ emissions in Dilovası, Kocaeli, using artificial intelligence (AI)–based models, highlighting the transportation sector’s major contribution and the need for effective mitigation strategies. Two models, the Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) and the Artificial Neural Network (ANN), were developed using vehicular and meteorological data to predict nitrogen oxide (NOₓ) and carbon monoxide (CO) emissions and were compared with the results of the Calculations of Emissions from Road Transport (COPERT 4) model. The ANFIS model achieved high accuracy, with mean squared error (MSE) values of 0.0003 for NOₓ and 0.0000 for CO, root mean squared error (RMSE) values of 0.0178 and 0.0092, and mean absolute error (MAE) values of 0.0115 and 0.0055. In contrast, the ANN model produced higher errors with MSE values of 0.0027 and 0.0009, RMSE values of 0.0523 and 0.0310, and MAE values of 0.0297 and 0.0156. Determination coefficients (R2) were 0.9993 and 0.9997 for ANFIS, compared with 0.6366 and 0.8506 for ANN. The results, consistent with previous studies, confirmed the superior performance of ANFIS and demonstrated that integrating AI-based modeling with Intelligent Transportation Systems offers an effective approach for emission reduction and sustainable transport management.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Climate change, Greenhouse gas emission, Intelligent transportation systemsSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Greenhouse gases (GHGs) such as carbon dioxide (CO₂), water vapor (H₂O), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O), and ozone (O₃) trap heat in the atmosphere, leading to global warming and climate change1. Among anthropogenic sources, the transportation sector contributes about 15% of energy-related CO₂-equivalent emissions, with road transport dominating2. Without sustainable policies, transport emissions could double by 20503. Urban areas, responsible for disproportionate emissions due to high traffic density and congestion, intensify pollutant accumulation4,5. Therefore, accurate modeling of traffic emission rates is crucial for mitigation planning. The Calculation of Emissions From Road Transport (COPERT 4) model6,7 is widely used for calculating cold start, hot running, and non-exhaust emissions across vehicle categories. However, traditional numerical models are often complex and time-consuming8,9. To overcome these limitations, Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques such as Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) have gained attention. ANFIS combines fuzzy logic and neural-network learning, effectively modeling nonlinear relations between environmental and traffic parameters10–12. Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) integrate software, hardware, traffic engineering, and communication technologies to enhance efficiency and safety13,14. ITS architectures typically include:

Perception layer—sensors (Light Detection and Ranging (LIDAR), radar, inductive loops, etc.) for vehicle and environment monitoring;

In-road sensors such as inductive loops and piezoelectric cables enable accurate vehicle classification, forming the foundation of real-time emission monitoring17. Despite the close connection between ITS and emission reduction, studies addressing ITS-based control of road transport emissions, particularly in high-traffic zones, remain limited.

The study highlights the environmental advantages of ITS by demonstrating their effectiveness in reducing vehicle emissions, improving transportation efficiency, enhancing economic performance, and supporting climate change mitigation. There is a strong correlation between ITS and vehicle emission control, and road transportation is the largest source of transportation-related GHG emissions. However, research on the control of road transport emissions, particularly in areas with high traffic density, remains limited. Related works

Artificial Intelligence (AI) models have demonstrated significant potential for mitigating CO₂ emissions through diverse strategies18. Studies highlight that AI applications are particularly effective in industrialized regions, supporting long-term decarbonization efforts19,20. At the urban scale, AI integration contributes to carbon neutrality goals by enhancing energy and emission management21. The transportation sector, which accounts for nearly 16.2% of global CO₂ emissions, has increasingly benefited from AI-driven innovations22. Among these, fuzzy logic, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference Systems (ANFIS) have been widely applied to model and predict air quality parameters. Comparative studies reveal that ANFIS generally yields more reliable predictions than ANN when modeling emission values23. The ANN model, capable of processing large datasets and complex nonlinear relationships, remains an essential tool for developing effective emission reduction strategies24. As universal nonlinear function approximators, ANNs offer flexible, adaptive, and assumption-independent modeling capabilities for environmental systems25. Reported predictive accuracies vary by pollutant: O₃ (50–65%), PM₁₀ (75–90%), and SO₂ (69–80%)26,27. Overall, AI’s potential in reducing CO₂ emissions is well-documented across multiple sectors—especially in transportation, where it supports both predictive modeling and sustainable policymaking. Study area and data sources

Dilovası (40° 50′ 04″ N, 29° 35′ 22″ E) is a district characterized by a high concentration of industrial facilities and severely polluted air (Fig. 2). The district’s basin-like topographical structure causes emissions released from factory chimneys to have long-term impacts on the local population34. Moreover, vehicles traveling on the D-100 Highway and the E-80 TEM Motorway, which pass through the district and accommodate various classes of vehicles, constitute a significant source of GHG emissions and air pollution in the area35.

Dilovası district.

The models have been trained using four years of traffic data (2015–2018) from the Dilovası district of Kocaeli Province. In this study, comprehensive information is provided on how the emissions of vehicle types passing through the district vary depending on the months in the data sets obtained from Figshare. The dataset consists of data covering a period of 4 years from the official website of the General Directorate of Highways. In addition, information about fuel types and segments of vehicles and environmental factors are also included.

Data collection and classification

For model development, both training and testing datasets have been constructed using the number of vehicles passing through the district by type, along with environmental variables, including monthly average daily minimum temperature (℃), maximum temperature (℃), and average relative humidity (%), collected from the Kocaeli Cengiz Topel Naval Air Station Meteorological Station36. To determine the monthly average daily data for all vehicle types passing through the district, vehicles have been initially categorized into two main groups: light and heavy vehicles. For a more detailed analysis, both light and heavy vehicles have been further classified into specific vehicle types based on distribution ratios obtained from the Turkish Statistical Institute (TÜİK)37. Passenger cars have been categorized into three subgroups according to fuel type: gasoline, diesel, and Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG). Medium-duty commercial vehicles, including passenger cars, pickup trucks, and minibuses, have been classified as "light commercial vehicles," while buses, trucks, and articulated lorries have been considered "heavy vehicles." It has been assumed that all heavy vehicles operate on diesel fuel. The statistical data required to estimate the number of vehicles according to type and composition were obtained from the General Directorate of Highways (KGM) for the Gebze-Dilovası section, which represents the current pattern38. After acquiring environmental data, more detailed vehicle data have been generated using ratios obtained from relevant institutions. The COPERT 4 emission estimation program has been utilized to predict transportation-related emissions, while ANFIS and ANN tools modeled GHG emissions by employing the necessary environmental and vehicle data to produce prediction results. The workflow for model development is illustrated in Fig. 2. Raw data have been initially subjected to preprocessing. Except for meteorological variables, data related to vehicle classification inputs underwent processing before modeling.

Data preprocessing

In this study, a data preprocessing procedure was applied to ensure that the dataset was suitable for analysis. The primary goal of this step was to eliminate inconsistencies and anomalies that could potentially affect the performance of the predictive models.

Dataset preparation

Initially, the dataset was examined for missing values to prevent incomplete or biased data from influencing the model outcomes. A comprehensive check was also performed to identify and remove duplicate or redundant entries. Subsequently, outlier detection was conducted to ensure that pollutant-related parameters would yield accurate and reliable prediction results.

After removing these outliers, a refined dataset with a dimension of 48 × 10 was obtained, which was then used as input for the modeling phase.

Dataset splitting

To evaluate the performance of the developed models, the dataset was divided into two subsets: data from 2015–2017 were used for training, while data from 2018 were reserved for testing. This partitioning enabled an objective assessment of the models’ predictive capabilities under unseen data conditions.

Cross-validation procedures

K-fold cross-validation (commonly k = 5 or 10) helps minimize overfitting and enhance generalization by dividing the dataset into multiple training and testing subsets42 For small or highly correlated datasets, leave-one-out validation offers a more comprehensive evaluation. Additionally, bootstrap resampling can be applied to estimate prediction uncertainty and compute confidence intervals for key metrics such as R2 and RMSE.

External benchmarking and reference validation

The model outputs can be compared against established emission frameworks such as COPERT 4 or MOVES to ensure external consistency43 Regression-based comparisons (e.g., slope ≈ 1, R2 ≥ 0.9) and relative error ratios are frequently used to assess alignment between predicted and reference emissions44. Seasonal or temporal validation, testing model performance across different months or years, further confirms adaptability to varying environmental and traffic conditions45.

Independent and empirical validation

For additional robustness, models can be validated using independent datasets collected from other regions or meteorological conditions. When available, measured pollutant concentrations (e.g., NOₓ or CO sensor data) provide an empirical basis for direct model–observation comparison46. Scenario validation under Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) applications,such as route redirection or dynamic traffic control, offers real-world verification of predictive behavior.

AI-specific performance diagnostics

Learning-curve analysis can be used to examine how performance metrics evolve with training data volume, identifying overfitting or underfitting tendencies47. Sensitivity analysis and feature-importance ranking reveal the relative contribution of vehicular and environmental predictors48. Residual analysis and uncertainty quantification further detect systematic bias and assess prediction stability across pollutant types.

Graphical and statistical evaluation

Visual tools such as observed–predicted scatter plots, Taylor diagrams, and residual histograms can illustrate model accuracy and variance structure49. These diagnostics complement quantitative measures and improve interpretability of model behavior.

Comparative literature benchmarking

Model performance was benchmarked against previous AI-based emission prediction studies. While comparable research typically reports R2 values between 0.85 and 0.9850, the proposed ANFIS model achieved R2 ≈ 0.999, placing it among the most accurate frameworks to date. These validation results confirm that both the ANFIS and ANN models exhibit high predictive accuracy, reproducibility, and robustness across diverse transportation and environmental applications.

ITS implementation

Introduction of the routes to which vehicles will be redirected

Two alternative routes—the Designed Alternative Route (DAR) and the Northern Marmara Motorway (NMM)—were selected to evaluate the potential of vehicle redirection strategies for reducing transportation-related emissions in the Dilovası district (Fig. 5). The DAR, developed specifically for this study using Microstation Inroads software and following the project design standards established by51. was conceptualized as a locally optimized, emission-minimizing corridor. The selected 9.32 km alignment was determined through multi-criteria evaluation of geometric, structural, and environmental parameters, including viaduct and tunnel lengths, pier height, span distances, and cut–fill volumes, all optimized to minimize earthwork imbalance and land-use disturbance. Routed through sparsely populated areas, the DAR aims to divert short-haul and intra-district heavy-duty vehicles away from the urban core, thereby mitigating localized NOₓ and CO concentration peaks.In contrast, the NMM represents an existing high-capacity intercity motorway forming part of Türkiye’s national transportation corridor. A 41.35 km segment of the 170.2 km Kurtköy–Akyazı Section was analyzed to assess the effects of long-distance traffic diversion, particularly for transit heavy vehicles that contribute substantially to regional emissions. Owing to its uninterrupted, controlled-access design, the NMM offers a realistic framework for evaluating the benefits of existing bypass routes in alleviating urban air quality stress. A comparative cost assessment revealed that while the DAR’s estimated construction cost is approximately $5.17 million, far lower than the $2.2 billion total cost of the NMM Section51, its localized emission reduction and traffic redistribution impacts are significant for the Dilovası region. The NMM, by contrast, provides regional-scale emission relief through the redirection of through-traffic away from densely populated zones. Overall, comparing these two alternatives—one as a newly engineered emission-optimized design (DAR) and the other as an operational high-capacity bypass (NMM)—provides a comprehensive framework for assessing the environmental, operational, and economic benefits of infrastructure innovation versus adaptive utilization.

Operational protocols

When pollution levels surpass the defined thresholds:

Phase 1—Alert dissemination:

VMS panels display real-time warnings (“High Air Pollution – Use Alternative Route”) and provide navigation guidance toward the DAR or NMM.

Phase 2—Compliance enforcement:

The EES utilizes Automatic Number Plate Recognition (ANPR) to identify non-complying vehicles. Violation data are transmitted to the Traffic Enforcement Database.

Phase 3—Data feedback and system optimization:

Data analytics modules assess sensor inputs, traffic density, and meteorological changes to adjust message frequency, rerouting logic, and response timing.

Sensor and system selection

Meteorological analysis for Kocaeli indicates precipitation during nearly half of the year52, which limits the reliability of optical and LIDAR-based sensors under adverse weather. Consequently, vibration sensors were chosen for their stability in high-humidity conditions and resistance to rain interference. A hybrid configuration of Vibration Sensors + VMS + EES was found to provide optimal operational resilience, accuracy, and enforcement capacity. Sensor evaluation criteria included data precision, detection range, installation feasibility, maintenance cost, and transmission medium53.

Economic evaluation and payback period

Excluding maintenance and operational expenses, cost–benefit analysis was conducted using emission data derived from COPERT 4 (Table 3). The maximum daily average emissions of NOx and CO were converted into CO₂-equivalent (CO₂eq) values to estimate economic feasibility. The COPERT 4 output indicated 960 tons of CO₂eq, while the emission threshold calculated per sensor investment corresponded to 235 tons of CO₂eq. Thus, the payback period, when cumulative emission cost savings offset the initial ITS and route investment, was determined to be less than one year, demonstrating exceptional cost-efficiency.

Results

Results showed that NOₓ concentrations increased in September, consistent with previous findings54, with peak daily averages in September 2016 of 0.84 tons NOₓ and 4.77 tons CO, reflecting heavy post-summer traffic pollution. Based on these outputs, vehicle and environmental data were employed as inputs for the ANFIS and ANN models to predict future NOₓ and CO levels. Both models were validated against COPERT 4 results, with determination coefficients (R2) close to 1, indicating strong agreement (Table 4). Regression analyses demonstrated that ANFIS achieved higher predictive accuracy than ANN, particularly for vehicle-related emissions, confirming its superior performance (Fig. 7).

Benchmarking with previous studies

To validate robustness, the developed AI models were compared with results from previous emission and air-quality prediction studies. Study55 achieved R2 = 0.9872 and RMSE = 0.0287 using ANFIS with Gaussian and constant membership functions, while study56 found ANFIS more reliable than ANN for CO prediction in Tehran due to its higher R2 and lower MAE. In a hybrid approach, Praveenchandar et al.50 improved ANFIS performance using a genetic algorithm (ANFIS-IGA), reducing RMSE from 0.1176 to 0.0527. In comparison, the present ANFIS model achieved R2 = 0.9993 (NOₓ) and R2 = 0.9997 (CO) with RMSE values of 0.0179 and 0.0093, respectively—surpassing most reported results. These comparisons confirm that the proposed model ranks among the top-performing AI frameworks for emission prediction, effectively capturing complex nonlinear relationships between vehicular and environmental factors and strengthening the external validity of the study’s findings.

The vehicle numbers, determined using threshold emission values converted into CO₂-equivalent for CO and NOₓ, were employed as input sources for the ANFIS model to assess its performance and forecast future emissions in the Dilovası district. Calculation of the ratio of vehicles to be routed based on threshold emission values of CO and NOx pollutant parameters expressed in CO₂ equivalent

This section outlines how vehicle routing thresholds were determined based on CO₂-equivalent emissions derived from the ANFIS model results. The analysis identifies the vehicle count at which the ITS should be activated once CO₂-equivalent threshold values are exceeded. According to the ANFIS model, the annual average daily emissions were 0.504 tons for NOₓ and 0.690 tons for CO. For the selected ITS configuration, Vibration Sensor + EES + VMS, the threshold emission limits were set at 0.131 tons (NOₓ) and 0.473 tons (CO). To calculate the vehicle routing ratio, the annual average daily emission values were divided by the corresponding threshold emission values derived from sensor cost data By converting pollutant quantities to CO₂ equivalents (900 for NOₓ and 25,039 for CO)39, the model estimated 453.6 tons CO₂ eq. for NOₓ and 172.5 tons CO₂ eq. for CO, yielding a total of 626.1 tons CO₂ eq. Based on sensor-cost thresholds, the corresponding threshold CO₂ equivalents were 118.39 tons for NOₓ and 118.39 tons for CO, totaling 236.78 tons CO₂ eq. The ratio of these totals indicates that threshold emissions correspond to approximately 38% of the ANFIS-predicted CO₂-equivalent emissions. Thus, when the annual average daily vehicle count reaches around 38% of the modeled value, vehicle redirection to alternative routes should be activated to mitigate air pollution. This evaluation specifically examines GHG reduction scenarios in which light vehicles, divided into passenger cars and medium commercial vehicles, are rerouted to alternative corridors to reduce emissions in the Dilovası district.

The impact of GHG emissions under the DAR scenario

The DAR-PC (Designed Alternative Route – Passenger Car) and DAR-MCV (Designed Alternative Route – Medium Commercial Vehicle) scenarios assess the environmental and operational effects of rerouting different categories of light vehicles. In the DAR-PC scenario, passenger cars were redirected, resulting in a fuel consumption cost of $7,564,509, while the DAR-MCV scenario, involving light commercial vehicles, incurred $6,424,000. Hence, the DAR-PC scenario’s fuel cost was about 1.15 times higher Considering the route lengths, 9.327 km for the DAR and 41.35 km for the NMM, the emission reduction capacity (the route’s ability to lower pollutant emissions compared with baseline conditions) was found to be approximately three times greater in the DAR-PC scenario than in the DAR-MCV scenario (Fig. 9). This shows that, despite slightly higher fuel costs, the DAR-PC configuration provides substantially higher environmental benefits, achieving greater emission reduction efficiency per unit distance traveled.

It was concluded that, within the DAR scenario, passenger cars exhibit a greater emission reduction capacity compared to light commercial vehicles, relative to their respective fuel consumption costs.

Impact of GHG emissions under the NMM scenario

This section refers to the NMM-PC (Northern Marmara Motorway–Personal Car) scenario, which directs passenger cars, and the NMM-MCV (Northern Marmara Motorway–Medium Commercial Vehicle) scenario, which directs medium commercial vehicles to the Northern Marmara Motorway. According to the scenario results, the total fuel consumption cost, calculated based on the number of vehicles, fuel price, fuel consumption per vehicle, and travel distance, was estimated at $33,536,231 for NMM-PC and $28,479,941 for NMM-MCV. When examining the emission reduction capacity in Fig. 9, similar to the DAR scenario, the NMM-PC scenario yielded approximately three times the reduction compared to the NMM-MCV scenario. This outcome confirms that passenger cars have a greater potential for emission reduction.

Investigation of the seasonal variation in transportation-related emission reduction

The seasonal variation in the capacity for reducing transportation-related GHG emissions in the Dilovası district, derived from all rerouting scenarios, is illustrated in Fig. 9. Analysis of CO₂ concentration profiles indicates that the highest emission levels occur during spring and summer, while the lowest values are observed in winter. Across all modeled cases, emissions follow a bell-shaped seasonal trend, increasing from winter to summer and decreasing toward autumn. The seasonal CO₂ reduction capacity, defined as the potential of a route or traffic management scenario to lower CO₂ emissions relative to baseline conditions, varies notably throughout the year. This variation reflects seasonal differences in vehicle activity, fuel combustion efficiency, and meteorological conditions such as temperature and mixing height. During warmer months, increased traffic volume, particularly from passenger and light commercial vehicles, leads to higher baseline CO₂ emissions. Consequently, the rerouting effectiveness of DAR and NMM scenarios becomes more evident during these periods.Between spring and summer, traffic intensity rises by approximately 1.3–2 times, with major arterials and intercity routes becoming emission hotspots within the district57. As a result, the CO₂ reduction capacity of optimized rerouting scenarios, especially NMM-PC and NMM-MCV, peaks during summer, when high traffic density and limited atmospheric dispersion amplify pollution levels. This pattern confirms that long-distance CO₂ transport and accumulation are most significant in summer, highlighting the need for seasonally adaptive traffic management strategies. In summary, seasonal CO₂ reduction capacity serves as a diagnostic indicator for analyzing temporal emission dynamics and guiding ITS-based interventions, ensuring effective mitigation during the most emission-intensive months.

In several studies58, CO₂ emissions from light vehicles have been identified as the dominant contributor among greenhouse gas sources. In the present research, the CO₂ reduction capacity of passenger cars over long distances was found to be higher than that of MCVs (Fig. 10), reflecting the direct relationship between total emissions, distance traveled, and traffic intensity. Figure 11 provides a statistical overview of the seasonal variability of CO₂ concentrations across all rerouting scenarios. Using synchronized datasets, box plots illustrate the distribution and variability of CO₂ reduction capacity under varying climatic and operational conditions. Each color-coded box represents a specific scenario, DAR-PC (blue), DAR-MCV (yellow), NMM-PC (green), and NMM-MCV (red). The median, interquartile range (IQR), and whiskers capture central trends, data spread, and extreme observations caused by short-term traffic surges or weather fluctuations. Analysis of the box plots shows that variability is greatest in winter, driven by unstable meteorological conditions such as low temperatures, inversions, and reduced atmospheric dispersion, which impair combustion efficiency. Conversely, spring displays the most stable CO₂ reduction performance, with a narrower IQR linked to moderate weather and smooth traffic flow. Across all scenarios, MCV cases exhibit lower CO₂ reduction capacity than PC cases, mainly due to differences in engine characteristics, payload, and fuel consumption. Seasonal analysis also indicates a decline in reduction capacity from autumn to winter, associated with cold-start emissions and limited atmospheric mixing. In contrast, the NMM-PC scenario shows only a slight decrease between summer and autumn, reflecting sustained private-car use and steady traffic volumes. Among all scenarios, NMM-PC demonstrated the highest overall CO₂ reduction, attributed to long-distance, high-speed travel that enhances combustion efficiency and reduces emissions per kilometer. Additionally, mild skewness observed in several winter datasets suggests occasional extreme emission behavior under unfavorable driving or weather conditions, highlighting the value of box-plot analysis as a diagnostic tool for understanding both central tendencies and variability in emission dynamics.

Analysis of scenario results in terms of seasonal variation.

Diagram illustrating the seasonal variations of the DAR and NMM scenario results.

Previous studies59 have established a strong correlation between air temperature and GHG emissions, showing that elevated temperatures generally improve combustion efficiency, resulting in lower CO₂ output per distance traveled. This correlation is confirmed in the present study, where the temperature-dependent variability of transportation-related emissions is evident through the higher CO₂ reduction capacity observed in PC scenarios, particularly during warmer months. Conversely, MCV scenarios display smaller seasonal reductions, reflecting their less fuel-efficient operating cycles and higher baseline emission factors. Overall, the box-plot analysis highlights that while route optimization and ITS strategies significantly improve average emission outcomes, the degree of variability between seasons remains a key factor for policy planning. Understanding the statistical distribution of reduction performance enables more precise design of season-specific mitigation measures to achieve effective, data-driven management of transportation-related CO₂ emissions.

Discussion

When comparing CO2 levels under the DAR and NMM scenarios, directing vehicles to a more distant route has significantly contributed to the reduction of vehicle-related emissions in Dilovası district. In this context, the CO2 reduction capacity under the NMM emission scenario yields substantially better results than the DAR scenario. Despite the differences in emission reduction amounts, the fuel consumption costs are higher in the NMM scenario compared to the DAR scenario. Considering the contribution of emission reductions by vehicle type to GHGs in the district, the CO2 reduction capacity under the NMM-PC scenario is 38.6% higher than that of the NMM-MCV scenario. Within the NMM scenario itself, the NMM-PC scenario demonstrates the lowest fuel consumption cost despite a high emission reduction rate. Similar to the results of the DAR-PC and DAR-MCV scenarios, these findings support the new regulations aiming for zero CO2 emissions from new passenger cars by 203560, making it plausible that passenger car emissions are relatively higher than those from light commercial vehicles. According to the scenario outcomes, the average fuel consumption cost is higher for medium commercial vehicles, reflecting recent efforts to improve fuel efficiency in this vehicle category61.

Conclusions and future work

This study investigated the environmental impacts of transportation-related GHG emissions in the Dilovası district of Kocaeli Province, a region characterized by severe air pollution. Two artificial intelligence models, ANFIS and ANN, were developed to predict CO₂ emissions and evaluate the effectiveness of vehicle rerouting strategies for emission reduction. The ANFIS model achieved superior predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.99) compared to the ANN model, demonstrating strong potential for supporting intelligent transportation planning. The results showed that redirecting vehicles—especially passenger cars—to the Northern Marmara Motorway significantly reduced CO₂ concentrations within the district, particularly during spring and summer when emissions peaked. Medium commercial vehicles exhibited higher average fuel consumption costs than passenger cars, indicating the importance of fuel efficiency improvements in this category. Seasonal variations and vehicle types were found to be key factors influencing CO₂ reduction potential. Despite its high accuracy, the ANFIS model faces challenges such as computational complexity and potential overfitting with limited datasets. Conversely, ANN offers better scalability but lower precision. The study therefore recommends exploring hybrid or ensemble models that integrate the strengths of both techniques. Future research should extend the analysis beyond CO₂ to include other pollutants such as particulate matter (PM) and non-methane volatile organic compounds (NMVOC), as well as factors like road structure and driving behavior, to enhance model robustness and generalizability. Integrating data from high-traffic regions and applying alternative AI-based approaches could further improve the model’s applicability. Additionally, evaluating the health implications of vehicular emissions would provide a more comprehensive understanding of transportation’s environmental and societal impacts. Overall, the study highlights the critical importance of emission monitoring and predictive modeling in highly polluted areas like Dilovası, emphasizing that data-driven transportation management can play a vital role in achieving sustainable mobility and mitigating climate change.
