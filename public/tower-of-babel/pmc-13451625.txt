Use of Fibrin Sealant Combined With Demineralized Freeze‐Dried Bone Allograft in the Management of a Recurrent Mandibular Aneurysmal Bone Cyst: A Case Report
Authors: Zakaria Mokhtari, Christophe Politis, Firas Shall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecurrent aneurysmal bone cysts of the jaw present significant therapeutic challenges. This case suggests that fibrin sealant combined with demineralized freeze‐dried bone allograft, as an adjunct to curettage, may offer a conservative option in selected young patients after failure of conventional therapies. Further studies are needed.

Introduction

The aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) is a rare, benign, expansile osteolytic lesion. The World Health Organization describes it as “an expansive osteolytic lesion consisting of various‐sized blood‐filled spaces or channels separated by connective tissue septa that can contain multinucleated giant cells and osteoid tissue” [1, 2]. ABCs may arise de novo (primary ABC) or develop secondarily within a pre‐existing lesion such as ossifying fibroma, giant cell granuloma, or fibrous dysplasia [3]. ABCs are most commonly found in long bones (approximately 50%), the spine (12%–30%), and more rarely in the jaws (2%–12%), with a predominance in the mandible [1, 2, 4, 5, 6]. ABCs typically appear before the age of 20, with a mean age of onset of 14.3 years [6, 7, 8, 9].

Clinically, ABCs most often present as a slowly evolving, painless swelling, although rapid and aggressive progression can occur [1, 5, 6, 8]. The natural history encompasses several evolutive phases, from active osteolysis to stabilization and potential spontaneous ossification, which may complicate diagnosis [10]. Therapeutic options include curettage, en bloc resection, embolization, percutaneous sclerotherapy, and denosumab [4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 12, 13]. Recurrence rates following curettage alone remain high, ranging from 19% to 56% [4, 7, 14].

Fay et al., in 2009, reported the use of fibrin glue (Tissucol Duo, Baxter AG) as an intraoperative adjunct during posterior resection of a spinal ABC at C2, with no recurrence at 1 year [9]. To our knowledge, no study describes the use of fibrin sealant combined with demineralized freeze‐dried bone allograft (DFDBA) in the management of maxillofacial ABCs. We report the case of a 13‐year‐old female with a recurrent mandibular ABC treated by extensive curettage with DFDBA and fibrin sealant (Tisseel, Baxter AG), with no recurrence at 2 years.

Case History/Examination

A 13‐year‐old female patient was referred by her orthodontist for a right mandibular swelling that had appeared 3 months earlier. The swelling was occasionally painful in the morning. Clinical examination revealed a hard swelling in the vestibule of the fourth quadrant. There was no hypoesthesia of the inferior alveolar nerve territory. Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Extraction of Tooth 48 and curettage were performed under general anesthesia. An empty cavity with a thin cortical wall was observed; notably, there was no hemorrhagic content. Histopathological analysis described epithelium‐lined connective tissue consistent with a dentigerous cyst, though the epithelial lining may have originated from the dental follicle of the adjacent impacted tooth rather than representing a true dentigerous cyst. At 4 months postoperatively, the PAN showed osteogenesis at the curettage site.

A second curettage under general anesthesia was performed. Intraoperatively, the cavity was again devoid of hemorrhagic content, with no abnormal bleeding. Histological evaluation described fibro‐osseous material consistent with ossifying fibroma. However, this diagnosis was considered incongruent with the macroscopic finding of an empty cavity rather than the solid mass expected in ossifying fibroma. Molecular analysis showed no GNAS mutation, ruling out fibrous dysplasia.

After a period of observation, a third intervention was performed, 1 year after the second surgery. Preoperative MRI showed a mixed lesion extending to the coronoid process with preservation of the condyle. Extensive curettage with biopsies from multiple sites was performed. Histological analysis described cystic fluid composed of erythrocytes and fibrin, consistent with an ABC (Figure 6). A retrospective review of the second biopsy specimens by an independent pathologist confirmed findings compatible with ABC, including blood‐filled spaces and connective tissue septa with giant cells.

Molecular testing for USP6 rearrangement was not performed at the time of diagnosis. Retrospective analysis was conducted on archived formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissue from the third curettage specimen (collected February 2021, analyzed May 2026) using the Archer FusionPlex Sarcoma V2 panel, an RNA‐based next‐generation sequencing (NGS) assay targeting fusion genes in 61 oncogenes including USP6. No fusion transcript was detected; however, sequencing quality was suboptimal. Three factors compromised the analysis: (1) the bone specimen had undergone decalcification during histological processing, which significantly degrades nucleic acids and reduces NGS success rates [15]; (2) tumor cellularity was only 10%, substantially below the threshold for reliable fusion detection; and (3) the available material was limited. These technical limitations preclude definitive exclusion of a USP6 rearrangement. Notably, USP6 rearrangements are characteristically absent in secondary ABCs arising within pre‐existing lesions [3], a possibility discussed below.

After three unsuccessful curettages, segmental resection with free fibula flap reconstruction was considered but deferred given the patient's young age and the significant morbidity of such an intervention. In July 2021, a combined procedure was performed by interventional radiology: selective arterial embolization followed by percutaneous sclerotherapy, using ethoxysclerol (polidocanol) and n‐butyl cyanoacrylate glue (Glubran) (Figure 3). Leakage of the sclerosing agent prevented complete filling of the cystic cavity.

Approximately 1 year after the second sclerotherapy, the patient presented with recurrent pain and swelling. CT scan demonstrated persistent hypodense areas with no significant improvement, confirming that neither embolization nor sclerotherapy had achieved definitive healing.

A fourth extensive curettage was performed under general anesthesia via an intraoral vestibular approach. A mucoperiosteal flap was raised with a cold blade incision, exposing the buccal cortex. Two distinct residual cavities were identified and meticulously curetted using hand curettes to remove all pathological tissue. The graft material was prepared by combining 6 cm3 of DFDBA (particle size 300–500 μm; Erasme Hospital Tissue Bank) with 4 mL of fibrin sealant (Tisseel, Baxter AG)—a volumetric DFDBA‐to‐fibrin ratio of 1.5:1—to form a moldable, cohesive matrix. The fibrin sealant, composed of human fibrinogen with factor XIII and fibronectin combined with bovine thrombin and calcium chloride, bound the DFDBA particles into a stable composite. Each cavity was sequentially packed using a bone syringe and a bone plugger to ensure firm adaptation of the graft material to the bony walls. No collagen membrane was used. The flap was closed with resorbable Vicryl Rapide 4‐0 sutures. The procedure lasted approximately 1 h. Histological analysis of curettage material described fibrous scar tissue with inflammatory remodeling and foreign body giant cells.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

CT scan at 11 months postoperatively demonstrated significant bone graft integration with increased trabecular density. Coronal CT showed reconstitution of the buccal and lingual cortices (Figure 5A). Axial CT at the mandibular body level showed trabeculated remodeling with bone consolidation (Figure 5B). However, axial CT at the occlusal level demonstrated preservation of all teeth with areas of incomplete graft integration (Figure 5C). Despite this partial integration, there was no evidence of cystic recurrence or expansile behavior. A transient hypoesthesia of the inferior alveolar nerve resolved completely within the first months. At 2 years postoperatively, the patient was asymptomatic with no pain, swelling, or sensory deficit. Orthodontic treatment was initiated, consistent with adequate mandibular stability.

Ongoing surveillance consists of PAN radiographs every 6 months during the first 2 years, then annually, with CT or cone‐beam CT (CBCT) performed every 3 years or earlier if clinical or radiographic suspicion arises. This protocol is maintained for a minimum of 5 years, consistent with published recommendations, as more than 90% of ABC recurrences occur within the first 2 years after treatment [16, 17].

Given the cumulative radiation exposure from multiple CT examinations over the treatment course in a growing adolescent, imaging surveillance follows the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle. PAN radiographs serve as the primary screening tool, with CT or CBCT reserved for suspected recurrence or scheduled milestone evaluations, thereby minimizing radiation dose while maintaining adequate surveillance sensitivity.

Discussion

ABC was first described in 1942 by Jaffe and Lichtenstein as a blood‐filled pseudocyst with giant cells and bony trabeculae [1, 14]. ABCs account for approximately 1% of all bone lesions, with the mandibular posterior region most commonly affected [5, 7, 18, 19]. To our knowledge, this is the fourth reported case involving the coronoid process [11, 12, 19].

The diagnostic evolution in this case—from dentigerous cyst to ossifying fibroma to ABC over three interventions—warrants careful interpretation. We favor the hypothesis of a secondary ABC arising within a pre‐existing lesion for several reasons. First, the initial histological finding of a dentigerous cyst associated with the impacted Tooth 48 [20] and the subsequent identification of fibro‐osseous tissue compatible with ossifying fibroma suggest that an underlying odontogenic or fibro‐osseous lesion may have preceded the development of ABC‐type changes. Secondary ABCs arising within ossifying fibromas of the jaws have been well documented and represent the most common association among secondary jaw ABCs [21, 22]. Second, the negative USP6 result, despite its technical limitations, is consistent with the known absence of USP6 rearrangements in secondary ABCs, as demonstrated by Oliveira et al., who showed that USP6 and CDH11 oncogenes are present in primary but absent in secondary ABCs [3]. Third, incomplete or nonrepresentative biopsies during the quiescent phases likely sampled non‐ABC components of the lesion, contributing to the initial diagnostic discordance.

A distinctive feature of this case is the absence of hemorrhagic content at the first two surgical interventions. Dabska and Buraczewski described four evolutive phases: osteolysis, growth with progressive bone destruction, stabilization with sclerotic borders and internal septations, and healing with progressive ossification [10]. We hypothesize that the ABC component was encountered during its stabilization phase, at which point the hemorrhagic content had undergone spontaneous resorption. The finding of an empty cavity with a thin cortical wall is consistent with a blood‐depleted cystic space in this quiescent state. Only at the third intervention, when the lesion had re‐entered its active phase, were the classic histological features of ABC identified: blood‐filled cystic spaces, fibrous septa with multinucleated giant cells, hemosiderin‐laden macrophages, and reactive osteoid (Figure 6).

The diagnostic challenge in this case underscores the necessity of including ABC in the differential diagnosis of aggressive jaw lesions, even without hemorrhagic content at surgery. The differential diagnosis includes ossifying fibroma, giant cell tumor, telangiectatic osteosarcoma, ameloblastoma, and cherubism [6, 18]. Liu et al. found that pathological classification and surgical treatment significantly influenced recurrence rates in 44 jaw ABCs [21], while a recent systematic review emphasized USP6 rearrangement testing for definitive diagnosis of primary ABC [23].

Several therapeutic modalities exist, including curettage, en bloc resection, embolization, sclerotherapy, and denosumab [4, 5, 7, 24, 25, 26]. Curettage alone carries a 19%–56% recurrence rate [4, 7, 14], while curettage with adjuvant therapy reduces this to under 10% [16]. In our case, en bloc resection with fibula flap reconstruction (0%–5% recurrence) [4, 7, 8] was deferred given the patient's age, favoring a stepwise conservative approach.

Selective arterial embolization can achieve results comparable to curettage and bone grafting in selected cases [27]. Percutaneous sclerotherapy with polidocanol or NBCA has shown a 97% cure rate in 72 patients [25] and healing in 93% of cases [26]. Gonzalez et al. recently reported successful mandibular ABC treatment combining sclerotherapy with curettage [28]. In our patient, selective embolization with sclerotherapy was attempted in a first session, followed by sclerotherapy alone in a second session. Technical difficulties—leakage of the sclerosing agent—prevented complete cavity filling, and the response remained partial despite two sessions.

Denosumab has shown favorable responses in recurrent ABCs [24], but the risk of rebound hypercalcemia in pediatric patients [29, 30] led us to prefer a local surgical alternative.

The rationale for fibrin sealant was twofold: hemostasis and promotion of bone healing. Fibrin sealant mimics the final phase of coagulation, and its fibronectin component has been shown in preclinical studies to facilitate extracellular matrix adhesion, cell differentiation, and growth factor binding [13, 31]. Fibrin has been reported to promote osteoblast differentiation more effectively than collagen in vitro [32] and may serve as a scaffold maintaining graft particles within the defect [33, 34]. Fay et al. previously reported trabecular bone regeneration at 1 year following posterior resection of a spinal ABC with intraoperative fibrin glue [9]. In our case, the DFDBA particles (300–500 μm) provided osteoinductive and osteoconductive properties, while the fibrin sealant maintained them in situ.

The preceding embolization and sclerotherapy, although insufficient for definitive healing, induced partial bone condensation that may have created more favorable conditions for subsequent grafting. Whether this resulted from vascular occlusion, a direct sclerosing effect, or both remains unclear. CT at 12 months showed areas of incomplete graft integration with persistent hypodense zones. Even partial integration may be sufficient to mechanically fill the cavity, prevent fluid reaccumulation, and provide a framework for continued remodeling.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this is a single‐case report, and the specific contribution of each component—fibrin sealant, DFDBA, prior embolization, and sclerotherapy—cannot be disentangled; a synergistic effect is plausible. Second, the USP6 molecular analysis was inconclusive due to the technical limitations of the decalcified specimen, precluding definitive classification as primary or secondary ABC. Third, the 2‐year follow‐up, although encouraging, remains relatively short for a lesion known to recur years after treatment, and areas of incomplete graft integration persist. Fourth, the biological mechanisms attributed to fibrin sealant in this context are extrapolated from preclinical data and a single prior case report [9]; no controlled clinical evidence supports its superiority over DFDBA grafting alone in ABC management. This approach should therefore be considered exploratory and not generalized as an alternative to resection.

At 2 years, our patient is asymptomatic with progressive bone densification and has begun orthodontic treatment. Ethics Statement

This case report did not require formal ethics committee approval as it describes routine clinical care without experimental intervention. Both DFDBA and fibrin sealant are individually approved and routinely used in oral and maxillofacial surgery; their combined use represented a clinical decision based on available evidence. The patient's parents were fully informed and consented to the procedure. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines [35].
