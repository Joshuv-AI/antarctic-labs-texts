Oriented hydrogel biomimicking muscle fibers to treat pelvic organ prolapse via integrated immunomodulation and electrical stimulation
Authors: Xinting Yang, Hongru Li, Yubin Feng, Chenke Wei, Yukai Guo, Bai Yang, Tianmin Xu, Quan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPelvic organ prolapse is a displacement disorder caused by the weakening of pelvic support tissues, leading to severe urinary incontinence, compromised organ support, and a high recurrence risk. Although clinically used polypropylene meshes provide mechanical reinforcement, their inherent stiffness often causes persistent mechanical overstimulation and chronic inflammation, ultimately resulting in implantation failure. Here, inspired by the parallel-aligned architecture of muscle fibers, we fabricate an oriented and hydrophilic hydrogel via directional freezing, composed of poly(vinyl alcohol), glycyrrhizic acid, Zn2+, and an aniline tetramer–grafted oxidized alginate. The oriented microstructure not only delivers stable mechanical support but also facilitates directional cell proliferation and migration, thereby promoting reconstruction of the damaged pelvic floor architecture. Glycyrrhizic acid and Zn2+ synergistically scavenge reactive oxygen species and regulate macrophage-associated inflammatory responses, establishing a regeneration-favorable immune microenvironment. Simultaneously, the aniline tetramer–grafted oxidized alginate endows the hydrogel with electroactivity, and when in combination with electrical stimulation, promoting the migration and functional maturation of collagen-producing cells and myogenic cells at the prolapse site. In vivo, the oriented hydrogel exhibits good adaptation to pelvic tissues, effectively attenuating inflammation and inducing orientation-dependent tissue ingrowth. Overall, this muscle–inspired hydrogel with a parallel-aligned architecture, when integrated with electrical stimulation and immunomodulation, promotes connective tissue repair and offers a highly promising strategy for treating pelvic organ prolapse.Keywords: Pelvic organ prolapse, Electrical stimulation, Conductive hydrogel, Oriented hydrogel

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is a highly prevalent pelvic floor disorder caused by the progressive degeneration of pelvic floor muscles and connective tissues, affecting approximately 50% of parous women and nearly 25% of elderly women [1]. POP is associated with obstetric injury, age-related tissue degeneration, multiple childbirths, obesity, and sustained mechanical loading, which collectively compromise the structural integrity of pelvic floor muscles and connective tissues [2]. The disrupted collagen composition, disorganized muscle fiber architecture, and reduced tensile strength of the pelvic fascia ultimately permit the descent of pelvic organs along the vaginal axis, resulting in urinary, defecatory, and sexual dysfunction [3]. As the incidence and severity of POP increase with age, the demand for surgical intervention is expected to rise further with increasing life expectancy, which brings a heavy psychological and economic burden to the patients [4].

In clinical, PP meshes are commonly used for POP reconstruction [5]. PP is characterized by high rigidity, hydrophobicity, and non-degradability, resulting in significant biological and mechanical mismatches with the pelvic floor soft tissues [6]. Although PP meshes can provide short-term passive mechanical support, their inherent stiffness often causes persistent mechanical overstimulation [7]. The hydrophobic surface promotes nonspecific protein adsorption and inflammatory cell adhesion, leading to chronic inflammation and foreign body reactions [8]. The increased inflammation, poor tissue integration, and fibrotic encapsulation not only reduce mechanical support but also contribute to mesh curling, displacement, exposure, and erosion, ultimately resulting in implantation failure [9]. Therefore, it is essential to develop new repair materials that are hydrophilic, biocompatible, and capable of promoting collagen reconstruction.

In recent years, regenerative medicine and tissue engineering strategies have been considered as alternative approaches for treating POP [10,11]. Hydrogels have been widely studied as biomimetic materials for tissue engineering because of their high hydration, softness, and good biocompatibility [12]. Among these, conductive hydrogels can not only provide extracellular matrix–like microenvironments but also enhance electrical communication between cells and regulate the electrophysiological microenvironment to facilitate cell infiltration and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Several hydrogel systems have been reported to repair the pelvic floor, including drug-loaded hydrogels for promoting pelvic floor repair by regulating inflammation, bioactive hydrogels loaded with stem cells to promote pelvic tissue regeneration, and hydrogel coatings to mitigate inflammation induced by PP meshes [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. These hydrogel-based strategies for pelvic organ prolapse repair have focused on inflammatory regulation and extracellular matrix–mimicking microenvironment to improve tissue compatibility and regeneration. However, the therapeutic effects of oriented hydrogels in pelvic organ prolapse repair have not yet been systematically investigated. Pelvic skeletal muscle and connective tissues exhibit intrinsic anisotropy, with collagen fibers and muscle fibers aligned along principal stress directions to enhance mechanical performance and guide physiological load transfer [21]. Designing oriented hydrogels biomimicking muscle fibers may offer an opportunity to improve direction-dependent mechanical support and promote cell alignment and tissue ingrowth consistent with native connective tissue organization. Based on these considerations, oriented hydrogel systems integrating electroactivity and immunomodulatory properties have the potential to act as an effective therapeutic strategy for pelvic organ prolapse treatment.

In this study, inspired by the parallel-aligned architecture of muscle fibers, we fabricated an oriented and hydrophilic PGZA hydrogel via directional freezing, composed of poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), glycyrrhizic acid (GA), Zn2+, and an aniline tetramer–grafted oxidized alginate (OAlg-AT). The anisotropic architecture of the biocompatible PGZA hydrogels not only delivers stable mechanical support to avoid tissue abrasion and complications caused by traditional PP meshes, but also guides directional cell migration and organized collagen deposition for pelvic floor reconstruction [22,23]. To further improve the regenerative microenvironment, GA and Zn2+ are integrated to synergistically scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), modulate macrophage-related inflammatory responses, and accelerate connective tissue regeneration [[24], [25], [26]]. Meanwhile, GA cooperating with Zn2+ enhances the mechanical stability of the hydrogel through the formation of the second crosslinked network by self-assembly [27]. Additionally, the electroactive OAlg-AT component, coupled with the oriented microchannel structure, facilitates efficient electrical signal propagation along the alignment direction, thereby enhancing cellular electrophysiological responsiveness and promoting the functional maturation of collagen-producing and myogenic cells [28]. To assess these integrated effects in vivo, we use a rat abdominal wall defect model as a surrogate for pelvic floor repair, owing to its collagen-rich structure and load-bearing characteristics relevant to pelvic connective tissues [29]. This study evaluates the influence of PGZA2 hydrogel and electrical stimulation on connective tissue regeneration and immune-related responses through histological analyses and assessment of inflammatory markers. The multifunctional PGZA2 hydrogel patch integrates anisotropic mechanical adaptation, immunomodulation, and electrically enhanced tissue regeneration, offering a promising strategy for pelvic organ prolapse treatment.

Materials and methods

Materials

PVA (average Mw = 130,000, 99+% hydrolyzed), N-phenyl-p-phenylenediamine, phenylhydrazine and sodium periodate were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Glycyrrhizic acid ammonium salt (≈99.0%) and sodium alginate were purchased from Aladdin. Zinc sulfate (ZnSO4 ≈99.0%), aniline, acetone (AR, 99%), N, N-dimethylformamide (AR, 99%), dimethyl sulfoxide (AR, 99%) and ethylene glycol (AR, 99%) were obtained from Beijing Chemical Works (Beijing, P. R. China).

Synthesis of OAlg-AT

Oxidized sodium alginate (OAlg) was synthesized by using a procedure with some modifications [30]. After getting 100 mL 1% (wt/v) sodium alginate solution, 0.5 g sodium periodate solution was added to the solution, then stirred for 4 h at room temperature in the dark. After that, 1 mL ethylene glycol was added to the solution and stirred for 1 h. The solution was dialyzed (MW = 3500) for 3 days against deionized water, and the final product was obtained by a freeze-dried method.

Aniline tetramer (AT) and Aniline tetramer–grafted oxidized alginate (OAlg-AT) were synthesized according to previous reports with modifications [31]. Briefly, after 3 g (NH4)2S2O8 dissolved in the mixture of cold 15 mL acetone and 15 mL H2O, the solution was added dropwise to the mixture of 3 g N-phenyl-p-phenylenediamine, 150 mL acetone, and 2 M 110 mL hydrochloric acid at 0°C under constant stirring within 30 min. The mixture was stirred 4-5 h at ice bath. After that, AT was doped with 0.5 M 170 mL ammonium hydroxide, and 5.5 mL phenylhydrazine was added to the solution. The final product was centrifuged at 8800 rpm and vacuum dried to obtain AT powder. Then, AT was dissolved in DMF and added to the OAlg solution, followed by stirring for 4 h at 50°C under nitrogen. The mixture was then centrifugated and the supernatant was precipitated with alcohol. The precipitate was collected by vacuum filtration and dried in vacuum overnight.

Synthesis of PVA hydrogels, PGZA hydrogels with random network, and oriented PGZA hydrogels

Synthesis of PVA Hydrogels: Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, 1.8 g) was dissolved in 15 mL of deionized water to obtain a 12 wt% PVA solution. The solution was poured into molds and frozen at −20°C for 12 h, followed by thawing at room temperature to obtain the PVA hydrogel.

Synthesis of PGZA Hydrogels with Random Network: Glycyrrhizic acid (GA, 150 mg) and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, 1.8 g) were dissolved in 15 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 300 μL of 0.01 M ZnSO4 solution was added under continuous stirring until a homogeneous solution was obtained. The mixture was then heated in a water bath at 95°C, followed by the addition of a certain amount of aniline tetramer grafted oxidized alginate (OAlg-AT) (0 mg, 15 mg, 30 mg, and 45 mg). After thorough mixing, the resulting solution was poured into molds and frozen at −20°C for 12 h, followed by thawing at room temperature to obtain the PGZA hydrogels with a random network.

Synthesis of Oriented PGZA Hydrogels: The oriented structure of PGZA hydrogels was constructed by using a procedure with some modifications [32]. The solution above was poured into molds and placed on the top of the thick brass block. The bottom of the mold was contacted with liquid nitrogen for 20 min. After the solution was completely frozen, the mold was transferred to refrigerator (T = −20°C) for 12 h, followed by thawing at room temperature to obtain the oriented PGZA hydrogels. The tensile specimens were cast in molds of 10 mm × 5 mm × 20 mm (Length × Width × Height), whereas the compression specimens were cast in molds of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm (Length × Width × Height). Unless otherwise specified, samples for other tests were cut from hydrogels prepared using the compression-test molds.

Electrical measurement

The electrical measurement of the PGZA hydrogel was recorded by an electrochemical workstation (CHI660C). The detailed test method followed our previous work [32]. In the cyclic voltammetry (CV) test, the voltage range was −0.4–0.4 V, the scan rate was 0.1 V/s, and the scan cycles were repeated 50 times.

Characterization

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (VG ESCALAB MKII) was used to determine the chemical composition of PGZA hydrogel. A JEOL FESEM 6700F electron microscope was used to obtain scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) images with a primary electron energy of 15 kV. The oriented microstructure of PGZA hydrogels were tested by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) using Xeuss 3.0. The orientation order parameter of the hydrogels was calculated from the azimuthal intensity profiles of the 2D SAXS patterns using the Hermans orientation factor: f=(3(cos2ϕ)−1)/2, where ϕ is the angle between the structural orientation and the reference direction. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired on a VERTEX 80V FTIR spectrometer, testing from 4000 to 400 cm−1. The mechanical properties were tested on an Instron 5944 universal material testing machine. The dynamic mechanical properties of PGZA hydrogels were tested on a rheometer (TA Discovery DHR-2). For all rheological, compression, and tensile measurements, each condition was tested on three independently prepared hydrogel samples (n = 3). The Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra were recorded on a spectrophotometer Shimadzu UV-2600.

Cell culture and cell viability assay

Standard L929 fibroblasts (Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin double antibiotics at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cell viability was evaluated using a cell counting kit 8 (CCK-8, Dojindo Molecular Technologies) assay to assess the effect of PGZA hydrogels and electrical stimulation (ES) on cell proliferation over time. The cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well, and treated with different hydrogel formulations (PGZA0, PGZA2, and PGZA2 + ES). After culturing for 24 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added, and the cells were cultured for another 20 min. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured, and the cell viability was calculated. The electrical stimulation culture plates were purchased from Changchun Ubbiotech Co., Ltd. (EC-001, UBBIOTECH, China). Electrical stimulation was generated using a signal generator (UTG-932, Uni-trend Technology China Co., Ltd.). The parameters were set according to the previous research: 6 V, 1 Hz, 10 ms for 4 h per day [33].

Live/dead assay

To confirm the findings from the cell viability assay, a live/dead staining assay was conducted on Day 7. Cells were stained with a mixture of calcein-AM (live cells) and propidium iodide (dead cells) to visualize and quantify the proportion of live cells in each group. Images were captured under a fluorescence microscope, and the live cell counts were compared.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration was assessed using a scratch assay. Cells were cultured to confluence in 6-well plates, and a scratch was made using a sterile pipette tip. The cells were then treated with PGZA hydrogels and electrical stimulation as described above. The migration distance was measured at 0 h and 48 h post-scratch. Images were captured at both time points, and the migrated distance was quantified.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement

The antioxidant efficiency of hydrogels was evaluated by the method of scavenging the stable 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical and 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) [34].

DPPH scavenging (%)=(AC-AHydrogel)/AC ×100%, where AC, AHydrogel are the absorption in 519 nm of the Control (DPPH + ethanol) and the absorption of the hydrogel (DPPH + ethanol + hydrogel), respectively.

ABTS scavenging (%)=(AC-AHydrogel)/AC ×100%, where AC, AHydrogel are the absorption in 734 nm of the Control and the absorption of the hydrogel, respectively.

The intracellular levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured using a ROS assay kit (DCFDA, ab113851, Abcam company, China). Cells were cultured and treated with the hydrogel formulations and electrical stimulation as described above. After treatment, cells were incubated with DCFDA for 30 min at 37°C, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader. ROS levels were quantified, and data were normalized to the control.

In vitro vascularization assay

To assess the effect of PGZA hydrogels on angiogenesis, an in vitro vascularization assay was performed. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with different hydrogel formulations and electrical stimulation. After 72 h of incubation, the formation of blood vessel-like structures was observed.

In vitro degradation assay

Lyophilized PGZA2 hydrogels (n = 3) were weighed to establish an initial dry mass (W0) and submerged in 10 mL of PBS solution (pH = 7.4) containing 100 U/mL collagenase and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Samples were incubated at 37°C water bath and the enzymatic medium was refreshed every 48 h over the 12 weeks. The samples were re-lyophilized to determine the residual mass (Wt) every week. The degradation ratio was calculated: (W0-Wt)/W0 × 100%.

Cellular organization and alignment

Cellular organization and alignment were observed under a microscope after treatment with PGZA hydrogels and electrical stimulation. The degree of cellular alignment was evaluated by cell orientation.

Transcriptome sequencing and data processing

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to obtain comprehensive gene expression data. Sequencing reads were aligned to the reference genome using the STAR aligner, and gene expression levels were quantified using featureCounts. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was conducted using DESeq2, with statistical significance determined by an adjusted P < 0.05.

Identification of DEGs

A volcano plot was constructed to visually represent the downregulated and upregulated genes. Heatmap analysis of DEGs was performed using the pheatmap R package to cluster genes and highlight differential expression patterns between the sham, PGZA2, and PGZA2 + ES treatment groups.

Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and reactome pathway analysis

GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the DAVID bioinformatics resources to identify significant biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions associated with the DEGs. KEGG pathway analysis was performed to identify enriched signaling pathways linked to the DEGs. The KEGG database was accessed through the cluster Profiler R package to visualize the data. Reactome pathway analysis was used to further explore the role of immune signaling.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and macrophage polarization analysis

GSEA was performed to analyze the overall enrichment of predefined gene sets related to immune and inflammatory responses. The expression levels of macrophage markers, Ifit3 (M1 macrophage marker) and Lgals9 (M2 macrophage marker), were measured to assess macrophage polarization.

In vivo animal experiment

All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the College of Basic Medical Science, Jilin University, Changchun, China (Approval number:2025096) and the license number for the use of experimental animals is SYXK2022-0015. To simulate the clinical manifestations of POP, an abdominal wall muscle defect model was established in female Sprague‒Dawley rats (200 g, Charles River company, Beijing, China). All rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital before the experiments. After shaving the abdomens of the rats, a U-shaped abdominal incision was made. The subcutaneous tissue and muscle layer were removed, and the peritoneal tissue was preserved. Then, a round full-thickness abdominal wall muscle defect with a diameter of 1.0 cm was created at the end. The rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6): control group (without treatment), PGZA0 group, PGZA2 group, and PGZA2 + ES group. On the 14th and 42nd days post-surgery, 3 rats in each group were sacrificed, and the defects and the surrounding tissues were removed for histological analysis. The intensity of ES was 15 Hz, 10 mA, 0.3 VA,20 min [33].

Tissue sample preparation and sectioning

Obtained tissues were immediately fixed in 10% formalin for 24 h and subsequently processed for paraffin embedding. Sections (5 μm thick) were cut using a Leica RM2135 microtome (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and mounted onto glass slides for histological staining.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

H&E staining was performed to assess general tissue morphology, cellular organization, and inflammatory responses. The tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. After staining with hematoxylin for 5 min, sections were washed and counterstained with eosin for 2 min. The slides were then dehydrated and mounted with a coverslip using Permount (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The extent of tissue disorganization, inflammatory infiltrates, and cellular arrangement was evaluated qualitatively.

Elastica Van Gieson (EVG) staining

EVG staining was used to visualize elastic fibers, which are important indicators of tissue elasticity and maturation. After deparaffinization and rehydration, tissue sections were stained using the Elastica Van Gieson method. The slides were stained with elastic van Gieson solution, which specifically stains elastic fibers in black, followed by counterstaining with Weigert's iron hematoxylin to stain the cell nuclei and collagen fibers. The slides were then dehydrated and mounted with coverslips.

Masson's trichrome staining

Masson's Trichrome staining was employed to assess collagen deposition and fibrosis in the tissue samples. Tissue sections were stained with Masson's Trichrome staining kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), which differentiates collagen fibers by staining them blue, while muscle fibers and cytoplasm are stained red, and cell nuclei appear dark brown. The slides were deparaffinized, stained, and mounted with a coverslip for analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the expression levels of TNF-α and TGF-β in tissue sections. The tissue sections were deparaffinized using xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for TNF-α and TGF-β to unmask the epitopes. The sections were boiled in a microwave for 15 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 20 min. After antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature to block non-specific binding. Tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-TNF-α (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-TGF-β (1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA). After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After washing, the sections were counterstained with DAPI (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 min to stain the cell nuclei. Finally, slides were mounted with fluorescence mounting medium (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). Immunofluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX83, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The expression levels of TNF-α and TGF-β were quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Multiple groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's test. All histological evaluations were performed on three independent tissue samples per group. All the quantifications of images were analyzed by ImageJ software on high-resolution images. GraphPad Prism 8.0 (IBM company, USA) was used for data analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result and discussion

Preparation and characterization of the oriented PGZA hydrogels

To address the clinical limitations of current POP repair materials, we developed an oriented multifunctional hydrogel composed of poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), glycyrrhizic acid (GA), Zn2+, and aniline tetramer-grafted oxidized alginate (OAlg-AT). Compared to non-degradable and highly rigid polypropylene (PP) mesh, PGZA hydrogel combines flexibility and support with regeneration regulation. The oriented network architecture was introduced via a directional freezing strategy, endowing the hydrogel with anisotropic structural stability and providing topographical guidance for cell adhesion and alignment. Meanwhile, OAlg-AT confers stable electroactivity, while glycyrrhizic acid and zinc ions synergistically contribute to local inflammatory immune modulation. These features result in an oriented, conductive, and anti-inflammatory hydrogel that is well-suited for pelvic floor tissue repair. Scheme 1 illustrates the fabrication process for obtaining the PGZA hydrogel. In brief, PVA, OAlg-AT, GA, and Zn2+ which were confirmed to have good biocompatibility were mixed at 90°C. The GA and Zn2+ form the first network by self-assembly. After that, the directional freezing was used to order alignment of PVA, OAlg-AT and GA/Zn2+ chains along the direction of ice crystal growth, forming a muscle-like parallel architecture.

The synthesis procedures of PGZA hydrogels and the mechanism of treating pelvic organ prolapse.

The energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) image of PGZA hydrogels revealed a homogeneous distribution of Zn and N elements, confirming the Zn2+ and OAlg-AT are uniformly distributed along the oriented network of PGZA hydrogel (Fig. 1A). As shown in the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectrum of PGZA hydrogels, the peaks of C, N, O, and Zn elements futher proves this result (Fig. 1B). The binding energy of C 1s XPS spectrum at 284.8 eV corresponds to the C-C bonds, while the peak at 286.5eV attributes to C-O bonds. Meanwhile, the peaks at 288.9 eV is assigned to the C=O groups which comes from OAlg-AT and GA (Fig. 1C). FTIR spectroscopy was then used to characterize the chemical structures of the PGZA hydrogel. The vibration at 1000–1100 cm−1 is associated with C-O groups of PVA, OAlg-AT and GA, while the peaks at 2930 cm−1 represent the hydrogen bond association in PGZA hydrogel (Fig. S1).

To construct the anisotropic architecture, PGZA hydrogels were fabricated by directional freezing. During freezing, the PVA, OAlg-AT, and GA/Zn2+ components were arranged along the ice-crystal growth direction (Fig. S2). SEM images showed an ordered porous network in the PGZA parallel hydrogel, whereas PGZA random prepared without directional freezing displayed an isotropic and disordered structure (Fig. 1A and Fig. S3). This interconnected porous architecture may support nutrient transport and provide topographical cues for cell migration. The structural difference was further examined by 2D small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) (Fig. 1D). Without directional freezing, the PGZA hydrogels with a random network show an isotropic ring in their 2D SAXS pattern. Meanwhile, elongated spots appear in the 2D SAXS patterns of the anisotropic PGZA hydrogels constructed by directional freezing. In the 1D azimuthal scan profiles no visible peak is observed in the PGZA random groups, while clear peaks emerge in oriented PGZA hydrogels (Fig. 1E). To quantitatively evaluate the degree of orientation, Hermans orientation factor (f) was calculated from the SAXS azimuthal intensity profiles. The f close to 0 indicates a random orientation, whereas a value approaching 1 indicates a highly aligned structure. The oriented PGZA hydrogel showed the value of 0.37, while that of random PGZA hydrogel was 0.01, confirming the formation of the aligned microstructure induced by directional freezing. These quantitative results are consistent with the 2D SAXS patterns and SEM observations. These results demonstrate that directional freezing generated an aligned microstructure, providing the structural basis for the direction-dependent mechanical, electrical, and cellular responses evaluated below.

The muscle fibers–inspired hydrogel with a parallel-aligned architecture enhanced mechanical and electrical conductivity properties in parallel directions. We demonstrate the anisotropy of the hydrogel by comparing the mechanical and electrical properties of PGZA2 hydrogel with random network (PGZA2 random) and with parallel network (PGZA2 parallel). PGZA2 means the PGZA hydrogels with 2 mg/mL OAlg-AT. The results of the rheological tests show that the PGZA2 parallel with an oriented network exhibits an increase in its viscoelasticity in the parallel direction. As shown in Fig. 1F, the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of PGZA2 random are 3068 Pa and 601 Pa, while G′ and G″ of oriented PGZA hydrogels in parallel direction are 4058 Pa and 827 Pa, respectively. The increased G′ and G″ of the PGZA2 parallel indicates the enhanced polymer network and improved energy dissipation capacity, which enable the PGZA2 parallel hydrogels to withstand higher stresses in the aligned direction. In compression tests (Fig. 1G), the maximum compressive stress of PGZA2 parallel is 29 kPa, which is higher than that of PGZA2 random. In tensile tests, the PGZA2 parallel hydrogel has enhanced tensile properties. The PGZA2 parallel can withstand 229 kPa stress which is nearly twice as high as that of PGZA2 random (122 kPa). (Fig. 1H). The Young's modulus of PGZA2 parallel is up to 65 kPa and toughness is up to 181 kJ/m3, while those of PGZA2 random are only 50 kPa and 82 kJ/m3, respectively (Fig. 1I). The mechanical reinforcement observed in the PGZA parallel can be attributed to the oriented architecture, which is inspired by muscle fibers. The aligned pore channels and interconnected polymer frameworks enable stresses to be transmitted more efficiently along the orientation direction, minimizing discontinuities in load transfer. In parallel, crystalline domains formed within the PVA during freeze–thaw cycling function as robust physical crosslinks, further restricting network deformation under loads. Through the combined effects of anisotropic framework alignment and crystallization-induced crosslinking, the PGZA parallel demonstrates improved structural stability and enhanced mechanical support along the aligned direction. Therefore, the oriented PGZA hydrogels have better mechanical properties and can provide stable support in pelvic organ prolapse sites. Apart from the mechanical properties, the electrochemical workstation is used to measure the conductivity of the PGZA2 random and PGZA2 parallel hydrogels. The conductivity of the PGZA2 parallel was 0.32 S/m, which is 1.2 times that of PGZA random (Fig. 1J). These results are attributed to OAlg chain in the PGZA hydrogels, which is highly ordered along the hydrogel backbone, where the electrical signals can propagate more efficiently along the alignment direction. The better mechanical and electricals properties of PGZA2 parallel indicated that PGZA2 hydrogel with anisotropic structure can provide mechanical support and good electrophysiological environment which is of great importance to repair pelvic organ prolapse.

Mechanical properties of PGZA hydrogels

For POP repair, a scaffold must provide sufficient support while avoiding the persistent mechanical mismatch associated with relatively stiff PP meshes [35]. Hydrogels with suitable mechanical properties not only offer reliable mechanical support but also establish a compliant, tissue-like environment [36]. Therefore, hydrogels with mechanical properties that are compatible with the organization and can also provide mechanical support are necessary.

We adjusted the mechanical properties by varying the concentration of OAlg-AT under the condition of maintaining the PVA concentration at 12% and the GA concentration at 1.5%. The PGZA hydrogels with 0, 1, 2, and 3 mg/mL OAlg-AT was named ad PGZA0, PGZA1, PGZA2 and PGZA3, respectively. The mechanical properties of the PGZA hydrogels are adjusted with different OAlg-AT content and are first investigated through a rheological study. With the OAlg-AT concentration is increasing, the storage modulus (G′) and the loss modulus (G″) of PGZA hydrogels gradually increased (Fig. 2B). This result is attributed the non-covalent interactions between OAlg-AT and the other components of the hydrogel. As shown in Fig. S4, the PGZA2 hydrogel withstands shear stress up to 12219 Pa before the sol–gel transition, reflecting strong shear resistance and mechanical stability of PGZA2 hydrogel. The strain sweep of PGZA2 hydrogels demonstrates that the gel-sol transition point is around 200% (Fig. 2C). The angular frequency sweep test of PGZA2 hydrogels indicates that G′ is larger than G″ across the angular frequency range from 0.1 to 100 rad/s, suggesting the network of PGZA2 hydrogels is stable (Fig. 2D). The pelvic floor tissues experience complex, multi-axial stress states rather than simple uni-directional loading. To further evaluate whether the oriented PGZA2 hydrogel could provide mechanical support beyond the aligned direction, the modulus was measured in directions parallel and perpendicular to the fiber alignment. Directional testing further showed a modulus of 4254 Pa parallel to the aligned channels and 3287 Pa perpendicular to them (Fig. S5). The higher parallel modulus confirms anisotropic reinforcement, whereas the retained perpendicular modulus indicates that the scaffold is not mechanically unsupported across the alignment. This balance is more relevant to the multiaxial pelvic floor environment than a comparison limited to the parallel and random architectures.

The stretch and compression photographs of the PGZA2 hydrogels show that they could be stretched to 150% and pressed to 60% without breaking, suggesting that the hydrogels have good stretchable and compressive elasticity (Fig. 2A). As the concentration increases, the tensile stress and strain first grow and then fall, reaching their maximum value at PGZA2 which is 230 kPa and 180%, respectively. (Fig. 2E). The toughness of the PGZA2 hydrogel improves to 181 kJ/m3 (Fig. 2F), and the Young's modulus of the PGZA2 hydrogel also increases to 66 kPa (Fig. S6). Similar to the tensile-stress curve, the same trend is shown in the compressive-stress curve, and the compressive stress in 60% of PGZA2 was 28.9 kPa (Fig. 2G). OAlg-AT can not only provide good conductivity but also influence the strength of PGZA hydrogels. The hydrogen bonds between PVA and the carboxyl groups on the long chains of alginate, together with the π-π stacking among aniline, enhance the mechanical properties. The rising OAlg-AT content increases the non-covalent interactions and improve the mechanical properties of the PGZA hydrogel. But excessive OAlg-AT will hinder the aggregation and crystallization of PVA molecules, resulting in a decline in the mechanical properties of the PGZA hydrogel. The PGZA2 hydrogels exhibited the best mechanical performance based on the results above. Therefore, we use oriented PGZA2 hydrogel, which contains 2 mg/mL OAlg-AT to perform the cyclic tensile and cyclic compression tests. The cyclic tensile stress–strain curves of oriented PGZA2 hydrogels with different strains from 40% to 160% proves that the PGZA2 hydrogels have good recoverability, which is adapted to deformation of tissue at the pelvic organ prolapse site (Fig. S7). The load-unload tension and compression curves of oriented PGZA2 hydrogels in parallel direction suggest that the PGZA2 hydrogels could be stretched and compressed to 10 cycles and recover with slightly decline of stress (Fig. 2H and I). The mechanical stability of the PGZA hydrogel under physiological-like conditions is vital for its performance as a tissue repair scaffold. Rheological analysis conducted before and after being immersed in 37°C PBS for 7 days that the storage (G′) and loss (G″) modulus decreased slightly, confirming that the hydrogel's crosslinked network and mechanical properties are well-preserved under physiological conditions (Fig. S8). Then we performed an in vitro enzymatic degradation study for 12 weeks. The PGZA hydrogel showed gradual degradation, with approximately 60% degradation after 12 weeks, indicating that the hydrogel did not rapidly disappear during the early repair stage and could continue to provide mechanical support with the tissue regeneration (Fig. S9). Although the in vivo environment is substantially more complex than a single-enzyme collagenase system and the mechanical properties of the hydrogel inevitably decline as it degrades, histological examination at Week 6 revealed partial muscle regeneration at the defect site, with increased and well-organized collagen and elastic fibers, particularly in the PGZA2 and PGZA2 + ES groups (Fig. 6). As the hydrogel gradually degrades, these newly formed tissues begin to share mechanical loads, enabling the repair construct to transition from scaffold-dominated early support toward increasing load-bearing by tissue. Whether the rates of tissue ingrowth and mechanical maturation are fully synchronized with PGZA2 degradation, however, remains to be determined. Future studies should integrate longitudinal degradation monitoring with directional mechanical testing of both residual scaffolds and hydrogel–tissue composites, while further tuning degradation kinetics through PVA crystallinity, GA/Zn2+ coordination density, and component ratios to match the extended regeneration timeline required for human pelvic floor repair. These results might contribute to the orientation structure which transfer stress along the aligned direction and the diverse non-covalent bonds that enables energy dissipation in PGZA2 hydrogels. All in all, the oriented PGZA2 hydrogels possessed ideal tensile and compression properties, which can adapt to muscle movement and are stable in the implanted area.

The electrical properties of the oriented PGZA hydrogels

Electrical stimulation (ES) has been proved to activate multiple signaling pathways associated with cell migration, differentiation, and ECM synthesis, and has demonstrated therapeutic potential in improving pelvic floor function in clinical studies [37,38]. The oriented conductive hydrogels with good electroactivity can transmit electrical signals and guide cell growth, which can improve pelvic tissue repair [39].

The conductivity of PGZA hydrogels were demonstrated visually using a LED circuit experiment. As shown in Fig. 3A, when the PGZA hydrogels were inserted to the circuit as a conductive bridge, the LED indicator was light up, which suggesting that the PGZA hydrogels possess good conductivity. With the PGZA cut into two pieces, the LED indicator was shut down with the circuit broken. The circuit restored while the cut PGZA hydrogels were reconnected. The phenylamine on the OAlg-AT chain enable rapid electron transfer through π–π interactions and provide favorable electroactivity to the PGZA hydrogel. In this case, we adjust the resistance and conductivity of PGZA hydrogels by changing the concentration of OAlg-AT. The hydrogels with different OAlg-AT content are varied from 0 mg to 3 mg/mL and were named as PGZA0, PGZA1, PGZA2, PGZA3, respectively. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to quantify the impedance of PGZA hydrogels containing different OAlg-AT contents (Fig. S10). The Nyquist plots exhibited a linear trend, suggesting that charge transport in PGZA hydrogels is a non-Faradaic conduction process. The X-axis intercept of each EIS curve was taken to calculate the conductivity of each PGZA hydrogel. The conductivity of PGZA hydrogels with growing OAlg-AT concentration is gradually improved from 0.22 to 0.38 S/m (Fig. 3B). The PGZA2 hydrogel is finally chosen because of its good conductivity of 0.32 S/m and cell biocompatibility. To further clarify whether Zn2+ contributed to the electrical conductivity of the hydrogel, we compared the conductivity of the hydrogels prepared with (PGZA hydrogels) and without Zn2+ (PGA hydrogels) under the same conditions. The two groups showed comparable conductivity values, indicating that Zn2+ ions were not the main contributor to the electrical conductivity of the PGZA hydrogel (Fig. S11). The conductivity was mainly attributed to the OAlg-AT component, where the aniline tetramer segments enabled electron transfer through conjugated structures and π–π interactions. Zn2+ mainly participated in the construction of the secondary crosslinked network and contributed to the immunomodulatory function of the hydrogels. Then we evaluate the electrical anisotropy of the oriented PGZA2 hydrogels by measuring their conductivity in directions parallel and perpendicular to the aligned microchannels. As shown in Fig. S12, the conductivity in the parallel direction was 0.33 S/m, higher than that in the perpendicular direction (0.26 S/m), confirming the anisotropic electrical behavior of the oriented hydrogel.

(B) Conductivity of the oriented PGZA hydrogels with varying OAlg-AT concentrations in the parallel direction. As shown in Fig. 3C, the resistance of PGZA0, PGZA1, PGZA2, and PGZA3 hydrogels at 1 kHz is 449.6 Ω, 329.2 Ω, 315.7 Ω, and 269.5 Ω, respectively, which displays a clear reduction in resistance with increasing OAlg-AT content. Such low resistance of PGZA2 hydrogels in this frequency range is advantageous for rapid transmission of bioelectric signals, and thereby able to accelerate CV curves indicates cell migration, and collagen deposition. For pelvic organ prolapse treatment, the stable electroactivity is equally important to persistently transmit electrical signals required by muscular tissue in the pelvic area. Therefore, to evaluate electrochemical stability of these hydrogels, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was further analyzed (Fig. 3D). Consistent with the EIS result, the hysteresis loop enlarged as the OAlg-AT concentration increased, indicating enhanced charge storage and transport behavior. Importantly, PGZA2 exhibited only slight changes in the hysteresis loop area after 50 cycles (Fig. 3E). These stable CV curves indicates that the PGZA2 could has stable electroactivity, which can support sustained electrical cue transmission to cells and stimulate the migration of cells at the pelvic prolapse site. Together with ES, the PGZA2 hydrogels have advantages in transmitting bioelectric signals of the pelvic floor tissue and promoting pelvic floor reconstruction.

Evaluation of PGZA hydrogels with ES on cell function

Cell viability assays were conducted to assess the impact of PGZA hydrogels and electrical stimulation (ES) on cell proliferation. Electrical stimulation (ES) can activate multiple signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT pathway and the MAPK/ERK pathway to promote cell migration, differentiation, and ECM synthesis [40]. As shown in Fig. 4A, no significant differences in cell viability were observed on Day 1 and Day 3 among the groups, indicating that all hydrogel formulations were biocompatible in the short term. However, by Day 7, PGZA2 and PGZA2 + ES demonstrated significantly higher cell viability compared to the control and PGZA0 groups (P < 0.05), suggesting that PGZA2 hydrogel provides a favorable microenvironment for cell growth. Interestingly, electrical stimulation (PGZA2 + ES) further enhanced cell viability compared to PGZA2 alone, confirming that the application of ES may stimulate cellular proliferation over time. The increase in cell viability observed in PGZA2 and PGZA2 + ES can be attributed to the inherent biocompatibility of the hydrogel material, as well as the antioxidant and mechanical properties that PGZA2 provides. To further evaluate cell proliferation, a live/dead assay was performed, and the results corroborated the viability findings. As shown in Fig. 4B and C, the proportion of live cells in the PGZA2 group was significantly higher than in the control and PGZA0 groups (P < 0.05). Additionally, PGZA2 + ES exhibited the highest percentage of live cells, with a statistically significant difference compared to PGZA2 alone (P < 0.05), suggesting that ES plays a critical role in enhancing cell proliferation. The enhanced live cell proportion in PGZA2 and PGZA2 + ES groups is particularly noteworthy. This result demonstrates that PGZA2 provides a supportive scaffold for cell attachment and survival. The addition of ES significantly enhanced cell proliferation, aligning with previous studies that demonstrated the positive effects of electrical stimulation on cellular growth and metabolism. Electrical fields have been shown to promote cellular activities such as ion transport, protein synthesis, and gene expression, all of which contribute to the observed increase in cell viability in the PGZA2 + ES group.

Cell migration, an essential process for tissue regeneration and wound healing, was assessed using a scratch assay. As shown in Fig. 4D and E, PGZA2 significantly enhanced cell migration compared to the control and PGZA0 groups (P < 0.05). The addition of ES further increased migration, with PGZA2 + ES exhibiting significantly higher migration distance than PGZA2 alone (P < 0.05). These results indicate that both the hydrogel and ES synergistically promote cellular motility, which is crucial for tissue integration and healing. The enhanced migration observed in the PGZA2 and PGZA2 + ES groups may be associated with the unique structural orientation of the hydrogel network, which mimics the natural extracellular matrix (ECM) and guides cell movement. Additionally, electrical stimulation has been reported to enhance cell motility by influencing cytoskeletal rearrangement and focal adhesion dynamics. The increased migration in PGZA2 + ES supports the hypothesis that electrical stimulation augments the hydrogel's ability to facilitate tissue integration.

The mechanical injury of pelvic floor tissue leads to oxidative stress in the pelvic floor areas, resulting in ROS accumulation and disordered ECM metabolism [19]. ROS accumulation serves as a critical trigger in activating macrophages to produce inflammatory bodies and the polarization of macrophages is more likely to the M1 type when encouraged by ROS [41]. Therefore, removing excessive ROS is one of the key methods for treating pelvic prolapse.

The ROS scavenging ability of PVA, PGZA0 and PGZA2 hydrogels was first analyzed by using 2,2′-azinobis (3ethyl benzothi-azoline-6-sulfonic acid) ammonium radical (ABTS•+) and1,1-diphenyl-2-picryl-hydrazyl radical (DPPH•) as the representative reactive oxygen species. The DPPH• and ABTS•+ had their absorbance recorded at 519 nm and 734 nm, respectively (Figure S13A and Figure S13C). The significantly lowered absorbance levels of both DPPH and ABTS after PGZA2 treatment confirm the substantial ROS scavenging efficacy of PGZA2 hydrogels. The results showed that the clearance efficiency of PVA, PGZA0 and PGZA2 against ABTS•+ can reach 4%, 59% and 82%, respectively (Fig. S13B), while their scavenging ratio against DPPH• can reach 13%, 28% and 66%, respectively (Fig. S13D). These results indicated that the ability of the PGZA2 hydrogel to remove ROS is due to the synergistic effect of GA and the absorption of electrons by aniline on OAlg-AT chain. In the presence of PGZA2 at 37°C, the DPPH solution transitioned from dark purple to yellow, and the ABTS solution shifted from blue to green, which indicate that PGZA2 effectively removed DPPH• and ABTS•+ from the mixture. As shown in Fig. S13E–F, when the PGZA2 hydrogels were added in the ABTS•+ and DPPH• solution for 12 h, almost 81% ABTS•+ and 67% DPPH• could be effectively eliminated, indicating their good potential in relieving the oxidative stress.

The levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) were evaluated to determine the effect of PGZA hydrogels on cellular oxidative stress. Fig. 4F illustrates that ROS levels were significantly reduced in cells treated with PGZA0 and PGZA2, compared to the control group (P < 0.05). This reduction suggests that PGZA2, due to its antioxidant properties, may mitigate oxidative stress, a key factor in inflammatory processes and tissue damage. The reduction of ROS levels in PGZA2 groups highlights the potential of PGZA2 as an antioxidative agent. The incorporation of bioactive compounds such as glycyrrhizic acid, known for its antioxidant properties, likely contributes to this effect. The antioxidative capacity of the hydrogel protects cells from oxidative damage and promotes a healthier regenerative environment. This finding is crucial for pelvic floor reconstruction, where oxidative stress can hinder tissue healing and function.

The ability of PGZA2 to promote vascularization was evaluated in vitro by assessing the formation of blood vessel-like structures. As shown in Fig. 4G, PGZA2 significantly enhanced vascularization compared to PGZA0, and PGZA2 + ES exhibited even greater vascularization. These findings suggest that PGZA2 hydrogels, particularly when combines with electrical stimulation, can enhance vascularization which ensures adequate nutrient and oxygen supply for pelvic tissue regeneration. Angiogenesis is a key factor in successful tissue engineering, and the ability of PGZA2 to promote vascularization is a promising result for its application in pelvic floor reconstruction. The further enhancement in vascularization observed with electrical stimulation indicates that PGZA2 + ES not only provides a scaffold for cell growth but also actively promotes the formation of new blood vessels, which is essential for sustaining tissue regeneration. Electrical stimulation has been shown to modulate angiogenic pathways, likely through the activation of endothelial cells and the release of angiogenic factors.

The oriented PGZA hydrogel offers direction-dependent mechanical support and together with ES guides cell alignment and ingrowth. Cellular organization and alignment were observed through microscopy to evaluate how PGZA hydrogels influence tissue formation. Fig. 4H shows that cells in the control and PGZA0 groups exhibited disorganized morphologies without an obvious preferential orientation. Meanwhile, the cells cultured on PGZA2 showed a partial preferential alignment that was broadly consistent with the uniaxially oriented microchannels of the hydrogel, although local deviations in cell spreading were still observed. Notably, PGZA2 + ES induced more pronounced directional cellular organization, suggesting that electrical stimulation further enhanced the guidance effect of the oriented conductive hydrogel. These observations connect the quantified material anisotropy with a cellular response. The electroactive properties of PGZA2 likely guide cell alignment along the direction of the applied electrical field, a process that mimics the natural orientation of muscle fibers. Such directional cellular organization is relevant for pelvic floor repair, where ordered cell arrangement and extracellular matrix deposition are important for reconstructing mechanically functional tissue. Therefore, the combination of oriented PGZA2 hydrogels and ES may provide a favorable microenvironment for promoting organized tissue regeneration and functional repair. Collectively, the findings underscore the significant influence of PGZA hydrogels in supporting cell viability, migration, oxidative stress reduction, and vascularization, all of which are essential for tissue regeneration. The application of electrical stimulation (ES) coupled with the aligned scaffold can translate an externally applied cue into improved cellular organization and ECM remodeling.

For future clinical translation, non-invasive or minimally invasive ES may be realized through wireless capacitive or inductive coupling systems that activate the implanted conductive hydrogel generating current [42]. Another promising strategy is to integrate piezoelectric or triboelectric materials into the hydrogel, allowing physiological motion, tissue deformation, externally applied mechanical stimulation or ultrasound stimulation to be converted into localized electrical cues [43,44]. These approaches may enable patient friendly electrical stimulation assisted pelvic floor repair without the need for implanted batteries or wired electrodes.

Tissue transcriptome profiling reveals the modulation of immune microenvironment

Transcriptome sequencing of muscle tissues revealed significant changes in gene expression between different experimental groups. A volcano plot (Fig. 5A) showed 266 downregulated and 385 upregulated genes in the tissues compared to controls. Heatmap analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the sham, PGZA2, and PGZA2 + ES groups further highlighted the expression patterns (Fig. 5B). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed significant enrichment in innate immune response pathways (P < 0.001, Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis identified enrichment in key signaling pathways such as TNF and IL-17 (Fig. 5D). Additionally, Reactome pathway analysis showed significant enrichment in innate immune response (Fig. 5E). The results of the tissue transcriptome profiling underscore the complexity of the immune microenvironment and the involvement of both pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive pathways. The upregulation of genes associated with IFN-γ response, TNF signaling, and IL-17 signaling suggests that the muscle is characterized by an inflammatory environment.

These coordinated transcriptional changes indicate that the GA/Zn2+-containing PGZA2 network, together with ES transmitted through the electroactive scaffold, redirects innate immune signaling toward a reparative state. By reducing persistent inflammation, this immune regulation facilitates the development of a more organized tissue architecture at the repair site.

GSEA further corroborated the involvement of the immune microenvironment, showing significant enrichment of inflammatory response (P < 0.001, Fig. 5F) and immune response (P = 0.006, Fig. 5G) pathways. Ifit3 is associated with the pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage phenotype, whereas Lgals9 is associated with the anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair M2 phenotype. Ifit3 expression was significantly downregulated and Lgals9 expression was significantly upregulated (P < 0.001; Fig. 5H and I). These reciprocal changes indicate a shift from an M1-dominated inflammatory response toward an M2-associated repair state. Together with reduced TNF-α expression, increased TGF-β expression, and improved collagen organization, the transcriptomic data demonstrate that PGZA2, particularly when combined with ES, suppresses persistent inflammation and establishes an immune environment favorable for tissue remodeling.

Histological analysis of PGZA hydrogels with ES on tissue regeneration

To assess the tissue morphology and structural changes over time, histological staining was performed using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), Elastica Van Gieson (EVG), and Masson's Trichrome (Masson) at both Week 2 and Week 6 following treatment with different hydrogel formulations and electrical stimulation (ES). As shown in Fig. 6A–B, H&E staining revealed a progressive reduction in tissue disorganization over time. At both week 2 and week 6, PGZA0 showed noticeable tissue disorganization with scattered cellular infiltration, whereas PGZA2 exhibited a more compact and organized cellular structure. Notably, PGZA2 + ES exhibited the most well-organized tissue architecture, with reduced inflammatory infiltrates and enhanced cell alignment, suggesting improved tissue remodeling and regeneration under the influence of electrical stimulation. EVG staining, which highlights elastic fibers, showed no significant differences among groups at week 2 (Fig. 6C and D). However, at week 6, PGZA0 displayed an increase in elastic fiber content, indicating some degree of tissue repair but with less mature fibers. PGZA2 demonstrated more elastic fiber deposition compared to PGZA0, suggesting better tissue regeneration. Importantly, the PGZA2 + ES group exhibited the most extensive network of elastic fibers, indicating superior tissue maturation and remodeling, potentially aided by the electroactive properties of PGZA2 in combination with electrical stimulation, which enhances the alignment and organization of extracellular matrix components. Masson's Trichrome staining, which stains collagen fibers blue, revealed no significant differences between the groups at week 2 (Fig. 6E and F). However, at week 6, PGZA0 exhibited a slight increase in collagen deposition, suggesting early-stage fibrosis. PGZA2 showed a more organized deposition of collagen fibers, indicative of better tissue integrity and less fibrotic scarring. The PGZA2 + ES group exhibited the highest amount of well-organized collagen, with a dense and structured collagen network, suggesting that electrical stimulation further enhanced the collagen maturation and overall tissue structure. This enhanced collagen deposition in PGZA2 + ES indicates a more favorable environment for tissue regeneration, promoting stronger and more functional tissue repair. Collectively, the histological findings from HE, EVG, and Masson staining corroborate the improved tissue regeneration observed with PGZA2, with ES further augmenting tissue repair and extracellular matrix organization. These results suggest that the anisotropic architecture channels both cell infiltration and ECM deposition along physiologically relevant directions, directly countering the random fibrotic encapsulation typical of isotropic polypropylene meshes. Concurrently, the diminished inflammatory infiltrates and superior matrix organization observed in PGZA2 + ES correspond with our transcriptomic profile, suggesting that this electroactive system establishes a pro-resolving immune milieu. Such a microenvironment promotes constructive remodeling instead of chronic inflammation or dense scar formation, which might be caused by traditional PP meshes.

Anti-inflammatory regulation of inflammatory microenvironment via TNF-α suppression and TGF-β activation

To verify the immunomodulatory effects indicated by transcriptomic analysis, immunofluorescence staining was performed for TNF-α and TGF-β. TNF-α expression was lower in all treatment groups than in the control, although the reduction in PGZA0 was not statistically significant. PGZA2 significantly reduced TNF-α expression (P < 0.01), consistent with its ROS-scavenging activity and the lower intracellular ROS level observed in vitro (Figs. 4F, 7A and C). These findings show that the antioxidant components of PGZA2 alleviate the pro-inflammatory environment at the repair site.

Immunofluorescence analysis of inflammatory cytokines. (A) TNF-α staining showing reduced pro-inflammatory response. (B) TGF-β staining showing enhanced anti-inflammatory response. (C-D) Quantitative analysis of TNF-α and TGF-β staining. Scale bar = 100 μm *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01. Data are represented as mean ± S.D. of three independent experiments.

Notably, PGZA2 + ES exhibited the weakest TNF-α staining, with the most pronounced reduction in cytokine expression, suggesting that electrical stimulation further enhances the anti-inflammatory effect of PGZA2, likely contributing to a more favorable tissue regeneration environment (Fig. 7C). TGF-β, a cytokine crucial for anti-inflammation, was also assessed. As shown in Fig. 7B, PGZA0 exhibited an increase in TGF-β expression, and PGZA2 demonstrated a stronger TGF-β signal, suggesting enhanced anti-inflammation. Interestingly, PGZA2 + ES showed the highest expression of TGF-β (Fig. 7D), suggesting most enhanced anti-inflammation in the presence of electrical stimulation. These findings underscore the ability of PGZA hydrogels, especially in combination with electrical stimulation, to modulate both pro-inflammatory (TNF-α) and anti-inflammatory (TGF-β) cytokine expression. The reciprocal decrease in TNF-α and increase in TGF-β confirm that PGZA2 + ES shifts the local microenvironment from persistent inflammation toward anti-inflammatory and reparative regulation. This cytokine pattern agrees with the transcriptomic shift from M1-to M2-associated gene expression and helps explain the more organized collagen deposition and ECM remodeling observed at Week 6. These results suggest that PGZA2 + ES creates an optimal microenvironment for tissue regeneration by balancing inflammatory responses and promoting anti-inflammatory processes essential for effective tissue healing. The designed oriented PGZA2 hydrogel combined with ES promotes pelvic tissue regeneration and improves the mechanical support of the pelvic floor through its biocompatible, oriented, conductive, and anti-inflammatory features, suggesting its potential utility in the treatment of pelvic organ prolapse.

We used a rat abdominal wall defect model as a surrogate for pelvic floor repair, as both are collagen-rich connective tissues sharing similar repair mechanisms (inflammation, collagen remodeling, and mechanical restoration) [29,45]. Despite these model limitations, existing mesh procedures offer a clinical entry point. Laparoscopic lateral suspension (LLS; Dubuisson procedure) for middle-compartment POP avoids sacral dissection but relies on permanent mesh with unresolved long-term outcomes [46]. Initially, PGZA would serve as an adjunct at mesh-tissue interfaces or over native repairs, providing compliant early support, modulating inflammation, and guiding organized ingrowth while permanent constructs maintain suspension. Full substitution requires validated long-term performance.

PGZA integrates anisotropic architecture, electroactivity, and immunomodulation into a single degradable scaffold, shifting pelvic floor repair from passive structural substitution to active biological guidance. The oriented pores direct organized tissue ingrowth, electrical stimulation accelerates vascularization and matrix maturation, and immunomodulation resolves inflammation, enabling the scaffold to progressively transfer load to regenerated host tissue. Future work should establish long-term fixation stability, degradation-ingrowth synchronization, and functional restoration in anatomically relevant models to determine whether PGZA serves as a biodegradable adjunct or a partial replacement for permanent mesh.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a multifunctional PGZA2 hydrogel patch that integrates anisotropic mechanical adaptability, immunomodulatory capability, and electroactivity for pelvic organ prolapse repair. In vitro experiments, the PGZA2 hydrogel has good biocompatibility and ROS scavenging ability. The oriented PGZA2 hydrogel together with ES, guides cell alignment and ingrowth, promoting vascularization. In vivo evaluation using a rat abdominal wall defect model demonstrated that the PGZA2 hydrogel exhibits good biocompatibility, attenuates local inflammatory responses, and induces orientation-dependent connective tissue regeneration. Tissue transcriptome profiling and regulation of representative inflammatory mediators demonstrate that the PGZA2 hydrogel can reduce inflammation and modulate the immune microenvironment. The histological analysis shows that the PGZA2 hydrogel with electroactivity enables effective coupling with electrical stimulation to further enhance collagen deposition and extracellular matrix remodeling. This study integrates structural anisotropy, immune microenvironment modulation, and electrical stimulation within a soft scaffold and presents a promising strategy for efficient pelvic floor reconstruction and other connective tissue disorders.
