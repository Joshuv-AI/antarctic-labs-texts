Is drain placement necessary in transoral endoscopic thyroidectomy via vestibular approach?
Authors: Jiang Jiang, Yu-jing Weng, Xiu-ting Zhang, Ben-ling Hu, Qing Ai, Zhi-heng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransoral Endoscopic Thyroidectomy via Vestibular Approach (TOETVA) offers a scar-free alternative for thyroid surgery. Traditionally, drains are used to prevent hematoma, seroma, and infection, yet the need for routine drain placement in TOETVA remains uncertain.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of TOETVA patients from August 2021 to September 2024 at a single tertiary center. Patients were divided into drain and non-drain groups. All patients underwent unilateral TOETVA, with or without central lymph node dissection. Key outcomes included operative time, postoperative pain, hospital stay, and complications. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to minimize selection bias. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 408 TOETVA patients were included, with 214 in the drain group and 194 in the non-drain group. After PSM, 174 matched pairs were analyzed. Operative time, pain scores, hematoma, seroma, and infection rates were comparable between groups. However, the non-drain group had a significantly shorter hospital stay (3.38 ± 0.94 vs. 4.37 ± 1.02 days, p < 0.001). No significant differences in hospitalization costs were observed.ConclusionRoutine drain placement may not be necessary in TOETVA patients, as omission of drains did not increase complication rates and was associated with shorter hospitalization in our cohort. Prospective multicenter studies are required to validate the optimal criteria for selective drain use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03310-z.Keywords: Transoral endoscopic thyroidectomy via vestibular approach, Drain placement, Complication rates, Infection, Hematoma

Introduction

Endoscopic techniques have significantly advanced thyroid surgery, enabling procedures that minimize visible scarring and enhance patient recovery. With the development of endoscopic thyroidectomy, various surgical approaches have been explored in recent years [1–4]. Among these, Transoral Endoscopic Thyroidectomy via Vestibular Approach (TOETVA) stands out as an innovative method that completely avoids external scarring while providing a full view for central compartment lymph node dissection [5, 6]. Surgeons access the thyroid gland through a small incision inside the mouth, just behind the lower lip, achieving excellent cosmetic outcomes [7].

Placing a drain to manage fluids and potential contaminants is widely regarded as a precautionary measure to prevent complications such as hematoma, seroma, and infection after thyroid surgery. With the advancement of surgical techniques, more surgeons are opting to place drains only in specific patients during traditional open surgery, rather than routinely in all patients. Surgical site infections in transoral thyroidectomy may theoretically arise from oral cavity bacteria, including resident microbiota. Nevertheless, existing literature, including several meta-analyses, consistently reports a very low incidence, typically between 0.1% and 1% [8–11]. The lower infection rate may be due to the implementation of appropriate preventive measures during surgery—such as prophylactic antibiotics, thorough sterilization, and drainage—which effectively reduced the known and predictable risk of postoperative infection [12–15]. The 2018 Chinese Expert Consensus recommended routine placement of a drainage tube during TOETVA [16]. Furthermore, a review of the literature indicates that more studies support the use of drainage rather than non-drainage following TOETVA [17–21]. However, this practice is largely based on tradition and theoretical reasoning rather than specific evidence tailored to TOETVA.

This study aims to address this gap by comparing TOETVA patients with and without drains to assess the necessity of drain placement. By analyzing key factors such as postoperative complication rates, hospital stay duration, and pain levels, this research seeks to provide an evidence-based evaluation of the need for drains in TOETVA.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study, reviewing TOETVA cases performed at a single tertiary hospital from August 2021 to September 2024. Prior to March 2023, a drainage tube was routinely placed in the thyroid fossa during TOETVA. With increasing surgical experience and evidence from open thyroidectomy supporting the safety of omitting drains, the surgical team shifted to a non-drain approach to reduce unnecessary interventions. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Hong Kong University Shenzhen Hospital (Ethics approval No. 2024371).

The retrospective review included all patients who underwent transoral endoscopic thyroidectomy via the vestibular approach (TOETVA). Inclusion criteria were: (1) transoral endoscopic unilateral thyroidectomy, with or without central compartment lymph node dissection. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Da Vinci robotic-assisted surgery, (2) previous neck surgery or radiation, and (3) use of immunosuppressive medications.

To maintain homogeneity of the surgical intervention, we included only patients who underwent unilateral thyroidectomy with or without central compartment lymph node dissection. At our center, the indications for thyroid surgery follow the 2015 ATA guidelines [22], with the criteria for unilateral thyroidectomy as follows: (1) For patients with a preoperative diagnosis or suspicion of differentiated thyroid cancer, the criteria include: a tumor smaller than 4 cm, with no gross extrathyroidal extension (clinical T4), no clinically evident lymph node metastasis (clinical N1), and no distant metastasis (clinical M1); (2) For benign cases, the criteria include: a tumor larger than 4 cm, or the presence of compressive symptoms or cosmetic concerns that necessitate surgery.

All TOETVA procedures were performed by a single experienced surgical team following a standardized technique. After general anesthesia and intubation, the patient was placed in the supine position with slight neck extension. A 1.5-cm midline incision was made in the oral vestibule behind the lower lip, followed by two additional 5-mm incisions lateral to the canine teeth for instrument access. Blunt dissection was used to create a working space in the subplatysmal plane using a visual dilator and CO₂ insufflation at a pressure of 6 mmHg. A 30-degree endoscope was introduced through the central port, and endoscopic instruments were inserted through the lateral ports. The strap muscles were separated in the midline and retracted laterally to expose the thyroid gland. Unilateral thyroid lobectomy was performed using ultrasonic energy devices for vessel sealing. When indicated, central neck dissection was carried out under direct visualization, carefully preserving the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands. Hemostasis was meticulously achieved using bipolar coagulation and hemostatic agents.

In the drain group, a 5-mm closed suction drain was placed in the thyroid bed through an areolar incision and removed once total drainage output was under 40 ml within a 24-hour period (usually calculated from 7:00 a.m. to 7:00 a.m. the following day). All vestibular incisions were closed with absorbable sutures, and patients were monitored postoperatively with standardized protocols. All patients included in this study had their drains removed prior to discharge.

The outcomes assessed included operation time, patient-reported pain scores, hospital stay duration, hospitalization costs, and complication rates (hematoma, seroma, and infection). All patients were followed for a minimum of one-month post-surgery to evaluate short-term complications related to drainage.

The analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared with the independent samples t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. We performed propensity score matching (PSM) analysis to balance baseline clinicopathological characteristics. Patients in the drain group were matched to those in the non-drain group at a 1:1 ratio. The caliper value, which defines the maximum allowable difference in propensity scores for matched pairs, was set at 0.02 on the logit of the propensity score. This threshold was chosen because it is commonly used in clinical studies to balance the precision of matching with an adequate sample size. Validation of PSM was achieved by comparing the two groups for each observed variable before and after PSM. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

A total of 408 patients were enrolled in the study, with 214 patients in the drain group and 194 in the non-drain group. To achieve better baseline comparability, we performed matching based on sex, age, and BMI, with stratified matching specifically for tumor size. After 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), 174 matched pairs were generated. The remaining 60 patients could not be matched because suitable counterparts meeting the matching criteria were not available. A flowchart detailing the patient inclusion process is shown in Fig. 1. The two groups were comparable in terms of gender, age, BMI, presence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, tumor size, and the proportion of benign versus malignant pathology, with no significant differences observed (P > 0.05; Table 1). All TOETVA procedures were successfully performed by a single surgical team, with no conversions to open surgery.

The complication rates were also comparable before and after matching. Seroma occurred in 3 patients (1.5%) in the non-drain group and 4 patients (1.9%) in the drain group (p = 1.0); post-PSM, the rates were 2 (1.1%) vs. 4 (2.3%) (p = 0.69). Hematoma was found in only one patient in the drain group (0.5% pre-PSM, 0.6% post-PSM), who underwent a second endoscopic hemostasis. Regional infection occurred in one patient (0.5%) in the non-drain group, presenting with signs of purulent infection on postoperative day 2, which resolved completely after aspiration and antibiotic therapy. Vocal cord paralysis rates were similar (7 vs. 8 pre-PSM; 4 vs. 6 post-PSM, all p > 0.05). The only infected patient in the non-drain group presented on the second postoperative day with neck redness, swelling, pain, and low-grade fever, indicating a purulent infection at the surgical site. The infection was successfully treated with multiple ultrasound-guided aspirations and sensitive antibiotics, resulting in complete recovery within 10 days without any long-term complications such as hoarseness.

Discussion

Transoral endoscopic thyroidectomy via vestibular approach (TOETVA) has introduced a novel, scar-free option for thyroid cancer surgery, making it a preferred choice for patients desiring minimal cosmetic impact [23]. The complication rates of TOETVA are similar to those of open thyroidectomy, and complications include vocal fold paralysis, hypocalcemia, seroma, hematoma, and wound infection [24].

Postoperative hemorrhage is a well-recognized complication of thyroid surgery, with an incidence of about 1.48% [25], which has not been reduced over time. Drain placement is traditionally used by surgeons to reduce the risk of bleeding and prevent acute airway obstruction caused by hemorrhage. However, the role of drains following thyroidectomy remains uncertain and varies across specialties. A meta-analysis of 11 randomized clinical trials [26] found no significant difference in the incidence of neck hematoma or seroma between routine drainage and no drainage following thyroidectomy in traditional open surgery. In a retrospective analysis of 2016–2019 National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) data, Kelvin Memeh et al. [27] found that overall drain use is declining, with general surgeons using drains less frequently than ENT surgeons. An increasing number of studies indicate that strict and effective intraoperative hemostasis is critical for preventing postoperative bleeding, suggesting that drainage tube placement in conventional open thyroidectomy may not be necessary [28–32].

Unlike conventional open thyroidectomy, research on drain management in endoscopic thyroid surgery is limited [33], and reports on drainage management in transoral endoscopic thyroidectomy are even rarer. Existing studies primarily focus on drain placement methods and drain size. Gao-Xiang Chen et al. [34] demonstrated that adjusting the drainage tube position in endoscopic thyroidectomy can improve the restoration of the suprasternal fossa, leading to better cosmetic outcomes and increased patient satisfaction without affecting drainage efficacy. Zeyang Liu et al. [35] investigated the optimal drain size for effective drainage and aesthetic outcomes in transoral endoscopic thyroidectomy via the vestibular approach, concluding that small-diameter drains offer superior cosmetic results without compromising drainage efficacy. Their findings indicate that most wound exudation occurs within the first 8 h post-operation, with safe extubation recommended at 32 h for 95% of patients.

Compared to traditional open thyroid surgery, TOETVA involves a greater extent of flap dissection, longer operative times, and more postoperative drainage [24], leading more surgeons to favor drain placement. In TOETVA, surgical trauma-related complications, including seroma, emphysema, and hematoma, accounted for 2.91% [36]. Our present study found no significant difference in hematoma rates between groups, with both showing a low incidence. These findings underscore that hematoma formation in TOETVA can be managed effectively without routine drain use. Some researchers propose that enhanced visualization under endoscopy facilitates the detection of fine vasculature, thereby contributing to better hemostasis and potentially reducing the requirement for drainage [37, 38]. Similarly, seroma rates were comparable, with a 1.5% incidence in the drain group and 1.9% in the non-drain group, further supporting the notion that the absence of a drain does not increase fluid-related complications.

Another important reason why drains are commonly placed in TOETVA surgery is that it is considered a clean-contaminated procedure, and the placement of a drain is believed to help manage fluid accumulation, thereby reducing the risk of infection. The bacterial flora of the oral cavity is diverse, comprising both gram-positive aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. However, with perioperative antibiotic coverage targeting the polymicrobial flora of the mouth and aseptic preparation for surgery, the reported infection rates in TOETVA cases are negligible. Angkoon Anuwong et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 200 TOETVA cases, in which no postoperative infections were observed [39]. In a review of 28 TOETVA studies, Ellada Akritidou et al. observed an infection rate of 0.64%, with 12 infections among 1,887 cases [36]. Consistent with previous reports, our study data also indicate a low incidence of regional infections following TOETVA. Our study found no statistically significant difference in infection rates between the drain and non-drain groups. Notably, the only infection observed in the non-drain group was associated with a prolonged surgical duration of five hours, which may have introduced additional risks independent of drainage. This case highlights that the risk of infection in TOETVA may be more influenced by factors such as operative time and patient-specific characteristics than by the presence or absence of a drain.

There was no significant difference in pain levels between the two groups. Although patients with drains theoretically had the opportunity to experience discomfort related to the drain, such as sensations of pulling and minor irritation, our data did not record the number of cases of pain directly attributed to the drain. The non-drain group had a shorter hospital stay. On one hand, the drain group had to wait for the drainage output to be less than 40 ml within 24 h before removal and discharge, which was the main reason for the longer hospital stay. On the other hand, the absence of a drain seems to facilitate a faster recovery process.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, due to its retrospective design and single-center setting, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Second, although propensity score matching was performed to reduce selection bias, unmeasured confounding may still exist. Third, the majority of patients in our cohort had thyroid tumors ≤ 2 cm, while cases with tumors > 2 cm accounted for a small proportion. Therefore, the safety and feasibility of omitting drains in patients with larger tumors remain uncertain, and our conclusions should be interpreted with caution in that subgroup. Lastly, as drain placement required an additional axillary incision, cosmetic outcomes were not assessed and should be evaluated in future studies.

Another important limitation is the difference in surgical experience between the groups. From August 2021 to March 2023, procedures were performed when the surgical team had less experience, mainly corresponding to the drain group, whereas from April 2023 to September 2024, the team was more experienced, mainly corresponding to the non-drain group. Surgical experience may influence the incidence of complications, including hematoma, and thus the groups may not be entirely comparable.

Conclusion

In conclusion, for appropriately selected patients undergoing TOETVA—particularly those with thyroid tumors smaller than 2 cm—routine drain placement may not be necessary to reduce postoperative complications such as hematoma, seroma, or infection. Our findings suggest that omitting drains does not compromise safety and may promote faster recovery and shorter hospital stays. However, as the majority of patients in this study had small tumors, these results may not be generalizable to larger or more advanced thyroid lesions. Therefore, a selective and individualized approach to drain placement is recommended, taking into account tumor size and other patient-specific risk factors. Future prospective, multi-center studies with broader inclusion criteria are needed to validate these findings and further define optimal indications for drain use in TOETVA.
