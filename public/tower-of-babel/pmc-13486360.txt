Complete mitochondrial genome characterization of the haemosporidian parasite Nycteria heischi in bats Megaderma spasma in Thailand
Authors: Pattaranatcha Charnwichai, Juthathip Poofery, Dimas Novianto, Siwaporn Tuangpermsub, Thongchai Ngamprasertwong, Morakot Kaewthamasorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHaemosporidian parasites are vector-borne protozoans that infect a wide range of vertebrate and invertebrate hosts. Among mammals, bats harbor species of at least seven recognized haemosporidian genera, including Plasmodium, Polychromophilus, and Nycteria, highlighting the remarkable diversity of these parasites in chiropteran hosts. Thailand is a major biodiversity hotspot, supporting more than 146 bat species and their associated pathogens. This study investigated haemosporidian parasites in bats sampled between 2023 and 2025, and bat flies collected between 2019 and 2022 in Thailand. Bat flies that tested positive were further analyzed to determine the origin of blood meals. In addition, the complete mitochondrial genome of Nycteria heischi from Megaderma spasma from Thailand was characterized for the first time. A total of 405 samples, comprising 285 bats representing 19 species and 120 bat flies belonging to five species, were collected from nine sampling sites across four provinces of Thailand. Most samples were obtained from Phra Cave in Kanchanaburi Province, accounting for 67.16% (272/405) of all analyzed specimens. Haemosporidian infections were detected most frequently in samples from Manow Phee Cave, where the bat-specific prevalence reached 62.0% (31/50 bats). Among the host species examined, Myotis siligorensis exhibited the highest species-specific prevalence, with 71.79% (28/39; 95% CI: 55.13–85.00%) testing positive for haemosporidian parasites. Among the successfully sequenced samples from M. spasma, Nycteria spp. represented 88.9% (8/9) of the identified haemosporidian sequences. In addition, the bat fly Brachytarsina macrops tested positive for haemosporidian parasites, which were identified as N. heischi; however, host bat DNA was also detected in the same samples. This finding suggests that the bat flies had either recently fed on infected bats or retained parasite DNA from a previous blood meal. However, the present study does not provide evidence of established parasite infection within bat flies or their vector competence.Keywords: Haemosporidian, Bat, Bat fly, Nycteria, Mitochondrial genome

Introduction

Haemosporidian blood parasites are arthropod-borne protozoans belonging to the phylum Apicomplexa and order Haemosporida that infect a wide range of vertebrate hosts, including reptiles, birds, and mammals (Rosskopf et al., 2019; Musa, 2023; Pacheco and Escalante, 2023; Poofery et al., 2023). These parasites belong to multiple genera, and their occurrence in several host systems, particularly humans, has been extensively studied. Among mammals, bats (order Chiroptera) represent one of the most species-rich groups and are recognized as crucial hosts of diverse haemosporidian parasites (Schaer et al., 2015; Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020; Tsague et al., 2021).

With approximately 1511 recognized species worldwide, bats constitute the second-largest order within the class Mammalia and are the only mammals capable of sustained powered flight over long distances (Simmons and Cirranello, 2026). In addition to hosting a wide range of pathogens such as bacteria, viruses and blood parasites, bats also harbor diverse communities of hematophagous ectoparasites such as bat flies, mites, ticks, and bugs (Szentivanyi et al., 2019; Novianto et al., 2025; Russo and Dechmann, 2025; Soomro et al., 2025). Southeast Asia is a major hotspot of bat biodiversity, and Thailand alone supports more than 146 species spanning 11 families (Karnpun et al., 2023; Pratumthong et al., 2024). Despite this broad diversity, studies on bat-associated parasites remain limited, largely due to logistical challenges faced during sampling, as well as legal restrictions associated with bat conservation, the capture and handling of which require permits (Mickleburgh et al., 2002; Russo and Dechmann, 2025).

Nine genera of haemosporidian parasites have been reported from bats: Biguetiella, Bioccala, Dionisia, Hepatocystis, Johnsprentia, Nycteria, Plasmodium, Polychromophilus, and Sprattiella (e.g. Rosin et al., 1978; Landau et al., 1984; Tsague et al., 2021). However, Biguetiella and Bioccala are now regarded as synonyms of Polychromophilus (Witsenburg et al., 2012; Pacheco and Escalante, 2023; Ceballos-Perez et al., 2024). A large proportion of records describing these parasites are based primarily on blood smear examinations or histopathological observations, often without accompanying molecular characterization. For instance, five species of Nycteria infecting microchiropteran bats from Central Africa (Gabon and the Republic of the Congo) were described based on blood smear examination and histopathological observations (Rosin et al., 1978). Similarly, Hipposideros larvatus bats in Thailand were reported to be infected with two novel haemosporidian taxa, namely Nycteria brucechwatti and Biguetiella minuta, based on the observation of intrahepatocytic schizonts through histopathological examination (Landau et al., 1984). Even when such data are available, they are frequently limited to the mitochondrial cytochrome b (cytb) gene, due to which comprehensive genomic information across multiple loci is lacking. Consequently, the genetic diversity and evolutionary relationships of many haemosporidian taxa, such as N. brucechwatti and B. minuta previously documented in Thailand (Landau et al., 1984) remain unresolved. Species of Nycteria are considered strictly bat-associated; however, they remain largely understudied, particularly in Southeast Asia, where molecular investigations are still limited (Schaer et al., 2015; Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020). To date, only one peer-reviewed study has completely characterized mitochondrial genomes of Nycteria parasites, including N. medusiformis, N. gabonensis, Nycteria sp. from African bats, and Nycteria heischi from Cambodian bats (Karadjian et al., 2016). It should also be noted that Pacheco et al. (2026) investigated the evolutionary relationships among Nycteria lineages from Gabon and Sierra Leone together with Polychromophilus from Gabon to examine the existence of distinct bat-associated parasite clades. However, the corresponding GenBank data were not publicly available at the time this study was submitted. Nevertheless, molecular investigations of Nycteria spp. in Southeast Asia, particularly in Thailand, remain largely limited, leaving significant gaps in our understanding of its host range, genetic diversity, and transmission ecology.

Bat flies are obligate hematophagous ectoparasites of bats belonging to the order Diptera and represent key components of bat-associated parasite systems (Porter et al., 2022; Novianto et al., 2025). In bats, haemosporidian parasites such as Plasmodium, Hepatocystis, and Polychromophilus are transmitted by dipteran vectors from the families Culicidae, Ceratopogonidae, and Nycteribiidae, respectively (Witsenburg et al., 2012; Mathias et al., 2026). In contrast, the vectors that transmit Nycteria spp. parasites remain unknown, representing a critical knowledge gap regarding their life cycle and epidemiology.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to address these knowledge gaps by investigating the occurrence and genetic diversity of haemosporidian parasites in bats and their associated bat flies in Thailand, with a particular focus on the molecular characterization of Nycteria spp. using the cytb, clpC, and complete mitochondrial genome sequences. The results provide new insights into the diversity, phylogenetic relationships, and observed host associations of bat haemosporidian parasites in Southeast Asia.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, sampling sites, and morphological identification

This study was conducted as part of ongoing surveys on bat pathogens and bat-associated parasites, reported previously (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020; Riana et al., 2022; Novianto et al., 2025). Bats were sampled between 2023 and 2025, and bat flies were sampled between 2019 and 2022 from nine locations across four provinces in Thailand: Phra Cave (PC; 14°24′35.0″N, 98°51′13.0″E), Pomelo Farm (PF; 14°24′51.0″N, 98°51′04.0″E), Manow Phee Cave (MNP; 14°21′19.6″N, 98°56′14.1″E), and Ma Gleua Cave (MG; 14°21′31.0″N, 98°56′21.0″E) in Kanchanaburi Province; Koh Kham (KK; 12°34′19.0″N, 100°55′59.8″E), Koh Chan (KC; 12°31′18.0″N, 100°58′07.0″E), and Koh Samae San (KSS; 12°35′11.0″N, 100°56′50.0″E) in Chonburi Province; Phra Khayang Cave (PKY; 10°19′34.1″N, 98°45′55.2″E) in Ranong Province; and Lainan Research and Technology Transfer Station (LRTTS; 18°33′14.3″N, 100°47′33.2″E) in Nan Province (Fig. 1).

Geographical distribution of the nine study sites across four provinces in Thailand. The map was generated using ArcGIS v.10.8. Sampling sites are as follows: 1 PKY, Phra Khayang Cave (10°19′34.1″N, 98°45′55.2″E; n = 21); 2 KC, Koh Chan (12°31′18.0″N, 100°58′07.0″E; n = 5); 3 KK, Koh Kham (12°34′19.0″N, 100°55′59.8″E; n = 22); 4 KSS, Kon Samae San (12°35′11.0″N, 100°56′50.0″E; n = 2); 5 MNP, Manow Phee Cave (14°21′19.6″N, 98°56′14.1″E; n = 61); 6 MG, Ma Gleua Cave (14°21′31.0″N, 98°56′21.0″E; n = 20); 7 PC, Phra Cave (14°24′35.0″N, 98°51′13.0″E; n = 272); 8 PF, Pomelo Farm (14°24′51.0″N, 98°51′04.0″E; n = 1); and 9 LRTTS, Lainan Research and Technology Transfer Station (18°33′14.3″N, 100°47′33.2″E; n = 1). n denotes the total number of bat and bat fly samples examined at each sampling site.

Bats were captured with harp traps, tunnel nets, or mist nets, depending on the characteristics of each sampling site. Harp traps, which are generally more efficient and less stressful for capturing small bats in cluttered environments or at roost exits, were primarily deployed at cave entrances. Tunnel nets, designed as sleeve-like netting systems, were used in narrow passageways to intercept bat movement. Mist nets, which are more versatile for sampling open areas and large flyways, were positioned along flight paths. Collected bats were placed individually in cloth bags and identified to the family and species levels based on external morphological characteristics and echolocation calls, following previously described methods (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020; Tuangpermsub et al., 2025).

For blood collection, the sampling area was disinfected with cotton soaked in 70% ethanol. The uropatagial vein of the hind limb was punctured with a sterile 26G needle, and ∼10 μl of blood was collected using a capillary tube pretreated with the anticoagulant, acid citrate dextrose. All procedures followed a non-lethal approach (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020), and the bats were released immediately after sampling. Blood samples were maintained on dry ice during transportation.

Bat flies were collected from captured bats using fine forceps. Specimens were morphologically identified to the genus level based on external characteristics by following the taxonomic checklist of Thai Nycteribiidae and Streblidae (Samoh et al., 2021; Novianto et al., 2025). Each specimen was preserved individually in a 1.5-ml tube containing absolute ethanol and stored in an icebox during transport.

All specimens were transported to the Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University, and stored at −20 °C until further analysis. For each bat, data, including species, sex, body weight, and reproductive status, were recorded.

DNA extraction and identification of bat host and parasitic fly species

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted individually from bat blood samples using the NucleoSpin® Blood Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with a minor modification to the initial step as described by Arnuphapprasert et al. (2020). Briefly, after thawing, 10 μl of blood was treated with 100 μl of 0.15% (w/v) saponin (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) for 5 min to facilitate hemolysis. Samples were then centrifuged at 11,000× g for 5 min and washed with 500 μl of 1× PBS.

The bat fly gDNA from the entire specimen was extracted using the NucleoSpin® Tissue Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol, with a modified elution step to increase DNA concentration (Novianto et al., 2025). All DNA samples were stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

To confirm species identity and assess DNA quality, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to amplify the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene of bat and bat fly samples, followed by Sanger sequencing. Only the bat samples that could not be reliably identified based on morphology or echolocation were selected for cox1 amplification using the COI_VF1d and COI_VR1d primers (Ivanova et al., 2007). In contrast, DNA of all bat fly samples was amplified using the LepF1 and LepR1 primers (Hebert et al., 2004).

PCR was performed in a total volume of 12.5 μl containing 6.25 μl of 2× KOD Fx Neo Buffer, 2.5 μl of dNTP mixture, 0.375 μl of each primer (10 pmol/μl), 1.75 μl of sterile distilled water, 0.25 μl of KOD Fx Neo polymerase (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), and 1 μl of DNA template. Amplification was conducted on a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Primer sequences and thermal cycling conditions are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Amplicons were visualized on 1.5% (w/v) agarose gels stained with RedSafe™ Nucleic Acid Staining Solution (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam-si, Korea) and electrophoresed at 100 V for 40 min. Target bands were excised, purified using the NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany), and Sanger sequenced bidirectionally at U2Bio Co., Ltd. (Seoul, South Korea).

Haemosporidian parasite detection in bat and bat fly samples

Haemosporidian parasite DNA, including Plasmodium, Nycteria, Polychromophilus, and Hepatocystis, was amplified from bat and bat fly samples using PCR assays targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome b (cytb) and the apicoplast caseinolytic protease C (clpC) genes, following previously described protocols (Martinsen et al., 2008; Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020; Poofery et al., 2023). Genomic DNA from bats previously confirmed to be haemosporidian-positive (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020) was used as a positive control, while sterile distilled water served as a negative control. PCR was performed using the primer sets Pan_Haemos_CytBF/Pan_Haemos_CytBR and clpc-F/clpc-R on a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Detailed thermal cycling conditions are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Amplified PCR products were visualized on agarose gels. Only samples yielding moderate-to-high-intensity bands were selected for further analysis. These products were excised from the gel, purified, and subsequently Sanger sequenced.

Amplification of the mitochondrial genome of Nycteria isolated from captured bats

Samples that tested positive for Nycteria spp. in the present study, as confirmed by cytb and/or clpC sequencing analyses, together with previously identified Nycteria-positive samples from Arnuphapprasert et al. (2020), were selected for complete mitochondrial genome amplification. Sample selection was based on the availability and quality of DNA templates suitable for long-range PCR amplification. Nested PCR was employed to amplify the mitochondrial genome using Takara LA Taq polymerase (TakaraMirus Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan). Primary PCR reactions (12.5 μl) contained 1.5 μl of DNA template and the Nyc_hesMtOF/Nyc_hesMtOR primer pair. Subsequently, 3 μl of the primary PCR product was used as a template for a 25-μl nested PCR reaction using the Nyc_hesMtIF/Nyc_hesMtIR primers. Thermal cycling employed a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA); primer sequences and cycling conditions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Amplicons (∼6 kb) were purified and cloned into the pGEM®-T Easy vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Colony PCR used the M13_F (−20) and M13_R primers to confirm the insertions. At least three positive clones per sample were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform with BTSeq™ MiSeq Reagent Kits v.2 (Illumina, San Diego, USA) at U2Bio Co., Ltd. The multiple clones analyzed for each sample represent technical replicates generated to verify sequence accuracy and minimize errors introduced during PCR amplification or cloning; they should not be interpreted as independent biological replicates. Any single nucleotide mutation identified during sequence alignment was confirmed by re-sequencing to distinguish genuine sequence variation from PCR or cloning artifacts.

Detection of vertebrate host blood meals in bat flies

Bat flies that tested positive for haemosporidian parasites were further analyzed to identify traces of vertebrate host blood meals. The vertebrate mitochondrial cox1was amplified using the VertCOI_7194_F and Mod_RepCOI_R primers (Reeves et al., 2018; Supplementary Table S1), following the protocol described by Soomro et al. (2025). PCR and gel visualization were performed as described above.

Analyses of the sequences of cytb, clpC, and complete mitochondrial genomes of Nycteria heischi

Only high-quality chromatograms were analyzed, whereas sequences with ambiguous signals were excluded. Raw sequence data were manually inspected, edited, and trimmed based on chromatogram quality. Forward and reverse reads were assembled into consensus sequences and aligned with other sequences generated in this study using BioEdit v.7.7.1.0 (Hall, 2011). Any singleton mutations, when detected, were confirmed by resequencing independently amplified PCR products. Protein-coding genes (PCGs) were translated into amino acid sequences employing the ExPASy translation tool to verify the absence of any internal stop codons. Species identity was confirmed via BLASTN searches against the NCBI database.

Mitochondrial genomes were annotated using the GeSeq platform, with those of N. heischi (GenBank: KX090648) and N. gabonensis (GenBank: KX090647) serving as reference genomes (Tillich et al., 2017). Genome maps were visualized using SnapGene® Viewer v.8.2.1. Nucleotide diversity across mitochondrial genome sequences was analyzed with DnaSP v.6.0 and a sliding-window approach (window length: 90 bp; step size: 3 bp) (Rozas, 2009). Reference sequences were retrieved from the GenBank database. Only bat-associated haemosporidian parasite sequences containing complete datasets for all target loci (cytb, clpC, and the complete mitochondrial genome) were included in the comparative analyses (Supplementary Table S2). In addition, only reference sequences comparable in length to the newly generated sequences were retained to ensure consistency across analyses.

Tripartite network, TCS haplotype network analysis, and pairwise genetic distances analysis

A tripartite network incorporating sampling locations, parasite species, and host species (bats or bat flies) was constructed using SankeyMATIC (https://sankeymatic.com/build/) to visualize the observed associations among sampling locations, host types, and parasite species. Haplotypes were identified from aligned DNA sequences using DnaSP v.6.0 based on nucleotide polymorphisms (Librado and Rozas, 2009). Genetic relationships among parasite haplotypes were then inferred using Templeton, Crandall, and Sing (TCS) haplotype networks generated in PopART v.1.7 (Leigh and Bryant, 2015), incorporating sequences generated in this study together with reference sequences listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Pairwise genetic distances among eight mitochondrial protein-coding gene (PCG) sequences were estimated using the p-distance model implemented in MEGA 12 (Kumar et al., 2024). Reference sequences of N. heischi and N. gabonensis, together with representative bat haemosporidian parasites belonging to the genera Hepatocystis, Plasmodium, and Polychromophilus, were included in comparative analyses. In addition, representative sequences of non-bat haemosporidian parasites infecting avian hosts, non-human primates, humans, rodents, and ungulates were included, as the latter two host groups are phylogenetically closely related to bat-associated Plasmodium and Polychromophilus lineages (Poofery et al., 2023; Werb et al., 2024).

Phylogenetic analysis

The sequences generated in this study, together with reference sequences retrieved from GenBank (Supplementary Table S2), were aligned using ClustalW implemented in BioEdit. Representative bat-associated parasite sequences from across the globe, with sequence lengths comparable to those obtained in this study, were selected for analysis; the genus Leucocytozoon was included as the outgroup. Phylogenetic relationships among parasites were reconstructed based on complete mitochondrial genome sequences and concatenated PCG datasets. Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic analyses were performed in MEGA 12 (Kumar et al., 2024), with the best-fit evolutionary models determined utilizing MODELTEST.

For analyzing the mitochondrial genomes, the GTR+G+I model was selected, and tree robustness was assessed using 1000 bootstrap replicates. For partial gene assessments, cytb, clpC, and concatenated cytb + clpC datasets were analyzed individually. The best-fit models were GTR+G+I for cytb and T92+G+I for clpC and the concatenated datasets, each with 1000 bootstrap replicates. All phylogenetic trees were visualized in FigTree v.1.4.4.

Statistical analysis

Infection prevalence was calculated as the number of PCR-positive samples divided by the total number of samples examined within each study group (e.g. province, sampling site, host species, or bat fly taxon). Proportions were used solely to describe the composition of infected or successfully sequenced samples, with the corresponding denominator explicitly stated for each analysis. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using the exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson) method implemented in Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Results

Sampling of bats and bat flies and spatial distribution of haemosporidian parasite prevalence

A total of 405 samples, comprising 285 bats collected between 2023 and 2025 and 120 bat flies collected between 2019 and 2022, were obtained from nine sampling sites across four provinces in Thailand (Fig. 1). Prior to this study, haemosporidian parasites had not been investigated in Chonburi or Ranong Provinces. Most samples originated from Kanchanaburi Province in western Thailand, accounting for 354 of 405 specimens (87.41% of total analyzed samples), including 235 bats and 119 bat flies collected from four sampling sites. Chonburi Province contributed the second-largest number of samples (29/405 bats, 7.16%), followed by Ranong Province (21/405 bats, 5.19%) and Nan Province (1/405 bat flies, 0.25%). At the site level, Phra Cave (PC) yielded the greatest number of samples (272/405, 67.16%), comprising 184 bats and 88 bat flies, followed by Manow Phee Cave (MNP) (61/405, 15.06%), with 50 bats and 11 bat flies. Both sites are located in Kanchanaburi Province (Table 1).

Combined morphological and molecular identification recognized 24 host taxa, including 19 bat species and five bat fly species, across all sampling sites. Among bats, Hipposideros gentilis was the most frequently sampled species in both Kanchanaburi Province (133/235, 56.60%) and Phra Cave (129/184, 70.11%). Among bat flies, Raymondia sp. was the predominant taxon in Kanchanaburi Province (81/119, 68.07%) and Phra Cave (70/88, 79.55%).

PCR screening identified haemosporidian parasites in 68 of 405 samples, yielding an overall infection prevalence of 16.79% (95% CI: 13.28–20.79%), including 62 bats and six bat flies. The highest number of infected samples was recorded in Kanchanaburi Province (62/354 combined bat and bat-fly samples; 17.51%, 95% CI: 13.70–21.88%), comprising 56 bats and six bat flies. At the site level, Manow Phee Cave exhibited the highest infection prevalence, with 31 of 61 samples testing positive (combined bat and bat-fly samples) (50.82%, 95% CI: 37.70–63.86%).

Among the 24 identified host taxa, haemosporidian parasites were detected in nine (37.50%, 95% CI: 18.80–59.41%), including seven bat species and two bat fly taxa. Infected bat species included Myotis siligorensis (28/68 infected samples; 41.18%, 95% CI: 29.37–53.77%), Hipposideros gentilis (16/68; 23.53%, 95% CI: 14.09–35.38%), Megaderma spasma (9/68; 13.24%, 95% CI: 6.23–23.64%), Taphozous melanopogon (6/68; 8.82%, 95% CI: 3.31–18.22%), Miniopterus magnater (1/68; 1.47%, 95% CI: 0.04–7.92%), Myotis ricketti (1/68; 1.47%, 95% CI: 0.04–7.92%), and Hipposideros larvatus (1/68; 1.47%, 95% CI: 0.04–7.92%). Haemosporidian parasites were also detected in two bat fly taxa, Raymondia sp. (5/68; 7.35%, 95% CI: 2.43–16.33%) and Brachytarsina macrops (1/68; 1.47%, 95% CI: 0.04–7.92%). The latter was previously reported as Brachytarsina sp. 2 but has since been formally identified as Brachytarsina macrops (Rattananupong et al., 2026). A summary of sample distribution and infection status across all sampling locations is provided in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S3.

Haemosporidian species identification, tripartite network, and mitochondrial genome annotation

Molecular detection and Sanger sequencing of the cytb and clpC genes were performed to identify haemosporidian parasites. A total of 68 samples tested positive by PCR (Supplementary Table S4). Of these, 39 (57.35%, 95% CI: 44.77–69.28%), including 36 bat and three bat fly samples, yielded high-quality sequencing chromatograms suitable for downstream analyses. Among the successfully sequenced samples, 14 (35.90%, 95% CI: 21.20–52.82%) generated high-quality sequences for both cytb and clpC, 19 (48.72%, 95% CI: 32.42–65.22%) yielded cytb sequences only, and six (15.39%, 95% CI: 5.86–30.53%) yielded clpC sequences only.

Analysis of the cytb sequences identified 33 haemosporidian sequences belonging to two genera: Nycteria (12/33; 36.36%, 95% CI: 20.40–54.88%), comprising 11 bat samples and one bat fly sample, and Polychromophilus (21/33; 63.64%, 95% CI: 45.12–79.60%), comprising 20 bat samples and one bat fly sample. The Nycteria sequences included Nycteria heischi (7/12; 58.33%, 95% CI: 27.67–84.83%) and unidentified Nycteria spp. (5/12; 41.67%, 95% CI: 15.17–72.33%). The Polychromophilus sequences comprised Polychromophilus melanipherus (1/21; 4.76%, 95% CI: 0.12–23.82%), Polychromophilus murinus (18/21; 85.71%, 95% CI: 63.66–96.95%), and unidentified Polychromophilus spp. (2/21; 9.52%, 95% CI: 1.17–30.38%).

Analysis of the 20 clpC sequences identified three haemosporidian genera: Nycteria (4/20; 20.00%, 95% CI: 5.73–43.66%), Polychromophilus (14/20; 70.00%, 95% CI: 45.72–88.11%), and Hepatocystis (2/20; 10.00%, 95% CI: 1.23–31.70%). The Nycteria sequences were all classified as unidentified Nycteria spp. (4/4; 100.00%, 95% CI: 39.76–100%). The Polychromophilus sequences comprised P. melanipherus (1/14; 7.14%, 95% CI: 0.18–33.87%), P. murinus (4/14; 28.57%, 95% CI: 8.39–58.10%), and unidentified Polychromophilus spp. (9/14; 64.29%, 95% CI: 35.14–87.24%). The remaining two sequences were classified as unidentified Hepatocystis spp. (2/2; 100%, 95% CI: 15.81–100%). A complete summary of all PCR-positive samples and their molecular identifications is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

The observed associations among sampling sites, parasite taxa, and host types were visualized using a tripartite network analysis (Fig. 2). Myotis siligorensis was the host species most frequently associated with haemosporidian infection and predominantly harbored P. murinus (18/28; 64.29%, 95% CI: 44.07–81.36%). Likewise, most Megaderma spasma individuals were infected with Nycteria spp. (8/9; 88.89%, 95% CI: 51.75–99.72%). Nycteria spp. were detected in M. spasma, Taphozous melanopogon, Hipposideros gentilis, Myotis siligorensis, and the bat fly Brachytarsina macrops. In contrast, Polychromophilus spp. were identified in Miniopterus magnater, H. gentilis, My. siligorensis, My. ricketti, and the bat fly Raymondia sp., whereas Hepatocystis spp. were detected only in Hipposideros larvatus.

Blood-meal analysis confirmed the presence of vertebrate host DNA in all haemosporidian-positive bat flies, as each specimen was also PCR-positive for vertebrate cox1 fragments (Supplementary Fig. S1). These findings suggest that the ectoparasites had recently fed on infected hosts and, consequently, may have acquired parasite DNA during blood-feeding. However, the detection of parasite DNA alone does not demonstrate established infection within the bat flies or their vector competence.

A total of eight distinct mitochondrial genome haplotypes of N. heischi, ranging from 5993 to 6007 bp in length, were obtained from two of the ten M. spasma individuals examined, corresponding to a 20.0% success rate for long-range PCR amplification and cloning. Genetic distance and pairwise identity matrix analyses of mitochondrial PCG sequences

Pairwise genetic distance analysis based on mitochondrial protein-coding gene (PCG) sequences revealed relatively low levels of nucleotide divergence among the N. heischi haplotypes, with p-distance values ranging from 0 to 0.02, indicating high genetic similarity within the species. In contrast, comparisons between N. heischi and N. gabonensis showed higher divergence values ranging from 0.05 to 0.06, supporting clear genetic differentiation between the two Nycteria species. The reference sequence of N. gabonensis consistently exhibited greater genetic distance from all N. heischi lineages, whereas intra-lineage divergence within N. heischi remained comparatively low (Fig. 6A).

B Eight Nycteria heischi haplotype mitochondrial protein-coding gene (PCG) sequences compared with other haemosporidian references. Comparisons with other haemosporidian parasites demonstrated substantially greater genetic divergence. Pairwise distances between Nycteria and Hepatocystis spp. ranged from 0.12 to 0.17, representing the highest divergence values observed in this dataset. In contrast, comparisons with Plasmodium and Polychromophilus murinus showed intermediate divergence values ranging from 0.08 to 0.10. Among the Plasmodium taxa, relatively low divergence was observed between Plasmodium caprae and P. bubalis (0.03), as well as among several avian Plasmodium species (0.04–0.08), indicating closer evolutionary relationships within the genus (Fig. 6B). Overall, these genetic distance patterns were consistent with the pairwise identity matrix analysis generated using the SDT, which demonstrated high nucleotide similarity within N. heischi lineages and progressively lower similarity among distinct haemosporidian genera.

Phylogenetic relationships of haemosporidian parasites from bats and bat flies

ML phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using two datasets: complete mitochondrial genomes and concatenated three PCGs of N. heischi obtained from M. spasma in this study, together with reference sequences of haemosporidian parasites belonging to the family Plasmodiidae retrieved from GenBank.

Although the two sets produced slightly varying tree topologies, all N. heischi sequences generated in the present study consistently clustered within the same clade as the reference sequence for N. heischi from M. spasma originating from Cambodia (GenBank: KX090648). The mitochondrial genome-based phylogeny further indicated that the newly generated sequences for N. heischi shared a recent ancestor with N. gabonensis (GenBank: KX090647) identified from an African bat (R. alcyone).

In contrast, the phylogenetic tree based on the concatenated three PCG dataset revealed two major clades within haemosporidian parasites, separating N. heischi from other taxa. Nycteria gabonensis formed a sister clade with Hepatocystis spp., distinct from the N. heischi lineage. Within the N. heischi clade, the eight haplotypes identified in this study were further divided into two subclades: one comprising six N. heischi sequences, and another consisting of two sequences (GenBank: OP380895 and OP380898) that formed a sister lineage to the Cambodian N. heischi sequence. Additionally, N. medusiformis and Nycteria sp. were more distantly related within this genus (Fig. 7A).

Trees were constructed based on the sequences of the complete mitochondrial genome and concatenated three protein-coding genes (3 PCGs) of Nycteria spp. (A), concatenated cytb + clpC sequences (B), and clpC sequences (C). The best-fit evolutionary models were applied for each dataset, with 1000 bootstrap replicates used to assess node support. To further resolve the phylogenetic relationships among haemosporidian parasites detected in this study, additional ML trees were constructed using partial gene datasets, including cytb, clpC, and concatenated cytb + clpC. Sequences of Leucocytozoon spp. (GenBank: EU254518, EU254520, EU254609, and EU254611) were used as the outgroups. These analyses consistently showed that all haemosporidian sequences generated in this study clustered with the reference sequences belonging to their respective taxa. The cytb-based phylogenetic tree revealed two primary clades, separating Nycteria spp. and Polychromophilus spp. from Plasmodium spp. and Hepatocystis spp.; it also confirmed the presence of N. heischi in bat fly samples (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Similarly, the clpC- and cytb + clpC-based trees demonstrated a clear phylogenetic separation between Nycteria spp. and Polychromophilus spp. Moreover, clpC-based phylogeny indicated that Hepatocystis sp. sequences generated in this study clustered with other Hepatocystis reference sequences but formed a distinct subclade relative to sequences previously reported from Nigeria, South Sudan, and the USA. Despite such divergence, all Hepatocystis sequences shared a common ancestral lineage with Nycteria spp. (Fig. 7B and C).

Discussion

Studies of haemosporidian parasites in Thai bats have been sporadic and geographically limited, resulting in fragmented datasets and limited molecular data available in public databases. These limitations have hindered a comprehensive understanding of parasite diversity, observed host associations, and host-parasite relationships (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020; Tsague et al., 2021). The present study expands current knowledge of bat haemosporidian parasites in Thailand by investigating their geographical distribution, host diversity, infection prevalence, and observed host-parasite associations across multiple regions of the country. In addition, this study provides the first complete mitochondrial genome characterization of N. heischi from Thailand.

In this study, bats (sampled between 2023 and 2025) and bat flies (sampled between 2019 and 2022) were collected from nine study sites across four regions of Thailand: northern, western, southern, and eastern. Among these, the southern province of Ranong and the eastern province of Chonburi had never been included in investigations before this study. Among all bat species sampled, H. gentilis was most frequently captured, particularly in Kanchanaburi. This species belongs to the family Hipposideridae (Old World leaf-nosed bats), a prominent bat taxon widely distributed across Southeast Asia (Yuzefovich et al., 2021). Similarly, Raymondia sp. was the most abundant bat fly species identified; it parasitizes H. gentilis (Poon et al., 2023). Hipposideros gentilis is typically associated with a limited diversity of bat fly species, and other nycteribiid flies are rarely found on this host (Poon et al., 2023). Such an ecological association likely explains the high abundance of Raymondia sp. observed in this study.

Despite the high abundance of H. gentilis, the greatest proportion of haemosporidian-infected samples was observed in My. siligorensis. This finding suggests that host abundance does not necessarily correspond to parasite occurrence and highlights the potential influence of host ecology on infection patterns. However, because bat and bat fly samples were collected during different time periods, ecological interpretations of site-specific associations among host types, parasites, and sampling locations should be made with caution.

Notably, no haemosporidian parasites were detected in samples collected from Ranong Province in southern Thailand. This observation is consistent with a previous study that likewise failed to detect haemosporidian parasites in bats from Songkhla Province, southern Thailand (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020). Similarly, no positive samples were identified from Nan Province in northern Thailand. However, this finding is more likely attributable to the limited sample size from this locality than to the true absence of infection.

This study identified three genera of haemosporidian parasites: Nycteria, Polychromophilus, and Hepatocystis. Among these, Polychromophilus spp. were the most frequently detected, particularly P. murinus, which was predominantly identified in My. siligorensis. This finding suggests a broad geographical distribution of P. murinus and is consistent with previous studies from Brazil, Thailand, and Europe demonstrating that bats of the genus Myotis commonly serve as hosts for this parasite (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020; Mathias et al., 2023; Timm et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the host associations reported here should be interpreted cautiously, as only samples yielding high-quality sequence chromatograms were included in the molecular analyses. Furthermore, demonstrating host specificity requires broader taxonomic sampling and more rigorous ecological investigations. Therefore, the present dataset should be regarded as observational because it is constrained by uneven host representation and sequencing success.

In contrast, most M. spasma individuals were infected with Nycteria spp., consistent with previous reports indicating that species of Nycteria commonly parasitize this bat species (Schaer et al., 2015; Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020). However, Nycteria parasites have also been reported from a variety of other bat taxa. In addition, Hepatocystis sp. infections were detected in H. larvatus. A post-blood-feeding Brachytarsina macrops bat fly also tested positive for N. heischi; however, host bat DNA was detected in the same specimen. Consequently, the detection of Nycteria DNA in bat flies indicates that these ectoparasites had either recently fed on infected bats or retained parasite DNA from a previous blood meal, but it does not demonstrate established parasite infection or vector competence. Moreover, bat flies were not dissected to examine the presence of parasite oocysts or sporozoites in the salivary glands, representing an important limitation of this study. Therefore, additional investigations integrating parasite developmental stages within bat flies and experimental transmission studies are required to clarify their vector competence and role in the life cycle of bat haemosporidian parasites.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to characterize eight complete mitochondrial genome haplotypes of N. heischi obtained from two M. spasma individuals in Thailand. The mitochondrial genomes exhibited the same genomic organization previously reported for N. gabonensis and N. heischi but differed from those of N. medusiformis and unidentified Nycteria spp. Haplotype analyses based on complete mitochondrial genomes and concatenated protein-coding genes (PCGs) identified eight distinct haplotypes corresponding to the eight N. heischi variants detected in this study. All haplotypes were genetically distinct from previously reported Cambodian N. heischi sequences, as well as from other Nycteria species. Furthermore, haplotype analysis based on the cytb gene demonstrated both intra- and interspecific variation among host species, indicating genetic variation within haemosporidian parasites (Novianto et al., 2025; Mathias et al., 2026). In contrast, pairwise genetic distance analysis based on concatenated PCG sequences revealed high genetic similarity among the eight N. heischi haplotypes, although these haplotypes still exhibited intermediate to high divergence relative to other haemosporidian taxa. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the N. heischi mitochondrial sequences were obtained from only two M. spasma individuals, partly due to the technical challenges associated with long-range PCR amplification and cloning. Therefore, the observed intraspecific mitochondrial variation may not fully represent the diversity present within the parasite population, particularly among haplotypes with low parasitemia or low population frequency. Moreover, alternative explanations for the observed sequence variation, including mixed infections, intra-host mitochondrial variation, PCR artifacts, or cloning and sequencing errors, cannot be completely excluded. However, these possibilities were minimized through rigorous quality control procedures, including independent resequencing to confirm all singleton mutations. Additional sampling of N. heischi from broader geographical regions and multiple bat hosts will be necessary to determine the extent and biological significance of mitochondrial diversity of this parasite. Phylogenetic analyses based on complete mitochondrial genomes and concatenated PCGs produced trees with slightly different topologies, particularly in the placement of N. gabonensis. Such discrepancies are likely attributable to variations in dataset size and phylogenetic resolution. Complete-genome datasets generally provide more robust and stable phylogenetic signals as they incorporate larger amounts of sequence data and may include coding and non-coding regions. In contrast, analyses based solely on PCGs may lack sufficient resolution to distinguish closely related lineages (Yokono et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024). Despite these differences, both datasets consistently placed the Thai N. heischi sequences within the same clade as N. heischi from Cambodia. Interestingly, the eight Thai haplotypes formed two different nested branches, one containing six and the other comprising two sequences (GenBank: OP380895 and OP380898), which formed a sister clade to the Cambodian N. heischi isolate. These results suggest genetic variations and intra-specific diversity within N. heischi infecting the same host species, a pattern similar to the broad genetic diversity reported for Polychromophilus spp. in eastern Europe (Sandor et al., 2021).

Phylogenetic analyses based on partial gene sequences further clearly separated Nycteria spp. and Polychromophilus spp. Notably, trees constructed using clpC and cytb + clpC datasets indicated a sister relationship between Nycteria spp. and Hepatocystis spp. Previous phylogenetic analyses on Hepatocystis parasites from Australian Pteropus bats suggested that parasites from Australia and Asia form a lineage separate from those infecting primates and bats in Africa and other regions of Asia (Schaer et al., 2018). In contrast, the Hepatocystis sequences identified in this study clustered with the available Hepatocystis sequences but formed a distinct subclade relative to reference sequences from Nigeria, South Sudan, and the USA.

This study has several limitations. Sampling effort was uneven across locations, and some samples yielded incomplete genetic data, which may have limited the resolution of parasite diversity and prevalence estimates. In this study, we acknowledge that bat and bat fly samples were collected during different time periods, with bats sampled between 2023 and 2025 and bat flies collected between 2019 and 2022. This temporal mismatch may complicate the interpretation of contemporary associations among host type (bats or bat flies), parasites, and sampling sites. Consequently, ecological interpretations of associations among host types, sampling sites, and parasites should be approached with caution. Nevertheless, the findings presented provide important new data that help fill existing knowledge gaps regarding haemosporidian parasites in bats and bat flies, particularly Nycteria spp. parasites. Furthermore, this study updates current knowledge of infection prevalence among bat species in Thailand.

Conclusions

The present study confirmed the infection of bats with haemosporidian parasites across various locations in Thailand and investigated the prevalence of host and parasite species, providing genetic data via public databases for further study. Moreover, these findings unveiled the relationships among haemosporidian parasites within the Plasmodiidae, especially the genus Nycteria, which provides vital information on the distribution.
